## What the reviewer found

Three things, none of them a false thesis:

1. **The maximalist reading is dismissed as "shallow reading" without being
   named as a classical position.** Ibn Kathīr's own edition records it: the
   verse "was called the Āyah of the Sword, about which Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk bin Muzāḥim
   said, *it abrogated every agreement of peace*," plus al-ʿAwfī's report from
   Ibn ʿAbbās that no idolator retained a treaty after Barāʾah. A reader who
   later meets that in Ibn Kathīr will conclude the file hid it.
2. **The scope is narrowed in a way the sources do not carry.** The file
   repeatedly conditions the command on "declared hostility" / "the active
   hostile party". Al-Ṭabarī defines the targets by **treaty status** — those
   with no treaty, those who broke one, those whose term expired.
3. **No tadabbur-vs-fatwā line**, on the most contested passage in the Qurʾān.

The reviewer's key observation: the honest version is *stronger*. Al-Ṭabarī
records the dispute over whether 9:6 was abrogated **and rules that it was
not** — which is direct classical support for the asylum reading the file is
already arguing. The file left its best evidence on the table.

## Proposed insertion

Placement: after "This is not softness replacing seriousness… In between stands
the hard command…", before "The hook of the passage is this:".

Also change, in that same paragraph, "those who remain in declared hostility
after the sacred interval passes" → "those left with no treaty once the sacred
interval passes" (matching al-Ṭabarī's own criterion).

> ### What the commentators actually disagreed about
>
> It would be dishonest to present this passage as though the tradition read it
> in one voice. It did not, and the disagreement is worth knowing before anyone
> quotes the verse at you.
>
> Ibn Kathīr records that 9:5 came to be called the Verse of the Sword, and he
> preserves Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Muzāḥim saying it abrogated every peace agreement,
> alongside a report from Ibn ʿAbbās that no idolator held a treaty after
> Barāʾah was announced. That is a real classical position, held by real
> authorities, and it reads the verse far more broadly than this reflection
> does.
>
> Al-Ṭabarī sets the boundary differently. For him the question is not who is
> behaving with hostility but who stands in what treaty relationship: those who
> never had a pact, those who broke one, those whose fixed term has run out.
> That is a narrower criterion than "enemies," and a more precise one.
>
> And on the verse that matters most for how this passage ends, al-Ṭabarī
> records that some held 9:6 abrogated too — and rules against them. The asylum
> command stands. The escort stands. That is not this reflection's wishful
> reading imposed on the text; it is the judgment of the commentator who
> examined the abrogation claim and rejected it.
>
> Notice also what sits immediately before the command. Verse 4 exempts the
> treaty partners who kept faith — *complete their treaty to their term* — and
> both al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr stress that unbroken agreements were honoured
> to their end. The limit on verse 5 is not supplied by commentary. It is the
> verse directly above it.
>
> None of this is a legal ruling, and nothing here settles a question of war or
> treaty for anyone. This is a tadabbur: it reads what the passage says and
> reports where the commentators divided. For rulings, ask a qualified scholar.

## Why this shape

- **Names the opposing position with its authorities**, so the file cannot be
  accused of hiding it — Noor's adversarial-quotation standard.
- **Does not adjudicate.** Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk is reported, not refuted. Al-Ṭabarī's
  ruling on 9:6 is reported as his ruling.
- **Adds evidence rather than hedging.** 9:4 and al-Ṭabarī's 9:6 judgment make
  the file's own argument better, which is why the omission was a loss.
- **Ends with the tadabbur/fatwā line** in the file's own register.
