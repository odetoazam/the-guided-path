The passage is ahkam-bearing (kinship dues, definition of squandering, the spending balance) and the file acknowledges no ikhtilaf anywhere and never distinguishes tadabbur from ruling. al-Tabari's commentary on 17:26 opens with the words 'اختلف أهل التأويل في المعنيّ بقوله ﴿وآتِ ذَا القُرْبى﴾' and lays out two positions -- one's own paternal/maternal kin (al-Hasan, Ikrimah, Ibn Abbas) versus the kin of the Messenger (the report from Ali b. al-Husayn) -- before ruling for the first. He records a second ikhtilaf immediately after on ibn al-sabil: whether the due is general assistance or specifically three days' hosting ('وقد قيل: إنما عنى بالأمر بإتيان ابن السبيل حقه أن يضاف ثلاثة أيام'). The file presents one settled reading of both. Given the flagged tabdhir gap above, a reader could take this file as a spending ruling.
  > The ayah is teaching that wealth is never purely private in meaning. It carries claims upon it.
- **minor**: An obligation-shaped claim stated with no anchor, and the sources pull in two directions the file does not mention. Supporting it: al-Tabari's report of al-Hasan, asked whether zakah may be given to relatives -- 'إن لهم في ذلك لحقا سوى الزكاة' (they have a right in that besides zakah); and Ibn Kathir's hadith of the man from Banu Tamim ('وَتَعْرِفُ حَقَّ السَّائِلِ وَالْجَارِ وَالْمِسْكِينِ'). Limiting it: al-Jalalayn glosses حقه as 'مِن البِرّ والصِّلَة' -- his due of kindness and kinship-maintenance, i.e. moral rather than legally enforceable -- and al-Muyassar likewise reads it as ihsan and birr. The file cites neither side, so the reader cannot tell whether 'owed' means enforceable.
  > That word `haqqahu` matters. The ayah does not frame this first as optional generosity. It frames it as a right. Something owed. Something fitting. Something not invented by your mood.
- **minor**: The gloss on qawlan maysura is confined to gentleness of manner and drops the concrete content the mufassirun assign to it. Ibn Kathir: '(then, speak unto them a soft, kind word.) meaning, with a promise. This was the opinion of Mujahid, Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Qatadah and others.' The classical reading is that the easy word is a promise to give once Allah provides -- which also explains the ayah's own 'ibtigha'a rahmatin min rabbika tarjuha'. Nothing in the file is false here; the omission just costs the section its strongest classical support.
