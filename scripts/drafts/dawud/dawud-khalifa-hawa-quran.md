<article class="prose-blog">
  <p class="text-lg leading-relaxed">Of everyone the Quran describes holding power — kings, commanders, a man given the reach of the horizons, a prophet whose kingdom included the wind — one individual is addressed with the singular word <em>khalifa</em>. It happens in Surah Sad, immediately after the worst moment of his life, and the title arrives welded to a prohibition.</p>

  <h2>The charge</h2>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" data-ayah="38:26" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">يَـٰدَاوُۥدُ إِنَّا جَعَلْنَـٰكَ خَلِيفَةً فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَٱحْكُم بَيْنَ ٱلنَّاسِ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَلَا تَتَّبِعِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ فَيُضِلَّكَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يَضِلُّونَ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌۢ بِمَا نَسُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْحِسَابِ</p>
    <p class="translation">"O Dawud, We have made you a khalifa on the earth, so judge between people with truth, and follow not desire, for it will mislead you from the path of God. Indeed those who stray from the path of God will have a severe punishment for having forgotten the Day of Reckoning."</p>
    <cite>Sad (38:26)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <p>Al-Jalalayn glosses the title in four words: <em>manage the affairs of the people</em>. Al-Muyassar renders it as being given charge and given dominion, then reads the rest as an instruction about courtrooms — judge with justice and fair dealing, and let no leaning enter the rulings. Ibn Kathir opens his comment by widening the address past the man being spoken to: this is counsel from God to everyone in a position of authority.</p>

  <p>So the tradition receives 38:26 as the Quran's clearest statement of what public power is for. What arrives with it is a single prohibition, and the prohibition is the part worth slowing down on, because of the word it uses.</p>

  <h2>A word built out of falling</h2>

  <!-- morphology:38:26:24 root=هوي pos=N -->
  <!-- morphology:38:26:8 root=خلف pos=N -->
  <p><strong>ٱلْهَوَىٰ</strong>, <em>al-hawa</em>, is usually translated "desire," which sends an English reader toward appetite — wanting things, indulgence, the pull of pleasure. The Arabic root sits somewhere colder. <strong>هوي</strong> describes falling: descending, dropping, plunging downward. It is the physics word. A thing let go of does <em>hawa</em>. The same root gives the Quran its picture of a heart that plummets and of the star at the opening of Surah An-Najm as it goes down.</p>

  <p>From that motion the language builds its word for personal inclination — and the metaphor inside it is precise. <em>Hawa</em> names what appetite does to a person who has stopped holding themselves up. It names the direction you go when nothing is bearing your weight. A preference has slope. Leaning is the beginning of falling, and Arabic files them under one root.</p>

  <p>Read the prohibition again with the physical sense restored: <em>do not follow the falling, for it will mislead you off the road.</em> The verse then repeats the verb of straying twice more, so the sentence carries one image throughout — a man on a path, a slope beside it, and a warning about which way his weight is going.</p>

  <p>Al-Jalalayn adds a two-word gloss that keeps it interior: <em>hawa al-nafs</em>, the self's own leaning. What threatens the courtroom is sitting in it already: the judge's own preference, present before the hearing opens, tilting it before either party has finished speaking.<!-- LINK: pattern=hawa-as-internal-tilt-not-appetite --></p>

  <h2>The other time the word is used</h2>

  <!-- morphology:2:30:15 root=خلف pos=N -->
  <p>The singular <em>khalifa</em> occurs in exactly one other verse in the Quran. Every other appearance of the idea is plural — <em>khalaif</em>, <em>khulafa</em>, whole generations who come after one another. The singular is used twice: for Dawud, and here, at the beginning of everything:</p>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" data-ayah="2:30" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">وَإِذْ قَالَ رَبُّكَ لِلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ إِنِّى جَاعِلٌ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ خَلِيفَةً ۖ قَالُوٓا۟ أَتَجْعَلُ فِيهَا مَن يُفْسِدُ فِيهَا وَيَسْفِكُ ٱلدِّمَآءَ وَنَحْنُ نُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِكَ وَنُقَدِّسُ لَكَ ۖ قَالَ إِنِّىٓ أَعْلَمُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ</p>
    <p class="translation">"And when your Lord said to the angels: I am placing on the earth a khalifa — they said: will You place in it one who will spread ruin in it and shed blood, while we glorify You with praise and sanctify You? He said: I know what you do not know."</p>
    <cite>Al-Baqarah (2:30)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <p>Al-Jalalayn identifies the one being placed there as Adam, and glosses the title as one who carries out God's rulings on the earth — the same administrative sense the commentators give it in Sad. Hold the two verses side by side and the shape they share becomes hard to miss. Both announce the appointment. And in both, the hazard is raised in the very next breath. At the founding, the angels name it from outside — ruin and bloodshed. In Sad, God names it from inside, and gives it an address: the appointee's own leaning.</p>

  <p>Between the two verses, the diagnosis has moved. The angels described what authority does when it goes wrong, in the language of outcomes: broken things and spilled blood. Sad describes the mechanism that produces those outcomes, and the mechanism turns out to be small, private, and entirely ordinary — a man following his own tilt while believing he is following the case.</p>

  <h2>Where the verse is standing</h2>

  <p>Where this verse sits changes everything about how it reads. Sad hands Dawud the title one verse after his worst moment — he ruled on a dispute after hearing only one of the two men, realized mid-verdict that the whole thing was a test, and fell down bowing.<!-- LINK: pattern=the-verdict-delivered-before-the-second-party-speaks --> He is forgiven, told he has nearness and a good place of return, and then — with the ink barely dry on the slip — given the largest title in the passage and told to go judge people.</p>

  <p>That order is making an argument. God confirms authority on this man immediately after showing him — from the inside — that he can get it wrong. The lesson was the qualification. What makes him fit to hold the office is that he now knows, from the inside and by experience, exactly which way he leans when a case moves fast — and that knowledge is precisely what the warning in 38:26 asks him to keep using.</p>

  <p>Which explains why the verse's one prohibition targets what it targets. God does not tell him to avoid cruelty, or bribery, or the abuse of the weak. Those are the failures of a bad man, and the Quran here is describing an excellent one — and telling him the thing an excellent one actually has to watch: his own certainty, arriving early, dressed as clarity.</p>

  <h2>The word taken all the way</h2>

  <p>Two other verses show how far the same leaning can be followed. One states the end of the road:</p>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" data-ayah="45:23" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">أَفَرَءَيْتَ مَنِ ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَـٰهَهُۥ هَوَىٰهُ وَأَضَلَّهُ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ وَخَتَمَ عَلَىٰ سَمْعِهِۦ وَقَلْبِهِۦ وَجَعَلَ عَلَىٰ بَصَرِهِۦ غِشَـٰوَةً فَمَن يَهْدِيهِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ أَفَلَا تَذَكَّرُونَ</p>
    <p class="translation">"Have you seen the one who has taken his own desire as his god — and God has let him stray despite knowledge, and sealed his hearing and his heart, and put a covering over his sight? So who will guide him after God? Will you not then be reminded?"</p>
    <cite>Al-Jathiyah (45:23)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <!-- morphology:45:23:9 root=هوي pos=N -->
  <p>Al-Muyassar unfolds the first clause as: one who desires nothing without simply doing it — and closes his comment by calling the verse a foundation for warning believers against letting <em>hawa</em> become the thing that drives their actions. A leaning followed long enough becomes the thing consulted about everything, which is a working definition of a god. And the consequences the verse lists are all sensory: hearing sealed, sight covered. A person who has been obeying their own preference for years has quietly lost the ability to take in anything that would contradict it. That is the same failure Dawud caught in himself, aged into a permanent condition — the man who no longer hears the second party because he no longer hears.</p>

  <!-- morphology:79:40:14 root=هوي pos=N -->
  <p>The other verse states the opposite motion in the plainest terms the Quran has: <em>as for the one who feared the standing before his Lord and restrained the soul from desire — the Garden is the refuge</em> (79:40–41). Two clauses, one condition, one outcome. The restraint named there is exactly what happened in the prayer-chamber when a king interrupted his own verdict.</p>

  <h2>The reason the verse gives</h2>

  <p>Sad 38:26 ends with a diagnosis that is easy to read past, because it arrives after the warning rather than before it. Those who stray have a severe punishment, the verse says, <em>for having forgotten the Day of Reckoning</em>.</p>

  <!-- morphology:38:26:44 root=نسي pos=V -->
  <!-- morphology:38:26:48 root=حسب pos=N -->
  <p>Forgetting is named as the cause. The word for the day itself is <strong>ٱلْحِسَاب</strong>, from the root for counting and reckoning up — the vocabulary of accounts, tallies, sums that have to balance. Al-Jalalayn draws the causal line and makes it plain: had they held certainty about a day of reckoning, they would have believed. The straying runs on an absence of memory.</p>

  <p>Put that beside the leaning and the mechanism completes itself. A person tilts toward their own preference in proportion to how alone they believe the decision is. A judge who genuinely expects the case to be re-heard — every word of it, by someone who was present — has a reason, at the moment of deciding, to slow down. Remove that expectation and the only remaining check on a verdict is the person's own sense of themselves, which is precisely the faculty the verse has just identified as the hazard.</p>

  <p>So the verse supplies both the disease and its treatment in one sentence, and the treatment is memory. Judge with truth. Refuse the lean. And keep in view the fact that an account is being kept — which is the same instrument that stopped a king mid-verdict when two men climbed his wall.</p>

  <h2>What the title actually contains</h2>

  <p>So the Quran's fullest description of legitimate authority reads, when unfolded: you have been placed over the affairs of people; decide between them by the truth; and the single thing that will take this from you is the pull of your own preference, because it is the beginning of a fall you will not notice you are in.</p>

  <p>Ibn Kathir's move — reading the verse as counsel for anyone holding authority — puts the address in range of a much smaller life than a kingdom. Authority is any position where your reading of a situation becomes somebody else's reality: a parent's verdict on which child started it, a manager's sense of who the difficult one is, a teacher's early conclusion about a student, the version of an argument you carry to a friend. In each of them the danger is identical and it is never announced as bias. It arrives as confidence, and it feels like knowing.</p>

  <p>The Quran's answer is a man on the floor of his own prayer-room, one verse before he is handed the title — kept in office, kept in power, and told the only thing that could unseat him is the way he leans.</p>
