<article class="prose-blog">
  <p class="text-lg leading-relaxed">One surah in the Quran begins with a preposition and never supplies the verb it belongs to. Read it on its own and there is a piece missing. Read the surah before it, and the missing piece may already have been given — which is the whole question that the discipline of <em>nazm</em> exists to ask.</p>

  <p>The surah is Quraysh, and here is its opening.</p>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">
      لِإِيلَـٰفِ قُرَيْشٍ إِۦلَـٰفِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ ٱلشِّتَآءِ وَٱلصَّيْفِ
    </p>
    <p class="translation">For the uniting of Quraysh — their uniting for the journey of winter and summer.</p>
    <cite>Quraysh (106:1-2)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <p>That opening word carries a prefix meaning <em>for</em> or <em>because of</em>. For the uniting of Quraysh — <em>for</em> it, what? Somebody did something, or should do something, and the doing is not in the sentence. The surah then moves to a command in the third verse, telling them to worship the Lord of this House. But the opening still hangs.</p>

  <h2>What nazm is</h2>

  <p>The word itself never appears in the Quran. It is the name scholars gave to a question they kept running into, and its root means to string things together — the way pearls are strung onto a single thread, each one placed where it is for a reason.</p>

  <p>Applied to the Quran, nazm asks a specific question about arrangement: why is this next to that? Not what does this verse mean, but what is it doing <em>here</em>, after the one before it and before the one after it. It is the study of order as something that carries meaning rather than merely containing it.</p>

  <p>Most of the time that question is answered inside a surah. The case of Quraysh is sharper, because the connection at issue may run across a boundary between two surahs.</p>

  <h2>The surah before it</h2>

  <p>Al-Fil, immediately preceding, is five verses about an army that came against the Ka'bah and was destroyed.</p>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">
      أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَـٰبِ ٱلْفِيلِ
    </p>
    <p class="translation">Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the companions of the elephant?</p>
    <cite>Al-Fil (105:1)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">
      فَجَعَلَهُمْ كَعَصْفٍ مَّأْكُولٍۭ
    </p>
    <p class="translation">And He made them like devoured straw.</p>
    <cite>Al-Fil (105:5)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <p>There is the verb. God did something — and the sentence in the next surah says <em>for the uniting of Quraysh</em>. Put the two together and you get a complete thought: He dealt with the elephant army so that this tribe would be held together and kept safe.</p>

  <h2>Two classical readings, and neither is hidden</h2>

  <p>Ibn Kathir sets the disagreement out plainly at the head of Surah Quraysh.</p>

  <p>On one side, he reports Muhammad ibn Ishaq and 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Zayd ibn Aslam holding that this surah is directly related to the one before it, and he states the joint meaning himself: that the elephant was prevented from entering Makkah and its people destroyed <em>in order to</em> gather Quraysh — to unite them and bring them together safely in their city.</p>

  <p>On the other side he cites Ibn Jarir al-Tabari, who takes the prefix differently. For al-Tabari the correct opinion is that this is a particle of amazement — as though the listener is being told to marvel at the uniting of Quraysh and at God's favour to them in it. And al-Tabari grounds his position in something other than grammar: he holds that the two are separate, independent surahs by the consensus of the Muslims.</p>

  <p>So the disagreement is not really about whether the two passages are connected in meaning. Everyone in the discussion can see that they are. It is about whether that connection is <em>grammatical</em> — whether one sentence physically runs from one surah into the next.</p>

  <h2>What the copy itself does</h2>

  <p>The most interesting witness here is the mushaf. Ibn Kathir notes that in the primary copy the two were kept separate, with the opening formula written on the line between them — and he says this was done <em>even though</em> the surah is directly related to the one that precedes it.</p>

  <p>Both things were preserved at once. The arrangement marks them as two surahs, with all the finality that implies. The meaning leans backwards across that mark anyway. Nobody smoothed the tension away in either direction: the boundary was not erased to make the sentence flow, and the sentence was not rewritten to respect the boundary.</p>

  <p>For a reader, that is worth more than a resolution would be. The text has been transmitted with its own difficulty intact.</p>

  <h2>The image that runs across the seam</h2>

  <p>There is one more thing to look at, and it needs no grammar at all.</p>

  <p>Al-Fil ends with the attacking army reduced to something <em>eaten</em> — straw that has been chewed through. Four verses later, Quraysh closes on the opposite side of the same picture:</p>

  <blockquote class="ayah-quote">
    <p class="arabic" dir="rtl" style="font-family: var(--font-amiri,'Amiri'),serif; font-size: 1.5rem; line-height: 2.2; color: rgba(201,168,76,0.85);">
      ٱلَّذِىٓ أَطْعَمَهُم مِّن جُوعٍ وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنْ خَوْفٍۭ
    </p>
    <p class="translation">Who fed them against hunger and secured them from fear.</p>
    <cite>Quraysh (106:4)</cite>
  </blockquote>

  <p>The two words come from different roots, so this is imagery rather than wordplay. But the picture lines up exactly. The army that came to destroy the House is consumed. The people of the House are fed. One group ends as food; the other is given food. And the second half of that verse answers the first surah just as directly — an army arrived to make them afraid, and what they were given instead was safety from fear.</p>

  <p>Whatever a reader concludes about the grammar, the pairing is doing something. Destruction on one side of the seam, provision on the other, described with images that answer each other.</p>

  <h2>Why any of this matters to an ordinary reader</h2>

  <p>Nazm has a reputation for being a specialist's subject, and the technical vocabulary does not help. But the question underneath it is one anybody can ask, and it costs nothing: <em>why is this here?</em></p>

  <p>Ask it at the level of a verse and a phrase turns out to arrive directly after the thing it was answering. Ask it at the level of a surah and arcs appear — an argument that starts one way and turns. Ask it at a boundary like this one, and you find scholars a thousand years ago disagreeing in public about how far back a sentence reaches, and preserving both answers for you to weigh.</p>

  <p>That is the useful part. The tradition did not hand down a tidy conclusion here. It handed down a question, two serious answers, the names of who held them, and a copy of the text that keeps the tension visible on the page. Reading with nazm in mind mostly means accepting the invitation: treat the order as deliberate, and let the arrangement be evidence.</p>
