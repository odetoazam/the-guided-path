Normal Arabic says "I worship You." Al-Fatiha says "YOU — I worship." That one change isn't poetic. It's structural.

In Arabic, word order carries grammatical meaning. The verb normally leads: *naʿbuduka* — "we worship You." That's standard. Fluid. Undirected.

Al-Fatiha 1:5 does something else. It fronts the object:
**إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ** — *iyyāka naʿbudu*.

This is called **ḥaṣr** — restriction. When you pull the object to the front of the sentence, you're not just emphasizing it. You're grammatically *excluding* every other possible object. The sentence structure performs the claim: You, and *only* You.

It's not decoration. It's not rhetorical flourish. It's the Arabic language doing theology.

And then the ayah does it again.

**وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ** — *wa-iyyāka nastaʿīn.*
Same structure. Same ḥaṣr. This time for help-seeking. The *ista-* prefix (*nastaʿīn*) adds a layer: not passive receipt, but active reaching. You alone we ask — and we have to reach toward You to do it.

Two ḥaṣr constructions. One ayah. Worship: exclusive. Help-seeking: exclusive.

You recite this at least 17 times a day. The grammar has been doing its work the whole time.

إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ
— Al-Fatiha 1:5
