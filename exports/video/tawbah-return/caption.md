The word you were taught means "repentance" doesn't.

تَوْبَة (tawbah) comes from the root ت-و-ب — and the classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, al-Mufradāt) define it simply: to return. To turn back. To come home.

Not a courtroom. A direction.

And here's the part that undoes the whole guilty picture:

The very first tawbah in the Quran isn't a human turning to God. It's God turning to a human.

فَتَابَ عَلَيْهِ — "then He turned to him." (2:37)

Adam catches the words his Lord sends him, and the response is instant — the فَ ("then") of no gap, no probation, no waiting period. He turned.

Then the verse seals with a name: هُوَ ٱلتَّوَّابُ — He is at-Tawwāb. Not tā'ib (one who turns once), but the intensive form: the One who keeps turning back, again and again, without limit. The doubled و carries the repetition inside the word itself.

So every time the translation hands you "repentance" and you feel the weight of a verdict — the Arabic is quietly saying something else:

You didn't turn to Him first. He turned to you.

It was never a courtroom. It's the road home — and He's already walking toward you.
