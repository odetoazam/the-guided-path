# Surah Architecture Skill

## Purpose

This skill produces one thing: a flowing, integrated piece of analysis that a
teacher can read from, speak from, and think from — all at once. And that a
reader can discover the surah through, as if for the first time.

Not a reference document in one section and a script in another. One continuous
movement, from the surah's opening identity and personality through its inner
architecture to the world it speaks into today.

The intellectual movement is the opposite of the ayah skill. Ayah tadabbur goes
impossibly deep into a small space. Surah architecture finds meaning and
coherence across a large space. One examines a gem. This skill maps the palace
it lives in.

---

## When to Use This Skill

✅ User wants the structure or architecture of a whole surah
✅ Teaching a surah series, course, or curriculum
✅ Preparing an opening lecture, overview session, or khutbah
✅ Building context before selecting specific ayahs for deeper work
✅ "Big picture" questions: what is this surah about, what holds it together
✅ Content creation at the surah level (YouTube series, podcast, study guide, web page)

❌ Deep dive into a specific ayah or passage → use **quranic-tadabbur** instead
❌ Quick translation or basic meaning questions
❌ Fiqh or legal ruling questions
❌ Very short surahs (Al-Fatiha, Al-Kawthar, Al-Ikhlas) — the ayah skill serves these better

## Core Philosophy

**THE SURAH AS A PERSON, THEN A PALACE.** Before you analyze a surah's structure,
you need to know who this surah *is*. Every surah has its own personality — its own
emotional world, its own way of seeing, its own face. Name that personality first,
before architecture. Make the reader feel like they've just been introduced to
someone remarkable. Then show them the palace that person lives in.

**WHAT'S ABSENT IS PART OF THE DESIGN.** One of the most powerful analytical moves
you can make is noting what Allah chose *not* to include — things that appear in
neighboring or similar surahs that are conspicuously missing here. A surah without
moral commands is making a statement. A surah that doesn't mention Allah by name is
doing something intentional. A surah with no prophets, no destroyed nations, no
direct instructions to the Prophet ﷺ — these absences are design choices, not
accidents. Study what's missing as carefully as what's present.

**THE SURAH FAMILY.** Surahs don't stand alone in the mushaf. They live in families —
groups of surahs connected by shared themes, paired structures, and mutual
illumination. Understanding which family this surah belongs to, which surah is its
twin or neighbor, and how they speak to each other is part of understanding the
surah itself. This is *munasabat al-suwar* and it should feel like a discovery, not
a footnote.

**THE SIMPLE MAP BEFORE THE DEEP DIVE.** Always give the reader a plain-language
bird's eye view of the surah before going deep. What are the major movements, in
simple terms? Not the nuanced structural analysis — just the floor plan. Once they
can hold the whole surah in their head simply, the deeper architecture becomes
something they can actually receive.

**STRUCTURE IS ARGUMENT.** Where sections begin and end, what drives each transition,
where the pivot falls, how the closing echoes the opening — these are not decorative
choices. They carry meaning. A surah that opens with a declaration and closes with a
question is making a claim about the relationship between certainty and dependence.
A chiasm does not just create symmetry; it creates a center of gravity that changes
how you read everything else. Read the architecture as you would read a sentence.

**KEYWORDS ARE THE SURAH'S VOCABULARY OF CONCERN.** When Allah returns to a word
three or four times across a surah, He is teaching you that this word is the lens
through which to read the whole. These are not repetitions for style — they are the
surah's recurring concerns. Study the visual or physical image the root carries in
classical Arabic. A keyword whose root means "to bridle a horse" tells you something
about restraint and guidance that no translation reaches.

**ANCHORS CREATE COHERENCE.** Beyond keywords, watch for phrases, images, or ideas
that appear at the beginning of a section and then again later — sometimes in a
different section entirely. These "anchors" are how the surah weaves its parts into
a whole. They are often invisible until you look for them, and then you cannot unsee them.

**THE COOL CONNECTION.** Every surah has at least one moment where it connects
subtly to something elsewhere in the Quran — a theme from a prophet's story, a
phrase that echoes another surah, an image that illuminates something you thought
you understood. These connections aren't forced. They emerge from honest study.
When you find one, share it as a discovery — not a point in a list, but a moment
of "wait, look at this."

**GROUNDED, NOT INVENTED.** Every structural claim must be traceable to specific
ayah numbers. Every keyword claim must cite actual occurrences. Every symmetry must
be shown, not asserted. If a root image is uncertain, say so. The Iron Rule: the
architecture you identify must be in the text, not imposed on it. Honesty about
uncertainty is not a weakness — it is part of what makes the analysis trustworthy.

## STEP 0: STRUCTURAL AUDIT (Mandatory — Internal, Never Shown to User)

Work through this completely before writing a single word of the actual output.
This is the foundation everything rests on.

### 0a. Surah Identity
- Makki or Madani (or mixed — note the consensus and any minority opinions)
- Approximate revelation period (early/middle/late Makkan; early/late Madinan)
- Number of ayahs
- Position in the mushaf: what surah comes before and after — is there a meaningful
  thematic relationship (*munasabat al-suwar*)? Does the close of the previous surah
  connect to the opening of this one?

### 0b. The Surah's Personality
Before any structural work, answer this question in 3-5 sentences:

*If this surah were a person, what would be their defining characteristic? What
emotional world do they live in? What do they want from the person reading them?
What is the feeling of being inside this surah?*

This is not the same as "what is this surah about." It's who the surah *is*.
A surah can be about judgment day and feel like a quiet warning, or feel like a
thunderclap, or feel like a letter from a friend. Name the feeling precisely.


### 0c. The Unifying Thesis

Before mapping sections or keywords, identify the single organizing argument —
the one claim this surah is making that holds everything else together.

Al-Ghazali's distinctive contribution to surah-level analysis was insisting that
every surah has a central thesis, not just a theme. The difference: a theme is
what the surah is *about*; a thesis is what the surah is *arguing* — the case it
builds, the answer it insists on, the claim it won't let go of.

Ask: If this surah were a single sentence, what would it say? Not a topic label
("this surah is about gratitude") but an argument ("this surah argues that the
moment you see creation clearly, ingratitude becomes philosophically impossible").

This thesis — once named — becomes the lens through which to read every section.
A section that seems disconnected will reveal its connection once you can see how
it serves the central argument. A transition that seems abrupt will reveal its
logic. An absence will reveal its necessity.

Name the thesis in one sentence before proceeding. Return to it after completing
the structural map to test whether it holds. Adjust if the structure reveals
something the first naming missed.

### 0d. The Simple Floor Plan
Write the surah's major movements in plain language — the kind of overview you
could give someone in 60 seconds before they read it. No technical terms. No
ayah numbers yet. Just: "First it does X. Then it moves to Y. Then at the center
you find Z. Then it closes with W." 

Two versions:
- **Easy division:** 3-4 movements in the simplest possible terms
- **Fuller division:** the same movements with slightly more granularity

This two-stage map is what you'll give the reader early in the output.

### 0e. The Absence Audit
This is mandatory. For this surah, what is conspicuously *missing* that you would
expect to find in surahs of this type, this period, or this genre?

Ask specifically:
- Are there moral commands or ethical teachings? If not — why not?
- Are prophets mentioned? If not — what does their absence signal?
- Are the words *Allah*, *kafiroon*, *mushrikoon*, *mu'minoon* present? If any are
  absent — what is being communicated by that choice?
- Are destroyed nations mentioned? If not in a Makki surah — what replaces that warning?
- Are there direct instructions to the Prophet ﷺ? If not — who is really being addressed?
- Are there things present in the surah's twin or neighbor that are absent here?

For each significant absence, name what it signals. An absence is a design choice.

### 0f. Surah Family and Neighbor Relationships
- Which group of surahs does this one belong to? (e.g., the Mufassal, a thematic
  cluster, a paired set)
- Does this surah have a twin — another surah it mirrors, complements, or is in
  direct conversation with?
- What do the surahs in this family have in common? What thread connects them?
- What does this surah do that its twin or neighbor cannot do alone?
- Note: if the surah has a clear twin, what happens when you read one as the
  mirror of the other — what do you see?


### 0g. Historical Landing Context

Before the section map, identify the historical moment this surah landed in —
not as background color, but as structural information.

Al-Ghazali consistently opened his surah analyses by naming the specific community
situation the surah arrived into: what was the community facing, what was the
pressing crisis or confusion, what had just happened or was about to happen? He
treated this not as a footnote to the analysis but as its foundation — because
knowing what the surah was *doing* for its first audience is the fastest path to
understanding why it is built the way it is.

Ask specifically:
- What was the state of the Muslim community when this surah came? (Not just
  Makki/Madani — but what specific moment, tension, or transition?)
- What need did this surah meet that no previous surah had addressed in quite
  the same way?
- Does understanding this historical situation explain any structural choices —
  why certain topics appear, why certain things are absent, why the surah opens
  the way it does?
- How does the surah's architecture reflect the specific demands of that moment?

This is different from the "world it speaks into" section. That section moves *from*
the surah *to* the present. This step moves *from* history *into* the surah's
structure — using the landing context to illuminate why the surah is built exactly
as it is, before you map it section by section.

### 0h. First Read — Unmediated Observations
Before structural analysis, note first impressions:
- What is the dominant feeling of this surah? (Warning? Tenderness? Urgency? Wonder?)
- What words or images appear often enough to notice on a surface read?
- Where does the surah feel like it changes direction?
- What does the surah's final image leave you with?

### 0i. Section Mapping

Divide the surah into its major movements. For each section record:

| Section | Ayah Range | Opening Signal | What It Does | Transition Out |
|---------|-----------|----------------|--------------|----------------|
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |

For each transition, identify **what drives it**: Is it a shift in who is being
addressed? A shift in subject? A grammatical shift — a sudden change in person,
number, or tense (*iltifat*)? A structural marker like قُلْ, أَلَا, وَإِذْ?

Also ask: **why does this section naturally follow the one before it?** Name that
logical relationship.


### 0j. Grammatical Audit

Skilled analysts notice grammatical patterns across a surah. This step makes
that attention systematic.

Survey the surah for these four grammatical signals:

**1. Person shifts (1st / 2nd / 3rd)**
Where does the surah shift between talking *about* someone and talking *to* them?
Where does it shift from first-person divine speech to third-person description
or second-person address? These shifts are not stylistic accidents — they signal
changes in relational distance or rhetorical strategy.
Example: in Al-Fatiha, Allah's blessings are mentioned in 2nd person ("those You
have favored") while His anger appears in 3rd person ("those who have earned anger")
— a grammatical choice encoding theological care. What similar choices appear here?

**2. Tense patterns**
How does the surah move across time — perfect (completed action), imperfect
(ongoing/future), imperative (command)? A surah that opens with completed past
actions and closes with imperatives is making a temporal argument. A sudden shift
from narrative past to present-tense command marks a pivot from story to application.
Note where tense shifts and what each shift is doing.

**3. Mood shifts (declarative → interrogative → imperative)**
Where does the surah move from statement to question to command? This rhetorical
movement often marks section boundaries and escalation points. The question is
usually the hinge: statement establishes the truth, question implicates the listener,
command demands response.

**4. Voice shifts (active → passive)**
Where does agency disappear into passive construction? Passive voice in Quranic
Arabic often signals either the hiddenness of the divine actor or the helplessness
of the human subject. Note any significant voice shifts and what they communicate.

For each pattern found: cite the ayah, name the shift, and propose what it is
doing rhetorically or theologically. If uncertain, flag it. Grammatical observations
that don't illuminate meaning can be set aside — the goal is significance, not
exhaustiveness.

### 0k. Recurring Keywords and Anchors

**Keywords:** 3-6 words that recur across the surah. For each:

| Keyword | Arabic | Root | Occurrences | Ayah Numbers | Root Image | Confident? |
|---------|--------|------|-------------|--------------|------------|------------|
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | [physical image if known] | Y/N |

The root image requires actual linguistic knowledge. If uncertain, describe the
semantic range only — never invent a physical image.

**Anchors:** Phrases, images, or ideas that appear early and return later — creating
threads of coherence across the whole.

### 0l. Structural Symmetry

Look for:
- **Maqta'/Matla' symmetry (opening/closing pair):** Al-Suyuti demonstrated
  across all 114 surahs that the opening and closing of each surah form a deliberate
  correspondence — not just thematic echoing but a precise structural pair. This is
  more specific than general "opening/closing echo." Ask:
    - Does the close return to the exact image, word, or name from the opening?
      (Al-Ikhlas: *ahad* opens and closes. Al-Nas: *al-nas* frames the whole.)
    - Does the close invert or resolve the tension the opening created?
      (Al-Mu'minun: opens "successful are the believers," closes "disbelievers
      will not succeed" — a mirror inversion that makes the surah's argument in two lines.)
    - Does the close answer a question the opening implicitly asked?
    - What does the distance between opening and closing *argue*? The surah is
      not just moving from A to B — it is making a case, and the pairing of first
      and last is the case in miniature.
  Identify the specific opening image/word/claim and the specific closing image/
  word/claim. Name the relationship: echo, inversion, resolution, or escalation.
  This is often where the surah's deepest argument becomes visible most clearly.
- **Ring composition (chiasm):** A-B-C-B'-A' structure. For each potential
  correspondence, cite specific ayahs on both sides and describe the relationship.
- **Opening/Closing Echo:** Does the final image answer the opening? What changes
  between the two? The difference is often the argument in miniature.
- **Internal Parallelism:** Two stories with the same arc, two scenes with the
  same form. Cite both sides.

For any symmetry, always ask: what is created *between* the mirrored elements?
What does the center reveal? Symmetry that doesn't illuminate meaning is just pattern.

### 0m. The Turning Point

What is the single most pivotal moment in the surah — the argumentative hinge that
everything before is building toward, and everything after is radiating from? Note
the ayah number and explain why *this* is the turn.

### 0n. The Cool Connection

Is there a subtle connection between this surah and somewhere else in the Quran —
a theme from a prophet's story, an echo of another surah's language, an image that
illuminates something unexpected? It must be real, not forced. If it's there and
you can ground it in the text, share it. If not, don't invent one.

### 0o. Audience Tracking

Who is being addressed at each point? The surah has a particular intended audience —
not "all humanity generically" but a specific group at a specific moment. Name them.
Track shifts in address throughout.

Then ask: what does understanding the actual audience unlock about why the surah
says what it says — and omits what it omits?

### 0p. The Unique Signature

What 3-5 things make this surah unlike any other in the Quran? These might be
structural (the only surah that does X), linguistic (unique vocabulary that appears
nowhere else), thematic (the only surah without Y), or formal (a repeated refrain,
an unusual opening). These are not trivia — they are clues to the surah's design
intention.

### 0q. Confidence Check

Before writing anything:
- Flag any section boundary that resists clean division
- Flag any keyword occurrence count that is approximate
- Flag any symmetry that is interpretive rather than textually demonstrable
- Flag any root image marked uncertain
- Flag any audience claim you're unsure about

The output may only assert what the audit confirms. Flag the rest honestly.

**Claim-tier audit (mandatory):** For every major interpretive claim you plan to make,
assign it one of these four tiers before writing it:

- **Textual** — directly stated or demonstrated in the ayahs
- **Literary observation** — a structural or stylistic inference from the text
- **Classical grounding** — supported by established tafsir tradition
- **Reflective application** — a contemplative or devotional synthesis

If a claim is tier 3 or 4, it must be *written* as such — not phrased with the
confidence of tier 1. The most common failure mode: a reflective synthesis presented
as if it were a textual fact. "The surah argues that..." is only warranted when the
surah demonstrably does so through its structure and language. If it is your synthesis
of what the surah implies, say so. Precision here is not weakness — it is what makes
the analysis trustworthy.

### 0r. The Breath Audit (Mandatory — Internal, Never Shown to User)

Before writing the output, identify:

1. **The still points** — which 2-3 moments in this surah call for the writing to
   slow down and sit rather than explain? Usually: the surah's most emotionally
   weighted verse, the cool connection, and one moment in the closing section. Name them now
   so you don't rush past them when you arrive.

2. **The register shifts** — map the emotional arc of the piece before writing it:
   - Where is it intellectual? (floor plan, section mapping)
   - Where is it contemplative? (anchor verses, hidden architecture)
   - Where is it human and felt? (the world it speaks into, closing)
   Mark the transition points. The writing should feel different in each register.

3. **The one moment of genuine wonder** — identify one thing in this surah that
   genuinely stops you. Not a teaching point. Not a structural observation. Something
   that when you arrive at it in the writing, the sentence slows, shortens, and gives
   it room. If you can't identify this before writing, you'll rush past it. Find it now.

4. **Closing section flag** — note before writing: the world it speaks into must feel different from everything
   before it. Less argued. More felt. If you catch yourself writing it in the same
   analytical register as the structural sections, stop and rewrite it.

5. **Thesis repetition check** — name the central thesis in one sentence. Then count
   how many times you intend to restate it across the piece. The answer should be: once
   explicitly, and otherwise only through demonstration. If the thesis appears in similar
   language more than twice, compress. The surah's motifs and structure should do the
   work of restating it — not the prose repeating it directly. A thesis that needs
   constant restatement is a thesis that hasn't been shown convincingly enough.

## THE OUTPUT: ONE FLOWING DOCUMENT

After completing Step 0, write a single integrated piece. Not Part A then Part B.
Not reference document then script. **One continuous movement** — the kind of
analysis that reads beautifully and also happens to be exactly what a teacher needs
to stand up and speak from, or what a thoughtful reader discovers the surah through.

**Voice:** Guide, not lecturer. "Notice what happens here..." "Watch this..."
"This is where it gets remarkable..." "Stay with me..." A quiet discovery voice —
like someone showing you something they genuinely love, not presenting a paper.

**Language:** Simple, clear, human words. If a plain word works, use it. Write for
someone who is intelligent and curious but not a scholar. Every technical term that
must appear gets an immediate plain-language translation — not in parentheses, but
woven into the sentence. The analysis should be reachable by anyone willing to pay
attention.

**The two-stage reveal:** Whenever diving into complexity, give the simple version
first. "Let me give you the easy picture first — then we'll go deeper." This is not
condescension. It is how genuine teaching works. People need to be able to hold the
whole in their head before they can receive the parts.

**Structure within the flow:** The document moves through these movements — but not as
labeled headers bolted together. Let each section grow naturally from the one before it.

**Rhythm and breath:** A great teacher does not hold the same pace for 4,000 words.
The writing must breathe — it must slow when the material calls for stillness, speed
when the argument is building, and pause when something lands that deserves to land.

Concretely:
- **Vary sentence length deliberately.** After a long analytical sentence, drop to
  something short. Even a fragment. Especially before and after moments of weight.
- **Find the still points** — 2-3 moments in the piece where you stop the forward
  movement and simply *sit* with something before explaining it. A verse. An image.
  A silence. Don't rush past it.
- **Let the closing section slow down.** The "world it speaks into" section should feel different
  from the structural analysis — less argued, more felt. Shorter sentences. More space.
  Write it the way the surah would want to be received, not the way an analysis would
  want to be delivered.
- **The emotional register should shift at least 3 times** across the full piece:
  from the intellectual (floor plan, structural mapping) to the contemplative (anchor
  verses, hidden architecture) to the human (the world it speaks into, closing). If the whole piece
  reads at the same pitch, something is wrong.
- **One moment of genuine wonder.** Somewhere in the piece — not performed, not
  announced — let the writing itself slow down and say: *this stopped me.* The cool
  connection is a natural candidate. So is a particularly dense keyword root. So is
  an absence so striking it feels like a locked door. Find it and give it space.

### The Surah at a Glance
*(Published header: "The Surah at a Glance")*

The piece opens here — but not with a formula. Find the entry point that belongs to
this surah alone: its most striking fact, the image it can't stop returning to, the
question it was sent to answer, the thing about it that should stop a reader in their
tracks. Open there. No template phrases. No "before we go deep." Just: here is this
surah — specific, alive, unmistakable.

The opening sentences carry the most weight for a reader who doesn't know the surah
yet. Lead with something that earns their attention. The surah's name and a clear
sense of what it is should land in the first two sentences — not as a label, but as
the natural consequence of opening with its most compelling truth.

Write for someone who is intelligent and curious but may know very little about Islam
or the Quran. Eloquence first — writing that a non-Muslim could find beautiful and a
scholar could find precise. Technical terms may appear when they genuinely serve the
reader, but always with an immediate plain-language translation woven into the same
sentence. Never parenthetical. Never a footnote.

Within this opening section — before the piece moves into personality or architecture
— give the reader a plain-language map of the whole surah. Not as an announced
feature, but as a natural part of opening the door. Two stages: first the simplest
possible overview (3-4 movements, no technical terms, 60 seconds to read), then the
same movements with slightly more granularity. Deliver it and move on. Don't frame
it as a teaching method. Don't announce "here is the map." Just give it, as part of
the natural movement of introducing something remarkable.

---

### The Character of This Surah
*(Published header: "The Character of This Surah")*

Open with the surah's personality — not background facts, but character. What kind
of place is this? What does it feel like to stand inside it? What is it doing that
no other surah does in quite the same way?

Make a specific, bold claim. "Al-Mulk is a surah of interrogation." "Al-Kahf is a
surah about four trials, dressed as four stories." Vagueness is the enemy. Make a
claim that could only be about *this* surah.

Then: its unique signature. What 2-3 things make this surah unlike any other?
Present these as genuine "did you know" moments — the kind of observations that make
someone stop and reconsider what they thought they knew. The fact that the word Allah
doesn't appear in Ar-Rahman. The fact that Al-Ikhlas doesn't use any verb in the
past tense. These are doors, not decorations.

Then: what's conspicuously absent. What did Allah choose not to include here that
appears everywhere else? Move through 2-3 significant absences and name what each
one signals. This is where a good analysis becomes a great one.

Then: the surah's family and neighbors. Which group does it belong to? Does it have
a twin? What does knowing its family tell you about what this surah is doing that it
can't do alone? Present this as a discovery — "here's something remarkable about
where this surah sits..."

Weave in the Makki/Madani context and revelation period naturally — not as a
fact-dump, but as part of the personality. A surah from the hardest years of Mecca
is made differently than one from Medina. That matters.

Aim for 4-6 paragraphs that leave someone feeling they've just been introduced to
someone remarkable, and they want to know more.

---

### Walking Through the Surah
*(Published header: "Walking Through the Surah")*

Walk through the surah's sections as a guide walking through rooms. Show what each
section *does*, then show why the next one naturally follows.

Name each transition. "Watch how the surah moves here — it just showed you X, and
now it turns and asks: well, then why don't you see Y?" or "This is an *iltifat* —
a sudden shift in who is being addressed, from talking *about* the disbelievers to
talking *to* them directly. Feel what changes in that shift."

Weave in keywords and anchors **as they arise** — not in a list, but as threads you
keep pulling. When a keyword first appears, name it briefly. When it appears again
later in a new context, return to it: "And here's that word again — notice what it
means now that we're deeper in."

For each section:
- Give it a name that captures its movement (not "Section 2" — something that
  describes what it's doing, like "The Accusation" or "The Reckoning")
- State the ayah range
- Describe what it does and why it matters
- Name the transition into the next section
- Explain why this section naturally follows — name the logical link

After all sections, write one paragraph on the overall arc: what journey does the
surah take the listener on from the first word to the last?

### What the Structure Is Doing
*(Published header: "What the Structure Is Doing")*

Present this as a discovery — something the reader might not have seen on their
own first read. Not a structural checklist. A moment of genuine "wait, look at this."

Move through:

**The opening/closing echo** — what the first few ayahs and the last few say to each
other. What has changed across the distance of the surah? What does that distance argue?

**Ring composition, if present** — walk through the mirrored elements. "This section
near the opening mirrors this section near the close. But notice what's different
between them..." Then: "And that means the center — right here — is where the whole
surah's weight rests."

**The turning point** — the argumentative hinge. "This is where everything turns.
Everything before it is building to this moment. Everything after flows from it."

**The cool connection** — share it here as a genuine moment of discovery. A thread
that connects this surah to somewhere else in the Quran — a prophet's story, a
shared image, an echo that illuminates both places. Share it the way you'd share
something you just noticed and can't stop thinking about.

If any structural claim here is interpretive rather than textually certain, say so
explicitly. Frame it as "an observation worth sitting with" rather than a proven
fact. Honesty here actually increases trust.

---

### Why It Still Speaks
*(Published header: "Why It Still Speaks")*

This is the emotional and practical landing of the whole piece. Write it as one
continuous flow — not three labeled paragraphs.

First: the world the surah arrived into. What was happening to the early community
when these words came? What were they facing — fear, mockery, grief, confusion? Be
specific. What did *this surah* do for them that no other surah was doing at that moment?

Then: the permanent version of that experience. What is the dimension of that same
challenge that belongs to every human being in every generation — not just 7th
century Mecca but every time and place?

Then: the person reading this today. Not a general moral lesson — but what specifically
*this surah*, with its particular architecture, its particular personality, its
particular absences and signatures, offers someone encountering it right now. What
does it restructure in how you see something you're already living?

The movement through these three levels should feel like the analysis coming home.
Let the voice slow here. Give it space.

---

### To Carry With You + Virtues & Recitation
*(Published headers: "To Carry With You" / "Virtues & Recitation")*

**To Carry With You** contains:
- Three questions — genuine invitations to sit with the surah's particular concerns.
  Each one comes directly from the surah's own material. Not rhetorical. Not obvious.
  Questions that actually open something.
- One sentence portrait of the surah — specific enough to be about this surah and no
  other. Not a summary. A portrait.
- A brief du'a (2-3 lines) that grows directly from the surah's themes.
- 2-3 ayahs flagged for deeper work with the quranic-tadabbur skill — the most
  structurally significant and linguistically rich, with a sentence on *why* each one.

**Virtues & Recitation** contains:
- Specific hadith narrations about this surah's virtues — cited by source (collection
  and book/chapter) and scholarly grading (sahih, hasan, da'if, mawdu'). Name the
  actual narration, name where it appears, name its status. Do not paraphrase vaguely.
- If a narration is disputed or weak, say so plainly: "This appears in X but is graded
  weak by Y" — not hedged language like "some scholars have noted concerns."
- If strong authenticated narrations about this surah's specific virtues do not exist,
  say so explicitly and without apology: "There are no well-authenticated hadith
  specifically about the virtues of reciting this surah." Then note what the surah
  says about itself internally, if relevant.
- Do not pad the absence with vague attributions, general principles, or unnamed
  narrations. An honest gap is more trustworthy than hedged filling.
- Any traditional guidance on when or how this surah is recited, with sourcing.

## THE REFUTATION PASS (Adversarial — Mandatory, Before Publication)

The quality checks above audit the piece as its author. This pass audits it as
**opposing counsel**: your only job is to break the piece's structural claims, and
the piece ships only if you fail. Structural analysis is where pattern-imposition
is most tempting — a chiasm is easy to assert and hard to unsee — so every
load-bearing claim gets attacked before it ships. Record every verdict in a short
refutation log; the log is the proof the pass happened.

### The attacks — run each one, in writing

**1. Attack the thesis (Step 0c).** Construct the strongest rival thesis — the
organizing argument a skeptical scholar would propose instead. If the rival
explains the same sections, keywords, and absences equally well, your thesis is a
reading, not *the* reading — downgrade its claim language accordingly.

**2. Attack the ring (Step 0l).** For every chiasm or symmetry claim, try to break
it: can the same ayahs be regrouped into a different pairing that reads equally
well? Is each correspondence *specific* (shared roots, repeated phrases, mirrored
grammar — citable on both sides) or *generic* (both sections "mention judgment")?
Generic correspondences do not survive. A ring that survives regrouping AND has
specific correspondences may be asserted; anything less is presented as "an
observation worth sitting with."

**3. Enumerate every universal.** For each "all/each/every N" claim and each item
in the `verify_claims.mjs` ledger: name the N members and verify the predicate
against each member individually. One member fails → rewrite to the true count or cut.

**4. Attack the counts and absences.** Keyword counts (Step 0k) must match the
Layer 4 verifier's corpus counts — and the count must be of the right thing (lemma
vs root vs spelling; the /names page shipped wrong counts by summing different
lemmas that share a spelling). Every absence claim must be corpus-verified AND
non-trivial: an absence shared by dozens of surahs of this type is not a design
observation.

**5. Cross-examine the classical record.** Does the tafsir report actually support
what is claimed as classically grounded? Are the Virtues narrations' gradings
checked against the named collections rather than recalled?

### Verdicts — one per claim
- **SURVIVES** — attack failed; assert as written
- **DOWNGRADE** — real but overclaimed; rewrite at honest strength and re-tier it
  (a "textual" claim that survives only as "literary observation" gets rewritten
  in that register)
- **REFUTED** — the section built on it gets rebuilt; a refuted thesis returns to Step 0c

### The gate
Zero DOWNGRADEs across a full surah analysis means the attacks were not really
run. Failed attacks are recorded too — they are what earns the surviving claims
their confidence.

### Re-run this pass after any repair

Editing a published analysis re-opens this pass. Observed empirically: a repair
that corrected a real error passed all four validators clean while introducing
**two fresh "all three" overclaims** in the prose written to knit the correction
into the surrounding argument. The connecting sentences — not the corrected fact —
are where repairs fail, because that is where you reach for a tidy generalisation.
Attack them by name before shipping.

## AUTOMATED VERIFICATION (Post-Generation — Mandatory)

After generating the surah architecture output, run the following verification
scripts before considering the piece ready for publication. These scripts are
bundled with this skill in the `scripts/` directory.

**All scripts require:** `npm install quran-validator` (one-time setup)

### Layer 1: Arabic Text Verification (quran-validator)

**What it checks:** Every Arabic verse quoted in the output is authentic and
correctly rendered against the complete Uthmani text database (6,236 verses).

**Tagging requirement:** When writing the output, tag every quoted Quranic Arabic
with an ayah reference using one of these formats:
- Markdown: `[ayah:15:9] إِنَّا نَحْنُ نَزَّلْنَا ٱلذِّكْرَ وَإِنَّا لَهُۥ لَحَـٰفِظُونَ`
- HTML comment: `<!-- ayah:15:9 --> Arabic text`

**Run:** `node scripts/verify_arabic.mjs output.md --scan`

The `--scan` flag also detects untagged Arabic that might be Quranic but has no
reference — catching places where a verse was quoted without attribution.

### Layer 2: Morphology Verification (corpus.quran.com)

**What it checks:** Root analysis, part-of-speech claims, and morphological form
assertions against the Quranic Arabic Corpus — the peer-reviewed academic
dataset from the University of Leeds.

**Tagging requirement:** When citing roots or making morphological claims in the
Recurring Keywords table (Step 0k), tag them:
`<!-- morphology:S:A:W root=xxx pos=XXX -->`

Where S=surah, A=ayah, and **W is the 1-indexed position in the ayah's flat
SEGMENT list — not the orthographic word count.** The corpus splits prefixes
(ال، و، ف، ب، ل) into their own segments, so counting visible words silently
verifies your claim against a different word and reports a pass. Use
Arabic-script roots (رغد), never transliteration.

**Setup (one-time):** `node scripts/verify_morphology.mjs --setup`
**Run:** `node scripts/verify_morphology.mjs output.md`

This is especially critical for:
- Root images claimed in the keyword table (Step 0k)
- Morphological forms cited in the grammatical audit (Step 0j)
- Any claim about verb form (II-X) or noun pattern

### Layer 3: Tafsir Cross-Reference (tafsir_api)

**What it checks:** Pulls classical tafsir commentary (Ibn Kathir, al-Tabari,
al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar) for every ayah cited in the output. Produces a
cross-reference report so you can see whether the AI's structural claims and
thematic interpretations align with or diverge from classical scholarship.

**Run:** `node scripts/cross_reference_tafsir.mjs output.md --output tafsir-report.md`

**How to use the report:** The report does NOT auto-approve or auto-reject. It
surfaces the classical commentary alongside the output's claims. Review the
report for:
- Major divergences where the output claims something classical scholars don't support
- Missed connections where classical scholars emphasize something the output overlooked
- Confirmation where the output aligns with scholarly consensus

This layer is particularly important for:
- The unifying thesis (Step 0c)
- Section boundaries and transitions (Step 0i)
- The cool connection (Step 0n)
- Claims tier'd as "Classical grounding" in the confidence check (Step 0q)

### Layer 4: Falsifiable-Claim Verification (verify_claims.mjs)

**What it checks:** Every countable claim in the prose against the morphology
corpus, counted three ways — root, lemma, exact surface form — because the three
diverge and prose rarely says which it means. For this skill the prime targets are
the **keyword occurrence counts (Step 0k)**, the **absence audit claims (Step 0e)**,
and every "only surah that / nowhere else in the Quran" uniqueness claim.

**Run:** `node scripts/verify_claims.mjs output.md --surah N`

- ❌ **FAILED** claims are corpus-refuted: use the real count or cut the claim —
  never vague it up ("appears repeatedly").
- 📋 **LEDGER** claims (universal quantifiers, unresolvable subjects) are the
  mandatory worklist for the Refutation Pass above.
- Tag hand-verified claims `<!-- claim:ok reason -->`; disambiguate with
  `<!-- claim:subject=WORD scope=surah|quran -->`.

### Verification Workflow

1. Generate the surah architecture output
2. Run `verify_arabic.mjs` → fix any failed Arabic quotations
3. Run `verify_morphology.mjs` → fix any wrong root/POS claims
4. Run `cross_reference_tafsir.mjs` → review tafsir report for divergences
5. Run `verify_claims.mjs` → fix FAILED counts/absences; carry the LEDGER into the Refutation Pass
6. Run the Refutation Pass and write the refutation log
7. Only publish after all four layers pass or divergences are consciously accepted

## ON SOURCES AND GROUNDING

At the surah level, the analysis is grounded through:

1. **Specific ayah citations** for every structural claim
2. **Actual occurrence counts** for every recurring keyword — verified, not estimated
3. **Epistemic honesty about root images** — give it if certain; describe semantic
   range and flag if uncertain
4. **Distinguishing textually demonstrable symmetry from interpretive observation**
5. **Drawing on the consensus tafsir tradition** where relevant — particularly for
   section boundaries in longer surahs, where scholars have established divisions
   that deserve acknowledgment
6. **Absence claims require verification** — don't assert a word is absent from a
   surah without checking; don't assert something is unique to a surah without
   reasonable confidence

The Iron Rule applies at both scales: what you claim must be in the text, not on top of it.

## HANDLING LARGE SURAHS

For very long surahs (Al-Baqarah, Al-Imran, An-Nisa, etc.):

Tell the user at the start how you are handling the scale.

- **The simple floor plan becomes even more essential.** For a surah of 286 ayahs,
  the reader needs the map before anything else. Give more time to the easy division.
- **Section mapping:** lean on broad scholarly consensus on major structural
  divisions. Acknowledge where scholars differ.
- **Keyword tracking:** track words that appear 5+ times for very long surahs,
  to stay focused on genuinely structural vocabulary.
- **The output functions as a series opener**, not a complete overview. Be explicit:
  "This gives you the floor plan of the palace. Each subsequent session takes you
  room by room."
- **For the Absence Audit:** large Madani surahs will have different "expected"
  features than Makki surahs — adjust accordingly.
- **Flag 4-6 specific ayahs** for deeper tadabbur work, since a longer surah has
  more structurally significant passages.

## WEB PAGE MODE

When producing content for a web page (AyahGuide or similar), adjust as follows:

**Same intellectual depth. Same fixed headers. Optimized for web reading.**

The published section headers are the same in web mode as in every other mode:
The Surah at a Glance / The Character of This Surah / Walking Through the Surah /
What the Structure Is Doing / Why It Still Speaks / To Carry With You / Virtues & Recitation.

- **The Surah at a Glance** functions as the page's hook and intro — the thing someone
  reads in 60 seconds to decide whether to go deeper. The plain-language map lives here.
- **The Character of This Surah** is the page's opening personality section — bold,
  specific, immediately gripping for someone who has never encountered this surah.
- **Walking Through the Surah** gets its own subheadings for each named section —
  this aids both readability and SEO.
- **Why It Still Speaks** closes with the most human, accessible writing — this is
  what people share and what search engines reward for depth.
- End with: a clear email capture CTA ("Go deeper — subscribe for ayah-level
  reflections on this surah"), 2-3 flagged ayahs as "Explore Further" links.
- Technical terms always get immediate plain-language translation woven into the
  same sentence — never parenthetical, never a footnote.
- Target length: 2,500–4,000 words for standard surahs. 4,000–6,000 for major surahs
  (Al-Kahf, Yasin, Ar-Rahman, Al-Baqarah, etc.).
