The Quran's Word for the Powerless Is a Verb in the Passive

English gives us "the weak" — an adjective, a description, a condition someone happens to be in. The Quran reaches instead for a verb, and puts that verb in the passive: ٱسْتُضْعِفُوا, istuḍʿifū — they were treated as weak, they were made weak. The exact word occurs five times, and the passive stamp extends to the family around it — the mustaḍʿafīn of 4:75, the people "who were being weakened" who inherit the east and west of the land in 7:137. A passive verb carries a hidden subject. Someone is doing this to them.

Two verbs, one pattern, opposite voices

Four of the five occurrences arrive paired with their opposite number, and the pairing is where the design shows. Both words are built on the same Arabic pattern — form X, the pattern of seeking or claiming something. One of them is active, the other passive:

قَالَ ٱلْمَلَأُ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ مِن قَوْمِهِۦ لِلَّذِينَ ٱسْتُضْعِفُوا۟ لِمَنْ ءَامَنَ مِنْهُمْ أَتَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّ صَـٰلِحًا مُّرْسَلٌ مِّن رَّبِّهِۦ

"…The chiefs of his people who had made themselves great said to those who had been made weak — to those among them who believed: do you know that Salih is sent from his Lord?…"

Al-A'raf (7:75)

ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا (istakbarū, root ك-ب-ر) is active: they sought greatness for themselves. Nobody made them great. They claimed it, and the verb hands them the credit. ٱسْتُضْعِفُوا (istuḍʿifū, root ض-ع-ف) is the same pattern in the passive: they were made weak. The morphological corpus tags them exactly this way — one active, one passive, both form X, sitting four words apart.

Two conditions face each other in that verse, and the grammar assigns responsibility for each. Bigness is self-issued. Smallness is inflicted. Whatever else the scene contains, the Arabic has already ruled on where each side's situation came from.

The argument that survives death

Three of the five occurrences land in a single scene in Surah Saba, where the two groups are standing before their Lord and still arguing. The weakened speak first:

وَلَوْ تَرَىٰٓ إِذِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ مَوْقُوفُونَ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ يَرْجِعُ بَعْضُهُمْ إِلَىٰ بَعْضٍ ٱلْقَوْلَ يَقُولُ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتُضْعِفُوا۟ لِلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ لَوْلَآ أَنتُمْ لَكُنَّا مُؤْمِنِينَ

"…If only you could see when the wrongdoers are made to stand before their Lord, throwing the words back and forth at each other. Those who had been made weak say to those who had made themselves great: were it not for you, we would have been believers."

Saba (34:31)

The reply is a denial of influence:

قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ لِلَّذِينَ ٱسْتُضْعِفُوٓا۟ أَنَحْنُ صَدَدْنَـٰكُمْ عَنِ ٱلْهُدَىٰ بَعْدَ إِذْ جَآءَكُم بَلْ كُنتُم مُّجْرِمِينَ

"Those who had made themselves great say to those who had been made weak: was it we who turned you away from the guidance after it reached you? No — you were criminals yourselves."

Saba (34:32)

And then the weakened name the method:

وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتُضْعِفُوا۟ لِلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَكْبَرُوا۟ بَلْ مَكْرُ ٱلَّيْلِ وَٱلنَّهَارِ إِذْ تَأْمُرُونَنَآ أَن نَّكْفُرَ بِٱللَّهِ وَنَجْعَلَ لَهُۥ أَندَادًا

"And those who had been made weak say to those who had made themselves great: rather, it was the scheming of night and day — when you were ordering us to reject God and set up rivals to Him…"

Saba (34:33)

Makr al-layl wa'n-nahār — "the scheming of night and day" — appears once in the Quran, here. The phrase describes an operation rather than an incident: something running continuously, on a schedule, filling the hours. And the verb that follows it is taʾmurūnanā, "you were ordering us." The relationship between the two groups is described as one of command. Persuasion has a weaker verb available; the Quran passes it over.

Neither side wins the argument. The verse ends with the shackles going onto the necks of all of them, and with a detail easy to miss — wa-asarrū an-nadāmah, they concealed their regret. The excuse gets spoken out loud; the remorse gets hidden.

The fifth occurrence

One appearance of the word sits outside the argument entirely. It comes at the opening of Surah Al-Qasas, and to see what it does you have to read the verse before it, where the same root appears in the active with its agent named:

إِنَّ فِرْعَوْنَ عَلَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَجَعَلَ أَهْلَهَا شِيَعًا يَسْتَضْعِفُ طَآئِفَةً مِّنْهُمْ يُذَبِّحُ أَبْنَآءَهُمْ وَيَسْتَحْىِۦ نِسَآءَهُمْ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْمُفْسِدِينَ

"Pharaoh exalted himself in the land and split its people into factions, weakening one group among them — slaughtering their sons and sparing their women. He was one of the corrupters."

Al-Qasas (28:4)

Here the verb is active and the subject has a name. Pharaoh weakens; the weakening is a policy, listed alongside splitting the population into factions. Then the very next verse turns the same verb passive and puts a different speaker behind it:

وَنُرِيدُ أَن نَّمُنَّ عَلَى ٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتُضْعِفُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَنَجْعَلَهُمْ أَئِمَّةً وَنَجْعَلَهُمُ ٱلْوَٰرِثِينَ

"And We intend to show favour to those who were made weak in the land, and to make them leaders, and to make them the inheritors."

Al-Qasas (28:5)

Al-Tabari reads the phrase directly against the previous verse: those whom Pharaoh weakened. Al-Muyassar glosses the two promises — aʾimmah, leaders in good and callers to it; al-wārithīn, those who inherit the land after Pharaoh and his people are gone. Al-Jalalayn is blunter about the estate being transferred: the kingdom of Pharaoh.

So of the five times the Quran uses this word, four sit inside a dispute over who is to blame, and the fifth is God announcing that He intends to reverse the arrangement — moving the people at the bottom to the front and handing them what the man at the top was holding.

What the grammar refuses to let you say

Both halves of the pair are built to resist a comfortable reading. Against the top: istakbarū is active and reflexive, which forecloses the idea that greatness was conferred on them. They reached for it. Against the bottom: istuḍʿifū is passive, which forecloses the idea that weakness is simply how some people are. It was applied.

And the Saba scene refuses one more thing. Standing before their Lord, the weakened say the true sentence — we were ordered, it was scheming, night and day — and the shackles go on anyway. The Quran grants the analysis and withholds the acquittal. Pressure was real; the response to it still belonged to them.

Which leaves the reader holding two facts that are usually kept in separate rooms. Someone made those people weak, and God has an opinion about that, filed at 28:4 under corrupters. And the people who were made weak still answered for what they did under the pressure. The passive names the crime without dissolving the person inside it.
