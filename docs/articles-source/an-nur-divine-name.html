An-Nur: What the Classical Scholars Said "Allah Is the Light" Actually Means

The Quran uses the word nur (نُور) forty-three times. Light comes down, light is given, light is walked in, light is taken away. In almost every one of those places light is something God sends. There is exactly one verse where light is something God is — and the earliest commentators of Islam did not read it the way a modern reader's eye reads it. Their disagreement about that one sentence is the whole story of the divine name An-Nur.

The sentence

۞ ٱللَّهُ نُورُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ مَثَلُ نُورِهِۦ كَمِشْكَوٰةٍ فِيهَا مِصْبَاحٌ ۖ ٱلْمِصْبَاحُ فِى زُجَاجَةٍ ۖ ٱلزُّجَاجَةُ كَأَنَّهَا كَوْكَبٌ دُرِّىٌّ يُوقَدُ مِن شَجَرَةٍ مُّبَـٰرَكَةٍ زَيْتُونَةٍ لَّا شَرْقِيَّةٍ وَلَا غَرْبِيَّةٍ يَكَادُ زَيْتُهَا يُضِىٓءُ وَلَوْ لَمْ تَمْسَسْهُ نَارٌ ۚ نُّورٌ عَلَىٰ نُورٍ ۗ يَهْدِى ٱللَّهُ لِنُورِهِۦ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَيَضْرِبُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْأَمْثَـٰلَ لِلنَّاسِ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

"Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The likeness of His light is as a niche within which is a lamp; the lamp is in glass, the glass as if it were a brilliant star, lit from a blessed tree — an olive, neither of the east nor of the west — whose oil would almost glow though no fire touched it. Light upon light. Allah guides to His light whom He wills, and Allah sets forth parables for people, and Allah is Knowing of all things."

An-Nur (24:35)

Four words open it: Allahu nuru's-samawati wa'l-ard. The grammar is a simple equation — subject, then predicate, with no verb in between, which is how Arabic states an identity. And the predicate is not the bare word "light." It is nur in a construct: light of the heavens and the earth. The name arrives already tied to something.

The surah is named after this word. The verse sits at the centre of it.

The near miss

One other verse puts the name of God and the word nur beside each other, and the difference between the two is a single preposition.

يَـٰٓأَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ قَدْ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولُنَا يُبَيِّنُ لَكُمْ كَثِيرًا مِّمَّا كُنتُمْ تُخْفُونَ مِنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ وَيَعْفُوا۟ عَن كَثِيرٍ ۚ قَدْ جَآءَكُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ نُورٌ وَكِتَـٰبٌ مُّبِينٌ

"O People of the Scripture, there has come to you Our Messenger, making clear to you much of what you used to conceal of the Scripture and overlooking much. There has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book."

Al-Ma'idah (5:15)

Min Allahi nurun — from Allah, a light. This is the Quran's ordinary way of speaking. Light travels outward from Him and lands on someone. Al-Ma'idah 15 is the pattern; An-Nur 35 is the exception. Once, in the whole Book, the preposition drops out and the sentence becomes an identity.

What the earliest readers said

The commentary tradition on this clause is unusually well documented, and it splits early. Ibn Kathir gathers the positions in sequence.

Ibn Abbas, through the route of Ali ibn Abi Talha, glosses it in a single word: hadi. Allah is the Guide of the inhabitants of the heavens and the earth. Read this way, "light" is the Quran's image for guidance, and the verse is saying what the second half of it goes on to illustrate at length.

Mujahid, and Ibn Abbas again through Ibn Jurayj, take it another way: He is the one directing their affairs — their stars, their sun, their moon. Light here points at the management of the visible world.

As-Suddi takes the third road: by His light the heavens and the earth are illuminated. This is the reading closest to the plain English sense, and it belongs to the same early generation as the other two.

So three positions, all from the first layers of tafsir, and they pull in different directions. One makes the word mean guidance. One makes it mean governance. One keeps it as radiance.

The grammatical move

Al-Jalalayn handles the clause with one word, and the word is a piece of grammar. Allahu nuru's-samawati wa'l-ard, it says, means munawwiruhuma bi'sh-shamsi wa'l-qamar — the One who illuminates them both, by means of the sun and the moon.

Munawwir is an active participle from the same root: the one who lights. The gloss converts a noun into an agent. The heavens and the earth have light in them; the verse names the source rather than describing a substance.

Al-Muyassar holds several threads at once. It opens with the governance reading — He directs the affair in them and guides their people — then says plainly that He is light and His veil is light, that by Him the heavens and earth and all in them are lit, and that God's Book and His guidance are a light from Him. It ends the clause with an image: were it not for His light, darkness would pile up layer on layer.

Why the question came up at all

The stakes in this discussion are visible if you read the surah's own next move. Having stated the identity, the verse immediately shifts register.

Mathalu nurihi — the likeness of His light. The word mathal announces a parable, and what follows is one of the most elaborate images in the Quran: a niche, a lamp inside it, the lamp inside glass, the glass like a brilliant star, fed from the oil of an olive tree that belongs to neither east nor west, oil so clear it is on the verge of glowing before any flame reaches it. Light upon light. Then the verse names what it has been doing: God sets forth parables for people.

The verse states the identity and then hands the reader a comparison for it. Ibn Kathir records that the commentators split on whose light the parable belongs to — Ibn Abbas took the pronoun in nurihi as referring to God, so the parable illustrates His guidance as it sits in a believer's heart; others took the pronoun as referring to the believer, so the parable is of the light already placed inside him. Either way, the second half of the verse is describing something in a human being.

The classical readings are careful for a reason that runs through the whole tradition of divine names: God is described in the Quran by attributes that also have created counterparts, and the tradition's standing rule is that the counterpart never sets the terms. Light in the world is a physical thing — it has a source, a speed, an end. Reading An-Nur as hadi, or as munawwir, keeps the name from collapsing into the created thing that shares its name. The same discipline governs how the tradition reads As-Samad, a name whose plainest sense would have described a physical solid.

The image the surah sets against it

Five verses after the lamp, the surah builds the opposite picture, and the contrast is what makes the name legible.

أَوْ كَظُلُمَـٰتٍ فِى بَحْرٍ لُّجِّىٍّ يَغْشَىٰهُ مَوْجٌ مِّن فَوْقِهِۦ مَوْجٌ مِّن فَوْقِهِۦ سَحَابٌ ۚ ظُلُمَـٰتٌۢ بَعْضُهَا فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ إِذَآ أَخْرَجَ يَدَهُۥ لَمْ يَكَدْ يَرَىٰهَا ۗ وَمَن لَّمْ يَجْعَلِ ٱللَّهُ لَهُۥ نُورًا فَمَا لَهُۥ مِن نُّورٍ

"Or like darknesses in a deep sea, covered by a wave, above it a wave, above that a cloud — darknesses piled one above another. When he puts out his hand he can hardly see it. And whoever Allah has appointed no light for has no light."

An-Nur (24:40)

The lamp verse stacked light: glass, star, oil, flame, nur 'ala nur. This one stacks the reverse in the same architecture — wave over wave over cloud, zulumat in the plural piled one on another. Two towers built the same way, pointing opposite directions.

The final clause settles the grammar question the whole passage has been circling. Wa man lam yaj'ali'llahu lahu nuran fa-ma lahu min nur — the verb is ja'ala, to appoint or make. Light is described as something God assigns to a person, and the surah says outright that whoever has been assigned none has none.

Al-Muyassar's paraphrase of that clause closes the circle on the whole discussion. It renders the ending as fa-ma lahu min had — he has no guide — and specifies the light in question as the light of God's Book and His Prophet's Sunnah. Five verses after the lamp, the commentary reaches for the same word Ibn Abbas used to gloss the name itself.

The same verb carries the promise elsewhere, in a verse that also closes on the name of forgiveness: God will appoint for the believers a light to walk by, and forgive them, "and Allah is Ghafur, Rahim" (Al-Hadid 57:28). Read together, the two verses treat light the way the Quran usually does — as a grant.

The supplication and the hadith

Ibn Kathir supports the discussion with a report recorded in both Sahih collections, from Ibn Abbas: when the Prophet ﷺ rose to pray at night, he would say — praise be to You, You are the Sustainer of the heavens and the earth and whoever is in them; praise be to You, You are the Light of the heavens and the earth and whoever is in them.

The clause of the verse is being said back to God in prayer, with an addition: wa man fihinna, and whoever is in them. The supplication extends the reach of the sentence to include people.

Ibn Kathir also cites a saying of Ibn Mas'ud: there is no night or day with your Lord; the light of the Throne comes from the light of His Face.

Reading the name

An-Nur sits on most lists of the ninety-nine names, and it is the one whose single Quranic occurrence carries the most interpretive weight. Every other name on those lists can be checked against a spread of verses. This one has one sentence, a parable attached to it, and a body of early commentary that took the sentence seriously enough to argue about it for generations.

What the early readers agree on is the direction of travel. Light in the Quran moves from God to creation — sent down with the Book, given to those who believe, walked in on the Day of Judgement. The one verse that turns the word into a name for Him is the verse explaining where all of that comes from.

Ibn Abbas said it meant Guide. Al-Jalalayn said it meant the One who lights them. As-Suddi said the heavens and earth are lit by His light. Read together, the three answers are one answer approached from three sides: whatever is illuminated was illuminated by Him, and the illumination the surah goes on to describe at length happens inside a human heart.
