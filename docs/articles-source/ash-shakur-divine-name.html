Ash-Shakur: The Divine Name That Calls God Grateful

Ash-Shakur (ٱلشَّكُور) is the strangest name on the list, because gratitude is what a creature owes its Creator, and the Quran turns the word around and applies it to God. The same intensive form describes both parties. The adjective shakūr appears ten times — four of them naming Allah, the other six describing human beings — and the Quran keeps the two groups apart by what it pairs each one with. (The root produces a separate word, shukūr, meaning the act of thanks itself, twice more at 25:62 and 76:9; those are not this name.)

The root, and the odd shape of the claim

The root is sh-k-r (ش ك ر). Beyond thanking, Arabic lexicographers record a second field for it drawn from animals: a beast that yields abundantly on little grazing was called shakūr — one that returns much from small input. That sense sits underneath the theology neatly, because what the name describes is a return out of proportion to what was given.

The pattern is faʿūl, the same shape as ghafūr and ṣabūr, denoting a quality that is abundant and characteristic. Applied to a servant it means deeply grateful. Applied to God it cannot mean that He owes anything, and the classical commentators say so by translating the name into action rather than feeling.

What the commentators do with it

The clearest gloss comes at Surah Fatir, in a verse about people who recite the Book, establish prayer, and spend from what they were given.

لِيُوَفِّيَهُمْ أُجُورَهُمْ وَيَزِيدَهُم مِّن فَضْلِهِۦٓ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ غَفُورٌ شَكُورٌ

"That He may pay them their rewards in full and increase them from His bounty. Indeed He is Forgiving, Appreciative."

Fatir (35:30)

Al-Jalalayn splits the two names in four words: ghafūr — of their sins; shakūr — of their obedience. Al-Muyassar makes the symmetry sharper still: forgiving of their bad deeds, appreciative of their good ones, rewarding them abundantly for them.

So the pair covers a person completely. One name takes what they did wrong; the other takes what they did right. Between them there is nothing left over.

The verse before the name explains what the appreciation looks like in practice — pay them their rewards in full, and then increase them from His bounty. Payment discharges the obligation; the increase is the part that is not owed.

The pattern the four divine uses follow

Ash-Shakur names Allah four times, and three of those four put Al-Ghafur immediately before it: Fatir 35:30, Fatir 35:34, and Ash-Shura 42:23. The fourth swaps the partner.

إِن تُقْرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَـٰعِفْهُ لَكُمْ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

"If you lend Allah a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you. And Allah is Appreciative, Forbearing."

At-Taghabun (64:17)

Al-Jalalayn keeps the same division across the new pair: shakūr — rewarding obedience; ḥalīm — forbearing in punishing disobedience. The partner name changed and the structure held. Whatever stands beside Ash-Shakur is doing the work on the other side of the ledger.

The verse also supplies the surrounding vocabulary. The believer is described as lending God a loan, and the return is multiplication plus forgiveness. Al-Jalalayn notes the multiplication runs from tenfold to seven hundred and beyond.

The six that describe human beings

The other occurrences apply the same word to people, and there the recurring partner is a different one: ṣabbār, the deeply patient.

Four verses close on the identical phrase — li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūr, for every one who is deeply patient and deeply grateful. They appear at Ibrahim 14:5, Luqman 31:31, Saba 34:19, and Ash-Shura 42:33, and in three of the four the sign being described involves ships running on the sea or the days of God.

The pairing is precise. Ṣabr is what a person does with what is taken; shukr is what they do with what is given. Naming them together describes someone who handles both directions, which is why the phrase attaches to those who can read a sign either way.

And one verse gives the title to a single named man.

ذُرِّيَّةَ مَنْ حَمَلْنَا مَعَ نُوحٍ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ عَبْدًا شَكُورًا

"Descendants of those We carried with Nuh. Indeed he was a grateful servant."

Al-Isra (17:3)

Nuh preached for nine hundred and fifty years and was answered by a handful. The one word the Quran chooses to sum him up, in a verse addressed to the descendants of the people who boarded with him, is the word for gratitude.

The verse that says how rare it is

Saba closes the passage on what was built for Sulayman with a command and a count.

يَعْمَلُونَ لَهُۥ مَا يَشَآءُ مِن مَّحَـٰرِيبَ وَتَمَـٰثِيلَ وَجِفَانٍ كَٱلْجَوَابِ وَقُدُورٍ رَّاسِيَـٰتٍ ۚ ٱعْمَلُوٓا۟ ءَالَ دَاوُۥدَ شُكْرًا ۚ وَقَلِيلٌ مِّنْ عِبَادِىَ ٱلشَّكُورُ

"They made for him what he willed of chambers, statues, basins like reservoirs, and cauldrons fixed in place. Work, family of Dawud, in gratitude. And few of My servants are grateful."

Saba (34:13)

The command is iʿmalū — work in gratitude, not feel it. Gratitude is given as something done with the hands, addressed to a household surrounded by more than most people will ever hold. Then the sentence that follows undercuts any comfort in it: few of My servants are shakūr.

The scarcity is stated in a passage about abundance. Having much and being grateful are set out as separate questions, and the second is described as the rarer. The word runs in both directions and the Quran reports the traffic honestly: characteristic of God, rare among people.

The name at the end of grief

Fatir 35:34 gives the third of the Ghafur-Shakur pairings to people who have already arrived.

وَقَالُوا۟ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَذْهَبَ عَنَّا ٱلْحَزَنَ ۖ إِنَّ رَبَّنَا لَغَفُورٌ شَكُورٌ

"And they said: praise be to Allah, who has removed grief from us. Indeed our Lord is Forgiving, Appreciative."

Fatir (35:34)

It is the inhabitants of the Garden speaking, and the two names they choose are the same two the surah used four verses earlier about their deeds. Forgiven for what was wrong, appreciated for what was right — said this time by the people it was said about.

Reading the name

English translations avoid rendering Ash-Shakur as "the Grateful," and the avoidance is doing real theological work. "The Appreciative," "the Most Ready to Appreciate," "the Rewarder of Good" all move the word from feeling to response, which is exactly what al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar do with it in Arabic.

What the name says is that small obedience gets a disproportionate return, and that the return is not payment. The verse in Fatir pays the wage in full and then adds from His bounty. The verse in At-Taghabun multiplies the loan and forgives the lender.

A name built on a root that once described getting much from little, given to the One who has no need of any of it.
