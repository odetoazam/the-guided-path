Two Verses Forbid the Same Thing. The Word Order Tells You Who Was Afraid.

The Quran twice forbids parents from killing their children out of poverty. It is the same prohibition, and at a glance the two lines look like the same sentence. They are not. Two small things are rearranged between them — the kind of poverty named, and the order of two pronouns — and neither change is decoration. Each verse is aimed at a different fear, and the words are re-sequenced to answer the fear it is aimed at. Read together, they show the Quran doing something more precise than issuing a rule: it is consoling two different kinds of frightened parent, each in the order they are frightened.

Where each one sits

The first comes inside Al-Anʿam's great list of foundational commands — the verse often called the Quran's ten commandments:

قُلْ تَعَالَوْا۟ أَتْلُ مَا حَرَّمَ رَبُّكُمْ عَلَيْكُمْ ۖ أَلَّا تُشْرِكُوا۟ بِهِۦ شَيْـًٔا ۖ وَبِٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ إِحْسَـٰنًا ۖ وَلَا تَقْتُلُوٓا۟ أَوْلَـٰدَكُم مِّنْ إِمْلَـٰقٍ ۖ نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُكُمْ وَإِيَّاهُمْ ۖ وَلَا تَقْرَبُوا۟ ٱلْفَوَٰحِشَ مَا ظَهَرَ مِنْهَا وَمَا بَطَنَ ۖ وَلَا تَقْتُلُوا۟ ٱلنَّفْسَ ٱلَّتِى حَرَّمَ ٱللَّهُ إِلَّا بِٱلْحَقِّ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمْ وَصَّىٰكُم بِهِۦ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

"Say: come, I will recite what your Lord has forbidden you — that you associate nothing with Him; and to parents, good treatment; and do not kill your children out of poverty — We provide for you and for them; and do not approach indecencies, open or hidden; and do not kill the soul God has made sacred, except by right. This He has charged you with, that you may reason."

Al-Anʿam (6:151)

The second stands nearly alone in Al-Isra, one prohibition given its own weight:

وَلَا تَقْتُلُوٓا۟ أَوْلَـٰدَكُمْ خَشْيَةَ إِمْلَـٰقٍ ۖ نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُهُمْ وَإِيَّاكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ قَتْلَهُمْ كَانَ خِطْـًٔا كَبِيرًا

"And do not kill your children out of fear of poverty — We provide for them, and for you. Indeed, killing them is a great sin."

Al-Isra (17:31)

The first difference: the poverty you have, and the poverty you dread

In Al-Anʿam the phrase is min imlāq — out of poverty. The little word min marks a cause already present: the poverty is here, in the house, now. Al-Muyassar reads it exactly this way — because of a poverty that has befallen you. In Al-Isra the phrase changes to khashyata imlāq — out of fear of poverty. Now the poverty is not in the room; it is a projection, a dread of what has not yet come. Al-Muyassar again, on this one: out of fear of poverty. One verse speaks to a parent who is poor. The other speaks to a parent who is afraid of becoming poor. The same act is forbidden, but two different fears are driving it.

The second difference: who gets named first

Now watch the reassurance that follows each, because this is where the rearrangement becomes remarkable. Both verses promise the same provision — but the order of the two pronouns flips.

To the parent already in poverty (6:151): naḥnu narzuqukum wa iyyāhum — "We provide for you, and for them." The word for you comes first.

To the parent who fears future poverty (17:31): naḥnu narzuquhum wa iyyākum — "We provide for them, and for you." The word for them comes first.

The classical reading of that choice — and this is a balāgha observation about the word order, not something the four core commentaries spell out — hears the flip as the mercy tracking the fear. The parent who is already poor is gripped by an immediate, bodily worry: I can barely feed myself; how will another mouth survive? The fear begins at his own empty hands. So the reassurance begins there too: We provide for you — your own subsistence is not in question — and for them. But the parent who has enough today and dreads a future collapse is not frightened about tonight's bread. His fear is thrown forward, onto the child: what will become of them when the money runs out? So the reassurance is thrown forward to meet it: We provide for them — the child you are afraid for — and for you. Each verse names first, in its promise, exactly the person that parent's fear was circling.

The precision is the mercy

Set the two lines together and nothing is merely repeated. The kind of poverty, the direction of the fear, and the order of the promise all shift together, as one adjustment made twice. A flat prohibition could have said do not kill your children; God provides and left it there. Instead the Quran forbids the act to a poor man in the words a poor man needs, and forbids the same act to an anxious man in the words an anxious man needs — and in each case it places, at the very front of its comfort, the name that the parent's fear kept circling back to. The command is identical. The consolation is fitted. That two-word difference is the Quran doing what a distant lawgiver never does: bending to the exact shape of the fear in front of it, and answering the frightened person first about the one they could not stop thinking of.
