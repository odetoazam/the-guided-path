Al-Afuww: The Divine Name for Erasing, and the One Verse Where It Meets Power

Al-Afuww (ٱلْعَفُوّ) is usually translated "the Pardoner," which is accurate and slightly flat. The root is more physical than that. ʿAfw is what happens to a footprint when the wind works on it — the mark is not covered over, it stops being there. That is the difference the classical scholars draw between this name and Al-Ghafur, whose root means to cover something so it is no longer exposed. One hides the trace. The other removes it.

The name occurs five times, and all five name God. Four of the five pair it with the name for covering. The fifth pairs it with something else entirely, and that is the verse worth the article.

The four, and where they land

Take the four ʿafuww-and-forgiving verses first, because their contexts are unusually specific:

  • 4:43 — the rules for prayer when a person is not in a state to pray: intoxicated, or in need of a bath, or ill, or travelling, or without water. It ends ʿafuwwan ghafūrā.
  • 4:99 — the people who could not emigrate because they genuinely had no way. Al-Muyassar: these weak ones are the ones for whom pardon is hoped, because He knows the reality of their situation.
  • 58:2 — a man who has told his wife she is to him "as his mother's back," the pre-Islamic formula the surah is legislating against. Al-Muyassar: God is ʿafuww ghafūr toward whoever has fallen into some of these violations and then made it good with a sincere turning back.
  • 22:60 — a person who retaliated in kind and was then wronged again. Al-Muyassar reads the closing name as pardoning the sinners so that He does not hurry them to the punishment.

These are not grand scenes of repentance. They are people who could not manage the requirement, people with no travel options, a man who said a stupid thing in anger, and someone caught in a cycle of retaliation. The name for erasure attaches to the ordinary and the awkward.

The fifth verse

One of the five does not pair ʿafuww with forgiveness. It pairs it with capability.

إِن تُبْدُوا۟ خَيْرًا أَوْ تُخْفُوهُ أَوْ تَعْفُوا۟ عَن سُوٓءٍ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ عَفُوًّا قَدِيرًا

"If you show good, or hide it, or pardon an evil — then indeed God is Pardoning, Powerful."

An-Nisa (4:149)

ʿAfuwwan qadīrā. Pardoning, and able.

Al-Muyassar does not leave the pairing to the reader. He reads the verse as an encouragement to pardon, and then explains why the second name is there: among His attributes, exalted is He, is pardoning His servants while having the power over them.

That is the whole point of the verse in one clause. Pardon from someone who could not have done otherwise is not pardon; it is just an absence of options. The name for ability is placed beside the name for erasure so that the first cannot be read as the second's cause.

And it is placed there in a verse instructing human beings to pardon. The logic runs downhill: you are being asked to let go of a wrong you could act on, and the model given is a God who could act on every wrong and does not.

The verse before it, which is the harder one

4:149 does not stand alone. The verse immediately before it says that God does not like the open utterance of evil speech — except by one who has been wronged.

So the passage grants the wronged person the right to speak, and then, in the next breath, recommends the other option. Al-Jalalayn's gloss on aw taʿfū ʿan sūʾ is one word: ẓulman — a wrong done to you.

Read as a pair, the two verses are careful in a way worth noticing. The right to complain is not withdrawn. It is stated first, and then something better is described. The passage does not tell a wronged person that their grievance is invalid.

Where the tradition puts this name

The best-known use of al-ʿafuww in Muslim practice is not a verse at all. It is the supplication the Prophet ﷺ taught ʿA'ishah to say on the night of Laylat al-Qadr when she asked what to say if she found it: O God, You are Pardoning, and You love pardon, so pardon me.

Which is a striking choice. Given one request on the best night of the year, the instruction is not to ask for provision, or for paradise, or for knowledge. It is to ask for the erasure of what is there. The site treats that supplication and its night in the Surah Al-Qadr overview.

ʿAfw and maghfirah, kept apart

The distinction between the two roots is not a modern refinement; it is why the Quran can put both names in one phrase without repeating itself. Ghafara is from the same family as mighfar, the mail hood a fighter wore under a helmet — something placed over a thing to keep it from harm and from view. ʿAfā is used of a trace being effaced.

Put in that order — ʿafuwwan ghafūrā — the sequence has a shape. The offence is erased, and then covered. A person who has been pardoned in both senses is not walking around with a covered-over record; there is nothing under the cover.

That is a reading of the pair, offered as a reading. The four verses put the two names side by side; they do not spell out the relationship between them.

Reading the name

Five occurrences, all divine, in verses about people who could not perform the ritual, could not make the journey, said something they should not have, or hit back. Four of them attach the name for covering.

The fifth attaches the name for power, and a commentator states the reason outright: He pardons His servants while having every capacity over them. That is what separates pardon from mere inaction, and it is offered in a verse that is asking the reader to do the same thing on a smaller scale.
