The Storm Changes the Grammar: The Verse That Stops Calling You "You" the Moment the Sea Turns Rough

There is a single verse in Surah Yunus that describes a sea voyage — calm start, sudden storm, desperate prayer, rescue. And as the scene moves, the grammar moves with it. God begins by speaking directly to you. Then, exactly when the good wind is blowing and everyone is comfortable, the verse stops addressing "you" and starts describing "them" from a distance. The only time the direct address comes back is in the drowning-panic prayer — and by then it is the sailors talking, and the "You" is aimed at God.

The classical commentators noticed the grammatical turn centuries ago and even named it. What it seems to mean is left more open — but the shift is undeniably there, and once you see where it happens, the verse reads like a diagram of how people hold God close in danger and let go of Him in comfort.

Watch the pronouns move

هُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُسَيِّرُكُمْ فِى ٱلْبَرِّ وَٱلْبَحْرِ ۖ حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا كُنتُمْ فِى ٱلْفُلْكِ وَجَرَيْنَ بِهِم بِرِيحٍ طَيِّبَةٍ وَفَرِحُوا۟ بِهَا جَآءَتْهَا رِيحٌ عَاصِفٌ وَجَآءَهُمُ ٱلْمَوْجُ مِن كُلِّ مَكَانٍ وَظَنُّوٓا۟ أَنَّهُمْ أُحِيطَ بِهِمْ ۙ دَعَوُا۟ ٱللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ لَئِنْ أَنجَيْتَنَا مِنْ هَـٰذِهِۦ لَنَكُونَنَّ مِنَ ٱلشَّـٰكِرِينَ

"He is the One who carries you across land and sea — until, when you are aboard the ships and they sail with them on a fair wind, and they rejoice in it, a violent wind overtakes them, and the waves come at them from every side, and they think they are engulfed — they call upon God, sincere to Him in faith: 'If You save us from this, we will surely be among the grateful.'"

Yunus (10:22)

Follow the persons. The verse opens with God speaking to you: "He carries you (yusayyiru-kum) across land and sea, until when you are (kuntum) aboard the ships." Second person. You are being addressed, held inside the sentence.

Then, at the exact word where the ship sets sail on the fair wind, the grammar turns away: "and they sailed with them (bihim)… and they rejoiced… a wind came at them… they thought they were engulfed." Third person now — described from the outside, like a camera pulling back. You have become "them."

And the second person only returns inside the panic: "they called upon God… 'if You save us.'" But notice who is speaking now — it is the sailors, and the "You" is pointed at God. The direct address that God opened with has been handed back to Him, in a prayer, by people who only remembered to say "You" when the water rose.

The turn the commentators named

This is not a pattern imposed on the verse from outside. Al-Jalalayn, the classic concise commentary, stops at the precise word where it happens — wa-jarayna bihim, "and they sailed with them" — and writes plainly: "in this there is an iltifāt away from direct address." Iltifāt — literally "a turning" — is the recognized Arabic term for exactly this move: a sudden, deliberate shift of person mid-sentence. The tradition catalogued it as one of the Quran's rhetorical signatures. So the grammatical fact is solid and old: the verse turns from "you" to "them" at the moment the voyage turns easy.

What a careful reading owes here

Here is the honest line between what is established and what is reflection. That the shift happens, and happens at that word, is classical balāgha — named by the commentators, checkable in the grammar. What the shift means is where the reading becomes a modern reflection, offered as one, not as a claim the old commentators made: that the pronouns seem to track the heart's distance from God. Intimate "you" while He is carrying you; distant "them" the moment the fair wind makes people forget who sent it; desperate "You" again only when comfort is torn away. The grammar, read this way, quietly diagnoses a faith that addresses God directly in the storm and drifts into forgetting Him — into "he," into "them" — on calm water. That is an interpretation the verse invites; it is not something the classical sources spell out, and it should be held as reflection.

One more note, for precision. This voyage appears elsewhere in the Quran too, and Surah Yunus itself is reflected on across these verses for other reasons — the mercy-then-scheming of people, the parable of worldly life that follows. What is specific here is the pronoun choreography of this one verse: the second-to-third-to-second turn is unusually complete in 10:22, and it is the grammar, not the story, that carries the point. The sea narrative is familiar. The way the sentence changes who it is talking to, right as the weather changes, is the thing worth stopping on.
