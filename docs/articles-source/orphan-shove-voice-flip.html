You Shove the Orphan Away. The Same Verb Shoves You Into the Fire.

There is a small, violent verb in the Quran — yadu''u, "he shoves." Not a gentle push. The classical commentators gloss it as shoving someone away with force. As a plain verb it is used only twice in the whole Quran, and the two uses face each other like a mirror. The first time, a man is doing the shoving. The second time, he is the one being shoved.

You do not need any Arabic to feel what happens between the two verses. The Quran takes one hard little word, points it at the most defenseless person in the room, and then — in a different surah — turns the exact same word around so that it is now pointed at the person who used it.

The man who shoves the orphan

The first use is in Surah Al-Maʿun. The surah opens by asking who really denies the faith — and then answers with a portrait, not a doctrine:

أَرَءَيْتَ ٱلَّذِى يُكَذِّبُ بِٱلدِّينِ

"Have you seen the one who denies the Faith?"

Al-Maʿun (107:1)

فَذَٰلِكَ ٱلَّذِى يَدُعُّ ٱلْيَتِيمَ

"That is the one who shoves the orphan away."

Al-Maʿun (107:2)

The verb is yadu''u — he shoves. Al-Jalalayn spells it out: he pushes the orphan away, with force, from his right. Al-Muyassar adds the reason — out of hardness of heart. The picture is a child who has already lost a father, coming to claim what is owed him, and a grown man putting a hand on him and shoving him back out the door. That, the surah says, is what denying the faith actually looks like — not an argument, a gesture.

The same verb, turned around

The only other place this verb appears is in Surah At-Tur, in a scene on the Day of Judgment. The people being described are the deniers — the ones who called the reckoning a lie:

يَوْمَ يُدَعُّونَ إِلَىٰ نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ دَعًّا

"The Day they will be shoved into the Fire of Hell with a violent shove."

At-Tur (52:13)

Same verb — and al-Jalalayn glosses it with the identical words he used for the orphan: they are shoved, with force. But the grammar has flipped. In 107:2 the verb is active: a man does the shoving. In 52:13 it is passive: they are shoved, and no human hand is named, because the sentence has taken the doing away from them entirely. There is even a second grammatical shift underneath the first: the man in Al-Maʿun is singular — one person, one gesture — while the shoved in At-Tur are plural. The single shover has become the crowd being shoved.

And the verse does not stop there. It turns and speaks to them directly:

هَـٰذِهِ ٱلنَّارُ ٱلَّتِى كُنتُم بِهَا تُكَذِّبُونَ

"This is the Fire that you used to deny."

At-Tur (52:14)

What actually ties the two scenes together

It would be easy to overstate this and say the Quran is describing the same people in both places — the orphan-shover getting shoved. It isn't, and the honest reading is better than that anyway. What links the two scenes is not a shared cast; it is a shared word used twice, and a second word that brackets both.

Look at what surrounds each shove. In Al-Maʿun, the shover is introduced one verse earlier as "the one who denies the Faith" — yukadhdhibu bi-d-dīn. In At-Tur, the ones being shoved are, in the verses right around this, "the deniers" (52:11) and are told to their faces, "this is the Fire you used to deny" (52:14). The same root — k-dh-b, to deny, to call the truth a lie — sits on both sides of the mirror. So the pairing is not "this individual gets his comeuppance." It is that the Quran files the man who shoves a child and the crowd shoved into the Fire under the same heading: people who denied the reckoning. One of them acts out his denial with his hands, on someone small. The other meets the same verb from the other end.

What a careful reading owes here

A few things, stated plainly. This is a two-verse pattern in the plain verb — yadu''u as an active or passive verb occurs only at 107:2 and 52:13, corpus-checked. There is a third appearance of the root in the very same At-Tur verse, but as a noun — da''ā, "a shoving," the word doubled for emphasis ("shoved with a shove"). That is why the claim is scoped to the verb: two verbs, one active, one passive.

The link between the scenes is a resonance the Quran builds with vocabulary, not a statement that the same individuals appear twice. It earns the connection the honest way — through the repeated verb and the shared root of denial around both — and a reader should hold it exactly that firmly and no firmer. The Quran is not saying "the man who shoved the orphan will personally be shoved." It is showing you one violent little verb from both ends: aimed by a hard heart at a child, and aimed back at a category of people who spent their lives denying that any of it would ever be aimed at them.

It is a strange mercy that the warning is this concrete. The Quran could have described the punishment of the deniers in any words it liked. It chose the exact verb it had already used for what a cruel man does to an orphan at the door — so that anyone who has ever shoved someone smaller than himself would recognize the motion instantly, from the wrong side of it.
