Al-Hayy: The Divine Name for Life, and the Verse That Calls the Other Gods Dead

Al-Hayy (ٱلْحَىّ), the Living, is the plainest of the divine names and the easiest to skim past. Of course God is living. The word occurs twenty-four times in the Quran, and only five of those name God — the other nineteen describe created things: souls brought out of the dead, the martyrs who are not to be called dead, every living thing made from water, and, in one verse, the gods people were praying to.

That last one is where the name earns its weight.

The five

Three of the five divine uses put al-Ḥayy immediately before al-Qayyūm — at 2:255, 3:2 and 20:111. The site covers that pairing in the article on Al-Qayyum, whose whole subject is that the second name never appears without the first.

The other two are the interesting ones here, because in both the name stands alone and in both it is immediately followed by an instruction.

وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱلْحَىِّ ٱلَّذِى لَا يَمُوتُ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِهِۦ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِهِۦ بِذُنُوبِ عِبَادِهِۦ خَبِيرًا

"And rely upon the Living who does not die, and glorify Him with His praise. And He suffices as one aware of the sins of His servants."

Al-Furqan (25:58)

هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ فَٱدْعُوهُ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ ۗ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"He is the Living. There is no god but He. So call upon Him, making the religion sincerely His. Praise belongs to God, Lord of all the worlds."

Ghafir (40:65)

Rely, in the first. Call, in the second. In both, the name is not offered for contemplation — it is the reason given for an action, and the action is the same kind of thing both times: directing yourself at someone.

Al-Furqan adds a qualifier the other four do not: alladhī lā yamūt, who does not die. Al-Muyassar reads it as the One who has every meaning of complete life, as befits His majesty, and who does not die.

The qualifier is doing something specific. Reliance is a relationship with a future in it. You lean on something because you expect it to still be there. Adding "who does not die" to the object of that leaning is a statement about duration, not about vitality.

The nineteen

The rest of the word's occurrences are about creation, and they cluster into three kinds.

Life brought out of death. Eight of the nineteen belong to one formula — He brings the living out of the dead and the dead out of the living — which appears in four verses (3:27, 6:95, 10:31, 30:19), each using the word twice because the sentence runs in both directions. It is always offered as an argument rather than as a fact; the verses around it are asking who does this.

Life as the substance of everything.

أَوَلَمْ يَرَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَـٰهُمَا ۖ وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلْمَآءِ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ حَىٍّ ۖ أَفَلَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

"Have those who disbelieved not seen that the heavens and the earth were joined together, and We parted them, and We made from water every living thing? Will they not then believe?"

Al-Anbiya (21:30)

And the ones about people. The martyrs are not to be called dead — bal aḥyāʾ, rather they are living (2:154, 3:169). Yahya and Isa are each given peace on the day they are raised ḥayyan, alive (19:15, 19:33) — the same word closing the same formula for both of them, a few verses apart in Surah Maryam.

The verse that uses it against the idols

Surah An-Nahl, arguing about what people worship, reaches for this word and uses it as a disqualification.

أَمْوَٰتٌ غَيْرُ أَحْيَآءٍ ۖ وَمَا يَشْعُرُونَ أَيَّانَ يُبْعَثُونَ

"Dead, not living. And they do not perceive when they will be raised."

An-Nahl (16:21)

Two words, then two more. Amwātun — dead. Ghayru aḥyāʾ — not living.

Al-Jalalayn treats the second phrase as emphasis on the first: dead, meaning no spirit in them; not living, which repeats it. And then he does something a commentator does not have to do — he draws the conclusion out loud:

So how are they worshipped, when none can be a god except the Creator, the Living, the Knower of the unseen?

That is al-Jalalayn putting the divine name into a verse that does not contain it, because the verse's argument depends on it. Al-Muyassar reads the same way: they are inert objects with no life in them, and they do not know when their own worshippers will be raised.

The logic is short enough to hold in one hand. Worship is a relationship. A relationship requires two parties. The verse's objection to the idols is not that they are weak or that they are man-made; it is that there is nobody there.

Why the plainest name is not the emptiest

Put the five and the nineteen together and a shape appears that a single verse would not show.

Nineteen times the Quran says something is alive, and every one of those is contingent — a soul drawn out of the dead, a martyr on the far side of a death, a man warned while he is still alive to be warned, every living thing made out of water. The word is being applied to things that received life from outside themselves.

Five times it names God, and one of those five attaches the clause that separates the two senses: who does not die. Everything else in the list is alive on loan.

And in the one verse where the word is denied to something, the thing being denied is a god. Which is what the name is for. The site treats the same problem from the other side in As-Samad — the One everything turns to, who turns to nothing.
