Four Women, Two Parables: What At-Tahrim Says About Who You Marry and Who You Are

At the close of a surah, God offers four women as examples for all of humanity, and arranges them to demolish one of the most comfortable assumptions a religious person can hold. Two of the women were married to prophets — the closest a human being can stand to guidance — and they are in the Fire. One was married to the cruelest tyrant in the Quran, a man who called himself a god, and she is in Paradise. The fourth had no husband at all, and she is held up as the summit. Proximity to a righteous man saved no one. Distance from him damned no one. Each woman was weighed as herself.

The Quran frames the four deliberately: God strikes an example for those who disbelieved, then God strikes an example for those who believed. Two panels, hung facing each other, and the women in them are sorted by something no marriage certificate can transfer.

The two who stood closest and still fell

The first panel names two wives of prophets:

ضَرَبَ ٱللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِّلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ ٱمْرَأَتَ نُوحٍ وَٱمْرَأَتَ لُوطٍ ۖ كَانَتَا تَحْتَ عَبْدَيْنِ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا صَـٰلِحَيْنِ فَخَانَتَاهُمَا فَلَمْ يُغْنِيَا عَنْهُمَا مِنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا وَقِيلَ ٱدْخُلَا ٱلنَّارَ مَعَ ٱلدَّٰخِلِينَ

"Allah presents an example for those who disbelieved: the wife of Nuh and the wife of Lut. They were under two of Our righteous servants, but they betrayed them, so those two availed them nothing against Allah, and it was said, 'Enter the Fire with those who enter.'"

At-Tahrim (66:10)

These women shared the homes of Nuh and Lut. They heard revelation at the breakfast table. They watched prophecy live at close range for years. The word for their failure is khānatāhumā — they betrayed them — and the commentators are careful that this was betrayal in faith, not the marriage bed: they sided against the message, mocked it, tipped off its enemies. And the verdict is stated as a law of physics: their prophet-husbands availed them nothing against Allah. The holiest marriage on earth transferred exactly zero credit across the aisle. When the door closed, each woman stood on her own record, and her own record put her among those who enter the Fire.

The one who stood closest to evil and rose

The second panel opens with a woman whose address was the opposite in every way:

وَضَرَبَ ٱللَّهُ مَثَلًا لِّلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱمْرَأَتَ فِرْعَوْنَ إِذْ قَالَتْ رَبِّ ٱبْنِ لِى عِندَكَ بَيْتًا فِى ٱلْجَنَّةِ وَنَجِّنِى مِن فِرْعَوْنَ وَعَمَلِهِۦ وَنَجِّنِى مِنَ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

"And Allah presents an example for those who believed: the wife of Pharaoh, when she said, 'My Lord, build for me a house with You in Paradise, and save me from Pharaoh and his deed, and save me from the wrongdoing people.'"

At-Tahrim (66:11)

She lived in the house of the man who claimed to be God, and prayed to be saved from him — a prayer explored in its own right in our reading of the two "build for me" requests that echo across this palace. What the panel adds is the symmetry. If the wives of prophets prove that nearness to good cannot save you, she proves the mirror truth: nearness to evil cannot condemn you. She breathed the same air as the worst man in the Quran, ate at his table, and walked out of the marriage, in God's ledger, headed for the very house she asked Him to build.

The one who stood alone

Then the panel does something unexpected. Having sorted women by the men they were bound to, it closes on a woman bound to no man at all:

وَمَرْيَمَ ٱبْنَتَ عِمْرَٰنَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهِ مِن رُّوحِنَا وَصَدَّقَتْ بِكَلِمَـٰتِ رَبِّهَا وَكُتُبِهِۦ وَكَانَتْ مِنَ ٱلْقَـٰنِتِينَ

"And Mariam, the daughter of 'Imran, who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into it from Our Spirit; and she confirmed the words of her Lord and His scriptures, and she was of the devoutly obedient."

At-Tahrim (66:12)

The grammar of identity shifts across the four. The two lost women are named only by their husbands — the wife of Nuh, the wife of Lut — and are never quoted; the surah speaks about them. The wife of Pharaoh begins to break free, named still by her husband but given her own voice, her own prayer set down word for word. And Maryam arrives with her own name and her father's, defined entirely by her own acts: she guarded, she confirmed, she was devout. The progression across the four is a steady handing-back of selfhood. The further a woman's salvation rests on what she herself did, the more the Quran lets her stand as herself — until the summit of the passage is a woman who needed no husband in the frame at all.

The inheritance that isn't

Put the two panels together and the argument is unmistakable, and it reaches far past marriage. We are endlessly tempted to bank on borrowed righteousness — a pious spouse, a scholar father, a devout family name, a community we belong to — as though standing near guidance deposits some of it into our account. The wife of Nuh stood nearer to a prophet than any scholar's student ever will, and it availed her nothing. And we are just as tempted to write people off for the house they come from — the wrong family, the compromised background, the tyrant's table — as though proximity to evil were a stain that fixes fate. The wife of Pharaoh came from the worst house imaginable and outran it to Paradise.

So the four women leave a question that strips away every name we borrow. If the marriage to a prophet transferred nothing, and the marriage to a tyrant blocked nothing, then the account being read on that Day is single, and it is yours. Strip away the spouse, the lineage, the community you stand inside — what is left when you are weighed, like each of these four, entirely as yourself? One woman shared a prophet's bed and entered the Fire. Another shared a tyrant's and asked for a house in Paradise. The distance between them was never their address. It was what each one chose to be inside it.
