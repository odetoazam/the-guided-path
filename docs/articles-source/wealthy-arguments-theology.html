When the Quran Quotes the Wealthy, They Argue Theology

The Quran preserves a surprising amount of direct speech from the people it is arguing against. It lets them finish their sentences. And when the speaker is someone with money and the subject is money, a pattern shows up in what comes out of their mouth: they answer with religion. Not with economics, not with self-interest openly stated — with a claim about God.

"Shall we feed someone God could feed Himself?"

The clearest case sits in Surah Ya-Sin. A request is made, and the reply turns divine power into a reason for refusal:

وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ أَنفِقُوا۟ مِمَّا رَزَقَكُمُ ٱللَّهُ قَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ أَنُطْعِمُ مَن لَّوْ يَشَآءُ ٱللَّهُ أَطْعَمَهُۥٓ إِنْ أَنتُمْ إِلَّا فِى ضَلَـٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ

"And when it is said to them: spend from what God has provided you — those who disbelieve say to those who believe: shall we feed someone whom God, had He willed, would have fed? You are in nothing but plain error."

Ya-Sin (36:47)

Al-Jalalayn fills in who is standing where: the poor among the Companions are the ones asking, and the answer comes back as mockery. Al-Tabari sharpens the possessives in the reply — shall we feed our wealth and our food to someone whom God, had He willed, would have fed?

The theology in that sentence is sound. God does feed whom He wills; nothing in the Quran disputes it. The work is done by where the premise is aimed. A true statement about divine power is pointed at a hungry person and used as a door. And the last clause completes the inversion: the people asking for food are the ones in plain error. Refusal has been reframed as the theologically serious position.

"We have more, so we must be right"

Surah Saba records a second argument, this one running from wealth backwards to a verdict:

وَقَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أَكْثَرُ أَمْوَٰلًا وَأَوْلَـٰدًا وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُعَذَّبِينَ

"And they said: we are greater in wealth and in children, and we will not be punished."

Saba (34:35)

Al-Tabari lays out the unstated middle step: had God been displeased with the religion and the conduct we are on, He would have withheld the wealth and the children and narrowed our provision. Al-Muyassar puts it in one line — God gave us these blessings only because He is pleased with us.

Again the premise is orthodox. Provision does come from God. The argument simply reads a bank balance as a message and treats the message as final. Whatever the messenger has come to say about their conduct arrives too late; the verdict was already delivered, in cash.

"Does your prayer have jurisdiction over our accounts?"

The people of Madyan give the sharpest version, because they say out loud what the other two leave implied — that religion has a proper territory, and their wealth lies outside it:

قَالُوا۟ يَـٰشُعَيْبُ أَصَلَوٰتُكَ تَأْمُرُكَ أَن نَّتْرُكَ مَا يَعْبُدُ ءَابَآؤُنَآ أَوْ أَن نَّفْعَلَ فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِنَا مَا نَشَـٰٓؤُا۟ إِنَّكَ لَأَنتَ ٱلْحَلِيمُ ٱلرَّشِيدُ

"They said: Shu'ayb, does your prayer command you that we abandon what our fathers worshipped, or leave off doing with our wealth whatever we please? You, surely, are the forbearing, the sensible one."

Hud (11:87)

Ibn Kathir, al-Tabari, al-Muyassar and al-Jalalayn all read the closing compliment as mockery. Al-Muyassar renders the sarcasm in the register they intended: you are the level-headed one, so good at managing money.

Two demands are bundled into one complaint — the idols and the ledger — and al-Tabari names what the second one actually covered in Madyan: clipping and cutting the silver coins, and shortchanging people in weight and measure. That is currency tampering and false scales. Their protest is that a man's prayer has wandered out of the mosque and into the marketplace, where it has no business being.

"Why wasn't this given to a rich man?"

The fourth objection concerns the messenger rather than the message — and it is an argument about net worth:

وَقَالُوا۟ لَوْلَا نُزِّلَ هَـٰذَا ٱلْقُرْءَانُ عَلَىٰ رَجُلٍ مِّنَ ٱلْقَرْيَتَيْنِ عَظِيمٍ

"And they said: why was this Quran not sent down on some great man from the two towns?"

Az-Zukhruf (43:31)

Al-Muyassar identifies the two towns as Mecca and Ta'if; al-Jalalayn goes further and names the candidates they had in mind — al-Walid ibn al-Mughira in Mecca, or 'Urwa ibn Mas'ud al-Thaqafi in Ta'if. Revelation, on this view, is a form of recognition, and recognition should track standing.

The answer is the most interesting reply in the set, because it moves the fight onto the objectors' own ground:

أَهُمْ يَقْسِمُونَ رَحْمَتَ رَبِّكَ نَحْنُ قَسَمْنَا بَيْنَهُم مَّعِيشَتَهُمْ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا وَرَفَعْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ فَوْقَ بَعْضٍ دَرَجَـٰتٍ لِّيَتَّخِذَ بَعْضُهُم بَعْضًا سُخْرِيًّا وَرَحْمَتُ رَبِّكَ خَيْرٌ مِّمَّا يَجْمَعُونَ

"Are they the ones who apportion your Lord's mercy? We have apportioned among them their very livelihood in the life of this world, and raised some of them above others in degrees, so that some may take others into service. And your Lord's mercy is better than what they accumulate."

Az-Zukhruf (43:32)

Al-Jalalayn reads "your Lord's mercy" in the opening question as prophethood itself, which makes the reply an argument from the smaller case to the larger: the livelihood you are so proud of was distributed to you. The tiers you are standing on were set. Al-Jalalayn's gloss on sukhriyyan is the ordinary economic arrangement it describes — one person employing another for a wage. The men proposing themselves as more suitable recipients of revelation are informed that they do not even set their own salaries.

Then the closing clause reprices everything named in the verse: your Lord's mercy is better than what they accumulate.

Why the arguments are always religious

Four speeches — three from particular scenes, one recorded as the habit of every town a warner ever entered — and the same manoeuvre in each: take a true statement about God — He wills, He provides, He apportions, He honours whom He chooses — and place it in front of an obligation so the obligation cannot get through. Nobody in these verses says I would rather keep my money. That sentence has no defence and everyone in the room knows it. What they say instead is that God has already settled the matter.

Which explains the shape of the replies. Saba answers the wealth-as-approval argument by leaving the wealth in place and denying it any purchasing power in the next life. Az-Zukhruf answers the great-man argument by pointing out who did the apportioning. The Quran meets a theological argument with theology, and takes back the premises one at a time.

The move survives well, which is what makes the record worth having. It is still possible to explain that someone's hardship is God's will, and to say it in a tone of real piety, and to finish the sentence with an untouched wallet. The people in Ya-Sin got there first. God preserved their exact words, and set them down among the sayings of those who turned away.
