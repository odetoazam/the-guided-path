The Quran Has a Tell for Whether It Will Answer Its Own Question

The Quran likes to stop and ask you a question about a word it has just used: and what will make you know what this is? It happens again and again — about the Night of Decree, about a fire called al-Ḥuṭamah, about the Day of Judgment, about a crushing calamity. And there is a rule hidden in the grammar of that question that tells you, before you read another word, whether an answer is coming or whether the door is about to close. The rule is a single letter. Once you see it, you can never un-see it.

The version that always answers

Thirteen times the Quran asks with the verb adrāka — a past-tense form, what has made you know…? — and every single time, the very next line answers it. The question is not there to withhold. It is a doorway held open. Watch it work:

وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا هِيَهْ

نَارٌ حَامِيَةٌۢ

"And what will make you know what it is? — A blazing fire."

Al-Qariah (101:10–11)

The question lands, and the answer is right there in the next breath. Same with the fire named al-Ḥuṭamah:

وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْحُطَمَةُ

نَارُ ٱللَّهِ ٱلْمُوقَدَةُ

"And what will make you know what al-Ḥuṭamah is? — The kindled fire of God."

Al-Humazah (104:5–6)

The pattern holds without exception across all thirteen: al-Ḥāqqah, Saqar, the Day of Decision, Sijjīn, ʿIlliyyūn, the night-comer, the steep ascent, the Night of Decree, the Striking Calamity. Every time the Quran asks with adrāka, it is clearing its throat to tell you. The message underneath the question is: you could never have reckoned this on your own — so here it is.

The version that never answers

Now the tell. Exactly three times, the verb shifts by one letter — from the past-tense adrāka to the present-tense yudrīka: what will make you know…? And every time it does, the answer does not come. Instead of an explanation, the next word is laʿalla — perhaps — and then the door quietly shuts:

يَسْـَٔلُكَ ٱلنَّاسُ عَنِ ٱلسَّاعَةِ ۖ قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ تَكُونُ قَرِيبًا

"People ask you about the Hour. Say: its knowledge is only with God. And what will make you know? — perhaps the Hour is near."

Al-Ahzab (33:63)

The question is asked and left open on purpose. Perhaps it is near — and that is all you get. The Hour's timing is named, in the same verse, as something whose knowledge is only with God. The yudrīka is not a prelude to an answer; it is the boundary line of what a human being is allowed to hold. The third instance turns the same withholding on a single human soul:

وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّهُۥ يَزَّكَّىٰ

"And what will make you know? — perhaps he will purify himself."

ʿAbasa (80:3)

This is God's answer to His Prophet for turning from the blind man who came seeking. Not he will purify himself, and not he won't — but perhaps, and you cannot know which. A soul's future, like the Hour, is filed under yudrīka: real, weighty, and sealed off from your reckoning.

The tense is the signal

So the whole tell rests on one grammatical difference. The perfect tense — adrāka, "what has made you know" — always opens onto an answer: this was beyond you, and I am about to hand it to you. The imperfect tense — yudrīka, "what will make you know" — always opens onto a sealed door: this is beyond you, and it will stay that way. Thirteen times the Quran invites you in; three times it marks a limit. And it signals which is which not with a heading or a warning, but with the aspect of a single verb — a difference an attentive reader feels before the sentence even finishes.

That leaves two very different kinds of not-knowing, and the grammar is teaching you to tell them apart. One is an invitation: you are ignorant of something the Quran is about to give you, so lean in. The other is a boundary: some things — when the world ends, how another person's story finally turns out — are simply not yours to carry, and the verse is asking you to make peace with the perhaps. Much of the anxiety of being human comes from treating the second kind like the first — demanding an answer where God has written only yudrīka. The Quran has already sorted them for you. It is in the tense.
