"Pour Patience Over Us": The Same Prayer From a Soldier and a Sorcerer

Two groups of badly outnumbered believers, in two eras that never touched, prayed the exact same words. One was a small band of soldiers walking onto a battlefield against a giant and his army. The other was a guild of magicians who, one hour earlier, had been the paid servants of a tyrant — and who now stood before that tyrant as fresh believers, listening to him promise them mutilation and death. Facing forces that dwarfed them, both reached for the identical sentence: our Lord, pour patience over us. But the words they added on the end reveal two entirely different things a believer can ask for when the odds are hopeless.

The prayer rabbanā afrigh ʿalaynā ṣabran — "our Lord, pour patience over us" — appears in exactly two places in the Quran. The verb afrigh means to empty a vessel out completely, to pour until nothing is held back. Patience, in both prayers, is something poured into a person from outside, like water filling a cup to the brim — never manufactured by the person under pressure, only received. Who prays it, and what each group asks for next, is the whole story.

The soldiers at the river

A small force of believers under the king Talut crosses to face Jalut — Goliath — and his army. Most of the men have already lost their nerve. The few who remain step onto the field and pray:

وَلَمَّا بَرَزُوا۟ لِجَالُوتَ وَجُنُودِهِۦ قَالُوا۟ رَبَّنَآ أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَثَبِّتْ أَقْدَامَنَا وَٱنصُرْنَا عَلَى ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ

"And when they went forth to face Jalut and his soldiers, they said, 'Our Lord, pour patience over us, and plant our feet firmly, and give us victory over the disbelieving people.'"

Al-Baqarah (2:250)

Their prayer for patience is a prayer to win. It runs forward in three steps, each one further into the fight: pour patience over us, then anchor our feet so we hold the line, then hand us victory over the enemy. This is the supplication of people who intend to survive the day and are asking Heaven to make their stand succeed. And it is answered — from among that outnumbered band, a young Dawud steps out and kills the giant, and the small believing force breaks the great one. Patience was poured, feet held, victory given, in the order they asked.

The magicians in the court

Now the other prayer, from people no one would have predicted. Pharaoh's magicians have just watched Musa's sign overturn theirs, and they have fallen into belief on the spot. Pharaoh, enraged that his own servants have slipped his grip, threatens to cut off their hands and feet on opposite sides and crucify them all. Their answer to that threat:

وَمَا تَنقِمُ مِنَّآ إِلَّآ أَنْ ءَامَنَّا بِـَٔايَـٰتِ رَبِّنَا لَمَّا جَآءَتْنَا ۚ رَبَّنَآ أَفْرِغْ عَلَيْنَا صَبْرًا وَتَوَفَّنَا مُسْلِمِينَ

"'And you resent us only because we believed in the signs of our Lord when they came to us. Our Lord, pour patience over us, and take us in death as those who submit.'"

Al-A'raf (7:126)

The identical opening — pour patience over us — and then the request bends the opposite way. The soldiers asked to be helped through the battle to victory. The magicians ask to be helped through their execution to a good death: take our souls as those who submit. They do not pray to escape Pharaoh's blade or to defeat him. They pray only that when it falls, it finds them still believing. Their whole horizon has collapsed from surviving the hour to dying well within it, and the patience they beg for is the strength to keep faith through torture rather than the strength to win a fight.

Two shapes of the same courage

The Quran hands the same rare prayer to a soldier and to a sorcerer-turned-believer and lets the endings diverge, because patience is asked for in two different situations and it wears two different faces. Sometimes the outnumbered believer is meant to stand and win, and ṣabr is the steadiness that holds the feet on the line until victory comes — the patience of Talut's men. Sometimes there is no winning the worldly battle at all, and ṣabr is the strength to keep the faith intact while everything else is taken — the patience of the magicians, who could not save their bodies and asked instead to save their submission. One prayer runs toward triumph; the other runs toward a grave with iman still burning in it. Both are poured from the same source, and the Quran calls both by the same name.

The magicians are the more staggering of the two, and they reset what we assume patience requires. These were not lifelong worshippers with decades of discipline behind them. An hour before, they were on Pharaoh's payroll, competing to defeat a prophet. Belief entered them in a single afternoon, and it aged them instantly into the kind of servant who can face crucifixion and ask only to die submitting. Their story quietly dismantles the excuse that steadfastness belongs to the spiritually senior — that a person needs years of faith before they can be brave in it. The strength was never a reserve they had built. It was patience poured — afrigh — into a vessel that had existed as a believer for less than a day. Which means the prayer is available to anyone at any stage: you do not generate the patience by being advanced. You ask, and the One who empties it over the veteran soldier empties it, in full measure, over the newest believer in the room.
