Al-Halim: The Divine Name for the Punishment That Has Not Come Yet

Al-Halim (ٱلْحَلِيم) is usually translated "the Forbearing." That English word has gone soft; it sounds like mildness, or a pleasant temperament. The classical commentaries define it much more sharply, and they define it the same way almost every time it appears. Al-Jalalayn's gloss is four words long: bi-taʾkhīr al-ʿuqūbah ʿan mustaḥiqqihā — by delaying the punishment away from the one who deserves it.

Not "God is not angry." Not "God does not punish." Something more uncomfortable and more precise: a penalty has been earned, and it has not arrived. The name is for the gap.

Fifteen times, and four of them are not about God

The word ḥalīm occurs fifteen times in the Quran. Eleven of those name God. The other four name people — and one of those four is an insult.

That count matters, because the eleven are remarkably consistent about where they land. Six of them arrive immediately after a human failure that God has just declined to punish: oaths blurted without meaning (2:225), private thoughts during a widow's waiting period (2:235), a donor who taunts the person he gave to (2:263), soldiers who ran at Uhud (3:155), questions the community was told to stop asking (5:101), and what people are carrying in their hearts and have not said (33:51).

The commentators say so plainly. On 2:225, al-Muyassar: ḥalīm toward the one who disobeyed Him, in that He did not rush him to the penalty. On 3:155 — the verse about the men who turned and fled — al-Jalalayn writes: lā yuʿajjil ʿalā'l-ʿuṣāt, He does not hurry against those who disobey. On 5:101, al-Muyassar again: forbearing toward them, so He does not punish them.

Every reading points the same direction. The name describes something that is not happening.

The verse where it is sharpest

Al-Baqarah gives the name to a small, ugly, entirely recognisable situation.

۞ قَوْلٌ مَّعْرُوفٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِّن صَدَقَةٍ يَتْبَعُهَآ أَذًى ۗ وَٱللَّهُ غَنِىٌّ حَلِيمٌ

"A kind word and a covering-over are better than a charity followed by injury. And God is Free of need, Forbearing."

Al-Baqarah (2:263)

The scene behind it is a man who gave money and then made the recipient pay for it — a remark, a reminder, a story told to someone else. The verse says the kind word he could have given instead would have been worth more than the money he actually gave.

Then it closes with two names. Al-Jalalayn glosses both. Ghanī — free of need of His servants' charity. Ḥalīm — by delaying the punishment away from the one who reproaches and the one who injures.

Read that as a sentence about the man in the verse. He is standing there having wounded someone with his own generosity, and the verse names God as the One who has not yet done anything about it. The consequence is real. It is simply later.

It never stands alone

In all eleven divine occurrences, ḥalīm has a partner name attached to it. It is never given on its own. The partners split into three groups:

  • Forgiveness — six times. Four as ghafūr ḥalīm (2:225, 2:235, 3:155, 5:101), and twice in the reverse order, ḥalīman ghafūrā (17:44, 35:41).
  • Knowledge — three times, ʿalīm beside it (4:12, 22:59, 33:51).
  • Two singles — ghaniyy ḥalīm, free of need (2:263), and shakūr ḥalīm, appreciative (64:17).

The knowledge pairing is the one that closes the obvious loophole. A delay can mean two very different things: the judge has not noticed, or the judge has noticed and is waiting. Putting al-ʿAlīm next to al-Ḥalīm settles which. Al-Jalalayn on 22:59 is almost blunt about it — ʿalīm: of their intentions; ḥalīm: regarding their punishment. Al-Muyassar on 33:51 gives the same shape: knowing what is in the hearts, forbearing in that He does not rush the penalty on the one who disobeys.

The forgiveness pairing points the other way. Six of eleven times, the name for the delay is sitting beside the name for the pardon — which is what the delay is for.

The cosmic version

Once, the Quran takes this from the scale of one rude donor to the scale of everything.

۞ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُمْسِكُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ أَن تَزُولَا ۚ وَلَئِن زَالَتَآ إِنْ أَمْسَكَهُمَا مِنْ أَحَدٍ مِّنۢ بَعْدِهِۦٓ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ حَلِيمًا غَفُورًا

"God holds the heavens and the earth, lest they cease. And if they were to cease, no one could hold them after Him. He has always been Forbearing, Forgiving."

Fatir (35:41)

A verse about the physical universe not falling apart ends with the name for a postponed punishment. Al-Jalalayn ties the two together directly: ḥalīman ghafūrā — in delaying the punishment of those who reject Him. Al-Muyassar says the same at more length: forbearing in deferring the penalty from the disbelievers and the disobedient, forgiving to whoever turns back from his sin.

So the reading the classical commentaries give is this: the world continuing to hold together is not a neutral background fact. It is the same attribute, running at full size. Nothing has collapsed yet — and that is al-Ḥalīm.

Surah At-Taghabun does the small-scale version of the same move, attaching the name to a verse about lending God a good loan: He multiplies it, He forgives you, and He is shakūr ḥalīm. Al-Jalalayn splits the last two words down the middle — shakūr: rewarding obedience; ḥalīm: regarding the punishment for disobedience. One clause covers what you did right. The next covers what you did wrong. Both are held in the same breath.

إِن تُقْرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَـٰعِفْهُ لَكُمْ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

"If you lend God a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you. And God is Appreciative, Forbearing."

At-Taghabun (64:17)

The four times it is not about God

This is where a count can mislead you if you do not check who each verse is talking about. Four of the fifteen describe human beings.

Ibrahim, twice. And the placement is not decoration. Surah Hud has just shown him arguing with the angels who have come to destroy the people of Lut. He is pleading. The very next line is:

إِنَّ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ لَحَلِيمٌ أَوَّٰهٌ مُّنِيبٌ

"Indeed Ibrahim was forbearing, deeply tender, always turning back."

Hud (11:75)

He is named ḥalīm at the exact moment he is trying to get a punishment postponed. Al-Muyassar's gloss on the word here could be a definition of the divine name: he does not like the hastening of punishment. Al-Jalalayn renders it kathīr al-anāh, a man of great slowness to act, and then reports the dialogue in full — would you destroy a town with three hundred believers in it? Two hundred? Forty? Fourteen? One?

At-Tawbah names him ḥalīm again (9:114), this time about the years he kept praying for a father who had threatened to stone him. Al-Jalalayn's gloss shifts to fit a human being: ṣabūr ʿalā'l-adhā, enduring under injury. Al-Muyassar goes further — much pardoning of the slips that came from his people.

The promised son. At 37:101, Ibrahim asks for a righteous child and is given the news of ghulāmin ḥalīm, a forbearing boy — the son who, a few verses later, answers the dream about his own sacrifice without arguing. Al-Muyassar notes the obvious: the description is of who he would be when he grew.

And once, as a taunt. The people of Madyan tell Shuʿayb, after refusing everything he has asked of them, that he must surely be al-ḥalīm ar-rashīd — the forbearing one, the sensible one.

قَالُوا۟ يَـٰشُعَيْبُ أَصَلَوٰتُكَ تَأْمُرُكَ أَن نَّتْرُكَ مَا يَعْبُدُ ءَابَآؤُنَآ أَوْ أَن نَّفْعَلَ فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِنَا مَا نَشَـٰٓؤُا۟ ۖ إِنَّكَ لَأَنتَ ٱلْحَلِيمُ ٱلرَّشِيدُ

"They said: Shuʿayb, does your prayer command you that we abandon what our fathers worshipped, or that we stop doing what we like with our own wealth? You, surely — you are the forbearing one, the sensible one."

Hud (11:87)

Both al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar mark this one word for word: they said it istihzāʾan, mockingly. Al-Muyassar even renders their meaning — you are the level-headed one, so good at managing money. It is sarcasm, and the Quran preserves it.

Which is worth sitting with. The same word is a divine name, a description of Ibrahim mid-intercession, a description of a boy about to be tested, and a sneer. The tradition is untroubled by this: shared word, unshared manner. The site has met this pattern before with Al-Hafiz, where Yusuf uses the divine word of himself.

What the name is not saying

Two things follow, and the second is the one people skip.

The first: a delayed consequence is still a consequence. The commentators never gloss ḥalīm as cancellation — they consistently use the verb for deferring. Al-Baqarah's own verse says the better option was the kind word, not the taunt. The delay is room, not a verdict.

The second: from the outside, forbearance is indistinguishable from weakness. That is precisely what the people of Madyan were counting on when they called Shuʿayb al-ḥalīm. Nothing was happening to them, so they read the silence as permission. Surah Fatir gives the reader the answer to that in advance — nothing is happening to the heavens and the earth either, and the reason is not that no one is holding them.

Reading the name

Al-Halim is one of the few divine names whose classical definition is stated in the negative: what has been earned has not yet been collected. Eleven verses, never on its own, six of them beside the name for forgiveness and three beside the name for complete knowledge — so that the delay can never be mistaken for inattention.

And the human being the Quran calls ḥalīm is the one caught arguing for someone else's postponement.
