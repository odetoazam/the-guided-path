"My Lord, I Have Wronged Myself" — the Four Words Said by a Pagan Queen and a Future Prophet

There is a four-word sentence in the Quran that is a whole act of repentance folded into almost nothing: rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī — "My Lord, I have wronged myself." No excuses, no explanation, no blaming anyone else. Just: I did this, to myself, and I am turning to You.

Exactly two people say those precise words in the entire Quran. One is a sun-worshipping queen at the moment she leaves her old religion behind. The other is a man who has just killed someone with his own hands and will one day be a prophet. Two people who could not be more different, at two completely different kinds of turning point, reach for the identical sentence.

The queen, at the threshold

The first speaker is the queen who ruled Sheba. Earlier in her story she led a people who worshipped the sun. Now she has come to Sulayman's court, and been shown a hall so polished she mistook its floor for water. In that moment of being completely disarmed, she says:

قِيلَ لَهَا ٱدْخُلِى ٱلصَّرْحَ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَن سَاقَيْهَا ۚ قَالَ إِنَّهُۥ صَرْحٌ مُّمَرَّدٌ مِّن قَوَارِيرَ ۗ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"…She said: 'My Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit, with Sulayman, to God, the Lord of all the worlds.'"

An-Naml (27:44)

Read what she says next, in the same breath: "and I submit, with Sulayman, to God." The confession is not a dead end; it is the doorway. She names the wrong — a life pointed at the sun instead of its Maker — as a wrong she did to herself, and in the very next words she steps through into something new. Her paganism is where she is coming from. The four words are the hinge.

The killer, in the aftermath

The other speaker is Musa, long before he is called as a prophet. He has stepped in to break up a fight, struck a man to defend another — and the man dies. Standing over what he has done, before he flees the city, he says:

قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى فَٱغْفِرْ لِى فَغَفَرَ لَهُۥٓ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"He said: 'My Lord, I have wronged myself, so forgive me' — and He forgave him. Indeed He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."

Al-Qasas (28:16)

Same four words — rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī — and again the sentence does not stop there. "So forgive me." And the verse tells you, immediately, what happened: "and He forgave him." The confession is answered on the spot. A man who has just taken a life says the same words a queen says when she leaves her false religion, and the same door opens.

The shape they share

Put the two moments side by side and the shared shape is striking, because the two situations have almost nothing else in common. One speaker is a woman, a ruler, at the height of her power, changing what she worships. The other is a fugitive, powerless, who has just done something irreversible. One "wrong" is a whole life's direction; the other is a single act in a single afternoon. And yet both land on the exact same words, in the exact same order: turn to the Lord, name the harm as something done to your own self, and move — she toward submission, he toward asking forgiveness.

The Quran does not reserve this sentence for one kind of person or one size of wrong. It hands the identical opening line to a queen changing gods and to a man standing over a body, and lets both of them through.

What a careful reading owes here

A few things, so the pattern is held at exactly its real strength. The claim is about this precise four-word phrase, rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī, corpus-checked as the words of exactly two individual speakers, at 27:44 and 28:16. Nearby verses are close but not the same sentence: in 7:23 it is two people speaking together — Adam and his wife — who say "our Lord, we have wronged ourselves" (plural), a different grammatical form; and Yunus, in his own moment of distress, turns to God with entirely different words (21:87). The two-speaker pattern is specific to the singular "I have wronged myself."

Two more notes. In the order of the mushaf, the queen's confession (Surah An-Naml) comes just before Musa's (Surah Al-Qasas) — even though, in lived time, Musa came long before Sulayman and the queen; the two surahs simply sit next to each other in the book. And nothing here is a legal verdict on Musa's act — the Quran itself has him name it as a wrong he did to himself and ask forgiveness, and records that he was forgiven; that is the reading, and it is his own. What the two verses give you, together, is a single sentence the Quran treats as wide enough for a queen and a killer both: the wrong is yours, you say so plainly, and you turn.
