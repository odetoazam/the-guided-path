Al-Aziz: The Divine Name That Is Also an Egyptian Job Title — and Who Ends Up Holding It

Al-Aziz (ٱلْعَزِيز) is one of the most frequent divine names in the Quran — the word occurs 101 times — and it is usually translated "the Almighty." The classical glosses are more specific than that. Al-Jalalayn at 14:47: ghālibun lā yuʿjizuhu shayʾ, dominant, nothing renders Him incapable. Al-Muyassar at 5:118: al-ʿAzīz alladhī lā yughālab, the Mighty who cannot be overcome.

Might that does not lose. That is the name. And four of its 101 occurrences are in one surah, attached to two different men.

The title in Egypt

In Surah Yusuf the word is not a divine name at all. It is an office. The man who buys Yusuf and installs him in his household is called al-ʿAzīz, and his wife is identified by it twice — not by her own name, which the Quran never gives, but as imraʾat al-ʿAzīz, the wife of the ʿAzīz (12:30, 12:51).

Then the story runs its length. Yusuf is accused, imprisoned, forgotten, remembered, brought out, and put in charge of the storehouses of the land. And when his brothers arrive from Canaan for the third time, not knowing who they are speaking to, this is what they call him:

فَلَمَّا دَخَلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِ قَالُوا۟ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْعَزِيزُ مَسَّنَا وَأَهْلَنَا ٱلضُّرُّ وَجِئْنَا بِبِضَـٰعَةٍ مُّزْجَىٰةٍ فَأَوْفِ لَنَا ٱلْكَيْلَ وَتَصَدَّقْ عَلَيْنَآ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَجْزِى ٱلْمُتَصَدِّقِينَ

"So when they entered upon him they said: O ʿAzīz, hardship has touched us and our family, and we have brought poor merchandise. So give us full measure and be charitable to us. Indeed God rewards the charitable."

Yusuf (12:88)

They had used it once before, ten verses earlier, when they begged him to take one of them instead of Binyamin (12:78). Both times al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar read it straightforwardly as the address due to the office.

So the title moves. It belongs at the start of the surah to the man in whose house Yusuf was a purchased servant, and by the end it belongs to Yusuf. The Quran does not comment on the transfer. It simply lets the same word arrive at a different man, and lets the brothers use it while they are asking for food.

The surah's other title choice runs alongside it: across all of Surah Yusuf the ruler is al-malik, the king, and never Pharaoh, which the site treats in the article on Al-Malik. Egypt in this surah has a king and an ʿAzīz, and the Quran keeps the two offices distinct.

What the name does everywhere else

Away from Egypt, the word names God, and it almost never stands alone. Counting what follows it:

  • Al-Ḥakīm, the Wise — 47 times. Only one pairing of divine names in the Quran runs longer: Al-Ghafur followed by Ar-Rahim, at 71.
  • Ar-Raḥīm, the Merciful — 13 times.
  • Al-ʿAlīm, the Knowing — 6 times.
  • Al-Ḥamīd, the Praised — 3 times; al-Ghafūr — twice; and once each with al-Wahhāb, al-Muqtadir and al-Jabbār.
  • Four times it takes dhu'ntiqām, possessor of retribution.

The Ḥakīm pairing is the one to sit with, because of what it rules out. Power on its own is arbitrary; that is the whole complaint people have about power. Attaching wisdom to it forty-seven times is a systematic refusal of the reading in which might simply does what it likes.

Surah Al-Ma'idah closes on that pair in the most demanding place it could. Isa, asked on the Day of Judgment whether he told people to worship him and his mother, says what he said, and then hands the verdict over:

إِن تُعَذِّبْهُمْ فَإِنَّهُمْ عِبَادُكَ ۖ وَإِن تَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ فَإِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

"If You punish them, they are Your servants. And if You forgive them, then indeed You are the Mighty, the Wise."

Al-Ma'idah (5:118)

Al-Jalalayn splits it in four words: al-ʿAzīz — over His command; al-Ḥakīm — in His making. Al-Muyassar reads the verse as praise of God's wisdom, justice and complete knowledge.

Notice which half of the sentence the names are attached to. Not the punishment. The forgiveness. Where a reader might expect might to be invoked as the licence to punish, it is invoked as the licence to pardon — which is the same move the Quran makes with Al-Afuww at 4:149, pairing pardon with power so that neither can be mistaken for weakness.

The one pairing that sounds different

Four times the name takes dhu'ntiqām, and the plainest of them closes a promise rather than a threat.

فَلَا تَحْسَبَنَّ ٱللَّهَ مُخْلِفَ وَعْدِهِۦ رُسُلَهُۥٓ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَزِيزٌ ذُو ٱنتِقَامٍ

"So do not think that God will fail His promise to His messengers. Indeed God is Mighty, Owner of retribution."

Ibrahim (14:47)

The verse is about a promise being kept. Al-Muyassar: God is ʿazīz, nothing can be withheld from Him, and He takes the severest retribution from His enemies. The might is named because a promise is only as good as the power behind it — which is the same logic as the Ḥakīm pairing, coming from the other side.

Reading the name

101 occurrences. Four of them belong to an Egyptian household, and inside that surah the word travels from a man who owned a servant to the servant himself. The rest name God, and 47 of those attach the name for wisdom — the second most repeated pairing of divine names in the Quran, behind Al-Ghafur and Ar-Rahim.

Put those two facts next to each other and the name reads better than "the Almighty" suggests. In Egypt it is a post: a man holds it, and then another man holds it, and the brothers who once threw one of them into a well address him by it while asking for grain. Everywhere else it names the One whose holding of it is not an appointment — and forty-seven times, the Quran immediately says that it is wise.
