The Man Who Comes Running: One Archetype, Two Endings

Twice in the Quran, at the moment a life hangs in the balance, a man appears from the same place, moving the same way, described in almost the same words. He comes from the farthest part of the city, and he comes running. One of these men saves a future prophet and vanishes back into the story alive. The other defends a group of messengers, is killed for it, and is shown Paradise before his body has finished falling. They are, textually, the same figure — and the Quran gives him both endings a courageous man can have.

The phrase min aqṣā l-madīnah — "from the farthest part of the city" — introduces a human being in exactly two places in the Quran. Both times the man is nameless. Both times he is running. Reading them together turns two scattered episodes into a single portrait of what it costs to be the one who moves.

The runner who saved Musa

The young Musa has killed an Egyptian and the palace has decided on his death. Into the gap steps a stranger:

وَجَآءَ رَجُلٌ مِّنْ أَقْصَا ٱلْمَدِينَةِ يَسْعَىٰ قَالَ يَـٰمُوسَىٰٓ إِنَّ ٱلْمَلَأَ يَأْتَمِرُونَ بِكَ لِيَقْتُلُوكَ فَٱخْرُجْ إِنِّى لَكَ مِنَ ٱلنَّـٰصِحِينَ

"And a man came from the farthest part of the city, running. He said, 'O Musa, indeed the chiefs are conferring over you to kill you, so leave. Indeed, I am to you of the sincere advisors.'"

Al-Qasas (28:20)

He knows the council's private deliberations, which means he stands close to power. He crosses the whole width of the city to reach the one man that power wants dead. He risks his own standing to carry a warning, calls himself a sincere advisor, and disappears from the account. His running buys Musa the years in Madyan — the marriage, the flocks, the burning bush waiting at the far end of the road. A prophet's entire formation runs on this stranger's legs.

The runner who defended the messengers

Now a town that has rejected three messengers, and a man who comes the same distance at the same speed to take their side:

وَجَآءَ مِنْ أَقْصَا ٱلْمَدِينَةِ رَجُلٌ يَسْعَىٰ قَالَ يَـٰقَوْمِ ٱتَّبِعُوا۟ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ

"And there came from the farthest part of the city a man, running. He said, 'O my people, follow the messengers.'"

Ya-Sin (36:20)

Set the two openings against each other and the craft shows. Both say a man came from the farthest part of the city, running. In the story of Musa the word for "man" comes first, then the place. In Ya-Sin the place comes first, then the man. The Quran runs the same clause in two orders, as if the camera has moved a step — the same figure filmed from a different angle. And where the first runner rescued one hunted man in private, this one stands up in the open square and tells a whole hostile town to follow the very messengers they are about to reject:

ٱتَّبِعُوا۟ مَن لَّا يَسْـَٔلُكُمْ أَجْرًا وَهُم مُّهْتَدُونَ

"'Follow those who ask of you no payment, and who are rightly guided.'"

Ya-Sin (36:21)

The town kills him for it. And the Quran does something with his death it does almost nowhere else — it cuts, without pause, from the ground where he fell to the door he walked through:

قِيلَ ٱدْخُلِ ٱلْجَنَّةَ ۖ قَالَ يَـٰلَيْتَ قَوْمِى يَعْلَمُونَ

"It was said, 'Enter Paradise.' He said, 'Would that my people knew...'"

Ya-Sin (36:26)

بِمَا غَفَرَ لِى رَبِّى وَجَعَلَنِى مِنَ ٱلْمُكْرَمِينَ

"'...of how my Lord has forgiven me and placed me among the honored.'"

Ya-Sin (36:27)

His first thought on the other side of death is his people — the same people who just killed him. He wishes they could see what he sees, so they might come too. The runner who gave his life in the square is still, in Paradise, trying to save the town.

The same legs, running the other way

The verb under both men is yasʿā — to run, to strive, to move with urgent purpose toward something. The Quran attaches that verb to one other figure at a decisive moment, and the pairing is deliberate. When Musa throws down his staff and the truth stands plain before him, Pharaoh's response is:

ثُمَّ أَدْبَرَ يَسْعَىٰ

"Then he turned his back, running."

An-Nazi'at (79:22)

The same running. The opposite vector. The two nameless men run toward the endangered truth, one to shield a prophet, one to endorse messengers before a mob. Pharaoh, given the same legs and the same urgency, turns his back and runs to organize against it. The Quran uses one verb for both the rush to defend the truth and the rush to escape it, and lets the direction carry the whole moral weight. Effort is never the question. Everything depends on which way you are pointed when you start to move.

Two endings, one door

The Quran declines to tell us either runner's name, and the silence is its own argument. Strip away the biography and what remains is the pure act — a person who hears that the truth is under threat and moves toward it faster than caution allows. That figure has, in this life, two possible outcomes, and the Quran refuses to hide either one. Sometimes the runner saves the prophet and lives to see the plan bear fruit, as Musa's warner did. Sometimes the runner is killed in the square, as the man of Ya-Sin was. But the second man's ending reframes the first, because we are shown where he arrived — through the door, forgiven, honored, still praying for the people who stoned him. The town took everything the world could take from him and he called it, from the far side, being placed among the honored.

So the question the two runners leave is not whether moving toward the truth is safe. One of them proves it is not. The question is the one their shared verb keeps asking: when you feel the pull to cross the city for something true — to warn the hunted man, to speak for the messengers your whole town is mocking — which way do your legs turn? Pharaoh had the same urgency in his body. He spent it running the other way.
