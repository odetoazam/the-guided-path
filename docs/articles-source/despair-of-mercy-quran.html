The One Despair the Quran Files Under Unbelief

The Quran lets a great deal of hard feeling stand. Yaqub grieves for Yusuf until his eyes go white with sorrow, and the grief is never rebuked. Musa is afraid at the burning bush, and the fear is answered rather than shamed. Prophets are exhausted, hurt, pressed to the edge of what a person can carry, and the Book records all of it without correction. There is one interior state, though, that the Quran refuses to let a believer occupy for even a single verse. Search every place it names the despairing of God's mercy, and the despair is always the same thing: forbidden outright, or handed to the disbelievers as the mark of who they are. The Quran does not treat this despair as a sadness to be comforted. It treats it as a kind of unbelief.

Named only to be forbidden

Where the despairing of divine mercy appears as a command, the command points one direction. Yaqub, sending his sons back to search for Yusuf after years of loss, gives them the instruction that names the state and bars it in the same breath:

يَـٰبَنِىَّ ٱذْهَبُوا۟ فَتَحَسَّسُوا۟ مِن يُوسُفَ وَأَخِيهِ وَلَا تَا۟يْـَٔسُوا۟ مِن رَّوْحِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يَا۟يْـَٔسُ مِن رَّوْحِ ٱللَّهِ إِلَّا ٱلْقَوْمُ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ

"O my sons, go and seek news of Yusuf and his brother, and do not despair of the relief of God. Indeed, none despairs of the relief of God except the disbelieving people."

Yusuf (12:87)

The verse does two things at once. It forbids the despair — lā tāy'asū, do not despair — and then it defines who despairs: the disbelieving people, and them alone. The prohibition and the diagnosis arrive in one sentence. To despair of God's relief is, by the verse's own accounting, to do the thing that only al-kāfirūn do. The same directional force runs through the verse many carry as the single greatest line of hope in the Quran:

۞ قُلْ يَـٰعِبَادِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا۟ مِن رَّحْمَةِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ ٱلذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful."

Az-Zumar (39:53)

The address goes to the people with the most reason to despair — those who have wronged their own souls, run past every limit — and the one thing they are told is to hold the door of mercy open against the despair that would slam it. The forgiveness of all sins is offered as the reason the despair has no standing.

The mark of the astray

Where the despairing of mercy appears without a command, it appears as a description of a specific kind of person. Ibrahim, given news of a son in his old age, is asked by the angels why he receives it strangely, and his answer draws the line himself:

قَالَ وَمَن يَقْنَطُ مِن رَّحْمَةِ رَبِّهِۦٓ إِلَّا ٱلضَّآلُّونَ

"He said: And who despairs of the mercy of his Lord except those who are astray?"

Al-Hijr (15:56)

A prophet treats the despairing of his Lord's mercy as a thing that identifies a person — the ḍāllūn, the strayed. And where the disbelievers are described directly, the same despair is folded into the description of who they are:

وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ وَلِقَآئِهِۦٓ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ يَئِسُوا۟ مِن رَّحْمَتِى وَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

"And those who disbelieve in the signs of God and the meeting with Him — they have despaired of My mercy, and for them is a painful punishment."

Al-Ankabut (29:23)

Disbelief in the signs and despairing of the mercy are named in one motion, as though the second follows from the first. And when the Quran shows where this all arrives, it puts the despair on the faces of the guilty at the end of everything — the criminals, on the Day the Hour stands, falling into a mute despair that the Arabic calls iblās:

وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ يُبْلِسُ ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ

"And the Day the Hour appears, the criminals will fall into despair."

Ar-Rum (30:12)

Forbidden to the believer, definitional of the disbeliever, and the final expression on the criminal's face — the despairing of mercy has a single trajectory across the Book, and a believer stands at no point on it.

The despair the Quran does allow

The claim needs its narrow edge, or it overreaches. The Arabic verb for despair, ya'isa, has an ordinary sense too — simply to stop expecting a thing. The Quran uses it that way without any blame: of women past the age of menstruation, who have ya'isna — ceased to expect — their monthly cycle; and of the messengers themselves, who reached the point of having given up that their people would ever respond, until the help of God arrived. That giving-up-on-an-outcome is a plain human fact, and the Quran records it flatly. What it never permits is the other object. The prophets despaired, at their lowest, of their people; they are shown despairing of the response, of the timing, of the road. They are never once shown, and their followers are expressly forbidden, to despair of God Himself. The object is everything. To give up on a result is human. To give up on the mercy is the one door the Quran nails shut, because on the far side of it, by the Book's own repeated word, stands unbelief.
