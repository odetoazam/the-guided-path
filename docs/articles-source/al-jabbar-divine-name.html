Al-Jabbar: The Quran Uses This Word Ten Times. Nine of Them Are an Accusation.

Al-Jabbar (ٱلْجَبَّار) is on every list of the ninety-nine, usually rendered "the Compeller" or "the Irresistible." The word occurs ten times in the Quran. Nine of those ten describe human beings, and every one of the nine is hostile — the Quran's word for a bully, a tyrant, a man who hits without hesitating. The tenth is the divine name.

That ratio is not a curiosity. It is the reason the name has to be read carefully, and the classical commentaries read it carefully.

The nine

Here is the whole distribution, in order:

  • 5:22 — the Israelites refuse to enter the land because "there are jabbārīn in it," a people too strong to face.
  • 11:59 — ʿĀd "followed the command of every jabbār ʿanīd," every obstinate tyrant. Al-Jalalayn identifies who is meant: their own chiefs.
  • 14:15 — "and every jabbār ʿanīd came to ruin."
  • 19:14 — Yahya "was not a jabbār, disobedient."
  • 19:32 — Isa: "and He did not make me a jabbār, wretched."
  • 26:130 — Hud to his people: "and when you strike, you strike as jabbārīn."
  • 28:19 — a man to Musa: "you only want to be a jabbār in the land."
  • 40:35 — "thus God seals every heart of one arrogant, jabbār."
  • 50:45 — to the Prophet: "you are not over them a jabbār."
  • 59:23 — the divine name, inside the closing cascade of Al-Hashr.

Read down that list and the pattern is hard to miss. Three of the nine are negations — the word appears specifically to say that someone is not this. Two of those three are prophets.

Two prophets, cleared of it, in the same surah

Surah Maryam describes Yahya and Isa a few lines apart, and it uses the same two-part formula for both: dutiful to a parent, and not a jabbār.

وَبَرًّۢا بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ وَلَمْ يَكُن جَبَّارًا عَصِيًّا

"And dutiful to his parents, and he was not a tyrant, disobedient."

Maryam (19:14)

وَبَرًّۢا بِوَٰلِدَتِى وَلَمْ يَجْعَلْنِى جَبَّارًا شَقِيًّا

"And dutiful to my mother, and He has not made me a tyrant, wretched."

Maryam (19:32)

The word before jabbār in both verses is barran, from the root that also gives the divine name Al-Barr — dutiful, good to someone. The two are placed as opposites: what you are toward the person who raised you, and what you are not toward everybody else.

Al-Jalalayn glosses jabbāran at 19:14 with one word, mutakabbiran — arrogant. At 19:32 he uses mutaʿāẓiman, one who makes himself large. Al-Muyassar reads 19:14 as not arrogant toward obedience to his Lord, nor toward his parents.

So the trait being ruled out is not strength. It is a posture: a person who has grown large in his own estimation, and who therefore handles other people accordingly.

What it looks like in practice

Two verses show the behaviour rather than naming the attitude, and both are about hands.

وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُم بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ

"And when you strike, you strike as tyrants."

Ash-Shuʿara (26:130)

Hud is speaking to ʿĀd, and the verse before it is about the towers they built on every high place. Al-Jalalayn's gloss on jabbārīn here is three words long and it is the sharpest definition in the whole set: min ghayri raʾfah — without any tenderness. Al-Muyassar renders it "overpowering and wronging."

That phrase matters, because raʾfah is itself a divine name — Ar-Ra'uf, the tender. The commentary defines the tyrant by the absence of a specific quality of God, not by the presence of force.

The second is the accusation levelled at Musa the day after he killed a man.

فَلَمَّآ أَنْ أَرَادَ أَن يَبْطِشَ بِٱلَّذِى هُوَ عَدُوٌّ لَّهُمَا قَالَ يَـٰمُوسَىٰٓ أَتُرِيدُ أَن تَقْتُلَنِى كَمَا قَتَلْتَ نَفْسًۢا بِٱلْأَمْسِ ۖ إِن تُرِيدُ إِلَّآ أَن تَكُونَ جَبَّارًا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا تُرِيدُ أَن تَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلْمُصْلِحِينَ

"And when he was about to strike the one who was an enemy to them both, he said: Musa, do you want to kill me as you killed a soul yesterday? You only want to be a tyrant in the land, and you do not want to be one of those who set things right."

Al-Qasas (28:19)

The line sets jabbār directly against muṣliḥ, one who repairs. Two ways to use the same strength, named in one sentence. Al-Muyassar renders the accusation ṭāghiyatan fi'l-arḍ, a despot in the land.

And the charge is unfair — the man is misreading a rescue as a second killing — which is part of why the verse is worth sitting with. From the outside, intervening and dominating can look identical. The difference is in what the intervener is for.

The verse that tells the Prophet he is not one

Surah Qaf, near its end, draws the line for the man delivering the message.

نَّحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَقُولُونَ ۖ وَمَآ أَنتَ عَلَيْهِم بِجَبَّارٍ ۖ فَذَكِّرْ بِٱلْقُرْءَانِ مَن يَخَافُ وَعِيدِ

"We know best what they say, and you are not over them a tyrant. So remind by the Quran whoever fears My warning."

Qaf (50:45)

Al-Jalalayn glosses the phrase directly: tujbiruhum ʿala'l-īmān — you are not one who compels them into faith. Al-Muyassar: you have not been given power over them to force them into Islam; you were sent as one who conveys.

The same root that produces the divine name produces this prohibition. Compelling belief is a thing the Prophet is told he is not there to do.

So what is the name doing?

Once, at 59:23, the word names God — in the cascade at the end of Al-Hashr that runs through a dozen names in a row.

Al-Jalalayn's gloss there is four words: jabara khalqahu ʿalā mā arāda — He brings His creation to what He wills. Al-Muyassar: the Jabbār who has overwhelmed all His servants, and to whom the whole of creation yields.

Notice what the divine gloss is about. It is a statement that God's will is effective — that what He intends for creation actually happens. It is not a description of temperament, and it says nothing about arrogance, because arrogance is a false estimate of one's own size and there is no false estimate available here.

That is the whole distinction, and the tradition holds it firmly. When a creature is jabbār, the word names a lie he is living: he is behaving as though he were larger than he is, and other people pay for it. The same word said of God names something simply true. The site has run into this before with Al-Hafiz, where Yusuf uses the divine word of himself — shared word, unshared manner. Al-Jabbar is the sharpest case of it, because here the human version is not merely smaller. It is a vice.

Al-Hashr's own cascade makes the point structurally. Al-Jabbār arrives there in a sequence, between al-ʿAzīz and al-Mutakabbir, and the verse ends by declaring God far above what people associate with Him. That reflection is covered in full elsewhere on the site; what concerns us here is the other nine.

Reading the name

Nine hostile uses and one divine one is an unusual shape for a name on the list of ninety-nine, and it is worth being plain about what follows from it.

A reader who wants to "take on" Al-Jabbar as a quality to imitate has the ratio exactly backwards. Two prophets are described by the Quran as not this. The Prophet himself is told he is not this. The people who are called it are ʿĀd, the chiefs whose orders were obeyed, the man who strikes without pausing, and the heart God seals.

What is left for the reader is the reverse of imitation. If the divine version means that God's will is effective, then the nine are portraits of people who wanted that for themselves.
