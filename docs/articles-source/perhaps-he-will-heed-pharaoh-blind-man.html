"Perhaps He Will Heed": The Same Hope God Held for Pharaoh and for a Blind Beggar

Twice the Quran shows a man who comes running toward the truth and is received as a hero — the stranger who sprints across the city to save Musa (28:20), the believer who runs into the square to defend the messengers and is killed for it (36:20). Both are described with the same two words: jāʾa … yasʿā, he came, running. There is a third man in the Quran who comes the same way, running, and he is not received as a hero at all. He is turned away — and the one who turns from him is the Prophet ﷺ himself.

The third runner

وَأَمَّا مَن جَآءَكَ يَسْعَىٰ

وَهُوَ يَخْشَىٰ

"But as for the one who came to you running, and in awe [of God] —"

ʿAbasa (80:8–9)

The scene behind the verse is the reason the whole chapter is named for a facial expression. The Prophet ﷺ was in deep conversation with the powerful men of Quraysh, the people whose acceptance of Islam could change everything, when a blind man — the tradition names him Abdullah ibn Umm Maktūm — came to him, unable to see that he was interrupting, asking to be taught what God had taught. And the Prophet, absorbed in the greater prize, let his face tighten and turned slightly away. The chapter opens by reporting it, in the third person, as if from a distance:

عَبَسَ وَتَوَلَّىٰٓ

أَن جَآءَهُ ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ

"He frowned and turned away, because the blind man came to him."

ʿAbasa (80:1–2)

Then the verse turns and speaks to him directly — a rebuke, delivered to the Messenger of God, inside the very revelation he was charged to carry to the world. Consider what it costs to transmit faithfully a scripture that corrects you by name. He did. That the surah exists at all is a proof of the Prophet's truthfulness: a man inventing a religion does not publish the verse where God frowns at his frown.

The reason God gives

God does not rebuke the turning-away with "he was a believer" or "he had rights." He rebukes it with a possibility:

وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّهُۥ يَزَّكَّىٰٓ

أَوْ يَذَّكَّرُ فَتَنفَعَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰٓ

"And what would make you know — perhaps he would purify himself, or take heed, and the reminder would benefit him?"

ʿAbasa (80:3–4)

Laʿallahu yazzakkā aw yadhakkar — perhaps he will purify himself, or heed. The whole weight of the correction rests on that perhaps. You do not know what this man might become, so you do not get to rank him beneath the meeting you would rather be having.

Where else God says exactly this

Here is the connection the surah is quietly making. That clause — perhaps he will heed — is not new. God had used it once before, at the opposite end of the human spectrum. When He sends Musa and Hārūn to the single most powerful and tyrannical man alive, the one who claims godhood and slaughters children, His instruction is not to thunder. It is to be gentle, for a reason phrased in almost the same words:

فَقُولَا لَهُۥ قَوْلًا لَّيِّنًا لَّعَلَّهُۥ يَتَذَكَّرُ أَوْ يَخْشَىٰ

"So speak to him gently — perhaps he will take heed, or fear [God]."

Ta-Ha (20:44)

Set the two perhaps clauses side by side. Over Pharaoh: laʿallahu yatadhakkaru aw yakhshā — perhaps he heeds, or fears. Over the blind beggar: laʿallahu yazzakkā aw yadhakkar — perhaps he purifies himself, or heeds. The mightiest oppressor in the Quran and the poorest seeker in it are handed to God's messengers under the identical hope, built from the same two verbs — heeding and awe. God levels the tyrant and the beggar to one thing: a soul that might turn. Neither the height of Pharaoh's power nor the lowness of the blind man's station changes the calculation, because the calculation was never about station. It was always about the perhaps.

The math the world runs, and the math God runs

Read what the Prophet ﷺ was doing when he frowned, because the surah names it precisely. He was attending to the one who considered himself beyond need:

أَمَّا مَنِ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰ

فَأَنتَ لَهُۥ تَصَدَّىٰ

"As for the one who thinks himself self-sufficient — to him you give your attention."

ʿAbasa (80:5–6)

This is the world's arithmetic, and it is so natural it can catch even a prophet for a moment: give your best effort to the one whose acceptance carries weight, and let the one who needs you but can do nothing for you wait. The surah does not merely forbid it. It inverts it. The self-sufficient man, for all his weight, came asking nothing and might purify nothing. The blind man came running, in awe, needing exactly what you carry — and the reminder would benefit him. In God's math the eager nobody outranks the important somebody, because guidance is measured by who will receive it, not by who will repay it.

What the Prophet did with the correction

The most moving part of the story is not in the chapter; it is in what came after. The tradition records that from that day, whenever Ibn Umm Maktūm came to him, the Prophet ﷺ would welcome him with the words, "Welcome to the one over whom my Lord corrected me," and would spread out his own cloak for the blind man to sit on. The rebuke did not wound him into defensiveness. It reorganized him into tenderness. The verse that recorded his single frown became, in his own life, a permanent honoring of the man he had frowned at.

So the surah leaves two things in your hands. The first is a question about who you make wait — the seeker who needs you but can do nothing for you, or the important person whose favor you are chasing. The world will tell you the second is the wise investment. God spent a chapter of His book saying otherwise. The second is gentler, and it is aimed at anyone who carries something true to others: the same hope God asked His messengers to hold for the worst tyrant, He asks you to hold for the humblest person who comes to you running. You do not get to decide in advance who is worth the effort. You were never shown the perhaps.
