Al-Karim: The Divine Name a Man in the Fire Is Reminded He Claimed

The word karīm (كَرِيم) appears thirty times in the Quran, and most of those describe something other than God — a noble reward, good provision, an honourable entrance, the Book itself. Among the places it names Allah, one stands apart from every other use of a divine name in the Quran. In Surah Ad-Dukhan the two words al-ʿAzīz al-Karīm are said to a man being dragged into the Fire. The commentators are unanimous about why, and the reason is that he had said them about himself.

What the root carries

The root is k-r-m (ك ر م). It runs across two fields that English splits: nobility of origin and generosity of hand. A karīm is well-born and open-handed at once, and Arabic treats these as the same quality rather than two — the assumption being that giving freely is what nobility looks like when it acts.

The Quran uses the adjective for many things. A rizq karīm is generous provision. An ajr karīm is a noble reward. A maqām karīm is an honourable station. The word describes the Quran itself at 56:77. So a reader meeting karīm in a verse has to check what it is attached to.

Where it names Allah

Two verses name Him with it plainly, and they sit at opposite ends of the emotional register.

يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ مَا غَرَّكَ بِرَبِّكَ ٱلْكَرِيمِ

"O man, what deceived you concerning your Lord, the Generous?"

Al-Infitar (82:6)

The question is a rebuke, and the name inside it is the reason the rebuke lands. The verse could have asked what deceived you concerning your Lord the Almighty, or the Severe in punishment. It picks the name for open-handedness, so that the thing being described as deceiving the reader is God's own generosity — the long delay, the unpunished years, the provision that kept arriving.

The second is Sulayman's, at the moment the throne of Bilqis appears in front of him.

قَالَ ٱلَّذِى عِندَهُۥ عِلْمٌ مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَرْتَدَّ إِلَيْكَ طَرْفُكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَءَاهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُۥ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّى لِيَبْلُوَنِىٓ ءَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّى غَنِىٌّ كَرِيمٌ

"The one who had knowledge from the Scripture said: I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you. And when he saw it settled before him, he said: this is from the bounty of my Lord, to test me — whether I am grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful is grateful only for himself; and whoever is ungrateful — then indeed my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

An-Naml (27:40)

Al-Muyassar reads the closing pair as a unit: free of any need for his gratitude, karīm in that His goodness extends broadly regardless. The generosity is stated precisely at the point where it would be withdrawn if it were a transaction.

The verse in Ad-Dukhan

Surah Ad-Dukhan describes the food of the sinful, then a seizing, then a pouring of scalding water over the head. Then a sentence is spoken to the man.

ذُقْ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْكَرِيمُ

"Taste — indeed you are the mighty, the generous."

Ad-Dukhan (44:49)

The two words are among the ninety-nine. Here they are addressed to a human being in Hell, and every commentary in the classical tradition reads them the same way.

Al-Muyassar states the register outright: this is said to him ʿalā wajhi al-tahakkumi wa'l-tawbīkh — by way of mockery and rebuke. You are the one who was mighty among your people, honoured by them.

Al-Jalalayn supplies what makes it mockery rather than mere irony. The words are quoted back: bi-zaʿmika wa qawlika — by your own claim, by your own words. And it preserves the sentence being quoted — that there was no one between its two mountains mightier or more generous than him.

Ibn Kathir reports that more than one commentator identified the man as Abu Jahl, and the English rendering of the verse in that commentary inserts the qualification directly into the translation: you were pretending to be the mighty, the generous.

So the verse is not taking two divine names and applying them to a man. It is repeating a man's boast to him at the moment it has been disproved. He had reached for a description of himself that belongs to God, and it is handed back at the point where nothing he claimed is available to him.

What the two verses do together

Al-Infitar and Ad-Dukhan describe the same person at two stages.

In Al-Infitar he is still alive, and the question put to him is what made him bold — with the answer sitting inside the question, in the name al-Karīm. The generosity had been running the whole time, and he read it as permission.

In Ad-Dukhan the account has closed, and the word he had applied to himself is spoken over him. Between the two verses lies a life in which the same attribute was first misread and then borrowed.

The Throne described with the same word

The adjective also attaches to the Throne, at the close of Surah Al-Mu'minun.

فَتَعَـٰلَى ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمَلِكُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۖ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ رَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ ٱلْكَرِيمِ

"So exalted is Allah, the Sovereign, the Truth. There is no god but He, Lord of the Noble Throne."

Al-Mu'minun (23:116)

Here karīm qualifies the Throne rather than its Lord, which is the ordinary behaviour of the word across the Quran — reward, provision, entrance, Book, Throne. Thirty occurrences, and only a handful name Allah directly.

That distribution is what makes the Ad-Dukhan verse legible. A word that spends most of its Quranic life describing honoured things was, according to al-Jalalayn, claimed by a man for himself. The claim was not exotic; it was a common adjective. What made it a boast was the comparison he attached to it — that nobody between the two mountains outranked him.

Reading the name

English translations give Al-Karim as "the Generous," "the Bountiful," "the Most Honourable." All three are reaching for a word that means noble and open-handed simultaneously.

Where the Quran puts it is the part worth carrying. It names Him al-Karīm in a verse asking a man why he was deceived, and in a verse where a prophet with a throne in front of him says his Lord needs nothing from him either way. Both times the generosity is described from the position of someone who has received a great deal and has not yet been asked to account for it.
