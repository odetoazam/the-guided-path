Al-Muqit: The Divine Name Built on the Word for a Day's Ration

Al-Muqit (ٱلْمُقِيت) occurs once in the Quran. Its root occurs twice in the entire book — this name, and one other word. That other word means the food supply of the earth. Which is why the name is usually translated "the Nourisher," and why al-Tabari, having read all the early opinions, went a different way.

A root that appears exactly twice

The root q-w-t produces two words in the whole Quran. The first is the name:

مَّن يَشْفَعْ شَفَـٰعَةً حَسَنَةً يَكُن لَّهُۥ نَصِيبٌ مِّنْهَا ۖ وَمَن يَشْفَعْ شَفَـٰعَةً سَيِّئَةً يَكُن لَّهُۥ كِفْلٌ مِّنْهَا ۗ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ مُّقِيتًا

"Whoever intercedes with a good intercession will have a share of it, and whoever intercedes with an evil intercession will have a portion of it. And God has always been Muqit over every thing."

An-Nisa (4:85)

The second is in Fussilat, in the account of how the earth was made ready:

وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَٰسِىَ مِن فَوْقِهَا وَبَـٰرَكَ فِيهَا وَقَدَّرَ فِيهَآ أَقْوَٰتَهَا فِىٓ أَرْبَعَةِ أَيَّامٍ سَوَآءً لِّلسَّآئِلِينَ

"And He placed in it firm mountains above it, and blessed it, and measured out in it its provisions, in four days — equal for those who ask."

Fussilat (41:10)

Aqwātahā, its provisions. Al-Jalalayn glosses the verb before it, qaddara, with one word: qasama — He divided. Al-Muyassar reads the whole clause as the earth's food being apportioned for its people. The singular behind that plural is qūt, which in Arabic is not "food" in general but the measured amount that keeps a person going — the ration, the daily portion.

So the name is built on the vocabulary of rationing, and the only other place the root is used, it is God dividing up the earth's food supply.

Al-Tabari lays out four readings and then picks one

This is one of the verses where al-Tabari opens with his own formula — the people of interpretation differed — and then reports the positions with their chains before giving his own.

  • Ibn ʿAbbas: ḥafīẓ — guardian, the one who keeps.
  • Mujahid: shahīd — witness. In another transmission from him: witness, reckoner, guardian. In a third: al-ḥasīb, the one who takes account.
  • A reading transmitted by Ibn Jurayj: al-wāṣib — the one who stays constantly over a thing and manages it well.
  • As-Suddi and Ibn Zayd: al-qadīr — the one with power over it.

Al-Tabari's own verdict is the last of these: the correct view is that muqīt means al-qadīr. His reason is linguistic rather than theological — that this is the word's meaning in the speech of Quraysh — and he supports it, as he often does, with a line of early Arabic verse:

"And a man with a grudge, from whom I held myself back — and I was muqīt over doing him harm."

Cited by al-Tabari at 4:85

The speaker is saying he had the power and did not use it. Al-Tabari's gloss on the word in that line is a single word: qādiran, able. (The editions of al-Tabari disagree about who composed the line, so it is safest to describe it as a line of pre-Islamic verse he cites rather than to name a poet.)

The two shorter commentaries land on either side of his choice. Al-Jalalayn: muqtadiran, fully able — so He recompenses everyone for what they did. Al-Muyassar: witness and guardian, which is Ibn ʿAbbas's and Mujahid's reading.

Why "the Nourisher" is not simply wrong

The food sense does not disappear when al-Tabari prefers "able." He keeps it in the same passage, citing a prophetic report about a man's obligation to those he feeds — that it is sin enough for a person to neglect the ones he provides for. The commonly transmitted wording of that report uses the verb from this very root.

So the tradition holds both. The root means apportioned sustenance; the name, in al-Tabari's judgment, means the power that does the apportioning. Those are not competing ideas so much as two ends of one action — a ration implies someone with the authority to set it.

What the translation "Nourisher" loses is the second half. Muqīt in the poetry line is a man who could have struck and did not. That is capability, held.

What the verse is about

Now put the name back where it sits. An-Nisa 4:85 is about intercession — stepping in on someone else's behalf, for good or for harm — and the verse assigns the intervener a naṣīb, a share, of the good outcome, or a kifl, a portion, of the bad one.

Al-Jalalayn reads the verse straightforwardly as intercession between people, good meaning in line with the revealed law and evil meaning against it. Al-Tabari prefers a narrower original setting — rallying others to the fighting the preceding verse commands — but then says something a modern reader should notice: it is not objectionable that the verse came down about that and then was generalised to every person who intercedes for good or ill. He records the wider reading too, from Mujahid: people interceding for one another.

Either way, the mechanism is the same. You put your weight behind someone else's outcome, and a portion of that outcome is assigned to you. The verse is about shares — and it closes with a name whose root means the measured portion.

Reading the name

Al-Muqit is one of the quietest names in the list: a single occurrence, at the end of a verse about who gets credited for what. Its root is used only twice in the Quran, and the other use is God dividing the earth's food between the creatures that need it.

The classical readings cluster around watching, keeping, reckoning and power — and al-Tabari, reaching for how Quraysh actually spoke, settles on power, with a line of poetry about a man who had every reason to retaliate and chose not to.

That is worth putting beside Al-Halim, the name the commentaries define as a punishment deferred. Al-Tabari's line of verse describes exactly that posture in a human being — the capacity present, the blow withheld. Two names, arrived at from different roots and different verses, describing the same shape.
