God Changed One Verb: Zakariyya, Maryam, and the Grammar of Two Miracles

Seven verses apart in the same surah, two people receive news beyond any earthly arranging. An old man whose wife has never conceived is told he will have a son. A young woman no man has touched is told the same. Each of them puts almost exactly the same startled question to God — how can I have a child, when — — and each receives almost exactly the same answer. The two answers run word for word in parallel until they reach the verb at their center, and there the Quran changes one word. To the old man: God does what He wills. To the young woman: God creates what He wills. The entire distance between a birth stretched out of an exhausted nature and a birth with no natural cause at all sits in that single substitution.

Before the reading begins, a boundary on what it is for. This is an observation about how the Quran narrates the beginning of ʿĪsā — Jesus — in its own words, using its own verbs. It stays inside the Arabic the Quran chose and speaks only to how the Quran tells this birth; it reaches across the aisle at no one else's scripture. That is the whole of it.

Two questions, almost identical

Zakariyya, told in his old age that a son is coming, answers first:

قَالَ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِى غُلَـٰمٌ وَقَدْ بَلَغَنِىَ ٱلْكِبَرُ وَٱمْرَأَتِى عَاقِرٌ ۖ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ ٱللَّهُ يَفْعَلُ مَا يَشَآءُ

"He said: My Lord, how can I have a boy when old age has overtaken me and my wife is barren? He said: Thus God does what He wills."

Aal-Imran (3:40)

Maryam, told that a son is coming though no man has come near her, answers seven verses later:

قَالَتْ رَبِّ أَنَّىٰ يَكُونُ لِى وَلَدٌ وَلَمْ يَمْسَسْنِى بَشَرٌ ۖ قَالَ كَذَٰلِكِ ٱللَّهُ يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ ۚ إِذَا قَضَىٰٓ أَمْرًا فَإِنَّمَا يَقُولُ لَهُۥ كُن فَيَكُونُ

"She said: My Lord, how can I have a child when no man has touched me? He said: Thus God creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it 'Be,' and it is."

Aal-Imran (3:47)

Lay the two questions along one line. Both open with the same startled word to the same Lord — rabbi annā yakūnu lī, my Lord, how can there be for me. Both name the impossibility that provokes the question. The frame is shared down to its particles. Four things move between them, and each movement is a decision.

Four forks

The first fork is the speaker. Zakariyya's line opens qāla — he said, masculine. Maryam's opens qālat — she said, feminine. The grammar carries the two different bodies into the sentence before either says a word about a child.

The second fork is the noun each asks about. Zakariyya asks after a ghulām, a boy, a young male. Maryam asks after a walad, a child, offspring in the barest sense — the word for the sheer fact of a descendant, before it is sexed or aged. The old man imagines the son who will inherit his calling. The young woman, further from any natural cause, reaches for the plainer, more elemental word: a child, at all.

The third fork is a single vowel, and it is the kind of thing only the recited Arabic preserves. God's answer to Zakariyya begins kadhālika, ending in an open a. God's answer to Maryam begins kadhāliki, ending in an i — the second-person feminine, "thus, for you." The reply is bent grammatically toward the woman being addressed. Even the demonstrative that means merely thus turns to face her. A reader with only a translation loses it; every reciter says it aloud.

The fourth fork is the verb, and it is the one the whole passage is built to deliver.

Does, and creates

To Zakariyya: Allāhu yafʿalu mā yashā' — God does what He wills. The root is f-ʿ-l, the most general verb of acting there is, the word for bringing about, carrying out, making happen. It is the right verb for Zakariyya's case exactly. His son will come through a stretched and unlikely nature — an aged man, a wife long past conceiving — but through a nature that stands. There are two parents. There is a lineage. What God does is override the odds inside a working order: He acts upon a cause that is present but spent, and makes it yield.

To Maryam: Allāhu yakhluqu mā yashā' — God creates what He wills. The root shifts to kh-l-q, the origination word, the verb the Quran reserves for bringing into being — the verb of the first making, of forming from dust. Maryam's case hands the verb something else to work with: a single mother, no father, a lineage that simply stops. So the verb changes its kind. Where Zakariyya's son arrives through a womb, Maryam's arrives through sheer origination — and here alone the passage continues into its formula: when He decrees a matter, He only says to it "Be," and it is. The verb of creation pulls the language of creation-from-command in behind it. Zakariyya's answer stops before that formula, because his miracle runs through a cause. Maryam's runs through the bare word of God.

One root for the improbable within nature. Another root for the unprecedented outside it. The Quran holds the two cases seven verses apart precisely so the reader can feel the verb turn under identical pressure.

The Book names its own verb

Twelve verses after Maryam's answer, the same surah returns to ʿĪsā and says outright what the annunciation encoded:

إِنَّ مَثَلَ عِيسَىٰ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ كَمَثَلِ ءَادَمَ ۖ خَلَقَهُۥ مِن تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ قَالَ لَهُۥ كُن فَيَكُونُ

"Indeed, the likeness of ʿĪsā with God is as the likeness of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him 'Be,' and he was."

Aal-Imran (3:59)

The verb is khalaqa — created — the same root that answered Maryam, now stated plainly and attached to a name. And the comparison the verse reaches for is Adam, the one human being who also arrived with no parent at all, formed from dust and called into being by kun. A birth without a father is set beside a making without either parent, and both are filed under the origination verb, both sealed with Be, and it was. The word the annunciation slipped in quietly at 3:47 is the word the surah says out loud at 3:59. The verb was the argument the whole time.

The proof in the other surah

One more passage confirms that the two verbs were a deliberate choice. Zakariyya's annunciation is told a second time, in Surah Maryam, and this time his own answer carries the creation verb — but watch where it lands:

قَالَ كَذَٰلِكَ قَالَ رَبُّكَ هُوَ عَلَىَّ هَيِّنٌ وَقَدْ خَلَقْتُكَ مِن قَبْلُ وَلَمْ تَكُ شَيْـًٔا

"He said: Thus your Lord has said: it is easy for Me. I created you before, when you were nothing."

Maryam (19:9)

The origination verb khalaqa does appear in Zakariyya's story — but attached to the one thing in it that was an origination from nothing: Zakariyya's own coming into existence, when he too was once not a thing. When the Quran speaks of a cause working within nature, it uses yafʿalu. When it speaks of being drawn out of sheer nothing, it uses khalaqa — for Zakariyya's own first making, for Adam's dust, and for the child given to Maryam. The verbs sort the miracles by kind, consistently, across surahs. That consistency is the signature of design.

The parallel in plain sight

Readers of the Gospel of Luke will recognize the shape here: two annunciations set side by side, an aged priest told of a son and then a virgin told of one, the second greater than the first. The Quran is telling a structure that older scripture also tells. Naming that openly costs nothing and gains everything, because the interesting fact is what the Quran does with the inherited diptych. It keeps the two-annunciation architecture and then works a distinction into the grammar that the shared structure left open — one verb for the son through a womb, another for the son through the bare command. The parallel poses no threat to be managed; it is the backdrop against which the Quran's own editorial choice becomes visible. Two miracles, one surah, seven verses apart. God changed one verb, and told you the entire theology of how ʿĪsā began.
