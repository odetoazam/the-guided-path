Al-Qahhar: The Divine Name the Quran Only Ever Uses to Argue About Number

Al-Qahhar (ٱلْقَهَّار) is the divine name people reach for when they want the frightening one. It gets translated "the Subduer," "the Dominant," "the Irresistible," and it turns up in lists of names to recite against enemies. The Quran uses it six times. In all six, the subject under discussion is not power. It is arithmetic — how many gods there are.

And in all six, the name is preceded by the same word: al-Wāḥid, the One.

Six occurrences, six times bolted to "the One"

Every occurrence of al-Qahhār in the Quran reads al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār. There is no exception, not one instance where the name stands by itself or takes a different partner. Here are the six, with what each verse is arguing:

  • 12:39 — Yusuf, in a prison cell, asking two fellow prisoners whether scattered lords are better than the One.
  • 13:16 — a chain of questions to people who assigned partners to God, ending with: God is the creator of everything, and He is the One, the Overwhelming.
  • 38:65 — "there is no god except God, the One, the Overwhelming."
  • 39:4 — the answer to the claim that God took a son.
  • 14:48 — the Day the earth is exchanged for another earth, and everyone comes out before God, the One, the Overwhelming.
  • 40:16 — the Day the question is asked, whose is the kingdom today?, and answered.

Four of the six are direct arguments against worshipping more than one. The other two are scenes on the Day of Judgment, where the number question gets settled by the seating arrangement rather than by debate.

The cell in Egypt

The first occurrence in the order of the muṣḥaf is the one nobody expects, because of who is speaking and where he is standing.

يَـٰصَـٰحِبَىِ ٱلسِّجْنِ ءَأَرْبَابٌ مُّتَفَرِّقُونَ خَيْرٌ أَمِ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْوَٰحِدُ ٱلْقَهَّارُ

"My two companions of the prison — are scattered lords better, or God, the One, the Overwhelming?"

Yusuf (12:39)

Yusuf is a prisoner. He has been asked to interpret two dreams and he answers with a question about idolatry first. Al-Jalalayn marks the grammar of that question: istifhām taqrīr — a question asked to make the hearer concede the answer, not to gather information.

The word he chooses for the contrast is mutafarriqūn, scattered. Many lords, pulling in different directions. Against that: one, and overwhelming. The name is not being used to threaten the two men. It is the second half of a comparison about how many masters a person can usefully have.

What the commentaries actually gloss it as

Al-Jalalayn's gloss on the name is the same two words at 38:65, at 39:4, and at 40:16: li-khalqihi — over His creation. That is all. The name states a relation, not a temperament.

Al-Muyassar fills it out. At 38:65: the Overwhelming, who has overwhelmed everything and prevailed over it. At 39:4: the Overwhelming, who overwhelmed His creation by His power, so that everything is lowered and yielding before Him. At 40:16, on the Day when the question whose is the kingdom today? is asked and God answers His own question: the One, alone in His names and attributes and acts, the Overwhelming, who overwhelmed all created things by His power and might.

Read against those glosses, the pairing with al-Wāḥid stops looking like a stylistic habit. If everything that exists is held in that relation, then there is no second candidate — not because rivals were defeated, but because a rival would have to stand outside the relation, and nothing does.

The form is the argument

Arabic builds intensity into the shape of a word. Qāhir is the plain participle — one who overpowers. Qahhār is the faʿʿāl pattern, the form for something done constantly, at scale, as a permanent occupation. The Quran uses both, and it distributes them carefully.

The site has met this exact move before, on a different root, with Al-Ghafur and Al-Ghaffar — one root, two names, two grammatical registers. Here the split is not between two divine names. It is between God and a human court.

وَهُوَ ٱلْقَاهِرُ فَوْقَ عِبَادِهِۦ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْحَكِيمُ ٱلْخَبِيرُ

"And He is the One who overpowers, above His servants. And He is the Wise, the Aware."

Al-Anʿam (6:18)

The plain participle, with the preposition fawqa, above. Al-Anʿam uses that clause twice (6:18 and 6:61). Al-Muyassar reads it as sovereignty that necks bow before and tyrants are humbled by.

Now the third and last place the plain participle appears. Pharaoh's chiefs are advising him about Musa, and Pharaoh replies with a policy and then a boast:

وَقَالَ ٱلْمَلَأُ مِن قَوْمِ فِرْعَوْنَ أَتَذَرُ مُوسَىٰ وَقَوْمَهُۥ لِيُفْسِدُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَيَذَرَكَ وَءَالِهَتَكَ ۚ قَالَ سَنُقَتِّلُ أَبْنَآءَهُمْ وَنَسْتَحْىِۦ نِسَآءَهُمْ وَإِنَّا فَوْقَهُمْ قَـٰهِرُونَ

"And the chiefs of Pharaoh's people said: will you leave Musa and his people to spread corruption in the land, and abandon you and your gods? He said: we will kill their sons and spare their women — and we are above them, overpowering."

Al-Aʿraf (7:127)

Both words are there. Fawqahum qāhirūn — above them, overpowering. The same participle and the same preposition the Quran gives to God, reversed in order and moved into the first person plural. Al-Muyassar renders Pharaoh's meaning without softening it: we are above them by the overwhelming force of kingship and authority.

That is the whole distance the Quran is measuring. A man can be qāhir, and Pharaoh was, over a specific population, for a specific stretch of years, by specific methods the verse has just named. Nobody in the Quran is ever qahhār. And the Day that answers Pharaoh's boast is the one at 40:16, where the question about who owns the kingdom is asked out loud and the answer comes back in the intensive form.

The one command built from this root

The root q-h-r appears ten times in the Quran. Six are the divine name, three are the plain participle, and one is a verb. The verb is an imperative, it is negative, and it is addressed to the reader.

فَأَمَّا ٱلْيَتِيمَ فَلَا تَقْهَرْ

"So as for the orphan — do not crush him."

Ad-Duha (93:9)

Al-Jalalayn glosses the prohibition concretely: do not do it by taking his property, or otherwise. Al-Muyassar: do not treat him badly.

So the one thing a human being is told to do with this root is to not do it, and the person it is not to be done to is the one with the least power in the room. The word that describes God's relation to everything that exists is forbidden to you in your relation to a child with no father.

This is not the Quran inviting anyone to imitate the name. It is the opposite — the classical usage keeps qahr as a relation only God stands in, and the single time the root is handed to a human being, it comes as a fence.

Reading the name

Al-Qahhar is not the Quran's vocabulary for divine anger. The names that carry consequence do that work elsewhere — Al-Halim, the penalty that has not arrived yet, and Al-Fattah, the verdict that settles what is between people. This one appears six times, always after al-Wāḥid, always inside an argument about how many gods there are, and glossed by al-Jalalayn as a two-word statement of relation: over His creation.

A prisoner used it to ask two other prisoners a question about scattered masters. A tyrant reached for the weaker form of the same word to describe what he was doing to a nation. And a surah of consolation used the only verb the root produces to tell one man not to be hard on an orphan.
