The Four Words People Read as a Curse — and the Half of Them Everyone Drops

There is a phrase in the Quran that gets pulled out and posted as proof that the book curses its enemies: qātalahumu-llāh, usually translated "may God destroy them" or "God curse them." It lands hard in English. It sounds like a God who loses His temper and hurls a curse.

The classical commentators would not argue that it is soft — they gloss it as an imprecation, a genuine "God ruin them." But almost everyone who quotes it stops there, at the curse, and drops what comes next. Because the phrase is not two words. It is four, and it appears in exactly two verses of the whole Quran, both times ending the same way: qātalahumu-llāh — annā yu'fakūn, "God ruin them — how are they so deluded?" Read the two verses together, and the half everyone drops turns out to be the more unsettling one.

The first verse: a saying about God

The first appearance is in Surah At-Tawbah. The verse reports something people said — attributing a son to God — and then reacts to it:

وَقَالَتِ ٱلْيَهُودُ عُزَيْرٌ ٱبْنُ ٱللَّهِ وَقَالَتِ ٱلنَّصَـٰرَى ٱلْمَسِيحُ ٱبْنُ ٱللَّهِ ۖ ذَٰلِكَ قَوْلُهُم بِأَفْوَٰهِهِمْ ۖ يُضَـٰهِـُٔونَ قَوْلَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ مِن قَبْلُ ۚ قَـٰتَلَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ ۚ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

"…That is their saying with their mouths, imitating the saying of those who disbelieved before them. Qātalahumu-llāh — how are they deluded?"

At-Tawbah (9:30)

Notice what the verse says right before the phrase: "that is their saying with their mouths." The whole focus is on a claim people made — words that came out of their own mouths, repeating a thing older peoples had said. Then comes the imprecation, qātalahumu-llāh, and then a question: how are they turned away from the truth like this?

The second verse: the same four words

The only other place the four words show up together is in Surah Al-Munafiqun, describing the hypocrites of Madinah — people who looked impressive and spoke well but had turned against the community from the inside:

۞ وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ ۖ وَإِن يَقُولُوا۟ تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ ۖ كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُّسَنَّدَةٌ ۖ يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ هُمُ ٱلْعَدُوُّ فَٱحْذَرْهُمْ ۚ قَـٰتَلَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ ۖ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

"When you see them, their bodies impress you; and when they speak, you listen to their speech. They are like propped-up timber… They are the enemy, so beware of them. Qātalahumu-llāh — how are they deluded?"

Al-Munafiqun (63:4)

Two very different groups — one making a statement of belief, the other playing a double game inside the community — and the Quran closes on both of them with the identical four-word ending. When a phrase lands in exactly two places and nowhere else, the two places are worth reading as a pair.

What the first two words are — and aren't

Start with the honest part: the classical commentators do read qātalahumu-llāh as an imprecation. Al-Jalalayn glosses it plainly — at 9:30 as la'anahum, "He cursed them," and at 63:4 as ahlakahum, "He destroyed them." Al-Muyassar renders it "God disgraced them and cast them from His mercy." So this is not a case of a harsh phrase being explained away. It is severe, and the tradition says so.

But two things sit under that severity that the English "God will kill them" gets wrong. The word is qātala, built on what Arabic grammar calls Form III — the pattern for doing something against another party, the mutual-combat shape — not the ordinary verb qatala, "to kill." And it plainly cannot be read literally: no one imagines God entering combat with anyone. So the commentators take it the only way it can be taken — as a set expression of condemnation, an idiom of the language, the way English says "confound them" or "damn them" without meaning it as a procedure. Real in force, figurative in form.

The three words everyone drops

Now the half that almost never gets quoted — and it is exactly half the phrase. Annā yu'fakūn means "how are they turned away?" How does a person get diverted from something this plain? Every commentator reads it the same way: al-Jalalayn, "how are they turned from the truth while the proof stands right there"; al-Muyassar, "how do they turn from the truth to what they are in?" It is astonishment — the sound of watching someone argue himself out of what is in front of him.

And that half-phrase is not rare the way the curse is. It appears three more times in the Quran, and each time with no imprecation anywhere near it. In Surah Al-Maidah, after explaining plainly that the Messiah and his mother were people who ate food like anyone else: "See how We make the signs clear to them, then see annā yu'fakūn" (5:75). In Surah Al-Ankabut: if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth they would say "God" — "so annā yu'fakūn, how are they deluded?" (29:61). And almost word for word again in Surah Az-Zukhruf (43:87). Three times, the same bewilderment, and not one carries a curse.

So the part of the phrase that recurs across the Quran — the engine of it — is not the imprecation. It is the astonishment: how can people who half-know the truth keep turning from it? In two verses that astonishment gets the weight of qātalahumu-llāh in front of it. But strip the curse and the question remains, three more times, standing on its own.

What the two twin verses share

Read 9:30 and 63:4 together and the thread is easy to see. Both verses are about people undone by their own words. In At-Tawbah it is a saying — "that is their saying with their mouths." In Al-Munafiqun it is impressive speech hiding a turned heart — "when they speak, you listen to their speech." In both, the mouth says one thing while the truth stands somewhere else, and the four-word ending lands right on that gap. The curse names the verdict; the question — annā yu'fakūn — names the mystery the verse actually keeps circling: how did a person talk himself this far out of what he could plainly see?

What a careful reading owes here

Two things need saying plainly. First: both 9:30 and 63:4 are severe condemnations, and nothing here softens that. The Quran is not being warm about assigning a son to God, or about people who betray a community from within. The commentators call the phrase a curse because it is one.

What the popular quotation gets wrong is narrower and more specific. It reads qātala as the flat "kill," when the form is the figurative fight-against that the tradition itself takes as an idiom of condemnation, not a literal event. And it stops at the first two words, dropping the second two — the half the verse spends its astonishment on, the half that recurs three more times with no curse attached. The phrase people wave as proof that the Quran curses its enemies is, read whole and in its own language, doing something stranger than cursing: standing back from a person talking himself out of the truth, and asking — in exactly two verses of the entire book — how.

One note on the first verse's content, for honesty's sake. At-Tawbah 9:30 reports two sayings — one attributing sonship to God through "Uzayr," one through the Messiah — and the early commentators themselves discussed who exactly said the first and how widely it was held (al-Ṭabarī records more than one account). The verse's own framing is that this is a saying being quoted and answered — "that is their saying with their mouths" — not a census of a whole community. The reveal here does not depend on settling that question. It depends on four words that sit in these two verses and nowhere else, and on the plain fact that the Quran spends most of their weight not on the curse, but on the astonishment.
