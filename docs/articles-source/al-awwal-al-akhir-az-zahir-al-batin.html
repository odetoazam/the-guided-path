Al-Awwal, Al-Akhir, Az-Zahir, Al-Batin: The Four Divine Names That Appear in One Verse and Nowhere Else

Four of the ninety-nine names sit inside a single short sentence in Surah Al-Hadid, and none of the four is used as a name of God anywhere else in the Quran. Al-Awwal (ٱلْأَوَّل), Al-Akhir (ٱلْـَٔاخِر), Az-Zahir (ٱلظَّاهِر), Al-Batin (ٱلْبَاطِن) — the First, the Last, the Outer, the Inner. Eighteen words, and Ibn Kathir reports that the commentators produced "about ten and some odd" different explanations of them.

هُوَ ٱلْأَوَّلُ وَٱلْـَٔاخِرُ وَٱلظَّـٰهِرُ وَٱلْبَاطِنُ ۖ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

"He is the First and the Last, the Outer and the Inner. And He is Knowing of every thing."

Al-Hadid (57:3)

How unusual "only here" actually is

Three of these four words are common in the Quran in ordinary senses, so the claim needs stating carefully.

Al-Awwal in this masculine definite form appears twice. The other time is at 50:15, where it describes the first creation — a-fa-ʿayīnā bi'l-khalqi'l-awwal, "were We then wearied by the first creation?" Only 57:3 uses it of God.

Al-Akhir is the extreme case. The word is everywhere — but almost always as al-ākhirah, the hereafter, or in the phrase al-yawm al-ākhir, the Last Day, where it describes the day and not God. Out of well over a hundred definite occurrences, exactly one names God, and it is here.

Az-Zahir and al-Batin occur together twice elsewhere, and neither time is about God: 6:120 tells people to leave "the outer of sin and its inner," and 57:13 — thirteen verses after our verse — describes a wall on the Day of Judgment whose inner side holds mercy and whose outer side faces torment. In the definite singular, each names God once.

So the sentence is genuinely a one-off. Four names, one verse, no repeats.

The commentators do not agree, and they say so

Ibn Kathir does something unusual here. Rather than settling the verse, he reports the size of the disagreement: there are "about ten and some odd number of different sayings" among the scholars of tafsir about this ayah. He then quotes al-Bukhari relaying a reading from Yahya — identified as Yahya ibn Ziyad al-Farra' — that az-Zahir means knowing all things and al-Batin means knowing all things. Both names, one meaning.

The two shorter commentaries split cleanly, and the split is worth seeing because it is a real fork, not a difference in wording.

Al-Jalalayn reads the second pair as a statement about evidence. Al-Awwal — before everything, with no beginning. Al-Akhir — after everything, with no end. Az-Zahir — manifest through the proofs that point to Him. Al-Batin — hidden from the grasp of the senses. On this reading the last two names are about how God is known: unmistakable in the traces, unreachable by sight.

Al-Muyassar reads the second pair as a statement about position. The First, with nothing before Him; the Last, with nothing after Him; the Outer, with nothing above Him; the Inner, with nothing below Him — and nothing hidden from Him in earth or sky.

Both readings keep the four names as two pairs, each pair closing off a direction. The disagreement is about which axis the second pair runs on — how God is perceived, or where God stands.

Where al-Muyassar's wording comes from

Al-Muyassar's phrasing is not a paraphrase he invented. It is the Prophet's own supplication, and Ibn Kathir quotes it at length in his commentary on this verse, from Abu Hurayrah, recorded by Ahmad and by Muslim: O God, You are al-Awwal, nothing is before You; and al-Akhir, nothing is after You; and az-Zahir, nothing is above You; and al-Batin, nothing is below You.

That is the verse's own vocabulary turned into address — the four names spoken back to God, each with its negation attached.

And then the supplication ends, in both of the versions Ibn Kathir cites, with this: settle our debt for us, and free us from poverty.

It is a strange landing and it is the most interesting thing in the whole report. The most abstract sentence in the Quran — before time, after time, outside, inside — is recited in the sunnah at bedtime, and the request attached to it is about money owed. Whatever these four names are for, the tradition did not treat them as material for contemplation only.

The verse before, and the verse after

Surah Al-Hadid opens with everything in the heavens and the earth glorifying God, then a verse about kingship and life and death, then our verse. Ibn Kathir reads that sequence as one movement: whatever exists praises Him, He owns it, He gives life and takes it, and He is the First and the Last.

What follows is the part that changes the temperature.

هُوَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ ٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْعَرْشِ ۚ يَعْلَمُ مَا يَلِجُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا يَخْرُجُ مِنْهَا وَمَا يَنزِلُ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَمَا يَعْرُجُ فِيهَا ۖ وَهُوَ مَعَكُمْ أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُمْ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

"He is the One who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then rose over the Throne. He knows what enters the earth and what comes out of it, what descends from the sky and what ascends into it. And He is with you wherever you are. And God sees what you do."

Al-Hadid (57:4)

One verse says nothing is before Him, after Him, above Him, or beneath Him. The next says He is with you wherever you are. The surah puts those two sentences side by side without any attempt to reconcile them, and it is the ordering that does the work: the reader gets the size first and the nearness second.

Ibn Kathir also preserves a small scene about this verse that reads like practical advice. A man described the whispering that arrives when a person starts thinking about beginnings — the questions that keep going backwards until there is nowhere left to stand. The counsel he received was not an argument. It was: when you feel that in your heart, recite He is the First and the Last, the Outer and the Inner, and He is Knowing of every thing.

The claim about a thousand verses

Ibn Kathir opens his commentary on the surah with a report from ʿIrbad ibn Sariyah, collected by Abu Dawud, at-Tirmidhi and an-Nasa'i, that the Prophet used to recite the musabbiḥāt — the surahs that open with glorification — before sleeping, and said that among them is a verse better than a thousand verses. At-Tirmidhi graded the report ḥasan gharīb.

The Prophet, in that report, does not say which verse. Ibn Kathir names 57:3 as the likely one and adds the standard caution — wa'llāhu aʿlam, and God knows best. That qualifier belongs in any retelling of it. The identification is a scholar's inference, carefully marked as such, not part of the report.

Reading the four names

Each pair works by closing both ends of something. First and Last close time. Outer and Inner close whatever the second axis is — knowledge on al-Jalalayn's reading, direction on al-Muyassar's. Nothing is left outside the sentence for something else to occupy, which is the same argument As-Samad makes with a single word instead of four.

And the fifth name in the verse is the one that is easy to walk past. It ends wa huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm — and He is Knowing of every thing. Al-Muyassar attaches it directly to what came before: nothing hidden from Him in the earth or in the sky. The four names describe a God with no boundary in any direction. The last clause says that this same God is paying close attention.
