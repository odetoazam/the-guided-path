Al-Qayyum: The Divine Name That Never Appears Alone

Al-Qayyum (ٱلْقَيُّوم) occurs three times in the Quran. Every one of those three times, the same name stands directly in front of it: al-Hayy (ٱلْحَىّ), the Living. Every occurrence puts the two names in the same order, in the same position, joined the same way. Al-Hayy, by contrast, does appear on its own — twice, in Al-Furqan and in Ghafir. One name of the pair can stand alone. The other arrives attached, every time.

The three verses

The first is the most recited verse in the Book.

ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ ٱلْقَيُّومُ ۚ لَا تَأْخُذُهُۥ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ ۚ لَّهُۥ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۗ مَن ذَا ٱلَّذِى يَشْفَعُ عِندَهُۥٓ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِۦ ۚ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَىْءٍ مِّنْ عِلْمِهِۦٓ إِلَّا بِمَا شَآءَ ۚ وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ ۖ وَلَا يَـُٔودُهُۥ حِفْظُهُمَا ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

"Allah — there is no god but He, the Living, the Qayyum. Neither drowsiness takes Him, nor sleep. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is before them and what is behind them, and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His Kursi extends over the heavens and the earth, and their preservation tires Him not. And He is the Most High, the Most Great."

Al-Baqarah (2:255)

The second opens Surah Aal 'Imran, three verses in, and repeats the first clause word for word.

ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ ٱلْقَيُّومُ

"Allah — there is no god but He, the Living, the Qayyum."

Aal 'Imran (3:2)

The third is in Surah Ta-Ha, and it moves the pair into a different case ending and a completely different scene.

۞ وَعَنَتِ ٱلْوُجُوهُ لِلْحَىِّ ٱلْقَيُّومِ ۖ وَقَدْ خَابَ مَنْ حَمَلَ ظُلْمًا

"And faces will be humbled before the Living, the Qayyum. And whoever carried wrongdoing has failed."

Ta-Ha (20:111)

What the root does

The root is q-w-m (ق و م), and it is one of the most ordinary roots in Arabic. It gives qama, to stand up. It gives qawm, a people — a group who stand together. It gives iqamah, the call that stands the prayer up, and istiqamah, standing straight on a path. The physical picture underneath all of it is uprightness: something on its feet, holding its position.

From that root, qayyum is built on an intensive pattern — the doubled middle letter that Arabic uses to say a quality is present continuously and at full strength. The word carries two things at once, and the classical commentators name both.

Al-Muyassar, glossing the pair at 20:111, renders Al-Qayyum as al-qa'im 'ala tadbir kulli shay', al-mustaghni 'amman siwahu — the one who stands over the management of everything, self-sufficient of everyone besides Himself. At 3:2 the same commentary gives al-qa'im 'ala kulli shay', the one standing over everything.

So the word says two things in one breath: He stands by Himself, and He is what everything else is standing on. The first meaning is about His independence. The second is about the world's dependence. A single word holds both ends of that relationship.

What follows the name in Ayat al-Kursi

The most famous of the three verses does something the other two leave implicit: it immediately explains the name. After al-Hayy al-Qayyum comes the clause about drowsiness and sleep.

Sleep is the daily proof that a living thing has limits. Being alive costs something, and the cost is paid in unconsciousness every night. A creature that is alive is a creature that will eventually need to stop. The verse names God as living, then removes the one condition that makes created life intermittent — not even sinah, the drowsiness that arrives before sleep does.

Placed where it is, the clause is an argument. If everything is standing on Him, then any lapse in Him is a lapse in everything. The verse rules out the smallest possible lapse, and then continues into what He holds: the heavens, the earth, all knowledge, a throne wide enough to contain both, and no fatigue in the holding.

Ayat al-Kursi is the verse many Muslims recite before sleeping. It is a verse about the One whom sleep never reaches, said by someone about to lose consciousness for eight hours.

The same pair, a very different scene

The third occurrence lands somewhere else entirely. Surah Ta-Ha 111 is a Day of Judgement verse, and the pair arrives with a preposition attached: li'l-Hayy al-Qayyum, before the Living, the Qayyum.

The verb is 'anat, from a root that describes being brought low and held there — a face pressed downward and kept down. Al-Jalalayn glosses it plainly: khada'at, they submitted. Ibn Kathir reads the verse alongside the intercession language in the two verses before it, where intercession works only by permission.

The same two names that promise a sleepless guardian in Al-Baqarah are the names the faces go down before in Ta-Ha. Nothing about the pair changes between the two scenes. What changes is where the reader is standing.

The one that can stand alone

Al-Hayy appears as a divine name in five places. Three of them are the pairings already quoted. The other two are on their own.

وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱلْحَىِّ ٱلَّذِى لَا يَمُوتُ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِهِۦ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِهِۦ بِذُنُوبِ عِبَادِهِۦ خَبِيرًا

"And rely upon the Living, the One who does not die, and glorify Him with His praise. And sufficient is He, regarding the sins of His servants, as Aware."

Al-Furqan (25:58)

هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ فَٱدْعُوهُ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ ۗ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

"He is the Living; there is no god but He. So call upon Him, making the religion sincerely His. Praise be to Allah, Lord of the worlds."

Ghafir (40:65)

In Al-Furqan the name is followed by a relative clause instead of a second name — the Living who does not die. In Ghafir it carries the sentence by itself. Both times the verse asks for something practical: put your weight on Him, call on Him and mean it.

Al-Qayyum arrives attached, three times out of three, and the attachment runs one way. Life comes first, and the standing-over-everything follows from it. Something has to be alive before it can hold anything up.

What comes after the humbling

The Ta-Ha verse has a companion one line further on, and reading the two together changes the temperature of the passage.

وَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَلَا يَخَافُ ظُلْمًا وَلَا هَضْمًا

"And whoever does righteous deeds while being a believer will fear neither injustice nor deprivation."

Ta-Ha (20:112)

The two closing words are precise. Zulm is having something placed on you that you did not earn. Hadm is having something you did earn taken off the total. One is addition, the other subtraction, and the verse rules out both directions of error at once.

The guarantee follows directly from the name. A judge who dozes can be deceived while asleep; a judge who depends on reports can be misinformed. The verse two lines earlier had already said that He knows what is in front of them and behind them while their knowledge cannot encircle Him. The faces go down before the Living, the Qayyum — and the security of the people going down is grounded in the same two words as the humbling.

The report about the Greatest Name

Ibn Kathir, commenting on Aal 'Imran 3:2, refers back to a hadith he cited at Ayat al-Kursi: that Allah's Greatest Name — ism Allah al-a'zam — is contained in these two verses. He names them together, 2:255 and 3:1-2, and what the two verses share is precisely this clause. Allahu la ilaha illa huwa al-Hayy al-Qayyum, identical in both places, down to the word.

Two of the three occurrences of Al-Qayyum sit inside the same repeated sentence, in the two longest surahs of the Quran, which the tradition has long treated as a pair. The third stands at the end of Ta-Ha and shows what the sentence will look like from the other side.

Reading the name

English translations of Al-Qayyum tend to pick one half. "The Self-Subsisting" takes the independence. "The Sustainer" and "the Maintainer" take the holding-up. "The Eternal" takes the continuousness of the intensive pattern and leaves the rest.

The Arabic keeps all three in one word, and then the Quran keeps that word beside al-Hayy every single time it uses it. A name for the One who is alive without interruption, joined to a name for the One who holds everything else in position — and the second half of that description has always been said with the first.
