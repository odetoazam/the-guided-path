Al-Malik: The Quran Calls Ten Other People King Before It Ends on This Name

Al-Malik (ٱلْمَلِك), the King, is one of the most familiar names on the list and one of the most commonly miscounted. Look it up and you may be told the word appears more than 150 times in the Quran. It does not. That figure comes from adding up different words that happen to be spelled alike — malak, angel, which occurs 88 times, and mulk, dominion, which occurs 48.

The word malik, king, occurs fifteen times. Five of those name God. The other ten name human beings.

The ten kings

The Quran is not sparing with the title. It hands it out:

  • 2:246, 2:247 — Talut, appointed over a people who then objected that he had no money.
  • 5:20 — Musa reminding the Israelites that God made prophets among them and made you kings.
  • 12:43, 12:50, 12:54, 12:72, 12:76 — the ruler of Egypt in the account of Yusuf, five times.
  • 18:79 — the king seizing every serviceable boat, in the account of Musa and the man he travelled with.
  • 27:34 — the Queen of Sheba's own observation: when kings enter a town, they ruin it.

That distribution is worth pausing on, because the ten are not a neutral set. Three of them are studies in what kingship does to a place. The Queen of Sheba states the general rule from experience. The king in Al-Kahf is the reason a boat has to be damaged to be saved. And the objection to Talut is a claim about who is entitled to rule.

The Quran's most sustained use is the Egyptian ruler in Surah Yusuf, and there the choice of word is itself the point: across that whole surah the ruler is al-malik and never Firʿawn, while the ruler in Musa's account is always Pharaoh and never the king. The site treats that split at length in the article on the believer in Pharaoh's court.

The five that name God, and where they sit

Two of the five are the same four words.

فَتَعَـٰلَى ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمَلِكُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۗ وَلَا تَعْجَلْ بِٱلْقُرْءَانِ مِن قَبْلِ أَن يُقْضَىٰٓ إِلَيْكَ وَحْيُهُۥ ۖ وَقُل رَّبِّ زِدْنِى عِلْمًا

"So exalted is God, the True King. And do not hurry with the Quran before its revelation to you is completed, and say: my Lord, increase me in knowledge."

Ta-Ha (20:114)

فَتَعَـٰلَى ٱللَّهُ ٱلْمَلِكُ ٱلْحَقُّ ۖ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ رَبُّ ٱلْعَرْشِ ٱلْكَرِيمِ

"So exalted is God, the True King. There is no god but He, Lord of the noble Throne."

Al-Mu'minun (23:116)

The opening of both is identical, word for word: fa-taʿāla'llāhu'l-Maliku'l-Ḥaqq. The Quran uses that phrase exactly twice, and it uses it as a correction — in Ta-Ha after a long passage about haste, and in Al-Mu'minun at the very end of a surah that has spent itself asking whose the earth is and who owns everything.

The qualifier al-Ḥaqq is doing work that the ten human uses explain. There are kings, and then there is the true one. Set beside a list that includes a man who confiscates boats and a queen describing what kings do to towns, "the True King" reads less like an honorific and more like a distinction being drawn.

The third divine use closes the first verse of Surah Al-Jumuʿah, where everything in the heavens and the earth is glorifying God — al-Malik, al-Quddūs, al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm. Al-Jalalayn glosses the second of those, al-Quddūs, as the One far removed from anything unbefitting, and al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm as mighty and wise in His dominion and in His making. The fourth is inside the closing cascade of Al-Hashr (59:23), where the name heads a run of a dozen.

And the fifth is the last thing in the Quran

The fifth divine use is in the final surah, in its second verse.

مَلِكِ ٱلنَّاسِ

"The King of mankind."

An-Nas (114:2)

Two words. It is the middle term of the three that open An-Nas — Lord of mankind, King of mankind, God of mankind — and al-Muyassar glosses it as the One who disposes of all their affairs and has no need of them.

What makes the placement striking is what the surah is for. It is a request for shelter from a whisper. The three names escalate, and the middle one is the name for authority over people, placed in a prayer about something that gets in without asking permission. The site's reflection on the surah takes that whisper as its subject; the point here is only that the Quran's last mention of kingship is inside a plea for protection.

The two words the lists confuse

One more thing is worth stating plainly, because it is the reason the inflated figure circulates.

Malik (king) and malak (angel) are written with the same three letters. So is mulk (dominion), and mālik (owner), which is a different word again — the one in Māliki yawmi'd-dīn, Master of the Day of Judgment, in Al-Fatihah, and in Mālika'l-mulk at 3:26.

Surah Yusuf contains an example of the confusion in miniature. At 12:31 the women of the city, seeing Yusuf, say he is no human being — in hādhā illā malakun karīm, this is nothing but a noble angel. The same three letters as the king who first appears a dozen verses later, at 12:43. A different word.

Counting by spelling turns Al-Malik into a name that appears 152 times. Counting the word gives fifteen. It is the sort of error that makes a number more impressive and less true.

Reading the name

Fifteen occurrences, and two thirds of them are about human rulers — a man with no wealth, an Egyptian administrator, a boat-confiscating king, and a queen's warning about what her own class does to a town.

Against that, five. Two of them share a phrase, and the phrase specifies: the True King. One glorifies. One heads the cascade at the end of Al-Hashr. And the last of them is a word spoken by a person asking to be sheltered — which is a strange and exact place for the Quran to leave the idea of a throne.
