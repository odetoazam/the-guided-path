One Fire, Three Tellings: The Night Musa Went to Borrow a Flame

It is night in the desert. Musa is traveling with his family — years of exile in Madyan behind him, Egypt somewhere ahead — and by the classical accounts they are cold and they have lost the road. Then he sees it: a fire, far off, on the side of the mountain. He turns to his family and says a sentence that will turn out to be the last thing he says as an ordinary man.

The Quran records that sentence three times, in three different surahs. And the wording is different in each one. A quick reader might call that loose storytelling. Read the three side by side, slowly, and something else appears: each surah has kept exactly the part of his sentence that it needs — and the differences turn out to be a map of what each surah is about.

The three tellings

Here is the moment in Surah Ta-Ha:

إِذْ رَءَا نَارًا فَقَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ ٱمْكُثُوٓا۟ إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًا لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِقَبَسٍ أَوْ أَجِدُ عَلَى ٱلنَّارِ هُدًى

"When he saw a fire and said to his family: 'Stay here. I have caught sight of a fire. Perhaps I can bring you a flame from it, or find guidance at the fire.'"

Ta-Ha (20:10)

Here it is in Surah An-Naml:

إِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِأَهْلِهِۦٓ إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًا سَـَٔاتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ ءَاتِيكُم بِشِهَابٍ قَبَسٍ لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

"When Musa said to his family: 'I have caught sight of a fire. I will bring you news from it, or bring you a burning brand, so that you may warm yourselves.'"

An-Naml (27:7)

And here it is in Surah Al-Qasas:

۞ فَلَمَّا قَضَىٰ مُوسَى ٱلْأَجَلَ وَسَارَ بِأَهْلِهِۦٓ ءَانَسَ مِن جَانِبِ ٱلطُّورِ نَارًا قَالَ لِأَهْلِهِ ٱمْكُثُوٓا۟ إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًا لَّعَلِّىٓ ءَاتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ جَذْوَةٍ مِّنَ ٱلنَّارِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

"When Musa had completed the term and was traveling with his family, he caught sight of a fire on the side of the Mount. He said to his family: 'Stay here. I have caught sight of a fire. Perhaps I can bring you news from it, or an ember from the fire, so that you may warm yourselves.'"

Al-Qasas (28:29)

The part that never changes

Lay the three versions on top of each other and one phrase sits identical in all of them: innī ānastu nāran — "I have caught sight of a fire" — followed each time by the same promise-shape, ātīkum minhā bi-, "I will bring you from it…" That is the fixed core. Everything around it moves; this does not.

And the fixed core contains a curious verb. Musa does not use the ordinary word for "I saw." The verb is ānastu, from the same family as the Arabic word uns — closeness, comfort, friendly company. It is the word for spotting something that makes you feel less alone. In Ta-Ha the narrator says plainly that he "saw" a fire — ra'ā — but when Musa opens his mouth, his own word for it is warmer than that. Out in the dark with a cold family and no road, he doesn't just see a fire. He sees company.

This exact verb appears five times in the whole Quran. Four of them are this fire — the three announcements, plus the narrator's own use of it in Al-Qasas. The fifth is in Surah An-Nisa (4:6), where guardians are told to test orphans until they "perceive" — ānastum — sound judgment in them, and can hand over their property. Five uses, one job: this is the Quran's verb for catching the first glimpse of something hopeful. A light in the distance. A child turning into an adult who can be trusted. The verb never appears anywhere else, for anything else.

What each surah keeps

Now the differences. Musa names what he hopes to bring back, and the list shifts from surah to surah — but not randomly.

Ta-Ha is the only telling in which he hopes to find hudā — guidance — at the fire. The classical commentators read this at ground level: the family had lost the road in the dark, and fires were where travelers and their guides sat. Al-Muyassar and al-Jalalayn both gloss it as "someone at the fire to show us the way." He meant directions. He walked toward that fire hoping, at best, for a man who knew the route — and the voice that met him there said: "Indeed, I am your Lord" (20:12), then "I have chosen you, so listen to what is revealed" (20:13). He asked for a guide to the road. He was given the Guide, and a road that ran the rest of his life. Ta-Ha is also the surah that keeps circling back to this exact word — "whoever follows My guidance will not go astray and will not suffer" (20:123) — as if the whole surah keeps answering the thing he asked for at the fire.

An-Naml keeps a different item at the front of his sentence: khabar — news. And An-Naml is the Quran's great surah of news traveling: a few verses later, the hoopoe bird returns to Sulayman with the words "I have encompassed what you have not encompassed, and I bring you certain news from Sheba" (27:22). In the surah about reports arriving from beyond the horizon, Musa's fire-errand is quoted as a news-errand — and the news he actually received was of his own prophethood.

An-Naml's telling has two more fingerprints. It is the only version where Musa speaks with certainty: sa-ātīkum, "I will bring you," where the other two tellings hedge with laʿallī, "perhaps I can." (Al-Jalalayn pauses on that "perhaps" in Ta-Ha: a person shouldn't promise what he cannot guarantee.) And it is the only version where the flame he offers to carry home is called a shihāb. Everywhere else in the Quran — four other places — a shihāb is a missile of burning light in the sky, chasing away devils who try to eavesdrop on heaven's news before it can be delivered. This is the only shihāb in the Quran that fits in a man's hand. The Quran's word for the flame that protects the news, given to the man walking toward the biggest news of his life, in the surah of news. It is hard to believe the word landed there by accident.

Al-Qasas — "The Stories" — takes its very name from a verse four ayat before the fire: Musa arrives in Madyan, meets an old man, "and told him the story" — qaṣṣa ʿalayhi l-qaṣaṣ (28:25). In the surah named for storytelling, his announcement again promises khabar, news, a story to bring back. But the physical item has changed to the humblest word of the three: not a flame, not a blazing brand, but a jadhwa — an ember, a stick with fire dying at one end. That word appears exactly once in the whole Quran, here. And it fits the man who says it: Al-Qasas is the telling that gives us his full circumstances — the murder charge behind him, the years of shepherding for his father-in-law just completed, a man at the lowest material point of his story, hoping out loud for a piece of burnt wood.

Did he say it three ways, or did the Quran change it?

The honest question deserves an honest answer. The traditional explanation is also the most natural one: a man standing in the cold with his family does not say one clipped sentence and walk off. He reassures, he repeats, he adds — stay here; I'll bring a flame; maybe someone there knows the road; at least you'll warm yourselves. All of it was said. Each surah then quotes the slice of that speech that serves the surah's own argument — guidance in the surah of guidance-restored, news in the surah of news, the story and the ember in the surah of stories. Classical scholars devoted entire books to exactly these repeated-with-variation passages, comparing them word by word — this is not a modern patch; the tradition noticed, and treated the variations as deliberate selection, not error.

What makes the selection visible in this case is that it can be checked. The words each surah "keeps" are not vague themes; they are countable. Khabar, in the singular, appears exactly twice in the entire Quran — both times in this announcement, in the two surahs whose stories run on news. The guidance-word appears in this scene only in Ta-Ha. The ember-word exists nowhere in the Quran except Al-Qasas's telling. Each surah is holding a piece of the sentence no other surah holds.

The warmest word in the Quran's coldest root

One more detail hides in the endings. Two of the tellings close with laʿallakum taṣṭalūn — "so that you may warm yourselves." The verb comes from the root ṣ-l-y, and if that root sounds familiar, it is because the Quran uses it constantly — for the Fire of Hell. Across the whole Quran this root occurs twenty-five times. Twenty-three of them are people burning, roasting, entering the Blaze. The only two gentle uses of the burning-root in the entire Book — a family warming their hands — are these two verses, on the night Musa met God.

Same root, same fire-flesh contact, opposite meaning. The Quran's own vocabulary is quietly telling you that fire is not one thing: the same heat that is punishment in twenty-three verses is mercy in two, and the difference is not in the fire. On this night, the fire on the mountainside was not even fire — when Musa reached it, what blazed was light, and what came out of it was a voice.

The borrowed flame

The last thread is the strangest. In Ta-Ha and An-Naml, the thing Musa hopes to carry home is a qabas — a borrowed flame, fire taken from someone else's fire. That root appears exactly three times in the Quran. Two of them are Musa's requests on this night. The third is on the Day of Resurrection:

يَوْمَ يَقُولُ ٱلْمُنَـٰفِقُونَ وَٱلْمُنَـٰفِقَـٰتُ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱنظُرُونَا نَقْتَبِسْ مِن نُّورِكُمْ قِيلَ ٱرْجِعُوا۟ وَرَآءَكُمْ فَٱلْتَمِسُوا۟ نُورًا فَضُرِبَ بَيْنَهُم بِسُورٍ لَّهُۥ بَابٌۢ بَاطِنُهُۥ فِيهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةُ وَظَـٰهِرُهُۥ مِن قِبَلِهِ ٱلْعَذَابُ

"On the Day the hypocrite men and hypocrite women will say to those who believed: 'Wait for us — let us borrow from your light.' It will be said: 'Go back behind you and seek light.' Then a wall will be struck between them, with a gate: inside it is mercy, and outside, facing it, is punishment."

Al-Hadid (57:13)

The hypocrites' verb — naqtabis, "let us borrow a light" — is Musa's word for the flame he wanted to carry home. Three occurrences of one rare root, and they split into two scenes: a man in the dark walking toward the light himself, asking its Owner directly — and people in the dark asking other people to lend them a light secondhand, at the one moment when light can no longer be transferred. Musa asked for a borrowed flame and was given prophethood. The hypocrites ask for borrowed light and are given a wall. The word is the same. The direction of the asking is not.

The shopping list, answered

Count what Musa named across his three quoted hopes: a flame, an ember, warmth for his family, news, and someone to show him the road. It is a shopping list — the shortest, humblest set of requests in any prophet's story. He was not on a retreat. He was not seeking a vision. He was a cold man with a cold family, off to run a five-minute errand.

And the fire answered every single item on the list, in a currency he could not have imagined. He came for directions and received guidance he would carry for the rest of his life. He came for news and left as the news. He came to borrow a flame from a stranger's campfire and found that the fire had been lit for him. The Quran keeps his modest shopping list on record three times — one copy per surah, each keeping the line it loves best — perhaps because the distance between what he asked for and what he was given is the whole story of that night, and no single telling could hold it.
