A Prisoner States God's Own Decree — Word for Word

There is a short command in the Quran, four words in Arabic, that God gives about Himself in His own voice: allā taʿbudū illā iyyāh — "that you worship none but Him." It sits inside one of the most famous verses in the whole book, the verse that opens the Quran's instructions on how to treat your parents.

That exact wording — with the pronoun "Him," not the name "Allah" — appears in only one other place. And the person who says it there is not a prophet on a mountain or a king on a throne. He is a slave, falsely imprisoned, speaking to two fellow inmates in a cell. He states the law of the universe in the identical words God uses for it, from the bottom of a prison.

The decree, in God's voice

The famous verse is in Surah Al-Isra. It is the one people quote about honoring parents — but notice what comes first, before the parents are even mentioned:

۞ وَقَضَىٰ رَبُّكَ أَلَّا تَعْبُدُوٓا۟ إِلَّآ إِيَّاهُ وَبِٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ إِحْسَـٰنًا ۚ إِمَّا يَبْلُغَنَّ عِندَكَ ٱلْكِبَرَ أَحَدُهُمَآ أَوْ كِلَاهُمَا فَلَا تَقُل لَّهُمَآ أُفٍّ وَلَا تَنْهَرْهُمَا وَقُل لَّهُمَا قَوْلًا كَرِيمًا

"And your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him, and to parents, good treatment…"

Al-Isra (17:23)

The verb that opens it is qaḍā — "decreed," "ordained," the word for a settled command handed down from the highest authority. Your Lord has decreed: worship none but Him. This is the thing stated at the very top, the order everything else in the verse hangs from. It is as high and as final as the Quran's language gets.

The same words, from a prison cell

Now go back several surahs, into the story of Yusuf. He has been sold as a slave and thrown into prison on a false accusation. Two other prisoners ask him to interpret their dreams. Before he answers, he uses the moment to tell them about God — and this is what he says:

مَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ أَسْمَآءً سَمَّيْتُمُوهَآ أَنتُمْ وَءَابَآؤُكُم مَّآ أَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَا مِن سُلْطَـٰنٍ ۚ إِنِ ٱلْحُكْمُ إِلَّا لِلَّهِ ۚ أَمَرَ أَلَّا تَعْبُدُوٓا۟ إِلَّآ إِيَّاهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ ٱلدِّينُ ٱلْقَيِّمُ وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ ٱلنَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

"…The command belongs to none but God. He has commanded that you worship none but Him. That is the upright religion, but most people do not know."

Yusuf (12:40)

There it is again — allā taʿbudū illā iyyāh, "that you worship none but Him," the identical four-word clause, pronoun and all. In Al-Isra it is what "your Lord has decreed." Here it is what a shackled prisoner tells two men who have no reason yet to take him seriously. He even frames it with the same idea sitting under the decree: "the command belongs to none but God." Yusuf, who at that moment commands nothing — not his freedom, not his name, not the next hour of his life — announces that all command belongs to God, and repeats God's own words for it.

Why the exact wording matters

The Quran talks about worshipping God alone in many places, and says it many ways. What is unusual here is not the idea — it is the wording. This precise clause, built on the pronoun iyyāh ("Him") rather than the name Allāh, lands in exactly two verses: 17:23 and 12:40. Elsewhere the Quran phrases the same call with the name — "worship none but Allah" (as in 11:2, 41:14, 46:21). Those are the same message; they are not the same sentence. The verbatim clause, "worship none but Him," is spoken twice: once as the decree of the Lord of everything, and once by a man who owns nothing.

That pairing is the whole reveal. The Quran lets the highest voice in existence and one of its lowest-placed human beings say the exact same words about who is worth worshipping. The decree does not sound different coming from the cell. It is not a smaller truth because a slave is the one saying it. The law that the King of all things has ordained is the same law a prisoner can hold onto with nothing left in his hands — and state, word for word, to the man in the next cell.

What a careful reading owes here

The claim here is deliberately narrow, and it is stronger for being narrow. It is not "only these two verses teach worshipping God alone" — that idea runs all through the Quran. It is that this specific clause, allā taʿbudū illā iyyāh with the pronoun, is used in exactly two places, corpus-checked: the decree of 17:23 and Yusuf's speech in 12:40. The versions that use the name "Allah" instead are real and nearby; they are simply not the same string of words, and the reveal is about the string of words.

The classical commentators read 12:40 exactly as it looks. Yusuf, in the prison, is calling his two companions away from the invented gods of Egypt and toward the God who alone holds command — "that is the upright religion." He is not softening the message for his audience or his circumstances. He gives them the decree at full height, in the words the decree is given in. And the Quran, by letting those words repeat in his mouth, quietly makes a point about where truth can be spoken from: the floor of a prison is not too low for it.
