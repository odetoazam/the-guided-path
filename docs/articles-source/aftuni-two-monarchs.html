"Counsel Me": The Two Rulers Who Asked, and the One Who Never Did

Only two rulers in the entire Quran turn to the people around them and say aftuni — "counsel me, give me your reading." One is the king of Egypt in the time of Yusuf. The other is the queen of Sheba. They use the identical phrase, the same opening address to the same kind of assembly, separated by fifteen surahs. And they are the only two monarchs in the Quran whose stories end with them standing on the side of a prophet. The word that saved them was a question.

The verb aftā means to ask for a considered ruling, an expert reading of something you cannot read alone. The command form aimed at an audience, aftūnī — "give me your reading" — falls from a royal mouth twice in the Quran, and only twice. Lay the two moments side by side and a pattern surfaces that has nothing to do with Egypt or Sheba and everything to do with what power does to the ear.

The king who admitted he could not read his own dream

The ruler of Egypt is troubled by a dream, and instead of pronouncing on it, he opens his hands to his court:

وَقَالَ ٱلْمَلِكُ إِنِّىٓ أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَٰتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَـٰتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَـٰتٍ ۖ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَأُ أَفْتُونِى فِى رُءْيَـٰىَ إِن كُنتُمْ لِلرُّءْيَا تَعْبُرُونَ

"And the king said, 'Indeed I have seen seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry. O council, give me your reading of my vision, if you can interpret visions.'"

Yusuf (12:43)

Yusuf's story keeps one word for this man: al-malik, the king — and reserves Pharaoh for another era. That distinction is its own subject, but the ear he shows here belongs to the title. He describes what he saw plainly, names it a thing he does not understand, and asks. And when his courtiers fail him — "confused dreams," they shrug — the asking does not stop. It travels down through a freed cupbearer, into a prison cell, and reaches a forgotten prisoner named Yusuf. The chain of the question is what pulls Yusuf out of the dungeon and sets him over the storehouses of Egypt. The king's willingness to say "I cannot read this" is the hinge on which a prophet's whole rise turns.

The queen who would not decide alone

Now the queen of Sheba, holding a letter from Sulayman that has unsettled her whole court. Watch the shape of her response:

قَالَتْ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْمَلَؤُا۟ أَفْتُونِى فِىٓ أَمْرِى مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ

"She said, 'O council, give me your reading in my matter. I would never decide a matter until you are present with me.'"

An-Naml (27:32)

The same royal address, the same verb of asking — and then she says the thing tyrants cannot say: I do not settle a matter until you bear witness with me. Her council answers with the exact temptation that undoes lesser rulers. They offer her force:

قَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أُو۟لُوا۟ قُوَّةٍ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ وَٱلْأَمْرُ إِلَيْكِ فَٱنظُرِى مَاذَا تَأْمُرِينَ

"They said, 'We are men of strength and men of great might, but the command is yours, so consider what you will command.'"

An-Naml (27:33)

They tell her she has the muscle to fight. She sets the muscle aside, reasons that kings ruin the towns they enter, sends a gift to test the man, reads his refusal correctly, travels to see for herself, and submits — not to Sulayman, but with him, to the Lord of all the worlds. Every step is a further instance of the same instinct: gather more sight before you move. The ruler who began by asking her court to witness ends by witnessing the truth herself.

The ruler who saw only himself

Set against these two is the ruler who never once said aftuni. When a believer in his own court begins to reason with him, Pharaoh cuts the reasoning off with a sentence that is the exact opposite of a question:

يَـٰقَوْمِ لَكُمُ ٱلْمُلْكُ ٱلْيَوْمَ ظَـٰهِرِينَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَمَن يَنصُرُنَا مِنۢ بَأْسِ ٱللَّهِ إِن جَآءَنَا ۚ قَالَ فِرْعَوْنُ مَآ أُرِيكُمْ إِلَّا مَآ أَرَىٰ وَمَآ أَهْدِيكُمْ إِلَّا سَبِيلَ ٱلرَّشَادِ

"'O my people, sovereignty is yours today, dominant in the land — but who will help us against the might of Allah, should it come upon us?' Pharaoh said, 'I do not show you anything but what I see, and I do not guide you except to the path of right guidance.'"

Ghafir (40:29)

"I do not show you anything but what I see." It is the perfect inversion of aftuni. The two rulers who prospered opened a gap between what they knew and what they needed to know, and invited someone to fill it. Pharaoh seals that gap shut and declares his own eye the only instrument in the kingdom. Where the king of Egypt said "read my dream, for I cannot," Pharaoh says the reading is finished before anyone else has looked. His court is decoration. His question is a monologue.

The reading that outranks the crown

The Quran is quietly precise about who these three rulers ended up beside. The king who asked lifted a prophet out of prison and handed him the country. The queen who asked knelt beside a prophet before the Lord of the worlds. The ruler who refused to ask drowned with his army chasing one. Their thrones were comparable. Their armies, in Sheba's case and Pharaoh's, were both described as mighty. What separated them was a single verb — whether a person with total power could still say aftuni, still treat their own sight as partial, still want another eye in the room.

Which turns the word back on the reader, because most of us hold more power over some small kingdom than we admit — a household, a team, a comment section, our own certainties. When something arrives that unsettles us — a claim, a correction, a dream we cannot read — do we open the matter to counsel the way two rulers did and prosper, or do we announce, with Pharaoh, that we show ourselves only what we already see? The crown never decided it. The ear did.
