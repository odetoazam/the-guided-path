Ar-Ra'uf: The Divine Name Given to a Human Being Exactly Once

Ar-Ra'uf (ٱلرَّءُوف) is usually translated "the Most Kind" or "the Compassionate," which puts it uncomfortably close to Ar-Rahman and Ar-Rahim and makes it look redundant. It is not redundant. Raʾfah is a particular thing — the tenderness that flinches at someone else's pain — and the Quran distributes the word in a way that says so.

It occurs eleven times. Ten name God. The eleventh names the Prophet ﷺ, and it sits four verses from the end of Surah At-Tawbah.

The one that is not about God

لَقَدْ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولٌ مِّنْ أَنفُسِكُمْ عَزِيزٌ عَلَيْهِ مَا عَنِتُّمْ حَرِيصٌ عَلَيْكُم بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

"There has come to you a Messenger from among yourselves. What burdens you is heavy on him. He is anxious over you, and to the believers he is tender, merciful."

At-Tawbah (9:128)

Al-Jalalayn walks the verse clause by clause. ʿAzīzun ʿalayhi mā ʿanittum — your hardship, and your meeting with what you dislike, is hard on him. Ḥarīṣun ʿalaykum — he wants intensely that you be guided. And then the two names: raʾūf, which al-Jalalayn glosses shadīd ar-raḥmah, intense in mercy, and raḥīm, he wants good for them.

Al-Muyassar reads the opening the same way: what you undergo of harm and difficulty weighs on him.

Both names in that pair are names of God. Being handed to a human being, together, in that order, is the thing that makes this verse remarkable — and it is worth saying carefully what the corpus does and does not show. The pair raʾūf raḥīm occurs nine times in the Quran — eight of God, and this one of the Prophet. That is the count. Nothing here needs exaggerating.

What the word actually means

The commentaries treat raʾfah as mercy at its most acute — not mercy in general but the reaction to a specific person's specific difficulty.

Look at where it lands. Surah An-Nahl attaches it to livestock:

وَتَحْمِلُ أَثْقَالَكُمْ إِلَىٰ بَلَدٍ لَّمْ تَكُونُوا۟ بَـٰلِغِيهِ إِلَّا بِشِقِّ ٱلْأَنفُسِ ۚ إِنَّ رَبَّكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

"And they carry your loads to a land you could not have reached except with hardship to yourselves. Indeed your Lord is tender, merciful."

An-Nahl (16:7)

Al-Jalalayn's comment is one clause: because He created them for you. The verse is about an animal carrying a weight so that a human back does not have to. The name attaches to the removal of a specific, physical difficulty — the reason the verse gives is not abstract kindness but a bag someone would otherwise be carrying.

Al-Hajj does the same with two other loads at once — ships that carry people across water, and the sky held up so it does not fall (22:65). Muyassar reads the closing name as covering both: the harnessing and the holding.

The tenderness that shows up as a delay

Twice, the commentaries gloss the name with the same phrase they use for Al-Halim — a punishment that has not been rushed.

At 16:47, in a passage listing the ways a people can be seized — the earth swallowing them, a punishment arriving from where they did not expect, being taken while travelling — the verse closes with raʾūf raḥīm. Al-Jalalayn: ḥaythu lam yuʿājilhum bi'l-ʿuqūbah, in that He did not hasten them to the punishment.

At 24:20, in the middle of the passage about a rumour that tore through Madinah, the same closing. Al-Jalalayn again: had it not been for God's favour and mercy and that He is raʾūf raḥīm, He would have hastened you to the punishment.

وَلَوْلَا فَضْلُ ٱللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ وَرَحْمَتُهُۥ وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

"And were it not for God's favour upon you and His mercy, and that God is tender, merciful —"

An-Nur (24:20)

The sentence is deliberately left hanging in the Arabic too. What would have happened is not said.

So raʾfah is not only softness of feeling. In two of its eleven appearances it is the name for something not happening to you.

The verse that defines a tyrant by lacking it

The most exact definition of raʾfah in the classical commentaries is not in a verse about mercy at all. It is in a verse about violence.

وَإِذَا بَطَشْتُم بَطَشْتُمْ جَبَّارِينَ

"And when you strike, you strike as tyrants."

Ash-Shuʿara (26:130)

Hud is describing his own people. Al-Jalalayn glosses the word jabbārīn — tyrants — with three words: min ghayri raʾfah, without any tenderness.

That is a definition by subtraction, and it is the most useful one available. The tyrant in that verse is not defined by how hard he hits or by what he wants. He is defined by what is missing at the moment of the blow: the flinch. Al-Jabbar, the name for that condition, is used nine times of human beings in the Quran and every one of them is an accusation.

Put the two verses beside each other and the shape is complete. The Prophet at 9:128 is the man on whom your difficulty weighs. The people of ʿĀd at 26:130 are the ones on whom nothing weighs at all.

Where the name arrives

Two more placements are worth noticing, because they are not where a name for tenderness would be expected.

At 2:207, the verse about a man who sells himself seeking God's approval — al-Jalalayn names the occasion as Ṣuhayb, who left his wealth behind to the Makkans and emigrated — closes on raʾūfun bi'l-ʿibād, tender with His servants. The reason al-Jalalayn gives for the name being there is that God guided them to what pleases Him. The tenderness is in the direction-giving, not in the outcome.

At 3:30, the name closes a verse about the Day when everyone finds every good and every evil deed present in front of them and wishes there were a vast distance between themselves and what they did. Al-Muyassar's reading of the pairing is unusually direct: fear the seizing of the Compelling God — and along with the severity of His punishment, He is the One described by complete mercy toward His servants.

That is the same combination the Quran keeps making. The verse that names the reckoning names the tenderness in the same breath.

Reading the name

Eleven occurrences. Nine of them pair raʾūf with raḥīm; the two exceptions (2:207, 3:30) read raʾūfun bi'l-ʿibād, tender with the servants. Ten name God and one names the Prophet, in a verse whose first clause is about your difficulty being heavy on him.

And the definition that stays with you comes from a commentary on the opposite word. A tyrant, said al-Jalalayn, strikes without raʾfah. Whatever else this name means, it names the thing whose absence makes the blow possible.
