I Checked Every Verse That Uses the Word "Despair." Mercy Is Always Right There.

The Quran has a specific word for the deepest kind of despair — not sadness, not worry, but giving up entirely on the idea that things could get better. That word is qunūṭ, and its verb forms appear exactly six times in the whole Quran. This piece checks all six, one by one. Here is what they have in common: every single time the Quran uses this word for total despair, the word for God's mercy — raḥmah — shows up either in the very same verse, or in the verse right next to it. There are no exceptions. Six for six.

The rule, stated plainly

Before walking through the six verses, it's worth being exact about the claim, because a vague version of it is easy to challenge. Four of the six times, despair and mercy are two words apart in the same sentence. The other two times, mercy is one verse away — the verse right before or right after. So the honest way to say it is: four in the same breath, and all six within one verse. That is the actual pattern, checked against the Quran's own wording, not a looser claim dressed up to sound more impressive than it is.

Ibrahim's visitors

The first two appearances sit back to back, in the story of the angels who came to give Ibrahim news of a son in his old age:

قَالُوا۟ بَشَّرْنَـٰكَ بِٱلْحَقِّ فَلَا تَكُن مِّنَ ٱلْقَـٰنِطِينَ

"They said, 'We have given you good news in truth, so do not be among the despairing.'"

Al-Hijr (15:55)

قَالَ وَمَن يَقْنَطُ مِن رَّحْمَةِ رَبِّهِۦٓ إِلَّا ٱلضَّآلُّونَ

"He said, 'And who despairs of the mercy of his Lord except those who are astray?'"

Al-Hijr (15:56)

Ibrahim is an old man being told he will have a son. The natural human reaction is disbelief — this doesn't happen at my age. The angels name that reaction "despair" in one breath (15:55), and Ibrahim's own reply, in the very next verse, answers with the word "mercy" (15:56). The two words are one verse apart, and Ibrahim's own sentence supplies the answer: despairing of God's mercy is not caution or realism. It is the specific error of someone who has lost their way.

The weather

The next appearance moves from a personal crisis to something everyone experiences — the weather:

وَإِذَآ أَذَقْنَا ٱلنَّاسَ رَحْمَةً فَرِحُوا۟ بِهَا ۖ وَإِن تُصِبْهُمْ سَيِّئَةٌۢ بِمَا قَدَّمَتْ أَيْدِيهِمْ إِذَا هُمْ يَقْنَطُونَ

"And when We let people taste mercy, they rejoice in it. But when hardship strikes them because of what their own hands have sent forth, at once they despair."

Ar-Rum (30:36)

Here "mercy" and "despair" sit in the same sentence, describing two moods of the same person — happy when things go well, despairing the moment they don't. The Quran doesn't hide the swing. It names it, in one breath, and lets the contrast do the teaching: the mercy people were just celebrating didn't disappear. Their memory of it did.

The verse people call the most hopeful in the whole Quran

The third pairing is the most direct of the six, and it is one of the most quoted verses on repentance in the entire Quran:

۞ قُلْ يَـٰعِبَادِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا۟ مِن رَّحْمَةِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ ٱلذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

"Say: O My servants who have wronged themselves — do not despair of the mercy of God. Indeed, God forgives all sins. Indeed, it is He who is the Forgiving, the Merciful."

Az-Zumar (39:53)

This is the verse the companion Ibn Umar named as the single most hope-giving line in the entire Quran, for people who assume their sins have gone past the point of forgiveness. Ibn Abbas went further still, in a warning rather than a title: anyone who makes God's servants despair of His mercy after this verse has rejected the Book of God. The verse is addressed to people who have "wronged themselves," a phrase that covers the worst version of a person's own record, and its command is a direct negation of the same root: do not despair. "Despair" and "mercy" sit four words apart, in the sentence written for the person with the most reason to think they're past saving.

The person hit by hardship

The fifth and sixth appearances are the two that land one verse away rather than in the same sentence, and they describe the same emotional swing from two different angles:

لَّا يَسْـَٔمُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ مِن دُعَآءِ ٱلْخَيْرِ وَإِن مَّسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ فَيَـُٔوسٌ قَنُوطٌ

"Man does not tire of asking for good, but if evil touches him, he becomes despondent and despairing."

Fussilat (41:49)

وَلَئِنْ أَذَقْنَـٰهُ رَحْمَةً مِّنَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِ ضَرَّآءَ مَسَّتْهُ لَيَقُولَنَّ هَـٰذَا لِى وَمَآ أَظُنُّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ قَآئِمَةً وَلَئِن رُّجِعْتُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّىٓ إِنَّ لِى عِندَهُۥ لَلْحُسْنَىٰ ۚ فَلَنُنَبِّئَنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ بِمَا عَمِلُوا۟ وَلَنُذِيقَنَّهُم مِّنْ عَذَابٍ غَلِيظٍ

"But if We let him taste mercy from Us after hardship has touched him, he will surely say, 'This is [due] to me, and I do not think the Hour will occur; and if I am returned to my Lord, indeed for me with Him will be the best.' But We will surely inform those who disbelieved about what they did, and We will surely make them taste a harsh punishment."

Fussilat (41:50)

The despair verse names the low point. The very next verse names what actually follows it — mercy arriving, though this time the Quran adds a second observation: the same person who despaired now claims the recovery as his own doing. Even the correction comes one verse after the collapse, not chapters later.

Rain after drought

The final appearance closes the set with the most visible mercy in daily life — rain after a dry spell:

وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُنَزِّلُ ٱلْغَيْثَ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا قَنَطُوا۟ وَيَنشُرُ رَحْمَتَهُۥ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْوَلِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ

"And it is He who sends down the rain after they had despaired, and spreads His mercy. And He is the Protector, the Praiseworthy."

Ash-Shura (42:28)

Here "despaired" and "mercy" sit five words apart in a single sentence about rain — the most ordinary form of relief there is. The Quran doesn't save this pattern for extraordinary crises. It uses the same design for the sky.

What the count actually shows

Six occurrences of a specific, strong word for despair. Six times, the word for mercy sits either in the same verse or in the verse beside it. No sixth-verse exception, no case where despair is left to sit alone on the page without an answer nearby. That is the whole finding — not a claim about how the reader should feel, but a plain count of where two words land relative to each other across the entire Quran.

It is worth being honest about what this does and doesn't prove. The adjacency window used here is same-verse-or-next-verse, and that is a real methodological choice, not an accident of the text — it is stated here up front so the reader can judge it rather than discover it later. A stricter test (same verse only) would still hold four times out of six; a looser one would obviously hold more. The six-for-six claim is scoped exactly to this word, qunūṭ, not to every Arabic word connected to hopelessness in the Quran — a related word, ya'is, appears elsewhere and would need its own separate count.

What remains true, checked verse by verse, is this: every time the Quran names this particular kind of total despair, it does not leave the word to stand by itself. Mercy is always close enough to reach.
