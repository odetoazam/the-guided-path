"Show Me": The Two Prophets Who Asked to See

Two prophets, in two different centuries, said the same word to God: arini — "show me." One asked to see how the dead are brought back to life. The other asked to see God Himself. Both already believed. Neither was rebuked for asking. But one request was granted with a gentle, almost playful demonstration, and the other was met with a single word — never — followed by a mountain turning to dust. The Quran lets the two prayers stand apart so we can read, in the gap between the answers, the difference between what God will show a heart and what no heart in this world could survive seeing.

The plea arini, "show me," is spoken to God by exactly two people in the Quran. Setting Ibrahim's request beside Musa's turns a pair of famous scenes into a single lesson about longing, certainty, and the one sight that is kept back.

Ibrahim asks to see the dead revived

Ibrahim puts his request plainly:

وَإِذْ قَالَ إِبْرَٰهِـۧمُ رَبِّ أَرِنِى كَيْفَ تُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ ۖ قَالَ أَوَلَمْ تُؤْمِن ۖ قَالَ بَلَىٰ وَلَـٰكِن لِّيَطْمَئِنَّ قَلْبِى

"And when Ibrahim said, 'My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead,' He said, 'Do you not believe?' He said, 'Yes, but so that my heart may be at rest.'"

Al-Baqarah (2:260)

God's reply is a question that goes straight to the nerve: do you not believe? And Ibrahim's answer draws the sharpest line in the Quran between two very different inner states. Balā — of course I believe — but so that my heart may be at rest. He is not asking because faith has failed him. He is asking because certainty of the mind and stillness of the heart are two different possessions, and he wants the second one. The knowledge that God revives the dead already lives in him. He wants to watch it, so that the truth he holds by conviction he might also hold by sight. And God grants it: take four birds, draw them to you, then call them, and they will come rushing back to life. The request to see the resurrection is answered with a private rehearsal of the resurrection.

Musa asks to see God

Musa, at the appointed meeting, having just been spoken to directly by his Lord, reaches for something further:

قَالَ رَبِّ أَرِنِىٓ أَنظُرْ إِلَيْكَ ۚ قَالَ لَن تَرَىٰنِى وَلَـٰكِنِ ٱنظُرْ إِلَى ٱلْجَبَلِ فَإِنِ ٱسْتَقَرَّ مَكَانَهُۥ فَسَوْفَ تَرَىٰنِى ۚ فَلَمَّا تَجَلَّىٰ رَبُّهُۥ لِلْجَبَلِ جَعَلَهُۥ دَكًّا وَخَرَّ مُوسَىٰ صَعِقًا

"He said, 'My Lord, show me, that I may look at You.' He said, 'You will never see Me; but look at the mountain — if it remains firm in its place, then you will see Me.' And when his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust, and Musa fell down unconscious."

Al-A'raf (7:143)

The same opening word — arini — and a different object. Ibrahim asked to see God's work; Musa asks to see God's self. The answer arrives in a word of absolute negation, lan tarānī, "you will never see Me," and then a demonstration that turns the refusal into mercy. God does not simply deny the request; He shows Musa why. A mountain — the most stable, enduring mass a person can point to — cannot hold still under the faintest unveiling of its Maker; it collapses into powder. The lesson lands in Musa's body before it reaches his mind: he drops unconscious. What he asked to look upon would have unmade him the way it unmade the peak. The never was not a door slammed on a favorite servant. It was God refusing to let him stand where he would be annihilated.

What is shown and what is borne

Read together, the two prayers sort the seeable from the unseeable. Ibrahim's request pointed at what God does in creation — the giving of life, an act with edges a human mind can watch and survive. That was granted, because the resurrection is a thing God intends His servants to be sure of. Musa's request pointed past the acts to the Actor, at the direct sight of the divine self, and that is the one thing this world is built too small to contain. The refusal of Musa and the granting of Ibrahim are the same wisdom facing two directions: God opens His works to the eye and keeps His essence beyond it, because one steadies a heart and the other would shatter the vessel that tried to hold it.

And there is a quiet dignity the two scenes share that is worth carrying. Both men come out of the encounter more devoted, not less. Musa wakes from his collapse and says glory be to You, I turn to You, I am the first of the believers — the man told never loves God more on the far side of the refusal. Ibrahim, given his sight, is settled into the stillness he asked for. Neither of them was punished for wanting to see; the wanting was itself a form of love, the love that is not content to know its Beloved only by report. What the pairing leaves is permission and its limit at once: bring the longing to see — it is the posture of the closest servants — and trust that when God answers one arini with a demonstration and another with not yet, not here, both answers are cut from the same care. Some sights He grants to settle you. One He withholds to keep you whole, until a life where you could finally bear it.
