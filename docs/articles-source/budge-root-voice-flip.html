The Funeral Verse Is Passive. Its Only Twin Isn't.

You have heard this verse at almost every Muslim funeral: "whoever is drawn away from the Fire and admitted into Paradise has truly succeeded." It is Aal-Imran 3:185, and it is one of the most recited lines in the whole Quran. Most people who hear it never notice one small thing about its grammar — the word for "drawn away" is passive. It doesn't say the soul pulls itself clear. It says the soul is pulled clear, by someone else.

That word — "drawn away," zuhziha in Arabic — is rare. It comes from a root that appears in the Quran in exactly one other place. And in that other place, the grammar flips. Read the two verses side by side and you get a complete, two-part statement about who actually gets you out of trouble.

The man who wants a thousand years

The first verse comes earlier in the Quran, in Surah Al-Baqarah. It describes a person clinging to life so hard that a thousand years still wouldn't feel like enough:

وَلَتَجِدَنَّهُمْ أَحْرَصَ ٱلنَّاسِ عَلَىٰ حَيَوٰةٍ وَمِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَشْرَكُوا۟ ۚ يَوَدُّ أَحَدُهُمْ لَوْ يُعَمَّرُ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ وَمَا هُوَ بِمُزَحْزِحِهِۦ مِنَ ٱلْعَذَابِ أَن يُعَمَّرَ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ بَصِيرٌۢ بِمَا يَعْمَلُونَ

"…and you will surely find them the most eager of people for life — even more than the idolaters. One of them wishes he could be granted a thousand years — but even if he were granted it, that would not budge him from the punishment. And God sees all that they do."

Al-Baqarah (2:96)

The key word is bi-muzahzihihi — "would budge him." It comes from an unusual root, z-ḥ-z-ḥ, built by doubling a smaller root that means "to push aside." Doubling it makes the pushing repeated, effortful — the sound of shoving at something that won't move. Here the word is what grammarians call an active participle: a "doer" word, the kind that names something as the source of an action. And the sentence denies that quality to the long life itself. Even a thousand years granted to him would not have it in itself to push him clear of the punishment. Length of life is not a rescue. It cannot do the pushing.

The verse you already know

The only other place this root shows up in the entire Quran is the funeral verse, seven surahs later:

كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَآئِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ ۗ وَإِنَّمَا تُوَفَّوْنَ أُجُورَكُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ ۖ فَمَن زُحْزِحَ عَنِ ٱلنَّارِ وَأُدْخِلَ ٱلْجَنَّةَ فَقَدْ فَازَ ۗ وَمَا ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا مَتَـٰعُ ٱلْغُرُورِ

"Every soul will taste death, and you will only be paid your wages in full on the Day of Resurrection. So whoever is drawn away from the Fire and admitted into Paradise has truly succeeded. And the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion."

Aal-Imran (3:185)

Same root. Same basic meaning — being pushed clear of something. But the verb has changed shape. In 2:96 it was active, a word about doing the pushing, and it was denied. In 3:185 it is passive: zuhziha, "he was drawn away." No one asks who did the pushing here, because the grammar has already answered that question by taking the action away from the person entirely. The soul doesn't draw itself away from the Fire. It is drawn away.

Reading the two together

Put the two verses next to each other and a simple, complete idea appears. In 2:96, a person tries to buy safety with sheer duration — more years, more time, more of his own life stacked up as a kind of payment. The Quran answers, in the active voice, that this cannot work: length of life does not have the power to push anyone clear of punishment, no matter how long it runs. In 3:185, the same rescue is described again, and this time it happens — but in the passive voice, with the person doing nothing. He is not the one who moves. He is moved.

The rare root appears exactly twice in the Quran, and both times it is attached to the same subject: getting away from punishment. One verse tells you what cannot do the rescuing — your own lifespan, however long. The other tells you, without naming an agent in the sentence, that the rescue still happens. The Quran doesn't need to say "God draws him away." The passive voice already carries the weight of an unnamed rescuer standing behind the sentence.

What a careful reading owes here

One classical reading of 2:96 deserves to be stated precisely, because it is easy to get slightly wrong. The thing denied the power to "budge him" grammatically is not the man himself — it is the long life he was wishing for. The Arabic reads as: even if he is granted a thousand years, that granted life would not be his pusher-away from the punishment. This is also how classical commentators read it: the sentence measures the years, not the man, and finds the years powerless. That is a small but important distinction — the verse is not saying a person cannot ask God for rescue. It is saying that time itself, stacked up on its own, is not the currency rescue is bought with.

The second thing worth saying plainly: this is a two-verse pattern, not a rule that appears throughout the Quran. The root z-ḥ-z-ḥ is genuinely rare — these are its only two appearances, corpus-checked word by word. The strength of the reading is exactly that smallness. It is not a general grammar lesson about active and passive verbs. It is one word, used twice, about one subject — getting free of the Fire — with the grammar answering, both times, the same question: can a person do this alone? The first time, no. The second time, it happens anyway — just not by his own hand.

The verse recited at the graveside has always carried this quiet detail. The soul does not walk itself out of the Fire. It is walked out. That is not a small thing to hear standing over a grave.
