Al-Ghafur and Al-Ghaffar: The Quran's Two Names for Forgiveness, and the Difference Between Them

One root gives the Quran two names for a forgiving God, in the same way one root gives it Ar-Rahman and Ar-Rahim. Al-Ghafur (ٱلْغَفُور) appears ninety-one times. Al-Ghaffar (ٱلْغَفَّار) appears five. The two are built from the same three letters and differ only in shape, and the difference in shape is the reason the Quran can use one of them constantly and the other almost never. The rarer name is the more intense one, and every place it lands, something about the surrounding sentence explains why it was chosen.

What the root means before it means forgiveness

The root is gh-f-r (غ ف ر). Its concrete sense is covering — putting something over a thing so that what is underneath stops being exposed. The same root gives Arabic mighfar, the mail hood a fighter wears under a helmet, laid over the head and neck.

The picture underneath the theology is a covering laid over a fault. The fault is still there. What changes is its exposure: it is hidden from view, and it stops counting against the person the covering is laid over.

Two patterns, two temperaments

Arabic builds intensity into the shape of a word rather than adding an adverb.

Ghafur follows the pattern fa'ul. It describes a settled, characteristic quality — the sort of thing a person simply is. The same pattern gives shakur, sabur, wadud.

Ghaffar follows the pattern fa''al, with the middle letter doubled. Arabic uses this shape for two overlapping ideas: a quality present in great abundance, and an action done over and over. It is the pattern behind trade names — najjar, carpenter; khabbaz, baker — the person who does the thing repeatedly, as what they do.

Applied to forgiveness, the second pattern adds something the first leaves out: the covering happens again. Pardon as a repeated act rather than a single event.

Where the rare name appears

Five verses. They fall into two groups, and both groups justify the pattern.

Attached to a process rather than a moment

وَإِنِّى لَغَفَّارٌ لِّمَن تَابَ وَءَامَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَـٰلِحًا ثُمَّ ٱهْتَدَىٰ

"And indeed, I am Ghaffar toward whoever repents, and believes, and does righteous work, and then is guided."

Ta-Ha (20:82)

Four stages, joined in a chain. Al-Jalalayn walks through each: repents from associating partners with God, believes in the sense of affirming His oneness, does righteous work covering both the obligatory and the voluntary, and then ihtada — which the commentary glosses as continuing in what was mentioned until death. Al-Muyassar reads the last stage the same way: reaching the truth and staying straight on it.

The intensive name is matched to a condition that itself extends over a lifetime. Repeated covering for a repeated returning.

The other verse in this group is Nuh's report of his own preaching, nine hundred and fifty years of it.

فَقُلْتُ ٱسْتَغْفِرُوا۟ رَبَّكُمْ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ غَفَّارًا

"So I said: ask forgiveness of your Lord; indeed, He has always been Ghaffar."

Nuh (71:10)

The verb kana puts the quality in the past and leaves it running — He has been, and continues to be. And the command Nuh reports giving is istaghfiru, a plural imperative to a people who had refused him for centuries. The name that means forgives-repeatedly is offered to the audience that had needed the offer repeated most.

What Nuh promises next

The verse in Surah Nuh sits inside an argument, and the two lines that follow it are the payoff.

يُرْسِلِ ٱلسَّمَآءَ عَلَيْكُم مِّدْرَارًا

"He will send the sky upon you in abundant showers."

Nuh (71:11)

وَيُمْدِدْكُم بِأَمْوَٰلٍ وَبَنِينَ وَيَجْعَل لَّكُمْ جَنَّـٰتٍ وَيَجْعَل لَّكُمْ أَنْهَـٰرًا

"And He will supply you with wealth and children, and make for you gardens, and make for you rivers."

Nuh (71:12)

The verbs after the command sit in the jussive — the form Arabic uses for the consequence of a condition. Al-Muyassar reads it exactly that way: if you repent and seek forgiveness, God will send down rain in abundance, increase your wealth and children, and give you gardens and rivers. What is promised is entirely material. A prophet arguing with a people who had refused him for centuries makes his case for seeking forgiveness by describing crops.

Al-Jalalayn adds the detail that gives the promise its edge: wa kanu qad muni'uhu — the rain had been withheld from them. The drought was already on when Nuh spoke. He was pointing at an empty sky and naming the reason.

The name sits at the hinge of that argument. Nuh names God Ghaffar — the one who covers repeatedly — and the repetition carries weight, because the people he is addressing would need to come back many times before the sky ever opened.

The pairing that shows up three times out of five

The remaining three occurrences share a feature that the common name almost entirely lacks. In each, Al-Ghaffar is preceded immediately by Al-Aziz (ٱلْعَزِيز), the Mighty.

رَبُّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفَّـٰرُ

"Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatever is between them, the Mighty, the Ghaffar."

Sad (38:66)

خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ بِٱلْحَقِّ ۖ يُكَوِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ عَلَى ٱلنَّهَارِ وَيُكَوِّرُ ٱلنَّهَارَ عَلَى ٱلَّيْلِ ۖ وَسَخَّرَ ٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ ۖ كُلٌّ يَجْرِى لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى ۗ أَلَا هُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفَّـٰرُ

"He created the heavens and the earth in truth. He wraps the night over the day and wraps the day over the night, and He has subjected the sun and the moon, each running for an appointed term. Unquestionably, He is the Mighty, the Ghaffar."

Az-Zumar (39:5)

تَدْعُونَنِى لِأَكْفُرَ بِٱللَّهِ وَأُشْرِكَ بِهِۦ مَا لَيْسَ لِى بِهِۦ عِلْمٌ وَأَنَا۠ أَدْعُوكُمْ إِلَى ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْغَفَّـٰرِ

"You invite me to disbelieve in Allah and associate with Him that of which I have no knowledge, while I invite you to the Mighty, the Ghaffar."

Ghafir (40:42)

Al-Jalalayn's gloss on the pair at 39:5 sets the two names against each other deliberately: al-Aziz is the one who prevails in His affair and takes retribution from His enemies, and al-Ghaffar is forgiving li-awliya'ihi, to those close to Him. Two audiences named in one breath. Al-Muyassar renders the second half as forgiver of the sins of His servants who repent.

The same split appears at the other two. Al-Jalalayn gives the identical gloss at 38:66. Al-Muyassar reads 40:42 as an invitation to the One "mighty in His retribution, forgiving to whoever repents to Him after disobeying." Three verses, three commentaries, one division: the power faces one direction and the pardon faces the other, and the sentence holds both without softening either.

The context in each case makes the pairing land. At 39:5 the two names close a passage about the creation of the heavens and the earth, the night wound onto the day, the sun and moon each running to an appointed term. At 40:42 the words belong to the believing man of Pharaoh's household, arguing with a court that wants him to disbelieve — and he sets the name of overwhelming power beside the name of repeated pardon in the same invitation.

The common name behaves differently here. Al-Ghafur, across all ninety-one of its occurrences, follows Al-Aziz only once, at Fatir 35:28, and there both words are indefinite: 'azizun ghafur. The definite pairing al-Aziz al-Ghaffar belongs to the rare name.

What the common name does instead

Ninety-one occurrences give Al-Ghafur a completely different job. It appears at the ends of verses across the whole Quran, and overwhelmingly it appears attached to a second name. Sixty-four of the ninety-one are followed directly by Ar-Rahim. A handful take Al-Halim or Ash-Shakur instead.

It closes rulings. It closes warnings. It closes concessions granted to people under hardship. It arrives after the law has been stated, as the clause that tells the reader what kind of God stands behind the ruling they have just been given. The Quran builds another name on the same idea of returning made mutual.

The distribution tells its own story. A name that has to appear at the close of ninety-one different passages, in surahs of every period and on subjects ranging from marriage to war to food, needs to be the flexible one. The settled, characteristic pattern does that work. The intensive pattern is held back for the five places where repetition is the point being made.

Two names, one covering

English translations blur the two. Al-Ghafur usually comes out as "the Forgiving" or "the Oft-Forgiving." Al-Ghaffar gets "the Ever-Forgiving," "the Repeatedly-Forgiving," sometimes the same phrase as the other name. The distinction that Arabic builds into the shape of the word resists an easy English equivalent, so it tends to disappear.

What survives in the Arabic is a division of labour. One name says that covering faults is what God is like, and says it ninety-one times, at the end of almost every kind of passage the Quran contains. The other says that the covering happens again and again, and says it five times: twice to people whose returning was going to take a lifetime or a millennium, and three times standing directly beside the name for overwhelming power.

Both come from a root that means to lay something over a thing so it stops being exposed.
