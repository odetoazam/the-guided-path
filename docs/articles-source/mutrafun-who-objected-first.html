The Quran Says Who Spoke First Against Every Prophet

Read enough of the Quran and the shape becomes familiar: a messenger arrives, a town refuses him, the refusal hardens, something breaks. What is easy to read past is that the Quran twice stops the narration to state a rule about the refusal — and the rule identifies the objectors by a single social fact: their money.

وَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَا فِى قَرْيَةٍ مِّن نَّذِيرٍ إِلَّا قَالَ مُتْرَفُوهَآ إِنَّا بِمَآ أُرْسِلْتُم بِهِۦ كَـٰفِرُونَ

"And We never sent a warner into a town except that its mutrafūn said: we reject what you have been sent with."

Saba (34:34)

That is a rule about all of them — every town, every warner — and it puts the objection in one mouth. Nine surahs later the same sentence returns with the excuse swapped out:

وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَآ أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ فِى قَرْيَةٍ مِّن نَّذِيرٍ إِلَّا قَالَ مُتْرَفُوهَآ إِنَّا وَجَدْنَآ ءَابَآءَنَا عَلَىٰٓ أُمَّةٍ وَإِنَّا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاثَـٰرِهِم مُّقْتَدُونَ

"And in the same way, We sent no warner into a town before you except that its mutrafūn said: we found our fathers on a way, and we are following in their footsteps."

Az-Zukhruf (43:23)

The frame holds and the content moves. One group rejects the message flatly; the other appeals to inherited custom. Across both, the speaker stays the same.

The word is a passive

مُتْرَف comes from the root ت-ر-ف, which the lexicons attach to soft living — the kind of ease that takes the edge off a person. The Quranic morphological corpus tags every occurrence of the word as a passive participle. So the sense is closer to one who has been made soft than to one who is luxurious. English reaches for "the affluent," "the wealthy," "those given ease," and each of those quietly turns something done to a person into something a person owns.

Someone did it to them, and the Quran says who — inside the very sentence reporting their objection:

وَقَالَ ٱلْمَلَأُ مِن قَوْمِهِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَكَذَّبُوا۟ بِلِقَآءِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ وَأَتْرَفْنَـٰهُمْ فِى ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا مَا هَـٰذَآ إِلَّا بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُكُمْ يَأْكُلُ مِمَّا تَأْكُلُونَ مِنْهُ وَيَشْرَبُ مِمَّا تَشْرَبُونَ

"And the chiefs of his people who disbelieved and denied the meeting of the next life — and whom We had made soft with ease in the life of this world — said: this is only a human being like you, eating from what you eat and drinking from what you drink."

Al-Mu'minun (23:33)

The clause wa-atrafnāhum fi'l-ḥayāti'd-dunyā is God's aside, dropped into the middle of the objectors' résumé. Their comfort was issued. It came from the same hand that sent the messenger they are dismissing, and it is working as the test they are failing while they speak.

Five appearances, one direction

The word occurs five times, and it lands the same way every time. The heaviest of the five sits in Al-Isra:

وَإِذَآ أَرَدْنَآ أَن نُّهْلِكَ قَرْيَةً أَمَرْنَا مُتْرَفِيهَا فَفَسَقُوا۟ فِيهَا فَحَقَّ عَلَيْهَا ٱلْقَوْلُ فَدَمَّرْنَـٰهَا تَدْمِيرًا

"And when We intend to destroy a town, We command its mutrafīn, and they transgress in it; so the word comes due against it, and We destroy it utterly."

Al-Isra (17:16)

Commentators have long disagreed over that verb. Ibn Kathir opens his comment by saying the exegetes differed over its meaning; al-Tabari records that the reciters themselves read it differently. The reading most of them settle on is plain enough: the command was to obedience, and transgression was the answer to it. Al-Muyassar adds the clause that makes the mechanism visible — the town's comfortable are commanded, and the rest follow them. A town's direction gets set at the top.

Which is why the classical glosses on this word keep sliding from wealth into power. Al-Jalalayn explains mutrafīhā as munaʿʿamīhā — its pampered ones — and then supplies the equivalence: bi-maʿnā ruʾasāʾihā, meaning its chiefs. Al-Tabari, at 34:34, reads mutrafūhā as "its great ones and its chiefs." Money and command were describing one set of people, and the commentators saw no need to separate them.

The remaining two occurrences finish the arc. In Al-Mu'minun, the comfortable are the ones the punishment finds first:

حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَخَذْنَا مُتْرَفِيهِم بِٱلْعَذَابِ إِذَا هُمْ يَجْـَٔرُونَ

"Until, when We seize their mutrafīn with the punishment, at that moment they cry out."

Al-Mu'minun (23:64)

And in Al-Waqiah, describing the companions of the left hand, the first clause of the verdict is economic:

إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ قَبْلَ ذَٰلِكَ مُتْرَفِينَ

"They were, before that, mutrafīn."

Al-Waqiah (56:45)

The argument the comfortable make

Right after stating the rule, Saba lets the objectors keep talking, and what comes out is a piece of theology:

وَقَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أَكْثَرُ أَمْوَٰلًا وَأَوْلَـٰدًا وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُعَذَّبِينَ

"And they said: we are greater in wealth and in children, and we will not be punished."

Saba (34:35)

Al-Tabari spells out the reasoning behind that sentence: had God been displeased with the way we live, He would have withheld the wealth and the children and narrowed our provision. Al-Muyassar compresses it to a line — God gave us these blessings only because He is pleased with us. Comfort gets read backwards, as a verdict already delivered in their favour.

The reply comes two verses later, and it declines to dispute the premise. Their wealth is real. Its meaning is the thing under correction:

وَمَآ أَمْوَٰلُكُمْ وَلَآ أَوْلَـٰدُكُم بِٱلَّتِى تُقَرِّبُكُمْ عِندَنَا زُلْفَىٰٓ إِلَّا مَنْ ءَامَنَ وَعَمِلَ صَـٰلِحًا

"Your wealth and your children are not what brings you near to Us in rank — only whoever believes and acts well…"

Saba (34:37)

A status nobody applies for

The passive is doing quiet work across all five verses. A mutraf is a person things have been going well for. That is the whole entry requirement. There is no boast in the word, no membership card, no threshold of wealth the Quran specifies — only the fact of a life that has been made easy, by a Giver the verses keep naming.

Which means the category is less exotic than it sounds. Anyone whose circumstances have softened has been quietly written into these sentences, and the five occurrences pose one question to all of them: what does a person do with a warning once life stops hurting? The chiefs in 23:33 had an answer ready. They looked at the man in front of them, saw that he ate what they ate, and used it against him — the ordinariness of the messenger became the reason to dismiss the message. God had already put the reason for their blindness in the middle of the sentence, one clause before they opened their mouths.
