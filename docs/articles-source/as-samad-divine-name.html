As-Samad: The Divine Name That Appears Only Once in the Quran

Most of the names of God arrive in the Quran many times over. Ar-Rahman (ٱلرَّحْمَـٰن) appears in dozens of places. Al-Ghafur (ٱلْغَفُور) turns up more than ninety times. A reader who wants to know what one of these names means can gather the verses, lay them side by side, and let the Quran define its own vocabulary. As-Samad (ٱلصَّمَد) works differently. It occurs one time, in a surah of four short lines, and then the Quran closes the subject. The earliest scholars of Islam had to settle its meaning from that single line — and the record of what they concluded is one of the most instructive disagreements in classical tafsir.

The one verse

Surah Al-Ikhlas was revealed as an answer. Several early reports, gathered by al-Tabari in his commentary, describe the same scene from different angles: people came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked him to give them the lineage of his Lord — to say what God is made of, and who made Him. The surah is the reply.

قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

"Say: He is Allah, One."

Al-Ikhlas (112:1)

ٱللَّهُ ٱلصَّمَدُ

"Allah, the Samad."

Al-Ikhlas (112:2)

Two words. The divine name, then the description. English translations reach for a phrase rather than a word — "the Eternal Refuge," "the Self-Sufficient Master," "the Absolute," "the Uncaused." The variety in English is a faithful reflection of something real: the variety was there in Arabic first.

The root and what it did before the Quran

The root is ṣ-m-d (ص م د). In ordinary Arabic speech the verb ṣamada ilayhi describes a person heading straight for someone — going to them deliberately, usually because they need something and that person is the one who can supply it. You aim yourself at them. The word carries direction and dependence at the same time.

The same three letters also gave Arabic an adjective for a thing that is solid the whole way through, packed all the way to its surface. A rock is ṣamad in that sense — something you would strike and find dense the whole way down.

Both senses were live in the language when the Quran arrived, and both would shape the readings that followed.

Where the early scholars landed

Al-Tabari opens his treatment of this word with a sentence he uses when the tradition splits: wa-khtalafa ahl al-ta'wil fi ma'na al-Samad — the people of interpretation differed over the meaning of As-Samad. He then lists the positions, each with its chain of transmission back to a named early authority.

Solid, with no hollow inside

This is the reading recorded from Mujahid, from Sa'id ibn Jubayr, from al-Hasan al-Basri, from al-Dahhak, from Ikrimah, and from Ibn Abbas through one chain. Al-Tabari cites several routes for it, which tells you how widely it circulated. God is solid throughout — the reading rules out any interior in which something else might be housed.

The One who takes no food or drink

Reported from al-Sha'bi. A narrower version of the same instinct: whatever requires filling belongs to creation.

The One from whom nothing issues

Reported from Ikrimah. Read this way the second verse is already the third — lam yalid wa lam yulad, He did not father and was not fathered — arriving one line early.

The One who did not father and was not fathered

Reported from Abu al-'Aliyah and from Muhammad ibn Ka'b, with the reasoning attached: anything born will die, and anything that dies leaves an inheritance behind it, while God remains beyond both death and inheritance.

The master whose mastery has reached its end point

Reported from Abu Wa'il, through al-A'mash. Here the word is about rank rather than substance — the summit of a hierarchy, with everything else somewhere below.

The One perfect in every kind of eminence

This is Ibn Abbas again, through a different chain, and it expands into a list: perfect in sovereignty, perfect in honour, perfect in magnitude, perfect in forbearance, perfect in wealth, perfect in knowledge, perfect in wisdom. The reading turns the single word into a summary of every other name.

The One who remains after His creation is gone

Reported from al-Hasan and from Qatadah, who also glossed it simply as ad-da'im, the permanent.

Seven positions, from the first two generations, on a word of five letters. The tradition preserved the disagreement rather than smoothing it over.

How al-Tabari settled it

After laying out the reports, al-Tabari gives his own conclusion, and the way he reaches it is worth watching. He writes that as-Samad, in the speech of the Arabs, is the chief who is turned to — alladhi yuṣmadu ilayhi — the one above whom there is no one. Then he does what a lexicographer does: he produces evidence from poetry that predates the Quran. He quotes an elegy for two men of Banu Asad that calls one of them as-sayyid as-samad, and a line from al-Zibriqan using the same pairing.

His stated principle is that the reading with the best claim is "the meaning known from the speech of the people in whose tongue the Quran was revealed." The word was already in use. Its everyday sense is the sense a first listener would have heard.

Then he adds a qualification that says a great deal about classical method. He notes that one of the reports he cited traces back, possibly, to the Prophet ﷺ himself through Ibn Buraydah from his father — and he says that if that chain were sound, it would outrank everything else he has argued, because the Messenger ﷺ knows best what God meant by His own words. Al-Tabari builds a linguistic case, publishes it, and then tells the reader exactly what would have overturned it.

What Ibn Kathir carries forward

Ibn Kathir, writing centuries later, preserves the same spread and adds the report that has become the most widely quoted gloss in English. Through Ikrimah, Ibn Abbas defines as-Samad as the One whom all of creation depends on for their needs and their requests. He also records Abu Wa'il's version — the master whose control is complete — and the report from At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Jarir that ties the word directly to the verse that follows it.

Ibn Kathir also cites a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari in which God says of Himself, in the course of correcting those who claim He has a son: "I am al-Ahad, as-Samad. I did not father and I was not fathered, and there has never been anyone comparable to Me." The two names of the surah appear together, in the first person, inside the hadith.

The two verses that follow

The surah does something that helps the reader without settling the argument. Having named God as-Samad, it immediately says two more things about Him, and both of them touch the same territory the commentators were arguing over.

لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ

"He did not father, and He was not fathered."

Al-Ikhlas (112:3)

وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌۢ

"And there has never been anyone equal to Him."

Al-Ikhlas (112:4)

The third verse pairs an active verb with its passive twin from the same root, w-l-d (و ل د) — fathering and being fathered, both ruled out with the same particle. Al-Tabari reads the pair as a statement about time: the first clause places God outside decay, since whatever produces offspring is itself perishable, and the second places Him outside origination, since whatever is born came into existence after having been absent from it. He describes God instead as qadim lam yazal, ancient without beginning, and da'im, enduring.

The fourth verse closes with kufuwan, from a root meaning equivalence. Al-Tabari notes that the commentators divided here too: Abu al-'Aliyah and Ibn Abbas read it as likeness in general, while Mujahid read it specifically as a spouse. Al-Tabari records that the word kufu' and its variants all mean the same thing in Arabic speech — likeness, a match — and quotes a line of al-Nabighah to show it. He also notes two accepted recitations of the word and rules both of them correct.

Placed after these two verses, the readings of as-Samad that had seemed most physical start to look less like descriptions of substance and more like the surah's own argument, stated one line early.

Two directions, one word

Set the readings side by side and they sort into two families that point opposite ways.

One family looks inward. Solid the whole way through, self-contained, complete in itself. Al-Sha'bi's version and Sa'id ibn al-Musayyab's version belong here, and so does Qatadah's ad-da'im. These readings describe God in terms of His own sufficiency.

The other family looks outward. The one who is headed for, resorted to, depended on — the address every need eventually arrives at. Ibn Abbas's gloss through Ikrimah belongs here, and so does Abu Wa'il's, and so does al-Tabari's own. These readings describe God in terms of everything else's relationship to Him.

The two families rest on each other. A being with a hollow in it would carry needs of its own, and something carrying needs of its own makes a poor final destination for anyone else's. The solidity is what makes the dependence safe. Read the whole surah in order and the structure holds: verse one establishes that He is one of a kind, verse two that He is the one turned to, verses three and four that He stands outside the chain of birth and death entirely — nothing behind Him, nothing ahead of Him, nothing beside Him.

Which is why the surah works as an answer to the question that prompted it. The questioners wanted a lineage. A lineage places something in a series: this came from that, that will produce this. The reply names God as the point where the series stops.

What the single occurrence does

A word that appears many times gets defined by its contexts. It accumulates. A word that appears once has only the space around it, and here the space is three other verses, all of them about the same thing.

The surah has a name — Al-Ikhlas, from a root meaning to make something pure by taking everything foreign out of it. Al-Hasan and Qatadah, according to al-Tabari, described it as a surah with nothing in it about this world or the next, only about God. As-Samad is the one word in it that the tradition had to argue about, and the argument produced a set of readings that, taken together, cover more ground than any single translation manages.

Someone who reads the surah in prayer every day is saying a word that seven early authorities defined seven ways, and each of them was reaching for the same thing from a different side.
