As-Sami and Al-Basir: The Divine Names for Hearing and Seeing, and the Woman Who Proved One of Them

As-Sami (ٱلسَّمِيع) and Al-Basir (ٱلْبَصِير) are the Quran's names for God as the One who hears and the One who sees. Each appears around fifty times; seven times they stand directly beside each other. One of those seven opens a surah, and the reason it opens that surah is a woman who came to the Prophet ﷺ with a complaint about her husband, argued with him about the answer, and was told — in the first three words of the chapter — that God had heard her.

The two roots

S-m-ʿ (س م ع) is hearing. B-ṣ-r (ب ص ر) is seeing, and it reaches further than the eye: baṣīrah is insight, the perception that grasps a situation rather than merely registering light. Both names sit on the faʿīl pattern, which denotes a settled and constant attribute rather than an occasional act.

Between them the two cover the whole field of what can be perceived. Anything a person does is either something said or something done, and the pair takes one each.

The seven places they stand together

Adjacent as a pair, the two words appear at 4:58, 4:134, 22:61, 22:75, 31:28, 58:1 and 76:2. Six of the seven name God. The seventh does something else, and it is worth holding until the end.

۞ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَأْمُرُكُمْ أَن تُؤَدُّوا۟ ٱلْأَمَـٰنَـٰتِ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهَا وَإِذَا حَكَمْتُم بَيْنَ ٱلنَّاسِ أَن تَحْكُمُوا۟ بِٱلْعَدْلِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ نِعِمَّا يَعِظُكُم بِهِۦٓ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ سَمِيعًۢا بَصِيرًا

"Indeed Allah commands you to render trusts to their owners, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice. Excellent is that which Allah instructs you. Indeed Allah is ever Hearing, Seeing."

An-Nisa (4:58)

The pair closes a verse about returning what was entrusted to you and judging fairly. Both are things done where enforcement is weak — a trust has no witness by definition, and a judge is the last authority in the room. The two names arrive as the answer to that gap.

The surah that opens with hearing

Surah Al-Mujadila takes its name from a woman. Al-Muyassar states it plainly in its note on the surah: it is called Al-Mujadila because it opens with the account of the woman who disputed with the Prophet ﷺ about her husband's ẓihār against her.

Ẓihār was a pre-Islamic formula by which a man declared his wife unlawful to him by comparing her to his mother's back. Al-Jalalayn preserves what the husband had said to her and notes that she asked the Prophet ﷺ about it, and that he answered according to what was then understood — that she had become forbidden to him. She kept arguing.

قَدْ سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ قَوْلَ ٱلَّتِى تُجَـٰدِلُكَ فِى زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَٱللَّهُ يَسْمَعُ تَحَاوُرَكُمَآ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَمِيعٌۢ بَصِيرٌ

"Allah has heard the words of she who disputes with you concerning her husband and complains to Allah. And Allah hears your dialogue. Indeed Allah is Hearing, Seeing."

Al-Mujadila (58:1)

The verb comes first and it is in the perfect tense: qad samiʿa'llāh — Allah has heard. Before the ruling, before the name, the surah reports a completed act of listening.

Then the verse names two hearings, not one. It has heard her words, and it hears your dialogue — taḥāwurakumā, a dual form covering her and the Prophet ﷺ together. The exchange itself, argument included, is on the record.

Only after all of that does the name arrive. Inna'llāha samīʿun baṣīr. The attribute is stated last, as the conclusion drawn from the incident rather than the premise laid over it.

The verses that follow overturn the practice. Al-Muyassar summarises the surah's purposes: the ruling of Islam abolishing ẓihār, its expiation, and that this was among the matters God rejected, overturning what the people of ignorance had held.

What the placement does

A surah could have introduced the ruling on its own. Instead the chapter is named after the woman, opens with the fact that she was heard, and reaches the legal content afterwards.

The sequence puts the complaint before the verdict. She had no standing in the arrangement she was arguing against — the formula was her husband's to pronounce, and the answer she first received went against her. The verse records that the argument reached higher than the room it was made in.

Why the pair rather than one name

Hearing alone would leave a gap, and the Quran tends to close it by attaching the second name. Words can be arranged; conduct is harder to stage. Naming both attributes together covers the account a person gives and the life behind it.

The pattern holds across the other divine pairings. Surah Al-Hajj uses them after describing night folded into day, and again after the statement that God chooses messengers from among the angels and from among people. Surah Luqman uses them after the claim that creating and resurrecting all of humanity is as one soul. Surah An-Nisa uses them twice — once after the command about trusts, once after a verse about people who want only the reward of this world.

The seventh, which is not a divine name

One of the seven belongs to a human being.

إِنَّا خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ أَمْشَاجٍ نَّبْتَلِيهِ فَجَعَلْنَـٰهُ سَمِيعًۢا بَصِيرًا

"Indeed We created man from a fluid-drop, a mixture, that We may test him — and We made him hearing, seeing."

Al-Insan (76:2)

The verb is jaʿalnāhu, We made him. The same two words that close six verses as names of God close this one as a description of the creature, and the sentence says where they came from: they were made, and made for a stated purpose — nabtalīhi, that We may test him.

Read against the other six, the verse sets out the whole relationship in one line. The attributes are His without origin and constant; they are the human being's by grant, issued at the start of a test. The faculties a person is judged by are on loan from the One doing the judging.

Reading the names

English gives As-Sami as "the All-Hearing" and Al-Basir as "the All-Seeing." Both are accurate, and both are easy to read as passive — a recording device left running.

The verse at the head of Al-Mujadila argues otherwise. Hearing there is followed by legislation. A woman spoke, the words were heard, the practice was abolished, and the chapter carries her description in its title permanently.

The last clause of the verse is the name. Everything before it is the evidence.
