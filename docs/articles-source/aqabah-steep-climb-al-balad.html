The Quran Names a Steep Climb, Then Tells You What Is on It

Near its opening, Surah Al-Balad quotes a man boasting about how much he has spent. Within five short verses the surah has taken the boast apart, and by verse eleven it has replaced it with an image — a steep pass he failed to get up. Then, unusually, the surah stops and defines the pass. The definition runs four lines, and every item on it involves money leaving a hand.

The boast

يَقُولُ أَهْلَكْتُ مَالًا لُّبَدًا

"He says: I have burned through piles of wealth."

Al-Balad (90:6)

The verb he chooses for his own spending is ahlaktu — I destroyed, I wasted. It is a boast in the shape of a complaint, the kind of sentence a person makes when the size of the sum is the point. The verse on either side of it asks the same question twice, and both times the question is about being observed: does he think nobody has power over him (90:5), does he think nobody saw him (90:7).

What follows is an inventory of what he was issued:

أَلَمْ نَجْعَل لَّهُۥ عَيْنَيْنِ

وَلِسَانًا وَشَفَتَيْنِ

وَهَدَيْنَـٰهُ ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ

"Did We not make for him two eyes, and a tongue and two lips, and guide him to the two highlands?"

Al-Balad (90:8–10)

An-najdayn — "the two highlands" — is glossed by al-Muyassar as the two paths of good and evil, laid out for him to see. The list is eyes, mouth, and a fork in the road: equipment for seeing, equipment for speaking, and a choice.

The climb he skipped

فَلَا ٱقْتَحَمَ ٱلْعَقَبَةَ

"Yet he made no attempt at the steep pass."

Al-Balad (90:11)

The verb is ٱقْتَحَمَ, from the root ق-ح-م, which the Quran uses exactly twice — both times in this same verbal form. The other occurrence is in Surah Sad, where a crowd arrives in the fire:

هَـٰذَا فَوْجٌ مُّقْتَحِمٌ مَّعَكُمْ لَا مَرْحَبًۢا بِهِمْ إِنَّهُمْ صَالُوا۟ ٱلنَّارِ

"This is a crowd plunging in along with you — no welcome for them; they are entering the fire."

Sad (38:59)

One word, two directions. A crowd throws itself downward into the fire without hesitating; a man declines to throw himself upward at the pass. Al-Jalalayn glosses the verb in Al-Balad plainly as jāwazahā, he crossed over it, and al-Muyassar spells out what crossing meant: getting past the hardship of the next life by spending his wealth.

The definition

The surah then goes further than the image required. It asks a question about its own image:

وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْعَقَبَةُ

"And what will make you know what the steep pass is?"

Al-Balad (90:12)

Whenever the Quran asks with this particular verb, the answer arrives in the next line. Al-Jalalayn reads the question as an aside inserted to magnify the thing being named, followed immediately by the reason it can be crossed. Here is the answer:

فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ

أَوْ إِطْعَـٰمٌ فِى يَوْمٍ ذِى مَسْغَبَةٍ

يَتِيمًا ذَا مَقْرَبَةٍ

أَوْ مِسْكِينًا ذَا مَتْرَبَةٍ

"The freeing of a neck. Or feeding, on a day of hunger, an orphan who is a relative, or a poor man in the dust."

Al-Balad (90:13–16)

Freeing a slave. Feeding someone on a day when food is short. Two names for who gets fed. The uphill climb of a human life, offered as a definition, comes out as a short list of transfers.

Words the Quran uses once

The passage is built out of vocabulary that appears nowhere else. Masghabah — hunger — is the only appearance of its root in the entire Quran. Matrabah, from the root for dust and soil, occurs once, and it describes the poor man by the ground he is sitting on. An-najdayn occurs once. When the surah ends with people who counselled each other to mercy, the word for that mercy, al-marḥamah, is also used once and never again.

Part of this is rhyme — six consecutive lines of the passage land on the same -abah ending, and the sound pulls rare words toward it. What the rhyme cannot explain is the direction they all point. The Quran assembled a fresh set of words to describe the hardest climb a person can make, and used them to name hunger, an orphan, and someone sitting in the dirt.

Three expiations, one climb

The singular raqabah — one neck, freed by one act — appears in four verses of the Quran. (The plural riqāb belongs to the charity lists of 2:177 and 9:60.) In three of the four, the freeing functions as an expiation, owed for something that went wrong: killing a believer by mistake (4:92), breaking a deliberate oath (5:89), and the pre-Islamic divorce formula of ẓihār (58:3). Each of those attaches the freeing to a fault, as the way out of it.

Al-Balad is the fourth, and it carries no offence behind it. The slate is clean, and the freeing stands on its own as the first item of the climb — the thing a person does when the only pressure on them is the hill itself.

What the surah does with faith

Only after the four items does belief enter the passage:

ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِٱلصَّبْرِ وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِٱلْمَرْحَمَةِ

"Then he was among those who believed, and counselled one another to patience, and counselled one another to mercy."

Al-Balad (90:17)

Al-Jalalayn heads off the obvious misreading: the thumma here orders the telling rather than the timeline, and the sense is that he was among the believers at the moment of the climb. Faith and the climb are the same event, seen from two sides. What the surah chose to put on the page first, though, was the neck, the meal, the orphan, and the man in the dust.

Al-Balad, in the end, comes back for the boast. A man had said he burned through a fortune and the surah asked whether he thought nobody had seen. Then it listed the eyes and the mouth he was given, named a hill he never attempted, and defined the hill as noticing one specific hungry person and doing something about it. He had the eyes for it the whole time.
