The Quran Declares War on Exactly One Thing

The Quran forbids a great deal, and it has a range of ways to say so. Some prohibitions come with a warning, some with a curse, some with a description of the fire. One prohibition, and only one, is followed by a notice of war — with God and His Messenger named as the party going to war. It concerns money.

فَإِن لَّمْ تَفْعَلُوا۟ فَأْذَنُوا۟ بِحَرْبٍ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَإِن تُبْتُمْ فَلَكُمْ رُءُوسُ أَمْوَٰلِكُمْ لَا تَظْلِمُونَ وَلَا تُظْلَمُونَ

"And if you will not do it, then take notice of a war from God and His Messenger. But if you turn back, you may have your capital — wronging no one, and wronged by no one."

Al-Baqarah (2:279)

Which direction the word usually runs

The root ح-ر-ب occurs eleven times in the Quran. Five of those are the noun miḥrāb, the prayer chamber — one of them in its plural. The verb ḥāraba — to wage war on — occurs twice, and both times the sentence runs from human beings toward God: those who wage war against God and His Messenger (5:33), and a mosque built as a lookout for whoever had warred against God and His Messenger before (9:107).

That leaves the plain noun حَرْب, war, which appears four times. Three of them describe ordinary human fighting. Surah Muhammad speaks of holding prisoners "until war lays down its burdens" (47:4). Al-Anfal speaks of encountering an enemy "in war" (8:57). Al-Ma'idah describes a fire kindled for war and put out (5:64).

The fourth is 2:279, and it is the one place in the Quran where war is announced with God and His Messenger as its source, aimed at people defined by what they are doing with their money.

The verb that delivers the notice

Everything turns on فَأْذَنُوا, from the root أ-ذ-ن — the root of hearing, of permission, of the call to prayer. Al-Tabari records that the reciters read the word two different ways. Most of the Madinan readers took it as faʾdhanū: be aware, take it as known. The Kufan reading is fa-ādhinū: then notify others — announce it. Al-Jalalayn glosses it simply as aʿlamū, know, and adds that the verse carries a severe threat. Al-Muyassar renders it as certainty: be sure of a war from God and His Messenger.

Under either reading, the grammar is doing something unusual. The verse addresses the offending party and instructs them to register the war — the way one is served notice. The sentence hands them the information and steps back.

The last four words

Having issued the notice, the verse offers the exit in the same breath: if you turn back, you may have your capital. The principal is yours. The dispute is only over the increase.

Then the closing clause, four words that are the same verb twice:

lā taẓlimūna wa-lā tuẓlamūn — the first in the active, the second in the passive. You commit no wrong, and no wrong is committed against you. Al-Jalalayn supplies the referents: no wrong by taking an increase, and no wrong by receiving back less than you lent. The lender is protected in the same clause that restrains him. The verse's idea of a settled transaction is one where neither party's hand is in the other's pocket.

And the sentence that follows the war notice is about mercy toward the debtor:

وَإِن كَانَ ذُو عُسْرَةٍ فَنَظِرَةٌ إِلَىٰ مَيْسَرَةٍ وَأَن تَصَدَّقُوا۟ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

"And if the debtor is in hardship, then let there be a postponement until ease comes. And that you remit it as charity is better for you, if only you knew."

Al-Baqarah (2:280)

Al-Jalalayn attaches a hadith recorded by Muslim to this verse: whoever grants a delay to one in hardship, or cancels the debt for him, God will shade him in His shade on the day when there is no shade but His. The passage moves from a declaration of war to an offer of shade in about a line and a half, and both are addressed to the creditor.

A root that means growth

The Arabic word under discussion is ٱلرِّبَا, from the root ر-ب-و — to increase, to swell, to grow. Two verses before the war notice, the Quran uses that same root against itself:

يَمْحَقُ ٱللَّهُ ٱلرِّبَوٰا۟ وَيُرْبِى ٱلصَّدَقَـٰتِ

"God erases ribā, and grows the charities…"

Al-Baqarah (2:276)

Yurbī, "He grows," is built from the very root that gives ribā its name. The thing that calls itself growth gets erased; growth is applied instead to the money given away. A Meccan verse works the same root even harder:

وَمَآ ءَاتَيْتُم مِّن رِّبًا لِّيَرْبُوَا۟ فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِ ٱلنَّاسِ فَلَا يَرْبُوا۟ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ

"Whatever you give in ribā so that it may grow through other people's wealth — it does not grow with God…"

Ar-Rum (30:39)

Three appearances of one root in half a verse: the practice, the intention behind it, and the verdict on whether it works. The increase is visible on the ledger and absent from the account that the verse says matters.

What this piece is arguing, and what it is leaving alone

Where the boundaries of ribā fall in a modern economy — which contracts, which instruments, which forms of finance — is a live question that jurists have worked on for a long time and continue to disagree about. That question belongs to qualified scholars and to the schools of law, and this article makes no ruling on it.

The observation here is narrower and sits entirely in the text. Of every practice the Quran restricts, one arrives with a notice of war from God and His Messenger. It is a financial practice. The escalation is unique, the exit is offered in the same verse, and the sentence immediately after it asks the creditor to be gentle with a debtor who is struggling. Whatever a reader concludes about the scope of the term, the placement of that war notice is a fact about the Quran, and it is worth sitting with before opening any argument about the details.
