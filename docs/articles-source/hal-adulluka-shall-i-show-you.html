"Shall I Show You…": The One Sentence the Deceiver and the Guide Both Use

There is a sentence in the Quran that means let me point you toward what you are looking for. It is the grammar of a helping hand — someone who knows the way offering it to someone who does not. The striking thing is who speaks it. The same offer of guidance is made by Shaytan, by a frightened child inside a tyrant's palace, and by God Himself. One sentence, three fingers pointing — and they do not all point the same way.

The phrase is hal adulluka / adullukum ʿalā — "shall I show you the way to…" The verb is dalla: to indicate, to point out a road, the root behind dalīl, a guide. It is guidance offered as an invitation, and the Quran puts it in the mouths of the two forces that spend the whole book competing for exactly that — the one who calls toward God, and the one who calls away from Him, both using the identical opening words.

The pointing that ruins

The first time a being turns to a human and says shall I show you, it is the fall of Adam. The whisper does not arrive as a threat or a command. It arrives as help:

فَوَسْوَسَ إِلَيْهِ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ قَالَ يَـٰٓـَٔادَمُ هَلْ أَدُلُّكَ عَلَىٰ شَجَرَةِ ٱلْخُلْدِ وَمُلْكٍ لَّا يَبْلَىٰ

"Then Shaytan whispered to him. He said, 'O Adam, shall I show you the tree of eternity, and a kingdom that never decays?'"

Ta-Ha (20:120)

The verse opens with waswasa — the whisper — and then quotes the whisper itself, and the whisper is a sentence of guidance. Shaytan offers to point Adam toward something: a tree that ends death, a kingship that never wears out. He is not, in that moment, arguing against God. He is playing the guide — the one who knows a shorter road to what the heart wants. Everything false about the offer is downstream of a form that is indistinguishable from true help. The counterfeit of guidance is not an attack on guidance. It is guidance's own opening line, borrowed.

The pointing that rescues, from inside the palace

Now the same words, spoken by a child. Baby Musa has been drawn from the river into Pharaoh's own household, and he refuses every wet-nurse they bring. His sister has been following at a distance. She steps forward with a suggestion for the palace:

وَحَرَّمْنَا عَلَيْهِ ٱلْمَرَاضِعَ مِن قَبْلُ فَقَالَتْ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰٓ أَهْلِ بَيْتٍ يَكْفُلُونَهُۥ لَكُمْ وَهُمْ لَهُۥ نَـٰصِحُونَ

"And We had already forbidden for him wet-nurses, so she said, 'Shall I show you a household who will raise him for you, and who will be sincere to him?'"

Al-Qasas (28:12)

To Pharaoh's staff it sounds like a servant being useful — shall I point you to a family who can nurse the child. What she is actually pointing them toward is Musa's own mother. The retelling in Ta-Ha names the outcome plainly: "so We returned you to your mother, that her eye might be comforted and she would not grieve" (20:40). Guidance here is smuggled, in the guide's own grammar, right through the front door of the house that means to destroy the child. And note the household she points to are called nāṣiḥūn — sincere advisers — the very word the believer will use crossing the city to save the grown Musa years later (28:20), and the very word Shaytan had to counterfeit with an oath when he deceived Adam (7:21). The true pointing does not have to swear it is sincere. It just is.

The pointing that saves

And then the sentence is spoken by the source of guidance itself. God turns to the believers with the identical offer:

يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ تِجَـٰرَةٍ تُنجِيكُم مِّنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

"O you who believe, shall I show you a trade that will save you from a painful punishment?"

As-Saff (61:10)

Al-Muyassar reads hal adullukum here as hal urshidukum — "shall I guide you" — and al-Jalalayn notes the offer is built to draw a yes: it hangs in the air as if they said, "Yes." The next verse names the trade — faith, and striving in God's way with wealth and lives (61:11). It is the same opening move as the whisper to Adam: an offer, phrased as a shortcut to what you most want. Salvation is even called a trade, the language of gain. The form is the whisper's form exactly.

The tell is not the sentence. It is the direction of the finger.

So here is the problem the three verses lay bare: you cannot tell true guidance from its counterfeit by the way it is offered. Both say shall I show you. Both promise rescue from something you fear or delivery to something you want. Both invite a yes. The deceiver does not announce himself with a different grammar; he uses the Guide's own grammar. If you are waiting for the false offer to sound false, you will take it.

What separates them is only where the finger points — and there the difference is total. Watch what each pointing is toward. Shaytan points Adam to a tree of eternity and a kingdom that never decays: something to keep, forever, for himself. The offer flatters the self with endless life and unending possession. But the sister points Pharaoh's house toward handing the baby back — a giving-up. And God points the believers toward a trade paid in wealth and lives — a spending. The counterfeit always points you toward something that swells the self and never ends. The true guidance points you toward something you must release. One says here is what you can keep. The other says here is what is worth giving away.

That is the test the Quran hands you, and it works even when the voice is expert and the offer is beautiful. When something inside you says shall I show you the way to what you want — do not first ask whether it sounds trustworthy. Ask which direction the finger is pointing. Toward a self that gets larger and lasts forever, or toward a self that gives, and spends, and is saved.
