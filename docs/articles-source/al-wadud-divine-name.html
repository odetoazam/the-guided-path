Al-Wadud: The Divine Name for Love, and the Two Verses That Carry It

Al-Wadud (ٱلْوَدُود) is the Quran's name for God as the one who loves. It appears twice. Both times, it arrives holding hands with forgiveness — once in a prophet's closing appeal to people who were about to be destroyed, once four verses after a scene in which believers are burned alive. The name for divine love shows up in the Quran only in places where someone has done something that needs pardoning.

The root

The root is w-d-d (و د د), and Arabic keeps it distinct from the other word English flattens into "love." Hubb, from h-b-b, comes from the world of seeds and kernels — a dense little core, love as concentrated attachment. Wudd is warmer and more outward-facing: fondness, affection, the wish for someone's nearness. It is love with a social surface, the kind that shows.

The Quran uses both, and it keeps them apart. The verses about hubb describe what the heart clamps onto. Wudd appears when the Quran is describing affection extended toward someone, most famously in Surah Maryam, where the believers are promised that Ar-Rahman will appoint wudd for them.

The name Wadud is built on the pattern fa'ul — the same shape as ghafur and shakur, a pattern Arabic uses for a quality that is abundant and characteristic rather than occasional.

The first verse: a prophet's last offer

Shu'ayb was sent to Madyan, a trading people who cheated at the scales. His argument runs through most of the second half of Surah Hud: worship God, stop shorting the measure, and remember what happened to the peoples who were destroyed before you. He names three of them — the people of Nuh, of Hud, of Salih — and then adds that the people of Lut are "not far off from you."

Then he stops warning and offers the alternative.

وَٱسْتَغْفِرُوا۟ رَبَّكُمْ ثُمَّ تُوبُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهِ ۚ إِنَّ رَبِّى رَحِيمٌ وَدُودٌ

"And ask forgiveness of your Lord, then turn to Him. Indeed, my Lord is Merciful, Loving."

Hud (11:90)

The sentence has an order to it. First istighfar — asking for cover over what is already done. Then tawbah — turning back, changing direction. Ibn Kathir reads the two as covering different territory: the asking deals with past sins, the turning deals with what comes after. And then the two names, given as the reason the whole thing is worth attempting.

The commentators tie the names directly to the people being addressed. Ibn Kathir glosses Rahim Wadud with three words: li-man taba — to whoever repents. Al-Muyassar expands it: abundant in affection and love for whoever repents to Him and turns back, He has mercy on them and accepts their return. Al-Jalalayn is the most compressed: rahim to the believers, wadud means muhibb lahum, loving them.

Shu'ayb is speaking to people who have spent the whole surah refusing him. The name he closes on is the one for affection — a move that earned him, in the tradition, the title of orator among the prophets.

The second verse: after the trench

Surah Al-Buruj opens with an oath and then puts a scene in front of the reader stripped of names, dates and places. People dug a trench, filled it with fire, sat down beside it, and watched.

وَهُمْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَفْعَلُونَ بِٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ شُهُودٌ

"And they were witnesses to what they were doing to the believers."

Al-Buruj (85:7)

وَمَا نَقَمُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ إِلَّآ أَن يُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلْحَمِيدِ

"And they resented them only because they believed in Allah, the Mighty, the Praiseworthy."

Al-Buruj (85:8)

The verse that follows this passage is the one worth slowing down for. The surah turns to the people who lit the fire, and the sentence it gives them contains a hinge.

إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ فَتَنُوا۟ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتِ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَتُوبُوا۟ فَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابُ ٱلْحَرِيقِ

"Indeed, those who persecuted the believing men and believing women and then did not repent — for them is the punishment of Hell, and for them is the punishment of the Burning."

Al-Buruj (85:10)

Thumma lam yatubu — and then did not repent. The clause is doing real work. The sentence could have condemned the act. Instead it condemns the act plus the refusal to return afterwards, which means the return was on the table for the people who dug the trench.

Four verses later, after a line about how severe God's seizing is and a line about His beginning and repeating creation, the surah names Him.

وَهُوَ ٱلْغَفُورُ ٱلْوَدُودُ

"And He is the Forgiving, the Loving."

Al-Buruj (85:14)

Two verses earlier the surah said that the seizing of your Lord is severe. Ibn Kathir reads that line as being about God's vengeance on those who rejected His messengers, and then reads 85:14 as covering whoever repents and humbles himself, "no matter what the sin may be." The two statements sit three lines apart in a surah whose subject is people burned alive for believing.

The gloss that changes the picture

Al-Jalalayn's comment on 85:14 is short, and the grammar in it does something the English translations lose. Al-Wadud, it says, is al-mutawaddid ila awliya'ihi bi'l-karamah — the One who extends affection toward those close to Him through honouring them.

Mutawaddid is a Form V participle. In Arabic that form describes a subject actively doing something to itself or putting itself forward — here, God making the overture. The gloss reads the name as a movement rather than a state — affection extended rather than affection held.

Ibn Kathir preserves a simpler note from Ibn Abbas and others on the same word: Al-Wadud means al-Habib, the Loving. Al-Muyassar renders it, as it did at 11:90, as abundant in affection toward His close servants.

Set that gloss beside where the name actually occurs and the picture is consistent. In Surah Hud the affection is announced to people who have done wrong and been told to come back. In Al-Buruj it is announced in a passage where the door was held open even for the ones sitting beside the fire. Both times the love is presented as the thing that arrives when someone turns around — and in Al-Jalalayn's reading, it is God who is doing the reaching.

The same root, pointing the other way

The root shows up once more in a form worth setting beside the divine name, near the end of Surah Maryam.

إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ سَيَجْعَلُ لَهُمُ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ وُدًّا

"Indeed, those who believe and do righteous deeds — Ar-Rahman will appoint for them affection."

Maryam (19:96)

The verb is saj'alu, with the prefix that puts it in the future: He will appoint. And the object is wuddan, the bare noun from the same root as the divine name, given indefinitely.

The commentators locate where it is placed. Al-Muyassar reads it as love and affection put in the hearts of His servants. Al-Jalalayn takes it further: affection among the believers themselves, so that they hold each other in warmth — and adds that God loves them too.

Where 11:90 and 85:14 name God as the one who holds this affection, 19:96 describes Him placing it somewhere. The name and the noun sit on either side of a single transaction: the quality belongs to Him, and He assigns it. Surah Maryam is also, from beginning to end, a surah about people whose families and communities turned on them — Maryam accused by her people, Ibrahim told to leave by his father, Zakariyya asking for an heir. The promise of appointed affection lands at the close of that particular surah.

Two occurrences, one shape

Lay the two verses next to each other and the pattern is hard to miss.

At 11:90 the sequence runs: ask forgiveness, then turn back, because your Lord is Rahim, Wadud. Forgiveness language first, the name for love last.

At 85:14 the sequence is compressed into a single half-line: al-Ghafur, al-Wadud. The Forgiving, then the Loving. Same order, two words instead of two clauses.

Out of every place the Quran could have put its name for divine affection, it put it in these two, and in both it stands immediately downstream of pardon. The name is offered to people mid-return, and offered as the reason to make the return at all.

Reading the name

English translations render Al-Wadud as "the Loving," "the Affectionate," "the Most Loving," sometimes "the Loving-Kind." Each of them is reaching for a warmth beyond what rahmah alone conveys. Mercy can be extended across a distance; wudd closes it.

The Quran says it twice. A prophet says it to a town that is about to be levelled. A surah says it about a God whose severity it has just described, in a chapter about believers who were killed for believing. Both times it is the last thing said before the subject changes.
