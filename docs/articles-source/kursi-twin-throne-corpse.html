You Recite Ayat al-Kursi Before Sleep. Its Only Twin Verse Holds a Corpse.

Many people say it in the dark, the last thing before sleep — the verse that promises a guard until morning. And the verse they say against the night is a verse about the One the night leaves wholly untouched: no drowsiness takes Him, no sleep. Read the words closely and something stranger surfaces than the familiar majesty. The verse describes a throne, kursī, that holds the heavens and the earth without the faintest fatigue. That word — throne — appears in only one other place in the entire Quran. And the other throne has a dead body lying on it.

Two thrones in the whole Book

Here is the verse, recited more than almost any other:

ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ٱلْحَىُّ ٱلْقَيُّومُ ۚ لَا تَأْخُذُهُۥ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ ۚ لَّهُۥ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۗ مَن ذَا ٱلَّذِى يَشْفَعُ عِندَهُۥٓ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِۦ ۚ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَىْءٍ مِّنْ عِلْمِهِۦٓ إِلَّا بِمَا شَآءَ ۚ وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ ۖ وَلَا يَـُٔودُهُۥ حِفْظُهُمَا ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

"God — there is no deity except Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all. Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is before them and what will be after them, and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills. His throne extends over the heavens and the earth, and their preservation tires Him not. And He is the Most High, the Most Great."

Al-Baqarah (2:255)

Search the whole Quran for the word kursī and it returns twice. Once here, holding the cosmos. And once in the story of a prophet-king at the lowest point of his record:

وَلَقَدْ فَتَنَّا سُلَيْمَـٰنَ وَأَلْقَيْنَا عَلَىٰ كُرْسِيِّهِۦ جَسَدًا ثُمَّ أَنَابَ

"And We certainly tried Sulaymān, and We cast upon his throne a body; then he turned back."

Sad (38:34)

The Quran leaves the body unexplained — a body, cast upon the throne, and the verse stops there. The traditions that try to fill the gap are the kind the classical scholars flagged as borrowed and uncertain, and the verse stands on its own without them. What the text itself gives is enough: the greatest throne any human being ever held, the seat of a king whose dominion the Quran says was unmatched, and on it a jasad — a lifeless form — during a trial. The word for the throne is identical to the word in the verse recited every night. The two thrones sit at opposite ends of the same noun. One bears the weight of the heavens and the earth and is not wearied by keeping them. The other could not keep its own occupant from the trial that laid a body across it. And the king's response is the single verb that ends the verse — anāba, he turned back, the posture of return — carried in the next breath into rabbi ghfir lī, my Lord, forgive me. Human sovereignty at its summit meets the throne and turns to repentance. Divine sovereignty holds the throne and never tires.

The words used once

The second design is inside the recited verse itself, and it shows only when the vocabulary is weighed. The verse makes a series of claims about what God is beyond — and the Quran spends its rarest words on exactly those claims.

Take the denial of tiredness. Sinah — the drowsiness that comes just before sleep, the head beginning to nod — appears in the whole Quran one time, here. Sleep itself, nawm, the Quran uses nine times across its surahs; the lighter thing, the mere heaviness of the eyelids that precedes it, it names only once, only to deny it of God. The verse rules out sleep and even the first hint of it, reaching for a word it spends nowhere else to do so. The classical readers noticed the gradation and drew the obvious inference: the One free of the drowsiness that precedes sleep is, all the more, free of sleep.

Then the denial of fatigue. Yaʾūduhu — to weigh Him down, to burden Him — from a root the Quran also uses exactly once, here, in the statement that guarding the heavens and the earth does not weigh on Him. And in the very next verse, the word for a bond that cannot break — infiṣām, severance — again a word used a single time in the whole Book, describing the handhold of faith that has no snapping point. Drowsiness, burden, severance: three claims about what cannot reach God or the faith He offers, and three words the Quran uses once each and nowhere else. The uniqueness of the words matches the uniqueness of the One they describe.

Why the rarest words

The verse a person recites against fear turns out to be built, at its load-bearing joints, from vocabulary the Quran spends nowhere else. Where it says He does not doze, the word is a hapax. Where it says He is not burdened, the word is a hapax. Where it says the handhold does not break, the word is a hapax. The language runs out of ordinary words at the precise points where the claim is that God is beyond the ordinary — because the thing being described is used for nothing else, so the words are too. And the one throne the Book sets beside His had a lifeless body laid across it. Said in the dark, the verse guards the reciter with a portrait of the only sovereignty that never sleeps, spelled in words kept for it alone.
