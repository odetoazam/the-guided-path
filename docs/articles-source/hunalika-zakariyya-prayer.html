"Right There": Where Zakariyya Learned to Pray Again

An old man is the caretaker of a young girl set apart for God. Every time he steps into her prayer-chamber he finds food already there — provision he did not bring and cannot explain. He asks her where it comes from. She tells him: it is from God, who provides to whomever He wills without limit. And in that instant, standing in a room where the rules of ordinary supply have quietly stopped applying, the old man — long past the age of children, married to a barren wife — turns and asks God for a son. The Quran marks the moment with a single word, hunālika, "right there." It is the same word the Quran uses, everywhere else, for the moment the ground gives way.

The word hunālika appears nine times in the Quran. Eight of them fall on scenes of collapse — a sorcerer's defeat, a soul discovering the truth too late, the instant every false support fails. Once, and only once, it marks the birth of a prayer. Following that one exception into the chamber of Maryam shows how one person's miracle becomes the key that unlocks another's.

The provision he could not explain

Zakariyya has taken charge of Maryam, and his visits keep turning up something impossible:

كُلَّمَا دَخَلَ عَلَيْهَا زَكَرِيَّا ٱلْمِحْرَابَ وَجَدَ عِندَهَا رِزْقًا ۖ قَالَ يَـٰمَرْيَمُ أَنَّىٰ لَكِ هَـٰذَا ۖ قَالَتْ هُوَ مِنْ عِندِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَرْزُقُ مَن يَشَآءُ بِغَيْرِ حِسَابٍ

"Every time Zakariyya entered upon her in the prayer-chamber, he found provision with her. He said, 'O Maryam, from where is this to you?' She said, 'It is from Allah. Indeed, Allah provides for whom He wills without account.'"

Aal 'Imran (3:37)

The word for what he keeps finding is rizq, provision, and the classical commentators describe it as fruit out of its season — summer's harvest in the depth of winter, winter's in the heat of summer. The detail matters because it strips the food of any natural supply line. Rather than a servant delivering a meal, this is sustenance arriving from outside the calendar, and Maryam names its source at once: min ʿindi-llāh, from God, who gives bighayri ḥisāb — beyond measure, beyond accounting, beyond whatever the season is supposed to permit.

The word that turns

Zakariyya has carried a private grief for years. He is old, his bones have gone weak, his hair has caught fire with grey, and he and his wife have never had a child. He had, it seems, made his peace with a closed door. And then he watches a closed door open in front of him — food that the year says should not exist — and hears a young woman explain that God simply provides outside the rules. Watch what the next verse does:

هُنَالِكَ دَعَا زَكَرِيَّا رَبَّهُۥ ۖ قَالَ رَبِّ هَبْ لِى مِن لَّدُنكَ ذُرِّيَّةً طَيِّبَةً ۖ إِنَّكَ سَمِيعُ ٱلدُّعَآءِ

"Right there, Zakariyya called upon his Lord. He said, 'My Lord, grant me from Yourself good offspring. Indeed, You are the Hearer of supplication.'"

Aal 'Imran (3:38)

Hunālika — right there, in that place, at that moment. The prayer fires on the spot — in the chamber, in the middle of an ordinary visit, while the evidence of God's unaccountable giving is still in his hands. The logic runs underneath the word without being spoken: if God can bring fruit into a room against the season, then age and barrenness are just another calendar He is not bound by. Maryam's miracle rewrote what Zakariyya believed he was allowed to ask for. He had been rationing his own hope to what seemed possible. The provision in her chamber showed him that possibility was the wrong measure entirely — that God gives without account — and the request he had buried came back to life.

The one hopeful "there" in the Quran

This is where the single word carries its full weight. Elsewhere the Quran reaches for hunālika at the hinge of catastrophe. There, at the sorcerers' contest, the truth overturns Pharaoh's court. There, on the Day of reckoning, every soul is left to test what it sent ahead. There, when the sky closed over the disbelievers, their belated faith could no longer help them. The word lands, again and again, at the instant a person's supports collapse and reality arrives uninvited. It is the Quran's word for the moment the floor drops.

And once — in a quiet room, over a bowl of out-of-season fruit — the same word marks the opposite event. Here the floor does not drop; a ceiling lifts. Hunālika becomes the moment an old man's sense of the possible breaks open and a prayer he had given up on rushes back through the gap. The Quran spends that word eight times on the collapse of false hope and once on the return of true hope, and the single exception is a man learning, from someone else's gift, that he was allowed to ask again.

Learning to ask from someone else's answer

The angels interrupt Zakariyya mid-prayer with the yes — a son named Yahya — before he has even left the chamber. But the part worth carrying is not the speed of the answer. It is where the prayer came from. Zakariyya did not manufacture fresh faith by force of will. He saw God provide for another person, past the limits he assumed were fixed, and that sight reopened his own shuttered request. His grief had told him the matter was settled. Maryam's chamber told him the One who provides keeps no such calendar.

Which is the quiet instruction hidden in that one hopeful there. When you watch God open a door for someone else — an answer you were sure the rules forbade, a provision arriving out of season in another person's life — the honest first response is often a pang, a comparison, a private accounting of why it did not come to you. The old man in the chamber shows the other path. He let another person's miracle become evidence about his own Lord, and the very sight that could have soured into envy turned instead into the boldest prayer of his life. What you witness God do for someone else is not a verdict on what He has withheld from you. It may be the exact thing sent to teach you what you are still allowed to ask for.
