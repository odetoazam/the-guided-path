# Flocks Without a Singular: The Birds and Pebbles That Undid an Empire

> وَأَرْسَلَ عَلَيْهِمْ طَيْرًا أَبَابِيلَ ۝ تَرْمِيهِم بِحِجَارَةٍ مِّن سِجِّيلٍ
>
> *wa-arsala ʿalayhim ṭayran abābīl · tarmīhim bi-ḥijāratin min sijjīl*
>
> **"And He sent against them birds in flocks, striking them with stones of baked clay."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me put you on a road outside Makkah, somewhere around the year a child named Muhammad ﷺ would be born.

Down that road comes the most terrifying thing the Arabian Peninsula has ever seen. Not a man. Not a sword. An **elephant**. Possibly several. To the tribes of the Hijaz, who had never laid eyes on such an animal, this was not just a large beast — it was a moving fortress, a living siege engine, the absolute apex of military technology in their world. If you want a modern feeling for it, don't picture a zoo elephant. Picture the first time a population sees a tank roll over the horizon. Picture something against which your spears are jokes.

And behind the elephant: an organized army, led by a governor named Abraha, marching north from Yemen with one stated goal — to **demolish the Ka'ba**. He had built a grand cathedral in San'a and was enraged that the Arabs still turned their hearts toward that small cube of stone in Makkah. So he would erase it. He had the men. He had the elephant. He had the plan.

The people of Makkah did the math and the math was hopeless. ʿAbd al-Muttalib, the Prophet's grandfather, is reported to have famously told Abraha he was only concerned about his own camels that had been seized — *the House has a Lord who will protect it.* And then the Makkans withdrew to the mountains to watch their sanctuary be destroyed.

That is the world. An unstoppable force. A defenseless target. A sacred House about to fall. Everyone watching knows exactly how this ends.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the hook. When Allah recounts how this actually ended, He does not reach for the obvious tools. He does not send an army of angels with drawn swords, the way He did at Badr. He does not split the earth as He did with Qarun. He does not drown them as He drowned Pharaoh. The mightiest military machine of the age is met by — and stay with this — **small birds carrying small stones.**

Why *birds*? Why *pebbles*? Why does the King of all the heavens, who could have ended Abraha with a single word, choose the most almost-comical, the most contemptibly small instrument available?

Two ayahs answer that. And the answer will follow you home.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَرْسَلَ — The Verb That Usually Brings Mercy

Let me teach you something about a family of words before we touch the ayah.

In Arabic there is a root, **ر-س-ل (r-s-l)**, and from it grows an entire vocabulary you already half-know. From this root comes **رَسُول (rasūl)** — *messenger*. From it comes **رِسَالَة (risāla)** — *a message, a letter*. The base sense of the root is something *let loose to flow forward* — water released down a channel, a thing sent on its way with ease.

Now, the verb in our ayah is **أَرْسَلَ (arsala)**. Grammatically this is Form IV of the root. Form IV often takes a root's basic state and makes someone *cause* it — so from the idea of "flowing loose," Form IV gives you "**to let loose, to dispatch, to send forth.**" Allah lets a thing go, and it goes where He aims it.

Here is what I want you to notice. **This is Allah's mercy-verb.** Listen to where He uses it across the Qur'an:

- *"And We have not **sent** you except as a mercy to the worlds"* — He **arsala** the Prophet ﷺ.
- He **sends** the winds bearing good news of rain.
- He **sends** the rain itself down in measure.
- He **sends** messengers, scriptures, guidance.

When Allah *arsala* something, it is almost always a gift descending toward you. The same root that gives us *rasūl* — the most beloved gift ever sent to humanity — is the root in front of us now.

[PAUSE]

And what does He *send* here?

> وَأَرْسَلَ عَلَيْهِمْ — *"And He sent **against** them..."*

Sit with that little word **عَلَيْهِمْ (ʿalayhim)**. The preposition عَلَى carries the sense of *upon, over, down onto* — and in this construction, *against*. It is directional and it is adversarial. The sending that usually comes *to* you (إلى) as a kindness here comes down *upon* them (على) as a verdict.

Do you feel what the language just did? **The same verb of mercy has been turned into a verb of judgment** — and Allah signals the turn not by changing the verb, but by changing the preposition. He didn't need a different word for "punish." He took His sending-verb, the one that brings prophets and rain, and aimed it *down* on Abraha.

Here is what this single word reveals: **the One who sends mercy and the One who sends ruin are the same One, doing the same act, with the same ease.** Releasing birds onto an army costs Him exactly what releasing rain onto a field costs Him — nothing. *Arsala.* He let it loose. That's all.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: أَبَابِيل — The Flock With No Singular

Now we come to one of the strangest, most beautiful words in the entire Qur'an. And I want to teach you the concept first, because the wonder is *in* the grammar.

In Arabic, most plurals have a singular you can point to. *Kutub* (books) comes from *kitāb* (a book). *Rijāl* (men) comes from *rajul* (a man). You can always zoom in from the many to the one.

Some words, though, exist **only as a multitude.** They have no agreed-upon singular form at all. The classical grammarians actually argued about this very word, and the Qur'an commentators preserve the debate for us. **Al-Jalalayn** records that this word means "*group after group*," and then notes the disagreement: some said it has *no singular at all*, like the word *asāṭīr* (legends); others tried to reconstruct a singular — *ibbawl*, or *ibbīl*, or *ibāla*. **Al-Baghawī** preserves the same scene: al-Farrā' saying flatly "**it has no singular from its own form**," al-Kisā'ī recalling that he used to hear the grammarians searching for one and never settling.

Do you see what just happened? **The grammarians went looking for the "one bird" inside the word — and the word would not give them one.**

The word is **أَبَابِيل (abābīl)**. And notice one more thing: when you recite it, there is no *tanwīn*, no "-un" ending — it is what's called a diptote, a word that resists the normal endings, a word that even grammatically refuses to be domesticated.

Let me tell you what the lexicographers say it *means*, because every camera angle adds a layer. **Al-Baghawī** gathers them: *kathīra mutafarriqa* — "many, scattered, one batch following the next." Abū ʿUbayda: "groups in dispersion — you say *the horses came in abābīl*, from here and from there." Others: like droves of heavily-laden camels coming wave after wave. **Al-Muyassar** renders it *fī jamāʿātin mutatābiʿa* — "in successive throngs."

So put the picture together. The word names **waves**. Flocks behind flocks behind flocks, from every direction at once, with no front and no end — and *no single bird you can isolate as "the" bird.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is what's remarkable. Why would Allah, who chooses every syllable, reach for a word that *has no singular* to describe His army?

Because the absence in the word **is** the message. Abraha came as a *gathering of named men around one named, towering, singular weapon* — the elephant, *al-fīl*, definite, singular, the whole army labeled by it: *aṣḥāb al-fīl*, "the companions of the elephant." Their power had a face, a name, a shape you could point to.

And Allah answered the One Great Singular Thing with a **multitude that has no singular at all.** You cannot point to the bird that did it. You cannot name the species — **al-Qurtubī** records the view of Saʿīd ibn Jubayr that these were "birds the like of which had never been seen before, nor since." Anonymous. Uncountable. Coming from everywhere. The instrument is deliberately blurred into a swarm precisely so that **no created thing gets the credit.** When the weapon has no name and no number and no face, the eye is forced to travel past it — to the One who sent it.

The grammarians couldn't find the one bird. **You're not supposed to.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: تَرْمِيهِم — The Verb That Refuses to Stay in the Past

Now look at the second ayah, and let me show you something happening at the level of *tense* that most people read straight past.

> تَرْمِيهِم بِحِجَارَةٍ مِّن سِجِّيلٍ — *"striking them with stones of baked clay."*

Let me teach the device first. In Arabic, as in English, you can narrate a past event in the **present tense** to make it vivid — to drag it out of "what happened" and into "what is happening *right now, before your eyes*." We do this when we tell a gripping story: "So I'm walking down the street, and this car *comes* flying around the corner..." The event is over, but we switch to present to make you *watch* it. The grammarians of the Qur'an have a name for this — *ḥikāyat al-ḥāl*, "narrating the scene as if present."

Now watch the verbs of this whole passage in sequence:

- فَعَلَ — "did" — **past**
- أَلَمْ يَجْعَلْ — "did He not make" — **past**
- وَأَرْسَلَ — "and He sent" — **past**
- **تَرْمِيهِم — "striking them" — PRESENT**
- فَجَعَلَهُمْ — "so He made them" — **past**

Do you see it? In a river of completed past verbs, **one verb breaks into the present tense** and stands there throbbing. *Tarmīhim.* The sending is finished — *arsala*, done, over. But the *striking* refuses to be finished. Allah pulls that single moment out of history and freezes it in the live present: the birds are pelting them **right now**, in front of you, stone after stone, and it does not stop.

This is not decoration. This is Allah doing to your imagination exactly what the ayah describes. The whole surah opened with **أَلَمْ تَرَ** — "*Have you not seen?*" — and now the grammar makes you *see it*. The past becomes a window you are looking through. The stones are still falling.

[PAUSE]

And what are the stones? **حِجَارَةٍ مِّن سِجِّيلٍ** — "stones of *sijjīl*." Two things about this haunting little word.

First, the lexicographers note that **sijjīl** describes stone that is *baked, hardened clay* — **al-Muyassar** glosses it precisely: *ḥijāratin min ṭīnin mutaḥajjir*, "stones of clay turned to stone." Think of that. Not boulders. Not meteors. **Hardened mud.** The humblest material on earth — the stuff you'd scrape off your sandal — fired into a projectile.

Second, this exact word *sijjīl* appears only a handful of times in the Qur'an, and the other places are the destruction of the people of Lūṭ (11:82, 15:74). So the baked-clay stone is, in the Qur'anic vocabulary, a **stamp of decree** — the same kind of judgment that fell on a people who thought themselves untouchable now falls on Abraha. Some commentators, reported in the classical tradition, even connected *sijjīl* to the idea of something *written, recorded, marked* — as though each stone carried an address. We hold that gently as narration. But the core, attested sense is enough to break you open: **the apex weapon of the age was answered by pellets of dried mud, each one arriving exactly where it was decreed to arrive.**

[PAUSE]

**The bridge:** So the language has shown us three things. A mercy-verb turned downward into judgment with the flick of a preposition. An army-word with no singular, so no creature could claim the victory. And a frozen present-tense verb pelting an empire with baked mud, forever, before your eyes.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Weapon of the Small

Let me start where you actually live.

You live in a civilization that worships *scale*. We measure power in megatons, in market caps, in followers, in horsepower, in square footage. We are trained, deep in the bone, to believe that the bigger thing beats the smaller thing. The elephant beats the bird. The empire beats the village. The institution beats the individual. This is not a belief we examine; it is the water we swim in.

And then, every so often, reality quietly mocks the whole assumption.

A man with everything — wealth, status, a body trained and strong — is brought down by a clot the size of a grain of rice. An entire planet, with its armies and economies and skyscrapers, is shut into its homes by a virus you cannot see without a microscope. A towering reputation, built over forty years, collapses in an afternoon over a single sentence. **The thing that undoes the mighty is almost never another mighty thing. It is, with eerie regularity, something small.**

Now bring that back to the road outside Makkah.

[PAUSE]

Abraha represents the maximum of what created power can assemble: the war-elephant, the apex predator of ancient warfare, an organized state behind it, a clear plan, no credible opposition. If you scored this conflict on every metric human beings use — size, technology, numbers, momentum — Abraha wins in a walk.

And Allah answers him with **birds. And dried mud.**

Sit with the *deliberateness* of that choice. This is the part most people miss because they're impressed by the miracle. Don't be impressed by the size of the miracle — Allah could have made it enormous. **He made it small on purpose.** A flood would have said "a great force defeated a great force." An earthquake would have said "the strong were beaten by the stronger." But *birds and pebbles*? Birds and pebbles say something a flood never could:

**The gap between the mightiest thing in creation and the weakest thing in creation is, to Allah, exactly zero.**

The elephant and the sparrow are not at opposite ends of a power scale in Allah's hand. There *is* no scale. When the Master decides, the sparrow carries more force than the elephant, because force was never in the elephant to begin with — it was always, only, on loan from Him. Abraha thought he held power. He held a *prop*. And Allah revealed it was a prop by defeating it with a toy.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this turns on *you*, and on me.

We are all, quietly, building our own elephants. The career we believe makes us secure. The savings that we think make us safe. The plan we are sure will work. The reputation we are guarding. We march these elephants down the road, and somewhere in us is Abraha's assumption: *this is big enough; this will hold.*

The lesson of this ayah is not "don't have an elephant." ʿAbd al-Muttalib had camels; he went and got them back. **The lesson is: never confuse the size of the means with the source of the safety.** Abraha's sin was not that he was strong. It was that he mistook his strength for *his own* — and aimed it at the House of the One who lent it.

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes where your eyes go when you feel powerful, and where your eyes go when you feel powerless. When you feel strong — remember the elephant, and hold your strength loosely, as a loan that can be answered by a bird. When you feel weak, outnumbered, outgunned by some "elephant" in your own life — remember that Allah's smallest soldier is enough, and that He has never once needed the bigger thing to win.

### Theme Two: The Rescue You Live On and Have Forgotten

Now I want to walk you toward something quieter and, I think, even more piercing. To get there, start with a question.

Whose safety was actually being defended on that road?

The Ka'ba's, yes. But think one step further. **Makkah's.** The tribe of Quraysh's. The people who, after that day, would walk through Arabia under a kind of invisible shield — *these are the people of the House that the elephant could not reach.* The very next surah, al-Quraysh, picks up this exact thread: the reason Quraysh could send their great trade caravans north in summer and south in winter, in *security*, prosperous and respected, was the prestige and protection of the sanctuary — the sanctuary that this event had just proven Allah Himself defends.

In other words: **the comfortable, profitable, secure life that Quraysh was living was built directly on top of this rescue.** The caravans, the wealth, the status — all of it stood on the foundation of a day the birds came.

And here is the piercing part. By the time the Prophet ﷺ began reciting to them, that generation of Quraysh was *trading on the rescue while having forgotten the Rescuer.* They enjoyed the safety and ignored its source. They sat in the house the storm spared and stopped thanking the One who turned the storm.

That is why the surah does not open "Remember the elephant." It opens — **أَلَمْ تَرَ** — *"Have you not seen what your Lord did?"* It is a question with an edge. *You are living on this. How are you not looking at it?*

[PAUSE]

Now let me bring it home, because this is not really about Quraysh.

You woke up this morning on top of a thousand rescues you will never think about. The heart that beat 100,000 times yesterday without your permission. The clot that didn't form. The car that didn't drift into your lane. The illness your body fought off before you ever knew its name. The decision someone else made years ago that quietly protected you. **You are living, right now, atop a stack of "birds came" moments you have completely forgotten** — because, like Quraysh, you've been busy enjoying the *security* the rescue produced, and the security feels like just... how things are.

This is the deepest trick of comfort: **a rescue, once it succeeds, becomes invisible.** The danger you were saved from never happened to you, so it doesn't feel real, so the salvation doesn't feel like salvation. It just feels like a normal Tuesday. The birds that saved Makkah became, within two generations, a fact people *traded on* but no longer *trembled at.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change tomorrow?

It puts the word **أَلَمْ تَرَ** in your own mouth, pointed at your own life. The practice is almost embarrassingly simple, and it is this: **once a day, look back at one rescue you've been treating as a normal Tuesday — and name its Rescuer.** The safe arrival. The body that worked. The catastrophe that, for no reason you earned, simply did not come. Don't let it stay invisible. Drag one of your forgotten birds back into the light and say: *that was You.*

Because the tragedy of Quraysh was never that they weren't rescued. They were rescued magnificently. The tragedy is that they **forgot**, and built a comfortable life on a mercy they no longer looked at. And the Qur'an froze that moment in the present tense — *tarmīhim*, the stones still falling — so that it would never become a closed chapter. It is always *now*. The rescue is always available to be seen again.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Destruction That Was a Provision

Here is the structure, revealed now that we've earned it.

There is a connection that many reciters and a number of the commentators draw between this surah and the one that follows it, al-Quraysh — Ibn Kathīr, among others, narrates that some of the early community treated them as deeply linked, even reciting them together, because al-Quraysh opens *li-īlāfi Quraysh* ("for the security/familiarity of Quraysh...") as if completing a thought begun here. We hold the "single surah" view gently, as a reported reading rather than a settled ruling. But the *thematic* link is undeniable and stunning:

**The very same act was, at one address, total destruction — and at another address, the foundation of an entire people's safety.** The birds that turned Abraha's army into *chewed-up chaff* (105:5) are the same birds that secured the sanctuary that secured the caravans that fed Quraysh. One descent of Allah's decree. Two faces. Judgment on the arrogant. Provision for the protected.

That is the architecture: **what looks like only destruction in one frame is mercy in another.** You almost never see both frames at once. Quraysh saw the provision and forgot the judgment underneath it.

### Bringing It Together

Look how the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be **one insight from two directions.**

- *Arsala* — the mercy-verb aimed downward — is the grammar of **"one act, two faces."** Sending is sending; mercy and judgment are the same hand.
- *Abābīl* — the flock with no singular — is the grammar of **"the weapon of the small."** No creature gets the credit because no creature held the power; the swarm is anonymous so your eyes go to Allah.
- *Tarmīhim* — the frozen present tense — is the grammar of **"the rescue you must keep seeing."** Allah refuses to let the moment close, because the moment we let it close is the moment we become Quraysh.

The mind, by now, is satisfied. That is not the point. The point is whether your **eyes have moved** — from the elephant to its Lord, from your forgotten Tuesdays to your Rescuer.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the "elephant" in my life right now — the big thing I'm quietly trusting to keep me safe? And whose loan is it, really?
2. When was the last time I felt powerless before some force much larger than me — and did I remember that Allah's smallest soldier is enough?
3. What rescue am I living on top of, today, that I've stopped seeing because it succeeded?
4. If I am, like Quraysh, prosperous and secure — am I *trading on* a mercy I no longer *tremble at*?
5. What would change in how I walk through tomorrow if I genuinely believed the gap between the elephant and the bird is, to my Lord, exactly nothing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The mightiest power humanity could assemble was undone, deliberately, by the smallest things in creation — so that no eye would rest on the means, and every eye would be forced back to the One who sends both the birds and the safety we forget to thank Him for.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't need to fear your elephants or despise your strength. You need to *re-aim your eyes.* Let this be the surah you reach for when something enormous is bearing down on you and you've run out of spears — and let it also be the surah you reach for on an ordinary, safe, comfortable Tuesday, when everything is fine, *because* everything is fine, so that you remember Who is making it so. Don't wait to be Abraha to learn this. Don't stay Quraysh to keep forgetting it.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, who turned baked mud into Your army and a sparrow into the downfall of an empire — teach our eyes to stop resting on the size of things. Let us never mistake the elephants we lean on for the safety only You give. And of the thousand rescues we are living on top of and have forgotten — O Allah, let us see at least one of them today, and call it by Your Name.*

*Āmīn.*
