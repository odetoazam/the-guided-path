# Have You Not Seen — The Question That Was Already Answered Before You Were Born

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first. In Arabic:

[ayah:105:1] أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَـٰبِ ٱلْفِيلِ

*Alam tara kayfa faʿala rabbuka bi-aṣḥābi-l-fīl.*

"Have you not seen how your Lord dealt with the companions of the elephant?"

Seven words. One question. No answer given in this verse — the answer comes after, in the verses that follow, with the birds and the stones and the army left like chewed-up straw. But the door into the whole surah is this question. And I want us to spend our time today not rushing past the question to get to the dramatic ending we already half-remember, but standing inside the question itself. Because the question is doing something strange. And once you see what it's doing, you can never read it the same way again.

### Rebuild the world

Close your eyes for a moment and put yourself in Makkah.

It is roughly the year 570 of the common era. Makkah is not a power. It has no standing army. It has no walls worth the name, no king, no fortress. It is a cluster of stone and mud houses gathered around one ancient cube of a building — the Kaaba — sitting in a dry valley with no river, no farmland, nothing that an empire would normally want. The Quraysh who live there are traders and guardians of that House. That is their whole identity. They are the people of the Sanctuary.

And now imagine the news arriving. Coming up from the south, from Yemen, is an army the likes of which the Arabs of the Hijaz have never faced. Its commander is Abraha — a viceroy of the Abyssinian kingdom, ruling Yemen. The classical commentators, al-Baghawī among them tracing it through Ibn Isḥāq, al-Qurṭubī, al-Jalālayn — they tell us Abraha had built a magnificent church in Ṣanʿāʾ. He wanted to pull the pilgrimage of all Arabia away from Makkah and toward his new cathedral. And when that did not happen — when, as the narrations report, a man defiled the church in contempt — Abraha swore he would not rest until he had torn the Kaaba to the ground, stone by stone.

And to do it, he brought elephants.

Now hold that image, because the elephant is the whole point and we will come back to it. The Arabs of the Hijaz had likely never seen a war elephant. Picture the people of Makkah standing on the hills, looking down at this thing — a moving mountain of grey flesh, draped for war, with an army behind it that could erase their city in an afternoon. There is nothing they can do. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, the grandfather of the Prophet ﷺ who has not yet been born, is said to have told his people to leave the city and take to the mountaintops, and he turned to the Kaaba and said, in effect: *this is Your House; if You will not protect it, no one can.*

That is the world. A defenseless town. An unstoppable army. A sacred House, and no human hand able to save it.

[PAUSE]

### The strand

And here is something we must not miss. This is the year — by the strong tradition — that the Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ is born. The Year of the Elephant. So the man who will receive this revelation decades later, who will be told *alam tara*, "have you not seen" — he was, in a sense, present in that very year, an infant in the protected city, carried by the same protection that turned the army back. And notice too what the next surah will say. Surah Quraysh opens — and the early reciters often recited Al-Fīl and Quraysh almost as one breath — "*For the security of the Quraysh... let them worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and made them safe from fear.*" The strand running through these two surahs is a single thread: **the House was protected, therefore its people were secured, therefore — worship the One who did it.** Al-Fīl is the proof. Quraysh is the conclusion drawn from the proof.

### The Hook

So here is what I want you to feel the weight of before we begin. Allah does not open this surah by saying "Remember what happened." He does not say "Let me tell you a story." He opens with a question aimed at one person — *Have you not seen?* — and He aims it at a man who, with his physical eyes, never saw the army, never saw the birds, never saw the stones fall. He was an infant or not yet born.

So what does it mean that Allah asks a man whether he has *seen* something he could not possibly have witnessed? And why — when an entire empire's army with a battalion of elephants stands at the edge of the Sanctuary — why does Allah file the whole catastrophe under the name of the one animal, calling them not Abraha's army, not the Abyssinians, but simply **the companions of the elephant**?

Stay with those two questions. They are where everything is hidden.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Alam tara* — the question that is not asking

Let me teach you something about how questions work before we open this one.

In every language, we have a kind of question that is not really looking for information. If I turn to you after we both watched someone act with great courage and I say, "Did you *see* that?" — I am not worried that you missed it. We both saw it. My question is not a request for data. My question is an invitation: *come stand next to me in front of this thing and feel its weight with me.* The question is a way of pointing. A way of saying, *look — really look — at what just happened.*

Arabic has a precise name for this. The grammarians call it **istifhām** — a question in *form* — that carries the meaning of **taqrīr** — establishment, affirmation, the drawing-out of an acknowledgment that is already settled. The form is a question mark. The function is a finger pointing and a voice saying *you know this is true.*

Now look at the opening:

**أَلَمْ تَرَ** — *alam tara.*

Break it into its pieces. There is the questioning *hamza* at the front — *a-*. Then *lam*, the particle of negation that throws a verb into the past and pushes it down: *lam tara*, "you did not see." Put them together and the literal shape is: *Did you not see?* The root of that final verb, *tara*, is **r-ʾ-y** — the root of seeing, of vision, of the eye and of insight both.

Now here is the first thing to sit with. Listen to the great early commentators gathered around this single word, because they do not all reach for the eye.

Al-Qurṭubī lays them side by side. *Alam tara*, he says, some read as *alam tukhbar* — have you not been **informed**? Others as *alam taʿlam* — have you not come to **know**? And Ibn ʿAbbās, he reports, read its sense as *alam tasmaʿ* — have you not **heard**? Al-Muyassar renders it *alam taʿlam ayyuhā ar-rasūl* — have you not known, O Messenger? So the commentators are circling a single point: the "seeing" here is not the seeing of the eyeball. It is a *knowing so certain that it stands in the place of having seen with your own eyes.*

[PAUSE]

Let that land. Allah chose the verb of **vision** — *tara* — and then directed it at a man who did not physically watch the event. And the scholars, far from treating that as a problem, treat it as the whole power of the word. Because there is a kind of knowing that is more vivid than eyesight. When a fact is so established, so witnessed, so handed down by a whole city that lived it, that doubting it would be madness — then *knowing* it becomes a form of *seeing* it. Allah is saying: this event is not something you heard as a rumour and might forget. It is something you can **see** — standing in the streets of Makkah, in the memory of the people around you, in the very safety of the city you walk in. *You have seen it. Look at it.*

And now go one layer down, into the tense. The verb *tara* is in the imperfect — the present-and-ongoing form, not the past. Arabic could have framed this entirely in the past, sealed and finished. Instead the event is past — *faʿala*, "He did," is past — but the *seeing* is kept in the present. **The deed is done; the seeing is forever.** It is as though Allah holds the act of protection in the perfect past, completed and unrepeatable, but keeps your *vision* of it permanently open, permanently now. You are not asked, "Did you once see?" You are asked, "Are you not, even now, seeing?"

[PAUSE]

And now the deepest layer, and here I lean on al-Qurṭubī directly, because he says something that turns this verse from history into a summons. The form is a question, he writes, *wa-l-maʿnā taqrīr* — and the meaning is establishment. The address is to the Prophet ﷺ, *wa-lākinnahu ʿāmm* — but it is general, it reaches everyone. And then listen to how he unfolds what is really being said: *you have indeed seen that, and you have come to know the place of My favour upon you — so what is it with you that you do not believe?*

Do you feel the floor shift? The question *Have you not seen?* is not really about the elephant at all. It is the opening move of an argument whose conclusion is **faith**. Allah is laying down a fact you cannot deny — your city was saved, your safety is a miracle, you have *seen* it — in order to press on you a question you have been avoiding: *then why do you still withhold your belief from the One who did this?* The interrogative of history becomes the interrogative of the heart. *Have you not seen?* becomes *what, then, is your excuse?*

So this single opening — *alam tara* — gives us three things stacked on top of one another. A vision deeper than eyesight. A seeing kept permanently in the present tense. And a settled fact deployed as the ground of a demand for faith.

One word. Hold it.

### Element Two: *Faʿala rabbuka* — the verb He left blank, and the name He chose

Now move to the centre of the verse. Two words: **فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ** — *faʿala rabbuka*, "your Lord did."

Let me start with the verb, *faʿala*. Its root is **f-ʿ-l**, and I want you to feel how plain this verb is. *Faʿala* is the most generic verb of doing in the entire Arabic language. It is so generic that grammarians use its very letters — *faʿala* — as the template, the dummy word, onto which they map every other verb in the language. It means, simply, *he did.* He acted. He made-happen.

Now ask the question this methodology always asks: **what is absent here?** What does Allah *not* say?

He does not say "how your Lord *destroyed* them." He does not say "how your Lord *shattered* them," or "*crushed* them," or "*annihilated* them." The dramatic, specific, violent verbs — those are all available in Arabic, and the Quran uses them elsewhere without hesitation. Here, at the head of the surah, Allah reaches past every one of them and selects the blankest, most unspecified word for action there is: *He did.*

[PAUSE]

Why withhold the specific verb? Sit with this, because the absence is doing precise work.

First: by leaving the verb open, Allah leaves the *imagination* open. *How* did your Lord deal with them? The blank verb is a held breath. It invites you to lean forward and ask *how — how exactly?* — which is the very next word, *kayfa*, "how." A specific verb would have closed the question. The generic verb *faʿala* keeps you reaching for the answer the rest of the surah will pour out: the birds, the stones, the bodies left like devoured chaff. The plainness of *faʿala* at the front is what makes the detail at the back land like a blow.

Second, and deeper: when the deed is too total to be captured by any single verb, you reach for the verb that contains all of them. To say "He destroyed them" would have been *too small* — it names one mode of ruin. *Faʿala*, "He did" — the doing here is so complete, so beyond the categories of ordinary warfare, that it overflows any specific word. The smallest verb is used because the deed is the largest. **He did — and what He did cannot be held by a narrower word than "did."**

Now the second word, and this is where intimacy enters the verse like warmth into a cold room: **رَبُّكَ** — *rabbuka.* "Your Lord."

The root is **r-b-b**, and *rabb* is not merely "lord" in the sense of a ruler on a throne. *Rabb* is the one who owns, nurtures, raises, sustains, brings a thing stage by stage to its completion. It is the word a child's whole upbringing lives inside. *Rabb* is care plus authority — the authority of the one whose authority is *for your sake.*

And notice what Allah does *not* say here. He does not say *faʿala-llāhu* — "Allah did." He does not say "the Lord of the worlds did," or "the Almighty did." He says *rabbuka* — **your** Lord. He attaches the deed to a personal pronoun. *Ka.* You.

[PAUSE]

Feel the asymmetry of address. The event was public — a whole army, a whole city. But the naming of the Doer is made intensely private. *Your* Lord. The same Lord who, in the very year of this event, was raising you, guarding you, an infant in the protected valley. The Lord who saved that House is being introduced to you not by His cosmic title but by His relationship to *you.* The verse does not say "behold the power of God." It says "behold what *your own* Lord — the One who has been tending you all along — did." Power, here, is folded inside belonging.

And this is why the verb being generic and the Lord being personal belong together. Combine them and you get the whole theology of the surah in two words: an act so total it needs no specific name — *faʿala* — performed by a power so intimate it is named only by His care for you — *rabbuka.* The biggest possible deed, signed with the most personal possible name.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Aṣḥāb al-fīl* — an empire filed under an animal

Now the final two words, and to me they are the most astonishing in the verse: **بِأَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْفِيلِ** — *bi-aṣḥābi-l-fīl,* "with the companions of the elephant."

Let me teach the word *aṣḥāb* before we use it. The root is **ṣ-ḥ-b**, and a *ṣāḥib* is a companion — but not a casual one. A *ṣāḥib* is one who *accompanies*, who keeps company, who is bound up with a thing, attached to it, identified by his closeness to it. The *aṣḥāb* of something are the people *of* it — the people defined by their attachment to that thing. We speak of the *aṣḥāb* of the Prophet ﷺ — his Companions — as those whose entire identity is their nearness to him. The word carries belonging, association, *being-known-by-what-you-keep-company-with.*

And *al-fīl,* the elephant — root **f-y-l** — the single animal, named in the definite, *the* elephant.

Now stop and feel the strangeness of the naming. This was an *empire's* expeditionary force. It had a famous commander, Abraha, whose name the histories record in detail. It had cavalry, infantry, the backing of the Abyssinian crown, a religious mission, a king's church behind it. There were a hundred ways to name them with their power on display: *the army of Abraha. The host of Abyssinia. The soldiers who came against the House.*

Allah names them by **the elephant.** *The companions of the elephant.* The whole roaring war-machine is filed, in the divine record, under the name of the dumb beast they brought with them.

[PAUSE]

Read this absence the way we have been trained to. Where is Abraha's name? Gone. Where is the empire, the crown, the cause? Gone. Where is any word of their power? Withheld entirely. Allah strips every signifier of might off these people and pins them to the one thing that was supposed to be their terror-weapon — the elephant, the moving mountain the Arabs had never faced.

And here is the devastating reversal hidden in the word *aṣḥāb.* That word, "companions," makes the human beings the *accessories* of the animal — not the other way around. We would expect the elephant to belong to the army: *the army's elephant.* Allah inverts it. He makes the army belong to the elephant: *the elephant's companions.* The men are no longer the masters of the beast; they are reduced to its entourage. The thing they thought made them unstoppable has become the only thing they are remembered by — and it has *demoted* them to its hangers-on.

[PAUSE]

Go deeper still. There is a quiet justice in the choice. They came trusting in the elephant. They placed their confidence in visible, overwhelming, animal power — flesh and bulk and the fear it would put into men's hearts. So Allah names them by exactly the thing they trusted. *You leaned on the elephant? Then the elephant is your name forever — and look what your elephant could do against your Lord.* For the tradition adds — al-Qurṭubī and others — that the great elephant itself, at the edge of the Sanctuary, knelt and would not advance toward the House. The very creature they were named for refused to carry their crime. Their weapon became their witness against them.

And there is one more layer, the one that turns the screw all the way. To be named *aṣḥāb* of a thing is to be *permanently bound* to it. These men are stuck, for all of history, sealed into the Book of Allah, identified eternally not by their faith, not by their deeds, not even by their leader — but by the animal they rode to their destruction. Their names are dust. Their empire is a footnote. And the only label that outlived them is the one that records their humiliation: *the companions of the elephant.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

Look at what the three pieces of this verse have shown us.

*Alam tara* — a question that is really a finger pointing at a fact too certain to deny, deployed to press the heart toward faith. *Faʿala rabbuka* — the blankest verb of doing, because the deed overflows all specific verbs, signed with the most personal of names, *your* Lord. *Aṣḥāb al-fīl* — an empire stripped of every emblem of power and filed forever under the name of the beast it trusted.

Three pieces, and notice they are all moving in the *same direction.* Each one takes something that looked enormous — a forgotten historical event, an indescribable act of destruction, a world-empire's army — and quietly *reframes the size of everything.* The event becomes something you can see right now. The destruction becomes something too total for words. The empire becomes the elephant's entourage. Every word in this verse is rearranging your sense of *what is actually big and what is actually small.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The arithmetic of power is a lie your eyes keep telling you

Let me start where you live, not in seventh-century Arabia.

You have, somewhere in you, a running calculation. We all do. It runs all day. It is the calculation of *who has the power here.* You walk into a room and within seconds you have done the math — who is bigger, who is funded, who is connected, who can hurt me, who can help me. We are constantly weighing visible force. The company with the bigger budget. The person with the louder voice. The institution with the lawyers. The diagnosis with the statistics. The bully with the size. We look, we measure, and we feel our own smallness against the visible mass of the thing in front of us.

This is not a flaw you can simply switch off. It is how the eyes are built. The eyes report *mass* and *motion* and *number,* and the heart reads that report as *power.* The bigger it looks, the more real its threat feels. The elephant is, in this exact sense, the perfect symbol — because an elephant is *visibly* the largest thing on the battlefield. It is power you can see from a mile away. It is the budget, the army, the diagnosis, the mountain, made flesh.

Now bring the verse in.

Allah takes the largest visible power in that world — the elephant and the empire behind it — and in the architecture of this surah He reduces it to *chewed straw,* to *ʿaṣf maʾkūl,* the dry husk left after the grain is eaten and the animal has passed it through. And He does it with what? The smallest visible things. Birds. Stones the size of pebbles. The verse before us doesn't even tell us *how* yet — it just plants the question, *kayfa,* "how" — and the answer, when it comes, is an answer designed to humiliate the arithmetic of the eyes. The mountain of flesh is undone by flocks of small birds carrying small stones.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what that does to your running calculation. The whole point of the manner of the destruction — birds, pebbles — is that it could not be mistaken for a fair fight. If a greater army had come and beaten Abraha, the lesson would have been *bring a bigger elephant next time.* The power would still be in the visible mass; only the numbers would have changed. But that is not what happened. The defeat came from a direction the calculation *cannot even score* — from the sky, from creatures with no military value, by a means that turns the entire logic of force into a joke. Allah did not out-mass them. He **demonstrated that mass was never the real variable.**

And here is the unseen reality the physical image is pointing to — this is the *istidlāl,* the reasoning from the seen to the unseen. The elephant, the birds, the stones — these are not the subject. They are the stepping stones. What they are pointing at is a truth your eyes can never report to you: **that visible power and actual power have no necessary relationship at all.** The thing that looks unstoppable is held, every instant, in a grip it cannot see. The army stood at the edge of the Sanctuary in full command of every visible factor — and was, in reality, already finished, because the only variable that ever mattered was the will of *your Lord,* and that variable does not appear in the eyes' report.

This is why the verse opens with *seeing.* *Alam tara* — have you not *seen.* It is summoning you to a second sight, a sight that corrects the first. Your eyes saw an unbeatable army. The *seeing* Allah summons you to saw an army that was already chewed straw before the first stone fell.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Tomorrow you will stand again in front of something that *looks* like the elephant. A force, a fear, a power, a verdict, a person, a system that your eyes measure and report back as overwhelming. The running calculation will run. And in that moment this verse asks you to do something almost physically difficult: to *not believe your eyes' final number.* Not to deny the elephant — it is really there, it is really large — but to refuse the conclusion your eyes draw from its size. To remember that the army with every visible advantage stood one command away from being husks, and that the means of its undoing was so small it would have been beneath your notice. The practical shift is this: **stop letting the visible mass of a thing be your measure of its power, and start asking instead where it stands with the One whose will is the only variable that decides.** That is not naïve. It is the most realistic thing you can do — because it is reading reality at the level where reality is actually settled.

### Theme Two: He protected you before you could thank Him — the care that precedes the faith

Now let me bring you closer in, to something more tender than power.

There is a particular kind of love that no one ever earns: the care you received before you were old enough to know you were receiving it. You were kept alive, kept warm, kept safe, for years, by people whose protection you could not yet thank, could not yet understand, could not yet even perceive *as* protection. By the time you were aware enough to recognize it, it had already been holding you for a very long time. The care came *first.* The awareness came *after.* And there is something in the human heart that, when it finally turns around and sees how long it was carried before it knew, breaks open a little.

Now hold that, and look again at who is being addressed and when.

*Faʿala rabbuka* — your Lord did this. The Prophet ﷺ, by the strong tradition, is born in this very year. He is an infant — or about to be — in the city that this protection saved. The hand that turned back the army was, in that same season, the hand cradling the child who would only decades later be told *alam tara,* "have you not seen." He was *inside* the protection before he could perceive it. He was secured before he could believe. Al-Qurṭubī's reading we met earlier now lands with full weight: *you have come to know the place of My favour upon you — so why do you not believe?* The favour came first. The believing is being asked for after. **The protection is not a reward for the faith; it is the ground that precedes and invites the faith.**

[PAUSE]

And this is why, of all the names, Allah chose *rabbuka.* Not "the Almighty did," not "God did" — *your Lord* did. *Rabb* — the one who nurtures, raises, brings you stage by stage to your completion. The name itself says *I was tending you before you turned to Me.* When Allah recalls this enormous public act of power, He files it under the most intimate, most cradle-like of His names — because the lesson is not merely *I am powerful enough to destroy an army.* The lesson is *I am the One who has been raising you, and this is how I deal with what threatens the ones I am raising.* The power in the verse is real, but it is wrapped entirely inside relationship. It is not power on display. It is **a parent's strength, glimpsed by a child who only later understood what was done for them.**

Now widen it from the Prophet ﷺ to you — because al-Qurṭubī told us the address is *ʿāmm,* general, it reaches everyone.

How much of your safety, right now, today, is protection you have never perceived? How many elephants turned back at the edge of your life that you never even knew had set out? We notice the trials that strike. We almost never notice the disasters that were quietly diverted before they reached us — the illness that never came, the accident that missed by a second, the door that closed and saved us from a room we were begging to enter. The whole genre of *un-happened catastrophe* is invisible to us. It does not appear in the eyes' report, just like the army's real weakness did not. And this verse is, among other things, a summons to *see that too.* To turn around like the grown child and realize how long, and how invisibly, *your Lord* has been turning back elephants you never saw coming.

[PAUSE]

And then the verse does what mercy always does in the Quran — it converts the realization into a question, not a debt. *So what is it with you that you do not believe?* It is not a bill presented for payment. It is a Father — forgive the human word, He is beyond it — gently asking the child who has finally understood: *now that you see how long I have held you, will you not turn to Me?* The protection asks for nothing but your turning.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Two things, very concrete. First: build the habit of counting the *un-happened.* When you do your gratitude — and you should — do not only thank Him for what arrived. Thank Him, deliberately, for what you suspect was turned back before it reached you: the elephants you never saw. That single practice rewires the heart from entitlement toward awe, because it forces you to see the protection that precedes your awareness. Second: when your faith feels weak, when belief feels like a mountain you cannot climb, remember the *order* in this verse. He did not wait for your faith to protect you. The favour came first. So you are not being asked to earn your way to a distant God. You are being asked to *turn around* and recognize the One who has been holding you the entire time. The faith is not a wall to scale. It is a turning-around toward a care that was always already there.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two directions meet.

The linguistic journey showed us a verse obsessed with *resizing* — a forgotten event made present and visible (*alam tara*), a destruction too total for any specific verb (*faʿala*), an empire shrunk to the entourage of its own beast (*aṣḥāb al-fīl*). The thematic journey showed us *why* the resizing matters: because the eyes lie about power, reporting the elephant as unstoppable when it was already husks; and because the heart misreads the order of love, imagining it must earn a protection that, in truth, came first and only asks to be recognized.

These are the same insight from two directions. The whole verse is a correction of *measurement.* Your eyes mis-measure power — so Allah files the empire under the elephant to show you what it was really worth. Your heart mis-measures love — so Allah signs the act *rabbuka,* your Lord, to show you it was always relationship, always care preceding your awareness. **Have you not seen** is the command underneath both: *look again, and measure again, because everything you thought was big and everything you thought you had to earn — you have been measuring it wrong.*

And notice the architecture of the surah this single verse opens. It begins with a question and no answer — *how did your Lord deal with them?* — and it withholds the answer precisely to make you lean in. The blank verb *faʿala* is a held breath; the *kayfa,* "how," is the gap; and the rest of the surah is the exhalation: the birds, the stones, the chewed straw. The verse is built like a question mark hanging in the air, and the genius of it is that the question mark is aimed at *you* — not "did this happen" but "have *you* seen it, and what will you now do with your faith."

### Questions to carry

- What is the elephant in your life right now — the visible power your eyes have already declared unbeatable? And what would it mean to refuse your eyes' final verdict on it?
- If you turned around like a grown child and counted the disasters that were quietly turned back before they ever reached you, what would be on that list — and have you ever once thanked Him for them?
- Allah named these people forever by the thing they trusted instead of Him. If you were named, today, by the thing you most lean on for safety — what would your name be?
- Al-Qurṭubī said the real question underneath *have you not seen* is *so why do you not believe.* What, honestly, is your answer to that question?
- The protection came before the faith was asked for. Where in your life are you still acting as though you must earn a care that has, in fact, been holding you the whole time?

### One-sentence distillation

Allah points you at an army that owned every visible advantage and was undone by birds and pebbles — and signs the deed *your Lord* — to teach you that power was never in what your eyes can measure, and that the One who protected you before you could even believe is only asking you, now, to turn around and see.

### The closing invitation

So here is what I would invite to shift. Not a new piece of information — you already half-knew the story of the elephant. A new *seeing.* The next time you stand in front of something that looks like the elephant — and you will, this week, in some form — let this verse rise in you. Not as a denial of the thing's size, but as a refusal of its claim to be the final word. And the next time your faith feels like too much to summon, remember the order: He protected the House, and the infant in it, before anyone in that valley turned to Him in this way. The favour is first. Your turning is all He asks. *Alam tara.* Have you not seen. Look again.

### A brief du'ā

O Allah, You who turned back the elephant from Your House before its companions could even understand who they had defied — open in us the seeing that is deeper than our eyes, so that we measure power by Your will and not by what looms large in front of us. And as You protected us, again and again, before we ever knew enough to thank You, let this be the moment we finally turn around — and believe.
