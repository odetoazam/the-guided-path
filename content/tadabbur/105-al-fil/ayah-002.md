# When the Plan Itself Gets Lost

### Surah Al-Fil, Ayah 2

> [ayah:105:2] أَلَمْ يَجْعَلْ كَيْدَهُمْ فِى تَضْلِيلٍ
>
> *A-lam yajʿal kaydahum fī taḍlīl*
>
> **"Did He not make their scheme go utterly astray?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuild the World

Before we open this ayah, I want you to stand somewhere.

Stand on the road north of Makkah, sometime around the year a child named Muhammad ﷺ would be born. And let me show you what is coming up that road.

There is an army. Not a raiding party — an *army*, marching out of Yemen, sent by a viceroy named Abraha who serves the empire of Abyssinia. And at the head of this army is something no one in the Hijaz has ever seen advancing toward their city: **an elephant.** A war elephant. To the people of Makkah, this is not just a large animal. This is a piece of military technology from another world — a moving wall of muscle and tusk, the tank of its age.

Now understand *why* they are coming. This is the part we tend to skip. Abraha had built a magnificent cathedral in Sanʿāʾ — al-Qulays — and he wanted the pilgrimage of all Arabia to flow to *it* instead of to the Kaʿbah in Makkah. When the Arabs continued to revere the ancient House, Abraha's pride turned to fury. So he organized an expedition with one stated goal: **to demolish the Kaʿbah.** To erase the House of Allah from the earth and redirect the worship of a peninsula.

So this is not a mob. This is a *plan*. A well-resourced, well-engineered, ideologically-motivated, state-sponsored plan, carried on the back of the most advanced weapon of the era, aimed at the most sacred point on earth.

And here is the detail that should make the hair on your arms stand up: **the Quraysh could not stop it.** The keeper of the Kaʿbah, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib — the Prophet's own grandfather — when Abraha's men seized his camels, he asked for his camels back. Abraha was stunned. *You ask about your camels and say nothing about the House I have come to destroy?* And ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib gave the answer that frames this entire surah: *"I am the lord of the camels. The House has a Lord who will protect it."* Then he and his people withdrew to the mountains. They left the city. They were not the defense. There was no defense.

[PAUSE]

Now — into a world holding its breath, a world where every human calculation says *the House is about to fall* — Allah sends down a surah. And the second ayah, the very first thing He tells us after asking "Have you not seen what your Lord did?", is not a description of birds. It is not a description of stones. It is a **question.**

*Did He not make their scheme go utterly astray?*

### The Hook

Sit with one strange thing before we go further. Allah does not say "Did He not destroy them?" — not yet. He does not say "Did He not defeat their army?" He targets something more precise, more surgical, than the army. He goes after **the plan itself.** He says their *kayd* — their scheming, their design — was put *into* something. Into *taḍlīl*. Into a state of being made-to-wander, made-to-be-lost.

What does it mean for a *plan* to get lost? Not the people — the plan. How do you make a scheme lose its way? And why would Allah describe the destruction of an elephant army using a word that means *misguidance* — the same family of words used for a soul that strays from the path?

Stay with me. Because the answer is going to reach much closer to your own life than an elephant ever could.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three things to open in this ayah, and they sit in a perfect sequence: a **question**, a **scheme**, and a **getting-lost**. Let's take them one at a time, and let each one prepare us for the next.

---

### Element One: The Question That Isn't Asking — *A-lam* (أَلَمْ)

Let me teach you something about questions before we touch the ayah.

There is a kind of question that does not want information. You already use it. When a mother turns to a child standing over a shattered glass and says, *"Didn't I tell you not to run in here?"* — she is not gathering data. She knows she told him. The child knows she told him. The question is not a request for an answer; it is a way of *making the listener admit something he already knows.* It pulls the truth out of the listener's own mouth.

In Arabic, the scholars of balāghah call this **istifhām taqrīrī** — an interrogative whose purpose is *taqrīr*, affirmation. It is a question shaped to extract a "yes." And it is more powerful than a statement, because a statement *tells* you a fact, but a taqrīrī question makes you *testify* to it. You become a witness, not an audience.

Now look at how the ayah opens:

> **أَلَمْ — *a-lam* — "Did not...?"**

This is the interrogative *hamza* fused onto the negative particle *lam*. Literally: "Is it not the case that He did *not*...?" And here is the beauty of the construction: **a negative, placed inside a question, flips into the strongest possible affirmation.** "Did He not make their scheme go astray?" can only be answered one way. *Yes. He did. We saw it. Everyone saw it.*

[PAUSE]

Notice *who* is being asked. This event — what later generations would call the Year of the Elephant — was within living memory of the first listeners. There were old men in Makkah who had been there. The grandfather of the very Messenger reciting these words had stood before Abraha. So when Allah says *a-lam* — "Did He not?" — He is not informing the Quraysh of something new. **He is calling them to the witness stand about something they already lived through.**

And this is the genius of opening with a question instead of a report. A report you can receive passively. A question demands a response *from inside you.* Allah is not lecturing the Quraysh about His protection of the House. He is making them say it themselves: *Yes, You did. We have no other explanation. We were there, and we had nothing to do with it.*

Here is what this single particle reveals before we even reach the main verb: **the proof of Allah's care is not something you are asked to believe. It is something you are asked to remember.** It is already in your history. The question is only whether you will testify to what you have already seen.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: The Most Intelligent Word for Evil — *Kayd* (كَيْد)

Now to what they actually did. Allah calls it **كَيْدَهُمْ — *kaydahum* — "their kayd."**

Let me teach this word, because English flattens it. We translate *kayd* as "plot" or "scheme," and that's not wrong, but it loses the texture. *Kayd*, in the Arabic lexicons, is not just any plan. It is **scheming that is hidden, layered, and clever** — a design whose outward face is different from its inner intent. It is the cunning of someone who has thought several moves ahead. *Kayd* implies *intelligence.* It implies *effort.* It implies a mind that has engineered a path to a goal and concealed the seams.

This is not a word of contempt. The Quran does not call Abraha's campaign *jahl* (stupidity) or *ʿabath* (random folly). It honors it, in a sense, with the word *kayd* — because it *was* clever. It was strategic. It had logistics, technology, ideology, and a clear objective. Allah, in the very next surahs and across the Quran, even uses a form of this root for *Himself*: *"wa akīdu kaydā"* — "and I too devise a devising" (86:16). So this is not a small or foolish thing being described. **Kayd is human intelligence at full stretch, bent toward a goal.**

Now hold that. Allah has just named their effort with the most respectful word available for an evil design. He is *not* underestimating Abraha. He wants you to see the plan in its full sophistication — the elephant, the army, the engineering, the years of resentment turned into a campaign.

Why build it up like this?

[PAUSE]

Because *the bigger you make the kayd, the more devastating what comes next becomes.* If Allah had said "their foolishness," then its collapse would prove nothing. Fools fail on their own. But when the *cleverest* plan, the *best-resourced* plan, the plan that should have worked — when *that* is the thing undone, you learn something about the One who undid it. **The size of the scheme is a measurement of the One who broke it.**

And notice the pronoun: kayda-**hum** — "*their* scheme." Allah attaches the plot firmly to them. It is *theirs*. They own it completely. Which means whatever happens to it, happens to a thing that belonged entirely to human will and human cleverness. There is no ambiguity about whose plan failed.

So far we have: a question that makes you testify, and a scheme that Allah deliberately presents at its full, intimidating strength. Now — what did He do to it?

---

### Element Three: He Didn't Just Defeat the Plan — He Made It *Wander* — *Taḍlīl* (تَضْلِيل)

Here is the word the whole ayah has been building toward. Allah did not put their scheme into *defeat*. He did not put it into *failure*. He put it:

> **فِى تَضْلِيلٍ — *fī taḍlīl* — "into a making-astray."**

Let me teach the shape of this word, because the *shape itself* is carrying meaning.

The root is **ض-ل-ل (ḍ-l-l)** — and you already know this root, even if you didn't know you knew it. It is the root of *ḍāllīn* — *"ghayr il-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa lā **ḍ-ḍāllīn**"* — "not those who went **astray**" — the last word of Surah al-Fātiḥah you say in every rakʿah. It is the root of being **lost.** Of wandering off the path. Of losing your way.

So the first thing to feel: Allah chose the vocabulary of *spiritual misguidance* to describe what happened to a *military campaign.* He is reaching across categories on purpose. We'll come back to why.

But now look at the *form*. *Taḍlīl* is not the simple maṣdar *ḍalāl* (the bare state of being astray). It is the **Form II maṣdar** — the *tafʿīl* pattern — of the doubled, intensified verb *ḍallala*. And Form II, here, carries a *causative* and *intensive* force. It is the difference between *being lost* and *being made lost.* Between *going astray* and *being driven astray.*

Do you feel the difference?

[PAUSE]

If the ayah had said their scheme was *in ḍalāl*, you might picture a plan that simply wandered off, that failed on its own, that fizzled. But *taḍlīl* — Form II — means **someone took that scheme and actively made it lose its way.** There is an Agent. There is a Hand. The plan did not get lost; the plan *was lost*, by Someone. The very next thing the surah will tell you is *how* — the birds, the stones. But the *verdict* comes first: this clever, well-engineered design was *taken* and *made to wander.*

And there is one more thing the form is doing — a tiny mark with enormous weight. Look at the ending: *taḍlīl-**in***. The *tanwīn*, the indefinite "-in." In Arabic, leaving a word indefinite this way can carry the sense of *magnitude, totality, a kind beyond description.* Not "a misguidance" as in one specific failure, but **utter, immeasurable, complete astray-ness.** A getting-lost so total it can't even be pinned to a single name. Their plan didn't take a wrong turn. It dissolved into pure lostness.

[PAUSE]

Now let me bring you the classical scholars, because they stood around this single word *taḍlīl* like people circling a diamond, each describing a different facet — and together they show you the whole stone.

- **Al-Baghawī** holds it up to the light and says: *fī taḍlīl* means *ʿammā arādū* — their scheme was made to wander **away from what they wanted.** The plan was *aimed* — at the Kaʿbah — and Allah knocked it off its aim. It lost its target. This is *misdirection.*
- **Al-Jalālayn** turns the diamond and sees: *khasāra wa halāk* — **loss and destruction.** Not just deflected, but ruined. The scheme didn't merely miss; it became the cause of their own annihilation.
- **Al-Muyassar and al-Qurṭubī** turn it again: *ibṭāl wa taḍyīʿ* — **nullification and squandering.** Every ounce of effort that went into the *kayd* — the years, the army, the elephant, the logistics — all of it rendered void, *wasted*, poured out onto the ground for nothing.
- And **al-Baghawī** adds the voice of Muqātil: *fī khasāra* — **in pure loss** — and another report: *fī buṭlān* — **in sheer voidness.**

Put the facets together and you see the whole diamond: **their scheme was knocked off its target, stripped of its result, drained of all its invested effort, and turned into the very instrument of their ruin.** That is what one word, *taḍlīl*, holds.

[PAUSE]

There is one more quiet thing here — an *absence* worth noticing. Allah tells you the scheme was put *into* taḍlīl — but He does not tell you *where it ended up.* "Into a making-astray" has no destination. That's the nature of being lost: there *is* no destination anymore. A defeated plan ends *somewhere* — in surrender, in retreat. But a plan made to *wander* just... dissolves into nowhere. The grammar withholds an endpoint because *lostness has no endpoint.* The absence of a destination *is* the meaning.

So the element resolves like this: **Allah did not lower Himself to merely defeating Abraha. Defeat would imply a contest. Instead He reached into the cleverest plan of the age and made it lose its own way — drained it, misdirected it, voided it, and turned it into the road to its owners' ruin — so completely that it has no destination left to name.**

---

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have done together. A **question** that puts you on the witness stand. A **scheme** presented at full, frightening strength. And a **getting-lost** so total that Allah borrows the language of spiritual misguidance to describe it.

That last move — using *the root of being astray* for the fate of an *army* — is not an accident of vocabulary. It is a door. Allah is telling you that what happened on that road north of Makkah is the same thing that happens, every day, in a place much closer to you than Yemen.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Illusion of the Airtight Plan

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, somewhere in your life right now, a *kayd.* I don't mean an evil scheme. I mean a *plan you are counting on.* The career path you've mapped five years out. The financial model in the spreadsheet. The relationship you are managing carefully so it goes a certain way. The version of your future you have engineered in your mind, with contingencies, with backups, with the moves thought several steps ahead.

We are, all of us, in the business of *kayd* — clever, layered, intelligent planning. We live in an age that *worships* it. We have project management apps, life-optimization systems, five-year plans, the entire industry of "having it all figured out." And there is a deep, often unspoken belief underneath all of it: **that if I plan well enough, I am safe.** That sufficient intelligence, applied to my circumstances, puts me in control.

Now meet Abraha.

[PAUSE]

Abraha is what that belief looks like at full scale. He had *everything* you wish your plan had. He had the technology — the elephant, the apex weapon of his world. He had the resources — a state army. He had the motive and the conviction — he genuinely believed he was right. He had the *kayd*, the layered intelligent design, aimed at a clear and achievable goal: a building, made of stone, defended by no one, in a valley whose own people had fled to the hills. By every metric of planning that you and I respect, **Abraha's plan was excellent.** It should have worked. There was, humanly speaking, *nothing in its way.*

And Allah made it *fī taḍlīl.* Lost. Wandering. Voided. Not even defeated in battle — there *was* no battle. The plan simply *lost its way to its own target* and became the road to the planners' destruction.

Here is what I need you to feel, and it is not what you think.

The lesson is *not* "planning is bad." The Quran calls Abraha's effort *kayd* — intelligent design — without insult. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib himself made a plan: he took his people to the mountains. **The point is not that you should stop planning. The point is about where you locate your *safety.***

ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib said the line that splits the whole universe in two: *"I am the lord of the camels. The House has a Lord."* He planned *and* he knew his plan was not his protection. He acted with full intelligence *and* placed his security entirely outside his own cleverness. Abraha did the opposite: his cleverness *was* his god. His plan was his protection. And the One who actually holds the threads reached in and made the plan wander into nothing.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question this theme leaves on your desk tonight. Not *"should I plan?"* — yes, plan, plan well, plan like ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib went to the mountains. The question is: **where have I quietly located my safety?** In the plan, or in the One who can put any plan *fī taḍlīl* with a flock of birds? Because every plan you have ever made sits, right now, in the hands of the One who decides whether it reaches its target or loses its way.

**The practical shift:** Tomorrow, when you make your plan — and you should — add one sentence to the end of it. The sentence the believer adds: *"...in shāʾ Allāh."* Not as a verbal tic. As a *relocation of safety.* As the conscious admission: *I have done my kayd; the destination belongs to my Lord.* That single phrase is the difference between Abraha and ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib standing on the same road.

---

### Theme Two: The Trap That Swallows the Trapper

Now I want to take the word *taḍlīl* somewhere deeper, because it has a second edge.

Think about the ordinary experience of setting a trap. You dig a pit and cover it with branches so your enemy falls in. You write a deceptive contract so the other party loses. You spread a rumor to bring someone down. There is a universal moral intuition — across every culture, in every grandmother's warning — that the trap has a way of turning around. *He fell into his own pit. The dog returned to its own vomit. The schemer got schemed.* We feel, instinctively, that evil designs have a boomerang built into them.

The Quran states this not as folk wisdom but as a *law of reality*: **"wa lā yaḥīqu al-makru s-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlih"** — "the evil scheme does not encircle anyone except its own people" (35:43). The trap closes on the trapper. This is not poetic justice as a hope. In the Quran it is a *mechanism* — as reliable as gravity.

Now look again at *taḍlīl* and feel the second edge of it.

Abraha came to cause *ḍalāl* in the deepest sense. He came to *misguide* a peninsula — to demolish the House of guidance and redirect the worship of a people toward his own cathedral. His entire project was an act of *making people astray.* He was a *muḍallil* — one who tries to lead others off the path.

And what does Allah do? He puts *his scheme* **fī taḍlīl.** **The man who came to make others lost was himself made lost.** The very thing he intended to inflict became the thing inflicted upon him. He didn't get a *different* punishment; he got *his own intention, reflected back.* He aimed *ḍalāl* at the Kaʿbah and Allah returned *taḍlīl* upon his army. The word is the mirror.

[PAUSE]

This is the most precise kind of divine justice — not just that the wrongdoer is stopped, but that *the wrong he designed becomes the shape of his undoing.* Pharaoh built his power on drowning Israelite sons; he drowned. The people of the Cave-builders... the schemers of every prophet's story — *"they schemed a scheme, and We schemed a scheme, while they perceived not"* (27:50). The kayd returns to its sender. Always. It is structural.

And now turn the mirror toward yourself, because this is not only about Abrahas.

Every time you and I are tempted to "manage" a situation with a little deception — the strategic half-truth, the manipulation dressed as wisdom, the quiet undermining of a rival, the trap we tell ourselves is justified — we are doing a small *kayd*. And the Quran is telling us something sobering and merciful at the same time: **that scheme has a destination, and the destination is *us*.** *Lā yaḥīqu al-makru s-sayyiʾu illā bi-ahlih.* The branches over the pit will hold *your* weight, not your enemy's.

[PAUSE]

There is mercy in this law, not just warning. Because if the evil scheme *always* circles back to its owner, then **you never need to build a counter-scheme to defend yourself against someone else's.** ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib didn't out-plot Abraha. He went to the mountain and let the law of reality do what the law of reality does. When someone schemes against you, you are not required to become a schemer to survive. You are required to do your honest *kayd* — go to your mountain — and trust the One who turns traps around.

**The practical shift:** Two parts, because the mirror cuts both ways. First — audit your own small schemes. Is there a "plan" in your life right now whose mechanism is deception, even slightly? Take it out before it finds its way home to you. And second — when you are *the target* of someone's scheming, resist the pull to answer cunning with cunning. Do your honest work, take refuge in your Lord, and let *taḍlīl* do its silent work on a plan you never had to touch.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bring It Together

Watch how the two halves of this ayah were saying the same thing from two directions all along.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a deliberate ascent: a *question* that makes you testify to what you already witnessed, a *scheme* (kayd) presented at its full intimidating intelligence, and then a *getting-lost* (taḍlīl) so total that Allah borrowed the vocabulary of spiritual misguidance and the grammar of "no destination" to describe it.

The **thematic depths** showed us why those exact words: because the airtight human plan and the boomeranging human trap are the *same illusion* viewed from two angles. The illusion that my cleverness is my safety (Theme One), and the illusion that my cleverness can be aimed at others without returning to me (Theme Two). And both illusions die on the same road north of Makkah, killed by the same five words.

The *question* (*a-lam*) was never really about an elephant. It was Allah saying: *you have already seen, in your own history, exactly what happens to the airtight plan and the clever trap. Now testify.*

### The Architecture

And here is the proof in the structure. Notice that Allah delivers the **verdict before the evidence.** Ayah 2 — *"Did He not make their scheme go astray?"* — is the *ruling*. Only afterward, in ayahs 3, 4, and 5, do the birds arrive, and the stones, and the image of devoured chaff. The court announces the judgment, and *then* shows the camera footage. Why this order?

Because Allah wants you to know the *meaning* before the *mechanism.* The mechanism — birds, stones — could have been anything. He could have used a flood, a wind, an earthquake. The *how* is almost incidental. What matters, what comes *first*, is the *verdict*: **the scheme was made to wander into nothing.** Get the meaning, and the means become a detail. That is why the question of ayah 2 stands at the gate of the surah.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, honestly, have I located my safety — in my plan, or in the One who holds my plan?
2. Is there a *kayd* in my life right now whose mechanism is deception, however small — and do I understand that its destination is *me*?
3. When I am the target of someone's scheming, do I reach for a counter-scheme, or do I go to my mountain and trust the law that traps return to their setters?
4. What "airtight" certainty in my life have I never once said *in shāʾ Allāh* over?
5. The Quraysh testified to what Allah did with the elephant. What event in *my* own history is Allah asking me to testify to — a rescue I survived and then explained away?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The cleverest, best-resourced, most determined human scheme aimed at what Allah protects does not merely fail — He reaches into it and makes it lose its own way into nothing, so that the trap becomes the road to the trapper's ruin.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will make a plan today. You should. Plan with all the intelligence Allah gave you. But somewhere in the making of it, let this ayah lean over your shoulder and whisper the question it asked the Quraysh — *did He not make their scheme go astray?* — and let it relocate, gently, where your heart is resting its weight. Not on the plan. On the One who decides whether any plan ever arrives.

That is not a smaller way to live. It is the only way to plan without fear.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, You who reached into the scheme of the elephant's army and made it wander into nothing — we have our plans, and we lay them before You knowing they are not our safety; You are. Guard us from the arrogance of Abraha, who trusted his own cleverness, and grant us the certainty of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, who said: the House has a Lord. And if any evil is schemed against us, turn it, by Your law, back upon nothing — and keep our own hands clean of every scheme whose destination is ourselves. Āmīn.*
