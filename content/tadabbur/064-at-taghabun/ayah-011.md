# No Calamity Except by His Permission — The Arrow That Became Direction

## The Ayah

[ayah:64:11] مَآ أَصَابَ مِن مُّصِيبَةٍ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ ۗ وَمَن يُؤْمِنۢ بِٱللَّهِ يَهْدِ قَلْبَهُۥ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

*Mā aṣāba min muṣībatin illā bi-idhni-llāh. Wa man yuʾmin bi-llāhi yahdi qalbah. Wa-llāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm.*

**No calamity strikes except by the permission of Allah. And whoever believes in Allah — He guides his heart. And Allah is, of every thing, All-Knowing.**

---

## Rebuilding the World

Surah at-Taghābun is named after the Day its own listeners will one day stand on — *yawm at-taghābun*, the Day of Mutual Loss and Gain, the Day the cosmic exchange-rate is finally revealed. On that day two people who looked identical in this life — same job, same neighborhood, same funerals attended — will be separated. One will have gained everything. One will have lost everything. And the tragedy of the loser is not only the loss; it is that the loser *traded* for it, willingly, over a lifetime, without noticing what he was giving up.

The whole surah is a meditation on that quiet divergence. What makes two lives that look the same end up at opposite destinations? The surah answers in stages — belief (v. 9), obedience to the Messenger (v. 8), recognition that your family and wealth can become fitna (v. 14-15). And then, right before the end, it gives us verse 11 — the moment the divergence actually *happens* in real time, in an ordinary life.

Because here is the truth the surah is quietly circling: **taghābun is not mainly made on Fridays or in mosques. Taghābun is made in hospital waiting rooms. In layoffs. In the phone call at 3 a.m. In the miscarriage. In the diagnosis.** The ordinary musiba is the place where two identical lives begin to diverge — and neither of the people involved realizes it is happening.

Verse 11 names that moment.

[PAUSE]

Situate yourself in the Madinan community this arrived into. They had already buried martyrs at Uḥud. They had watched the hypocrites smirk when believers lost. They had seen the question settle into their own children: *if Allah loves us, why did we lose at Uḥud? Why did my father not come home?* The surah is arriving into a community that is *raw.* Into hearts that still flinch when the word "musiba" is spoken.

And into that rawness, Allah gives them — and us — eleven Arabic words that have carried the *umma* through fourteen centuries of hospitals and funerals and exiles and layoffs and heartbreaks we will never know the names of.

**The Hook:**

There is a word in this ayah, *muṣība*, that the listener thinks they already understand. Calamity. Trial. Bad thing. That translation is correct — and that translation is also why we miss what Allah actually said. The root image underneath this word changes the entire theology of suffering. And then — just when you think the ayah has done its work by telling you nothing is random — it does something even more surprising. It makes a promise. A very specific promise. About a very specific organ. And it leaves one word unspecified that, once you notice it, reveals the entire architecture of divine mercy embedded in eleven words.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### I. *Muṣība* — the word that is shaped like an arrow

Before we open this word, let us talk about something that has nothing to do with the Quran.

Imagine you are standing at an archery range. An archer draws, aims, releases. The arrow travels through the air and lands. Where does it land? If the archer is skilled — *exactly where he aimed*. Not approximately. Not somewhere near the target. *At* the target. An arrow that strikes the bullseye has done one thing: it has *hit its mark.*

Hold that picture.

In Arabic, there is a family of words built on the root **ṣād-wāw-bāʾ** (ص-و-ب). From this root comes *ṣawāb* — correctness, hitting the mark (we still use this word; we say "anta ʿalā ṣawāb" — you are correct, literally "you are on-target"). From this root comes *ṣayyib* — a downpour of rain, used in Surah al-Baqarah (2:19), because rain comes down and *strikes* the ground with purpose. And from this root comes the Form IV verb *aṣāba* — "to cause something to hit its target."

Now look at what the ayah says: *mā aṣāba min muṣībatin.*

*Muṣība* is not just "a bad thing." It is literally *a thing that strikes its target*. A *direct hit*. An arrow that found exactly where it was aimed.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it is the whole theology of the ayah compressed into a single word.

In English, "calamity" carries the feel of something that *happens* — random, ambient, tragic. A storm rolls in. An illness arises. Bad things occur. The grammatical structure of the English word is passive, undirected, cosmic. It is weather.

*Muṣība* is not weather. *Muṣība* is **archery**.

When Allah calls the thing that struck you a *muṣība*, He has already embedded in the noun itself the claim that **it was aimed**. Before the ayah makes any statement about divine permission, the word He chose to name the event has already said: *this was not drift. This was not accident. Something was loosed, and it found you.*

And notice the sonic texture. Say the word aloud: *mu-ṣī-ba.* The *ṣād* is a heavy, plosive sound — it *strikes* against the palate. The word *sounds* like a hit. Arabic is doing with your mouth what the meaning is doing in your life.

Now add the grammar. Allah says *mā aṣāba min muṣībatin.* That little *min* there is not translated in English — and almost every English rendering just drops it. But in Arabic, this *min* is what grammarians call **min zāʾida** — an "additional" *min* that exists purely to intensify the negation. It is the Arabic equivalent of italicizing "any." The ayah is not saying "no calamity strikes." It is saying *no musiba — no, not any musiba whatsoever, not even the smallest one — strikes except by the permission of Allah.*

And *muṣībatin* is indefinite. Un-particularized. Which in this grammatical position means: **every possible instance**, with nothing excluded. The stubbed toe. The bounced check. The terminal diagnosis. The child who will not speak to you. Every one of them. Every. Single. One.

No arrow has ever reached you that was not aimed.

[PAUSE]

This is what the word has said before the theology has even begun. The theology is just going to make explicit what the morphology has already whispered.

### II. *Bi-idhni-llāh* — not decree, but *conscious permission*

We now arrive at what most translations call "the permission of Allah." And here the listener must slow down, because something important is at stake.

When a Muslim hears "by Allah's permission," the mind immediately reaches for the word *qadar* — divine decree. And there is a sense in which *qadar* is what is being described here. But Allah did *not* use the word *qadar*. He chose a different word. And the word He chose changes how the decree *feels* to the heart receiving it.

The word is *idhn*. Root: **ʾalif-dhāl-nūn** (ء-ذ-ن).

Here is what is remarkable. This same three-letter root gives us another word that you know intimately: *udhun* — the ear. The organ of hearing. The organ of attention.

The same root that gives us *permission* gives us *the ear*.

Sit with that. What is the Arabic language telling us about the inner nature of permission?

To give *idhn* is not merely to allow. It is to *hear* the request, *attend* to it, and *authorize* it *consciously.* It is the opposite of ambient, absent-minded, mechanical decree. *Idhn* is the word you use when a father, looking directly at his child's face, says *yes, you may go.* The eye contact is part of it. The attention is part of it. The permission is *witnessed.*

So when Allah says this musiba reached you *bi-idhni-llāh*, He has not just told you it was aimed. He has told you **He was watching it be loosed.** He has told you that before this arrow left its bow, it passed through His conscious, attentive, deliberate authorization. It did not slip past Him. It did not happen while He was attending to something else. It reached you through **an attention so complete it bears the name of the ear.**

[PAUSE]

Here is where most of us need to pause and say something honestly. For many believers, the phrase "Allah permitted this" has historically been more *chilling* than *comforting*. It can sound cold — like a cosmic bureaucrat signing off on paperwork. The word *idhn* refuses that reading. *Idhn* is not a signature. *Idhn* is a gaze.

The musiba that reached you was witnessed into your life.

And so — before the ayah has made any promise, before it has said anything about what this suffering is *for* — it has already said something to the wounded heart: **you were not alone when it happened. Even before anyone else noticed, He was noticing.**

### III. *Yahdi qalbahu* — and the missing word that is the whole mercy

Now we come to the promise. *Wa man yuʾmin bi-llāhi yahdi qalbah.* "And whoever believes in Allah — He guides his heart."

Before we open the promise, let me name two small grammatical facts that most people walk past, because walking past them is how we miss what is being given.

**First fact:** *yuʾmin* and *yahdi* are both in the jussive mood (*majzūm*). This is the conditional construction in Arabic: *man yuʾmin... yahdi.* "Whoever believes... He guides." The grammatical logic is cause-and-effect, locked together. The guidance is not a *possibility* for the believer; it is the *automatic consequent.* If the antecedent is there, the consequent follows. Grammar itself has made the promise airtight.

**Second fact** — and this is the one I want you to feel. Look at the object of *yahdi*.

Allah does *not* say *yahdihi* — "He guides *him*." He says *yahdi qalbah* — "He guides **his heart.**"

[PAUSE]

Why specify? Why name the organ?

Because a person under musiba does not primarily need his *feet* redirected, or his *hands* redirected, or his *mind* redirected. He needs his **heart** redirected. The musiba is not, at the deepest level, a physical problem. The hospital bill, the funeral, the layoff — those are surface. Underneath them is a heart that is asking: *why me? does He love me? is the universe hostile? am I being punished? should I still pray?* The battle of the musiba is fought in the chest, not in the limbs.

And Allah, knowing exactly where the real wound lives, names exactly the organ He will reach.

He does not promise to remove the arrow. He promises to **turn the heart that was struck by it.**

Now stay with me, because the most beautiful linguistic fact about this ayah is something Allah **did not say**.

He said *yahdi qalbah* — He guides his heart. But He did **not** say *guides his heart to what*. There is no *ilā* ("to") phrase. There is no destination.

In Arabic, when the verb *hadā* ("to guide") is used, the listener expects a *to-where* clause. Guide to the straight path. Guide to the truth. Guide to Himself. The Quran provides such clauses constantly. But here — in the ayah specifically about the believer hit by musiba — the destination is **left unspecified.**

That absence is not an oversight. That absence is the whole mercy.

Because every believer's musiba is different, and every believer's heart needs to be guided to a different thing at the moment of the strike. One heart needs to be guided to *ṣabr* — the capacity to wait. Another heart needs to be guided to *riḍā* — the capacity to be at peace with what happened. Another heart needs to be guided to *tawba* — to realize this is a call back. Another needs to be guided to *action* — a door to walk through that only opens after the old door closes. Another simply needs to be guided to *the next breath.*

If Allah had specified the destination, He would have narrowed the mercy. By leaving it open, He has told every believer in every musiba across fourteen centuries: **whatever your heart specifically needs to be guided to in this particular strike — that is where I will take it.** The unspecified destination is the generous destination.

Most English translators smuggle in a word to fill the gap. They write "He will guide his heart *aright*" or "guide his heart *to the truth*." They do this because English is uncomfortable with the open end. But the open end is the point. The open end is where every believer's specific musiba is met by a specific mercy the believer himself could not have named in advance.

[PAUSE]

So read the whole ayah again with what you now know.

*No arrow that reached you was unaimed. Every arrow passed through His attentive gaze before it was loosed. And if you are a believer — the very thing that was struck, your heart, is the very organ He will reach into and turn toward whatever you most need to see.*

That is what eleven Arabic words have said.

### Bridge

Before we move into the themes, let us name what the linguistic construction has shown: Allah did not give the believer a reason for his suffering. He gave him a **direction** inside it. The difference between *reason* and *direction* is the difference between answering the mind and guiding the heart — and verse 11 very deliberately chose the second.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words, but about the universe the believer lives inside.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Two Comforts — and which one you are actually choosing

There is a conversation happening in our cultural moment that most Muslims are downstream of without realizing it.

The dominant secular framework for suffering today is what we might call *meaningless-universe comfort*. It goes like this: *the cancer was random. The miscarriage was random. The accident was random. The universe does not care about you specifically — and you should find peace in that, because if no one aimed this at you, no one is punishing you, and you are free of the weight of asking "why me."* This is offered as comfort. And to be fair, for many people, it *is* a kind of comfort. It absolves.

The opposite framework — the one verse 11 presents — is *meaningful-universe comfort*: the musiba was aimed. It passed through His attentive gaze. It is addressed to you, specifically, by name.

Here is the honest part that believers rarely say aloud. *Meaningful-universe comfort is heavier.* Because if it was aimed, then the question "why" is a real question with a real answer — even if that answer is hidden from you. The random-universe view says: *there is no question, so stop asking.* The Quranic view says: *the question is real, and the One Who aimed it is also the One Who sees you asking it.*

Which of these is actually more comforting?

Here is the test. The person standing over a fresh grave has two possible whispers available. Whisper one: *this was an accident of atoms, and your grief is a chemical response to a random event.* Whisper two: *this was witnessed. Loosed deliberately. And the One who loosed it knows your name and is close enough to your chest right now that if you turn toward Him, your heart will be redirected.*

Grief actually knows which of these is closer to the truth. Grief has never, in the history of human beings, responded well to *"it was random."* Grief responds to *"it was seen."*

This is why the random-universe framework fails precisely where it is needed most — in hospital waiting rooms, at gravesides, at the edge. It works as an intellectual position in a coffee shop. It collapses at the bedside.

What the Quran offers is the opposite. It is heavier as a *concept* and lighter as a *companion*. It does not make the arrow easier to bear — but it walks with you while you bear it.

**And here is the practical application:** the next musiba that reaches you — and one will — watch which comfort you reach for. Watch whether your first thought is *"bad luck"* or *"bi-idhni-llāh."* The word you reach for first is your actual theology. Not the one you post about. The one you *flinch toward* when struck.

If your flinch is toward *bi-idhni-llāh*, the ayah's second half activates automatically. If your flinch is toward *bad luck*, the ayah's second half is unreachable — not because Allah withheld it, but because you stood outside the conditional that the jussive construction set up.

### Theme 2: The taghābun in miniature — why two people in the same ICU do not end up in the same place

Come back to the name of the surah. *At-Taghābun*. Mutual loss and gain. The day the exchange-rates are revealed.

The thing that makes taghābun uncomfortable is that it is not made on Judgment Day. It is *revealed* on Judgment Day. It was *made* in life — and the trading happened in moments both parties thought were ordinary.

Verse 11 shows you one of those moments.

Two people are in the same ICU, on the same night. Both have received bad news. The same diagnosis, the same prognosis, the same doctor standing awkwardly in the hallway. From the outside — identical circumstances. Not merely similar. *Identical*. An observer would say: *these two are going through the same thing.*

They are not going through the same thing.

For the first person, a heart is being guided. *Yahdi qalbah* is happening *right now, in real time*, in that ICU, in that chest. Maybe the direction is toward *ṣabr.* Maybe it is toward a phone call that needs to be made to an estranged sibling. Maybe it is toward a prayer that the person has not prayed in years. Maybe it is simply toward *Allah is here*. Whatever it is, this person is being *turned*. The musiba that struck them is becoming a *compass*. The arrow that landed in the chest is being used, from the inside, to point somewhere. The wound is still a wound. But a direction is growing out of it.

For the second person, the heart is flailing. The same event is happening externally, but nothing inside is being redirected. The event arrives and leaves. It leaves bitterness, or confusion, or numbness, or rage — but it does not leave a direction. Because the jussive construction of the ayah requires *yuʾmin* as its antecedent, and if that antecedent is not firing, the *yahdi* is not activating.

Here is what is haunting. If you were standing in the hallway watching, you would see two identical scenes — two patients, two families, two sets of tears. You would not be able to tell which one was in the ayah's first clause and which one was not. Taghābun is invisible *in the moment*. You only see the divergence later — in the year after, in the decade after, on the Day.

This is why the surah is named the way it is. Because the believer who has internalized verse 11 is, quite literally, on a different trajectory from the person next to him, even when their circumstances are carbon-copies. The divergence is being made *inside the chest*, one musiba at a time, across a lifetime.

**Practical application:** the next time you are hit with something, do not ask the surface question. The surface question is *why did this happen?* and that question is almost never answered. Ask the verse 11 question instead: *where is my heart being guided, right now, inside this?* The second question has an answer. And the answer is usually discoverable within the first minute of honest attention. A direction appears — sometimes small ("call your mother"), sometimes enormous ("this career was the wrong one"), but a direction appears. That direction is Allah doing *yahdi qalbahu* in real time. Catch it before it fades. That is the move that builds the gain side of your personal taghābun.

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Look now at what the ayah has quietly been doing as a piece of architecture.

It opens with a universal fact — *no musiba strikes except by His permission.* This fact is true *of everyone*, believer or not. The arrows are aimed for everyone. The attentive gaze is there for everyone. The ayah begins in the third person, impersonal: *mā aṣāba min muṣībatin.* It is describing the world.

Then it pivots — *wa man yuʾmin bi-llāhi* — "and whoever believes." The ayah narrows. Now we are inside the conditional. The universal has become particular. And the particular consequence is an *inward* one: *yahdi qalbah* — He guides his heart. The ayah has moved from the outer universe (arrows flying) to the inner chamber (a heart being turned).

And then it closes: *wa-llāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm.* "And Allah is, of every thing, All-Knowing." The camera zooms back out. From the inner chamber, back to the cosmos.

Do you see what just happened?

**Outer (the aimed arrow) → Inner (the guided heart) → Outer (the knowing God).**

The ayah is shaped like a chamber. The musiba is the door. The heart-guidance is what happens *inside*. And then the ayah exits you back into the world, but now the world you re-enter is different — because it is a world whose God *knows every thing.* Including the exact hit that reached you. Including the exact direction your heart needed. Including what He is going to do with this pain before you are even able to ask.

The architecture of the ayah is *the architecture of the musiba itself*. Something strikes you from outside. Something is transformed on the inside. And you return to the outside, carrying a redirection you could not have manufactured alone.

This is not an accident of composition. This is the ayah enacting what it describes.

And notice the final word: *ʿalīm*. Intensive form — *faʿīl* — "the One whose knowledge is saturating." The ayah does not end with a verb of action. It does not end with "He will reward" or "He will replace." It ends with *knowledge*. Because the last thing a believer under musiba needs is the assurance that this was *seen* — all the way down, including the parts you cannot articulate, including the parts you yourself do not yet know are wounded. *ʿAlīm* is the word that says: *He got all of it.*

### Bringing it Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic finding was: **nothing that reached you was unaimed, and the thing Allah promised to move inside you was left deliberately unspecified so that every specific wound could meet a specific mercy.**

The thematic finding was: **two people inside the same musiba are not actually inside the same event — because belief is the hinge that turns an arrow into a direction, and the divergence of two identical lives is made one musiba at a time.**

These are not two lessons. They are one lesson standing in two places. The reason the destination of *yahdi* was left unspecified is the same reason that no two believers' taghābun is identical — because Allah is building each of us through *our* specific hits, toward *our* specific redirections, with an attention (*idhn*) so precise it bears the name of His ear.

### Questions to Carry

1. The last real musiba that reached you — small or large — what was your first flinch-word? Did you reach for *"bad luck,"* or did you reach for *"bi-idhni-llāh"*? What does that flinch reveal about what you actually believe, underneath what you say you believe?

2. Inside that same musiba — did you ever stop to ask *"where is my heart being guided right now?"* If you had asked, what do you think the answer might have been? Is that answer still available to you?

3. When you picture Allah's permission of your pain, do you picture a signature on a form, or a gaze? If it is the form — what would change in your heart if you let the word *idhn* replace the word *qadar* in how you internalize this? Just for a week. Just as an experiment.

4. Taghābun is being made in the small hits, not only the large ones. What is the next small musiba you can already see coming — a conversation you are dreading, an appointment, a difficulty — and what would it mean to walk into it *expecting* a *yahdi qalbahu* moment inside it?

5. If your heart were guided today — right now, reading this — where do you suspect it would be turned? Do you already know the direction, and have you been avoiding it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The same arrow reaches every life; belief is what lets the arrow become a direction.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will be hit. That is not the question. The ayah takes it as a given. The question is whether, when it happens, you will stand inside the first clause or outside it — whether the universe you inhabit is one where the arrow drifted in from nowhere, or one where it was loosed through an attentive gaze toward the exact chest that would, by belief, turn it into direction.

The invitation is small and enormous at the same time. Move the flinch-word. When the next thing strikes — and it will — let your first whisper be *bi-idhni-llāh*. Not as resignation. As recognition. As the doorway through which the second clause is allowed to activate inside your chest.

The rest of the ayah is waiting on the other side of that whisper.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma,* whatever arrow You have loosed toward us today, let us receive it as *muṣība* — as something aimed, not something drifted. Teach our first flinch-word to be *bi-idhnika* and not *bad luck*. And when we are struck — guide our hearts. Not to where we think they should be guided. To wherever You, who know every thing, know they most need to be turned. *Āmīn.*
