# Left Standing
### A Reflection on Surah Al-Jumu'ah, Ayah 11

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch the meaning.

> **وَإِذَا رَأَوْا تِجَارَةً أَوْ لَهْوًا انفَضُّوٓا۟ إِلَيْهَا وَتَرَكُوكَ قَآئِمًا ۚ قُلْ مَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ خَيْرٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهْوِ وَمِنَ ٱلتِّجَـٰرَةِ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ خَيْرُ ٱلرَّٰزِقِينَ**

*Wa-idhā raʾaw tijāratan aw lahwan infaḍḍū ilayhā wa-tarakūka qāʾiman. Qul mā ʿinda-llāhi khayrun mina-l-lahwi wa-mina-t-tijārah. Wa-llāhu khayru-r-rāziqīn.*

> "And when they saw a transaction or an amusement, they scattered off toward it and left you standing. Say: 'What is with Allah is better than amusement and than transaction. And Allah is the best of providers.'"

### Let me rebuild the world this verse came down into.

It is Friday in Madinah. Not Friday as a weekend — Friday as the day Allah had just made the axis of the believing community's week. The surah you are at the end of opened with something enormous: *yusabbiḥu lillāhi mā fī as-samāwāti wa mā fī al-arḍ* — everything in the heavens and everything in the earth is glorifying Allah, right now, continuously. The galaxies. The dust. Then the surah narrows, like a camera pulling down from the cosmos to a single city, to a single hour: *when the call is made for the prayer on the day of Jumuʿah, hasten to the remembrance of Allah and leave off trade* (62:9).

So the stage is set with the highest possible stakes. The whole universe is in a state of *tasbīḥ*. And the believers have just been told: for one hour, on one day, drop the marketplace and come stand in that current of glorification with the rest of creation.

Now picture the Prophet ﷺ standing on the minbar. Standing — *qāʾiman*. Delivering the khuṭbah. The words coming out of his mouth are not his own agenda; they are *dhikr Allah*, the remembrance the verse just commanded them toward.

And into this hour, a caravan arrives.

A trade caravan from Syria — *ʿīr* — long-awaited, carrying goods the city needed. In some narrations it came with the beating of drums, the customary fanfare announcing that the merchants had returned. It is reported in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, on the authority of Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh, that the people streamed out toward it until almost no one was left with the Prophet ﷺ — in the narration, only about twelve remained. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both transmit this occasion.

So hold the image. The man Allah chose to carry His final speech to humanity is standing, mid-sermon, mid-*dhikr*. And the congregation — the believers — turn at the sound of the drums and the sight of the goods, and they pour out of the mosque toward the marketplace.

And the verse freezes that exact instant. Forever.

### Here is the hook.

There is one word in this verse for what the people did. Not "they left." Not "they went." A single, specific, almost violent verb that Arabic reserves for a particular kind of motion. And once you understand what that word is actually describing — what it pictures — you will never read this verse as a story about a caravan again. You will read it as a verse about you, and about a particular thing your heart does many times a day.

And there is a second thing: notice that the Prophet ﷺ is left **standing**. Of all the words Allah could have used to describe what was done to him, He chose *qāʾiman*. Stay with that. We will come back to why being left *standing* is a different wound than being left at all.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Infaḍḍū* — The Scattering

Before I open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work, because this verb is built in a particular way and the construction is doing the work.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — what scholars call *forms* or *abwāb*, numbered I through X. The base form, Form I, is the bare action: *faʿala*, "he did." But Arabic can take that same three-letter root and pour it into a different mold, and the mold changes the meaning in a predictable way.

There is one mold — **Form VII**, the pattern *infaʿala* — that has a very specific flavor. Grammarians call it *muṭāwiʿ*: the **reflexive** or **resultative** form. It describes something *happening to* the subject, an effect that overtakes them, often something that cannot be undone. *Kasara* (Form I) means "he broke it." *Inkasara* (Form VII) means "it broke" — it shattered, it came apart, the breaking happened *to* it. *Qaṭaʿa*, "he cut it"; *inqaṭaʿa*, "it was severed, it snapped off."

Form VII is the form of things coming apart.

Now. The verb in our verse:

> **ٱنفَضُّوٓا۟** — *infaḍḍū*

Hear the construction: *in-faḍḍ-ū*. Form VII. The root is **fā-ḍād-ḍād**, *f-ḍ-ḍ*.

Let me tell you what this root means, because this is where it opens. *Faḍḍa* — in the classical lexicons — is used for breaking a seal, for scattering a gathering, and, most vividly, **for a string of pearls when the thread snaps and the pearls burst apart and roll in every direction across the floor.** That is the home image of this root. *Infiḍāḍ* — the noun — is exactly that: the bursting-apart of something that *was* held together.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened to the verse?

Allah did not say the people "walked out." He did not say they "left." He chose the verb that pictures **a strung necklace whose thread is cut.**

Because that is precisely what the congregation was. Look at the surah they were standing inside of. The opening verse: everything in the heavens and the earth is in a single, unified act of *tasbīḥ*. The command of 62:9: come together, all of you, to the remembrance. A congregation in the Friday prayer is not a crowd. It is a *strung* thing — bodies in rows, aligned, threaded together on the single thread of *dhikr Allah*, every pearl held in its place by the same cord.

And the drums sound, the caravan appears, and *infaḍḍū* — the thread snaps. The pearls burst. They scatter, each one rolling toward the thing that pulled it.

Notice something about Form VII here. It is the *reflexive* form — the form of something that *happens to you*. The grammar is quietly telling the truth about distraction: it does not feel like a decision. It feels like a force. The thread didn't ask permission. One moment you are strung, aligned, held in the row; the next moment something glints in the corner of your eye and you have *come apart* and you are already moving and you barely noticed the snap. That is not *I chose to leave*. That is *I scattered*. The grammar knows the difference.

[PAUSE]

And now listen to the *sound* of the word. *Infaḍḍū.* That heavy, emphatic **ḍād** — the letter Arabs themselves called the hardest letter in the language, the letter they named their tongue after, *lughat al-ḍād*. And it is **doubled** — *faḍḍa*, with the shadda, the consonant struck twice. The word does not glide. It bursts. Say it: *in-FAḌ-ḍū*. There is a snap in the middle of the mouth, a scattering of breath. The word *sounds like* a thread breaking and beads spilling.

This is one word. And it has already told you: what looks like a crowd leaving a mosque is, in the sight of Allah, a necklace coming apart.

Here is what this single verb reveals: **distraction is not subtraction, it is disintegration.** When the seen thing pulls and you turn toward it, you are not simply *somewhere else* — you have come *apart* from the thing that was holding you together.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Qāʾiman* — Left Standing

Now look at what they left.

> **وَتَرَكُوكَ قَآئِمًا** — *wa-tarakūka qāʾiman* — "and they left **you** standing."

Two things to notice, and the first is small but it is a needle in the heart.

*Tarakū-**ka**.* They left **you**. The *kāf* — the "you," second person, singular. The address turns. A moment ago the verse was narrating in the third person: *they* saw, *they* scattered. And suddenly Allah pivots and speaks directly to His Messenger: *they left **you***. This turn — the rhetoricians call it *iltifāt*, the sudden swivel of address — is not decoration. It is intimacy. Allah is no longer describing an event to a bystander. He has turned to face the one who was wounded, and He names the wound to his face: *they left you.* Before any correction is issued, before any argument is made, Allah first stands beside His Messenger and acknowledges what was done to *him*, personally.

And the second thing — the word He chose for the wound. Not "they left you speaking." Not "they left you on the minbar." **Qāʾiman.** *Standing.*

Let me make you feel what *standing* means here, because we have all lived it.

Think of the most ordinary version. You are mid-sentence, telling someone something that matters to you, and they glance at their phone, stand up, and walk toward someone more interesting — and you are left there. Standing. Your sentence still hanging in the air, unfinished. The particular humiliation of that is not that they disagreed with you. It is that they did not even let you *finish*. You were still *up*, still *given*, still in the posture of offering — and they had already turned the page on you.

*Qāʾiman* is the posture of someone in the middle of giving. To stand and address people is to be exposed, elevated, *offering* yourself. The Prophet ﷺ was not seated and at rest. He was **up** — in the very posture of service, mid-gift, his hands still open — and the room emptied around him.

[PAUSE]

Now go deeper. Whose words was he standing to deliver? Not his own. The *dhikr Allah* — the remembrance the verse two ayāt earlier had commanded them to *hasten* toward. So when they walked out on the man standing and speaking, they were not merely rude to Muḥammad ﷺ. They turned their backs on the very *remembrance* they had been told to run toward. The drum of a caravan outranked, for them in that instant, the speech of Allah on the lips of His Prophet.

And here is the quiet devastation of the word *qāʾiman*: **it preserves his dignity even as it records their offense.** He did not chase them. He did not sit down in defeat. He did not run after the caravan to call them back. He remained *standing*. The verb of their scattering is *infaḍḍū* — a bursting, a coming-apart, all motion and noise. And against it, one still word: *qāʾiman*. Standing. Upright. Unmoved.

The whole moral architecture of the verse is in the contrast between those two words. **They scattered. He stood.** Everything that was true and worth staying for did not move. It was the ones who left who came apart.

Here is what this word reveals: **the thing you abandon for the glittering distraction does not collapse when you leave it. It stands. It remains, upright and whole, waiting — and the loss is entirely yours.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Reversal — and the Pronoun That Confessed

This last element is the most delicate, and it rewards slow reading. There are two pieces.

**First, a single pronoun.**

Look again at the opening: *when they saw a transaction **or** an amusement* — *tijāratan **aw** lahwan*. Two things. Trade, or amusement. The caravan was the trade; the drums and fanfare were the amusement. Two distinct things are named.

But then: *infaḍḍū **ilayhā*** — "they scattered off toward **it**." Toward *it* — singular. Feminine singular. *Ilayhā*, not *ilayhimā* (toward the two of them).

Two things were seen. But they rushed toward **one**.

Which one? The feminine singular pronoun *-hā* points back to *tijārah* — "trade," which is grammatically feminine — not to *lahw*, "amusement," which is masculine. Al-Jalālayn says it plainly: *ilayhā* — meaning toward the trade, *because it was their true object, not the amusement.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with what the grammar just did. The verse offered them two covers — *trade or amusement*. But the single pronoun **quietly confesses what the heart actually wanted.** The drums were the excuse; the goods were the magnet. We dress our distractions in the language of the serious — "I have to take this," "it's about work," "it's a transaction" — but Allah's grammar reaches past the stated reason and names the real pull. One pronoun, and the alibi collapses. The heart wanted the *tijārah*, and *-hā* knows it.

**Second, the reversal.**

Now watch the order of the two words across the verse. When the people *see*, the verse lists them: **trade** first, then amusement — *tijāratan aw lahwan*. Trade leads. That is how it presented itself to them: a serious matter, a livelihood, with a little fanfare attached.

But when Allah issues His correction, the order **flips**:

> *What is with Allah is better than the **amusement** and than the **trade*** — *khayrun mina-l-**lahwi** wa-mina-t-**tijārah***.

Now amusement comes first and trade comes last.

This is a *chiasmus* — the rhetorical mirror, where the second list reverses the first: A-B becomes B-A. And it is never accidental in the Qur'an. So what is it doing?

Consider this. In their eyes, the *tijārah* was the weighty, defensible thing — real, important, worth breaking the row for — and the *lahw* was the lighter, almost embarrassing add-on. The order *tijārah, then lahw* is the world's ranking: serious thing first, frivolous thing second.

When Allah answers, He does not merely outbid both. He **re-ranks** them. By placing *lahw* first in His correction, He addresses their excuse on its own terms — *even your "amusement," the lightest thing, what is with Allah is better than that* — and then He moves to the thing they were proud of: *and better than your trade too, the thing you thought justified all this.* He climbs from their flimsiest reason to their strongest one and overtops both. There is nothing left to stand on.

And there is a second reading the order invites — offer it as a possibility, not a certainty: that when you abandon the remembrance of Allah to chase it, **your serious trade has been reduced to the level of mere amusement.** The thing you called *tijārah* — weighty, justified — Allah lists it last, after the *lahw*, as if to say: in the scale that actually matters, the deal you broke the prayer for and the drumbeat you turned your head at are the same kind of thing. Both are *dunyā* glinting. Both are the seen pulling you off the thread.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the three elements together for a moment before we leave the language behind.

The verb told us *how* they left: they came apart, a necklace bursting. The participle told us *what* they left: someone standing, mid-gift, who did not move. And the structure — the confessing pronoun and the reversed list — told us *why* and exposed the lie inside the "why": they chased the seen thing and dressed it as serious, and Allah named both the real magnet and its true, diminished weight.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of the Seen

Let me begin not with the verse but with your day.

Your phone is face-down on the table. You are in a conversation, or in prayer, or reading something that matters. And it buzzes. Or the screen lights up — you didn't even hear it, you just *saw* the glow in your peripheral vision. And something in you, faster than thought, faster than any decision you would endorse, turns toward it.

You know this. We all know this. There is a force in us that bends, automatically, toward whatever is *immediate and visible* — and bends *away* from whatever is real but unseen, deferred, requiring patience. The buzzing phone defeats the present person. The notification defeats the prayer. The visible defeats the invisible almost every time, and it does not even feel like a contest, because the invisible never gets to make its case before you have already turned your head.

Now look at the first word of the people's action in our verse. Not "when they *wanted* trade." Not "when they *remembered* their business." **Wa-idhā *raʾaw*** — "and when they *saw*." The trigger is the eye. The seen thing appears, and the scattering follows. *Raʾaw* — they saw — and the very next word is *infaḍḍū* — they came apart. The sequence is almost instantaneous. See, scatter. There is barely a verb of decision in between. The seen thing reached past their reason and pulled the thread directly.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the deepest patterns the Qur'an traces in the human being: we are creatures dangerously governed by the *present and the visible*. The Qur'an has a name for the part of reality you cannot see but must believe — *al-ghayb*, the Unseen. And it tells us, again and again, that the entire test of faith is the test of whether the *unseen-but-real* can hold your heart against the *seen-but-lesser*.

Look at how the very next surah, just after this one, opens its own diagnosis of the hypocrites' hearts. Or look at how the Qur'an describes the lover of the immediate: *they love the fleeting one (al-ʿājilah) and leave behind a heavy Day* (75:20-21). *ʿĀjilah* — the thing that is *right here, right now.* The caravan is the *ʿājilah*. The drumbeat is the *ʿājilah*. And what was on the minbar — the remembrance of Allah, the path to *mā ʿinda-llāh* — that was the *ghayb*, the deferred, the thing you have to believe in to value.

And here is the hard mercy of this verse: the people who scattered were not disbelievers. They were *the believers* — Companions, sincere, the best generation. And still, in that unguarded instant, the seen thing snapped the thread. Which means this is not a flaw of the wicked. **It is a vulnerability of the human heart itself**, and even faith does not make you immune to the *pull* — it only gives you something to hold the thread with.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the battle of your faith is not fought mainly in moments of grand decision. It is fought in the half-second between *raʾaw* and *infaḍḍū* — between *seeing* the glinting thing and *scattering* toward it. That half-second is the whole arena. And you win it not by willpower in the moment — the moment is too fast for willpower — but by *what you have valued in advance*, by training your heart, before the caravan ever appears, to know that *mā ʿinda-llāh* is the weightier thing. You strengthen the thread when the drums are silent, so that it holds when they sound.

A concrete application: the next time you are in *ṣalāh* and the thought of the unanswered message, the undone task, the glinting "I should just quickly—" pulls at you, recognize it by its true name. That is the caravan. That is *infaḍḍū* trying to happen inside your own chest. And the answer the verse gives is not "feel guilty." It is *qul* — say, remind yourself, right there: *mā ʿinda-llāhi khayr.* What is with Allah is better. Hold the thread.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Chasing Provision, Abandoning the Provider

Here is the second theme, and it turns the whole verse inside out.

Why did the people run to the caravan? Not for sport — the pronoun told us. They ran for the *tijārah*. They ran for **provision**. Livelihood. Sustenance. The very real, very legitimate human need to eat, to feed a family, to secure tomorrow. Let us be fair to them: this was not greed for its own sake. It was the deep, ancient anxiety of *rizq* — will there be enough? — that lives in every one of us and pulls hardest of all.

And it is *that* anxiety — the fear of missing provision — that snapped the thread.

Now read how the verse ends. After *qul mā ʿinda-llāhi khayr*, the final clause:

> **وَٱللَّهُ خَيْرُ ٱلرَّٰزِقِينَ** — *wa-llāhu khayru-r-rāziqīn* — "And Allah is the best of providers."

[PAUSE]

Feel the irony land. They left the gathering to chase *rizq* — and the verse closes by reminding them *who the source of all rizq actually is.* They ran toward the provision and away from **the Provider.** They abandoned the Messenger of *ar-Razzāq* in order to catch the caravan of a merchant — as if the goods on those camels were a more reliable source of sustenance than the One who put the goods on the camels, who sent the caravan, who made the buyer and the seller and the very hunger that drives the trade.

The word *rāziqīn* — "providers," plural — is pointed. The verse acknowledges that there *are* others who seem to provide: the merchant, the employer, the marketplace. People who hand you sustenance. But Allah is *khayru-r-rāziqīn* — the *best* of them, the *real* one behind all of them. Every caravan that ever brought goods to a city was a delivery from Him. The merchant is just the hand; Allah is the source. To bolt toward the hand and leave the Source standing is the most basic confusion of cause there is.

[PAUSE]

Let me bring this into your world, because we make this exact trade constantly.

How often do we sacrifice the *source* of our barakah for the *appearance* of provision? We skip the prayer to make the meeting. We cut the Qur'an short to answer the email. We tell ourselves we cannot afford the time for what is *with Allah* — because the deadline, the deal, the income is *right here* and the reward of Allah is *out there*, unseen, deferred. We are running to the caravan. Every time.

And the verse does not say your trade is evil. Read it carefully. It does not forbid commerce — the same surah, one verse earlier, says *when the prayer is finished, disperse through the land and seek the bounty of Allah* (62:10). Trade is not the enemy. The error was one of **ranking and timing** — placing the lesser, the seen, the *tijārah*, above the greater, the unseen, the *dhikr* — during the one hour Allah had reserved.

So the correction is not "abandon the world." It is the re-ranking we saw in the language: *mā ʿinda-llāhi khayr* — what is with Allah is *better*. Not "the world is nothing." Better. A comparison. Keep your trade — but know where it sits on the scale, and know who supplies it.

And notice, finally, what Allah does *not* specify. *Mā* ʿinda-llāh — "**what**ever is with Allah." He does not name it. He does not say "Paradise is better" or "the reward is better" or list it out. He leaves it as an open, unbounded *mā* — "what is with Allah." Why?

Because the moment you *name* and *bound* the reward, you have made it a quantity, and the human heart immediately starts to compare quantities: *this much reward versus this much profit.* But the unspecified *mā* cannot be weighed against the caravan, because it has no edges. It is *whatever is with Allah* — and what is with Allah is *Allah's*, which means it is without limit. The verse sets a finite, defined thing — *al-lahw, at-tijārah*, named, bounded, countable — against an infinite, undefined thing — *mā ʿinda-llāh*, the open "what." And then it says: the unbounded is *khayr*. Of course it is. You were trading the limitless for a camel-load.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means that every time the anxiety of *rizq* tightens in your chest and pulls you to cut a corner on what you owe to Allah, you have the answer the verse gives: *wa-llāhu khayru-r-rāziqīn.* The caravan does not provide for you. The job does not provide for you. The deal does not provide for you. They are hands; He is the Source. You will not lose your provision by giving Allah His hour — because your provision was never coming from the caravan in the first place. It was coming, the whole time, from the One you were tempted to leave standing.

A concrete application: when work and worship collide — when the *halal* deadline and the prayer fall in the same window — practice trusting the equation of this verse *before* you feel the squeeze. Pray on time and watch what happens to the work. The Companions who stayed standing with the Prophet ﷺ did not starve. The provision came. It always comes from where it was always coming from.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Step back and see the whole shape now, because the verse is built as a perfect mirror.

The first half is the **disease**, narrated: *they saw → they scattered → they left you standing.* Motion, fragmentation, abandonment. Three beats of coming-apart.

The second half is the **cure**, commanded: *Say: what is with Allah is better → than amusement → than trade → and Allah is the best of providers.* Three beats of re-ranking and re-anchoring.

And the hinge between disease and cure is one word: **Qul.** *Say.* The remedy for the scattering is *speech* — the very thing they walked out on. They left the *dhikr*, and the cure is more *dhikr*: *say it.* The thread that snapped is re-strung by the tongue that remembers.

And notice the surah's own arc, end to beginning. It *opened* with all creation in perfect, unbroken *tasbīḥ* — the whole universe strung on one thread of glorification, never scattering. And it *closes* with the believers scattering off a single minbar at the sound of a drum. The surah holds up the cosmos as the model — *everything in the heavens and earth stays on the thread* — and then shows us, in its final verse, how easily we fall off it. The galaxies did not scatter for a caravan. We did.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two sides.

The **verb** *infaḍḍū* — the necklace bursting — *is* the tyranny of the seen: distraction is disintegration, the thread snapping the instant the eye catches the glint.

The **participle** *qāʾiman* — the one left standing, unmoved — *is* the truth about the Provider: what you abandon for the caravan does not fall. It stands. Allah, *khayru-r-rāziqīn*, remains the source whether you run to the caravan or not. The loss of leaving is entirely yours; the One left standing loses nothing.

And the **reversal** — *trade, then amusement* becoming *amusement, then trade* — *is* the re-ranking the second theme demanded: the world insists the *tijārah* is the serious thing; Allah quietly files it last, beneath even the *lahw*, and sets against both the unbounded, unnamable *mā ʿinda-llāh.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *caravan* in my life — the recurring seen thing whose glint, every time, snaps the thread and pulls me off the row?

2. In the half-second between *seeing* and *scattering* — the moment too fast for willpower — what have I valued *in advance* that could hold me? Have I strengthened the thread while the drums were silent?

3. When the anxiety of provision tightens and pulls me to shortchange what I owe to Allah, do I actually believe *wa-llāhu khayru-r-rāziqīn* — that the caravan is only a hand, and He is the source?

4. Whom, or what, have I left *standing* — patient, whole, waiting — while I ran toward something lesser that glittered?

5. When I dress a distraction in the language of the serious — "it's work," "it's important," "it's just a transaction" — what would the confessing pronoun say my heart actually wanted?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the seen and immediate glints, we come apart and scatter toward it and leave the Unseen standing — yet what is with Allah is unnamed and unbounded and *better*, because He Himself is the source of the very provision we abandoned Him to chase.

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not asking you to despise the world or to never trade or to feel guilty for needing *rizq*. It is inviting one quiet correction — a re-ranking. Keep the caravan. Seek the bounty of Allah in the land as the verse before it commands. But know the order. Know who supplies the caravan. And when the drum sounds during your hour with Allah — and it will — let the answer rise in you before your head turns: *mā ʿinda-llāhi khayr.* Hold the thread. Stay standing.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, *Khayr ar-Rāziqīn*, when the seen things glint and pull, let the thread of Your remembrance hold us in the row. Do not let us scatter toward the caravan and leave Your remembrance standing. Make what is with You weightier in our hearts than anything our eyes can catch — and let us trust, without flinching, that our provision was always, only, coming from You.

Āmīn.
