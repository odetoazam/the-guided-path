# The Appointment You Cannot Outrun

> [ayah:62:8] قُلْ إِنَّ ٱلْمَوْتَ ٱلَّذِى تَفِرُّونَ مِنْهُ فَإِنَّهُۥ مُلَـٰقِيكُمْ ۖ ثُمَّ تُرَدُّونَ إِلَىٰ عَـٰلِمِ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَـٰدَةِ فَيُنَبِّئُكُم بِمَا كُنتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

*Qul inna-l-mawta-lladhī tafirrūna minhu fa-innahu mulāqīkum, thumma turaddūna ilā ʿālimi-l-ghaybi wa-sh-shahādati fa-yunabbiʾukum bimā kuntum taʿmalūn.*

**"Say: Indeed, the death from which you keep fleeing — it is going to meet you. Then you will be returned to the Knower of the unseen and the witnessed, and He will inform you of what you used to do."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Moment Before These Words

Before we open a single word, I want you to feel the heat of the room these words walked into.

This is Surah Al-Jumu'ah, in Madinah. A few verses earlier, Allah turns to a community that has made a remarkable claim. They have said — in effect — *we* are the friends of God. *We* are the ones who love the next life, who are close to Him, the chosen, the beloved. And Allah does not argue with the claim philosophically. He does something far more piercing. He proposes a test.

**"Say: O you who claim to be Jews — if you assert that you are the friends of Allah to the exclusion of all other people, then wish for death, if you are truthful"** (62:6).

Think about the surgical precision of that. You say you love to be with God. Fine. Death is the door to being with God. So — long for it. Want it. If your claim is true, the door should look like a homecoming.

And the next verse delivers the verdict: **"But they will never wish for it, because of what their hands have sent ahead"** (62:7). They will never long for it. Not once. Not ever.

[PAUSE]

Now sit in that silence for a moment, because this is the hinge. There is a gap that has just opened — a gap between what the tongue says and what the body does. The tongue says: *I love the Hereafter.* The body says: *get me away from the door that leads there.* And in that gap, something is exposed that no amount of speech could hide.

Verse 8 is the Prophet's commanded reply into that gap. It begins with a single imperative — *Qul.* **Say.** Don't soften it, don't negotiate it. Say this.

And here is the hook, the thing I want you to hunger for as we walk in: this ayah does not threaten them with death. It does something stranger and more unsettling. It tells them that the very thing they are sprinting away from — is sprinting toward them. That the harder they run, the faster the appointment closes. And it does this with one small, grammatically *unexpected* word that classical grammarians stumbled over and could not stop discussing.

Let me show you what they saw.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Pants — تَفِرُّونَ

Let me teach you something simple about Arabic before we touch this word.

In Arabic, the *sound* of a word is rarely an accident. There is a quality the grammarians and the reciters were acutely aware of — that some words are built to *do* with their mouth-feel what they *mean* with their meaning. A word for something soft will often pour; a word for something jagged will often catch in the throat.

Now look at the verb Allah chose for the act of running from death:

**تَفِرُّونَ — *tafirrūna* — "you flee."**

Its root is *fā–rā–rā* (frr). And notice: the rā is doubled. There is a *shadda* sitting on it — a knot of pressure, the same consonant struck twice, trilled, rolling. *Fir-r-r.* Say it out loud and feel what your mouth does. It trills. It vibrates. It is breathless.

Let's sit with this. The root *farra* in the language of the Arabs was the word for the bolting of a startled animal — the panic-flight of a creature that has caught a scent of danger and breaks into a run before the mind has even decided to. It is not a dignified, considered departure. It is a *bolt.* A flinch made into motion.

And Allah did not say *tabʿudūna* (you distance yourselves) or *tatrukūna* (you leave). He said *tafirrūna.* You **bolt.** Like prey.

Notice something else, and this is where it deepens. The verb is in the imperfect tense — *tafirrūna* — which in Arabic carries the sense of the ongoing, the repeated, the *still-happening.* This is not "the death you once fled." It is **the death you are fleeing, right now, continuously, as a way of life.** The fleeing is not an event. It is a posture. A whole orientation of the self.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. Stay with me. They have built an entire identity around loving the afterlife — and the Quran reaches past the identity, past the speeches, and points at one involuntary thing: *the bolt.* The flinch. The body's honest answer. Because you can lie with your tongue, but the animal-flinch away from death does not lie. It reports exactly what you believe about where you are going.

And so the first word of the unmasking is the truest one. Not *what they said* — but *which direction they ran.*

What this single verb reveals: **your flight is your confession.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word That Should Not Be There — فَإِنَّهُۥ

Now I need to slow all the way down, because we have arrived at the place where the grammarians could not stay quiet.

Let me first teach you the structure, plainly. In Arabic, when you open a sentence with *inna* ("indeed"), you are setting up a frame: *inna* + a subject + then its predicate, the thing you say about it. *Inna Zaydan munṭaliqun* — "Indeed Zayd is departing." Clean. The predicate follows the subject directly. You would **never** stick the connector *fa-* ("then/so") in front of the predicate. You would never say *inna Zaydan fa-munṭaliqun* — "Indeed Zayd, *so* he is departing." It breaks the grammar. It sounds wrong to a native ear.

Now read what Allah actually said:

**إِنَّ ٱلْمَوْتَ ٱلَّذِى تَفِرُّونَ مِنْهُ فَإِنَّهُۥ مُلَـٰقِيكُمْ**

*"Indeed the death which you flee from — **fa-innahu** — then surely it will meet you."*

There it is. The forbidden *fa-.* "Indeed the death... **so** surely it will meet you." By the ordinary rules, that *fa-* should not be there.

The grammarian al-Zajjāj noticed this centuries ago, and al-Qurtubī preserves his analysis for us. Al-Zajjāj said: you don't say *inna Zaydan fa-munṭaliqun.* So why here? And his answer opens a door into the meaning.

Here is the composite picture the scholars assembled — and I want you to hear it not as competing theories but as two cameras pointed at the same truth.

**The first camera** (the reading carried in al-Jalālayn): the *fa-* is *zāʾida* — added, extra, a particle of pure emphasis. It is there to *thicken* the certainty. As if the sentence cannot wait for its own predicate and lunges forward: "this death — *and indeed!* — it is going to meet you."

**The second camera** (al-Zajjāj, in al-Qurtubī): that little phrase *alladhī tafirrūna minhu* — "which you flee from" — is secretly carrying the weight of a **condition.** Hidden inside the relative clause is an *if.* The sentence is really saying: *"As for this death — no matter how you flee from it — then surely it will meet you."* And in Arabic, when a clause carries a conditional sense, the answer to that condition is introduced precisely by... *fa-.* So the "wrong" *fa-* is the fingerprint of a hidden *if.* The grammar is bent because a condition is buried inside it.

Do you see what just happened? Put the two cameras together and a single image resolves. **The deviation in the grammar is the futility of the flight made visible in the sentence itself.**

Let me say that again, slowly. The verse is *about* running and not escaping. And the *sentence* literally cannot run in a straight line — it stumbles over an extra *fa-*, it doubles back on itself, *inna* then *fa-inna*, "indeed" then "so indeed" — because the meaning has folded a condition into a statement: *however far you flee, so it meets you anyway.* The structure does, with its broken syntax, exactly what death does to the fleeing soul: it closes the gap. It will not let the sentence get away clean either.

[PAUSE]

This is the kind of thing that should make us tremble a little. Allah took the futility of running and pressed it down into the *grammar* — into one particle that, by the rules, should not exist. The escape is denied even at the level of the letters.

What this feature reveals: **the closing of the gap is written into the bones of the verse. There is no clause clean enough to flee through.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Encounter, Not the Event — مُلَـٰقِيكُمْ

Now hold what we have — the bolting flight, the closed gap — and look at the word that meets it.

**مُلَـٰقِيكُمْ — *mulāqīkum* — "it will meet you."**

Let me teach you the shape of this word, because the shape is the whole point. Its root is *l–q–y* (lqy), the root of *meeting.* But Allah did not use the simplest form of "to meet." He used what Arabic grammarians call **Form III** — the pattern *fāʿala*, here giving us the active participle *mulāqī.*

What is Form III? It is the form of Arabic verbs built specifically to express an action done **toward another — a mutual, two-sided engagement.** Think of *kātaba* — not just "to write" but to *correspond,* to write back and forth with someone. *Jālasa* — not just "to sit" but to *sit with,* to keep someone company. Form III takes a solitary action and makes it a meeting of two parties, an encounter, a facing.

So *mulāqīkum* does not mean death will merely *happen to* you, the way rain happens to a field. It means death will **meet** you — face you — as one party meets another. It is an *encounter.* The word the Quran uses elsewhere for the believer meeting his Lord — *liqāʾ Allah* — comes from this same root and this same posture. A meeting. An appointment kept between two.

Now feel the collision we have built. On one side: *tafirrūna* — the frantic, breathless, trilling bolt of prey. On the other: *mulāqīkum* — calm, long-voweled, flowing, certain, the unhurried language of an appointment that will be kept. Listen to the two sounds against each other. *Tafirrūn* — agitated, doubled, panting. *Mulāqīkum* — smooth, open, serene.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is breathtaking. You are running *away* from death — that is one vector, a body in flight. But death is described not as a force chasing you, but as one who is coming *to meet you* — a second vector, moving toward you with the composure of someone keeping an appointment. Two parties, closing on a single point. And the one who is *fleeing* is frantic, while the one who is *coming* is calm — because only one of them is in any doubt about the outcome.

And then sit with the final irony, the one this whole ayah has been building toward. The believers are promised a *liqāʾ* — a meeting — with Allah as the reward they long for. These people *claimed* to long for that meeting. And here the very same root, the language of the longed-for encounter, is turned around to describe the thing they bolt from. **The meeting you were made for is the meeting you are fleeing.** You were built for *liqāʾ*, and you are spending the imperfect tense of your whole life running from its doorway.

What this word reveals: **death is not an accident that befalls you. It is an appointment that meets you — and it is the door of the very encounter you were created to desire.**

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things, and they are really one thing. A verb that exposes the involuntary flight of the body. A particle that bends the grammar to seal off every exit. And a Form-of-meeting that reveals death to be not a catastrophe rushing at you but an appointment composed and certain, the threshold of the encounter you were made for.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way a human being actually *lives.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Thing You Run From Runs the Field

Let me begin not with death, but with something small and recognizable.

There is an email in your inbox. You know the one. It has been there for nine days. It is from someone you owe an answer you don't want to give, or it contains a number you are afraid to read. And every time your eye passes over it, your stomach tightens and you click something else. You are *managing* it. You are keeping it at bay. And here is the strange psychology of it: the unanswered email does not shrink while you avoid it. **It grows.** The avoidance feeds it. The thing you will not look at begins, slowly, to organize your whole inbox — your whole day — around not looking at it.

Psychologists have a precise name for this now. They call it *experiential avoidance* — the human reflex to push away an unwanted inner experience, a fear, a grief, a truth. And the central, almost cruel finding of an entire field of therapy is this: **avoidance is the engine of suffering, not the escape from it.** The harder you press the thing down, the larger the part of your life it silently controls. You think you are running the field. The thing you flee is running the field.

[PAUSE]

Now lift that up to its highest power. There is one fact that the human being is built, by reflex, to flee. The fact of death. We bolt from it. *Tafirrūna.* And we are extraordinarily inventive about the fleeing. We flee it with noise — fill every silence so the question can't surface. We flee it with busyness — the calendar so full there is no room for the one appointment that matters. We flee it with the endless scroll, the next episode, the next acquisition, a whole civilization engineered, you might say, as a machine for not-looking-at-the-email.

And the ayah does something that the best therapist in the world cannot do. It does not say *stop avoiding, it isn't good for you.* It says something far more disarming. It says: **the thing you are fleeing is, at this very moment, coming to meet you.** *Fa-innahu mulāqīkum.* The flight does not even change the trajectory. The appointment is kept whether you run toward it or away from it; running only means you arrive out of breath and afraid.

Al-Qurtubī, at this very verse, quotes a line of pre-Islamic poetry from Zuhayr that the Arabs already knew in their bones:

> *Whoever dreads the means of death, they reach him still —*
> *even were he to seek the ropes of heaven on a ladder.*

Climb. Build the towers — the Quran says this exactly elsewhere: *"Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, even were you in towers raised high"* (4:78). There is no altitude of distraction, no fortress of wealth, no ladder to the sky, that the appointment does not reach.

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the turn, and it is liberating, not crushing. If the thing you flee is coming regardless — then the fleeing is pure loss. It buys you nothing. It does not delay the meeting by a single breath; it only poisons the breaths you have left with dread. The believer is not asked to *love* dying in some morbid way. The believer is invited to **stop running** — to turn around and face the appointment, and in facing it, to be freed from its tyranny over every other moment. The companions who could say *we love death the way you love life* were not death-obsessed. They were simply people who had stopped fleeing — and so the fear that organizes everyone else's life had no grip on them.

The practical application is almost embarrassingly concrete: **the soul that remembers death often fears it least.** Turn and look at the email. Sit, for two minutes tonight, with the fact you spend the day outrunning. The remembrance you avoid because it seems dark is the precise thing that drains the darkness out of it.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Returned to the One Who Saw the Whole of It

Now follow where the ayah goes *after* the meeting. Because death is not the destination. It is a doorway, and the ayah walks us straight through it.

**ثُمَّ تُرَدُّونَ — *thumma turaddūna* — "then you will be returned."**

Stop on the grammar one more time, because it is doing quiet work. *Turaddūna* is **passive.** You do not *go.* You **are returned.** You are the object, not the subject, of this final motion. Every other journey of your life you chose — where to live, whom to marry, what to chase, what to flee. This last one is the one journey in which you have no agency at all. You are *sent back,* like a thing returned to its owner, to the One from whom you came.

And to whom are you returned? Here the ayah opens out into one of the most total descriptions of Allah in the Quran:

**إِلَىٰ عَـٰلِمِ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَـٰدَةِ — to the Knower of the unseen *and* the witnessed.**

Let me make this human first. Every one of us lives a divided life. There is the *shahādah* — the witnessed self, the part on display: the polished words, the public face, the version of you that others see and that you have curated for them. And there is the *ghayb* — the unseen self: the thought no one heard, the intention behind the smile, the thing you did when the door was locked and the lights were off, the private resentment, the secret kindness, the real reason you did the good thing. Every human being is two people — the witnessed and the unseen — and we spend enormous energy keeping the seam between them hidden.

Now hear it. You are returned to the One for whom **there is no seam.** *ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa-sh-shahādah.* He knows the displayed life *and* the hidden life with a single, equal, undivided knowing. The face you showed the world and the face you showed no one are, to Him, the same brightness. There is no backstage. There is no off-camera. The *ghayb* you guarded so carefully your whole life was only *ghayb* to other people. To Him it was always *shahādah.*

[PAUSE]

And then the final verb, and we end where the ayah ends:

**فَيُنَبِّئُكُم — *fa-yunabbiʾukum* — "and He will inform you."**

The root is *n–b–ʾ* (nbʾ), and Allah uses **Form II** — *nabbaʾa* — the form that takes the noun *nabaʾ* and turns it into the act of delivering it. And *nabaʾ* in Arabic is not just any news. The lexicographers are careful here: *nabaʾ* is news of **weight** — momentous tidings, news of great consequence, the kind of report that changes the one who hears it. From this very root comes *nabiyy* — a prophet — one who carries *nabaʾ.* This is not "He will tell you." It is **"He will break to you the momentous news."**

And here is the quiet devastation of it. The momentous news He breaks to you — *bimā kuntum taʿmalūn* — is the news of **what you yourself used to do.** You will be informed, as if it were stunning new tidings, of your own life. The deeds you forgot. The deeds you rationalized. The deeds you reframed until you half-believed your own version. He will *inform* you of them — show you the *ghayb* of your own actions, the part even you had stopped seeing — and it will land like news, like a *nabaʾ*, because you will be meeting the truth of your own life for the first time without the filter of self-deception.

The kuntum taʿmalūn — *"you used to do"* — is the habitual past: not one deed but the texture, the accumulated grain, of an entire life of small repeated actions, the ones you thought no one was counting.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow? It collapses the seam *now,* before I am made to face it collapsed *then.* The man who lives knowing his *ghayb* is already *shahādah* to his Lord stops performing for the witnessed audience and starts living for the One who sees the unseen. He tends the hidden life — the private intention, the secret deed — *more* carefully than the public one, because he knows which of the two is the real one. The practical application is a question to carry into every locked room: *the part of this that no one will ever see — who is it for?*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Stand back now and look at the whole.

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths have been the same insight approached from two directions.

From the side of the *words*: a panting verb of flight (*tafirrūna*), a grammar bent to seal every exit (*fa-innahu*), and a Form-of-meeting that turns death from accident into appointment (*mulāqīkum*) — then a passive return (*turaddūna*) to the One of no seam (*ʿālim al-ghayb wa-sh-shahādah*), who breaks to you the momentous news of your own forgotten life (*yunabbiʾukum*).

From the side of the *heart*: the thing you flee runs the field, and only by turning to face it are you freed from its grip — and what waits past it is not annihilation but a reckoning held by the One who watched both your selves with equal sight.

And see how the architecture of the ayah is itself the argument. Look at the shape: it opens with **your motion** — *you* flee, frantic and active — and it closes with **you made passive** — *you are returned, you are informed.* The verse begins with you as the one running and ends with you as the one being carried, told, shown. That arc — from frantic agency to total receptivity — is the journey of every soul. You spend your strength running; in the end you are simply *returned,* and *informed.* The grammar moves you from subject to object, because that is precisely what the appointment does.

And the deepest symmetry of all: the surah opened by asking these people to *wish for the meeting* — *tamannawu-l-mawt,* long for death (62:6) — and verse 8 reveals why they could not. Because they had made of death an enemy to flee, when it was always meant to be a *liqāʾ* — a meeting — to long for. The whole tragedy is one of mistaken posture: they ran from the very door they claimed to love.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the "email in the inbox" of my soul — the one fact I reorganize my whole day around not looking at?
2. If I am honest, does my *body* — where I spend my hours, what I fill my silence with — confess the same love of the Hereafter my *tongue* claims?
3. Of the two selves, the *ghayb* and the *shahādah* — which one am I tending more carefully? Which one am I actually living for?
4. If He were to *inform me* — break to me as momentous news — what I have *used to do* this past week, which part would land as the heaviest *nabaʾ*?
5. What would change in this single day if I lived it as someone walking *toward* an appointment, rather than someone fleeing one?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The death you spend your life bolting from is not chasing you — it is coming to meet you, the keeper of an appointment that opens onto the One who saw both the self you displayed and the self you hid, and who will break to you the momentous news of your own forgotten life.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing here asks you to become morbid. The invitation is the opposite of morbid. It is the invitation to *stop running* — and to discover that the dread which organized so much of your life had no power except the power your fleeing gave it. Turn around. Look at the appointment. Tend the hidden self as carefully as the shown one. And let the certainty of the meeting drain the fear out of every moment that leads to it — so that when you are *returned,* you arrive not as panicked prey, but as one who, somewhere along the way, simply stopped being afraid of the door.

### Du'ā

O *ʿĀlim al-ghayb wa-sh-shahādah,* You who know the self I show and the self I hide with a single sight — let me stop fleeing the meeting You have written for me, and let me make my unseen life worthy of the One who has always seen it. And on the day I am returned to You and informed of all I used to do, let the news You break to me be news I am not ashamed to hear. Āmīn.
