# The Overflow That Reaches Those Who Have Not Yet Arrived

## Surah Al-Jumu'ah, Ayah 4

> ذَٰلِكَ فَضْلُ ٱللَّهِ يُؤْتِيهِ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ ذُو ٱلْفَضْلِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ
>
> *Dhālika faḍlu-llāhi yu'tīhi man yashā' — wa-llāhu dhū-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm.*
>
> **"That is the bounty of Allah, which He gives to whom He wills. And Allah is the Possessor of great bounty."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me take you back to a city of stone and heat, fourteen centuries ago.

Imagine you are sitting on the edge of the Arabian desert. The world's two great civilizations are to the north of you and to the east — Byzantium with its cathedrals and libraries, Persia with its empire and its fire-temples and its bureaucracy older than memory. They have books. They have philosophers. They have written law.

And you? You are from a people the world has more or less forgotten. The Quran will use a single word for who you were: **al-ummiyyīn** — the unlettered, the ones without a scripture, the ones the great nations did not count. You worship stones you carved with your own hands. You bury infant daughters in the sand. Your highest virtue is the honor of your tribe, and your tribes are at one another's throats over a stolen camel for forty years at a time.

Nobody is looking at you. Nobody expects anything from you. You are, by every measure the civilized world keeps, the periphery of the periphery.

And then — into *this* — a man stands among you. Not a man imported from the centers of power. A man *from you*. He speaks words that don't sound like the words of men. And those words begin to do something no philosophy, no empire, no library had ever managed to do to a human being: they begin to *clean* you. To lift the rust off the heart. To teach you not just information, but *wisdom* — the thing the great nations with all their books had somehow never learned to transmit.

The surah has just described all of this — across verses two and three. The Messenger. The recitation. The purifying. The teaching. And then, almost as a shock, verse three reaches forward in time and says: this is not only for *you*. It is for **others who have not yet joined them** — people not yet born, peoples on the far edges of the earth who have never heard an Arabic word.

And then comes verse four. One line. It does not add a new piece of information. It does something stranger. It reaches back and gathers *all of it* — the Messenger, the cleansing, the wisdom, the reach across centuries — into a single pointing finger and says one word about the whole thing:

**That. That is faḍl.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want to plant in you before we begin, and I do not want to answer it yet. Why would Allah, after describing the single greatest event in human history, choose to *seal* it not with a word about greatness, not with a word about power, but with a word that means something closer to *leftover*? Something that means *overflow*? Something that means *more than was owed*?

Stay with that. Because the answer is going to rearrange how you understand everything good that has ever happened to you.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Word "That" — Why Allah Pointed From So Far Away

### Let's begin with something you do every day without noticing.

When you point at something, you make a choice you are not even aware of. You can say *this* — or you can say *that*. In English they feel almost the same. But every language marks a distance, and Arabic marks it with unusual care.

Arabic has a near-pointer: **hādhā** — *this*, the thing right here, in my hand, at my side. And it has a far-pointer: **dhālika** — *that*, the thing over there, at a distance, away from me.

Now think about how you use these in your own speech. You don't only use them for physical distance. When something is precious, when something is above you, when you want to set it apart and honor it — you instinctively point at it from *farther away*. You say, *now* that *— that was a man.* The distance in the word is not measuring space. It's measuring *rank*.

### Now open the ayah.

Allah has just spent two verses describing the Messenger ﷺ and his mission. It is right there. It is the nearest possible thing in the passage. Grammatically, the easiest, most natural word would be **hādhā** — *this* is the bounty of Allah.

But that is not what He says.

He says **ذَٰلِكَ** — *dhālika*. **That.** The far one. The exalted one.

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened. Allah took something He had placed right in front of you — and pointed at it from a great height, from a great distance, as though it were a mountain on the horizon rather than a sentence you just read.

### Let's sit with this, because it is doing something to you.

This is the same word that opens the second surah of the Quran: **dhālika al-kitāb** — *that* is the Book. Scholars of language paused there for the same reason. The Book is in your hands. Why point to it as *that*, from far away?

Because the far-pointer does something the near-pointer cannot. It tells you: *the thing I am pointing at is so elevated that even when it is near you, it is above you.* You do not get to hold it casually. You crane your neck to look at it.

So when Allah says *dhālika faḍlu-llāh* — *that* is the bounty of Allah — He is not being vague about which thing He means. He is telling you the *altitude* of the thing. He is saying: everything I just described — the Messenger sent to the forgotten, the cleansing of hearts, the wisdom poured into the unlettered, the reach across the centuries — gather all of it up, and understand that as a single reality it stands so high above you that you point to it the way you point to the stars.

And this is exactly how the classical commentators read the pointer. Al-Jalālayn, glossing *dhālika*, says plainly that it points back to *the Prophet ﷺ and those mentioned alongside him* — the whole described reality of verses two and three, not one isolated piece of it. The far-pointer, they confirm, does not single out the Messenger and leave the rest; it reaches back over the entire scene and lifts all of it at once.

### And here is what is remarkable.

The far-pointer is *singular*. **Dhālika** — *that*, one thing. But it is gathering up *many* things: a Messenger, a recitation, a purification, a teaching, a future. Arabic lets you point at a whole assembled reality with one finger and call it *one*.

So this single word is quietly telling you: do not break the gift into pieces. Do not say, *I'll take the guidance but the Messenger was just a historical figure,* or *I'll take the wisdom but the cleansing is optional.* It is **one** bounty. One overflow. You point to the whole of it from far below, with one word, and that word is **that**.

[PAUSE — let this settle.]

## Element Two: Faḍl — The Word That Means "Left Over"

### Here is the heart of the ayah. And to feel it, you have to forget the English for a moment.

We translate **فَضْل** — *faḍl* — as "bounty," or "grace," or "favor." Those are not wrong. But they are smooth, polished, religious-sounding words, and they have lost the texture of the original.

Let me give you the texture.

The root **f-ḍ-l** in Arabic does not begin in a mosque. It begins at a table. When you have eaten your fill, and food *remains* — that remaining food is **faḍla**. The surplus. The excess. What is *left over* after the need has been met. From this same root comes the everyday word for *the rest of it*, *the remainder*, *that which goes beyond*.

Sit with that for a second. **Faḍl is overflow.** It is, at its root, *more than was required*. It is the part beyond the edge of necessity.

### Now feel what this does to the ayah.

Allah did not say, *that is the wage of Allah.* He did not say, *that is the payment of Allah,* or *the due of Allah,* or *what Allah owed you.* Every one of those would have framed the gift as a transaction — something that closes a debt, something that balances an account.

He said: *that is the* **overflow** *of Allah.*

[PAUSE]

Think about what overflow *is*. Overflow is not aimed at a need. Overflow is what happens when the source is so full that it cannot help spilling past every container you bring to it. When a spring overflows, it is not paying you back. It does not even know you are there. It is simply *that full*.

So the Messenger, the cleansing, the wisdom poured into a forgotten people — Allah is telling you the *category* this belongs to. It does not belong to the category of *things you were owed*. It does not belong to the category of *things the Arabs had earned* — remember, the verse just before reminded them that "before this they were in plain error." It belongs to the category of **what spilled over from a fullness that has no edge.**

### And now watch the word do something across the verse.

It appears twice. Listen:

First: **faḍlu-llāh** — *the bounty* of Allah. Indefinite in its force, attached to Him: a specific spilling-over, *this* gift, *this* overflow that landed on the unlettered.

Then, at the very end: **dhū-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm** — Possessor of *the* bounty, *the* immense one. Now it is **definite**. Now it is not *a* gift He gave. It is *the* faḍl — the overflow as such, the entire reservoir, named as something He simply *is the owner of.*

Do you feel the camera pulling back?

The first *faḍl* shows you the gift in your hands. The second *faḍl* lifts your eyes from the gift to the *Giver* — and tells you the gift you received was a single drop from an ocean that is His permanent possession. The verse moves you from *what you got* to *Who He is*. From the spilled water to the spring.

And the early commentators heard the same fullness in that closing phrase. When al-Muyassar reaches *dhū-l-faḍli-l-ʿaẓīm*, he renders it: *He alone is the Possessor of iḥsān and al-ʿaṭāʾ al-jazīl* — beneficence, and *giving that is copious, unstinting, poured out without measure.* Notice that the gloss does not reach for a word like *reward* or *recompense*. It reaches for *jazīl* — abundance, the gift that comes in heaping amounts. The classical reading hears in this name exactly what the root told us: not a measured wage handed across a counter, but giving so plentiful it spills.

[PAUSE]

### And listen to how the word *sounds*.

**Faḍl.** The heart of it is the letter **ḍād** — that heavy, full-throated Arabic consonant the language is so proud of it calls itself *the language of the ḍād*. It is not a thin sound. It is weighted, rounded, *full in the mouth*. And then the closing word: **al-ʿaẓīm** — *the Immense* — opens out on a long *ī* and a deep *ẓā*, the mouth widening as if to make room for something that won't fit. The sounds themselves overflow. The verse does in your mouth what it means in your life.

### So what has this one word revealed?

That everything good that came to those people — and as we will see, everything good that came to *you* — was never a wage. It was overflow from a fullness with no edge. You did not earn your way up to it. It spilled down to you.

[PAUSE — major pause.]

## Element Three: "He Gives It" — A Verb That Never Stops, and a Recipient Left Blank

### One more piece, and it changes the *time* of the ayah.

In the middle sits a single verb: **يُؤْتِيهِ** — *yu'tīhi* — "He gives it." And I want you to notice two things about it that are easy to walk past.

**First: it is in the present-continuous form.**

Arabic, like every language, can place an action in the past — *He gave* — or in the ongoing present — *He gives, He keeps giving, He is giving.* Allah did not say *He gave it* — a finished act, sealed in history, locked behind the Messenger's lifetime. He said **yu'tīhi** — *He gives it*, *He keeps giving it*, in the tense that does not close.

Why does that matter? Go back one verse. Verse three reached forward to **"others who have not yet joined them"** — people not yet born. So when verse four says *He keeps giving it to whom He wills*, the open tense is not an accident. **The overflow is still flowing.** It did not stop in Madinah. It did not stop with the Arabs. The verb itself is a door left open into the future.

[PAUSE]

And here is something you may not have considered. *You are standing in that tense.* When the Quran said "others who have not yet joined them," and then said "He keeps giving it" — that ongoing verb was reaching, across fourteen centuries, toward someone exactly like *you*, reading these words in a language and a land the first listeners could not have imagined. The continuous tense is how the verse touches *you* specifically. You are not reading about a gift given to other people long ago. You are inside the sentence.

### Second — and read this absence as carefully as any word that is present:

**He does not say to whom.**

*Yu'tīhi* **man yashā'** — He gives it *to whom He wills.* The recipient is left completely blank. **Man** — *whoever, whichever one.* No name. No tribe. No qualification. No résumé. No condition you could point to and say, *ah, so* that *is who deserves it.*

Notice how the language *refuses* to fill in the blank. In a verse about the most consequential gift in history, the one place where you most want a *reason* — *why them? why the unlettered and not the great nations? why me and not the person beside me?* — Allah leaves the door of "why" deliberately, eloquently empty. **Whom He wills.** Full stop.

This blank is not an oversight. It is the whole point. The moment you could name the qualification, the gift would become a *wage* again — payment for the quality you possessed. By leaving the recipient unspecified and anchoring it only in *His will*, the verse guards the meaning of *faḍl* we just uncovered. Overflow has no entry requirement. If it had one, it would not be overflow.

### Now let the classical scholars stand around this verse, each at a different angle.

When the early commentators asked *what, exactly, is this faḍl that He gives*, they did not all say the same thing — and that is a gift to us, because each answer is a different camera angle on one reality:

- Ibn ʿAbbās, as al-Qurṭubī reports, saw it in the *joining of the non-Arabs to the Arabs* — that Islam reached out and gathered in the ʿajam, the outsiders, the ones nobody counted.
- Muqātil saw it as **the revelation and the prophethood itself** — the waḥy descending on a human being.
- Al-Baghawī named it simply: **Islam and guidance** — *al-islām wa-l-hidāyah*.
- And al-Muyassar widens the lens all the way: **the very sending of a Messenger** to the Arabs *and to others*, an overflow given to whichever servants He wills.

Do not choose between these. *Layer* them. The faḍl is the revelation *and* the Messenger *and* guidance itself *and* the astonishing fact that it leapt the walls of one people to reach the whole earth. They are not four opinions competing for the blank space. They are four witnesses describing the same overflow from four sides.

There is one more thread worth pulling, and it lives one verse back — in the "others who have not yet joined them" that the open verb keeps reaching toward. Al-Ṭabarī pauses on a small grammatical fact most readers slide past: the word *ākharīn* — *others* — is in the genitive case because it is *conjoined* (ʿaṭf) directly onto *al-ummiyyīn*, the unlettered. Grammatically, the not-yet-arrived are *strung onto the same cord* as the first listeners. They are not a separate, lesser category appended as an afterthought; the syntax itself binds them into the very people the Messenger was sent among. The grammar is doing what the meaning claims — it refuses to let "the others" stand apart.

And al-Ṭabarī is honest that the early scholars *differed* over who, exactly, these "others" were. Some, he reports, said the *ʿajam* — the non-Arabs, the outsiders the verse would gather in. Others read it more widely: everyone who comes after the first Companions, generation upon generation, until the end of time. The disagreement is not a problem to be solved; it is a width to be received. Whether you read "the others" narrowly as the non-Arabs or broadly as all who come later, *you* are standing inside the phrase either way — and the open verb keeps the door from ever closing on the question.

And here is where the open verb and the open recipient meet the early community's lived experience. There is a sound, marfūʿ report — narrated in the collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim — that when the verse about "others who have not yet joined them" was revealed, the Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ who these others were. He ﷺ placed his hand on Salmān al-Fārisī — the Persian, the outsider, the one not of Quraysh — and indicated that if faith itself were as far away as the stars, men from *his* people would reach it.

[PAUSE]

Feel the weight of that. *Whom He wills* was answered, in part, by a hand laid on a man from outside the tribe. The blank in the verse was filled — not with a bloodline, not with a homeland — but with *whomever the overflow reaches*. And you are downstream of that same overflow.

### What have these three words shown us together?

That the gift points to us from a great height (**dhālika**), that it is overflow and not wage (**faḍl**), and that it is still flowing, toward an unnamed *whoever*, in a verb that never closed (**yu'tīhi man yashā'**).

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about your own heart, and the exhausting thing it does every single day.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Quiet Exhaustion of Needing to Deserve

### Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular tiredness that has nothing to do with sleep. You feel it when you finally get the thing you worked for — the job, the acceptance, the recognition — and instead of rest, a quiet voice starts up: *Do I really belong here? Did I actually earn this? When will they find out I'm not as good as they think?*

Psychologists have a name for one version of it: impostor syndrome. But it goes deeper than a syndrome. It is woven into the whole modern way of being. We are raised inside a story called *meritocracy* — the story that says everything you have, you got because you deserved it, and everything you deserve, you must keep proving you deserve. It sounds fair. It sounds even noble.

But live inside it long enough and notice what it does to you. It means **every good thing in your life is now a debt you have to keep servicing.** You can never simply *rest* in a blessing, because the moment you rest, you might be exposed as someone who didn't earn it. So you grip. You perform. You stay anxious in the middle of your own success.

[PAUSE]

This is the prison of the *wage*. In a world of wages, you are only ever worth your last performance. And no performance is ever enough, because tomorrow the meter resets.

### Now let the ayah walk into that prison.

*Dhālika faḍlu-llāh.* **That is the overflow of Allah.**

Remember what we uncovered. Not wage. Not payment. Not debt. *Overflow.* And remember who received it first: a people the verse itself describes, one line earlier, as having been *in plain error*. They did not climb up to deserve the Messenger. They were at the bottom. The overflow came *down* to them — not because the meter read in their favor, but because the Source was that full.

Do you see what this does to the prison?

If the greatest gift in human history — prophethood, revelation, the cleansing of the heart — was *faḍl* and not wage, was overflow and not earnings, then **the deepest goods in your life were never things you deserved in the first place.** Your faith. Your being born within reach of guidance. Your heart's capacity to be moved by these very words right now. None of it is a wage you must keep servicing. All of it is overflow you were *given*.

### And here is the movement that frees you.

When you understand a good thing as a wage, gratitude is impossible — because you don't thank someone for paying you what you earned. But when you understand it as *faḍl*, as overflow, gratitude becomes the only honest response. You stop gripping. You stop performing for a meter that was never running. You receive.

This does not make you lazy. Watch closely — it does the opposite. The one who knows their faith is a wage is *anxious*; they obey to keep the account balanced, and they burn out. The one who knows their faith is *faḍl* is *grateful*; they obey not to earn what they have but to honor the One who already overflowed it onto them. Anxiety exhausts. Gratitude renews. They look like the same actions from outside. Inside, they are opposite universes.

### What changes tomorrow?

Tomorrow, when you do something good — pray, give, hold your tongue, show up — catch the voice that says *good, I've earned my standing.* And gently correct it: *No. The very fact that I was guided to do this at all is faḍl. I am not paying down a debt. I am thanking a Giver.* Let one act tomorrow be done not to deserve, but simply to say *thank You*.

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: "Why Me?" — Standing in the Tense That Reached Forward

### Begin with a feeling you've had but maybe never named.

Sometimes, in a quiet moment, a strange question surfaces. *Why me?* Why was I born within reach of this, when someone just as intelligent, just as sincere, just as searching, was born somewhere the words never reached? I did nothing to choose my parents, my century, my language, the fact that the call to prayer was in my ears before I could speak. Why did the overflow run toward *me*?

It can come as guilt — the survivor's unease. It can come as wonder. Often it comes as both at once. And the modern mind has no good answer for it, because the modern mind only knows the language of *deserving*, and there is no deserving that explains an accident of birth.

### Now hear the verse answer it — by refusing to answer it.

*Yu'tīhi* **man yashā'.** He gives it **to whom He wills.**

We saw that the recipient is left blank on purpose. There is no qualification you could name. And here is the mercy hidden in that blank: **it means the question "why me?" has no answer in you — and it was never supposed to.** You are looking for the reason inside yourself — your merit, your worthiness, your specialness. The verse turns you away from yourself entirely and points to the *will* of the Giver. The reason is not in the receiver. The reason is in the fullness of the One who overflows.

This is profoundly freeing in two directions at once. It frees you from *pride* — you cannot boast of an overflow you did nothing to attract. And it frees you from *guilt* — you cannot be ashamed of an overflow you did nothing to divert from another. You were not chosen because you were better. You were reached because He is *generous*, and generosity does not need the receiver to justify it.

[PAUSE]

### And now feel the tense reach for you.

Remember the verb: *yu'tīhi* — *He keeps giving it* — the present that never closed. And remember verse three: *others who have not yet joined them.*

Stand inside that for a moment. When those words were first recited in Madinah, **you** were one of the "others who have not yet joined." You were the not-yet-born, the far-off, the one on the edge of a future the first listeners couldn't see. The continuous verb was a hand extended across fourteen centuries, and the hand has reached *you* — here, now, reading.

The same overflow that landed on the unlettered, that leapt to the Persians, that crossed empires and centuries — *it is still moving.* You are not at the end of it. You are *in* it. And if it reached you, then the verb is still open, which means there are still "others who have not yet joined" — people downstream of *you*, who have not yet been reached, and who may be reached *through* you.

### Do you see what that asks of you?

If you are not the end of the overflow but a point along its flow, then you are not only a *receiver* of faḍl. You are meant to be a *channel* of it. The water that filled you was never meant to stop at you. The one who was reached becomes the one who reaches. The Persian whom the Prophet's hand pointed toward became, generations later, the means by which whole nations were reached. The overflow keeps overflowing — *through* the ones it lands on.

### What changes tomorrow?

Tomorrow, find one person who is one of the "others who have not yet joined" — not necessarily a non-Muslim; perhaps simply someone whose heart has gone dry, someone who has not yet been reached by the depth of what you have. And let a little of your overflow spill toward them. A verse shared. A kindness. A door held open. Not from a position of *I have, you lack* — but from *this was given to me as overflow, and overflow is not meant to be hoarded.*

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: How the Verse Holds Itself Together

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the verse, because it is built with a quiet symmetry.

It opens by pointing **up and far** — *dhālika*, *that*, the gift held at a great height. It closes by pointing **up and far** again — *al-ʿaẓīm*, *the Immense*, the Giver named as beyond measure. And held between those two heights, in the exact center, is the only verb in the verse: *yu'tīhi* — *He gives it*. 

So the architecture is this: **a giving, suspended between two infinities.** Above it, the exalted gift. Below and around it, the immense Giver. And in the middle, the act of giving itself, in a tense that never closes. The verse is shaped like the very thing it describes — overflow, descending from an immeasurable height, still in motion.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are one insight approached from two sides.

The grammar told us: *dhālika* (it is exalted above you), *faḍl* (it is overflow, not wage), *yu'tīhi man yashā'* (it is still flowing, to an unnamed whoever).

The heart heard the same thing: *you do not have to deserve what was always overflow* — and — *you were reached not for your merit but for His generosity, and the reaching has not stopped.*

The far-pointer and the freedom from impostor-anxiety are the same truth: the gift is above you, so stop trying to prove you earned it. The open verb and the call to become a channel are the same truth: the overflow is still moving, so do not let it stop at you.

## Questions to Carry

1. Which blessings in my life have I been treating as *wages* I must keep servicing — and what would it feel like to receive them, instead, as *faḍl*?
2. When the question *"why me?"* arises, do I look for the answer in my own merit — or have I let the verse turn me toward the generosity of the Giver?
3. Where in my life am I gripping a blessing out of fear I don't deserve it, when I could be resting in it out of gratitude?
4. Who are the "others who have not yet joined" within my own reach — and is my overflow stopping at me, or flowing through me?
5. If everything good I have is overflow and not earnings — what, then, do I actually *owe*? And to Whom?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Everything you have of guidance was never a wage you earned but an overflow Allah chose to pour from a fullness with no edge — and that same overflow is still moving, in a verb that never closed, toward those who have not yet arrived, possibly through you.**

## The Closing Invitation

So here is the shift, offered not as a command but as a door left open — the way the verb itself was left open.

You can keep living inside the story of the wage: anxious, gripping, forever proving you deserve your place. Or you can hear *dhālika faḍlu-llāh* and let it set you down. The gift was always above you. You were always reached by mercy, not merit. And the water that filled you was never meant to stop at the edge of you.

Receive it as overflow. Then let it overflow.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, Possessor of the immense overflow — we did not climb up to Your gift; You poured it down to us when we were in plain error. Let us never mistake Your faḍl for our wage. And as You reached us though we were once among the not-yet-arrived, make us a channel of Your overflow to those still waiting to be reached.* Āmīn.
