# The Donkey and the Books

## When the Carrier Never Reads What He Carries

---

> **مَثَلُ ٱلَّذِينَ حُمِّلُوا۟ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا كَمَثَلِ ٱلْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًۭا**
>
> *Mathalu alladhīna ḥummilū at-Tawrāta thumma lam yaḥmilūhā ka-mathali al-ḥimāri yaḥmilu asfārā.*
>
> "The likeness of those who were entrusted with the Torah and then did not carry it is like the likeness of a donkey carrying great books."

---

## Before We Open the Ayah

I want you to picture something very ordinary before we touch a single word of Arabic.

Picture a delivery truck. Inside that truck, packed floor to ceiling, are boxes of medicine. Life-saving medicine. Enough to heal a whole city. The truck drives all day. It carries that cargo over mountains, through traffic, in the heat. By the end of the day the engine is tired, the tires are worn, the fuel is spent. The truck has *carried* the cure for a thousand diseases.

And the truck is not one bit healthier than it was that morning.

It bore the weight of the medicine. It never received the benefit of the medicine. Because a truck cannot take medicine. A truck can only haul it.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that image gently, off to the side. Because Allah is about to give us a parable that is even sharper, even more uncomfortable — and it is not a parable about a truck or a stranger or some distant nation. By the time we are finished, you will understand why this surah, just four ayahs later, suddenly grabs the believers by the collar and shouts: *run.* Run to the remembrance. Don't walk. **Run.**

To understand that command, we have to first understand this warning.

### The world this ayah landed in

Madinah. The early years. The Muslim community is small, raw, learning. And living right beside them are communities who have had Scripture for centuries — who know how to read, who have scholars, who memorize, who possess actual books when the Muslims barely have parchment. People of learning. People of pedigree.

And the surah has just said something staggering in ayah 2: Allah sent, among the **unlettered**, a Messenger from among themselves, who recites His verses, *purifies them,* and teaches them the Book and the wisdom — though before this they were in clear misguidance.

Purifies them. Teaches them. The Book is not just being *delivered* to this new community. It is being *worked into them.* Recitation, then purification, then teaching. The Book is going *inside.*

And immediately — immediately — the surah turns to a people who had received a Book long before, with their own great teacher, Mūsā عليه السلام, and asks: what happened? What does it look like when a Book is given to a people and it never goes inside? When it stays *on* them but never *in* them?

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Listen carefully, because the whole reflection lives in this question:

**There is a way to carry something so heavy, for so long, so faithfully — and gain nothing from it at all. The Quran is about to name it. And then it is going to turn the mirror around.**

Let's open it.

# PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: Two Ways to "Carry" — and the Gap Between Them

Before we look at the ayah, I want to teach you one small thing about Arabic verbs. Just one. It will unlock everything.

In Arabic, you can take a simple verb and "double" its middle letter — we call this Form II — and doing that changes the meaning in a specific way. It adds force. It often means *to make someone do the thing,* or *to do the thing intensely, repeatedly, deliberately.*

Take the simple verb in English: "to learn." Now imagine a version that means "to be *made* to learn — to be sat down, handed the material, held responsible for it." That second, heavier version is the flavor Form II adds.

Hold that distinction: **doing a thing** versus **being made to bear a thing.** Keep it in your hand.

Now look at the ayah. Allah uses the root for "carrying" — *ḥ-m-l* — **four times** in a single verse. Four. And He deliberately switches between the two forms.

> **حُمِّلُوا۟ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا**
>
> *ḥummilū* at-Tawrāta *thumma lam yaḥmilūhā*

The first word, **حُمِّلُوا — *ḥummilū*** — is Form II. The heavy form. And it is *passive.* It does not mean "they carried." It means **"they were *made* to carry."** They were *laden* with it. They were *entrusted,* loaded, given a charge to bear. Someone placed this weight upon them. The classical mufassirūn are unanimous here — al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Jalālayn all gloss it the same way: *kullifū* — *they were assigned, charged, made responsible* to act upon the Torah. Al-Qurṭubī even records a second shade from al-Jurjānī: it carries the sense of *kafāla,* of taking on a guarantee, a sacred liability.

So: they did not pick this up casually. Allah Himself placed the Torah upon their shoulders as a trust. An honor. A weight of dignity. *Ḥummilū.*

[PAUSE]

Now the next phrase: **ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا — *thumma lam yaḥmilūhā*** — "then they did not carry it."

And here Allah switches forms. This is **يَحْمِلُوهَا** — the *simple* Form I. The everyday, your-own-two-hands "carry."

Do you feel what just happened in the grammar?

**They were *made to bear it* (the heavy, entrusted form) — but then they did not *bear it themselves* (the simple, personal form).**

The weight was placed on them by Allah. The carrying was supposed to be done by *them.* And the second never happened. The trust was given. The trust was never *lived.*

Let's sit with this, because it is exquisite. There is a kind of "having" the Torah that is entirely real — they truly *had* it, it was genuinely upon them, the responsibility was authentic — and yet there is a *second* carrying, an inner one, that simply never took place. The Book was on their backs. It was never in their stride.

And notice the little word between them: **ثُمَّ — *thumma.*** Not *wa* ("and"). *Thumma* means "then" — and it usually implies a gap, a delay, a *space* between two events. It is the "then" of *time passing.* They were entrusted... and then, after all that time, after generations of having it... they still did not carry it.

The *thumma* is the sound of decades going by. The Book sitting there. Unopened in the only way that matters.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the part that should make us uncomfortable. The grammar has just described a state that any of us can be in.

You can be genuinely *entrusted* with something sacred — born into it, handed it, responsible before Allah for it — and at the same time genuinely *not carrying it.* Both true at once. The entrustment is real. The neglect is real. They do not cancel each other. That is precisely the horror the ayah is naming: a person fully loaded with the truth, walking around fully empty of it.

That is what one word — *ḥummilū* — versus another — *yaḥmilūhā* — does. One verb. One shift of form. And an entire spiritual disease is diagnosed.

What this single contrast reveals: **to be given the truth is not the same as to bear it. The first is a gift placed on you. The second is a life you have to walk.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Two: *Asfār* — The Books That Were Made to Unveil

Now the image arrives. And it is brutal in its mercy.

> **كَمَثَلِ ٱلْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًۭا**
>
> "...like the likeness of a donkey carrying *asfārā.*"

We'll come to the donkey. But first I want you to look at this last word — **أَسْفَارًۭا — *asfāran*** — because Allah did not choose an ordinary word for "books," and the choice is doing quiet, devastating work.

*Asfār* is the plural of *sifr.* And a *sifr* — al-Baghawī quotes al-Farrāʾ directly — is not just any book. It is **a great book. A weighty volume. A heavy tome of knowledge.** These are not pamphlets. These are the massive, important books — the ones full of light.

But here is what I want you to see. The root is *s-f-r.* And this root, in Arabic, carries a beautiful core meaning that runs through all its branches: **to uncover, to unveil, to make something shine out from behind a covering.**

From this same root: *safara* — when a woman uncovers her face, removes the veil. From this same root: *asfara aṣ-ṣubḥ* — "the dawn unveiled itself," when morning breaks and light pours over the darkness. The Quran itself uses it — *wa-ṣ-ṣubḥi idhā asfar* (74:34), "by the morning when it brightens."

So why is a book called a *sifr?* Because **a book uncovers.** It removes the veil from meaning. It takes what was hidden and makes it shine. A *sifr* is, by its very name, *a thing that unveils.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel the irony Allah has built into a single word.

The donkey is carrying the very objects whose *name means "to unveil."* He is loaded with light. He is hauling dawn itself on his back. Books whose entire purpose, whose etymological soul, is to *uncover* and *illuminate.*

And he sees nothing.

For the donkey, the *asfār* — the unveilers — stay veiled. The things that exist to lift the covering off of reality lie sealed against his comprehension. He carries dawn and walks in darkness. He bears the light and is not lit.

Notice something almost unbearable: it would be one thing to be ignorant in the absence of knowledge. But this is ignorance *while pressed up against* knowledge. Touching it. Sweating under its weight. The unveiling is *right there,* inches from him — and the veil over his own eyes never moves.

That is the precision of *asfār.* Allah could have said *kutuban* — "books," plainly. He chose the word that means *unveilers,* and set it on the back of a creature who will never be unveiled to. The tragedy is not the absence of light. **The tragedy is proximity to light without reception of it.**

What this word reveals: **the most painful kind of darkness is the darkness of the one who is carrying the lamp.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: *Kadhdhabū* — Denial Is Not Passive. It Is Labor.

We move to the second half of the ayah, and there is one more verb I cannot let pass.

> **بِئْسَ مَثَلُ ٱلْقَوْمِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَذَّبُوا۟ بِـَٔايَـٰتِ ٱللَّهِ**
>
> "Wretched is the likeness of the people who *denied* the signs of Allah."

The word for "denied" — **كَذَّبُوا — *kadhdhabū*** — is, again, that heavy Form II. The doubled, intensive form.

Here is why that matters. There is a simple form, *kadhaba,* which just means "to lie." But *kadhdhaba* — the doubled form — does not mean "to lie." It means **to actively, repeatedly, emphatically declare something to be false. To labor at denial. To work at calling the truth a lie, again and again.**

This is not the quiet drift of someone who simply forgot. This is not passive neglect. *Kadhdhabū* is **effortful.** It is the person who is shown the sign, and pushes it away — and is shown it again, and pushes it away again. Denial as an *occupation.* Denial as something you have to keep *doing,* because the truth keeps coming back and you keep having to shove it back down.

[PAUSE]

And do you see the terrible logic of the ayah's sequence?

First: they were entrusted but did not carry (the neglect). *Then* they actively denied (the labor). The neglect came first. The emptiness came first. And emptiness does not stay neutral. The man who refuses to bear the light does not simply remain in a pleasant dark — over time he begins to *work against* the light, because the light has become an accusation he cannot stand.

The unopened Book becomes the resented Book becomes the denied Book. Notice the awful efficiency of it: the same energy that could have *carried* the truth gets spent *pushing it away.* The labor was always going to be spent. It was only ever a question of direction.

This is the inner momentum of turning away. It rarely stays still. *Ḥummilū* — they were given it. *Lam yaḥmilūhā* — they didn't carry it. *Kadhdhabū* — and so, in the end, they fought it.

What this verb reveals: **walking away from the truth is never the end of the story. The truth follows you, and eventually you have to decide whether to carry it — or to spend your life pushing against it.**

## The Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, laid side by side.

*Ḥummilū / lam yaḥmilūhā* — the gap between being given the truth and bearing it.
*Asfār* — the unveilers carried by the one who is never unveiled.
*Kadhdhabū* — the laborious denial that fills the empty space where carrying should have been.

The grammar has drawn a complete portrait of a soul. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about ancient words — but about *us.* About reality itself. Because this ayah was never really about donkeys.

# PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Hard Drive Full of Light

Let me start where you actually live.

On the device in your pocket, right now, there is almost certainly a complete copy of the Quran. Probably several translations. Probably hundreds of hours of lectures. Tafsīr. Hadith collections that scholars once traveled across deserts for years to assemble — sitting in your downloads, searchable, instant.

Your phone *carries* more sacred knowledge than the greatest libraries of the classical world. Your phone is loaded with *asfār* beyond the dreams of Imām al-Bukhārī.

And your phone has never once felt khushūʿ. It has never wept in sujūd. It has never been changed by a single ayah it stores.

[PAUSE]

I am not insulting your phone. I am asking you a question. Because there is a version of being a Muslim that is *exactly like the phone.* A version where the data is all present, perfectly stored, instantly retrievable — and the *heart that holds it* is unmoved. Where we have *access* to everything and *transformation* from nothing.

We have confused **storage** with **carrying.** We think because we *have* it, we *bear* it. But the donkey *has* the books. The truck *has* the medicine. The phone *has* the Quran. Having is not bearing. *Ḥummilū* is not *yaḥmilūhā.*

The ayah is diagnosing the precise disease of the information age, fourteen centuries early. It is not that we lack knowledge. We are *drowning* in knowledge. It is that the knowledge sits *on* us — in our bookmarks, our saved posts, our half-watched lectures — and never goes *in.*

Let it illuminate the experience. You know the feeling. You save the powerful reminder to watch "later." You download the tafsīr you'll "get to." You collect the knowledge the way the donkey collects mileage — faithfully, even sincerely — and at the end of the day you are not one degree closer to Allah than when you started. You *carried* it. You did not *take* it.

[PAUSE]

And here is the movement that should stop us cold. We were warned this could happen to *us* specifically. This whole ayah is in a surah whose first ayah is about a Messenger sent to *purify* and *teach* — to take the Book *inside* people. The surah holds up the previous community not to let us point fingers, but to ask: *and now that you have the final Book — which carrying will you choose?*

Imām al-Qurṭubī and the early scholars applied this parable to those who possessed the Torah and didn't act on it. But the Prophet ﷺ himself, and the scholars after him, understood that the parable is a *category,* not just a *people.* Anyone entrusted with revelation who does not carry it steps into the likeness. The mirror does not have a name carved on it. **Whoever stands in front of it sees themselves.**

The early generations understood this so well that one of them said: *"Recite the Quran as long as it forbids you. When it no longer forbids you, you are not really reciting it."* Carrying is not the recitation that passes over the tongue. Carrying is the recitation that *forbids* you, that *moves* you, that costs you something.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is one concrete thing. Stop measuring your relationship with the Quran by how much you *consume* and start measuring it by how much it *changes.* One ayah that alters how you treat your spouse this week is more "carried" than a whole surah memorized and unlived. Take less. Let it weigh more. Read tomorrow's portion not asking "how much can I get through" but "what is this asking me to *do* before sunset."

[PAUSE — major]

## Theme Two: The Burden That Was Meant to Be Wings

Now I want to turn to something tender, because the donkey image can sound like pure rebuke — and there is a mercy hiding in it that we must not miss.

Why is carrying the Book a *weight* at all? Why does Allah use the language of *load,* of being *laden* — *ḥummilū* — for receiving guidance?

Because guidance *is* heavy. It really is. It asks things of you. It interrupts your desires. It wakes you before dawn, empties your hand of forbidden gain, ties your tongue from the easy lie. Revelation is not a feather. To receive it is to receive a weight.

But here is the secret the donkey will never learn. **For the one who actually carries it — who takes it inside — the weight becomes the very thing that lifts him.**

Think of it like this. To a creature who does not understand flight, a wing is a burden. It is extra weight strapped to the body, dragging, heavy, useless cargo. But to the bird who *uses* the wing — who lets the wing become part of how it moves — that same weight is the only reason it leaves the ground. **The wing is a burden until you open it. Then it is the only thing carrying you.**

[PAUSE]

The donkey and the eagle can carry the identical weight. For the donkey it is dead cargo — it presses him into the dirt, it tires him, it gives nothing back. For the eagle the weight is *integrated* — and the heavier muscle that bears it is the same muscle that lifts him into the sky.

The Torah, the Quran, the whole trust of revelation — *ḥummilū,* it was laid on them as a weight — was never meant to stay cargo. It was meant to be *opened* like a wing. It was meant to become part of how the soul moves, until the very thing that was heavy is the thing that carries you up to your Lord.

The donkey's tragedy is not that he was given weight. The eagle was given weight too. The donkey's tragedy is that **he never opened it.** It stayed strapped to his back as load, when it could have become the architecture of flight.

[PAUSE]

And feel the mercy in this, especially if you have been the donkey. If today the deen feels like nothing but burden — like a list of heavy obligations pressing you into the ground, exhausting, joyless cargo you haul out of guilt — the ayah is not only rebuking you. It is *diagnosing* you. It is saying: *you are carrying it like a load because you have not yet opened it like a wing.* The heaviness you feel is the heaviness of cargo not yet integrated. The same prayer that feels like weight on the back of the heedless becomes the *miʿrāj,* the ascent, "the coolness of my eyes," for the one who has let it inside.

Nothing about the weight changes. *You* change. And the identical weight that crushed becomes the wing that lifts.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** When the next act of worship feels like dead weight — the wuḍūʾ in the cold, the portion of Quran, the charity that pinches — don't try to make it lighter. Try to *open* it. Ask, even for ten seconds before you begin: *what is this for? Who is this in front of? What in me is meant to move?* That ten seconds is the difference between strapping cargo to your back and unfolding a wing. It is the difference between *ḥummilū* and *yaḥmilūhā.*

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## How the Pieces Fit Together

Step back and see the whole architecture of the ayah, now that we've done the work.

Allah builds it in a perfect, descending diagnosis:

**حُمِّلُوا** — they were *entrusted.* The honor. The weight laid on them by their Lord.
**ثُمَّ لَمْ يَحْمِلُوهَا** — *then* they did not carry it. The neglect. The wing never opened.
**كَمَثَلِ ٱلْحِمَارِ يَحْمِلُ أَسْفَارًۭا** — the image that exposes it: a beast under a load of *light,* unlit.
**كَذَّبُوا** — and the neglect, left to ferment, became active *denial.* The wasted labor.

And then the verdict — and notice its grammar, because Allah leaves a deliberate gap. **بِئْسَ مَثَلُ ٱلْقَوْمِ — "wretched is the likeness of the people..."** In Arabic, after *biʾsa* ("wretched is"), the thing being condemned is supposed to be named at the end — *"wretched is the likeness... [this one here]."* And the mufassirūn note that the final word, the *"this,"* is **deleted.** *Al-makhṣūṣ bi-dhamm maḥdhūf* — the one singled out for blame is left unsaid.

Why leave the blank? [PAUSE] Because a blank is a space you can fall into. The ayah ends with an unnamed "this" — *wretched is the likeness of... ___.* And every reader who has carried the Book without bearing it feels the silence reaching for them. The name is not written because the name is meant to be *recognized.*

And the seal of the ayah: **وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ** — "and Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people." Note the precision: it is not *the ignorant* who are abandoned to misguidance here. It is *the wrongdoers* — *aẓ-ẓālimīn.* And ẓulm, at its root, means *putting a thing where it does not belong.* What greater misplacement is there than to be handed the light — and to set it on your back as cargo instead of in your heart as guidance? They were not denied guidance because they couldn't find it. They were sitting on top of it.

The linguistic journey and the themes turn out to be one truth seen from two sides: **The grammar showed us the gap between *being given* and *bearing.* The themes showed us that the gap is the whole story of a human life — and that the same weight that buries the donkey is the wing that lifts the eagle.**

## Questions to Carry

1. What sacred knowledge am I *storing* right now that I have never let *change* me? Name one specific thing.
2. When the deen feels heavy this week — will I try to make it lighter, or will I try to *open* it?
3. Is there a truth I already know — that I have quietly been *pushing away* — because carrying it would cost me something I am not ready to pay?
4. If the blank at the end of *"wretched is the likeness of..."* were filled with my name, what part of me would deserve it — and what would I do about it before tonight?
5. What is the *one ayah* I will let forbid me, move me, or change me this week — not the hundred I will scroll past?

## One Sentence

**To be entrusted with the light and never let it inside is to become a beast of burden beneath the very dawn you are carrying — and the cure is not to put the weight down, but to finally open it like a wing.**

## The Invitation

You are not the donkey. You were never meant to be. The donkey cannot read; *you can.* The donkey cannot be unveiled to; *you can.* The whole reason this ayah stings is that you are *capable* of the other kind of carrying — the inner one, the *yaḥmilūhā,* the wing.

So here is the invitation, gently. Tomorrow, take one small portion of the Book — small — and refuse to merely transport it. Sit with it until it does something to you. Let one verse find one corner of your life and rearrange it. That is carrying. That is the difference, finally, between a heart and a hard drive.

And now you understand why, four ayahs later, this same surah will cry out: *when the call to Jumuʿah comes, leave your trade and **run** to the remembrance of Allah.* Run — because the alternative to running toward the light is standing still beneath it, loaded and unlit. The surah shows you the donkey precisely so it can then show you how to be the eagle.

## Du'ā

O Allah, You who entrusted us with Your words — do not let us merely carry them; let us *bear* them. Open in us the Book that we have only been storing, until the weight that pressed us into the earth becomes the wing that lifts us to You. And let us never be among those who sat upon the light and called it darkness. Āmīn.
