# They Were Never Commanded Anything But This

### Surah Al-Bayyinah, Ayah 5

> وَمَآ أُمِرُوٓاْ إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُواْ ٱللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ حُنَفَآءَ وَيُقِيمُواْ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَيُؤْتُواْ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ ٱلْقَيِّمَةِ

*Wa mā umirū illā li-yaʿbudū Llāha mukhliṣīna lahu d-dīna ḥunafāʾa wa yuqīmū ṣ-ṣalāta wa yuʾtū z-zakāh, wa dhālika dīnu l-qayyimah.*

*"And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, making the religion purely His, inclining toward truth, and to establish the prayer and give the zakah. And that is the religion of uprightness."*

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Word

Let me ask you to stand somewhere before we open this ayah.

Imagine a people who have been waiting. For generations they have carried scriptures that promise a final messenger. They have read the descriptions. They have told their children: *when he comes, we will be the first to follow him, because we have been preparing all along.* Their entire identity rests on this readiness. They are the people who know. They are the people who were given the Book.

And then he comes.

The surah we are sitting inside, Al-Bayyinah — "The Clear Proof" — tells the story of that arrival with a strange and almost unbearable precision. It says that the disbelievers among the People of the Book and the idolaters were *not going to break off* from their ways **until** the clear proof came to them. A Messenger from Allah, reciting purified pages. And then — listen to the heartbreak in the sequence — the ayah just before ours says:

*"And those who were given the Scripture did not become divided until after the clear proof came to them."* (98:4)

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. They did not split **until** the proof arrived. The clarity itself became the occasion of their division. The thing that should have united them shattered them. The light came, and instead of gathering toward it, they scattered.

And now you are standing at the edge of ayah 5. After describing the splitting, after describing the refusal, Allah does something we might not expect. He does not, in this verse, thunder about their punishment — that comes later. Instead, He pauses the whole indictment to say something quiet and devastating about *what they were actually asked to do.*

Here is the hook. Here is what I want you to feel the hunger for before we go in:

**What if the thing they rejected was not new at all? What if it was not some alien demand, some foreign religion, some difficult novel law? What if — and the ayah is about to say this with surgical economy — what they turned away from was the exact same thing their own scriptures had been commanding them all along? What if the entire content of every revelation God ever sent could be compressed into a single sentence — and that sentence is sitting right here in front of us?**

This ayah claims to hold the whole of religion in sixteen words. Not a summary of it. The *whole* of it. Everything the prophets came with, everything written in the Torah and the Gospel and the Qur'an, distilled to its irreducible core.

Let's find out what that core is.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "They Were Not Commanded Except" — The Architecture of Restriction

Before we touch the Arabic, let me teach you a structure that exists in your own speech, because you use it every day without naming it.

There is a difference between saying *"I want you to be honest"* and saying *"I want nothing from you but honesty."*

The first is a request among possible requests. The second is a wall. It fences off everything else and leaves one thing standing alone in the field. In Arabic, this fence has a name and a shape. It is built by pairing a negation — *mā*, "not" — with an exception — *illā*, "except." You knock down everything, and then you let exactly one thing back through the gate.

*Mā ... illā.* Not... except. Nothing... but.

This is one of the most powerful tools in the Arabic language for what scholars call *qaṣr* or *ḥaṣr* — restriction, confinement, the act of taking a vast open space of possibilities and collapsing it to a single point.

Now look at how this ayah opens:

> وَمَآ أُمِرُوٓاْ إِلَّا
> *Wa mā umirū illā* — "And they were not commanded except..."

[PAUSE]

Notice what has just happened. Allah does not say "they were commanded to worship Him." He says they were **not** commanded **anything except** to worship Him.

Do you feel the difference? The first would be one instruction among many. The second is a demolition. It clears the entire field of every other possible command — every ritual they prided themselves on, every law they argued over, every tradition they built their identity around — and leaves one thing standing in the cleared field.

And here is where it cuts deepest. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Jalālayn are united on this point: the "they" here are the disbelievers among the People of the Book, and the command being referenced is the command **in the Torah and the Gospel themselves.** *"Wa mā umirū"* — and these very people *were not commanded, in their own scriptures,* anything except this.

Sit with the weight of that. The structure is not just grammatically restrictive. It is biographically devastating. It says to a people who imagined that following the final Messenger would mean *abandoning* their religion: *You were never asked to abandon anything. The thing you are running from is the thing your own book wrote down for you.*

There is one more feature here, and it is in what the verb does **not** say. Look:

> أُمِرُوٓاْ
> *umirū* — "they were commanded"

This is a passive verb. They were commanded — but by *whom?* Arabic, when it wants to, can name the commander. Here it does not. And in Arabic, when the agent of a passive verb is omitted, it is most often because the agent is so obvious, so overwhelming, that naming Him would almost diminish Him. *They were commanded* — and there is only One in all existence whose command constitutes religion. The silence around the Commander is itself a form of reverence. You don't point at the sun to ask which light source lit the room.

So before we have even reached the content, the form has already told us three things: **everything else is cleared away** (the restriction), **this is not new** (their own scriptures), and **the One behind it needs no naming** (the passive). 

[PAUSE]

Now — what was the one thing left standing in the cleared field?

### Element Two: The Lām of Purpose — Worship as the Reason You Exist

> إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُواْ ٱللَّهَ
> *illā li-yaʿbudū Llāha* — "except to worship Allah."

There is a tiny letter at the front of *li-yaʿbudū* — the prefix *li-*, the *lām*. And the classical commentators stop here, because something subtle is happening.

Let me teach the concept first. In Arabic there are different ways to introduce a "that" clause. The plainest is the particle *an* — *an yaʿbudū*, "that they worship." It is functional, neutral, a simple complement: *they were commanded that they worship.* But this ayah does not use *an*. It uses the *lām*.

Al-Jalālayn says it directly: *"ḥudhifat an wa zīdat al-lām"* — the *an* was dropped and the *lām* was added. Al-Qurṭubī confirms it: *"al-lām fī li-yaʿbudū bi-maʿnā an"* — the *lām* here carries the meaning of *an*. He even cites the parallel from elsewhere: *yurīdu Llāhu li-yubayyina lakum* — "Allah intends *to* make clear for you."

So why swap a neutral *an* for a *lām?*

Because the *lām* is not just grammatical glue. It is, in its native life, the *lām of taʿlīl* — the **lām of purpose, of reason, of telos.** When you say *"I came li-* see you," the *lām* means *in order to, for the purpose of.* It does not merely report what happened; it names the *reason* a thing exists.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what this does to the verse? Read with the *lām* fully alive, it no longer says merely "they were commanded to worship." It says: **they were not commanded except *for the purpose of* worshipping Allah.** Worship is not one item on the list of commands. Worship is the *reason for* the command. The *lām* quietly reframes the entire thing. Every instruction God ever gave was given *for the sake of* this one orientation of the heart.

And this lines up exactly with the great verse in Surah Adh-Dhāriyāt — *"I did not create the jinn and humankind except*" — *illā li-yaʿbudūn* — same *lām*, same restriction structure — "*except to worship Me.*" The reason you were made and the reason you were commanded are the same reason, written with the same little letter.

Notice something: the word *worship* itself, *ʿibādah*, comes from a root that in old Arabic described a path made smooth by being walked on — a road worn down and made level by the feet of travelers, by submission. To worship is to let your life be walked smooth in one direction, until the path of your days is paved by Him and toward Him.

So now the field is cleared, and the one thing standing in it has been named not as a duty but as a *purpose.* And then — as if even that single word *worship* could be misunderstood, could be faked, could be hollowed out — Allah immediately specifies *how.*

### Element Three: Mukhliṣīn and Ḥunafāʾ — The Two Directions of a Single Turn

> مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ حُنَفَآءَ
> *mukhliṣīna lahu d-dīna ḥunafāʾa* — "making the religion purely His, inclining toward truth."

These two words — *mukhliṣīn* and *ḥunafāʾ* — are what Arabic grammar calls *ḥāl*, states of being. They describe the *condition* in which the worship happens. Not just *that* they worship, but *in what state* they worship. And I want to show you that these two words are not a redundant pair. They are two directions of one single motion of the heart.

**Let's open the first: مُخْلِصِينَ.**

It comes from the root *kh-l-ṣ*, *khalaṣa* — to be pure, to be unmixed, to be free of any foreign element. In classical Arabic this is the word you use for something that has had all its impurities removed. The Qur'an itself gives us the most vivid picture of this root in Surah An-Naḥl, describing milk that comes forth *khāliṣan* — pure — *from between dung and blood.* Milk that emerges, clean and white and drinkable, from a body full of waste. That is *khulūṣ*: the miracle of the pure thing extracted from a mixture, carrying nothing of the mixture in it.

*Mukhliṣīn* is the active form: those who **make** something pure, who do the work of purifying. And what are they purifying? *Lahu d-dīn* — "the religion, **for Him.**" They are extracting from their devotion every element that is not Him. Every glance toward an audience. Every secret hope for reputation. Every divided loyalty. Until what is left is *khāliṣ* — devotion with nothing in it but Allah.

Ibn ʿAbbās, as al-Baghawī narrates, glosses exactly this: they were commanded *bi-l-ikhlāṣ fī l-ʿibādah*, "with purity in worship," *muwaḥḥidīn*, "affirming His oneness." Ikhlāṣ *is* tawḥīd lived inwardly.

[PAUSE]

Notice the placement of *lahu* — "for Him." It comes *before* its object, *al-dīn*. In Arabic, moving a word forward like this throws emphasis onto it. It is not "purifying the religion for Him" in a flat order. It is, with the stress falling hard: **"purifying — for Him alone — the religion."** The exclusivity is baked into the word order. Not mostly for Him. Not mainly for Him. *For Him* — and the sentence structure itself shoves everyone else out of the frame.

**Now the second: حُنَفَآءَ.**

The root is *ḥ-n-f*. And here the lexicographers tell us something beautiful: at its base, *ḥanafa* means *to incline, to lean, to turn away* from one thing toward another. The *aḥnaf* in old Arabic was someone whose feet turned inward — but the Qur'an takes this physical word for *turning* and makes it spiritual. The *ḥanīf* is the one who **turns away** from every false object of worship and **leans toward** the one true God. Al-Baghawī defines it precisely: *māʾilīn ʿan al-adyān kullihā ilā dīn al-islām* — "inclining *away from* all the false ways *toward* the way of submission." Al-Jalālayn ties it to *dīn Ibrāhīm* — the way of Abraham, the original ḥanīf, who turned his face away from his people's idols and toward the One who created the heavens.

[PAUSE — let this land]

Now hold the two words side by side and feel what they're doing.

**Mukhliṣīn** is a motion *inward and toward*: purifying your devotion, refining it down, aiming it *at Him.*

**Ḥunafāʾ** is a motion *outward and away*: turning your back on every false claimant, leaning *away* from all of it.

They are the same turn seen from two sides. When you turn your face toward the sun, you are — in the very same motion — turning your back on the dark. You cannot do one without the other. To become *mukhliṣ* — purely His — is, by the same act, to become *ḥanīf* — turned away from all else. Purity *toward* and inclination *away* are not two commands. They are the front and the back of one human being orienting itself.

This is why the heart cannot, in truth, serve two. Not because it is forbidden — though it is — but because *the geometry does not allow it.* To face one thing wholly is to have turned from everything else. *Mukhliṣīn... ḥunafāʾ.* The pure and the turned. One motion. One direction. One God.

### Element Four: The Religion That Stands — Qwm Echoing Through the Verse

I want to show you one last thing in the architecture, and it requires me to point at a root and ask you to listen for it like a note that gets struck twice.

The root is *q-w-m*. Its base meaning is the simplest, most physical thing imaginable: **to stand, to rise, to be upright.** A person who was sitting and now *qāma* — stood up. A pillar that *qāʾim* — stands erect. This root is about verticality, about not-collapsing, about being held up straight against the pull that wants everything to fall down.

Now listen for it in the verse. It is struck twice, near the end:

> وَيُقِيمُواْ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ ... وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ ٱلْقَيِّمَةِ
> *...wa yuqīmū ṣ-ṣalāta ... wa dhālika dīnu l-qayyimah.*

**First strike:** *yuqīmū.* This is the Form IV of *q-w-m* — the *factitive* form, the form that takes "to stand" and turns it into "**to make** something stand." When you make the prayer *yuqīmū*, you are not merely *doing* the prayer — performing the motions and leaving. You are *making the prayer stand up.* You are erecting it like a structure, raising it like a pillar, setting it upright so it holds. Al-Baghawī notes the command is for the prayer *fī awqātihā* — established *in its times,* standing firm in the architecture of the day.

This is why the Qur'an never says merely "pray." It says *aqīmū ṣ-ṣalāh* — *make the prayer stand.* A prayer can be performed and still be lying down — going through the forms while the heart sags. To *establish* it is to make it rise, to give it spine, to set it upright in your life as a load-bearing column.

**Second strike:** *al-qayyimah.* Same root. *Wa dhālika dīnu l-qayyimah* — "and **that** is the religion of *al-qayyimah*." Al-Jalālayn glosses it: *al-mustaqīmah* — the upright, the straight-standing. Al-Muyassar: *dīn al-istiqāmah* — the religion of standing-straight. The religion that does not lean, does not collapse, does not buckle. The religion that *stands.*

[PAUSE]

Do you hear what the verse has done? It has placed the *standing prayer* and the *standing religion* within a breath of each other, struck from the same root. **You make the prayer stand — and that act of standing is the very religion that stands.** The vertical posture of the worshipper and the vertical integrity of the whole religion are the same word, the same root, the same upward refusal to collapse.

And between the two strikes, almost tucked in, is *wa yuʾtū z-zakāh* — "and give the zakah." If *ṣalāh* is the standing of the body in worship, *zakāh* is the standing of the wealth in worship — and *zakāh* itself comes from a root meaning *to grow, to purify, to increase.* Wealth that is purified by being given away. So even here, the theme of purity from element three returns: *mukhliṣīn* purifies the heart, *zakāh* purifies the wealth. The inside and the outside, both made *khāliṣ.*

So the structure of the whole verse, root by root, is this: a religion *cleared* of all else (the restriction), *aimed* at Him alone (the *lām* and the *ikhlāṣ*), *turned away* from all falsehood (the *ḥanīf*), *stood upright* in prayer and *purified* in wealth — and *that*, exactly that and nothing more, is *the religion that stands.*

### The Bridge

We have walked through the bones of this verse. We have seen the wall of restriction that clears the field. We have seen the little *lām* that turns a command into a purpose. We have seen the two-directional turn of the pure heart, facing Him and turned from all else. And we have heard the root *q-w-m* struck twice, binding the standing prayer to the standing religion.

But these are not just facts about words. The verse claims to hold the whole of religion. So now let's ask what it reveals not just about language — but about *us.* About what it actually means to live as a creature whose entire purpose collapses to a single point.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of the Divided Heart, and the Mercy of *Ikhlāṣ*

Let me start where you live.

You have had this experience. You did something good — you helped someone, you gave something, you prayed, you spoke a kind truth — and then, in the quiet afterward, you noticed a second voice. A voice that was already composing the story of what you did. Already imagining who would hear about it. Already adjusting your posture, ever so slightly, toward an invisible audience. And you felt it: the good thing had been *adulterated.* Something pure had something mixed into it. You couldn't quite get the milk clean of the blood.

This is the universal human condition, and modern life has a thousand names for it. We talk about *performing* our lives for an audience that may not even be watching. We talk about *the gap between the self we present and the self we are.* We talk about exhaustion — the specific, bone-deep tiredness of *managing impressions,* of being slightly different in every room, of never being one thing all the way through. Psychologists call it self-monitoring. The soul has an older name for it. It is the tyranny of the divided heart.

And here is the thing we rarely admit: **this division is not freedom. It is fatigue.** The person who lives for many eyes is enslaved to all of them. Every audience holds a fragment of your peace. Please one, you've offended another. The more masters of your reputation, the more anxious your every act becomes. To live divided is to be a servant with a hundred owners, each pulling.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the verse again: *mukhliṣīna lahu d-dīn.* Making the religion **purely His.**

We usually read *ikhlāṣ* as a *demand* — one more thing required of us, a high standard to live up to. But turn it over. *Ikhlāṣ* is not first a demand. **It is a mercy.** It is the offer of *one* audience. One. The collapse of the hundred owners into a single Owner who sees everything, who cannot be deceived, and who therefore cannot be *performed for.* You cannot impress the One who already sees the inside. And so, before the One, the entire machinery of impression-management simply... powers down. There is no posture to adjust. No story to compose. He already knows the story. He wrote you.

This is why *ikhlāṣ* feels, when you taste even a moment of it, less like effort and more like *rest.* The milk comes clean of the blood. The act becomes *khāliṣ* — and a *khāliṣ* act is a *light* act, because it isn't carrying the weight of an audience. You did it for One. The One has seen it. It is done. You can put it down.

The divided heart asks, after every deed: *did they see? was it enough? what will they think?* — and never receives an answer, because the audience is many and fickle and mostly absent. The pure heart asks one question — *did I do it for Him?* — and receives, in the asking, its answer. He saw. That is the whole of it.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is one concrete thing. Tomorrow, take one single act — a prayer, a kindness, a piece of work — and before you do it, say silently: *this one is only for You; no one else needs to know it happened.* And then guard it. Tell no one. Let it stay *khāliṣ.* You will discover that the secret deed has a flavor the public deed never had. That flavor is *ikhlāṣ.* That flavor is what your scriptures — all of them — were pointing you toward the entire time.

### Theme Two: The Religion That Stands — Why Worship Has Posture

Let me show you something about the human body, and then about the human soul.

When a person gives up — really gives up, on the inside — the first thing that goes is the spine. The shoulders round. The head drops. The frame folds toward the ground. We say someone is "crushed," "deflated," "brought low." And the reverse is just as true: when a person finds something worth living for, they *straighten up.* They rise. The body knows before the mind admits it. **Posture is theology made visible.** To stand upright is to declare that you are held by something, aimed at something, refusing to collapse.

This is not a metaphor the Qur'an stumbled into. It is the deliberate architecture of this verse. *Yuqīmū ṣ-ṣalāh* — **make the prayer stand.** *Dīn al-qayyimah* — the religion that **stands straight.** Worship, in the language of revelation, has *posture.* It is the soul's refusal to fold.

Think about what *salah* physically is. Five times in the cycle of the day, against the gravity of habit and fatigue and distraction — all of which pull you *down,* toward the screen, toward the bed, toward the endless horizontal scroll — you *stand up.* You interrupt the falling. You make something rise. And the word for it is not "do the prayer." It is *aqim* — *erect it, raise it, make it stand.* Because a prayer can be performed while lying down inside. You can move through every motion with a heart slumped on the floor. To *establish* the prayer is to give it a spine.

[PAUSE]

And now feel why the verse binds *yuqīmū* and *al-qayyimah* together. The religion that stands — *dīn al-qayyimah* — is not standing on its own, hovering as an abstraction. **It stands because you stand it up.** Five times a day, you raise the pillar. And a religion is nothing but pillars raised, day after day, by people who refused to let it fall. *Al-istiqāmah* — uprightness — the very thing we are commanded to ask for seventeen times a day in *ihdinā ṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm,* "guide us to the straight path" — that uprightness is built one standing-up at a time.

Here is the deep thing. We tend to think of "having faith" as a state we possess, a thing stored somewhere inside us, a noun we either own or lack. But this verse describes faith as a *verb of posture.* It is not something you *have.* It is something you *stand up,* again and again, against the pull that wants it flat. The religion does not stand on its own and ask you to lean on it. **You stand it up, and in standing it up, you yourself are stood upright.** The one who erects the prayer is, in the same motion, erected by it.

There is a paradox here worth sitting in without rushing to resolve it: you are commanded to *make stand* the very thing that *makes you stand.* You raise the prayer; the prayer raises you. You hold up the religion; the religion holds you up. Which is the support and which is the supported? The verse does not let you separate them — they are bound by one root, *q-w-m,* precisely because in lived reality they cannot be pulled apart. You give it your standing; it gives you back your spine.

[PAUSE]

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Stop asking yourself *do I have enough faith?* — it's the wrong question; faith is not a quantity you possess. Ask instead: *did I stand it up today?* When the prayer time comes and everything in you and around you is pulling you horizontal — *stand up.* That single act of rising, against gravity, against mood, against the slump — that *is* the religion of *al-qayyimah.* You don't need to feel upright to stand up. You stand up, and the uprightness follows. The posture comes first; the soul straightens to match it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built like a structure with a center and rings around it.

At the very **center**: *li-yaʿbudū Llāh* — "to worship Allah." The single point left standing after the restriction cleared the field.

Around that center, the **inner ring — the heart**: *mukhliṣīna lahu d-dīna ḥunafāʾ* — purified toward Him, turned away from all else. The two-directional turn of the inward self.

Around that, the **outer ring — the limbs and the wealth**: *yuqīmū ṣ-ṣalāh* (the body stood upright in prayer) and *yuʾtū z-zakāh* (the wealth purified by giving). The inside made visible in action.

And then the **seal** over the whole thing: *wa dhālika dīnu l-qayyimah* — "and *that* is the religion that stands." The demonstrative *dhālika*, "that," gathers everything that came before — the worship, the purity, the turning, the standing prayer, the given wealth — and stamps it: *this, all of this together, is the upright religion.* Not a part of it. The whole of it.

The structure mirrors its own meaning. A cleared center, a purified heart, an upright body, sealed as the religion that stands. The form *is* the message.

### The Two Roads Meet

Notice now that the linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same insight approached from two directions.

The grammar of **restriction** — *mā... illā,* nothing except — and the theme of **the divided heart finding rest in one Audience** are the same truth. The verse *grammatically* clears the field to one point; the soul *experientially* finds peace when it clears its devotion to one Owner. *Ikhlāṣ* is *mā... illā* lived in the heart: nothing, except Him.

And the root **q-w-m** struck twice — the standing prayer, the standing religion — and the theme of **faith as posture** are the same truth. The verse *linguistically* binds the act of standing-up to the religion that stands; the soul *experientially* discovers that it is held upright by the very thing it holds up.

Restriction and posture. *Purify it to one,* and *stand it up.* That is the entire architecture of a saved life, and it is the entire architecture of this verse.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I do something good, how many audiences am I secretly performing for — and what would it feel like to do one thing, tomorrow, for an Audience of One?

2. What have I mixed into my worship that is not Him — and am I willing to do the work of *khulūṣ,* of extracting the milk clean from the blood?

3. Is there something I am still *leaning toward* that I have not yet *turned away from* — some idol of approval, or comfort, or fear — that keeps me from being fully *ḥanīf?*

4. When the prayer time comes and everything pulls me horizontal, do I *establish* it, or merely perform it? Do I stand it up, or let it lie down?

5. The verse says this was *all they were ever commanded.* What complicated version of religion have I built that this simple, terrifying core might be asking me to set down?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**Everything God ever commanded, in every scripture and every age, was only this: empty your worship of all but Him, turn your face from all else toward Him, and stand that devotion upright in prayer and in giving — for that, and nothing more, is the religion that stands.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to become someone new to live this verse. You do not need a more complicated religion. The invitation here is, if anything, an invitation to *subtraction* — to clear the field, to extract the impurities, to put down the hundred audiences and the divided self. It is an invitation to discover that the thing you may have feared was a heavy demand — *ikhlāṣ,* purity, total devotion — is in fact the lightest thing there is, because it is the only state in which the soul is finally undivided, finally at rest, finally standing up straight.

Let the field be cleared. Let one thing stand in it. And then stand up.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who need no audience and see what no eye sees — purify our devotion until nothing remains in it but You. Turn our faces, as You turned the face of Ibrāhīm, away from every falsehood and toward Your light alone. And when everything within us and around us pulls us toward the ground, give us the strength to stand the prayer upright, that we may be counted among the people of dīn al-qayyimah — the religion that stands.* Āmīn.
