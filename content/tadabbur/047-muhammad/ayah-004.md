# The Grammar That Bends Toward Mercy

## Introduction: Standing Where the Verse Was Heard

Let me ask you to do something difficult before we begin.

I want you to set down, just for the length of this reflection, the version of this verse you may have already met. Because if you have heard about Surah Muhammad, verse 4, there is a good chance you heard it quoted by someone who wanted to frighten you with it. Four words, ripped from their sentence, brandished like a banner: *strike the necks.* You have perhaps seen them on a screen behind a newscaster. You have perhaps had them thrown at you in an argument you did not ask to be in.

So here is what I want us to do instead. I want us to walk all the way into the verse — not around it, not past the uncomfortable part, but *through* it, word by word, in the order Allah arranged them. Because something happens inside this sentence that you will never see when it is chopped into a slogan. The verse begins with a sword. And then watch what it does. **Watch where its own grammar carries it.**

Here is the ayah:

> فَإِذَا لَقِيتُمُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ فَضَرْبَ ٱلرِّقَابِ حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَآ أَثْخَنتُمُوهُمْ فَشُدُّوا۟ ٱلْوَثَاقَ فَإِمَّا مَنًّۢا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَآءً حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ ٱلْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ وَلَوْ يَشَآءُ ٱللَّهُ لَٱنتَصَرَ مِنْهُمْ وَلَٰكِن لِّيَبْلُوَا۟ بَعْضَكُم بِبَعْضٍ ۗ وَٱلَّذِينَ قُتِلُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ فَلَن يُضِلَّ أَعْمَٰلَهُمْ

*Fa-idhā laqītumu-lladhīna kafarū fa-ḍarba-r-riqāb, ḥattā idhā athkhantumūhum fa-shuddū-l-wathāq, fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan ḥattā taḍaʿa-l-ḥarbu awzārahā. Dhālik. Wa-law yashāʾu-llāhu la-ntaṣara minhum wa-lākin li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍ. Wa-lladhīna qutilū fī sabīli-llāhi fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum.*

"So when you meet those who disbelieve in battle, strike the necks — until, when you have thoroughly subdued them, bind firmly the bond. Then either favor afterward, or ransom — until the war lays down its burdens. That. And had Allah willed, He could have taken retribution from them Himself; but [this is] so that He may test some of you by means of others. And those who are killed in the way of Allah — never will He let their deeds be lost."

### Rebuild the world

Picture the people who first heard this.

This is Madinah. The community is young — a few years old. These are not professional soldiers. They are date-farmers and merchants and former shepherds. Many of them were, until recently, persecuted in Makkah: boycotted, starved, some tortured, all of them eventually driven from their homes. They did not seek an empire. They sought a city where they could pray without being hunted. And the armies of their former tribesmen have followed them even there, intent on extinguishing the whole experiment.

So when this verse comes, it does not come to conquerors. It comes to people who have just learned, painfully, that there are moments when refusing to fight is not mercy — it is the abandonment of the weak to the strong. The surah has just drawn its great dividing line: those who disbelieve and *bar others from the path of Allah*, against those who believe and do what is right. Verse 4 is where that line becomes a battlefield.

And notice — this is *jus in bello*. This is the law of conduct *within* a war that already exists. The verse does not say "go find disbelievers." It says, "So **when you meet** those who disbelieve" — *when the meeting on the field has already happened.* The fighting is assumed; the verse is here to govern it, to put walls around it, to tell these frightened farmers what they may do and — far more strikingly — what they may *not* do once the enemy is in their hands.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to hold onto as we begin.

This verse contains the most physically violent image in this entire surah. And by the time it ends — in the very same breath, the very same sentence — it has arrived at *grace*, at *release*, at a war so personified it grows tired and sets down its bags, at a God who tells you He never needed your sword at all, and at a promise that nothing done sincerely will ever, ever be lost.

The question I want us to answer is this: **How does a single sentence travel that far?** How do you start at the neck and end at the imperishable? [PAUSE]

The answer is hidden in the grammar. Let's go and find it.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: The Word That Is Missing — *fa-ḍarba-r-riqāb*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse. Stay with me, because it will change what you see.

In Arabic, there is a construction grammarians call the *maṣdar* — the verbal noun. It is the naked idea of an action, stripped of who does it and when. Not "he struck," not "strike!" — just *striking*. The pure act, held up like an object.

Now, ordinarily, if Allah wanted to issue a command here, the natural form would be a full imperative verb: *fa-ḍribū* — "so **strike**." Subject built in, addressee built in, a complete order: *you all, strike.* That is what the grammar leads you to expect.

But that is not what the verse says.

Look closely:

> فَضَرْبَ ٱلرِّقَابِ
>
> *fa-ḍarba-r-riqāb*

This is not the verb *fa-ḍribū*. This is the **verbal noun** — *ḍarb*, "a striking" — followed by *al-riqāb*, "the necks." Literally, almost untranslatably: "then — a striking of the necks."

Notice what has gone missing. **There is no verb. There is no "you."** The command has been compressed into a bare noun.

The grammarians saw this immediately. Al-Zajjāj, quoted by al-Qurṭubī, explains it as a *maṣdar* standing in for its own verb — *fa-ḍribū-r-riqāba ḍarban*, "so strike the necks with a striking" — where the original verb has been deleted and the verbal noun left to carry the whole weight of the command. Al-Jalālayn says the same: it is a *maṣdar badal min al-lafẓ bi-l-fiʿl* — a verbal noun *substituting* for the explicit verb. Al-Baghawī offers a second angle: *naṣb ʿalā-l-ighrāʾ* — the accusative of *incitement*, the grammatical case Arabic uses when you thrust a thing in front of someone in the heat of urgency. Think of how, in English, in a crisis we drop the verb entirely and just shout the noun: *"Fire!" "The door!" "Now!"*

Hold these together — they are not rival readings, they are two cameras on one reality. **This is the language of emergency.** [PAUSE]

Let's sit with what that means. Allah did not give a calm, complete sentence here, because a calm complete sentence is not what a battlefield is. When the lines have met and the swords are out, there is no time for grammar's full architecture. There is only the act, thrust forward, naked and immediate: *striking — the necks.* The very *form* of the words enacts the very *moment* they describe. The deletion of the verb *is* the compression of time in combat. The sentence is breathless because the moment is breathless.

Do you see what this means for the people quoting it as a banner? They quote it as if it were a calm, standing, eternal slogan. But grammatically it is the *opposite* of a slogan. It is a gasp. It belongs only to the split-second of an engagement already underway. A slogan is leisurely; this construction has no leisure in it at all.

Now stay with me, because there is a second thing in these two words.

Why **necks**? *Al-riqāb.*

The plain answer, given by al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both, is anatomical and almost merciful in its logic: the neck is named *because killing most often happens there* — it is the swiftest, the most decisive, the least prolonging of suffering. Even in the act of war, the language reaches for the quickest end, not the cruelest.

But the word *raqaba* — neck — carries something else across the Quran. The *raqaba* is, again and again, the site of bondage and the site of pride. *Fakku raqaba* — "the freeing of a neck" — is how the Quran names the liberation of a slave. The neck is what a yoke sits on. The neck is what we *stiffen* when we are arrogant — in Arabic as in English, to be unbending is to have a stiff neck. The disbelief the verse confronts is, at its root, the arrogance that *bars others from the path of Allah*. And the verse strikes precisely at the seat of that arrogance.

Here is what this single missing verb reveals: **the harshest command in the surah is delivered in the briefest, most urgent, most narrowly-bounded grammar available** — confined to the one anatomical site that ends suffering fastest and that symbolizes the very pride being confronted. The verse is already, in its first two words, refusing to be the open-ended license its abusers want it to be. [PAUSE — let this land]

## Element Two: The War That Grows Tired — *ḥattā taḍaʿa-l-ḥarbu awzārahā*

Now follow the sentence forward, because it is about to do something extraordinary.

After the striking comes *athkhantumūhum* — "when you have thoroughly subdued them." This is a Form IV verb from a root, *th-kh-n*, that means **thickness, density, heaviness**. To *athkhana* an enemy is to render them *thick* — beaten down, weighed heavy, decisively overwhelmed, their resistance made dense and immovable. It is intensive and transitive: not merely "you fought them," but "you brought them to the point of total subduing." And the Quran elsewhere ties this exact word to the question of captives — *"It is not for a prophet to have captives until he has thoroughly subdued the land"* (8:67). The taking of prisoners comes *after* the battle is genuinely decided, not as its opening move.

And then — *fa-shuddū-l-wathāq* — "bind firmly the bond." The hand that struck the neck now *ties* it instead. The same enemy, moments later, is alive and bound. The sentence has already turned from killing to keeping-alive. We'll return to that turn in the themes.

But I want to take you to the clause that comes after, because it is one of the most beautiful images in the Quran's language of war:

> حَتَّىٰ تَضَعَ ٱلْحَرْبُ أَوْزَارَهَا
>
> *ḥattā taḍaʿa-l-ḥarbu awzārahā*
>
> "until the war lays down its burdens."

Let me teach the device first. This is **personification** — *tashkhīṣ*, in the Arabic rhetorical tradition: granting a living agency to something that is not alive. We do it instinctively. We say "the storm *wanted* the coastline." We say "time *heals*." We say "the night *fell*." When we personify, we are reaching for a truth that the literal cannot hold.

So watch what Allah personifies here: not a soldier, not an army — **the war itself.** *Al-ḥarb.* The war is made into a being. And what does this being do? It *taḍaʿu* — it sets down, it lays down, it puts off — *awzārahā* — its *awzār.*

Sit with that word, *awzār.* Its root, *w-z-r*, means **a heavy load, a burden, a weight you carry**. It is the same root from which the Quran builds the word *wizr* — the burden of *sin*. It is the root of *wazīr* — a "minister," literally the one who *carries the weight* of governance for the ruler. *Awzār* are the heavy things — the armor, the weapons, the loads of a campaign — and at the same time, by the root's own resonance, the *moral weight*, the grievous heaviness, that war drags behind it.

Now put the whole image together and let it move. **The war is a creature bent double under a great weight.** It is staggering forward, loaded with iron and grief, carrying its burdens on its back. And the verse looks past all the striking and binding to a single horizon: *the moment this exhausted creature finally stops and lets the weight slide off its shoulders to the ground.* [PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The grammatical engine of this clause is the word *ḥattā* — "until." And *ḥattā* always points at an *endpoint*, a *terminus*, the place where a thing is *meant to stop*. Read the verse again and count how many times it reaches for an ending: *ḥattā* you have subdued them — *ḥattā* the war lays down its burdens. The sentence is structurally obsessed with *cessation*. Its grammar keeps straining toward the stopping point. War, in this verse, is never a state to inhabit; it is a weight to be *put down*. **The natural resting position of the sentence — and of the world it describes — is peace.** The fighting is the anomaly that the grammar cannot wait to end.

Notice something that absence teaches us. The verse never says "until you have conquered them all," never "until none remain who disbelieve," never "until the earth is yours." It says: *until the war lays down its burdens.* The goal is not the annihilation of the other. **The goal is the laying-down of the weight.** The objective named is the *end of the carrying*, not the end of the carried.

Do you feel how far we have already traveled inside one sentence? We began at a neck and a blade in a breathless half-clause with no verb. We are now watching war as a tired animal longing to set down its load. The same sentence that started in violence is now aching, grammatically aching, toward rest. [PAUSE — major]

### The bridge

So look at what the construction has shown us. The harshest command arrives in the most urgent, most bounded, most quickly-ending grammar Arabic has. And the trajectory of the whole sentence — *ḥattā*, *ḥattā*, *ḥattā* — is a trajectory of *stopping*, of *laying down*, of *binding rather than killing*, of *reaching the rest on the far side of the weight*.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About what God is doing with us when He hands us something as terrible as a war. About where mercy hides inside command. And about the strangest sentence in the verse — the one where God tells you He never needed any of it.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Mercy You Were Not Forced to Give

Let me start where you live, not where the verse is.

Think of the last time you had power over someone who had wronged you. Not a battlefield — your life. The colleague who undermined you, finally exposed and now needing a reference. The sibling who hurt you, now needing your help. The person who, after months of being against you, is suddenly *in your hands* — their fate, for one moment, genuinely yours to decide.

You know that moment. There is a kind of heat in it. The whole weight of everything they did rises up and says: *now. Now you can make it even.* And what the heart wants, in that moment, is not justice. It is the *pleasure* of the other person's diminishment. We have all felt it. It is one of the most human things there is, and one of the most quietly poisonous.

Now bring that feeling to the verse, because the verse walks a believer straight into exactly that moment — at its most extreme.

The enemy who came to destroy your community is now defeated, *athkhantumūhum*, thoroughly subdued, and *bound*, *fa-shuddū-l-wathāq*. He is alive, helpless, kneeling, tied, in your hand. The heat is at its absolute maximum. This is the man who killed your friends.

And what does the verse say next?

> فَإِمَّا مَنًّۢا بَعْدُ وَإِمَّا فِدَآءً
>
> *fa-immā mannan baʿdu wa-immā fidāʾan*
>
> "then either favor afterward, or ransom."

Stop. Read what is *there* — and then, the way we learned in Part One, read what is *not.*

What is there: *mannan* — *mann*, from the root *m-n-n*, which means an act of *gracious favor*, a giving that expects nothing back. The same root from which Allah names Himself *al-Mannān*, the Ever-Bestowing. *Mann* is to release the captive **for nothing** — no payment, no exchange, simply set free as a gift. And *fidāʾ* — ransom — to release him in exchange for wealth or for the freeing of Muslim prisoners held by the other side.

Now read what is *not* there. **In this clause, in this sentence, after the binding, the verse names two doors, and both of them open outward. Both of them are release.** [PAUSE]

I want to be careful and honest here, because this is a verse the scholars discussed at length, and you deserve their actual disagreement, not a tidy version. The jurists differed over the fate of captives in the broader law: al-Qurṭubī records the full debate — some held that *mann* and *fidāʾ* are the only two options this verse leaves standing; others, drawing on verses like Sūrat al-Tawba, held that the imam retains discretion that can include other outcomes for combatants; and there was disagreement over whether this verse abrogates or is abrogated by others. Al-Muyassar notes that the broader juristic options scholars derived also included enslavement or, for some categories, execution. This is a genuine *ikhtilāf*, and it belongs to trained jurists ruling on real cases with all the conditions in view — not to you or me, and certainly not to a slogan. I am not here to settle the fiqh.

But I *am* here to make you feel the *rhetoric* — what Allah chose to place in the foreground of the sentence itself. And in the foreground, **the only two acts He names by name are an act of pure grace and an act of mutual exchange.** When the heat was at its maximum, when the heart most wanted to make it even, the verse's own vocabulary turns toward *mann* — the word that belongs to God's own generosity. [PAUSE]

Let's go deeper. Here is the thing about *mann*, about releasing your enemy for nothing: **you were not forced to.** The verse leaves it open — *either* favor *or* ransom. The mercy is not extracted from you. It is *offered* to you as a possibility you must *choose* to enter.

And this is the secret of how Islam refines a soul. It does not abolish the moment of power — that would be to abolish the test. It walks you *into* the moment of maximum heat, when every instinct says *make him pay*, and there, at the peak of your justification, it whispers the word *mann.* **It dignifies you by making the mercy yours to give.** The defeated enemy cannot give you that. Only you can give it — to him, and in truth, to yourself.

Connect this to your own life now. The verse is not asking most of us to release prisoners of war. It is exposing a *mechanism of the heart* — the moment when you have someone at a disadvantage and the heat rises. The reference you could refuse to write. The apology you could withhold. The forgiveness you could ration out. The text message you could leave on read to make them feel it. In every one of those moments, you are standing, in miniature, exactly where this verse places the believer. Bound enemy before you. Two doors. And the word *mann* — grace given freely, expecting nothing — waiting to see if you will reach for it *when you did not have to.*

**Tomorrow, find the one person you currently have a small power over, and open the door of *mann* you were not forced to open.** That is the verse, lived. That is the neck, untied.

## Theme Two: The War God Did Not Need

Now we come to the sentence that, the first time you truly hear it, can stop your breath.

The verse has commanded the fighting, the binding, the choice of mercy. And then it says one word, standing alone like a closed door:

> ذَٰلِكَ
>
> *Dhālik.*
>
> "That."

Just — *that.* A single demonstrative, abrupt, almost dismissive, as if Allah sets the whole bloody business down on the table and steps back from it. *That is the matter.* And then, immediately, He says something that recasts everything that came before:

> وَلَوْ يَشَآءُ ٱللَّهُ لَٱنتَصَرَ مِنْهُمْ وَلَٰكِن لِّيَبْلُوَا۟ بَعْضَكُم بِبَعْضٍ
>
> *wa-law yashāʾu-llāhu la-ntaṣara minhum wa-lākin li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍ*
>
> "And had Allah willed, He could have taken retribution from them Himself — but [it is] so that He may test some of you by means of others."

Let the first half of that land fully. *La-ntaṣara* — Form VIII of the root *n-ṣ-r*, victory and vindication — "He would have vindicated Himself, taken His own retribution, secured the victory directly." **Had Allah willed, He would never have needed you to lift a finger.** [PAUSE]

Sit in that, because it overturns everything the slogan-quoters and the frightened both assume. They both assume that the violence is *for God* — that God *needs* it, that the enemy is destroyed *because God requires their destruction* and depends on His soldiers to accomplish it. And here is Allah Himself, in the middle of the verse, saying: *No. I needed none of it. One word from Me and they were finished. I do not require your armies. I am not made greater by their defeat or weaker by their existence.*

So if God did not need it — *why?* The verse answers with the most important word in it:

> لِّيَبْلُوَا۟ بَعْضَكُم بِبَعْضٍ
>
> *li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍ*
>
> "so that He may test some of you by means of others."

*Yabluwa* — from *b-l-w*, the root of *ibtilāʾ*, the divine *testing*, the trial that *refines*. Its root meaning touches on *wearing a thing out by use*, the way you test a sword by striking with it until you know its true metal. The entire arena of conflict — the neck, the bond, the heat, the choice between vengeance and *mann* — **the whole thing is not about the enemy at all. It is about you.** [PAUSE]

This reframes the universe. Here is the modern lie we all half-believe: that our struggles are *about our opponents.* That the difficult colleague, the hostile critic, the person who came against us, is the *point* — that life is a series of external enemies to be overcome. And the verse reaches into that whole frame and turns it inside out. The opponent, Allah says, is *the instrument*, not the purpose. *Li-yabluwa* — *I am testing you by means of them.* The other person is the chisel; *you* are the stone being shaped. God does not need them defeated. **God wants you revealed** — to yourself, in the moment of power, when the only thing being measured is whether *you* reached for the neck or for the *mann.*

Do you see how the two themes are one? The mercy you were not forced to give — *that* was the test. The whole point of placing a bound enemy in your hands and leaving the door of *mann* unforced was *li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍ.* The battlefield is a mirror. Every conflict in your life, down to the smallest, is God testing *you* by means of *them* — wearing you against another soul to see what metal you are made of.

And then — having told you that you yourself are the subject of the test, having stripped away the comforting fiction that any of this was about destroying an enemy God needed destroyed — the verse turns, in its final clause, to console the one who paid the highest price:

> وَٱلَّذِينَ قُتِلُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ فَلَن يُضِلَّ أَعْمَٰلَهُمْ
>
> *wa-lladhīna qutilū fī sabīli-llāhi fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum*
>
> "And those who are killed in the way of Allah — never will He let their deeds be lost."

Look at the verb: *yuḍill* — Form IV of *ḍ-l-l*, the root of *misguidance, going astray, being lost.* The surah *opened* with this exact root used against the enemy: those who disbelieve and bar God's path, *aḍalla aʿmālahum* — "He has made their deeds go astray, rendered them lost." And now, at the verse's close, the same root is *negated* for the believer: *fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum* — "He will **never** let their deeds go astray." [PAUSE]

Feel the architecture. The disbeliever's deeds: *lost.* The martyr's deeds: *never lost.* The same verb, one affirmed and one denied, framing the human possibility. And notice the tenderness of the chosen word. Allah does not merely say "He will reward them." He says their deeds will never be *misguided*, never *go astray*, never *wander off and fail to arrive.* It is the image of a deed as a traveler on a road — and God's promise is that this traveler will *never lose its way home.* The one who fell on the field worried, perhaps, in his last breath, whether it had all been *for nothing.* And the verse answers across the centuries: *nothing you did in My way will ever fail to arrive.*

**This is your practical anchor:** the fear underneath so much of our struggle is the fear of *waste* — that our sacrifice, our patience, our unseen effort, will simply evaporate, unrecorded, lost. This verse, born on a battlefield, speaks to every quiet sacrifice you make that no one sees. *Fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum.* Tomorrow, when you do the hard right thing that no one will applaud, plant it in *this* soil: it is a traveler on a road, and God Himself guarantees it will arrive.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole sentence at once. Watch what it does as a single shape.

It **begins at the neck** — *fa-ḍarba-r-riqāb* — in a breathless, verb-less half-clause, the grammar of pure emergency.

It moves to the **bond** — *fa-shuddū-l-wathāq* — the hand that struck now ties, the enemy now alive.

It opens the **door of grace** — *mannan* — the word of God's own generosity, mercy you were not forced to give.

It reaches for the **laid-down burden** — *taḍaʿa-l-ḥarbu awzārahā* — war as a tired creature longing to set down its weight, the whole sentence aching toward rest.

It strips away the **illusion** — *wa-law yashāʾu-llāhu la-ntaṣara minhum* — God never needed any of it.

It names the **true subject** — *li-yabluwa baʿḍakum bi-baʿḍ* — the test was always you.

And it closes on the **imperishable** — *fa-lan yuḍilla aʿmālahum* — nothing in His way is ever lost.

Do you see it now? **This is a descent that the grammar will not let stay down.** Every clause that follows the sword pulls upward — toward binding instead of killing, toward grace, toward rest, toward your own refinement, toward permanence. The verse begins in the most physical violence the surah contains and ends in the most spiritual promise it makes. And it is *one sentence.* The journey we asked about at the very beginning — from the neck to the imperishable — is accomplished by a grammar that, from its first breathless word, was already straining toward the stopping point.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **missing verb** in *fa-ḍarba-r-riqāb* showed us a command bounded by urgency, refusing to be a standing slogan — and the theme of the **test** told us why: because this was never about open-ended conquest, it was about a *moment* designed to refine *you.*

The **war laying down its burdens** showed us a grammar obsessed with stopping — and the theme of **unforced mercy** told us what waits at that stopping point: the door of *mann* that God leaves you to open, or not, by your own hand.

The harshness was always the frame. The mercy, the rest, and the refinement of your soul were always the picture inside it.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life right now do I hold a small power over someone who has wronged me — and which door am I keeping my hand on?
2. When I feel the *heat* of wanting someone diminished, can I learn to hear, underneath it, the question God is actually asking: *what metal are you?*
3. Have I been living as though my struggles are *about my opponents* — when the verse says they are the chisel and I am the stone?
4. What sacrifice am I afraid has been *wasted* — and can I trust the promise that nothing in His way ever fails to arrive?
5. When I quote a verse, or hear one quoted, do I read the whole sentence in the order God arranged it — or do I let it be chopped into a banner?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Allah commands the believers to fight, and in the very same breath bends every clause of the sentence toward binding, grace, rest, and the refining of their own souls — for He needed no army, only that you be revealed, and that nothing done in His way ever be lost.

## The Closing Invitation

Let something shift. The next time the heat rises — the small daily battlefield, the person in your hand — remember that you are standing inside this verse. The blade and the bond are behind you. In front of you are two doors, and one of them is *mann*, the grace you were never forced to give. Reach for it *because* you were not forced to. That is the whole test. That is the neck, untied. And know, as you do the unseen hard thing, that it is a traveler on a road who will never lose its way home.

## Du'a

Test us and find us faithful, refine us by every soul You set against us, and let nothing we have done in Your way ever wander off and fail to arrive. *Āmīn.*
