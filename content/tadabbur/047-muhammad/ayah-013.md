# The Town That Cast You Out — and the Ruins That Were Stronger

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first, and then let me take it away again — because before this verse can land, we need to build the ground it stands on.

> [ayah:47:13] وَكَأَيِّن مِّن قَرْيَةٍ هِىَ أَشَدُّ قُوَّةً مِّن قَرْيَتِكَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَخْرَجَتْكَ أَهْلَكْنَـٰهُمْ فَلَا نَاصِرَ لَهُمْ

*Wa-ka'ayyin min qaryatin hiya ashaddu quwwatan min qaryatika 'llatī akhrajatka, ahlaknāhum, falā nāṣira lahum.*

"And how many a town — far stronger in power than your town which has driven you out — have We destroyed, and there was no helper for them."

Thirteen words. And I want you to feel, before anything else, the *situation* these words were spoken into.

### Rebuild the world

Picture a man walking out of the only city he has ever known. He was born there. His mother is buried near there. He learned to walk on those streets, did business in that marketplace, prayed at that ancient House his forefather built. Every memory he owns is pressed into the stone of this place.

And now he is leaving — not on a journey, not by choice. He is being *driven out*. The people of his own city have decided there is no longer room for him in it. They have plotted against his life. He slips out in the dark, takes shelter in a cave, and at some point — the classical commentators preserve this — he turns. He looks back at the skyline of the city behind him.

And what comes out of his mouth is not a curse. Al-Baghawi narrates that the Prophet ﷺ, leaving Mecca, looked back toward it and said: *"You are the most beloved of Allah's lands to Allah, and the most beloved of Allah's lands to me. Had the idolaters not driven me out, I would never have left you."*

Read that again. He is being expelled — and he is calling the place that expelled him *the most beloved land to me.*

[PAUSE]

That is the human heart at its most raw. To be cast out by the very thing you love most. To have your home become the hand that pushes you away. There is no clean word for this pain in any language — it is grief and love and rejection and longing, all firing at once.

And into that exact moment, Allah sends down these thirteen words.

### The Hook

Now here is what should stop us. When you comfort someone who has been wronged, you usually do one of two things. You either tell them *the ones who hurt you will be punished* — you point forward to justice. Or you tell them *you didn't deserve this* — you affirm their worth.

Allah does something stranger here. He does not, in this verse, talk about Mecca's punishment at all. He talks about **other cities. Cities that were stronger.** And He buries inside these thirteen words a grammatical movement so precise that the heart can feel itself being held — even as the mind is still trying to figure out what just happened.

There is a word here that means *uncountably many.* There is a town that is somehow both the one that *threw him out* and still, tenderly, called *his.* And there is a final phrase built on a kind of negation in Arabic that doesn't just say "no" — it slams a door on every possibility, forever.

Let's earn each one.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: وَكَأَيِّن — "And how many…"

Let me teach you something about how language counts before we look at the word.

There is a difference between counting and *overwhelming.* If I tell you "three people came to the door," I've counted. You can picture three. But if I throw up my hands and say "you would not *believe* how many people came" — I haven't given you a number at all. I've given you a feeling. I've told you the number is so large that counting is the wrong tool for it.

Arabic has a word built precisely for that second move. **وَكَأَيِّن** — *wa-ka'ayyin.*

The classical commentators are unanimous on its meaning. Al-Qurtubi says plainly: *"wa-ka'ayyin"* here *bi-maʿnā kam* — it means *"how many."* And he quotes the poet Labīd to show its flavor in the living Arabic: *"How many kings and commoners have we seen…"* — meaning *so many we lost count.*

So notice what Allah does not say. He does not say "We destroyed *seven* cities" or "*forty* nations." A number would let you file it away. A number is finite; you can hold it in one hand and set it down.

[PAUSE]

Instead He opens with a word that means: *the ones who came before you, who were in this exact position of arrogant power, are beyond counting.* Stay with this for a moment. To the man walking out of Mecca — alone, outnumbered, his community shrunk to a handful of believers — the deepest temptation is the feeling of being *singular.* That this has never happened before. That his enemies are uniquely strong, his situation uniquely hopeless.

And the very first word of the consolation dissolves that loneliness: **how many.** You are not the first. This town that feels so mighty to those inside it is not even rare. History is *littered* with stronger.

Here is what's remarkable. The word doesn't comfort by minimizing the enemy — it comforts by *contextualizing* him. Mecca is not nothing. Mecca is real, and powerful, and dangerous. But Mecca is one entry in an uncountable list — and Allah has the full list. He's seen this story *ka'ayyin* — countless — times before.

One word. And the prisoner's sense of being uniquely trapped is gone.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: قَرْيَتِكَ ٱلَّتِىٓ أَخْرَجَتْكَ — "your town, which drove you out"

This is the heart of the verse, and to feel it we need to slow down to the level of single words.

First, teach yourself one grammatical habit. In Arabic, when you attach the little suffix **ـكَ** (*-ka*, "your") to a noun, you are claiming a relationship. *Kitābuka* — your book. *Baytuka* — your house. The suffix is the grammar of belonging. It says: this is *yours.*

Now look at what Allah calls Mecca.

He does not call it *al-qarya* — "the town." He does not call it *qaryat al-mushrikīn* — "the town of the idolaters." He calls it **قَرْيَتِكَ** — *qaryatika* — **your town.**

[PAUSE]

Let that sink in. At the precise instant Allah is describing how this town expelled His Messenger, He keeps the word *your* attached to it. The expulsion does not sever the belonging. Mecca threw him out — and Allah still calls it *his.*

This is the divine mirror of what the Prophet ﷺ said at the cave: *"the most beloved land to me."* Allah is not asking him to stop loving the place that wounded him. Allah Himself, in the grammar, refuses to let go of the bond. *Your* town. It is still yours, even now, even as it casts you out.

Now hold that tenderness in one hand, because watch what the *next* word does.

**ٱلَّتِىٓ أَخْرَجَتْكَ** — *allatī akhrajatka* — "which drove you out."

The verb is **أَخْرَجَتْكَ**. Its root is *kh-r-j*, the plain meaning of which is to *go out, to exit.* But notice the form. This is not the simple form. This is **Form IV** — *akhraja* — and Form IV here is transitive-causative: it takes the simple act of "going out" and makes someone *do it to* another. Not "you left." Not even "they expelled you" in the abstract. **It made you go out. It forced your exit.** The –*ta* on the end is *you* — you are the object, the one acted upon. The violence of it is built into the morphology: a hand on your back, pushing.

But here is the feature I want you to truly see — the thing that absence reveals.

*Who* is the grammatical subject of "drove you out"? Who is doing the pushing?

It is **the town itself.** *Al-qarya* — the feminine noun "town" — is the one performing the verb *akhrajat* (note the feminine *–at* ending, agreeing with *qarya*). Grammatically, the *streets* drove him out. The *walls* expelled him. The *place* did it.

[PAUSE]

Why would Allah attribute the expulsion to the stones and not to the people? Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both stop here and quietly correct the surface: *"akhrajatka"* — *ay akhrajaka ahluhā* — "meaning: *its people* drove you out." The people did it. Of course the people did it; buildings don't plot.

So why does the verse say *the town* did it? Here is what this invites us to consider. When the people of a place turn on you, it doesn't *feel* like individuals turning on you. It feels like *the place itself* has rejected you. The whole atmosphere, the streets you walked, the air you breathed — all of it suddenly feels like it's pushing you out. The Quran captures the *felt experience* of betrayal: it isn't Abu Jahl who expelled you, it's *Mecca* that expelled you. Your home became a thing with a will, and the will turned against you.

And now — the most precise movement in the entire verse. Watch the pronoun.

We have been speaking about a *qarya* — a town — which is grammatically **feminine, singular.** Every agreement so far has been feminine: *hiya* (she/it is stronger), *akhrajat* (she drove out). If Allah wanted to stay consistent, the next word should be *ahlaknāhā* — "We destroyed **her**," **it**, the town, feminine.

But that is not what He says. He says:

**أَهْلَكْنَٰهُمْ** — *ahlaknāhum* — "We destroyed **them.**" Masculine. Plural. *Them.*

[PAUSE]

The grammarians have a name for this. Al-Baghawi points to it directly: *"qāla ahlaknāhum wa-lam yaqul ahlaknāhā"* — "He said *We destroyed them*, and did not say *We destroyed it.*" The verse pivots from the *form* of the word (*qarya*, feminine town) to its *meaning* (the *people* of the town, masculine plural). The Arabs call this *al-ḥaml ʿalā al-maʿnā* — carrying the sentence on the *meaning* rather than the surface word.

Feel the precision of this. When it was time to describe the *crime* — the expulsion — Allah let the *town* be the doer: *the place* drove you out. The stones took the blame; the felt experience of being rejected by a *place* was honored. But when it comes time for the *reckoning* — *who actually gets destroyed* — the grammar snaps to the **people.** Stones aren't morally accountable. *People* are. You cannot destroy a "town" in any way that matters; you can only hold accountable the human beings who chose.

So in a single verse: the expulsion is felt as the whole place turning on him — that's the *wound,* honored in the feminine "town." But the justice falls precisely where the choice was made — on *them,* the people, the masculine plural. **Allah grieves the wound at the scale you feel it, and delivers the justice at the scale it was earned.**

[PAUSE — let this one sit]

### Element Three: فَلَا نَاصِرَ لَهُمْ — "and there was no helper for them"

We end on a single short phrase, and it turns on one of the most powerful structures in the Arabic language.

Teach yourself the structure first. There are weak ways to say "no" and there are total ways to say "no." If I say "there isn't a helper *here right now*," I've negated one instance. Maybe there's one elsewhere. But Arabic has a construction that negates not one instance — it negates **the entire category, absolutely, for all time.** It is called *lā al-nāfiya li-l-jins* — the *lā* that negates the *whole genus.* You build it by putting **لَا** directly before an indefinite noun with a particular ending — and the meaning becomes: *not a single one of this kind exists. None. Not now, not ever, not anywhere.*

Now look at the closing words: **فَلَا نَاصِرَ لَهُمْ** — *falā nāṣira lahum.*

**نَاصِر** — *nāṣir* — is "helper," from the root *n-ṣ-r*, the root of *naṣr*, divine aid and victory. And it sits in exactly that construction: *lā* + the indefinite *nāṣir.*

So this does not say "they had few helpers." It does not say "their allies failed to arrive in time." It says: **for them — not one helper of any kind, anywhere in existence.** A categorical, sealed, total absence. Al-Muyassar renders the sense exactly: *fa-lam yakun lahum naṣīrun yanṣuruhum min ʿadhāb Allāh* — "there was no helper at all to help them against the punishment of Allah."

[PAUSE]

And now feel the full architecture of the verse close around this.

These were the *stronger* cities — *ashaddu quwwatan.* The root is *q-w-y*, raw strength; paired with *ashaddu* from *sh-d-d*, intensity, severity. Mightier than Mecca. More men, more walls, more wealth, more weapons. Everything a civilization counts as security, they had *more* of than the city that just expelled the Prophet.

And when the decree came — **falā nāṣira lahum.** Not a single helper. All that strength, and at the one moment it was needed, the helper-column reads *zero,* categorically, forever.

Hold this against the man walking out of Mecca. By every visible measure, *he* is the one with no helper — alone, outnumbered, expelled, a price on his head. The mighty cities had everything; he has nothing.

But the entire verse is built to invert that. The cities that *had* everything end with *lā nāṣira lahum* — no helper, none. And the man who *has* nothing is the one being spoken to *directly by Allah* — *Who is, at this very moment, his nāṣir.* The same root, *n-ṣ-r.* They had every form of strength and no Helper. He has no form of strength and the only Helper that has ever mattered.

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So look at what these thirteen words have done, just as language.

*Wa-ka'ayyin* dissolved his loneliness — *you are one of uncountably many.* *Qaryatika 'llatī akhrajatka* honored his wound at the scale he felt it — *your* town, the whole place turned on you — while *ahlaknāhum* placed the justice precisely where the choice was made. And *falā nāṣira lahum* sealed the fate of all that mighty, helper-less strength with a negation that admits no exception.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The wound of being driven out by your own

Let me start where you already live.

Almost everyone, by a certain age, has had the experience of being pushed out of somewhere they belonged. Maybe it was literal — a family that no longer had room for you, a community that closed its ranks, a country you had to leave. Maybe it was quieter — a friend group that moved on without you, a workplace that made it clear you were no longer wanted, a marriage that ended with you carrying a box out a door.

There is a specific, terrible quality to *that* kind of loss. It is not the same as a stranger hurting you. When a stranger wrongs you, the world stays intact; one bad actor, but home is still home. But when *your own* turn you out — when the people and the place that were supposed to be your belonging become the thing that expels you — something deeper breaks. It isn't just that you lost a place. It's that the place you trusted *to be safe* became the source of the harm. The betrayal and the homelessness arrive together.

And the cruelest part is the love. You don't get to stop loving it just because it hurt you. The Prophet ﷺ looking back and saying *"the most beloved land to me"* — that is the exact texture of this wound. You are expelled, and you *still love the place that expelled you,* and the love makes it worse, not better.

[PAUSE]

So watch what Allah does *not* do here, because it's everything.

He does not tell the Prophet ﷺ: *stop loving Mecca, it betrayed you.* He does not say: *now it is your enemy, harden your heart.* Instead — remember the grammar — He keeps the word *your* attached. *Qaryatika.* Your town. Allah lets him keep loving it. The Lord of the worlds, comforting His most beloved servant, does not ask him to amputate the love. He validates that the bond is real even though the people in it failed.

This is a profound permission. When you are driven out by your own, the voices around you will often tell you the way to heal is to *hate* what you lost. To call it toxic and burn the memory down. But Allah's consolation runs the other way: *you are allowed to still love it. The love is not your weakness. The failure was theirs, not the place's, and not yours.*

And then He widens the frame. *Wa-ka'ayyin* — how many. He lifts the Prophet's eyes off his own singular grief and sets it inside the vast company of everyone this has ever happened to. You are not uniquely cursed. You are walking a road *uncountably many* have walked — and the One narrating it has watched every single one.

Here is the movement, then, for anyone carrying this wound. Three things, all at once, all in thirteen words:

First — *your pain is real and your love is allowed.* He kept the word *your* on the town.
Second — *you are not alone or singular in this.* He opened with *how many.*
Third — *the One speaking to you right now is your Helper, precisely as everyone else's helper count drops to zero.* He closed with *falā nāṣira lahum.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? When you next feel the ache of a place or people that pushed you out — notice the reflex to either hate it or to feel uniquely abandoned. And gently refuse both. You are allowed to love what you lost. You are one of countless who've walked this. And the Helper who walked the Prophet ﷺ out of Mecca and into the most consequential journey in human history is not finished writing what your expulsion becomes. *Akhrajatka* — they drove you out. But out is not the end of the sentence. Out is where the Hijra begins.

### Theme Two: The illusion that strength is safety

Now begin somewhere else you already live.

Quietly, almost everyone runs a private calculation about safety. We measure how secure we are by how much we've *accumulated.* Savings in the account. A stable job. A strong body. Powerful friends, a good name, a defensible position. We feel safe in proportion to our *quwwa* — our strength, our resources, our walls. The more we have stacked up, the more we believe nothing can touch us.

It is the oldest civilizational instinct. Build higher walls. Field a bigger army. Stockpile more. Strength equals security — it feels like a law of nature.

The verse walks straight into that law and breaks it.

*Hiya ashaddu quwwatan min qaryatika* — these towns were **stronger in power than your town.** Allah is explicit. The destroyed cities were not weak. They were not poor. They were not defenseless. They were the superpowers of their age — ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people whose ruins the early Muslims would pass on their trade routes, civilizations whose mastery dwarfed Mecca's. By the metric the whole world trusts, they had *more.* More strength. More security. More everything you stack against catastrophe.

[PAUSE]

And then: *ahlaknāhum, falā nāṣira lahum.* We destroyed them — and not one helper.

Sit with the demolition of the logic here. If strength were truly safety, *these* cities should have survived. They had the most of it. The fact that the *strongest* are the ones lying in ruins is not a footnote — it is the entire argument. It proves that the thing we trust for safety was never the thing keeping anyone safe. The walls were always decoration. The armies were always for show. *Quwwa* was never *naṣr.*

Because notice the two different roots the verse uses. What the cities *had* was **quwwa** — *q-w-y* — accumulated, visible strength. What they *lacked* at the end was a **nāṣir** — *n-ṣ-r* — a helper, one who steps in *for* you when your own strength has run out. And the Quran's claim is that these are completely different things. You can max out *quwwa* and have zero *nāṣir.* You can stack strength to the sky and still, at the decisive moment, have *no one to actually save you.*

[PAUSE]

So where does real safety live? The whole verse is built as a contrast, and the answer is the figure standing *outside* the list of ruins — the Prophet ﷺ, who at this moment has almost no *quwwa* at all. No army. No city. No wealth. By the world's calculation, the least safe man in Arabia. And he is the one being addressed, protected, and walked forward by the only *Nāṣir* there is.

The lesson is not "strength is bad." Strength is a gift; use it. The lesson is sharper: **strength is not where safety comes from, and the moment you mistake the two, you are building on the exact ground that swallowed every civilization stronger than yours.** Safety was never in the walls. It was always in your relationship with the One who decides whether the walls stand.

This reframes the modern anxiety completely. We exhaust ourselves stacking *quwwa* — the next raise, the bigger cushion, the harder body, the safer position — as if a high enough pile will finally make us untouchable. The ruins of stronger cities say it never will. Not because effort is wrong, but because you have been pouring it into the column marked *quwwa* while the column that actually decides your fate is marked *nāṣir.*

What does this change about tomorrow? Keep building, keep working, keep your strength — but relocate your *sense of safety.* Each time you feel that grip of "I need more before I'm secure," ask: am I stacking *quwwa* and calling it *naṣr*? Real security is not a number in an account or a wall you've raised. It is being among those for whom Allah is *Mawlā* — the protector named just two verses earlier (47:11). Tie your safety to the only Helper whose absence ends the strongest cities, and whose presence carried a lone, expelled man into history.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now stand back and see how the whole verse is shaped, because the structure *is* the argument.

It opens wide — *wa-ka'ayyin,* **how many,** the uncountable sweep of history. It narrows to a single point — *qaryatika 'llatī akhrajatka,* **your** town, the one personal wound. It pivots — from the feminine *town* that did the wounding to the masculine *them* who bear the reckoning. And it seals — *falā nāṣira lahum,* a categorical negation that closes like a door.

Wide, then narrow, then pivot, then seal. The verse takes the Prophet's eyes from his own grief (narrow) out to the vast company of the fallen-strong (wide), and then closes the case on every one of them — *no helper.* And by closing *their* case with zero helpers, it silently opens *his*: the one being spoken to has the Helper they all lacked.

### Bringing it together

The two discoveries are the same insight from two directions.

The *language* showed us Allah honoring the wound where it was felt (*the town* drove you out) and placing justice where it was earned (We destroyed *them*) — and refusing to let go of the bond (*your* town).

The *themes* showed us the same thing as lived reality: you are allowed to still love what expelled you; you are one of uncountably many who've walked this; and the strength you thought was safety was never safety at all — only the Helper is.

They converge on one point. The man with everything stripped away — home, safety, the protection of his own people — is the safest man alive, because the only Nāṣir there is, is holding his hand. And the cities with everything intact are dust, because they had *quwwa* but no *Nāṣir.* The whole world's calculation of who is secure is exactly inverted.

### Questions to carry

1. Is there a place or a people that drove me out — and have I been told I must *hate* it to heal? What would it mean to let Allah keep the word *"your"* on it, the way He did for His Prophet?

2. When I feel uniquely trapped, uniquely abandoned — what changes if I truly hear *wa-ka'ayyin,* "how many," and know I am one of an uncountable company the One narrating has watched all the way through?

3. Where am I stacking *quwwa* — strength, savings, position — and quietly calling it safety? If I'm honest, what is my *nāṣir* column? Is Allah actually in it, or just the walls?

4. The cities that fell were *stronger* than the one that survived. Where in my life am I trusting the very kind of strength that has failed everyone who ever leaned on it?

5. *Akhrajatka* — they drove you out. For the Prophet ﷺ, "out" was where the Hijra began. What if the place I was pushed out of was never the end of my sentence?

### One-sentence distillation

The very town that cast you out is weaker than mighty cities now lying in ruins with no one to help them — so the One who keeps calling it *"your"* town and holds your hand as you walk away is, and always was, the only strength that could ever save anyone.

### The closing invitation

If you are carrying the wound of being driven out — by a family, a community, a home, a people you still love — let this verse do for you what it did for the man at the cave. You don't have to stop loving what you lost. You are not the first, and you are not alone; *how many* have walked this exact road. And the strength you feel you've been stripped of was never the thing that kept anyone safe. There is one Helper. The cities that had everything else but Him are gone. You, who may feel you have nothing but Him, are the one being walked forward.

Let your sense of safety quietly move — out of the walls, out of the account, out of the approval of the people who could expel you — and into the only column that has ever decided anyone's fate.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah — You who kept the word "my own" on the place that wounded Your Beloved, keep it on every door that has closed on me, and make of every expulsion a Hijra. You are the only Nāṣir; the strongest who trusted other than You are dust. Let my safety rest in You alone, and let "out" be where You begin to write something I could not have imagined. Āmīn.*
