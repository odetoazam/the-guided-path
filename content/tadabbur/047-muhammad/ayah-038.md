# The One Who Withholds Only Withholds From Himself

## Surah Muhammad — Ayah 38

> هَـٰٓأَنتُمْ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ تُدْعَوْنَ لِتُنفِقُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ فَمِنكُم مَّن يَبْخَلُ ۖ وَمَن يَبْخَلْ فَإِنَّمَا يَبْخَلُ عَن نَّفْسِهِۦ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ ٱلْغَنِىُّ وَأَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ ۚ وَإِن تَتَوَلَّوْا۟ يَسْتَبْدِلْ قَوْمًا غَيْرَكُمْ ثُمَّ لَا يَكُونُوٓا۟ أَمْثَـٰلَكُم

*Hā-antum hā'ulā'i tudʿawna li-tunfiqū fī sabīli-llāhi fa-minkum man yabkhalu wa-man yabkhal fa-innamā yabkhalu ʿan nafsihi wa-llāhu-l-ghaniyyu wa-antumu-l-fuqarā'u wa-in tatawallaw yastabdil qawman ghayrakum thumma lā yakūnū amthālakum.*

**"Here you are — these very ones — called to spend in the way of Allah. Yet among you is one who withholds. And whoever withholds only withholds from himself. Allah is the Rich, and you are the poor. And if you turn away, He will replace you with another people; then they will not be the likes of you."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Last Word of a Surah About War

Let me put you in the place where these words first landed.

This is a surah named after the Prophet ﷺ himself. From its opening it has been about one thing above all others: holding your ground. It speaks to a community at war — not war as a metaphor, but actual fighting, with all the fear and cost and blood that involves. It tells the believers, in verse 35, *"Do not grow faint and call for peace when you are the uppermost."* It describes, again and again, two kinds of people: those who go forward, and those who, when matters get serious, find a reason to hang back. There is a recurring figure haunting this surah — the one whose heart has a sickness in it, who turns his face away the moment the cost becomes real.

So you would expect this surah to end on the battlefield. You would expect the final test, the last challenge, to be: *will you risk your life?*

And instead — listen to where it lands. The very last ayah of the surah does not ask you to die.

It asks you to spend.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means. After all that talk of courage and standing firm and not weakening — the surah closes by reaching not for your sword but for your wallet. As if to say: you want to know whether your faith is real? Forget the battlefield for a moment. I will show you a smaller door, a quieter test, one nobody else even sees you take. **Will you give your money away when you are asked?**

Because here is the secret the surah has been building toward: the hand that clings to its wealth is the same hand that will cling to its life. The hesitation is one hesitation. The man who withholds his coins in the dark is the man who will withhold himself in the light. Spending is the battlefield in miniature. It is courage you can practice on a Tuesday afternoon.

And there is something stunning hidden in how Allah describes the person who fails this test. He does not call him a thief. He does not call him a traitor. He uses a word, and then attaches a single small preposition to it, and that preposition quietly turns the entire accusation inside out — until you realize the stingy person is not the criminal in this story.

He is the victim.

Stay with me, because that is where we are going.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: هَـٰٓأَنتُمْ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ — Caught in the Act

Before we open the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic points.

In English, if I want to draw your attention sharply, I have to add words: *"Look — it's you. Yes, you, right there."* Arabic can do all of that with structure alone. There is a particle, **هَا** (*hā*), whose entire job is to be a pointing finger. It means roughly *"here!"* or *"behold!"* And there is a class of words called demonstratives — *this, that, these, those* — whose job is to single something out and hold it up.

Now watch what Allah does in the opening two words of this ayah.

> هَـٰٓأَنتُمْ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ
> *hā-antum hā'ulā'i*

He does not just say *antum* — "you." He stacks the pointing on top of the pointing. **هَـٰٓأَنتُمْ** — *hā-antum* — "here-you-are." And then immediately, again: **هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ** — *hā'ulā'i* — "these very ones." The pointing finger comes down twice. *Here you are. These ones. You. Right here.*

Let's sit with the feeling of that.

This is not the language of a distant announcement. This is the language of a man caught. You know this moment. It is the moment a teacher stops mid-sentence, turns, and says your name — and the whole room goes quiet, and you realize you have been seen. It is the moment someone holds up the evidence in front of your face and says, *"This. This right here. This is you, isn't it?"*

Notice something. The double demonstrative doesn't add information. Grammatically, *antum* alone — "you" — would have communicated who is being addressed. Everything after *hā-antum hā'ulā'i* is pure **intensity**. It is Allah leaning in. The classical commentators read this opening as exactly that — a direct, almost confrontational summons to the believers, *yā hā'ulā'i ayyuhā-l-mu'minūn*, "O you, these very believers." Al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi, the Jalālayn — they all hear the address landing squarely, personally, on the community.

And here is what is remarkable about *where* this lands. The very next words are **تُدْعَوْنَ لِتُنفِقُوا۟** — "you are *called* to spend." The verb *tudʿawna* (root *dʿw*, the same root as *duʿā'*, a call) is in the **passive** voice. You are *being called*. Someone is doing the calling, and it is not named — which means it is Allah Himself, through His Messenger, through the need of the moment.

So put the two pieces together. *Here you are — these very ones — being called.* [PAUSE]

This is the structure of a roll-call. A name is read out, and the room waits to see if you will answer *present*. The doubled pointing finger is not there to flatter you. It is there because **a summons has gone out, and Allah is watching to see who steps forward and who suddenly finds something fascinating to look at on the floor.**

You have been named. The call has been issued. What this single opening reveals is that giving is never anonymous to Allah. The moment of being asked is itself a moment of exposure. He is pointing at you as you decide.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: يَبْخَلُ عَن نَّفْسِهِ — The Preposition That Turns the Knife Around

Now we reach the heart of the ayah. And to feel it, you need to feel the word first, before you even understand it.

The word for stinginess here is **بُخْل** (*bukhl*), and the verb is **يَبْخَلُ** (*yabkhalu*). Say it out loud. *Yab-kha-lu.* Notice what your throat does. There is that letter in the middle — **خ**, the *kh* — a harsh, scraping, guttural sound, the sound of something constricting and tightening at the back of the throat. The word itself **closes up** as you say it. It does not flow; it clenches. This is a word that sounds like what it means. *Bukhl* is the soul tightening its fist, and the mouth tightens to match.

Now, here is the construction. Allah says:

> وَمَن يَبْخَلْ فَإِنَّمَا يَبْخَلُ عَن نَّفْسِهِ
> *wa-man yabkhal fa-innamā yabkhalu ʿan nafsihi*
> "And whoever withholds — he only withholds **from himself**."

I need you to notice the preposition, because everything turns on it. The word is **عَن** (*ʿan*). And *ʿan* does not mean "on" or "against." It means **away from** — motion *off of* a thing, *back from* a thing.

Let me teach you the contrast, because Arabic had another option here and deliberately did not take it. If the ayah had wanted to say *"he withholds against himself"* — the idea of harming oneself, a wound inflicted — the natural preposition would be **عَلَى** (*ʿalā*), "upon, against." And in fact, some of the great mufassirūn, like al-Qurtubi, gloss the meaning *as if* it were *ʿalā* — *yabkhalu ʿalā nafsihi*, "he is stingy *toward* himself," he denies his own self the reward and the wage. That is one true camera angle, and we will keep it.

But Allah did not write *ʿalā*. He wrote *ʿan*. **Away from.** And that opens a second, deeper angle.

Picture what *ʿan* describes physically. A man is asked to release something — to let it flow *outward*, into the way of Allah, toward the poor, toward the cause. Instead, *he withholds*. He pulls it back. The arrow of the wealth was meant to fly out from him into the world — and he yanks it back *off of* its course. *ʿan nafsihi.* He withholds **away from his own self.**

Do you see the reversal? [PAUSE]

He *thinks* he is withholding his wealth *from them* — from the poor, from the cause, from the people with their hands out. He pictures himself as the one keeping something *back from* the world. But the grammar quietly turns the whole map around and shows him the true direction of the loss: the only thing he succeeded in withholding **was good from his own soul.** The flow he blocked was a flow that was going to circle back and feed *him*. He stood at his own door and turned away his own provision.

This is the genius of *ʿan*. *ʿalā* would have said: *you hurt yourself.* That's bad enough. But *ʿan* says something stranger and more devastating: *the very thing you were keeping back from others was always, secretly, on its way to you.* You did not deprive them. You can't. Look at the next line — Allah is *al-Ghaniyy*, He needs nothing. The cause will be funded with you or without you. The only person in the entire transaction who actually lost anything... is the one who clenched his fist.

And now go back to the sound of the word. *Yabkhalu* — the throat tightening. That is not just the sound of a miser. It is the sound of a man **strangling his own supply line.** The constriction he performs on the world is a constriction he performs on himself.

The word **فَإِنَّمَا** (*fa-innamā*) seals it. *Innamā* is the particle of restriction — it means *"only, nothing but, exclusively."* The ayah is not saying he *mostly* harms himself, or *also* harms himself. It is saying: **this is the only thing that happened.** Strip away his illusion that he denied anyone else, and what remains is one fact and only one fact — *innamā yabkhalu ʿan nafsihi* — the sole victim of his stinginess is standing in his own shoes.

[PAUSE]

What this single preposition reveals: **you cannot withhold good from Allah's world. You can only withhold yourself from it.** The fist that closes around the coin closes around nothing — except your own hand.

## Element Three: ٱلْغَنِىُّ … ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ — The Asymmetry of the Definite Article

There is a third thing here, and it lives in two words that sit facing each other like two sides of a scale.

> وَٱللَّهُ ٱلْغَنِىُّ وَأَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ
> *wa-llāhu-l-ghaniyyu wa-antumu-l-fuqarā'u*
> "And Allah is **the** Rich, and you are **the** poor."

Let me teach you what the small word *al-* — "the" — is doing, because in Arabic the definite article on a description does something it does not do in English. When you take an adjective like *rich* and you put *al-* on it and make it the predicate — *al-Ghaniyy*, **the** Rich — you are no longer saying someone *is* rich, the way many people are rich. You are saying richness, in its true and total sense, **belongs to this one and is restricted to him.** It is the difference between "he is wealthy" and "he is *the* Wealth itself." The *al-* fences the quality off and hands it over completely.

So *wa-llāhu-l-Ghaniyy* does not mean "Allah is rich among other rich beings." It means: **the Rich. The only one for whom richness is the truth.** Free of all need. The commentators are precise here — al-Ghaniyy *ʿan nafaqātikum*, "free of need *of your spending*." Al-Baghawi: free of your charity and even of your obedience. Your giving does not fund Him. He was not waiting for your contribution.

Now — and this is the part to slow down for — look at the **asymmetry** between the two sides of the scale.

On one side: **ٱلْغَنِىُّ** — *al-Ghaniyy*. Singular. One. The Rich is **one**, and richness is gathered, whole and undivided, in Him.

On the other side: **ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ** — *al-fuqarā'*. **Plural.** "The poor *ones*." Not *al-faqīr*, the single poor one — *al-fuqarā'*, the poor in their multitude.

Stay with that shape. Why is the richness singular and the poverty plural? [PAUSE]

Because that is the actual structure of reality. There is **one** source, and it does not divide — *al-Ghaniyy*, complete in Himself, needing nothing, diminished by no withdrawal. And there are **the many**, all of us, every single one without exception, and our defining condition is not that we *have* poverty but that we *are* "the poor." Notice the *al-* again — it is on *both* words. We are not "needy *sometimes*." We are **the needy**, by definition, by category, by nature. Need is not something that happens to us. It is what we are made of.

And here is where the asymmetry detonates the whole illusion of the stingy man.

He was clutching his wealth as though he were a small reservoir of self-sufficiency — as though by holding on he were becoming a little bit *al-Ghaniyy*, a little bit needless. The very instinct of stinginess is the fantasy of *not needing* — "I will keep this so that I am secure, so that I depend on no one." But the ayah has just told him the truth about his category. **You are not, and can never be, the Rich. You are the poor. One of the many poor.** The thing you are clinging to does not belong to a self-sufficient being; it is passing through the hands of a needy creature who will, very soon, need everything and own nothing.

There is one more quiet detail. Did you notice that *ʿan* — "away from" — has appeared *twice* in this ayah? *Yabkhalu ʿan nafsihi*, he withholds away from himself. And the commentators gloss *al-Ghaniyy* as rich *ʿan* you — free, turned *away from* needing you. The same preposition. The man tries to keep his wealth *away from* the world; Allah is forever *away from* needing the man's wealth at all. The miser's whole strategy is aimed at a being who was never going to be affected by it.

What these two facing words reveal: **the one place richness lives is the one place that needs nothing from you, and the place you actually live — among the poor — is the place that needed the giving most of all.** Your charity was never charity to Allah. It was, all along, the poor reaching toward the only Rich there is.

## Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three pieces have shown us, just from the grammar.

The doubled pointing finger — *hā-antum hā'ulā'* — told us that giving is a roll-call, and you are named in it, and your answer is seen. The preposition *ʿan* told us that withholding does not deprive the world; it deprives the one who withholds. And the asymmetry of *al-Ghaniyy* and *al-fuqarā'* told us that you have been clinging, in your poverty, to wealth that was meant to flow *back toward you* — and clinging to it as if it could make you something you can never be.

Three pieces of grammar. One single arrow, pointing in one direction.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about how a soul actually works, and about what it means to be the kind of person who can be replaced.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Strange Economics of the Soul

Let me start where you live.

Imagine you have a glass of water, and you are standing by a river. You are thirsty. Someone asks you for a sip. And you say no — you hold the glass tight against your chest, because it is *yours*, and what if you need it later?

Now multiply that. Imagine the glass refills the instant you give it away. Imagine that the river is infinite, and the more water you pour out for others, the more flows back into your glass — and the *only* way the glass ever empties is if you refuse to pour. In that world, the person clutching the glass to their chest is not protecting anything. They are the only one going thirsty.

That is the economics this ayah describes. And here is the thing — we don't believe it. We say we do. But every instinct in us runs on the *first* picture, the glass-by-the-river picture, where holding on is safety and giving away is loss. The whole machinery of fear in the human chest is built on the assumption that **wealth withheld is wealth kept.**

The ayah quietly says: *no, it isn't.* *Man yabkhal fa-innamā yabkhalu ʿan nafsihi.* The withholding does not keep anything. It only blocks the return flow. The wealth you "saved" by not giving was never saved — it was the one portion guaranteed to bring you nothing back.

Let's go deeper, because there's a second layer most people miss.

We tend to think the harm of stinginess is *external* — that you fail to do a good deed, you miss out on reward, like skipping an extra credit assignment. But the Quran's diagnosis is sharper and more frightening. Stinginess is not a missed opportunity. It is **a wound to the self that performs it.** Look at how the Quran elsewhere makes this exact move: in Surah al-Layl, *"as for the one who is stingy and thinks himself self-sufficient... We will ease him toward hardship"* (92:8). In Surah al-Hashr, *"whoever is protected from the stinginess of his own soul — those are the successful"* (59:9). Notice: it's not *whoever gives a lot*. It's *whoever is **saved from** the bukhl of his own nafs.* Stinginess is framed as a disease you need rescuing *from*, a thing that has hold of *you*.

Here is why. Every act of giving is a small act of trust — a small declaration that says *Allah is enough, the river is real, I am not the source of my own security.* And every act of withholding is the opposite declaration: *I am the source. I must hold on, because there is no river, only this glass.* So the miser is not just losing money. With each clenched fist he is **training his own heart** to believe a lie about reality — the lie that he is *al-Ghaniyy*, that his security lives in his account. He is, slowly, teaching his soul to be afraid. That fear *is* the punishment. It arrives long before the Day of Judgment. It is the tightness in the chest right now, the same tightness as the *kh* in *yabkhalu*.

And the way out is almost embarrassingly simple. You loosen the fist *once*. You give when you don't feel like it, when the voice says *keep it, you'll need it*. And you discover — every single person who has ever done this has discovered — that the sky does not fall. The glass refills. And the soul learns something it could not learn from any sermon: that letting go is safe. That the river was real all along.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the specific, concrete thing. The next time you feel that tightening — that little internal *no* when you are asked to give, whether it's money, or time, or help — recognize it for what the ayah says it is. It is not protection. It is *ʿan nafsihi*. It is your own hand closing around your own good. Treat that tightening as a signal, not an instruction. **Give precisely the thing the tightness tells you to keep** — not recklessly, but deliberately, as medicine. You are not losing it. You are unblocking the return.

## Theme Two: The Mercy and the Terror of Being Replaceable

Now I want to turn to the last line of the surah, because it is one of the most sobering sentences in the Quran, and almost nobody lingers on it long enough.

> وَإِن تَتَوَلَّوْا۟ يَسْتَبْدِلْ قَوْمًا غَيْرَكُمْ ثُمَّ لَا يَكُونُوٓا۟ أَمْثَـٰلَكُم
> "And if you turn away, He will replace you with another people; then they will not be the likes of you."

Let me bring you to where this lives in your world first.

There is a feeling almost everyone secretly carries: *I am needed. They can't do this without me.* The job that would collapse without you. The family that depends on you. The cause that needs your particular gifts. And there is real comfort in that feeling — it tells us we matter, that our absence would leave a hole.

And then, sometimes, life teaches us the brutal lesson. You leave the job. And a month later, the work is getting done. Someone else sits in your chair. The hole you imagined you'd leave — closes over, like water. It is one of the most humbling experiences a human being can have: to discover that you were *replaceable.*

Now hear what Allah says — to the *best* generation of believers who ever lived, the Companions of the Messenger ﷺ themselves. *If you turn away* — *in tatawallaw.* And notice that verb. *Tatawallaw* is a reflexive form (Form V) of the root that means turning — it is not someone turning you away, it is **you turning your own self away**, deliberately revolving your face from the call. *If you do that* — *yastabdil qawman ghayrakum.* He will bring others in your place.

And look at the verb for replacement: **يَسْتَبْدِلْ** (*yastabdil*), Form X of *badal*, exchange. The Form X pattern (*istafʿala*) carries the sense of *bringing about, effecting, putting into action* a substitution. This is not Allah passively letting someone else show up. This is the deliberate exchange — the swapping out — of one people for another. The way you would replace a tool that failed in your hand.

Let's sit with how frightening this is, and then with how merciful it is. Because it is both.

First the terror. **You are not indispensable to Allah.** Re-read the middle of the ayah — *Allah is the Rich, you are the poor.* This last line is simply that truth applied to the community as a whole. Just as your individual money does not fund Him, your individual presence does not sustain His religion. The dīn does not need you. The cause does not need you. If you turn your face from the call, the call does not go unanswered — it simply gets answered by someone else, and the procession moves on without you, and the hole closes over like water. That should shake something loose in anyone who has ever felt entitled, ever felt that their belonging was guaranteed, ever coasted on the assumption that *of course* they're on the inside.

But now the mercy — and it is enormous. [PAUSE]

If the religion *needed* you, then your participation would be a kind of slavery — Allah extracting from you something He lacks. But He is *al-Ghaniyy*. He needs nothing. Which means: **every time He invites you to give, to strive, to step forward — He is not taking from you. He is offering *you* a share in something that would happen anyway.** The roll-call we started with, *hā-antum hā'ulā'*, "here you are, these very ones, being called" — that call is not a demand for help. It is an *invitation to be included.* He is rich; you are poor. When the rich invite the poor to the table, the poor are not doing the rich a favor. They are being fed.

So being replaceable is the flip side of being *invited.* You were never needed — which means your inclusion was never a transaction. It was pure grace. The terror and the mercy are the same fact seen from two directions: *He doesn't need you* — therefore *you cannot put Him in your debt*, and therefore *everything He gives you to do is a gift, not a burden.*

And there's a final, beautiful detail in the last three words: **ثُمَّ لَا يَكُونُوٓا۟ أَمْثَـٰلَكُم** — "then they will not be the likes of you." The replacements will *not be like you.* The classical commentators read this two ways, and both are true. Al-Baghawi reports the obvious one: they will not be like you *in your turning away* — they will be more obedient, more willing, *amthal*, more upright. But hear the quieter implication: it is also a kind of lament. *They will not be your likes.* The specific configuration of grace that was *you* — your moment, your chance, your particular invitation — that does not come again. Allah will not be left short; the next people will be better. But *you* will have been the ones who walked away from a thing that was offered to *you* and to no one else in quite the same way.

(On *who* these replacements are, the early commentators differed — al-Baghawi preserves several views: that they were Kindah and al-Nakhaʿ, or the non-Arab peoples generally, or the Persians and Romans. The point of the ayah does not rest on settling that; the names are held loosely in the tradition, and so should we hold them.)

What does this change about tomorrow? When you are next asked to step forward — to give, to serve, to carry a weight for the sake of something larger than you — do not hear it as a demand on your scarce resources. # CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic discoveries and the two themes turn out to be one single insight, approached from different doors.

The grammar showed us three things: a *pointing finger* that names you in a roll-call (*hā-antum hā'ulā'*), a *preposition* that proves your withholding harms only you (*ʿan nafsihi*), and an *asymmetry* that reveals you as the poor clinging to wealth meant to flow back to you (*al-Ghaniyy* / *al-fuqarā'*).

The themes showed us the same arrow from the inside: that withholding is a wound to the self and a lie the heart tells itself, and that you are gloriously, terrifyingly *replaceable* — which means your every invitation to give was never a debt collected but a gift offered.

It is all one truth. **Allah does not need your giving. You need it.** Every word of the ayah is that single sentence, said again and again from a new angle, until there is nowhere left to hide from it.

## The Architecture

And now notice the shape of the whole ayah, which we could not see until now. It opens with **you** — *here you are, these very ones.* It moves to your **failure** — *among you is one who withholds.* It diagnoses the **truth of that failure** — *he only withholds from himself.* It names the **two parties** — *Allah the Rich, you the poor.* And it closes with the **consequence** — *if you turn away, you will simply be replaced.*

It begins by pointing at you and ends by removing you. That is the arc: *here you are* … *and here is how easily you could not be.* The surah about standing firm in battle ends by showing you that the first ground you must hold is the ground of an open hand — and that even *that* ground is held only by grace.

## Questions to Carry

1. When I feel that tightening — the internal *no* before giving — have I been treating it as protection, when the ayah calls it self-harm? What would change if I treated it as the symptom and gave *because* of it?
2. Where in my life have I quietly believed that the cause, the family, the work, *needs* me — and have I been resting in that belief instead of being grateful for the invitation?
3. If Allah is *al-Ghaniyy* and I am among *al-fuqarā'*, what exactly do I think I am securing when I withhold?
4. The replacements "will not be the likes of you" — better, more willing. If a more grateful version of me were offered my exact opportunities tomorrow, how differently would *they* answer the call?
5. What is the one thing — money, time, effort — that I have been keeping back *from the world*, that the grammar says I have only ever been keeping back *from myself*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**You cannot withhold anything from Allah's world — you can only withhold yourself from it — because He is the Rich who needs nothing, and you are the poor whose every act of giving was, all along, an invitation flowing back toward you.**

## The Closing Invitation

This surah does not end with a command to do something heroic. It ends with the gentlest, most ordinary test there is: *you are being called to spend.* Not to die. To give. And it leaves you with a choice that you will face, in some form, before this day is over — a request, a need, a hand held out. When it comes, remember the pointing finger. *Here you are. These very ones.* Your name has been read. You are not needed — and that is the mercy — so when you answer *present*, you are not paying a debt. You are accepting an invitation to be fed at a table you could never have earned a place at.

Unclench the hand. Nothing you were holding was ever keeping you safe.

## A Du'ā

O Allah, You are *al-Ghaniyy* and we are *al-fuqarā'* — free us from the stinginess of our own souls, the *bukhl* that tightens the heart and blocks the good that was flowing back to us. Let us hear our names in Your roll-call and answer *present* with an open hand, knowing that to be invited by the One who needs nothing is itself the gift. And do not let us be among those who turn away and are quietly replaced. *Āmīn.*
