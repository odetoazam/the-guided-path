# The Unfinished Question: How Will It Be?

> [ayah:47:27] فَكَيْفَ إِذَا تَوَفَّتْهُمُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ يَضْرِبُونَ وُجُوهَهُمْ وَأَدْبَـٰرَهُمْ
>
> *Fa-kayfa idhā tawaffat-humu-l-malā'ikatu yaḍribūna wujūhahum wa-adbārahum*
>
> **"Then how will it be when the angels take them in death, striking their faces and their backs?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Before we open a single word of this ayah, I want you to feel the weather of the surah it lives in.

Surah Muhammad is not a calm surah. It moves fast, it strikes hard, and it has one wound it keeps returning to. The wound is not the disbeliever far away who never heard. The wound is closer than that. The wound is the person who *heard* — who sat in the gathering, who nodded, who said the words "we will obey" — and then, when no one was watching, turned around and walked the other way.

The surah calls them by a posture. Just two ayahs before ours, it says of them:

> *"Indeed, those who reverted back on their backs (ٱرْتَدُّوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ أَدْبَـٰرِهِم) after guidance had become clear to them — Shayṭān enticed them..."* (47:25)

Hold onto that image. **They turned their backs.** They didn't argue. They didn't debate. They simply rotated their bodies away from the light after the light had already reached their eyes. That is the specific failure this surah is grieving — not ignorance, but a turning-away that happens *after* knowing.

Now imagine you are sitting in Madinah, in the early years, and the community is full of exactly these people. They pray beside you. They fight beside you, sometimes. They smile and say the right things. And no one — not you, not the most discerning believer — can fully see into their hearts. The hypocrite is, by definition, the person you cannot read.

So the question the early listener carries, half-spoken, is: *what happens to them? They get away with it. They blend in. They turn their backs and nobody catches them.*

And into that exact silence, Allah drops our ayah.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. I want you to notice something strange about this verse before we even begin, and then let it bother you for the next hour.

**It is a question that is never answered.**

"Then how will it be...?" — *fa-kayfa* — and then the sentence runs forward, describes a scene of terrible vividness, and *stops*. Allah never tells you how it will be. He asks. And He leaves the asking open.

Why would the All-Knowing ask a question He does not answer? That gap — that missing answer — is not an accident of style. It is, I will argue, the single most merciful thing in the verse. But to see why, we have to earn it.

Let's begin.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Question With No Floor Beneath It — *Fa-kayfa*

Let me teach you something about how questions work before we touch the Arabic.

In ordinary speech, a question is a container waiting to be filled. "How was the meeting?" expects "fine" or "tense" or "long." The question opens a small empty space, and the answer closes it. We are so used to this closing that we barely notice it. A question is a door; an answer shuts it behind you.

Now — what happens when a door is opened and *never* shut?

You keep standing in the doorway. You keep looking through. The mind cannot rest, because the space the question opened has not been filled. You lean forward, involuntarily, trying to see what is on the other side.

That straining-forward — that is a rhetorical instrument. And Allah plays it here with a master's hand.

The word is **فَكَيْفَ** — *fa-kayfa*. *Kayfa* means "how." The *fa* at the front means "then" or "so" — it connects this to everything before it, as if to say: *given all of that — given that they turned their backs — then HOW?*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. In Arabic, a question like this expects a predicate — a "how will WHAT be?" Grammatically, the sentence is reaching for a missing word. *Kayfa* + a verb or state that completes it. "How will their condition be." "How will they fare." Al-Qurṭubī, reading this verse, actually supplies the missing piece for us — he says the meaning is *fa-kayfa takūnu ḥāluhum*, "then how will their *state* be."

But look at what the Qur'an itself does. **It does not supply the word.** It leaves *kayfa* standing in the open air with nothing beneath it. The grammatical floor that should hold up the question — *the answer, the described state* — is simply removed.

Let's sit with this. Why?

Think about what an answer would do. If Allah completed the sentence — "how will it be? It will be agony; it will be regret; it will be the worst of ends" — then the verse would become a *report*. A piece of news about other people. You would file it away: *ah, the hypocrites suffer at death, noted.* The closed door lets you walk away.

But the open door will not let you walk away. Because an unanswered "how will it be?" does something a statement can never do: **it turns to face you.**

When the answer is missing, the listener's own imagination is conscripted to fill it. You cannot help it. The mind, handed an open "how," begins — against its will — to picture. And the moment you begin to picture *how it will be*, you have stopped reading about them and started standing in their place.

Here is what is remarkable. The grammatical absence — the missing predicate — is not a gap in meaning. **It is the mechanism by which the verse reaches out and grabs you.** A finished sentence describes a fate. An unfinished question *assigns* one — to whoever is foolish enough to keep reading. And we are all reading.

So what this single word reveals is this: **Allah did not come to inform you about the death of the hypocrites. He came to make you imagine your own.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Two: The Word That Means "To Collect in Full" — *Tawaffat-hum*

Now we come to the heart of the verse, and to a word that will change how you hear the word "death" for the rest of your life.

First, the concept — before the Arabic.

Imagine a merchant. He has sold his goods on credit over many months. Small debts, large debts, scattered across many customers. Some have paid a little, some nothing, some have been avoiding him in the market, slipping down side streets when they see him coming. Then one day, the day of settlement arrives. The merchant goes out and *collects everything that is owed* — every coin, every last fraction, in full, with nothing left outstanding. He does not take a portion. He takes the *whole*. The account is closed completely. Nothing remains on the books.

Arabic has a precise word for this act of *taking-in-full, collecting-the-entire-due*. The root is **و-ف-ي** (*w-f-y*), and it is one of the most beautiful roots in the language. From it comes *wafā'* — faithfulness, the keeping of a promise in full. From it comes *awfā* — to pay or fulfill completely, as in "fulfill the covenant." From it comes the divine name *al-Wafiyy* — the One who is utterly true to His word.

And from it, in the fifth form — **تَوَفَّىٰ** (*tawaffā*) — comes the meaning: *to take in full, to claim the entire due, to collect completely and leave nothing behind.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at our verse:

> إِذَا **تَوَفَّتْهُمُ** ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ
>
> *"...when the angels **take them in full**..."*

The Leeds corpus confirms this is Form V of *w-f-y* — *tawaffat*, perfect tense, feminine (agreeing with *al-malā'ikah*, the angels). And here is the thing I need you to feel: the most common Arabic word for death in the Qur'an is not *qatl* (killing) or *mawt* used as a verb of seizure. Again and again, the Qur'an describes the soul's departure with **this root** — *tawaffā*. Allah *yatawaffā* the souls. The angel of death *yatawaffā*.

So pause and absorb what that means. In the Qur'an's own vocabulary, **death is not a taking-away. It is a collection-in-full.**

Death is the merchant's day of settlement. It is the moment the entire account — every coin owed, every promise made, every "we will obey" spoken and abandoned — is collected completely, with nothing left outstanding. *Tawaffā.* The full due, claimed.

Stay with me, because this cuts two ways, and both ways are in the verse.

For the one who turned his back — who said the words and walked away, who thought he had *gotten away with it*, who slipped down the side streets of this world avoiding the reckoning — *tawaffā* means: **the day you cannot avoid the merchant has come.** Everything you deferred is now collected. In full. The debts you thought had been quietly forgotten because no one chased you — they were never forgotten. They were *on the books*. And the angels have come to settle the account *completely*.

But notice the other edge. The same root, *w-f-y*, is the root of *faithfulness*. For the believer, *tawaffā* means something almost tender: you who kept faith, who were *wafiyy* to your covenant — now you receive, in full, what was promised to *you*. Allah, *al-Wafiyy*, the One who never breaks a word, collects you to Himself and pays your due in full. Not a fraction. The whole.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is breathtaking about the choice of this single word. Allah could have described death as a tearing, a seizure, a snatching. Instead He reaches for the language of *accounting completed*. And in doing so He tells you something the disbeliever in this verse fatally misunderstood: **nothing in your life is being written off.** Every turning-away you thought was unobserved is an entry on a ledger that will, on a fixed day, be collected in full.

The hypocrite's whole gamble was that some of it wouldn't count. That he could turn his back *quietly* and the cost would somehow not be collected. *Tawaffā* is the one word that destroys that gamble. **It all counts. It is all collected. In full.**

So what this word reveals is this: death is not the end of your account. Death is the day your account is *closed* — and a closed account is one where everything owed has been paid, on one side or the other.

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: Faces and Backs — *Wujūhahum wa-Adbārahum*

Now we arrive at the most physically violent words in the verse, and I want to handle them with care, because their violence is *pointing* at something — it is not the point itself.

> يَضْرِبُونَ **وُجُوهَهُمْ وَأَدْبَـٰرَهُمْ**
>
> *"...striking their **faces and their backs**."*

First, a small grammatical observation that carries weight. The verb "striking" — **يَضْرِبُونَ** (*yaḍribūn*) — is in the imperfect tense. The present-continuous. Al-Qurṭubī notes it functions as a *ḥāl*, a circumstantial state: the angels take them *while striking*, the blows ongoing, unfolding, not finished.

Feel the contrast with the word we just studied. *Tawaffat* — the taking — was in the *perfect* tense. Done. Complete. The collection is *finished* in an instant, total. But *yaḍribūn* — the striking — is *imperfect*, ongoing, stretching out before your eyes. The account is closed in a flash; the consequence keeps unfolding. The Qur'an chose its tenses with the precision of a surgeon: **completed taking, continuing blow.**

[PAUSE]

Now — *faces and backs*. Why these two?

Let me teach you the structure first. When Arabic names two opposite extremities to make a point, it is using a device called *merism* — naming the two poles to mean *everything in between and all around*. "From the faces to the backs" is a way of saying: there is no surface of them left untouched. Front and back. The direction they advance and the direction they flee.

But here it is far more than coverage. Here it is *justice rendered visible*.

Think about what a face is and what a back is — in human gesture, in the language of the body.

Your **face** is what you turn *toward* something. To "face" someone is to confront them, to present yourself, to engage. Your **back** is what you turn when you *leave*. To "turn your back" on someone — in every human language — is to reject them, abandon them, flee from them.

Now go back two ayahs. Go back to where this surah described these very people:

> *"...those who reverted **back on their backs** (ٱرْتَدُّوا۟ عَلَىٰٓ **أَدْبَـٰرِهِم**)..."* (47:25)

[PAUSE]

There it is. The same word. **أَدْبَار** — *adbār* — backs.

In 47:25, they turn their *backs* on guidance. They rotate away from the light. *Adbār* — the backs that walked them out of the truth.

In 47:27, the angels strike their *backs*. *Adbār* — the same backs.

**The part of them that committed the crime is the part of them that receives the consequence.** They turned their backs in flight from the truth — and their backs are struck as they flee. They never gave the truth their face — they gave it their back — and now both are struck: the face they refused to turn toward guidance, and the back they used to escape it.

Stay with how exact this is. This is not random violence. This is the body's own grammar of betrayal, answered in the body's own grammar of consequence. The faces that turned away. The backs that walked away. Struck. Both. Whether they stand to confront or turn to run — *there is no posture left that is safe*, because the crime was committed in *every* posture. Al-Qurṭubī even preserves a reading that the faces are struck *as they advance* and the backs *as they flee* — there is no direction of escape, because the turning-away itself was the sin, and you cannot turn in any direction without it being the very motion that condemned you.

[PAUSE]

So what do these two words reveal — *faces and backs*?

The physical image of the blow is the stepping stone. Do not stop at the blow. The blow is pointing at an unseen reality, and the unseen reality is this: **the consequences of turning away are not added onto the turning away from the outside. They are the turning away, arriving.** The back you turned is the back that is struck. Sin, fully collected, is simply the shape of your own action handed back to you.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

An *unfinished question* that turns to face the reader and makes him imagine his own end. A word for death — *tawaffā* — that means *collected in full*, nothing written off, every deferred debt called in. And a pair of body-words — *faces and backs* — that hand a person the exact shape of his own turning-away as his consequence.

Three words. One message, approached from three sides.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because there are two human realities buried here, and you have lived inside both of them.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Bill You Thought You Skipped

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of relief we all know. It is the relief of *getting away with it.* You were supposed to do something — file the form, answer the message, repay the debt, have the hard conversation — and you didn't. You turned your back on it. And then... nothing happened. No one chased you. The world kept turning. And you felt that small, illicit lightness: *I think I'm in the clear.*

We are addicted to that feeling. The whole architecture of human avoidance is built on it. We "ghost" the message we don't want to answer and feel lighter for it. We let the bill sit unopened on the counter and the not-knowing feels almost like not-owing. We turn our backs on the thing we should face, and because the back is turned, *we can no longer see it* — and we let "I can't see it" quietly become "it isn't there."

This is the deepest self-deception the human being is capable of, and it is the exact psychology of the people in our verse. The hypocrite is not primarily a liar to others. He is, first, a man who has gotten away with it — and *believes* the getting-away is the same as the not-owing.

[PAUSE]

Now let the verse illuminate this.

*Tawaffā.* The merchant's day of settlement. Remember what it means: the debts you have been avoiding in the market, slipping down side streets to escape — they were never cancelled by your avoidance. **The merchant not coming to your door is not the same as the merchant forgiving the debt.** It only feels the same. The silence of this world is not the verdict. It is the *deferral* of the verdict.

And here is the movement I want you to make, because it is the whole turn of the verse. We hear "the angels struck their faces and backs" and our instinct is to think: *that is about the worst people. That is about open enemies of God.* But read the surah. These are people who *prayed*. Who said the *shahādah*. Who said "we will obey." The verse is not describing the atheist across the sea. It is describing the person who said yes and lived no.

Which means the verse is not asking about *them*. It is asking about the gap. **The gap between what we said and what we did.** And every one of us has a gap.

[PAUSE]

Let me deepen this one more turn.

Why does the lightness of "getting away with it" feel so good? Because in the moment, the deferred consequence is *invisible*, and the human being treats the invisible as the unreal. We are creatures of the seen. If we cannot see the cost, we behave as though there is no cost.

This is precisely why Allah wrote the verse as a *question about a future moment*. *"How will it be when..."* He is doing something for us that we cannot do for ourselves: **He is making the invisible bill visible while there is still time to pay it.** The hypocrite's tragedy is that he saw the bill only at *tawaffā* — at the settlement, when nothing could be done. The verse drags that moment of seeing *backward*, into your living room, today, so that you might see the bill *before* the merchant comes.

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

Here is the practical edge, and it is sharp. Tonight, find the thing you have turned your back on — the prayer you keep deferring, the wealth that isn't yours that you haven't returned, the relationship you've let rot because facing it is hard, the *tawbah* you keep postponing as if you had a contract guaranteeing tomorrow. Name *one*. And understand: the comfort you feel about it right now is not safety. It is *tawaffā* deferred. The account is open. And an open account is not a forgiven one — it is one still waiting to be collected in full.

**Pay it now, while paying is still possible. That is the entire mercy of being shown the bill early.**

[PAUSE — major]

## Theme Two: The Most Merciful Unanswered Question Ever Asked

I told you at the very beginning that the missing answer in this verse is the most merciful thing in it. Now we have walked far enough to see why. Let me bring you there slowly.

Start, again, in human experience. Think about the difference between two kinds of warnings.

The first kind is the *report*. A man dies, and afterward you are told: *here is what happened to him, here is what it was like, here is the end he met.* You receive it as history. It is closed. It happened to someone else, in the past, and there is nothing for you to do but note it.

The second kind is the *open question*. Someone turns to you — someone who loves you — and before the road ends, while you are still walking it, they ask: ***"And you? How will it be for you, when it comes?"*** And they *don't answer.* They leave it hanging. They leave it for *you* to answer with your life.

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference in your chest. The report informs. The open question *recruits.* The report is about the dead. The open question is about *you, who are still alive.*

Now — which one did Allah choose?

He chose **فَكَيْفَ**. *"Then how will it be...?"* And He did not answer.

We established the grammar earlier: the predicate is missing, the floor beneath the question removed. But now you can see *why* it is missing. **Because an answered question is closed, and a closed question is for the dead. Allah left it open because He is addressing the living.** He is not reporting to you the fate of the hypocrites. He is turning to you, while you are still walking the road, and asking — *and you? How will it be, for you, when the angels come to collect in full?*

And He stops. He hands you the silence. Because the answer to that question is not His to write. **It is yours.** You are writing it right now, with this hour, with the back you are or are not turning, with the bill you are or are not paying.

[PAUSE]

Let the paradox sit here, because it is a real one and I do not want to rush it. The verse describes a scene of striking, of faces and backs, of terror. By every surface reading it is a verse of warning, even of threat. And yet its deepest mechanism — the unanswered question — is an act of *love*. Because you only ask someone "how will it be for you?" *before* it is too late. You only leave the answer open for someone who can *still change the answer.*

A judge reading a finished sentence to a condemned man does not ask him "how will it be?" There is no point — the answer is fixed. You ask the open question *only* to the one whose fate is still unwritten.

So the very fact that this verse is a *question* and not a *verdict* is the proof — grammatical, structural, undeniable — that **for you, who are reading it, the answer is not yet fixed.** The door is open. That is what an open question *is*. The door is open, and you are being asked, by the One who loves you more than your mother, to walk back through it before *tawaffā* closes it for good.

[PAUSE]

One more movement, and then I'll let you rest with it.

Notice *who* the verse never says this happens to *by name*. It does not say "the believers" or "so-and-so." The faces and backs belong to *them* — the ones who turned away. But because the answer is left open, **the verse refuses to seat you on either side of it.** It will not let you sit comfortably among the saved, and it will not condemn you among the lost. It holds you in the doorway and asks: *which way will you turn your face?*

And that is the whole gift. As long as you are alive, you are in the doorway. The question is still open. The answer is still yours.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

This: stop reading verses like this one as *information about other people*, and start hearing them as *questions addressed to you*. When the Qur'an asks "how will it be?" — do not answer in your head with theology about hypocrites. Answer it with your *hands*. The correct response to an unanswered question from your Lord is not understanding. It is a changed life. Tomorrow, give the question an answer it would be safe to be collected upon.

[PAUSE — major]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Beneath the Verse

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how the pieces were fitted together, because the design is exact.

The surah, two ayahs earlier, names the crime in the language of the body: *they reverted **on their backs** (adbār)*. The crime is a posture — a turning-away.

Then, in our verse, three things are placed in a precise order:

1. **فَكَيْفَ** — an open question, predicate removed, that turns to face the reader.
2. **تَوَفَّتْهُمُ** — death named as *full collection*, every deferred debt called in.
3. **وُجُوهَهُمْ وَأَدْبَـٰرَهُمْ** — and the consequence lands on the *backs* (adbār) — **the same word, the same body-part, that committed the crime in 47:25.**

Do you see the architecture? The crime was *adbār* — turning the back. The consequence is *adbār* — the back struck. The surah closed the loop on purpose. And it wrapped that closed loop inside an *open question*, so that the loop which has already closed on *them* would stay open for *you*.

**Closed account for the one who waited. Open question for the one still reading.** That is the whole design.

## Bringing It Together

Our three words and our two themes were always one insight, approached from different doors.

The linguistic door: an *unfinished question* + a word meaning *collected in full* + *the back that sinned is the back that's struck*.

The human door: *the bill you thought you skipped is still on the books* + *the question is left open because, for you, it can still be answered.*

Both doors open into the same room. The room contains a single truth: **Nothing is written off, and nothing is yet fixed.** Everything you defer is collected in full — and precisely *because* you are still being asked "how will it be?", the collection has not yet come. You are in the only window that exists: after the warning, before the settlement.

## Questions to Carry

1. What is the one thing I have turned my back on — telling myself that because I can no longer see it, it is no longer there?
2. If *tawaffā* came tonight and collected my account *in full*, which entries am I praying simply won't count? And why do I believe they won't?
3. The verse asks "how will it be?" and leaves it open. **If I had to answer it honestly today — what would my answer be?**
4. Where in my life is the gap between the words I say ("we will obey") and the back I actually turn?
5. Do I hear the Qur'an's warnings as news about other people, or as questions addressed to me — and what would change if I heard them as the second?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The unanswered question *"how will it be?"* is itself the mercy — because death is a *full collection* of every account you thought you had quietly skipped, and the only reason you are still being *asked* rather than *told* is that, for you, the answer is not yet written.

## The Closing Invitation

I am not going to tell you how it will be. The verse refused to, and I will not either — because the refusal *is* the gift. The answer is not mine and it is not the scholar's. It is yours, and it is being written in this hour, with the back you are choosing to turn or to turn back.

So let the invitation be simple. Find the open account. Stop slipping down the side street. Turn your face — *wajh* — back toward the One you said you would obey, before the One who is *al-Wafiyy* comes to collect, in full, what is owed. The door is open because the question is open. Walk through it while it is still a question.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who are al-Wafiyy, who collects every account in full and writes nothing off — do not let the angels find me with my back turned. You have asked me "how will it be?" while there is still time to answer; let my answer be a face turned wholly toward You and a debt paid before the settlement. Collect me in faithfulness, not in flight — and let the question, when it finally closes, close on Your mercy.*

*Āmīn.*
