# The Verb He Repeated and the Deeds He Warned You Could Lose

## Surah Muhammad 47:33

> ۞ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓاْ أَطِيعُواْ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُواْ ٱلرَّسُولَ وَلَا تُبْطِلُوٓاْ أَعْمَـٰلَكُمْ

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū aṭīʿū Allāha wa-aṭīʿū al-rasūla wa-lā tubṭilū aʿmālakum*

**"O you who believe! Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and do not nullify your deeds."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Place This Verse Was Spoken Into

Before we open this verse, we have to stand where it was first heard.

Surah Muhammad is not a gentle surah. Read it from the start and you feel the air get heavy. It is a surah about two kinds of people, drawn against each other again and again. There are those who disbelieved and "barred others from the way of Allah" — and Allah says of them, *aḍalla aʿmālahum*, "He has made their deeds go astray," let them wander off and come to nothing. And there are those who believed and did righteous deeds — and Allah says of *them*, *kaffara ʿanhum sayyiʾātihim wa-aṣlaḥa bālahum*, He covered over their wrongs and set their state right.

So far it is a clean line. Believers on one side. Disbelievers on the other.

But then, as the surah goes on, a third group walks into the room. And these are the most unsettling of all. They are the ones in whose hearts is a disease. The ones who **say "we will obey"** — *ṭāʿah* — and then, when the matter is resolved and the command is firm, they betray it. The ones who heard the path, saw it become clear, *and then turned back on their heels*. Not strangers to the message. People who had been *inside* it, and walked back out.

[PAUSE]

That is the world this verse drops into. The surah has just finished describing people who started on the road and abandoned it. People whose obedience was a sentence they spoke, not a life they lived. People who let what they had built quietly collapse.

And it is into *that* heaviness — right after that portrait — that Allah turns His face away from them and toward you, the believer, and says:

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū.* **O you who believe.**

Now, when Allah calls the believers by their faith — "O you who *believe*" — there is almost always a reason. He is about to ask of you something that only faith makes possible. And here is the strange thing about what He asks. He does not say to the believers, "become believers." You already are. He does not say, "start doing good." He assumes you are.

He says: **obey** — and then He says something that should stop your heart for a moment.

*Wa-lā tubṭilū aʿmālakum.* **Do not nullify your deeds.**

Not "do not fail to do deeds." Not "do not do bad deeds." Something far more frightening. *Do not make void the good you have already done.* Do not reach into the account you have spent your life filling — and empty it.

Here is the question I want us to carry through everything that follows. Why, after a whole surah about disbelievers losing their deeds, does Allah suddenly warn the *believers* that they can lose theirs too? What is the danger that comes not from outside your faith — but from inside it? And why, to guard against it, does He give a command in a very particular shape — repeating one single word in a way that, once you see it, you can never un-see?

Let's go and find out.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Verb He Said Twice

Let me start with something so quiet you would read straight past it ninety-nine times out of a hundred.

Before we look at the verse, let's understand the tool. In any language, when you give two instructions about two things, you have a choice. You can use the verb once and let it carry both: *"Respect your father and your teacher."* One verb, *respect*, doing double duty. Or you can repeat it: *"Respect your father and respect your teacher."* Two verbs.

Now in ordinary speech we'd say the second version is just wordier — same meaning, more syllables. But in the speech of Allah, where not one letter is spent without purpose, a repeated verb is never just a repeated verb. Repetition costs something. When the Most Eloquent chooses to *pay* that cost, He is buying something with it.

So look. Look at exactly what He does:

*aṭīʿū Allāha* — **obey Allah**

*wa-aṭīʿū al-rasūla* — **and obey the Messenger**

He said *aṭīʿū* — obey — and then He said it **again**. He did not say *aṭīʿū Allāha wa-rasūlahu*, "obey Allah and His Messenger," folding them into one command with one verb. He could have. It is shorter. It is, grammatically, completely available to Him.

He chose the longer road. He chose to say *obey* twice.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because there is more here than style.

The word *aṭīʿū* comes from the root **ṭāʿah** — ṭāʾ, wāw, ʿayn — the root of obedience, compliance, yielding. And notice it is the very same word the hypocrites were using just a few verses earlier. *Wa-yaqūlūna ṭāʿah* — "and they say: obedience." They had the *word* on their tongues. What they lacked was the verb made real in their limbs. So Allah takes the word they hollowed out — *ṭāʿah* — and He hands it back to the believers as a living command: *aṭīʿū*. Not say it. Do it.

But why say it twice?

Here is where it opens. There is a well-known principle the scholars draw from exactly this construction, and al-Qurṭubī states it plainly when he comes to this verse: *obey Allah in His commands* — and obey *the Messenger in his sunan*, in his Prophetic practice. By repeating the verb, Allah gives the Messenger's authority its **own standing**. The obedience owed to the Messenger ﷺ is not a footnote to the obedience owed to Allah, something you'd only honor where the Messenger happens to be quoting the Qur'an. It is an independent, free-standing obligation. The Sunnah is not optional commentary on the Book. It carries command in its own right — because Allah gave it its own verb.

Now stay with me, because here is the proof that this is deliberate and not accidental. Allah uses this exact construction elsewhere, and there He shows us the contrast Himself.

In Sūrat al-Nisāʾ, verse 59:

*aṭīʿū Allāha wa-aṭīʿū al-rasūla wa-ulī al-amri minkum*

**"Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger — and those in authority among you."**

Look carefully. He repeats the verb for the Messenger: *aṭīʿū al-rasūl*, obey the Messenger. But for "those in authority" — the leaders, the scholars, the rulers among you — He does **not** repeat it. He does not say *wa-aṭīʿū ulī al-amr*. He just attaches them: *and those in authority.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what that does?

The dropped verb is not laziness. It is *theology written in grammar.* The obedience owed to those in authority has no independent verb because it has no independent source. It is **derivative**. You obey them *only* insofar as their command flows from the command of Allah and His Messenger. The moment a human authority steps outside that — *lā ṭāʿata li-makhlūqin fī maʿṣiyati al-khāliq*, "no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator" — the obligation evaporates, because it never had its own verb to stand on.

But the Messenger ﷺ *got his own verb.* His obedience does not evaporate, does not turn conditional, does not get weighed against anyone. Allah secured it grammatically, here in our verse and there in al-Nisāʾ, by an act as small and as enormous as **saying one word twice.**

That is what this single repeated *aṭīʿū* reveals: the Sunnah of the Messenger ﷺ stands beside the command of Allah on its own two feet — and no human voice, however high, was ever given that.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

## Element Two: The Word for Faith Is a Word for Safety

Now let's go back to the very opening of the call, to the word that names the people being addressed.

*alladhīna āmanū* — "those who believe."

We translate it "believe," and that is right, but the root underneath is going to reframe the entire warning that comes at the end of the verse. So let me teach the root first, on its own, before we put it back.

The verb *āmanū* comes from the root **amn** — alif, mīm, nūn. And the bare meaning of *amn*, before any religion is attached to it, is **safety. Security. Being free from fear.** It is the word a mother uses when the danger has passed and the child is *safe*. It is the opposite of dread. *Al-Masjid al-Ḥarām*, Allah calls a place of *amn*, a sanctuary, somewhere you are not hunted.

Now — and this is the morphology doing real work — the verb in our verse, *āmana*, is **Form IV**. In Arabic, Form IV characteristically takes a root and makes it *transitive*, makes you *enact* the meaning, *bring it about*. So if *amn* is safety, then *āmana* — Form IV — at its root carries the sense of *entering into safety*, *making oneself secure*, *giving and receiving trust*. The lexicographer al-Rāghib draws exactly this line: that *īmān*, faith, is rooted in the heart's arrival at *security* — *taṣdīq*, an affirmation that the heart rests in, no longer pacing, no longer afraid.

So *alladhīna āmanū* does not only mean "those who hold certain beliefs." Underneath the word it means **those who have come into safety.** Those who, by trusting Allah and His Messenger, found the one shelter that does not collapse. Faith, at the level of the root, is the name for *arriving somewhere safe.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold that meaning in your hand — *faith is having reached safety* — and walk it to the end of the verse.

Because look at what He warns them against. He warns the people who have *reached safety* that they can **nullify their own deeds.** He warns the secure that their security is something they can spend. The very people whose name means "those who made it to shelter" are told, in the same breath: *you can take the roof off it yourselves.*

The word for faith is a word for safety. And this verse exists to tell you that the safe can make themselves unsafe — not through some external enemy breaching the walls, but through their own hands, from the inside. That is why He had to call them by *this* name. Only someone who has something to lose can be warned about losing it. He named them by their safety so that the warning about *forfeiting* it would land where it hurts.

## Element Three: The Word "Nullify," and the Object He Left Open

Now we arrive at the heart of the verse. The prohibition. The one *lā* — the one "do not" — in the entire ayah.

*wa-lā tubṭilū aʿmālakum* — **"and do not nullify your deeds."**

Two things here will change how you read it. The first is the verb. The second is something Allah *did not say* — an absence, and in the Qur'an absence speaks.

First, the verb. *Tubṭilū* comes from the root **bāṭil** — bāʾ, ṭāʾ, lām — and *bāṭil* is one of the great words of the Qur'an. It means **falsehood, vanity, the void, that which has no reality, that which comes to nothing.** It is the opposite of *ḥaqq*, the real, the true, the enduring. When Allah says *jāʾa al-ḥaqqu wa-zahaqa al-bāṭil* — "truth has come and falsehood has vanished" — *bāṭil* is the thing that evaporates because it never had substance to begin with.

Now the morphology. *Tubṭilū* is **Form IV** of this root — and here, unlike with *āmana*, Form IV is doing its clean **causative** work: to *make* something *bāṭil*. To *render* a thing void. To take something that was real, that had weight, that counted — and turn it into nothing.

So feel the precision. Allah is not saying "do not do *bāṭil* deeds," do not do worthless things. He is saying: *do not take your deeds — your real ones, your good ones, the ones that already have weight on the scale — and convert them into the void.* Do not be the one who reaches into your own treasury of *ḥaqq* and turns it to dust with your own hand. The verb assumes the deeds are *good*. The catastrophe is not in the doing. It is in the *un-doing*.

[PAUSE]

And now the absence. This is the part I most want you to see.

Allah says *do not nullify your deeds* — and He does **not tell you how.** He gives the verb no method. He names no specific sin, no single mechanism. *Wa-lā tubṭilū aʿmālakum* — and then He stops. The "how" is left wide open.

In Arabic, this kind of silence is loud. When a verb that could take a "by means of what" is left without one, the gap is not an oversight — it is an *invitation*, and often a *totality*. He left it open because the ways to void your deeds are *many*, and He meant to warn you against all of them at once.

Watch the classical scholars walk into that open space — and notice they do not contradict each other. They are standing at different windows looking into the same room:

- **Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī** said: by **sins** — your good is undone by your disobedience.
- **Al-Zuhrī** said: by **major sins** specifically — the great breaches that swallow the good.
- **Ibn Jurayj** said: by **riyāʾ and sumʿah** — doing the deed to be *seen*, to be *talked about*; the act done for the eyes of people instead of the face of Allah.
- **Muqātil and al-Thumālī** said: by **mann** — by reminding people of your favors, parading your charity, throwing your good deed back in someone's face. (And there is a reading that this was directed at those who would *remind the Messenger* ﷺ of their service to Islam.)
- **ʿAṭāʾ** said: by **doubt and hypocrisy** — the inner crack that hollows the deed from beneath.

[PAUSE]

Do you see? Sins. Major sins. Showing off. Reminding people of favors. Doubt. Hypocrisy. These are not five competing answers to a quiz with one right box. They are five *doors* into the same void — and Allah left the verb open precisely so it would name *all* of them and any you might invent next. He did not hand you a list to check off. He handed you a principle to fear: **everything you have built is exposed, from more directions than you can count.** Every door of pride, every door of insincerity, every door of cruelty to others, every door of doubt — each one opens onto the same drop.

That is what the open verb gives us that a specified one never could. A specified verb — "do not nullify your deeds *by sinning*" — would have let the show-off relax. An open verb lets no one relax. **It indicts every one of us at our own particular weak door.**

---

### Bridge

So three things have surfaced from the words themselves.

A verb said *twice* — establishing that the Messenger's command stands on its own, beside Allah's, while no human authority was ever given that.

A name built from *safety* — telling us that faith is the state of having arrived somewhere secure, which is exactly why it can be tragically forfeited.

And a verb of *un-doing*, deliberately left open — telling us that the good we have built is real, is valuable, and is exposed to ruin from every direction at once.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the spiritual life itself. About the strange, fragile, precious thing that a lifetime of deeds actually *is*.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Account You Can Empty

Let me start where you already live.

You have, somewhere, done work that took you years — and you understand, in your bones, that *having done a thing is not the same as keeping it.* Think of the writer who fills a thousand pages over a decade and then the manuscript burns. Think of the person who builds a business across twenty years and watches it dissolve in one bad season. Think — and this is the one that gets closest — of the savings account. The years of careful deposits. The discipline of putting a little aside, again and again, the slow mountain of it. And then think of how *fast* a mountain like that can go to zero. One catastrophic decision. One betrayal. One signature on the wrong line. Decades in. Minutes out.

We understand this perfectly about money. We have built an entire civilization of locks and passwords and insurance precisely *because* we know that accumulation is fragile — that what took years to gather can be lost in a moment, and that the gathering and the *guarding* are two different skills.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what this verse does. It takes that bone-deep, money-shaped knowledge — *what you accumulate, you can lose* — and it tells you: **this is also true of your deeds.**

This is not how most of us secretly think about our religious life. Most of us carry an unspoken accounting in the back of the mind that goes: *I'm building up credit. Every prayer, every fast, every charity — it goes into the account, and the account only ever goes up.* We treat good deeds like deposits in a vault that can be added to but never robbed. We imagine the scale of our deeds as a one-way ratchet, clicking ever higher.

And our verse walks into that comfortable picture and says, quietly: *wa-lā tubṭilū aʿmālakum.* That account can be **emptied.** Not by Allah's cruelty — Allah is never unjust by an atom's weight — but by *your own hand.* The verb, remember, is causative and the doer is *you*: do not *you* be the one who renders *your* deeds void.

There is an athar that captures the moment this realization hit the first community, and Abū al-ʿĀliya reports it — you find it in al-Baghawī's commentary. He says the Companions of the Messenger ﷺ used to believe that **no sin harms you as long as you have sincere faith, just as no good benefits you as long as you have shirk.** Faith was the magic shield; with it, nothing could touch your record. And then *this verse came down.* And — listen to his words — *"they feared the major sins, after it, lest they nullify the deeds."*

[PAUSE]

Something shifted in them. The shield was real, but it was not unconditional. The account was real, but it was not robbery-proof. They had thought the spiritual life was all about *deposits.* This verse taught them it is also about *security.*

And the Messenger ﷺ gave that abstraction a face you cannot forget. He asked his companions: *"Do you know who the bankrupt one is?"* — *al-muflis.* They answered the way we all would: the bankrupt is the one with no money, no possessions. And he ﷺ corrected them. No. The truly bankrupt of my nation is the one who **comes on the Day of Resurrection with prayer, and fasting, and charity** — comes with a mountain of deeds, the savings of a whole life — *but* he had insulted this one, and slandered that one, and spilled the blood of another, and seized the wealth of another. So this one is given from his good deeds, and that one is given from his good deeds, until **his deeds run out** while the claims against him remain — and then their sins are heaped upon him, and he is thrown into the Fire.

Do you see the verse living inside that hadith? Here is a man who *did the deeds.* The deposits were real. He was not lazy; he was not a disbeliever. He arrived at the vault with mountains. And he walked away *muflis* — bankrupt — because while he was busy filling the account, he was also, through the wrongs he did to people, *emptying it.* He nullified his own deeds. *Tubṭilū aʿmālakum.* Right there. In flesh.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the question of your spiritual life is no longer only *"What did I do today?"* It is also *"What did I undo today?"* You can pray the whole prayer and dissolve it on the way out the door with a single act of contempt for a person Allah made. You can give the charity and void it with the reminder you drop a week later — *after all I did for you.* The doing and the guarding are two different skills. Most of us have only ever practiced the first.

The practical shift is brutally simple and very hard: **at the end of the day, audit the withdrawals, not only the deposits.** Where today did I reach into my own account and pull something out — through a tongue that wounded, a deed performed for eyes that were not Allah's, a favor I turned into a leash? That audit, done nightly, is the whole point of this half of the verse.

## Theme Two: Why "Obey" Comes Before "Don't Nullify"

Now let's ask about the *order* of the verse, because the order is the answer to the fear the first theme just raised.

Begin again where you live. Imagine someone hands you the warning of theme one — *everything you've built can be lost from a hundred directions* — and nothing else. What happens to a person who only hears that? They become **paralyzed.** They become the anxious soul who is so terrified of ruining the prayer with showing-off that they can barely pray; so frightened of voiding the charity that they freeze before giving it. The warning alone, with no remedy, breeds despair. It makes you clutch your account so tightly that you stop *living* from it.

[PAUSE]

So look at what Allah actually did. He did not lead with the warning. He led with the *work.*

*aṭīʿū Allāha wa-aṭīʿū al-rasūla* — **obey Allah, obey the Messenger** — *then* — *wa-lā tubṭilū aʿmālakum* — do not nullify your deeds.

The two commands come *first.* The prohibition comes *last.* And that order is the medicine.

Here is why. Notice that the deeds He tells you not to nullify — *aʿmālakum*, **your** deeds — are the very deeds the first two commands *generate.* Obeying Allah and obeying the Messenger is *how the account gets filled in the first place.* So the architecture of the verse is: **keep building, keep building, and don't tear down what you build.** The remedy for the fear of losing your deeds is not to stop and guard them anxiously. The remedy is *to keep obeying.*

This is something we know from every other accumulation in life and forget only here. The way you protect a body you've built is not to stop moving and wrap it in cotton — it is to *keep training.* The way you protect a language you've learned is not to stop speaking it for fear of mistakes — it is to *keep speaking.* The living thing is protected by its own continuation. The moment it stops, the decay starts. *Istiqāma* — steadfastness, staying on the straight road — is not a wall you build around your deeds. It is the *motion* that keeps them alive.

[PAUSE]

And now the two halves of the verse lock together. The hypocrites of this very surah — remember them — said *ṭāʿah*, "obedience," and then *turned back on their heels after guidance became clear.* What was their actual crime? It was not that they never started. It was that **they stopped.** They had been on the road. They walked back. They *nullified*, by turning back, the very obedience they had professed. They are the living negative of this verse: they said the word *ṭāʿah* without the verb *aṭīʿū*, and so the road they had begun came to *bāṭil*, to nothing, in their hands.

So when Allah turns to the believers and says *aṭīʿū... aṭīʿū... wa-lā tubṭilū*, He is saying: do not let your story end the way theirs did. The danger is not only the dramatic sin. The danger is the slow stop. The quiet drifting off the road. The obedience that was a phase. *Keep obeying* — present, ongoing, unfinished — *and you will not be among those whose deeds came to nothing.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It reframes the entire warning from *fear* into *direction.* You are not asked to stand guard, trembling, over a finished pile of deeds. You are asked to **stay in motion.** The protection of what you have done is in the continuation of doing. So the practical application is not "become anxious about your record." It is the opposite: *do not stop.* When you feel the drift — the prayer getting lighter, the Qur'an getting more distant, the old discipline loosening — recognize it for what it is. That drift is not neutral. That is the *beginning* of *tubṭilū*. The slowing is the leak. And the only patch for it is the first half of the verse: *aṭīʿū.* Obey. Again. Today. Keep the road under your feet.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Now That We Can See It

Let's stand back and look at the whole verse at once, because now its shape will mean something.

> *O you who believe* — **(the secure ones, those who reached safety)**
> *Obey Allah* — **(build)**
> *and obey the Messenger* — **(build — on an authority all its own)**
> *and do not nullify your deeds.* — **(do not tear down)**

It is a verse with a clean and devastating structure: a **named people**, two acts of **construction**, and one prohibition against **demolition.** Building, building — and *don't unbuild.* And the genius of it is that the thing you are told not to demolish (*your deeds*) is the very thing the building commands produce. The verse is a closed circuit. The cure for the fear in its last clause is contained in its first two.

And around it, the whole surah clicks into place. Surah Muhammad showed us, first, the disbelievers — *Allah let their deeds go astray.* Then the hypocrites — who *said obedience and turned back, their deeds coming to nothing by their own retreat.* And then, in this single verse, He turns to *you* and says: you have seen two ways for deeds to be lost — the way of those who never truly built, and the way of those who built and then walked away. **Do neither. Build, and stay.**

## Two Roads to One Place

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same truth approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us that the verb *aṭīʿū* was repeated to give the Messenger's command its own standing, and that *āmanū* is built from *safety*, and that *tubṭilū* was left open to indict every weak door at once. The themes showed us that deeds are an account you can empty, and that the only protection is to keep building.

They meet here: **faith is not a possession you secure once; it is a road you stay on.** The repeated *obey* is the road. The safety in *believe* is what's at stake if you leave it. And the open-ended *do not nullify* is the warning that you can leave it by any of a hundred quiet exits — and that the leaving, not only the dramatic fall, is how the secure become bankrupt.

## Questions to Carry

1. When I examine my day, do I only count what I *did* — or do I also look for what I *undid*? What did I withdraw from my own account today, and through which door?
2. Abū al-ʿĀliya's report says the Companions began to *fear* after this verse came. Have I ever felt that fear — the holy fear that my deeds are not robbery-proof? Or do I still live as though my account only goes up?
3. The hypocrites *said* obedience and stopped *doing* it. Where in my own life has obedience become a word I once meant rather than a verb I still perform?
4. The remedy for fear was *more obedience*, not less living. Where has fear of insincerity actually made me *do less good* — and is that fear from Allah, or from the one who wants my account to stay small?
5. The Messenger ﷺ was given his own verb, his own standing. Does my life actually treat his Sunnah as an independent command — or only as a footnote I honor when it's convenient?

## The Distillation

**Keep building your obedience and never tear it down — for faith is not a safety you own, but a road you stay on, and the deeds of a lifetime can be emptied by your own hand from a hundred quiet doors.**

## The Closing Invitation

So here is what this verse invites — not demands, invites.

Tonight, before you sleep, do the audit the verse asks for. Not the audit of deposits — you already love counting those. The audit of *withdrawals.* Find the one place today where you reached into your own account and pulled something out. Name it. And then do not despair over it — because the same verse that warned you handed you the cure in its first breath. *Aṭīʿū.* Obey. Again. Tomorrow. The road is still under your feet. The account is not closed. The only true bankruptcy is to stop building while the demolition continues — and you, of all people, the one Allah called by the name of *safety*, were never meant to stop.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who named us by the safety we found in You — let us not be the ones who tear down with our own hands what Your guidance helped us build. Keep us obeying You and obeying Your Messenger, present and unbroken, until the last breath. And on the Day the accounts are opened, do not let us be among the bankrupt — those who came with mountains and left with dust.* Āmīn.
