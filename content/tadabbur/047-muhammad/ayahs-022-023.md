# The Hand That Cuts: How Turning Away Severs the Ties of Mercy

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **فَهَلْ عَسَيْتُمْ إِن تَوَلَّيْتُمْ أَن تُفْسِدُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَتُقَطِّعُوٓا۟ أَرْحَامَكُمْ**
>
> *Fahal ʿasaytum in tawallaytum an tufsidū fī-l-arḍi wa-tuqaṭṭiʿū arḥāmakum*
>
> "Then would you perhaps, if you turned away, spread corruption in the land and sever your ties of kinship?"

And immediately after:

> **أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَعَنَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ فَأَصَمَّهُمْ وَأَعْمَىٰٓ أَبْصَـٰرَهُمْ**
>
> *Ulāʾika alladhīna laʿanahumu-llāhu fa-aṣammahum wa-aʿmā abṣārahum*
>
> "Those are the ones whom Allah has cursed, so He made them deaf and blinded their eyes."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel the weight of this, you have to stand where the first listeners were standing.

This is Madinah. Not Makkah — Madinah, where the community has a name now, a mosque, a structure, obligations. And with obligations come the people who want the belonging without the cost. The surah just before these verses has been describing them with surgical precision. There are people, it says, *in whose hearts is a disease* (47:20). When a clear, decisive chapter is revealed — one that names a hard command — you can watch their faces. They look at the Prophet ﷺ, the Quran says, **"with the look of one whose eyes roll back, fainting at the approach of death."**

Picture that. Not open hostility. Not a raised voice. Just a face draining of color when the command lands. The flinch. The internal *no* that never quite makes it to the lips.

These are not the enemies outside the walls. These are people *inside* the community — people who said *we hear and we obey*, and then, when the thing got real, began edging toward the door.

And it is to *these* people that the question in our verse is suddenly turned.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to hold onto from the very first moment.

Allah does not say to them: *if you turn away, you will be punished.* He does not even, at first, threaten them. He asks them a question. And the question is not about their prayer, not about their fasting, not even directly about their faith.

He asks: **if you turn away — do you know what your own hands will do?**

And the answer He gives — *corruption in the land, and the cutting of your own blood* — seems, at first, to come out of nowhere. What does turning away from a revealed chapter have to do with spilling blood and severing family?

[PAUSE]

Stay with that gap. Because inside that gap is one of the most precise diagnoses of the human heart anywhere in the Book. By the end, I want you to see that these two things — turning from God and tearing apart your own people — are not two things at all. They are one motion, viewed from two ends.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: عَسَيْتُمْ — The Verb of Hope, Turned Into a Verdict

Let me teach you a small piece of Arabic before we touch the word, because the whole effect depends on it.

There is a family of verbs in Arabic called *afʿāl al-rajāʾ* — "verbs of hoping" or "verbs of expectation." The chief of them is **عَسَى** (*ʿasā*). When a person says *ʿasā* about something, they are saying: *perhaps... it may be... one hopes that...* It leans forward. It is the word of a parent saying "maybe this year things will turn around," the word you reach for when you are looking toward a future you want.

In ordinary speech, *ʿasā* carries warmth. Hope. The tilt toward the good.

Now look at where it sits in our verse:

> **فَهَلْ عَسَيْتُمْ**
>
> *Fahal ʿasaytum* — "Then is it perhaps the case with you..."

Notice something strange. A word of *hope* has been bent into a word of *dread*. The structure says "perhaps you will..." — but what follows the "perhaps" is not a hope. It is a horror: that you would spread corruption and cut your kin.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the discomfort of that for a moment. It is as if someone took the gentlest, most forward-leaning word in the language and used it to point at the darkest possible outcome. *"Is it to be hoped of you... that you would destroy?"* The sweetness of the word makes the content land harder, the way a soft voice delivering terrible news cuts deeper than a shout.

But there is a second thing happening here, and the classical commentators caught it precisely. Al-Jalalayn notes it in a single phrase: **وفيه التفات عن الغيبة إلى الخطاب** — *"in it is an iltifāt, a turn, from third-person to direct address."*

Let me teach you *iltifāt*, because it is one of the signature moves of Quranic speech.

Imagine you are in a room and someone is talking *about* a person — "he keeps making the same mistake, he never learns" — and then, mid-sentence, without warning, they spin on their heel, look that person dead in the eye, and say: **"...don't you?"** That sudden swing of the spotlight, from talking *about* someone to talking *at* them — that is *iltifāt*.

That is exactly what happens here. The whole surrounding passage has been describing the diseased-hearted in the third person: *they* look like fainting men, in *their* hearts is sickness. And then — the camera whips around. **"Then is it perhaps the case with *you*...?"**

[PAUSE]

Feel what that does. As long as it is "them," you can listen from a safe distance. You can nod along, comfortable, certain the verse is about someone else. The instant it becomes "you," the floor tilts. There is nowhere to stand outside the question anymore.

And here is the genius of pairing *iltifāt* with *ʿasā*. The verse does not accuse outright. It does not say "you *will* corrupt the earth." It says: *is it perhaps to be expected of you?* It hands you the question and steps back. It forces you to answer it yourself. **What would I do, if I turned away? Do I actually know what is in me?**

What this single opening reveals: God does not need to convict you of the crime. He only needs to turn and ask you — gently, in the language of hope — whether you are sure of what your own turning-away would set loose. And the honest heart goes quiet.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: تَوَلَّيْتُمْ — The Word That Means Both "Turn Away" and "Take Charge"

Now to the hinge of the whole verse: **تَوَلَّيْتُمْ** (*tawallaytum*), "if you turned away" — or is it?

Let me first show you the root, because this root is doing something remarkable. The root is **و-ل-ي** (*w-l-y*). And this root has a kind of double life in Arabic.

On one side, *w-l-y* gives you *walā* — to turn, to turn your back, to turn away. *Tawallā* (Form V, the reflexive form our verb is built on) literally means *to turn oneself away*, to avert, to recoil and go.

On the other side, the *exact same root* gives you *walī* — a guardian, a protector, the one in charge. *Wilāya* — authority, governorship, taking command of an affair. A *walī* is the one to whom the matter is *turned over*.

One root. Two directions. *To turn away* — and *to be turned to, to take charge.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is what is beautiful, and it is something the classical scholars themselves were divided over — not as a flaw, but as a richness. When the commentators came to **تَوَلَّيْتُمْ**, they read it two different ways, and al-Qurtubi records the debate openly.

One camp read it as *turning away*. Al-Baghawi and al-Jalalayn take this road: *"if you turned away"* — if you averted yourselves from the Quran, abandoned its rulings, recoiled from the command, you would slide back into what you were in the days of ignorance — corruption, transgression, the spilling of blood, the shattering of the unity Islam had gathered you into. Ibn Jurayj, al-Qurtubi tells us, read it precisely this way: *if you turned away from obedience.*

The other camp read it as *taking authority*. Al-Qurtubi reports Abū al-ʿĀliya: *"if you were given charge — if you became rulers — that you would corrupt the earth by taking bribes."* And al-Kalbī: *"if you were put in charge of the affairs of the community, that you would corrupt the earth through injustice."*

Now — which is it?

[PAUSE]

Here is the thing I want you to see. **You do not have to choose.** The word holds both, because the *root* holds both. This is not confusion in the text. This is the text saying two true things with one breath.

Because watch what they have in common. Whether you *turn your back* on God's guidance, or you *grasp the reins* of power over people — the verse names the *same* result: *corruption in the land, and the cutting of kinship.* 

Turning away from God and taking command over others are, spiritually, the same dangerous moment. They are both the moment the human being takes the wheel from his Maker. One does it by walking off. The other does it by climbing up. And the root *w-l-y* — which means *both* to walk off *and* to climb up — catches both with one word.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete. Think of the two ways a person loses their soul. Some lose it in retreat — they drift from God quietly, turn away, stop answering. Others lose it in conquest — they get a little power, a title, control over others, and it corrodes them. The withdrawn man and the powerful man look like opposites. But the verse says: same root, same outcome. The hand that lets go of God and the hand that seizes the throne are, underneath, the same hand.

What this single word reveals: there is no neutral place to put down the burden of submission. The moment you take it off — whether by walking away or by sitting on a throne — something in the world starts to break.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: تُقَطِّعُوٓا أَرْحَامَكُمْ — The Repeated Cutting of the Wombs of Mercy

Now we come to the phrase that I believe is the secret heart of these two verses. **وَتُقَطِّعُوٓا۟ أَرْحَامَكُمْ** — *"and you would cut up your ties of kinship."*

There are two pieces of grammar here, and each one is carrying enormous weight. Let me take them one at a time, slowly.

**First: the *shape* of the verb.**

The verb is **تُقَطِّعُوا** (*tuqaṭṭiʿū*), from the root **ق-ط-ع** (*q-ṭ-ʿ*), to cut. But it is not the simple form of "to cut." Look at the doubled middle letter — that *shadda* on the *ṭ*. The Leeds morphological corpus confirms this is **Form II** (*faʿʿala*, تَفْعِيل).

Let me teach you what Form II does. The simple verb *qaṭaʿa* means "he cut" — one clean stroke. When you double the middle letter to make Form II — *qaṭṭaʿa* — Arabic grammarians call this *takthīr*, **intensification and multiplication**. It does not mean *to cut*. It means **to cut repeatedly, to hack, to cut into many pieces, to keep cutting.**

This is not a guess. Al-Baghawi states it explicitly: the dominant reading is the heavy form, *tuqaṭṭiʿū*, **مِن التقطيع، على التكثير** — *"from al-taqṭīʿ, in the mode of multiplication,"* he says, *because of the wombs* — *li-ajl al-arḥām*.

[PAUSE]

Hear the difference. The verse does not say *you would cut your kinship*, a single severing. It says you would **hack it apart, again and again, into pieces.** Not one wound — a butchering.

And listen to how the word *sounds*. *Tu-qaṭ-ṭi-ʿū.* That doubled, stopped *ṭṭ* in the middle — the tongue strikes the roof of the mouth and stops, strikes and stops. The word itself *chops* as you say it. The mouth performs the meaning. This is a verb that sounds like an axe.

**Second: the *object* of the verb — what, exactly, is being hacked apart.**

The object is **أَرْحَامَكُمْ** (*arḥāmakum*), "your *arḥām*." We translate it "ties of kinship," and that is correct. But you must hear the root, because the root is everything here.

The root is **ر-ح-م** (*r-ḥ-m*).

Where else have you heard those three letters?

[PAUSE]

*Al-Raḥmān. Al-Raḥīm.* The Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate. *Raḥma* — mercy itself. The very first words you say in every chapter, every prayer, every meal — *Bismillāhi-r-Raḥmāni-r-Raḥīm* — come from this root.

And *raḥim* — the singular of *arḥām* — is the **womb**. The organ in which a human being is first held, first fed, first kept alive. The place of origin. And by extension, *arḥām* means everyone connected to you through that origin — your blood, your kin.

So look at what the root *r-ḥ-m* binds together: **mercy** and **the womb** and **kinship** are the *same word*. They are not three ideas that happen to be related. In the architecture of the Arabic language, they are one thing.

[PAUSE]

Now read the phrase again with new eyes:

**وَتُقَطِّعُوٓا۟ أَرْحَامَكُمْ** — *"and you would hack to pieces... your wombs-of-mercy."*

The thing the verse warns you against destroying is not just "family relationships" in some abstract civic sense. It is the *living tissue of mercy itself* — the bonds that came out of the womb, the bonds that carry the very name of God's compassion.

And the Messenger ﷺ made this connection explicit. In a hadith recorded by al-Bukhārī, he reported that Allah said of the *raḥim*, the womb-tie: **"الرَّحِمُ شُجْنَةٌ مِنَ الرَّحْمَنِ"** — *"The womb-tie is a branch / an offshoot of the Most Merciful (al-Raḥmān)."* And then: **"مَنْ وَصَلَهَا وَصَلْتُهُ، وَمَنْ قَطَعَهَا قَطَعْتُهُ"** — *"Whoever joins it, I join him; and whoever cuts it"* — and here is our root again, *qaṭaʿa* — *"whoever cuts it, I cut him off."*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what has just happened linguistically? The kinship-tie is *literally derived from* the name al-Raḥmān. It is a thread that hangs from the Throne of Mercy. So when you take Form II — the hacking, multiplying cut — and bring it down on the *arḥām*, you are not just hurting your family. You are taking an axe to a branch of God's own mercy, hanging in your own home.

What this phrase reveals: the worst thing turning away from God does is not "out there" in the land. It comes home. It picks up the axe and walks into the family — and what it cuts is the very tissue through which God's mercy was reaching the world.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Let us gather what these words have shown us before we go deeper.

*ʿAsaytum* with its sudden turn — God spins to face *you* and asks, in the gentle language of hope, whether you are sure of what your turning-away contains.

*Tawallaytum* — and the turning-away is the same motion as the seizing of power; both are the hand letting go of God, and both end in the same place.

*Tuqaṭṭiʿū arḥāmakum* — and that place is a home, with an axe in it, hacking apart the threads of mercy that hang from God's own name.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being, and a human society, actually comes apart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why Cutting God Loose Always Ends in Cutting People Apart

Let me start where you live, not where the verse is.

We are living through what researchers have flatly named an *epidemic of loneliness*. People are more "connected" than any generation in history — more contacts, more feeds, more ways to reach anyone — and more *alone* than ever measured. Families scatter and do not reassemble. A startling number of people cannot name a single person they would call in a crisis. We have built a civilization remarkably good at producing individuals and remarkably bad at keeping them attached to one another.

And here is the part that should stop us: almost nobody *chose* this. No one woke up and decided to dismantle the family, to sever themselves from kin, to end up unreachable. It happened by drift. By a thousand small turnings-away.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the verse to that.

The verse names two things in one breath: *turning away from revelation*, and *the hacking-apart of kinship*. To our ears those sound like two separate problems — one religious, one social. The verse treats them as cause and effect. **Turn from God, and the bonds between people begin to come apart.** Not as a punishment imposed from outside. As a consequence that grows from inside.

Why? Let me build this carefully, because this is the engine of the whole passage.

What is it that makes you honor a tie you would rather walk away from? Think honestly. There is a difficult relative — someone who has wronged you, someone tedious, someone who costs you. Every natural instinct says *cut it, you don't owe them anything.* What overrides that instinct? What makes a person keep showing up for kin who give them nothing back?

[PAUSE]

It is something *above* the relationship. A command, a sacred sense that this tie is not mine to cut, that it was given to me, that I will answer for it to Someone. The bond between two people is held in place by a third thing *above* both of them.

Pull out that third thing — pull out the One above — and what is left? Just two people, doing a cost-benefit analysis on each other. And on pure cost-benefit, most ties *lose*. The difficult parent, the draining sibling, the cousin who never calls back — on a ledger, you cut them all. 

This is the mechanism the verse is exposing. *Tawallaytum* — you turn from the One above. And the moment the One above is gone, the only thing holding the horizontal ties in place is gone too. They were never held up by the two people. They were held up by the third. So they fall. And — *tuqaṭṭiʿū* — they don't fall gently. They get hacked apart, one decision at a time, *takthīr*, the multiplying cut.

[PAUSE]

Now go back to the loneliness epidemic with this lens. We did not lose our bonds because we stopped valuing connection — we talk about connection constantly. We lost them because we lost the *vertical* thing that held the *horizontal* things up. A society that turns from the transcendent does not *decide* to fragment. It fragments because it pulled out the one nail every other bond was hanging from.

And notice the order in the verse, because it is exact: corruption in *the land* — *fasād fī-l-arḍ* — is named *before* the cutting of kin. The big, abstract, civilizational rot is mentioned first. But then the verse zooms all the way in, from the whole earth down to *your own arḥām, your own blood.* As if to say: you want to know where the corruption of the entire land begins? It begins at your kitchen table. The macro-collapse is just the micro-cut, multiplied across millions of homes.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? It means *ṣilat al-raḥim* — keeping the kinship tie — is not a nicety. It is not "being a good family person." It is the front line. The single most concrete thing you can do to resist *fasād fī-l-arḍ*, the corruption of the whole earth, is to refuse to cut the one difficult tie in your own life that you have every "reason" to cut. 

Pick up the phone to the relative you have been avoiding. Not because they deserve it. Because the tie hangs from the Throne, and you have decided not to be the one who takes the axe to it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Deafening and the Blinding — When Turning Away Becomes Unable to Turn Back

Now to the second verse, and to its most haunting image:

> **أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ لَعَنَهُمُ ٱللَّهُ فَأَصَمَّهُمْ وَأَعْمَىٰٓ أَبْصَـٰرَهُمْ**
>
> "Those are the ones whom Allah has cursed, so He made them deaf and blinded their eyes."

Let me begin, again, where you live.

You know the experience of a sense going numb. Walk into a room with a strong smell — within minutes, you cannot smell it anymore. Live next to a railway and within a month you no longer hear the trains. The signal hasn't stopped. *Your capacity to receive it has shut down from overexposure.* Your nervous system, protecting itself, simply stops registering what you have refused, again and again, to respond to.

Now there is a moral version of exactly this, and you have felt it. The first time you do something you know is wrong, there is a loud alarm — the conscience screams. The tenth time, it is a murmur. The hundredth time, silence. Nothing changed about the act. What changed is *you stopped being able to hear the alarm.* Psychologists call it desensitization. The Quran is describing its spiritual mechanism a millennium and a half early.

[PAUSE]

Watch the precise sequence the verse gives, because it is a chain, not a list.

First: *laʿanahumu-llāh* — Allah cursed them. What does "curse" actually mean? The root *l-ʿ-n* in Arabic, as al-Rāghib defines it in his *Mufradāt*, means **distancing and expulsion** — specifically, expulsion from mercy. To be cursed is not, at root, to be struck by lightning. It is to be *put at a distance from raḥma.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold that against what we just learned in verse 22. What did these people do? They *cut their arḥām* — they hacked apart the threads of *raḥma*. And what is the consequence in verse 23? They are *expelled from raḥma.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? It is the most exact reciprocity. Remember the hadith: *"whoever cuts it, I cut him off."* They took the axe to the branch of the Merciful — and so they are severed from the Merciful. The curse is not an arbitrary punishment bolted on from outside. **The curse is the cut, returning to its sender.** You cut *raḥim*; you are cut from *raḥma*. The same root, *q-ṭ-ʿ*, hangs over both ends of it. The wound you deal to your kin is the wound you wake up wearing yourself.

And *then* — only then — come the deafening and the blinding. Look at the connector: *fa-aṣammahum* — "**so** He deafened them." The *fa* means *as a result, as a consequence.* The deafness and blindness are not a separate disaster. They flow *out of* the expulsion from mercy. Cut yourself off from the Source of mercy, and the faculties by which you might have found your way *back* go dark.

[PAUSE]

This is the most frightening logic in the passage, so let me say it plainly. Turning away (verse 22) is something you *do.* But if you do it long enough, it stops being something you do and becomes something you *are.* It graduates from a *choice* into a *condition.* The man who keeps turning away eventually loses the capacity to turn back — because the very instruments of return, hearing and sight, have been switched off. The door is not locked from the outside. He has lost the eyes to find it and the ears to hear it being knocked on.

Now let me show you one more thing — an asymmetry hidden in the grammar that I find almost unbearably precise.

Read the two halves again: *fa-aṣammahum* — "He deafened **them**." And *wa-aʿmā abṣārahum* — "and blinded **their eyes**."

Notice the difference. For deafness, the object is *them* — the whole person. *He deafened them.* It is total, absolute, unqualified. But for blindness, He does not say "He blinded them." He says "He blinded **their eyes**" — *abṣārahum.* He localizes it. He attaches it to the organ.

Why the asymmetry? Let me offer a reading — and I want to be honest that this is interpretive, an invitation to consider rather than a settled grammatical ruling.

[PAUSE]

In the Quran, *hearing* is the primary channel of guidance. Revelation comes as recitation — you *hear* the Quran; faith enters through the ear. *"So give good news to My servants — those who listen to the word and follow the best of it"* (39:17–18). To be made deaf, then — fully, *them*, the whole self — is to be cut off from the very medium through which guidance travels. It is the most total severance possible: the message can no longer get *in.*

Sight, by contrast, is the faculty of reflection — you *see* the signs in creation, you *look* and you ponder. And so the blindness is fixed to the *abṣār*, the eyes that should have been reading the signs and were not. The signs are still there, blazing across the heavens and the earth. The eyes that should have read them have simply gone dark.

[PAUSE]

And there is a sonic seal on all of this. Listen to *aṣammahum* — *a-ṣam-ma.* That doubled *m*, the lips pressing shut and held. The word *closes* the mouth as you say it, the way deafness closes the ear. The language performs the sealing. And then *aʿmā* — the long, open *ā* that goes nowhere, trails off into nothing, like a gaze that finds no object. The sounds *are* the conditions they name.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means responsiveness is a *use-it-or-lose-it* faculty. Every time the alarm of conscience sounds and you *respond* — you answer the prayer when you hear the call, you soften when the ayah lands, you turn back when you catch yourself drifting — you are keeping the channel open. And every time you hear it and override it, you are turning the volume down by one notch on the only sense that can save you. The terror of verse 23 is not really about *them.* It is a warning to *us*, while the ears still work, while there is still something on the other side of the alarm besides silence.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back, and let me show you how these two verses are built — because the structure *is* the argument.

Watch the single root **ر-ح-م / ق-ط-ع** thread through both verses like a wire pulled taut:

- Verse 22: *tuqaṭṭiʿū* — you **cut** (*q-ṭ-ʿ*) — your **arḥām** (*r-ḥ-m*), the wombs-of-mercy.
- Verse 23: *laʿanahum* — Allah **expelled them from mercy** (*raḥma*, *r-ḥ-m*) — which the hadith renders, in the same breath, as *"I will **cut him off**"* (*q-ṭ-ʿ*).

[PAUSE]

The crime and the consequence are written with the *same two roots.* The verse is not describing a transgression followed by an unrelated penalty. It is describing a single circle. **The cut you make is the cut you receive.** You take the axe to the branch of the Merciful that hangs in your own home — and you find, on the far side of the verse, that you have been severed from the Tree.

That is the proof. Not asserted — *constructed*, in the very roots of the words, so that the law of reciprocity is not something the verse *tells* you. It is something the verse *is.*

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The words showed us: *turning away* (the root that also means *seizing power*) leads to *hacking apart* (the multiplying cut) the *wombs of mercy* (the root of God's own name).

The themes showed us *why*: pull out the One above, and the bonds between people — held up only by Him — collapse; and keep turning away long enough, and you lose the very senses by which you could have turned back.

One sentence reaches it from the grammar; the other reaches it from the heart. They meet in the middle: **to turn from the Source of mercy is, in the end, to be cut off from mercy — and the first thing the cut destroys, and the last thing it destroys, is your own ability to feel it happening.**

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them genuinely open.

1. Is there a tie of kinship in my life that I have been quietly *cutting* — not in one dramatic break, but in the *takthīr* way, a thousand small turnings-away, an unreturned call at a time?

2. When I honor a difficult relationship, what is actually holding me there — the person, or the One above us both? And if it is only the person, how long will it really last?

3. Where in my life have I gone from *turning away* as a choice to *unable to turn back* as a condition? Where has the alarm gone quiet — and is it quiet because the danger passed, or because I stopped being able to hear it?

4. When an ayah lands and I feel the flicker of response — do I answer it, or do I override it? And what am I training my soul to do each time I override?

5. If "curse" means *distance from mercy* — what, today, is increasing the distance, and what could close it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

To turn away from God is to pick up the axe that hacks apart the very ties of mercy you were made to hold — and the hand that cuts the womb of mercy is, by the same stroke, cut off from the Merciful.

### The Closing Invitation

Here is what I would invite to shift after sitting with these words.

Do not hear verse 23 as a verse about *them* — the cursed, the deaf, the blind, safely other. Hear it as a verse about the *direction of a road*, and notice that you are somewhere on that road right now. The whole point of God turning, mid-passage, to ask *"is it perhaps to be expected of **you**?"* is that the question is still open. The ears still work. The eyes still read. The tie is frayed but not yet severed.

So make one repair. Not a hundred. One. The one relationship you have every reason to cut and have been slowly cutting — reach back toward it this week, for no reason except that it hangs from the Throne and you have decided not to be the hand that drops it. That single act of *ṣila*, of *re-joining*, is you turning *toward* instead of *away.* It is you keeping the channel open. It is, in the deepest sense the language allows, you choosing mercy — and being chosen by it in return.

### Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who named Yourself al-Raḥmān and hung the ties of our kinship from that name — do not let us be among those who take the axe to Your mercy in our own homes. Keep our hearing open to Your word and our sight clear to Your signs, and let us be those who join what You commanded to be joined, so that You join us, and never among those who are cut off. Āmīn.*
