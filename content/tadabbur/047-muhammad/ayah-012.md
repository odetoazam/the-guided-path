# The Ones Who Are Admitted and the Ones Who Graze: A Verse of Two Verbs

## Introduction: A Sentence Built Like a Fork in the Road

Let me read it to you first. Slowly. In Arabic, then in plain English.

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُدْخِلُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ جَنَّـٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ ۖ وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ يَتَمَتَّعُونَ وَيَأْكُلُونَ كَمَا تَأْكُلُ ٱلْأَنْعَـٰمُ وَٱلنَّارُ مَثْوًى لَّهُمْ

*Inna-llāha yudkhilu-lladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū-ṣ-ṣāliḥāti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā-l-anhār. Wa-lladhīna kafarū yatamattaʿūna wa yaʾkulūna kamā taʾkulu-l-anʿām, wa-n-nāru mathwan lahum.*

"Indeed, Allah admits those who believe and do righteous deeds into gardens beneath which rivers flow. But those who disbelieve enjoy themselves and eat as the cattle eat — and the Fire will be an abode for them."

Now, you have heard verses like this before. The Quran sets the believers beside those who reject, the garden beside the Fire, over and over. And there is a danger in that familiarity. We read a verse like this and our mind files it under "the usual contrast" and moves on. The intellect nods. The heart stays seated.

So I want us to slow down — because this verse is not built the way you think it is built. The contrast here is not really between *garden* and *Fire*. Watch the verbs. On one side of the verse, **Allah does something to people**. On the other side, **people do something by themselves**. That is the real fault line running down the middle of this ayah. One group is *acted upon* by their Lord. The other group is left to *act for themselves* — and look what they choose to do with that freedom.

Let me set the scene before we go in.

### The World This Verse Arrived Into

This is Surah Muhammad. The early commentators also called it *Sūrat al-Qitāl* — the surah of struggle — because it was revealed in Madinah at a time when the young community faced people who were not merely indifferent to the message but actively working to turn others away from the path of Allah. The surah opens with exactly that phrase: *those who disbelieve and turn others from the way of Allah* — He renders their deeds astray.

So this is not an abstract theological seminar. This is a community under pressure, watching their opponents. And here is the thing about watching your opponents when they seem to be doing well: you start to wonder. They eat well. They are comfortable. They are enjoying their lives, untroubled, while you carry the weight of struggle and sacrifice. The whisper comes: *what exactly are they getting away with?*

And just before our verse, Allah has said something stark: *Allah is the protector (mawlā) of those who believe, and the disbelievers — they have no protector at all.* No mawlā. No one who carries them. Hold onto that word. Because verse 12 is about to show you, in its very grammar, what it looks like to have a Protector — and what it looks like to have none.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to listen for. In this one sentence, Allah is going to *admit* one group somewhere — He is the one who acts — and He is going to let the other group *enjoy and eat* entirely on their own. He gives the believers a verb of being-received. He gives the disbelievers two verbs of self-feeding.

And then — pay attention to this — He compares that second group to something. Not to demons. Not to monsters. To **cattle**. The gentlest, most ordinary comparison imaginable. Grazing animals in a field.

Why is *that* the comparison that lands hardest? Why is being compared to a peaceful, contented, well-fed cow one of the most sobering images in the entire Quran?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Who Is the Subject of the Sentence?

Before we touch the verse, I want to teach you one small thing about how sentences work — and you already know it, you've just never named it.

In any sentence there is a *doer* and there is a *done-to*. "The mother carries the child." The mother is the doer; the child is carried. Now notice something about the child in that sentence: the child is safe precisely *because* he is not the one doing the work. He is being carried. His arrival depends not on his own strength but on the one who holds him.

Hold that picture. Now look at how Allah builds the first half of our verse.

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ **يُدْخِلُ** ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟

*Inna-llāha **yudkhilu** alladhīna āmanū* — "Indeed, Allah **admits** those who believe…"

The verb is **yudkhilu**, from the root *d-kh-l*, "to enter." But notice — it is not in the simple form that would mean "they enter." It is in the **fourth form**, *yudkhilu*, which takes the action and makes it transitive: not *they enter*, but **He brings them in. He causes them to enter. He admits them.**

Who is the doer of this verb? **Allah.** Who are the done-to? The believers. They are the grammatical object. They are the child being carried.

Let's sit with this, because it is easy to rush past. The believers do not *get themselves* into the garden. The verse does not say *they enter gardens.* It says **Allah admits them.** Their entire arrival is in the grip of someone else's verb. They are received. They are brought in. The door is opened *for* them and they are led through it by the One who said, three verses earlier, that He is their *mawlā* — their protector, their patron, the one who handles their affairs.

[PAUSE]

Now — and this is the heart of it — look at what happens in the second half of the verse. Watch the subject change hands.

> وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ **يَتَمَتَّعُونَ وَيَأْكُلُونَ**

*Wa-lladhīna kafarū **yatamattaʿūna wa yaʾkulūna*** — "But those who disbelieve, **they enjoy themselves and they eat**…"

Do you feel the grammar shift under your feet? Suddenly *they* are the doers. **They** enjoy. **They** eat. Allah has stepped out of the verb. On the believers' side, the sentence's main action belonged to Allah — *He admits*. On the disbelievers' side, the main action belongs to *them* — *they enjoy, they eat.*

This is the absence I want you to read as carefully as any presence in the verse. **Allah does not act upon the disbelievers here.** He does not carry them anywhere. He simply... lets them. He releases them to their own devices and stands back. *The disbelievers have no protector*, He had just said. And now you see what that looks like in a sentence: it looks like a group of people left entirely to their own verbs.

We tend to imagine that being "left alone" by God, free of His interference, sounds like freedom. The verse quietly shows you it is the opposite of safety. The believer is the carried child. The disbeliever is the one no one is carrying — free, yes, and utterly on his own.

What this single grammatical asymmetry reveals: **the deepest gift in the verse is not the garden. It is being the object of Allah's verb instead of the subject of your own.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Verb That Grabs at Itself — *yatamattaʿūna*

Now let's open that first verb the disbelievers were left with: **yatamattaʿūna**.

Let me teach you the form first, because the form is the meaning here.

In Arabic, there is a family of verbs we call the **fifth form** — *tafaʿʿala*. Its signature is a doubled middle letter and a *ta-* on the front, and one of its core jobs is to make a verb **reflexive and self-directed** — the doer does the action *to and for himself*, often with effort, often with a kind of grasping. Take the root for "knowing," *ʿ-l-m*. The fifth form, *taʿallama*, doesn't just mean "to know" — it means **to make oneself know, to actively take knowledge for oneself.** The form turns an action into something you reach out and seize *for yourself*.

Now the root here is *m-t-ʿ*. On its own, this root gives us *matāʿ* — and *matāʿ* in the Quran's vocabulary is a very specific kind of thing. It is **provision that passes**. Goods you carry on a journey. The pots and bags and supplies you use up and leave behind. *Matāʿ* is, by its very nature, **temporary**. The Quran elsewhere calls this whole world *matāʿ al-ghurūr* — the provision of delusion, the enjoyment that deceives precisely because it does not last.

So now put the form together with the root. *Yatamattaʿūna* is the fifth form of *the root of passing-enjoyment*. It means: **they make themselves enjoy, they actively grab for themselves, they grasp at the fleeting provision** — with the whole weight of the self thrown into the grabbing.

Notice something. The form tells you the energy is enormous — this is a reaching, seizing, self-directed effort. The root tells you the object is nothing — *matāʿ*, supplies that are used up and gone. **Maximum grasping for minimum substance.** The grammar itself is the tragedy: all that reaching, aimed at something already evaporating in the hand.

Here's what's remarkable. The classical commentators caught a precise gradation in this. Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both preserve a beautiful saying: **"The believer in this world provisions himself (*yatazawwad*), the hypocrite adorns himself (*yatazayyan*), and the disbeliever enjoys himself (*yatamattaʿ*)."** Three men, three verbs, in the same marketplace of the world.

Sit with that for a moment. The believer is in this world *taking provisions* — *zād* is what you pack for a journey you intend to complete; he treats the world as a road. The hypocrite is *decorating himself* — performing, keeping up appearances for an audience. And the disbeliever is simply *enjoying* — *yatamattaʿ* — taking the passing thing as if it were the destination itself. Same food. Same wealth. Same pleasures. **Three completely different relationships to them.** The difference is never in the object. It is always in the horizon behind the eyes.

What this one verb reveals: **the disbeliever's tragedy is not that he enjoys the world. It is that he throws the entire force of his self into grasping a thing that was already designed to pass through his fingers.**

### Element Three: "As the Cattle Eat" — and the Word With No Object

Now we come to the comparison that should stop us. And to do it justice, I need you to notice something that is *missing* — an absence again.

> وَيَأْكُلُونَ كَمَا تَأْكُلُ ٱلْأَنْعَـٰمُ

*Wa yaʾkulūna kamā taʾkulu-l-anʿām* — "and they eat as the cattle eat."

Here is the absence. When Allah says **they eat** — *yaʾkulūna* — He does not tell you *what* they eat. The verb is left without an object. In Arabic, a verb left deliberately without its object opens up and becomes *general* — it stops being about a particular meal and becomes about *the act of eating as such, eating for its own sake, eating with nothing beyond the eating.* The omission is the point. There is no *purpose* attached to the eating because, for this kind of life, there *is* no purpose attached to the eating. Just the mouth, and the food, and the next mouthful.

And then the comparison: *kamā taʾkulu-l-anʿām* — "as the cattle eat." The word **anʿām** is the root *n-ʿ-m*, and these are specifically the **grazing livestock** — sheep, cattle, camels. The peaceful animals in the field, heads down, untroubled, contentedly working through the grass.

Now — why is *this* the most devastating comparison Allah could have chosen?

Let's think about what a grazing animal actually is. A cow in a green field is not evil. It is not malicious. It is, in fact, the very picture of contentment. It has everything it needs: grass, water, sun, safety. It eats, it is satisfied, it eats again. And here is the entire point: **the cow has no tomorrow.** It does not graze with any thought of where the field ends. It does not look up. It does not ask who made the grass, or who owns the pasture, or what the farmer is fattening it *for*. It is perfectly, peacefully, completely absorbed in the next mouthful. Its horizon is the length of its own neck.

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why this comparison cuts deeper than any monster could? Allah is not saying the disbelievers are *wicked* like beasts. He is saying they are **content** like beasts. Satisfied. Heads down. Untroubled. The whole tragedy is in how *peaceful* it looks. As al-Jalalayn and the early commentators put it with painful directness: such a person has no concern beyond his stomach and his appetites — *lāhūn sāhūn ʿammā fī ghad* — heedless, oblivious to what waits in tomorrow. The animal cannot be blamed for having no horizon beyond the grass. **The human can.** That is the entire weight of the comparison: you were given a neck that could lift, eyes that could look up, a mind that could ask *what is the field for* — and you chose to graze.

And now the last word. After all that enjoying, all that grazing, the verse lands:

> وَٱلنَّارُ مَثْوًى لَّهُمْ

*Wa-n-nāru **mathwan** lahum* — "and the Fire will be an **abode** for them."

The word is **mathwā**, from the root *th-w-y*, and it does not mean merely "a place." *Mathwā* is **the place where you settle down and stay. A dwelling. A residence. Where you take up permanent lodging.** The same root gives the word for the host's home where a traveler *settles in* for the night.

Now hold the whole verse in your hand and feel the bitter symmetry. They spent their lives in *tamattuʿ* — grasping at *matāʿ*, the provision that **passes**. And their final address is *mathwā* — the dwelling where you **settle and remain.** Listen to the two words sitting beside each other: *yatamattaʿūn... mathwā.* Almost the same sounds in the mouth — *mat-... math-...* The thing they treated as permanent (this world's enjoyment) was the thing that passed. And the thing they never thought about (the Fire) is the thing that became permanent. **They confused the temporary for the home and the home for the temporary** — and the verse encodes that exact inversion in two words that nearly rhyme.

What these final words reveal: **the grazing life is not punished by having its pleasures taken away in this world. It is allowed to graze to the end — and then it discovers that the only home it ever actually built for itself was the Fire.**

### The Bridge

So look at what the grammar has shown us. One verb where **Allah is the doer** and the believers are carried in — *yudkhilu*. Two verbs where the disbelievers are **left to their own devices** — *yatamattaʿūna, yaʾkulūna* — one of them a self-grasping reach for what passes, the other an eating with no object and no horizon, *like the cattle eat*. And a final word — *mathwā* — that quietly reveals which of the two ways of living was actually building a permanent home, and which was just grazing toward one.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how *we* are living. Because you and I also eat. You and I also enjoy. The question this verse forces is not *are you eating* — it is *which verb is your life conjugated in?*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Hedonic Treadmill, and the Field With No Fence

Let me start where you already live.

There is a thing psychologists named decades ago, and they called it the **hedonic treadmill**. The finding is simple and a little frightening. You get the raise. You get the new car, the bigger house, the long-awaited thing. And there is a spike of joy — real joy. And then, within weeks, sometimes days, your baseline resets. The new thing becomes the normal thing. And you find yourself reaching, again, for the *next* thing, certain *that* one will finally do it. Researchers found people who won lotteries returned, within a year, to roughly the happiness they'd had before. The treadmill keeps moving. You keep walking. You never actually arrive.

Now here is what I want you to notice. We usually diagnose this as a problem of *quantity* — "I just haven't reached the amount that will satisfy me yet." But the Quran is making a far more radical diagnosis. The treadmill is not a quantity problem. **It is a horizon problem.** *Yatamattaʿūna wa yaʾkulūna kamā taʾkulu-l-anʿām.* The grazing animal is *also* on a treadmill — one mouthful, then the next, then the next, forever, with no destination. And it never bothers the cow, because the cow was never built to arrive anywhere. It was built to graze.

[PAUSE]

So here is the uncomfortable question the verse plants in us: **what if the restlessness you feel — the way nothing stays satisfying — is not a malfunction? What if it is the one piece of equipment the cow doesn't have, going off like an alarm?**

Think about it. The animal is content in the field because the field is genuinely enough for an animal. If *you* are not content in the field — if the raise wears off, if the acquisition goes flat, if a quiet voice keeps asking *is this it?* — that is not a defect in you. **That is the part of you that is not a cow.** That is the horizon the animal doesn't have, registering that you were made for somewhere the field cannot contain. The dissatisfaction is a compass needle. The tragedy of the life this verse describes is that the person *silences the needle* — treats the restlessness as just one more appetite to feed, grabs the next thing, eats the next mouthful, heads down, *kamā taʾkulu-l-anʿām*.

The believer is not someone who refuses to eat. Remember the three-verb saying: the believer in this world *takes provisions* — *yatazawwad*. He eats the same food off the same table. But he eats it the way you eat a meal at a rest stop on a long drive: gratefully, fully, and **without mistaking the rest stop for home.** His head comes up. He sees the fence at the edge of the field, and the road beyond it, and he knows where the road goes.

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the specific practice. The next time you get the thing you've been reaching for — and the joy spikes and then, days later, begins to fade — do not interpret the fading as a signal to *reach again*. **Interpret it as the verse speaking.** Let the fading remind you: *this was matāʿ. It was always going to fade. It is supposed to fade. It is pointing past itself.* Take the enjoyment, say *alḥamdulillāh*, and let it lift your head toward the One who admits people into a garden that does *not* fade. Make the very fleetingness of pleasure into a form of dhikr. That is the difference between eating like the believer and grazing like the cattle — and it costs you nothing but the direction of your gaze.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Difference Between Being Welcomed and Squatting

Let me start again from inside ordinary life.

There is a profound difference between two ways of being in a house. In the first, someone *brings you in*. They open the door, they say *come in, this is yours, stay.* You are a guest who has been made family. Your belonging does not depend on you defending it, because someone with authority over the house has *granted* it to you. You can relax. You are held.

In the second, you are in a house *that no one invited you into.* Maybe it's empty, maybe it's grand, maybe you've made yourself quite comfortable in it. You eat, you sleep, you enjoy the rooms. But underneath the comfort there is a thing you can never quite put down: **no one welcomed you, and no one will defend your right to stay.** You are not held. You are only, so far, undisturbed.

Now go back to the grammar of our verse, because this is exactly the distinction it drew. *Allah **admits** those who believe* — *yudkhilu* — He brings them in. They are the welcomed guests, made family, *carried* through the door. And just before, He told us why: He is their *mawlā*, their protector, the One who handles their affairs. **They are held.**

And the disbelievers? *They enjoy, they eat.* Self-directed verbs. No one brings them anywhere. *The disbelievers have no protector* — no *mawlā*, no one whose job it is to bring them in and defend their place. They are in the house of the world, eating well, undisturbed — for now. **But no one welcomed them, and no one is holding them.** And the verse tells you exactly how that arrangement ends: the only dwelling that finally has their name on it — the only *mathwā* — is the Fire.

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox I want you to sit with and *not* rush to resolve. From the outside, in this life, the welcomed guest and the undisturbed squatter can look identical. Both are eating. Both are in the house. Both, on a given evening, may be perfectly comfortable. You cannot tell them apart by their meal. This is precisely what troubled the believers of Madinah watching their well-fed opponents, and it is what troubles us watching the comfortable lives of those who want nothing to do with God. *They seem fine. Better than fine.*

The verse does not deny that they're eating well. **It refuses to let you measure a life by its meal.** It tells you to ask a different question entirely — not *how good is the food*, but *whose verb are you living inside?* Are you the object of *Allah admits* — carried, welcomed, held? Or are you the subject of *they enjoy* — free, comfortable, and utterly on your own?

And notice the deep mercy hiding in this, even toward the second group. Allah does not snatch the food away. He lets them eat. He lets them enjoy. The door of the world stays open to them every single day — which means the door of *return* stays open too. *Yatamattaʿūna* is in the present tense, ongoing, **still happening.** As long as a person is still in the field, still eating, the invitation to lift his head and be *brought in* has not closed. The Fire is named as a *mathwā* — a destination — not as a present cage. The grazing can still become a journey. The squatter can still be made a guest. **Up until the last mouthful.**

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow? Here is the practice. Stop auditing your life by its comforts — that meter is broken; the cattle score perfectly on it. Audit it by its *grammar.* Once a day, ask yourself a single question: *In the thing I just did — the meal, the work, the pleasure, the rest — was I lifting my head toward the One who admits people into gardens, or was I grazing with my head down?* You don't have to give up the field. You have to keep lifting your head. Because the entire difference between the two destinies in this verse is not the eating. **It is the looking up.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this verse mirror each other, now that we've done the work.

| The believers | The disbelievers |
|---|---|
| **Allah admits** them (*yudkhilu*) — He is the doer | **They enjoy, they eat** — they are the doers |
| Carried, welcomed, held by their *mawlā* | Left alone — *no protector* |
| Into **gardens** beneath which rivers flow | Eating **as the cattle eat** — no object, no horizon |
| A home that is given and that lasts | A *mathwā* — the only dwelling they built: the Fire |

And here is the single insight, seen from both directions. The **linguistic** discovery was that Allah is the verb on one side and absent from the verb on the other — one group is carried, one group is left. The **thematic** discovery was that you cannot tell these two lives apart by their pleasures, only by their horizon — whether the head is lifted or lowered. **These are the same truth.** To be carried by Allah *is* to live with your head lifted toward Him; to be left to your own verbs *is* to graze with your head down. The grammar and the life are one thing. *Yudkhilu* is a lifted head. *Kamā taʾkulu-l-anʿām* is a lowered one.

### Questions to Carry

1. The restlessness you feel when a pleasure fades — have you been treating it as a hunger to feed, or as an alarm telling you that you were made for more than the field?
2. If you measured your life this week not by how good the meals were, but by *whose verb* you were living inside — what would you find?
3. The grazing animal is content because its horizon is the length of its neck. How long is yours? What is the farthest thing you actually thought about today?
4. They chased *matāʿ* — the thing that passes — and it became their *mathwā* — the thing that stays. Where in your life are you treating something temporary as if it were home?
5. Allah did not snatch the food from the disbelievers; He let them keep eating, the door still open. What would it mean to take *that* mercy seriously — that as long as you're still in the field, you can still be brought in?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The believer is *carried* by Allah into a home that lasts; the disbeliever, left to his own appetites, grazes contentedly on a pleasure designed to pass — and discovers too late that the only dwelling he ever built for himself was the Fire.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to leave the field. You were put in the field on purpose; the food is real and the enjoyment is a gift. The invitation of this verse is smaller and harder than renunciation. It is simply this: **lift your head.** Eat like someone provisioning for a journey, not like someone who has mistaken the rest stop for home. Let every pleasure that fades remind you it was pointing somewhere. And keep your life in the grammar of *being admitted* — held, carried, welcomed — rather than the grammar of grazing alone. The door is still open. The eating is still happening. The head can still come up.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who *admit* the believers into gardens — make us of those You bring in, carried and welcomed, and not of those left to graze alone. Let every fleeting pleasure lift our heads toward You instead of bending them toward the grass. And let our final dwelling — our *mathwā* — be a garden of Your mercy, never the Fire. Āmīn.
