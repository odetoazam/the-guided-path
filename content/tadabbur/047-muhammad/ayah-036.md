# The Life That Is Only a Game — and the God Who Gives Without Taking

## Surah Muhammad · Ayah 36

> [ayah:47:36] إِنَّمَا ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَا لَعِبٌ وَلَهْوٌ ۚ وَإِن تُؤْمِنُوا۟ وَتَتَّقُوا۟ يُؤْتِكُمْ أُجُورَكُمْ وَلَا يَسْـَٔلْكُمْ أَمْوَٰلَكُمْ

> *Innamā l-ḥayātu d-dunyā laʿibun wa lahwun, wa in tuʾminū wa tattaqū yuʾtikum ujūrakum wa lā yasʾalkum amwālakum.*

> "The life of this world is only play and amusement. And if you believe and are mindful of Allah, He will give you your rewards and will not ask you for your wealth."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Words

Let me put you where the first listeners were standing.

This is Madinah. The community is young, and it is at war — not a metaphorical war, but a real one, with real costs. Surah Muhammad is sometimes called *Sūrat al-Qitāl*, the Surah of Fighting. Its verses move through the heat of battle: striking necks, binding captives, the testing of believers, the unmasking of those who say "we believe" with their tongues while something hardens in their chest.

And then, as the surah comes toward its close, the battlefield goes quiet — and a different battlefield opens. A subtler one. The one inside the ribcage.

Because here is what Allah knows about this community, and about us: a person can be willing to give his *body* to a cause and still flinch when asked to give his *wealth*. A man will march toward swords and then grip his coins tighter. The next two verses after ours will say it plainly — that if Allah were to demand all your wealth and press you hard for it, *you would withhold, and the rancor buried in your hearts would surface* (47:37). That when you are *invited to spend in the way of Allah, some of you are stingy* (47:38).

So before Allah speaks about wealth at all, He does something a master physician does before the painful part of the treatment. He resets your sense of what things are worth. He takes the thing you are clutching — this life, these holdings, this accumulation — and He names it.

[PAUSE]

Thirteen words. A diagnosis, a condition, and a promise so generous it should stop us in our tracks. The life you are gripping is *only play and amusement.* And the One who is asking you to loosen your grip — listen to who He turns out to be.

Here is the hook. Every relationship you have ever entered has asked something of you. Every transaction has a price. Every "yes" has a cost written somewhere in the fine print. We have come to assume that whoever gives must also take — that there is no such thing as a gift without an invoice trailing behind it.

This ayah describes the single relationship in all of existence that breaks that rule. And the way the Arabic breaks it — the precise asymmetry of two verbs — is something we could walk past a thousand times and never see.

Let's not walk past it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Laʿib wa Lahw* — Two Different Ways to Waste a Life

Before we touch the ayah, let me hand you a distinction you already live by, even if you've never named it.

There are two ways to lose an afternoon.

The first: you do something with no point to it. You shuffle a deck of cards alone. You move objects from one pile to another. There's no aim, nothing is built, nothing is gained — but at least you *know* it's nothing. It's empty, and the emptiness is honest.

The second is more dangerous. You do something that *pulls you in*. You meant to glance at your phone for thirty seconds and you surface two hours later, blinking, with no memory of the time passing, and a vague feeling that something was taken from you while you weren't looking. This one isn't merely empty. It's *captivating*. It doesn't just waste your time — it makes you *forget* there was anything else you were supposed to be doing.

Hold both of those. Arabic has a word for each.

Now look at what Allah says this life is:

> **لَعِبٌ وَلَهْوٌ** — *laʿibun wa lahwun*

Two nouns. Notice first that both carry *tanwīn* — both are indefinite: "*a* play and *an* amusement." Not "the play," not a specific game. Just... play, of any kind, all of it. The indefiniteness widens the net to catch everything.

But the real treasure is the difference between the two words, and the classical lexicographers preserved it with care.

**لَعِب (laʿib)** is purposeless play. Ar-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* connects its very letters to the play of a small child — activity pursued with no rational end. It is the deck of cards alone. It is motion without destination. It is *empty* — and at least it is honestly empty.

**لَهْو (lahw)** is something else. Its root carries the meaning of *that which diverts you, distracts you, pulls your attention away from what actually concerns you.* The verb *alhā* — to make someone heedless, to occupy them so they forget. Lahw is not merely empty. **Lahw is the thing that makes you forget.** It is the two hours that vanished. It is the diversion that, while it entertains you, quietly relocates your attention away from the one thing that mattered.

[PAUSE]

So sit with the order Allah chose. *Laʿib*, then *lahw.* Play, then distraction.

This life is first *empty* — and then, worse, *captivating in its emptiness.* It is not only that the dunya gives you nothing of lasting weight. It is that, while giving you nothing, it absorbs you so completely that you forget you were ever meant to be reaching for something else.

Here's what's remarkable: this is the most precise description of the modern condition I know of, and it was given fourteen centuries before the first screen lit up. We did not invent distraction. We only industrialized it. We built entire economies — staffed by some of the most brilliant minds of our generation — devoted to the manufacture of *lahw*: experiences engineered to be empty enough to require nothing of you and captivating enough that you cannot put them down. Allah named the disease before the symptom had a delivery system.

And notice — He does not say the dunya *contains* play and amusement. He says, with the restriction particle **إِنَّمَا** (*innamā*, "only," "nothing but"), that the life of this world, *taken as an end in itself*, **is** play and amusement. The scholars are careful here, and so should we be: this is the dunya *pursued for its own sake* — al-Jalālayn glosses it as "the *preoccupation* with it." The same wealth, the same work, the same world, turned toward Allah, becomes something else entirely. We'll arrive there. For now, hold the diagnosis: pursued as a destination, this life is *laʿib wa lahw* — empty, and then captivating in its emptiness.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Al-Dunyā* — The Name That Admits There Is Somewhere Higher

We've been translating one word as "the world" this whole time, and that translation has been quietly lying to you.

Look at it: **ٱلدُّنْيَا** — *al-dunyā.*

In English, "the world" sounds like a noun. A thing. A place with a name. But *al-dunyā* is not a noun at all in its origin. Its root is **د-ن-و (d-n-w)**, which means *to be near, to be close, to be low.* And the form of the word — *dunyā* — is a **comparative**, a superlative-feeling form. It does not mean "the world." It means **"the nearer one." "The lower one." "The closer one."**

Let that register. Every single time the Quran calls this life *al-ḥayāt ad-dunyā*, it is not naming it "worldly life." It is calling it **"the nearer life."**

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing about the word "nearer."

You cannot say "nearer" about something unless there is a *farther.* The word is comparative — it has no meaning standing alone. "Taller" requires someone shorter. "Closer" requires something more distant. The instant you call this life *the nearer one*, you have, in the very act of naming it, **confessed that there is another one — higher, farther, and by implication, greater.**

This is the genius hidden in the vocabulary. Allah did not need to argue for the existence of the ākhirah in this ayah. He encoded it into the *name of this life.* You cannot even *refer* to the dunya, in the Arabic the Quran uses, without your own tongue admitting that it is only the *first* of two. The grammar will not let you forget the second.

So when this ayah says *innamā l-ḥayātu d-dunyā laʿibun wa lahwun* — "the **nearer** life is only play and amusement" — the word *dunyā* is already doing half the work. It isn't condemning life as such. It is telling you that the thing you've mistaken for the whole story is merely the *near* end of something that stretches much, much farther. The play is only a problem when you think the playground is all there is.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: He *Gives* You Yours — and Does Not *Ask* for Yours

Now we come to the two verbs that hold the heart of this ayah, and I want to slow all the way down.

A relationship, in our experience, runs on exchange. I give you something; I expect something back. Even love, in its lesser forms, keeps a ledger. The deepest assumption we carry — so deep we rarely see it — is that *anyone who gives to me will, sooner or later, ask of me.* Generosity is a loan. Kindness has terms.

Now read the second half of the ayah with that assumption in your hands:

> **يُؤْتِكُمْ أُجُورَكُمْ وَلَا يَسْـَٔلْكُمْ أَمْوَٰلَكُمْ**
>
> "He will **give** you your rewards, and will **not ask** you for your wealth."

Two verbs, set against each other with deliberate symmetry. Let me open them.

**يُؤْتِكُمْ** — *yuʾtikum*, "He gives **to you**." This is Form IV of the root **أ-ت-ي (Aty)** — the transitive, bestowing form: to bring something *to* someone, to hand it *over*. Allah is the one extending. The motion is from Him, toward you.

**يَسْـَٔلْكُمْ** — *yasʾalkum*, "He asks **of you**" — here negated: *wa lā yasʾalkum*, "He does **not** ask of you." This is the root **س-أ-ل (sAl)**, to request, to ask for. The motion here would run the other way — from you, toward Him.

So watch the two arrows. One verb is Allah *extending toward you.* The other verb is the demand *coming back from you* — and Allah cancels it. **لَا** — no. That arrow does not get drawn.

[PAUSE]

But the precision goes deeper, and it lives in two words you might read right past: **أُجُورَكُمْ** and **أَمْوَٰلَكُمْ.** Both end in **ـكُمْ** — "your." *Your* rewards. *Your* wealth.

Look at what He calls the reward: not "a reward," not "My reward to grant," but **ujūra-kum — your rewards.** Before you have even earned them, He speaks of them as already belonging to you. He gives you what He has already named as *yours.*

And look at what He declines to take: not "wealth," not "a contribution," but **amwāla-kum — your wealth.** He does not ask you to surrender what is yours.

So assemble the whole picture. **He gives you what is already yours, and He does not ask for what is yours.** In this transaction, *nothing of yours ever leaves your possession against you.* The reward is yours and He hands it over. The wealth is yours and He lets you keep it. There is no column in this ledger where you come out behind. It is the only arrangement in the universe where both arrows point *toward* the human being, and none point away.

[PAUSE]

Now, the scholars open *lā yasʾalkum amwālakum* — "He does not ask you for your wealth" — from several angles, and like a sculpture seen from different sides, each one shows you the same astonishing form.

Ibn ʿUyaynah and others, as al-Qurṭubī and al-Jalālayn report, read it this way: *He does not ask you for **all** of your wealth.* The obligation — the zakāh, the spending in His path — is only a *portion.* He could have demanded everything. He asks for a fraction.

Al-Baghawī adds another angle: *He does not ask your wealth because He **needs** it* — and he points us to the verse where Allah says of His servants, **"I do not want from them any provision"** (51:57). The asking is never for His sake. He is *al-Ghanī*, the Utterly Free of need.

And al-Qurṭubī gives us the angle that should undo us completely: *He does not ask for Himself or out of any need of His own — He commands you to spend in His path **so that the reward returns to you.*** The "asking," such as it is, is not a withdrawal from your account. It is a *deposit into it.* When He invites your wealth, He is not taking from you; He is giving you a way to make the perishable into the permanent — to convert *amwālakum*, your wealth, into *ujūrakum*, your reward. The two words are not opposites in His economy. They are the same value, passing from a currency that rots into one that lasts.

[PAUSE]

So here is what these three words reveal, when you hold them together: **The One asking you to loosen your grip is the only one who has never once reached into your pocket for His own sake.** Every other hand that has ever extended toward you wanted something back. His extends to *fill* yours.

---

### Bridge

The linguistic construction has shown us three things. That this life is *empty and then captivating in its emptiness* — *laʿib wa lahw.* That its very name, *al-dunyā*, "the nearer," confesses there is a farther and higher life it is only the threshold of. And that the One calling us toward that higher life *gives* us what is ours and *does not take* what is ours.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange, gripping thing the human heart does with what it owns.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why the Empty Thing Is So Hard to Put Down

Start where you actually live.

You know the dunya is *laʿib wa lahw.* You have probably said as much, out loud, to someone. You have nodded along to a khuṭbah about it. At the level of the intellect, the case is closed: this life is temporary, the next is eternal, obvious, agreed.

And yet.

And yet you still refresh the feed. You still feel the pull toward the thing you know is empty. You still — and be honest — feel a small contraction in your chest at the thought of giving away money you don't even need. The mind has signed the contract. The heart hasn't read it.

Why?

Here is what I think the ayah is teaching, in its order. It did not say the dunya is *worthless.* It said the dunya is *laʿib wa lahw* — and remember the second word. **Lahw is the captivating one.** The danger of this life was never that it is empty. We can walk away from empty. The danger is that it is *empty in a way that holds us* — that it diverts and absorbs and makes us *forget.* You are not weak for being pulled by it. You are being pulled by something *engineered, at the deepest level of reality, to pull.* That is what *lahw* means.

[PAUSE]

And notice the cruelty of the trick, now that you see the word *al-dunyā* clearly. The distraction works by *shrinking your sense of scale.* While you are inside the *lahw* — the scroll, the acquisition, the next thing — the *near* life fills your entire field of vision. You forget it is the *near* one. You forget there is a *farther.* The whole spiritual disease can be described as a single error of perception: **mistaking the nearer for the all.** The dunya does not lie to you by claiming to be eternal. It lies by *filling the frame* so completely that the question of eternity never gets asked.

This is why the cure in the ayah is not "despise the world." It is *belief and taqwa* — and watch what those two actually do to your perception.

**تُؤْمِنُوا** — *tuʾminū*, "you believe," Form IV of the root **أ-م-ن**, the same root as *amān*, security, and *amānah*, trust. To believe, in this construction, is to enter a state of *security* — to plant your weight on something that will not move. The one who has *īmān* is *secured* against the panic that drives the grip. Why do we clutch the dunya? Because at some level we are afraid it is all we have. *Īmān* is the removal of that fear. You can open your hand because you finally know there is a farther shore.

[PAUSE]

And here is one more thing about the cure that we should not rush past. What does the ayah promise the one who believes and is mindful? *Yuʾtikum ujūrakum* — "He will give you **your** rewards." The very thing the dunya only *pretends* to give — a payoff, a reward, something *yours* — the ākhirah *actually* gives. The *lahw* of this life dangles the appearance of reward and delivers emptiness. Allah names the reward *yours* and hands it over. So the choice was never between *reward* and *no reward.* It was between a counterfeit reward that captivates and vanishes, and a real one, already stamped with your name.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** This: the next time you feel the pull of the empty-but-captivating thing, do not fight it with willpower and lose. Name it. Say the word: *this is lahw — it is built to make me forget.* Naming it breaks its only power, which was to operate while you weren't looking. And then widen the frame back out. Remind yourself that the thing filling your whole vision is the *near* one — *al-dunyā* — and there is a *farther* with your reward already inscribed in your name. The distraction survives only in the dark. Bring the scale back into the light and it shrinks to what it is: a game.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The God Who Gives and Does Not Take

Now the harder attachment. Not the screen. The wallet.

Every one of us has felt it — that small, involuntary tightening when money is asked of us. It is not even about need; people with far more than enough feel it just as sharply. There is something about *parting with what is ours* that touches a nerve deeper than logic. The next verse in this surah names it without mercy: if Allah pressed you hard for your wealth, *"you would withhold, and He would bring out your raw resentment"* (47:37). The stinginess is not in the wallet. It is in the *heart*, and money is just the probe that finds it.

So begin inside that human experience, honestly. We are, by default, holders. Grippers. We treat what we own as an extension of the self, so that giving it feels like *amputation.*

Now let the ayah illuminate that.

The first mercy is in *what He asks* — which is *almost nothing.* Remember al-Qurṭubī and al-Jalālayn: He does not ask for **all** your wealth. He asks for a *portion.* Sit with how strange that is. The owner of the heavens and the earth, who could call in the entire debt at once, who *gave* you every coin you have ever held — asks back only a sliver, and lets you keep the rest as though it were yours to begin with.

But the second mercy is deeper, and it's hidden in the next verse, so let me draw it out. *Why* does He ask for only a little? Al-Muyassar, following the flow into 47:37, gives the reason: *because if He asked for it all and pressed you, you would turn stingy and refuse — and the rancor in your hearts would be exposed.* 

[PAUSE]

Do you see what that means? **The partial demand is not just lighter on your wallet. It is a veil over your weakness.** Allah, knowing exactly how tightly you grip, asks for an amount small enough that you *can* give it — small enough that your stinginess never has to surface and shame you. He calibrates the test to a level you can pass. He protects you not only from the loss of wealth, but from the *exposure of your own smallness.* The mercy is not merely that He asks little. It is that He asks little *so that you can succeed* — so that the ugliness He can see in the heart need never become something you have to see in yourself.

That is not a creditor. That is not even a generous king. That is the gentleness of one who loves you and does not want you humiliated.

[PAUSE]

And now lay the two verbs back down beside this: *yuʾtikum* — He gives — and *lā yasʾalkum* — He does not ask. We said the arrows both point toward the human being. Here is why that should reorganize your entire relationship with giving.

When the call comes to spend in Allah's path — the zakāh, the ṣadaqah, the cause that needs your wealth — you have been hearing it, all your life, as an *outward arrow.* Something leaving you. A subtraction. And so the heart tightens, because that is what hearts do at the edge of a loss.

But the ayah has just told you the truth of the transaction. He does not *take.* The wealth He invites is recategorized the moment it leaves your hand — from *amwālakum*, your perishable wealth, into *ujūrakum*, your imperishable reward, *already named yours.* Al-Qurṭubī said it cleanly: He commands the spending *so that the reward returns to you.* You are not being asked to *lose* anything. You are being handed the one mechanism in existence for taking the thing that will rot in the ground when you die and converting it into something that meets you on the far shore. The outward arrow was an illusion. Every arrow in this ayah points *home.*

**What does this change about tomorrow?** This: the next time you are asked to give — and you feel that old tightening — pause and re-read the direction of the arrow. You are not at a checkout. You are at an *exchange counter,* trading a currency that decays for one that doesn't, and the One running the counter has already proven He never pockets a cent for Himself. Give the portion. Not because it is demanded of you, but because it is the single most self-interested thing you will ever do — converting *yours-that-perishes* into *yours-that-lasts.* And give it knowing He kept the demand small precisely so your hand *could* open. He set you up to win.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Step back and see the shape of these thirteen words, because the structure is the argument.

The ayah has two halves, and they are a *diagnosis* followed by a *cure*, perfectly matched.

The first half names the disease: *innamā l-ḥayātu d-dunyā laʿibun wa lahwun* — the nearer life, pursued as an end, is empty and then captivating in its emptiness. It is a misvaluation. You have priced the playground as though it were the whole world.

The second half administers the cure — and notice it is structured as a *conditional* (the particle **وَإِن**, "and if"): *if* you believe and are mindful, *then* two things follow — He gives you your reward, and He does not ask for your wealth.

And here is the architecture's quiet proof: the disease was *over-valuing what you own.* The cure ends precisely there — *He will not ask you for your wealth.* The ayah opens by telling you the thing you grip is a game, and closes by telling you that the One asking you to set it down won't even take it from you. **The beginning loosens the grip; the ending shows you that the hand you feared was reaching for your wealth was, the entire time, reaching out to fill it.** The first half empties your hands of an illusion. The second half fills them with something real.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

*Laʿib wa lahw* and *al-dunyā* taught us a problem of *perception*: we mistake the near, empty, captivating thing for the whole of reality, and we grip it out of fear that it's all we have.

*Yuʾtikum* and *lā yasʾalkum* taught us the *resolution*: the fear was unfounded. There is a farther life, your reward is already named yours within it, and the God who governs it gives without taking. Once you *see* that — once *īmān* secures you against the fear — your hand opens on its own. You stop gripping the game, not because you forced yourself to, but because you finally saw it was only a game, and saw who was waiting beyond it.

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life right now is *laʿib* — honestly empty — and what is *lahw* — empty *and* captivating, quietly making me forget what I was reaching for?
2. When money is asked of me, which arrow do I feel — *loss leaving me*, or *reward returning to me*? What would have to change in my heart for the second to feel as real as the first?
3. The word *al-dunyā* means *the nearer.* When did I last live as though there were a *farther*? What would today look like if that second life were as vivid to me as this one?
4. He asks only a portion — *so that I can succeed.* Where in my life has Allah's mercy looked like a demand that was kept *small enough for me to meet*?
5. If nothing of mine ever truly leaves me against me — if even what I give becomes *mine, made permanent* — what am I actually still afraid of losing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The life you are gripping is only the near edge of a game — and the One asking you to loosen your hold is the very One who gives you what is already yours and reaches for nothing of yours in return.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this ayah is an order barked at you. It is a reframe offered to you — a quiet adjustment of the lens, after which the grip relaxes on its own. You don't have to *force* yourself to despise the world; you only have to see it for what it is — *near, empty, captivating* — and see the One beyond it for who He is — *the Giver who does not take.* Let that be the shift. Not "I must hold this life more loosely," but "I finally see why there was never anything here worth holding so tightly, and Who has been waiting, hands open, the whole time."

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who give us what is already ours and ask back nothing for Yourself — loosen our grip on the near and empty thing before it makes us forget the far and lasting one. Secure our hearts with such certainty in You that our hands open without fear, and let every portion we give in Your path return to us, made permanent, on the day we meet You. Āmīn.*
