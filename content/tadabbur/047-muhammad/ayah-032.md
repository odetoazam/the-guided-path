# The Cleaving That Splits Only Yourself

## Surah Muhammad, Ayah 32

---

### The words before us

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَصَدُّوا۟ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ وَشَآقُّوا۟ ٱلرَّسُولَ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ ٱلْهُدَىٰ لَن يَضُرُّوا۟ ٱللَّهَ شَيْـًٔا وَسَيُحْبِطُ أَعْمَـٰلَهُمْ

*Inna lladhīna kafarū wa-ṣaddū ʿan sabīli-llāhi wa-shāqqū r-rasūla min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahumu l-hudā lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan wa-sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum.*

**"Indeed, those who disbelieved, and turned others away from the way of Allah, and split themselves off in opposition to the Messenger — after the guidance had made itself clear to them — never will they harm Allah in anything. And He will make their deeds collapse into nothing."**

---

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this verse, you have to stand in Madinah in the years when the community was small, surrounded, and watched.

This is a Madinan surah, and you can hear the climate in every line of it. The surah opens with a contrast that runs all the way through: those who disbelieve and *bar* from the path of Allah, and those who believe and do righteous deeds. It speaks of striking necks in battle, of testing one believer against another, of those whose hearts carry a sickness they cannot hide forever. And just a few verses before our ayah, it describes a particular kind of person — someone who *turned back on his heels after the guidance had become clear to him.* Not a stranger who never heard. Someone who heard, who saw, who understood — and then turned.

So this is not a verse about the distant unbeliever in some far land who never encountered the message. The whole surah has been building a specific portrait: the person who was *near.* The one to whom the truth came close enough to touch. The one for whom the fog actually lifted.

And here is the emotional pressure the original listener felt — and this is the thing we have to recover, because we live in comfort and they did not. When you are a small, embattled community, and you watch powerful people set themselves *against* your Prophet, set themselves against the path, pour their wealth and their plotting into stopping it — a fear creeps in. *Can they win? Can they actually damage this? Can all that force, all that wealth, all that cleverness, harm what we love?*

This verse answers that fear. But notice — it does not answer it the way you'd expect. It does not say "Allah will protect the believers." It says something far stranger, far larger.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Three different words in this verse describe what these people *do* — they disbelieve, they bar, they oppose. Three active, forceful, energetic verbs. People expending enormous effort. And then the verse turns and tells us the total sum of all that energy, all that motion, all that opposition.

The sum is **nothing.** Not "a little." Not "some, but Allah will outweigh it." *Nothing at all.* `lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan.*

And then — and this is where it stops being only comfort and becomes something that should make every one of us go quiet — the verse turns the harm around. It tells us *whose* deeds collapse. And it uses a word for that collapse that does not mean "erased." It means something much more unsettling. It means something *bloated.*

Stay with me. We are going to walk into three words slowly, and then we are going to ask what they mean for a life — not theirs. Ours.

## PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### First, a small lesson before the first word

In English, when we want to make a verb stronger or change its flavor, we add words around it. "He spoke" becomes "he spoke *back*," "he spoke *against*." We bolt on prepositions.

Arabic does something more beautiful. It changes the *shape* of the verb itself. The same three root letters can be poured into different molds, and each mold carries a meaning. One mold means "to do." Another means "to make someone else do." Another means "to do something *to and against another party* — mutually, in a back-and-forth."

That last mold — grammarians call it Form III, the *mufāʿala* pattern — is the mold of *confrontation between two sides.* You don't use it for something you do alone. You use it for *qātala* (to fight one another), *jādala* (to argue back and forth), *sābaqa* (to race against). It always implies a *facing-off.* Two parties. Two sides. A contest.

Hold that idea — *two sides facing off* — because the verse is about to use it, and it is going to use it with a root whose literal meaning makes the whole thing detonate.

---

### The first word: وَشَآقُّوا — *and they split themselves off in opposition*

> وَشَآقُّوا ٱلرَّسُولَ

The verb is **shāqqū**, from the root **shīn-qāf-qāf** (*sh-q-q*). And before it ever meant "to oppose," this root meant something physical, something you can see with your eyes.

*Shaqq* is **a split. A crack. A cleaving in two.** When the earth cracks open, that's *shaqq.* When a garment tears down the seam, that's *shaqq.* A *shiqq* is *one half of a thing that has been split* — one side of a divided whole. The crack down the middle of a stone. The fissure in dry ground.

Let's sit with this, because the choice of word is doing something the translation cannot carry. The verse does not say they "disagreed with" the Messenger. It does not say they "rejected" him. It says — using that Form III mold of two-sides-facing-off, built on the root of *cleaving* — that they **made themselves into the opposite half.** They took the whole — the single body of truth and the one called to it — and they cracked it, and they planted themselves on the far side of the crack. *You are one piece; I will be the other piece. You are one side of the fracture; I make myself the other.*

Notice something. To do that, you have to *first acknowledge there is a whole to break from.* You cannot stand on the opposite *shiqq* of something you do not recognize is there. The very word admits the truth it resists. The one who cleaves himself off from the Messenger has, in the act of cleaving, conceded that the Messenger is the center, the trunk, the thing worth breaking from.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is remarkable, and it only appears when you put the literal root next to the rest of the verse. They *split.* They cracked the unity and set themselves as the opposing half. That is an act of enormous self-importance — *I am a side. I am a faction. I am a counterweight.*

And the verse responds to that self-importance four words later: `lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan` — **they will not harm Allah in anything.**

Do you feel the deflation? You thought you split the world into two sides — your side and His. But there are not two sides. There was only ever one reality, and a man who imagined himself a wall against the ocean. You cannot cleave yourself off from the One who holds the whole. You can crack *yourself* off. You cannot crack *Him.* The split you made runs through *you,* not through Him. **The fracture line you drew to divide the world from God — it only ever divided you from yourself.**

This is what this single word reveals: opposition to truth is not the building of a rival kingdom. It is the breaking off of a fragment. And the fragment that breaks from the living tree does not weaken the tree. It only stops living.

[PAUSE]

---

### The aggravating clause: مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ ٱلْهُدَىٰ — *after the guidance had made itself clear to them*

Before we leave the splitting, we have to notice the wound that makes it severe.

> مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا تَبَيَّنَ لَهُمُ ٱلْهُدَىٰ — *after the guidance had become clear to them*

The verb here is **tabayyana**, and again the *shape* matters. This is Form V, the *tafaʿʿala* pattern. One of its core functions is *the thing happening to itself, becoming so by its own unfolding.* *Tabayyana* does not mean "they figured it out" — as if they did the work and squinted until it came into focus. It means **the guidance clarified itself. It became plain. It stepped forward and showed its own face.**

This matters enormously, because it removes every excuse. The verse is not describing people who searched honestly and could not find. It is describing people *to whom* the truth came forward on its own and stood plainly in the open — `tabayyana lahum` — *it made itself clear to them.* The light did not stay hidden waiting to be earned. It rose. It was unmistakable.

And then — *min baʿdi* — **after** that, after the clarity, they cleaved themselves off.

Let's be honest about how rare and how heavy this is. There is a kind of rejection that comes from never having seen. The Qur'an treats that with a different tone entirely. But this is rejection *after sight.* This is the seam in the human soul where the most dangerous thing happens — not ignorance, but **knowing, and turning anyway.** The classical commentators are united here: *ʿalimū annahu nabiyyun bi-l-ḥujaji wa-l-āyāt* — they *knew* he was a prophet, by the proofs and the signs (this is how al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī render it). The tragedy is not in the eyes. It is in the will.

And notice the absence — read the silence here as carefully as the words. The verse never tells us *why* they turned after clarity. It gives no reason. Because there is no reason that survives daylight. When you reject what you genuinely could not see, you can give an account of yourself. When you reject what *made itself clear to you,* there is no account left to give. The verse leaves the "why" blank because the blank *is* the verdict.

This is what the clause reveals: the gravest spiritual catastrophe is not the dark. It is choosing the dark *with the lamp already lit in your hand.*

[PAUSE]

Now let's turn to the verdict itself.

---

### The second word: لَن يَضُرُّوا ٱللَّهَ شَيْـًٔا — *never will they harm Allah in anything*

> لَن يَضُرُّوا ٱللَّهَ شَيْـًٔا

Let me teach one small grammatical thing first, because it is the engine of this phrase.

Arabic has two ways to say "not." There is a plain "no," and there is **lan** — and *lan* is the heavy one. *Lan* negates the future and slams the door. It is not "they won't" in the casual sense of *probably won't.* It is the absolute sealing-off of a possibility — **never, not ever, under no circumstance, into all the future.** When you want to say a thing is *impossible going forward,* you reach for *lan.*

And then comes the word that makes the phrase total: **shayʾan** — *a thing, anything, the least thing.* It is indefinite, and in this position it functions to sweep the field clean: not in any matter, not in any degree, not in the smallest particle.

Put it together: `lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan` — **never, into all the future, will they harm Allah in even the smallest thing.**

Now sit with what this is actually claiming, because we read past it too fast.

We just spent all that time on three forceful verbs — they *disbelieved,* they *barred others from the path,* they *cleaved themselves into the opposing faction.* This is not passivity. This is a *campaign.* Wealth spent. Armies raised — remember al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī's note that one tradition reads this of the wealthy ones who fed the armies at Badr, who *poured their fortunes* into stopping the path. Real effort. Real force. Real, organized, funded, intelligent opposition.

And the total damage inflicted on its target is: `shayʾan.* **A thing.** Negated by *lan.* **No thing. Ever.**

[PAUSE]

Let's deepen this, because it is easy to hear "they can't harm Allah" as an obvious statement — *of course you can't harm God, He's God* — and miss how strange and how merciful it actually is.

Read the absence here. The verse very deliberately says they will not harm *Allah* in anything. It does *not* say "they will not cause harm." Harm is absolutely happening in this verse. Something *is* being damaged, ruined, brought to collapse. The whole second half tells us so. The verse is not denying that the opposition is destructive. It is telling you, with surgical precision, **where the destruction lands.** Not on Him. The arrow they fired at the heavens — it had to come down somewhere. The verse is about to tell us exactly where.

There is a parallel in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān that says it even more nakedly: *innahum lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan* — *they will not harm Allah in anything* — and then, *He intends to give them no share in the Hereafter.* Same structure. Same turn. You aimed at the sky; you hit yourself.

And do you feel why this is *mercy* and not just majesty? If the truth of God could be diminished by human rejection, then your salvation would depend on everybody else behaving. Every disbeliever's campaign would chip away at the reality you're standing on. The ground under your faith would be eroding with every act of opposition in the world. But it cannot be touched. `Lan... shayʾan.* The reality you have anchored your life to is *unharmable.* The waves do not move the seabed. So you can stop being afraid *for* God. He was never the one at risk.

This is what the phrase reveals: the most furious rebellion that humanity can mount reaches the throne of God with the force of a thrown pebble reaching the moon. It never arrives. It was always going to fall back down.

Back down — onto what? That is the third word.

[PAUSE]

### The third word: وَسَيُحْبِطُ أَعْمَـٰلَهُمْ — *and He will make their deeds collapse into nothing*

> وَسَيُحْبِطُ أَعْمَـٰلَهُمْ

This is the most important word in the verse, and almost everyone walks past it thinking it just means "their deeds are cancelled." It means something far more vivid, and far more frightening, and we only see it when we go down to the root.

The verb is **sa-yuḥbiṭu**, from **ḥā-bā-ṭā** (*ḥ-b-ṭ*). Now, the standard meaning in religious usage is *iḥbāṭ* — the *nullification of deeds,* their reward made void. True. But where does that meaning *come from*? What is the picture underneath it?

The lexicographers preserve it, and once you see it you will never read this word flat again. *Ḥabiṭa* in its root sense describes **an animal that grazes greedily, eats and eats of rich or poisonous pasture until its belly swells up, bloats, and the very fullness kills it.** The beast eats itself sick. It looks, in the moment, *fat, full, thriving* — its belly is huge, it has consumed so much. And that very swelling is the disease. The bloat is fatal. The animal that ate the most is the animal that dies.

This is not a fringe reading. It is the attested root-sense in the classical lexicons, and it is why the Prophet ﷺ, in a famous narration about wealth, warned of the beast that *eats until its flanks are distended* — using this exact imagery of fatal fullness.

[PAUSE]

Now go back and read the word in the verse with that picture alive in your mind.

`sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum` — He will make their **deeds** undergo *iḥbāṭ.* Not: He will erase a blank ledger. The verse is not describing people who did nothing. It is describing people who did **a great deal** — they spent, they built, they fed armies, they organized, they achieved. Their record of *aʿmāl,* of works and accomplishments, is *full.* Bloated. Swollen with activity.

And the verse says: that very fullness is the death. The works that look so impressive, so abundant, so *fat* with effort and money and result — God will let them swell to nothing. The bloat *is* the verdict. **The animal that ate the most pasture is the one that dies of it. The people whose ledger looked fullest will find it was the fullness of poison.**

Do you feel the difference between "your deeds will be erased" and *this*? Erasure is clean. This is not clean. This is a stomach distended with rich food that turns out to be lethal. The deeds were *consumed* by these people the way the greedy beast consumes the poisoned field — eagerly, abundantly, with the appearance of thriving — and the consumption was the killing.

And notice the little prefix on the verb: **sa-** — *sa-yuḥbiṭu.* This is the prefix of the *near, certain future.* Not "perhaps, someday." **Soon. It is coming. It is on its way.** The bloat is already setting in.

This is what the third word reveals: the deeds of those who knowingly cleave from the truth are not cancelled like an entry struck from a list. They are *let swell* — full of effort, heavy with worldly success — until the day comes when all that swollen weight is revealed to contain nothing at all. *Fa-lā yarawna lahā fī l-ākhirati thawāban* — *they will see no reward for them in the Hereafter,* as al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn both put it. The fattest ledger. Weighed. Found empty.

[PAUSE — a long one.]

### The bridge: from three words to the shape of reality

Let's gather what these three words have shown us before we ask what they mean for a life.

**shāqqū** — they cracked themselves off and stood as the opposing half, not realizing you cannot crack the whole, only the fragment.

**lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan** — and so the entire force of that opposition reached its target with the force of nothing, because the target was unharmable.

**sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum** — and the force, having to land somewhere, fell back on the only thing it could touch: their own works, which it bloated into a fatal, empty fullness.

See the single shape underneath all three? It is one motion told three times. *A blow aimed at God, returning to the one who threw it.* The crack you make runs through you. The harm you fire falls back on you. The deeds you pile up bury you. **Three windows onto one law: rebellion against the Real is, in every case, a form of self-harm wearing the costume of power.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about three Arabic words — but about the way reality is actually built. Because this law does not only apply to the funders of Badr. It runs straight through the center of every human life, including yours and mine.

## PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: You cannot starve God by refusing to eat

Let me start somewhere very human, very small, before we go anywhere near God.

You have seen — maybe you have *been* — the person who, in pain, decides to hurt someone by hurting themselves. The teenager who slams the door and refuses dinner to punish a parent — and the parent eats, sleeps, lives, while the child lies awake hungry. The employee so furious at a company that they quietly stop trying — and the company posts record profits while the employee's own skills rust and their reference dies. The spouse who withholds love as a weapon — and finds, years later, that the only person who lived a loveless life was them.

There is a specific, terrible logic in all of these. *I will damage you by withdrawing from you. I will make you feel my opposition.* And in every case, the withdrawal lands on the wrong target. **The boycott starves only the one who refuses to eat.**

Now lift that ordinary human pattern up to its cosmic scale, and you have the spiritual condition this verse is describing. To disbelieve, to bar the path, to cleave yourself off from the Messenger after the truth has made itself plain — this *feels,* from the inside, like striking a blow. *I reject you. I oppose you. I will not be part of this.* It has all the emotional texture of power, of agency, of *making a dent.*

`Lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan.* You made no dent. There is no dent to make.

[PAUSE]

Stay inside this, because there is something the verse is protecting us from, and something it is exposing in us.

What it protects us from is the secret fear I named at the start — the believer's quiet anxiety that the enemies of truth might *win,* that all that wealth and cleverness and force arrayed against the path might actually damage it. The verse cuts that fear off at the root. The reality you love is not in danger. It was never in danger. *You can stop carrying that weight.* Your job was never to *protect* God; you couldn't if you tried, and you don't need to. Your job is only not to cleave yourself off from Him.

But what it *exposes* in us is more uncomfortable. Because every one of us, in smaller ways, plays at this same self-defeating game. Every time we sulk away from prayer to express some grievance against our circumstances — *fine, if life is going to be hard, I won't pray.* Every time we withhold our obedience as though it were leverage — as though God needed it and would feel its absence. Every time we treat our turning-away as a *statement,* a protest, a power move.

The verse holds up a mirror: *who exactly do you think you are starving?* The One who is *al-Ghaniyy,* the utterly Self-Sufficient, the One who would lose nothing if every creature that ever lived turned away in a single breath — or yourself, the fragment, who lives only by staying joined to the trunk?

There is a sacred ḥadīth that says it with breathtaking clarity: *O My servants, if the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, were all as wicked as the most wicked heart among you, that would not decrease My dominion in anything* — *shayʾan,* the very word of our verse. The withdrawal of all creation diminishes Him by *nothing.* You cannot starve the One who feeds the worlds.

**The practical turn:** So here is what changes about tomorrow. The next time you feel that pull to *withhold* — to skip the prayer as a protest, to turn from God because you're angry at your life — catch the lie inside the impulse. The lie is that your turning-away is *aimed at Him* and will *land on Him.* It is not, and it will not. It will land, with perfect accuracy, on the only person it can reach: you. Knowing that doesn't make the pain go away. But it strips the rebellion of its false dignity. There is nothing powerful about it. It is a child refusing dinner in an empty house. Eat.

[PAUSE]

---

### Theme Two: The fullest ledger and the lightest scale

Now the harder theme — and this one is not about *them* at all. This one is about the most devout among us.

Let me begin, again, somewhere ordinary.

You have seen a résumé that was *full* — every line crammed with titles, achievements, awards — and you have learned, on meeting the person, that the fullness was the cover for an emptiness. You have seen a building that was *grand* on the facade — and condemned behind the wall. You have held a banknote that looked exactly like money — crisp, detailed, convincing — and discovered it was counterfeit, that its very resemblance to value was the proof it had none. The thing that looked the most full was the most empty, and the fullness was the disguise.

This is the *iḥbāṭ* image. The bloated beast. The swollen, fatal fullness. `sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum.*

Now, the verse applies this to the knowing opposers of truth. But here is why we cannot read it and feel safe — here is why this should make the *praying* person, the *fasting* person, the *charitable* person sit up rather than relax. Because the same Qur'an that uses this word here uses the *same root, the same concept of iḥbāṭ,* about deeds done *by believers* that were poisoned at the source — deeds done to be seen, deeds done with the heart turned elsewhere, deeds piled high and weighed empty.

The Qur'an gives us, in another place, the most chilling sentence on this: *Shall We tell you of those who are greatest losers in their deeds? Those whose striving went astray in the worldly life while they thought they were doing good work* — *yaḥsabūna annahum yuḥsinūna ṣunʿan.* They *thought* the ledger was full of treasure. They had eaten and eaten of the rich pasture. They felt *full.* And it was the bloat. It was poison wearing the shape of nourishment.

[PAUSE]

So let's ask the question this verse forces on the sincere: **what makes a deed swell instead of weigh?**

The whole verse has already told us. Look at what corrupts the works of the opposers. It is not that they did too little. It is the *root* their works grew from — disbelief, the cleaving-off, the turning after clarity. The works were watered by a poisoned spring. No matter how *much* fruit grew on that branch, the branch was severed from the trunk. *Shaqqū.* They cleaved off. And a branch cut from the tree can still be heavy with fruit for a few days — but the fruit will not ripen, because the life has stopped flowing into it. It will swell. It will rot. It will fall.

That is the terror and the gift of this image for the believer. The amount of your deeds is not the question. The *connection of the branch to the trunk* is the question. A small deed flowing with the life of sincerity — *for Allah, joined to Him* — ripens. A mountain of deeds severed from that life — done for the eyes of people, done while the heart cleaves quietly off in its own direction — swells and rots, however large the mountain.

The commentators are precise about *why* the opposers' works collapse, and it should land on us like a warning bell: al-Muyassar says *li-annahum lam yurīdū bihā wajha-llāhi taʿālā* — **because they did not intend by them the Face of Allah.** That is the poison in the pasture. Not the size of the meal. The intention behind the eating.

[PAUSE]

Let's not soften this. We accumulate. We count. There is a part of every religious person that keeps a quiet inner ledger — *look how much I've prayed, how much I've given, how much I've built* — and finds comfort in the *fullness* of it. This verse walks straight up to that ledger and asks one question: *is it heavy, or is it bloated?* Is it weight, or is it air? Because on the Day when the scales are set, the bloated ledger and the heavy ledger look identical right up until the moment of weighing — and then one sinks the scale, and the other floats, light as nothing, full of nothing. *Fa-lā yarawna lahā... thawāban.* They will see no reward for it. They were so sure it was there.

**The practical turn:** So here is what changes about tomorrow, and it is not "do more." It is the opposite. It is to stop, before the deed, and check the *spring* it is flowing from. Before the prayer, before the gift, before the post about the good thing you did — one breath, one question: *am I joining this to Him, or feeding it to the eyes of people? Is this branch on the trunk, or cut?* It is a small habit and it changes everything, because it is the difference between a deed that ripens and a deed that bloats. Do less, joined to Him, rather than more, cleaved off. **A handful of weight outlasts a mountain of air.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

---

### Bringing it together

Stand back now and see the whole verse as one piece.

It opens with three verbs of furious human energy — *kafarū, ṣaddū, shāqqū* — disbelieving, barring, cleaving off. Three forms of effort. And the rest of the verse is the *accounting* of that effort: where it goes, what it touches, what it costs.

What does it touch? Of God — *nothing.* `lan yaḍurrū llāha shayʾan.* The pebble thrown at the sky never arrives.

What does it cost? *Everything they built.* `sa-yuḥbiṭu aʿmālahum.* The pebble, having to fall somewhere, falls back and shatters the thrower's own house.

And the two themes we walked are simply this one law seen from two sides. *You cannot starve God by refusing Him* — that is the *lan yaḍurrū* side, the impotence of the rebellion. *The fullest ledger can be the emptiest scale* — that is the *sa-yuḥbiṭu* side, the bloat of the severed deed. They are the same truth: **everything you aim against the Real returns to you, and everything you build apart from Him collapses into the shape of nothing.** Rebellion is self-harm. Severance is self-emptying. The arrow always comes home.

And underneath even that is the single image hidden in the root of *shaqq* — the crack. They thought they were cracking the world in two, God on one side and themselves on the other. But there are not two sides. There is the One who holds the whole, and there is the fragment that imagines it can stand apart. The crack they made did not run through the heavens. It ran through themselves. **To cleave from God is not to oppose Him. It is to break off a piece of yourself and watch it stop living.**

---

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. Where in my life am I "refusing dinner in an empty house" — withholding my obedience or my turning-toward-God as if it were a blow against Him, when it only ever lands on me?

2. When I feel the quiet comfort of my own religious ledger — the count of my prayers, my giving, my good works — am I feeling *weight,* or am I feeling *bloat*? How would I even tell the difference?

3. Is there a place where the truth has *made itself clear to me* — `tabayyana` — and I have quietly chosen the dark anyway, with the lamp already lit in my hand?

4. Which of my deeds are branches *on the trunk* — flowing with sincerity, joined to Him — and which are heavy with fruit but quietly *cut*?

5. If I truly believed that nothing I do can harm God and everything I do apart from Him harms only me — what is the very first thing I would stop doing tomorrow?

---

### The one sentence

**Every blow you aim at God lands on you, and every deed you build apart from Him swells into nothing — so the only sane life is the one that stays joined to the trunk.**

---

### The closing invitation

This verse came, first, as comfort to a frightened community: *the ones arrayed against what you love cannot touch it.* Receive that. Put down the weight of fearing *for* God; He was never at risk, and neither is the truth you've anchored to.

But do not walk away having only pointed the verse outward at the enemies of the path. Turn it inward, where it is sharpest. The same law that makes the opposer's campaign land on himself makes *my* every turning-away land on me, and makes *my* every severed deed swell into emptiness. The invitation is not to fear the people who oppose the truth. It is to make sure I am not, in some quiet corner of my own heart, cleaving off from it after it has already made itself clear to me. It is to check the spring before I pour, to weigh the branch's connection before I count its fruit. Not more deeds. *Joined* deeds.

Stay on the trunk. That is the whole of it.

---

### A du'ā from these words

*O Allah, You whom no rebellion can reach and no withdrawal can diminish — keep me from ever cleaving myself off from Your Messenger after Your guidance has made itself plain to me. Let my deeds be weight and not bloat, branches joined to You and not fruit cut from the tree; and on the Day the scales are set, do not let me find that the ledger I thought was full was only ever full of air. Keep me joined to You, for everything apart from You is the shape of nothing.* Āmīn.
