# The Locks They Forged Themselves

> أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ أَمْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَآ
>
> *Afalā yatadabbarūna l-Qur'āna am ʿalā qulūbin aqfāluhā*
>
> **"Then do they not reflect deeply upon the Qur'an — or are there locks upon hearts, their own locks?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To feel this ayah, you have to stand inside the surah it lives in.

Surah Muhammad is a surah of *parting*. From its opening verses, two roads diverge and keep diverging. On one side: *those who believe and do righteous deeds and believe in what was sent down upon Muhammad.* On the other: *those who disbelieve and turn away from the path of Allah.* The whole surah is the widening gap between these two — like two travelers who set out from the same gate and, step by step, end up on opposite sides of a mountain.

But the surah's sharpest attention is not on the open disbelievers. It is on a third group — the most painful group — the ones who *say* they are on the first road while their hearts are quietly walking the second. The hypocrites. The people of the divided interior.

And what exposes them, in this surah, is a very specific test: the moment the Qur'an asks something *costly*. The surah is also called **al-Qitāl** — "the Fighting" — because the trigger that splits the community is the command to *struggle*, to stand and defend, to put the body where the tongue has already spoken. And the moment that command comes down, you see something:

*"...but when a decisive sūrah is revealed and fighting is mentioned in it, you see those in whose hearts is sickness looking at you with the look of one fainting at death."* (47:20)

The Word arrives. And in some chests, it lands like life. And in other chests — the same exact Word — lands like a death sentence. Same verses. Same Messenger ﷺ. Same Arabic. Completely opposite effect.

So a question hangs in the air of this surah, unspoken but enormous: **Why?** Why does the identical Word open one heart and bounce off another? Is the Qur'an unclear? Is the proof insufficient? Is Allah's speech somehow failing to communicate?

[PAUSE]

Verse 24 is where Allah turns and answers that question directly. He stops describing the hypocrites' behavior and goes underneath it — to the mechanism. To the *organ*. And He answers in the form of a question that is really a diagnosis.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even open the words.

When something we send out fails to arrive, we usually blame the *signal*. The message was unclear. The connection was bad. The other person didn't get it.

In this ayah, Allah does something that should stop us cold. The Word is His. It is perfect. It cannot be the failed signal. So when it doesn't arrive, He points — not at the speech, and not even, at first, at the people — but at a **door**. A door with something hanging on it.

And the most unsettling word in the entire verse is one tiny suffix at the very end — a single attached pronoun — that tells you *whose lock it is.*

Stay with that. Because by the time we understand that one suffix, the whole ayah will have turned from an accusation about *them* into a mirror held up to *us*.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yatadabbarūn* — The Word That Means "To Walk Around to the Back"

Before we touch the ayah, let me give you a concept from ordinary life.

Imagine someone hands you a sealed package. Most people glance at the front label and put it down. But suppose you actually want to *know* what it is. What do you do? You turn it over. You look at the back. You read what's underneath. You follow it to where it came from and ask where it's going. You don't stop at the face it shows you — you go *around* it.

Now hold that picture, because the Arabic word at the center of this ayah is built from exactly that motion.

**أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ — *afalā yatadabbarūn*** — "do they not *reflect deeply*?"

The verb is **yatadabbarūn**, and its root is **د-ب-ر (d-b-r)**. Now here is the thing the translation cannot carry: this root does not originally mean "to think." It means *the back, the rear, the hind-part of a thing.* The **dubur** of something is its *behind* — what comes after it, what's at its tail end, where it leads.

So when the classical lexicographers define **tadabbur**, they do not say "to ponder" in some vague way. Imam al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī, in his *Mufradāt*, defines it precisely: **al-tadabbur is to look into the *adbār* — the back-ends, the outcomes, the consequences — of matters.** To follow a thing to its rear. To walk around to its back.

[PAUSE]

Let that reorganize what you thought reflection was.

To make *tadabbur* of the Qur'an is not to *read* the Qur'an. It is not even to *understand* the Qur'an at the level of its front face. It is to refuse to stop at the surface — to turn the verse over, to ask where it leads, to chase it to its consequence, to look at the *back* of the words and see what stands behind them.

Notice something about the grammatical *shape* of this word, too. **Yatadabbarūn** is a **Form V** verb — *tafaʿʿala*. And Form V in Arabic carries a specific flavor: it is **reflexive and effortful**. It means to do something *to yourself*, gradually, with sustained labor. It's the difference between *teaching* (an outward act) and *taʿallama* — *learning*, which is something you do *to yourself*, piece by piece, with effort, over time.

So Form V tells us: tadabbur is not a passive thing that happens *to* you when the recitation is beautiful. It is a thing you *do to yourself*. Deliberately. Repeatedly. With the kind of effort that turns the package over and over in your hands until it finally opens.

Here is what is remarkable. **The Qur'an is, in this very verse, naming its own method of being received.** It is telling you how it is meant to be approached — not skimmed, not merely heard, but *circled, opened, followed to its back.* And it does so by *rebuking* the people who didn't.

So the first word already contains the whole drama. The hypocrites had the Qur'an. They heard it constantly. What they did *not* do was **yatadabbarūn** — they never walked around to its back. They stopped at the front label. And the surah is asking: how is that even possible? How do you stand in front of *this* and not turn it over?

That question — "how is that possible?" — is exactly what the next part of the ayah answers.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Question That Becomes a Verdict — *Afalā... am*

Let me teach you a small rhetorical structure first, in plain English.

There's a kind of question people ask when they already know the answer and the question itself is the accusation. A teacher finds a student who has failed the same test five times and says: *"Are you not studying? Or is there something wrong with your books?"* The teacher is not genuinely asking about the books. The second half of that sentence is rhetorical — it forces the student to confront that the books are *fine*, which means the problem is somewhere the student doesn't want to look.

Arabic has a refined version of this. Watch the architecture of the ayah:

**أَفَلَا يَتَدَبَّرُونَ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ** — *Do they not reflect deeply upon the Qur'an?*
**أَمْ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَآ** — *Or, upon hearts, are their locks?*

The hinge is that word **أَمْ — *am*** — usually translated "or." And here the classical mufassirūn are united on something crucial. Al-Jalālayn says it plainly: **"am" here means "bal"** — meaning *nay, rather, in fact.* Al-Baghawī says the same: *"am" is in the meaning of "bal."* This is what grammarians call **iḍrāb** — a *turn*, a transition that moves you off the first thought and onto a heavier one.

So the ayah is not offering two neutral options — "either they don't reflect, or maybe their hearts are locked." That soft reading dissolves the moment you see the *am*. The real movement is:

*"Will they not reflect upon the Qur'an? — Nay. Rather. The truth is: upon hearts are locks."*

[PAUSE]

Feel what just happened. The verse opens as a **question** — gentle, almost an invitation, *won't you reflect?* — and then **turns**, mid-breath, into a **verdict.** The question was rhetorical all along. The Qur'an does not stay in the posture of asking. It asks, and then it answers its own asking with a diagnosis so heavy it lands like a gavel.

Now look at something in the second half that the translations almost always smooth over. He says **عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ — *ʿalā qulūbin*** — "upon **hearts**." Not *"upon their hearts."* Not *qulūbihim.* He drops the possessive. He says, simply, **"hearts."** Indefinite. Bare.

This is an **absence you have to read.** Arabic absolutely had the word *qulūbihim* — "their hearts" — available, and uses it constantly elsewhere. Here, deliberately, the possessive is withheld. And the indefinite, bare "hearts" does something cold and precise: it **distances**. It refuses to dignify these hearts with a clean attribution. It is the difference between "the locks are on *your* heart" and "there are locks — on *hearts* — the kind of hearts this happens to." The indefinite makes them a *category*, a type, a class of organ that has reached a certain state. It steps back from them the way you step back from something you no longer expect to open.

So in the span of seven words, the ayah has moved from *invitation* (won't you reflect?) → to *turn* (nay, rather) → to *cold diagnosis* (there are locks, on hearts of this kind). The grammar itself is enacting a door closing.

But it is the **last** word that tells you who closed it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Aqfāluhā* — The Lock, and the One Letter That Names Its Owner

**أَقْفَالُهَآ — *aqfāluhā*** — "locks." More precisely: *aqfāl* (the plural of **qufl**, a lock) **+ hā** (an attached pronoun: *its / their own*).

Let's open the lock itself first, then that final letter.

The word **qufl** — a lock, a bolt, the thing that seals a door. Listen to it: *qufl.* The deep, guttural **qāf**, the hard **fā**, the abrupt **lām** — the mouth itself closes on the word. It *sounds* like a thing snapping shut. The sonic texture is doing the work the meaning describes.

But there's something deeper in the root that al-Qurṭubī pauses to tell us, and it changes everything. He writes: **"aṣl al-qufl al-yubs wa al-ṣalāba"** — *the original meaning of "qufl" is dryness and hardness.* He notes that what dries out and hardens on a tree — the brittle, sapless wood — was called by a word from this same family. **A lock, in Arabic, is named after the quality of having gone dry and rigid.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that, because now the image is no longer mechanical. We tend to picture a "locked heart" as a heart with a *padlock* hung on it from the outside — an external device, someone else's act. But the Arabic is telling you the lock is not a foreign attachment. **The lock is what the heart *became* when it dried out.** A living heart is soft, moist, supple — it receives, it's moved, it turns (the very root of *qalb*, heart, is *to turn*). A heart stops receiving not because someone bolted it shut, but because it lost its moisture. It hardened. It went sapless and brittle, like dead wood. And a heart that has dried into rigidity *is itself the lock.* The seal is not on the heart. The seal is what the heart turned into.

This is the physical image pointing at the unseen reality. You cannot see a soul harden. But you have seen wood dry, crack, and lose every drop of life until nothing can grow from it again. Allah hands you the visible — *yubs, ṣalāba,* dry hard wood — to let you feel the invisible: a self that has lost its capacity to be moved.

Now — the final letter. **هَآ — *hā*** — "its." *Aqfāluhā* — "**its own** locks." The locks belonging to those very hearts.

This is the suffix I asked you to wait for at the very beginning. And here is its weight.

Look at the strange asymmetry the ayah builds. The **hearts** are left *indefinite* — bare, unowned, distanced: "hearts." But the **locks** are made *definite and possessed*: "**their** locks." Think about how backwards that is. The heart is the precious thing, the God-given organ of perception — and it's the thing left unclaimed, generic, kept at arm's length. The **lock** — the defect, the failure, the deadness — *that's* the thing tied to them with a possessive pronoun. **The one thing that is truly, definitely *theirs* in this verse is the lock.**

[PAUSE]

When a heart seals, the seal becomes its most defining possession. Not "their hearts, which happen to be locked" — but "hearts... and *the locks that belong to them.*" The lock has become the identity.

And that single *hā* answers the question we have been carrying since the introduction: *whose lock is it?* Not Allah's, hung on them arbitrarily. Not the world's, forced on them from outside. **Their own.** *Aqfāluhā.* The locks they forged.

Now — and we must be honest and complete here, because the classical scholars are — there is a second face to this. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Muyassar both also affirm that these are locks **Allah placed** upon hearts that chose this state: *"locks that Allah, Exalted, has locked upon them, so they do not comprehend."* And this is not a contradiction — it is the full reality seen from both sides. This is the Qur'anic pattern you find again in Sūrat al-Baqarah's *"Allah has set a seal upon their hearts"* (2:7). The two are one truth from two angles: **the servant dries his own heart by refusing, again and again, to turn the Word over — and Allah, in perfect justice, then confirms upon that heart the very lock the servant manufactured.** The *hā* — "its own" — guards us from ever imagining Allah locks a heart that was reaching for Him. The lock is built from the inside. Allah's decree seals what the servant had already sealed.

So the three words have given us the whole anatomy:
- **Yatadabbarūn** — the act they refused: turning the Word over to its back.
- **Am ʿalā qulūb** — the turn from invitation to verdict, the cold distancing of "hearts."
- **Aqfāluhā** — and the diagnosis: dried, hardened, self-made locks that have become the only thing truly theirs.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So the linguistic architecture has shown us something with frightening precision. The Word did not fail. The grammar refuses to let us blame the signal. It traces the failure to a *door* — and then, with one suffix, traces the door to the hand that bolted it.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about *words* — but about *reality itself*. About what is actually happening, right now, in human hearts. Including, perhaps, ours.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lock You Build by Not Opening the Door

Let me start where you already live.

Think about a capacity you once had and quietly lost. Maybe there was a language you spoke as a child and let slip — and now the words are *there*, somewhere, but the door to them won't open. Maybe there was an instrument your hands once knew. Maybe there was a kind of crying you could do, a softness you had at sixteen that you can't seem to access at forty.

Here is the thing about every one of those losses: **nobody took the capacity from you.** No one broke in and stole your French, your music, your tears. They dried up from *disuse.* The neuroscientists have a phrase for it — *use it or lose it.* The brain prunes the pathways you stop walking. The road you never take grows over until, one day, you couldn't take it if you tried. The not-doing didn't stay a *choice.* It hardened into an *inability.*

[PAUSE]

This is the exact mechanism the ayah is describing — but in the deepest organ of all.

The hypocrites of Surah Muhammad did not wake up one morning unable to be moved by the Qur'an. There was a time the words could reach them. But every time the Word came and asked something — *give, struggle, stand, surrender* — they declined to turn it over. They heard it and set it down at the front label. They did not *yatadabbarūn.* And the refusal, repeated, did what every repeated refusal does: **it dried the pathway until it sealed.** The choice not to be moved became the inability to be moved. *The lock is built by the hand that keeps not opening the door.*

Notice the terrifying order the ayah preserves. It does **not** say: "their hearts are locked, *therefore* they cannot reflect." That would make them victims — sealed first, then helpless. It says the reverse motion: *do they not reflect* — **rather** — *there are locks.* The non-reflection comes *first* in the verse, and the locks are named as the state arrived at. The diagnosis is not "they are locked, poor things." The diagnosis is: **they stopped turning the Word over, and a heart that is never turned over hardens into a thing that can't be.**

And here is the most sobering layer. *Willful* blindness, repeated long enough, becomes *actual* blindness. The man who keeps choosing not to see eventually arrives at a place where he genuinely cannot. He is still responsible — because he built the wall, brick by brick, with full knowledge — and yet the wall is now *real.* This is why the ayah is simultaneously a *rebuke* (you did this) and a *diagnosis* (this is now your state). Both are true. That is the horror and the justice of it.

**So what does this change about tomorrow?**

It means the small refusals are not small. The verse you skim past because it asks something inconvenient. The reminder you mute because it touches a sin you're not ready to leave. The moment of softness in the prayer that you rush past because feeling it would require you to change. Each of these, by itself, feels like nothing — like one day of not speaking the language. But the ayah is screaming: *these are how locks are forged.* Not in a single dramatic act of rejection, but in the quiet accumulation of *not turning the Word over* when it asked you to.

The practical application is almost unbearably concrete: **the next time a verse asks something of you and you feel the urge to set it down at the front label — that is the exact moment the lock is being made or unmade.** Turn it over instead. Walk around to its back. Ask where it leads. Because the alternative is not "I'll get to it later." The alternative is that the door to *getting to it* slowly dries shut.

### Theme Two: The Word Was Never the Problem — The Heart Is the Receiver

Let me bring you to a second place you already know.

You have had the experience of reading something true and *not feeling it* — and then, years later, reading the *same words* and being undone by them. Same sentence. Same ink. The first time it was wallpaper. The second time it was a knife. What changed? Not the words. **You.** The receiver.

We live, more than any generation in history, drowning in *reception without contact.* We "consume content." We scroll past more wisdom before breakfast than our grandparents heard in a year — and almost none of it lands. We have trained ourselves into a posture where words enter the eyes and exit the back of the head, touching nothing. We have become, in a sense, *professionally* unmoved. And then we open the Qur'an in the same posture — eyes moving, mouth reciting, heart at the front label — and we wonder why it feels flat.

[PAUSE]

This ayah performs a profound mercy by relocating the problem. When the Qur'an doesn't move you, every instinct says: *maybe the text is the issue. Maybe it's too old, too distant, too repetitive, too hard.* The ayah forbids that conclusion. It will not let the Word be the failed signal. The Word is Allah's perfect speech — *"Had it been from other than Allah, they would have found in it much contradiction"* (4:82). The signal is flawless. **So when nothing arrives, the ayah turns you, gently and devastatingly, to the *receiver.***

And notice *where* it locates the receiver. Not the ears. Not the intellect. Not even the eyes. **The heart.** *ʿAlā qulūb.* The Qur'an is not, finally, an argument to be won at the level of the mind. The hypocrites in this surah were not refuted intellectually — many of them could quote it, debate it, recite it. Their *minds* received it fine. The lock was one level down, where reception actually becomes *transformation.* You can understand every word of the Qur'an and have it reach nothing, because comprehension is the front label and *tadabbur* is the back.

This is the quiet warning hidden inside a verse about hypocrites: **you can be a person who knows the Qur'an and is sealed from it.** Knowledge is not the same as contact. The scholar and the hypocrite can read the same line; in one it becomes light and in the other it dies at the door. The difference is not information. The difference is whether the heart is *moist* — soft, turning, willing to be moved — or *dry.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the hope folded inside the warning, because this ayah is not only a sentence — it is also a *door left ajar.* Notice that the very existence of the question — *afalā yatadabbarūn?* — implies that the path back is *tadabbur.* If the lock is built by *not* turning the Word over, then the lock is loosened by *turning it over.* The narration al-Qurṭubī records, raised to the Prophet ﷺ, describes locks upon these hearts *"like locks of iron — until Allah opens them."* Until *Allah* opens them. The same Hand that confirms the lock on the one who forged it is the Hand that *opens* — for the one who comes back to the door and pushes, with effort, with *tadabbur,* asking to be let in.

**So what does this change about tomorrow?**

It means the goal of opening the Qur'an is not to *finish* it. It is to be *reached* by it. The practical shift is to stop measuring your relationship with the Qur'an by *quantity received* — pages, verses, recitations — and start measuring it by *contact made.* One verse, turned all the way over until it touches something and you have to stop, is worth more than a juz' that died at the front label. The next time you sit with it, do not ask "how much can I get through?" Ask the receiver's question: **"Am I moist enough to be reached by this? And if I'm not — am I willing to push on the door?"**

And the most freeing part: if you ever feel the Qur'an has gone flat — *that flatness is not a verdict on the Word. It is a reading on your own receiver.* And a receiver can be softened. A dried heart can be watered. The lock you built, you can come and ask the One who holds the key to open.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole, because the seven words are built with terrible symmetry.

The ayah has **two halves**, and they mirror each other as cause and consequence:

**First half — the act:** *Afalā yatadabbarūn al-Qur'ān.* A living verb of motion — *turning, walking around, opening.* The Word, and the human invited to *move toward it.*

**Second half — the state:** *Am ʿalā qulūbin aqfāluhā.* No verb of motion at all. Just stillness. A noun — *locks* — sitting on top of an organ that has stopped turning. The Word's invitation, and the human who has gone *immobile* before it.

The verse is a hinge between *motion* and *stillness*, between *the heart that turns* and *the heart that has stopped turning into a lock.* And the pivot of the whole structure is that one word **am — "nay, rather"** — the turn from *what they should have done* to *what they became* by not doing it.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **grammar** showed us a verb of opening (*yatadabbarūn*) collapsing into a noun of sealing (*aqfāluhā*), with one suffix — *hā* — naming the hand that did the sealing. The **themes** showed us *why*: because a capacity refused becomes a capacity lost, and because the heart, not the Word, is the receiver that can dry shut.

From both directions, the same truth: **The Word never failed. The door was bolted from the inside. And the only one who can open it now is the One who holds the key — for the one who comes back and pushes.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time a verse asked something costly of me — and I quietly set it down at the front label instead of turning it over?
2. Are there capacities of the heart I once had — to weep in prayer, to be shaken by a reminder, to feel the weight of a verse — that have gone dry from disuse? What dried them?
3. Do I measure my relationship with the Qur'an by *how much I get through*, or by *how much reaches me*?
4. If a lock on the heart is built from the inside, brick by quiet brick — which brick am I laying this week?
5. And the hopeful one: where is the one door I could go back to, push on, and ask Allah to open?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The Qur'an asks why its truth doesn't land, and answers its own question — not because the perfect Word is unclear, but because some hearts, through their own repeated refusal to turn it over, have dried into locked vaults whose only true possession is the lock they forged themselves.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what could shift, if you let it.

Stop, just for a season, treating the Qur'an as something to *get through.* Treat it as a door. The next time you open it, pick *one* verse — not a page, one verse — and refuse to set it down at the front label. Turn it over. Walk around to its back. Ask where it leads, what it would cost you, what it is asking. Push on it until something behind it moves.

Because that pushing — that effortful, reflexive, Form-V *tadabbur* — is the exact opposite motion of the one that builds the lock. You cannot seal a door you keep opening. And every honest turning of the Word over is a drop of water on a heart that the world has been quietly drying out.

This is not an obligation laid on you. It is an invitation extended to you — by the only One who can open what has closed.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who turn the hearts — do not let our hearts go dry. We have set Your Word down at the door too many times; soften what we have hardened, and water what we have let wither. And the locks we forged with our own hands — open them, O Opener, for we cannot reach the key, but You hold it. Make us of those who turn Your Word over until it turns us. Āmīn.
