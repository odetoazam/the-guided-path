# The Question That Reveals Who Is Really Waiting

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 71

> وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هَـٰذَا ٱلْوَعْدُ إِن كُنتُمْ صَـٰدِقِينَ

> *Wa yaqūlūna matā hādhā al-waʿdu in kuntum ṣādiqīn*

> "And they say, 'When will this promise come, if you are truthful?'"

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Picture the marketplace in Makkah in the years before the migration.

A man stands speaking. He is reciting something that did not exist a moment before. He is telling people, with quiet certainty, that there is a day coming — a day when the sky will split, when the mountains will move like clouds, when every soul will stand and be questioned about what it spent its life on.

He has been saying this for years.

The mountains have not moved. The sky has not split. Trade caravans still leave on schedule. Lambs are still being born in the spring. The dates still ripen in the summer.

And so a group of men in that marketplace — men who have heard this warning hundreds of times — develop a habit. Whenever he speaks of the Day, whenever he mentions the *waʿd*, the promise, one of them turns and asks, with a smile that is not quite a smile:

*"When? When exactly? Give us a date."*

And the others laugh.

This was not a question. It was a weapon. It was the most reliable weapon the deniers of Makkah had — because every day that passed without the sky cracking was, in their minds, another small victory. Another piece of evidence that this man was, perhaps, mistaken.

The Quran records this exchange. Not once. Many times. Across many surahs. Because Allah is documenting something that will outlive Makkah by fourteen centuries — something that will repeat itself in every age, in every marketplace, in every comment section, in every laughing dismissal of the unseen.

This surah — An-Naml — has been showing us kingdoms. It has shown us Sulayman, whose authority extended to ants and birds and jinn. It has shown us the queen of Sheba, who recognized truth and submitted. It has shown us Thamud, who demanded a sign and were destroyed by the sign they demanded. Surah after surah, the pattern: those who heard, those who refused, those who waited too long.

And now, in this late stretch of the surah, Allah turns the camera. He stops showing us ancient kingdoms. He shows us a question being asked, right now, in the streets of Makkah.

*"When will this promise come, if you are truthful?"*

### The Hook

Seven words.

Look at them again. *Wa yaqūlūna matā hādhā al-waʿdu in kuntum ṣādiqīn.* Seven words.

There is something hiding inside this question that the questioners themselves do not see. They think they are challenging the Messenger. They think they are putting *him* on trial.

But Allah has chosen to record their exact words — preserved verbatim across centuries — because their question contains a confession they did not mean to make.

Stay with me. We are going to spend some time with seven words. Because the way Allah preserves a sentence is itself a form of evidence — and what He preserves here is the precise grammar of a particular kind of unbelief that is still alive today, still on our tongues sometimes, in disguised forms.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa yaqūlūna* — The Tense That Will Not Let Them Escape

Let me begin with something that might seem dry, but I promise you it isn't.

In English, when we want to describe an action, we have to decide: did it happen yesterday, is it happening now, will it happen tomorrow? Past, present, future. Three boxes. You pick one.

Arabic doesn't quite work like English here. Arabic has two main verb forms — and they are not really about "past" and "present" the way we think. The form called *al-māḍī* is for completed action. The form called *al-muḍāriʿ* is for action that is ongoing, repeating, unfolding. It is action that is *still happening*, action that has not closed off into the past.

Now look at how this ayah opens.

*Wa yaqūlūna.* "And they **are saying** / **keep saying** / **continue to say**."

This is not *qālū* — "they said," past tense, closed, sealed off. Allah did not say *wa qālū matā hādhā al-waʿd* — "and they said, when is this promise?"

He said *wa yaqūlūna*. They keep on saying it.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The verb form pulls the question out of one moment in Makkah and stretches it across time. The deniers are not just saying this once, on one Tuesday afternoon in the year 615. They are saying it. They will keep saying it. Every time they are warned, they will say it again.

And here is what is remarkable: by choosing this tense, Allah has built a sentence that does not age.

When this ayah was first heard, *they say* meant the men in the Makkan marketplace. A hundred years later, *they say* meant other men, in other cities, saying the same thing. Today, when someone scrolls past a verse about the Day of Judgment and types in a comment, *"when is this supposed to happen?"* — they have just said it. *Yaqūlūna.* Present. Continuous.

The grammar itself testifies that this question is not a moment but a pattern. It is what a certain kind of heart always says when confronted with a warning it would rather not consider.

And here is something else. The verb is in the third person plural — *they*. Not *you*, not *one of them*. *They.* The Quran is not addressing the questioners directly here. It is describing them to someone else. It is as if Allah has turned to the Messenger and said: *Here is what they keep doing. Look.*

The questioners are talked **about**, not talked **to**. They have been moved from the position of interlocutor to the position of specimen.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that for a moment. They think they are the ones interrogating. They think they are the prosecutors in this scene. But the grammar has quietly placed them in the witness box. Allah is the one narrating their behavior, in the third person, to His Messenger and to every reader who comes after. They are no longer asking the question; they are being described as the kind of people who ask this question.

That is what the form *yaqūlūna* does. One word. It dates them, generalizes them, and exhibits them all at once.

### Element Two: *Hādhā al-Waʿd* — The Word That Tells Them More Than They Want to Know

Now look at the noun they use for the thing they are mocking.

*Hādhā al-waʿd.* "**This** promise."

The word for promise here is *al-waʿd*, from the root *w-ʿ-d*. In classical Arabic, *waʿd* is what someone has committed to — it is a binding word, a word that creates an obligation upon the speaker. When you give your *waʿd*, you have placed yourself under it.

But here is the thing about *al-waʿd* in the Quran. The Arabic language actually has a sister word: *waʿīd*. Same root, different form. *Waʿd* tends to be used for a promise of something good — a promise of reward, of paradise, of victory. *Waʿīd* is the technical word for a threat, a promise of punishment, a warning of harm.

Now read what they said again. *"When will this **waʿd** come?"*

Not *waʿīd*. *Waʿd*.

This is strange. They are mocking a warning of punishment. They are scoffing at the threat of the Day. So why does Allah preserve their wording as *al-waʿd* — the word that classically leans toward a promised good?

Classical scholars notice this. Some explain it this way: the deniers are using sarcasm. They are calling it *al-waʿd* — "your *promise*" — the way someone today might roll their eyes and say *"oh, your great gift to humanity."* It is irony. They are taunting by using the dignified word for a thing they consider beneath them.

Others say something deeper. They say *al-waʿd* is a more comprehensive word. It can carry both senses — the promised good and the promised reckoning — because for those who lived for the Hereafter, the Day was a promised good, and for those who denied, it became a promised punishment. The same Day. Two faces. *Al-waʿd* is the umbrella word that holds both.

[PAUSE]

But notice what the demonstrative does. *Hādhā.* "**This** promise."

The word *hādhā* in Arabic is the near demonstrative — used for something close. It is the word for an object within reach, within view, within the immediate field of the conversation. If you wanted to dismiss something far away or abstract, you would more naturally use *dhālika* — "that."

By using *hādhā*, the deniers betray something. They are saying *this* promise — the one we keep hearing about, the one that keeps being mentioned, the one we cannot quite get away from.

Here is what is remarkable. They mean *hādhā* as contempt — *"this thing you keep going on about."* But the demonstrative carries a confession underneath the contempt. *Hādhā* means it is present to them. It is near. They cannot file it away under "old religions" or "far-off myths." Whatever else is true, this promise is **close** in their attention. They are aware of it. It has lodged itself in their daily speech.

You do not keep mocking what you have forgotten.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. The most reliable indicator that something is bothering a person is how often they need to dismiss it. Indifference is silent. Mockery is a kind of recognition that wears the costume of dismissal. *Hādhā* — *this thing, right here, that we cannot stop talking about even as we say it is nothing.*

The deniers thought they were demonstrating their distance from the warning. The grammar shows the opposite. They were the ones for whom the warning had become the most recurring near-presence in their lives.

### Element Three: *In Kuntum Ṣādiqīn* — The Condition That Convicts the Conditioner

Now we come to the final clause, and this is where the entire sentence turns on its hinge.

*In kuntum ṣādiqīn.* "If you are truthful."

Three things to notice here, slowly.

First: the word *in*. Arabic has two main conditional particles — *in* and *idhā*. They look similar in translation; both come out as "if." But they carry different weights. *Idhā* tends to be used for conditions the speaker treats as likely or expected. *In* tends to be used for conditions the speaker treats as uncertain, hypothetical, or even doubtful.

By choosing *in*, the deniers have built a doubt into the very grammar of their challenge. *If* — and we are not granting that you are — *you are truthful.* It is the conditional of suspicion.

Second: *kuntum*. This is a past-form verb being used to set up a condition about an ongoing state. "If you have been / are [habitually] truthful." The verb *kāna* in this construction is not just "to be" in the simple sense; it implies an established condition, a settled character. They are not asking *"if you happen to be saying something true right now."* They are challenging the very claim of his standing as a truthful person.

Third — and most striking — *ṣādiqīn*. **Plural**.

[PAUSE]

Stop here. Read it again. *In kuntum ṣādiqīn.* "If **you (plural)** are truthful."

But this entire scene, in the Makkan context, is one man speaking. One Messenger. Singular. Why have the deniers shifted to the plural?

There are several lines of interpretation among the classical scholars, and rather than choosing one, let's hold all of them at once like camera angles on the same scene.

One reading: they are speaking to the Messenger and to those who have already believed alongside him. *"If you and your followers are all truthful."* The plural is meant to humiliate by lumping the Prophet ﷺ with a small band of early followers and dismissing the whole group at once.

Another reading: the plural extends backward across history. The deniers are not just mocking one Prophet. They are taunting the entire chain — every messenger who ever warned of a Day. *"If you prophets, all of you down the centuries, are telling the truth — where is it?"* The challenge is meant to indict an entire tradition.

A third reading: the plural is a rhetorical generalization — *"if any of you, whoever speaks this way, is truthful."*

Each reading is a different focal length on the same insult. Together they produce a single sketch: the deniers have widened their target. They are not arguing with one man on one day. They are dismissing a whole class of warners and a whole class of believers, with one word.

[PAUSE]

And now look at what the verse has done.

The condition the deniers attach — *if you are truthful* — is meant to trap the Messenger. *Prove it. Bring the Day. Show us the date. Make it appear or admit you are lying.*

But by preserving the question exactly as they asked it, the Quran has set up a trap of a different kind.

Because the very next ayah — the one Allah places immediately after this one — answers them. *"Say: perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is already close behind you."* (27:72)

The condition *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* assumes that truthfulness must be proven by the immediate appearance of what was promised. But the Quran's response refuses that frame. It says: the promise is not delayed. **You** are the ones standing on the edge of it, and you do not know how close behind you it already is.

The questioners said: *if you are truthful, the Day will come now.*
Allah replies: *the Day's nearness is not on your timetable, and you may already be inside its shadow.*

Their condition was meant to humiliate the Messenger by exposing a gap between what he promised and what they could see. The reply reveals that the gap is not between promise and reality. The gap is between **their perception** and reality. The promise is intact. They are the ones who cannot see how close they stand.

The condition *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — *if you are truthful* — was designed to indict the speaker. The structure of the surah turns it around. By the next ayah, the question of *ṣidq*, truthfulness, has been redirected. Who is really speaking truthfully here? The one who reports about a Day, or the ones who think their own inability to see it means it isn't there?

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Three small grammatical observations. A tense that does not close. A demonstrative that confesses what the speakers mean to deny. A conditional that comes back around and asks itself.

Each one is a precise piece of linguistic architecture. Now let's ask what this construction reveals — not just about words, but about what this kind of question actually **is**, and what it has always done to the human soul.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Demand for a Date as a Defense Against Change

Imagine someone tells you, with full sincerity, that there is a fire approaching your house.

If you actually believe them, you do not negotiate the timeline. You do not say, *"Tell me exactly what minute the fire will arrive, and only then will I take it seriously."* You leave. Or you check. Or you investigate.

But if you do not want to leave — if your house is comfortable, if your routine is settled, if leaving would cost you something you do not want to lose — there is a remarkably effective rhetorical move you can make. You can demand a precise time.

*"When? Exactly when? Give me the date and the hour."*

And then, when they cannot, you can lean back and say: *"Well, then, I'll wait until you can."*

Notice what this move does. It looks like a reasonable request for evidence. It feels, to the one making it, like rigor. It performs intellectual seriousness. *"I am only asking for specifics. I am being precise. I am being scientific."*

But under the performance, the demand for a precise date is doing something else. It is buying time. It is converting an existential warning into a logical puzzle. It is shifting the burden from *me responding to the warning* to *you proving the warning to my satisfaction.* It is a way of staying still while looking like you are thinking carefully.

Every one of us has done some version of this. Not necessarily about the Day of Judgment. About smaller things, sometimes. A doctor tells us our habits will cost us. We say: *prove it. Show me the exact day my heart will give out.* A loved one tells us something is breaking in our relationship. We say: *give me a specific example, an exact date, and only then will I accept this.* A teacher tells us our character needs work. We say: *be more concrete. When, exactly, was I not honest?*

The demand for precise dating is one of the oldest defenses the human soul has against truths it would rather not act on. It is not always conscious. Often we believe we are being reasonable. But the function — the work the demand is doing — is to keep us where we are.

[PAUSE]

This is what the deniers in Makkah were doing. *Matā hādhā al-waʿd.* When? When exactly? Give us the date.

They were not asking because they planned to repent the day before. They were asking because the very absence of a date was the only argument they needed to keep their lives unchanged.

And the Quran's reply, in the very next ayah, refuses to give them what they want. It does not give them a date. Because the date was never the issue. The date was the alibi.

Instead, the Quran tells them — and tells us — *"perhaps some of what you seek to hasten is close behind you."* Which is to say: the day you cannot see is not the day that is absent. It is the day that is already breathing on the back of your neck.

[PAUSE]

What does this change for us, tomorrow?

It changes the way we listen to warnings — about ourselves, about our communities, about our hearts. The next time you find yourself demanding specifics before you will accept a hard truth, pause. Ask yourself, quietly: *Am I trying to evaluate this, or am I trying to dismiss it under the cover of evaluation?*

The demand for precise evidence is not always a sign of careful thinking. Sometimes it is a sign that the truth has reached too close, and we are reaching for the only weapon that buys us another day in the house we are about to lose.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Asymmetry of *Sidq* — Whose Truthfulness Is Really on Trial?

Now consider what the deniers actually said about truthfulness.

*"If you are truthful."*

They put truthfulness — *ṣidq* — on one side of the equation. The Messenger's side. They positioned themselves as the judges, the ones who would decide whether his claim of truthfulness was valid. And the test they set was simple: produce the Day, or accept that you are lying.

Here is the asymmetry. The questioners never put their **own** truthfulness on trial.

Yet think about what they were really doing. They were standing in front of a man who, before the revelation began, they had called *al-Amīn* — the Trustworthy. They had trusted him with their property, their secrets, their disputes. They had never caught him in a lie. And now, the moment he began speaking about something that would require them to change, they introduced the conditional: *if you are truthful.*

The condition was new. The man had not changed. What had changed was the cost of believing him.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it lives inside all of us.

Truthfulness — *ṣidq* — is one of the highest values in the Quran. The Quran praises the *ṣiddīqīn*, the truthful ones, second only to the prophets. But truthfulness is not just about speaking accurate sentences. *Ṣidq* runs deeper. It is the alignment between what is in the heart, what comes out of the tongue, and what is enacted by the limbs. It is integrity in the architectural sense — the whole structure holding together because every part is bearing weight honestly.

Now apply this to the questioners. Were *they* being *ṣādiqīn* when they asked *when?*

A truly truthful question would have been: *"If this is true, what would it require of me?"* That is the question of someone who has placed their own life on the table and is willing to be moved by an answer.

But that was not their question. Their question was *when?* — a question that places the entire burden on the other side and risks nothing of their own.

This is the asymmetry the verse exposes. The deniers framed the scene as a trial of the Messenger's *ṣidq*. But by the time you sit with their question, you see that the only person in the scene risking nothing is the one demanding everything be proven. Their question is not the question of someone seeking truth. It is the question of someone seeking to be left alone.

[PAUSE]

Here is the deeper layer. The Arabic *kuntum ṣādiqīn* — *if you have been habitually truthful* — is, in a way, a question they should have first turned on themselves. The phrasing assumes that being *ṣādiq* is a settled disposition, a character. If you ask whether someone "has been truthful," you are not asking about a single sentence; you are asking about the shape of their life.

Whose life, in that marketplace, had the shape of *ṣidq*?

The man who had never been caught in a lie, who had lived among them for forty years before the revelation began, who had renounced the wealth and status they offered him to recant, who endured boycott and exile rather than soften his message — *his* life had the architectural integrity of *ṣidq*.

The ones asking *when*, by contrast, had built lives in which it was convenient for the Day to never come. Their tongues said *when?*, but their hearts said *please, not yet, and ideally never.* Their question was not aligned with the rest of them. Their *ṣidq* was the missing thing in the room.

[PAUSE]

What does this change tomorrow?

It changes the question we ask when we are evaluating any claim — religious, ethical, relational. Instead of asking only *"Is the speaker truthful?"*, we also ask *"Am I being truthful in the way I am listening?"*

Am I asking *when* because I want to know, or because I want to delay?
Am I demanding evidence because I will act on the answer, or because the demand itself is my way of not acting?
Is my question the question of someone seeking truth, or the question of someone seeking exemption?

The questioners in Makkah forgot that *ṣidq* runs both ways across a conversation. They thought the burden of truthfulness sat only on the messenger. The Quran preserves their question for fourteen centuries so that we, reading it, can notice the absence they did not notice: the absence of *their own* truthfulness in the very moment they were demanding *his*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look back over the seven words.

*Wa yaqūlūna* — a tense that refuses to close, exhibiting the questioners across all time.
*Hādhā al-waʿd* — a demonstrative that confesses how close the warning had already come to their attention.
*In kuntum ṣādiqīn* — a condition that demands truthfulness from the one side never willing to examine its own.

Three pieces of grammar. Three pieces of one psychological architecture: the question that performs evaluation while actually performing avoidance.

The two themes we walked through — the demand for a date as a defense against change, and the asymmetry of *ṣidq* — are not separate from the grammar. They are the same insight, seen from two directions. The grammar shows us *how* the avoidance is built into the words. The themes show us *why* the human soul reaches for this particular construction whenever a warning gets too close.

And the surah's reply, one ayah later, refuses the trap. Not by giving them a date. By telling them that the Day they think they are waiting for may already be standing right behind them, while they are still busy crafting questions about its schedule.

### Questions to Carry

1. When have I asked *when* not because I wanted an answer, but because the asking allowed me to stay still?

2. When the truth about myself comes too close, do I find myself demanding more precise evidence than I would require for things I am willing to accept?

3. Is there a warning in my life — about my health, my relationships, my soul — that I have been holding at arm's length by interrogating its timeline instead of responding to its substance?

4. When I evaluate the truthfulness of others, do I ever turn the same evaluation on the way I am listening?

5. What would it cost me, today, to act as if the Day is close enough that asking *when* is no longer the most important question?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The demand for a precise date is often the soul's way of staying still under the appearance of asking carefully — and the very next breath of revelation will not give us the date, because the date was never what we needed.

### The Closing Invitation

This is not a verse asking you to memorize an eschatological timeline. It is a verse inviting you to notice a habit. The habit of asking *when* when the truer question is *what would change in me if I took this seriously?*

You will not find a date in the Quran for the Day. You will find, instead, an architecture of warnings designed to make the Day **near** in your heart, regardless of where it sits on the calendar. The questioners in Makkah wanted a clock. Allah gave them — and us — a mirror.

Let the question *when?* become, the next time it rises in you, an alarm. Not about the Day's schedule. About your own.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who preserved the questions of those who mocked so that we might learn from their mockery — protect us from the kind of asking that asks in order not to know. Make our questions truthful. Make our listening truthful. And when warnings come, do not let us hide behind the demand for a date when what You are really asking of us is a heart.*

*Āmīn.*
