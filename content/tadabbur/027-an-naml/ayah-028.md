# The Letter and the Withdrawal

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 28

### Arabic

> ٱذْهَب بِّكِتَـٰبِى هَـٰذَا فَأَلْقِهْ إِلَيْهِمْ ثُمَّ تَوَلَّ عَنْهُمْ فَٱنظُرْ مَاذَا يَرْجِعُونَ

### Transliteration

*Idh-hab bi-kitābī hādhā fa-alqih ilayhim thumma tawalla ʿanhum fanẓur mādhā yarjiʿūn.*

### Translation

> Take this letter of mine and deliver it to them, then withdraw from them and observe what they return with.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Picture a moment. A king has just heard a report — a serious one. A whole nation, somewhere south, is worshipping the sun. Their queen is intelligent, their kingdom is wealthy, their throne is magnificent. The report has come from an unusual source: a small bird who was missing from the morning gathering and now stands accused, defending its absence with information.

If you are a king — and not just any king, a king whose authority extends over wind and jinn and armies the size of which the world has not seen — what is the obvious next move?

You send soldiers. You send envoys with demands. You announce yourself with weight, with retinue, with the unmistakable signal: *we have arrived, and we expect submission.*

That is what kings do. That is what kings have always done.

Sulaymān ﷺ does something else.

He writes a letter.

Not a treaty. Not a declaration of war. Not even a sermon. A letter — small enough that a hoopoe can carry it. And then he gives his messenger an instruction so peculiar, so against the grain of what power usually does, that if we are not paying attention we will read past it without realizing what we have just witnessed.

He tells the hoopoe: deliver the letter. Then *withdraw*. Then *watch*.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Why would the most powerful king on earth, with armies of jinn at his command, instruct his messenger to **leave the room** the moment the letter is delivered?

What is Sulaymān protecting? What is he refusing to do? And why does Allah, in the very act of preserving this scene, want us to learn the architecture of how a believer invites another soul toward truth?

Let's sit with this. Because what's hidden in this short ayah is one of the most precise descriptions in the Quran of what *daʿwah* actually is — and what it is not.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to slow down on three things: the word **kitāb** with its possessive ending, the verb **alqih**, and the strange instruction **tawalla ʿanhum**. Each one carries the weight of the whole scene.

---

### Element One: *bi-kitābī hādhā* — "with this letter of mine"

Before we look at the word, let's notice something about how languages work. When a person hands you a document, they could say many things:

"Take this paper."
"Take this message."
"Take this letter of mine."

Each is grammatically valid. But each does something different to the listener. The first is neutral. The second names the content. The third — the third puts the sender into the object itself. *Mine.* The letter is not just paper. It is a piece of me, traveling without me.

In Arabic, the word here is **kitāb** — from the root *k-t-b*, which carries the meaning of binding, joining, fastening together. A *kitāb* is something *bound* — letters bound into words, words bound into meaning, meaning bound into permanence on a surface. It is the same root from which the Quran calls itself **al-Kitāb**.

Notice something. Sulaymān does not send a verbal message through the hoopoe. He could have. The hoopoe speaks — we just heard it speak. Sulaymān could have said: "Go tell her this and this."

He doesn't. He writes.

Stay with me. Because writing does something that speech cannot do.

Speech belongs to the moment. It arrives, it lands, and it disappears into the air. The hearer must respond — or fail to respond — in real time, with the speaker watching. A spoken message is a *demand for immediate reaction*.

A written letter, by contrast, can be **set down**. It can be read once, then read again. It can be carried into a council chamber. It can be deliberated over. It can be returned to in the silence of the night.

Sulaymān is, by the choice of *kitāb*, already giving Bilqīs something that a verbal envoy would not have given her: **time**. Space. The right to reflect before responding.

And then — *kitābī*. *My letter.* The possessive is doing work here. He is not sending a generic royal communication. He is sending **himself**, in the only form a prophet's self can travel without overwhelming the recipient: as text.

Here's what's remarkable. Sulaymān has the power to appear in person, escorted by jinn that could rearrange mountains. If he wanted to coerce, the means are at his disposal. What he chooses instead is the smallest possible vessel — paper and ink and a bird. And he calls it *mine*.

What this invites us to consider is that the prophetic method always reduces the *force* of presentation and increases the *clarity* of content. The truth is in the letter. The messenger is small. The pressure of the sender is, deliberately, **removed from the room**.

---

### Element Two: *fa-alqih ilayhim* — "then cast it to them"

Now we come to the verb. And here, a question.

If you were going to translate "deliver a letter" into Arabic, the most ordinary verbs would be *aʿṭi* (to give), *sallim* (to hand over), *awṣil* (to convey). These are the standard verbs of delivery.

Sulaymān uses none of them. He uses **alqi** — from the root *l-q-y*, the root of *meeting*, but in this Form IV (*ilqāʾ*), it carries a very specific texture: to **cast**, to **throw down**, to **drop into**. The same root and pattern is used elsewhere in the Quran for the casting of Mūsā's staff to the ground, for what is *cast* into the fire, for what is *cast* into a well.

Why this word for a letter?

Let's sit with this. *Alqi* — cast it. Drop it. Set it down in their midst.

Notice what this verb *does not* do. It does not require the recipient's hand to be extended to receive it. It does not require negotiation. It does not require the messenger to wait for acceptance.

The hoopoe is being told: **deposit the letter and step back.** Don't argue with whoever picks it up. Don't explain what it says. Don't lobby. Drop it where it can be found and leave it there.

There is a profound theological pattern in this verb. In the Quran, *truth* is repeatedly described as something *cast* — *yaqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqqi* — Allah *hurls* truth at falsehood. Truth, in the Quranic imagination, is not negotiated into being. It is *deposited* into the world, and it does its work on the world by being **there**, present, available — whether or not the world chooses to receive it.

Sulaymān's letter is going to do the same thing. It will be *cast* into Bilqīs's court. From the moment it lands, the work is no longer the hoopoe's. The work is the **letter's**. The letter, by being what it is, by saying what it says, will produce its effect — or fail to produce it — by its own internal weight.

Here's what's remarkable. The messenger does not have to *convince*. The messenger only has to *deliver clearly*.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is something most of us, when we try to share faith with another person, get backwards. We think the work is in the *persuasion*. In the back-and-forth. In our cleverness, our responses, our ability to answer every objection in the moment.

The Quran is saying: no. The work is in the **clarity of what you cast**, and then in your **willingness to step back from it**. Truth is not your performance. Truth is the letter itself.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Three: *thumma tawalla ʿanhum* — "then withdraw from them"

This is the strangest instruction in the ayah. And it is the heart of everything.

Look at the sequence. *Fa-alqih ilayhim* — *then* (*fa-*) cast it to them. The *fa-* is immediate — drop the letter, then immediately the next step. But the next step is connected with *thumma* — *then*, with a delay, with a deliberate space. *Thumma tawalla ʿanhum* — **then withdraw from them**.

The Arabic grammarians note that *fa-* indicates close succession while *thumma* indicates a more spaced sequence. Sulaymān's instruction has both. *Drop it immediately, then — after a beat — turn away.*

Why the pause? Because the messenger has to make sure the letter has been received before they can turn away. The withdrawal is not a flight. It is a deliberate, observed, *chosen* departure.

And the verb: **tawalla**. From the root *w-l-y*, which means to be near, to be a guardian, to face toward. But in Form V (*tawalla*), the same root *inverts* — it now means *to turn one's back*, to turn away, to withdraw one's face from. The same root that produces *walī* (intimate friend, protector) produces *tawallā* (turning away). The root contains both directions of the relationship.

So Sulaymān is telling the hoopoe: deliver the letter, **and then physically turn your back and leave the space.**

Stay with this. Because in court protocol, in diplomacy, in every culture, this is *not* how a messenger behaves. A messenger waits. A messenger awaits a response to carry back. A messenger stands attentive, ready to argue, ready to clarify, ready to plead.

Sulaymān strips all of that away.

Why?

Here is where the ayah opens completely. Think about what the *presence* of a powerful king's messenger does to a room. Even a small bird, if it is known to be from Sulaymān, exerts a kind of pressure. People in the room cannot deliberate freely if they know they are being watched. They cannot voice doubt. They cannot push back. They cannot say *"What if he is lying?"* or *"What does this really mean?"* or *"Let us consult."*

The messenger's continued presence would **distort** the response.

By withdrawing, the hoopoe protects something that Sulaymān deeply wants Bilqīs to have: **the freedom to respond as her real self.** Not the self that performs for a watching power, but the self that turns to her council, weighs the matter, considers her people, considers her gods, considers her gold and her throne and her dignity, and *chooses*.

[PAUSE]

There is no daʿwah without this withdrawal. Let me say that again. **There is no daʿwah without this withdrawal.**

If we hover, we coerce. If we hover, the other person performs for us instead of deliberating with themselves. If we hover, what we get back is not their real response but their *managed* response — the response shaped by their awareness of our gaze.

What this invites us to consider — and we will return to this in the second part — is that *the space of withdrawal is itself part of the gift*. The letter without the withdrawal is pressure. The letter *with* the withdrawal is invitation.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Four (a small but crucial one): *fa-nẓur mādhā yarjiʿūn*

Before we close the linguistic journey, one more piece. After the withdrawal, Sulaymān says: *fa-nẓur* — *then observe*. **Observe what they return with.**

Notice: he doesn't say *fa-smaʿ* (then *listen*). He doesn't say *fa-stalim al-jawāb* (then *receive the answer*). He says **observe** — *nẓur*, the verb of *looking* with attention.

The hoopoe is being told to position itself somewhere it can *see* the response without being *in* it. Watch from a distance. Notice not just what they say but how they receive, how they deliberate, what their bodies and their decisions reveal.

And the response itself is described as *mādhā yarjiʿūn* — *what they return with*. *Yarjiʿūn* is from *r-j-ʿ*, *return*. It implies that the response will come *back* to where the letter was *cast*. The letter was deposited; the response will return to that same point.

Even the verb of response carries the architecture of the whole ayah: **the letter is set down, space is created, and the response comes back into that space on the other person's own time.**

[PAUSE]

---

### The Bridge

We have looked at four words: *kitāb*, *alqi*, *tawalla*, *nẓur*. Each one strips away a different form of pressure that a powerful sender would normally exert. The letter (not the speech) gives time. The casting (not the convincing) gives content without coercion. The withdrawal (not the hovering) gives the freedom to deliberate. The observing (not the demanding) gives space for the response to emerge.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About how truth actually reaches a soul, and about what role we are permitted to play in that reaching.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Difference Between Pressure and Invitation

Imagine you are trying to convince someone you love — a sibling, a parent, a close friend — of something you believe is true and important. Maybe it is about their health. Maybe it is about their relationships. Maybe it is about faith itself.

You sit across from them. You say your piece. And then, almost immediately, you watch their face. Are they nodding? Are they pushing back? Are they convinced yet? You add another argument. You restate. You quote a study. You bring in another example. You feel yourself getting warmer, more insistent, more *present* in the room.

And what happens to them?

They retreat. They go quiet. They say "I'll think about it" — which both of you know means *please stop*. Or worse, they argue back, not because they have considered the substance but because your *pressure* has provoked their defense. The room is now full of two egos, not one truth.

This is the failure mode that Sulaymān's instruction guards against. The hoopoe is being trained not to do this.

There is a difference between a *presented* truth and an *imposed* truth, and the difference is not in the content. It is in **how much space the presenter takes up after the presentation**.

A truth presented and then *held in the room by the presenter's continued presence* becomes pressure. The recipient is no longer free to weigh it; they are weighing it under surveillance. Their response is shaped by you.

A truth presented and then *released* — the presenter withdrawing, stepping back, removing their gaze — becomes an invitation. The recipient is now alone with the truth. They can argue with it. They can sit with it. They can hate it for a week and then return to it. They can let it ripen.

[PAUSE]

This is what Sulaymān understood. And it is what almost none of us understand when we are trying to be useful in someone else's spiritual life.

Notice the asymmetry. Sulaymān has *more* power than any other daʿī in human history. He commands jinn. He understands the speech of birds and ants. He could, with a word, transport Bilqīs's throne across vast distances — and indeed, later in the surah, he does. He could compel her in a thousand ways.

He chooses, instead, **to compel her not at all.**

The withdrawal is not because Sulaymān is weak. The withdrawal is because Sulaymān knows that *belief produced by pressure is not belief*. It is performance. And Allah does not want the performance. He wants the *real return* — *mādhā yarjiʿūn* — the response that actually comes from the depths of the person, not the response that comes from their fear of the messenger.

Here is the universal principle: **the only faith worth eliciting is the faith that survives your absence.**

If Bilqīs's submission requires Sulaymān's armies in the room, it is not submission to Allah. It is submission to Sulaymān. The withdrawal is what makes it possible for her response, when it comes, to be hers — and therefore, to belong to Allah rather than to the king who delivered the message.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how we live tomorrow?

When you next try to share something — a verse, an idea, a concern — with someone you love, try this. Say it once. Say it clearly. And then **leave the room**. Not physically necessarily, but inwardly. Stop watching their face for the convinced look. Stop adding the second argument. Stop hovering on whether they have come around. *Cast the letter and turn your back.*

You will discover something startling: people respond to truths they were given permission to wrestle with. They almost never respond to truths someone insisted they accept while standing over them.

The withdrawal *is* the daʿwah. The letter is just the seed.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Believer's Relationship to Outcomes

There is a deeper layer here, and it concerns what we believe about *who actually moves a heart*.

When we hover after speaking truth — when we cannot let the room go, when we keep watching, keep arguing, keep adding — we are revealing something about our theology. We are revealing that, somewhere underneath, we believe that *we* are the cause of the other person's belief. That if we step away, the truth will not work without us. That our continued presence is what is going to push them across the line.

Sulaymān's instruction is a quiet, devastating correction to that belief.

Listen to the structure again: *cast the letter, turn away, observe what comes back*. The verb of observation, *nẓur*, places the hoopoe in the position of a *spectator* to the unfolding of someone else's response. Not a *participant*. A spectator.

Why? Because what happens between Bilqīs and her own heart, between Bilqīs and the truth she has just been given, is **not the hoopoe's territory**. It is not even Sulaymān's. It is Allah's.

> إِنَّكَ لَا تَهْدِى مَنْ أَحْبَبْتَ وَلَـٰكِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَهْدِى مَن يَشَآءُ
>
> *Indeed, you do not guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills.* (28:56)

This ayah, addressed to the Prophet ﷺ himself about his beloved uncle, reveals the same architecture. Guidance is not produced by the messenger's exertion. Guidance is given by Allah, into the space the messenger creates by their clarity and their **restraint**.

Stay with me on this. Because this is the door through which every parent, every friend, every daʿī, every teacher must walk if they are going to last.

If you believe *you* are the cause of someone's belief, then their disbelief becomes *your* failure. Every time they don't respond, every time the letter doesn't seem to land, you experience it as a wound. You add more pressure. You become more strident. You burn out, you become bitter, you start to resent the very people you are trying to help.

If you believe Allah is the cause of someone's belief, and your role is to **cast clearly and then withdraw**, then their response is *between them and Allah*. Their delay is *between them and Allah*. Their resistance is *between them and Allah*. Your job ended when the letter was delivered.

You can love them and stay free. You can hope for them and not collapse on their answer. You can return, years later, and ask gently — and discover, perhaps, that the letter has been working in them all along, quietly, in your absence.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most freeing teachings in the Quran. And it is hidden inside what looks like a simple instruction to a bird.

What does this change about how we live tomorrow?

It changes how you carry your loved ones who have not yet found what you have found. You stop performing rescue. You start performing *delivery and withdrawal*. You speak the truth in love, clearly, with care for how it is cast. And then you turn your back — not on *them*, but on your own *anxiety about their response*. You leave them in the only Hands that can actually do the work.

You become, like the hoopoe, a small messenger of something much larger than yourself. You carry the letter. You drop it where it can be found. You step away. And you let Allah do what only Allah can do.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

The **linguistic discoveries** — *kitāb* instead of speech, *alqi* instead of plead, *tawalla* instead of hover, *nẓur* instead of demand — and the **two themes** — pressure versus invitation, and the believer's relationship to outcomes — are the same insight reaching us from two directions.

From the linguistic direction: every word in this ayah is calibrated to **reduce the messenger's presence** and **increase the recipient's freedom**.

From the thematic direction: this is because faith is something Allah produces in the freedom of a soul, not something a messenger manufactures in the constraint of one.

The ayah is, in effect, a perfect miniature of prophetic methodology. Eleven words. A whole theology of how to invite a heart toward truth without violating the freedom that makes the heart's response meaningful in the first place.

And notice the architecture: the ayah moves from *closeness* to *distance* to *observation*. Sulaymān's letter is intimate (*kitābī* — *my* letter). Its delivery is decisive (*alqi* — cast). Its aftermath is space (*tawalla* — withdraw). Its closure is patient attention (*nẓur* — watch). The whole shape is the shape of love that does not coerce. Of authority that does not impose. Of presence that knows when to **disappear so that something larger can act.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When you speak truth to someone you love, do you cast the letter and step back — or do you stay in the room, watching their face, ready to push?

2. Whose belief, in your life right now, are you trying to produce through your own continued pressure? What would it look like to *withdraw* and let Allah work in your absence?

3. Is the truth you carry strong enough that you trust it to do its work without you hovering over it? If not, what does that say about your relationship to the truth itself?

4. Have you confused *love* with *surveillance*? Have you confused *care* with *control*?

5. What letter has Allah cast into *your* life — what truth that has been sitting in your awareness for years, doing its quiet work, waiting for your response in your own time?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> Truth is delivered clearly and then released into the freedom of the other — because the only faith worth eliciting is the faith that survives your absence.

---

### The Closing Invitation

Become a hoopoe. Carry the letter. Cast it where it can be found. And then turn your back — not from coldness, but from trust. Trust that the One who taught you the truth can deliver it to another heart without your help. Trust that withdrawal is not abandonment but reverence — reverence for the soul that has the right to deliberate, to wrestle, to take the time, to return at last with *mādhā yarjiʿūn* — a response that is genuinely theirs, and therefore genuinely Allah's.

The people in your life are not yours to convince. They are Allah's to guide. Your work is the clarity of the letter and the dignity of the withdrawal.

### Du'a

O Allah, teach us the wisdom of Sulaymān — to speak clearly, to cast truth without coercion, and to withdraw with trust in You. Free us from the anxiety that makes us hover over those we love. Let us be small messengers of Your great letter, content to deliver and step back, watching with patience for the response You alone can produce. And make us, ourselves, soft enough to receive the letters You have cast into our own lives — without performance, without pressure, in the quiet honesty of our own return. *Āmīn.*
