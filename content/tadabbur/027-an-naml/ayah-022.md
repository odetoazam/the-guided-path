# The Hoopoe's Return: When the Small Brings What the Great Did Not Know

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> فَمَكَثَ غَيْرَ بَعِيدٍ فَقَالَ أَحَطتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ وَجِئْتُكَ مِن سَبَإٍۭ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ

**Transliteration:**
> Fa-makatha ghayra baʿīdin fa-qāla aḥaṭtu bimā lam tuḥiṭ bihi wa-ji'tuka min Saba'in bi-naba'in yaqīn.

**Translation:**
> "But it stayed not long: it came and said, 'I have encompassed [in knowledge] that which you have not encompassed, and I have come to you from Saba with certain news.'"

---

### Rebuilding the World

To enter this ayah, you have to understand who is being spoken to.

Sulayman, peace be upon him, is not an ordinary king. The Quran has just shown us a man whose army contains jinn, humans, and birds — *all of them in disciplined ranks*. He commands the wind. He understands the language of ants. Earlier in the surah, an ant warned its colony of his approach, and he smiled. He has just inherited David's kingdom, and a few ayat earlier, he said something staggering: **"We have been taught the speech of the birds, and we have been given of every thing"** — *wa ūtīnā min kulli shay'*.

*Of every thing.*

This is the man who, moments before this ayah, looked up at his bird formation, scanned the ranks, and noticed one missing soldier. Not a battalion. Not a regiment. *One bird.* The hoopoe — *al-hudhud* — small, striped, crowned with a crest of feathers. And Sulayman, this prophet-king who has been "given of every thing," issued a thunderous threat: *"I will surely punish him with severe punishment, or slaughter him, unless he brings me a clear authority."*

Now picture the assembly. The court of Sulayman. Jinn standing in formation. Generals waiting. The wind perhaps still in the trees. And every eye turned to the empty space in the bird-rank where the hoopoe should be.

[PAUSE]

This is the world the next ayah enters.

The smallest creature in the assembly is about to return — and the first words from its mouth will not be apology. They will not be excuse. They will be something that, in any other court, would have ended its life on the spot.

It is going to tell the prophet-king: *I know something you don't.*

---

### The Hook

Notice the structural daring of this moment. The Quran is about to put, in the mouth of a *bird*, a verb that the Quran usually reserves for divine knowledge. And it is going to make that bird use that verb to draw a line — not between itself and other birds, but between itself and a *prophet*.

What is Allah teaching us, by letting the smallest voice in the room say the largest thing?

Stay with that question. We are going to spend the next hour discovering that this single ayah — fourteen words — is one of the Quran's most quietly devastating meditations on what knowledge actually is, who possesses it, and why the truest sign of wisdom is the ability to *receive* it from someone you outrank.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will open three things in this ayah. Each one, on its own, would be enough to sit with for a week. Together, they show us something extraordinary about how Allah composes meaning.

---

### Element One: *Fa-makatha ghayra baʿīd* — "He stayed, not long"

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, before you can describe an action, you can describe the *quality* of its duration. Time itself becomes part of the verb. We do this in English too, but loosely. We say "he came back quickly" or "he didn't take long." Arabic is more precise. It can negate the *distance* of a duration — *ghayra baʿīd* literally means "not far," "not distant" — used here for time, not space.

So Allah is not just saying "he came back soon." He is saying *the gap of his absence was not far*. The temporal distance was small.

#### Opening the word

The verb here is *makatha* — from the root *m-k-th* — which means to stay, to tarry, to remain in a place. It is the same root the Quran uses elsewhere for the staying of Musa with the people of Madyan, for the staying of the People of the Cave. It is a verb of *deliberate dwelling*. Not just "to be somewhere," but "to remain there with intention."

So already we have a tension. *Makatha* — he dwelt, he tarried, he stayed. But then immediately: *ghayra baʿīd* — not for long.

He stayed — but not long.

#### Sitting with this

Why does Allah build the sentence this way? Why not just say *fa-jā'a sarīʿan* — "and he came quickly"?

Notice something. The verb *makatha* does not describe the journey. It does not describe the flight back. It describes the *staying* — the time spent away gathering what he gathered. Allah is telling us: he was *somewhere*, doing *something*, and the duration of that doing was bounded.

Here's what's remarkable. The hoopoe was not late because he was negligent. He was *staying* — *makatha* — and what he was doing during that staying was *acquiring*. He was learning. He was, in the silent language of this verb, *being absent for a reason*.

And Allah qualifies the absence: *ghayra baʿīd*. Not far. Not long. Just enough.

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet teaching buried here for any of us who have ever been absent from a place we were expected — late to a duty, missing from a station, behind on a task — and have tried to explain ourselves. The hoopoe does not begin with explanation. The Quran begins with *narration*: he stayed, but not long. The narrator vouches for the duration before the speaker has to defend it.

What this invites us to consider is that **Allah Himself bears witness, in the very grammar of the verse, to the legitimacy of the hoopoe's absence**. Before the hoopoe says a single word, the syntax has already exonerated him. *Ghayra baʿīd* is the divine notation in the margin: *this absence was not what it looked like*.

How often have we judged someone's absence — from a meeting, from a friendship, from prayer, from the ranks — without knowing what they were *staying* over?

[PAUSE]

#### What this single phrase reveals

The first three words of the ayah teach us that the appearance of being missing and the reality of being missing are two different things. And Allah, before letting any human voice speak, places His own grammar between us and our judgment.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: *Aḥaṭtu bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih* — "I have encompassed what you have not encompassed"

This is the line that should stop us cold.

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, there is a verb root *ḥ-w-ṭ*, from which we get the word *ḥā'iṭ* — a wall. From the same root we get *iḥāṭa* — to surround, to encompass, to wall something in completely so that nothing escapes. To know something with *iḥāṭa* is not just to know *about* it. It is to have it *encircled*. Every side. No gap. No part of it standing outside your knowledge.

This is the verb the Quran uses, again and again, for the knowledge of Allah. *Wa Allāhu bi-kulli shay'in muḥīṭ* — "And Allah encompasses all things" (4:126). *Innahu bi-kulli shay'in muḥīṭ* — "Indeed He encompasses all things" (41:54). The Throne Verse — Ayat al-Kursi — tells us about creation: *wa lā yuḥīṭūna bi-shay'in min ʿilmihi illā bimā shā'* — "and they encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills."

*Iḥāṭa* — encompassing knowledge — is a divine signature. It is the kind of knowing that belongs, in its absolute form, only to Allah.

#### Opening the word

Now read what the hoopoe says. *Aḥaṭtu bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih*. "I have encompassed what you have not encompassed."

The bird uses *iḥāṭa* — twice. Once for itself: *aḥaṭtu*. Once for the prophet-king: *lam tuḥiṭ*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. A bird is using a divine-class verb. And it is using that verb to assert that *its* encompassing of a particular matter is greater than the prophet's.

#### Deepening through layers

How can this be permissible? How does Allah let this language stand?

Here is what we have to notice. The hoopoe does not say *aḥaṭtu bi-kulli shay'* — "I have encompassed *every* thing." It says *aḥaṭtu **bimā** lam tuḥiṭ bih* — "I have encompassed *what* you have not encompassed."

The *bimā* — "that which" — is the entire safety mechanism of the sentence. It is bounded. It is specific. The hoopoe's encompassing is not absolute. It is encompassing *of one matter*. And that matter happens to be a matter the prophet-king has not, in fact, encompassed.

This is staggering. Because Sulayman has just said, a few ayat earlier, *ūtīnā min kulli shay'* — "we have been given of every thing." And here Allah, almost immediately, sends a bird to say: *but not this thing*.

Notice what is happening structurally. The Quran is establishing a principle through narrative arrangement: even the prophet-king who is given of every thing has not been given *every* thing. There remains, in the kingdom of Sulayman, knowledge that resides in the hoopoe and not in the throne. And Allah lets the hoopoe *name* that asymmetry, in the language of *iḥāṭa*, the language of comprehensive knowing.

[PAUSE]

#### Reading the absence

Now here is something the linguistic table forces us to see. Look at what is *not* in this sentence.

The hoopoe does not say "I know something you don't." It does not use the verb *ʿalima* — to know — which is the common verb of knowing. It uses *aḥāṭa* — to encompass. Why?

Because *ʿilm* — knowledge — can be partial. You can know *of* something without knowing it fully. *Iḥāṭa* is the verb of *complete* grasp of a *bounded* thing. The hoopoe is saying: *with respect to this matter, my knowledge is not partial. It is complete. There is no part of this matter that has escaped me.*

And the prophet-king's knowledge, with respect to this same matter, is not just incomplete — it is, the hoopoe asserts, *absent*. *Lam tuḥiṭ bih*. You have not encompassed it at all.

What this invites us to consider is that the Quran is drawing the most precise possible line between two kinds of cognitive authority. The prophet has been given vast knowledge — knowledge of the speech of birds, knowledge of the languages of nations, knowledge of the courts of the unseen. But on this one bounded matter — the existence of a queen and her people in Saba — *the bird has the encompassing, and the prophet does not*.

[PAUSE]

#### Where the classical scholars converge

The mufassirūn — Ibn Kathīr, al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, al-Ṭabarī — all linger over this moment, and what is striking is that none of them reads the hoopoe's speech as impudence. They read it the same way: as a divinely permitted truth-telling that establishes a foundational principle. Al-Rāzī notes that the verse establishes *that the inferior may possess, by Allah's distribution, what the superior does not* — and that this is not a flaw in the superior, but a feature of how Allah distributes knowledge across creation. Al-Qurṭubī observes that Sulayman's response — which we will see in the coming ayah — is not anger but careful examination. He neither rejects the bird's claim nor accepts it uncritically. He says *sananẓuru* — "we will see." That measured response is itself a teaching.

Each scholar adds a layer. Together they sketch the same picture: this is the Quran teaching us that **possessing authority and possessing knowledge are not the same thing**, and that the wise authority is the one who can hear *aḥaṭtu bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih* from a smaller mouth without his throne shaking.

[PAUSE]

#### What this phrase reveals

The hoopoe's *iḥāṭa* claim is not arrogance. It is precision. It is bounded knowing claimed in bounded language. And the fact that Allah allows this claim to stand — and to stand against a *prophet* — is one of the most quietly radical moments in the Quran's vision of knowledge.

### Element Three: *Bi-naba'in yaqīn* — "with certain news"

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, there are several words for "news." The most common is *khabar* — a report, an item of information. Allah uses *khabar* often, including elsewhere in this very passage. But here, when the hoopoe describes what it brings, the word changes. It says *naba'*.

*Naba'* is not just news. *Naba'* — from the root *n-b-ʾ* — is news of *consequence*. News that has weight. News that, when it arrives, *changes* something. The same root gives us the word *nabī* — prophet — because a prophet is one who brings *naba'*, news that reorients the world. The Quran calls the Day of Judgment *al-naba' al-ʿaẓīm* — "the great news" — because no news will ever weigh more.

So when the hoopoe says it brings *naba'*, it is signaling: this is not gossip. This is not a report from the field. This is news of consequence. News that will move thrones.

#### Opening the word — and what qualifies it

But Allah does not let the word stand alone. He attaches a qualifier: *yaqīn*.

*Yaqīn* — from the root *y-q-n* — means certainty. Not opinion. Not strong belief. *Yaqīn* is the kind of certainty that has no doubt clinging to its edges. It is the highest cognitive station the Quran names for human beings. *Ḥattā ya'tiyaka al-yaqīn* — "until certainty comes to you" (15:99) — is what Allah commands the Prophet to worship until.

So *naba'in yaqīn* is "news of consequence, with certainty attached."

#### Sitting with the pairing

Now think about what the hoopoe is doing in this single phrase.

It is not just saying "I have important information." It is making three claims at once, stacked:

First: what I bring is *consequential* — *naba'*.
Second: what I bring is *certain* — *yaqīn*.
Third: I myself have *encompassed* it — *aḥaṭtu*.

The hoopoe is offering the prophet-king the highest possible epistemic credentials. *I have surrounded this matter completely. The matter is of consequence. And what I report is not conjecture but certainty.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. In the previous ayah, when Sulayman threatened the hoopoe, he demanded *sulṭān mubīn* — "clear authority." Clear evidence. Now the hoopoe returns and, in a single phrase, satisfies that demand in advance. *Aḥaṭtu* — I have encompassed it. *Naba'* — it is consequential. *Yaqīn* — it is certain.

The hoopoe has heard the threat and responded — not with self-defense, but with *epistemic structure*. It does not say "don't punish me." It says, in effect: *here is the authority you asked for, in the only form authority can take — comprehensive grasp of a consequential matter known with certainty.*

#### The sonic texture

Listen to the sound. *Bi-naba'in yaqīn*. The two words rhyme. *Naba'in* and *yaqīn* — both ending in the long *ī* with the *n* — twinned acoustically. They feel inseparable when you say them. This is not decoration. The Quran is *sonically binding* consequence to certainty. The bird is bringing news that *sounds* welded to its own truthfulness.

And notice how the phrase comes at the very end of the ayah. The ayah has been building — *fa-makatha ghayra baʿīd* — *fa-qāla aḥaṭtu* — *wa ji'tuka* — and lands on *bi-naba'in yaqīn*. Certainty is the resting note. The ayah closes on the word that closes doubt.

[PAUSE]

#### What this phrase reveals

The hoopoe is teaching us — in fourteen words — what it means to actually *bring* knowledge to someone in authority. You do not bring it as opinion. You do not bring it as suggestion. You bring it with structure: *I have grasped it fully (iḥāṭa), it is consequential (naba'), and it is certain (yaqīn)*. This is the architecture of trustworthy speech.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have stayed with three things. The grammar of absence — *ghayra baʿīd* — that vouches for the missing one before they speak. The verb of encompassing — *iḥāṭa* — that the bird uses to assert knowledge a prophet does not possess. And the pairing of consequence with certainty — *naba'in yaqīn* — that gives the hoopoe's speech its epistemic weight.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Distribution of Knowledge

#### Where the listener already lives

You have, in your life, met someone you outrank — by age, by position, by education, by station — who knows something you do not.

Maybe it was a child who saw something you missed. Maybe it was a junior colleague who understood the politics of the room better than you did. Maybe it was a parent with no formal schooling who read a person more accurately in five minutes than you read them in five years. Maybe it was the cleaner in the office who knew which executives were genuinely kind and which were performing.

Stay there. That moment. The moment when the smaller voice in the room held the larger truth.

What happens, usually, in that moment? Often, the larger person discounts the smaller. The senior dismisses the junior. The expert overrides the layperson. The teacher stops listening to the student. The parent stops hearing the child. The hierarchy of *position* gets confused with the hierarchy of *knowing*, and authority refuses to receive what authority did not produce.

#### Letting the ayah illuminate

This is precisely the moment the Quran is illuminating.

Sulayman has every reason to dismiss the hoopoe. The bird was absent without permission. The bird was *threatened* moments earlier. The bird is, in any visible hierarchy, the smallest creature in the assembly. And the bird walks back into the court and says, with no hedging, *I have encompassed what you have not encompassed*.

And what does the Quran do? It does not punish the bird for its language. It vindicates the bird's language. The next ayah will show that the news *is* certain. Saba *does* exist. The queen *does* worship the sun. Sulayman will write the letter, and the entire next movement of the surah — the queen's conversion, the throne brought across continents — *all of it* unfolds because the smallest member of the assembly was permitted to say *I know what you do not*.

[PAUSE]

#### The deeper movement

Here is what the Quran is teaching us about the architecture of knowledge itself. Knowledge is not distributed by rank. It is distributed by *placement*. Allah places certain knowings in certain creatures because *that is where He has chosen to place them*. The hoopoe knew Saba because the hoopoe had been there. The hoopoe had flown over those people, seen the queen, noticed the worship of the sun, registered the wealth and the throne. Sulayman, for all his vastness, had not been to Saba. The knowledge lived where the witness lived.

The prophet had been given of every thing — but not the witnessing of every place.

What this invites us to consider is that Allah has, by design, *scattered knowledge*. He has not piled it all in one place. He has not given any single creature, any single position, any single rank, the entirety of what can be known. And the wise person — even the prophet-king — is the one who knows that wisdom often arrives from below the throne, not from above it.

#### The paradox

Notice what does *not* happen in this story. The hoopoe is not promoted. After this episode, it does not become a vizier. It does not get a higher rank. It returns, presumably, to its place in the bird-formation. Its station does not change.

But its *knowledge* mattered. Its station and its knowledge were not the same thing.

This is the paradox the Quran wants us to hold. Station and knowledge are *not* identical. A creature may have higher station and lower knowing on a particular matter. A creature may have lower station and higher knowing on a particular matter. And the just society — the just court, the just family, the just heart — is the one that can receive each according to what it actually has, without confusing the two.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Tomorrow, you will be in a room with someone you outrank. A child, an employee, a younger sibling, a student, a junior. They will say something. And in the half-second before you respond, ask yourself: *am I about to discount this because of where they sit, or am I about to weigh it because of what it actually is?*

The hoopoe taught a prophet. Allah recorded it in His book. He recorded it forever. He recorded it because He wanted us to know that the smallest mouth in the room may be carrying *naba'in yaqīn*, and the cost of dismissing it is not a small cost.

Sulayman did not pay that cost. He listened. The next ayah opens with him saying *sananẓuru* — *we shall see*. He neither accepted blindly nor rejected reflexively. He examined.

That is what wisdom looks like when knowledge arrives from below.

### Theme Two: The Architecture of Trustworthy Speech

#### Where the listener already lives

Think about how, in your life, important news has been delivered to you.

A doctor walks into the room and tells you something about your body. A lawyer calls and tells you something about your case. A teacher tells you something about your child. A friend tells you something about a person you love. In each case, you are receiving *naba'* — news of consequence. And in each case, the *form* in which the news arrives shapes how you receive it.

Some news arrives wrapped in hedges. *I think maybe possibly perhaps it could be that...* — and you find yourself unable to act on it, because the speaker has not given you anything firm enough to stand on.

Other news arrives wrapped in overconfidence. *Trust me, I know exactly what's happening...* — and you find yourself suspicious, because no human grasps anything that completely.

The trustworthy speaker, the one whose news actually moves you, sits in a third place. They tell you what they have *encompassed*, they tell you it is *consequential*, and they tell you what level of *certainty* it carries. They give you the structure. They let you weigh it.

#### Letting the ayah illuminate

This is what the hoopoe does in fourteen words.

*Aḥaṭtu* — I have encompassed it. *(Here is the scope of my grasp.)*
*Bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih* — what you have not encompassed. *(Here is what is new in what I'm bringing.)*
*Wa ji'tuka min Saba'* — I have come to you from Saba. *(Here is the source.)*
*Bi-naba'in yaqīn* — with certain news. *(Here is the weight, and the certainty.)*

That is a complete epistemic packet. The hoopoe has, in a single sentence, declared: scope, novelty, source, weight, and certainty.

[PAUSE]

#### Building outward

Now think about your own speech. When you bring news to someone, when you bring an opinion to a meeting, when you bring a concern to a spouse, when you bring a question to a teacher — does your speech carry this architecture?

Or does it slide out without scope, without source, without certainty? *I just feel like... maybe... I don't know, but...*

The Quran is not asking us to be theatrical. It is showing us, through a *bird*, what it looks like when speech is *built* — when a speaker has done the interior work of grasping what they grasp, knowing what they don't, registering the weight, and reporting honestly.

There is a kind of speech that *deserves* to be heard, and there is a kind of speech that does not. The Quran, by placing the hoopoe's speech at this hinge of the surah, is teaching us the difference.

#### The deeper teaching

Notice something. The hoopoe does not begin its speech with *yā nabī Allāh* — "O prophet of Allah." It does not begin with apology. It does not begin with praise. It begins with *aḥaṭtu*.

This could read as rudeness. But the Quran does not record it as rudeness. Why?

Because in the Quranic vision, when *naba'in yaqīn* — consequential certain news — is at stake, *the news itself is the priority*. The hoopoe has come not to perform deference but to deliver. And deference, in that moment, would have been a form of disrespect to the urgency of what it carried.

What this invites us to consider is that **sometimes the most respectful thing you can do for someone in authority is to deliver them, fast and structured, the truth they need — without burying it in courtesy.** The hoopoe respected Sulayman by *not wasting his time*. The structure of the speech itself was the deference.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Tomorrow, you will need to bring something to someone — a concern, a piece of information, a difficult truth. Before you speak, build the architecture.

What have I actually grasped? *(Aḥaṭtu — what is the real scope of my knowing?)*
What does the listener not yet have? *(Bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih — what's new?)*
Where did this come from? *(Min Saba' — what's my source?)*
How weighty and how certain is it? *(Naba'in yaqīn — am I bringing rumor or news?)*

When you have built this, then speak. Without hedge. Without overflow. Without ornament.

The hoopoe, in fourteen words, taught a prophet. We have a lifetime, and we still struggle to say one true sentence cleanly.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the ayah's full shape.

> *Fa-makatha ghayra baʿīd* — He stayed, but not long. **(Allah vouches for the absence.)**
> *Fa-qāla aḥaṭtu bimā lam tuḥiṭ bih* — He said, "I have encompassed what you have not." **(The bird names the asymmetry.)**
> *Wa ji'tuka min Saba'in bi-naba'in yaqīn* — "And I have come to you from Saba with certain news." **(The bird offers the structure.)**

Three movements. First, divine narration that protects the speaker. Second, the speaker's claim about knowledge. Third, the speaker's offering of structured news.

Notice what this means. **Allah Himself sets up the ground for the bird to speak.** Before the hoopoe says anything, the syntax has already cleared the air — *ghayra baʿīd*. The Quran does not let us judge the bird before it has opened its mouth. Allah does the work of *receiving* the bird first, so that we, the listeners, are softened toward what comes next.

This is mercy in the structure. This is mercy in the grammar.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side, we saw three things: a verb of staying that means more than absence, a verb of encompassing that asserts bounded but complete knowing, and a pairing of consequence with certainty that builds an epistemic structure.

From the thematic side, we saw two things: knowledge is distributed across creation by Allah's placement, not by rank — and trustworthy speech has an architecture that any speaker, even a small one, can build.

Both arrive at the same place: **the universe is built such that the smallest voice may carry the largest truth, and the wise listener — even the prophet — is the one who has trained themselves to hear it.**

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Let them stay with you for the week.

**1.** Whose absence have I judged without knowing what they were *staying* over?

**2.** Who in my life — younger, junior, smaller in station — has been telling me something I have been refusing to hear because of where they sit, not what they said?

**3.** When I bring news to others, do I build the architecture — *what I have grasped, what is new, what the source is, how certain* — or do I leak hedged speech that no one can act on?

**4.** Where, in my own knowledge, am I confusing *being given of every thing* with *being given every thing*? What might I be missing because I have stopped expecting to learn from below?

**5.** What is the *Saba* in my life — the place I have not flown to, the people I have not yet seen — and who is the *hoopoe* trying to bring me news from there?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> Allah scatters knowledge across creation by His own placement, and the truest sign of wisdom is the capacity to receive a structured truth from a smaller mouth without your throne shaking.

---

### The Closing Invitation

This week, listen for the hoopoe.

It will not announce itself. It will not arrive with credentials. It will be the small voice in the room — the child, the junior, the stranger, the person you would normally discount. And it will say something that, if you let it land, will change what happens next.

Do not punish it. Do not dismiss it. Do not require it to wrap its truth in deference before you weigh it.

Say what Sulayman said in the next ayah: *sananẓur* — *we shall see*. Neither accept blindly nor reject reflexively. Examine. Weigh. Send the letter if the news warrants it.

The Quran recorded this moment forever because Allah wants us to know: there is a hoopoe in every assembly. And the kingdom that listens is not weakened by listening. It is enlarged.

---

### Du'a

O Allah, who placed knowing in the small as You placed it in the great — open our ears to the voices we have trained ourselves to discount.

Teach us to weigh what is brought to us by what it actually is, not by where the bringer sits. Make our own speech, when we speak, an architecture of *what we have grasped, what is consequential, and what is certain* — and protect us from the speech that hedges what we know and the speech that overflows what we don't.

And when we, like the hoopoe, are the smaller voice in the room carrying *naba'in yaqīn* — give us the courage to deliver it cleanly, and place us before listeners whose thrones do not shake when truth arrives from below.

Āmīn.
