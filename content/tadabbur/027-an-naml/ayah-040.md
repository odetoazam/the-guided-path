# The Throne Before the Blink: When Power Becomes Gratitude

## Introduction — The World Before the Words

Listen to the verse first. Sit with the Arabic before any explanation:

> قَالَ ٱلَّذِى عِندَهُۥ عِلْمٌ مِّنَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن يَرْتَدَّ إِلَيْكَ طَرْفُكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَءَاهُ مُسْتَقِرًّا عِندَهُۥ قَالَ هَـٰذَا مِن فَضْلِ رَبِّى لِيَبْلُوَنِىٓ ءَأَشْكُرُ أَمْ أَكْفُرُ ۖ وَمَن شَكَرَ فَإِنَّمَا يَشْكُرُ لِنَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَفَرَ فَإِنَّ رَبِّى غَنِىٌّ كَرِيمٌ

*Qāla alladhī ʿindahu ʿilmun mina-l-kitābi anā ātīka bihi qabla an yartadda ilayka ṭarfuk. Falammā raʾāhu mustaqirran ʿindahu qāla hādhā min faḍli rabbī liyabluwanī a-ashkuru am akfur. Wa man shakara fa-innamā yashkuru linafsih. Wa man kafara fa-inna rabbī ghaniyyun karīm.*

"The one who had knowledge from the Scripture said: I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you. Then when he saw it set firmly in his presence, he said: This is from the favor of my Lord — to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful. And whoever is grateful, his gratitude is only for himself. And whoever is ungrateful — indeed, my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

### Rebuild the World

To enter this ayah, you have to feel where you are standing.

Sulayman ﷺ — a prophet *and* a king. A man given a kingdom unlike any other. The wind beneath his command. The jinn at his disposal. A language with the birds. He is, by any measure history can hold, the most powerful human being who has ever sat on a throne.

And right now, in his court, he has just learned of another throne — the throne of Bilqis, queen of Saba, far to the south. He says: bring it to me before they come in submission. A formidable jinn — one of the *ʿifrīt* — speaks first: I will bring it before you rise from your seat. A long time, but possible. Then a second voice. Quieter. Not a jinn. A human, or perhaps an angel — the scholars differ — but someone *with knowledge from the Scripture*. And he offers something else.

Not before you rise.

Before your glance returns to you.

[PAUSE]

This is the climate. A king at the height of his power, surrounded by beings of fire and beings of light, and a knower of the Book is about to do something that will collapse distance itself — and Sulayman is about to see it.

### The Hook

Here is what is strange: the surah does not pause to describe the miracle. It does not show the throne traveling. It does not narrate the wonder of the watching court. The Quran skips all of that.

It only tells us what Sulayman *said* when he saw it.

Why? Why is the Quran so uninterested in the spectacle, and so interested in the speech? What was Allah preparing him for in that single instant — the instant between the glance going out and the glance returning — that mattered more than the throne itself?

Stay with that question. The whole ayah is going to answer it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We will sit with three things in this verse. Three places where the language is doing something that, once you see it, you cannot unsee.

### Element One: *Qabla an yartadda ilayka ṭarfuk* — "Before your glance returns to you"

Let's begin with a small grammatical idea, because the entire ayah turns on it.

In Arabic, when you want to say *to come back, to return,* there is a simple verb: *raja-ʿa*. To return. Plain. Ordinary. The word you'd use for someone walking home.

But there is another verb in this family: *irtadda* — Form VIII of the root *r-d-d*. The *r-d-d* root means *to push back, to turn back, to repel*. When you put it in Form VIII, you make it reflexive: it doesn't just return — it *snaps back upon itself*. It is the word you use for an arrow rebounding, an echo bouncing off a wall, a force returning along the line of its origin.

So already we have a choice the verse made. Not "before your glance comes back" — *before your glance snaps back to you.*

Now stay with the second word: *ṭarf*. Most translations render it "glance" or "eye." But *ṭarf* in Arabic is something more specific. *Ṭarf* is the *edge* of something — the edge of a sword, the edge of a cloth, the edge of vision. When applied to the eye, it does not mean the eye itself — it means *the moving edge of the gaze*, the line of sight as it travels outward.

So now read it again with both words restored:

**"Before the *line of your sight* — extended outward — *snaps back* upon you."**

Sit with this. What is the knower of the Book actually describing?

Notice something. He is not measuring time in seconds. He is not measuring it in the rising of a king. He is measuring it in the only unit smaller than seconds that the human body can perceive — *the time it takes for the gaze you cast to return to you*.

How long is that? Try it. Look across the room. The light from that wall is reaching your eye in a fraction of a fraction of a second. The "return of the glance" is the limit of perception itself. It is the smallest time-unit a human being has any felt access to.

[PAUSE]

And the knower is saying: in *less* than that.

Here's what is remarkable. This phrase is not measuring the *speed of the throne*. The throne is not moving at the speed of light. The phrase is doing something else. It is collapsing the distinction between *intending* and *receiving*. Between the mind reaching outward and the world arriving back. The knower is saying: there will be no gap. The cause and the effect will arrive in the same instant.

And the Arabic carries this in its bones. *Yartadda* — the snapping-back, the recoil — is the sound of something *already returning*. It is a verb that contains its own completion.

What this invites us to consider is something subtle. Power, when it is pure, leaves no residue of waiting. The most authentic miracle is not the one that astonishes by *delay* — the long journey, the slow build — but the one in which the will of Allah and the world of Allah are *not separable*. The knower of the Book is not boasting of speed. He is testifying that *with Allah, there is no distance*.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Mustaqirran ʿindahu* — "Set firmly in his presence"

Now the verse continues: *Falammā raʾāhu mustaqirran ʿindahu* — "Then when he saw it set firmly in his presence."

Pause on this word: *mustaqirran*. Form X of the root *q-r-r*.

The root *q-r-r* is one of the most beautiful in Arabic. It means *to settle, to come to rest, to find stillness*. When a person's eye finds *qurrat al-ʿayn* — coolness of the eye — it means their gaze has stopped roaming, stopped searching. *Qarār* is a stable resting place. *Qarra* is what water does when it stops moving and becomes a clear pool.

Form X — *istaqarra*, with the active participle *mustaqirr* — means *to take up settled residence, to be firmly established as if it had always been there*.

Now feel what this is doing.

The throne did not arrive *in transit*. It did not appear panting, as if it had just been carried. It did not shimmer into half-existence and stabilize. The verse tells us Sulayman saw it *mustaqirran* — already at rest, already at home, already as if it had been sitting there forever.

Stay with me on this. Because this single word — *mustaqirran* — is doing theological work that we miss in translation.

When Allah causes a thing, that thing does not arrive *partially*. It is not summoned from somewhere else as if Allah were borrowing it. It is *placed* — and in being placed, it is *fully placed*. The throne is not "transported." It is *re-established*. Its new location is as native to it as its old location.

[PAUSE]

What this points to, beyond the throne, is unseen. Think of how Allah grants you something — a job, a relationship, a child, a moment of clarity, a turn in your life. It does not arrive panting. It arrives *mustaqirr*. It arrives as if it had always been waiting for you. As if the universe had been holding it in place, exactly there, exactly for you, and you only had to walk into the room where it was already settled.

The phenomenon Allah describes here — the throne sitting *as if it had always been there* — is the texture of every gift He gives. We mistake the suddenness for chance. The verse is teaching us: there is no in-transit with Allah. What you receive, you receive *settled*.

And notice: *ʿindahu* — "in his presence," "with him." The word *ʿinda* in Arabic is not just spatial. It is the word used for nearness, custody, possession of a non-material kind. The knower of the Book had said *anā ātīka bihi* — I will bring it *to you*. But the verse says Sulayman saw it *mustaqirran ʿindahu* — settled *with him*. The throne did not just arrive at a location. It entered his *ʿindiyyah* — his sphere of nearness.

Allah is not delivering objects. He is rearranging proximity.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Ghaniyyun Karīm* — "Free of need, Generous"

Now we come to the closing. *Wa man kafara fa-inna rabbī ghaniyyun karīm* — "And whoever is ungrateful — indeed, my Lord is Free of need, Generous."

I want you to notice something almost no one notices on a first reading. Read the symmetry of the verse. The grateful person is described — *innamā yashkuru linafsih*, his gratitude is only for himself. So you would expect the ungrateful person to be described in parallel — *innamā yakfuru ʿalā nafsih*, his ingratitude is only against himself. That would complete the structure.

But the Quran does not say that.

Where the description of the *kāfir* should be — there is *silence*. And in that silence, Allah inserts something else: a description of *Himself*. Not the consequence to the human. The attribute of Allah.

This is one of the most piercing absences in the Quran.

Stay with me. Why is it constructed this way?

When a grateful person says *thank you*, the gratitude lands somewhere — it shapes him, refines him, returns to him as growth. The verse names that: *yashkuru linafsih*. His gratitude *flows back into his own self*.

But when an ungrateful person refuses — what is the consequence? The expected answer is: it harms him. And eventually it does. But Allah does not lead with that. He leads with this: *fa-inna rabbī ghaniyyun karīm.*

*Ghanī.* Root *gh-n-y*. To be free of need. To be self-sufficient. To possess in oneself everything one could ever require — not because one has acquired it, but because one *is* it. *Al-Ghanī* is the One whose richness is identity, not accumulation.

*Karīm.* Root *k-r-m*. Generous. But the Arabic *karīm* is much deeper than the English *generous*. *Karam* is a generosity that flows *because of who the giver is, not because of who the receiver is*. A *karīm* gives without being asked. A *karīm* gives without expecting return. A *karīm* gives even to those who have refused him.

Now read what Allah is saying.

Your refusal does not subtract anything from Me — *I am ghanī*.

And — astonishingly — even your refusal does not stop My giving — *I am karīm*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The verse, where it should rebuke the ungrateful, *praises Allah instead*. Where the human ear expects judgment, the Quran offers a divine self-disclosure. *Even your kufr cannot make Me less. Even your refusal cannot make Me ungenerous.*

This is why the absence is not a gap. It is a presence. The space where the kāfir's punishment should be — Allah fills with His own attributes, and in doing so He says something more devastating than any threat. *Your refusal is not an injury to Me. It is a forgone gift to yourself. I keep giving anyway.*

And notice the order. Not *karīmun ghaniyy* — generous and free of need. The Quran says *ghaniyyun karīm* — free of need *first*, generous *second*. Why? Because if generosity came first, you might suspect the giver of needing to give. The order locks it: He is independent *and then* generous. The generosity is not from need. It is from essence.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Three words. *Yartadda. Mustaqirran. Karīm.*

The recoil of a glance. The settling of a throne. The generosity that needs nothing.

The first showed us that Allah's power leaves no gap between will and world. The second showed us that what He gives arrives already at home in your life. The third showed us that even your refusal of His gifts does not stop His giving.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about the words — but about the soul that received them.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Test Inside the Gift

Let me start somewhere very ordinary.

You are waiting for something. A job offer. An acceptance letter. A diagnosis. A text back. The phone is in your hand. And you are doing what every human being does — you are bargaining with the future. *If this comes through, I will pray more. I will give more. I will be a better person. I will not forget who got me here.*

And then it comes through.

What happens?

For most of us — for me, for you — there is a window. A small window. Maybe an hour. Maybe a day. Inside the window, the gratitude is real. We are tender. We feel the closeness of Allah. And then the window closes. The gift becomes background. Within a week, the new thing is just *the thing we have*. Within a month, we are bargaining again — for the next thing.

This is the human pattern. We do not fail to feel gratitude. We fail to *sustain* it.

Now look at Sulayman.

The throne arrives. *Mustaqirran ʿindahu* — already at rest. The whole court is watching. He has just witnessed something that makes the laws of distance bend. By any reasonable measure, this is the moment for a king to feel his own greatness. To say something kingly. To turn to his court and let the awe land where it should land — on him, the man who commanded this miracle into being.

And what does he say?

*Hādhā min faḍli rabbī.*

This — is from the favor of my Lord.

Stay with this for a moment. Don't let it pass too fast. *Faḍl* — the root means *overflow, surplus, what is given beyond entitlement*. Sulayman is not saying *this is mine*. He is not even saying *this is what I deserve*. He is saying *this is overflow. This is more than I had any right to.*

And then — and here is the place where most people miss the depth of the verse — he says: *liyabluwanī*.

*That He may test me.*

[PAUSE]

He calls the gift a *test*.

Now — when do *we* call something a test? When it is hard. When it is painful. When something is taken from us. We say "Allah is testing me" when the diagnosis is bad, when the marriage is breaking, when the money runs out. We almost never call good news a test. Good news is a relief, a reward, a vindication.

Sulayman calls it a test in the *moment of triumph*.

Why?

Because he understands something about the architecture of the human heart that most of us never grasp until it is too late: **gifts test you more severely than losses do.**

Loss humbles you by force. The grief itself sits you down. You did not have to choose to be humbled — circumstance imposed it. But a gift — a gift leaves you *standing*. It does not bend you. It elevates you. And in that elevation, it asks a quiet question: *will you remember?*

Most of us fail this test. Not because we are bad, but because we are not paying attention. The gift is so pleasant that it becomes invisible to us as a moral situation. We treat the bad news as the moment of decision and the good news as the moment of celebration. Sulayman flips this. He treats *the good news as the moment of decision*.

And here is the human application. Every gift you have ever been given — every breath, every relationship, every moment of comfort, every flicker of clarity — is a moment Allah has placed before you and said: *will you see this as Mine, or will you absorb it into the story of yourself?*

[PAUSE]

Notice the verb form. *Liyabluwanī*. *That He may test me.* Present tense. Continuous. Not *that He has tested me* — past, finished. The test of the gift is not done when the gift arrives. The test is *every day you continue to hold it*. Every day you wake up and the throne is still there. Every day the marriage is still there. Every day the children are still alive. Every day your body still works. Every day is the test renewed.

This is why Sulayman's words land at the *moment* of the throne's arrival. Because he knows — the moment the throne lands is the moment the test begins, not the moment it ends.

And then comes the question — the most piercing question in the verse:

*A-ashkuru am akfur* — *Will I be grateful, or will I be ungrateful?*

Notice what Sulayman is *not* asking. He is not asking *will I be grateful or will I be neutral?* He is not asking *will I be grateful or will I forget?* He is asking *will I be grateful or will I refuse?*

The Arabic does not give a middle option. *Shukr* and *kufr* are the only two doors. There is no third door called *taking it for granted*. Because in the moral world Allah constructs, *taking for granted is a form of refusal*. To receive a gift and not see it as a gift — that is not neutrality. That is *kufr*. It is the same word the Quran uses for disbelief, because at root they are the same act: the failure to recognize the source.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow?

Tomorrow, the gift you currently have — the one you are taking for granted right now, as you read this — will still be there. The body, the breath, the eyes that move across this page, the mind that follows. Sulayman is not asking you to be grateful for what you do not have. He is asking you to recognize that *what you already have is the throne*. It arrived *mustaqirran* — settled, as if it had always been yours. And the test is not over.

The test is whether, somewhere in tomorrow, you will pause for thirty seconds and say: *Hādhā min faḍli rabbī* — *this — even this — is from the favor of my Lord.*

Not for what you are about to receive. For what already sits, settled, in the room where you are.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Gratitude That Cannot Reach Allah

Now I want to take you somewhere strange.

Read this part of the verse again carefully:

*Wa man shakara fa-innamā yashkuru linafsih. Wa man kafara fa-inna rabbī ghaniyyun karīm.*

*Whoever is grateful — his gratitude is only for himself. Whoever is ungrateful — my Lord is Free of need, Generous.*

Sulayman is making a startling claim about the metaphysics of gratitude. He is saying: *your gratitude does not reach Allah*.

Stop and feel how strange that is.

Because in every other relationship we have, gratitude *is the bridge*. You say thank you, and the thank-you travels from you to the giver, and the relationship is reinforced. The whole social architecture of human life runs on this — gratitude as the currency that connects giver and receiver. Take it away and the whole web of human reciprocity collapses.

Sulayman is saying: *this is not how it works with Allah*.

When you are grateful to Allah, the gratitude does not travel from you to Him and add something to Him. He is *ghanī*. He cannot be added to. He cannot be enriched. He cannot be made happier in the way a parent is made happier when a child remembers their birthday. He does not need it.

Where does it go, then?

*Innamā yashkuru linafsih.* *His gratitude is only for himself.*

The Arabic word *innamā* is a particle of restriction. It means *only, exclusively, nothing but.* So Sulayman is not saying *gratitude is mostly for the grateful one.* He is saying *gratitude is exclusively for the grateful one.*

Here's the bridge to your life. When you say *Alhamdulillah* — when you genuinely, in the depths of yourself, say *all praise belongs to Allah* — the praise does not travel up and decorate Allah. The praise travels *inward* and decorates *you*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it changes everything.

We have grown up, in a certain kind of religious culture, with the unspoken sense that worship is a transaction. You give Allah praise, and Allah gives you mercy back. Worship is something Allah *needs* in some way, and your performance of it is what triggers His favor.

Sulayman is dismantling this picture word by word.

Allah does not need your gratitude. He is *ghanī*. So when you offer it, you are not feeding Him. You are feeding *yourself*. The act of saying *thank You* to Allah is not a gift you give Him — it is a gift He has already arranged for you to give *yourself*, by giving you a tongue and a heart and a Lord worthy of being thanked.

Even the *capacity* to be grateful is a gift from Him. Which means that when you exercise it, you are receiving a second gift inside the first. The gratitude itself is *ʿibādah* in the original sense — not a payment, but a transformation. Every act of *shukr* polishes the soul that performs it.

This is why Allah says elsewhere — *la-in shakartum la-azīdannakum* — *if you are grateful, I will increase you.* Increase what? The thing you have, of course — but at a deeper level, increase *you*. Increase the *capacity* of the soul that is doing the thanking. Gratitude is the muscle by which the heart enlarges.

[PAUSE]

Now the inverse. *Wa man kafara fa-inna rabbī ghaniyyun karīm.*

If your gratitude does not reach Allah, then *your refusal does not reach Him either*. He does not lose anything when you refuse. He does not feel rejected. He does not become smaller because you have failed to thank Him.

But — and this is the devastating thing — neither does the *giving stop*. Because He is *karīm*. He keeps giving. The breath does not stop the day you decide not to thank Him for it. The vision does not dim. The provision does not cease. The mercy does not retract.

Think of what this means. You are inside an economy where the giver gives whether or not you acknowledge Him, and where your acknowledgment, when it comes, lands inside *you*, not inside *Him*.

This means two things at once.

First — Allah's giving is utterly unconditional. There is no transaction. Even the most ungrateful soul is being given to right now, breath by breath. This is the *karīm* part.

Second — your gratitude is utterly self-interested in the deepest possible sense. Every *Alhamdulillah* you say is an investment in your own soul. You cannot lose by saying it. You cannot fail to benefit. The one thing in the universe that has zero downside is *shukr*.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how I live tomorrow?

Tomorrow, the next time you catch yourself with the small impulse to say *Alhamdulillah* — for traffic that moved, for a meal, for a child's laugh, for a moment of cool air, for the sound of someone you love calling your name — say it.

Not because Allah needs it.

Because your soul does.

Because every single time you complete the loop — *gift recognized, source named, thanks offered* — something in you grows that nothing else can grow. And that growth is the only currency that travels with you when you leave this world. The throne does not travel. The kingdom does not travel. The body does not travel. The *grateful soul* — the one that learned to see *fadl* in everything and to name its source — that soul travels, and that soul stands.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Beneath the Story

Now look at the verse as a whole, and you will see something the surah has been arranging quietly.

It begins with *power* — the knower of the Book offering to collapse distance.

It moves to *settling* — the throne arriving as if it had always been there.

It moves to *recognition* — Sulayman naming the source.

It moves to *test* — the gift identified as a moment of moral decision.

It ends with *attribute* — Allah's *ghinā* and *karam* enclosing the whole scene.

Read it from the outside in: the verse opens with what seems to be a story about *the speed of a miracle* and closes with a description of *who Allah is*. The throne is not the destination. The throne is the doorway. The destination is the heart that received it — and the Lord who gave it.

This is why the Quran skips the spectacle. The miracle is not the throne moving. The miracle is the king *not moving* — the king staying inwardly still in the face of what would have unbalanced any other king. *Mustaqirran ʿindahu* applies to the throne. But by the time Sulayman finishes speaking, you realize it applies to *him too*. He is *mustaqirr*. He is settled. The throne did not unsettle him. The miracle did not lift him. He stayed *qarīr al-ʿayn* — eye-cool, soul-still — because he had already named whose gift it was.

[PAUSE]

### The Linguistic and the Thematic — One Insight from Two Directions

The linguistic showed us: Allah's gift arrives without delay (*yartadda*), already settled (*mustaqirran*), from a giver who needs nothing (*ghaniyyun karīm*).

The thematic showed us: gifts test more than losses do, and gratitude is a gift to yourself.

These are the same insight from two angles. *Because* the gift comes from a Lord who gains nothing from your thanks, the test of the gift is *entirely interior*. There is no external validation Allah needs. The whole drama is within the soul of the receiver. Sulayman's words are not for Allah's ear — Allah is *ghanī*, He doesn't need them. They are for *Sulayman's own soul*, which Allah has lovingly given the chance to be transformed by the saying.

The throne moves from Saba to Sulayman in less than a glance.

The transformation moves from Sulayman's mouth to Sulayman's heart in the same instant.

Same speed. Same Lord.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the throne in your life right now — the gift that has arrived, settled, *mustaqirran*, and that you have stopped noticing?

2. When was the last time you treated good news as a *test* and not just a relief?

3. If your gratitude does not reach Allah, but only enlarges yourself — does that make you more willing to offer it, or less? Why?

4. Where in your life have you been treating Allah as if He needed your thanks — and how would your worship change if you fully believed He did not?

5. What would it feel like to spend tomorrow assuming that every breath is *karam* from a *Ghanī* — that He is giving without need, generously, regardless of what you offer back?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> *The miracle is not the throne arriving in less than a glance — it is the king who, in that same instant, recognized whose throne it was, and named the gift as a test he would have to pass for the rest of his life.*

### The Closing Invitation

You will not be given a flying throne. But you will be given, today, a hundred things that arrived *mustaqirran* — already settled in your life, as if they had always been there. The breath that just left you. The eyes still moving down the page. The Lord who arranged for these words to find you on this day.

You can let those gifts pass through you, unrecognized, into the haze of *taken-for-granted*.

Or you can do what Sulayman did. Pause. Name the source. Call it *fadl*. Call it *test*. And ask the only question that matters in the moment of any blessing:

*A-ashkuru am akfur?*

Will I be grateful, or will I refuse?

Allah does not need your answer. But your soul is waiting for it.

### Du'a

O Allah — You who are *Ghanī*, who needs nothing from us, and *Karīm*, who gives without ceasing — open our eyes to the thrones already settled in our lives. Let us not wait for losses to remember You. Let our gratitude rise the moment the gift arrives — and let it rise again, every day it remains. And when we forget, send us back to this verse, and to the king who knew that the test of the gift is the test of a lifetime.

*Āmīn.*
