# The Knowledge That Bends the Knee

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 15

> وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيْمَـٰنَ عِلْمًۭا ۖ وَقَالَا ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِى فَضَّلَنَا عَلَىٰ كَثِيرٍۢ مِّنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

> *"And We had certainly given Dawud and Sulayman knowledge, and they said, 'All praise belongs to Allah who has favored us over many of His believing servants.'"*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where we are in the surah

Surah An-Naml is, among other things, a surah about *what people see*. It opens by calling itself guidance and good news for those who establish prayer — and immediately announces that some hearts have been adorned with their own evil so that they wander blindly. Two kinds of vision. Two kinds of seeing.

Then the surah pulls us into a desert at night. Musa, alone, sees a fire in the distance. He goes toward it expecting to bring back warmth and direction. He finds Allah.

That story ends. The page turns.

And the very next thing the surah does — without transition, without warning — is hand us this single sentence about two of the most powerful men who ever walked the earth.

Dawud — the shepherd boy who killed Goliath, the prophet whose voice made mountains echo back the praise of Allah, the king who founded a dynasty.

Sulayman — his son. The one who understood the speech of birds. The one whose army included jinn and humans and creatures we cannot see. The one whose wealth was such that the Queen of Sheba would arrive at his palace and think she was walking on water.

If you were going to introduce these two, where would you start?

You'd probably start with the kingdom. With the armies. With the wind that carried Sulayman from one end of the earth to the other in a morning. With the bronze flowing like water for Dawud. With the temple. With the throne.

That is not where Allah starts.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Allah introduces these two giants of human history with one gift — and then, before we are even told what they did with that gift, before we hear about a single victory or wonder, He tells us *what came out of their mouths the moment they noticed it.*

He doesn't lead with the gift. He leads with the gift *and the response to the gift, fused into one sentence.*

That's strange. Why?

And there is something else strange. When they thank Allah, they don't compare themselves to disbelievers. They don't say, "praise be to Allah who favored us over the rejecters." They compare themselves to *believers.* And not to all believers. To **many** of them.

A specific word. A careful word. A word that, the moment you sit with it, opens up a question about how the most exalted people in the world saw themselves in relation to the unexalted.

Stay with me. We are going to walk into this slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1 — *ʿilman*: a single, indefinite, unspecified word

Look at the gift.

> وَلَقَدْ ءَاتَيْنَا دَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيْمَـٰنَ عِلْمًۭا
> *"And We had certainly given Dawud and Sulayman **knowledge**."*

One word. **ʿIlman.** Knowledge.

Before we open it, let's teach a piece of Arabic grammar.

In Arabic, when you mention a noun, you can mention it definitely — *al-ʿilm*, "the knowledge" — or indefinitely — *ʿilman*, "knowledge." Definite means specified. The listener knows which knowledge. Indefinite means *un*specified. It can mean "some knowledge," and it can also mean — and this is the part that matters — *a kind of knowledge so significant that to specify it would be to limit it.*

In English we have a weak echo of this. If a friend tells you, "I went through something last year," and refuses to say what — the indefiniteness is doing work. *Something.* Not "a divorce" or "an illness" — *something.* The listener is invited to feel the magnitude without being given the contour.

Arabic does this with grammar. Indefiniteness, in the right context, becomes *exaltation through omission.* The grammarians have a name for this. They call it *tanwīn al-taʿẓīm* — the indefiniteness of magnification.

Now hold that, and come back to the ayah.

Allah does not say, "We gave Dawud and Sulayman *the* knowledge." He does not say, "We gave them knowledge of judgment," or "knowledge of psalms," or "knowledge of birds," or "knowledge of metalwork." All of those are mentioned elsewhere in the Quran for these two prophets. He could have said any of them.

He said: **ʿilman.**

Knowledge. Just — knowledge.

[PAUSE]

Notice what that does. It doesn't tell you *which* knowledge. It tells you *that they were given knowledge*, and leaves the *kind* unspecified — and the unspecification is the point.

Whatever knowledge a human being can carry — they were given some of it.

Now here is the second move. Sit with the *order*.

Dawud and Sulayman were given a kingdom. They were given prophethood. They were given wealth, dominion, the obedience of birds and winds and jinn, the ability to fashion armor from iron, the understanding of the speech of ants. The Quran narrates all of this. Right after this ayah, in fact, we will hear Sulayman himself say, *"We have been taught the speech of the birds and have been given of all things."*

So when Allah, looking at the entire toolkit of these two prophets — kingship, wealth, dominion, miraculous gifts — chooses *one* word to summarize the whole inheritance He gave them, He chooses **ʿilm.**

Not power. Not kingdom. Not wealth.

**Knowledge.**

[PAUSE]

What does this tell us? It tells us how Allah ranks His own gifts. The kingdom is downstream of the knowledge. The wind is downstream of the knowledge. The understanding of the ant — downstream of the knowledge. *The kingship is the fruit. ʿIlm is the root.*

And here is what's remarkable. The two of them — looking at everything they had been given, the throne and the army and the prophets' speech and the bronze flowing like water — they too summarized it the same way. They did not praise Allah for the kingdom. They praised Him *for being the One who gave.*

Which leads us to the second word.

### Element 2 — *al-ḥamdu lillāh*: the praise that is not mere thanks

> وَقَالَا ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ
> *"And they said, 'All praise belongs to Allah.'"*

Before we open *al-ḥamd*, let's teach the concept underneath it.

In Arabic there are two words that get translated as "thanks" or "praise" in English, and they are not the same thing.

There is *shukr* — gratitude. *Shukr* is a response. Someone gives you something, you say *shukran*. It is reactive. It is bound to the gift.

And there is *ḥamd* — praise. *Ḥamd* is different. *Ḥamd* is praising someone for *who they are*, not just for what they did for you. *Ḥamd* describes the praiseworthy one as praiseworthy whether you received anything from them or not. The classical Arabic lexicons distinguish them this way: *shukr* is for the favor done; *ḥamd* is for the qualities of the One who does favors.

A simple way to feel the difference. *Shukr* says: *"thank you for giving me water."* *Ḥamd* says: *"praise belongs to the One who is generous"* — even if today you happen to be the one drinking.

So when Dawud and Sulayman receive a magnificent gift and they respond with **ḥamd**, not just *shukr* — they are doing something subtle and enormous.

They are saying: *the gift was real, but the praise is not for what the gift gave us. The praise is for the One who is the kind of God who gives.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at the structure: **al-ḥamdu li-llāh.**

In Arabic the *al-* on *ḥamd* is the definite article. It is not "*a* praise" or "*some* praise." It is **the** praise. *All* of it. Every kind of praise that has ever existed or will ever exist. *That praise* — it belongs to Allah.

And the *li* — the *lām* of ownership — does not say *"goes to Allah"* or *"is offered to Allah."* It says *belongs to Allah.* Not given. **Owned.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a second. Two of the most accomplished men who ever lived, looking down at an inheritance that included the wind and the speech of birds and the throne of David, do not say, *"We thank Allah for this."* They do not even say, *"All praise is offered to Allah."*

They say, **the praise — every drop of it that has ever been or will ever be — already belongs to Him.** *We are not even producing it. We are noticing what was already true.*

This is what knowledge does to a person. Real knowledge. *ʿIlm* in the sense Allah uses here.

It does not make them announce themselves. It makes them notice that they were never the source of anything.

Notice the sequence in the ayah: *We gave them knowledge — and they said al-ḥamdu lillāh.* The very first thing the *ʿilm* produced was a sentence that pointed away from themselves.

That is how you know it was real knowledge.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element 3 — *ʿalā kathīrin min ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn*: the comparison that should not be there

This is the heart of the ayah. Stay close.

> ٱلَّذِى فَضَّلَنَا عَلَىٰ كَثِيرٍۢ مِّنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ
> *"…who has favored us over **many** of His **believing** servants."*

We have to teach two pieces of grammar here.

**First piece.** In Arabic, when you want to compare yourself favorably to a whole group, the natural construction is: *faḍḍalanā ʿalā [the group].* "He favored us over [them]." Whoever you put after *ʿalā* is the group you are being elevated above.

So watch carefully who Dawud and Sulayman placed there.

They did not say: *He favored us over the disbelievers.* They did not say: *over the wrongdoers.* They did not say: *over the kings of the earth.* They did not even say: *over the people.*

They said: ***ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn*** — **His believing servants.**

[PAUSE]

The grammatical comparison they chose for themselves was — other believers.

Think about what that means. They are kings. They are prophets. They have been given a kingdom such that nothing remotely like it will ever exist again on earth — Sulayman literally asks Allah for *"a kingdom that will not befit anyone after me,"* and Allah grants it. By any worldly measure, the entire human race lies beneath them.

When they look up to find someone they could legitimately compare themselves favorably to — *they don't look at the human race.* They look at one slice of it. **The believing servants of Allah.**

Why?

Because in their mental universe, *those* are the only people whose situation is real. The disbelievers — their kingship, their wealth, their accomplishment — none of it is, to Dawud and Sulayman, even a category. The disbelievers are not above them or below them on a scale. They are simply outside the conversation about who is favored, because favor only makes sense in the framework of *ʿubūdiyyah* — servanthood to Allah.

So when they reach for a frame of reference, they reach for *the only people whose lives count.*

And then — and this is what stops me every time — within that group, they do not place themselves at the top.

[PAUSE]

**Second piece of grammar.** Look at the word *kathīr*. *Kathīr* means "many."

In Arabic, when you want to say someone has been favored over *all* of a group, you say *ʿalā kullihim* — "over all of them," or you simply omit the qualifier: *faḍḍalanā ʿalā ʿibādihi* — "He favored us over His servants." That construction implies the whole.

But Dawud and Sulayman insert a qualifier. **Kathīr.** *Many.*

Not all. **Many.**

This means — by the careful, deliberate grammar of their own ḥamd — they are saying: *Allah has favored us over many of His believing servants… but not all of them.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with that.

Two prophets. Two kings. The recipients of arguably the most spectacular worldly inheritance ever granted to two human beings — and when they describe their own rank, they explicitly leave room above themselves. *Many, but not all.*

Who is above them? They do not name. They do not need to. Their ḥamd carries inside itself the consciousness that *somewhere among the believing servants of Allah, there are those who are higher than us — and we know it, and we wanted Allah to hear that we know it.*

This is what the classical mufassirūn dwell on: that the very form of their thanks contains the form of their humility. They cannot even celebrate the gift without making space, in the sentence that celebrates it, for the people who are above them.

[PAUSE]

A composite picture from the classical commentary tradition: some scholars say *al-muʾminīn* points to the rank of the prophets and especially the saints among Muḥammad's ﷺ ummah, who would come later and stand higher in some respects. Others say it points to those among the believers who, in Allah's hidden reckoning, occupy ranks unknown to Dawud and Sulayman themselves. Still others say it is a general acknowledgment of the principle that *no servant is the top of the ladder.* These are not competing claims. They are the same insight from three angles: **the highest people are the ones who can feel a higher above them.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have three discoveries from the words.

The gift was *ʿilm* — unspecified, magnificent, the root from which all the other gifts grew.

Their response was *ḥamd* — not thanks for receiving, but the recognition that all praise was already owned by the One who gave.

And their comparison was *over many of His believing servants* — a sentence that, by its grammar, holds the door open for those above them.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about their words — but about what knowledge actually *is.*

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1 — Knowledge that humbles vs. knowledge that inflates

Start where you live.

You have probably watched this happen in your own life. Someone learns something. A new skill. A new field. A new framework. And in the early days of learning, *they cannot stop telling people about it.* Every conversation gets routed back to the new knowledge. Every problem they encounter gets reframed through the new lens. They wear the knowledge like a coat that hasn't been broken in yet.

And then — sometimes — years pass. And you meet that same person again. And now they barely mention it. They use it quietly. When you ask them about it, they say things like, *"I'm still figuring it out,"* or *"there's a lot I don't know."* They have *more* knowledge than they had before, but they speak of it less, and what they say has more weight.

What changed?

The first kind of knowledge was a possession. The second kind was an *opening.* The first showed them something about *themselves* — that they had a coat now, that they belonged to a category. The second showed them something about *the world* — that the world is bigger than they thought, and they are smaller than they thought, and there is more above them than below them.

[PAUSE]

The Quran knows this difference.

There is the knowledge of Iblīs, who said *"I am better than him,"* and there is the knowledge of the angels, who said *"we have no knowledge except what You have taught us."* Both had knowledge. One of those sentences ended a career. The other began one.

Dawud and Sulayman were given *ʿilman* — the magnifying indefinite. And the very first thing it did inside them was bend them toward the floor. Not in performance. In recognition. They saw, with the clarity that only real knowledge gives, *that there were levels above them that they could not see.*

This is the test of any knowledge you ever acquire. Does it make you announce yourself, or does it make you notice the One you didn't make? Does it make you compare yourself favorably to the people below you, or does it make you reach upward and say, *"there are many — and there are some above us"?*

The same gift produces opposite men depending on the heart it lands in. Iblīs and Sulayman were both intelligent. Both saw. The one whose seeing made him say *"I am better"* was destroyed. The one whose seeing made him say *"praise belongs to the One who favored us over many"* was lifted.

Knowledge is not, by itself, light. **Knowledge is light when it points away from the one carrying it.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change for tomorrow?

When you learn something new — when you finish a book, when you understand a concept, when you become the most knowledgeable person in some small room about some small thing — listen to the first sentence that wants to come out of your mouth. Is it a sentence that announces *you*, or is it a sentence that points to the One who gave you the capacity to know? Is it a sentence that compares you favorably to those who don't have it, or is it a sentence that makes space above you for those who have more?

Dawud and Sulayman are not asking you to be silent about what you know. They are asking you to let your knowing produce the right first sentence.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme 2 — The hierarchy you cannot see, and the one you can

Here is the harder reflection.

We live in a world that is obsessed with horizontal hierarchies. Wealth. Power. Visibility. Followers. Square footage. Job titles. The hierarchies you can photograph. The hierarchies you can rank.

If Dawud and Sulayman had cared about that hierarchy, they would have stood, justly, at the very top of it. By every horizontal measure available to a human being in their century, they were *the most* — the most powerful, the most wealthy, the most spectacular.

And yet — *they did not place themselves at the top of any ranking.* When they reached for the only ranking that mattered to them, they reached for ***ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn*** — the believing servants. And within that ranking, they put themselves above *many.* Not above all.

[PAUSE]

What does this mean? It means there were, in their own mental world, people *they could not name and could not see and could not photograph* who were ranked above them in the only hierarchy that was real.

A villager praying in a hut at night. A widow remembering her Lord between her tears. A young person resisting a sin no one knows about. A scholar in some forgotten valley. A heart, somewhere, that loves Allah more than they did.

Dawud and Sulayman could not see any of them. They didn't have to. They knew, with the certainty that comes from prophethood, that *the visible hierarchy is not the real one.*

This is the most subversive idea in the ayah. It is a quiet sledgehammer to the architecture by which the world tells you to measure yourself.

You might be ranked very high in the hierarchy you can see. That tells you almost nothing about where you stand in the hierarchy that counts. You might be ranked very low in the hierarchy you can see. That tells you almost nothing about where you stand in the hierarchy that counts.

The hierarchy that counts is one *only Allah can see in full.* Even the prophets — even *these* prophets — could only see that there were *many* above whom they had been favored, and *some* (un-named, un-numbered) above them.

[PAUSE]

And here is the practical edge.

If even Dawud and Sulayman, with their kingdoms and their prophethood, could not place themselves at the top of the rank — *what business do you and I have ranking ourselves against each other?* What business does our heart have looking down at the brother whose Arabic is worse than ours, the sister whose practice we judge, the believer whose understanding seems shallow to us?

We don't know. **We literally do not know** where they stand in the hierarchy that counts. They might be — and you should hold this with full seriousness — *the ones above us in a ranking we cannot see and Dawud and Sulayman themselves bowed before.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change for tomorrow?

Try this. The next time you catch yourself ranking another believer below you in your own head — pause and remember the sentence of two prophets who, by every visible measure, towered above the human race, and who *could not bring themselves* to place themselves above all the believing servants of Allah.

If they couldn't, neither can you. The sentence to keep in your chest is a small one: ***and I do not know where this person stands with Allah.*** Hold that, and watch the inner pose collapse.

The other practical edge is gentler. When you look at a believer whose external rank is clearly above yours — wealthier, more learned, more famous, more eloquent — you do not need to feel small. Their rank in Allah's hierarchy may be lower than yours. *You don't know either way.* What you owe them is what they owe you: respect, du'a, and the same refusal to rank.

The horizontal hierarchies the world hands you are not the real ones. *Stop standing in line in them.* Stand only in the line of *ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn* — the line of His believing servants. And in that line, do not even try to measure your spot. Just be in it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Look at the ayah now from above. You can see what it is doing.

It is one sentence in two halves. The first half is what *Allah did:* **We gave Dawud and Sulayman knowledge.** The second half is what *they said:* **Praise belongs to Allah, who favored us over many of His believing servants.**

The first half is short. *Wa-laqad ātaynā Dāwūda wa-Sulaymāna ʿilman.* Nine words in Arabic. The second half is longer. *Wa-qālā al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī faḍḍalanā ʿalā kathīrin min ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn.*

The response is longer than the gift.

That is the architecture. *The praise outweighs the favor in the very arithmetic of the verse.* They were given knowledge. They produced a sentence that, in length, in care, in carefully placed humility, in the choice of every preposition — *exceeded what they had been given.*

Because that is what knowledge actually does, when it is real. It produces a praise larger than itself, because it has shown the praiser that the One who gave it is larger than every gift.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar said: **knowledge unspecified, praise total, comparison limited.** Unspecified knowledge — because the gift was so vast no single category contained it. Total praise — because the One who gave is praiseworthy in His being, not just in His giving. Limited comparison — because even at the height of human exaltation, there is room above.

The themes said: **knowledge that humbles, hierarchy you cannot see.** Knowledge whose first product is *al-ḥamdu lillāh* and *ʿalā kathīr* — not *over all*, but *over many*. Hierarchy that prophets refused to place themselves at the top of, in deference to a ranking only Allah holds.

It is the same lesson. Real knowledge of Allah produces, instantly, a sentence that points away from the self and leaves the door open above.

### Questions to carry

1. When you learn something — a verse, a concept, a skill — what is the first sentence that wants to come out of your mouth? Is it about you, or about the One who taught you?

2. When you compare yourself favorably to other believers in your own mind — what would change if you remembered that two prophets refused to do this even at the height of human achievement?

3. Is there a believer in your life whose external rank is below yours — whom you secretly think of as beneath you — but whose rank with Allah you have *no idea* about?

4. If you could only ever frame your blessings against the believers you cannot see and cannot judge — would you still be able to say *"praise be to Allah who favored us"?* Or would the sentence start to taste different?

5. What is the ratio in your day between *shukr* (thanks for what you got) and *ḥamd* (praise for who He is)? When was the last time you praised Allah for nothing — for who He is, when no fresh gift had arrived?

### The one-sentence distillation

When Allah gives knowledge to a heart that can hold it, the very first thing that knowledge produces is a sentence that *empties* the one who received it — pointing all praise back to the Giver, and refusing to place itself at the top of the ranks of His servants.

### The closing invitation

This ayah is not asking you to memorize a virtue. It is showing you a *grammar of the soul.*

When good comes to you — a job, a child, an understanding, a knowledge, a kindness — try, for a moment, to respond in the grammar of Dawud and Sulayman.

Don't lead with the gift. Lead with the Giver.
Don't say *thank you* and stop. Say *all praise belongs to Him.*
And when you locate yourself in the world after the gift, place yourself in the line of *His believing servants.* And in that line — leave space above you. *Many, but not all.* Always *many, but not all.*

That is what the *ʿilm* of the prophets sounded like the moment it landed. May it sound like that in us too.

### Du'a

O Allah — You who gave Dawud and Sulayman knowledge, and the better gift of knowing what to say the moment they had it: give us the kind of *ʿilm* that bends us toward You and not toward ourselves. Let every gift You give us produce, first and immediately, a praise larger than the gift. And let our hearts never seat themselves above any of Your believing servants — for we do not know, and we do not need to know, and we trust that the only ranking that counts is in Your hands. *Al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī faḍḍalanā ʿalā kathīrin min ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn.*
