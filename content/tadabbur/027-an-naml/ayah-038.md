### Rebuild the World

Let's place ourselves in that court.

Solomon — *Sulayman* (peace be upon him) — is a king unlike any the world has seen or will see again. Allah has given him sovereignty over human beings, over jinn, over the wind itself. Animals speak to him. The hoopoe brings him intelligence from faraway lands. His armies move across the earth in configurations that no general could command.

And the news has just arrived: Bilqis, the Queen of Sheba, is coming.

She received his letter. The letter that began not with threats, not with terms of surrender, not with a list of his military capabilities — but with *Bismillah ir-Rahmān ir-Rahīm*. She consulted her inner council. She weighed her options. And she made the decision: she would send gifts first, then come herself.

Now Solomon knows she is on the way. Her delegation is moving. She is coming.

And in that moment — with the most powerful queen in the known world approaching — Solomon turns to his assembly and asks a question that has puzzled commentators for centuries.

He doesn't ask: *How shall we receive her?*
He doesn't ask: *What terms should we offer?*
He doesn't ask: *Is our court prepared?*

He asks: **Which of you will bring me her throne before they come?**

[PAUSE]

Here is what's stunning. The word Solomon uses for her arrival — for the moment *before* which he wants the throne moved — is not simply "before they arrive." He says: *before they come to me as* **muslimīn** — in submission.

That one word tells us everything Solomon already knew about what was going to happen. It tells us what the whole exchange had been about from the beginning.

And it tells us something about power, about knowledge, and about what it means to move a heart, that reaches far beyond a throne made of gold.

Stay with me.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *Al-Malaʾ*: Those Who Fill the Space

Before we can enter the question Solomon asks, we need to understand *who* he is asking.

In Arabic, there is a rhetorical device worth naming first as a general concept. Sometimes a speaker addresses a specific group not simply to communicate information, but to *establish a frame* — to tell the audience watching something about what kind of conversation is about to happen. Teachers do this. Leaders do this. You address the room before you ask the pivotal question.

With that in mind, let's look at the word Solomon uses.

**ٱلْمَلَأُ** — *al-malaʾ*.

The root is م-ل-أ — the same root as the verb "to fill." *Malaʾa* means: he filled it until it was full, until no space remained. And *al-malaʾ* — as the scholars of Arabic explain — refers to those who *fill* the assembly hall. Not merely those who are present. Those whose presence is felt. The notables. The chiefs. The ones who take up space — not in a petty sense, but in the sense of weight, of authority, of presence.

These are the people Solomon gathered around him: the full complement of his power.

Notice something. Solomon has unlimited resources available to him. He commands jinn. He commands wind. He commands armies. If he simply needed the throne moved, he could have commanded it done. He didn't need to ask.

The fact that he *asks* — that he turns to al-malaʾ and throws open the question — is itself a choice. A deliberate rhetorical act.

Why does a king who has everything, who could simply order, instead *ask*?

Because he wants something revealed. He wants to see who steps forward. He wants the assembly — and perhaps Bilqis herself, who would later hear of this — to see what is possible, and who possesses it.

The address to *al-malaʾ* is not just a greeting. It is the opening of a contest.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Ayyukum Yaʾtīnī*: The Open Challenge

Now the question itself.

**أَيُّكُمْ يَأْتِينِى** — *ayyukum yaʾtīnī*.

Let's teach the grammatical form briefly. *Ayyukum* is an interrogative structure: *ayy* (which one?) + *kum* (of you). It's not addressed to a named individual. It's not a command assigned to the most capable person present. It is an *open competition question*. Which one of you — of this entire assembled power — can do this?

The verb *yaʾtīnī* — "will come to me" / "will bring to me" — is third person singular. Solomon is asking about a single actor. Not: all of you together, coordinate. But: is there *one* among you who can do this alone?

This matters. Because what follows are two answers — and the contrast between them is where the reflection lives.

The Ifreet — one of the powerful jinn — speaks first (27:39). He says: *I will bring it to you before you rise from your station* — meaning before your court session ends. Scholars estimate this as hours.

Then a second voice. A man — human, not jinn — named Asif ibn Barkhiya (27:40). He says: *I will bring it to you before your gaze returns to you* — meaning before you can blink.

And then: the throne is there.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what just happened. The Ifreet's timeframe was hours. Asif's timeframe was an eye-blink. The Ifreet had brute force, vast power, the capacity to cross distance at speed that defied physics. And yet Asif — a human scholar — did something that took less than a blink.

Asif is described as having *ʿilm min al-kitāb* — **knowledge from the Book**. Not the Ifreet's physical capacity. Not the power accumulated over centuries by a supernatural being. Knowledge. Proximity to revelation. Access to something the Ifreet, for all its strength, simply did not have.

Here is what the open challenge of *ayyukum* reveals: different kinds of capacity are not equal. The one who is *closer to the source* — to divine knowledge, to the Book, to spiritual nearness with Allah — can move what no amount of physical force alone can move.

This was always Solomon's point. He wasn't testing who was strongest. He was creating a moment to reveal what *nearness to Allah* makes possible.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three — *Muslimīn*: The Destination Hidden in the Deadline

We've reached the word that unlocks everything.

Let's notice first what Solomon *could* have said. He could have said: before they *arrive*. Before they *reach* me. Before they *enter* my court. Any of these would be grammatically natural — it would simply mark the deadline.

But Solomon says: **قَبْلَ أَن يَأْتُونِى مُسْلِمِينَ** — *before they come to me as muslimīn*.

The word *muslimīn* is the active participle of the Form IV verb *aslama* — to surrender, to submit, to give oneself over to peace. It is the same root from which Islam takes its name. At this point in the narrative, it is likely carrying its pre-Islamic Quranic sense: those who have submitted to Allah, those who have surrendered to the truth.

Grammatically, *muslimīn* functions as a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial accusative. It describes the state in which the subject is performing the action. They don't just *come* — they come *while being submitters*. That is the state they are in when they arrive.

Now ask: why does Solomon use this word as his deadline?

He could be predicting their arrival. He believes they will come in submission — not as conquerors, not as diplomats playing games — but as people who have genuinely surrendered. He is so confident in the outcome of this exchange that he describes their arrival *by its spiritual outcome*, not its physical fact.

Or — and this is what invites us to sit longer — he is stating his actual goal. The throne is not the objective. **The arrival of Bilqis in a state of submission is the objective.** Every step Solomon has taken — the letter beginning with *Bismillah*, the throne maneuver about to unfold — is aimed at that single outcome. He wants to move her heart before her body arrives.

The deadline is not a logistical deadline. It is a spiritual deadline. Get the throne here before she arrives *already transformed* — so that when she walks in and sees her throne, the transformation that has been building in her can complete itself.

[PAUSE]

What this single word *muslimīn* tells us: **Solomon's power was never the point. His goal was always a heart.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — Who Steps Forward When the Challenge Is Open

Think about a moment in your own life when someone threw open a question — *who can do this?* — and the room went quiet.

You know what happens in that silence. Everyone measures themselves. Everyone does the calculation: do I have what this requires? Everyone feels the gap between what they are and what the task demands. And most people — most of the time — wait to see who else moves.

What breaks the silence is always a question of *what kind of capacity you trust*. The person who steps forward is the person who believes their particular kind of strength matches this particular kind of challenge.

The Ifreet steps forward. It has enormous, established, documented strength. It knows it can cross the distance. It knows it can move the throne before the court rises. This is its *wheelhouse* — physical capacity over distance and time. It is confident because it has earned that confidence through what it is.

And it is outpaced. In an eye-blink.

Not by someone stronger. By someone closer.

*ʿIlm min al-kitāb* — knowledge from the Book. The scholars differ on exactly what this means for Asif specifically: was it the Greatest Name of Allah? Was it deep knowledge of the divine attributes and how to invoke them? Was it the intimacy with revelation that makes du'ā a tool that moves reality rather than a wish sent into air?

Each of these scholarly perspectives adds a layer. The Greatest Name interpretation tells us that certain invocations unlock realities the physical world cannot contain. The divine attributes interpretation tells us that proximity to understanding *who Allah is* translates into proximity to what Allah does. The intimacy-with-revelation interpretation tells us that the one who knows the Book most deeply is the one through whom the Book's power most completely flows.

Together, these are not competing answers. They are one composite reality: **nearness to Allah, expressed through knowledge and sincerity, moves what strength alone cannot reach.**

This is not abstract. You have lived some version of this.

There is a du'ā that a sincerely broken person makes in the middle of the night that changes their situation in ways no amount of effort or networking or strategy could have arranged. There is a person whose prayer is answered — whose circumstances genuinely shift — not because they were powerful, but because they were *near*. Because they had, in Asif's terms, some ʿilm min al-kitāb — some rootedness in what Allah has revealed, some intimacy with the Quran that means their turning to Allah is not a transaction but a homecoming.

And what Solomon's open challenge reveals — *ayyukum* — is that this nearness is not given by status, by species, by strength. The jinn, for all its power, was outpaced by a human scholar whose capacity was simply: *I know something about how this works*.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow? It asks you to reconsider where you are investing your capacity-building. Strength is useful. Position is useful. Resources are useful. But *proximity to Allah through knowledge and practice* — this is the investment that can move what nothing else can move.

Asif ibn Barkhiya didn't have a larger army. He had a deeper Book.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two — The Throne That Was Never the Point

Let's talk about what Bilqis had built her life around.

She ruled one of the most spectacular kingdoms of the ancient world. The Quran tells us her throne was *ʿarsh ʿaẓīm* — a magnificent throne (27:23). The hoopoe described it in terms that made even Solomon pause. Her kingdom had power, trade routes, military capacity, and — most importantly — a *symbol* at the center of it all.

Her throne.

A throne is never just furniture. In the ancient world — in any world — a throne is the physical embodiment of a ruler's legitimacy. It is where the person who has the right to decide sits. It is the object that says: *this person rules here*. When you possess the throne, you possess the sovereignty. When the throne is moved, something about the order of the world has shifted.

Bilqis had built her identity around being the person who sat on that throne. Not in an ego-driven sense — the Quran doesn't portray her as arrogant, but as wise and consultative. She *earned* her throne through governance and judgment. It represented her.

Now imagine. You are Bilqis. You have sent your delegation ahead. You have made the journey. You are approaching Solomon's court, still carrying the identity of a sovereign, still internally framing this meeting as a negotiation between equals.

And you walk in. And there — in Solomon's court — is your throne.

Not a copy. Not a reproduction. *Your throne*. The throne that was, as far as you knew, safe in Sheba. The throne that represents everything you are.

It is already there. It arrived before you did.

What happens in that moment? Something that no argument, no miracle-performance, no demonstration of military power could have produced. Your entire framework for understanding the world — the framework that said *I have sovereignty here, I have power here, I have built something real and lasting here* — that framework quietly collapses. Because whatever power transferred your throne across impossible distance, following laws of reality you didn't know existed, is operating in a world *larger* than the one you thought you lived in.

Bilqis's response, when she sees the throne, is profound (27:42). She is asked: *Is your throne like this?* And she says: *It is as if it were it* — meaning she recognizes it, but something in her can't quite process the fact of it. She is already inside the crack in her worldview that the throne's presence created.

And then she says the words that complete her journey (27:44): *I have been doing wrong to myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allah, the Lord of the worlds.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what Solomon knew that he couldn't have said directly. You cannot argue someone out of a worldview. You cannot debate a person into submission. You cannot offer a queen the intellectual case for why her framework is insufficient — especially a queen whose framework has worked. She has a magnificent kingdom. Her intelligence has served her well. Her governance has been sound.

What you can do — what Solomon understood — is create a crack. A moment where something happens that her framework cannot explain. Not a threat. Not a defeat. Something *beautiful and impossible* that arrives before she does, in the exact shape of her most prized possession.

The throne move was a mercy.

It was Solomon, under divine guidance, creating the precise experience that would give Bilqis's wisdom — which was genuine — the evidence it needed to make the leap her heart was already inclined toward.

She had already been moving toward this. The letter beginning with *Bismillah* had shaken her. Her council's advice — *it is up to you, O queen* — had left her carrying the weight of the decision alone. She had been internally processing something that her world hadn't given her a category for.

The throne appearing in Solomon's court was the final stone across the river. Not a argument. An experience.

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is the invitation the ayah offers each of us. We spend enormous energy trying to *argue* people toward transformation — our children, our spouses, our friends, ourselves. We build cases. We marshal evidence. We explain why the position they hold is insufficient.

But transformation — real transformation — rarely comes through argument. It comes through the moment where someone's existing framework meets something it genuinely cannot explain. And instead of defending the framework, they let it expand.

Solomon's wisdom was knowing that Bilqis's intelligence would do the work, if the crack was created. He trusted her. He didn't try to argue her into submission. He moved her throne.

What "throne" — what prized possession, what settled framework, what identity-object — might need to be gently displaced in someone you love? Not by force. Not by argument. But by the kind of patient, wise action that creates an experience which does what words cannot?

And — more personally — what throne in your own life has you organized your identity around that Allah, in His mercy, might be gently beginning to transpose? Not to humiliate you, but to show you: there is a world larger than the one you've been living in, and it is more magnificent, not less.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Look now at what this single ayah contains.

On one side: *al-malaʾ* — those who fill the assembly. Power assembled. Capacity displayed.
At the center: *ayyukum* — which of you? The open challenge.
On the other side: *muslimīn* — those who arrive in submission. The goal declared.

Between the challenge and the goal: everything. The competition that will unfold in the next two verses. The throne that will appear. The queen who will walk in. The moment of recognition. The declaration of surrender.

All of that is latent in this single verse. Solomon, in one question, has already named both the method (*who can accomplish this?*) and the destination (*arrival in submission*). The throne is the bridge. And the bridge was always aimed at a heart.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey showed us: *muslimīn* is the key. Solomon's stated deadline wasn't a logistical marker — it was a theological destination. And *ayyukum* — who among you? — opened the contest that revealed what nearness to Allah makes possible that power alone cannot.

The thematic journey showed us: these are the same insight from two directions. Whether we're asking who can move a throne (capacity question) or what it takes to move a heart (transformation question) — the answer is the same. Not strength. Nearness. Not argument. Experience. Not force. The patient creation of the moment in which someone's wisdom can make the leap it was already inclined to make.

**The throne was never the point. The heart was always the point.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life are you trying to argue someone into transformation, when what they need is an experience? What would it mean to trust their wisdom enough to stop explaining and start creating a different kind of moment?

2. If Solomon had *ʿilm min al-kitāb* — knowledge of the Book — as his primary resource, what does that suggest about how you should be investing your spiritual energy? What is your relationship with the Quran functioning like: a text you have access to, or a *source* you are in proximity to?

3. What "throne" have you built your identity around — an achievement, a role, a relationship, a self-concept — that, if it were gently displaced, might reveal that the world is larger and more magnificent than you've been allowing?

4. The Ifreet was not wrong to offer. It was capable. But there was a greater capacity in the room. How do you hold genuine confidence in your own capacity while remaining open to the possibility that someone near you has access to something your strength cannot match?

5. Solomon described their arrival *before it happened* as an arrival *in submission*. He was certain enough of the outcome to name it in the question. What would it mean to hold that kind of certainty — not arrogance, but quiet trust — about someone you love who hasn't yet arrived at the destination you can see for them?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Solomon's question reveals that his real goal was never the throne but the heart, and that it was Asif's nearness to Allah — not the Ifreet's power — that made the impossible move, because what is closest to the source can move what force alone cannot reach.**

### The Closing Invitation

After this reflection, perhaps the invitation is simple but demanding.

Invest in nearness. Not in strategy or strength alone — but in the kind of proximity to Allah through the Quran and through ʿibādah that transforms your du'ā from a request into a homecoming. Asif's ʿilm min al-kitāb wasn't a title — it was a relationship. Cultivate that relationship.

And when someone you love is approaching their Bilqis moment — when they are close but not yet there — trust the process enough to stop arguing. Create the experience. Move their throne gently. And trust that their wisdom, if it is genuine, will do the rest.
