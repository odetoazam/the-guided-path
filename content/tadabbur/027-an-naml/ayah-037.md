# The Threat That Never Names Its Weapon

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 37

**Arabic:**
ٱرْجِعْ إِلَيْهِمْ فَلَنَأْتِيَنَّهُم بِجُنُودٍ لَّا قِبَلَ لَهُم بِهَا وَلَنُخْرِجَنَّهُم مِّنْهَآ أَذِلَّةً وَهُمْ صَٰغِرُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Irjiʿ ilayhim fa-la-naʾtiyannahum bi-junūdin lā qibala lahum bihā wa-la-nukhrijannahum minhā adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūn.*

**Translation:**
"Return to them — for we shall surely come to them with armies they have no power to face, and we shall surely drive them out of it — humiliated and made small."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Picture the scene the original listener of this ayah was being asked to picture.

A queen — and not a small queen. The Queen of Saba ruled a kingdom in southern Arabia so wealthy the Quran calls her throne ʿaẓīm — *magnificent*. Her people had a sun-temple culture, sophisticated irrigation, trade networks that ran the length of the peninsula. She was a real political power, and she knew how power worked.

She had just received a letter. A letter from a king to her north — a king named Sulayman. The letter was short. The letter said, *do not exalt yourselves above me, and come to me as Muslims.*

This is not the kind of letter queens receive. This is the kind of letter queens declare war over.

But she does not declare war. She is wiser than that. She gathers her council. She says — and this is in the previous ayahs — *I have never decided a matter without you present.* Her chiefs respond: *we are people of strength, of severe might, but the command is yours.* She makes a calculation. She says: *kings, when they enter a city, ruin it. They make the noble of its people the lowest. That is what they do.*

So she does not fight. She tries something subtler. She sends a gift. A diplomatic gift. The classical commentators describe it in extraordinary detail — gold bricks, slave boys dressed as girls and slave girls dressed as boys to test his perception, jewels, riddles. It is a gift designed to do a specific thing: **find out what kind of man this is.** Is he a king who can be bought? A king who can be impressed? A king who is, underneath the throne, just another king?

The gift arrives at Sulayman's court. The envoy stands before him. He sees the gold. He sees the slaves. He sees the riddles.

And he refuses it.

[PAUSE]

The ayah we are sitting with today is what he says next. It is his reply. It is what he tells the envoy to carry back to her.

It is sixteen words long. And in those sixteen words, the entire diplomatic situation reverses.

### The Hook

There is a phrase in this ayah — three small words in Arabic — that does something almost no other threat in scripture does. It threatens with a weapon, and **refuses to name what the weapon is.** Sulayman tells her armies are coming, and then he leaves the most important detail of the threat completely empty.

Why? What is in that emptiness?

That is what we are going to sit with today.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Irjiʿ ilayhim* — "Go back to them"

The ayah opens with a single command. *Irjiʿ.* Go back. Return.

Now — before we look at the word, look at who it is being said to.

The envoy. The man the queen sent. The man standing in Sulayman's court holding the bribe.

Think about how diplomatic exchanges normally work. When a head of state sends a gift, the receiving king accepts the gift, hosts the envoy, perhaps holds a feast, perhaps composes a written reply. Even a refusal is normally elaborate. There is protocol.

Sulayman does none of that.

His first word is *go back.*

Let's sit with this. *Irjiʿ* is from the root *r-j-ʿ* — to return, to send back, to revert something to its origin. The morphology is a simple imperative: command form, second person masculine singular. The most direct grammatical address Arabic has.

There is no *please.* There is no *and tell your queen, with all due respect.* There is no preamble.

There is just — *go back.*

Notice something. In the ayah just before this one — ayah 36 — Sulayman has already spoken at length to the envoy. He has rejected the gift. He has said *what Allah has given me is better than what He has given you.* He has given a speech. So *irjiʿ* here is not the beginning of his reply. It is the end of it. It is the dismissal.

And here is what is remarkable about that dismissal. Compare it to how the Queen, just ayahs later, will treat Sulayman when she comes herself. She enters his court. She sees the floor that looks like water. She lifts her skirt. She is corrected gently — *it is not water, it is glass.* She is hosted. She is taught. She is honored.

The contrast is the whole point. **Sulayman treats the gift with no ceremony, and the seeker with full ceremony.** When she sends him objects to test him, she gets a one-word dismissal. When she comes herself to learn, she gets a glass court.

The word *irjiʿ* is not rude. It is precise. It tells her — through her envoy — *the channel you have chosen is closed. If you want to deal with me, you come yourself.* And that is exactly what she does in the next ayah.

[PAUSE]

One word. And it has already restructured the entire diplomacy.

### Element Two: *bi-junūdin lā qibala lahum bihā* — "with armies they have no power to face"

Now we come to the threat.

*Fa-la-naʾtiyannahum bi-junūdin* — *we shall surely come to them with armies.*

I want you to notice the grammar before we touch the meaning, because the grammar is doing work.

*La-naʾtiyannahum* contains two emphasis markers stacked on top of each other. The *lām* of emphasis at the front. The *nūn* of emphasis at the end — what classical Arabic grammarians call *nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīla*, the heavy nūn of emphasis. When Arabic wants to make an oath-like, unbreakable assertion, this is the construction it uses. Sulayman is not predicting. He is swearing.

*We will surely, certainly, without question, come to them.*

Then: *bi-junūdin* — *with armies.* Note the indefinite. Not *with my armies* — *with armies.* Plural. Unspecified. We will return to that emptiness in a moment.

And then the phrase the whole ayah pivots on:

*Lā qibala lahum bihā.*

Let me teach this construction before we open it. *Lā qibala lahum bihā* is a classical Arabic idiom built from the root *q-b-l*, which carries the meaning of *facing, fronting, being in front of.* When you say someone has no *qibal* of something, you are saying they have no **front** to put against it. No face to oppose it. No surface that can stand toward it.

Lexicographers gloss it as: *they have no power to confront, no capacity to stand in front of, no ability to face.*

Now think about what that image actually says. It is not *they cannot defeat it.* It is something stranger. **They cannot even orient themselves toward it.** They cannot bring themselves to face it. The thing is so beyond them that the very act of standing in its presence is impossible.

Think of a child trying to face a tidal wave. The problem is not that the child will lose. The problem is that *facing* is not a category that applies. There is no posture you can take toward the thing.

That is what *lā qibala lahum bihā* is saying.

[PAUSE]

But here is where the linguistic work gets remarkable. Stay with me. Look at what Sulayman has said and what he has *not* said.

He has said: *armies will come.*
He has said: *they cannot face them.*

He has not said:
- How many armies.
- What kind of armies.
- Where they will come from.
- What weapons they have.
- When they will arrive.

Every detail that a real military threat would require — he has withheld.

Compare this to how human kings threaten each other. Human threats are catalogues. *I have ten thousand cavalry. I have war elephants. I have archers from the eastern provinces.* Specificity is how human power proves itself, because human power is finite and must be inventoried to be intimidating.

Sulayman's threat does the opposite. He empties the threat of specifics. *Armies. Indefinite. Unfaceable.* No inventory.

And here is what the unspecified threat does that the specified threat cannot do: **it cannot be planned against.** A specified threat can be matched. *He has cavalry, we recruit cavalry. He has archers, we build walls.* But how do you plan against *armies you have no power to face*? How do you defend against a weapon that has not been named?

You can't. The only response to an unspecified, unfaceable threat is surrender — or submission of a different kind.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more layer. Remember what the queen has at her court. Her chiefs said in ayah 33: *we are people of strength, of severe might.* They listed their resources. They counted their power. That is human kingship — power that knows itself by counting itself.

Sulayman replies with the opposite. He does not count anything. The empty inventory is the point. **A king who does not need to count his armies is a king whose armies are not the limit of his reach.**

What Sulayman is really saying — through what he leaves unsaid — is: *I am not the kind of king you have prepared for. The categories you used to assess me do not apply.*

The classical commentators note a beautiful detail here. Some of them — al-Rāzī among them — observe that Sulayman could have named jinn, named winds, named birds. He had all of these at his command, and earlier in the surah we have already seen him reviewing them. **He chose to name none of them.** The threat is more terrifying because it is unspecified than it would have been if specified. The unnamed army is bigger than any named one.

This is what the empty word does.

### Element Three: *adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūn* — "humiliated, and they made small"

Now we come to the closing phrase, and it is built from a doubling that is doing real work.

*Wa-la-nukhrijannahum minhā* — *and we shall surely drive them out of it.* Same emphatic construction as before — the *lām* and the heavy *nūn*. A second oath. The verb *nukhrij* is from the root *kh-r-j*, to exit, in the form-IV causative — *to make to exit, to expel, to drive out.*

Out of what? *Minhā.* Out of *it.* Feminine singular pronoun referring back to *qaryatihim* — their city, their land. Sulayman threatens to drive them not just to defeat but **out of their place.** This is not a threat of conquest where they remain in their city as subjects. It is a threat of displacement.

And then the qualifier: *adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūn.*

These are two words that, on the surface, look like synonyms. *Adhilla* means *humiliated, debased.* *Ṣāghirūn* means *small, made small, brought low.* English translations often collapse them: *humbled and disgraced,* or *abased and humiliated.* Two words that mean roughly the same thing.

But the Quran does not use synonyms.

Let me teach the distinction the classical lexicographers draw.

*Dhilla* — from the root *dh-l-l* — refers to a state of *outward humiliation.* It is the lowering of one's station. A king becomes *dhalīl* when he loses his throne, when his name is mocked, when he is paraded through the streets. *Dhilla* is what other people see and what other people do to you. It is **public, social, externally inflicted.**

*Ṣaghār* — from the root *ṣ-gh-r,* the same root as *ṣaghīr,* meaning small — refers to something different. The lexicographers gloss *ṣaghār* as *the smallness one feels in oneself.* It is the inward collapse of stature. The Mufradāt of al-Rāghib makes this point clearly: *ṣaghār* is *adh-dhull alladhī yalḥaqu al-nafs* — the lowliness that attaches to the soul itself. It is what you feel about yourself when you have been humiliated long enough that the humiliation has gone inside.

Now look at the order in the ayah.

*Adhillatan* first. *Ṣāghirūn* second.

Outer first. Inner second.

[PAUSE]

What Sulayman is describing is a sequence. First the public lowering — they will be driven from their city, their throne, their visible stature. Then the inner consequence — they will become small in their own eyes. The outward humiliation will produce the inward collapse.

And notice the grammatical shift. *Adhillatan* is a *ḥāl,* a circumstantial accusative — *in a state of humiliation.* It describes the condition under which they are driven out. It is something done *to* them. *Ṣāghirūn,* by contrast, is a separate clause — *wa-hum ṣāghirūn* — *while they themselves are small.* The pronoun *hum* is there. *They themselves.* It is no longer something done to them. **It is what they have become.**

This is a precise psychological observation. The ayah is mapping a real human sequence: *the things that are done to you become the things you become.* When humiliation is sustained long enough, it stops being a treatment and starts being an identity. The outer becomes the inner. *Adhilla* hardens into *ṣaghār.*

The classical commentators on this ayah note that this doubling is not redundancy — it is the destination. Sulayman is not just saying *they will be defeated.* He is saying *they will be defeated in such a way that they will know themselves as defeated.*

And here is the unseen reality this physical-political image is pointing toward.

The ayah is using the language of conquest to describe what happens to **anyone who places themselves above Allah's call after the call has reached them clearly.** The expulsion from the city is the visible image. But the inward becoming-small — *ṣaghār* — is the destination. **When a soul refuses guidance after seeing it, the soul does not stay the size it was. It becomes small in itself.** This is a psychological law, not just a political threat.

The queen will hear this threat. She will understand both halves. She will not let either happen to her. In ayah 44 she will say *I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulayman to Allah, the Lord of all the worlds.* She will choose to lower herself voluntarily — and by doing so escape both the *dhilla* and the *ṣaghār.*

The voluntary lowering before Allah is the only thing that prevents the involuntary *ṣaghār.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have looked at three things.

A one-word dismissal that restructures the diplomacy. A threat whose power lies in what it refuses to specify. A doubling that maps how outer humiliation becomes inner smallness.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the kind of authority Sulayman represents, and the kind of submission the queen will eventually choose.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Authority That Doesn't Need to Itemize Itself

Think about the people in your life whose authority you actually trust.

Not the people who tell you they are powerful. The people who don't have to.

There is a quality to certain kinds of authority — usually the rarest kinds — where the person carrying it does not need to display it. They do not list their credentials when they enter a room. They do not name-drop. They do not catalogue their resources. They walk in, they speak, and the room reorganizes around them. They have nothing to prove because nothing has been called into question that they need to defend.

You have met people like this. Maybe a teacher. Maybe an elder. Maybe a doctor in a moment of crisis when everyone else was panicking. The signature of their authority was not the inventory of their power. It was the **absence of any need to inventory it.**

Now think about the opposite. The person who tells you constantly how senior they are. The person who lists their degrees, their titles, their connections. The person whose every sentence is a small assertion of standing. We have all met those people too. And the pattern is so reliable it is almost a law: **the more someone has to tell you about their power, the less of it they actually have.**

The empty inventory is the signature of the real thing.

Now look back at Sulayman's threat.

*Bi-junūdin lā qibala lahum bihā.* Armies. Unspecified. Unfaceable.

He is the king who, more than any other king in the Quran, has *cause* to inventory. He has the jinn. He has the wind. He has the language of birds. He has a court that contains the *ʿifrīt* who can move thrones. If any king ever had a reason to list, he is that king.

He lists nothing.

And the queen — who has just received from her own chiefs an inventory of *their* power, *we are people of strength, of severe might* — receives this empty inventory in reply. The contrast must have been deafening. Her chiefs counted. He did not need to.

[PAUSE]

Here is the theme: **the deepest authority is the authority that does not have to argue for itself.** It speaks, and the speaking is the proof. It does not need to be propped up by display because it does not stand on display. It stands on something else.

What does it stand on?

In Sulayman's case — and this is what the surah has been building toward across many ayahs — it stands on the fact that he is not the source. He is the channel. The armies are not *his* in the possessive sense; they are armies Allah has placed at his command. The *junūd* the queen cannot face are, ultimately, *junūd Allāh* — and *no one* can face those. Sulayman does not need to list them because he is not the one who owns them.

The empty inventory is theological. It is saying: *what is coming for you is bigger than what I would be able to list, because what is coming for you is not really mine.*

And this is a psychological key the ayah is offering us.

**The need to inventory ourselves is the symptom of standing alone.** When we feel we have to prove our standing — list our accomplishments, name-drop our connections, argue for our worth — what we are confessing is that we believe our standing depends on us. That if we don't make the case, no one will.

The person who has internalized that Allah is the source of their standing has nothing to prove. Their *junūd* are not theirs to inventory. They can let the inventory stay empty.

The practical application: **notice, this week, the moments when you feel the urge to itemize yourself.** The moment you reach for the credential, the title, the connection, the achievement. That moment is a diagnostic. It is telling you that, in that moment, you are standing on yourself. You believe your worth depends on your inventory.

And then ask: *what would it look like to leave the inventory empty?* What would it look like to walk into the room, do the work, and let the work be the work — without the constant background hum of *do they know who I am, do they know what I've done?*

Sulayman left his inventory empty because he knew Whose armies they were. The deepest peace is the peace of knowing whose armies you have access to, and not needing to count them.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Mercy of the Threat

Here is something that takes a moment to see. Sit with it.

Sulayman threatens her.

A prophet — a *prophet* — threatens a queen with conquest, displacement, humiliation, the inward collapse of her people's stature. This is not gentle. The ayah is not gentle. *Adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūn* is not gentle.

Why?

Why is this the prophetic register at this moment? Why not the soft word, the patient invitation, the long road of slow persuasion?

Here is the answer, and it is one of the most important things the surah teaches.

She had already been invited gently. The letter — *do not exalt yourselves above me, and come to me as Muslims* — was the gentle invitation. She had received it. She had read it. She had understood it. Her response was not to come. Her response was to send a gift to see if she could buy her way out of having to come.

The gift was the moment of evasion. The gift said: *I have heard your call, and instead of answering it, I would like to negotiate it. I would like to convert this from a question of submission to a question of transaction.*

If Sulayman accepts the gift, the channel of the call closes. She walks away thinking she found a king who could be bought, a prophet who could be flattered, a religion that could be paid off. She loses the only chance she had at the truth.

The threat is what reopens the channel.

[PAUSE]

The threat is **mercy disguised as severity.** It refuses the transaction. It tells her: *the call was real. There is no negotiating it. There is no buying your way around it. You will face it, one way or another. You can come voluntarily — or it will come for you involuntarily.*

Notice what she does. Read what comes next in the surah.

She does not send another gift. She does not raise an army. She does not even reply with words.

She comes herself.

In the very next sequence of ayahs, she begins the journey to Sulayman's court. The threat worked — but it did not work by frightening her into surrender. It worked by **clarifying what the situation actually was.** She had been treating it as a diplomatic problem. The threat told her it was something else. So she came to find out what.

And when she came, what did she find? Not armies. Not conquest. She found a glass floor she mistook for water — and a teacher who corrected her gently. She found a court where she was *honored,* not humiliated. She found, in ayah 44, the words that are the true destination of the entire narrative: *rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī, wa-aslamtu maʿa Sulaymāna lillāhi rabbi al-ʿālamīn* — *my Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit, with Sulayman, to Allah, Lord of the worlds.*

The threat never had to be executed. The threat existed *so that it would never have to be executed.* It was the verbal severity that prevented the actual severity. It was the small humiliation of being told *you cannot face me* that prevented the catastrophic humiliation of *adhillatan wa-hum ṣāghirūn.*

[PAUSE]

There is a principle here that runs through how Allah deals with souls.

**The warning is part of the mercy.** The verses of warning in the Quran — and there are many — are not the opposite of mercy. They are mercy in another voice. They are the voice that says: *do not walk further down this road. There is something at the end of it you do not want to find. Turn now, while turning is still cheap.*

The soul that hears only the gentle invitation and ignores it has not yet been fully addressed. The warning is what completes the address. The warning is what says: *this is real. The choice you are making is a real choice. There are real ends to the roads you are choosing between.*

We live in a moment that is allergic to the language of warning. We want spirituality to be all invitation, all softness, all *come as you are.* And there is truth in that — the call begins with invitation. But the call does not end with invitation. At some point, for some souls, the call requires the second voice — the voice that says: *you have heard me. Do not pretend you have not.*

[PAUSE]

The practical application is uncomfortable but worth sitting with.

**Are there warnings in your own life that you have been treating as harshness when they were actually mercy?** Words from a teacher, a parent, a friend, a verse — words that you flinched from because they did not match the gentle register you wanted, but which you now suspect were trying to clarify the road for you?

The queen could have stayed offended. She could have read Sulayman's reply as arrogance, as belligerence, as a foreign king's overreach. She would have been within her rights as a queen to read it that way. And if she had, she would have lost everything.

She did not. She read past the surface of the words to the situation underneath them. She understood: *this man is not threatening me because he is hostile. He is threatening me because he is trying to wake me up.*

And she came.

May we be the ones who hear the warning the way she did.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what the linguistic and the thematic have shown — they are the same insight from two directions.

**Linguistically:** Sulayman speaks with maximum compression. One-word dismissal. Empty inventory. A doubled phrase that maps outer to inner. Every grammatical choice tightens the threat into something that cannot be evaded.

**Thematically:** The compression is the mercy. The empty inventory is the signature of authority that does not stand on itself. The threat is the voice that prevents the threat from being executed.

The ayah is doing something very specific. It is showing us what prophetic speech does that diplomatic speech cannot do. **Diplomatic speech negotiates. Prophetic speech clarifies.** It does not enter the bargain. It refuses the gift. It refuses the inventory. It says: *here is what is real. Choose.*

And the queen — and this is the great gift of the whole Saba narrative — chose.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life do you reach for the inventory? When you feel the urge to list your credentials, your accomplishments, your standing — what is that urge defending?

2. Is there a warning you have been treating as harshness that might actually have been mercy in another voice?

3. The queen sent a gift to avoid having to come herself. What gifts have you sent to Allah — what offerings of effort, money, ritual — to avoid having to come yourself?

4. Sulayman left his inventory empty because he knew whose armies they were. Whose armies do you act as if you depend on?

5. *Adhilla* hardens into *ṣaghār.* The outer becomes the inner. Where in your own life have you let an external state become an internal identity — and is it time to unmake it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The deepest authority does not have to inventory itself, the truest mercy sometimes wears the voice of warning, and the soul that voluntarily lowers itself before Allah will never have to be lowered by Him.

### The Closing Invitation

The ayah is short. The lesson is not.

This week, try one thing. When the urge to inventory yourself rises — in a meeting, in a conversation, in your own internal monologue — notice it, and let it pass without acting on it. Leave the inventory empty for one moment. See what it feels like to not need the listing.

And when a hard word reaches you — from a verse, from a person, from your own conscience — pause before you flinch. Ask whether the harshness might be mercy in another voice. Ask whether the speaker might be Sulayman, and whether the gift you have been sending is an attempt not to come yourself.

If the answer is yes, then do what the queen did.

Come.

### Du'a

Allah, You who do not need to inventory Your power because all of it is Yours — teach us to leave our own inventories empty before You. Make us the ones who hear Your warnings as mercy, not as harshness. And let us come to You, the way she came — voluntarily, in our own feet, before any *junūd* have to be sent. Ameen.
