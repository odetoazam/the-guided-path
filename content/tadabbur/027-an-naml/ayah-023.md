# Surah An-Naml 27:23 — The Throne, the Everything, and the Hoopoe's Astonishment

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:27:23] إِنِّى وَجَدتُّ ٱمْرَأَةً تَمْلِكُهُمْ وَأُوتِيَتْ مِن كُلِّ شَىْءٍ وَلَهَا عَرْشٌ عَظِيمٌ

**Transliteration:**
*Innī wajadtu mraʾatan tamlikuhum wa-ūtiyat min kulli shayʾin wa-lahā ʿarshun ʿaẓīm.*

**Translation:**
*Indeed, I found a woman ruling them, and she has been given of all things, and she has a magnificent throne.*

---

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, you have to feel where you are standing.

You are standing in the court of Sulaymān, peace be upon him. Not just any king. The king whose father David received the *Zabūr*. The king who asked Allah for a kingdom *that no one after him would have* — and was given it (Surah Ṣād 38:35). The wind moves at his command. The jinn build for him. He understands the speech of birds. His military reviews include creatures the rest of us cannot see.

Imagine the scale of that court. Imagine the silence required for such a king to notice that one bird is missing. And he does notice. In the previous ayah he says: *"Why do I not see the hoopoe? Or is he among the absent?"* He threatens the bird with severe punishment unless it brings *sulṭānin mubīn* — clear authority — for its absence.

Now the bird returns. Out of breath, presumably. Standing before a court that includes jinn and wind and prophet-kingship. And the first words out of its mouth are not an apology. They are a *report*.

[PAUSE]

Now look across the map. The hoopoe has flown south, far south, over deserts and mountains, until it reached Sabaʾ — what we today call Yemen. In the centuries before Islam, Sabaʾ was a wonder. Its dam at Maʾrib irrigated gardens that the Quran will later call *jannatān* — two paradises (Surah Sabaʾ 34:15). Its trade in frankincense and myrrh moved through the entire ancient world. Its queen, in the memory of the region, was a woman of legendary intelligence.

And Sulaymān's empire — the empire of *tawḥīd*, the empire of the prophet-king — has, until this moment, *not known about her*.

That is the climate. A prophet who has been given everything. A queen who, the bird is about to say, has *also* been given everything. And a small bird standing between two thrones, about to speak.

### The Hook

Listen closely to what the hoopoe chooses to mention. Not her army. Not her wealth measured in gold. Not the size of her territory. Three things, in this order: **a woman ruling them, given of all things, and a magnificent throne.**

Why these three? Why this order? And why does a bird that has just escaped capital punishment for being late spend its first breath describing not its own situation but *hers*?

[PAUSE]

There is something the hoopoe sees that we have to be prepared to see. Let us prepare.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wajadtu* — "I Found"

Before we touch this word, let us think about what it means to *find* something.

In English, when you say "I found a coin in the street," you are reporting an encounter you did not plan. The coin was already there. You did not put it there. You simply *came upon* it. There is humility in the word *found*. It implies that the thing existed before you noticed it, and that your noticing did not create it.

Now contrast that with "I discovered a new island." There is more ego in *discover*. As if the island was hiding from you and you, the great explorer, wrenched it into visibility. Cook "discovered" Australia. The aboriginal peoples who had lived there for sixty thousand years would beg to differ.

Hold that distinction. Hold the difference between *found* and *discovered*.

Now look at what the hoopoe says: **innī wajadtu** — *indeed, I found.*

The Arabic verb is from the root **w-j-d**. Its primary lexical sense is *to come upon, to encounter, to find present.* It is the same root from which Arabic builds *wujūd* — existence, being-found-there. To say *wajadtu* is to say: *I came upon something that was already there.*

Notice what the hoopoe does *not* say. He does not say *iktashaftu* — I uncovered, I exposed. He does not say *raʾaytu* — I saw. He says *wajadtu* — I found her there. She was already ruling. She was already there. My flight did not create her. My report does not invent her. I am simply telling you what was *already true* before I arrived.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. A bird in the court of a prophet-king is about to describe a kingdom of disbelievers — a kingdom that worships the sun, as we will learn two ayahs later. And the bird's grammar is *humble*. The bird does not say "I exposed them." It says "I found them." As if to say: *They exist. They have been existing. The fact that you did not know about them, O Sulaymān, does not change that they were here.*

There is something tremendous in this. The hoopoe will, in just two ayahs, condemn what these people worship. But before judgment, the hoopoe gives *witness*. And witness begins with the humility of *finding* — not the arrogance of *discovering*.

This is the first lesson the bird teaches us about how to encounter the world. **Before you judge, witness. Before you witness, find. And finding requires you to admit that the world existed before you arrived in it.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Wa-ūtiyat min kulli shayʾin* — "And She Has Been Given Of All Things"

This is the heart of the ayah. And we have to take it slowly because almost everything is doing work.

Let me first teach you a feature of Arabic grammar. Arabic has a distinction between the *active* voice and the *passive* voice — like English. *He gave her a book* versus *She was given a book*. In English we usually prefer the active. We are a culture obsessed with agency. *Who did this? Who is responsible? Who gets credit?*

The Quran will sometimes deliberately choose the *passive* — and when it does, the choice is almost always meaningful. Because in Arabic, when you remove the named giver, you create a *gap*. And the listener's mind cannot help but ask: *if she was given, then by whom?*

Now look at the verb here. Not *malakat* — *she possessed.* Not *jamaʿat* — *she gathered.* But **ūtiyat** — *she was given*. From the root **ʾ-t-y**, which carries the sense of *being brought, being made to come to.*

This is a passive verb with a *deleted agent*. The grammar refuses to name who did the giving.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Why would the hoopoe — describing a queen who is, at this moment in history, a *disbeliever* who worships the sun — use the language of *being given*? Why not just say "she has a lot of stuff"?

Because the hoopoe is a teacher. And the hoopoe knows that whatever this queen *has*, she did not *make*. The throne she sits on. The gold in her treasury. The wisdom that allows her to rule. The intelligence that her ministers will praise in the next passage. None of it was self-generated. All of it was *given*.

By whom? The grammar refuses to say. But everyone in Sulaymān's court — and every listener of the Quran — knows the answer. There is only One who gives in this absolute sense.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* in miniature. The bird describes a *seen* kingdom — gold, throne, palace — and through one passive verb, points the listener toward the *unseen* Giver behind it. The bird never says the word "Allah" in this ayah. It does not need to. The grammar has already pointed.

Now look at what she was given: **min kulli shayʾin** — *of all things.*

The construction here is precise. It is not *kulla shayʾ* — *every thing* — which would be a totalizing claim. It is *min kulli shayʾ* — *some of every thing*, *a portion of all things*. The little particle *min* — "from, of" — is doing tremendous work. It says: *of every category of good, she has been given a share.* Not all of everything. But a piece of everything.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where it gets stunning. Stay with me.

Just a few surahs earlier, in Surah Ṣād, Sulaymān asked Allah: *"My Lord, forgive me and grant me a kingdom that none after me will have."* And Allah granted it (38:35). Sulaymān has been given a kingdom that *no one else will ever have*.

So when the hoopoe says of Bilqīs: *she has been given of all things* — the listener should feel a small jolt. Because Sulaymān, *also*, has been given of all things. And more.

The bird is making, with the lightest possible touch, a *comparison*. *O Sulaymān, you have been given of all things. And so has she. The same Giver gave both.*

Why does this matter? Because in three ayahs the hoopoe will tell us something terrible: she and her people prostrate to the sun. They have taken what was *given* and directed their gratitude *to the gift*, not to the *Giver*.

This is the entire spiritual diagnosis of the human condition in five Arabic words. **Ūtiyat min kulli shayʾ** — she was given of all things. *And she does not know who gave them.*

[PAUSE]

How many of us live this way? You have been given of all things. Health you did not manufacture. A heartbeat you do not regulate. A mind you did not construct. A morning you did not summon. *Min kulli shayʾ* — of all things. And the question the ayah is silently planting in you, through one passive verb, is: *do you know who gave them?*

Or have you, like the queen at this stage of the story, mistaken the gift for the source?

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *ʿArshun ʿaẓīm* — "A Magnificent Throne"

Now we come to the third thing the hoopoe mentions, and this is where the linguistic precision becomes architectural.

The word **ʿarsh** — from the root **ʿ-r-sh** — in classical Arabic refers to a raised structure, a canopy, a seat of authority. When attached to a king, it means *throne* — the physical seat from which sovereignty is exercised.

The word **ʿaẓīm** — from the root **ʿ-ẓ-m**, the same root as *ʿaẓm*, *bone*, the structural core of the body — means *great, magnificent, immense*. Not just big in size. Great in weight, in significance, in gravity.

So: *ʿarshun ʿaẓīm* — a magnificent throne. A throne with weight.

That is the surface. Now let us go deeper.

[PAUSE]

This exact phrase — *ʿarshun ʿaẓīm* — appears in *only one other place* in the entire Quran. And once you hear where, this ayah will never sound the same again.

Surah At-Tawbah, ayah 129. The ayah every Muslim child memorizes:

> حَسْبِىَ ٱللَّهُ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ ۖ وَهُوَ رَبُّ **ٱلْعَرْشِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ**
> *Sufficient for me is Allah; there is no god except Him. Upon Him I have relied, and He is the Lord of the Magnificent Throne.*

The phrase *al-ʿarsh al-ʿaẓīm* — the Magnificent Throne — in Quranic usage, refers to the throne of *Allah*. The throne over the seven heavens. The throne which the Footstool, in the Throne Verse, only touches the edge of.

And the hoopoe — a *bird* — has just used that phrase to describe a piece of furniture in Yemen.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Do not rush past it.

What is the bird doing? Is the hoopoe being blasphemous? Is it claiming Bilqīs's throne is equal to Allah's?

No. The hoopoe is doing something more subtle and more devastating. The hoopoe is showing us how *the human mind confuses thrones*.

Here is a queen sitting on a throne so magnificent that a bird flying overhead reaches for the same vocabulary used for the throne of the Lord of the worlds. The throne is, in human terms, *that impressive*. Gold. Jewels. Scale. Weight. The kind of throne that makes a visiting ambassador's knees soften.

And what does this magnificent throne become for her people? An object of pride. A symbol of sovereignty. The center of a kingdom that worships the sun — because the sun is the most magnificent visible thing in the sky, and her throne is the most magnificent visible thing in the palace, and the human heart, untrained, *worships whatever is visibly magnificent*.

The bird, by using the phrase *ʿarshun ʿaẓīm*, is not exalting her throne. The bird is *exposing* the trap. *Look at how a created throne, magnificent enough, can pull human worship toward itself, away from the Throne whose Lord made every throne possible.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold this with me. Bilqīs's *ʿarshun ʿaẓīm* will not stay in Yemen for long. In ayah 38, Sulaymān will ask his court: *"Which of you can bring me her throne before they come to me in submission?"* A jinn offers to bring it before the king rises from his seat. A man with knowledge of the Book offers to bring it *before the blink of an eye*. And the throne arrives.

What does Sulaymān do with the throne when it arrives? He has it *altered* (ayah 41). And when she arrives and sees it, she is asked: *"Is your throne like this?"* — and her recognition of it becomes the beginning of her recognition of *something larger*.

The architecture of the surah is this: a throne that pulled a kingdom toward sun-worship is *moved across distances by Allah's permission* and made into the *door* through which a queen finds the One whose Throne cannot be moved by anyone.

The bird's word choice in ayah 23 — *ʿarshun ʿaẓīm* — is the *seed* of the entire transformation. The bird names the trap. The surah, ayah by ayah, springs the trap open.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

The bird has shown us three things: the *humility of finding*, the *passive voice of being given*, and the *trap of magnificent thrones*. Each one a seed.

Now let us ask what these reveal not just about words — but about reality itself. About how human beings encounter the world. About what we worship without realizing we are worshipping it. And about what a true witness sounds like when one heart stands before another.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Witness Who Reports Before He Judges

Let me start somewhere you have already been.

You are at a dinner. Someone you love is talking about a person they have just met. Within thirty seconds of describing this person, your loved one has rendered a verdict. *He is arrogant. She is fake. They are not for us.* The information about who this person actually *is* — what they said, what they did, what they were going through — never arrives. The judgment arrives first, and the description, if it comes at all, is shaped to defend the judgment.

We do this constantly. We *judge* before we *witness*. We arrive at conclusions before we have allowed the world to actually appear to us.

Now look at how the hoopoe speaks.

The hoopoe has just flown over a kingdom of *disbelievers*. A people who, the bird is about to say, *prostrate to the sun instead of Allah*. The hoopoe has every theological reason to lead with condemnation. To open his mouth and say: *O Sulaymān, I have found a kingdom of mushrikīn, deserving of conquest.*

He does not. He says: **I found a woman ruling them. And she has been given of all things. And she has a magnificent throne.**

Three sentences. Pure description. Pure witness. Not a single word of evaluation. Not yet.

The judgment will come — in ayah 24. *I found her and her people prostrating to the sun instead of Allah.* But the bird earns the right to deliver that judgment by *first describing the world fairly*. By acknowledging that this queen is *real*, her sovereignty is *real*, her giftedness is *real*, her throne is *real*.

[PAUSE]

This is a method of seeing. And it is a method we have largely lost.

We live in a culture where the *first* thing said about a person, a movement, a community is the verdict. He is on the wrong side. She is part of the problem. They are the enemy. And the description of who they actually are, what they have actually built, what they have actually been given — that description either disappears or is bent to serve the verdict.

The Quran is teaching us something different through this small bird. **It is teaching us that fair witness is the precondition of legitimate judgment.** That you do not have the moral right to call someone misguided until you have first acknowledged what is real and present and given in their life.

Notice that the hoopoe acknowledges three things about a queen who worships the sun:
- She *rules* — sovereignty is a real thing she possesses.
- She has been *given* — her resources are not stolen but bestowed.
- Her throne is *magnificent* — its grandeur is not denied or minimized.

And only *after* this honest seeing does the bird name the spiritual problem.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how we live tomorrow?

It changes the order of our sentences. When you encounter someone whose worldview offends you — a person whose politics you find dangerous, a relative whose practice you find lacking, a public figure whose influence you find harmful — try the hoopoe's discipline. Before the verdict, the witness. Before "they are wrong about X," the acknowledgment of what is real about them. *They have built something. They have been given something. They are not nothing.*

You may still arrive at the verdict. The hoopoe certainly does. But the verdict will land differently — on you and on the listener — when it has been preceded by fair seeing.

The reason this matters spiritually is because *Allah sees fairly*. Allah, who knows the queen's sun-worship perfectly, also knows what He has given her. And the angel who reports to the prophet-king models a seeing that participates in *Allah's* seeing — not a seeing distorted by partisan urgency.

To witness before you judge is to imitate, in your small way, the perfect Witness.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: When the Gift Becomes the God

Now let us go to the deepest layer of this ayah. The layer that makes Surah An-Naml a mirror you cannot turn away from.

The hoopoe says: she has been *given* of all things. The verb is passive. The Giver is unnamed but unmistakable. And in the very next ayah, the bird tells us: *she and her people prostrate to the sun.*

Hold these two facts side by side.

Allah gave her of all things.
She prostrates to the sun.

The sun is *one of the things she was given.* The sunlight that ripens her gardens. The warmth that makes Sabaʾ habitable. The cycle of day and night that organizes her kingdom's labor. All of it given. And of all the things in the gift-pile, *the most visible, the most magnificent, the most reliably generous* — the sun — she has elevated into the *role of giver*.

She has confused *the gift* with *the source*.

[PAUSE]

This is not an ancient Yemeni problem. This is the human problem. Read it slowly: *we tend to worship whatever is most generously, visibly giving to us.*

For the people of Sabaʾ it was the sun. For the modern person, it is rarely the sun. It is more often:

- The *career* that gives you identity, income, purpose. So you organize your hours around it, sacrifice family for it, fear losing it more than you fear losing your prayer.
- The *relationship* that gives you safety, recognition, comfort. So you bend your conscience to keep it, accept what should not be accepted, become unable to say no.
- The *platform* that gives you attention, validation, voice. So you check it before you check on your soul, perform for it, and feel — when it withdraws its blessing — that something existential has been lost.
- The *child* who gives you meaning. The *image* you have built that gives you status. The *intellect* that gives you mastery.

None of these things are evil. The sun is not evil — Allah made it. Bilqīs's throne is not evil — Allah let her have it. Your career, your love, your platform, your child — all *given*. *Min kulli shayʾin* — of all things.

The problem is not the gift. **The problem is when the heart's prostration silently relocates** — from the One who gave the thing, to the thing itself.

[PAUSE]

How do you know when this has happened? Here is the diagnostic the surah quietly offers.

Ask yourself: *What would terrify me to lose more than I fear losing my relationship with Allah?*

Whatever answers that question — *that* is the sun you have been prostrating to.

Bilqīs would have lost her sanity at the thought of the sun disappearing. Her entire kingdom's economy was built on its reliability. Of course her heart bowed to it. And we, in our way, have built kingdoms whose entire economy of meaning depends on a particular gift remaining in place.

The mercy of the surah is what happens *next*. Bilqīs is not destroyed for this. She is not condemned. She is *invited*. Sulaymān sends a letter. The letter quotes *bismillāh ar-raḥmān ar-raḥīm.* She convenes her council. She visits. She is shown her own throne, altered. She sees the palace floor that looks like water. And by ayah 44, she says:

> رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ
> *My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit, with Sulaymān, to Allah, Lord of the worlds.*

What changes between ayah 23 and ayah 44? *Her seeing.* She comes to *recognize* that the things she was given — including her magnificent throne — were *given*. That the One who gave them is the only One worthy of the heart's prostration. That she has been worshipping *a thing inside the gift-pile* instead of *the One who set the gift-pile down*.

[PAUSE]

The hoopoe's grammar in ayah 23 was the seed of her later confession. **Ūtiyat** — she was given. The passive voice was already pointing toward the missing Giver. The whole surah is the working out of what that one passive verb implied.

What does this change about how we live tomorrow?

It changes the question we ask when we wake up. The instinct, when life is good, is to ask: *what should I do with all this?* That is the wrong first question. The first question, the question the hoopoe's grammar is teaching us, is: *who gave me all this?*

Because when you remember the Giver, the gift becomes a *door* — a passage back to the One it came from. When you forget the Giver, the gift becomes a *wall* — a magnificent throne you start to prostrate to.

A small daily practice: when something good arrives — a conversation, a meal, an unexpected ease — say *ūtītuhu* — *I was given this*. Not *I earned it. I built it. I made it happen.* Just: *I was given it.* Let the passive voice do its work. Let the grammar of your inner life keep pointing you back toward the unnamed Giver who is, in fact, named on every page of His book.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Look now at what the bird actually did in eleven words.

The hoopoe gave a triple structure that mirrors the entire spiritual situation of the human being:

1. **A woman ruling them** — *the visible reality.* What appears to be in charge.
2. **Given of all things** — *the unseen truth behind the visible.* That whatever appears in charge has, in fact, been given.
3. **A magnificent throne** — *the trap that hides the truth.* The thing whose grandeur seduces the heart away from the Giver.

This is the entire human condition compressed. We see the visible (a queen, a sun, a career). We forget the unseen (that all of it was given, by One). And we get caught by what is most magnificent (the throne, the platform, the gift) and prostrate to it instead of to the One whose Throne is the *real* magnificent throne.

The bird saw all of this in a single overflight of Sabaʾ. And in eleven Arabic words, the bird gave Sulaymān — and gave us — the entire diagnosis.

### Bringing It Together

The two journeys we walked turn out to be one journey.

*The witness who reports before he judges* — the hoopoe's discipline of fair seeing — is the *prerequisite* for understanding what is actually wrong with Bilqīs's kingdom. Until you have honestly acknowledged that she has been *given*, you cannot accurately name that she is *prostrating to the gift*. Cheap condemnation that skips the seeing produces self-righteousness, not transformation.

And *when the gift becomes the god* — the diagnosis the bird is silently delivering through a passive verb — is the *content* the witness is preparing us to receive. The hoopoe's careful grammar teaches us *how to see* so that the surah can teach us *what to see*.

Linguistic precision and spiritual diagnosis are not two things. They are one thing. *The way the Quran speaks is part of what the Quran is saying.*

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I *judging* before I have *witnessed* — pronouncing verdicts on people I have not first acknowledged as real, given, and present?

2. What in my life am I most afraid of losing? Am I willing to be honest about whether that fear has migrated into the seat of worship?

3. When I describe my own success — to others, to myself — do I use the passive voice that points to a Giver, or the active voice that gathers credit to me?

4. What is the *magnificent throne* in my life right now? The visibly impressive thing my heart has begun to bow toward without realizing it?

5. Bilqīs took an entire surah's worth of letters, journeys, and signs to recognize her Giver. What sign am I currently being shown — and not yet recognizing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

A small bird teaches a great king that the most dangerous thing about a magnificent throne is not the throne itself but the way it can pull a heart's prostration *toward the gift and away from the Giver* — and that fair seeing of the gift is the first step back to the One who gave it.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to fly to Sabaʾ. You are already in a kingdom of given things. Look around the room. The light. The breath. The body. The relationships. The capacities. *Ūtīta min kulli shayʾ* — *you have been given of all things.*

The invitation is simple and difficult. **Find your magnificent throne.** Name it honestly. And then move your prostration — gently, daily, sentence by sentence in your own grammar — back to the One whose Throne neither moves nor depends on your noticing.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who give of all things to those who recognize You and to those who do not — let me not be among those who prostrate to the gift while forgetting the Giver. Train my eyes in the hoopoe's discipline: to see fairly before I judge, and to see what is given as given. And when a magnificent throne rises before me, let my heart find its way past it to the only Throne worthy of my submission — the Throne of Yours, the One, the Magnificent.*

*Āmīn.*
