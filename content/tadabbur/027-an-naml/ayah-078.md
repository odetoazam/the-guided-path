# The Judge Who Already Knows

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 78

> إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَقْضِي بَيْنَهُم بِحُكْمِهِۦ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ

**Inna rabbaka yaqḍī baynahum bi-ḥukmihi, wa huwa al-ʿAzīzu al-ʿAlīm.**

*Indeed, your Lord will judge between them by His judgment. And He is the All-Mighty, the All-Knowing.*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

Picture a courthouse. Not a modern one with fluorescent lighting and rows of wooden benches — picture an ancient courthouse, the kind where the king himself sat at the end of the great hall and listened to the disputes of his people. Two parties enter. Each has a story. Each is convinced. Each has waited months, sometimes years, for this day. And the room is heavy with the knowledge that whatever the king says is final.

Now hold that image and step into the world of Surah An-Naml.

This surah has been doing something extraordinary. It has paraded before us four prophets in succession. Musa standing before a fire that did not burn. Dawud and Sulayman receiving knowledge that bent the world to their listening. Salih warning a people who would not warn themselves. And in every single one of these stories, the same pattern: a community split. Some who saw. Some who refused to see. Two camps standing in a courtroom of their own making, each certain, each defending its ground, each convinced that history would vindicate it.

And then, as the surah moves toward its close, the camera turns. It pans away from the ancient prophets and lands on the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah, surrounded by his own divided people. They are arguing about the same thing the ancient nations argued about. They are mocking the warning. They are splintering into factions. They are demanding signs and refusing the signs that come.

And the Prophet ﷺ is standing in the middle of it. Tired. Outnumbered. Watching the disputes pile up with no resolution in sight.

It is into that exhaustion that Allah speaks this ayah.

### The Hook

Nine words. That is all. Nine words containing a verdict that has not yet been delivered, a judge who is named twice over, and a quiet promise that every unresolved argument in human history is being held — not lost, not forgotten, not abandoned to whoever shouts loudest — but **held**, until the One who sees everything decides to speak.

Stay with me. Because if we listen carefully, we will discover that this short ayah is not just a promise to the Prophet ﷺ. It is a promise to anyone who has ever stood inside an argument they could not win, watched truth get trampled, and wondered if anyone was keeping score.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Inna Rabbaka* — "Indeed, your Lord"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open this word.

In Arabic, when you want to make a statement absolutely certain — when you want to remove all doubt, all hesitation, all *maybe* from a sentence — you place a particle in front of it called **inna**. English has no real equivalent. The closest we can get is "indeed" or "truly," but those are weak compared to what *inna* does. *Inna* is a hand placed on the listener's shoulder. It is the speaker leaning in and saying: *listen — what I am about to say is not opinion, not estimate, not hope. This is fixed.*

Now here is what is striking. Arabic does not require *inna* every time you make a statement. Most statements in the Quran do not begin with it. *Inna* is reserved. It appears when something is being said either against doubt, or to settle something the listener might be tempted to question.

So when Allah opens this ayah with *inna*, He is responding to something. He is responding to a hesitation that has not been spoken aloud — but is hanging in the air. The hesitation of a community watching their Prophet mocked. The hesitation of a heart watching injustice go unanswered. The unspoken question: *will this ever be resolved?*

And before the question even forms on the lips, Allah answers it: **inna**. Indeed. It is settled.

Now notice what comes next: *Rabbaka*. **Your Lord.**

Allah does not say *Allah* here. He does not say *the Lord*. He says *your Lord* — speaking directly to the Prophet ﷺ. And the word *Rabb* is itself remarkable. It does not just mean "Lord" or "Master." A *Rabb* is the one who nurtures, who raises up, who tends to growth, who takes responsibility for the becoming of what is under His care. A gardener tending a vine. A mother tending a child. *Rabb* carries that intimacy.

So before Allah says a single word about the verdict, He has placed two pillars under His Prophet:

**Indeed** — this is certain.
**Your Lord** — the One who has been raising you, tending to you, who knows you by name, who has not forgotten a single moment of what you have endured.

Listen to what this does. Imagine telling an exhausted person, "the case will be decided." That is information. Now imagine telling them, "the case will be decided — by the One who has been with you the whole time, who has watched every tear, who has not missed a single thing they did to you." That is not information. That is a hand on the shoulder.

[PAUSE]

The verdict has not been delivered yet in this ayah. But before we hear what the verdict is, we have already been told who is delivering it. And that — by itself — is half the comfort.

### Element Two: *Yaqḍī Baynahum bi-Ḥukmihi* — "Will Judge Between Them by His Judgment"

Now here is where the ayah does something quietly stunning. It uses two different words for judgment in the same breath.

The first word is *yaqḍī*. The root is **q-ḍ-y**. In Arabic, *qaḍāʾ* means a decisive ending — a matter brought to its final close, the moment a thing becomes done and cannot be undone. When a debt is settled, you say it has been *qaḍā*. When a matter is concluded with no possibility of reopening, it is *qaḍāʾ*. The word carries finality. It is the door closing.

The second word is *ḥukm*. The root is **ḥ-k-m**. *Ḥukm* is wisdom-laden judgment — the kind of decision that is not just an outcome but an enactment of *understanding*. The same root gives us *ḥikmah*, wisdom. A *ḥakīm* is one whose judgments arise from depth, from seeing the whole. *Ḥukm* is not just a verdict; it is a verdict that *makes sense* of the entire matter being decided.

Now look at what Allah has done. He says: *yaqḍī baynahum bi-ḥukmihi.* He will *qaḍī* — bring it to its decisive close — *by His ḥukm* — by His wisdom-judgment.

In other words: the closing is not arbitrary. The finality is not raw power slamming a gavel. The end is *qaḍāʾ*, but the means is *ḥukm*. The door closes — but the closing is the enactment of perfect understanding.

This matters enormously. Because human courts often do one without the other. We have judges who close cases without understanding them — verdicts handed down by exhausted clerks, by overworked systems, by people who never quite saw the parties before them. The case ends, but no wisdom was enacted; only a procedure was completed. And we have wise people who understand everything but cannot close anything — counselors who see the truth perfectly and have no power to enforce it.

Allah's judgment is both. It is a closing — final, irrevocable, the door shut — and it is a wisdom — the kind of judgment that makes the parties themselves, finally, see.

Now stay with me, because there is one more thing here that we cannot miss.

Notice the preposition. *Baynahum* — "**between** them." Allah does not say He will judge **against** them. He does not say He will judge **for** one side. He says He will judge **between** them. That little preposition is doing real work. It tells us that this is not yet a settled question of guilt and innocence in the language of the verse — it is a *dispute*, two parties, and Allah is positioning Himself as the One standing between them, ready to issue the only verdict that will be honored on the Day no other verdict matters.

Why is that comforting? Because if you are the one being wronged, you are not waiting for Allah to take your side as a favor. You are waiting for the One who already stands *between* — neutral in the sense that He will not be swayed, present in the sense that He sees both — to render the only judgment that gets the case right.

[PAUSE]

So we have *qaḍī* — the closing. *Ḥukm* — the wisdom. *Baynahum* — the position of perfect equidistance from which true judgment can be made. And all of it tied together in nine syllables.

### Element Three: *Wa Huwa al-ʿAzīzu al-ʿAlīm* — "And He is the All-Mighty, the All-Knowing"

Now we reach the closing of the ayah, and this is where we have to slow down most carefully. Because Allah ends this verse by naming Himself with two of His attributes — and which two He chooses, and the **order** in which He places them, is doing something we cannot afford to miss.

Let me first teach you something about how the Quran ends its verses with divine names.

Allah closes hundreds of ayahs with pairs of His attributes. *Al-Ghafūr al-Raḥīm* — the Forgiving, the Merciful. *Al-Samīʿ al-Baṣīr* — the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing. *Al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — the All-Mighty, the All-Wise. And classical scholars noticed something remarkable: the pair is never random. The two names that appear are always the two names that the **content of the ayah requires**. They are the seal of the ayah — the divine signature that confirms what the ayah just said.

So when we see *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm* at the end of this verse, we have to ask: why these two? Why not *al-Ḥakīm* (the Wise) — wouldn't that fit a verse about *ḥukm*? Why not *al-ʿAdl* (the Just) — wouldn't that fit a verse about judgment?

Let us take them one at a time.

**Al-ʿAzīz** comes from the root **ʿ-z-z**, which carries the meanings of strength, of being unconquerable, of being of such elevation that nothing can reach you to harm you. *ʿIzzah* is the dignity that comes from being beyond the reach of those who would diminish you. *Al-ʿAzīz* is the One whose decisions cannot be appealed, whose verdicts cannot be overturned, whose judgments are not subject to a higher court.

Now ask yourself — for a verdict to actually mean something, what does it require? It requires the **power to enforce it**. A judge in a kingdom where no one obeys is just a man with opinions. A verdict that cannot be carried out is paper in the wind. And the disbelievers in Makkah were essentially betting on this — betting that whatever Muhammad ﷺ said about a Day of Judgment, no power existed that could actually make it happen. So Allah names Himself *al-ʿAzīz*: the One whose decree, when issued, **lands**. There is no court above this court. There is no power that can suspend this sentence.

**Al-ʿAlīm** comes from the root **ʿ-l-m**, knowledge. But not just any knowledge. *ʿIlm* in its divine perfection is exhaustive, comprehensive, reaching what is hidden, what is whispered, what is forgotten by everyone but its owner.

And this is the second thing a verdict requires. A verdict needs **knowledge of the case**. A judge with absolute power but no knowledge would be a tyrant. A judge with absolute knowledge but no power would be a sage. Allah pairs them: the power that cannot be resisted, and the knowledge that cannot be deceived.

Now here is where it becomes breathtaking. Look at the *order*.

In many ayahs of the Quran, when these two names appear, *al-ʿAlīm* comes first. Why? Because most often the ayah is highlighting the foundation of judgment — *first* He knows, *then* He acts. Knowledge precedes power, in the logical sequence.

But here, in this ayah, the order is reversed. *Al-ʿAzīz* first. *Al-ʿAlīm* second.

Why?

Because of who is being addressed. The Prophet ﷺ in this moment is not anxious that Allah does not *know*. He has no doubt that Allah knows. He is exhausted by the appearance that nothing is being *done*. The disbelievers are mocking him openly. They are walking around free. They are arguing, plotting, gathering. And from the outside, it looks like the case has been forgotten.

So Allah leads with *al-ʿAzīz*: the One whose power is real, whose verdict will be enforced, whose patience is not absence. And only then does He follow with *al-ʿAlīm*: and yes, He knows everything you have endured.

The order is responsive. It is responsive to what the heart in that moment most needs to hear. Power first — *something will be done*. Knowledge second — *and it will be done with full sight of every wrong that was committed*.

[PAUSE]

This is the divine signature on this ayah. Not arbitrary. Not stock. Chosen.

### Bridge

We have spent time with three linguistic features:

The opening — *inna rabbaka* — that placed a hand on the Prophet's shoulder before any verdict was named. The middle — *yaqḍī baynahum bi-ḥukmihi* — that wedded finality to wisdom and placed Allah *between* the parties. And the closing — *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm* — the divine signature, ordered to answer exactly the fear in the listener's heart.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Argument You Cannot Win Has Already Been Heard

Let me start somewhere you have been.

You have been in an argument you could not win. Not because you were wrong — but because the other person held the microphone, or the room, or the institutional power, or the social capital, and you knew that whatever you said was going to be twisted, dismissed, or buried under the weight of who they were and who you were not.

Maybe it was at work. Maybe it was in a family. Maybe it was a public moment where you watched something true get shouted down, and you knew that if you spoke up you would only become the next thing being shouted down. So you stayed quiet. And the room moved on. And the false thing settled into the record as if it were the true thing. And you carried the loss home with you, and it sat in your chest, and it stayed there for years because there was no court you could take it to.

Now I want you to feel something specific. Not the loss — the *loneliness* of the loss. The sense that nobody saw it. That the wrong was committed and then absorbed into the silence of a world that did not notice.

That is the emotional landscape this ayah enters.

The Prophet ﷺ was standing in a much larger version of that room. The disbelievers of Makkah were not just arguing with him; they were rewriting the record of what had been said. They were calling him a liar to his face and a poet behind his back. They were instructing children not to listen to him. They were turning every confrontation into a public spectacle in which he was outnumbered and they controlled the narrative. And every day he watched something true get covered over by the noise of those who had no interest in the truth.

Into that, Allah says: **indeed your Lord will judge between them.**

Notice what He does not say. He does not say, "do not worry, you will win the argument tomorrow." He does not say, "their mockery is hurting them more than you." He does not give the Prophet ﷺ a tactical answer. He gives him a **temporal** answer: there is a court that has not yet convened. Its judge has been listening the whole time. The fact that no one has called the case yet does not mean the case has been dismissed. It means it is being held.

This is one of the most consequential reframes in the entire Quran. Allah is saying: the absence of immediate justice is not the absence of justice. The silence is not abandonment. The silence is the patience of *al-ʿAzīz* — the One who could close the case in an instant but is allowing the parties their full moment to enter their own evidence into the record before He renders the verdict.

Now sit with what this means for the argument *you* could not win.

It means that the argument was heard.

Not by the room you were in. Not by the people who had the power to dismiss you. By the One who was standing **between** you and the one who wronged you. The One whose verdict will not be subject to the social pressures, the institutional incentives, the tribal loyalties that buried the truth in this life.

And here is what changes when you know that. You stop needing the room. You stop needing the verdict of the people who never had the standing to render one in the first place. You can let the argument go — not because it did not matter, but because it has been *taken up* by a court that you trust to handle it properly.

This is not passivity. This is repositioning your sense of where the real verdict lives.

The practical application: the next time you carry an injustice that you cannot resolve — and we all carry several of these — do not try to litigate it in your own head over and over. That is what we do. We replay the moment. We rewrite our lines. We rehearse the better answer we should have given. Stop. The case has already been filed. *Inna rabbaka yaqḍī baynahum.* Hand the case to its rightful court — and free your own attention to live the rest of your life.

### Theme Two: Knowledge That Forgets Nothing — and Why That Should Move You Twice

Let us turn now to the divine signature: *al-ʿAlīm.* The All-Knowing.

I want you to think about something specific. Think about a moment in your life when someone *saw* you do something good — something small, unwitnessed, that you did not do for credit — and they noticed. And they mentioned it later. And you felt something almost embarrassing rise up in your chest: *they saw that?* It was such a small thing. You did not even remember it being a thing. But somehow the fact that another human being noticed it, kept it, and named it back to you — that did something to you. It made the small thing feel meaningful. It made you feel *seen*.

Now think about the opposite. Think about a moment when something was done *to* you — something cruel, something diminishing — and the person who did it walked away as if it had never happened. As if the moment had no weight. And you carried it for years not because the act was so large, but because nobody else seemed to know it had occurred. The lack of witness was almost worse than the act.

Human beings are creatures who need to be witnessed. The good we do feels light when nobody sees it. The wrong done to us feels heavy when nobody knows.

*Al-ʿAlīm* speaks to both.

The good you have done that no one saw — *He* saw. The patience you exercised when no one knew you were exercising it — *He* knew. The temptation you walked away from in a private moment — *He* recorded it. The kindness you showed the person who never thanked you — *He* counted it. Nothing has been lost. Nothing has been forgotten. The ledger of your life is not the version that the world remembers; it is the version that *al-ʿAlīm* keeps.

And the wrong done to you that no one acknowledged — *He* acknowledged. The pain you carried that no one validated — *He* validated. The injustice that was buried under the noise of those who held the microphone — *He* recorded it perfectly, with full context, with every shade of motive, with every consequence that rippled outward. Nothing was missed.

This should move you twice. Once with comfort: *I have been seen.* And once with sober attention: *I have also been seen.*

Because *al-ʿAlīm* is not selectively the witness of our wrongs and the recorder of others' wrongs against us. He is the witness of everything. Which means the same name that is comfort for the wronged is also accountability for the wrongdoer — and we are all, at different times in our lives, both.

Here is the practical thing. Most of us spend a great deal of our spiritual energy worrying about how we are perceived. We curate. We adjust. We perform. We let the perceptions of other people set the terms of what counts as our worth. And the moment we internalize *al-ʿAlīm* — really internalize it, not as a theological label but as a lived presence — something quietly collapses. The performance becomes unnecessary. There is one Audience whose perception is the only one that finally matters, and He already sees us as we are. There is no point performing for a court that has no jurisdiction.

This frees enormous energy. Energy you can now spend actually doing the good rather than arranging for the good to be witnessed.

[PAUSE]

The next time you find yourself adjusting your behavior because of who is watching — pause. Ask: am I adjusting toward Allah's gaze, or away from it? *Al-ʿAlīm* is in the room. The only gaze that needed adjusting toward was already there.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what the linguistic and the thematic have shown us — the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic showed us a verse that opens with intimacy (*your Lord*), moves through the wedding of finality and wisdom (*qaḍī* and *ḥukm*), positions Allah *between* the parties, and seals itself with two names ordered precisely to answer the heart's deepest fears: power that cannot be evaded, knowledge that cannot be deceived.

The thematic showed us a soul standing inside an argument it could not win, learning that the case was never lost — only held — and learning that the One holding it has the power to enforce a verdict and the knowledge to render it perfectly.

These are the same insight. The linguistic is the structure. The thematic is the experience of standing inside that structure.

And both rest on a single foundation: that there is a court above the courts of this world, and that court is not asleep.

### Questions to Carry

1. What argument am I still litigating in my head — that I should hand over to the court that has actually been hearing it the whole time?

2. Whose perception of me has been quietly running my behavior — and what would change if I lived as if *al-ʿAlīm* were the only audience that mattered?

3. Where in my life am I confusing the silence of justice with the absence of justice?

4. Is there a wrong I committed that I have never named — that *al-ʿAlīm* has been holding patiently, waiting for me to bring it to His court before His court convenes for it?

5. When I look at the world and see truth being trampled — do I respond with despair, or with the patience of one who knows the verdict is being held, not lost?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Every unresolved argument in human history is being held by the One whose power cannot be appealed and whose knowledge has not missed a single thing.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need the verdict of the room you were in. You do not need the world to acknowledge what was done. You do not need to keep replaying the moment to keep it alive in some court — because the only court whose verdict will finally matter is already in possession of the full record.

Let it go. Not because it did not matter. Because it has been *taken up* by the only court that can do it justice.

And live the rest of your life — the part that is still in front of you — as if you already know how the case ends. Because *inna rabbaka yaqḍī baynahum bi-ḥukmihi*. Indeed your Lord will judge between them by His judgment. And He is *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm*.

### Du'a

O Allah, You are *al-ʿAzīz* — let our hearts rest in the knowledge that no wrong done to us has escaped Your power to address. You are *al-ʿAlīm* — let our hearts rest in the knowledge that no good we have done has escaped Your record. Free us from the exhausting work of seeking verdicts from courts that were never qualified to render them, and let us live as those who have already entrusted their case to the One who judges between, by His judgment, with finality and with wisdom. Āmīn.
