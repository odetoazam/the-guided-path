# The Inheritance That Speaks: When Sulaymān Heard What Was Always Being Said

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَوَرِثَ سُلَيْمَٰنُ دَاوُۥدَ ۖ وَقَالَ يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ عُلِّمْنَا مَنطِقَ ٱلطَّيْرِ وَأُوتِينَا مِن كُلِّ شَىْءٍ ۖ إِنَّ هَٰذَا لَهُوَ ٱلْفَضْلُ ٱلْمُبِينُ

**Transliteration:**
Wa waritha Sulaymānu Dāwūda, wa qāla yā ayyuha-n-nāsu ʿullimnā manṭiqa-ṭ-ṭayri wa ūtīnā min kulli shayʾin, inna hādhā lahuwa-l-faḍlu-l-mubīn.

**Translation:**
*"And Sulaymān inherited from Dāwūd, and he said: 'O people, we have been taught the speech of the birds, and we have been given from every thing. Indeed, this is the clear bounty.'"*

---

## Introduction: The Surah of the Listener

Before we enter this ayah, you need to know which surah you've walked into.

This is **An-Naml** — *The Ant*. A surah named not after a prophet, not after a kingdom, not after a battle or a law. A surah named after an insect. After the smallest, easiest-to-overlook creature in the desert. The kind of thing your foot crushes without noticing on the walk to the mosque.

And not just an ant. **A speaking ant.** An ant who, in a few ayahs from now, will warn her colony to move out of the way of Sulaymān's army — and Sulaymān will hear her, and smile, and praise his Lord *because* he heard her.

This whole surah is about hearing what others cannot hear.

It is about a hoopoe — a small bird — who flies in with news from a kingdom Sulaymān does not yet know about. It is about a queen whose throne arrives before she does, because someone in his court has knowledge from the unseen. It is about Mūsā at the burning bush hearing his Name spoken from a tree. It is about Ṣāliḥ's people who refused to hear, and Lūṭ's people who refused to hear, and a Quran that ends by saying: *Allah will show you His signs, and you will recognize them — but your Lord is not unaware of what you do.*

It is the surah of the ear that has been opened.

And this ayah — ayah 16 — is the doorway. Before any of the wonder begins, the Quran tells us how Sulaymān got there. How does someone end up able to hear an ant? How does someone end up understanding a hoopoe?

The answer is one word: **inheritance.**

But not the inheritance you think.

[PAUSE]

Here's the hook. Sulaymān stands before his people and announces two gifts: *we have been taught the speech of the birds, and we have been given from every thing.* Read it casually and it sounds like a king bragging. Read it carefully — read it with the surah's whole architecture in view — and something else appears. Something almost the opposite of a boast.

The grammar of this ayah is hiding a confession. The verbs are doing something strange. And the order of the announcement — speech of the birds *first*, then "every thing" — that order is doing the heaviest work of all.

Let's open it slowly.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Waritha* — The Verb That Doesn't Mean What You Think

The ayah opens with a single word: **waritha** — *he inherited*. وَرِثَ.

Before we look at what Sulaymān inherited, sit with this verb. In Arabic, *wirātha* — inheritance — has a very particular range of meaning. The root و-ر-ث is what you find when wealth passes from a parent to a child after death. Dawūd died; Sulaymān received what was his. That's the obvious surface.

But here's something to teach first, before we even return to the ayah.

In Arabic legal and Quranic usage, *wirth* is **not** the word you would naturally use for the transmission of skill, training, or talent. If a master craftsman teaches his apprentice and the apprentice becomes excellent, you don't say the apprentice *waritha* the craft. You say he *taʿallama* — he learned. Or *akhadha* — he took. *Wirth* is reserved for something else: a transfer that happens not because the receiver earned it, but because of a relationship — a covenant — that already existed between them.

You inherit because of *who you are*, not because of *what you did*.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the ayah again. *Wa waritha Sulaymānu Dāwūda* — *and Sulaymān inherited Dāwūd*. Notice that the object of the verb is not "from Dāwūd" — it is "Dāwūd" himself, in the accusative. Sulaymān didn't inherit Dāwūd's *property*. The grammar lets it sit ambiguously. He inherited *Dāwūd*. The whole reality of his father.

What was that reality? Look one ayah back. Ayah 15: *We gave Dāwūd and Sulaymān knowledge, and they said: "All praise is for Allah, who has favored us above many of His believing servants."* Dāwūd was a man whose mountains glorified with him. A man whose iron softened in his hand. A man whose voice, when he recited the Zabūr, made the birds pause and listen — *yusabbiḥna bil-ʿashiyyi wal-ishrāq*, as Surah Ṣād describes them.

Dāwūd was a *listener* before he was a king. The Quran's whole portrait of him is of a man whose voice and whose heart were tuned to creation, and creation answered.

So when ayah 16 says *Sulaymān inherited Dāwūd*, the surah is telling us something about the receiving of prophethood: this gift of hearing the birds did not arrive in Sulaymān like a lightning strike from nowhere. It came down a line. It was prepared for him. His father's life — his father's relationship with creation — opened the channel through which Sulaymān would now hear.

Notice what this verb is *not*. It is not *kasaba* — he earned. It is not *istaḥaqqa* — he deserved. It is not *balagha* — he attained. It is *waritha*. Received. By relationship. By position in a chain.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because something important is shifting.

We tend to read prophets the way we read self-made heroes — as people who, through extraordinary virtue, earned an extraordinary station. The Quran is about to tell us, in Sulaymān's own mouth, that this is exactly the wrong picture. Sulaymān is going to use a verb-form in the next clause that completes what *waritha* has already started: a posture of pure receiving.

The ayah opens with inheritance because inheritance is the frame for everything else.

### Element Two: *ʿUllimnā* and *Ūtīnā* — The Voice That Confesses

Now look at what Sulaymān says: **ʿullimnā manṭiqa-ṭ-ṭayri wa ūtīnā min kulli shayʾin** — *we have been taught the speech of the birds, and we have been given from every thing.*

Here is where the grammar does something the English translation almost completely flattens.

Let me teach you a feature of Arabic before we use it. Arabic verbs come in what grammarians call *active* and *passive* voice — the *maʿlūm* and the *majhūl*. The active voice tells you who did the action. *ʿAllamtu-hu* — *I taught him*. The passive voice removes the doer and centers the receiver. *ʿullima* — *he was taught*. By whom? The verb does not say. The verb deliberately does not say.

In Arabic, the passive voice is often called *ṣīghat al-majhūl* — the "form of the unknown." Not because the agent is genuinely unknown, but because the speaker is choosing to *not name* the agent. Sometimes for politeness. Sometimes for emphasis. Sometimes — and this is the Quranic case — because the agent is so obvious, so towering, that to name Him would almost diminish Him by putting Him on the same grammatical plane as the receiving servant.

When Sulaymān says **ʿullimnā** — *we have been taught* — the agent is unspoken. But every listener knows. *Allah taught us.* The grammar puts Allah behind the action like the sun behind the sky — everywhere illuminating, never in the frame.

[PAUSE]

Now think about what this means in a king's mouth.

Sulaymān is the most powerful human being in his world. He commands wind. He commands jinn. He has armies of men and birds and creatures the Quran does not even fully describe. He could stand before his people and say: *I have mastered the speech of the birds. I have acquired knowledge of every thing.*

He says the opposite.

He uses two passive verbs back to back. *ʿullimnā* — *we were taught*. *Ūtīnā* — *we were given*. He erases himself from the doing of either gift. He does not say *ʿalimnā* (we know) or *akhadhnā* (we took). He says: *we were taught*; *we were given*. We are the receivers, not the achievers. We did not climb to this. It descended to us.

This is what you confess when you actually understand whose hand the gift came from.

[PAUSE]

And notice — *we*, not *I*. *ʿUllimnā* and *ūtīnā* are first-person plural. Some classical scholars read this as the royal *we* — appropriate to a king. Others read it as Sulaymān deliberately including his father Dāwūd, his court, his community — *we as a people have been gifted*. Others read it as the prophetic *we*, the pluralis majestatis humbly attributed not to the speaker but to the household of revelation.

These readings are not competing. They are layers. The same plural pronoun carries all three: *I as king speak with the dignity my office requires; I as son acknowledge the inheritance I received; I as prophet refuse to claim singular ownership of what was given to a chain of servants.* All three meanings are doing work simultaneously.

This is what Quranic Arabic does. A two-letter pronoun ending — *-nā* — and an ocean of theological humility opens beneath it.

### Element Three: *Manṭiq al-Ṭayr* — The Word That Should Not Exist

Now the most stunning element. *Manṭiqa-ṭ-ṭayri* — *the speech of the birds*.

The word *manṭiq* comes from the root **ن-ط-ق** — *nuṭq*. And here is what classical Arabic lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib) record about this root: *nuṭq* is not just "sound." It is not the noise an animal makes. It is *articulated speech* — speech that has *meaning*, that conveys *intent*, that can be *understood*.

In fact, classical philosophers translating Aristotle into Arabic chose this exact word — *nāṭiq* — to translate the Greek *logos* in the famous definition of the human being as *the rational animal*. *Al-ḥayawān al-nāṭiq* — the speaking animal, the meaning-making animal. *Nuṭq* is what was thought to *separate humans from animals*.

So when the Quran says *manṭiq al-ṭayr* — *the articulated, meaningful speech of the birds* — the Arabic ear hears something almost shocking. It is taking a word that defines human distinctiveness and applying it to creatures we usually classify as *non-speaking*. The birds, the Quran is saying, *are speaking*. Not chirping. *Speaking*. There is *meaning* in what they say. There is *intent*. There is communication that can be taught and learned the way any language can be taught and learned.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what this implies about creation.

In our usual picture, the world divides cleanly: humans speak, animals make noise, plants and stones are silent. The Quran's picture is radically different. Surah Al-Isrāʾ tells us *wa in min shayʾin illā yusabbiḥu bi-ḥamdihi* — *there is not a thing except that it glorifies His praise* — *wa lākin lā tafqahūna tasbīḥahum* — *but you do not understand their glorification.*

The world is not silent. The world is loud with praise. Mountains glorify. Birds glorify. Stones glorify. Trees glorify. Every leaf, every drop, every grain of sand has a voice that means something.

The deafness is on our side.

So what was Sulaymān given? Not the gift of *making* the birds speak. The gift of *hearing* what they were already saying. The miracle is not that creation suddenly developed a voice in his presence. The miracle is that one human ear was finally tuned to what creation had been saying since the beginning.

[PAUSE]

This is the *istidlāl* — the stepping stone. The physical image is birds chirping in a garden. The unseen reality is: *all of creation is in articulate worship, and the human is the one creature deaf to the chorus he was made to lead.* Sulaymān's gift exposes the rest of us. We are not in a quiet world. We are in a singing world. And we cannot hear.

Notice now the ordering of his announcement. He does not say: *we were given dominion of every thing, and also we were taught the speech of the birds.* He says the speech of the birds *first*. Then *every thing*. Why?

Because the speech of the birds is the *test case*. If you can hear the smallest, the most overlooked, the most easily-dismissed voices in creation — *then* you are fit to be given dominion over every thing. The smallness comes first because the smallness is the qualification. A king who cannot hear an ant is a king who cannot rule justly. A king who can — is given the wind.

The ayah's word order is its argument.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

So look at what these three linguistic features have done together.

*Waritha* told us this gift is received through relationship, not earned through striving. *ʿUllimnā* and *ūtīnā* told us that even at the height of his power, the prophet refuses to take the active voice — refuses to be the subject of the verb. *Manṭiq al-ṭayr* told us that the universe is articulate; that all creation speaks; and that Sulaymān's miracle is the opening of the human ear, not the opening of the bird's mouth.

Three linguistic features. One unified picture: **a man who knows that everything good in him was placed there, and a creation that is constantly telling its Lord what its Lord already knows — and we, you and I, walking through it without hearing.**

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Difference Between Acquiring and Inheriting

Let me start somewhere very ordinary.

You know what it feels like to have worked hard for something. You stayed up late. You sacrificed weekends. You earned the degree, the promotion, the body, the relationship, the reputation. And there is a particular flavor to that achievement. A flavor of *I made this*.

You also know — if you stop and think — what it feels like to have *received* something. The face you see in the mirror. The voice you speak with. The instincts that fire before you can think. The capacity for love. The fact that your eyes work this morning. The fact that your heart, right now, is beating without you having to remember.

Most of what you are was *given*. A small fraction of what you are was *earned*. And yet — confess it — the small fraction is what you talk about. The small fraction is what you put on your resume. The small fraction is what you take credit for. The vast given foundation underneath — *that* you barely thank Allah for, because it never even crosses your mind that someone gave it.

This is the disease the Quran is treating with this ayah.

[PAUSE]

Sulaymān stands at the absolute peak of human attainment. By any worldly measure, he *earned* his throne — through rectitude, through worship, through wisdom. And the first words out of his mouth as inheritor-king are: *we were taught. we were given.*

This is not false modesty. This is not the polite deflection a wealthy person makes when complimented. This is **a man who has actually seen the architecture of his own life** — who has noticed that even his worship is a gift (*how would I worship without a heart?*), even his wisdom is a gift (*how would I be wise without a mind that was placed in me?*), even his striving is a gift (*how would I strive without breath that is being given to me right now without my permission?*).

The deepest spiritual maturity does not feel like *I have achieved much*. It feels like *I have been given much*. The first sentence is the sentence of a self-made man. The second is the sentence of a servant.

[PAUSE]

And notice what this does to the *experience* of having things. Sulaymān has *every thing*. That phrase — *min kulli shayʾin* — should crush a normal man. The endless wind, the endless wealth, the endless knowledge — these are exactly the conditions under which most human beings forget Allah. The Quran tells us repeatedly that human beings tend toward *ṭughyān*, transgression, when they see themselves as *istaghnā* — self-sufficient, having no need.

Sulaymān received the maximum dose of "every thing" — the dose that breaks most people — and stood up under it. How? Because his grammar didn't change. Even with everything, he still said *ūtīnā* — we were given. The wealth never moved into the active voice in his speech. The wind never became *I command*; it remained *I was permitted to command*. The throne never became *mine*; it remained *entrusted*.

The defense against being destroyed by what you have is to remember whose hand placed it in yours.

**Practical application:** Tonight, before you sleep, list five capacities you exercised today — the ability to walk, to think, to taste, to recognize a face, to speak a language. For each one, place it in the passive voice. Not *I walked* — *I was given the ability to walk, and the ability worked today.* Do this until your speech, even silently, begins to confess what is true: that you are the receiver, not the source.

This is the entry-point of *ʿullimnā* in your own life.

### Theme Two: The Smallness That Qualifies You for the Largeness

Now to the second movement of the ayah. The order: *speech of the birds, then every thing*.

Think for a moment about how the world tells a powerful person to behave.

A king is supposed to be *removed*. A king's ear is supposed to be reserved for matters of state — for ambassadors, for generals, for the great voices of his time. A king who bends down to listen to peasants is considered weak. A king who listens to *animals* — to *insects* — would be considered insane.

Sulaymān is the exact inverse. The first thing he announces, the first sign of his prophethood that he names publicly, is: *I can hear the birds*. And in the very next ayahs, he is going to listen — actually listen, with seriousness — to an ant warning her colony, and to a hoopoe explaining where it has been.

[PAUSE]

A king who can hear an ant has solved a problem most kings never even know they have.

The problem is this: power deafens. The higher you climb, the more your ear gets crowded with the voices of people who want something from you. The poor get filtered out. The voiceless stay voiceless. Your own greatness becomes a kind of soundproofing. You can no longer hear the people whose lives are most affected by your decisions, because they have no way to reach your ear.

Sulaymān, by being given *manṭiq al-ṭayr*, was given a permanent immunity to this disease. His ear was structurally tuned to the voices of creatures with no political power. The hoopoe in his court is not a courtier. The ant in his path is not a citizen who voted. They are the lowest, the most overlookable. And he hears them as fully and seriously as he hears anyone.

This is why the order of the ayah matters. *Manṭiq al-ṭayr* comes *before* *kulli shayʾin*. Hearing the small comes before ruling the large. The listening qualifies the dominion. Take away the listening and the dominion would have destroyed him, the way it destroys every tyrant.

[PAUSE]

Now turn this on yourself.

You will, in your life, have power over someone. A child. An employee. A student. A spouse. A patient. A reader. Someone who, in some sphere, has less voice than you. The question this ayah is asking you, quietly: **can you hear them when they speak in the smallest voice?**

When the child says *I'm scared*, in a voice that is barely audible — do you hear it? Or are you waiting for them to scream before you turn around?

When the employee says *I don't think this is right* in the most cautious tone they can muster — do you hear it? Or do you require them to file a formal complaint before their words register?

When the elderly parent says *I'm okay* but their face says something else — do you hear what is *underneath* the words? Or only what is on top?

When your own *nafs* whispers, in the quietest interior voice, *something is wrong with how I'm living* — do you hear it? Or do you turn up the volume of your distractions until you don't have to?

[PAUSE]

This is what *manṭiq al-ṭayr* is asking of us. Not literal animal speech. The capacity to hear what is being articulated by the small, the overlooked, the inarticulate, the timid, the easily-dismissed. The Quran is telling us that the prophet who could hear an ant was the one given dominion over every thing — because Allah does not entrust the large to those who are deaf to the small.

You will not be given an army of jinn or a wind that obeys your will. But you will be given, in some measure, *every thing* — your relationships, your time, your influence, your platform. Whether that givenness blesses you or destroys you will depend, in significant part, on whether your ear is tuned to the smallest voice in your life.

**Practical application:** This week, identify one person in your life with the smallest voice — the person whose preferences, fears, or hesitations you most consistently overlook. It might be a child. It might be a service worker you encounter daily. It might be a sibling. Sit with them, in actual silence, and listen for what they are saying *underneath* what they are saying. Do not advise. Do not fix. Just hear. This is the entry-point of *manṭiq al-ṭayr* in a life without prophethood.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now look back. The ayah has a hidden symmetry.

It opens with **what Sulaymān received** — *waritha Sulaymānu Dāwūda*. It closes with **what Sulaymān recognized** — *inna hādhā lahuwa-l-faḍlu-l-mubīn*. Between those two anchors, sandwiched in the middle, are the two passive verbs: *ʿullimnā* and *ūtīnā*.

The structure is: *received → was taught → was given → recognized as faḍl*.

Every verb in the central core is in the passive voice. The active voices belong only to the bookends — *waritha* (he inherited, which is itself a passive kind of receiving) and *qāla* (he said, the only thing Sulaymān actually does on his own).

Read the ayah's grammar as a portrait, and what do you see? **A man whose only verbs of his own are inheriting and acknowledging.** He inherits, he is taught, he is given, and he says — out loud, publicly — *this is the clear bounty.*

That is the entire profile of the prophetic life. Receive. Acknowledge. Repeat.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes have been telling the same story from two directions.

The grammar showed us a king refusing the active voice. The themes showed us why: because the active voice is a lie. Even our striving was first given the strength to strive. Even our hearing was first given the ear to hear. Even our worship was first given the heart with which to worship.

The grammar showed us *manṭiq* — articulated speech — applied to creatures we dismiss as voiceless. The themes showed us why: because what disqualifies a ruler is deafness to the small, and what qualifies one is the ear that hears them as fully as it hears the great.

The grammar showed us *waritha* — inheritance, not achievement. The themes showed us why: because anything you call *mine* you eventually destroy yourself with, but anything you call *given* you handle with the trembling care that gifts deserve.

One ayah. One unified vision: **the human being is a receiver standing in a singing world, and the only proper response is to listen, and to confess.**

### Questions to Carry

Stay with these. Do not answer them quickly.

1. Where in your life are you using the active voice — *I built, I earned, I deserve* — when the truer description would be *I was given*?

2. Who is the smallest voice in your life right now? Whose words do you consistently overhear without actually *hearing*? What might happen if, this week, you treated their voice the way Sulaymān treated the ant's?

3. The Quran says creation is constantly glorifying its Lord and *we do not understand its glorification*. When was the last time you walked outside and listened — really listened — as if there were something being said you were missing?

4. Sulaymān announced his gifts publicly: *yā ayyuha-n-nāsu* — *O people*. Not as a boast, but as a confession of *fadl*. When you receive a blessing, do you announce it as *I made this happen*, or do you announce it the way Sulaymān did — as a gift you are merely passing along the news of?

5. If your power deafens you to the small voices in your life, what *is* your power actually doing for you? What is it costing you that you cannot yet see?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The prophet who heard the ant was the one given the wind, because Allah does not entrust the largeness of dominion to those who are deaf to the smallness of His creation.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will not, in this life, be given *manṭiq al-ṭayr*. The literal speech of the birds is not on offer for you or me.

But the *posture* of Sulaymān is.

The grammar of *ʿullimnā* — the willingness to use the passive voice when describing yourself, even when the world is begging you to use the active. The ordering of *manṭiq al-ṭayr* before *kulli shayʾin* — the discipline of training your ear to the small before you reach for the large. The closing acknowledgment of *al-faḍlu-l-mubīn* — *this is the clear bounty* — the public confession that whatever good has come through you came from somewhere else first.

This is not a king's privilege. This is a servant's option. Available tonight, in your home, in the silence after this reflection ends.

The world is loud with the praise of its Lord. You were not made to be deaf.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, kamā ʿallamta Sulaymāna manṭiqa-ṭ-ṭayri, ʿallim-nā an nasmaʿa mā quddira lanā an nasmaʿahu — min ṣaghīri khalqika qabla kabīrihi, wa min al-ṣamti qabla al-ṣawti, wa min al-aṣwāti-llatī tusabbiḥuka fī kulli ḥīnin wa naḥnu lā nafqahuhā.*

*O Allah, as You taught Sulaymān the speech of the birds, teach us to hear what we have been given the capacity to hear — the smallest of Your creatures before the greatest, the silence before the sound, and the voices that praise You in every moment while we walk past, not understanding.*

*And place in our mouths, when we speak of the gifts You have placed in our hands, the grammar of Your prophet: that we were taught, that we were given, that this — all of this — is from You, and is the clear bounty.*

*Āmīn.*
