# The One You Cannot Lead: When Guidance Stops at the Door of the Heart

## Introduction

**The ayah, in Arabic:**

> وَمَآ أَنتَ بِهَـٰدِى ٱلْعُمْىِ عَن ضَلَـٰلَتِهِمْ ۖ إِن تُسْمِعُ إِلَّا مَن يُؤْمِنُ بِـَٔايَـٰتِنَا فَهُم مُّسْلِمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa mā anta bi-hādī al-ʿumyi ʿan ḍalālatihim. In tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā fa-hum muslimūn.*

**A translation that breathes:**
> "And you — you are not the one who guides the blind out of their misguidance. You can only make hear those who already believe in Our signs — and it is they who become the ones who surrender."

---

### Rebuild the world

Imagine, for a moment, what it is to spend twenty-three years of your life inviting people to the most beautiful thing you have ever encountered.

You have walked into hostile rooms. You have buried children. You have been pelted with stones until your sandals filled with blood. You have lost an uncle who shielded you, a wife who anchored you. You have watched your own cousins choose disbelief in front of your face. You have stood at the Ka'bah reciting verses of staggering beauty — and watched the people you grew up with cover their ears, mock you, call you a magician, a poet, a madman.

And the question — *the question that haunts the gentle heart* — must have begun to whisper at the edges of his ﷺ being: **Could I have done more? Was there a word I did not say? A door I did not knock on? A way of speaking I did not try?**

The Prophet ﷺ was, by Allah's own description, *raʾūf raḥīm* — exquisitely tender, almost unbearably compassionate. The Quran itself records moments where his sorrow over the disbelief of his people threatened to consume him. Allah had to say to him, more than once, *do not grieve yourself to death over them.* (18:6, 26:3)

This is the climate. This is the air the ayah breathes into.

The surah we are inside — **An-Naml, "The Ants"** — has been telling stories. Musa at the fire. Sulayman with his armies and his birds. Salih with his she-camel. Lut with his city. In every story there is one pattern: **a sign arrives, and people split into two.** Some see. Some refuse to see. The prophet does his work, and then — *and this is the part we always miss* — the prophet steps back. Because there is a line he cannot cross.

This ayah is where that line is named.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

There is a word in this ayah that Allah uses for those who reject the truth. It is not "the disbelievers." It is not "the stubborn." It is not "the proud."

It is *al-ʿumy* — **the blind.**

And the Prophet ﷺ — the man who could split the moon, the man whose night journey crossed seven heavens, the man whom Allah Himself praised as being *on a magnificent character* — this Prophet is told something extraordinary about *al-ʿumy*:

**You cannot lead them.**

Not "you will not." Not "you might fail." The construction is absolute. *Wa mā anta bi-hādī al-ʿumyi.* And you — you, Muhammad — are not, in any sense, the one who guides the blind out of their misguidance.

[PAUSE]

What is Allah doing here? Why is He saying this *to His most beloved messenger*? And what does it tell us — *us*, the ones still walking around trying to convince a brother, a sister, a parent, a friend, a stranger on the internet — what does it tell us about the limit of what we are allowed to carry?

Stay with me. Because the answer is not what we expect.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to slow down and look at three things. The structure of the negation. The word "blind." And a single tiny particle of restriction that flips the whole verse.

---

### Element One: *Wa mā anta bi-hādī* — the architecture of absolute negation

Before we touch this ayah, I want to teach you something about how Arabic refuses.

In English, when we want to say someone *cannot* do something, we usually say it in one move. "You cannot guide them." Done. The negation is a single word.

In Arabic, when Allah wants to make a negation **as strong as language can make it**, He does something different. He uses a construction that classical grammarians call *nafy bi-ism* — negation by means of a noun rather than a verb. The construction looks like this: *mā anta bi-...* — "you are not, in any way, characterized as..."

Notice three things working together:

First, *mā* — the negative particle. Already a refusal.

Second, *anta* — "you." A pronoun. Allah does not say "the one who guides is not you." He says "**you** — you are not." The pronoun sits there like a weight. It singles you out. It says: *this thing we are about to deny — it is being denied of you, specifically, in your very person.*

Third, *bi-hādī* — "with/by guiding." That little prefix *bi-* is doing enormous work. In Arabic, when you negate something and then attach *bi-* to the predicate, you intensify the negation. It is the difference between saying "he is not a teacher" and saying "he is not in any way to be characterized as a teacher." The *bi-* makes the denial cling to the entire identity of the person.

So when Allah says *wa mā anta bi-hādī*, He is not saying "you happen not to guide them." He is saying, in the most emphatic register Arabic allows:

**It is not, in any sense, in your nature, your power, or your role to be the one who leads them out.**

[PAUSE]

Now sit with who this is being said to.

This is being said to the man who, by every measure of human history, *did* guide more hearts than perhaps anyone who has ever lived. Billions of people, across fourteen centuries, trace the orientation of their souls back to his ﷺ tongue. If anyone in creation could claim to be a guide, it would be him.

And Allah looks at him and says: **not even you.**

Why?

Because there are two kinds of guidance, and only one of them lives in any human hand.

The classical scholars name them. There is *hidāyat al-irshād* — the guidance of showing the way. Pointing. Teaching. Reciting. Explaining. Knocking on the door. This is what prophets do. This is what teachers do. This is what *you* can do when your cousin asks you a question about Islam at the dinner table.

And then there is *hidāyat at-tawfīq* — the guidance of *the heart actually receiving it.* The opening of the chest. The melting of the resistance. The moment the meaning lands and the soul says *yes*. This second kind — Allah has reserved entirely for Himself. It is not delegated. It is not loaned out. Not to angels. Not to messengers. Not to anyone.

The ayah is drawing a surgical line between these two. The Prophet ﷺ has done — and is still doing — the first. Perfectly. Completely. Without flaw.

But the second?

*Wa mā anta bi-hādī.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. This isn't a rebuke. It looks like one if you read quickly. But read it slowly and it is the opposite. **It is a release.**

Imagine carrying a weight for twenty years. The weight is the suffering of every soul you tried to save and could not. Every cousin who turned away. Every friend who laughed. Every elder who shook his head. Imagine you have been telling yourself, quietly, *if I had only been more eloquent. If I had only been more patient. If I had only loved them better.*

And then the One who created them, who created you, who knows every fiber of every soul in the cosmos — that One leans down and says:

*It was never yours to carry. The hearts were never in your hand. Stop bleeding for what was never your responsibility to fix.*

This is what the construction is doing. The grammar of absolute denial — *mā anta bi-hādī* — is, in its emotional register, the grammar of *mercy toward the one who has been trying too hard.*

### Element Two: *Al-ʿumy* — the blind. And the silence around the deaf.

The second word I want you to notice is the word Allah chooses for those who refuse.

He does not say *al-kāfirīn* — the disbelievers. He does not say *al-mushrikīn* — those who associate partners. He does not say *al-jāhilīn* — the ignorant.

He says *al-ʿumy* — **the blind.**

Now, before we open this, let me tell you something about how Arabic builds words. The root *ʿ-m-y* gives you the verb *ʿamiya* — to be blind. But the noun *al-ʿumy* is a particular form. It is what grammarians call a plural of an adjective behaving as a substantive — *the blind ones, as a category, as a settled condition.*

Why does this matter?

Because Arabic could have said *alladhīna ʿamū* — "those who became blind." That would have been a verbal form, suggesting an event, something that happened to them.

Instead, the form chosen — *al-ʿumy* — describes blindness as **a stable, defining state.** It is no longer something that happened to them. It is what they now *are*.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question. Who is actually blind in this ayah?

It cannot be physical blindness. There are blind Companions of the Prophet ﷺ — beloved, central, like ʿAbd Allāh ibn Umm Maktūm whose physical blindness was never an obstacle to his faith. Surah ʿAbasa itself was revealed about him.

The blindness here is not in the eyes. The Quran tells us this explicitly elsewhere: *fa-innahā lā taʿmā al-abṣāru wa-lākin taʿmā al-qulūbu allatī fī al-ṣudūr* — "for it is not the eyes that are blind, but the hearts inside the chests." (22:46)

So *al-ʿumy* here means: those whose inner organ of perception — the *qalb*, the heart — has lost the capacity to register what is in front of it.

But wait. Allah did not choose to say "hearts." He chose to say "blind." Why?

Because **blindness is the perfect physical image for the unseen reality He wants you to feel.**

This is the principle of *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — pointing to the unseen by way of the sensible. Allah is not interested in the physiology of blindness. He is using blindness as a *stepping stone* to a spiritual condition you must feel in your gut.

Think about what it means to be physically blind in a world full of light.

The sun is shining. The colors are everywhere. The trees are flooded with green. The faces of your loved ones are right in front of you. **And none of it reaches you.** Not because the light is failing. Not because the colors are absent. But because the *receiving organ* is no longer functional.

This is what Allah is saying about the heart that refuses. The signs of Allah are everywhere. The ayahs are being recited. The mercy is flooding the world. The Prophet ﷺ is right there, in front of them, speaking. **None of it gets in.** Not because the signs are deficient. Not because the speaker is unclear. Because the receiver has gone dark.

[PAUSE]

Now I want you to notice something that the ayah is doing by *omission*, which is just as important as what it is saying.

In the surrounding ayahs — verse 80, just before this one — Allah talks about two other categories: the **dead** (*al-mawtā*) and the **deaf** (*al-ṣumm*). "Indeed, you cannot make the dead hear, nor make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs."

Then verse 81 comes and shifts. Now it is *al-ʿumy* — the blind.

So we have a triad: dead, deaf, blind. Three images. Three impossibilities.

But look at what each one denies.

The dead cannot **hear.** The deaf cannot **hear.** And the blind — the blind cannot be **guided** *(hādī)*.

Hearing is the channel of revelation. The Quran enters through the ear. So denying hearing to the dead and deaf says: *the message cannot even land.*

But guidance — guidance is something more. Guidance is the *journey out.* To "guide someone out of misguidance" — *yahdī … ʿan ḍalālatihim* — is to lead them on a road. To take them by the hand and walk them from the place where they are to the place where they should be.

So the architecture is this: the dead and the deaf cannot even receive the call. The blind — *the blind can receive the call, but cannot find the road.* They have ears. They have intellect. They have heard the verses. They are not insensible. They are *lost in plain sight.* The map is being read out to them, and they cannot see the landmarks. The road is being described, and they cannot find their feet.

That is a different kind of darkness. And it is more devastating. Because the deaf can be pitied as a victim of circumstance. The blind, here — the spiritually blind — are the ones who *had everything they needed to see* and still did not.

[PAUSE]

And here is what stuns me about this verse. Allah does not say "you cannot guide the blind." He says "you cannot guide the blind **out of their misguidance**" — *ʿan ḍalālatihim.*

That tiny preposition *ʿan* means "away from." It implies motion. It implies that *ḍalāl* — being lost — is the place they are currently inhabiting, and the question is whether they can be moved out of it.

And notice the possessive: *ḍalālat-i-him* — "**their** misguidance." Not the misguidance. *Theirs.* It belongs to them. They own it. They have made it home.

This is the deepest cut in the verse. The Prophet ﷺ is not being told he cannot reach strangers in a faraway land. He is being told he cannot drag people out of a darkness *they have chosen to live inside.*

You cannot relocate someone who refuses to move.

### Element Three: *In tusmiʿu illā…* — the particle that flips the universe

The third thing I want you to see is the smallest. It is two letters of Arabic.

The second half of the ayah opens with: *in tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā.*

"You can only make hear those who believe in Our signs."

The construction here is called *al-ḥaṣr* — restriction. Limitation. Exclusivity. And it is built from two tiny pieces: the negative particle *in* (which here functions as "not") plus the exception particle *illā* ("except").

*In tusmiʿu illā…* literally reads: "**You do not make hear — except…**"

[PAUSE]

Now I want to teach you something about *illā* in Arabic, because the English word "only" cannot carry it.

When Arabic wants to say "only X," it usually does *not* use a single word meaning "only." It uses this two-step move: first it denies the whole field, then it pulls one exception out of the denial. *I do not love — except him.* The negation lands first. The field goes completely dark. *Then* the single exception lights up.

The emotional effect is staggering when you feel it in the original. The first half of the construction empties the room. *In tusmiʿu* — *you do not make anyone hear.* Period. Silence. Nothing.

And only then, *illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā* — *except the one who believes in Our signs.*

Allah is showing you the entire crowd. And He is telling His Prophet ﷺ: of all of them — all the faces, all the listeners, all the seekers and rejecters and the in-between — you are not actually reaching any of them. Not a single one. **Except one type.** And it is the one whose belief is already taking shape.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with me, because the next part is the part most people miss.

Look at the order of the words. *Man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā fa-hum muslimūn.* "The one who believes in Our signs — *fa-hum muslimūn* — and so they are the ones who submit."

The little *fa-* is a particle of consequence. It means "and so," "and therefore." It links cause to result.

What is the cause? *Belief in Allah's signs.*
What is the result? *Becoming a muslim — one who has surrendered.*

Why does this order matter?

Because in the daʿwah model we usually carry around in our heads, the order is reversed. We think: *first* the person submits, joins the religion, takes the shahādah, *and then* belief grows inside them.

The ayah inverts this. It says: there is a movement of belief — a *yuʾminu*, present tense, ongoing — *that is already happening inside the person before they appear to be submitting.* And it is that inner movement that makes them capable of hearing in the first place. The hearing follows the believing. The believing precedes the surrendering. The surrendering is the visible result of an invisible event.

Which means — and this is hard to absorb — **the Prophet ﷺ is being told that the ones who appear to be responding to his daʿwah are actually responding to something Allah has already begun inside them.**

He is not the cause. He is the *channel.* The transformation is between the soul and its Lord. He just happens to be standing there reciting the words at the moment the soul opens.

[PAUSE]

This should bring you to your knees if you let it.

Every person you have ever seen "respond to Islam" — every revert story, every moment of someone weeping at the recitation, every brother or sister who finally prayed — *that was not made by the one speaking.* It was made by Allah, in them, before the speaker even arrived. The speaker was simply present at the moment of opening.

And every person who has *not* responded — every loved one you have prayed for, every parent, every friend, every estranged spouse — **their non-response is also not your failure.** You did not cause their blindness. Your words did not fail. Your love did not fall short. Their heart was not yours to open.

The two halves of this ayah, together, are the most precise theology of human responsibility ever uttered in seventeen Arabic words.

The first half: *the failure to guide is not on you.*
The second half: *the success of guidance is not on you either.*

Both are returned, with full ownership, to the One they always belonged to.

[PAUSE — major pause]

So look at what these three pieces — the absolute negation, the image of blindness, and the restriction construction — have shown us. They have shown us that there is a wall between human effort and human transformation. The wall is real. The wall is final. And the wall is *merciful*, because what is on the other side of it was never something we could have carried.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About how we are living, right now, in 2026, with the people we love.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The exhaustion of trying to save someone

I want you to think about someone in your life.

Maybe it is a sibling who has drifted from prayer. Maybe it is a parent who never came to the deen. Maybe it is a child who is questioning everything. Maybe it is a spouse who has lost the spark. Maybe it is the version of yourself you used to be, or that you fear becoming.

You have tried. You have sent the article. You have shared the lecture. You have made the du'a. You have started the conversation seven times and watched it die seven times. You have laid awake at 3am wondering what you could say differently. You have wept in sujood for them. You have begged Allah for them.

And nothing has moved.

Or — and this is sometimes worse — *something has moved, but in the wrong direction.* The more you push, the more they pull away. The more clearly you explain, the more they harden.

There is a name for the place this leaves you. Modern psychology calls it *caregiver burnout.* Or *compassion fatigue.* It is the specific kind of exhaustion that comes from carrying responsibility for another person's transformation — a responsibility you were never built to carry, because the lever for that transformation does not exist inside you.

Therapists who work with families of addicts know this terrain intimately. There is a phrase used in recovery communities: *you didn't cause it, you can't control it, you can't cure it.* It is meant to free family members from the impossible labor of trying to fix something that lives in another person's interior.

The Quran said this fourteen hundred years ago to its own Prophet ﷺ.

*Wa mā anta bi-hādī.* You did not cause their blindness. You cannot control whether they see. You cannot cure their refusal.

[PAUSE]

But here is where the ayah goes deeper than any therapist's framing. Because secular psychology stops at *let go.* It says: detach. Protect yourself. Set boundaries.

The Quran does not stop there. It says something more difficult and more freeing.

It says: **the work is still yours. The outcome is not.**

Read the ayah again. Allah does not tell the Prophet ﷺ to stop reciting. He does not tell him to give up on Quraysh and go home. The very next ayahs continue to address the disbelievers, continue to warn them, continue to call them. *The daʿwah does not end.*

What ends is the *internalization* of failure. What ends is the Prophet's ﷺ identification of his own worth with their response. The work is still his. The result is Allah's.

This is a different psychology than "detach and protect yourself." This is: **stay engaged, stay loving, stay calling — but locate the outcome where it actually lives, which is not in your hands.**

[PAUSE]

Imagine a farmer. He plants the seed. He waters. He tends. He weeds. He waits. He does everything he is supposed to do.

But he does not make the seed *grow.*

If the seed does not grow, it is not because the farmer failed to plant. It is because something inside the seed — its life — did not respond. The farmer can do nothing about the life inside the seed. He can only create the conditions and wait.

You are the farmer. The seed is the soul you love. Allah is the One who decides what grows.

If you stop watering, you have abandoned your work. If you take credit for the bloom, you have stolen what is not yours. If you blame yourself for the failure to bloom, you have claimed a power you never had.

The freedom in this ayah is not the freedom to stop. It is the freedom to *continue without being destroyed by the outcome.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the temperature of every conversation you have with someone you are trying to reach. The pressure comes off. The desperation lifts. You can love them without needing them to change. You can speak without needing them to agree. You can leave the room without the dread of *did I say enough?*

You did. Or you didn't. Either way, **the part of them that opens or doesn't open is not yours to operate.** Allah will reach them in His way, in His time, through His instruments — of which you may or may not be one. You are not the only door. You are not the last door. You are not the door at all. Allah is the door, and He opens or closes it according to a wisdom that is not on display in your living room.

This is not resignation. This is *trust placed where trust belongs.*

### Theme Two: The blindness you carry, and the question of whether you can see your own

Now I want to flip the verse. Because everything we have said so far has placed *us* on the side of the Prophet ﷺ — the caller, the lover, the one bleeding for the souls of others.

But this ayah is also a mirror. And the mirror is uncomfortable.

Because *al-ʿumy* — the blind — is a category, not a person. It describes a *condition of the heart*, not an identity card. Anyone can be in it. Anyone can be slipping into it. **Including you.**

The truly devastating question this ayah asks is not "who are the blind ones I am trying to guide?" It is: **how do I know I am not currently among them?**

[PAUSE]

Spiritual blindness, the way the Quran describes it, has a specific anatomy. It is not the absence of information. The Quraysh had all the information. They had Muhammad ﷺ himself in front of them — they had grown up with him, they had called him *al-Amīn*, the Trustworthy. They had heard the Quran with their own ears. They could not have been better informed.

What they did not have was the *capacity to be moved by what they knew.*

That is the anatomy of *ʿamā al-qalb* — heart-blindness. It is the strange condition where the truth is right in front of you, fully visible, fully articulated, and somehow it does not register as truth. It bounces off. It does not enter.

[PAUSE]

So ask yourself, gently. Is there a verse of Quran you have read a thousand times that no longer pierces you? Is there a teacher whose words used to make you weep and now make you scroll? Is there an aspect of your worship — your prayer, your dhikr, your relationship with the Book — that you can perform without feeling? Is there a sin you used to fear that now sits comfortably beside you?

These are not signs you are damned. They are signs of *the beginning of dimness in the eye of the heart.* It is fixable. It is *immensely* fixable. But it requires you to recognize it before it hardens into the settled state Arabic calls *al-ʿumy* — not "those who happened to go blind" but "**the blind**" as a defining condition.

Here is what the ayah suggests about how this hardening happens. Look again at the phrasing: *al-ʿumy ʿan ḍalālatihim* — the blind, in their misguidance. Notice the possessive: *theirs.* They own the misguidance.

The shift from *I am lost* to *this is who I am* is the catastrophic shift. As long as you are *lost*, you can be found. The moment you decide your lostness is your identity — *I'm just not a religious person, I've never been a deep believer, this just isn't me* — you have begun the process of moving into the category the ayah names.

The blindness is not the original wrongness. The blindness is the *making-home* of wrongness.

[PAUSE]

But here is the mercy folded into this same ayah. Because the second half says: *in tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā* — you can only make hear those who believe in Our signs.

If you are reading this — if any part of you is still asking the question *am I one of the blind?* — then by the logic of the verse itself, **you are not.** Because the question itself is a form of believing. The willingness to be moved is the beginning of being moved. The fear of dimness is a sign that the light has not yet gone out.

The blind, in the sense Allah means here, *do not ask whether they are blind.* They are too settled in their darkness to wonder. The asking is the symptom of sight returning.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

Two things.

First, it changes how you read the Quran. You stop reading it as a book about *those people* — the disbelievers, the hypocrites, the Quraysh — and you start reading it as a book about *you*. Every category in this verse — the blind, the deaf, the dead, and the one who believes — is a category your heart passes through, sometimes within a single day. You move between them. The question is not which one you are. The question is which one you are *becoming.*

Second, it changes what you ask Allah for. Most of our du'a is about circumstances. *Give me, fix this, change that.* The most precious du'a after this verse becomes: **O Allah, do not let me become one of those whose hearts You will no longer make hear.** Do not let me be the one You stop reaching. *Iyyāka naʿbudu wa iyyāka nastaʿīn* — to You we worship, and to You we turn for help — and the deepest help we need is the help to *keep being someone You can still reach.*

The blindness this ayah warns about is not assigned. It is grown. And the antidote is not knowledge. It is the daily, soft, repeated *willingness to be moved by what you encounter.*

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture

Look back, now, at the whole ayah.

> *Wa mā anta bi-hādī al-ʿumyi ʿan ḍalālatihim.*
> *In tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā fa-hum muslimūn.*

It is built in two halves, and the halves mirror each other.

The first half is a **denial of power**: you cannot guide.
The second half is a **restriction of effect**: you can only make hear those who already believe.

The first half ends with *their* misguidance — *ḍalālatihim* — possessive, settled, owned.
The second half ends with *their* submission — *muslimūn* — also a state, also belonging to them.

In both halves, the *outcome* — whether lost or surrendered — belongs to the person and their Lord. *Not to the messenger.*

The two halves are the same insight from two directions. From the side of failure: *not yours.* From the side of success: *also not yours.* Both halves return ownership of the human heart to its only true Owner.

This is the architecture of mercy. The Prophet ﷺ is being freed from both sides of the ledger at once. Not just the grief of those who refused. Also the pride of those who accepted.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight, seen from two angles.

The linguistic discovery was: Allah uses absolute negation, the image of blindness, and the construction of restriction to draw the most precise possible line between *what a human can do* and *what only Allah can do.*

The thematic discovery was: when we cross that line — whether by collapsing under the weight of someone else's refusal, or by quietly slipping into our own dimness — we are claiming territory that was never ours.

The line is the same line. The verse names it once and means it everywhere.

### Questions to carry

1. Who is the person whose non-response has been breaking you? Are you willing to keep loving them while releasing the demand that they change?

2. What is the temperature of your own heart, right now, when you read the Quran? Is it warmer or colder than it was a year ago — and what does that tell you?

3. Where in your life are you taking credit for guidance — your children's faith, a student's awakening, a friend's return — that you did not actually author?

4. Where in your life are you taking blame for blindness that you did not cause and cannot cure?

5. If the verse is true that *only those who already believe can be made to hear* — then what is the small belief, the small willingness, the small leaning-toward-Allah that you can offer Him today, so that He can use it to make you hear what He has been saying?

### One sentence

You were never the one who could open the heart — but you are still the one who is being asked to keep speaking, to keep loving, and to keep your own heart open enough that the One who *does* open hearts can keep working through you.

### The closing invitation

The next time you find yourself exhausted by trying to save someone — pause. Bring this ayah to mind. Say it slowly: *wa mā anta bi-hādī*. Notice the weight come off. Notice the work remain. Notice the difference between the two.

And the next time you find yourself harder than you used to be, dimmer than you used to be, less moved than you used to be — *also* pause. And ask, with whatever honesty you can find: *Ya Rabb, am I beginning to become one of those You will no longer reach? Open my eyes again before they close.*

The mercy of the ayah goes both ways.

### Du'a

O Allah,

You who alone guide hearts, free us from the burden of trying to be what only You can be for the souls we love. Let us speak Your words without claiming Your work. Let us love the ones who turn away without breaking under the turning.

And, O Allah, keep us — keep us — among those whom You still make hear. Do not let our hearts harden into the blindness this verse warns of. Soften us, again and again, every day, until the day we meet You.
