# The Feet Before the Heart — Journey, Sight, and the Ruins That Wait

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 69

### [ayah:27:69] قُلْ سِيرُوا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَٱنظُرُوا كَيْفَ كَانَ عَٰقِبَةُ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ

*Qul sīrū fī l-arḍi fanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu l-mujrimīn.*

**"Say: 'Journey through the earth, and observe how was the fate of the criminals.'"**

---

## Introduction: The Merchant Who Passed the Ruins

Imagine a merchant from Mecca. He has made this journey before — the caravan route north to Syria, cutting through the Hijaz, past the ancient stone formations near al-Ḥijr. The trip takes weeks. He sleeps under stars. He has done it enough times that the landscape has become familiar, which means it has become invisible.

Near al-Ḥijr, there are buildings carved directly into cliff faces — entire rooms hewn from living rock, facades ornate enough to stop you if you've never seen them. He has seen them. More than once. The first time, years ago, he slowed his camel and looked. He catalogued them: *abandoned city, impressive craftsmanship, old — very old.* And then he moved on. Because there were goods to deliver and a schedule to keep.

Since then, he passes without slowing. The buildings are part of the landscape now, the way mountains are part of the landscape. He doesn't see them. Not really. His eyes move across them the way a hand moves across a wall in the dark — registering surface but not stopping to feel what's there.

This merchant is us. And the command that arrives in this ayah is addressed directly to him.

[PAUSE]

Before we open the ayah, we need to understand what moment it arrives into. The three verses immediately before this one — 27:66-68 — record the Quraysh in a particular mode of dismissal. They've been told about resurrection, about divine accountability, about the Hereafter. Their response: *"When we and our fathers have become dust, will we really be brought forth? This is nothing but legends of the former peoples."*

Legends. Old stories. Fables that sophisticated people have moved past.

This is not simple intellectual doubt. This is the comfort of people who have decided that the consequences they've been warned about are abstract — theoretical — safely distant from the world they actually inhabit. The world where caravans have schedules and goods have prices and the buildings at al-Ḥijr are just scenery.

Allah's response is not a philosophical argument. He does not say: "Let me present you with three proofs of resurrection." He does not appeal to logic. He looks at these people — who travel, who move through the world, who pass ancient ruins twice a year on their trade routes — and He says:

**Go. Travel through the earth. And this time, actually look.**

Something about this should stop us. What does Allah know about human beings — about the specific kind of knowledge that is needed to move the heart — that leads Him to choose *physical travel* as the answer to *this* kind of denial?

That is what this ayah is going to teach us.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### The Sequence That Changes Everything

<!-- morphology:27:69:1 root=qwl pos=V -->
<!-- morphology:27:69:2 root=syr pos=V -->
<!-- morphology:27:69:5 root=nzr pos=V -->

Let me begin with what seems like the simplest feature of this ayah — not a word, but a sequence. There are two commands, and they arrive in a specific order.

**First:** *سِيرُوا* — *sīrū* — "Journey."

**Then:** *فَٱنظُرُوا* — *fanẓurū* — "Observe."

In Arabic grammar, when two imperative verbs are connected by this particular *fa* — the *fa* of consequence — the second action doesn't merely follow the first in time. It follows *because* of the first. It becomes possible *through* the first. Not "travel, and also observe." Not two parallel commands. But: travel, and *then* — as a result of having traveled — truly see.

Before we touch meaning, let me teach you what is embedded in each verb.

The word *sīrū* comes from the root *s-y-r* — *sāra*, to travel, to journey, to move through territory with purpose. It is not the word for a stroll, or for a look around. It is the word for covering ground. The camel moving. The feet pressing the earth. And then the command shifts: *unẓurū* from the root *n-ẓ-r* — and this is a word that the classical scholars take great care to distinguish from *baṣara*, which is simple visual reception, light reaching the eye. *Naẓara* is something else. It is directed, intentional, contemplative gaze — the will applied to the eye. You can *baṣara* without wanting to. You cannot *naẓara* without choosing to.

Notice something in what Allah commanded. He did not say: sit and think about the previous peoples. He did not say: read about them. He did not say: *imagine* what happened to them. He commanded the body to *move* — *sīrū* — and *then* the eye to *choose to look* — *fanẓurū*.

The body must go first. The heart sees after.

[PAUSE]

Why?

Let me offer something to carry this. Think about the difference between reading about grief and sitting with someone who is grieving. You can read about grief beautifully, systematically, from every angle that literature and psychology offer. You can know the stages. You can understand the neuroscience. You can hold a complete map of what grief is. And then a friend loses someone, and you sit with them in the same room, and something happens to you that all that knowledge did not produce.

It is not that you received new information. The map was accurate. But the *receiving* of grief — the real thing, the thing that sits in the chest — requires the body to be present in a way the mind being present cannot replicate.

This is what *sīrū then fanẓurū* is encoding. There is a category of knowledge — let us not call it information, let us call it *wisdom* — that can only enter through embodied presence. You cannot think your way into it. You cannot be told into it. The body must go. Then the heart can receive what the body has gone to.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what this means for the Quraysh receiving this command. They didn't need to plan an expedition. The ruins of Thamūd — the people whose story the Quran narrates in this very surah just verses earlier — were at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ, the ancient *al-Ḥijr*, five days from Mecca on the Syria trade route. The Quraysh passed those ruins twice a year, going and returning.

Allah is not sending them somewhere new. He is saying: you have already been there. You have already moved your bodies through that ground. And you have not *seen* a thing.

*Sīrū* — move with intention this time. *Fanẓurū* — choose to look.

**The feet before the heart. The body before the insight.** This is not poetic language. It is epistemological instruction. There are truths that are only receivable when the flesh is present with their physical evidence.

[PAUSE]

### The Heel of History: What *ʿĀqibah* Actually Means

<!-- morphology:27:69:8 root=aqb pos=N -->

Now let's open the word that carries the heart of this ayah.

**عَٰقِبَة** — *ʿāqibah.*

Let me teach you the root before using it. The root is *ʿ-q-b* — and what the classical lexicographers give us from this root is an image that, once you see it, you will not read this word the same way again.

*ʿAqab* — with that root — is the heel of the foot. The back of the foot. The part that touches the ground *after* you step. It is what comes behind you. It is the last trace you leave in soft earth as you walk away.

From this root: *ʿaqiba* means to come after, to follow behind. *ʿAqib* is what comes next — the following-after. And *ʿāqibah* — the word in our ayah — is the ultimate outcome, the final consequence, the thing that follows after everything a person or civilization has done.

But now look at what this root image does to the concept of consequence.

A consequence is not something dropped from outside onto a person from above. A *ʿāqibah* is a **heel-print** — it is what your own steps left behind you. You walked somewhere, you walked in a particular direction with a particular weight and a particular pace, and the earth recorded where you walked. The *ʿāqibah* is not an event that occurred. It is the footprint of a direction that was already being taken.

Stay with me here. Because this changes everything about what we're being sent to look at.

[PAUSE]

The ruins at al-Ḥijr are not evidence of a punishment that arrived one day from outside Thamūd. They are the *ʿāqibah* — the heel-print — of Thamūd's choices. When you stand at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ and look up at those empty carved rooms, you are not looking at the aftermath of a disaster. You are *reading a footprint.* You are looking at where those steps led. The depth of the print tells you something about the weight of the choices. The direction tells you about the trajectory. A person who knows how to read footprints can read everything.

The ruins are the heel-print. Allah preserved it in rock. Go read it.

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars make something of the word *kāna* — the past tense — that is also worth dwelling in. *Kayfa kāna ʿāqibah* — how *was* the fate. Not "how might be." Not "how could be." The past tense here is final, sealed, settled. The *ʿāqibah* of those civilizations is complete. It has been delivered. It is pressed into the earth and it is not going anywhere.

History has given its verdict. The heel-print is in the stone.

The only remaining question is whether you will go read it — or whether you will pass by, twice a year on the Syria route, with goods to deliver.

[PAUSE]

And notice something else about the word *ʿāqibah* that the composite of classical scholarship builds for us. Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī, commenting on similar verses, emphasizes that the *ʿāqibah* is not simply the ending but the *earned* ending — the culmination of a trajectory that was set in motion by the people themselves. Ibn al-Qayyim, writing about divine consequence, returns again and again to the root *ʿ-q-b* to make exactly this point: the end is not imposed from outside; it is the *following-after* of what came before. Like a shadow that walks behind you. Like the heel that presses last.

This is *ʿāqibah*. The heel-print of a civilization's choices, preserved in the earth, waiting to be read by anyone who will stop and look.

### The Ones Who Severed: Why *Mujrimīn* Is the Right Word

<!-- morphology:27:69:9 root=jrm pos=N -->

Now we come to the word that should make us pause the longest: *الْمُجْرِمِينَ* — *al-mujrimīn*.

We translate it as "the criminals," and that is not wrong. But notice what Allah chose *not* to say here. He could have said *al-kāfirīn* — the disbelievers. He could have said *al-ẓālimīn* — the oppressors, the wrongdoers. He could have said *al-fāsiqīn* — the corrupt ones. All of those words would have accurately described the destroyed peoples. All of them appear elsewhere in the Quran as descriptions of those who faced divine consequence.

He chose *mujrimīn*.

The root is *j-r-m* — and in the classical lexicons, *jarrama* carries a meaning that extends beyond crime or wrongdoing. The root describes *cutting*, *severing* — something that has been separated from something else. A *jurm* is not just a bad act; it is, at its root, an act of severance. The *mujrimūn* are not merely wrongdoers. They are people who have *cut.*

Cut what?

[PAUSE]

Look at what Surah An-Naml has been telling us for sixty-eight verses. Moses received light and was called to carry it. Solomon was given wisdom and used it to reflect back to Allah. The Queen of Sheba received a letter and submitted. Thamūd was given the she-camel as a sign — a living, breathing sign, given to them directly, undeniably — and they chose to hamstring it. The people of Lot were given a clear messenger with a clear warning. The pattern across every narrative in this surah is the same: clear light arrives, and the *mujrimīn* make an active choice to sever from it.

This is the precision of the word. The ruins are not evidence of what happens to people who are generically wicked. They are evidence of what happens to a people who, in the face of clear guidance, chose to cut themselves off from it.

And here is where the word becomes a mirror rather than a window.

The *ẓālim* harms others. The *kāfir* rejects divine reality. But the *mujrim* — the one whose signature act is *jurm*, severance — has done something specific to *themselves*. They have cut the connection. They have made themselves unable to receive what was being offered. And then the *ʿāqibah* — the heel-print of that severance — followed behind them as heels do.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with what this means for the ruins.

If the ruins were simply the punishment for being wrong, we could look at them and think: *those people had wrong beliefs. I have the right ones. This doesn't apply to me.*

But if the ruins are the *ʿāqibah* of *jurm* — the heel-print of a civilizational severance — then the question the ruins are asking me is more personal, and more honest:

Where am I cutting?

Not breaking laws. Not rejecting Islam. But slowly, quietly, incrementally severing — from a truth that keeps arriving and I keep filing without acting on, from a warning that I've heard enough times that I no longer actually hear it, from a light that arrived at my door and I said *come back later* so many times that I stopped noticing it knocking.

The ruins are not evidence of what happened to *them*. They are evidence of a pattern embedded in the nature of things — a pattern that does not take breaks or make exceptions. They are Allah saying: *I showed you what severing produces. Now ask yourself what you are producing.*

### Bridge

Three elements. Three windows into this nine-word ayah.

*Sīrū then fanẓurū* — the body moves before the heart can see. Certain truths require embodied presence to enter. You cannot be told into them.

*ʿĀqibah* — the heel-print: consequences are the natural following-after of direction, not punishments imposed from outside. The end is already encoded in the steps.

*Mujrimīn* — the severed ones: the ruins are not evidence of crime in general, but of the specific pattern of what happens when a civilization actively cuts itself from light.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Epistemology of the Ruins — Why the Body Must Go First

There is a phenomenon that researchers in psychology and neuroscience have documented extensively, though they call it different things. The most common name is **attentional blindness** — the cognitive reality that we stop seeing what we see every day.

In the now-famous experiment: people watching a basketball game, asked to count passes, completely fail to notice a person in a gorilla suit who walks through the middle of the scene. Not because their eyes don't work. Not because the gorilla is hiding. But because their attention is directed elsewhere — and the brain, running its familiarity-shortcut, routes everything through the model it has already built. The gorilla doesn't register because it is not tagged as relevant to the current task.

This isn't a flaw. It is efficient. The human brain cannot consciously process everything it receives, so it builds models of familiar environments and flags only what is *new* or *relevant to current goals*. The merchant who passes the ruins at al-Ḥijr twice a year without seeing them is not stupid. His brain is working exactly as it is designed to. The ruins have been categorized: *known object, irrelevant to the delivery schedule.* They vanish from his awareness.

And so Allah's command — *sīrū* — is not primarily about distance. It is about the state of mind that purposeful, intentional travel produces.

[PAUSE]

When you travel to a place you have never been, or to a place you have been but with a new and deliberate intention, you are neurologically and psychologically *more awake*. The brain cannot run its familiarity shortcut. It has to actually process what it is receiving. The ruins that were invisible to the merchant-brain become visible to the traveler-brain, because the traveler-brain has no shortcut for this.

*Sīrū then fanẓurū.* Travel — with intention — so that the habituated eye can be reset. Then observe. Then, with the eye freshly awake, choose to really look.

But there is something deeper here than neurological efficiency. There is a question that this ayah raises about the very nature of the knowledge that is needed for the heart to be moved.

Why could Allah not simply *tell* them what happened to Thamūd? He has, in fact, just finished doing that — the story of the she-camel, of Ṣāliḥ, of the nine men who plotted evil, fills the earlier verses of this very surah. Why, after all that telling, does He command: go *see*?

[PAUSE]

Here is a way to feel the answer.

There is a significant difference between knowing that the Holocaust happened and standing in one of the gas chambers at Auschwitz-Birkenau. Every educated person knows the facts — the chronology, the architecture of the system, the numbers. All of it is in books, on screens, in curricula. And then a person stands in one of those rooms, looks up at the ceiling where the fingernail marks are still in the concrete, and something *happens* that all the knowing did not produce.

Not new information. Not a single fact has changed. But the person standing there is changed in a way the person who read the book was not. Something has entered the chest that could not have entered through the ears alone.

The body held something the mind could not carry.

[PAUSE]

This is what *sīrū then fanẓurū* is pointing toward. Allah is not sending the Quraysh to the ruins because the ruins contain new information. Allah is sending them because **there is a category of divine communication that arrives only through physical presence with the earth's testimony, and you are cutting yourself off from it every time you pass this ground without stopping.**

The ruins are not artifacts. They are *āyāt* — signs. In the Quranic framework, every physical phenomenon that Allah draws attention to is a stepping stone pointing toward an unseen reality. The stars, the water, the growing of grain from dead earth — not because those things are the subject. They are pointing toward something that cannot be described directly. The ruins of the *mujrimīn* are stepping stones pointing toward the reality of divine consequence — a consequence so consistent, so embedded in the nature of creation, that it leaves physical evidence in the ground.

You can read about divine justice. You can believe in it abstractly. You can affirm it in conversation, and mean it.

Or you can stand at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ, look up at those empty carved rooms, feel the wind move through them, and *know* it — with the part of you that knowledge enters when the body has gone there.

The *naẓar* — that directed, intentional, chosen seeing — becomes possible at that moment in a way it is not possible at home, in your room, with a book.

**The feet before the heart.** Not metaphor. Epistemological instruction.

[PAUSE]

And here is the weight this places on those of us living now.

We are an age of extraordinary informational access. We can know about virtually anything from a chair. We can read the scholarship, watch the documentary, take the course, read the tafsir. And we have, without quite realizing it, begun to confuse *knowing about* with *knowing*. We have substituted information for presence. We have replaced the journey with the report on the journey.

Allah is interrupting this assumption here. He is saying: for certain things — especially the things that most need to transform you — the body must go. You must *actually* be present. You must *actually* sit with the scale of what remains of a destroyed civilization, let the wind move through the spaces where people once ate and laughed and dismissed the prophets, and let what the earth is testifying press into you in a way that the description of it cannot.

There are truths that only arrive on foot.

What in your life has been approached only informationally — that needs to be approached bodily? What have you been reading about that you need to go be *present with*?

### Theme Two: Reading the Heel-Print — History as Living Testimony

The ruins exist right now. Today. Not in a narrative. Not in a report. Physically, in the ground, under the sky.

Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ — the ancient al-Ḥijr of the Quran — is in the Tabuk region of northwestern Saudi Arabia. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Visitors go. Scholars study. Tour groups photograph the carved facades. And in every visit, there is the possibility of exactly what Allah commanded — and the possibility of exactly what the Meccan merchants were doing.

You can stand at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ and see: magnificent architecture. Stunning engineering. Evidence of extraordinary wealth and capability. Faces of rock transformed into buildings by human hands that knew what they were doing. And you can take a photograph. And you can go to dinner.

Or you can stand there and *read the heel-print.*

[PAUSE]

The image that the root *ʿ-q-b* gives us — the heel, the following-behind — is worth dwelling in as an actual image, not just an etymology.

When a person walks through wet earth and the earth dries, the footprint remains. It tells you things. Not just *a person walked here.* The depth of the print reveals something about their weight. The stride length tells you about their pace — were they rushing, or walking with the heavy step of someone who has stopped caring? The direction reveals where they were headed. A tracker who knows how to read footprints can reconstruct a story from nothing but pressed earth.

The ruins of Thamūd are civilizational footprints. They press into the earth the *direction* that people were walking, the *weight* of their choices, the *stride* of a people moving toward their own *ʿāqibah* without knowing they were. Stand at al-Ḥijr and you are not looking at a collapse. You are reading a footprint. The question is whether you know how to read.

[PAUSE]

Here is where the word *kayfa* — *how* — does something precise.

Allah did not say: go see *what* happened to the *mujrimīn*. He said: go see *how* — *kayfa* — it was. The question is not informational (*what*) but qualitative (*how*). It is the question a tracker asks, not a journalist. Not: what is the fact of the matter? But: *what kind of thing is this?* What does the manner of this tell me about the pattern that produced it?

*Kayfa kāna ʿāqibah* — how *was* the consequence. In this word *kayfa* is the invitation to read quality, not just record fact. To ask: what kind of following-after is this? What kind of direction produces this kind of heel-print? What does the *manner* of these ruins tell me about the pattern at work here?

[PAUSE]

And the pattern, once you see it, is not comfortably distant.

The *sunnat Allāh* — the consistent, reliable, unbroken pattern of divine consequence — is what the ruins are evidence of. Not one event in one time period. A *pattern*. Embedded in the nature of creation. As reliable as gravity. The ruins at al-Ḥijr and the ruins near the Dead Sea and the traces of ʿĀd in Yemen are not three separate catastrophes. They are three data points in the same pattern. Three heel-prints of the same direction.

And we are a civilization currently pressing our own heel-prints into the earth.

Not as a doomsday statement. As a structural one. The choices being made now — what we sever from, what warnings we dismiss as "legends of former peoples," what messengers we file under "interesting perspective, not actionable" — these are pressing footprints. The *ʿāqibah* is not a future event that might or might not occur. It is the heel-print already forming behind each step.

[PAUSE]

This is where the word *mujrimīn* becomes a question rather than a verdict.

The ruins are not asking: *are you as wicked as those people were?* That is the easy question, and the answer is almost always no — we are better in some ways, different in others. The question the ruins are asking — the question that *kayfa* is pointing toward — is this:

**In what direction are you walking?**

Not: have you committed their specific sins? But: are you in the habit of severing? Is there a consistent direction in your choices — away from light, away from warning, away from the guidance that keeps arriving at the door? Because the *ʿāqibah* is not judging the severity of a single act. It is the heel-print of a *direction*.

A ship that is one degree off course arrives in a completely different port after a long journey. The correction needed at departure is nothing. The correction needed after years of sailing is enormous. The *naẓar* that Allah commands at the ruins is, at its heart, a direction-check. *Kayfa kāna ʿāqibah* — how was the ending — read backwards: what direction was that civilization walking, that this is where the heel-print ends?

Now: what is your direction?

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of This Command

Now that the linguistic and thematic work has been done, let me show you what this ayah is built on — because the architecture is not accidental.

It opens with *qul* — *say* — a command to the Prophet ﷺ. It closes with *al-mujrimīn* — those who severed. The Prophet is being asked to relay a command to people who are themselves in the process of severing.

Between those two poles, the ayah moves in a perfect sequence: **movement → seeing → quality-discernment → consequence → the severed ones.** Every word does its job. *Sīrū* (travel with the body). *Fī l-arḍ* (within the earth — the preposition *fī* meaning immersion, not passage over). *Fanẓurū* (choose to look with intention). *Kayfa* (attend to the quality, not just the fact). *Kāna* (it has already happened — the verdict is settled). *ʿĀqibah* (the heel-print, the following-after). *Al-mujrimīn* (those who severed — this is the pattern's subject).

Speech. Motion. Immersion. Chosen sight. Quality. Finality. Consequence. Severance.

Nothing in nine words is there by accident.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths arrived at the same place from two directions.

From the language: the sequence *sīrū then fanẓurū* encodes that physical movement precedes the kind of seeing that transforms. The root *ʿāqibah* reveals that consequences are heel-prints — the natural following-after of a direction — not external impositions. The word *mujrimīn* specifies that the pattern being pointed to is the pattern of severance, not of generic wickedness.

From the themes: certain truths about divine consequence are receivable only through bodily presence with their physical evidence — and we live in an age that has largely substituted information for presence. The ruins of the *mujrimīn* are footprints in the earth, readable to anyone who knows how to ask *kayfa* — not *what*, but *how* — and who uses what they read as a direction-check on their own walking.

The same insight, approached from above and below:

**The ruins are the physical testimony of divine consistency, preserved in the earth for anyone whose feet bring them close enough and whose will directs the eye to actually see — because there are truths the heart can only hold when the body has gone to meet them.**

### Questions to Carry

When was the last time you traveled somewhere — not touristically, but with the intention to *read* what the place is telling you about the pattern of things?

Where in your own life have you passed something Allah preserved as testimony — a death, a consequence you witnessed, a civilization's collapse in the news — and moved on, like the merchant who passes al-Ḥijr twice a year without slowing?

If you were to apply *kayfa* — not *what* but *how* — to a significant consequence in your own life or in the life of your community: what direction does the heel-print reveal you have been walking?

Where is the gradual cutting happening? Not the dramatic severance — but the daily, quiet increment of severing from light that keeps arriving at the door?

What would it mean, in your actual physical life this week, to travel in the earth — to be bodily present with something that you have only been informationally present with?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The ruins of those who severed themselves from truth are pressed like footprints into the earth — and Allah commands us to move our bodies toward them, because there are truths that can only enter the heart through the feet.**

### The Closing Invitation

The ruins at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ are still there. They will be there tomorrow. Allah preserved them — in stone, not in story — precisely because He knew there would always be people passing by who needed to stop.

But the *sīrū* of this ayah is not only about a plane ticket to Saudi Arabia. It is a posture. A way of moving through all the preserved evidence that Allah has arranged around every life. The news story about a community in collapse. The neighbor who chose a particular direction and is now living its *ʿāqibah*. The moment in your own past where you can read the heel-print clearly now and see what direction you were walking. The friend who keeps describing what severing eventually produces without realizing that is what they are describing.

The earth is full of testimony. All of it preserved. None of it arbitrary.

The invitation is not to become morbid, or frightened, or preoccupied with doom. It is to become *awake*. To travel through the world the way Allah asked — with the *naẓar* that is directed, chosen, contemplative — and to let what the earth is testifying about divine consistency shift something in you that arguments and information alone cannot reach.

Stop at the ruins. Read the footprint. Then look down at where your own feet are pressing into the ground.

Your feet know the way to truths your mind cannot carry.

Go.

### Du'ā

*Yā Allāh* — You preserved the evidence of Your *sunna* in the very ground we walk on, so that no generation could say it was not shown. Make us of those who travel with eyes that truly see — not the merchants who passed al-Ḥijr without slowing, but the ones who stopped, and looked, and let the heel-print press into their hearts. Protect us from the *jurm* — the quiet, incremental cutting — that precedes every ruin. And let our own *ʿāqibah* be the kind that future travelers could read as evidence of Your mercy guiding a people home.

*Āmīn.*
