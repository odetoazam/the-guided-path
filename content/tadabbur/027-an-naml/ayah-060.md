# The Question That Cannot Be Answered

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 60

> أَمَّنْ خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ وَأَنزَلَ لَكُم مِّنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَأَنبَتْنَا بِهِ حَدَائِقَ ذَاتَ بَهْجَةٍ مَّا كَانَ لَكُمْ أَن تُنبِتُوا شَجَرَهَا ۗ أَإِلَـٰهٌ مَّعَ اللَّهِ ۚ بَلْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ يَعْدِلُونَ

*Amman khalaqa as-samāwāti wal-arḍa wa anzala lakum mina as-samāʾi māʾan, fa-anbatnā bihi ḥadāʾiqa dhāta bahjah, mā kāna lakum an tunbitū shajarahā. A-ilāhun maʿa Allāh? Bal hum qawmun yaʿdilūn.*

> "Or, Who created the heavens and the earth and sent down for you water from the sky by which **We** caused to grow gardens of beauty and delight — it was not for you to cause their trees to grow. Is there a god with Allah? No — but they are a people who equate (others with Him)."

---

## Rebuilding the World

Before we step into this ayah, we have to understand what just happened in this surah.

Surah An-Naml is one of the great storytelling surahs of the Quran. It walked us through Musa standing barefoot at the burning bush. It walked us through Sulayman commanding the wind, speaking with the ant, exchanging letters with the Queen of Sheba. It showed us the people of Salih plotting at night and being seized by morning. It showed us the people of Lut and what happens when a society finally stops being able to recognize its own corruption.

And then — after all that — the storytelling stops.

The surah turns. It turns directly to *you*.

It is as if a voice says: you have just watched Me deal with kings and prophets and queens and ruined cities. You have just seen Me speak to ants. Now look up. Look down. Look at the soil under your feet and the sky over your head. Let Me ask you something.

And what follows is not one question. It is **five**. Five questions, each beginning with the same haunting word — *amman*, "or who?" Each one shaped like a sword that only cuts one way.

This is the first of those five.

The setting in Makkah matters too. The original audience were people who knew rain. Many were merchants who had crossed deserts and watched a single thunderstorm transform a dead valley into something alive. They knew what gardens cost. They knew what grew without water and what didn't.

So when Allah opens His mouth and says *amman* — "or who?" — the question is not abstract. It is being asked of people who have **stood in those gardens**. Who have eaten the dates. Who have drunk from the wells.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. There is something inside the grammar of this ayah that, if you see it, will rearrange how you understand every garden you have ever walked through. There is a pronoun shift here so deliberate that the early grammarians stopped to mark it. There is a phrase about "trees" that contains a word so precise it tells you exactly where human capability ends and divine capability begins. And there is a question — *a-ilāhun maʿa Allāh* — that the surah will repeat **five times in a row**, each time daring an answer that cannot come.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Amman* — The Question That Already Knows

In Arabic, there are several ways to ask "who?"

You can simply say *man* — "who?" That is a direct, neutral question. *Man qāla hādhā?* — "Who said this?"

But this ayah does not begin with *man*. It begins with *amman* — أَمَّنْ.

Let me teach you what this construction is doing, because it will unlock everything that follows.

*Amman* is what classical Arabic grammarians call a compound — *am* (meaning "or") joined with *man* (meaning "who"). And that small word *am* — "or" — changes the texture of the question entirely.

Think about how "or" works in English. If I walk up to you and ask, "Who paid for dinner?" — that is one kind of question. I genuinely don't know.

But if I say, "**Or** — who paid for dinner?" — suddenly there is something behind the question. There is a previous claim hanging in the air. Maybe someone said *they* paid. Maybe a name was thrown out. The "or" makes the question into a challenge: *out of all the candidates we've been discussing, which one was it really?*

Notice something. *Am* in Arabic functions almost exactly this way. It is what the grammarians call *am al-munqaṭiʿah* — the "disjunctive *am*" — and its job is to introduce an alternative to a previously implied claim. It carries the meaning *bal* — "rather" — combined with *hamzat al-istifhām*, the questioning *a-*. It says: "**Setting aside whatever you were just claiming — who actually did this?**"

So when Allah opens with *amman khalaqa* — "**or** who created" — the surah is doing something subtle and surgical.

It is taking the entire backdrop of Makkan polytheism — all the names, all the idols, all the intermediaries, all the casual assumptions about which forces in the universe deserve attention — and it is sweeping them off the table. *Set them aside.* Now answer the real question.

[PAUSE]

There is a deeper layer here.

Notice that the question is rhetorical. The grammarians — Ibn ʿĀshūr and others — recognized that this is not Allah seeking information. Allah does not ask in order to learn. So why ask at all?

Because **the rhetorical question forces the listener to do the answering inside their own chest.**

When someone tells you the truth, you can disagree. You can argue. You can shake your head and walk away.

But when someone asks you a question whose answer you already know — and you have to admit it to yourself in the silence between asking and answering — something else happens. The truth is no longer being imposed on you. **You are conceding it.**

This is the genius of *amman*. It is not preaching at you. It is making you the witness against yourself.

And here is what is remarkable. The early Makkans, when they heard this, could not say "the idols did." They knew the idols did not. So the question hangs. And the longer it hangs, the louder the silence becomes.

This single word — *amman* — sets up the entire surgical structure of the next five ayahs. Five questions, each one cutting away another excuse, until there is nothing left to hide behind.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Pronoun That Shifts — *Anzala* and *Anbatnā*

Now look carefully at the verbs.

> *Amman khalaqa as-samāwāti wal-arḍa wa **anzala** lakum mina as-samāʾi māʾan, **fa-anbatnā** bihi ḥadāʾiqa dhāta bahjah*
>
> "Or who **created** the heavens and the earth and **sent down** for you water from the sky, by which **We caused to grow** gardens of beauty..."

Read them slowly. *Khalaqa* — He created. *Anzala* — He sent down. Then suddenly: *anbatnā* — **We** caused to grow.

The grammarians call this device *iltifāt* — "the turn." It is a deliberate shift in person, gender, or number, mid-sentence, that classical Arabic considers one of the highest rhetorical tools in the language.

Let me show you what *iltifāt* feels like in everyday speech first.

Imagine a teacher describing a student to a parent: "He came to class on time. He sat in the front. He turned in his homework. **And I tell you** — he is going to do well." Notice what happened. The teacher was speaking *about* the student in the third person — and then suddenly switches into the first person, "I tell you." Why? Because at that moment the teacher wants the parent to feel **the teacher's own presence** behind the claim. The shift announces: *what comes next is not just description — it is testimony*.

That is *iltifāt*. The shift is the message.

Now look back at our ayah.

Allah begins in the third person — *amman khalaqa* — "or who created." This is the question's frame. He is, as it were, standing back, letting His names be searched for.

He continues in the third person — *anzala* — "He sent down." Still describing.

And then, the moment the gardens appear — *fa-anbatnā* — **"We caused to grow."**

Stay with this.

Why does the shift happen *here*, on the verb of growing? Why not on creation? Why not on the rain?

Look at what the sequence is doing. Creating the heavens and the earth — that is so immense that no human even pretends to compete. Sending down rain — that too is unmistakably beyond human reach.

But **growing**? Growing is the place where humans get confused. Growing is where the farmer, the gardener, the agronomist starts to feel like they did it. They planted the seed. They watered. They weeded. They pruned.

So precisely at the verb where a human might smuggle in a claim of agency, Allah shifts the pronoun. *Anbatnā.* **We.**

Notice something. The shift to "We" in Arabic is what scholars call the *nūn al-ʿaẓamah* — the "We" of majesty. It is the same form a king uses when issuing a decree. It is not plurality of beings. It is intensity of presence. It is the divine voice stepping forward and saying: **whatever else is happening here, I am the one growing this.**

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The shift answers the human delusion before it even forms. Before you can say "but my hands planted the tree" — the verb has already announced **"We caused to grow."** Your hands placed a seed in dirt. That seed contained a coded blueprint you did not write. The dirt held nutrients you did not synthesize. The water came from a sky you did not stock. The sun fed leaves you did not engineer. The cell divided, the chlorophyll captured photons, the xylem pulled water upward against gravity by capillary action you did not design — and from this orchestration, *fruit*.

You did not grow that tree. You participated in something already in motion.

The pronoun shift is the proof inside the grammar. *Khalaqa, anzala* — He, He. *Anbatnā* — **We.** As if Allah leans in at the very moment we are most likely to claim credit, and says: "*This one. This is Mine.*"

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Ḥadāʾiq Dhāta Bahjah* and the Sentence That Closes the Door

Now we come to the gardens themselves.

*Fa-anbatnā bihi **ḥadāʾiqa dhāta bahjah***. "We caused to grow by it gardens of beauty and delight."

Two words deserve our attention. *Ḥadāʾiq* and *bahjah*.

*Ḥadāʾiq* is the plural of *ḥadīqah*. The root is *ḥ-d-q* — and the lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib) note something distinctive about this word. A *ḥadīqah* is not just any garden. It is specifically **a garden enclosed**. The same root gives us the verb *aḥdaqa* — "to surround, to encircle." The *ḥadaqah* of the eye is the dark pupil **encircled** by the iris.

So a *ḥadīqah* is a garden that has *boundaries*. It is cultivated. It is contained. It is the kind of garden a human walks into and walks out of — the kind a human plants and irrigates and tends.

This is striking. Allah did not say "trees in the wild." He did not say "vegetation across the earth." He chose precisely the kind of garden where humans most claim ownership. The walled garden. The orchard. The kind of place where someone would post a guard and say *this is mine*.

And about *that* garden — the human-claimed, human-tended, human-walled one — Allah says: *fa-anbatnā*. **We grew it.**

Now the second word. *Dhāta bahjah.*

*Bahjah* comes from the root *b-h-j*. Al-Rāghib defines it as the kind of beauty that produces joy at the sight of it — beauty that delights, that makes you smile when you see it. It is not utilitarian beauty. It is not the beauty of "this tree is useful." It is the beauty of *aesthetics for the soul*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment.

The trees of the garden were not just made to bear fruit. They were made to bring **joy at the sight of them.** Allah did not have to make the world beautiful. Calories could have come from grey, joyless plants. A nutrient-bearing world does not require *bahjah*.

But the gardens were given beauty. And Allah is naming this — calling it out, holding it up. As if to say: *look at what I did not have to do. Look at the layer of mercy I added on top of necessity.*

The beauty of the world is not an accident. It is a signature.

Now — and this is where the door closes — read the next phrase.

> *mā kāna lakum an tunbitū **shajarahā***
>
> "It was not for you to cause **its trees** to grow."

The grammarians stopped at this phrase. *Mā kāna lakum* — this is one of the strongest possible constructions in Arabic for declaring impossibility, not just absence. It does not say "you did not." It says "**it was never within your power to**." It states that this was never an option that was on the table. Never available. Never yours.

And notice what is being denied to you. Not the watering. Not the planting. Not the harvesting. Allah lets you keep all of those. What Allah denies you, with grammatical absoluteness, is the *causing-to-grow* — *an tunbitū shajarahā*.

You can do everything *around* growing. You cannot do *growing*.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing here. The word *shajarahā* — "**its** trees." Notice the suffix. The trees belong to **the garden**, not to you. Even when the ayah grants you proximity to the garden, even when it acknowledges your presence in it, it does not grant you ownership of its trees. The trees belong to the place that Allah made — and the place that Allah made belongs to Allah.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So now we have three things.

We have *amman* — the question that sweeps the table clean and demands you face the silence yourself.

We have the pronoun shift — *khalaqa, anzala, anbatnā* — the surgical "We" inserted at exactly the place where a human would claim credit.

And we have *ḥadāʾiq dhāta bahjah* — gardens that are walled, beautiful, and whose trees were never within your power to grow.

The grammar has done its work. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Author Behind Every Garden You Take Credit For

There is a quiet, slow form of forgetting that happens to every adult.

When you are a child, you watch a flower bloom and it feels like a miracle. Nobody told you yet that "flowers do that." So when you see one open, you stop. You stare. You point.

Then, slowly, you grow up. And someone tells you about photosynthesis. Someone tells you about pollination. Someone tells you that flowers "just work that way." And the wonder dies, because explanation has been confused with cause.

Here is what we miss. Knowing **how** something works is not the same as knowing **why** it works at all. The mechanism is a description of regularity — it does not generate the regularity. The fact that water rises through a tree by capillary action is not the *reason* water rises. It is the *name* we have given to the rising. The rising itself remains a gift.

This is what the modern mind loses, and what this ayah hands back to you.

The farmer in 7th century Arabia and the agronomist with a PhD in 2026 are in **exactly the same metaphysical position**. Both place a seed in dirt. Both wait. Both depend on something they cannot manufacture. The agronomist understands the chain of causes more deeply — but the chain still runs back, every link, to a Cause neither of them controls. The PhD has not closed the gap. **The PhD has only mapped it more thoroughly.**

This is what *anbatnā* — "**We** caused to grow" — restores. It pulls you out of the illusion that explanation equals authorship.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where it lands on your life.

Think about the gardens you have planted. Not literal ones — the gardens of your life. The career you built. The family you raised. The body you trained. The skills you cultivated. The friendships you tended. The wealth you accumulated.

For each of these, you remember the watering. You remember the late nights, the discipline, the choices, the sacrifices. And because you remember the watering, you assume you did the growing.

But ask yourself, slowly, the question this ayah teaches you to ask. *Could I have caused these to grow if even one upstream gift had been withheld?*

Could you have built the career without the intelligence you did not earn? Without the parents who fed you for eighteen years? Without the country that did not collapse around you while you were studying? Without the health that was given to you cell by cell every morning? Without the will to keep going — which is itself a gift, because there are people who cannot find it?

Could you have raised the family without the spouse you did not invent? Without the fertility you did not engineer? Without the children whose souls you did not author?

Each of these gardens is *your* garden in the sense that you tended them. None of these gardens is *your* garden in the sense that you grew them.

This is the slow restoration of *amman khalaqa*. It is not asking you to deny your effort. It is asking you to **place your effort in its proper relationship to the One who made the effort possible.**

[PAUSE]

The practical movement is this. The next time pride rises in you about something you have built — a beautiful family, a strong body, a successful project, a life you are proud of — pause for a moment. Trace the gift back. Just one step. Ask: who made the soil this seed grew in?

You don't have to answer out loud. The pride will dissolve on its own.

That is the ayah doing its work.

### Theme Two: *Bal Hum Qawmun Yaʿdilūn* — The Subtle Equation We All Make

The ayah ends with a phrase that is easy to read past but should stop you cold.

> *A-ilāhun maʿa Allāh? Bal hum qawmun **yaʿdilūn**.*
>
> "Is there a god with Allah? No — but they are a people who **equate**."

The verb *yaʿdilūn* is from the root *ʿ-a-d-l*. The lexicons confirm two related meanings: "to be just" — and "to make equal, to balance two things, to put one thing in place of another." It is the same root as *ʿadl* (justice) — because justice is, at its core, the act of weighing two things on a balance until they are equivalent.

But here, the verb is used negatively. *Yaʿdilūn* — they balance, they equate. They put something on one side of the scale and Allah on the other, **as if those two things could be weighed against each other**.

Stay with this.

The Quran is not, in this verse, accusing the listener of denying Allah outright. The Makkan polytheists did not deny Allah's existence — they affirmed it loudly. What they did was **equate**. They placed alongside Allah other things, other names, other powers, and treated those things *as though they belonged on the same scale*.

This is the ayah's diagnosis. *Bal hum qawmun yaʿdilūn.* The problem is not absence of belief. The problem is the **balance scale of the heart**.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this stops being about pre-Islamic Makkah and starts being about you and me.

Most of us, if asked, would say we don't worship anything alongside Allah. Of course not. We are believers. But the verb *yaʿdilūn* is not asking whether you have a stone idol in your living room. It is asking what you are *placing on the scale next to Allah* in your daily life of attention, fear, hope, and reliance.

Watch your own heart for one day. Just one day. Notice where the weight goes.

When you imagine your future, what is the variable you are most afraid will fail? Is it Allah's promise — or is it your career? When you think about what would devastate you to lose, is it the nearness of Allah — or is it your reputation, your savings, your relationship, your status? When you are sick, do you turn first to the Healer — or to the doctor whose hands you have placed on the scale next to Him?

The doctor is a means. *Asbāb*. Use them. The Quran does not condemn means. But the moment a means starts to feel like an *equal* — the moment you would feel safer with the doctor present and Allah absent than with Allah present and the doctor absent — *yaʿdilūn* has happened. Quietly. Without you noticing. The scale has been tilted.

This is the modern *shirk* — not the worship of stones, but the equating of created things with the One who created them on the inner scale of the heart.

[PAUSE]

And now look back at the entire ayah.

Why did Allah set up *this question* — the question of who grew the gardens — right before the verdict of *yaʿdilūn*? Why this particular setup?

Because the gardens were exactly the place where pre-Islamic Arabs (and we) most easily mis-balance the scale. The gardens were where *asbāb* (the means) were most visible. The farmer's labor was visible. The rain was visible. The soil was visible. The seed was visible. And somewhere in all that visibility, the One whose hand grew the actual tree became *invisible*.

The grammar of the ayah is the antidote. *Anbatnā*. **We** grew it. Not the rain alone. Not the soil alone. Not the farmer alone. **We.** The means did not produce the result. The means were the tools through which the Real Grower acted.

If you can see this in the garden, you can see it everywhere else. In the career. In the family. In the body. In the meal in front of you. In the breath in your chest right now.

[PAUSE]

The practical movement is this. Once a day — preferably at a moment when something is going well, not when something is going badly — pause and silently say: *anbatnā* was not me. Trace one gift, just one, back through the chain of *asbāb* to the One who arranged them. Let the scale rebalance.

That is the ayah, fifteen centuries old, restoring tawhīd to the inside of your chest.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Look back at what the ayah has done.

It opened with *amman* — a word that swept the table clean and demanded you face the question yourself.

It built up through three actions — *khalaqa* (created), *anzala* (sent down), *anbatnā* (We grew) — with the pronoun deliberately shifting at the verb where humans most claim credit.

It described the gardens with two precise words — *ḥadāʾiq* (walled, cultivated) and *bahjah* (beauty for the soul) — denying you both the bounding and the joy as your own work.

It closed the door with *mā kāna lakum* — it was never within your power.

It posed the impossible question — *a-ilāhun maʿa Allāh?*

And it diagnosed the human disease in one verb — *yaʿdilūn*. They balance. They equate.

The whole ayah is a single act of theological surgery. It removes the false claim of human authorship from the gardens of the world, and it removes the false equation from the scale of the heart.

---

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar denies you authorship. The theme denies you the right to balance other things against Allah on the scale of your heart. They are the same denial. Because the moment you admit you did not grow the garden, the scale collapses. Whatever you were placing next to Allah falls off the balance. The means stop pretending to be ends.

This is why the ayah is structured this way. The question is not abstract theology — *who is the true God?* The question is about gardens, water, and trees, because **that is where the false equation actually lives in human experience.** Not in philosophy. In the orchard. In the farm. In the career. In the bank account. In the body that has not yet failed.

Allah meets us where we live and asks the question that, if we answer honestly, dismantles every idol the modern world has built.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life have I been silently taking credit for, when really I only did the watering?

2. If I traced one gift I currently enjoy back through the chain of *asbāb*, where would the chain end?

3. What is currently sitting on the scale of my heart **next to** Allah — not instead of Him, but alongside Him as if it deserved the same weight?

4. When I see beauty in the world — a sunset, a child's laugh, a garden, a face — do I receive it as a signature, or do I let it pass through me unread?

5. If Allah denied me the *causing-to-grow* of every garden in my life for one season, how much of what I call "mine" would remain?

### One-Sentence Distillation

*Every garden you walk through was grown by Hands that were not yours, by water you did not send down, with beauty you did not have to be given — and the silent equation that places anything else on the scale beside the One who did all this is the disease the question is diagnosing.*

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow morning, when you walk outside, do not look at the trees as scenery. Look at one of them — a single one — and ask yourself: *did I grow this?*

You did not. Nobody did. The tree is a quiet, daily, public act of *anbatnā* taking place at the side of every road you have ever driven.

The invitation is this: let the world become a sentence again. Every leaf, every breath, every meal, every healthy day, every safe return — these are not background. They are the speech of the One who is still saying *anbatnā*. We grew it.

If you can let the world speak again, the question — *a-ilāhun maʿa Allāh?* — answers itself in the silence inside your chest. And the scale rebalances.
