# The Barrier Between Two Seas: When Mercy Looks Like Separation

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
أَمَّن جَعَلَ ٱلْأَرْضَ قَرَارًۭا وَجَعَلَ خِلَـٰلَهَآ أَنْهَـٰرًۭا وَجَعَلَ لَهَا رَوَٰسِىَ وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَ ٱلْبَحْرَيْنِ حَاجِزًا ۗ أَءِلَـٰهٌۭ مَّعَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Amman ja'ala-l-arda qarāran wa ja'ala khilālahā anhāran wa ja'ala lahā rawāsiya wa ja'ala bayna-l-bahrayni hājizan. A-ilāhun ma'a-llāh? Bal aktharuhum lā ya'lamūn.*

**Translation:**
Or He who made the earth a stable place, and placed rivers running through it, and set firm mountains upon it, and placed a barrier between the two seas. Is there a god alongside Allah? Rather, most of them do not know.

---

## Introduction: The Question That Won't Let You Sit Still

Imagine you are standing in Makkah, somewhere around the year 615. You are listening to a man recite words that came to him in the cave on the mountain. The marketplace is loud. Caravan dust hangs in the air. Your gods — Hubal, al-Lat, al-Uzza — sit in their stone bodies inside the Ka'bah. You have inherited them. Your father bowed before them. You have never thought to ask whether they actually do anything.

And then this man recites a question.

Not a statement. Not a sermon. A **question**.

*Or He who made the earth a place to settle on...?*

The question is not, "Did Allah make the earth?" It is sharper than that. It is: **Or who else, then?** Name the one. Bring forward the rival. Show me which of these stone bodies kept the ground from slipping out from under you while you slept last night.

[PAUSE]

Surah An-Naml is doing something specific here. From verse 60 to verse 64, Allah poses five rhetorical questions, each beginning with the same particle — **amman** — "Or who is it that...?" Five times the listener is dragged to the edge of a phenomenon they have lived inside their whole life, and asked: *who?*

This is the second of those five questions. The first asked them to look up — at the sky, the rain, the gardens. Now Allah brings the gaze down. To the dirt beneath their sandals. To the river they drink from. To the mountains on the horizon. To the place where the river meets the sea.

Here is what I want us to notice before we even open the Arabic:

The pagans of Makkah did not deny that Allah created the heavens and the earth. The Quran tells us elsewhere that they affirmed this. **What they denied was that He alone deserved worship.** They wanted intermediaries. Partners. Junior gods to handle the small stuff.

So this ayah is not arguing for the existence of a Creator.

It is arguing that **the Creator is still on duty.** That the world you are standing in right now is being held together — actively, continuously, mercifully — and that no stone in the Ka'bah has anything to do with it.

The hook I want to leave you with before we enter the words:

This ayah lists four acts of divine architecture. **Three of them are about giving you what you need. One of them is about keeping something away from you.** And that fourth one — the barrier between the two seas — may be the most merciful of them all. But you will only see why if we sit with it long enough.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Particle That Cannot Be Answered — *Amman*

Before we touch the content of the ayah, we have to understand the particle it opens with. Because if we miss the shape of the question, we will miss everything.

In Arabic, there is a difference between asking *"Who did this?"* and asking *"Or who, then, did this?"*

The first is a neutral question. It might have many answers.

The second is a **rhetorical trap**. It assumes a previous claim has been made, and it forces the listener to stand inside that claim and either defend it or abandon it.

**Amman** (أَمَّن) is a contraction. It is the particle *am* (which marks an alternative — "or") fused with the relative pronoun *man* ("the one who"). So it literally means: *"Or, the one who...?"*

Think about how this works in everyday speech. If I tell you, "I have a friend who owns three restaurants," and then I say, "Or who is it that gave you the food you ate yesterday?" — the second question is not asking for information. It is forcing you to compare. It is saying: *whatever claim you were holding a moment ago, hold it up next to this one.*

That is what *amman* does.

[PAUSE]

Now, where is the previous claim that *amman* is responding to?

Look at verse 59, just before our sequence begins. Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ to say: *"Praise be to Allah, and peace upon His chosen servants. Is Allah better — or what they associate with Him?"*

That is the first claim. The frame has been set: **Allah versus what they have set up alongside Him.**

And now five questions follow. Five times *amman*. Five times: *Or who is it that...?* Each one is implicitly completing the comparison: *Allah, who does X — or your idols?*

The genius of the construction is that **the answer is built into the question.** The pagan listener cannot say, "Hubal made the earth a stable place." Even he knows that's absurd. So the question itself extracts the confession. It does not argue with the listener. It positions the listener in a place where their own tongue must betray their idolatry.

This is not a debate. This is **a mirror held up at exactly the angle that shows you yourself.**

Stay with me. Because this matters for how we read the rest of the ayah.

When Allah lists what He has done — made the earth stable, ran rivers through it, set the mountains, placed the barrier — He is not informing you. **He is setting up four pieces of evidence in a courtroom where you are the only juror, and the verdict is already written on your heart.**

What does this single particle reveal?

That Allah's argument for His oneness is not built on abstract proofs. It is built on **the things you already know but have stopped seeing.** The first move is not to teach you something new. It is to make you notice something you have been standing inside your entire life.

## Element Two: *Qarār* — Not Just Ground, But a Place That Holds You

The first thing Allah names in His list is this:

*ja'ala-l-arda **qarāran*** — "He made the earth a *qarār*."

Most translations render this as "a stable place" or "a place of settlement." Both are correct. But neither captures what the word actually does.

Let me teach the concept first, because the word *qarār* opens up only when you see its root.

The root is **q-r-r** (ق-ر-ر). And this root, in classical Arabic, has a remarkable double life.

On one hand, it means **to settle, to come to rest, to stay put**. A river *yastaqirru* in its bed when it stops cutting new channels. A bird *yaqirru* in its nest when it has no need to fly. A heart *taqirru* when its anxieties have been answered.

On the other hand — and this is the part most translations miss — it means **to be cool, to find coolness**. *Qurrat al-'ayn* — "the coolness of the eye" — is the Arabic idiom for that which delights you, that which makes your tearful eye finally stop burning. When the desert traveler reaches the well and drinks, his eye finds *qurra*. The fever breaks; the body finds *qarār*.

Why are these two meanings in the same root?

Because in the Arabian world, **rest and coolness were the same thing.** To stop moving was to find shade. To settle was to escape the burning. The two ideas were so intertwined that the language refused to separate them.

So when Allah says He made the earth a *qarār*, He is not just saying it is geologically stable.

He is saying: **He made it a place where your wandering can end. Where the burning stops. Where the heart can lay itself down.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The earth is not described as *bayt* (a house). It is not described as *ma'wā* (a shelter). It is described with a word that means *the place where movement ends*.

You and I have lived inside this fact our whole lives without noticing. The earth holds us. We don't slip off it. We don't drift into space. The ground beneath your feet right now is, every second, refusing to abandon you.

Stay with this.

The pagans of Makkah did not invent gods of stability. They invented gods of war, gods of fertility, gods of harvest. They never imagined a god whose job was to keep the dirt from giving way. Why?

**Because they took the floor for granted.**

This is exactly what Allah is doing with the *amman* construction. He is asking: *who held the floor for you?* He is naming a mercy so constant, so silent, so foundational, that you have never once thanked anyone for it.

Now hold this next to your own life.

What in your life is *qarār*? What is the floor that you have been standing on so long you no longer notice it? Your health. The fact that your lungs filled this morning without your asking. The fact that the people you love woke up. The fact that there is a ground at all to walk on tomorrow.

The Quran's argument here is not, "Be grateful for the big things." It is, **"Be grateful for the floor."**

What does this single word reveal? That Allah's mercy is not primarily what He gives you when you ask. It is what He gives you in the silence — what He has been giving you so steadily that you forgot it was a gift.

## Element Three: *Ḥājiz* — The Barrier That Protects By Refusing to Mix

Now we come to the line that, if we slow down enough, will undo us.

*wa ja'ala bayna-l-bahrayni **ḥājizan*** — "and He placed between the two seas a *ḥājiz* — a barrier."

Let's teach this carefully.

The root is **ḥ-j-z** (ح-ج-ز). It means **to prevent, to hold back, to stop something from reaching something else**. A *ḥājiz* is anything that comes between two things and keeps them from merging. In classical usage, the same word is used for the diaphragm — the muscle between the chest and the belly — that keeps two cavities of the body separate.

So a *ḥājiz* is **not a wall**. A wall is solid. A *ḥājiz* can be invisible. It can be a force, a pressure, a difference. What defines it is not its substance — it is its **function**. It keeps two things from becoming one.

Now, what are *al-baḥrayn* — "the two seas"?

The classical scholars discussed this carefully. The dual form *baḥrayn* is specific — it does not mean *seas* in general. It means *the two seas* — a particular pair. And the reading the scholars converged toward, as a composite, is this: it refers to **the meeting of fresh water and salt water** — the rivers that flow into the oceans, and the strange, well-documented phenomenon that they do not immediately mix.

You can see this with your own eyes today. Where the Amazon meets the Atlantic, where the Mississippi pours into the Gulf — there is a visible line. Two waters touching, sometimes for kilometers, refusing to merge. Modern science calls it a *halocline* — a barrier of differing salinity, density, and temperature. Allah called it a *ḥājiz*.

But here is what I want us to see.

In a desert civilization, **fresh water is life**. The pagans of Makkah understood this in their bones. Their entire society was built around the well of Zamzam. Caravans tracked oases. A man who knew where water was knew where survival was.

And the great horror of fresh water is that **it can be ruined**. One drop of seawater does not poison a well. But enough seawater poisons it forever. If the boundary between the river and the sea collapsed, **every river on earth would become salt within a generation.**

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says He has placed a *ḥājiz* between the two seas, He is not just describing a physical curiosity.

He is saying: **the reason you are alive is that something is holding back the salt.**

Notice the rhetorical structure. The first three things Allah lists are gifts of *presence*: He gave the earth stability, He ran rivers through it, He set mountains upon it. But the fourth thing is a gift of *absence*. He **kept something away** from you.

This is asymmetric. The first three are positive: *here, take this*. The fourth is negative: *here, I am holding this back from reaching you*.

Stay with me. Because this is the heart of the ayah.

**Some of Allah's greatest mercies are not what He gives you. They are what He keeps from you.**

We are conditioned to count blessings as things we receive. The car. The job. The healthy child. We do not count, in our blessing-tally, the things that did not happen. The accident that did not occur. The illness that did not arrive. The salt that did not reach the river.

But the Quran is teaching us a different accounting. Allah's lordship is visible in **the barriers**. The *ḥājiz* between you and what would destroy you is, every second, holding. And you have never once thanked Him for it because you have never noticed it was there.

What does this word reveal?

That mercy can wear the face of separation. That a "no" from Allah can be a barrier between you and the thing that would have ruined you. That your life is being preserved not only by what flows toward you but by **what is being held back from reaching you.**

## Bridge to the Themes

We have walked through three linguistic features.

The particle *amman* — the question that forces you to confess what you already know.

The word *qarār* — the gift of a floor so steady you forgot it was a gift.

The word *ḥājiz* — the barrier that keeps the salt from your river.

Each one of these has been doing the same work from a different angle. Each one is asking: **what mercies are you living inside that you have stopped seeing?**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of your life itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Mercy of the Unnoticed

Let me start somewhere far from the ayah.

You have a friend who is going through something hard. A divorce, maybe. A diagnosis. Their life has cracked open. And you say to them, gently, "What can I do?" And they say back the thing people always say: *"I just don't know what I have to be grateful for anymore."*

It's an honest sentence. When suffering arrives, gratitude collapses. The mind narrows to what is hurting. Everything else gets dim.

What this ayah is doing — if we listen carefully — is **rebuilding the field of vision**.

It is not asking you to be grateful for the dramatic things. It is not pointing at gardens or sunsets or mountaintops. It is pointing at the **ground**. At the fact that there is ground.

And there is something almost shocking about this. The pagans of Makkah lived in a desert. They knew, viscerally, what it was to be far from water. They knew what it was to walk over land that could swallow a caravan in a sandstorm. So when Allah says, "He made the earth a *qarār*" — a place where wandering ends — they would have heard it as *real*.

We don't. We live in cities. The ground is so reliable to us that we have forgotten there is a ground.

[PAUSE]

Here is the practice the ayah is inviting.

For one day — just one — try to count not what you have been given but **what has been held in place.**

The fact that your bones did not break this morning. That the floor did not give way. That the river of your life did not flood with salt. That your mind kept its shape. That the people you love stayed alive through the night.

Every one of these is a *ḥājiz* — a barrier that Allah placed and is holding, right now, while you read this. The barrier between health and sickness. Between your loved ones and the accident that almost happened. Between your sanity and the moment that would have broken you. Between your river and the sea.

You will notice something strange when you do this. **You will run out of breath before you run out of items.** The list does not end. There is no horizon to it. Every second of every day, in every direction, Allah is holding back what would destroy you, while giving you what keeps you alive.

This is what the *amman* construction is forcing into view.

Not: *Is there a god who made the earth?*

But: *Is there a god still standing watch — right now, this second — over the floor you are sitting on, the river you drink from, the salt you don't taste?*

And the answer that the construction extracts from your own tongue is the only answer there is: **No. There is no other.**

The practical application here is simple but transformative. Develop a daily practice of naming one barrier. One thing that did *not* happen today that you should thank Allah for. Not "thank you for my health" — that is too abstract. Try: *"Thank you for the cell that did not turn cancerous today. Thank you for the driver who saw me and braked. Thank you for the salt that did not reach my river."* Make the barriers visible, one at a time. This is what tawhid looks like in your kitchen at 7 a.m.

## Theme Two: When His "No" Is the Same as His Love

Now I want to take this same image — the barrier between the two seas — and turn it inward. Because the ayah is not only about geography. It is, by its own logic, about the architecture of every life.

Think about the prayer you made last year that Allah did not answer.

The job you wanted. The marriage you thought was right. The opportunity that seemed obvious. You asked. You begged. And Allah, who hears every du'a, said no.

Most of us, when this happens, experience it as withdrawal. As a refusal. As a wall.

But what if it was a *ḥājiz*?

[PAUSE]

What if Allah, who knows the chemistry of every river and every sea, saw that this thing you were begging for was salt that would have destroyed your fresh water? What if the "no" was not a wall keeping you out — but **a barrier keeping the wrong thing out of you?**

The structure of this ayah is teaching us to read our lives the way we read the geography of the planet. The river is your life as it is — fresh, drinkable, alive. The sea is what would have destroyed you. The barrier is Allah's wisdom, holding them apart.

Now here is the part that requires courage to sit with.

**You cannot see the barrier from inside the river.** From inside the fresh water, the barrier is invisible. The river just keeps flowing. The river never thinks about salt. The river never says, "Praise be to the One who is keeping the salt away from me right now."

It is only when something goes wrong — when a little salt slips through, when a hard year arrives, when a prayer is denied and you are forced to reckon with what you almost had — that you start to see the architecture.

The Quran is asking us to see the architecture **before** we are forced to.

Here is the paradox that the ayah leaves sitting with you.

If Allah only ever gave you what you asked for, your life would have been salt long ago. You have wanted things that would have unmade you. You have begged for waters that, had you tasted them, would have stopped your heart.

His mercy is not only in what He gave you. **His mercy is in what He refused.**

[PAUSE]

This reframes the most painful moments of a believer's life. The prayer not answered. The door not opened. The relationship that ended. The opportunity that closed.

The believer's task is not to demand that the barrier come down. The believer's task is to recognize that **the barrier is the love.**

There is a hadith — narrated by Abu Sa'id al-Khudri and recorded in the Musnad of Imam Ahmad — in which the Prophet ﷺ said that no Muslim makes a du'a, free of sin and not asking for severance of family ties, except that Allah gives him one of three things: He answers his du'a immediately, or He stores it for him for the Hereafter, or **He averts from him an evil equivalent to what he asked for.** That third category is the *ḥājiz*. The unanswered prayer is, sometimes, the most powerfully answered prayer of all — because what flowed toward you in its place was protection.

So the practical application:

The next time Allah says no to you, do not ask, *Why is He keeping this from me?* Ask instead, *What is He keeping from me with this?* The "no" is rarely empty. It is almost always a barrier. The salt that did not reach you is not visible. But it is real.

This is how the believer learns to trust the architect. Not because every gift comes wrapped. But because the river is still flowing, and the salt is still being held back, and the floor is still beneath the feet, and the One who arranges all of it has never once stopped paying attention.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at what this ayah has actually been doing.

It listed four things:

1. **The earth — *qarār*** — the place where movement ends.
2. **Rivers running through it** — what nourishes you.
3. **Mountains upon it** — what holds it steady.
4. **A barrier between the two seas** — what protects what nourishes you.

This is not a list. **This is a system.**

A floor to stand on. Water to drink. Anchors to keep the floor from breaking up. And a guard around the water to keep it drinkable.

These are not four separate gifts. They are one architecture. They are the four-piece description of **a world that is being kept alive on your behalf.** Take any one of them away, and the whole thing collapses. The earth without anchors becomes unstable. The rivers without barriers become salt. The earth without rivers becomes desert.

The pagans of Makkah lived inside this system and never saw it. They walked on it, drank from it, were sheltered by it — and they bowed to stones.

Allah's question — *Is there a god alongside Allah?* — is not asking them to believe in a Creator. **It is asking them to see the system they are standing inside.**

And then the closing line: *bal aktharuhum lā ya'lamūn* — "rather, most of them do not know."

Notice the word. Not "they reject." Not "they deny." **They do not know.** Their problem is not stubbornness. It is unawareness. They have lived inside the architecture so long that they have forgotten there is an architect.

This is the diagnosis. And it is, very often, our diagnosis too.

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The mercies of Allah are not only what flows to you, but what is being held back from you — and most of us never learn to read the barriers as love.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in your life is *qarār* — what floor have you been standing on so long you forgot to thank the One who placed it?
2. What is the *ḥājiz* in your life right now? What barrier is Allah holding, this very second, between you and what would have destroyed you?
3. Think of one du'a Allah did not answer. Looking back, can you see the salt He was keeping from your river?
4. The pagans did not deny Allah; they failed to *notice* Him. Where in your daily life are you walking past mercies without seeing them?
5. If you trusted that Allah's "no" was always a barrier and never a wall — how would you pray differently tomorrow?

### The Closing Invitation

You will walk on the earth today. You will drink water today. You will pass by mountains, or rivers, or the line of the sea. The world will keep doing what it has done since you were born — holding you, feeding you, refusing to let the salt reach you.

The invitation is not to do something new. It is to **see what is already happening.**

Once a day, find a barrier. Name it. Thank the One who placed it. *"Ya Allah, You held this back from me. I did not see it. But You did."*

This is what it means to live inside tawhid. Not just to believe in one God — but to recognize that every floor, every river, every barrier is being held by His hand, right now, while you breathe.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who placed the floor beneath us and the barrier between the salt and the river — open our eyes to the mercies we have been walking past. Teach us to count not only what You give but what You hold back. And when You say no to our prayers, let us see Your "no" as the barrier of Your love. Ameen.*
