# Why Hasten the Bad Before the Good?

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
قَالَ يَـٰقَوْمِ لِمَ تَسْتَعْجِلُونَ بِٱلسَّيِّئَةِ قَبْلَ ٱلْحَسَنَةِ ۖ لَوْلَا تَسْتَغْفِرُونَ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Qāla yā-qawmi lima tastaʿjilūna bis-sayyiʾati qabla al-ḥasanati, lawlā tastaghfirūna Allāha laʿallakum turḥamūn*

**Translation:**
"He said: 'My people, why do you seek to hasten the evil before the good? If only you would seek forgiveness from Allah, that you might be shown mercy.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION: A People Who Demanded Their Own Ruin

Picture the city of Thamud. Stone houses carved into red mountain cliffs — homes that look like they will outlast civilization itself. A people who had inherited the earth after ʿĀd, who had been given strength, prosperity, fertile valleys, water, and skill. Allah Himself describes them in the Quran as a people whom He had given so much that they began to feel untouchable.

Among them stood a man they had once known and trusted. Salih, peace be upon him. Their brother, raised among them. He came to them with a single message: *worship Allah alone.*

By the time we arrive at this ayah, that simple message has fractured them. The city has split into two factions. Those who believed and those who didn't. And the disbelieving faction is no longer merely doubting — they are openly mocking. The previous verses in this surah show them turning hostility into a kind of theatre. They have been warned about a punishment that will come. And rather than tremble at the warning, they sneer back at the messenger and say: *bring it then. Hasten it. Where is your punishment, Salih? Show us.*

[PAUSE]

Stop and feel what that is.

A man comes to you, frightened for your soul, warning you about a fire he sees coming. And rather than ask him about the fire, you taunt him: *let it come faster. Light the match yourself. Prove it.*

This is not the psychology of a confident skeptic. A confident skeptic shrugs and walks away. This is the psychology of someone whose denial has become so brittle that the only way to defend it is to dare reality to break it. *If the punishment doesn't come right now, then I was right to ignore him.* They want the calamity, because the calamity is now the only thing that can settle the question.

And what does Salih say back?

He doesn't curse them. He doesn't match their volume. He doesn't even directly answer their challenge. He turns the entire conversation by asking one stunning question — and then offers them the door he has been holding open the whole time.

**The Hook:**
There is a word in this ayah that will tell us everything about what was actually wrong with these people. It is not the word for *evil.* It is not the word for *good.* It is a small verb, in a particular form, that exposes the exact psychological disorder of a soul running toward its own destruction. Once you see it, you will start seeing it in your own week.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Tastaʿjilūn* — The Form That Exposes the Disease

Before we open the word in the ayah, let me teach you something about Arabic that the listener of this verse already knew in their bones.

Arabic verbs come in **forms**. The same root can be poured into different molds, and each mold changes the meaning. The root **ʿ-j-l** carries the sense of *haste* — to be quick, to be in a hurry, to rush.

In its simplest form (Form I), *ʿajila* just means "he was quick." Neutral. Descriptive.

But Arabic has a tenth form — Form X — built on the pattern *istafʿala*. And this form usually carries a very specific meaning: **to seek, to demand, to actively try to bring about** the action of the root. *Istaghfara* — to seek forgiveness. *Istakhraja* — to seek to extract. *Istakbara* — to seek to make oneself great.

So when the root for *haste* is poured into Form X, it doesn't just mean "to be in a hurry." It means **to actively demand that something happen sooner. To pull the future toward yourself by the collar. To say to time: *come faster.***

Now look at what Salih says.

**لِمَ تَسْتَعْجِلُونَ** — *lima tastaʿjilūn* — "why do you seek to hasten?"

He didn't say *why are you in a hurry.* He said *why are you reaching out and grabbing the punishment by the wrist and dragging it toward yourself?*

Stay with this.

The form of the verb tells us this is not passive impatience. This is active demand. They are not merely failing to repent — they are *campaigning for the bad to come faster.* They are running toward the cliff and asking why they haven't fallen yet.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The Quran could have said *lima tuʿajjilūn* — "why do you make it come quickly" — using a different form. It didn't. The Form X here is doing surgical work. It tells us this is a soul that has organized its will around the wrong outcome. The will is intact. The energy is intact. The capacity to *seek* something is intact. It is just *pointed at the destruction.*

And notice: the verb is plural. *Tastaʿjilūn.* This is not one rebel in the corner. This is a collective psychology. A whole society demanding its own ruin together, each emboldened by the others. Modern social psychology has a name for this — *risk shift,* the way groups become more reckless than any individual would be alone. The Quran diagnosed it in one verb form fourteen hundred years ago.

What does this single word reveal? **Disbelief at its terminal stage is not apathy. It is appetite — pointed in the wrong direction.**

### Element Two: *Bis-Sayyiʾah Qabla al-Ḥasanah* — The Definite Articles That Aren't Naming What You Think

Now look at the objects of their hastening: *bis-sayyiʾati qabla al-ḥasanati* — "the evil before the good."

Most translations render this as "evil" and "good," and that is fine. But the Arabic is doing something more precise.

First — both nouns carry the definite article *al-*. Not *a* sayyiʾah, *the* sayyiʾah. Not *a* ḥasanah, *the* ḥasanah. In Arabic grammar, the definite article on a noun like this can mean one of two things: it can point to a specific, known referent, or it can name a category in its entirety. *Al-insān* can mean *the [particular] human* or *humankind as such.*

Here, the classical commentators read these as **referring to specific known things in the context.** *The* evil they had been demanding — the punishment Salih warned them of. *The* good they had been refusing — the mercy that comes through belief and istighfār.

But notice what Allah does *not* say.

He does not say *qabla at-tawbah* — "before repentance."
He does not say *qabla al-īmān* — "before belief."
He does not say *qabla aṭ-ṭāʿah* — "before obedience."

He says *qabla al-ḥasanah* — **"before the good."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this.

The opposite of the punishment they demanded was not, in this verse, framed as a hard religious demand. It was framed simply as **the beautiful thing.** The good outcome. The pleasant alternative. The gift.

Salih is not saying *why are you hastening punishment before submission?* He is saying *why are you hastening the ugly thing before the beautiful thing?* This is the language of a mother to a child who is about to swallow something poisonous when the sweet drink is sitting right next to it. *Habibi — why this, before that?*

The framing is devastating because it strips away every excuse. They cannot say *the alternative is too costly.* They cannot say *belief is too heavy.* He has reframed the choice as: *the bad thing or the good thing — and you are running toward the bad one.* The choice has been made aesthetically obvious.

And here is what the linguistic frame reveals: **Allah does not present His path to humanity primarily as a burden to be accepted. He presents it as a beauty to be received.** The Quran's word for the alternative to ruin is *al-ḥasanah* — that which is *beautiful, lovely, fitting.* The disbeliever is not someone who refused a hard duty. He is someone who turned away from a beautiful gift toward an ugly one. That is what makes the rejection so strange. That is why Salih's question begins with *lima* — *why?* It is genuinely bewildering behavior, and the Quran captures the bewilderment in the word choice itself.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Lawlā Tastaghfirūn* — The Particle That Is Almost a Plea

Look at the second half of the ayah.

**لَوْلَا تَسْتَغْفِرُونَ ٱللَّهَ** — *lawlā tastaghfirūna Allāh* — "if only you would seek forgiveness from Allah."

The word *lawlā* in Arabic has two distinct uses depending on context. In one use, attached to a past-tense verb, it means *had it not been for...* But here, attached to a present-tense verb (*tastaghfirūn*), the classical grammarians and lexicographers identify it as a particle of **gentle exhortation, urging, encouragement** — what the grammarians call *taḥḍīḍ.* It carries the force of: *won't you...? why don't you...? if only you would...*

This is not a command in the imperative form. Salih does not say *istaghfirū!* — "seek forgiveness!" — using the direct order. He uses *lawlā tastaghfirūn* — a softer, almost pleading construction.

Stay with that texture.

These are people who have just mocked him. Demanded their own destruction. Split his city against him. And his response is a verb form so gentle that the closest English equivalent is **"won't you, please...?"**

[PAUSE]

This is what prophetic patience sounds like in Arabic grammar. Not capitulation. Not weakness. The truth is unchanged — punishment is real, repentance is necessary. But the *delivery* is poured into the softest possible mold. *Lawlā tastaghfirūn.* Won't you turn back? Why won't you?

And notice the verb form again — *tastaghfirūn.* It is also Form X. The same form as *tastaʿjilūn* in the first half of the ayah. Allah has placed a mirror inside the verse.

You are *seeking to hasten* (*tastaʿjilūn*) the bad.
**Why won't you *seek to be forgiven* (*tastaghfirūn*) instead?**

The same energy, the same act of *seeking,* the same Form X — but redirected. Salih is not asking them to become passive. He is not asking them to stop being people of will and demand. He is saying: *you have all this seeking-energy. You are running toward something. Just turn around. Run toward the forgiveness instead of the fire.*

The diagnosis we found in the first word is now answered by its mirror. **The disease was misdirected seeking. The cure is redirected seeking.** Not the absence of will. The reorientation of will.

[PAUSE]

What this single grammatical pairing reveals: **conversion is not the death of desire. It is desire pointed home.**

### Bridge to the Themes

We have seen what the linguistic construction has shown.

A people whose will has not failed — it has misfired. Running toward ruin with the same force they should have run toward repentance. A messenger answering their mockery not with mockery but with a question and a gentle exhortation. And underneath all of it, an architecture: *tastaʿjilūn* mirroring *tastaghfirūn,* the disease and the cure built on the same grammatical bone.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the strange psychology of the soul, and about the patience of the One who keeps the door open.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Psychology of Demanding Your Own Destruction

There is a moment many of us have lived through, even if we have never named it.

You know something is wrong. You know what you are doing — to your body, to your relationship, to your faith, to your sleep, to your phone — is hurting you. And rather than stop, rather than change, you find yourself somehow *accelerating* it. You stay up later than the night before. You eat the worse thing instead of the better thing. You scroll past the place where you should have stopped. You pick the fight rather than de-escalate it. You mock the person who tries to warn you.

Modern psychology has a name for parts of this. *Self-sabotage.* *Approach-avoidance under cognitive dissonance.* *Counter-dependent reactance.* When someone you respect tells you that you are heading for a cliff, a particular kind of injured pride does not just keep walking — it *speeds up.* It says: *watch me.*

This is not rare. This is human.

Now bring the ayah back.

These are not aliens. These are not cartoon villains. These are people whose pride has been bruised by a man they grew up with telling them their gods are nothing. And rather than admit the wound, they organize their entire psyche around proving him wrong. *Bring the punishment, Salih. We dare you. Hasten it.* The escalation is not really about the punishment at all. It is about silencing the voice inside that suspects he might be right.

This is why Salih's *lima* — his *why?* — is so piercing. He is not asking a rhetorical question. He is asking the actual question. *Why are you doing this to yourselves?* The question is meant to make them stop and look at the absurdity of their own behavior. *Why are you running toward the bad when the good is right here?*

[PAUSE]

Here is the spiritual architecture this ayah exposes.

**Disbelief, in its terminal stage, is not the absence of faith. It is the active investment in the failure of faith.** It is the soul that has bet so much on the wrong horse that it now needs the right horse to lose. It is the heart that has constructed an entire identity around being unbroken by a particular truth — and so it cannot let that truth be true, no matter what it costs.

This is in us too. We don't have to be a Thamudi to know this state. Anyone who has ever gripped a position past the point where they could still see clearly, anyone who has ever made an argument they no longer believed because backing down felt worse than being wrong — we have all tasted it.

The cure in the ayah is staggering in its simplicity. Salih does not ask them to publicly recant. He does not ask them to humiliate themselves before him. He asks them, in the quietest grammar Arabic offers, to **seek forgiveness from Allah.** *Lawlā tastaghfirūn.* The transaction is between them and their Lord. No audience. No spectacle. Just a turning, in private, of the heart back toward its origin.

**Practical application:** This week, identify one place in your life where you are *tastaʿjilūn* — actively hastening the bad. Not just tolerating a problem, but *demanding* it faster through your behavior. The relationship you are picking apart. The habit you are deepening. The argument you keep relighting. Then ask Salih's question of yourself: *lima?* Why this, before the good? And then turn it — quietly, between you and Allah — into *istighfār.* No announcement required. Just the redirection of the same will.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: *Laʿallakum Turḥamūn* — The Door Mercy Holds Open

The ayah ends with a phrase that I want to spend the rest of our time on. *Laʿallakum turḥamūn.* "That you might be shown mercy."

Two things in this phrase will repay every minute we spend with them.

The first is the particle *laʿalla.* It is usually translated as *perhaps,* or *that...might,* or *so that.* In Arabic rhetorical theory, *laʿalla* expresses **expectation, hope, the opening of a possibility.** It is not the language of guarantee. It is not the language of contract. It is the language of *the door is open — walk through.*

When Allah says *laʿallakum turḥamūn* — "that you might be shown mercy" — He is not saying *and then I will be obligated to forgive you.* He is saying *and the path of mercy will be open to you.* The hope is real. The possibility is genuine. But the framing preserves something profound: **mercy is never a transaction Allah owes. It is always a gift He gives.**

This is delicate, so let me say it more carefully.

If repentance forced Allah's hand — if you put in the istighfār and the forgiveness automatically came out, like a vending machine — then mercy would no longer be mercy. It would be wages. It would be the thing you earned by doing the work. And the entire emotional texture of returning to Allah would change. You would walk back not as a beggar at the door of a King, but as a contractor coming to collect what is owed.

The *laʿalla* preserves the dignity of the gift. *If only you would seek forgiveness — that mercy might reach you.* The hope is overwhelming. The possibility is overwhelming. But the awe is preserved. You are returning to a Lord, not a machine.

[PAUSE]

The second thing is the verb *turḥamūn.* It is in the **passive voice.** Not *tarḥamūna anfusakum* — "that you might show mercy to yourselves." Not *tudriku ar-raḥmah* — "that you might attain mercy through your effort." But *turḥamūn* — "that you might *be shown mercy.*"

Notice what this does. The act of istighfār is yours. The *seeking* is yours. The turning is yours. But the mercy itself comes from outside you. It is given. It is poured into you. You cannot manufacture it. You can only open the door, and **stand in the doorway, and wait for it to come.**

This is the most healing grammar in the verse.

For the soul that is exhausted from trying to fix itself, the passive voice here is a release. *You do not have to generate your own mercy. You only have to seek the forgiveness that opens the channel. The mercy comes to you.* From outside. As gift.

This is the answer to the disease in the first half of the ayah. They were *seeking to hasten* the bad — actively pulling ruin toward themselves through their own will. The cure is not the equal and opposite — *seeking to manufacture the good through your own will.* The cure is asymmetric: **redirect your seeking toward forgiveness, and let mercy come to you as a gift.**

Effort on your side. Grace from Allah's side. The asymmetry is the point.

[PAUSE]

**Practical application:** When you make istighfār this week, do it twice. Once with your tongue — say the words. *Astaghfirullāh.* Then sit, in silence, for thirty seconds afterward — and **do not do anything.** Do not strain to feel forgiven. Do not perform contrition. Just sit in the doorway with the door open, and let the *turḥamūn* — the *being-shown-mercy* — come to you. The verse teaches that the moment of mercy is something you receive, not something you produce.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at what Allah built in fourteen words.

**First half of the ayah:** *Lima tastaʿjilūna bis-sayyiʾati qabla al-ḥasanah.*
The disease — diagnosed in **Form X** of the verb of haste. Active seeking, pointed at ruin.

**Second half of the ayah:** *Lawlā tastaghfirūna Allāha laʿallakum turḥamūn.*
The cure — delivered in **Form X** of the verb of forgiveness-seeking. Active seeking, pointed at the door.

The same grammatical bone runs through both halves. The same Form X. The same plural address. Salih is not telling them to become different people. He is telling them to **redirect the same will.** The energy that is currently demanding fire — point it at forgiveness instead. The seeking is intact. Just turn it.

And underneath that, the asymmetry: *you seek* (*tastaghfirūn*, active) — *and you are shown mercy* (*turḥamūn*, passive). You bring the turn. Allah brings the mercy. The transaction is honored, but it is not a transaction.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: a verse architected around the mirroring of two Form X verbs, one diagnosing misdirected will, the other prescribing redirected will, with mercy preserved as gift through *laʿalla* and the passive *turḥamūn.*

From the thematic side: a portrait of the human heart at its strangest moment — running toward its own destruction — and a prophetic answer that does not crush the running soul but redirects it. The cure for hastening ruin is not paralysis. It is the same energy, sent home.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I currently *tastaʿjilūn* — actively hastening something I know is bad — and what wound is my pride defending?

2. When was the last time I let myself be **shown** mercy, rather than trying to manufacture it through my own performance of contrition?

3. If Salih asked me his question — *why this, before the good?* — and I had to answer honestly, what would I say?

4. What does it mean that the alternative to ruin in this ayah is called *al-ḥasanah* — "the beautiful thing" — rather than *the duty,* or *the obedience,* or *the demand?*

5. Whose voice in my life is currently playing the role of Salih — frightened for me, telling me a truth I am tempted to mock — and what is it costing me to dismiss them?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The disease of the heart is not the absence of will but its misdirection — and the cure is not the suppression of will but the gentle, private turning of that same will toward the door of forgiveness, where mercy waits to be given.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, find the place in your day where you were *hastening the bad.* Not the dramatic places — the small ones. The conversation you sharpened past where it should have softened. The five extra minutes of scrolling after you knew you should stop. The thought you replayed when you knew it was poisoning you.

Don't punish yourself for it. Salih did not punish his people for it. He asked them *why* — and then he opened the door.

Open the door. Quietly. *Astaghfirullāh.* And then sit in the doorway and let the mercy come.

### Brief Du'a

*Yā Rabb, the One whose verb for mercy is in the passive — because the gift comes from You and the seeking is the only thing You have asked of us — turn our hastening from the fire toward Your forgiveness. Where our will is misfiring, redirect it. Where we are demanding our own ruin, give us the courage of Salih's question, asked of our own hearts: lima — why? And then, when we turn, let us be among those of whom You said: laʿallakum turḥamūn. Āmīn.*
