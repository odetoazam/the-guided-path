# We Shall See: The Patience of the One Who Already Knows

## Introduction

**The ayah:**

> [ayah:27:27] ۞ قَالَ سَنَنظُرُ أَصَدَقْتَ أَمْ كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ
>
> *Qāla sananẓuru aṣadaqta am kunta min al-kādhibīn*
>
> "He said: We shall see — whether you have spoken truth, or whether you have been of the liars."

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

A king is reviewing his army. Not just any king — a prophet-king, given dominion over wind and jinn, given a vocabulary the rest of creation does not possess. He understands the speech of birds. He understands the speech of ants. The Quran, just a few ayahs earlier, has shown us this: an ant, a tiny ant, gives a warning to her colony, and Sulaymān smiles at her words and asks Allah for gratitude.

This is a king who could hear the smallest creature in the dust.

And yet — in the middle of his royal inspection, he notices something missing. *"Why do I not see the hoopoe?"* A single bird, absent from formation. A king with armies of jinn and humans and birds, and his attention catches on one missing creature.

[PAUSE]

He doesn't shrug. He doesn't dismiss it. He declares a verdict in advance: *I will punish him severely, or I will slaughter him, unless he brings me a clear authorization.*

Now picture the moment. The bird returns. Late. And before Sulaymān can carry out the threat, the hoopoe speaks first:

*"I have encompassed what you have not encompassed, and I have come to you from Sabaʾ with certain news."*

A bird. Telling a prophet. *I know something you don't.*

And then the bird begins to describe a kingdom — a queen, a magnificent throne, a people prostrating to the sun instead of to Allah. A whole civilization Sulaymān's intelligence networks had somehow not surfaced.

This is the moment our ayah lives inside.

**Here is the hook.** What does a prophet do when a subordinate brings him explosive information — information that could trigger an invasion, a diplomatic mission, a religious reformation of an entire people? What does he do when the messenger is unreliable enough to have been late to formation, yet specific enough to be quoted in detail? What is the *correct posture* between trust and dismissal?

Allah gives us the answer in seven words. And in those seven words is buried a verification protocol, a theory of justice, and a glimpse into what restraint looks like when it is practiced not by the weak — but by someone who has every power to act immediately.

Stay with me. The smallness of this ayah is deceptive.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Letter That Refuses Now

Look at the very first word out of Sulaymān's mouth: **سَنَنظُرُ** — *sananẓuru.*

Before we open it, let me teach you something about Arabic.

Arabic has two distinct ways of pointing a verb into the future. One is the particle **سوف** (*sawfa*) — a separate word that precedes the verb. The other is the prefix **سـ** (*sīn*) — a single letter glued directly onto the front. To English ears they sound nearly identical: both translate as "will." But classical grammarians have long noted a difference in *texture.*

The standalone *sawfa* tends to gesture toward a more distant or expansive future — a "down the road, eventually." The attached *sīn* is the nearer, more immediate future — closer to the English "shortly we will" or "we are about to."

Now look at what Sulaymān does *not* say. He does not say *naẓarnā* — "we have looked." He does not say *naẓartu* — "I have looked." He does not even say *anẓuru* — "I look." He says **sananẓuru**: not now, but *soon*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The hoopoe has just made a stunning claim. A prophet — with armies, with jinn, with the wind itself at his disposal — could have launched verification within the hour. He could have sent a battalion. He could have summoned a jinn and demanded ocular confirmation. He could have rendered a verdict immediately, based on the bird's body language alone.

Instead he inserts a single letter — *sīn* — between the claim and the response.

That letter is the entire ayah.

Notice something remarkable: that one letter is doing the work of *suspending* judgment. Not refusing it. Not abandoning it. Not pretending it doesn't matter. Just — *deferring.* Creating a small but deliberate gap between *I have heard you* and *I have decided about you.*

Here's what's worth pausing on. In the structure of human reaction, the most dangerous moment is the gap of *zero seconds* — the moment when input becomes verdict without any space in between. That is where almost all injustice lives. That is where rumors become beatings. That is where a misread tone of voice becomes a divorce. That is where a forwarded message becomes a destroyed reputation.

The *sīn* is Allah teaching us, through a prophet's mouth, that the architecture of justice requires a small letter — a single sound — placed between *hearing* and *acting.*

And look at the verb itself: **نَنظُرُ** — *nanẓuru.* From the root **ن-ظ-ر** (*n-ẓ-r*). In the classical lexicons this root is not merely "to see." It is *naẓar* — to *look at with consideration*. To *examine*. To *direct one's gaze deliberately at something in order to understand it.* The Mufradāt of Rāghib distinguishes between **رأى** (*raʾā*) — to perceive, to passively see — and **نظر** (*naẓar*) — to *investigate*, to bring the eye to bear with the intent of judgment.

Sulaymān does not say "we will *find out*." He says "we will *examine.*" The verb itself carries methodology inside it. To do *naẓar* on something is to study it.

And here's the linguistic stillness most translations cannot capture: the verb is plural — *we shall examine*. Some grammarians take this as the royal plural; others as Sulaymān gesturing to the apparatus of his court, the witnesses he will employ, the process this will involve. Either way the message is the same: the verification will not be a private hunch. It will be a procedure.

[PAUSE]

What does one letter and one verb tell us? That when a prophet receives information, he creates *time* and he creates *method*. He does not collapse the gap between hearing and judging. He widens it on purpose.

### Element Two: The Asymmetry Between Truth and Lying

Now look at the two options Sulaymān names: **أَصَدَقْتَ أَمْ كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ** — *aṣadaqta am kunta min al-kādhibīn.*

The English flattens it: *whether you have spoken the truth or whether you are of the liars.*

But sit with the Arabic. Truly sit with it.

Allah, narrating Sulaymān's words, does not give us a symmetrical pair. He does not say *aṣadaqta am kadhabta* — "did you tell the truth, or did you lie." That would be the natural symmetry — verb against verb, action against action.

Instead He says: *aṣadaqta* — "did you speak truth" — **versus** *kunta min al-kādhibīn* — "or are you *of the liars*."

Look at the asymmetry. On one side: a single verb of action. *Did you speak truth in this instance?* On the other side: a verb of *being*, plus a noun of *category*. *Or are you of the liars?* Of the *kind* of person who lies. *Of the tribe* of those whose speech is unreliable.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most morally precise lines in the Quran, and almost no English translation can carry it.

Why? Because of what it reveals about how Sulaymān is *framing the question*. He is not asking, simply, *did you commit one lie?* He is asking something deeper: *is this an instance of truth-telling from you — or are you the sort of creature whose default state is dishonesty?*

In other words: *is this a moment, or is this a pattern?*

Here's what's remarkable. The Arabic grammatical phrase **مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ** — *min al-kādhibīn* — uses a definite plural with the partitive *min*. *Of the liars*. *Of that recognized category of beings who habitually lie.* It implies that liars are a *known class* — a category with members — and the question is whether the hoopoe belongs in that registry.

And here's where the absence speaks. Allah did not need to make this asymmetric. He could have given us perfect parallelism. The choice to break the symmetry is doing theological work. It is showing us that **truth-telling and lying are not symmetrical realities.**

Truth-telling can be a single act — *aṣadaqta*, did you, on this occasion, speak what corresponds to reality. But lying, at its full and final form, is a *condition*. A person becomes *one of the liars* not by a single slip but by the accumulation that makes deception the default.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means for the hoopoe — and for us. Sulaymān is not preparing to punish a single false report. He is preparing to discover whether this messenger belongs to the category of those whose information can never be trusted again.

And notice what it means about Sulaymān's mercy. The framing protects the hoopoe. Even *if* the report turns out to be exaggerated or wrong, the question is still: is this a one-time error from a fundamentally honest creature, or is this characteristic of him? The grammar leaves room for a non-liar to be temporarily mistaken. It refuses to collapse a single failed report into a verdict about character.

This is what justice sounds like when it is grammatically careful.

### Element Three: The Verb of Being Where We Did Not Expect It

Look one more time at the second clause: **كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ** — *kunta min al-kādhibīn.*

*Kunta* — "you have been." Past tense of the verb of being.

But wait. The whole question is about the *present* report. About *this* claim, made today, about the kingdom of Sabaʾ. So why is the verb of being in the *past*?

Stay with me. This is subtle.

The classical grammarians explain that **كان** (*kāna*) in Arabic often functions not as a simple past — *was* — but as a verb that establishes an *established condition*. When Allah says of Himself in the Quran *kāna Allāhu ʿazīzan ḥakīmā* — "Allah has always been Mighty and Wise" — the *kāna* is not locating that wisdom in some past time. It is establishing it as a *standing reality.*

So *kunta min al-kādhibīn* is not asking *were you a liar at some point in the past?* It is asking: *is it an established fact about you that you belong to the category of liars?*

This is even sharper than the asymmetry we just unpacked. Sulaymān is not asking whether the bird lied today. He is asking whether *being a liar* is part of the bird's established identity. Whether liarhood is *who he is* — a condition with history, a pattern that precedes this moment.

[PAUSE]

And here's the gentleness inside this severity. The use of *kāna* — the past-tense of being — implies that even *if* the hoopoe turns out to be a liar by category, that is something *that has been*, that *was the case.* It does not, by itself, foreclose change. The grammar locates the falsehood in the past as a fact to be verified, not in the future as a sentence to be carried out forever.

What this whole linguistic structure shows us is something almost shocking: even at the moment of suspecting a subordinate of deception, the prophetic mind preserves three things at once.

It preserves **time** — *sananẓuru*, we shall examine.
It preserves **methodology** — *naẓar*, looking-with-consideration.
And it preserves **the dignity of the one being investigated** — by asking whether *being a liar* is an established condition, not whether this single act condemns him forever.

### The Bridge

Three words. *Sananẓuru. Aṣadaqta. Kunta min al-kādhibīn.*

A letter that creates time. A verb that creates method. A grammatical asymmetry that protects character from being destroyed by a single act.

This is what the linguistic construction has shown us — the *anatomy* of a prophet's response to unverified information.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About how the world works. About what it costs to be just.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Cruelty of the Zero-Second Gap

Let me start somewhere far from this ayah, and then come back to it.

Imagine you are scrolling on your phone. You see a screenshot of a tweet. The screenshot says that a public figure you admire said something monstrous. You feel the heat in your chest — that quick rush of moral certainty. You don't check the date. You don't reverse-image-search it. You don't ask who screenshotted it or why. Within seven seconds you have forwarded it, quoted it, condemned them in a story, blocked them.

Two days later it turns out the tweet was fake. The figure never said it. The screenshot was fabricated.

What was the actual mechanism of the harm?

It wasn't the lie. The lie was an external event. The mechanism of the harm was *you* — and specifically, the absence of time inside your own mind. The collapse of the gap between input and verdict. The way *seeing* and *deciding* fused into a single instantaneous act with no air between them.

[PAUSE]

This is the disease of our age, but it is not new. It is what Sulaymān's *sīn* — that one tiny letter — is built to cure.

Think about how almost all the ugliness in human life travels. Gossip. Slander. Mob justice. Family estrangements. Divorces fueled by a single misread text message. Friendships severed by a forwarded WhatsApp. Communities torn by a rumor passed at a janazah. None of these require a powerful liar. They only require listeners with no *sīn* in their grammar — no small letter of delay between hearing and condemning.

Now look at Sulaymān again. He has *every* reason to skip the gap. He is a king. He is a prophet. His time is precious. He has armies waiting. He has already publicly declared, just a few ayahs earlier, that the missing hoopoe will be severely punished. His credibility, as a ruler, is on the line. He cannot afford to look soft.

And yet. He inserts the letter. He says *sananẓuru.*

Notice what this teaches: **restraint is not the absence of power. Restraint is power refusing to discharge itself.** A weak person who delays a verdict is just incapable. A king with armies who delays a verdict is *practicing justice as a discipline.*

In the Quran, when Allah wants to teach us what *ʿadl* — justice — actually looks like, He does not give us a textbook. He gives us a king listening to a bird, and the single letter the king places between the bird's report and the king's response.

**The practical application:** every time you are about to issue a verdict in your own mind — about a friend, a spouse, a coworker, a public figure, a sect, a community — *insert the sīn.* Not forever. Not as cowardice. Just as a discipline of *time before judgment.* Say to yourself, even silently: *I will examine.* Not *I have decided.* Just — *I will look.*

That one letter, practiced daily, will reorder your life.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Refusal to Reduce a Person to an Incident

Now stay with that asymmetry from Part One. *Aṣadaqta am kunta min al-kādhibīn.* Did you speak truth — or *are you of the liars.*

Here is what that asymmetry models for a heart trying to live justly.

Most of us know what it feels like to be reduced. To do one thing wrong — once, at a bad moment, in a season we wish we could forget — and to feel that this single act has now become our identity in someone's eyes. The friend who saw us at our worst and decided that *that* is who we are. The parent who never quite forgave the teenage rebellion. The spouse who keeps a ledger.

The collapse of *an act* into *a being* is one of the most violent things one human can do to another. And it is *constant*. We do it to others as easily as breathing. *He lied to me once. He is a liar.* *She missed that one prayer. She is unserious.* *They voted that way. They are evil.*

[PAUSE]

Look at what Sulaymān refuses to do. He does not say *kadhabta*. He does not say *you lied.* He says *kunta min al-kādhibīn* — *are you of the liars.* He is making the question *as serious as it should be*, but he is also keeping it *open*. He is not yet declaring; he is *asking*.

This is the prophetic refusal to skip a step. The refusal to take *the worst possible reading of someone* and treat it as confirmed before investigation.

And here's what's quietly stunning. The Quran is showing us this in a *king's* response to a *bird*. To a subordinate. To a creature whose word carries less institutional weight than the king's. If the king owes the bird this care of language — *aṣadaqta am kunta min al-kādhibīn*, asked as a question, framed with this much grammatical mercy — then how much do *we* owe each other, who are equals?

Here's the universal struggle. We all want to be seen as a *whole person* by others. We all want the people who love us to read our worst moments charitably — to ask *was this an act, or is this who you are?* — and to leave the answer open until evidence accumulates.

But we are not consistently willing to extend the same to others. We are quick. We are economical. We file people away into categories — *liar*, *fake*, *toxic*, *narcissist*, *fanatic*, *kufr*, *bidʿah* — and we close the file.

Sulaymān keeps the file open.

Even with a bird who was late. Even when he could have closed it instantly.

The Quran is teaching us, in a passing detail of a hoopoe's defense, that **the grammar of how we ask the question determines the morality of how we will receive the answer.**

If you ask *did he lie?* you are looking for confirmation. If you ask *is he of the liars?* you are looking for *pattern* — and patterns require *more than one data point*, which means you have already implicitly committed yourself to looking further before you decide.

[PAUSE]

**The practical application:** in the next conflict you have — with a child, a spouse, a friend, a parent — notice the silent question your mind is asking. Is it *did they hurt me?* Or is it *are they the kind of person who hurts me?* If it is the second, you have already overshot the evidence. Pull back to the first. Examine the act before you reorder the person.

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Let me show you something now that we couldn't see until the linguistic and thematic work was done.

Look at the architecture of the seven words:

**قَالَ** — He said. The frame.

**سَنَنظُرُ** — We shall examine. The *gap* — the deliberate creation of time and method.

**أَصَدَقْتَ** — Did you speak truth. The first possibility — *act-level*, narrow, specific to this report.

**أَمْ** — Or. The pivot.

**كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰذِبِينَ** — Or are you of the liars. The second possibility — *being-level*, broad, about category.

The structure is a perfect funnel of inquiry. First, *create time*. Then, *narrow to the act*. Then, *consider the pattern*. Each step earned. Each step held open until evidence arrives.

This is the structure of justice. Time. Then act. Then character. Never reversed. Never collapsed.

[PAUSE]

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: a prefix that defers, a verb that examines, an asymmetry that protects character from being destroyed by a single act.

From the human side: the cruelty of the zero-second gap, and the violence of reducing a person to an incident.

They are the same teaching. **Justice is a discipline of *time* and *categorization*. Time, so that examination can happen. Categorization, so that a single act is not mistaken for an identity.**

And the proof is in the architecture of the seven Arabic words themselves.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life do I close the gap between input and verdict too quickly? Whose face appears when I ask that?

2. Is there someone I have reduced — collapsed *an act they did* into *the kind of person they are* — without the further examination that would justify such a verdict?

3. Has anyone done that to *me*? What does it feel like? And what does that feeling tell me about what I owe others?

4. When I receive explosive information — about a public figure, a community, a family member — what is my equivalent of *sananẓuru*? Do I have one at all?

5. If a prophet-king with armies could afford to delay a verdict on a small bird, what does my refusal to delay say about how I see my own time and power?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> **Justice begins with one letter — the *sīn* of delay — and ends with one refusal: the refusal to mistake what someone *did* for who someone *is*.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will not leave this reflection a different person if all that happened is your mind enjoyed the grammar. The shift has to be smaller, and harder.

The invitation is this: for one week, every time you feel the rush of a verdict forming about someone — a friend, a stranger online, a member of your family, a leader, a sect — pause and silently say two Arabic words to yourself. *Sananẓuru.* We shall examine.

Not a debate. Not a denial. Just — *examination is owed before judgment.*

If a prophet owed it to a bird, you owe it to your spouse. To your child. To the brother in the masjid you don't quite trust. To the stranger whose tweet you almost shared.

The *sīn* is small. It is one letter. It does not feel like much.

But it is the entire difference between a community of justice and a community of mobs.

### Brief Duʿāʾ

> O Allah — slow my tongue.
> Place between my hearing and my verdict the small letter of restraint that Your prophet placed between the bird and his judgment.
> And when I am the one being examined — when others ask *did I speak truth, or am I of the liars* — let them, too, ask the question with mercy, and let You find me, when I answer, among those whose word can be trusted.
>
> Āmīn.
