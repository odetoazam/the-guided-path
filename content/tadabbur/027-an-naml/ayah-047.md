# The Bird of Bad Omen: When You Blame the Cure for the Disease

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first to the Arabic.

> **قَالُوا۟ ٱطَّيَّرْنَا بِكَ وَبِمَن مَّعَكَ ۚ قَالَ طَـٰٓئِرُكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ۖ بَلْ أَنتُمْ قَوْمٌ تُفْتَنُونَ**

*Qālū iṭṭayyarnā bika wa biman maʿak. Qāla ṭāʾirukum ʿinda-llāh, bal antum qawmun tuftanūn.*

"They said, 'We see an evil omen in you and those with you.'
He said, 'Your omen is with Allah. Rather, you are a people being tested.'"

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the world

Picture the valley of Thamud. A people of staggering achievement. The Quran tells us elsewhere that they carved their homes out of mountains — not built up against the rock, but cut *into* the rock, room by room, hall by hall. They were the architects of a civilization confident enough to make stone obey them.

Into this confident, prospering people walks a man they have known since childhood. One of their own. Salih, peace be upon him. And he tells them something that sounds, to ears used to power, like an insult: *Worship Allah. There is no other god for you.*

The ayah before this one — verse 46 — captures Salih in a moment of remarkable tenderness. They are rushing toward evil, and he says to them: *لَوْلَا تَسْتَغْفِرُونَ اللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ* — "Why don't you seek forgiveness from Allah, so that perhaps you might be shown mercy?"

He is not threatening them. He is pleading with them. He is the doctor at the bedside of a patient who keeps slapping his hand away.

And this ayah — verse 47 — is their response.

They don't argue with his theology. They don't dispute his evidence. They don't engage his call at all.

They say: *You are bad luck. You and the people with you. Things have gone wrong since you started talking like this. We catch a bad omen from your face.*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we begin. There is something stunning about how Allah records this exchange. The accusation they level — *iṭṭayyarnā bika* — "we take a bird-omen from you" — is one of the strangest words in the Quran. It is a verb built on the word for *bird*. We are going to spend real time with why.

And Salih's response is shorter than their accusation. Just five words in Arabic. But those five words diagnose a disease that we are still suffering from, fourteen centuries later — possibly more than they were.

What did Salih see in them that they could not see in themselves?

And what does this ayah want *us* to see in *ourselves*?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The bird-verb — *iṭṭayyarnā*

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work.

In Arabic, you can take a noun — any noun — and through a particular grammatical pattern, you can turn it into a verb meaning "to take that thing as a sign, to derive a meaning from it." It is a way of saying *I read this thing as ominous.*

Here is the everyday version: imagine a culture where black cats are bad luck. The English language doesn't have a single verb for "I-took-the-black-cat-as-an-omen-and-now-I'm-afraid." We have to use a whole sentence. But Arabic *can* do it in one verb.

Now hold that idea, and look at what they say to Salih:

> **ٱطَّيَّرْنَا بِكَ**
> *iṭṭayyarnā bika*

The root is **ط-ي-ر** — *ṭ-y-r* — which is simply the root for *bird*, *to fly*. From this root comes *ṭāʾir* (a bird) and *ṭayr* (birds collectively).

Why bird?

Because in pre-Islamic Arabia, one of the most common ways of telling the future was to watch birds. You would startle a flock. If they flew to the right, it was a good sign. If they flew to the left, it was a bad sign. The direction of the bird's flight became your guide. Decisions were postponed, journeys cancelled, marriages broken — based on a bird.

The word for this practice is *tatayyur* — *bird-reading.* It became so embedded in the Arab psyche that the verb generalized. You no longer needed an actual bird. *Tatayyara bi-fulān* came to mean *"to take so-and-so as a bad omen,"* whether or not any bird was involved.

So when Thamud says *iṭṭayyarnā bika*, the literal sense is: **"We have bird-omened ourselves through you."**

Notice something extraordinary about that construction.

Stay with me here. The grammatical structure is reflexive — *we bird-omened ourselves*. It isn't that *you, Salih, are causing us bad luck.* It is that *we have, by encountering you, derived a bad omen.* The omen is something *they* are generating from him. He is the surface they are reading. They are the readers.

And Allah is preserving this in the language. Because watch what Salih is about to do.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The very next word, **bika** — "through you" — is doing something the English translation can't carry. *Bi-* in Arabic often marks *means* or *instrument*. He is the instrument they are using to do the omening. Not the cause. The pretext.

If your child fails the math test because they didn't study, and they say "I failed because my pencil was bad" — the pencil didn't cause the failure. The pencil was the *means* by which they expressed the blame. The *bi-* preposition is doing exactly that work here.

They are turning Salih into a pencil to write their own failure onto.

And what is the omen they are reading? The classical commentators tell us Thamud was suffering — droughts, hardships, perhaps the beginning of the consequences Allah had warned them about. They felt their world contracting. And rather than ask *what have we done?*, they asked *who can we blame?*

The man calling them to repentance becomes the man blamed for the suffering that repentance would have healed.

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is one word. *Iṭṭayyarnā*. A verb that names a disease — the disease of reading our own decay in the face of the doctor who came to cure us.

### Element Two: The architecture of the reply — *ṭāʾirukum ʿinda-llāh*

Now look at Salih's response.

> **قَالَ طَـٰٓئِرُكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ**
> *Qāla ṭāʾirukum ʿinda-llāh*
> "He said: Your omen is with Allah."

Notice something. He doesn't deny that something is happening to them. He doesn't say *there is no bad luck.* He doesn't say *nothing is going wrong.* He doesn't defend himself.

He takes their word — *ṭāʾir*, the bird-omen — and he picks it up and gently turns it around.

Let's look at three things in this five-word reply.

**First: he keeps their word.**

He doesn't argue about whether *ṭāʾir* exists. He says *ṭāʾirukum* — "*your* ṭāʾir." Whatever it is you're calling an omen, whatever it is that's happening to you — *it.* The thing itself. The reality you're pointing at.

He's not arguing with their experience of suffering. He's relocating its address.

**Second: the word *ʿinda* — "with."**

In Arabic, *ʿinda* doesn't just mean "with" in the sense of standing-next-to. It carries a meaning closer to *in the keeping of*, *in the custody of*, *registered with*. When the Quran says certain knowledge is *ʿinda Allāh*, it means *only Allah holds the file on this.*

So *ṭāʾirukum ʿinda-llāh* doesn't mean *your bad luck is somewhere near Allah.* It means: **your omen is on file with Allah. The ledger of what happens to you — who writes it? Allah does. The cause is not me. The cause is not the people with me. The cause is in His hand, by His decree, registered in His knowledge.**

Read what they said and what he said back to back. They said: *we caught a bird from you.* He said: *your bird is filed with Allah.*

In one short phrase, he has moved the entire conversation from the horizontal plane — where one human being can curse another, where one face can carry another's misfortune — up onto the vertical plane, where everything that happens to you is in a ledger held by the One who made you.

[PAUSE]

**Third: the absence.**

This is where I want you to slow down, because this is the deepest thing in the ayah and the easiest to miss.

Look at what Salih does *not* say.

He does not say *the bird-omen does not exist*. He does not say *Allah is not sending you anything.* He does not say *nothing is wrong.* He does not offer them comfort. He does not soften it.

He simply tells them: whatever is happening to you, it is happening *from above*, not from beside. You are not being attacked by my face. You are being addressed by your Lord.

And then he does something I find almost unbearable in its precision.

He uses the word *bal* — **بَلْ** — which is one of the strongest correctives in Arabic. It means *rather*, *no — instead*. It is the word you use to interrupt and replace. To stop one sentence and start a truer one.

> **بَلْ أَنتُمْ قَوْمٌ تُفْتَنُونَ**
> *bal antum qawmun tuftanūn.*
> "Rather, you are a people being tested."

[PAUSE]

He doesn't just relocate the omen. He renames it.

What you are calling *bad luck from me* — that is not what is happening to you. What is actually happening to you is **a test.**

---

### Element Three: The passive that does the heaviest work — *tuftanūn*

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we look at this one.

In Arabic, a verb in the passive voice — what we call *al-majhūl*, "the unknown" — is named that way for a reason. The agent, the doer, is hidden. The grammar removes the subject from view. When Arabic wants to say *something is being done to you, and we are deliberately not naming who is doing it*, it uses this form.

And often — almost always in the Quran — when the passive is used about cosmic things happening to humans, the unnamed agent is Allah. The grammar veils Him out of reverence, even as it makes His action obvious to anyone who is listening.

Now look at the word.

> **تُفْتَنُونَ**
> *tuftanūn*
> "you are being tested" — but in the passive.

The root is **ف-ت-ن** — *f-t-n*. *Fitnah*. This is one of the densest roots in the entire Quran.

Originally — and this is where it gets staggering — *fitnah* is what a goldsmith does. He puts gold into fire. The fire doesn't *create* the gold. The fire *reveals* what the metal already is. If it is pure, it survives the fire and emerges shining. If it is alloyed with impurities, the fire burns the impurities away — or, if the impurities are dominant, the whole thing is consumed.

*Fitnah* is the fire that reveals.

So when Salih says *tuftanūn*, he is saying: **you are being put through the fire so that the truth of what you actually are can come out.**

Now place this against what they just said.

They said: *bad things are happening to us, and it's your fault.*

He says: *bad things are happening to you because you are gold being assayed, and the fire is exposing the metal underneath.*

[PAUSE]

The passive form does something else, too. *Tuftanūn* — you are *being* tested. You are not the doers here. You are the objects. You imagined yourselves as the powerful ones, the carvers of mountains, the readers of birds, the makers of meaning. But in this moment, in this fire — you are being made-meaning-of. You are being read. You are not the one with the pencil; you are the page.

And who is reading you?

The grammar doesn't say. The grammar veils. But everyone in earshot of Salih's voice knows.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridging the linguistic work

Look at what we have.

They built a sentence around *bird-omen* — a horizontal force, traveling from one human to another. He answered with a sentence built around *trust* — *with Allah* — a vertical line going up. And then he replaced their diagnosis with the real one: not curse, but test. Not bad luck, but fire. Not from me, but from above.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the structure of human suffering itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why we blame the messenger

I want to start somewhere that has nothing to do with prophets.

A man comes home from work. His marriage is failing. His finances are tight. His health is slipping. He has known for months that his life is heading somewhere bad. He has known what he needs to change. He has known there is a conversation he needs to have, a habit he needs to break, a friend he needs to call.

His wife sits him down that night and says, gently, *I think we need to talk about how things are going.*

And he — exhausted, defensive, terrified — looks at her and says: **"Why is it that every time you bring something up, my week gets worse?"**

He has just done *iṭṭayyarnā bika.*

He has taken the person trying to heal him and turned her into the cause of his disease.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the oldest and most reliable patterns in the human heart. We do not blame the truth-teller because the truth-teller is wrong. We blame the truth-teller because the truth is unbearable, and the truth-teller is *available.*

Allah is too high to attack. Reality is too vast to attack. Our own decisions are too painful to confront. But the person holding up the mirror — they are right here. They have a face. They have a name. We can blame them by name.

Thamud could not blame Allah for the drought. So they blamed Salih for the drought.

The husband could not blame himself for the failing marriage. So he blamed his wife for bringing it up.

The student who failed the exam could not blame his procrastination. So he blames the teacher who "always asks unfair questions."

The patient who refused the treatment could not blame his refusal. So he blames the doctor who "made him feel worse just by talking."

The civilization that refuses to repent could not blame its refusal. So it blames the prophet who called it to repent.

This is the disease *iṭṭayyarnā bika* names. And the disease has a specific shape: **we read our suffering in the face of the one trying to save us, instead of in the mirror.**

Salih's response is the cure. Not anger. Not defense. Not a counter-attack. Just six small words, in two movements.

First movement: *ṭāʾirukum ʿinda-llāh.* **Look up.** Whatever is happening to you, the file is with Allah. Stop looking sideways for someone to blame. Look up.

Second movement: *bal antum qawmun tuftanūn.* **Rename it.** What you are calling bad luck is actually a test. The fire is not your enemy. The fire is your assayer. The pain you are feeling is not random and it is not from me. It is a question being asked of you. The question is *what are you made of?*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

When someone confronts me with something I do not want to hear — a friend who tells me I have been distant, a spouse who tells me I have been harsh, a teacher who tells me my work is shallow, a parent who tells me I have drifted — there is a moment, a real moment, where I can do one of two things.

I can say *iṭṭayyarnā bika* — *I felt bad ever since you brought this up; you are why I feel this way.* And I can drive them out of my life, and feel relieved, and stay sick.

Or I can say *ṭāʾirī ʿinda-llāh* — *whatever I am feeling is on file with Allah; this person didn't cause this; this person is the mirror Allah is using.* And I can let them stay. And let the mirror do its work. And maybe, by the mercy of Allah, walk out of that conversation a slightly truer version of myself.

The choice between those two responses is the choice between Thamud and Salih's family.

### Theme Two: The fire as gift

There is a second theme buried in this ayah that I think is the more important one, because it speaks not to how we treat truth-tellers but to how we read our own pain.

Salih did not deny that Thamud was suffering. That is the first thing to notice. He did not say *nothing is happening to you; you are imagining it.* He did not say *you are not really in pain.*

He said *what is happening to you is real, but you are misreading it.*

Stay with this for a moment.

How many of us, in the middle of a hard season — illness, financial fear, broken relationship, spiritual dryness — go looking for the cause? *Why is this happening? What did I do? Who did this to me?*

We look at our enemies. We look at the people who let us down. We look at our own past selves. We look at coincidence. We look at the alignment of the world.

We look horizontally.

The Quran here is teaching a different posture. *Ṭāʾirukum ʿinda-llāh* — your circumstance is on file with Allah. He has not forgotten you. He is not absent from this. This thing that is happening to you — He registered it. He authored it. It did not slip past Him.

Now: that could be terrifying. *Allah did this to me?*

But notice the next word. *Bal antum qawmun tuftanūn* — *no — rather — you are being assayed.*

If Allah wanted to destroy Thamud, He would not have *tested* them. He would have ended them. The fact that Allah is testing you means Allah is **not done with you.** It means He has registered you as gold worth assaying. The fire is not the end of the relationship. The fire is the relationship.

The Prophet ﷺ said: *"Whomever Allah wills good for, He afflicts them."* Not because affliction is good in itself. Because affliction is the language Allah uses with the gold He intends to refine.

[PAUSE]

The tragedy of Thamud is not that they suffered. The tragedy of Thamud is that they suffered *and refused to read the suffering as a message from Allah.*

They had a chance — every drought, every hardship, every uncomfortable feeling — to look up and ask *what is being asked of me?* Instead they looked sideways and said *whose fault is this?*

Every hardship in a believer's life is a moment where the same choice is being offered.

You can be Thamud. You can keep your eyes level with the horizon and find someone to blame. The boss. The spouse. The economy. The community. The shaykh. The neighbor. The prophet, even.

Or you can be Salih's family. You can let the hardship lift your gaze.

There is a moment in everyone's suffering where the horizon stops giving answers, and you have to look up. The whole question of your life is *how long does it take you* to look up?

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

The next hard thing that happens — the next loss, the next disappointment, the next failure — there will be a few minutes, maybe a few hours, where my mind will be hunting for someone to blame.

The discipline this ayah is teaching is: catch yourself in the hunt. And say, quietly, *ṭāʾirī ʿinda-llāh.* *My situation is on file with Allah.* And then: *bal anā mimman yuftanūn* — *I am one of those being assayed.*

And from there, the question stops being *who did this to me?* and starts being *what is Allah revealing about me through this?*

That single rotation — from horizontal blame to vertical reception — is what the believer's heart was built to do.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Look at the structure of this ayah now that we have walked through it.

Their sentence is built on a verb of doing — *iṭṭayyarnā* — *we bird-omened.* They are the doers. They are the agents. They have the pencils. The world is a thing they read.

His sentence is built on a passive — *tuftanūn* — *you are being tested.* They are no longer the agents. They are the objects. They are being read.

In one short reply, Salih has flipped the entire frame. They walked in thinking they were the readers of bird-omens. He walks them out by showing them they are the pages being read.

And in the middle of his reply, balanced like a hinge, is *ʿinda-llāh* — *with Allah.* The pivot point. The vertical line. The reminder that everything horizontal — every blame, every omen, every accusation — is settled at a desk far above the desks where humans accuse each other.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar shows us a sick verb — *iṭṭayyarnā* — that humans use to write their own decay onto the faces of others. And then it shows us a divine passive — *tuftanūn* — that takes the pencil out of their hands and reminds them they are being written by Allah, not writing the world.

The themes show us a sick posture — looking sideways for someone to blame — and a healthy posture — looking up and asking what is being asked of me.

The two are the same. To break the verb *iṭṭayyarnā* in your own life is to learn to look up. To learn to look up is to stop conjugating *iṭṭayyarnā.*

[PAUSE]

### Questions to carry

1. Think of the last hard season of your life. Who did you blame? And was that blame the truest reading of what happened, or the easiest one?

2. Is there a person in your life right now whose role is to be a mirror you keep refusing to look into? What would it cost you to look?

3. When you hear advice you don't want to hear, what is the first move your heart makes? Toward the message, or away from the messenger?

4. If you were to take *ṭāʾirī ʿinda-llāh* as a phrase you whispered to yourself the next time something went wrong, what specific situation would you be applying it to?

5. What if the fire you are currently in is not a punishment but an assay? What would change about how you stood in it?

### One-sentence distillation

When you read your suffering in the face of the one trying to heal you, you have stopped being assayed and started being consumed.

### The closing invitation

The next time blame rises in your chest — toward a spouse, a friend, a leader, a community, a teacher, a parent — pause. Let the sentence form, and then catch it before it becomes a verb. Ask: *is this person actually the cause, or are they the mirror?*

And then look up. Even for a second. Even if you don't have words. Just up.

That single rotation is the difference between a heart that hardens and a heart that softens. Between Thamud and Salih's family. Between gold consumed and gold purified.

### Du'a

Allahumma, when hardship comes, lift our eyes from the horizon to You. Keep us from writing our pain onto the faces of those You have sent to heal us. And when the fire comes, let it find in us metal worth refining, not impurities worth burning away. Make us of those whose ṭāʾir, registered with You, is mercy.

Āmīn.
