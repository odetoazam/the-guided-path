# And We Saved Those Who Believed and Used to Be Conscious

## Opening the Ayah

**Arabic:**
وَأَنجَيْنَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَكَانُوا۟ يَتَّقُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa anjaynā alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn.*

**Translation:**
*And We saved those who believed and used to be conscious [of Allah].*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before This Verse

Before we step into these six words, we have to feel the silence around them.

The Thamud were not a small tribe. They were the architects of the mountains. The Quran describes them as people who **carved homes from cliffs** — engineers who looked at a stone face and saw a city. They had wealth. They had gardens. They had what every civilization wants: the sense that they had built something that would outlast them.

Salih, peace be upon him, had been sent to them — one of their own. He had stood among them and said: *worship the One.* He had given them a sign so unmistakable that even disbelief should have trembled — a she-camel that drank on alternate days, a living miracle walking among their herds.

They killed her.

And even after that — even after the prophet warned them that a punishment was coming in three days — nine men sat together in the dark and plotted. Not to repent. To finish the job. To kill Salih and his family in the night, and then to deny it the next morning. The ayahs just before this one describe their scheme. And then they describe Allah's response — almost casually — that He had a scheme of their scheme that they did not perceive.

The verse before this one shows you their houses. **"So these are their houses — desolate — for the wrong they did."** [PAUSE] An empty city. Carved stones standing in the wind. No one inside.

And then comes our verse. Just six words. A quiet clearing in the middle of the rubble.

*And We saved those who believed and used to be conscious of Allah.*

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us. In a surah filled with thunder — falling cities, kings and jinn, the trumpet of the resurrection, mountains moving like clouds — Allah chooses **six words** to describe the rescue of an entire community of believers.

Six words. No names. No numbers. No description of *how* they were saved or *where* they went afterward.

And inside those six words, Allah uses a verb construction that, if you are not looking carefully, will pass you by. Two different tenses. One for belief. A completely different one for taqwa.

Why?

Why did Allah, who could have said this rescue in any way, choose this exact grammatical shape? And why, in the middle of a story about destruction, did He pause to write this verse at all?

Stay with me. The answer changes how you understand what it means to be *saved*.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb that Begins Everything — *Anjaynā*

Before we go anywhere, let's notice how the verse opens.

It does not open with the people. It opens with the **verb**. *Anjaynā.* "**We rescued.**"

In Arabic, when you want to emphasize the *doer* of an action, you front the subject. When you want to emphasize the *action itself*, you front the verb. Allah does not say *alladhīna āmanū anjaynāhum* — "those who believed, We saved them." He says *anjaynā alladhīna āmanū* — "**We saved** those who believed."

The rescue is named first. Before we even know who was rescued, we are made to feel the act of rescue.

Now, the root here is *n-j-w* (ن-ج-و). In classical Arabic, this root carries a very specific picture. *Najā* originally meant: **to come out of something onto high ground.** Lexicographers describe it as the ground that rises above floodwater — the place that does not drown when everything around it drowns. The hill in the deluge.

Let that sit for a moment.

This is the same root used when Allah saved Nuh from the flood. The same root used when He saved Musa from Pharaoh. The same root used for the believer who is pulled out from under the wave of his community's destruction.

It is not a word that means simply "to escape." It is a word that means **to be raised above what is sinking.**

Here is what is remarkable: the form Allah uses — *anjaynā* — is the **causative** form (Form IV in Arabic morphology). It does not mean "they survived." It means "**We caused them to come out onto high ground.**" The believers did not climb. They were lifted.

Notice something. The Thamud carved their houses **into mountains** to be high. To be above. To be safe in the cliffs. The Quran is almost mocking this in surah after surah — *they took mountains as homes, feeling secure.* And what does Allah do? He brings their carved mountains down. And He raises the believers up. The very thing the disbelievers tried to manufacture for themselves — elevation, safety, height — Allah grants freely to the ones who trusted Him.

You cannot carve your way to *najāh*. You can only be *raised* to it.

[PAUSE]

What this single word reveals — before we even reach the rest of the ayah — is that salvation in the Quran is never something you achieve. It is something **done to you**. The active agent is always Allah. The believer's role is to be the kind of soul that Allah chooses to raise.

### Element Two: Two Tenses — The Hidden Grammar of Salvation

Now we come to the heart of this ayah. And to see it, you need a piece of Arabic grammar that will quietly change how you read dozens of other verses.

In Arabic, there are two main verb tenses:

- The **perfect** tense (*al-māḍī*) — a completed action. Translates roughly as "did" or "did once."
- The **imperfect** tense (*al-muḍāriʿ*) — an ongoing or repeated action. Translates as "does" or "is doing."

But Arabic has something English does not. It has a **compound construction**: the verb *kāna* ("was") followed by an imperfect verb. *Kāna yafʿalu.* Literally: "He was doing." But it does not mean a one-time past action. It means: "**He used to do**" — habitually, repeatedly, as a settled state.

Think of the difference in English between *"He prayed yesterday"* and *"He used to pray every day."* The first is a fact. The second is a **pattern**. The first describes an action. The second describes a person.

That distinction is everything for this ayah.

Look at how Allah describes the saved:

*Alladhīna **āmanū** — wa **kānū yattaqūn**.*

"Those who **believed** — and **used to be** conscious of Allah."

[PAUSE]

Belief is in the simple perfect: *āmanū.* They believed. It happened. It is recorded. One stamp on the soul that says: *yes, I accepted this.*

But *taqwa* is in the compound habitual: *kānū yattaqūn.* They were, ongoingly, continuously, repeatedly, **being conscious of Allah.**

Allah did not say *wa ittaqaw* — "and they feared Allah" (perfect). He said *wa kānū yattaqūn* — "and **they used to** fear Allah" (habitual).

Why this asymmetry?

Here is what is remarkable. Belief is, in one sense, a **moment**. There is a moment your heart says yes. A moment a convert testifies. A moment a doubt breaks and clarity floods in. *Iman* enters as a turning point. The Quran often grammatically marks it as a completed act — *āmanū* — because there is a "before" and an "after."

But taqwa is not a moment. **Taqwa is a texture.** It is the quality of how you live the seconds between the moments. It is the way you walk through your kitchen, the way you answer your phone, the way you close your eyes at night. You cannot have taqwa once. You can only have taqwa as a habit, as a quietly running awareness underneath everything else you do.

And that is exactly the grammatical shape Allah chooses. *Kānū yattaqūn* — they were *continuously* doing this, before the rescue, every day, for years, without anyone watching, when there was no flood coming, when there was no warning, when no punishment was on the horizon.

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again with this in your ear:

*We raised onto high ground those who **had said yes** — and who **used to live** as if Allah was watching.*

The saved are not described by a single dramatic deed. They are described by the **quiet, repeated, invisible posture of their daily life.**

Let's sit with this for a moment, because this is the doorway to the whole ayah.

When destruction came to Thamud, it came suddenly. The plotters did not get a second chance. The disbelievers did not get a final exam where they could perform piety on demand. The punishment arrived, and what saved a person was **what they had already been doing** when nobody was paying attention.

The believers were not lifted because, in that moment, they prayed harder. They were lifted because, for years before that moment, they **used to** pray. *Kānū yattaqūn.* Used to. The grammar of an unbroken habit.

This is one of the most quietly terrifying — and quietly hopeful — sentences in the Quran. **Terrifying** because it tells you: the rescue, when it comes, will reach for who you *already are*, not who you will pretend to be in the emergency. **Hopeful** because it tells you: every small act of taqwa you have done in private, every time you turned your eyes from what you should not have looked at, every time you whispered *istighfar* in your car — Allah is building a record of *kānū yattaqūn.* He is composing the grammatical shape of your rescue.

### Element Three: What Is Missing — The Object of *Yattaqūn*

Now I want you to notice something that is **not** in this verse.

The verb *yattaqūn* is from the root *w-q-y*, which means **to shield, to guard, to protect oneself from**. In its base meaning, taqwa is shielding. You shield yourself **from** something.

In Arabic, you would normally expect this verb to take an object. *Yattaqūna mādhā?* "They shield themselves from **what**?"

But Allah does not name the object. He does not say *kānū yattaqūn al-nār* — "they used to shield themselves from the Fire." He does not say *kānū yattaqūn Allāh* — "they used to shield themselves regarding Allah." He just says *kānū yattaqūn.* They used to **be conscious**. They used to **guard themselves**. Period.

When the Quran withholds the object of a verb, classical scholars say something important: the **unspecified is the universal.** When Allah leaves the object out, He is not being vague. He is being **comprehensive.** The omission tells you: they guarded themselves from **everything that needed guarding from**. They guarded themselves from sin. From heedlessness. From the eyes of others. From their own nafs. From what was lawful that might pull them toward what was not. From whatever a moment required.

If Allah had specified the object, He would have narrowed taqwa. By leaving it open, He has shown you the **architecture of the muttaqī's heart**: a soul on watch, in every direction, all the time.

[PAUSE]

And here is the second absence — and it is even quieter. Notice that this ayah, which is the **rescue verse**, does not name a single one of the rescued.

Not one name. Not Salih's wife. Not a companion. Not a number — "a hundred believers." Not a place — "to such-and-such valley." Just *alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn.* The ones who believed and used to be conscious.

Why?

Because Allah is not memorializing a historical group. He is writing a **standing offer**. Anyone who fills these conditions, in any age, becomes a member of the *alladhīna* in this verse. The anonymity is the invitation. The reason your name is not here is that the slot is still open. *Alladhīna* is not a closed list — it is a category.

If Allah had said "and We saved Yusuf ibn Salih and Maryam bint Hud and the forty-three believers of the southern valley" — beautiful. Specific. Historical. But you would have read it and thought: that was them. Their rescue. Their story.

Instead He said: *alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn.* And every time a believer reads this verse, the slot is asking: **are you in it?**

### Bridging to the Themes

So now look at what these six words have shown us. The verb of rescue is fronted — because the rescue is what the verse is *about*, not the rescued. The root *n-j-w* means lifting onto high ground — salvation is not escape, it is elevation. *Anjaynā* is causative — Allah lifts; we do not climb. Belief is in the perfect — a moment. Taqwa is in the habitual — a texture. And the object of taqwa is withheld — because the muttaqī guards in every direction.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how Allah saves people, and what we are being trained to become.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Saved are Those Who Were Already Living the Salvation

Imagine, for a moment, two people in the same office.

Both are decent. Both fast in Ramadan. Both go to jumu'ah. From the outside, you cannot tell them apart.

But here is the difference. One of them, before he opens his laptop in the morning, says *bismillāh* under his breath. The other does not. One of them, when his eyes catch something on the screen he should not look at, looks away — not dramatically, not performatively, just quietly, the way you turn your face from a draft. The other lingers a second longer. One of them, when a coworker offers a small lie that would be convenient, hesitates — not enough for anyone else to notice — and then declines. The other does not hesitate.

Neither has done anything visible. Neither would be remembered in a story. There is no biography being written about either of them.

But over ten years, something has been happening that no human eye can see. One of them has been quietly **shielding** himself, second after second, in a thousand invisible turns. The other has not.

If a crisis came tomorrow — a real one, a financial collapse, a moral pressure, a moment where principle costs something — which one would hold?

[PAUSE]

We assume crises *create* character. They do not. **Crises reveal it.** What you do in the emergency is just what you have already been practicing in private — performed under stress.

This is what *wa kānū yattaqūn* is telling us. The believers of Thamud were not saved because they passed a final test of bravery. They were saved because, for years, they **used to** turn their faces away from what their society normalized. They **used to** say no when no one was watching. They **used to** worry quietly about their own hearts. By the time the punishment came, they had already become the kind of people who would naturally be on the side of the rescued. The rescue did not transform them. It **gathered them as they already were.**

There is a modern psychological idea — Aristotle had a version of it, and contemporary virtue ethicists keep returning to it — that **character is not a moment, it is a pattern of small choices that gradually shape what your nervous system finds easy.** You do not become honest by deciding to be honest. You become honest by telling small truths in low-stakes situations, hundreds of times, until lying feels foreign in your mouth.

The Quran got there fourteen hundred years earlier — and put it in six words. *Kānū yattaqūn.* They **used to.** The grammar of a soul that has been quietly tuning itself for so long that, when the wind comes, it does not bend.

Now turn this on yourself. Not in self-recrimination — in honest inventory. What is your *kānū yattaqūn*? What is the small, invisible thing you do every day that is shaping you into either the rescued or the unrescued? It does not have to be dramatic. It is almost never dramatic. It is the moment you put your phone down before bed and reach for the Quran instead. It is the *bismillah* before you eat. It is the prayer you make for your enemy under your breath. It is the *istighfar* in the parking lot.

Nobody will see it. That is the point. Allah is keeping the record. He is writing the verb tense of your rescue.

**One practical application before we move on:** pick **one** small act of taqwa. One. The smallest one you can name. And do it daily, in private, for the next thirty days — not because it is heroic, but because it is the smallest unit of *kānū yattaqūn* you can begin to build. Allah is not waiting for your masterpiece. He is recording your habit.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Quiet Mercy in a Verse of Thunder

The second theme is harder to see, and it is one of the most tender movements in the Quran.

Look at the surrounding ayahs. They are loud. Plotting. Counter-plotting. Houses destroyed. Stones empty. The narrative voice is almost forensic — *here are their houses, look at them, count them, see what they did.*

And then, right in the middle of this, the verse changes register completely. *Wa anjaynā alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn.* Six words. Soft. Almost whispered.

There is no description of the believers' reaction. No "and they were grateful." No "and they praised their Lord." No description of where they went, what they did next, how many they were. Allah simply records that they were lifted out.

Why so quietly?

Because **rescue does not need to be loud.** Destruction announces itself; rescue can be silent. The disbelievers' end gets ayahs of imagery. The believers' rescue gets a sentence. And there is a mercy in that brevity.

Here is what I mean. Imagine you are one of the saved. You are not a celebrity. Your name is not Salih. You are an ordinary believer in Thamud, a craftsman, a mother, a young man. You believed quietly. You guarded yourself quietly. And on the day of destruction, you were lifted out — *quietly.* No spotlight. No record of your name. Just a sentence in the Book of Allah that says: *We saved those who used to be conscious.*

You are *in* that sentence. And so is every other anonymous believer of every other community. Generations of muttaqūn, none of them named, all of them held inside one pronoun — *alladhīna.*

There is something deeply consoling about this. Most of us will live and die without anyone writing our story. We will not have a chapter in any history book. Our children will forget half of what we did. Our names will not survive three generations on average.

But Allah, in His mercy, did not require that you be named to be saved. He required only that you be **the kind of person this sentence describes.** He widened the door so that anonymity is not a barrier.

[PAUSE]

And there is another mercy hidden here. Notice that Allah does not list the *level* of their iman. He does not say "those whose iman was strong." He does not say "those who were among the great." He says *alladhīna āmanū wa kānū yattaqūn.* Those who believed. Those who used to be conscious.

The bar is not perfection. The bar is **directionality**. Did you believe? Did you, on most days, in most moments, even imperfectly, try to be conscious of Allah? Then you are in the verse. Your failures along the way do not eject you from *alladhīna*. The construction *kānū yattaqūn* describes a habit, not a flawless record. Habits include relapses; they do not require their absence.

The mercy of this verse is that it has been written in a way that **most of the saved will not even know they qualified** until they are lifted out. They will not have felt special. They will not have felt holy. They will have just felt like ordinary believers stumbling through their days, asking forgiveness, trying again. And Allah, who sees what no one sees, will look at the pattern over years and write: *kānū yattaqūn.* And that will be enough.

**Practical application:** stop waiting to feel worthy. Stop waiting for some dramatic conversion of the self that will make you "really" pious. You are already in the pattern. Every small turn toward Allah is being recorded. Trust the grammar. *Kānū yattaqūn* describes you, too, on the day Allah writes it. Just keep being conscious.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Verse

Now look at the verse with everything we have seen.

*Wa anjaynā* — **the act of rescue, fronted.** Allah names what He does before He names who it is done for. The lifting comes first.

*Alladhīna* — **an open category.** Not a closed list. The slot is still accepting names.

*Āmanū* — **the perfect tense.** A moment. The yes.

*Wa kānū yattaqūn* — **the habitual past.** A texture. The pattern of years between the yeses.

And the object of *yattaqūn* — **withheld.** Comprehensive guarding. Every direction.

The whole architecture of the verse mirrors the architecture of a saved life: **one yes, sustained by ten thousand small, quiet, anonymous acts of consciousness — and then, when the wind comes, lifted.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discovery and the thematic discovery are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: Allah used a verb construction (*kānū yattaqūn*) that grammatically describes habit, not event. From the thematic side: salvation is gathered, not earned in a moment. From the linguistic side: He fronted the verb *anjaynā* and left the agent unspoken in the rescue — Allah is the only actor. From the thematic side: you do not save yourself by climbing; you are lifted because of what you have quietly been.

Both are saying: **the rescue is real, the conditions are simple, and the work is small and daily.**

### Questions to Carry

1. If Allah were to describe me with the construction *kāna yafʿalu* — "he used to do" — what habit would He name? What is the verb that, repeated over the last ten years of my life, has been silently writing my character?

2. Where in my life am I waiting for a dramatic transformation, when what Allah has actually asked for is a quiet, repeated, almost invisible shielding?

3. What is the smallest act of taqwa I can begin tonight — not to feel pious, but to begin building the tense *kānū yattaqūn* in my own life?

4. Am I performing my faith for the moment when someone might see it, or am I cultivating it for the moment when no one but Allah will?

5. When the surah leaves the rescued unnamed, am I willing to be one of those anonymous saved — content that Allah knows, even if no human ever will?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**On the day rescue comes, Allah reaches for who you already are — and "who you already are" is just the grammatical shape of the small, repeated, private acts of consciousness you have been quietly building all along.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to become someone else to be in this verse. You have to become **more consistently yourself, in the direction of Allah.**

The invitation of *wa kānū yattaqūn* is not to a grand spiritual project. It is to a habit so small that, when you start it, you will think it cannot possibly matter. The *bismillah* before the meal. The lowered gaze in the elevator. The *istighfar* under your breath at the red light. The two rakʿahs no one knows about.

These are not warmups for the real worship. **They are the real worship.** They are the grammatical material out of which Allah composes the sentence of your rescue.

Start one. Tonight. The smallest one. And do not stop.
