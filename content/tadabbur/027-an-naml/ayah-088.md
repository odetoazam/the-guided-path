# The Mountains That Pass Like Clouds

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 88

### وَتَرَى الْجِبَالَ تَحْسَبُهَا جَامِدَةً وَهِيَ تَمُرُّ مَرَّ السَّحَابِ ۚ صُنْعَ اللَّهِ الَّذِي أَتْقَنَ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ ۚ إِنَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَفْعَلُونَ

*Wa tara al-jibāla taḥsabuhā jāmidatan wa hiya tamurru marra al-saḥābi — ṣun'a Allāhi alladhī atqana kulla shay'in — innahu khabīrun bimā taf'alūn.*

**"And you see the mountains, thinking them solid, while they pass by like the passing of clouds — the making of Allah, who perfected every thing. Indeed, He is fully aware of all that you do."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world the listener must inhabit

Before we can hear what this ayah is saying, we have to remember what a mountain *was* to the people first hearing these words.

A mountain, to the seventh-century Arab — and to nearly every human being who has ever lived — was the very definition of *immovable*. It was the thing you used as a metaphor for what cannot be shaken. Tribes told their children: "as steady as Hira." Travelers oriented themselves by peaks that had stood there before their grandfathers' grandfathers. When you wanted to say something was reliable, unbreakable, eternal in a worldly sense, you compared it to a mountain. Mountains were what *the world stood on*.

And the Quran knows this. Earlier in this very surah, mountains are part of the catalog of stable things: pegs that hold the earth steady, foundations of permanence, witnesses that have outlasted empires.

So now imagine you are sitting in Makkah. The ayah before this has just described the trumpet — the *ṣūr* — being blown, and everyone in the heavens and the earth being seized with terror. The world is ending. And in the middle of that ending, the Quran turns to you and says: *and you see the mountains*…

You brace. You expect: *and the mountains will be crushed.* Or: *and the mountains will be uprooted.* Or: *and the mountains will become like carded wool.*

That is what other surahs say. That is the language of a world breaking.

But this ayah does something different. This ayah does not describe the mountains being destroyed. **It describes you finally seeing them as they actually are.**

### The hook

There are ayahs about the mountains in the Quran that describe their *destruction* on the Last Day — they are scattered, leveled, made into dust. This ayah is not one of them.

This ayah is doing something stranger and more disorienting. It is saying: the moment you think you are watching the world fall apart — that is the moment you finally see what the world has been doing all along.

Stay with that. Because what this verse asks of you is not just to imagine the end. It asks you to question whether you have ever, in your entire life, actually *seen* what is in front of you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to open three things in this ayah. Three small features that, once you see them, change everything else.

### Element One: تَحْسَبُهَا — "you reckon them"

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, there are several verbs for "thinking." There is *ẓanna*, which is to suspect or assume. There is *'alima*, which is to know with certainty. There is *raʾā* (the verb that opens this ayah), which is to see — physically, mentally, or in a vision.

And then there is *ḥasiba*, the verb at the heart of our phrase here.

*Ḥasiba* in classical Arabic is the verb of *reckoning*. It is the same root as *ḥisāb* — the calculation, the accounting, the tallying-up. When you *ḥasiba* something, you are not just thinking it. You are *computing* it. You are looking at the data your senses give you and arriving at a conclusion that feels like a sum.

In English, we have a faded version of this when we say: "I reckon she'll be home by now." It is a soft word for us. But in Arabic, the word carries the weight of *making a calculation*.

Now hold that.

#### Opening the word

The ayah does not say *taraw al-jibāla jāmidatan* — "you see the mountains as solid." It says: *tarā al-jibāla taḥsabuhā jāmidatan*.

"You see the mountains — *you reckon them* solid."

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah has, in a single word, separated the *seeing* from the *judgment*. The seeing is real. *Tarā* — you do see them. Your eyes are not lying. The photons hit your retina, the image forms. That part is true.

But the *judgment* you make about what you see — *that* is what the ayah is identifying as a calculation. As an inference. As something your mind is doing on top of the seeing.

Stay with me here, because this is the door the whole ayah opens through.

The Quran is not telling you that your eyes deceive you. Your eyes show you exactly what they show you. What the Quran is saying is: **the conclusion you draw — that what you are looking at is at rest — that conclusion is something you compute. And your computation is wrong.**

Think about this in your own life for a moment. The Earth, right now as you are listening, is moving through space at roughly 67,000 miles per hour. It is also rotating. The galaxy is spinning. Everything that you call "still" — your chair, your floor, your house — is, on every measurable scale beyond your immediate body, in motion that staggers the imagination.

You do not feel it. Your senses do not register it. You *reckon* yourself to be still.

You are not. You never have been.

The ayah is opening that gap. The gap between *what you see* and *what you reckon*.

#### Where this leaves us

This single verb — *taḥsabuhā* — is the ayah's quiet diagnosis of the human condition. **We are creatures who confuse our calculations for the world itself.** Allah does not tell us our eyes are broken. He tells us that our reckoning is one step removed from reality, and on the Day when reality is finally uncovered, we will discover that our reckoning was off.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: جَامِدَةً — "frozen"

#### Teaching the concept first

The word the listener might *expect* here is *thābitatan* — fixed, established, planted. Or *rāsiyatan* — anchored, rooted, the same word the Quran uses elsewhere for mountains as "pegs."

But Allah does not use either of those.

He uses *jāmidah*.

The root *j-m-d* in Arabic carries a very specific meaning: to *freeze*, to *congeal*, to become rigid through *the cessation of flow*. It is the word you would use for water that has frozen into ice. For oil that has solidified in cold. For blood that has clotted. For a tongue that has gone stiff and cannot speak.

*Jāmid* is not the word for "naturally solid." It is the word for *something that has stopped moving*.

#### Opening the word

Read the line again with this in your ear: *taḥsabuhā jāmidatan* — "you reckon them frozen." You reckon them as having stopped.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

Allah did not say the mountains are solid by nature, and on the Day they will move. Allah is saying: the mountains *have always been moving*. What you, the human observer, have been doing is *misreading flow as freeze*.

This is the language of someone who looks at a river that is moving so slowly the surface looks like glass — and mistakes glass for stone. The water was always flowing. Your eye just couldn't catch the flow.

And here is the deeper thing. The mountains in the Quran are repeatedly given the language of *praise*. They glorify Allah (17:44, 21:79, 38:18). They prostrate (22:18). They were offered the trust and shrank from it (33:72). They are not, in the Quran's universe, dead matter. They are participants in a cosmic liturgy that you and I have been deaf to.

So when this ayah says — *you reckon them frozen* — there is a quiet, stunning implication.

**You thought they were dead. They were never dead. You were just unable to perceive their motion, their tasbīh, their participation in the conversation between Allah and His creation.**

#### Where this leaves us

The word *jāmidah* is doing structural work that *thābitah* could never do. *Thābitah* would just mean "still." *Jāmidah* means *you assumed flow had stopped*. Which secretly admits: there was flow all along.

Your mistake was not thinking a moving thing is still. Your mistake was thinking a *living, glorifying, flowing* thing is *frozen*.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: تَمُرُّ مَرَّ السَّحَابِ — "they pass like the passing of clouds"

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, when a verb is followed by its own *maṣdar* (its verbal noun) — *tamurru marra* — it is called the *maf'ūl muṭlaq*, the absolute object. Its function is intensification or specification: not just "they pass" but "they pass *the passing of*…"

It is the construction you would use to insist on the *manner* of an action. Not the fact of it. The *how*.

#### Opening the words

*Tamurru marra al-saḥāb*. They pass — the passing of the clouds.

Now ask yourself: of all the things in the natural world that move, why clouds?

The Quran could have said *they pass like the wind*. Wind is invisible motion — fast, fierce, unmistakable. The Quran could have said *they pass like the floods*. Or *like the ships*. There are many images of motion in this Book.

But Allah chose clouds.

[PAUSE]

Stay here. Because clouds are the most particular kind of moving thing.

A cloud is the one element of the natural world that *appears* still while it is, in fact, moving — and that moves while *retaining its shape*. You can stare at a cloud for a full minute and not register that it has shifted. Then you look up two minutes later and the cloud you were watching is in a different part of the sky. It moved without seeming to move. It traveled while looking like it was just sitting there.

A cloud, in other words, is the perfect analogy for *something whose motion is hidden by its mass*.

And there is more. Clouds are also a Quranic symbol of *mercy*. Allah sends the clouds with the rain. The clouds are the carriers of rizq. They are heavy, they are silent, and they are gentle.

So when the ayah says *the mountains pass like the passing of clouds*, it is doing two things at once:

First, it is giving us the only image in our experience of *motion-disguised-as-stillness*. So we have a way to imagine what we have been missing.

Second, it is putting the mountains into the same category as the clouds — as things that move under Allah's command, *silently, gently, on a scale we cannot detect*.

[PAUSE]

#### Where this leaves us

The mountains are not pictured here as crashing. They are not splintering. They are not exploding into dust. They are *passing like clouds* — which is to say, **with a kind of dignity, on a current of divine command, the way they have always been moving, only now you can finally see it.**

This is not the language of catastrophe. It is the language of *revelation*. The veil over your perception has been lifted, and you see the mountains for the first time in your life.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Bridge

Three words: *taḥsabuhā* — you reckoned. *Jāmidatan* — frozen. *Tamurru marra al-saḥāb* — they pass like clouds.

Together they say one thing: **the world you trusted as still has been alive with motion the entire time, and the Day will not so much break the world as it will break the spell of your perception.**

Now let us ask what this means not just for words — but for how you and I have been living inside this world.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The mountains as a mirror — what else have you misread as frozen?

I want you to think about a phrase we use in English, almost without noticing it. We say someone is "set in their ways." We say a situation is "stuck." We say a relationship has "frozen over." We say "I am where I am, and that is just how it is."

We say these things, and we believe them, and we make decisions based on them.

We tell ourselves: *my heart is hard, and that is just how it is now*. We tell ourselves: *my parents will never understand, and that is just how it is*. We tell ourselves: *I am too far from prayer, my marriage is too cold, my faith is too dim, my anxiety is too entrenched, my anger is too old, this country is too lost, this ummah is too divided.*

We *reckon* these things to be frozen.

Now hear the ayah again. *You reckon them solid — and they are passing like clouds.*

[PAUSE]

What if your reading of your own life — and the lives around you — is exactly the reading the ayah is correcting? What if the things you have called *stuck* are not stuck at all, but moving on a timescale your impatience cannot detect? What if the heart you call hard is in motion, only the motion is too slow for you to register? What if the family member you have written off has been shifting, in tiny increments, under the weight of mercy and time, and you, in your *ḥisāb* — your reckoning — have already buried them while they are still being moved?

Think about a glacier. A glacier looks frozen. It is, in fact, a river of ice moving down a mountainside at a speed measured in inches per year. To the human eye in real time, it is a still wall. To Allah, it is a river.

Most things in your life that you have grieved as *finished* are moving like glaciers. Your nafs is a glacier. Your relationships are glaciers. Your tawbah is a glacier. The heart of someone you have given up praying for is a glacier. They look frozen because *you cannot see at the timescale at which Allah is working*.

The Day this ayah describes is the Day when, for the first time, you will be given the eyes to see what was actually happening. And on that Day, some people will weep with joy, because they will see the slow rivers of mercy that had been carrying them and theirs the whole time. And others will weep with regret, because they will see all the moments they wrote off as immovable — *and discover those moments were moving toward repair, and they walked away too soon.*

The practical thing this changes? **You stop diagnosing things as frozen.** You stop saying "this is just how it is." You start asking: am I seeing a frozen thing, or am I seeing a slow river? Allah does not work on the schedule of your perception. The mountains are passing like clouds. So is your heart. So is your tawbah. So is the duʿā you have been making for ten years and have started to believe is wasted. *None of it is frozen.* All of it is moving — only you cannot see it yet.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Itqān — that everything is on purpose, even what looks like ruin

Now look at the second half of the ayah. Right after the description of the mountains passing like clouds, Allah says:

*ṣun'a Allāhi alladhī atqana kulla shay'in*

**"The making of Allah, who perfected every thing."**

The word here is *atqana*. The root *t-q-n* is the language of craftsmanship. In classical Arabic, *itqān* is what a master craftsman does — the precision of a jeweler, the exactness of a carpenter, the flawlessness of a calligrapher's hand. It is the opposite of slapdash. It is *workmanship in which nothing is accidental*.

And note the placement. Allah does not say *atqana kulla shay'in* in a verse about gardens, or about the human body, or about the precise spacing of the stars. He says it *here* — in a verse about the dismantling of the world.

Stay with that.

[PAUSE]

The ayah is sitting in the middle of an apocalypse. The trumpet has been blown. Creation is in terror. The mountains are revealed to be in motion. And right there, at *that* moment, Allah says: **this — the unmaking of everything you thought was solid — is the work of the Master Craftsman, who perfected every thing.**

In other words: the dissolution of the world is not a *failure* of the world. It is part of the *itqān*. It was always meant to happen. It is not the workshop catching fire. It is the craftsman, on schedule, removing the scaffolding.

There is something here for the part of you that suspects, somewhere underneath, that life is chaotic. That nothing is going according to plan. That maybe the universe is just running and Allah is watching from far away. That maybe your suffering is a glitch, your loss is a bug, your confusion is a sign that no one is steering.

The ayah corrects all of that. *Atqana kulla shay'in* — He perfected *every* thing. *Kull*. Every. Including the things you cannot see the purpose of. Including the moment your world fell apart. Including the version of you that exists right now, in the configuration that you most want to escape. *Itqān* covers it.

This does not make pain not painful. The mountains *do* move on the Day, and people *do* tremble. The ayah is not telling you to feel nothing. It is telling you to know — underneath the feeling — that *the One running this is a master*. Not a beginner. Not improvising. Not surprised by what you are going through.

And the verse closes: *innahu khabīrun bimā taf'alūn*. He is *khabīr* — fully informed, intimately aware — of what you are doing.

The word *khabīr* is the word for *insider knowledge*. It is the knowledge of someone who has been there, seen the inside, knows the details that an outsider cannot guess. To say Allah is *khabīr* of your actions is to say He does not just see them — He knows them from the inside, including all the parts only you and He know.

So the architecture of the verse is this: **the world will be dismantled with perfection (itqān), and everything you ever did inside that world is fully known (khabīr).** Nothing of your life — not the parts that were public, and not the parts only you carried — is going to be lost in the dismantling. Allah is not breaking the world and forgetting what was in it. He is opening it. Like a craftsman opening up a watch. And every gear is accounted for.

The practical thing this changes? **You stop treating your life as if it is happening to you outside of any plan.** When the next hard thing arrives, before you reach for the language of randomness — *why is this happening, this isn't fair, this makes no sense* — you reach instead for the language of *itqān*. You may not understand the design. The ayah does not promise you will. But the ayah does promise that there *is* a design, that it is *perfect*, and that the One executing it knows you, your situation, and your every act *from the inside*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now look at how the ayah is built.

It opens by correcting your *vision*: you reckon things to be frozen, but they are moving. It moves through the analogy of the *clouds* — the only image you have for hidden motion. Then it names the *agent* behind it all: the *ṣun'a* — the making — of Allah, who perfected every thing. And it closes with the ultimate gaze: *He is khabīr of what you do.*

In other words, the ayah moves: **from your eye → to the world → to the One who made the world → to His knowledge of you.**

The eye starts on the mountain. The eye ends on the self.

That is the structure of every honest moment of tadabbur. You begin by looking at something out there — a verse, a sky, an ayah of creation. You notice your seeing was off. You correct toward what is actually there. And the correction always, *always*, comes back to you — to the One who is *khabīr* of what *you* do.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

The linguistic: *taḥsabuhā jāmidatan* — you reckoned them frozen, but they pass like clouds. The world you trusted as still is alive with motion you cannot detect.

The thematic: the things in your life you have called frozen — your heart, your relationships, your tawbah, your ummah — are not frozen. They are moving in *itqān*, perfected, known to Allah, on a current you have not been given the eyes to see. Yet.

The Day this ayah describes is the Day you are finally given those eyes.

The mercy is: you do not have to wait for the Day. The ayah is offering you, *now*, a glimpse of how to look. To stop trusting your *ḥisāb*. To stop calling *frozen* what is in fact *passing like clouds*.

### Questions to carry

1. What in your life, right now, have you written off as *frozen* — and what would it mean to consider that it might be passing slowly, like a cloud, beneath your perception?

2. When was the last time you were *certain* about how something would turn out — and were wrong? What does that say about the reliability of your *ḥisāb*?

3. If everything is *atqana* — perfected — what is the single hardest thing in your current life that this ayah is asking you to read as *part of the design* rather than *outside of it*?

4. When the verse says Allah is *khabīr* of what you do, what is the part of your life that you most hope is *not* known — and what does it mean that it is fully known, and that He chose to tell you so anyway, gently, at the end of an ayah about mountains?

5. If the Day will lift the veil and let you see what was always moving — what would you want to be moving *toward*, when the seeing finally comes?

### One-sentence distillation

**The world you have been calling still has been moving the whole time under the perfect craft of Allah, and on the Day the spell breaks, you will discover that nothing was ever frozen — neither the mountains, nor your heart, nor anything you ever did inside this world that He, the All-Aware, did not already know.**

### The closing invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, take one thing in your life — one — that you have been calling *finished*. A relationship. A part of yourself you have given up on. A duʿā you stopped making.

And try, just for a minute, to see it as a cloud. Moving slowly. Moving silently. Moving in a current you cannot feel.

You do not have to know where it is going. You only have to stop reckoning it dead.

That is the small training of the eye that this ayah is offering. The full vision will come later, on a Day Allah has named. But the practice can begin tonight.

### A brief duʿā

*Allahumma, You who perfected every thing — soften the calculations of my eye. Let me not call frozen what You are still moving. Let me not bury, in my impatience, what Your mercy is still carrying. And on the Day the mountains pass like clouds, let me find that what I had been quietly hoping for, in the parts of my life only You knew, had been moving toward me the whole time.*

*Āmīn.*
