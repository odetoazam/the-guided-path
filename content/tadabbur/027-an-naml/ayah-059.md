# The Unanswerable Question: Praise Before the Inquiry

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 59

### Arabic

> قُلِ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ وَسَلَـٰمٌ عَلَىٰ عِبَادِهِ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱصْطَفَىٰ ۗ ءَآللَّهُ خَيْرٌ أَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ

### Transliteration

*Quli'l-ḥamdu lillāhi wa-salāmun ʿalā ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā. Āllāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn.*

### Translation

> Say: **All praise belongs to Allah, and peace be upon His servants whom He has chosen.** Is Allah better — or what they associate with Him?

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

Surah An-Naml has, up to this point, told you stories. It has shown you Mūsā with a staff that transformed in his hand, Dāwūd and Sulaymān who inherited knowledge and were taught the speech of birds, an ant who warned her colony, a hoopoe who carried intelligence across continents, and Ṣāliḥ standing before nine men who plotted in the night. The surah has been a gallery — kingdom after kingdom, prophet after prophet, each given a particular gift, each facing a particular rejection.

And then suddenly the gallery ends.

The voice shifts. The Prophet ﷺ is told: *Qul* — say. Speak directly now. Stop telling them stories about Sulaymān and start asking them about themselves.

But before the asking begins, Allah does something that, if you slow down enough to notice, will stop your breath.

He commands a *prelude*.

He does not let the question come first. He does not let the Prophet ﷺ open with the cross-examination. He commands him to stand on a particular ground before he speaks — a ground of praise, a ground of peace, a ground of acknowledging *who has been chosen.*

### The Hook

Why? Why would Allah, before asking the most piercing question in the whole sequence — *Is Allah better, or what you associate with Him?* — first command the messenger to praise, and then to send peace upon the chosen servants?

What does the praise have to do with the question?

What does the peace have to do with the question?

And why — when Arabic offers a hundred ways to phrase a comparison — does Allah phrase this one in a way that the listener literally **cannot answer without convicting himself**?

Stay with me. There is something stationed in this ayah that will not let go of you once you see it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Qul* — The Commanded Voice

Before we touch any of the content, look at the very first word.

**قُلِ** — *Qul.* Say.

This is a small word. Two letters in the imperative. We pass over it constantly because it appears so often in the Quran. But pause and consider what it actually does.

Allah is not telling the Prophet ﷺ what is true. He is telling him what to *say*. There is a layer of address here that is easy to miss: the ayah is not a description of Allah's praise. It is a *script*. Allah is putting words into the mouth of His messenger.

Why does that matter?

Because what follows — *all praise belongs to Allah, and peace upon His chosen servants* — is being placed on the tongue of someone who is about to be accused. The Quraysh did not let Muhammad ﷺ speak in peace. They mocked him. They charged him with madness, with poetry, with sorcery. He was the rejected one in his own city.

And in that climate, Allah commands him: *Qul.* Say this. Open with this.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. When you are under attack, the natural instinct is to defend. To explain. To argue. The natural opening is "*Listen to me*" or "*You are wrong.*"

Allah teaches a different opening: **start with praise**.

Not praise of yourself. Not praise of your tribe. Praise of *Allah.* Then peace upon the ones who came before you — the chosen ones. Only after you have stood on that ground do you turn to the question.

The *qul* is teaching a sequence. The script is the lesson. Before you confront falsehood, root yourself in praise.

### Element Two: *Al-Ḥamdu Lillāh* — The Definite Article That Closes Every Door

Now look at the second word.

**ٱلْحَمْدُ** — *al-ḥamd.* Praise. With the definite article *al-* attached to the front.

In Arabic, this little prefix *al-* is doing something massive. In English we hear "all praise" and our mind treats it almost as decoration — a poetic flourish. In Arabic it is not decoration. It is a structural claim.

Let me teach the concept before we apply it. In Arabic grammar, when you place *al-* on a noun like this — *al-ḥamd* rather than *ḥamd* — grammarians describe it as **al-istighrāq**: the *al-* of total inclusion. It does not mean *some* praise. It does not mean *a* praise. It means *the entire genus of praise, every instance of it, wherever it occurs, whoever offers it.*

So *al-ḥamdu lillāh* is not "praise to Allah" the way we say "thanks to Allah" after a meal. It is a sweeping declaration: **the entire category called praise — all of it, every kind, every act of it that has ever existed or ever will — belongs to Allah.**

Stay with that.

If every act of praise — even the praise a man offers to another man for his generosity, even the praise a child offers to a parent for their kindness, even the admiration of a craftsman for a beautiful piece of work — if *all* of it belongs to Allah, what does that mean?

It means there is nothing praiseworthy in existence whose praiseworthiness does not trace back to Him. The generous man is generous because Allah gave him a generous heart. The beautiful object is beautiful because Allah is the source of beauty. The kindness in the parent is a borrowed kindness; the original belongs to *Ar-Raḥmān.*

Now consider the placement. This declaration is being made *immediately before* a question about partners. *Is Allah better, or what they associate with Him?*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what has happened?

The question has already been answered before it is asked.

If *all* praise belongs to Allah, then any "partner" you might be tempted to associate with Him has, by definition, **nothing of its own to be praised for.** Whatever good is in it is borrowed. Whatever beauty is in it is a loan. The idol cannot keep what does not belong to it.

The *al-* of total inclusion is doing the philosophical work *before* the comparison even begins. It closes every door through which a rival could enter.

### Element Three: *Wa-Salāmun ʿalā ʿIbādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā* — The Greeting That Names the Family

Now we come to what is, for me, the most quietly devastating phrase in this ayah.

**وَسَلَـٰمٌ عَلَىٰ عِبَادِهِ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱصْطَفَىٰ** — *and peace upon His servants whom He has chosen.*

Three things are happening here. Let us take them slowly.

**First:** *salām* — peace. Allah does not say *raḥmah* (mercy) here. He does not say *maghfirah* (forgiveness). He chooses *salām* — peace. The greeting of Paradise. The greeting between believers. The very name from which one of His own names is derived: *As-Salām*.

**Second:** the word *ibād* — servants. Plural. Allah does not say "*upon Muhammad*" or "*upon the prophet I am addressing.*" He widens the circle. *Servants*, plural. A community across time.

**Third — and this is where you have to pay attention** — Allah modifies this *ibād* with the relative clause *alladhīna'ṣṭafā*: **whom He has chosen.**

*Iṣṭafā* comes from the root *ṣ-f-w*, which carries the meaning of clarity, purity, the clear part of something separated from the cloudy part. *Ṣafwa* in classical Arabic is the *cream*, the *clarified essence* — what rises to the top when you let a substance settle. *Iṣṭafā* in Form VIII intensifies this: to actively *select out the pure*, to *pick the clarified portion for oneself.*

So *ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā* is not "His servants in general." It is "**His servants whom He clarified, separated, and chose for Himself.**"

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself: who, in the immediate context of this surah, has just been named?

Mūsā. Dāwūd. Sulaymān. Ṣāliḥ.

Surah An-Naml has, for fifty-eight ayahs, been a parade of *the chosen*. And now, before pivoting to the question, Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ to send *peace* upon them. Upon the *family* whose stories have just been told.

Notice what is *not* happening. Allah does not name them individually here. He gives the descriptor — *those whom He chose* — and lets the listener fill in the names from the surah's own narrative.

This is artistry that is hard to express. The surah has spent its first half *introducing the family*. And now, at the threshold of the question, the family is greeted.

But here is the deeper move. The greeting of *salām* upon the chosen is not just gratitude to past prophets. It is a structural statement about *what kind of voice the Prophet ﷺ is joining.* He is not speaking alone. He is speaking *as a continuation* of Mūsā, of Sulaymān, of Ṣāliḥ. The *salām* upon them is the acknowledgment that the message about to be delivered — *Is Allah better, or what they associate?* — is the same message they delivered.

He is not innovating. He is *inheriting*.

### Element Four: *Āllāhu Khayrun Ammā Yushrikūn* — The Comparison That Cannot Be Answered

Now we reach the question itself.

**ءَآللَّهُ خَيْرٌ أَمَّا يُشْرِكُونَ** — *Is Allah better, or what they associate with Him?*

Let us teach the concept first. In Arabic, when you ask a comparative question of the form *X khayrun am Y* — *Is X better or Y?* — you are using the elative form *khayr*, which can function both as "good" and "better." This kind of construction is called *istifhām taqrīrī* in Arabic rhetoric: an interrogative whose purpose is **not to elicit information** but to **establish what the questioner already considers settled.**

It is the same thing a parent does when they ask a child: "*Do you want to behave, or do you want to be sent to your room?*" The form is a question. The function is to corner the listener into the only reasonable answer.

Now look at how Allah constructs this one.

He places **اللَّه** — Allah — explicitly named — on one side of the comparison.

And on the other side, He places not the names of the idols. Not "Lāt or ʿUzzā or Manāt." He places: **مَّا يُشْرِكُونَ** — *what they associate.*

[PAUSE]

The *mā* here is the indefinite *what.* Not "the gods they associate." Not "the partners they associate." Just: *what.* Some thing. An unspecified thing.

Read closely the asymmetry. Allah is named. Their objects of worship are *not* named. They are demoted to a vague *whatever it is they associate.*

Why?

Because to name them is to grant them a dignity they do not possess. The grammar is enacting the theology. *Allah* on one side — proper noun, the Name above every name. On the other side — a faceless *whatever*.

And then notice the verb: **yushrikūn** — *they associate.* Present tense. Not *worship.* Not *call upon.* The verb chosen is *shirk* — the act of *making a partner.* The accusation is named even as the question is asked. The very act of comparing is itself the crime.

[PAUSE]

So the question is structured to be unanswerable.

If they say "*the partners are better,*" they have indicted themselves as fools by their own mouth.

If they say "*Allah is better,*" they have just *abandoned* the partners.

There is no third option. Silence is also conviction, because silence in the face of *Is Allah better?* is itself a kind of equivocation that no honest heart can sustain.

This is why the *al-ḥamdu lillāh* had to come first. Allah closed every escape route at the door — *all praise belongs to Him* — before opening the room of the question. By the time the listener reaches *Āllāhu khayrun*, the answer has been laid down so completely that the question is almost a courtesy.

---

### Bridge to the Themes

So look at what the linguistic construction has shown us. The *qul* — a script of how to open. The *al-ḥamd* — a sweeping claim that closes every rival's door. The *salām ʿalā ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā* — a greeting that names a family the Prophet ﷺ is being inducted into. And the *Āllāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn* — a question whose only honest answer is the surrender of the questioner.

But what does this *reveal* — not about words, but about reality itself? About how a believer is supposed to stand in a world that is constantly asking him to choose between Allah and something else?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Praise Before the Inquiry

Let me start somewhere familiar.

You have probably noticed that when you are anxious about a hard conversation — a difficult email you have to send, a disagreement with a family member, a confrontation at work — there is a particular *posture of mind* that decides whether the conversation goes well or goes badly.

If you walk into the conversation already defensive, already braced for attack, already scanning for what is wrong with the other person, the conversation will collapse into argument no matter how reasonable your position. Your tone will be off. Your body will be tense. You will say things you did not mean to say.

But if you can find a way — *before* entering the conversation — to settle yourself into a place of gratitude, of acknowledgment of the good in the other person, of remembering what is true regardless of how this conversation goes — then everything changes. The conversation can still be hard, but you are no longer fighting *from fear*. You are speaking *from ground.*

Therapists know this. Spiritual teachers across traditions know this. The *posture* before the speech determines what the speech can carry.

Now look at what Allah is doing in this ayah.

He is teaching the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every believer who recites this — *the posture* before the great confrontation with falsehood.

The Prophet ﷺ is about to ask the most piercing question. He is about to say: *Is Allah better, or what you associate?* This is not a conversation that will go softly. This is the question that will, in time, cost lives, divide families, and reshape the Arabian Peninsula.

And Allah does not let him open with the question. He stations him first in *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* He has him say *salām* upon the chosen ones who came before. Only then — *from that ground* — does the question come.

[PAUSE]

What Allah is teaching, beneath the words, is this: **the truth you carry is too heavy to be delivered from a fearful or angry posture.** It will land wrongly. It will be tainted by the medium that carries it.

So before you speak truth — even hard truth, even necessary truth — *praise.* Acknowledge the source of all good. *Greet* the ones who walked the road before you. *Then* speak.

And consider what this does for the speaker himself. When you open with *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, you have already said: *whatever happens in this conversation, the praise still belongs to Allah.* If you are believed, *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* If you are mocked, *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* If they convert, *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* If they reject you, *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* The outcome no longer controls you, because you have already given the praise away to the One it always belonged to.

When you open with *salām ʿalā ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā*, you have already said: *I am not alone in this. Mūsā stood here. Sulaymān stood here. Ṣāliḥ stood here. The chosen ones walked this road before me, and peace is upon them.* The loneliness of carrying the truth dissolves into a lineage.

**The opening has already done the work that the closing was supposed to do.**

By the time you get to *Āllāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn*, you are not fighting for an outcome. You are simply *announcing what is*, from a place that cannot be shaken.

#### The Practical Application

Tomorrow, before any hard conversation — with a spouse, with a child, with a colleague, with a parent who does not understand your *deen* — try this. Before you speak, station yourself in two phrases.

*Al-ḥamdu lillāh.* All praise belongs to Allah, regardless of how this conversation goes.

*Wa-salām ʿalā ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā.* Peace upon the ones who walked this road before me — every prophet, every believer, every parent who had this same conversation with their child a thousand years ago.

Then speak.

Notice what changes in your tone. Notice what changes in your breath. Notice what becomes possible from that ground that was not possible from the defensive posture.

This is not a technique. It is *imitation of prophetic posture.*

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Question That Is Not Really a Question

Let me start with a strange observation about how human beings actually choose.

You have probably noticed that people very rarely change their minds because of an argument. You can present someone with overwhelming evidence — well-cited, beautifully reasoned — and they will nod, and then continue believing what they believed before. The data is necessary, but the data is almost never sufficient.

What *does* change minds? Often, it is being asked the right question at the right moment.

Not a question that demands new information. A question that asks the person to *look honestly at what they already know.*

This is the form of question called, in Arabic rhetoric, *istifhām taqrīrī* — the establishing question. The question that does not ask for information but asks the listener to *acknowledge what is already true in their own heart.*

This is exactly what *Āllāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn* does.

The Quraysh, who first heard this, were not ignorant of Allah. They knew Allah. They called Him *Allah*. The Kaʿbah was His house. They prayed to Him in moments of crisis. They named their children *ʿAbd-Allāh.* The problem was not that they did not know Allah; the problem was that they had *added partners alongside Him* — partners they themselves admitted had no power, who could not hear them, who would simply *intercede* (so they claimed) on their behalf.

So Allah does not ask: *Does Allah exist?* That would be irrelevant; they already knew He did.

He does not ask: *Are your idols real gods?* That would be too easy to deflect with theological tap-dancing about how the idols were just intermediaries.

He asks the one question their evasions cannot survive: **Is Allah better, or what you associate with Him?**

[PAUSE]

Notice what this question does to the listener.

It does not require new information. It requires *honesty.* It requires the listener to look at the partner — the stone, the figure, the imagined intercessor, whatever it is — and look at Allah, and *answer with their own mouth*.

And nobody — *nobody* — can honestly say *the stone is better.* Even the most committed idol-worshipper, asked this question at three in the morning when he is alone with himself, knows.

The question is a mirror. It is held up to the heart, and it asks the heart to read what is already written there.

Now bring this into your own life.

You and I do not bow to stones. But the structure of the question still applies, because the structure of *shirk* still applies. *Shirk*, in its broadest sense, is whenever we set up something *alongside Allah* in our hearts — something we fear losing more than we fear losing Him, something we hope for more than we hope for Him, something whose approval we seek more than we seek His.

So ask yourself. Make the question personal.

*Is Allah better, or the approval of the people whose opinion you cannot stop checking?*

*Is Allah better, or the wealth you are arranging your entire life around?*

*Is Allah better, or the relationship you are afraid to lose even though it is pulling you away from Him?*

*Is Allah better, or the image of yourself you are constantly performing for strangers on a screen?*

[PAUSE]

The question is the same question. The form is the same form. *Āllāhu khayrun ammā tushrik*.

And the answer, when you let yourself answer honestly, is the same answer.

But here is the thing — and this is why the ayah is so quietly merciful — the answer is *easy* once you let yourself ask the question. The whole problem is not the difficulty of the answer. The whole problem is that we keep avoiding the question.

Allah, in this ayah, is forcing the question into the open. *Qul* — say it. Out loud. *Āllāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn.* And once it is said, once the comparison has been *spoken*, the heart cannot pretend not to know.

#### The Practical Application

Take one *thing* — one — that you suspect is occupying real estate in your heart that should belong to Allah. One worry, one ambition, one attachment, one fear.

Sit with it. Then ask yourself, out loud if you can: *Is Allah better than this thing?*

Do not answer quickly. Sit with the question. Let the heart answer in its own time.

When the answer comes — and it will come — let that be your prayer for the day. Not the answer alone, but the gap between the answer and how you have been living.

That gap is where *tawbah* lives. That gap is where the journey back begins.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at the whole ayah and see how it is built.

It opens with an ascending praise: *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — praise rising up to Allah.

It descends into a peace upon the chosen: *wa-salāmun ʿalā ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā* — peace coming down upon a community of human beings.

And then, between that vertical praise and that horizontal peace, the question arrives: *Āllāhu khayrun ammā yushrikūn?*

The question is *cradled* between the praise and the peace. It is not delivered as an attack. It is delivered from inside a sanctuary that has already been built.

The praise establishes that Allah is the source of all good.

The peace establishes that the messenger is part of a chosen lineage.

The question follows — and the question, between these two anchors, is not provocation. It is *invitation.*

This is the architecture: **praise above, peace around, question within.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

The *al-* of total inclusion in *al-ḥamd* — and the lesson of *praise before the inquiry* — are the same insight. Both are saying: settle the question of *who owns the praise* before you ask any other question. If all praise is His, then the comparison is over before it begins.

The relative clause *alladhīna'ṣṭafā* — and the lesson of *the question that is not really a question* — are the same insight. Both are saying: you are not alone, and the answer is already in the lineage. Mūsā answered it. Sulaymān answered it. Ṣāliḥ answered it. The question being asked of you has already been answered by every chosen servant before you. You are not being asked to *invent* an answer. You are being asked to *join the answer.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What conversations in your life are you currently entering from a defensive posture, that you could re-enter from the posture of *al-ḥamdu lillāh*?

2. Who, in the lineage of *ʿibādihi'lladhīna'ṣṭafā*, has walked the road you are currently walking? Whose *salām* could you place on yourself before stepping into your own difficulty?

3. If you asked yourself, today, *Is Allah better, or _____?* — what is the one word you would be most afraid to put in the blank? And what does the fear itself tell you?

4. When was the last time you opened a hard conversation by first praising Allah out loud — even silently in your own breath — before speaking? What might change if that became the opening of every difficult exchange?

5. The Prophet ﷺ was commanded to *say* this — *qul.* What in your life is currently being lived but not yet *said*? What truth is sitting in your chest that has not yet been brought to your tongue?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Before you confront falsehood, station yourself in praise and join the lineage of the chosen — for then the question you ask will not be an attack, but an invitation that the honest heart cannot refuse.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to win an argument with someone else. It is asking you to *stand somewhere* — somewhere that praise has built, somewhere that peace has surrounded, somewhere the chosen ones already stood.

When you stand there, the world's questions stop being threats. They become opportunities to say, calmly and clearly, what was always true.

The next time you feel the impulse to *defend* the *deen* — pause. Ask yourself if you have first *praised*. Ask yourself if you have first *greeted the family.* Then speak.

You will find the words come differently. You will find the listener receives differently. Because the words will be carrying not your fear, but the *salām* of every prophet who ever stood where you are standing now.

### A Brief Du'a

> O Allah, *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — for every act of praise that has ever been offered, and every one that ever will be, all of it belongs to You alone.
>
> Place us, O Allah, among Your *ʿibād alladhīna'ṣṭafayt* — Your servants whom You have chosen, clarified, and drawn near.
>
> And when the question comes — *Is Allah better, or what they associate?* — let our hearts answer before our tongues, and let our lives answer before our words. Āmīn.
