# The Dead Cannot Hear

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 80

### إِنَّكَ لَا تُسْمِعُ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ وَلَا تُسْمِعُ ٱلصُّمَّ ٱلدُّعَآءَ إِذَا وَلَّوْا۟ مُدْبِرِينَ

*Innaka lā tusmiʿu al-mawtā wa lā tusmiʿu aṣ-ṣumma ad-duʿāʾa idhā wallaw mudbirīn*

**"Indeed, you cannot make the dead hear, nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they have turned their backs, retreating."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Behind These Words

Imagine a man who has been carrying a single message for years. Not an opinion he can revise. Not a strategy he can adjust. A message — given to him from above, fixed, non-negotiable — and his entire purpose on earth is to deliver it.

Now imagine the people he was sent to are his own people. His tribe. His childhood streets. The faces that fed him as an orphan. The cousins he played with. The uncles who took him in.

And imagine that for over a decade, the response has not been argument. It has not even been violence first. It has been something stranger and more wounding: **they have turned their backs and walked away.** Not refuted him. Not engaged him. Just — turned. As if he had said nothing at all.

This is the Makkan moment. This is where the Prophet ﷺ stood when this ayah came down. He had been in the streets of Makkah pleading, calling, warning — and watching people he loved physically pivot their bodies away from him as he spoke. Some scholars say no human being in history has loved a community that refused him as much as the Prophet ﷺ loved Quraysh.

And the burden of that love — the sleepless weight of watching people walk toward a fire you can see and they cannot — is the wound this ayah is reaching toward.

### The Hook

Notice what Allah does *not* say here. He does not say *"they will not listen."* He does not say *"they refuse to hear."* He says something far stranger — something almost shocking when you sit with it.

He tells His Messenger ﷺ: **you cannot make them hear.**

Not "they don't want to." **You cannot.** It is outside the boundary of what a messenger is. The verse names the wall.

[PAUSE]

And tucked inside this consolation is one of the most precise diagnoses of the human condition the Quran ever offers — a diagnosis that uses the language of *death* and *deafness* not as insult, but as anatomy. We are about to sit with a verse that explains why some people, when truth arrives, are structurally unable to receive it. And why the Prophet ﷺ — and you, and I — were never meant to carry the weight of changing that.

Let's open it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tusmiʿu* — The Causative Form

Before we even look at the word in the ayah, let me ask you to think about something in your own language.

There is a difference between *to hear* and *to make someone hear*. To hear is something a person does on their own — sound enters their ear and they receive it. To make someone hear is something *you* do *to* them. It crosses a threshold. It implies you reached inside their faculty of hearing and *caused* reception.

In English, we collapse this distinction. We say "I told him" and we mean either "I spoke words at him" or "I got the message into his head." Two completely different acts, one verb.

Arabic refuses that collapse.

In Arabic, there is a verb form — what grammarians call *Form IV*, the *afʿala* pattern — that exists specifically to express *causation*. *Samiʿa* means "he heard." *Asmaʿa* means "he caused [someone] to hear." It is a separate verb. A separate moral and metaphysical category.

Now look at what Allah says to His Messenger ﷺ:

> **إِنَّكَ لَا تُسْمِعُ**
> *Innaka lā **tusmiʿu***

He does not say *innaka lā tukallimuhum* — "you do not speak to them." Of course the Prophet ﷺ speaks to them. He speaks until his throat is dry, until Khadijah radiya Allahu ʿanhā has to coax him home. The verse is not denying speech.

He does not say *innaka lā tunādīhim* — "you do not call them." Of course he calls them. He calls them at Safa. He calls them at Ṭā'if. He calls them in their assemblies.

He says: *lā tusmiʿu*. **You do not cause them to hear.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The verse is drawing a line — a precise, surgical line — through the act of communication. On one side: the messenger's job. To speak. To call. To deliver. To repeat. On the other side: what is *not* the messenger's job. To penetrate the inner ear of the soul. To force reception. To install the message inside the listener.

The Form IV verb tells us where the wall is.

And here is what is remarkable: this is the same Arabic structure that appears in Sūrah Fāṭir (35:22) — *wa mā anta bi-musmiʿin man fī al-qubūr* — "you are not one who can make those in graves hear." The Quran uses this causative again and again, in different surahs, to mark the same boundary. As if Allah is saying to His Messenger ﷺ over and over: *the line is here. Not further. Here.*

Why would Allah repeat this? Because the Messenger ﷺ kept trying to cross it. Because love does that. Because anyone who has ever watched someone they love walk into ruin has felt the impossible urge to reach inside them and *make* them see.

The Form IV verb is the mercy. It is Allah saying: **the part you are tearing yourself apart trying to do — that part was never assigned to you.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Al-Mawtā* and *Aṣ-Ṣumma* — Two Categories, Not One

Now look at the structure of the verse. Allah names two groups:

> **لَا تُسْمِعُ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ وَلَا تُسْمِعُ ٱلصُّمَّ**
> *lā tusmiʿu **al-mawtā** wa lā tusmiʿu **aṣ-ṣumma***

The dead. And the deaf.

Notice — and this is the kind of thing the verse will reward you for noticing — Allah did not collapse these into one. He did not say "you cannot make those who refuse to hear, hear." He gave us **two distinct categories**, and He repeated the verb *tusmiʿu* for each one. He did not say "you cannot make the dead and the deaf hear." He said: *you cannot make the dead hear, **and** you cannot make the deaf hear.*

The repetition is doing work. It is telling us these are two separate diagnoses. Two separate spiritual conditions. Two separate kinds of unreachability.

Let me ask you to sit with what each one means.

**Al-mawtā — the dead.** Death is the absence of life itself. The dead person is not refusing to hear. The dead person has *no faculty left*. The receiver is gone. Whatever you say at a grave, you are speaking into the absence of the thing that would have heard you. It is not stubbornness. It is not arrogance. It is non-being.

**Aṣ-ṣumma — the deaf.** The deaf person is alive. The faculty exists. Something is *blocking* the channel. The signal is going out but not landing.

So Allah, in one verse, gives the Messenger ﷺ — and gives us — a typology of the people who do not respond to truth.

Some are dead inside. The receiver itself is gone. The part of the soul that would have answered the call has, through long refusal and accumulated darkness, ceased to function as a living organ. The Quran calls these the *amwāt al-qulūb* — those whose hearts have died, even as their bodies walk and eat and laugh.

And some are deaf. Alive, but blocked. The faculty is intact, but something — pride, fear, the cost of admitting they were wrong, the loyalty to a tribe — sits between the call and the heart and absorbs every word before it can reach.

Both of them, the messenger cannot reach. But they are reached differently. The dead need *resurrection* — and only Allah resurrects. The deaf need *unblocking* — and only Allah opens what has been sealed.

Either way: **the messenger is not the surgeon.** The messenger is the voice. The reaching inside is His.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Idhā Wallaw Mudbirīn* — The Image That Breaks the Heart

Now look at the last phrase. This is where the verse stops being abstract and becomes a photograph:

> **إِذَا وَلَّوْا۟ مُدْبِرِينَ**
> *idhā **wallaw mudbirīn***

"When they turn away, retreating."

Both of these words come from roots about *the back*. *Wallā* — to turn (often, to turn one's face away). *Adbara* — to present one's back, to show one's *dubur*, the rear, the side that is the opposite of the face.

Allah uses *both*. Not one. *Wallaw* AND *mudbirīn*. The grammarians call this kind of doubling *ta'kīd* — emphatic confirmation. It is the language equivalent of pointing twice at the same gesture so you do not miss it.

Picture it. The Prophet ﷺ is speaking. A man is in front of him. The man does not stand and argue. The man does not raise his voice. The man simply — **turns** — and then **walks**. His face is gone. His back is what the Messenger ﷺ now sees, retreating into the distance.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most quietly devastating images in the Quran.

Because notice what the verse is *not* saying. It is not saying "the dead cannot hear, period." It is not saying "the deaf cannot hear, period." It is saying: **even the deaf — whose faculty still exists — cannot be made to hear when they have turned and are walking away.**

Why?

Because hearing is not just about the ear. Hearing requires a *posture*. A directionality. A facing-toward. When a human being turns their body away from a source of meaning and begins to walk, they are not just rejecting words — they are taking the receiver out of range. Even if the deafness were temporary, even if the heart could have softened, **the back is now what is presented**, and you cannot pour water into a vessel whose opening has been turned away from the spout.

This is istidlāl al-maʿqūl bi-l-maḥsūs — Allah is teaching us about an *unseen spiritual reality* using a *physical posture* we all know. We have all watched someone turn their back on us mid-conversation. We have all felt that specific wound. Allah takes that bodily image and uses it to name what happens *inside* a soul that does not want truth: **the inner self pivots, and the back of the heart is what is presented to the call.**

And once that has happened — once the inner posture is not just refusal but *retreat* — even a messenger sent by Allah Himself, even the most beloved of all creation, the seal of prophets, *cannot make that person hear*.

Not because the messenger is weak. Because the door is on the other side, and it has been turned away.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So we have three things.

A causative verb that names the limit of what speech can do.
Two categories — the dead and the deaf — naming two different conditions of unreachability.
And an image — turning, retreating — that names the bodily posture of refusal.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about the Prophet ﷺ, about the people he was sent to, and about you, sitting here, listening to this.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Mercy of Being Told *You Are Not in Charge of This*

Let me start somewhere familiar.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from loving someone who is harming themselves. A parent watching an adult child spiral. A friend watching another friend pick the same destructive partner for the fifth time. A sibling watching a sibling ignore the warning signs of an illness.

You can see it. They cannot. And the disproportion between what you can see and what they will let in becomes a kind of internal madness. You start rehearsing arguments in the shower. You start lying awake at night composing the perfect sentence — the one that, if you could just *say it right*, would finally land. You start to believe that if they do not change, **you have failed.**

This is what guidance-burden does to a heart that loves.

Now multiply it by a prophet. Multiply it by a man who is not just watching strangers harm themselves but watching his entire community walk toward a fire whose heat he can feel and they cannot. Multiply it by *revelation* — the certainty that the warning is not your opinion but is *true*, with the most absolute truth that exists.

The Prophet ﷺ was being eaten alive by this. Other ayahs tell us so directly: *laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka* — "perhaps you will destroy yourself with grief that they do not believe" (26:3). The verb *bākhiʿ* is gut-wrenching. It means to kill oneself with sorrow.

And into that wound comes this verse.

**"Indeed, you cannot make the dead hear."**

Read in isolation, that sounds harsh. Read in context, it is a *cool hand on a fevered forehead*. It is Allah saying to His most beloved: **stop. The part you are killing yourself trying to do is not your part. It was never your part. I did not assign it to you because I did not give it to anyone but Myself.**

The messenger's job is *al-balāgh* — the delivery. The messenger speaks. The messenger calls. The messenger persists. The messenger embodies. But the messenger does not *install* the message inside the listener. That installation — that opening of the inner ear, that resurrection of the dead heart, that unblocking of the spiritual deafness — belongs to Allah alone.

[PAUSE]

And here is the part that I want you to feel in your own life:

If Allah lifted the burden of "making them hear" off the shoulders of His Messenger ﷺ — *who do you think you are to carry it for your relatives?*

The friend who will not pray. The parent who will not soften. The child who will not return. The cousin who mocks the dīn at every gathering. The colleague whose questions you have answered a hundred times and who still, at the end of every conversation, **turns and walks**.

You are not their resurrector. You were never given that role. And the night-thoughts in which you replay the conversation, looking for the magic sentence — those night-thoughts are you trying to occupy a station that was reserved for Allah alone.

This verse is permission. Permission to keep speaking, yes — the messenger never stopped speaking. But permission to release the *outcome*. Permission to deliver and then *let go*. Permission to love them and *not be destroyed by their refusal*, because their refusal was always between them and Allah, and never something the structure of reality allowed you to override.

**Practical application:** Pick the one person in your life whose unbelief, or whose spiritual distance, you have been carrying like a stone. Tonight, in your duʿā', say their name to Allah, and then say out loud: *Yā Rabb, I have delivered. The hearing is Yours.* And then sleep. The verse is asking you to sleep.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Anatomy of Spiritual Death — and the Warning Hidden Inside the Consolation

The first theme was comfort for the messenger. Now we have to turn the verse around and ask the harder question.

Because while this verse is consoling the Prophet ﷺ, it is *also*, simultaneously, **diagnosing the listeners.** And we are all, in some part of our lives, listeners.

The verse names two conditions: dead, and deaf-and-retreating. It is worth asking with brutal honesty: *are there places in my own heart that match these descriptions?*

Here is the bridge into your life:

You have a topic. You know which one. There is some area of your dīn — maybe it is a sin you keep returning to, maybe it is a relationship you know is haram, maybe it is a piece of advice your parents have given you for ten years, maybe it is a knowledge of God that everyone around you keeps trying to invite you toward — and every time it comes up, something inside you *turns*.

You don't argue. You don't even consciously decide. You just — **pivot**. The conversation moves on. The reminder slides off. The article goes unread. The lecture gets closed. You feel a faint discomfort and then you turn your inner back, and the moment passes.

That, the verse is telling us, is *aṣ-ṣamam* — the spiritual deafness — combined with *al-idbār* — the inner retreat. And the verse is warning us: **this posture, repeated, calcifies into something worse.**

Because deafness can be lifted. The dead state cannot, except by direct divine resurrection.

And the Quran teaches us — across many ayahs — that hearts *die*. They are not born dead. They die through long sequences of small turnings-away. Each refusal to hear is a small turning. Each small turning narrows the aperture. And if the turnings continue, the receiver itself — the *qalb*, the spiritual organ that hears — eventually goes silent.

[PAUSE]

This is the warning underneath the consolation.

To the Messenger ﷺ: do not destroy yourself, because some hearts have already died.
To us: **do not let your heart be one of them.**

The frightening insight is that the *mawtā* of this verse were once living. They were once like you. They had moments when truth came near them and they could have leaned in. They turned instead. And they turned, and they turned, and they turned — until one day the turning was no longer a choice. It was their nature. The receiver had quietly stopped working, and they did not even notice.

Imam al-Ghazali writes — and the Quran confirms in many places — that the spiritual death of the heart is the most terrifying thing that can happen to a human being, *because it does not feel like anything*. The dead heart does not ache. The dead heart does not mourn its own death. The dead heart goes about its business, eating and earning and laughing, and feels itself perfectly fine. That is the horror of it. The signs of life — restlessness in the presence of sin, longing during dhikr, tears during the Quran, the quiet ache when you have missed a prayer — these are the signs of a *living* heart. Their absence is the silence of a dead one.

And what kills the heart? **The repeated turning of the back when the call comes.**

So this verse is not only a comfort to the one who calls. It is a mirror for the one who is called. It is asking each of us, gently but unflinchingly: *when truth shows up in your life — through a Quranic verse, through a reminder, through a person speaking sincerely, through your own conscience at 3 a.m. — what does your inner body do? Does it lean in? Or does it turn?*

**Practical application:** This week, notice the moment of inner turning. The next time you feel that subtle pivot — the article you don't want to read, the reminder you don't want to hear, the conversation you change — *stop*. Don't even respond yet. Just notice that you turned. Awareness of the turning is the first reversal of it. The dead cannot notice. The fact that you can still notice is the proof that you are still alive — and the invitation to use that life before it ebbs.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what these two themes are, side by side. They are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic work — the *tusmiʿu*, the doubling of *al-mawtā* and *aṣ-ṣumma*, the bodily image of *wallaw mudbirīn* — taught us that the act of receiving a divine call requires *something the listener does*: the turning-toward, the opening, the standing still long enough to be reached.

The thematic work showed us this from two angles. From the messenger's side: *you cannot do this part for them*. From the listener's side: *this is the part you must guard with your life, because it is the part of you that is still alive*.

It is the same line. The line between *what the speaker can do* and *what only the listener-with-Allah can do*. The verse draws it once, and it cuts in both directions: it releases the messenger, and it indicts the one who turns.

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose unbelief or whose distance from the dīn am I carrying as if their hearing were my responsibility? What would it mean to deliver, and then let go?

2. Where in my own life do I feel the inner *pivot* — the small turning of the back — when truth comes near? What is the topic I will not let my heart sit still for?

3. If the dead heart does not feel its own death, what are the signs of *life* I should be looking for in myself? When did I last feel them?

4. The Prophet ﷺ was told by Allah Himself to release the outcome and trust the delivery. Whose approval, whose change of heart, am I still treating as a measure of my own success?

5. If I were to die tonight in the spiritual posture I was in this morning — was my face turned toward the call, or was my back?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah lifts the burden of *making* people hear off His Messenger's shoulders, and in doing so reveals both the mercy of His sovereignty and the terror of what a soul does to itself when it makes the turning-away its habit.**

### The Closing Invitation

There is a posture this verse is asking for. It is not despair at those who turn. It is not pride that you have not. It is the quiet, steady, lifelong choice to **face** — to keep your inner body oriented toward the source of the call, even when your outer life is loud, even when your sins are real, even when you are tired.

The dead cannot hear. The retreating deaf cannot hear. But the one who is still alive, and still facing — that one hears, even faintly, even imperfectly. And that hearing, sustained over a lifetime, is the whole of what we were made for.

Keep your face turned. Deliver to those you love and release the rest. And trust that the One who alone causes hearing is also the One who alone hears your duʿā' for the ones who have turned.

### Du'ā'

*Allāhumma, You who alone make hearts hear — keep my face turned toward Your call, and do not let the small turnings of my days harden into the great turning that You named in this verse. Soften the hearts of those I love who have turned, for I have delivered as I could and the hearing belongs to You. And if there is any deafness still in me, open it — before the dead state I cannot lift becomes mine. Āmīn.*
