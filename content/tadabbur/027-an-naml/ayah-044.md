# The Mistaken Pool: When Certainty Is the Veil

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 44

### The Arabic

> [ayah:27:44] قِيلَ لَهَا ٱدْخُلِى ٱلصَّرْحَ ۖ فَلَمَّا رَأَتْهُ حَسِبَتْهُ لُجَّةً وَكَشَفَتْ عَن سَاقَيْهَا ۚ قَالَ إِنَّهُۥ صَرْحٌ مُّمَرَّدٌ مِّن قَوَارِيرَ ۗ قَالَتْ رَبِّ إِنِّى ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِى وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَـٰنَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

### Transliteration

*Qīla lahā adkhulī aṣ-ṣarḥ. Fa-lammā ra'at-hu ḥasibat-hu lujjatan wa kashafat ʿan sāqayhā. Qāla innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr. Qālat rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī wa aslamtu maʿa Sulaymāna li-llāhi rabbi-l-ʿālamīn.*

### Translation

> "It was said to her, 'Enter the palace.' But when she saw it, she thought it was a deep pool of water and uncovered her shins. He said, 'Indeed, it is a palace paved smooth with glass.' She said, 'My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Solomon to Allah, Lord of the worlds.'"

---

## Introduction: The Queen Who Almost Got Wet

Imagine the woman walking toward this floor.

She is not a desert nomad. She is the Queen of Sabaʾ — Bilqīs in the tradition — ruler of one of the most refined kingdoms of the ancient Near East. Her people have built irrigation systems that the Qur'ān itself will later mourn. She sits on a throne the surah has already described as *ʿarsh ʿaẓīm* — a tremendous throne. She has counsellors. She has eyes that have seen the inside of every chamber in her own palace. She is not someone you can fool with a trick of light.

And she has spent the last several days being thoroughly out-classed. She sent gifts; Sulaymān (peace be upon him) sent them back with contempt for the gesture. She arrived expecting to negotiate; she found her own throne sitting in his court before she could finish the journey. Each time, what she thought she knew was rearranged.

So now she walks toward the palace. The instruction is simple: *adkhulī aṣ-ṣarḥ* — *enter*. She lifts her gaze. And in front of her is what every eye in that climate would read instantly: water. A pool. *Lujjah* — not a puddle, not a bath, but **deep water**, the kind that swallows feet up to the knee. A queen does not wade into court in soaked robes. So she does the practical thing. She lifts her hem. She bares her shins.

And she is wrong. Catastrophically, intimately wrong. About a floor.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'ān has just spent fifty verses showing her conquered by Sulaymān's wisdom, his army, his jinn, his throne-relocation. None of it broke her. What breaks her is **glass mistaken for water.** A surface she misread by inches.

Stay with that. The thing that finally undoes the queen is not the army of Sulaymān. It is the discovery that she was certain about something she could see — and her certainty was wrong.

What is Allah doing with this scene? Why end the longest narrative in the surah with a woman almost stepping into a pool that does not exist?

That is what this reflection is about.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to dwell on three pieces of this ayah. A verb of cognition. A description of a floor. And a small word that almost no translation carries — the word *maʿa*.

Each one, by itself, is a key. Together they unlock something that the surah has been building toward for forty-three verses.

### Element One: *ḥasibat-hu* — She *Reckoned* It

Read it in Arabic again: *fa-lammā ra'at-hu ḥasibat-hu lujjatan*. "When she saw it, she reckoned it a pool."

There are several Arabic verbs for "to think." The Qur'ān uses each of them with care. There is *ẓanna* — to suppose, to incline toward an opinion that may or may not be true. There is *ʿalima* — to *know*, with certainty. There is *raʾā* — to see, to view, sometimes to hold an opinion. And there is the verb here: *ḥasiba*.

The root *ḥ-s-b* is the root of *ḥisāb* — calculation, accounting, reckoning. It is the root the Qur'ān uses for the *Day of Reckoning*: *yawm al-ḥisāb*. *Ḥasiba* is not lazy thinking. It is **thinking that has done the math.** It is the conclusion you arrive at after your senses have processed the evidence and your mind has run the calculation.

Let's pause on this. Because in English we say "she thought it was water" and the sentence sounds like a guess. In Arabic, *ḥasibat-hu lujjatan* is not a guess. It is the verdict of a competent observer who has looked, processed, and concluded. She did not glance and assume. She *assessed*.

This matters because of who she is. She is a queen. She is not a person prone to careless impressions. The Qur'ān has gone out of its way to show us her competence — she consults her advisors, she tests Sulaymān with gifts, she travels with her own intelligence. *Ḥasiba* tells us: **her best faculties were engaged, and her best faculties got it wrong.**

Notice something: the Qur'ān could have said *ẓannat-hu lujjatan* — "she supposed it." That would have made her a casual mistaker. *Ḥasibat-hu* makes her a careful mistaker. And a careful mistaker is a far more devastating figure. Because if competence can produce certainty, and that certainty can be wrong, then competence itself is not the destination.

[PAUSE]

There is a thread of this verb running through the Qur'ān, and once you see it you cannot un-see it. Allah uses *ḥasiba* repeatedly for the moments people are most confidently wrong:

- *Wa yaḥsabūna annahum yuḥsinūn* — "and they reckon that they are doing well" (18:104). The losers of the hereafter, *certain* their works are good.
- *Taḥsabuhum jamīʿan wa qulūbuhum shattā* — "you reckon them united, but their hearts are scattered" (59:14).
- *Aʿmāluhum ka-sarābin bi-qīʿatin yaḥsabuhu-ẓ-ẓamʾānu māʾan* — "their deeds are like a mirage in a flatland; **the thirsty one reckons it water**" (24:39).

Look at that last one. The thirsty traveller looks at the shimmering desert and *reckons* it water. The same verb. The same mistake. A surface that *looks* like water and is not.

Allah is doing something with this word. He is associating it, again and again, with the precise moment a confident mind takes a surface for a substance. The sun-glare of the desert. The works that look righteous. The community that looks united. The floor that looks wet.

And here, in our ayah, the most powerful queen in the world joins that lineage. *Ḥasibat-hu lujjatan.*

What this single verb is doing is preparing us for a conclusion the surah has been quietly building toward: **the problem is not stupidity. The problem is calibrated intelligence pointed at a surface.** Bilqīs is not foolish. She is excellent. And excellence, untouched by revelation, sees water where there is glass.

### Element Two: *ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr* — A Polished Palace of Glass

Now stay with the floor itself. Sulaymān corrects her: *innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr*. Three words and they are doing more than describing a floor.

*Ṣarḥ* — a high, exposed structure. The lexicons describe it as something built up clearly, visible from afar, with no concealment in its construction. It is the *opposite* of a hidden chamber. A *ṣarḥ* announces itself.

*Mumarrad* — from the root *m-r-d*. The root carries two related meanings that classical lexicons preserve together: **smoothness** and **strippedness**. A *mumarrad* surface is one that has been polished until nothing clings to it — no roughness, no obstruction. The same root, in a darker register, gives us *mārid* and *mutamarrid* — one stripped of restraint, a rebel. The image, on its physical level, is of a surface so finished that nothing can grip it.

*Min qawārīr* — made of *qawārīr*. Plural of *qārūrah*. Glass. Specifically the kind of clear, transparent vessel-glass.

So the floor Sulaymān has built is: **high, exposed, polished smooth, transparent.** Now hear the description as a whole. There is nothing hidden about this surface. It is the most disclosed thing in the palace. It is more visible than walls. It is more honest than stone. You can see *through* it. The water below — if there is water below, and many of the classical accounts describe fish swimming beneath it — is fully visible.

And yet she sees water *as* the floor.

[PAUSE]

Stop and feel this.

The floor was never hiding anything. The floor was glass — the very material whose nature is to *not* hide. The most transparent substance possible. And her eye, calibrated by every floor she had ever walked on, looked at maximum transparency and read it as maximum opacity. She looked through the glass and saw a pool.

This is not Sulaymān deceiving her. This is reality, fully on display, *being* the test. The transparency itself was the trial.

Let me say that again because it is the heart of the ayah: **what fooled her was not concealment. What fooled her was clarity she had never been trained to see.**

Let's sit with what *kashafat ʿan sāqayhā* — "she uncovered her shins" — does inside this picture. The verb *kashafa* is the same verb used throughout the Qur'ān for *unveiling, removing a covering*. It is the verb of *kashf* — disclosure. *Yawma yukshafu ʿan sāq* (68:42) — "the day a shin shall be uncovered" — uses it for the unveiling of an ultimate reality.

So here is the geometry of the moment: **a floor that does not need to be unveiled — because it is already glass — meets a queen who unveils her own shin to walk through water that is not there.** The unveiling is on the wrong side. The reality was already disclosed. The veil was on her perception.

And the moment she understands this, she does not say "I was tricked." She does not blame the floor. She does not blame Sulaymān. She turns inward.

This is where the ayah pivots.

### Element Three: *aslamtu maʿa Sulaymān* — She Submits *With* Him, Not *To* Him

Read what she says: *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī wa aslamtu maʿa Sulaymāna li-llāhi rabbi-l-ʿālamīn.*

"My Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit *with* Sulaymān, to Allah, Lord of the worlds."

There is one word in there that almost no English translation can carry, and it is the word that shows you what is actually happening in her heart.

*Maʿa.*

*With.*

She does not say *aslamtu li-Sulaymān* — "I submit *to* Sulaymān." That would have been the natural expectation. He is the king. She is the visiting queen, defeated, her court relocated, her certainty broken. The political grammar of the moment demands submission to him. He has out-thought her, out-built her, out-imagined her at every turn.

She refuses that grammar. She says *maʿa Sulaymān* — *alongside* him, *together with* him. Not under him. Not below him. **Beside him**, facing the same direction.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this.

Sulaymān (peace be upon him) is not the destination. He is a fellow worshipper. The whole staggering display — the throne, the army, the jinn, the glass floor — was never a demonstration of *his* greatness. It was a demonstration of *what becomes possible when a human being submits.* And she sees it. In the moment her sandal almost touches what she thought was water, she sees that the right response to his power is not to bow to him but to stand *next to him, facing the same way.*

The grammar of *maʿa* is the grammar of *islām*. Islām is never a vertical relationship between two humans. It is two humans, side by side, both bowed in the same direction.

And one more thing. Look at the order of her words. *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī* comes *first*. "My Lord, I have wronged myself." *Then* the submission. She names the wrong before she names the surrender. This is the architecture of every authentic turning in the Qur'ān: see the wrong, then turn. She does not skip the diagnosis. She does not rush past her own complicity. She sits in *ẓalamtu nafsī* — "I have wronged my own self" — and from that honesty, *aslamtu* emerges.

And note what she said *not*. She did not say "I was deceived." She did not say "I was misled." She did not say "the floor tricked me." Every one of those would have located the wrong outside her. She locates it inside: *I wronged myself.* The floor was honest. The glass was honest. The eye, untrained by revelation, was the deceiver — and the eye was hers.

This is *ẓulm al-nafs* — wronging the self — at its most precise. Not a moral crime. An *epistemic* one. She had been trusting a faculty that was not equipped for the reality in front of it. And the wronging she names is the wrong of having lived for so long inside a smaller picture, when a truer one was available.

### Bridge

Three words. *Ḥasibat-hu*. *Mumarradun min qawārīr*. *Maʿa.*

A verb that tells us her best faculties were engaged. A floor that tells us reality was fully disclosed. A preposition that tells us submission is horizontal among worshippers and vertical only toward Allah.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Pool That Wasn't — When Confidence Becomes the Veil

I want you to try something. Think of the last time you were absolutely certain about something — and you were wrong.

Not a small wrong. A wrong about something you would have staked your judgement on. About a person's character. About what a relationship was. About what a verse meant. About who you were.

Notice that the discovery, when it came, did not feel like learning. It felt like *the floor giving way.* That phrase exists in every language for a reason. Because something we trusted as solid turned out to be something else.

Now here is the difficulty: most of us, when the floor gives way, do what comes naturally. We blame the floor. We say *I was misled. I was lied to. The information was bad.* Sometimes it was. But often — more often than we let ourselves see — what was wrong was not the floor. **What was wrong was the eye.**

This is the trial Bilqīs walks into. The Qur'ān is showing us, at the climax of her story, that the most dangerous thing is not ignorance. The most dangerous thing is **confident misperception by competent people.** Because ignorance knows it is ignorance. Confident misperception thinks it is knowledge.

Modern psychology has a name for this. It is called the *Dunning–Kruger* effect at one end and *epistemic overconfidence* at the other. The pattern is the same: the more proficient your faculties, the more you trust their verdict, and the less likely you are to test that verdict against something outside it. You become the queen walking toward the pool, hem already gathered, calibration already complete.

What does revelation do, in this picture? Revelation is the voice that says *innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr.* "It is a polished palace of glass." Revelation does not work harder than your perception. It does not shout. It does not bully. It simply names what is actually there — and waits for you to lower your hem.

Stay with me. Because there is a question this raises that you must not skip past.

How much of your life have you been wading through, hem lifted, *certain* of pools that do not exist?

The grudge you have carried for fifteen years against someone — what if it was glass? The certainty that you are not the kind of person Allah forgives — what if it was glass? The conviction that this version of yourself is the final version — what if it was glass? The belief that you understand exactly what your spouse is thinking, what your child needs, what your community wants from you — what if some of those, *not all of them, but some*, are pools that are not there?

The Bilqīs response is not "be paralyzed by doubt." That is not what she does. She does not stop walking. She does not refuse to enter. She does not become permanently suspicious of every floor.

She says one thing: *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī.*

*My Lord, I have wronged myself.*

That sentence is not despair. It is the precise opposite of despair. It is the *recovery* of trust — not in her own eye, but in **the One whose disclosure does not deceive.** It is the moment a person stops trusting the calibration of their own senses as the final court, and accepts that there is a higher court whose seeing is not subject to the same surface-trick.

The practical work: this week, take one certainty — one thing you are sure you know about yourself, about a person in your life, about what Allah thinks of you — and bring it to Allah with the words *Rabbi, in kāna ẓulman, faḍiḥ-hu lī.* "My Lord, if this is a wrong I am committing against my own self, expose it to me." Not *fix it*. Not *change it*. Just *show it to me.* Because the queen did not need the floor changed. She needed the floor *named.* And the moment Sulaymān named it, she could walk.

### Theme Two: *Maʿa* — The Geometry of Submission

There is a temptation, when you read this story, to make it about Sulaymān's greatness. The Qur'ān does not make that mistake. The Qur'ān ends the entire narrative with Bilqīs's word *maʿa* — and that word disqualifies the temptation.

Let me tell you why this matters, because it touches something almost everyone listening to this is wrestling with whether they know it or not.

When a human being is overwhelmed by another human being's gift, intelligence, presence, knowledge, charisma, success — there is a gravitational pull to bow. Not always physically. But emotionally, intellectually, spiritually. We orient our lives around the person we admire. We start to want what they want. We measure ourselves by their gaze. We submit, in a thousand small ways, *to them.*

This is one of the great quiet idolatries of human life, and it is so common that we no longer recognize it as religious behaviour. The follower who orients his entire identity around a teacher. The young person whose every decision is calibrated to a parent's approval. The convert who attaches their faith to a single scholar and trembles when the scholar errs. The husband or wife who has slowly arranged their soul around their spouse's preferences. The professional whose moral compass has quietly become their employer's reputation.

In every case, the structure is the same: a human submitting *to* another human, instead of *with* them.

And in every case, the moment the human you submitted *to* falls — and they do fall, because every human falls — your faith falls with them. Because your faith was tied to a person, not to *rabb al-ʿālamīn*.

Bilqīs sees this. In the most dazzling presence in the world, she does not bow to the dazzler. She bows beside him.

Read it again: *aslamtu maʿa Sulaymāna li-llāhi rabbi-l-ʿālamīn.* The word *li-llāhi* — *to Allah* — is the only vertical preposition in the sentence. *Maʿa* is horizontal. *Li-* is vertical. She gets the geometry right. To Allah, vertically. With Sulaymān, horizontally.

Now here is what is breathtaking. Sulaymān does not correct her on this. He does not say "no, submit to me, I am the king and you are my subject." Because he, too, gets it. The whole reason the throne was brought, the whole reason the floor was built, the whole reason any of it happened was to bring a soul to the place where she could stand *beside him* — both of them facing Allah.

The greatest gift Sulaymān gave her was not the spectacle. It was the *direction*. He pointed. She looked where he pointed. And what she saw was not him. It was *rabb al-ʿālamīn*.

A great human being, in the framework of this ayah, is one who, when looked at, redirects the look. Sulaymān is great because to look at him is to be turned toward Allah. Bilqīs is great because she lets herself be turned. Neither of them keeps the gaze.

[PAUSE]

So ask: who in your life have you been submitting *to* when you should have been submitting *with*? Who has become so large in your inner sky that they have started to occupy the place reserved for *rabb al-ʿālamīn*? It might be a teacher. It might be a parent. It might be a spouse. It might be a movement. It might — and this is the hardest one — be a version of *yourself*: the imagined ideal self, the curated identity, the role you have been playing, the success you have been chasing. Anything you submit *to*, instead of submitting *with*, has quietly assumed the throne.

The remedy is not to disrespect the person. Bilqīs does not insult Sulaymān. She honours him precisely by standing beside him, both bowed in the same direction. The remedy is to *re-tilt the geometry*: keep the relationship, but move the verticality.

This week, take one relationship in your life where the gravity has tilted — and consciously rebuild the *maʿa*. Pray *with* them, not *for their approval*. Read the Qur'ān *with* them, both of you bowed toward the same horizon. The submission you owe each other is horizontal. The submission you owe Allah is the only vertical line in the room.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Look at the shape of this single ayah:

1. *Qīla lahā adkhulī aṣ-ṣarḥ* — a command to enter.
2. *Ḥasibat-hu lujjatan wa kashafat ʿan sāqayhā* — her perception, calibrated and confident, fails.
3. *Innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr* — revelation names the reality.
4. *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī* — she names the wrong.
5. *Aslamtu maʿa Sulaymāna li-llāhi rabbi-l-ʿālamīn* — she submits, horizontally with him, vertically to Allah.

Five movements. Command. Misperception. Disclosure. Confession. Submission.

Notice something: this is the architecture of every spiritual transformation in the Qur'ān, miniaturized. A call. A confident wrong reading. A higher voice that names the truth. A self-honest confession. A re-orientation. The story of Bilqīs is the story of every soul that has ever been called to Allah. The only difference is that hers happens in front of a glass floor and ours happens in front of a Qur'ān or a death or an illness or a quiet hour after Fajr.

### Linguistic and Thematic Convergence

The verb *ḥasiba* told us that her best faculties failed. The floor of *qawārīr* told us that the failure was not because reality was hidden, but because reality was more transparent than she had been taught to see. The word *maʿa* told us that her recovery was not to bow to the human in front of her, but to stand beside him and bow to Allah.

These are the same insight from three angles. Untrained perception will mistake the most disclosed thing in the universe for something else. The remedy is not better perception. The remedy is *islām* — alignment with the One whose disclosure is reality itself, alongside everyone else who is bowed in that direction.

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life am I currently *ḥasiba*-ing — calculating with confidence — that may be a glass floor I am reading as a pool?
2. Where have I lifted my hem when the reality in front of me did not need wading at all?
3. Who in my life have I been submitting *to* when I should have been submitting *with*?
4. When was the last time I said, with the precision of Bilqīs, *Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī* — locating the wrong inside myself rather than in the world?
5. If a voice tomorrow named one of my certainties as glass instead of water, would I respond like Bilqīs — or would I defend my reading of the floor?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the most competent perception in the world meets the most disclosed reality in the world and reads it wrong, the only honest response is to name the wrong inside yourself and fall in beside whoever is already bowed toward Allah.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to find a glass floor. You are already standing on one.

Every day, the world Allah has made is more transparent than your eye is trained to see. Every day, you are walking toward surfaces you have *ḥasiba*-ed into the wrong category. The Qur'ān is the voice that says, gently, again and again — *innahu ṣarḥun mumarradun min qawārīr.* It is not what you think it is. Lower your hem. Walk. And when you understand what you almost stepped into, do not blame the floor. Turn to *rabb al-ʿālamīn*, name the wrong as your own, and find someone bowed in the right direction to stand beside.

### Du'a

*Rabbanā, our eyes have read pools where Your floors were glass, and we have lifted our hems for waters that were never there. Strip the calibration of our perception until we see what You have placed before us as it is.*

*Allāhumma, do not let us submit to anything beneath Your throne. Place us beside Your servants who are bowed in Your direction, and keep the only vertical line in our hearts pointed toward You alone — rabb al-ʿālamīn.*

*Āmīn.*
