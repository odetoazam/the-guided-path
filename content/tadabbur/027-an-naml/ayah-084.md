# The Question That Cannot Be Answered

## When Speech Itself Becomes the Verdict

---

## Introduction: The Moment of Arrival

> حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا جَآءُو قَالَ أَكَذَّبْتُم بِـَٔايَـٰتِى وَلَمْ تُحِيطُوا۟ بِهَا عِلْمًا أَمَّاذَا كُنتُمْ تَعْمَلُونَ

*Ḥattā idhā jā'ū qāla a-kadhdhabtum bi-āyātī wa-lam tuḥīṭū bihā ʿilman ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn*

**"Until, when they arrive, He will say: 'Did you deny My signs without encompassing them in knowledge? Or what was it that you used to do?'"**

---

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine you are sitting in the early Meccan community. The surah you are listening to has just told you something extraordinary. It opened with the bismillah, then with the words: *Ṭā Sīn. Tilka āyātu-l-Qur'āni wa kitābin mubīn* — "These are the signs of the Qur'an and a clarifying Book." The whole surah, from its first letter, is about **signs** — *āyāt*.

And then it walked you through stories of people who **paid attention** to signs.

Mūsā at the fire — he turned aside to look. He didn't dismiss it. He went to investigate.

Sulaymān listened to an **ant**. An ant. The smallest possible voice in creation, and a prophet-king bent down to understand what it was saying.

A hudhud — a bird — brought him news, and instead of dismissing the messenger, he said: "We will see whether you have spoken truth or are of the liars." He **investigated**.

The Queen of Sheba received a letter. She didn't tear it up. She gathered her council. She thought. She tested. She came to see for herself.

Every story in this surah, until now, has been about people who **encountered something unfamiliar and chose to engage with it carefully**.

[PAUSE]

And now, near the end of the surah, Allah turns the camera. He shows us another kind of person. The opposite kind. The kind who heard the signs and dismissed them without ever turning aside to look.

And He shows us the moment they finally arrive — not on their terms, but on His.

---

### The Hook

There is a question in this ayah that, on the surface, looks like one question. It is actually two.

And the second one — the second one is more terrifying than the first.

Because the first question, at least, you could try to answer.

The second one **cannot be answered**. By design.

Let's find out why.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1: The Word That Decides Everything — *Tuḥīṭū*

Before we touch the ayah, let me show you a word that lives in your everyday Arabic — and probably in your everyday life — without you noticing.

In Arabic, when you want to say a **wall** surrounds a garden, you use a word from the root **ḥ-w-ṭ**. The wall is a *ḥā'iṭ*. It goes all the way around. Nothing missing. No gap. The garden is completely enclosed.

From the same root comes a word for the ocean — *al-muḥīṭ* — because it surrounds the continents. From the same root comes *iḥāṭa* — to surround, to enclose, to encompass on every side.

Now stay with me, because here is the key idea:

When this root is applied to knowledge — *iḥāṭa bi-l-ʿilm* — it does not mean "to know a little about something." It does not mean "to have heard of something." It means **to know it from every angle. To circle it completely. To leave no side unexamined.**

It is the knowledge of someone who has walked all the way around the garden wall and touched every stone.

[PAUSE]

Now open the ayah.

> *a-kadhdhabtum bi-āyātī wa-lam tuḥīṭū bihā ʿilman*

**"Did you deny My signs without encompassing them in knowledge?"**

Notice something. Allah does not say: "Did you deny My signs without knowing them?" He does not say: "without studying them," or "without reading them," or "without hearing them."

He says: **without encompassing them.**

Let's sit with this. The accusation is not ignorance. The accusation is a very specific kind of ignorance — **the ignorance of one who passed judgment before completing the investigation**.

You walked up to the garden, glanced over one part of the wall, decided you didn't like what you saw, and **declared the whole garden false**. You never walked around it. You never looked at the other sides. You never asked what was beyond the angle you happened to be standing at.

Here is what is remarkable about this word choice. Allah is not demanding that they had **achieved** complete knowledge. He is asking why they **rejected** something before they had even attempted to.

The verb is in the **negated past** — *wa-lam tuḥīṭū*. "And you did not encompass." Meaning: you closed the file before you opened it. You issued the verdict before you heard the case.

[PAUSE]

There is a sister ayah that lives in the same space — Sūrah Yūnus, ayah 39: *Bal kadhdhabū bimā lam yuḥīṭū bi-ʿilmihi* — "Rather, they denied that which they did not encompass in knowledge."

Two ayahs. Same diagnosis. Same verb. The Qur'an is telling us this is not an accident of a few people. This is a **pattern of human denial** — and Allah has named it.

The pattern is: rejection without investigation.

And what Allah's question reveals — quietly, but with surgical precision — is that **denial is a claim to total knowledge**. To say "this is false" is to say "I have walked around the whole thing and I have seen its falseness from every side." If you have not done that, your denial is not a conclusion. It is a posture.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is uncomfortable. Because the question is not only being asked of polytheists fourteen hundred years ago. The question is being asked of a faculty inside every human being.

How many things have we dismissed without encompassing them?

How many ayahs of the Qur'an — physical signs, internal signs, prophetic signs — have we glanced at, felt unconvinced, and closed the file on?

The garden was there. We never walked around it.

---

### Element 2: The Two-Headed Question — *A...am...*

Now I want to show you something about the **structure** of Allah's question. Because in Arabic, this is not one question. It is two questions joined by a particle that does not exist in English with quite the same force.

In Arabic, when you want to offer a real alternative — "Was it A, or was it B?" — you use the particle ***am***. Not *aw* (which is loose, like "or maybe"). ***Am*** is sharper. It says: **one of these two, and only one of these two**.

Now look at the ayah again:

> *a-kadhdhabtum bi-āyātī wa-lam tuḥīṭū bihā ʿilman **am**mādhā kuntum taʿmalūn*

**"Did you deny My signs without encompassing them in knowledge — *or* what was it that you used to do?"**

Allah is presenting **two options** and demanding one of them.

Option 1: "Yes, we denied Your signs without ever investigating them."

Option 2: "...what?"

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with option 2 for a moment. Because option 2 is where the architecture of this ayah becomes devastating.

Notice that option 1 is **specified**. Allah names the crime: *a-kadhdhabtum bi-āyātī* — did you deny My signs? He puts the accusation on the table.

Option 2 is **not specified**. He says: *ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "or **what was it** that you used to do?"

He doesn't name option 2. He **leaves it blank**.

And He hands the blank to the one being questioned.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable about this. Imagine a courtroom. The judge says: "Did you commit this crime? Or — tell me, what *were* you doing instead?"

There is no answer to that second question. Because anyone being asked it knows that the only acceptable alternative to "I denied" would be "I believed and I acted." And if they had believed and acted, they would not be standing in that courtroom on that day.

The blank is rhetorical, but it is not empty. The blank is **the space where their lives should have been**. Allah is essentially saying: if it wasn't denial, then what *was* it? What were the days for? What was the speech for? What was the breath for?

Tell Me what you did with the signs — because if you didn't deny them, you must have done **something** with them.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most quietly merciful and quietly terrifying constructions in the Qur'an. Mercy, because Allah opens a door — *He doesn't only accuse; He invites a response*. Terrifying, because the door opens onto a hallway with no exit.

There is no third option. You either engaged with the signs — encompassed them, struggled with them, submitted to them — or you didn't. And if you didn't, the question *ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn* is not a question. It is a verdict shaped like a question.

[PAUSE]

The grammar invites us to consider something that should shake us. On that Day, the question will not be: "What did you believe in your private heart?" The question will include: **"What were you doing?"** *Taʿmalūn* — from ʿ-m-l — work, action, deeds.

Belief and action. Both. Sitting inside that little particle **am**.

---

### Element 3: The Word That Allah Owns — *Āyātī*

There is one more thing I want you to see, and it is small. So small that you might miss it.

Look at the word for signs: *āyātī*.

In Arabic, when you attach the suffix *-ī* to the end of a noun, it means "my." *Kitāb* = book. *Kitābī* = my book.

So *āyātī* means: **My signs.**

Not "the signs." Not "those signs." Not "signs in general."

**My signs.** Possessive. Personal. Attached to Allah Himself by a single letter at the end of the word.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because something just shifted in the courtroom.

When you reject "an idea," you are rejecting a concept. When you reject "a teaching," you are rejecting a doctrine. When you reject "a sign," you are rejecting evidence.

But when you reject **someone's sign** — when the sign belongs to a Person, and the Person is naming it as theirs — you are not rejecting an abstraction.

You are rejecting **Him**.

The grammar makes the encounter personal. Allah does not say: "Did you deny the signs?" He says: **"Did you deny My signs?"**

Every star you walked under. Every breath you drew. Every ayah you heard recited. Every moment of doubt that whispered to you that maybe — *maybe* — there was something true here. All of those: **Mine.**

You were not in a dispute with a tradition. You were in a dispute with the One who placed every sign in your path.

[PAUSE]

And here is where the linguistic construction lands with full weight.

The verb *kadhdhaba* (Form II of k-dh-b) is **intensive**. Form II in Arabic does not just mean "to lie" or "to consider false." It carries the sense of **active, repeated declaration** that something is false. To *kadhdhaba* something is to put energy into rejecting it. To make rejection a habit.

So now read the question with everything we've seen:

**"Did you actively, repeatedly declare false — what I, Myself, called Mine — without ever walking around it to see what it actually was?"**

[PAUSE]

That is the question.

That is what will be asked.

---

### Bridge: From the Words to What They Reveal

We have looked at three things. *Tuḥīṭū* — the word that says denial requires complete knowledge, and almost no one has it. *Am* — the particle that opens a door onto a hallway with no exit. *Āyātī* — the single letter that makes the dispute personal.

Each, on its own, is a precise piece of grammar. Together, they form an interrogation that no one can escape.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about how denial actually works inside a human being, and how the Day of Judgment is structured to reveal it.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1: The Anatomy of Lazy Rejection

Let me start somewhere familiar.

You scroll through your phone. A headline appears about a topic you don't really know. You read three sentences. You feel something — annoyance, maybe, or skepticism. You decide: "This is wrong." You scroll on.

You did not read the article. You did not read the study. You did not read the response, the counter-response, the underlying argument, the history. You did not walk around the garden.

You **issued a verdict on the basis of a glance**.

This happens a hundred times a day. To all of us. It is, in fact, how the modern mind survives — by triaging information so fast that most of it gets sorted into "true / false / ignore" within seconds. We have no choice; the alternative is paralysis.

[PAUSE]

But here is what the ayah does. It takes that everyday cognitive habit — that triage instinct, that necessary mental shortcut — and it puts it on trial. It says: when you applied this habit to **the most important signs you ever encountered**, were you aware that you were doing it?

When the Qur'an was recited and something in you closed before the next ayah finished — did you ever pause and ask: am I rejecting this because I have encompassed it, or because I have not?

When the call to prayer crossed your hearing and you felt nothing — did you ever wonder if "feeling nothing" was the absence of a sign, or the absence of attention?

When a sign appeared in your life — a death, a survival, a coincidence so sharp it should have stopped you — did you walk around it? Or did you label it, file it, and move on?

[PAUSE]

The ayah is not condemning ignorance. Ignorance is a fact of being finite. What the ayah condemns is **the marriage of ignorance to confidence**. The willingness to say "this is false" without having earned that sentence.

And here is the diagnosis, gently named in a single grammatical construction:

**Denial is almost always premature.**

Belief, of course, can also be premature — and the Qur'an is full of warnings about that too. But this ayah names a specific human failure: we are creatures who reject quickly, and we mistake our quickness for clarity.

[PAUSE]

What does this change?

It changes how we hold our own dismissals.

The next time you find yourself rejecting something — a teaching, a person, a sign, a possibility — try a small experiment. Ask yourself: *am I encompassing this, or am I dismissing it?* Have I walked around the whole wall, or have I only seen the one side that's facing me right now?

If the honest answer is "I haven't walked around it" — that is not a command to believe. It is a command to be **honest about the status of your judgment**. To say: "I do not yet know enough to dismiss this."

That is not weakness. That is the only intellectual integrity that will survive the question in this ayah.

---

### Theme 2: The Question That Has No Answer

Now the harder theme. The one that lives inside option 2.

*Ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn* — "or what was it that you used to do?"

I want you to feel the weight of this question. Because we live inside it right now.

Every day of our lives is being inscribed onto a record. Every act, every silence, every choice to engage with the signs of Allah or to walk past them. The ayah is not telling us about a hypothetical future. It is telling us about a question that is being **built into the substance of our days**, right now, while we are alive and able to shape the answer.

[PAUSE]

Let me put it differently.

Imagine that tomorrow, somebody walked up to you and asked: "What was your life for?"

Not "what did you accomplish." Not "what did you achieve." But: *what was it for?* What was the underlying activity of your existence? What were you doing with the time, with the breath, with the signs that flooded your eyes from the moment you opened them?

For many of us — and I include myself here without exception — the honest answer would be a kind of stutter. A list of half-things. Some work. Some entertainment. Some relationships. Some moments of trying. A lot of moments of drifting.

The question hangs in the air.

[PAUSE]

What this ayah does is take that question — which sounds, in this life, like a slightly anxious philosophical musing — and place it inside the mouth of Allah on the Day of Judgment.

When *He* asks it, it is no longer a musing.

It is the question that organizes your eternity.

And the construction *kuntum taʿmalūn* — using *kāna* with the imperfect — does not mean "what did you do once." It means: **what was it that you used to do, habitually, continuously, as the pattern of your life?**

Not the highlights. The **pattern**.

[PAUSE]

Here is where the two themes meet.

The first question — "did you deny My signs without encompassing them?" — diagnoses **the lazy verdict**.

The second question — "or what was it that you used to do?" — diagnoses **the empty pattern**.

Between them, there is no third option. Either you took the signs seriously enough to investigate, and you acted on what you found — or you didn't.

If you investigated and submitted, you are not standing in this courtroom.

If you investigated and rejected after **truly encompassing** — well, the ayah does not contemplate this option, because the Qur'an's premise is that the signs, when actually encompassed, are not dismissable. The pattern of denial in the Qur'an is always: *kadhdhabū bimā lam yuḥīṭū* — they denied what they did not encompass.

So the two questions, together, leave one place to stand: **the place of those who took the signs seriously enough to walk around them, and then lived as if what they found was true.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the meaning of any ordinary day.

If the question *ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn* is the question, then **today is the answer being written**. Not in some abstract sense. In the most literal sense. The pattern of your hours today is the sentence that will be read back on that Day.

So a small practice: at the end of each day, ask yourself one question. Not "was I productive." Not "was I happy." Just: *ammādhā kuntu aʿmalu al-yawm?* — what was I doing today? What was the pattern? If Allah asked me right now to summarize this day in one verb — what verb would I have to use?

If the verb is "drifting," that is information. If the verb is "consuming," that is information. If the verb is "remembering," that is information. If the verb is "trying," that is information.

The point is not to torture yourself. The point is to **let the question of that Day reach you in this day** — early enough to still shape the answer.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Step back now and look at the ayah whole.

> *Ḥattā idhā jā'ū qāla a-kadhdhabtum bi-āyātī wa-lam tuḥīṭū bihā ʿilman ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn*

It begins with **arrival** — *idhā jā'ū*, "when they arrive." A moment of presence. They are now standing where they tried for a lifetime not to stand.

It moves to **speech** — *qāla*, "He will say." The One who was denied is now the One who speaks. The order of the courtroom is restored.

Then the **two questions**, joined by *am*:

— A specific accusation: did you deny what was Mine without encompassing it?
— An open blank: or what *were* you doing?

The first question diagnoses the **mind's failure** — the lazy verdict.
The second question diagnoses the **life's failure** — the empty pattern.

Between them is the only door — and we are still on this side of it.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: *tuḥīṭū* and *am* and *āyātī* together describe a personal interrogation where denial requires what no one has, and the alternative to denial is a pattern of life that no one can fake.

From the thematic side: lazy rejection and empty pattern are the two failures the Day of Judgment is designed to expose.

The Qur'an, in one ayah, names both. And it does so not to terrify us into paralysis — it does so to invite us, while we still have time, to **encompass before we conclude, and to live the pattern we would be willing to answer for**.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What have I dismissed in my life without having walked around it? Not abstractly — what specifically?

2. When was the last time I treated a sign — a moment, a death, a mercy, an ayah — as if it actually belonged to **Someone**?

3. If Allah asked me tonight, *ammādhā kunta taʿmal al-yawm* — what was it you were doing today — what verb would honestly summarize my hours?

4. Where in my life have I confused **quickness of judgment** with **clarity of understanding**?

5. If I knew that the pattern of this week would be read back to me on the Day, what one thing would I add, and what one thing would I subtract?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day of arrival, Allah will ask two questions that have only one answer between them — and the answer is being written in the pattern of our days right now.

---

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking us to fear. It is asking us to **encompass**.

To encompass the signs we have inherited and the signs we have been given. To walk around the wall of what we don't yet understand, instead of pronouncing on it from the angle we happen to be standing at. To let our days have a verb that we would not be ashamed to say out loud when the question comes.

The mercy is that the question has not yet been asked.

We are still in the time before *idhā jā'ū* — before the arrival.

The signs are still around us. The wall is still walkable. The pattern is still being shaped.

What you do with the next hour is part of the answer.

---

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who own the signs and call them Yours — do not let us be among those who dismissed what You spoke before we had the honesty to encompass it. Make our verdicts slow and our submission quick. And when the question comes — *ammādhā kuntum taʿmalūn* — let our days, by Your mercy, speak the answer for us.

*Āmīn.*
