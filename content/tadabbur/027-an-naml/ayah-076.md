# The Quran as Arbiter: When Allah Speaks to Settle What Israel Could Not

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
إِنَّ هَـٰذَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ يَقُصُّ عَلَىٰ بَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ أَكْثَرَ ٱلَّذِى هُمْ فِيهِ يَخْتَلِفُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Inna hādhā al-Qurʾāna yaquṣṣu ʿalā Banī Isrāʾīla aktharal-ladhī hum fīhi yakhtalifūn*

**Translation:**
"Indeed, this Quran narrates to the Children of Israel most of that over which they differ."

## Introduction: A Book That Steps Into a 1,500-Year-Old Argument

Imagine a family that has been arguing about the same questions for fifteen centuries.

Not arguing about minor things. Arguing about who their prophets really were. Arguing about what their scripture actually said. Arguing about whether a particular son was the one promised, whether a particular law still binds, whether a particular passage was added later, whether a particular story happened the way it was remembered. Arguing about God Himself — His attributes, His covenant, His name.

Imagine that family sitting in a room, and the volume of their argument has grown so loud over so many generations that no one can even remember what the original question was. The library is full of books. The books contradict each other. Each faction quotes different verses. Each faction accuses the others of distortion. Each faction has a tradition explaining why the others are wrong.

Now imagine that into this room — into this fifteen-hundred-year-old argument — a Book arrives.

Not arrives as one more voice. Not arrives to add a new commentary. The Book arrives as the **arbiter**. It walks into the middle of the room and says: *I am here to tell you what most of this is actually about.*

That is the moment Allah is describing in this ayah. Surah An-Naml has just finished walking us through Musa at the burning bush, Sulayman with the ant and the hoopoe, Salih with the she-camel, Lut with his people. These are not Quraysh's stories. These are the stories that the Children of Israel — and the Christian communities that grew from their soil — had been carrying, debating, ritualizing, fighting over, splintering over, for one and a half millennia before Muhammad ﷺ was born.

And right after telling those stories — stories the People of the Book thought they owned — Allah turns and says something that should make every listener stop:

*Indeed, this Quran narrates to the Children of Israel **most of that over which they differ**.*

[PAUSE]

What kind of Book makes that claim? What does it mean for a scripture revealed in Arabic, in Mecca, to a Prophet ﷺ who had no access to the libraries of Jerusalem or Alexandria, to walk into the most ancient theological argument on earth and say: *I will tell you what you have been fighting about?*

Stay with that question. Because the answer to it is not just about them. It is about us.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Hādhā al-Qurʾān* — "This Very Quran"

Before we look at the word, let us teach the structure.

In Arabic, when you want to refer to something, you can use the definite article — *al-* — and just say "the book," "the Quran," "the message." That is the neutral form. It points at the thing without drama.

But when you add a demonstrative pronoun — *hādhā*, meaning "this" — you are doing something different. You are pointing. You are gesturing with your hand. You are saying not "the Quran" in the abstract, but **this** Quran. The one in front of you. The one being recited right now. The one your ears can hear.

Think of the difference in your own life. If a friend says, "the contract is binding," that is one statement. If a friend pulls out a piece of paper, lays it on the table between you, taps it with their finger and says, "**this** contract is binding" — that is a completely different act. The second sentence presses the object into your awareness. It refuses to let you abstract it away.

Now look at the ayah:

*Inna **hādhā** al-Qurʾāna...* — "Indeed, **this** Quran..."

Allah does not say "the Quran." He says **this Quran**. He is pointing. He is laying it on the table. He is forcing the listener — and especially the People of the Book who are the addressees of this verse — to reckon with the specific Book that is being recited in their hearing.

Notice something. This is being revealed in seventh-century Arabia. There were Jewish communities in Khaybar, in Yathrib, in Yemen. There were Christian communities in Najran, in Ethiopia, in Syria. Each of them had their own scriptures. Each of them had their own internal debates about the meaning of those scriptures. And into that landscape, Allah does not say, "There is *a* book that arbitrates your disputes." He says, *this* one. The one being recited in Arabic. The one whose words your skeptics are dismissing as poetry. **This** one.

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet boldness in that demonstrative that we should not miss. The Quran does not present itself as one option in a marketplace of revelations. It presents itself as the specific, locatable, audible Book that has authority to settle what your traditions could not. Walk up to it. Touch it. Open it. **This** is the one.

Stay with that for a moment. What does it cost a Book to make that claim?

It costs everything. Because the moment you say *this*, you have made yourself testable. You have refused the safety of abstraction. You have invited every Jewish scholar, every Christian theologian, every doubter, every skeptic to come and check. You have placed yourself in the middle of the room and said: *examine me.*

That is the posture of *hādhā*. Not "the Quran somewhere," but **this Quran here**.

## Element Two: *Yaquṣṣu* — Not "Argues With," but "Narrates To"

Now we come to a verb that should genuinely surprise us.

In Arabic, when you want to talk about resolving a dispute, there are several verbs available. You could use *yaḥkumu* — "He judges between them." You could use *yafṣilu* — "He decides between them." You could use *yufaṣṣilu* — "He details out for them." Each of these has the flavor of a courtroom: a judge, a verdict, two parties, a ruling.

Allah uses none of those verbs here. He uses **yaquṣṣu**.

The root *q-ṣ-ṣ* means to narrate, to recount, to follow a trail. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of Rāghib — all anchor this root in the image of someone walking along a path, following the footsteps of what came before, telling the story of where the trail leads. *Qaṣaṣ* is "story." *Qiṣṣah* is "tale." A *qāṣṣ* is a storyteller. The verb carries the texture of narration, of unfolding, of laying out a sequence.

Notice what Allah did **not** say. He did not say: "This Quran **judges between** the Children of Israel concerning what they dispute." He said: "This Quran **narrates to** the Children of Israel most of what they dispute."

Stay with that difference. It is enormous.

A judge stands above the parties and pronounces a verdict. A narrator stands beside them and tells them what actually happened. The judge says: "You are wrong, you are right." The narrator says: "Let me tell you the story. Let the story do the work."

Why would Allah choose narration over judgment in this verse?

Here is what is remarkable. The Children of Israel were not in dispute because they lacked verdicts. They had verdicts in abundance. They had rabbinical rulings, patristic decrees, ecumenical councils, sectarian fatāwā stretching back centuries. Adding one more verdict to that pile would not have settled anything. It would have been one more voice in the noise.

What they had lost was the **story**. The original, uncorrupted, sequential account of what their prophets actually said and did and meant. The disputes had grown precisely because the narrative had been fragmented — different communities holding different pieces of the trail, each insisting their fragment was the whole.

So Allah does not send a verdict. He sends a **narration**. He restores the story. *Yaquṣṣu* — He recounts. He follows the trail of the prophets and lays it down again, end to end, so that the disputants can see where their fragments fit and where their fragments were wrong.

[PAUSE]

There is a profound mercy in this choice of verb. Imagine if you had been arguing with your siblings for years about something that happened in your childhood — what your father actually said, what your mother actually meant, what really happened that summer. And imagine someone who was there, who saw everything, finally sits you all down. Would you want them to walk in and start issuing verdicts? *You were wrong, you were right, you misremembered, you were correct.* No. You would want them to **tell the story**. To recount it from the beginning. To let the truth emerge from the narrative itself.

That is what *yaquṣṣu* does. It treats the disputants with the dignity of being told the story rather than handed the verdict. It lets the narrative itself become the arbiter. The Quran does not need to argue — it needs only to **recount**, and where its recounting differs from theirs, the difference itself is the correction.

This is a different kind of authority. It is the authority of the eyewitness, not the magistrate. And in a community that had lost the story, the eyewitness is exactly what was needed.

## Element Three: *Akthara* — "Most," Not "All" — and the Mercy of the Limit

Here is a word so small that you could read past it twenty times without noticing what it is doing.

*Akthara* — "most of."

The Quran narrates to the Children of Israel **most of** that over which they differ. Not all. **Most**.

Now teach the principle first. In Arabic, as in English, there is a precise distinction between *kull* (all) and *akthar* (most). *Kull* is total. It leaves nothing out. *Akthar* is the majority — the dominant share — but it explicitly preserves a remainder. A speaker who says *kull* is making a totalizing claim. A speaker who says *akthar* is making a measured claim that deliberately holds back from totality.

When you are reading the Quran, this kind of measured language should always make you stop. Allah is *al-Ḥakīm* — the Wise. His word choices are not casual. When He uses *akthar* instead of *kull*, He is doing something specific.

So ask the question: why "most" and not "all"? Why does Allah not claim that the Quran will narrate **everything** the Children of Israel disagree about?

Here is what classical scholars sat with. Some of what the Children of Israel disputed was substantive — disputes about tawḥīd, about prophets, about laws, about whether ʿĪsā was a divine son or a human messenger, about whether the Torah's commands still bound them. **These** are the disputes the Quran arbitrates. These are the *akthar* — the majority, the weighty share, the things that matter for salvation.

But some of what the Children of Israel disputed was **not** substantive. It was peripheral. Genealogical minutiae. Ritual details that no longer mattered. Stories whose resolution would change nothing about how a person should worship Allah or live in the world. The color of the cow. The names of the sleepers. The dimensions of the ark.

The Quran does not waste itself on those. It does not dignify peripheral disputes by pronouncing on every one of them. It speaks to **most** — meaning, to all that is necessary for guidance — and leaves the rest unresolved, because resolving them is not what guidance requires.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. There is a stunning principle hidden in *akthar*. **Revelation is not encyclopedic.** Revelation is **sufficient**. Allah does not flood the Quran with answers to every trivial question humans have ever fought about. He answers what salvation requires. The rest, He leaves silent — and the silence itself is a teaching. It tells us: this dispute did not matter. Stop fighting about it.

There is also a deep mercy here, a kind of corrective restraint. To pronounce on **every** dispute would be to fix every contested question forever. It would leave no room for legitimate scholarly difference, no room for context-sensitive ijtihād, no room for the reader to grow into the text. By speaking to *akthar* and leaving a remainder, Allah preserves both the authority of revelation **and** the space for human reflection.

And notice what this does for the addressee. If the Quran had said "all," it would feel like an avalanche — every dispute the Children of Israel ever had, suddenly judged. That would be overwhelming. But "most" feels like a hand on the shoulder. *I will speak to what matters. I will not bury you. The big things, I am settling. The small things, you can let go.*

This is a Book that knows the difference between what needs an answer and what needs to be released.

## Bridge: From Words to Reality

So far we have looked at three words.

**Hādhā** — *this* Quran. The pointing finger. The refusal of abstraction. A Book that places itself locatably in the room and invites examination.

**Yaquṣṣu** — *narrates*, not *judges*. A Book that arbitrates by recounting the story rather than issuing verdicts. The mercy of the eyewitness.

**Akthar** — *most*, not *all*. A Book that knows what salvation requires and leaves the rest silent. The mercy of the limit.

Three words. Three different facets of one staggering claim: that into the most ancient theological dispute on earth, a Book has arrived that is **specific** enough to be testable, **narrative** enough to be received, and **measured** enough to be merciful.

[PAUSE]

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. What kind of God speaks like this? And what does it mean that this Book is **still** speaking, fifteen centuries after this verse was revealed, into disputes you and I are living inside?

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Loneliness of an Argument That Has Outlived Its Memory

Have you ever been in a fight where you forgot what you were fighting about?

It happens in families. A grievance starts somewhere — a real one, with real causes. Someone said something. Someone did something. The pain was legitimate. But the argument lasts. It outlives the original incident. It gets passed to the next generation. The children inherit the fight without inheriting the cause. By the third generation, no one remembers what actually happened — but the sides are still drawn, the bitterness is still real, and the fight goes on.

This is not just a family dynamic. This is what happens to communities, to nations, to entire religious traditions. An original disagreement — perhaps even a legitimate one — calcifies into identity. The "we" who hold position A. The "they" who hold position B. The original facts get lost. The original question gets forgotten. What remains is the fight itself, performed by people who can no longer say what it is for.

This is the condition of the Children of Israel that this ayah is addressing.

By the time the Quran arrived, the Jewish and Christian communities had been arguing about their scriptures for centuries. Pharisees vs. Sadducees. Rabbis vs. Karaites. Trinitarian Christians vs. Arian Christians vs. Nestorian Christians vs. Monophysite Christians. Each held fragments of the original prophetic legacy. Each had built libraries of commentary defending their fragment. Each had stopped being able to hear the others.

And the deepest tragedy was not that they disagreed. The deepest tragedy was that they had lost access to **the story that would have settled the disagreement**. The original was no longer available to them in unmediated form. They were arguing about copies of copies, interpretations of interpretations, traditions about traditions.

[PAUSE]

This is a kind of loneliness that is hard to describe. It is the loneliness of a community that knows it is in a fight but has forgotten what it is for. It is the loneliness of holding a position you inherited but cannot fully justify. It is the loneliness of suspecting that your tradition might be wrong but having no way back to the source to check.

Into that loneliness, this ayah arrives. *This Quran narrates to the Children of Israel most of that over which they differ.*

Read it again with the loneliness in mind. The Quran is not coming to humiliate them. It is coming to **end the loneliness**. It is saying: you have been arguing in the dark for so long that you have forgotten what you were arguing about. Let me tell you the story. Let me restore the trail. Let me give you back the original you lost access to. Not as a verdict from above, but as a narration that lets you finally hear what was always meant to be heard.

This is a Book of breathtaking pastoral mercy toward a community that the desert Arabs of Mecca often thought of only as rivals. The Quran does not arrive in Mecca to dunk on the Jews. It arrives to **rescue** them from a fifteen-hundred-year-old argument they had no way of resolving on their own.

[PAUSE]

And here is the part that should land on you. **You are in fights like this too.**

Maybe not theological. Maybe with a sibling. Maybe with a parent. Maybe inside your own head, where two voices have been arguing for so long you have forgotten what the original wound was. We all carry inherited disputes. We all hold positions whose roots we have lost. We all have an inner Children-of-Israel who is fighting in the dark.

What this ayah teaches is that there is a kind of mercy whose entire purpose is to **return us to the story we forgot**. It does not pronounce winners. It tells the truth and lets the truth do the work. It offers the disputant the dignity of finally seeing what really happened.

What argument in your life has outlived its memory? And what would it mean to receive — not a verdict — but a narration that finally tells you the truth of what was?

The practical application is small but real: when you find yourself in a long-running argument, ask not "who is winning?" but "do we still remember what this is about?" If the answer is no, the way out is not more fighting. It is going back together to the source. That is what *yaquṣṣu* models.

## Theme Two: A God Who Speaks Across Communities — The Quran as a Letter to People Who Are Not Listening

Here is a strange feature of this ayah that we should not skip past.

*Inna hādhā al-Qurʾāna yaquṣṣu **ʿalā Banī Isrāʾīla**...*

The Quran is being recited in Arabic, in Mecca, to an audience of Quraysh polytheists. But the ayah specifies that its narration is *ʿalā Banī Isrāʾīla* — **to the Children of Israel**.

The Children of Israel are not in the room. Most of them are not even on the peninsula. The Jewish communities of Yathrib will not encounter the full Quranic discourse for years. The Christian communities of Syria and Egypt may never hear it at all. And yet the ayah addresses them directly. Allah is sending a message to a people who, at the moment of revelation, are not listening.

Stay with how strange this is.

Imagine you are a Meccan listener. You are not Jewish. You are not Christian. You have your idols, your tribe, your trade routes. And the Prophet ﷺ recites a verse that says: *This Quran narrates to the Children of Israel most of what they dispute about.* You are not the addressee. You are the **overhearer**. You are listening to a letter being written to someone else.

What does this teach? It teaches that the Quran's audience is **larger than the room it was revealed in**. It teaches that Allah's speech reaches across communities, across languages, across centuries — that He is willing to address absent people through present mouths, trusting that the message will eventually reach those for whom it was meant.

This is the posture of a Book that knows it is not just for Quraysh. It is for the world. And it announces that universality not by saying "I am for everyone" in the abstract, but by **doing it** — by speaking, in the middle of an Arabic recitation in Mecca, to a Hebrew-speaking, Aramaic-speaking, Greek-speaking community scattered across three continents.

[PAUSE]

There is a God-portrait hidden in this. It is the portrait of a Lord who does not speak only to those currently listening. He speaks **to those who need to hear**, even if they are not in the room. He addresses the absent. He sends letters to the ones who have not yet been ready to open them. He pre-writes the answer to a dispute the disputant has not yet asked Him to settle.

This is what it means for the Quran to be *raḥmah lil-ʿālamīn* — mercy to all the worlds. It does not wait to be invited. It speaks across the gap. It tells the Jewish community what their ancestors actually meant, even though they did not ask. It tells the Christian community what ʿĪsā actually was, even though they were not there. It tells the people of every century — including yours — what the prophets actually taught, even though you did not request it.

And here is what should genuinely move you: **you are also someone the Quran addresses across a gap**.

You did not live in the seventh century. You did not stand in the masjid in Madinah. You were not on the camel caravan to Syria. And yet, when you read the Quran, you will find verses that name your exact condition — your specific doubt, your specific ache, your specific wandering — with a precision that no human author could have arranged for someone fourteen centuries away.

The same mechanism that allowed the Quran to address the Children of Israel without their being present is the mechanism that allows it to address **you**. You are also being narrated to across a gap. The Book is also telling you the story of what you have forgotten.

[PAUSE]

What does this change practically?

It changes how you read. It means: when you open the Quran, you are not eavesdropping on a conversation that was meant for someone else. You are receiving a letter that was written, in part, **for you** — composed with full knowledge of your specific arguments, your specific inheritances, your specific lonelinesses. Allah did not need you to be present at the revelation. He addressed you anyway, trusting that the letter would arrive.

So read the Quran like someone reading a letter that names them. Because the Book that could speak to absent Israelites can also speak to you, in this room, on this day, in this exact spiritual condition. It is not a coincidence when an ayah lands on you. It was sent.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Look at what the linguistic journey and the thematic depths have shown us — from two directions, the same insight.

The linguistic side gave us **hādhā** (this specific Book), **yaquṣṣu** (narration not verdict), and **akthar** (measured sufficiency, not encyclopedic flood).

The thematic side gave us a Book that ends the loneliness of inherited arguments, and a Book that addresses absent communities across gaps of language, geography, and time.

These are the same insight. The Quran's authority is not the authority of a courtroom — it is the authority of an **eyewitness narrator** who arrives to restore the story you lost. It does not flood you with rulings on every dispute. It tells you what actually happened in the cases that matter for your salvation. And it does this not only for the people in the room with the Prophet ﷺ — it does this **across the gap**, for the Children of Israel who were not present, and for you, who are not present either.

This is what kind of Book the Quran claims to be: a specific, locatable, narrative, measured, gap-crossing arbitration of the disputes that matter. **This** Book. Right here. In your hands.

## The Closing Invitation

Let something shift after this.

The next time you open the Quran, do not open it as a book of rulings. Open it as a **narration** addressed to you across fourteen centuries — composed by a Lord who knew the specific arguments you would inherit, the specific lonelinesses you would carry, the specific stories you would lose access to.

And the next time you find yourself locked in a long-running dispute — with a sibling, a parent, a community, or your own self — pause and ask: *Have we forgotten what this is about?* Then go back to the source together. Not to pronounce verdicts. To recount what really happened. Let the story do the work.

That is *yaquṣṣu*. That is the posture this ayah teaches.
