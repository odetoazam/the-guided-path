# The Brothers Who Could Not Be Brothers

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 45

> وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَآ إِلَىٰ ثَمُودَ أَخَاهُمْ صَـٰلِحًا أَنِ ٱعْبُدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ فَإِذَا هُمْ فَرِيقَانِ يَخْتَصِمُونَ
>
> *Wa laqad arsalnā ilā Thamūda akhāhum Ṣāliḥan ani'budū Allāha fa-idhā hum farīqāni yakhtaṣimūn.*
>
> "And We had certainly sent to Thamūd their brother Ṣāliḥ, [saying], 'Worship Allah.' And at once they became two factions conflicting."

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Sentence

Imagine a town.

Not a town from a history book. Imagine your town. The one you grew up in, or the one you live in now. Imagine that on a Tuesday morning, ordinary like any other, someone you have known your entire life — someone whose mother knew your mother, whose hands you have shaken at weddings and funerals — stands up in the marketplace and says something so simple that a child could repeat it.

He says: **"Worship God."**

That's it. Three syllables in Arabic. A complete sentence. No theology lecture. No legal code. No demands for tribute, no political agenda, no army at his back. Just a man you have known forever — a man the text will explicitly call **your brother** — telling you, gently, that the One who made you should be the One you turn toward.

[PAUSE]

And by sundown, your town is no longer one town.

It has become two.

Friends who ate together yesterday do not speak today. Cousins look at each other across the well with eyes they have never used before. A line has been drawn — invisible, but everyone in the town somehow knows exactly which side of it they stand on. There has been no debate. No vote. No declaration of war.

There has only been a sentence. And the sentence has split the world.

This is the moment the Quran is pointing us toward.

We are about to enter Sūrat al-Naml — the Surah of the Ant — at a precise narrative hinge. The previous pages have shown us Sulaymān and Bilqīs, a story of two royal courts coming together in submission to Allah. Wealth, power, intelligence, and faith arranged in beautiful order. A queen who hears the truth and says, with stunning brevity, *"I submit, with Sulaymān, to the Lord of the worlds."* Unity. Harmony. The conversation that ends in *islām*.

Now turn the page.

We are dropped into a different town, with a different prophet, and a different ending. The contrast is not accidental. The surah is showing us, deliberately, what happens when the same invitation that produced Bilqīs's submission lands in soil that is not Bilqīs's soil.

The hook of this ayah is not in what it tells us about Thamūd. The hook is in how *fast* it happens. Fourteen Arabic words. Three of them are about the sending of the messenger. One is the message. And the rest — the rest are about what those three words *did* to the people who heard them.

Stay with me. Because what Allah has placed in these fourteen words is not a story about a dead people in a vanished town. It is a diagnosis of every single moment in your life when truth arrives, and something inside you splits.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word "Brother" — and the Wound It Opens

Before we look at the word in the ayah, let me ask you something.

Think of someone in your family — a sibling, a cousin, someone you grew up around. Now imagine that person disagrees with you about something deep. Not about the weather. Not about which restaurant to pick. About **God**. About what is real. About what you owe your existence to.

Notice what that disagreement *feels* like, compared to the same disagreement with a stranger on the internet. The stranger you can scroll past. The brother you cannot. The brother is in the photographs on the wall behind you. The brother shares your last name. The brother's argument arrives not as information but as **rupture**.

Hold onto that feeling. Because Allah is about to use it.

In Arabic, the word for brother is **أخ** (*akh*), from the root **أ-خ-و** (*ʾ-kh-w*). The root carries the sense of shared origin, shared belonging, shared blood. But in classical Arabic usage, the word stretches far beyond literal siblinghood. A man can be called *akh* of his tribe. *Akh* of his people. *Akh* in faith. *Akh* in profession. The word marks a bond of belonging — and the deeper the shared origin, the more the word presses on the heart.

Now look at the ayah:

> *arsalnā ilā Thamūda **akhāhum** Ṣāliḥan*
>
> "We sent to Thamūd **their brother** Ṣāliḥ."

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah does not say "We sent a messenger to Thamūd." He does not say "We sent the prophet Ṣāliḥ to Thamūd." He says: **their brother**.

Why?

The grammatical fact is small. The word *akhāhum* — "their brother" — is a noun in the accusative case (because it's the object of "We sent"), with the possessive pronoun *-hum* ("their") attached. It's standing in apposition to the proper noun *Ṣāliḥan*. The structure is: We sent to Thamūd / their brother / Ṣāliḥ. The word "brother" comes *before* his name.

Word order in Arabic is doing work here. The relationship is named before the man is named. Before you hear *who* he is, you hear *what he is to them*.

Let's sit with this.

What does it mean that Allah opens the story this way? It means the tragedy is being framed before it unfolds. Allah is telling us: **the man they will reject is not a foreigner. Not an enemy. Not an outsider with a strange accent.** He is one of them. He shares their lineage, their tongue, their childhood streets, their tribal name. When he speaks, his voice carries the cadences they were raised on. When he walks past, the elders remember his mother.

And this — *this* — is what makes their rejection a wound and not merely a refusal.

The same word appears at the same position in the parallel ayahs about ʿĀd (their brother Hūd), Madyan (their brother Shuʿayb), and Nūḥ's people. The pattern is consistent across the prophetic stories. Allah is not just informing us of genealogy. He is establishing a recurring truth:

**The voice of truth, when it comes, comes from inside the house.**

Not from a distant authority. Not from a conquering power. From the brother. From the one who has nothing to gain by lying to you, because if you are destroyed, his town is destroyed with you.

Here's what's remarkable. The word *akh* in this ayah is doing something even more delicate. It is being used by **Allah**, not by Thamūd. *Allah* calls Ṣāliḥ their brother. Thamūd, in the ayahs that follow, will call him something else entirely — they will accuse him of being a sorcerer, an obsessed man, a liar. They will *unbrother* him.

But Allah, in the very framing of the story, refuses to let them. The Quran will hold open the relationship that Thamūd is trying to slam shut. Every time you read this ayah, you are hearing Allah say, across millennia: *He was your brother. Whatever you called him afterward, whatever you did to him in the end — he was your brother, and I will not let you pretend otherwise.*

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is something very personal. When truth arrives in your life — not Truth in a book, but truth as a *demand on how you live* — it almost always arrives wearing the face of someone you cannot easily dismiss. A spouse. A parent. A friend who suddenly speaks plainly. A teacher whose words you cannot un-hear. The voice that changes you is rarely a stranger's voice. It is the voice of someone close enough that their words have the weight of relationship behind them.

And this is precisely why we so often reject them.

The stranger we can engage abstractly. The brother we have to answer. And so the deepest temptation, when truth comes through someone close, is to do exactly what Thamūd did: to *unbrother* them. To reframe the relationship in a way that lets us escape the demand. *He's changed. She's not who she used to be. I don't even know him anymore.*

Allah's choice to call Ṣāliḥ *their brother* is a refusal to let us pretend.

### Element Two: The Particle of Explanation — A Whole Theology in One Letter

Now look at what comes after his name:

> *akhāhum Ṣāliḥan **ani** 'budū Allāha*
>
> "their brother Ṣāliḥ — **that**, 'Worship Allah.'"

There is a tiny word here that English translations swallow almost entirely. The word is **أَن** (*an*). It's two letters. A hamza and a nūn. In English it gets translated as "that," or "to," or sometimes it gets absorbed into a comma. But in Arabic grammar, *an* in this position is a particle that does one of several things — and which one it is doing here matters enormously.

The classical grammarians call this construction *an al-mufassira* — the **explanatory** *an*. Its function is to introduce, after a verb of communication (sending, calling, saying), the *content* of what was communicated.

Let me teach the concept first, before we apply it.

In Arabic, when you say "I sent him a message," the language has many ways to introduce what the message actually said. You can use *qāʾilan* ("saying"). You can use direct quotation. Or you can use this little particle *an*, which functions like opening a door directly into the substance of the message. *Ani'budū Allāh* doesn't mean "I told him to worship Allah" with a layer of narration. It means: *the content of the sending was — worship Allah.*

The *an* peels back the wrapping and shows you what's inside.

Now notice what Allah has done.

The ayah introduces a messenger, names him as the brother of the people, and then, through this explanatory particle, opens the door to his message. And what is the message?

Two words.

**اعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ.**

***Worship Allah.***

[PAUSE]

That's it. That's the whole risāla. That's the entire content of what this brother was sent to deliver to his people.

Stay with me, because what this tells you about the prophetic mission is profound. Allah, who could have summarized Ṣāliḥ's message in any number of ways — could have said "to call them to truth" or "to warn them of destruction" or "to teach them the law" — chooses instead to give us the message itself, in its starkest, most irreducible form.

*Worship Allah.*

Not "stop oppressing the poor" — though Ṣāliḥ certainly addressed this.
Not "honor the sacred camel" — though that was the test.
Not "fear the punishment" — though warnings would come.

The message *underneath* every other message is this one. **Direct your worship to the One who actually deserves it.**

Why does the particle matter? Because *an al-mufassira* tells you that this is not Allah's summary of a longer speech. It is not a paraphrase. It is the **interpretive core** of what was sent. The grammar is saying: *if you want to know what Ṣāliḥ was sent to do, it is this. Strip everything else away, and this remains.*

And here is what should arrest us. The simplicity of the message is in absolute, devastating proportion to the *complexity* of the response. Two words go in. The community splits in half. The town fractures. Cousins stop speaking. A camel will die. A city will be destroyed.

All of that — over two words.

This is the diagnostic. **The size of the disruption tells you nothing about the complexity of the truth. It tells you everything about the resistance.** When something this small splits a town this completely, you are not looking at an intellectual problem. You are looking at the soul refusing to bend.

There is one more thing to notice about *an* here. It does not say *an i'bud Allāh* (singular — worship, you single person). It says *an i'budū Allāh* (plural — worship, all of you together). The plural form of the imperative. Ṣāliḥ is not calling individuals to private spirituality. He is calling the whole community — collectively, simultaneously — to reorient. And it is precisely the *collective* nature of the call that makes the rejection a *collective* fracture.

The grammar of the call shapes the grammar of the response.

### Element Three: The *Fā* of Immediacy and the Word "Faction"

Now we arrive at what is perhaps the most dramatic word in the entire ayah. Watch carefully.

> *... ani'budū Allāha **fa-idhā** hum farīqāni yakhtaṣimūn*
>
> "... that 'Worship Allah.' **And at once** they were two factions disputing."

Two things are happening grammatically here, and both of them are doing structural work.

First: the particle **فَ** (*fa*) attached to *idhā*. In Arabic, *fa* is a connecting particle that can mean "then," "so," or — most commonly — "and immediately as a result." When *fa* is followed by *idhā*, the construction is called *fā al-mufājaʾa* — the *fā* of **sudden surprise**. It introduces an outcome that follows so quickly upon the preceding action that the speaker is essentially saying: *and just like that — boom — here is what happened.*

Let me teach this with an example. In English we might say: "She opened the door, and suddenly — there he was!" The "and suddenly" carries the surprise of the reveal. The *fa-idhā* construction does exactly that in Arabic. It collapses the time between cause and effect into nothing.

Now notice. Allah does not say:

- "He preached for years and eventually they divided."
- "After much debate, two factions emerged."
- "Over time, the community fractured."

He says: *fa-idhā hum farīqān*.

***And — instantly — they were two factions.***

[PAUSE]

The grammar is telling you that the division did not require time. The division did not require argument. The division did not require evidence to be weighed or counter-evidence to be presented. The moment the words "worship Allah" landed in their midst, the community split. As if the sentence itself were a wedge driven into wood, and the wood cracked along a fault line that had been there all along.

This is the second thing the grammar is showing you. Allah is revealing that **the fault line existed before the message**. Ṣāliḥ did not create the division. He revealed it. The pressure of the call merely exposed what was already true: that this community contained, within itself, two fundamentally different orientations toward reality, and they had been living side by side without knowing it.

Some hearts were oriented toward truth. Some hearts were oriented toward self. They had eaten at the same tables and traded in the same markets and looked, from the outside, like one people. The call to *tawḥīd* — to single-pointed worship — was the test that finally distinguished them.

And here's what's remarkable. This is not unique to Thamūd. This is the *function* of every prophetic message. **The call to worship Allah does not make people different. It reveals that they already were.**

Now the second word: **فَرِيقَانِ** (*farīqān*) — "two factions."

The root is **ف-ر-ق** (*f-r-q*), which in classical Arabic carries the core sense of *separation, splitting, division.* You see this root throughout the Quran. It is the root of *al-Furqān* — "the Criterion," that which separates truth from falsehood. It is the root of *farq* — the parting in the hair. It is the root of *firqah* — a sect or splinter group. It is the root of *iftirāq* — schism.

Wherever this root appears, something that was one is now two.

The word *farīq* itself is a noun form meaning "a group that has been separated off." Not just *a group* — but a group that has been *broken away from something larger*. A faction. A splinter. The dual form *farīqān* tells us there are exactly **two** of them. Not three views. Not a spectrum of opinions. Two. The community has been bisected.

This is significant. The grammar refuses to let us imagine a complex landscape of opinions. The truth of tawḥīd does not produce gradients. It produces a binary. Either you submit, or you don't. The community looked diverse on the surface, but underneath, when pressure was applied, only two structures emerged.

And then the final word: **يَخْتَصِمُونَ** (*yakhtaṣimūn*) — "they are disputing."

This verb is from the root **خ-ص-م** (*kh-ṣ-m*), which means *opposition, hostility, adversarial contention*. But Allah uses a specific form — Form VIII, the reflexive/reciprocal form, *iftaʿala*. The Form VIII pattern carries the meaning of *doing the action mutually, to each other, back and forth*.

In other words: this is not one side attacking and the other side defending. Both sides are now actively engaged in *opposition to each other*. The faction of belief is contending with the faction of denial, and the faction of denial is contending with the faction of belief. The community is consumed by internal struggle. Hostility, friction, argument — all flowing in both directions.

What was a town has become a battlefield.

And all of it — every bit of it — because one of them stood up and said two words.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three linguistic elements have done.

The word *akhāhum* told us that the messenger was a brother — that the wound about to open would be a family wound, not a foreign one.

The particle *an* told us that the message was reduced to its absolute core — *worship Allah* — so that we could see, with clinical precision, how *small* the seed of disruption was.

And the construction *fa-idhā hum farīqān yakhtaṣimūn* told us that the response was immediate, total, binary, and mutual — that the division did not develop; it *erupted*.

These three pieces, together, are giving us not just a story but a *diagnostic instrument*. Allah is teaching us how to read what happens when truth enters a community — any community, any era, any scale.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the human heart. About what we are doing right now, today, when truth knocks at our own door.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Fault Line Was Already There

Let me start somewhere familiar.

Most of us have had the experience of a friendship or family relationship that suddenly broke over one conversation. You look back at it and you think: *how did that happen? We were fine for years.* And on the surface, that's true. You were fine. You had dinners. You laughed at the same jokes. You weathered each other's hard times.

And then one Tuesday, someone said one thing, and everything was different. And you tell the story to yourself afterward as if *the sentence* was the cause. As if, had they not said that thing, the relationship would still be intact.

But sit with it long enough and a different story emerges. The fault line was already there. For years. Quietly. Beneath the dinners and the jokes. There was a way you saw the world and a way they saw the world, and you had simply never pressed on the seam. The Tuesday sentence didn't *break* anything. It pressed the seam. And the seam gave way, because seams always give way when you press them.

What feels like rupture is often *revelation*. The relationship was not what you thought it was. The pressure exposed it.

Now scale this up.

Sūrat al-Naml is showing us this same dynamic at the level of a community. Thamūd had been a tribe for generations. They had a shared history, a shared genealogy, shared territory, shared customs. By every external measure, they were *one people*. And then Ṣāliḥ — their brother — said *worship Allah*, and in the next breath they were two factions tearing at each other.

The naïve reading is: Ṣāliḥ's words divided them.

The Quranic reading — the reading the grammar forces on us — is: **Ṣāliḥ's words revealed the division that had always existed.**

This is one of the most important truths the Quran teaches about prophecy, and it scales all the way down to your own heart. The function of truth is not primarily to *create* alignment. The function of truth is to *expose* the actual state of things. Most of us are walking around with internal divisions we have never named. We hold contradictory loyalties. We worship Allah on Fridays and ourselves on Tuesdays. We claim to value submission and operate by ego. We assume we are one self.

And then a moment of testing arrives. A loss. A temptation. A demand to choose. A clear command we have been avoiding. And suddenly we discover — *fa-idhā* — that we are two factions, disputing within a single body.

The Quran has a word for this internal civil war. It calls it *nafs*. The self that contends with itself. Some scholars have read the entire prophetic encounter as a mirror: what happens between Ṣāliḥ and Thamūd outside is what happens between the *rūḥ* (spirit) and the *hawā* (lower desire) inside every human breast.

Here's the spiritual movement this should produce in us. Stop being surprised when truth, when it lands, divides you. The division is not a sign that the truth was wrong. The division is a sign that the truth was *real*. Falsehood produces a kind of false unity — a comfortable consensus where nothing is at stake. Truth produces apparent rupture, because it exposes the seams that comfort had been hiding.

**The question is never: *how do I avoid being divided?*** That's the wrong question. The question is: *when truth divides me, which faction do I want to belong to?*

Practical application. The next time something true enters your life and you feel that internal split — the wave of resistance, the immediate counter-argument, the urge to dismiss, the sudden production of reasons why this can't be right — *recognize the moment*. That is *fa-idhā hum farīqān yakhtaṣimūn* happening inside you. Two factions of your own soul are now disputing. Don't run from the dispute. Don't suppress one side and pretend you are unified. Sit in the *yakhtaṣimūn* — the active mutual struggle — long enough to see which faction is actually you, and which is the part of you that needs to die.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Brother You Cannot Hear

Here's the second movement.

I want you to think about the people in your life whose advice you most quickly dismiss.

Not strangers. Strangers are easy to dismiss — they cost nothing. I mean the people who, the moment they start to speak about something deep, you feel yourself tightening. A parent. A sibling. A spouse. The friend who has known you since childhood. The teacher who watched you grow up.

Why is it harder to hear them than to hear a podcast from across the world?

Because they have receipts. They know you. The stranger's words come without history. The brother's words come carrying everything you have ever done together. When the brother says *you should pray*, embedded in those three words is every prayer they have watched you skip. When the brother says *you should be honest*, embedded in those three words is every small lie they have witnessed. The words from close range carry a *density* that the same words from far range simply do not have.

And this is exactly what Allah is highlighting by calling Ṣāliḥ *their brother*. The hardest message to receive is the *true* message from the *close* messenger. And so the temptation — universal, ancient, and very much alive today — is to **manufacture distance**. To unbrother the brother so that we don't have to answer him.

Thamūd will do this in the ayahs that follow. They will accuse Ṣāliḥ of being deranged. They will accuse him of sorcery. They will eventually plot to kill him. Every accusation is a fresh act of *unbrothering* — a fresh attempt to push the close one far enough away that they no longer have to answer the closeness.

We do this constantly. We do it in subtle ways. *He's changed. She's gone off the deep end. He used to be such a normal guy. I don't even know who she is anymore.* These sentences are *not descriptions of reality*. They are tools. They are the linguistic equivalent of pushing someone out of a window — they create the distance that lets us stop hearing.

The Quran, by holding Ṣāliḥ as *their brother* even after they reject him, is refusing to participate in our unbrothering project. The Quran is saying: *no matter what you call him, he is your brother. No matter what you accuse him of, he came from inside the house. You cannot escape his words by relocating him in the social map.*

This has two profound applications for us.

First, on the receiving end: when someone close to you speaks truth into your life, do not run to the linguistic toolkit of distance-making. Do not reach for *they've changed*. Do not reach for *they're being judgmental*. Do not reach for *they don't understand my situation*. These may all be true in some cases — but examine yourself first. Are you using them as descriptions or as escape hatches? The brother who tells you the truth at cost to himself is one of the rarest gifts a human being can receive. Most people will tell you what you want to hear because they have something to gain. The one who tells you what you need to hear — at the risk of your friendship, at the risk of your love — is doing prophetic work in your life. Receive them as Ṣāliḥ should have been received.

Second, on the giving end: if you are the one who must say a hard true thing to someone close to you — do not be surprised when they react with the speed of *fa-idhā*. Do not measure your faithfulness by their reception. Ṣāliḥ delivered the perfect message — two words, no excess, the very content of *waḥy* itself — and his people split instantly into hostile factions. If *his* delivery produced *that* response, do not let yourself believe that *your* delivery, if only it were better worded, would produce a different one. Your job is the speaking. The splitting is between them and Allah.

[PAUSE]

Practical application. Identify, this week, one person in your life whose voice has become *dense* to you — someone you've been quietly distancing yourself from because their words land too close to home. Before you reject what they've been telling you, ask: *am I rejecting this because it's wrong, or am I rejecting this because they are too close?* If it's the second, you are doing what Thamūd did. Pull back from the unbrothering. Sit, instead, with their words as if they had come from a stranger you respected. See what changes.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Look back at the fourteen words of this ayah and see what Allah has built.

> **We had certainly sent** | **to Thamūd** | **their brother** | **Ṣāliḥ** | **— that:** | **"Worship Allah"** | **— and instantly** | **they were two factions** | **disputing.**

There is a hidden symmetry here. The ayah moves from **divine action** (We sent) → through **relational framing** (their brother) → to **the singular core** (Worship Allah) → and then explodes outward into **division and conflict** (two factions, disputing).

The shape of the ayah is itself the shape of the event. It begins with **unity** — one God sending one messenger with one message — and ends with **multiplicity** — factions, disputes, fragmentation. The grammar enacts what the story describes. The reader is brought from the single point of *Allah* to the shattered field of *yakhtaṣimūn*.

And in the center of the ayah, like the still point of a turning wheel, sits the message itself: *worship Allah*. Two words. The pivot on which the whole of human history turns.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

The grammar showed us: a brother (close), a message of pure tawḥīd (small), and an instant binary split (immediate, total). The themes showed us: truth doesn't create divisions, it reveals them; the closer the messenger, the harder to receive; the speed of the split is the proof of the buried fault line.

This is one single insight viewed twice. **When the call to worship Allah enters any space — a community, a marriage, a heart — it does not produce gentle reform. It produces the exposure of what was already there. And the exposure feels, from the inside, like rupture.**

The whole of the prophetic story is contained in this dynamic. Every prophet was a brother. Every message was tawḥīd. Every response was the *farīqān*.

You are inside this story right now.

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose voice in my life have I been quietly *unbrothering*? What truth am I trying not to hear by pushing them away in the social map of my mind?

2. When was the last time I felt that *fa-idhā* moment inside myself — the instant split into two factions — and which faction did I choose to identify with afterward? Was that the right choice?

3. Am I living as one self, or am I a *farīqān yakhtaṣimūn* — two factions disputing — pretending to be one? Where is the fault line?

4. If Allah were to summarize my life's message in two words, the way He summarized Ṣāliḥ's mission as *uʿbudū Allāh*, what would those two words be?

5. Where am I being a brother — speaking truth at cost — and where am I being the silent neighbor who watches the brother speak and says nothing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The call to worship Allah does not create division; it reveals the division that was already there — and the speed of the revelation is the measure of how long we had been pretending to be one.

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this is not your knowledge of an old story. What should shift is your *posture* toward division itself.

When truth arrives in your life and you feel the split — in your family, in your community, in your own chest — do not reach immediately for the tools of repair. First, sit with the split. Ask what it has shown you about what was already true. Ask which faction was the buried real one and which faction was the comfortable surface. And then — slowly, without panic — choose.

The Quran does not promise that the call to *worship Allah* will make your life less divided. It promises that the call will make your life *more honest*. And honesty, in the long run, is the only soil in which real unity — the kind Bilqīs found, not the kind Thamūd lost — can grow.

### Du'a

اللهم اجعلنا ممن إذا سمع الحق انقاد له، لا ممن إذا سمعه انشق عنه. اللهم لا تجعلنا فريقَين في صدر واحد، بل اجمع شملنا على عبادتك وحدك. واجعل الإخوة الذين يقولون لنا الحقَّ من أعزِّ الناس علينا، لا من أبعدِهم.

*O Allah, make us among those who, when they hear the truth, yield to it — not among those who, when they hear it, split apart from it. O Allah, do not let us be two factions within a single chest; gather our fragments around the worship of You alone. And make the brothers who speak truth to us the dearest of people to our hearts — not the most distant.*
