# He Thought He Was Going for Warmth

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 7

---

### THE AYAH

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:27:7] إِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِأَهْلِهِۦٓ إِنِّىٓ ءَانَسْتُ نَارًا سَـَٔاتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ ءَاتِيكُم بِشِهَابٍ قَبَسٍ لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Idh qāla Mūsā li-ahlihi innī ānastu nāran sa-ātīkum minhā bi-khabarin aw ātīkum bi-shihābin qabasin la'allakum taṣṭalūn.*

**Translation:**
*"When Moses said to his family, 'I have perceived a fire. I will bring you news from it, or bring you a burning brand — perhaps you may warm yourselves.'"*

---

## INTRODUCTION

There is a man in the dark.

He is not a prophet yet — or rather, he doesn't know he is. He left Egypt as a fugitive, after striking a man who died. He spent ten years as a shepherd in Midian, married, built a quiet life, put distance between himself and everything that had happened. And now he is traveling through the wilderness of the Sinai Peninsula at night, with his family, trying to reach somewhere safe.

He is cold. They are cold. And they are lost.

The classical commentators — Ibn Kathir, al-Tabari, and others — describe the scene: Moses was leading his family at night when the cold became severe. Some narrations add that he had lost his way. The flint wouldn't strike. The darkness around them was complete.

And then, on the slope of a mountain, he saw something in the distance.

He saw a fire.

[PAUSE]

What happens next will change human history. He will hear a voice. He will be told to remove his sandals on holy ground. He will receive a mission he did not ask for, a staff that swallows serpents, and a light in his own hand that blazes white. He will stand before Pharaoh. He will part a sea.

But none of that has happened yet.

Right now he is just a man — cold, uncertain, responsible for the people behind him — who sees a fire in the distance and turns toward it.

And what is remarkable — what we will spend the next hour discovering together — is that the way Allah describes *this moment before the revelation* is not filler, not backdrop, not mere scene-setting. **This moment before the revelation is itself a teaching about revelation.** About how divine encounters begin. About what we are usually looking for when they find us. About what every human being in darkness actually needs.

Stay with me. Let's begin.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### I. The Verb That Holds a World: *Ānastu*

Before we can understand what Moses found at that fire, we need to slow down at the verb he chose to describe what he saw.

There are many ways to say "I saw" in Arabic. رَأَيْتُ (*ra'aytu*) — I saw. أَبْصَرْتُ (*abṣartu*) — I perceived with my eyes. نَظَرْتُ (*naẓartu*) — I observed. وَجَدْتُ (*wajadtu*) — I found.

Moses uses none of these.

He says: **آنَسْتُ** — *ānastu*.

Let me teach you the root first, because the wonder is there.

The root is **أ-ن-س** — *alif, nūn, sīn*. From this root we get:

- **أُنْس** (*uns*) — intimacy, warmth, the comfort of presence with another
- **إِنْسَان** (*insān*) — the human being, the creature of intimacy, the one who craves closeness
- **آنَسَ** (*ānasa*) — to perceive something from a distance in a way that brings comfort, ease, or recognition

That third meaning is the key. Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in the *Mufradāt* and Ibn Manẓūr in the *Lisān al-ʿArab* are precise here: *ānasa* doesn't merely mean to see. It means to perceive something from afar and feel, upon perceiving it, a kind of comfort — a recognition — a quiet relief.

Let me give you an everyday image of what this feels like.

You are walking in an unfamiliar neighborhood at night. The streets are dark, the surroundings unfamiliar, and you're uncertain whether you're heading in the right direction. And then, in the distance, you see a single lit window in a building.

You haven't arrived. You don't know whose window it is or whether it can help you. But something in you — before any conscious thought — is drawn toward it. Relaxes slightly. Takes the sight as a kind of signal: *there is life there. There is warmth. The human world is somewhere ahead.*

That drawing. That quiet softening before analysis. That recognition without reason.

**That is *ānastu*.**

[PAUSE]

Notice what Moses does *not* say. He does not use the neutral verb of seeing. He uses a word with *uns* — intimacy, comfort — embedded in its root. He perceived the fire with something like relief. Like recognition. As though the drawn eye and the eased heart arrived at the same moment.

And this is remarkable, because what Moses is about to find at that fire is nothing less than God speaking from inside a burning bush. The single greatest turning point of his life is waiting for him there — a commission, a mission, an identity he did not know he carried.

And the moment before it? **Allah describes it as *ānastu* — he perceived it with comfort. He was drawn to it with something like longing.**

The encounter with the divine began not with terror, not with spiritual preparation — but with the instinct of a cold, lost man turning toward available light.

Some scholars have noted something worth sitting with: the root **أ-ن-س** is the same root as **إِنْسَان**, the human being. The Quran may be telling us something here. **To turn toward light in darkness, with need and longing, is one of the most human things a human can do.** It is inscribed in the very word that names what we are.

[PAUSE]

Moses didn't go to that fire because he expected revelation. He went because it was cold. Because his family was cold. Because a man who knows the wilderness, who sees a fire on a mountain at night, does what human beings have always done: he moves toward the light.

And God was waiting in the light.

---

### II. The Two Things He Promised Before He Left: *Khabar* and *Qabas*

Now we need to slow down at what Moses told his family before he went.

He makes a double promise. Two things. And Allah is precise here — the precision is load-bearing.

**سَآتِيكُم مِّنْهَا بِخَبَرٍ أَوْ آتِيكُم بِشِهَابٍ قَبَسٍ**

*"I will bring you news from it, or I will bring you a burning brand."*

Two things: **خَبَر** (*khabar*) — news, information, a report — or **شِهَابٍ قَبَس** (*shihābin qabas*) — a flame-brand, a live coal.

Let me open these one at a time.

**خَبَر (*khabar*)** comes from a root meaning reliable news, a report that gives you orientation about your situation. Remember the context: they are lost in the Sinai wilderness at night. To know which direction to go, to know if there is a settlement nearby, to know if they are on the right path — that is what *khabar* offers. Moses doesn't go to the fire only for warmth. **He goes for direction.** He is seeking the small piece of reliable information that can tell a lost person where they are.

And then: **شِهَاب (*shihāb*)** — a flame-brand, a blazing torch. The word is used elsewhere in the Quran for shooting stars. Something radiant, bright, fast. And modifying it, clarifying it: **قَبَس (*qabas*)**.

*Qabas* is very specific. It does not mean fire in general. Al-Rāghib is explicit in the *Mufradāt*: a *qabas* is a portion of fire — an ember, a live coal — *taken from a larger fire to be carried somewhere else*. Fire you can hold. Fire you can transfer. Fire you can bring home to someone who is cold.

Moses is not promising to bring "fire." He is promising to bring **a piece of fire his family can use** — something taken from one source of warmth and carried to those who lack it.

[PAUSE]

Let's hold these two words together now.

خَبَر: information that tells you where you are and which way to go.
قَبَس: warmth that keeps you alive while you are still figuring out where you're going.

**Direction. And warmth.**

Before we go any further, I want to ask you something — not rhetorically.

Think of a moment of genuine darkness in your own life. A diagnosis. A grief. A season where the familiar world became unrecognizable. A failure that left you not just hurt, but disoriented.

In every such darkness, there are almost always exactly two things a person needs.

The first is **some news**. Some orientation. Even a small piece of reliable information: *Is this going to be okay? What do I do next? Which direction should I go?* The desperate need isn't always for rescue — it is often for reference. Just a point to aim toward. Just a signal that there is a path, even if you can't see it all yet.

The second is **warmth**. The energy, the emotional sustenance, to survive until morning. To keep going until the direction becomes clearer. Not necessarily the full answer — just enough heat to make it through the night.

**Moses names both. He promises his family both. He goes to find both.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is what is about to happen — and this is where the depth lives.

Moses will reach that fire. And what he will find there — in the next two ayahs — is not a small piece of news and a live coal. What he will find is the voice of Allah itself.

He asked for *khabar* — news, orientation, information. He received *Kalām Allah* — the speech of God. The most profound direction any human has ever received: not *"turn left at the next ridge"* but *"I am Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Wise"* (27:9). Not local direction. **Universal orientation.**

He asked for a *qabas* — a piece of warmth to carry back, something transferable. What he received were signs to carry to an enslaved people — a staff, a blazing hand, a mission that would warm and orient an entire nation for generations.

He went for the small thing. God gave him the infinite version of what he asked for.

[PAUSE]

There is something almost tender about this. Moses didn't ask for too much. He asked for what his family needed *tonight*. News and warmth. And God, in His generosity, heard the deeper structure of the request — what a human being in darkness ultimately needs — and answered it completely, at a scale Moses could not have imagined.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### I. Ordinary Need as Sacred Threshold

Let me start somewhere you may have been.

There's a particular experience that most people have at some point — a moment that later, looking back, you recognize as the moment everything changed. But in the moment itself, it looked completely ordinary. Even accidental.

You applied to that program because a friend was applying, not because you thought you'd get in. You took that class because it fit your schedule. You went to that gathering because you didn't want to cancel on someone. You stopped at that place because you needed gas.

And at that class, that gathering, that gas station — the thing happened. The meeting, the conversation, the turning point that reordered your life.

Looking back, you see the hand of God in it. Looking forward from the moment, you could not have known.

This is the universal structure of what we might call the *ordinary threshold* — the moment when the mundane becomes the sacred, when a practical need becomes the door to something you had no category for.

And Surah An-Naml is showing us the most famous ordinary threshold in human history.

Moses didn't go to that fire for revelation. He went because it was cold. Because his family was cold. Because a fire on a mountain at night, when you're a shepherd who knows the wilderness, means warmth and orientation — and his family needed both.

**He went for practical reasons. And God was waiting.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something careful here. The Quran does not say Moses had a premonition. It does not paint him as spiritually prepared or in a state of heightened awareness. It does not tell us he cleansed himself, or composed himself, or even thought about God before he walked toward that light.

He perceived a fire. He told his family: *Wait here. I'll see what I can bring back.*

This is completely ordinary human behavior. And God used it.

Which means something astonishing: **the divine encounter is not reserved for those who approach correctly.** Moses approached that fire practically, with need, with urgency, with his family's comfort on his mind. His motivation was entirely terrestrial — cold people, dark night, light in the distance.

And God spoke.

[PAUSE]

I want to push into this, because I think it carries a mercy that many of us miss.

We often imagine that divine encounter requires a certain kind of preparation. That God is waiting for us to approach in the right way — with the right intention, the right spiritual state, the right amount of prior knowledge. That we have to earn the conditions for the encounter.

But Moses's story suggests something different. **God was already at the fire. Moses didn't have to approach correctly — he just had to approach.**

The verb was *ānastu* — I perceived it with comfort, I was drawn to it. That instinctive drawing toward available light, that primordial human movement toward warmth when you're cold — this was enough. This was the threshold.

There is a concept in Arabic spiritual tradition: *al-tawajjuh* — the turning toward. The act of directing your face, your attention, your whole self toward God. And what the story of Moses at the fire is showing us is that *tawajjuh* doesn't have to be sophisticated. It doesn't have to be theologically literate. It doesn't have to be formally intended.

**A cold, lost man turned toward the only light available. And that turning was enough.**

[PAUSE]

What does this mean for those of us sitting with our own darknesses?

It means that the moments when we most genuinely turn toward God are often the moments of practical need — not the moments of elevated spirituality. The du'ā' you made in a hospital corridor at 3am. The prayer you prayed when you'd run out of plans and didn't know what else to do. The almost automatic turning of the heart toward the divine when everything else has stopped working.

These are not lesser forms of spiritual encounter. They may, in fact, be the most honest ones — the moments when need strips away performance and we turn toward light simply because we are cold and we see it.

And if Moses's story is any guide: **God has always been waiting in that fire.**

---

### II. Direction and Warmth — The Two Things Every Soul Needs in Darkness

Let me come at the two promises from another angle.

Different frameworks — psychological, philosophical, religious — have their own language for what human beings need in the worst passages of their lives. But when you strip away the language, they tend to circle the same pairing.

The first: **orientation**. A sense that there is a way forward. Even a small sense of direction — a reference point, a next step, a reliable piece of information — is enormously stabilizing. Without it, the mind cannot rest. It keeps spinning, unable to commit to any action because it doesn't know which way to turn. What the desperate soul often needs isn't rescue — it's a *direction to aim toward*.

The second: **sustenance**. Enough warmth, enough connection, enough emotional fuel to survive until morning. Not necessarily the full answer — just a piece of something real to hold, to carry through the night, to keep you from going cold before clarity arrives.

Direction. And warmth.

Moses named both. In the wilderness. In the dark. In the specific language of *khabar* and *qabas*.

[PAUSE]

Now let's look carefully at what God actually gave him, because the contrast is where the depth lives.

Moses asked for *khabar* — news from the fire, information, orientation. Something to tell him where he was. What he received was this, in the ayah immediately following: *"O Moses, indeed I am Allah, the Exalted in Might, the Wise."* (27:9)

He asked which way to go. God told him who God is.

This is not evasion. It is the complete answer — at infinite scale. The news that reorients everything. Not *"take the northern path"* but *"I am Allah."* This is the *khabar* that doesn't just tell you where your feet should go, but who your entire being should face. **He asked for local direction. God gave him universal orientation.**

And the *qabas* — the piece of warmth Moses wanted to carry back to his family? What he received were gifts to carry to an entire people who had been cold for four hundred years under Pharaoh. A staff that could part water. Signs of power that could face the greatest empire on earth. A brother sent alongside him so he would not go alone. These were not just demonstrations. They were things to carry — fire taken from the divine presence and brought to those who needed it.

**He asked for a coal to warm his family tonight. God gave him gifts to warm an entire people across generations.**

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here, because this can sound too tidy. And life is not always tidy.

There are seasons of darkness where we turn toward God with genuine need and what arrives feels small, not large. We asked for *khabar* and received what feels like silence. We needed *qabas* and the cold stayed cold. I do not want to flatten this.

But what Moses's story does — what it opens — is a posture. Not a guarantee of dramatic rescue, but an orientation toward a possibility: **what God gives in response to our need may be the infinite version of what we asked for.** Not always, not predictably, not always in the form we recognize immediately. But sometimes.

And the posture that keeps us available for that possibility is the posture Moses took at the beginning of this ayah: honestly, practically, without performance, turning toward the available light.

*Ānastu nāran.* I perceived a fire. I was drawn to it with something like comfort. I went because I needed something.

And the fire spoke.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer I want to name before we close.

Notice where Moses's mind was from the very moment of perception.

He *saw* the fire — *ānastu* — and what did he immediately say? Not *"I'll go warm myself."* But: **I will bring you** news. I will bring **you** a burning brand. The plural — his family, the people behind him — was already in the sentence before he had even taken a step toward the fire.

He perceived the light alone. But everything he planned to do with that perception was for others.

This is worth staying with. **The instinct to carry warmth back** — the instinct not just to find fire but to take a piece of it home — this is the *qabas* instinct. And it is not presented in this ayah as an advanced spiritual virtue. It is simply what Moses naturally does, in the dark, when he is cold and his family is cold.

He is drawn toward light. And he immediately thinks: *what can I bring back?*

This is the shape of prophetic consciousness at its most basic — and perhaps the shape of any genuine encounter with divine light: to be transformed by it, and then to look for who else needs it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we've done the linguistic and thematic work, let me show you how the ayah is built — because the structure itself is a teaching.

The ayah moves in a clear direction:

**One man's solitary perception → his promise to his family → two things he offers them → the purpose: their warmth**

From **إِنِّي آنَسْتُ** (*I* perceived) — first person singular, entirely alone in what he sees — all the way to **لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَصْطَلُونَ** (*perhaps you all* warm yourselves) — second person plural, the community behind him.

The verb *taṣṭalūna* is Form VIII in Arabic — a reflexive, intensive form. They don't just receive warmth passively. They *warm themselves* — they are active participants in what he is about to bring them.

**Moses perceived alone. He promises for all. And they are given agency in receiving what he will carry back.**

The whole ayah is a movement from solitary encounter to communal provision — from one man's drawn eye to a family's sustained warmth. This is its architecture. And this architecture will repeat, at scale, across the rest of Moses's life.

---

### The Convergence

The two discoveries of the linguistic journey turn out to be the same insight, seen from two directions.

*Ānastu* showed us: Moses approached the fire not through spiritual sophistication but through basic human need — drawn by an instinct that was already, unknowingly, a form of *tawajjuh*. A turning toward.

*Khabar* and *qabas* showed us: what Moses was looking for — direction and warmth — is exactly what God gave him, but at an infinite scale.

Together they say: **the act of honestly turning toward available light, even for practical reasons, even in a moment of ordinary cold — is the structure of genuine divine encounter.** And what is found there tends to exceed, enormously, what was sought.

---

### Questions to Carry

Here are questions I want to leave genuinely open — not to answer now, but to carry:

**1.** What fire have you been drawn toward recently — something that pulled at you, that you moved toward almost instinctively — that you haven't yet understood? What might God be offering in that fire?

**2.** When you turn toward God in difficult moments, are you more often seeking *khabar* — orientation, understanding, direction — or *qabas* — warmth, sustenance, emotional survival? Is there one you've been neglecting?

**3.** Moses said to his family: *Stay here.* He went ahead, alone, into unknown light. Are you in a moment where you've been called to go first — toward something uncertain — while the people you love wait?

**4.** The root of *ānastu* is the same root as *insān* — the human being. What does it mean to you that being human is, in some sense, being the creature drawn toward comfort and intimacy? How does that change how you read your own moments of longing?

**5.** Moses didn't know the fire was divine. He approached it with need, not knowledge. Has there been a moment in your life when you didn't know God was present in something — and later came to understand that He was?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Moses turned toward an ordinary fire for ordinary reasons — cold, lost, and needing both direction and warmth — and in that utterly human turning found that God was already there.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

Let something small shift.

The next time you find yourself in darkness — disoriented, cold, looking for the nearest available light — let the instinct to turn toward it be an act of faith, not just survival. The root of that impulse is the same root as what it means to be human. It is the **أ-ن-س** of your nature: the creature who craves warmth, intimacy, the comfort of presence.

You don't have to be spiritually prepared. You don't have to approach correctly. You don't have to know what is waiting in the fire.

Turn toward it. Bring your need. Tell God, honestly, what your family is cold and what you came looking for.

**Go to the fire. And let God speak.**

### Du'ā'

*O Allah, You were in the fire that Moses saw in the dark — present before he arrived, speaking before he understood where he was.*

*You are in every fire we turn toward in our darkness, when we don't know which direction to go and we've run out of other plans. Give us the khabar we need — not just local direction, but the orientation that reorders everything, the news that anchors us in who You are. And give us the qabas — a piece of warmth we can carry back to those who are cold around us, that does not diminish in the carrying.*

*Make our ordinary turnings toward light into the beginnings of something we did not dare to ask for.*
