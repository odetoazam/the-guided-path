# The Question That Cannot Be Answered

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 64

### Arabic

> أَمَّن يَبْدَؤُا۟ ٱلْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُۥ وَمَن يَرْزُقُكُم مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۗ أَءِلَـٰهٌ مَّعَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ قُلْ هَاتُوا۟ بُرْهَـٰنَكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَـٰدِقِينَ

### Transliteration

> *Amman yabda'ul-khalqa thumma yu'īduhu wa man yarzuqukum mina-s-samā'i wal-arḍ. A-ilāhun ma'a-llāh. Qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn.*

### Translation

> Or He who originates creation, then repeats it, and who provides for you from the sky and the earth? Is there a god alongside Allah? Say: Bring your proof, if you are truthful.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

Before we open this ayah, we need to understand what kind of room we have walked into.

For four ayahs before this one — ayahs 60, 61, 62, 63 — Allah has been asking a series of questions. They all begin the same way: *amman* — "or He who...?" Each one points to something the listener can see, touch, feel, or remember. Who created the heavens and the earth and sent down rain that grew the gardens? Who made the earth a stable resting place and placed rivers and mountains? Who answers the one in distress when he calls? Who guides you through the darkness of land and sea?

Four questions. Four phenomena. The original audience — the Quraysh of Makkah, who acknowledged Allah as Creator but worshipped intermediaries alongside Him — they could see every one of these things. The rain. The mountains. The dark night journey when only one voice answered.

And now we arrive at the fifth and final question. [PAUSE]

But notice — Allah does something different here. He does not point to one more visible thing. He pivots. He moves from what the eyes can verify to what only revelation can disclose: **origination of creation itself, and its return**. He pairs that with a phrase whose breadth is so total that nothing escapes it: *who provides for you from the sky and the earth*.

And then — for the first time in the sequence — He does not just ask. He prosecutes. He instructs the Prophet ﷺ to issue a courtroom demand:

> **Bring your proof, if you are truthful.**

### The Hook

This is the only ayah in the five-ayah sequence that ends not with a question but with a subpoena. It is the only one that names not what *is*, but what the opposition *must produce* — and cannot.

And here is what is stunning: the very thing Allah uses to silence the opposition is a thing the original audience never argued about. The Quraysh did not deny that Allah created. They denied that He would resurrect.

So watch what Allah does. He places the disputed thing — *the return* — inside a question they already had to answer "Allah" to. He smuggles the resurrection through the door of creation. And then He demands proof from anyone who would name a partner in any of it.

[PAUSE]

By the time we finish this ayah, we should understand why this question — out of all five — is the one that ends with silence.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Amman* — The Question That Forces a Choice

Before we open the word, let me teach you what this construction actually is.

In English, when we want to force someone to choose between two things, we say: "Did you do it, **or** did he?" The word *or* in that sentence is doing something specific. It is not offering a list. It is presenting a fork in the road. You must take one path. You cannot take both.

Arabic has a particle that is even sharper than this. It is the particle *am* — meaning "or." When *am* is used in a question (what grammarians call *am al-munqaṭiʿa* or *am al-muttaṣila*), it is forcing a comparison between two things that cannot both be true. And when *am* fuses with *man* — "who" — it produces *amman*: "or He who...?"

Now open the ayah. Allah does not say *wa man yabda'u* — "and who originates." He says *amman yabda'u* — "**or** He who originates."

Stay with this. The word "or" is implying a question Allah is not even bothering to write down. It is as if Allah is saying: *the alternative you have been clinging to* — your idols, your intermediaries, your imagined gods — *or He who originates creation and returns it?* Choose.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. This same particle *am* opens every one of the five questions in this sequence — 60, 61, 62, 63, 64. Five times Allah presents the fork. Five times He declines to even articulate the other side of the comparison, because the other side is so embarrassingly weak that to name it would be to dignify it.

Notice what the *am* does to the listener. It does not let you sit on the fence. It does not let you say "well, both have their merits." It is a grammatical hand on your back, pushing you toward one of two doors and locking the other behind you.

What this single particle reveals is that **tawhid is not a position you arrive at by adding things up. It is a position you arrive at by being forced to choose.** And the Quran, again and again, refuses to let the listener stay in the comfortable middle.

### Element Two: *Yabda'u... Yu'īduhu* — The Two Verbs That Bracket Existence

Now look at the first thing Allah claims He does.

> *yabda'u-l-khalqa thumma yu'īduhu*
> originates creation, then returns it

Two verbs. *Yabda'u* — from the root *b-d-ʾ* — means to begin, to initiate, to bring something into being for the first time, with no precedent. The lexicons of classical Arabic specify this: *al-bad'* is creation *ex nihilo*, the act of starting where there was nothing.

The second verb — *yu'īduhu* — comes from the root *ʿ-w-d*, the same root as *ʿāda* (to return) and *ʿīd* (the recurring festival, literally "the returning one"). The Form IV verbal noun is *iʿādah* — "to bring something back." It is what you do when something has gone and you produce it again.

Together, these two verbs are not random. They bracket all of existence. *Bad'* is the first word in the cosmic sentence. *Iʿādah* is the last. Everything you have ever experienced — every breath, every relationship, every joy, every grief — sits between these two verbs.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what the original audience would have heard.

The Quraysh did not deny that Allah originated creation. The Quran tells us elsewhere that if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth they would say *Allah* (29:61, 31:25). What they denied — fiercely, publicly, mockingly — was the second verb. The *return*. The resurrection. They said: "When we have become bones and dust, are we to be raised up again?" They thought it was absurd.

So watch what Allah does grammatically. He does not put the two verbs in separate sentences. He does not say "He originates creation. And He returns it." He links them with *thumma* — "then" — and uses a pronoun *-hu* ("it") that refers back to the same *khalq* (creation).

The grammar is doing the argument for Him. **The same grammatical object that begins is the same grammatical object that returns.** If you accept the first verb, the grammar has already made you accept the second. There is no seam to slip through. The pronoun *-hu* binds them.

This is why classical scholars — Razi, Zamakhshari, Ibn Kathir, Tabari — read this construction as one of the most compact arguments for resurrection in the entire Quran. Razi notes that the one who originated something from nothing is, by any measure, more capable of returning it from something. Zamakhshari focuses on the *thumma* — the temporal sequence — emphasizing that creation and return are two phases of one divine act, not two competing claims.

These are not alternative readings. They are the same realization seen from two angles. The grammar makes the resurrection inescapable for anyone who has already conceded the creation.

What this verbal pairing reveals is something we should sit with: **the things we accept logically often contain the very conclusions we resist emotionally.** The Quraysh's own theology, if followed honestly to its conclusion, demanded resurrection. They just refused to walk to the end of the road.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Hātū Burhānakum* — The Courtroom Word

Now we come to the ending. And this is where the ayah turns from question to subpoena.

> *qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn*
> Say: bring your proof, if you are truthful

Let me teach you what *burhān* actually is. We translate it "proof" or "evidence," but in Arabic the word is heavier than that.

*Burhān*, from the root *b-r-h-n*, refers to a proof so decisive it cannot be refuted. The classical lexicographers — Raghib al-Isfahani in *Mufradāt*, the compilers of *Lisān al-ʿArab* — define *burhān* as *al-hujjah al-bayyinah*, "the manifest, unassailable demonstration." It is not "evidence that suggests." It is "evidence that compels." In classical Arabic rhetoric, *burhān* is the kind of proof a judge cannot dismiss.

This is the word Allah chooses. Not *dalīl* (a sign that points). Not *ḥujjah* (an argument). *Burhān* — the proof that ends the argument.

And notice the verb that summons it: *hātū*. This verb, from the imperative root *h-ā-t*, is not the polite "please offer" of academic discourse. It is "hand it over." "Produce it now." It is the verb you would use if you were a judge ordering a litigant to surrender the document they claim exists. There is a kind of judicial impatience baked into it.

Stay with this for a moment. Picture the scene grammatically. The Prophet ﷺ is being commanded to stand in front of a people who have spent their lives saying *Allah has partners*, and to demand from them the *burhān* — the unassailable proof — for that claim. Not their tradition. Not their elders' authority. Not the comfortable consensus of their tribe. The proof.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is staggering.

There is no proof. There never was. The very absence of *burhān* — the silence that follows the demand — is itself the verdict. Allah elsewhere puts this exact form in the mouths of His enemies on the Day of Judgment: *qul hātū burhānakum* — "say: bring your proof" — in 2:111, 21:24, 28:75. The same imperative. Each time, it is a demand to which silence is the only honest response.

And then the conditional: *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* — "if you are truthful."

The conditional *in* in Arabic is the *if* of doubt. (Compare to *idhā*, which is the *if* of expected occurrence — "when.") *In* is the *if* you use when you do not actually expect the condition to be met. So the ending is not just "if you are truthful." It carries an implication: *if you are truthful — and we both know you are not.*

What this ending reveals is something tremendous about what real conviction requires. **Tawhid is not protected by sentimentality. It is protected by burhān.** And the burden of proof — Allah is teaching us through this ayah — does not rest on the believer who acknowledges the obvious. It rests on the one who would claim a partner where none can be shown.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what the linguistic journey has shown us.

The *am* forced a choice. The *yabda'u-yu'īduhu* welded creation and resurrection into a single grammatical object. The *hātū burhānakum* turned the question into a subpoena.

But these are not just rhetorical victories on a page. They are pointing toward something about reality itself — about how we hold belief, where the burden of proof sits, and what the universe is actually doing every moment we are alive. Now let us ask what this ayah reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Sustenance That Comes From Above and Below

Begin where you live.

You have eaten today. Or you will. And whatever you ate — bread, rice, fruit, meat — it came to your plate through a chain so long you cannot even trace it. Sunlight that struck a leaf months ago. Rain that fell in a place you have never been. Soil whose minerals were placed there by geological processes that took millennia. A farmer you will never meet. A truck driver whose name you do not know. A cashier. A grandmother who taught the recipe. The sweat of your own work to earn the money.

Every meal is the closing of a circuit that runs through the entire planet and the entire sky.

Now read the ayah again:

> *wa man yarzuqukum mina-s-samā'i wal-arḍ*
> and who provides for you from the sky and the earth

Notice what Allah does not say. He does not say "from the sky." He does not say "from the earth." He says **"from the sky and the earth."** Both. Together. The entire vertical span of your sustenance — from the photons hitting the wheat to the roots pulling minerals from the soil — is named in one phrase.

And notice what He withholds. He does not specify *what* the sustenance is. He does not say "your bread." He does not say "your fruit." Just *yarzuqukum* — "provides for you." The unspecified object swallows everything you can think of. Food. Water. Air. The medicine that healed you last winter. The friend who showed up. The thought that arrived in your mind when you needed it. *Rizq* in the Quran is never just material — Imam al-Razi notes that *rizq* includes anything that benefits you, including the rizq of guidance, the rizq of patience, the rizq of a believing companion.

[PAUSE]

Here is what we usually do. We look at our paycheck. We look at our skill, our network, our hustle. We trace our sustenance horizontally — from us, to our work, to our employer, to our income, to our table. The chain is human-shaped at every link.

And the ayah is saying — gently but firmly — *look up*. Look down. The vertical axis is the real one. Whatever runs along the horizontal (your skill, your effort, your paycheck) is sitting on top of a vertical column you did not build.

Stay with this image. You are not a self-supporting structure. You are a creature held in place by a sustenance descending from above and rising from below. The horizontal chain — the work, the wage, the meal — is real, but it is the dependent variable. The vertical chain is the independent one.

And here is the question the ayah is forcing you to ask: *if I am held this way every moment of every day, by whom?*

The Quraysh's answer was: by Allah. And then they would turn around and worship intermediaries, attaching their hopes and fears to creatures who could not feed themselves, much less feed a planet. The ayah is asking you, today: *what are the intermediaries to whom you have given your gratitude, your fear, your dependence — when the vertical column belongs to One alone?*

The practical application of this theme is small but transformative: **at every meal, name the column.** Not just "Bismillah" as a habit. The brief, conscious recognition that the food in front of you is the closing of a circuit Allah opened from the sky and the earth, and that no created intermediary can claim a single link of it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Burden of Proof Has Been Inverted

Now we sit with the second theme. And this one is going to ask something of us.

In our age — and this is not new, but it is acute — there is a quiet assumption that floats in the air: *the believer must prove. The skeptic gets to ask.* The default position is doubt; faith is the thing that has to defend itself. Every conversation with a skeptical relative, every late-night question from a child raised on internet content, every academic critique — they all sit on top of this assumption. *You make a claim about God; now justify it.*

The ayah inverts this completely.

> *qul hātū burhānakum in kuntum ṣādiqīn*
> Say: bring your proof, if you are truthful

Notice who is being asked to produce the proof. Not the believer. The one who would name a partner alongside Allah. The one who would say: *yes, Allah created — but there is also this other thing I depend on, this other intermediary I have given my heart to.*

Here is the human reality the ayah is exposing. **Everyone is making a metaphysical claim.** The person who says "there is no God" is making a claim. The person who says "there are many gods" is making a claim. The person who says "I depend on my career, my health, my family, my luck" — without ever naming it — is making a claim. The atheist, the polytheist, the secular pragmatist — none of them are sitting in a neutral default. They are all advancing a position.

The Quran's argument is not "trust us, we have proof for tawhid." The Quran's argument is: *tawhid is the only position whose proof is being delivered to you continuously, every moment, through your eyes, your breath, your meal, your morning.* The signs are everywhere — the very phenomena of the previous four ayahs — heavens, earth, mountains, rivers, the answering of the desperate. The proof for tawhid is the universe.

So what about the alternatives? *Hātū burhānakum.* Bring yours.

[PAUSE]

The Quraysh could not. The atheist cannot — there is no positive demonstration that the universe is uncaused. The polytheist cannot — there is no demonstration of a partner who created or sustains. The functional materialist who has quietly enthroned their career or their bank account cannot — they cannot show that those things actually do what they have been asked to do.

The silence that follows the demand *hātū burhānakum* is the most eloquent silence in scripture. It is the sound of every alternative to tawhid running out of ground to stand on.

And here is the personal application — and this is hard. Look at where in your life you have allowed yourself to operate as if something other than Allah were a partner. A career you have given the weight of providence. A relationship you have asked to bear the meaning only Allah can bear. A fear of opinions that has organized your decisions more than fear of Allah. A self-image you protect with more vigilance than you protect your prayer.

For each of those, the ayah is asking — kindly, but unflinchingly — *hātū burhānakum.* Bring the proof that this thing deserves the place you have given it.

The practical application of this theme: **conduct a quiet audit.** Once. List the three things in your life that compete most for the place that belongs to Allah. For each one, ask: what is the *burhān* that justifies the weight I have given it? Sit with the answer. The point is not guilt. The point is a return — *yabda'u thumma yu'īduhu* — to the right order.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at what this ayah has built.

The opening — *amman yabda'u-l-khalqa thumma yu'īduhu* — names the vertical of *time*. Beginning and return. Origination and resurrection. The two ends of existence.

The middle — *wa man yarzuqukum mina-s-samā'i wal-arḍ* — names the vertical of *space*. From the sky and from the earth. The full vertical column of sustenance.

So what is held inside this ayah? Time and space. The full grid of created reality. And one question runs through all of it: *a-ilāhun maʿa-llāh* — is there a god alongside Allah? Across the full grid of time and the full grid of space, is there a partner?

And then the demand: *bring your proof.*

The architecture is a courtroom built from the dimensions of the universe. The judge is Allah. The witness is creation itself. The defendant is anyone who would name another. And the verdict is silence.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic level showed us *am* forcing a choice, the verbs welding creation to resurrection, *burhān* demanding what cannot be produced. The thematic level showed us a vertical sustenance descending and rising every moment, and a burden of proof that the modern conversation has placed on the wrong shoulder.

Both are saying the same thing: **tawhid is the position the universe is already arguing for. Everything else has to bring evidence it does not have.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life have I treated as a partner — given the weight, the fear, the gratitude that belongs to Allah alone?
2. When I name my sustenance, do I trace it horizontally to my work and skill, or vertically to the One who opens the sky and the earth?
3. If Allah said to me today *hātū burhānak* — bring the proof — for the things I have feared more than Him, the things I have hoped from more than Him — what would I produce?
4. Do I believe in resurrection the way I believe in creation? Or do I, like the Quraysh, walk halfway down the road and stop?
5. Where in my conversation with myself, with my doubts, with my critics, have I allowed the burden of proof to sit on the wrong side?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The same Allah who originates and returns creation, and who holds you up from sky and earth in every breath, has placed the burden of proof on anyone who would name a partner — and the silence that follows is the verdict.

### The Closing Invitation

Let one thing shift after this ayah. Not a doctrine in your head. A small posture in your life.

The next time you eat — really eat, with attention — let yourself feel the vertical column. The sky. The earth. The One who opened both onto your plate. And let the gratitude land where it belongs.

The next time fear of an outcome, fear of a person's opinion, fear of a future scenario starts to organize your decisions — pause. Ask the ayah's question quietly: *a-ilāhun maʿa-llāh?* Have I let this thing become a god alongside Allah?

That is the invitation. Not a heavier prayer life. Not more guilt. A return to the right architecture.
