# The Question Before the Verdict: When Allah Names the Sin Out Loud

## Introduction

Listen to these words first. Don't analyze them. Just let them land.

[ayah:27:54] وَلُوطًا إِذْ قَالَ لِقَوْمِهِۦٓ أَتَأْتُونَ ٱلْفَـٰحِشَةَ وَأَنتُمْ تُبْصِرُونَ

*Wa Lūṭan idh qāla li-qawmihi: a-taʾtūna l-fāḥishata wa antum tubṣirūn.*

*"And Lut, when he said to his people: Do you commit the shameless deed while you see?"*

[PAUSE]

Nine words. That's all this ayah is. Nine words in Arabic.

But before we can even step inside them, we need to understand where we're standing. Because Surah An-Naml is not just any surah. Its name comes from an ant — a single ant who *saw* the army of Sulayman approaching and warned her colony. The whole surah is built around the act of **seeing**. Sulayman sees a hoopoe missing from his ranks. The Queen of Sheba sees a palace and mistakes it for water. The people of Salih plot in the dark thinking no one sees them. And now — in this ayah — a prophet stands before his people and asks them about something they're doing **with their eyes open**.

[PAUSE]

The world this ayah enters was not a private world. The people of Lut were not sinning in shame, hiding in cellars, weeping after. The classical tafsir tradition — Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi, al-Tabari — all converge on a picture of a community that had not just committed a sin but had **normalized** it. Made it culture. Made it identity. Made it the kind of thing one does in daylight, in gatherings, in public.

That's the climate Lut is speaking into. Not a community that doesn't know. A community that knows perfectly well and has decided not to care.

And here's what's stunning before we even begin: Lut doesn't open with a sermon. He doesn't open with threats of punishment. He doesn't open with verses or laws or warnings.

He opens with a **question**.

Why?

That question — why a question — is what we're going to spend the next hour with. Because hidden inside Lut's opening line is one of the most precise diagnostic tools the Quran ever gives us for understanding our own hearts.

[PAUSE]

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word That Starts Everything — *A-taʾtūna* ("Do you come to...?")

Before we touch Lut's word, let's understand what an interrogative does in Arabic.

In English when I ask "Are you doing this?" — it's almost always a request for information. I want to know yes or no.

In Arabic, the *hamzat al-istifhām* — the question particle at the start of a verb — can do that. But it has another function classical rhetoricians named centuries ago: **istifhām inkārī** — the questioning that *denies*. The question that is not asking for information but *delivering a verdict in the form of a question*.

Think about it in your own life. When a parent walks in and sees their child standing on the kitchen counter and says, *"Are you really standing on that counter?"* — they are not asking. They know. The child knows they know. The question is a mirror. It is forcing the child to look at themselves doing the thing they are doing.

That is exactly what Lut does in his opening word.

[PAUSE]

The verb here is **تَأْتُونَ** — *taʾtūna* — from the root **أ-ت-ي** (*ʾ-t-y*), which simply means *to come, to approach, to arrive at something*.

Now stay with me. Allah did not say *afʿalūna* — "do you do." He did not say *atartakibūna* — "do you commit." He said *a-taʾtūna* — **"do you come to?"**

In Arabic, *ityān* (the verbal noun of this root) carries the sense of *approaching something deliberately, moving toward it, making a journey to it*. You don't *come to* something by accident. You *come to* it by choice, by direction, by intent.

This is the same root used when Allah says **وَأْتُوا الْبُيُوتَ مِنْ أَبْوَابِهَا** — "and *come to* houses through their doors." It's the verb of deliberate arrival.

So when Lut says *a-taʾtūna*, he is not merely asking *do you do this thing?* He is asking: **do you keep walking toward this? Do you keep arriving at this? Do you keep making the journey to this sin, over and over, with full intent?**

[PAUSE]

This is the first wound the ayah opens in us. Because most of us, when we examine our sins, comfort ourselves with the language of accident. *It just happened. I slipped. I fell into it.*

But Lut's question strips that language away. He doesn't say *did you fall.* He says *did you walk there.* And the present tense — *taʾtūna* — is the present of **habitual action**. Not "did you once," but "do you keep doing this — even now?"

Notice something. Allah, the Most Merciful, could have softened this. He could have placed in Lut's mouth a gentler verb. He chose this one. Why?

Because the first step of repentance is the **honest naming of the journey**. Not "this happened to me" — but "I walked there. Again. And again. And I am still walking."

Until a person can say *I came to this,* they cannot turn back. You cannot reverse a path you refuse to admit you took.

### Element Two: *Al-Fāḥisha* — The Word That Refuses to Be Diluted

The object of Lut's question is **ٱلْفَـٰحِشَةَ** — *al-fāḥisha*.

The root **ف-ح-ش** (*f-ḥ-sh*) in classical Arabic means: that which **exceeds its proper limits**. *Fāḥisha* is not just a sin. It is a sin that has crossed every boundary that contains it. *Lisān al-ʿArab* records the meaning as something *exceedingly hideous*, something *whose ugliness has overflowed its container*.

Imagine a cup of dye. A drop is a stain. A spilled drop is a mess. But *fāḥisha* is dye that has soaked through the cloth, the table, the floor — that has stained everything it touched and cannot be undone by wiping. The word itself **carries the texture of overflow**.

Now look at how Lut uses it: **ٱلْفَـٰحِشَةَ** — *al-fāḥisha*, with the definite article *al-*.

This is not a passing detail. In Arabic, the definite article does precise work. He did not say *do you commit a shameless deed* (*fāḥishatan*). He said *the shameless deed* (*al-fāḥishata*).

[PAUSE]

What does *the* do here?

It does what *the* always does. It picks out a specific, known, definite object. When I say "did you eat *a* sandwich?" — I'm asking generally. When I say "did you eat *the* sandwich?" — both of us know which sandwich. We share knowledge of it.

So when Lut says **al-fāḥisha** — *the* shameless deed — he is saying: **you and I both know what I am talking about. There is no ambiguity here. There is no debate about what this is. We share a vocabulary for this sin, and you have decided to do it anyway.**

This is what makes Lut's question so devastating. Not just that he names the sin. But that he names it with a definite article that **removes their last hiding place** — the hiding place of *what do you even mean? Define your terms. Don't be specific.*

Lut refuses the fog. He says *the* fāḥisha. *That* one. The one we all know about.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with what this means for our own hearts.

One of the most reliable signs that a person is losing the battle with a particular sin is that they begin to **resist letting it be named**. They speak around it. They wrap it in euphemism. They prefer the phrase "my struggles" over the actual word. They say "I'm working through some things" instead of saying *I am doing this specific thing*.

Why? Because the unnamed sin is the protected sin. It can move freely under cover. It can be reframed, minimized, intellectualized.

The moment you say *the* before it — the moment you allow the definite article — you have done something powerful. You have made it a specific, known, definite object in your inner world. And now it can be confronted. Now it can be repented from. Now it can be cut.

This is why every authentic tradition of *tawba* in our tradition begins with what the scholars call *iqrār* — explicit naming. Not *I'm sorry generally.* But *Ya Allah, I did this specific thing.* Lut's question is the prophetic enactment of this principle.

He picks up the *al*.
He sets it before them.
And he asks: *can you still pretend you don't know what this is?*

### Element Three: *Wa antum tubṣirūn* — "While You See"

Now we arrive at the part of the ayah that should make us shudder.

**وَأَنتُمْ تُبْصِرُونَ** — *wa antum tubṣirūn* — "while you see."

The root is **ب-ص-ر** (*b-ṣ-r*). Now, classical Arabic has two main verbs of seeing. **Raʾā** — to see with the eyes. And **abṣara / tubṣir** — to see with **insight**. To perceive. To register. To understand what you are looking at.

*Baṣīra* — from the same root — is the word for **inner sight**, moral clarity, the ability to grasp what something *is*.

When Allah describes a person who has lost moral perception, He does not say their eyes have failed. He says **فَإِنَّهَا لَا تَعْمَى ٱلْأَبْصَـٰرُ وَلَـٰكِن تَعْمَى ٱلْقُلُوبُ ٱلَّتِى فِى ٱلصُّدُورِ** — "for indeed it is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts that are in the chests" (22:46). The *abṣār* there is the same root — but Allah is contrasting outer sight with the inner sight of the heart.

So when Lut says *wa antum tubṣirūn*, he is not making the obvious point that they have functioning eyeballs. He is making a far more serious accusation.

He is saying: **you can see this. You possess the inner faculty to register what this is. You have not lost moral perception. You know.**

[PAUSE]

Notice what the classical tradition does with this phrase. Some scholars read *tubṣirūn* as referring to their physical sight — they do it in the open, in front of each other. Others read it as their moral perception — they know it is wrong. Others read it as both.

The point is: **all three readings are present**, layered on top of each other. The Quran often does this — a single word vibrating across multiple meanings, each one true, all of them indicting at once.

So Lut is saying — *in the same breath* — three things:

1. You can see each other doing this. (Physical exposure)
2. You can see this is wrong. (Moral perception)
3. You see — and you do it anyway. (The final indictment)

[PAUSE]

The grammar here does something specific. *Wa antum tubṣirūn* is what Arabic grammarians call a *jumla ḥāliyya* — a circumstantial clause. It describes the state, the condition, in which the main verb is happening.

So the structure of the ayah, when fully unpacked, becomes:

**Do you keep walking toward the shameless deed — in a state of seeing?**

The seeing is the **condition surrounding the action**. The seeing is happening *at the same time* as the sin. Not before. Not after. *Simultaneously.*

This is the sin's most damning form. Not the sin of the ignorant who didn't know. Not the sin of the unconscious who didn't register. The sin of the one who is **seeing it happen, knowing what it is, and continuing**.

[PAUSE]

And here's where we have to stop pretending this ayah is about somebody else.

Because if we are honest — terribly honest — we know this state. We have lived in it. Every one of us has at some point committed a sin while a small voice in the chest said *you see what this is.* And we did it anyway. And the doing-it-anyway-while-seeing was the actual sin. The act itself was the symptom. The willingness to act *while seeing* was the disease.

This is what Lut diagnoses in one phrase.

This is what the Quran will not let us hide from.

### Bridging the Linguistic Journey

Let me show you what we just walked through.

Lut said only nine words. And in those nine words:

- The verb *taʾtūna* exposed that this was a **journey**, not an accident.
- The article *al-* in *al-fāḥisha* exposed that this was a **specific known thing**, not a vague struggle.
- The *ḥāl* clause *wa antum tubṣirūn* exposed that this was happening **with full perception intact**.

Three layers of exposure. Three places to hide — sealed off in a single sentence.

He did not lecture them. He did not threaten them. He did not warn them of punishment yet. He simply **closed every exit from honest self-knowledge** and then asked the question.

Now we have to ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Specific Cruelty of the Open-Eyed Sin

Let's start somewhere uncomfortable. Somewhere modern. Somewhere where you live.

Most of us have a habit we know is bad for us. Maybe it's a substance. Maybe it's a screen we can't put down. Maybe it's a way of speaking to people who love us. Maybe it's something larger that nobody else knows about.

And most of us have one moment — usually a quiet moment, often at night — where we *see it clearly*. Where the fog lifts and we know **exactly** what we are doing, exactly what it is costing us, exactly what we have decided to lose. And then, with the seeing fully intact, we keep doing it.

That moment — that specific, terrible, fully conscious moment — is what Lut is asking his people about.

[PAUSE]

The world has a word for this. Psychologists call it *cognitive dissonance* — the gap between what you know and what you do. The pop-spiritual response to cognitive dissonance is usually: *be kinder to yourself, you're only human, don't be so hard on yourself.*

The Quran does not do this. The Quran takes the dissonance and **names it**. Refuses to let you smooth it over. Sits you down and asks: *do you understand that you can see this and you are doing it anyway?*

Because here is what the Quran knows that the modern world has forgotten: **the dissonance is the doorway**. The discomfort of the gap between knowing and doing is not a problem to be soothed. It is a *signal* that the heart is still alive. It is the last alarm before the alarm stops ringing.

[PAUSE]

The Quran is terrified — and wants us to be terrified — of one thing more than the sin itself. It is terrified of *what happens after sustained open-eyed sin*.

Listen to this: **بَلْ رَانَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ** — "rather, a rust has covered their hearts from what they used to earn" (83:14).

When the open-eyed sin continues long enough, the eyes stop being open. The *tubṣirūn* fades. The clarity dims. The voice in the chest that used to say *you see what this is* gets quieter, and quieter, and quieter — until one day, you sin and **nothing inside you objects anymore**. And that is the moment the Quran weeps for, not the sin itself.

Lut's people are not yet there. The fact that Lut can still ask them *while you see* means **they still see**. The window is still open. The diagnosis is still possible.

That is why Lut leads with the question. He is reaching them while they can still be reached.

[PAUSE]

Now make this personal.

When was the last time you felt that gap? That moment where you knew exactly what you were doing and did it anyway? Most of us treat that moment as a moment of weakness, of failure, of shame to be buried.

The Quran treats it as **the moment of opportunity**. The moment when you still see is the moment you can still turn. Once the seeing fades, turning becomes harder by an order of magnitude. The heart that has gone numb to its own wrongdoing has to be revived before it can be redirected — and only Allah can revive it.

So the practical application here is almost shockingly simple. **Do not bury the moment of seeing.** When you feel that clarity — that uncomfortable, naked clarity about what you are actually doing — do not change the subject in your own head. Do not reach for the phone. Do not flood it with distraction. **Sit with it.**

That clarity is a mercy. It is the still-functioning alarm in a building that has been on fire for years. Do not silence it. *Walk toward it.* It is, perhaps, the only direction left that is not toward *al-fāḥisha*.

### Theme Two: Why a Question? The Method of the Prophets

Let's ask the structural question we set up at the beginning. Why does Lut open with a question?

He could have opened with a command: *stop doing this!*
He could have opened with a threat: *Allah will destroy you for this!*
He could have opened with a law: *Allah has forbidden this!*

He opens with a question.

[PAUSE]

Look across the Quran. Watch how often the prophets — and Allah Himself — confront wrongdoing with questions rather than declarations.

**أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ** — "Do you not then use reason?"
**أَفَلَا تَتَفَكَّرُونَ** — "Do you not then reflect?"
**أَتَعْبُدُونَ مَا تَنْحِتُونَ** — Ibrahim to his people: "Do you worship what you carve?"
**أَتَتَّخِذُونَنِى وَأُمِّىَ إِلَـٰهَيْنِ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ** — Isa being asked: "Did you tell people to take you and your mother as gods?"

The question is the **default prophetic mode**. Why?

Because a question does something a declaration cannot do. A declaration is an external imposition — *here is what is true, accept it.* A question forces the listener to **construct the answer inside themselves**.

When Lut asks *do you come to the shameless deed while you see?* — every person in that crowd is forced, even for a half-second, to answer the question internally. *Do we? Are we?* And in that half-second of internal answering, **the truth is briefly inside them, looking out from their own eyes**.

A truth delivered as a statement can be rejected as an attack from outside.
A truth that you have just spoken to yourself — even silently, even briefly — has been admitted into the house.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most powerful things prophetic communication teaches us, and almost no one practices it.

We — when we want to correct someone — almost always reach for declaration. *You're wrong. This is bad. You shouldn't do this.* And the listener, predictably, defends.

The prophets reach for the question. *What do you think you're doing here? Do you really believe that idol made the sky? Do you commit the shameless deed while you see?*

The question respects the listener's mind enough to make them use it. It says: *I am not going to tell you the answer. I am going to put you in a position where you must face it yourself.*

[PAUSE]

There is something profoundly humbling in this for us. Because if even Lut — a *prophet*, speaking to a people who had crossed every line — chose the question over the declaration, what business do we have **declaring** at the people in our lives whose conduct we disagree with?

The classical scholars notice another thing about prophetic questions. They are almost never sarcastic. They are not "gotcha" questions. They are not rhetorical traps designed to humiliate. They are *honest invitations into self-knowledge*. They assume — they actually assume — that the listener still has the capacity to answer correctly. They are acts of respect even in confrontation.

So when you stand before someone you love whose conduct frightens you — your child, your sibling, your friend, your spouse, yourself in the mirror — and you feel the urge to declare and condemn, remember Lut's nine words.

He named the sin specifically. He named the seeing specifically. And then he gave them the dignity of asking.

[PAUSE]

The practical application here is direct. The next time you have to speak hard truth to someone — including to yourself — try opening with a question instead of a declaration. Not a manipulative question. An honest one. *Do you see what you are doing here? Is this who you want to be? Do you know what this is costing the people who love you?*

You may find, as the prophets did, that a well-formed question opens doors no declaration ever could. Because a question lets the answer arise from inside the person. And the answer that arises from inside is the only answer that ever actually changes anything.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have now.

The **linguistic journey** showed us:
- *taʾtūna* — the sin is a journey, not an accident
- *al-fāḥisha* — the sin is a specific, knowable, nameable thing
- *wa antum tubṣirūn* — the sin is committed with perception intact

The **thematic depths** showed us:
- The open-eyed sin is the most dangerous because it numbs the eye itself
- The question is the prophetic instrument that puts the truth inside the listener

These are not two separate insights. They are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic precision of Lut's question is *itself* the prophetic method in action. He uses exact words because he is not lecturing — he is **diagnosing**. He is naming each element of their sin with surgical clarity so that when they answer the question in their own hearts, **there is nothing left to negotiate**.

The question and the precision are one act. The precision makes the question impossible to dodge. The question form makes the precision impossible to reject as external imposition.

This is what prophetic speech does. It puts the listener in a room with themselves and **closes the door behind them**.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

These are not rhetorical. Take them with you. Sit with them in the next quiet moment you have.

1. **What is the path I keep "coming to" — *taʾtūna* — even though I would never call myself someone who chooses to walk there?**

2. **What sin in my life have I refused to allow a definite article? What am I keeping in the fog of "my struggles" rather than calling by its specific name?**

3. **When was the last time I sinned while clearly seeing what I was doing? What did I do with that clarity afterward — bury it, or follow it?**

4. **When I correct someone I love, do I declare, or do I ask? What would change if I asked?**

5. **Is there a clarity I am currently feeling — about myself, about a habit, about a relationship — that I am rushing to silence? What would it cost me to sit with it instead?**

[PAUSE]

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Lut's question reveals that the most dangerous sin is not the one committed in darkness, but the one committed while the eyes are still open — because that is the sin that, if not turned from now, will eventually close the eyes forever.**

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to feel ashamed. Shame is the cheap response. Shame buries the seeing.

This ayah is asking you to do something harder. To not waste the moments when you can still see. To treat the gap between your knowing and your doing not as a private failure but as a **mercy that has not yet been withdrawn**. To say *al-* before your sin. To name the specific thing. To recognize the journey you have been walking. And then — gently, with the dignity Lut gave even to a people on the edge of destruction — to ask yourself the question.

Not *why am I so terrible.* That question changes nothing.

But Lut's question. *Am I still coming to this — while I see?*

And if the answer is yes, then the answer is also this: **the seeing is the way back**. While you can still see, you can still turn.

### Du'a

Ya Allah, do not let the rust cover our hearts. While we can still see what is wrong, give us the strength to walk away from it. And when we, in our weakness, find ourselves coming again toward what we know we should flee — let us hear, in the silence of our own chests, the question of Your prophet Lut. Let it stop us. Let it turn us. And let us answer it not with shame, but with the trembling honesty of one who has just remembered that they still can.

Ameen.
