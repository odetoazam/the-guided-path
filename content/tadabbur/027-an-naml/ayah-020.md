# The One Who Was Missing: A Prophet-King's Practice of Deliberate Attention

## Introduction: Imagine the Scale

*Arabic:*
[ayah:27:20] وَتَفَقَّدَ ٱلطَّيْرَ فَقَالَ مَا لِىَ لَآ أَرَى ٱلْهُدْهُدَ أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْغَآئِبِينَ

*Transliteration:*
Wa tafaqqada al-ṭayra fa-qāla mā liya lā arā al-hudhuda am kāna min al-ghā'ibīn.

*Translation:*
"And he inspected the birds and said: 'Why is it that I do not see the hoopoe? Or was he among the absent?'"

---

Before a single word of this ayah can land, we need to inhabit the world in which it happens. Because without that world, the verse sounds ordinary. And what is about to unfold is anything but.

Sulayman — peace be upon him — was not a king in the usual sense. The Quran does not let him be that. It tells us explicitly what he himself declared in gratitude to Allah: *uʿṭīnā min kulli shay'* — "we have been given of everything" (27:16). That phrase is breathtaking in its scope. Not "we have been given much." Not "we have been given a great kingdom." Everything.

And the verses surrounding our ayah give us a glimpse of what that encompassed. Just before 27:20, in verse 27:17, Allah describes something that has no parallel in human history:

**فَحُشِرَ لِسُلَيْمَانَ جُنُودُهُ مِنَ الْجِنِّ وَالْإِنسِ وَالطَّيْرِ**
"And assembled for Sulayman were his armies of jinn, humans, and birds."

Three realms. Not two — three. Jinn: creatures of smokeless fire, invisible to human eyes, capable of extraordinary power, operating across dimensions we cannot access. Humans: the full breadth of civilization under one command. And birds: every wing, every species, every creature of feather and flight — marshaled and ordered and moving together.

The word *ḥushira* — "assembled" — is not ordinary either. It is the verb used for the gathering of all humanity on the Day of Resurrection. The same word. Allah chose it deliberately. This was a gathering of totality — a bringing-together of completeness.

Now hold that image. Tens of thousands assembled. An army that covered the horizon in every direction — above it, below it, on land and through air. A spectacle that no human being before or since has witnessed.

And then: one small bird was not in its place.

And Sulayman noticed.

[PAUSE]

That is the moment this ayah opens. Not the battle that followed. Not the political intrigue that would emerge from the hoopoe's intelligence. The first thing. The founding act. The prophet-king with the largest, most impossible army in history stops and asks: *where is the hoopoe?*

How does a man with tens of thousands under his command know that one bird — one — is absent?

That question is the doorway into everything that follows. Hold it. We are going to walk through it slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### The Weight of a Single Verb

Let us begin with the very first word of the ayah. And before we enter the text, I want to teach you something about Arabic verb forms — because it will change what you hear.

In Arabic, a verb does not carry meaning in a flat, one-dimensional way. The same three-letter root can be arranged into up to ten standard patterns — called *awzān* — each of which adds a layer of semantic texture. One of the most significant is **Form V**, built by prefixing *ta-* and doubling the middle letter of the root.

Here is what Form V does as a general rule: it takes an action and makes it reflexive, intentional, self-initiated. It transforms something that might happen to you into something you actively pursue. It adds the weight of deliberateness.

The difference, in English terms, is something like this: between *glancing* and *scrutinizing*. Between *coming across* something and *actively hunting for* it. Between *noticing* and *inspecting*.

Form V is the verb of intention. The verb of practice. The verb of someone who made a choice to do something, not someone who fell into it.

Now look at the first word of the ayah:

**وَتَفَقَّدَ**

The root is **ف-ق-د** — *faqada*.

In Form I, *faqada* means: to lose something. To notice, after the fact, that something is gone. *Faqadtu al-miftāḥa* — "I lost the key." You reach for it. It isn't there. The loss finds you.

But Allah did not write *faqada*.

He wrote **tafaqqada** — Form V. Deliberately doubled qāf, deliberately prefixed *ta-*. This is not the verb of someone who accidentally discovered an absence. This is the verb of someone who sat down — metaphorically — and went through the assembly, one by one, deliberately, methodically, with the explicit intention of checking that everyone was there.

Not passive discovery. Active inspection.

[PAUSE]

Let me give you an analogy that carries real weight here.

Think of a shepherd counting sheep at dusk before closing the fold. He doesn't wait for a sheep to come back late and trip over it in the dark. He counts. Deliberately. One by one. And because he counts, he knows — before the darkness settles — whether all twenty are in or whether nineteen came home.

The counting is an act of responsibility. But more than responsibility — it is an act of *care*. You can only notice when one is missing if you first established, in your own attention, that each one matters. The count itself is the expression of that mattering.

*Tafaqqada* is the shepherd's count. It is Sulayman's deliberate roll call of the assembly Allah had given him.

And the classical commentators hear this too. Ibn Kathīr notes that Sulayman "would inspect" (*yatafaqqad*) his armies and the members of his court. The imperfective form there — *yatafaqqad* — suggests this was not a one-time act. It was characteristic. Habitual. A practice.

Al-Ṭabarī describes it as an active review: Sulayman looking carefully through the assembly, not simply scanning.

The form V is doing real work: it tells us this was not a moment of luck. It was a posture of attention that Sulayman had cultivated.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is stunning once you hear it: the man who practiced *tafaqqad* commanded an army that included jinn — beings of fire whose nature we cannot fully comprehend. He commanded human armies spanning civilizations. He commanded birds whose species number in the tens of thousands. By any human calculation, the loss of one small bird in that multitude should be undetectable. You cannot account for each individual when the assembly is that vast.

And yet.

The Quran records this as the *first* thing Sulayman did when his armies were before him. Not strategy. Not command. Not inspection of supplies or military formation.

He checked that everyone was there.

The priorities of a prophet-king.

### The Self-Directed Question

When Sulayman completed his inspection and found the empty space — when the place where the hoopoe should have been held nothing — he spoke.

And what he said deserves very careful attention.

**مَا لِيَ لَا أَرَى الْهُدْهُدَ**

*Mā liya lā arā al-hudhuda* — "What is the matter with me that I do not see the hoopoe?"

Read the grammar of that question slowly. The phrase مَا لِيَ — *mā liya* — is a specific construction in Arabic. It means: "what is to me?" It is a question about the speaker's own state. It is pointing *inward*.

Sulayman did not say: **أَيْنَ الْهُدْهُدُ** — "Where is the hoopoe?" — which would direct the question at the bird.

He did not say: **الْهُدْهُدُ غَائِبٌ** — "The hoopoe is absent" — which would be a declaration about external reality.

He did not say: **مَنْ رَأَى الْهُدْهُدَ؟** — "Who has seen the hoopoe?" — which would direct the inquiry outward to others.

He said: **مَا لِيَ لَا أَرَى** — "What is it about *me* — about my own state, my own perception — that I am not seeing?"

The question is pointed inward first. It puts the uncertainty where it belongs: with the observer. *Perhaps my vision is limited. Perhaps I have not looked in the right place. Perhaps there is something about this situation I have not yet understood.*

Before he accuses the bird of absence, he interrogates his own failure to see.

[PAUSE]

This is a profound model of epistemic humility. And it runs counter to almost everything our instincts tell us to do.

When we find someone missing — from our life, from our gathering, from their usual place — our first move is almost never to question our own perception. Our first move is to make a claim about the other person. *They left. They're absent. They didn't show up.* And then, almost immediately: *they're being irresponsible*, or *they're avoiding us*, or *they've stopped caring*.

The narration starts instantly. We fill the empty space with a story.

Sulayman's question does something different. It pauses at the threshold of narration and says: *wait. Why am I not seeing? What don't I know yet?*

This is not weakness. This is not avoidance. Sulayman will, in the very next verse, speak real consequences — he will name what punishment awaits the hoopoe if there is no valid reason. He is not a man who lets absence pass unremarked. But the movement of his thought is disciplined. It goes inward *before* it goes outward. It questions self *before* it questions other.

That sequence matters.

[PAUSE]

And then notice how the question continues. After turning inward, he pivots to a genuine alternative:

**أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ الْغَائِبِينَ**

*Am kāna min al-ghā'ibīn* — "Or was he among the absent?"

The word **أَمْ** in Arabic is a specific conjunction — it introduces a real disjunctive alternative within a genuine question, not a rhetorical one. Sulayman is holding two real possibilities simultaneously:

One: *there is something about my perception or my situation that I haven't understood yet.*
Two: *the bird is genuinely not here.*

Both are live. He has not collapsed the uncertainty prematurely. He has not decided which is true before he has information. And then — when he names the second possibility — look at the language he reaches for.

Not: *am kāna min al-fārirīn* — "or was he among those who fled?"
Not: *am kāna min al-ʿāṣīn* — "or was he among the disobedient?"
Not: *am kāna min al-muqaṣṣirīn* — "or was he among the negligent?"

He uses the most open, most morally neutral word available: **الْغَائِبِينَ** — *al-ghā'ibīn* — the absent ones. Those who simply are not present. No condemnation built into the label. No character judgment attached.

He leaves the door open. We will need to understand why — and what walked through that open door — in Part 2.

### The Weight of الْغَائِبِينَ

There is one more word in this ayah that deserves its own space, because it carries more than its surface meaning.

**الْغَائِبِينَ** — *al-ghā'ibīn* — "the absent ones."

The root is **غ-ي-ب** — *ghāba*. And in the Quran, this root is not ordinary.

The same root gives us **الْغَيْب** — *al-ghayb* — the Unseen. The realm that lies beyond human perception. The hidden reality that only Allah encompasses fully. The Quran places belief in al-ghayb as one of the first qualities of the people of taqwā: *alladhīna yu'minūna bil-ghayb* — "those who believe in the Unseen" (2:3). It is the most fundamental boundary condition of human knowledge. What we cannot see, we cannot narrate with certainty. What lies in the ghayb belongs to Allah's knowledge alone.

When Sulayman asks whether the hoopoe was "among the ghā'ibīn" — the absent ones — the word he uses is morphologically direct: the active participle of the same root. The *ghā'ib* is the one who has entered a state of *ghayb* relative to the observer. The one who has passed behind the veil of your perception.

They are still real. They are still somewhere. They may, in fact, be carrying something significant. But from your vantage point, they have entered the unseen.

[PAUSE]

And here is the revelation that the rest of the surah delivers with extraordinary precision.

The hoopoe returns in verse 27:22 and says to Sulayman:

**أَحَطتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ**
"I have encompassed knowledge of what you have not encompassed."

Think about the profound irony of that statement. The one who was "among the absent ones" from Sulayman's perspective — the one who had entered the ghayb of his social world — turns out to be the one who had *more* vision, not less. The hoopoe had gone somewhere Sulayman had not gone. It had seen something Sulayman had not seen. It had encompassed — *aḥāṭa*, a word carrying the sense of surrounding something completely, leaving nothing outside — what the prophet-king himself had not encompassed.

The ghā'ib was not empty. The ghā'ib was full.

The one who appeared to have dropped out of the assembly was in fact operating in a realm of intelligence that the assembly had not accessed.

Let the sonic texture of the word sit for a moment. **الْغَائِبِينَ** — begin with the *ghayn*, one of the heaviest phonemes in Arabic, produced deep in the throat, a sound with density and opacity, a sound that in classical Arabic prosody carries the sense of something thick, something passing behind a curtain. Then the long vowel opening into the emphatic *bā'* and closing with the nasal *n* of plurality. The sound of the word is like something substantial receding just past the edge of sight.

Allah chose this root — not "the runaways," not "the delinquent ones" — for a reason. Because the *ghā'ib* is not simply gone. The *ghā'ib* is *somewhere*. In the ghayb. And the ghayb is not empty.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Now let us step back. We have followed three linguistic threads:

The deliberate practice of *tafaqqada* — the active, Form V verb of intentional inspection that accounts for each individual, proactively, before absence becomes a crisis.

The self-directed question *mā liya lā arā* — the inward turning before outward accusation, the epistemic humility that asks "what am I not seeing?" before "what did they do?"

And the open, neutral naming of *al-ghā'ibīn* — the absent ones, rooted in the word for the unseen, leaving space for the meaning of the absence to declare itself rather than being narrated from the outside.

Three features. One posture. A complete moral and spiritual orientation toward the people in your care when they are not where you expect them to be.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### The Practice of Deliberate Attention

Let me begin where you already live.

Think of the last time you genuinely felt *noticed*.

Not complimented on something you did. Not thanked for a contribution. But noticed — as a person, as a presence — in a way that communicated: *I am aware of you specifically. I know whether you are here. I would know if you were not.*

When was the last time that happened?

For many of us, it is rare enough that we remember exactly when it occurred. A teacher who sought you out after class, not because you'd done something wrong, but to say: *I noticed you seemed somewhere else today.* A friend who called not to talk but to say: *you've been quiet and I wanted to check.* A parent who came to you not with advice or agenda but with: *I looked for you and I noticed you weren't quite yourself.*

To be seen that specifically is remarkable. Because we live, paradoxically, in the most "connected" era in human history — and also in an era of historic levels of loneliness. The statistics are not subtle: loneliness rates in the modern world have risen sharply even as digital connection has expanded. People report feeling invisible even inside their own communities. They sit in group chats and feel unread. They show up at gatherings and feel uncounted. They are surrounded and still feel that no one would notice if they quietly disappeared.

This is not a peripheral complaint. This is one of the deepest wounds of contemporary life: to be present but uncounted. To be *there* but not *seen*.

And into this wound, Allah places — in a single verb — a prophetic antidote.

[PAUSE]

Sulayman commanded armies from three realms of creation. The numbers are beyond human comprehension. By any human logic, the loss of one small bird should be undetectable. You cannot actively account for each individual in a gathering that vast.

And yet the Quran records *tafaqqada* — the deliberate, proactive inspection — as the *first* act of Sulayman when his assembly was before him.

Not strategy. Not command. Not a review of military readiness. He inspected for the presence of each member. He went through them with care. And because he went through them with care, he found — in a multitude of thousands — the one empty space.

What *tafaqqad* requires is this: you must know what *should* be there before you can notice what isn't. You cannot notice specific absence without having previously established specific presence. To notice that the hoopoe was missing, Sulayman had to know the hoopoe. Its place. Its face in the assembly. Its characteristic position. Which means that at some prior moment — in some prior practice of attention — he had *learned* each one.

That prior learning is what *tafaqqada* is built on.

[PAUSE]

Think of the people in your care right now. Your children. Your parents. Your friends. The students you teach, the colleagues you work alongside, the members of your community. Those whom Allah has placed in proximity to you.

Do you practice *tafaqqad* with them? Do you deliberately, proactively check — not just when something has gone wrong, not just when an alarm has been raised — but regularly, intentionally, as a discipline — whether each person is present? Whether they are showing up the way they characteristically show up? Whether something has changed?

Or do you only notice absence when it becomes loud enough to be disruptive? When the missing person's departure creates a problem that draws your attention?

There is a genuine difference between these two modes of relation. One is reactive management: you address problems when they surface. The other is prophetic attention: you cultivate the awareness that lets you notice a problem *before* it surfaces — or notice a person's withdrawal before it becomes a rupture.

*Tafaqqada* calls us toward the second.

[PAUSE]

The practical weight of this is not abstract. A parent who practices *tafaqqad* checks in with each child — not only the one who is struggling or making noise, but each one, individually, with specific attention to how they are *this week*, not just in general. A teacher who practices *tafaqqad* notices which student hasn't spoken in two weeks and asks privately — not to embarrass but to communicate: *I see your place here. I notice when it's quiet.* A friend who practices *tafaqqad* reaches out to the one who has been silent in the group chat — not demanding explanation, but communicating: *I looked for you. I found you less present. I wanted to check.*

The practice is the same in each case: deliberate, proactive attention to individual presence. Not waiting for someone to come to you with a problem. Going to them with the message: *I counted, and I noticed.*

That message — I counted, and I noticed — is one of the most profound gifts one human being can offer another.

And Allah places it as the first recorded act of a prophet-king reviewing his assembly.

[PAUSE]

There is one more dimension to this worth sitting with. Why did Sulayman notice one small hoopoe?

Because a hoopoe is not impressive. In the hierarchy of Sulayman's assembly, the hoopoe is arguably near the bottom. The jinn could move mountains. The human armies held political and military power. Among the birds, there were surely larger, stronger, more strategically important species. The hoopoe is a small, colorful bird — distinctive, yes, but not a creature of power.

And yet: it is the hoopoe's absence that stops Sulayman. Not the absence of a jinn general. Not the absence of a human commander. The small bird.

Which tells you something about the scope of *tafaqqad* as a spiritual practice. It does not only account for the impressive, the powerful, the indispensable. It accounts for the small. The one who does not push forward. The one who would be easy to overlook.

Because in Allah's economy, the hoopoe turned out to be carrying the most important intelligence of Sulayman's reign.

The smallest one. The one who would have been easy to miss.

### The Suspended Judgment — When the Absent One Is on a Mission

Now let us enter the second reality this ayah opens.

When Sulayman found the hoopoe missing, he did not simply observe and wait. The next verse (27:21) tells us he spoke real consequences:

*"I will surely punish him with a severe punishment or slaughter him unless he brings me a clear reason."*

Do not soften this. This is a real threat. There are real stakes. Sulayman is not a man who lets absence pass without accountability.

But here is what precedes the threat — and this is everything.

Before stating what he will do, he said: **أَمْ كَانَ مِنَ الْغَائِبِينَ** — "Or was he among the absent?" He built into his very first response the possibility that there was a reason. He named the absence in the most morally neutral terms available — not "the runaway," not "the rebel," but the *ghā'ib*, the absent one. And he explicitly entertained that the absence might be legitimate.

The threat is real. But the space for explanation comes first.

This sequence — *notice the absence, hold the question open, then state consequences* — is a model of justice that moves in a specific order. It does not collapse the investigation before the hearing. It does not sentence before the accused has spoken.

[PAUSE]

Let's step through a human door into this.

Think of someone in your life who has gone quiet. Perhaps a friend who stopped responding the way they used to. A family member who has retreated. A colleague who has become harder to reach. Someone who, for whatever reason, has gone from fully present to partially or entirely absent.

What is your first response to that kind of withdrawal?

For most of us, it is a story. We narrate the absence almost instantly. Sometimes the story is about them: *they've grown distant, they've changed, they're being difficult, they don't value the relationship anymore.* Sometimes the story is about us: *I must have done something wrong, they've lost interest in me, I'm not worth the effort.* Either way, the empty space gets filled — not with inquiry, but with interpretation.

And once the interpretation is in place, we act on it. We stop reaching out. We brace ourselves. We protect against a rejection we've already decided has happened. Or we confront before we understand. Or we write the person off.

The narration shapes the response. And the narration was built on an absence — on something we *didn't* see, on information we *didn't* have.

[PAUSE]

Sulayman's grammar refuses this move. His *mā liya lā arā* — "what is it about my seeing that I am failing" — is the pause before narration. It is the acknowledgment that absence is not yet information; it is still a question. And his *am kāna min al-ghā'ibīn* — "or was he among the absent?" — is the decision to hold the category open rather than fill it.

In the Arabic legal tradition, this posture has a name: *ḥusn al-ẓann* — assuming good. It is a disciplined practice, not a naive one. It does not pretend that absence has no meaning. It does not ignore the situation. It resists the premature narration. It says: *I do not yet know enough to judge. I will hold the door open until I do.*

And crucially — this is not passivity. Sulayman stated real consequences. He made clear that if there was no valid reason, there would be real accountability. He did not dissolve the stakes. He simply refused to foreclose the possibility of a legitimate explanation before he had heard one.

That combination — real accountability *and* real space for explanation — is the mark of justice.

[PAUSE]

And then the hoopoe returns.

In verse 27:22, it arrives and says:

**أَحَطتُ بِمَا لَمْ تُحِطْ بِهِ وَجِئْتُكَ مِن سَبَإٍ بِنَبَإٍ يَقِينٍ**

"I have encompassed knowledge of what you have not encompassed, and I have come to you from Sheba with certain news."

The bird that appeared absent, unauthorized, delinquent — was carrying intelligence that Sulayman's entire vast court had not accessed. It had flown to the kingdom of Sheba. It had witnessed a civilization worshipping the sun instead of Allah. It had seen a queen on a magnificent throne. It had perceived what no one in Sulayman's assembly — no jinn, no human, no other bird — had perceived.

The *ghā'ib* was not empty. The *ghā'ib* was carrying something.

The absent one was, in fact, on a mission that was about to change the course of an entire civilization.

[PAUSE]

Now trace the chain that followed.

Because the hoopoe brought its intelligence, Sulayman sent a letter to the Queen of Sheba. Because of that letter, she came to visit. Because of that visit, she witnessed the reality of Sulayman's prophethood. And at the end of that encounter, she declared:

**رَبِّ إِنِّي ظَلَمْتُ نَفْسِي وَأَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ سُلَيْمَانَ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ**
"My Lord, I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulayman to Allah, Lord of the worlds." (27:44)

The ruler of a great civilization came to Islam. One of the most profound conversions in the entire Quran. An entire kingdom turned toward its Lord.

Trace the chain: it began with one small bird being absent from assembly. It continued because a prophet-king noticed the absence. It developed because he held the space open — "or was he among the absent?" — rather than condemning before he understood. And it completed because the hoopoe was able to return, speak, and deliver what it carried.

If Sulayman had filled the absence with a judgment the moment he found it — if he had sentenced the hoopoe as rebellious before the bird could speak — that chain would have been severed before it began.

The suspended judgment made space for a sacred mission to complete itself.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this leaves with us, and I want it to be a genuine question, not a rhetorical one:

How many times has someone in your life been absent — truly absent, withdrawn, unreachable — and you filled that absence with a story? How many times did the story you told about their absence become the action you took: distancing yourself, confronting prematurely, closing the door? And how often did you discover, later, that they had been *somewhere* — carrying something — that you didn't know about yet?

The hoopoe's absence was not delinquency. It was a mission in the ghayb.

The absent ones in your life may also be carrying something you have not yet been given the chance to hear.

The question is: will the door still be open when they return?

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Echo

Before we close, I want to show you something about where this ayah lives within the surah.

Surah An-Naml is named *The Ants* — a creature that barely registers in human awareness. And yet Allah named an entire surah after this insect. Why? Because in verses 27:18–19, the ants demonstrated something remarkable: collective self-awareness. One ant, recognizing the approach of Sulayman's army, warned the community — communicated, protected, and then expressed the very posture of prophetic knowledge by acknowledging the limits of her own perception while asking for mercy.

The ants were aware of each other and of Sulayman's approach.

Then Sulayman was aware of each member of his assembly — and of the one who was missing.

And the hoopoe was aware of an entire civilization that Sulayman's court had not yet seen.

There is a cascade of perception running through this passage. Each entity is awake to something the previous one was not tracking. And the surah seems to be asking — in this sustained meditation on sight — who really sees? Who notices what others miss? What sacred reality is concealed inside what looks like absence or insignificance?

The ants. The hoopoe. The one small bird in an army of thousands.

Allah is building a world in which attention is redemptive. Where noticing — really noticing — ripples outward with consequences that no single observer can fully track.

### Where the Threads Converge

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are, in the end, the same insight approached from two directions.

The Form V verb *tafaqqada* does not just describe what Sulayman did with his army. It describes a posture of the heart — a decision, made habitually and in advance, that each person in your care will be accounted for, deliberately, before their absence becomes visible through disruption. It is the opposite of the reactive posture that only notices when something breaks. It is the proactive posture that goes looking: *is everyone here? And if not, who is missing?*

The self-directed question *mā liya lā arā* does not just describe Sulayman's epistemology in one moment. It describes a discipline of humility that turns inward before it turns outward — that asks "what am I not yet seeing?" before asking "what did they do?" It is the posture that keeps inquiry honest and keeps the door between observer and observed from slamming shut before both parties have spoken.

And the word *al-ghā'ibīn* — the absent ones, rooted in *al-ghayb* — does not merely categorize the hoopoe in a moment. It names something universal: that those who have passed from our perception are not simply *gone*. They have entered the ghayb of our awareness. And the fundamental posture of a believer toward the ghayb is: do not fill what you cannot see with conjecture. Hold the space. Trust that what is concealed has its own reality, its own reason, its own weight — which will declare itself if you leave the door open long enough.

Three words. One complete orientation toward others.

### Questions to Carry

Let these sit genuinely, not rhetorically, in the days ahead:

1. Is there someone in your life whose absence you have already narrated — filled with a story about who they are or what they think of you — before you have heard from them what they were carrying? What would it take to hold that space open?

2. Do you have a practice of *tafaqqad* — deliberate, proactive attention to the people in your care? Or do you only notice when absence becomes disruptive? What would it look like to change that?

3. Have you yourself been the absent hoopoe at some point — withdrawn, unreachable, seemingly delinquent — when in fact you were somewhere, carrying something, on a journey no one around you knew about? Was the door still open when you came back?

4. What does it mean to you that the Quran preserves this moment — a prophet-king with tens of thousands assembled, stopping because one small bird was not in its place? What does it say about whose absence matters to Allah?

5. Whose *tafaqqad* has shaped your life — who noticed when you were absent, held the space, and waited? What did that do for you?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**True authority over others begins where prophetic attention begins: in the discipline of deliberately accounting for each one — and in the wisdom to hold open, when one is missing, the possibility that what looks like absence is carrying something the assembly has not yet seen.**

### The Closing Invitation

After this, one practice might shift. Not a sweeping resolution — just one thing this week.

Practice *tafaqqad* with the people closest to you. Not when something has gone wrong. Not when someone has made enough noise to force your attention. Proactively — look for each one. Notice who is there, and notice who is not. And when you find an absence, resist the first story your mind reaches for. Turn inward first: *mā liya lā arā* — what might I not be seeing yet? And then hold the category open: *they are among the absent ones*. Not the rebels. Not the neglectful. The absent. Somewhere. Carrying something.

This is not naïve. Sulayman was not naïve — he named real consequences. But the space came first.

And in that space, a small bird returned with something that changed the world.

### Duʿāʾ

*Yā Allah — You are al-Khabīr, the All-Aware, the One who notices every absence, who counts each one before we notice we are missing. Teach us the practice of deliberate attention — to care for the people You have placed near us with the carefulness of a shepherd, with the precision of a prophet. Give us the courage to look for each one, and the humility to ask first what we are not yet seeing before we accuse.*

*And when we find someone absent — shield us from the rush to fill their absence with a story we invented. Let us leave the door open. Let us trust that what is hidden from us has its own reality, its own weight, its own sacred reason that has not yet found its way back to us.*

*And if we ourselves are the absent ones — withdrawn, quiet, on a journey no one in our assembly knows about — receive us when we return. Like the hoopoe, let us come back carrying something. Let those who wait for us leave the door open. And let what we carry be worthy of the space they held.*

*Āmīn.*
