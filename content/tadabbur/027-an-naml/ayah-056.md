# The Mockery That Names the Mocker

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 56

> ۞ فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ أَن قَالُوٓا۟ أَخْرِجُوٓا۟ ءَالَ لُوطٍ مِّن قَرْيَتِكُمْ ۖ إِنَّهُمْ أُنَاسٌ يَتَطَهَّرُونَ
>
> *Famā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā an qālū akhrijū āla Lūṭin min qaryatikum, innahum unāsun yataṭahharūn.*
>
> "But the only answer of his people was that they said, 'Expel the family of Lot from your town. Indeed, they are people who keep themselves pure.'"

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Verse

Before we open a single word, I want you to do something with me.

I want you to imagine a town. Not an ancient town. A town you know. A town with streets you've walked, neighbors you've nodded to, shopkeepers whose names you remember. Imagine that this town has, over years — slowly, then quickly — decided on a way of being. A way that injures the soul. A way that, if you described it to your grandmother, she would weep.

Now imagine that one person in that town — one family — has refused to follow.

They haven't preached at anyone. They haven't held protests. They've just declined. They've kept their own house clean. They've raised their own children differently. They've stayed.

Now ask yourself: **what would the town say about that family?**

Not what would the town *do*. What would they *say*?

Because long before any town raises its hand against a righteous family, it raises its tongue. And the tongue gives away what the hand is about to do.

This ayah is the tongue of Lot's people. It is the moment, captured exactly, where a society's moral compass has not simply broken — it has reversed. North is now south. Clean is now dirty. The pure is now the contaminant that must be removed.

[PAUSE]

The Surah we are sitting inside — An-Naml, "The Ant" — is a strange and luminous surah. It moves us through prophets. Musa at the fire. Dawud and Sulayman with the language of birds. Salih confronting the schemers of Thamud. And then, in a passage of only a few ayat, Lot. Each story is asking the same question from a different angle: **what happens when revelation enters a closed world?** What happens when a settled human society is forced, by the arrival of a prophet, to look at itself?

The Lot passage answers that question with a single sentence — the sentence the people themselves speak. They give us the answer. They don't realize they are giving it. That is what makes the verse devastating.

Here is what I want you to feel before we begin:

The people of Lot, in this moment, are not yet at the moment of destruction. They have not yet seen the stones rain down. They are at the dinner-table moment. The water-cooler moment. The "what did you hear about that family" moment. And out of their mouths, calmly, conversationally, falls a sentence that already contains their judgment — the judgment Allah will execute on them — fully written.

They sentence themselves. With one word. And we are going to find that word.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Architecture of Refusal — *Famā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā*

Let me teach you a structure in Arabic before we open the verse.

In Arabic, there is a construction called *qaṣr* — restriction, or limitation. It's the way the language closes a door. The most common form uses two words: *mā* (a negation — "not") paired with *illā* (an exception — "except"). Together they form what is sometimes called a *negative-exception clause*. It says: there is nothing of X except Y.

Now, you might think — couldn't Allah just say "Y"? Couldn't He just say "they said: expel them"? Why first say "their only answer was nothing but…"?

Let me give you an everyday example. Imagine you ask a teenager, "Did you do anything productive today?" They could answer two ways.

They could say: "I watched TV."

Or they could say: "All I did was watch TV."

Hear the difference. The second sentence is doing two things at once. It is telling you what they did. And it is highlighting what they did *not* do. It is saying — there was a whole field of possible responses, a whole horizon of things I could have done, and out of all of them, this one tiny shabby thing is what I chose. The structure mourns the absence of everything else.

Now look at the ayah:

> فَمَا كَانَ جَوَابَ قَوْمِهِۦٓ إِلَّآ أَن قَالُوٓا۟

*"And their people's answer was nothing except that they said…"*

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah is doing. He is not just reporting their reply. He is telling you what they *could have* replied. Lot had just spoken to them — the previous ayah tells us he questioned their conduct, asked them whether they would commit indecency knowingly. A whole world of possible answers was open. They could have wept. They could have argued. They could have asked for time. They could have explained themselves. They could have asked Lot what they should do instead. They could have laughed and walked away. They could have ignored him.

But Allah uses *mā… illā* — and that construction sweeps the entire horizon of possibilities into one bin and then points at a single response in another bin. **The bin of everything-they-could-have-said is empty. The bin of what-they-did-say has one item.**

And here is what's remarkable: the one item is not even a defense. It is not even a denial. It is an attack on the messenger. Stay with that. A man warns you that what you are doing will destroy you, and your only response — out of all possible responses — is to demand his deportation.

The *mā… illā* construction is the linguistic shape of moral exhaustion. It is what Allah uses to show us a community that has used up every other possibility. There is no argument left. No reasoning left. No self-examination left. There is only — *get him out.*

And the word *jawāb* — answer, reply — comes from the root *j-w-b*, which classical Arabic lexicons connect to the idea of cutting through, of crossing over, of penetrating. A *jawāb* is something that crosses the distance from one mind to another. It is supposed to be communication. It is supposed to *answer* the question that was asked.

Their *jawāb* does not answer Lot's question. It changes the subject from their behavior to his existence.

This is a tell. In any conversation, when one side has run out of substantive replies, the conversation shifts from "what we are doing" to "who you are." From content to character. From the act to the actor.

The *mā kāna jawāba qawmihi illā* construction is Allah's way of saying: **listen carefully. They had a chance to actually answer. They did not take it. They took the other path — the path that ends civilizations.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Verb of Banishment — *Akhrijū*

Now let us look at the word they chose. *Akhrijū.*

The root is *kh-r-j*. The basic meaning is to come out, to exit, to emerge. The simple verb *kharaja* means "he went out." But this is not the simple form. This is Form IV — *akhraja* — which in Arabic is a causative form. It does not mean *to exit*. It means *to make someone exit.* To eject. To expel. To force the going-out.

And here it is imperative and plural: *akhrijū* — "all of you, force them out."

Think for a moment about what verb you would expect a town to use against a family it disliked.

You might expect *uqtulū* — "kill them." You might expect *udribū* — "strike them." You might expect *aḥriqū* — "burn them."

They didn't say any of those. They said *akhrijū*. **Expel them.**

This is not mercy. Don't be fooled. This is something far more telling. Listen to what *akhrijū* assumes.

*Akhrijū* assumes that the land belongs to the people who are corrupt, and the corrupt have the moral standing to determine who is allowed to live among them. *Akhrijū* assumes that the standard for belonging in this town is *being like the town.* It assumes that the town, in its current state of moral collapse, is the *norm,* and that Lot's family — by being pure — is the *deviation* that needs to be removed for the system to function smoothly.

Do you see what has happened?

In a society of disease, the body that refuses to be sick is the foreign body. The healthy cell is the cancer. The system has inverted so completely that *health is what must be expelled* in order for the disease to continue uninterrupted.

[PAUSE]

And there's something else. *Akhrijū* — "expel" — uses the same root that the Quran uses elsewhere, devastatingly, for what is about to happen to *them.* In multiple places, when Allah describes punishment, He uses the language of being driven out, brought out, extracted. The very verb they used to demand Lot's removal will be the verb used about their own removal from existence.

They named the punishment before it came. They wrote it. They put the word in their own mouths. **They asked for exile, and exile is what they received — only it was permanent, and it was them.**

Here is what I want you to notice: a word in the Quran is rarely just a word. It is often a hinge. The same hinge that the people use to push Lot out is the hinge that Allah will use to push them out of the land of the living. Their tongue rehearsed their own end.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Word That Indicts Them — *Yataṭahharūn*

This is the word. If you remember nothing else from this reflection — remember this word.

*Yataṭahharūn.*

The root is *ṭ-h-r* — pure, clean, undefiled. It is the root from which we get *ṭahāra,* the foundational Islamic concept of ritual and moral purity. It is one of the most beautiful roots in the Arabic language. The very word a believer says before prayer, the state we seek before approaching Allah — *ṭahāra.*

Now look at the form. *Yataṭahharūn* is Form V — *tafa''ala* — and Form V in Arabic does something specific. It often carries the meaning of *taking on the action upon oneself, deliberately, with effort.* Not just being pure — but *making oneself pure.* Not just clean — but *engaged in the practice of cleaning.* Cultivating purity. Pursuing it. Working at it.

Let me teach you Form V with an everyday example. The simple verb *'allama* means "he taught." Form V of the same root gives you *ta'allama* — "he taught himself, he learned, he undertook learning." The Form V transforms the action into a sustained, self-directed effort. It is the form of *practice.*

So *yataṭahharūn* is not "they are clean." It is **"they are people who are constantly engaged in the practice of purifying themselves."**

This is — listen to this carefully — **one of the most beautiful descriptions of a believer in the entire Quran.** It is, in essence, what the Quran says about itself elsewhere: that the believers are those who love to purify themselves. It is what Allah praises about the people of Quba in another surah — *rijālun yuḥibbūna an yataṭahharū* — "men who love to keep themselves pure."

**The exact same word.** The exact same form. The exact same beautiful spiritual reality.

[PAUSE]

Now hold both things in your mind at once.

In one place in the Quran, Allah uses *yataṭahharūn* to praise believers — He honors them with it, He elevates them with it, He says He loves those who do this.

In our ayah, the people of Lot use the exact same word — and they use it as **an insult.**

Stay with me here. This is the heart of the whole verse. This is the moment Allah is pointing us toward.

What does it mean when a society uses, as a slur, the very word Allah uses as praise?

It means the inversion is complete. It means the language itself has flipped. It means that what is good has become the marker of what is to be expelled. They are not just doing wrong — they have rewritten the dictionary so that the word for "righteous" now serves as the word for "outcast."

And listen — they don't even need to call Lot's family *bad.* They don't accuse them of anything. They don't say "they are thieves" or "they are dangerous" or "they have done wrong." Read the verse again. **The entire reason they give for expelling Lot's family is that the family is engaged in the practice of being pure.**

That's it. That's the charge.

*"They are people who keep themselves pure"* — said with the same tone of voice a junior-high bully uses to say "they think they're better than us." Said with the same curl of the lip that a society uses against anyone who refuses its agreed-upon sin. Said the way every righteous person who has ever lived among a corrupted society has heard it said about them — by family, by colleagues, by neighbors, by online crowds.

[PAUSE]

Here is the linguistic miracle. **The sarcasm is the proof.**

Allah does not need to tell us that the people of Lot were wicked. He does not need to insert an editorial. He does not need to say "and they were corrupt." He simply lets them speak — and they, with their own tongues, deliver the verdict against themselves. **They tell us they are guilty by the very fact that they have made *purity* into a slur.**

A society that mocks the pure has named itself. There is no defense possible after that sentence is spoken. The language has betrayed the speaker.

[PAUSE]

And notice one more thing — a small, devastating absence. They could have said *yataṭahharūn min'a* — "they keep themselves pure from us" — making it a comparison, an arrogance claim, an accusation that Lot's family looks down on them. They could have said *yataṭahharūn ka-annahum* — "they keep pure as if they were…" — adding hypocrisy to the charge.

They didn't. They left the word naked. *Yataṭahharūn.* Period.

**They could not even bring themselves to attach a lie to it.** The accusation is just — *they're pure.* That's the crime. There is no rhetorical scaffolding. There is no claim of hypocrisy. There is no accusation of looking down on us. There is just the bare word — purity — offered as if it were self-evidently a reason to deport a family.

This is what a corrupt society looks like at its peak. Not when it argues against the good. **When it doesn't even need to argue. When the good itself is the indictment.**

### Bridging to the Themes

What has the language shown us? Three things, layered into each other:

The *mā… illā* construction has shown us a society with one possible response — moral exhaustion masquerading as a verdict.

The verb *akhrijū* has shown us a society that has inverted the question of who belongs — where the practitioners of corruption claim the moral right to deport the practitioners of purity.

And the word *yataṭahharūn* has shown us the final stage — when the language of praise has become the language of accusation. When *good* has been redefined as *unwelcome.*

Now let us ask: what does this reveal not just about words, but about reality itself? About what happens to a soul, a family, a community, a civilization, when this inversion takes hold?

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: When the Crowd Names the Pure as the Problem

Let me start somewhere you live.

You know that moment in school — maybe you experienced it, maybe you watched someone else experience it — when a kid in the class wouldn't go along with what everyone else was doing. Wouldn't cheat. Wouldn't laugh at the joke that was at someone's expense. Wouldn't drink at the party. Wouldn't gossip. Wouldn't.

And remember what happened next. Not always immediately. But eventually.

The kid became the problem.

Not the cheating. Not the cruelty. Not the gossip. **The kid who wouldn't participate.** The kid became *strange.* The kid became *uppity.* The kid became "too good for us." The kid became *the one we needed to push out.*

Hold that memory. Now expand it. Because what happened to that kid in your school is the same dynamic that happened to Lot's family in their town, the same dynamic that happens to the principled employee in a corrupt workplace, the same dynamic that happens to the believing teenager in a friend group, the same dynamic that happens to the practicing wife in a family that drinks, the same dynamic that happens to entire communities throughout history.

**The pure threaten the corrupt by their very existence — not by their words.**

Lot's family did not, in this ayah, attack the people. They didn't preach. They didn't picket. The verse before tells us Lot questioned them, yes — but his family is not described as activists. The accusation is not "they were preaching against us." The accusation is just that *they kept themselves pure.* That alone was unbearable.

Why?

Because the existence of a pure person — visible, present, breathing the same air, walking the same streets — is itself a refutation of the claim that purity is impossible. The corrupt soul tells itself a story: *everyone is like this. This is just how people are. There is no other way to live.* And then it sees the family across the street who lives differently — and the story breaks. The pure family is not even trying to argue. **The argument is their breathing.**

So the corrupt response is not to argue back. It is to *remove the counterexample.* Make them go away. Push them out. Cancel them. Shun them. Expel them. Because once the counterexample is gone, the story can resume: *everyone is like this. This is just how people are.*

This is the deep psychology of the verse. The corrupt do not hate the pure because the pure are arrogant. They hate the pure because **the pure are evidence that they themselves had a choice.** And nothing is more unbearable to a person who has chosen wrongly than the proof that they could have chosen otherwise.

[PAUSE]

Now let me tell you what this means for your life.

If you have ever been mocked for refusing to participate in something wrong — at work, in a family, in a friendship, in a community — and the mockery took the form of someone calling you "too righteous," "too pure," "thinks they're better than us," "preachy," "extreme," "old-fashioned"… **the Quran has already named what is happening.** The Quran has, fourteen hundred years ago, written into eternal scripture the exact dynamic you experienced.

And here is what the verse is asking you to consider: notice that the people of Lot were not, in this moment, accusing Lot's family of any specific wrong. They were not arguing with their reasoning. They were simply expressing that the family's *purity* was the problem. **If your accusers are doing the same — naming your goodness itself as the offense — they have, without realizing it, named themselves.** They have given you the verdict.

This is not, by the way, license for arrogance. It is the opposite. It is a release from the need to defend yourself. When someone attacks your goodness *as goodness,* there is no defense to mount. The attack has already convicted the attacker. Your job is simply to remain pure, and to remain kind, and to remain present, and to let the language do its work.

**Practical application:** Audit, this week, one moment where you have been pressured to compromise — and a voice (internal or external) has framed your resistance as superiority or strangeness. Reframe it. The pressure to participate is not evidence that purity is arrogance. It is the inversion the verse is naming.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Sarcasm That Becomes Sacred — When Mockery Backfires Across Eternity

This is, perhaps, the most stunning dimension of this verse, and I want to take real care with it.

The people of Lot intended *yataṭahharūn* as an insult. They said it with disgust. They said it with the curled lip of social derision. They thought they were delivering the killing blow — *"these are people who keep themselves pure"* — said the way you might say *"these are people who think they're better than us."*

And what did Allah do with their insult?

He **preserved it. Forever.** As the highest praise.

Stop and feel that for a moment.

The mocking sentence that the people of Lot spoke around their dinner tables — the gossip, the sneer, the dismissive shrug — has now been recited in every prayer, in every mosque, in every village, in every generation, in every language, for over fourteen hundred years. The mockery has become liturgy. The slur has become Scripture. **The insult has been canonized into the speech of Allah Himself.**

And here is what this reveals about the universe we live in. We tend to think of mockery as something that diminishes its target. The mocker speaks, and the mocked is reduced. But Allah is doing something here that scrambles that intuition entirely.

**He is telling us: mockery against the pure does not diminish them. It immortalizes them.**

Lot's family is forever, in the speech of Allah, *unāsun yataṭahharūn* — people who keep themselves pure. That is their name in the eternal record. They did not give themselves this name. Lot did not write a press release. The label was forged in the mouths of their enemies, who meant it as venom — and Allah accepted the description, sealed it into the Quran, and made it the definition of who they were.

And meanwhile — what is the name of the people who hurled the insult? They have no name. The Quran does not bother to record their personal names. They are *qawm,* "the people," a faceless crowd. Their existence as individuals has been erased from the universe. **The mockers vanished into anonymity. The mocked were named for eternity.**

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet, terrible justice in this. The people of Lot tried to deport Lot's family from their town. Allah deported their entire memory from existence. They tried to expel the family from the land. Allah expelled them from history. The very verb they used — *akhrijū,* expel — was turned upon them with infinite force.

And the family they tried to expel? **Their name is now in the mouth of every reciter of the Quran, every night, somewhere in the world.** Right now, as you read this, somewhere on earth a believer is reciting Surah An-Naml, and the words *unāsun yataṭahharūn* are crossing their lips. The family of Lot is praised, perpetually, in the speech of the One who created the universe — and the very phrase used to praise them was lifted from the mouths of those who wanted them gone.

[PAUSE]

What does this mean for us?

It means that when someone mocks your faith, your prayer, your modesty, your charity, your refusal to compromise — they are unknowingly working on your behalf. They are choosing the words by which you will be praised. They are giving you, in their venom, the very labels Allah may one day affirm.

A teenager whose friends mock him for praying is being marked as *one who prays.*

A woman whose colleagues mock her for her hijab is being marked as *one who veils.*

A man whose family mocks him for refusing the haram contract is being marked as *one who keeps himself pure.*

The mockers think they are insulting. They are, in fact, **enrolling their target in a register that they themselves cannot see.** They are writing, in their sneer, the deed of nobility to the very person they are trying to humiliate.

This is, I think, one of the most liberating realities the Quran offers. **It removes the fear of being called names by the corrupt.** Because the corrupt are not, in their mockery, attacking you. They are, without knowing it, decorating you with the very titles Allah loves.

[PAUSE]

**Practical application:** The next time you are mocked or insulted for an act of obedience to Allah, listen carefully to the exact word your accuser uses. Hold it. Do not flinch from it. That word — *prude, fundamentalist, holier-than-thou, fanatic, righteous* — might be, in Allah's register, your honorific. Let the insult settle into you as a sign of grace. Then return to your life with no defensive speech. The defense has already been written. It is in the Quran.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are, as they always are in the Quran, two views of one truth.

The *mā… illā* construction showed us a society whose entire moral horizon had narrowed to a single response. The theme of corrupt societies that cannot tolerate counterexamples filled that observation with meaning: this is what moral exhaustion looks like — when the only reply left to a prophet's call is *get him out.*

The verb *akhrijū* showed us the inversion of belonging — the assumption that the corrupt define the town and the pure are the foreigners. The theme of the pure as evidence of choice filled that observation with meaning: this is *why* the corrupt need to expel — because they cannot live with the counterexample standing.

And *yataṭahharūn* — the most precious of the three — showed us the moment when the language itself flipped, when the slur and the praise became the same word. The theme of mockery-becoming-canonization filled that observation with meaning: **the very sentence intended to destroy a family became the sentence by which Allah honored them forever.**

The architecture of the whole ayah is the architecture of a self-inflicted sentence. The people speak. The people choose their words. And every word convicts them, exiles them, and elevates those they tried to push down.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I, even subtly, using the language of praise as an insult — calling someone "too religious," "too strict," "too good," to dismiss something I do not want to confront?

2. Where am I currently being mocked for an act of obedience — and what is the exact word being used against me? Could that word, in Allah's register, be the name of a grace I am being given?

3. When I have power in a social setting — a family group chat, a workplace, a friend group — am I creating an environment where the pure must hide, or where the pure can breathe?

4. Have I been so afraid of being called names that I have already quietly expelled myself from the company of the pure — internally, before anyone needed to expel me externally?

5. When I run out of arguments in a moral conversation, do I shift to attacking the character of the one who is right, the way Lot's people did? What is the *jawāb* I tend to give — and what does it say about me?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> When a society begins to use the word for *pure* as an insult, it has already written its own indictment — and Allah, who hears every word, will preserve the slur forever as the praise it always secretly was.

### The Closing Invitation

Sit with this verse the next time you feel the pressure of being made strange by the corrupt. Not to harden yourself against them. Not to lecture them. Not to feel superior. **But to remember that the words they use against you may be the words Allah will use to describe you on the Day He gathers the people who kept themselves pure.**

And then turn back to your own life and ask: am I, in my own quiet way, *yataṭahharūn*? Am I *engaged in the practice* of purifying — not perfect, not finished, but *working,* in the deliberate Form V sense? Am I a verb of effort, not a noun of achievement?

That is the invitation. Not perfection. **Practice.** The slow, daily, deliberate work of cleaning the soul, knowing that the world may mock the cleaning, knowing that the mockery may sting, knowing that the One who hears it has already turned every word of it into honor.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who turned the insult of the corrupt into the praise of the pure — make us of those who keep themselves clean even when the cleaning is mocked, even when the purity is called arrogance, even when the world demands we be like it before it will let us belong. And when the words of the mockers fall upon us, let us hear them as You hear them, and let us not be moved.

Āmīn.
