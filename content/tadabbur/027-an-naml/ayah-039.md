# The ʿIfrīt's Boast — When Power Volunteers Itself

## INTRODUCTION

**The Ayah**

> قَالَ عِفْرِيتٌ مِّنَ ٱلْجِنِّ أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ قَبْلَ أَن تَقُومَ مِن مَّقَامِكَ ۖ وَإِنِّى عَلَيْهِ لَقَوِىٌّ أَمِينٌ

*Qāla ʿifrītun mina al-jinni anā ātīka bihi qabla an taqūma min maqāmika, wa innī ʿalayhi la-qawiyyun amīn.*

> An ʿifrīt from among the jinn said: "I will bring it to you before you rise from your place. Indeed, I am, for this, strong and trustworthy."

---

**Rebuilding the World**

Picture the scene. Sulaymān (ʿalayhi al-salām) is not just any king. He is a king Allah granted *mulkan lā yanbaghī li-aḥadin min baʿdī* — a kingdom unlike any after him. His court has humans, jinn, birds. The hoopoe has just returned from a reconnaissance flight with news of Bilqīs and her people in Saba'. Sulaymān has sent a letter. The letter has worked. Bilqīs is coming.

And now, in the moments before her arrival, Sulaymān turns to his court and asks: *who among you will bring me her throne before they arrive submitting?*

This is not an idle request. It is a test of the court itself. It is also a test of *what the court runs on.* Because in the next few seconds, two beings are going to volunteer. One will trade in the language of muscle. The other will trade in the language of knowledge.

And the speed difference between them — the speed difference between **brute strength** and **knowledge of the Book** — will be the entire point.

**The Hook**

Listen carefully to how the first volunteer speaks. He doesn't just say "I'll do it." He stamps his offer with two seals: *qawiyyun amīn* — strong and trustworthy. He recommends himself. He brags, but he brags in a structured, almost professional way. [PAUSE]

Why does the Quran preserve his exact phrasing? Why give him these specific two adjectives? And what happens to those two adjectives, those two seals, when we meet them again — in another surah, in another mouth, about another servant of Allah?

Stay with that question. We're going to come back to it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word *ʿifrīt* — when sound carries meaning

Before we open the Arabic, let me say something about how Arabic words work. In English, "thunder" sounds a little like thunder, "whisper" sounds a little like whisper. We call this onomatopoeia, and we treat it as a poetic flourish. In Arabic, the relationship between sound and meaning is far deeper. The Arabs noticed that words built on certain root structures — certain phonetic shapes — tend to carry certain kinds of meanings. Hard consonants for hard things. Doubled letters for intensified states. Words that *feel* like what they describe.

Now look at the word Allah uses for this volunteer.

> عِفْرِيتٌ — *ʿifrīt*

Say it out loud. Notice how it lands in your throat. The ʿayn — that deep guttural beginning. The fā — sharp, biting. The rā — rolling. The tā — clipped, final. This is not a smooth word. It is a word with edges. It scrapes coming out of the mouth.

In classical Arabic lexicons, the root *ʿ-f-r* is associated with the dust and dirt of the ground (*al-ʿafar*) — and from there, with strength, with rolling something in the dust, with overcoming. An *ʿifrīt* is not just a jinn. It is a jinn marked by intensity — by a kind of rough, dust-scattering power.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah did not say *qāla rajulun mina al-jinni* — "a man from among the jinn said." He didn't say *qāla aḥadun* — "one of them said." He chose a word whose very phonetic texture announces *this is a being made of force.* The sound of the word is doing work before the meaning even arrives.

And then He adds: *mina al-jinni* — *from among the jinn.* Why specify? Because the word ʿifrīt by itself can be used loosely in Arabic for a fierce or formidable person. By anchoring it — *mina al-jinni* — Allah is telling us this is the literal article. Not a metaphor. Not a strong man being called a demon. An actual jinn of the most formidable kind.

So before this being even speaks, the word announcing him has already told us: *you are about to hear from raw, elemental power.*

Now hold that. Because what follows from his mouth is a self-recommendation. And the question we are being prepared to ask is: **what happens when the most powerful entity in the room recommends itself for the job?**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Anā ātīka bihi* — the grammar of self-display

Here is a feature of Arabic that English does not have. In Arabic, verbs already contain their subject. If you want to say "I will bring it to you," the most economical form is just one word: *ātīka bihi* — the "I" is already inside the verb, in the conjugation. You don't need a separate pronoun.

So when an Arabic speaker *adds* the separate pronoun *anā* — "I" — before the verb, it is never accidental. It is emphasis. It is foregrounding. It is the speaker stepping out from behind the verb and pointing at himself.

Look at what the ʿifrīt says:

> أَنَا۠ ءَاتِيكَ بِهِۦ — *anā ātīka bihi* — "**I** will bring it to you."

He could have said *ātīka bihi* — three syllables, complete sentence, "I'll bring it to you." But he doesn't. He inserts the *anā.* He puts the "I" out front.

Stay with this. In Arabic rhetoric, this construction — fronted pronoun + verb — is one of the standard ways the language signals self-emphasis or self-display. It is the grammatical equivalent of stepping forward, raising your hand, pointing at your chest.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something else. The verb form itself — *ātīka* — is in the present-future tense. In context it functions as a promise: "I am bringing / I will bring." It is the language of a contract being offered in real-time, in court, before everyone.

So we have three things stacked on top of each other in just two words:
- The speaker steps out from behind the verb (*anā*)
- He uses the most direct future-promise form (*ātīka*)
- And he addresses Sulaymān directly with the second-person *-ka* — "to **you**"

This is not someone offering help quietly from the back of the room. This is someone making sure his offer is registered, witnessed, and personally addressed to the king.

Here's what this invites us to consider. **The form of his speech is the form of self-promotion.** Arabic has more humble grammatical options. He didn't choose them. The grammar is showing us his posture before the content has even told us anything.

Compare this — and we'll come back to this in the closing — to how a different person volunteers in the *very next ayah.* Watch what happens to the pronouns there. For now, just notice: the Arabic of this ayah is the Arabic of a man (or jinn) who wants to be seen.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *La-qawiyyun amīn* — the two seals

The ʿifrīt finishes his offer with a sworn closing:

> وَإِنِّى عَلَيْهِ لَقَوِىٌّ أَمِينٌ — *wa innī ʿalayhi la-qawiyyun amīn*
> "And indeed I am, for this, strong and trustworthy."

Three things to notice. First, *inna* — the Arabic particle that translates as "indeed" or "verily." Arabic uses *inna* to make a statement emphatic, to assert it firmly. Second, the *lām* prefixed to *qawiyy* — that little *la-* — is the *lām al-tawkīd*, another emphasis particle. So we have **double emphasis** stacked: *inna* + *la-*. He is not just saying "I am strong and trustworthy." He is saying "I am, *truly, certainly*, strong and trustworthy."

He is, in modern terms, **stamping his own résumé.**

[PAUSE]

Third — and this is where the ayah opens up — those two adjectives.

*Qawiyy* — strong. From the root *q-w-y*, denoting strength, power, capacity. Physical, in this context. Strong enough to lift the throne. Strong enough to traverse the distance. Strong enough to do the heavy lifting.

*Amīn* — trustworthy. From the root *ʾ-m-n*, the same root as *īmān* (faith) and *amān* (security) and *amāna* (the trust). Someone who can be entrusted with something — who will not steal it, will not damage it, will not betray the assignment.

Now here is where you have to sit very, very still.

These two words — *qawiyyun amīn* — appear together exactly one other time in the Quran in this kind of self-recommendation. **And it is not the ʿifrīt's voice that we hear them in.** It is in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ, when the daughter of the man in Madyan recommends Mūsā (ʿalayhi al-salām) to her father:

> إِنَّ خَيْرَ مَنِ ٱسْتَـْٔجَرْتَ ٱلْقَوِىُّ ٱلْأَمِينُ
> "Indeed, the best one you can hire is **the strong, the trustworthy.**" *(28:26)*

Same two words. *Al-qawiyy al-amīn.*

But notice the difference — and the difference is everything.

In Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ, *someone else* says it about Mūsā. A young woman who watched him at the well, watched how he carried himself, watched how he lowered his gaze, watched the *quality of his strength.* She speaks the words about him. He never claims them for himself.

In our ayah, **the ʿifrīt says it about himself.** He volunteers the praise. He stamps his own seal. He is his own witness.

[PAUSE]

This is the gap to live inside. The same two adjectives — *strong and trustworthy* — can describe a quality that is real or a quality that is being marketed. The words alone cannot tell you which. What tells you is **whose mouth they come out of.**

When Mūsā is called *al-qawiyy al-amīn*, it is testimony.

When the ʿifrīt calls himself *qawiyyun amīn*, it is advertisement.

And what this ayah invites us to consider — what the architecture of the Quran is doing by placing these two scenes in the same scripture — is the question every one of us has to ask about ourselves. **Are my qualities being witnessed, or am I being my own witness?** Because the words can be identical. The reality is not.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We've spent time with three things. The phonetic texture of *ʿifrīt* — a word whose very sound announces force. The fronted *anā* — grammar that puts the self on display. And the *la-qawiyyun amīn* — a self-stamped résumé, the same two words that elsewhere in the Quran are spoken by another mouth about a prophet.

Three linguistic features. One pattern. **This is the speech of someone selling himself.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About power, about volunteering, about the strange relationship between capability and humility.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The economics of self-recommendation

Let me start where you live, not where the ayah lives.

You have been in a meeting. Maybe at work. Maybe in a community organization. Maybe even at a family gathering where a project needs to get done. Someone needs to step up. The room goes quiet for a moment. And then — *someone speaks.*

Notice who tends to speak first.

Often it is not the most capable person in the room. It is the person *most willing to recommend themselves.* They volunteer fast. They volunteer loud. They list their credentials. They tell you what they bring to the table. They stamp their own résumé before anyone has asked.

And here is the strange thing we have all noticed but rarely articulated: **the speed of self-recommendation is often inversely related to the depth of capacity.** The deepest experts are usually the slowest to speak. The most reliable people are usually the most reluctant to claim reliability. The truly trustworthy almost never use the word "trustworthy" about themselves.

Why?

Because real capacity does not need to advertise. The work speaks. The track record speaks. Other people speak. *The thing itself speaks.* When you have to tell people you are *qawiyyun amīn*, it is often because the room has not yet concluded it on its own.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back to the ayah. The ʿifrīt is not weak. He is, by all classical accounts, genuinely powerful. He probably can do the job. But notice what he does with his power — *he markets it.* He puts the *anā* out front. He doubles the emphasis particles. He stamps his own résumé.

And the Quran is going to do something devastating in the next ayah. It is going to introduce someone who *does not market.* Someone whose entire offer is shorter, quieter, faster, and accomplished while the ʿifrīt is still talking. The one who *had knowledge of the Book* says, in effect: *I will bring it to you before your glance returns to you.* And then it is done.

The ʿifrīt is talking about how fast he is. The other one *is* fast.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this changes about how we live. We are formed by a culture that has industrialized self-recommendation. Résumés. LinkedIn. "Personal brands." Bios. Pitches. We have been trained to believe that *if you don't sell yourself, you don't get chosen.* And there is a worldly truth in that.

But the Quran is showing us a different economics. In the court of Sulaymān — which is, at the symbolic level, an image of the divine court — the one who gets to do the work is not the loudest volunteer. It is the one whose *capacity is real enough that knowledge of the Book moves through them faster than power can move muscle.*

The practical application is small but exacting. **Before you stamp yourself with an adjective, ask: would someone else say this about me, unprompted?** If yes, you probably don't need to say it. If no, *you definitely shouldn't.*

And ask one more thing. When you find yourself in a room and a need arises and your hand goes up fast — pause. Notice the *anā* in your throat. Ask whether the offer is coming from capacity, or from the desire to be seen. The work might still be yours to do. But knowing *which engine* is volunteering you matters more than the volunteering itself.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Power that knows it is borrowed

Stay with the ʿifrīt for one more moment, because there is something in his speech that is not just rhetorical excess. It is a theological tell.

He says: *anā ātīka bihi* — **I** will bring it to you.

Not *Allāhu yuʾtīka bihi* — Allah will bring it to you (through me).
Not *bi-idhni rabbī* — by my Lord's permission.
Not *in shāʾa Allāh* — if Allah wills.

Just *anā.* I.

Now compare this — gently, because we are not yet at the next ayah — to how Sulaymān himself speaks elsewhere in this very surah. When he sees the throne actually arrive, his response in 27:40 is: *hādhā min faḍli rabbī liyabluwanī a-ashkuru am akfur* — *this is from the favor of my Lord, to test me whether I will be grateful or ungrateful.* The king credits Allah. The ʿifrīt — the one who serves the king — credits himself.

There is a pattern in the Quran that you start to see once you have eyes for it. **The closer a being is to Allah, the more it credits Allah for what comes through it. The further a being is, the more it credits itself.** The prophets say *bi-idhnillāh.* The strugglers and the boasters say *anā.*

[PAUSE]

This is the unseen reality the ayah is pointing toward. The physical scene is a court, a throne, a contest of speed. But the deeper question is: *whose power is it really?* The ʿifrīt has strength because Allah gave him strength. The one with knowledge of the Book has knowledge because Allah gave him knowledge. *Every capacity in that room is borrowed.* The only question is whether the borrower remembers the lender.

You and I live this question every day. Your intellect — borrowed. Your health — borrowed. Your work ethic, your charm, your network, your savings, your skill — borrowed. The breath in your lungs as you read this — borrowed. Not earned. Not yours in the deep sense. Loaned to you for a window of time, after which it returns.

The ʿifrīt is not bad because he is strong. He is being shown to us because he is strong *and forgets where the strength came from.* He talks about his power as though it were his property. The Arabic puts it bluntly: *innī ʿalayhi la-qawiyyun amīn* — **I**, for this, am strong, am trustworthy. There is no Allah in the sentence. Just him.

[PAUSE]

Now look at yourself. The next time you accomplish something — a deal closed, a meal cooked, a child taught, a sentence written — listen to the language of the celebration in your own chest. Is it *I did this*? Or is it *Allah let me do this through these hands*?

The first is the language of the ʿifrīt. The second is the language of Sulaymān.

The work might be identical. The throne might still arrive. **But the relationship between the worker and the Lord of the throne is being formed in those exact words.**

The practical application is this. After you finish something good, before anyone praises you, *thank Allah out loud or in your heart.* Not as a religious flourish. As a corrective. As a way of refusing the *anā.* Because if you don't credit Him quickly, the *anā* will swell up and fill the room. And you will become — quietly, gradually, without noticing — an ʿifrīt of your own life. Strong. Trustworthy. And forgetful.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now, having lived in this ayah, look at where it sits.

Verse 39: *the ʿifrīt speaks.* He fronts the *anā.* He stamps himself *qawiyyun amīn.* He measures his speed by *before you rise from your place* — the time it would take a king to stand up from his seat.

Verse 40: *the one who had knowledge from the Book speaks.* His pronouns are quieter. His promise is faster — *before your glance returns to you.* And then, in the same verse, the throne is already there. Sulaymān turns and credits *the favor of my Lord.*

The structure is a deliberate diptych. **Strength volunteers loudly and is overtaken by knowledge that volunteers quietly.** The Quran does not need to editorialize. It just places the two side by side, and the reader sees.

And remember the deeper architectural echo: those two seal-words — *qawiyyun amīn* — appear again in Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ, but in someone else's mouth, about Mūsā. The Quran is teaching us, across surahs, *which voice* is permitted to speak those words.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic work and the thematic work are the same insight from two directions. *Linguistically*, we saw a being made of force, fronting his *anā*, doubling his emphasis particles, stamping his own résumé. *Thematically*, we saw the economics of self-recommendation and the theology of borrowed power.

They converge here: **what comes out of the mouth reveals where the heart locates the source of strength.** When the source is felt to be the self, the self steps forward. When the source is felt to be Allah, the self steps back.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time you volunteered something — a skill, a service, an opinion — and the *anā* was louder than the offer itself?

2. If someone wrote a one-line description of you, would you want them to call you *qawiyyun amīn*? And if yes — are you willing to never say it about yourself, and let them be the one who does?

3. What capacity do you most often forget you borrowed? Health? Intellect? Time? When did you last thank Allah for it specifically — by name?

4. Is there a place in your life right now where you are out-talking someone who would, if given the chance, out-deliver you?

5. When good things flow through your hands — what is the first sentence your heart says? Whose name appears in it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah preserves the ʿifrīt's exact boast so we can hear what self-credited power sounds like — and recognize, the next time those words form in our own throats, that we are about to be overtaken by someone quieter, faster, and more entrusted than us.**

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to stop being capable. Sulaymān's court is full of capable beings — the ʿifrīt is not expelled, he is just not chosen for this task. You are being invited, instead, to develop a particular reflex: **before the *anā* leaves your mouth, let the name of Allah enter it.** Before you stamp yourself, let someone else stamp you. Before you advertise your *qawiyyun amīn*, do the quiet work that lets others say it about you when you are not in the room.

This is not modesty as a costume. It is realism about where strength comes from.
