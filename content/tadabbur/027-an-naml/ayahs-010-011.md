# The Staff That Became a Snake — and the Fear That Became Mercy

## The Ayah

> وَأَلْقِ عَصَاكَ ۚ فَلَمَّا رَآهَا تَهْتَزُّ كَأَنَّهَا جَانٌّ وَلَّىٰ مُدْبِرًا وَلَمْ يُعَقِّبْ ۚ يَا مُوسَىٰ لَا تَخَفْ إِنِّي لَا يَخَافُ لَدَيَّ الْمُرْسَلُونَ ۝ إِلَّا مَن ظَلَمَ ثُمَّ بَدَّلَ حُسْنًا بَعْدَ سُوءٍ فَإِنِّي غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

> *Wa alqi ʿaṣāk. Fa lammā raʾāhā tahtazzu kaʾannahā jānnun wallā mudbiran wa lam yuʿaqqib. Yā Mūsā lā takhaf innī lā yakhāfu ladayya al-mursalūn. Illā man ẓalama thumma baddala ḥusnan baʿda sūʾin fa innī Ghafūrun Raḥīm.*

> "And throw down your staff." But when he saw it writhing as if it were a serpent, he turned and fled, and did not look back. "O Musa, do not fear. Truly, in My presence the messengers do not fear. Except one who has wronged — then substituted good after evil. Indeed, I am Forgiving and Merciful."

---

## INTRODUCTION — The Mountain, The Fire, The Man Who Has a Past

To enter this ayah, we have to enter the night.

A man is walking with his family through the Sinai desert. He has been gone from Egypt for ten years — the Quran records in Surah Al-Qasas that Shuʿayb set eight years as the minimum and ten as the completed term, and Musa fulfilled the longer pledge. He left in fear. He left because he had **killed someone**. Not in war. Not in self-defense planned out. In a flash of anger, he struck a man, and the man died, and Musa fled.

For a decade, he has been a shepherd. He has married into a family that took him in when he had nothing. He has learned to live small. The fire of Egypt — palaces, courts, the household of Pharaoh that raised him — that fire is far behind him. Now he tends sheep and watches his children sleep under desert stars.

And tonight, the family loses its way. Tonight, in the cold, he sees a fire on the side of a mountain.

He says to his family — go on, I will bring you a torch, or directions from whoever has lit this fire. He climbs alone. The fire grows nearer. And then a voice speaks his name.

[PAUSE]

This is the moment. The man who has been hiding for ten years is being summoned. The man who fled because of what he did is being told to go back to the very people he fled from. And the first command Allah gives him — after revealing Himself, after the staggering "I am Allah, Lord of all worlds" — the first concrete instruction is this:

**Throw down your staff.**

A small thing. The thing in his hand. The shepherd's tool. The most ordinary object in his life. Throw it down.

And what happens next is one of the most psychologically honest moments in the entire Quran. Because Musa — the chosen one, the future liberator, the prophet who will face Pharaoh — does what any of us would do.

He runs.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Allah will respond to this fleeing man with a sentence that contains a clause so unexpected, so apparently out of place, that the classical commentators paused over it for centuries. He says: *the messengers do not fear in My presence — except the one who has wronged, then substituted good after evil; indeed I am Forgiving and Merciful.*

Why is that exception there? Why, in this moment of comforting a terrified prophet, does Allah open a door for wrongdoers? What is that doorway doing in the middle of Musa's encounter with the fire?

That is what we are going to sit with tonight.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1: *Tahtazzu kaʾannahā jānn* — The Verb That Shudders

Let's begin with the staff.

Allah does not say "it became a serpent." He says: **fa lammā raʾāhā tahtazzu kaʾannahā jānn** — "when he saw it *writhing* as if it were a serpent."

Two pieces here. First the verb *tahtazzu*, from the root *h-z-z*. In classical Arabic, this verb describes a particular kind of motion — not walking, not crawling, but **shaking, vibrating, undulating**. It is the same root used for the trembling of branches in wind, for the shivering of a body, for the convulsive movement of something alive that cannot keep still.

Notice the sound. *Tahtazzu*. The doubled *zz* in the middle of the word — your tongue actually has to vibrate to say it. The word *moves* in your mouth the way the staff moved on the ground. This is one of those places where Arabic does not just describe the thing; the word *performs* the thing. You cannot say *tahtazzu* without a small shudder of the tongue.

[PAUSE]

Before moving to the second piece, notice something al-Ṭabarī points out about the structure of the verse itself: there is a **ḥadhf** here — a deliberate ellipsis. The text says "throw down your staff" and then immediately jumps to "when he saw it writhing." The full underlying sequence was: *he threw it, and it became a snake, writhing* — but the Quran suppresses the middle. The transformation itself is omitted. We move from the command directly to Musa's perception.

Al-Ṭabarī notes this compression explicitly, observing that the unstated was left out "because what was mentioned sufficed for what was omitted." But the sufficiency is not merely grammatical. The miracle itself is withheld from us. What we receive instead is the experience of the witness — the moment of seeing, not the moment of becoming. The Quran is insisting: the event is not the point. The *encounter* is.

[PAUSE]

Now the second piece: *kaʾannahā jānn* — "as if it were a *jānn*."

Here is something stunning. In other surahs, when this same scene is described, Allah uses different words. In Surah Ta-Ha He says it became *ḥayyatun tasʿā* — "a snake, gliding." In Surah Al-Aʿraf He says it was *thuʿbānun mubīn* — "a clear, large serpent." But here in An-Naml, He chooses the word *jānn*.

Al-Ṭabarī characterizes the jānn in this verse as "a great, formidable serpent — a known genus of snakes," and al-Muyassar adds a crucial quality of movement: it moved *fī khiffatin* — "with the lightness of movement of a swift snake." The classical commentators were thus attending to two things at once: it is a recognizable snake species, and it moves in a way the eye cannot hold. Not lumbering. Not slow. Light, continuous, untrackable.

The lexical tradition adds a third dimension: *jānn* carries the root *j-n-n*, which means **that which is hidden, covered, sudden — moving in a way that escapes the eye's grip**. The same root gives us the jinn (hidden beings) and *junūn* (the madness that seizes from within, invisible in its arrival). All three qualities converge: a recognizable creature, moving with the swiftness of the unseen, belonging to the same word-family as that which cannot be fully seen.

So why this word, in this surah?

Because Surah An-Naml is the surah of the **unseen world breaking into the seen**. It is the surah where Sulayman speaks the language of ants and birds. It is the surah where the throne of Bilqis is moved across continents in less than the blink of an eye. It is the surah where the jinn participate openly in the visible court. The whole surah is asking: *what is real beyond what your eye can verify?*

And here, in the opening encounter, the staff does not just become *a snake*. It becomes a *jānn* — something that moves with a swiftness the eye cannot hold, something that belongs to the same lexical family as the unseen.

The word choice is doing what the surah is doing.

[PAUSE]

But there is even more in this small phrase. *Kaʾannahā* — *"as if it were."* Allah does not say it *was* a jānn. He says Musa saw it *as if* it were one.

Why this hedging? Why the simile?

Because the perception is Musa's. From Allah's perspective, the staff is the staff is the staff — the object's reality is held in His will. From Musa's perspective, the thing on the ground is moving in a way that *looks like* a swift, hidden, dangerous creature. The *kaʾannahā* puts us inside Musa's eyes. We are seeing what he saw, with the limited certainty of a startled human in the dark.

Allah preserves the gap between **what is** and **what Musa perceives**. And that gap, that small word *kaʾannahā*, is the entire human condition. We are always looking at the thing in our hand and seeing something else. We are always misreading our own staff.

What this invites us to consider: the things in your life that are shaking right now — the diagnosis, the relationship that suddenly looks dangerous, the career that seems to be writhing out of control — what if your perception is *kaʾannahā*? What if you are seeing it *as if* it were the threat your eyes are reporting, while in Allah's hand it is still the staff?

[PAUSE — major]

### Element 2: *Wallā mudbiran wa lam yuʿaqqib* — The Three-Layered Flight

Now look at what Musa does. Allah uses three expressions, stacked on top of each other, to describe a single act:

**wallā** — he turned away
**mudbiran** — turning his back
**wa lam yuʿaqqib** — and he did not look back

In English this sounds redundant. In Arabic, every layer is doing something different.

Let me teach the structure first. In classical Arabic rhetoric, when you want to say someone *fled*, the simplest verb is enough: *haraba* — he fled. Done. But Allah, here, refuses the simple verb. He stacks three.

*Wallā* (root *w-l-y*) is the basic act of *turning* — pivoting away from a direction. It is the same root from which we get *walī* (a friend who is "turned toward" you) and *mawlā* (a guardian). At its core: orientation. Musa *re-oriented* — his face, which had been toward the fire and the voice, was no longer toward them.

*Mudbiran* (root *d-b-r*) intensifies. The *dubr* in Arabic is the back, the rear. *Mudbiran* is not just turning — it is **showing your back**. It is the body language of full retreat. He didn't sidestep. He didn't shuffle. He pivoted and gave the scene his back.

And then *wa lam yuʿaqqib* (root *ʿ-q-b*) — *and he did not return upon his heel*. The *ʿaqib* is the heel of the foot. To *yuʿaqqib* in this context means to look back over your shoulder, to glance behind you as you flee, to even consider returning. Allah negates it: *he did not even turn his heel*. He did not look back. He did not reconsider. He did not pause.

[PAUSE]

Three verbs. Three layers of fleeing. Why?

Because Allah is not just telling us Musa was scared. He is letting us *feel* the totality of his terror.

Think about a moment when you were genuinely frightened — not nervous, not anxious, but startled by something you thought might kill you. A car suddenly veering at you. A snake in the grass. An intruder in the house. What does the body do? It turns. It shows its back. It runs without looking back.

Allah is showing us the prophet's body. He is not embarrassed of it. He is not hiding it. He is documenting that **the future liberator of an entire people, the man who will stand before Pharaoh and split the sea, ran from a snake on the ground and did not look back**.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the great gifts of the Quran. It does not give us prophets as marble statues. It gives us prophets as men whose hands shake, whose hearts pound, whose feet flee. It refuses to airbrush the body.

And then — and here is what we have to sit with — Allah responds to that fleeing body with one of the gentlest summons in the entire Quran: *Yā Mūsā*. **"O Musa."**

He uses his name. Not "O Messenger," not "O Servant," not a title. His *name*. The same name his mother whispered when she nursed him. The same name Pharaoh's wife spoke when she pulled him from the water. *Yā Mūsā.*

The voice that just turned a staff into something terrifying now becomes, for one word, the voice of the most familiar tenderness imaginable.

What this invites us to consider: the moments in your life when you fled from what Allah was showing you — when the responsibility was too heavy, when the truth was too sharp, when you turned your back and did not look back — Allah was not angry at the running. He was waiting, with your name on His lips, for you to be ready to turn around.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element 3: *Illā man ẓalama* — The Clause That Changes Everything

Now we arrive at the strangest sentence in the passage.

Allah says: *innī lā yakhāfu ladayya al-mursalūn* — "Indeed, in My presence, the messengers do not fear."

That alone would be a perfect sentence. It would have closed the moment beautifully. Musa, you are a messenger; messengers don't need to fear in My presence; throw the staff again.

But Allah does not stop there. He adds an exception: *illā man ẓalama thumma baddala ḥusnan baʿda sūʾin fa innī Ghafūrun Raḥīm.*

**"Except one who has wronged — then substituted good after evil. Indeed, I am Forgiving, Merciful."**

[PAUSE]

Read it again. The sentence structure is almost dizzying. *Messengers do not fear in My presence — EXCEPT the one who wronged, THEN substituted good after evil, in which case I am Forgiving and Merciful.*

Wait. What?

If "messengers don't fear" is the rule, what is the exception doing? Are some messengers wrongdoers? Is Allah saying *those* messengers should fear? Or is He saying something else entirely?

The classical commentators debated this clause across centuries, and their disagreement is not a sign of confusion — it is a sign of depth. Al-Ṭabarī presents multiple positions, each attentive to a different grammatical and theological register. Let me give you the composite picture they built — not as competing errors, but as overlapping camera angles on the same reality.

**The first reading** rests on what the Basran grammarians called the structure of *istithnāʾ* — an exception whose referent is not literally what was just named. Al-Ṭabarī records this analysis, comparing the construction to the Arab idiom *"mā ashtakī illā khayran"* — "I only have good to complain of" — where the *illā* does not negate the prior statement but opens one implied by it. Applied here: "messengers do not fear in My presence" is the full rule — and the only thing that could have stood between any soul and that freedom from fear is ẓulm, and even that is dissolved by tabdīl. The clause is not creating a class of the fearful; it is sealing every remaining objection: *but what about the one who has wronged?* — *Even that one, if he substituted good for evil, is covered.*

**The second reading** is what the Kufan grammarians preferred. Al-Ṭabarī records their view with a precise formulation: the wrongdoer who substituted good for evil is *absorbed by Allah's promise into the category of those who do not fear in His presence* — made secure through the pledge of forgiveness and mercy, included in the number of the messengers who do not fear. Read this way, the exception is not a threat but an **absorption**: you were the exception, the one who should have feared, but through tabdīl you have been drawn into the circle of the non-fearing. The exception cancels itself through the act it describes.

**The third reading** attends to biography: Musa *has* wronged — he killed a man in Egypt, and he knows it. He would later say to Allah openly in the parallel account in Surah Al-Qasas, *"I killed a soul among them, and I fear they will kill me."* Allah, in the very moment of commissioning him, gently acknowledges: yes, you have a past. And the clause is not punishment — it is **the doorway out of the past**. *Then he substituted good after evil — I am Forgiving and Merciful.* The word *baddala* (substitution, exchange) is not asking him to erase what happened. It is asking him to move in a new direction. Throw the staff. Go back. That is your tabdīl.

[PAUSE]

The classical grammatical debate — whether the exception is structurally disconnected, absorptive, or biographically personal — does not resolve to a single answer, and it may not be intended to. Al-Ṭabarī presents both the Basran and Kufan readings as valid analyses of the same grammatical fact. What is striking is where all three readings arrive: the exception is not a condemnation. In every analysis, the wrongdoer who substitutes good for evil enters the circle of the non-fearing. The fear is dissolved not by the erasure of the past but by the direction of movement — *thumma baddala*. The word *thumma* (then) marks sequence, not simultaneity. The wrong came first. The substitution comes after. And the mercy is attached to that sequence, not to a state of original cleanliness.

The timing of this disclosure matters enormously. Allah is speaking this in the moment He is commissioning Musa. He is not waiting for a safer time, a cleaner moment, a future when Musa's past has been more thoroughly buried. He is saying it *now*, on the mountain, in the dark, with the transformed staff still moving on the ground: *you have a past; the past is already addressed; go.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Element 4: *Ghafūrun Raḥīm* — The Pairing That Seals

The passage ends not with a command, not with a warning, but with two names.

**Ghafūrun** — from the root *gh-f-r*, whose core meaning in classical Arabic is *to cover* — as in the protective layer applied over metal to prevent corrosion. The *ghufrān* of Allah is not erasure. It is covering. The wrong is real; the covering is complete; what is underneath is protected going forward.

**Raḥīm** — from the root *r-ḥ-m*, the same root as *raḥm* (womb). The *raḥmah* of Allah is not generosity at a distance. It is the closeness of the womb — structurally containing, actively nourishing, oriented entirely toward the well-being of what it holds.

Notice that Ghafūr comes first, then Raḥīm. The order is not arbitrary. First the covering — the past is addressed, its harm is sealed. Then the mercy — the one covered is held, nourished, actively sustained going forward. The sequence is: closing what was open, then opening what was closed.

[PAUSE]

These two names arrive at the end of a sentence about someone who has wronged. They do not arrive at the end of a sentence about someone who has been perfect. This is the placement that teaches: the divine names of covering and mercy are most precisely invoked *in relation to failure and substitution*. They are not abstract attributes displayed at a distance. They are responsive. They were waiting for exactly this kind of sentence to attach themselves to.

What this invites us to consider: when you look at your own past — the thing you did, the person you hurt, the years you turned your back and did not look back — the Quran is not asking you to perform a certainty of forgiveness you don't yet feel. It is asking you to notice which divine names are invoked at the end of the wrongdoer-who-substituted's story. Ghafūr. Raḥīm. Not Qahhār. Not Muntaqim. The names that close this verse are not about wrath. They are about what happens after the turn.

[PAUSE — major]

## PART 2 — THE ENCOUNTER WITHIN THE ENCOUNTER

There is a structural fact about this passage that most readings pass over too quickly.

This is not the first time the Quran records Musa at the burning bush. The same event appears in Surah Ta-Ha, Surah Al-Aʿraf, Surah Al-Qasas — and each time, the emphasis is different. In Ta-Ha, the divine self-disclosure is lengthiest and most majestic. In Al-Aʿraf, the focus falls on the miracles themselves. In Al-Qasas, the fear is addressed but briefly.

Here in An-Naml, uniquely, two things receive extended treatment: the terror of the jānn, and the mercy clause about the wrongdoer. Both receive more space, more grammatical precision, more theological weight than in any other account.

Why?

Because Surah An-Naml is structured around a single deep question: **who has real knowledge, and what does real authority look like?** The surah opens by announcing it is the surah of the *bayyinah* — the clear sign — and then immediately introduces Musa, the man with a past, receiving the clearest sign imaginable. The structural answer is already being given before the surah's questions are formally asked. Real authority does not look like flawlessness. Real knowledge includes knowing that you are known — known completely, including the past, including the killing, including the ten years of hiding. And real mercy is not mercy withheld until the account is clean. It is mercy offered at the moment of commissioning: *go, you wrongdoer who will substitute good for evil; I have already named what I am in relation to you.*

[PAUSE]

Now notice what Allah does not say.

He does not say: *Musa, before I commission you, let's address the man you killed.*

He does not say: *Your past disqualifies you, but I am overlooking it this once.*

He does not say: *Once you return to Egypt and liberate the people, then I will forgive you.*

He says: *In My presence, the messengers do not fear. Except the one who wronged and substituted. I am Forgiving and Merciful.* Present tense. Active. Already declared.

The commissioning does not come *after* the forgiveness is earned. The forgiveness is announced *as part of the commissioning itself*. The job description includes the divine promise.

[PAUSE]

This is the encounter within the encounter. Musa comes to the mountain looking for fire to guide his family. He finds himself being sent back to Egypt. But between the "you are being sent" and the "here is your mission," Allah stops. And He places this clause. This parenthetical that is not a parenthetical. This mercy that is not decorative.

It is as if Allah is saying: I know you will stand before Pharaoh and announce *I am a messenger from the Lord of the Worlds*, and when you do, you will carry inside you the memory of the man you struck down. And I want you to carry it correctly. Not as a disqualification. As a *baddala*. As the proof that you know firsthand what it means to wrong and then substitute, to fall and then move in the direction of ḥusn.

The wound is not an obstacle to the calling. It is part of the qualification for it.

[PAUSE — major]

What this invites us to consider: the things in your past you believe disqualify you from doing what you sense you are being called to — what if the clause was spoken to you too, on whatever mountain you stood on, in whatever dark? *You who wronged: then substitute good. I am Ghafūr, Raḥīm.* Not: go fix yourself and come back. But: the one who has known ẓulm from the inside, and turned from it, is precisely the one who can stand before the Pharaohs of their world and know what those Pharaohs will say — because he once said it to himself.

## CLOSING — The Staff Is Still in Your Hand

One final image to carry out of this passage.

The staff that became a jānn — Allah will tell Musa: *pick it up; do not fear; We will return it to its original state.* He picks it up. It is his staff again. The same object. The thing that terrified him, the thing he ran from, becomes the instrument in his hand with which he will part the sea.

The staff is still the staff. It was always the staff. What changed was not the object. What changed was what Musa was willing to hold after he had seen what it could become.

[PAUSE]

The thing you are afraid to hold — the responsibility, the truth, the calling that transformed in your hands into something you didn't recognize — it has not become what you fear it is. The *kaʾannahā* is your perception. In Allah's hand, it is still the object He gave you. The question is whether you will come back from the running and pick it up again.

And the voice that calls you back uses your name.

Not your title. Not your credentials. Not your track record. Your name. The one your mother gave you. The one that was spoken before you knew anything about staffs or snakes or callings or fear.

*Yā Mūsā.*

[PAUSE — major]

---

*O Allah, when we flee from what You are showing us, call us back by name. When we run from the staff that became a jānn — from the truth that moved faster than we could follow, from the calling that shook in our hands — do not let us run forever. We have wronged. We have turned our backs. We have not looked back. But here we are, on the mountain, in the dark, having climbed toward Your fire. We have substituted nothing yet, or very little. But we want to. We want the baddala. We ask You by Your name Ghafūr — cover what has been; by Your name Raḥīm — hold what we are becoming. Do not let the encounter on the mountain pass without our understanding what was being said. Āmīn.*
