# The Gift That Was Already an Answer

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 35

> [ayah:27:35] وَإِنِّى مُرْسِلَةٌ إِلَيْهِم بِهَدِيَّةٍ فَنَاظِرَةٌۢ بِمَ يَرْجِعُ ٱلْمُرْسَلُونَ
>
> *Wa innī mursilatun ilayhim bi-hadiyyatin fa-nāẓiratun bima yarjiʿu-l-mursalūn.*
>
> "But indeed, I will send to them a gift and see with what the messengers return."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world before the ayah

Let's place ourselves in her court.

A queen sits on a throne in Yemen. Her kingdom is wealthy — the Quran will later tell us about her two gardens, her abundance, her people who worship the sun. She is not a tyrant. She is, by every measure the Quran allows us to see, a competent, reflective ruler. When the hoopoe drops a letter at her feet — a letter from a king she has never met, delivered by a bird that should not be carrying mail — she does not panic. She gathers her council. She reads them the letter aloud. She asks for their counsel.

Her council answers the way councils almost always answer when a foreign power makes a demand. They flex. They remind her: **"We are people of strength and people of severe might."** Translation: *we can fight. Say the word.*

[PAUSE]

Now stay with what just happened. She is surrounded by men who are telling her they can solve this problem with force. Her kingdom is intact. Her power is real. The default human script, the default royal script, the default *imperial* script — is to accept the offer of force. To meet pressure with pressure. To not appear weak.

And in the previous ayah — ayah 34 — she has already pushed back against that script. She said something extraordinary: **"Indeed, kings, when they enter a town, ruin it, and make the noblest of its people the lowest. And thus they do."** She has named the *pattern* of power. She has seen, with unusual clarity, that kings break things. That an invading king does not just defeat — he humiliates, he inverts, he degrades.

And now, in ayah 35 — this ayah — she makes her move.

Her move is not war. Her move is not surrender. Her move is a *gift*.

### The hook

Here is what is strange. Here is what should stop us.

She sends a gift — and the next ayah will show us that Sulayman is *unimpressed*. He refuses the gift. He sends it back. By every diplomatic measure, her move *fails*.

And yet — within a handful of ayahs — she ends up on the floor of his palace, saying *"I have submitted with Sulayman to Allah, the Lord of all the worlds."*

[PAUSE]

So her gift failed. And then she was saved.

What if her gift was never about Sulayman accepting it? What if her gift was never *for* Sulayman at all — but for *her*? What if this single ayah, this nine-word ayah, contains the entire psychology of how a sincere soul moves from doubt toward truth?

Let's sit with that. Let's open this ayah word by word.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Means "To Send" — And Who Sends It

Before we look at her words, let's notice something about the *shape* of this whole episode. The Arabic root **r-s-l** — to send — runs through this entire passage like a thread. It is the root behind *rasūl* (messenger), *risālah* (a message, a sending), *mursil* (one who sends), *mursal* (one who is sent).

In the previous ayah, Sulayman has effectively *sent* his letter to her. In Islamic theology, Sulayman is a *nabī*, a prophet — and prophets are, by definition, *those who are sent*. They are *mursalūn*.

Now look at what she says.

**وَإِنِّى مُرْسِلَةٌ** — *wa innī mursilatun* — "And indeed, I am one who sends."

[PAUSE]

Notice what she has just done with language. She has placed herself in the grammatical position of *a sender*. The same morphological pattern that the Quran uses for prophets and angels — *mursil*, one who sends — she has applied to herself. *Mursilah*, with the feminine ending.

This is not a coincidence. This is a queen, in a moment of high pressure, mirroring back the framework of the man who has written to her. He sent? Fine. *I, too, will send.* He has messengers? Fine. *I, too, will have messengers* — and at the end of the ayah she will call them exactly that, *al-mursalūn*, "the ones sent."

She is matching his register. She is meeting his diplomatic move on its own terms.

But here is what is remarkable. The Arabic does not just say *"I will send."* It uses the **active participle** — *mursilah* — which functions in Arabic almost like declaring an identity in the moment of action. It is closer to: *"I am, right now, a sender."* It is a statement of *what she is doing as she speaks*, declarative and resolved.

Compare it to what she could have said. She could have used the simple future: *sa-ursilu*, "I will send." She didn't. She chose the participle. She chose to *embody* the role of sender rather than merely promise the action.

Why does this matter?

Because in this moment — surrounded by men who want her to fight — she is *taking back narrative authority*. She is not reacting to Sulayman's letter. She is *acting*. She is not the passive recipient of his demand. She is, at least in her own framing, a peer.

Here is what this single word reveals: **she is not yet ready to submit, but she is also refusing to be defined by anyone else's frame.** She is occupying her own ground first. Before she can find truth, she must first stop being reactive. She must first become a *subject* in her own story.

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is, by the way, what humility before truth actually requires. Not collapse. Not erasure. *Composure.* You cannot truly submit until you first stand upright. The ones who fall apart at the first pressure are not surrendering — they are merely being broken. Real submission, the kind that ends this story, requires a self that is intact enough to *choose* to bow.

She is composing herself. *Mursilah.*

### Element Two: The Gift — *Hadiyyah* — And What Is Missing From It

Now look at what she sends.

**بِهَدِيَّةٍ** — *bi-hadiyyatin* — "with a gift."

The root here is **h-d-y**, and this is one of the great roots of the Quran. From this root comes *hudā* — guidance. From this root comes *hidāyah* — being guided. From this root comes *hādī* — the one who guides. And from this same root — *hadiyyah* — a gift.

Stay with that. In Arabic, *a gift* and *guidance* share a root.

Why? Because etymologically, a *hadiyyah* is something offered *to lead someone somewhere*. A gift is a directional act. You give a gift to *move* the relationship — to open a door, to soften a boundary, to indicate something. A *hadiyyah* is not a transaction. It is a *gesture toward*. It is something that points.

So when she says *"I am sending a hadiyyah"* — at the linguistic root level she is saying *"I am sending something meant to indicate, to direct, to test which way the road runs."*

[PAUSE]

Now notice the absence. Notice what Allah did not tell us.

What is the gift?

We are not told. The Quran does not specify a single object inside this gift. The classical commentators have filled this gap with tradition — bricks of gold, slaves dressed identically to test his discernment, a sealed pearl — but the *Quranic text itself* leaves the gift undescribed. The container has no contents we can see.

This is one of those moments in the Quran where what is *missing* is the meaning. Because if Allah had named the gift, our attention would lock on the gift. We would be debating gold versus jewels, the worth of the gesture, the cleverness of the trick. We would be reading this as a diplomatic story about *objects*.

By withholding the contents, Allah keeps our attention on *the act of giving itself*. On the *gesture*, not the goods. On what it means about *her* that she chose to send a gift at all.

And what does it mean?

A gift is what you send when you do not yet know who you are dealing with. War is what you send when you already know — and you have decided they are an enemy. Tribute is what you send when you already know — and you have decided they are your master. A *gift* — a *hadiyyah* — is the middle space. It is the diplomatic equivalent of saying: *I have not yet decided who you are to me.*

[PAUSE]

She is buying time. But she is buying time *the right way*. She is not stalling out of fear; she is pausing out of *discernment*. There is a profound difference between the two, and the verb that comes next will show us exactly which one she is doing.

What this word reveals: **she is opening a channel of inquiry while keeping every option alive.** The gift is the diplomatic shape of *uncertainty held with dignity*.

### Element Three: *Fa-Nāẓiratun* — The Verb of the Sincere Investigator

Now we come to the word that, I want to suggest, is the hinge of this entire surah's portrait of her.

**فَنَاظِرَةٌۢ** — *fa-nāẓiratun* — "and then I am one who looks / one who watches / one who observes."

The root **n-ẓ-r** has a range. It can mean to see, to look, to gaze, to wait, to consider, to inspect. The Quran uses it in heavy moments — *unẓur ilā ṭaʿāmika*, "look at your food" (Baqarah 259); *fa-yanẓuru kayfa taʿmalūn*, "He will see how you act" (Aʿrāf 129); *wujūhun yawma'idhin nāḍirah ilā rabbihā nāẓirah*, "faces that day will be radiant, gazing upon their Lord" (Qiyāmah 22-23).

It is not the verb for *seeing-with-the-eyes*. There is a different root for that — *r-ʾ-y*. *Naẓar* is the verb of *looking-with-attention*. It is sight that has *purpose* inside it. It is the gaze of someone *trying to learn something*.

[PAUSE]

And again — like *mursilah* — she uses the active participle. *Nāẓirah*. Not *sa-anẓuru* ("I will look") but *nāẓirah* — "I am one-who-looks." It is, in Arabic grammar, an ongoing, embodied state. *I am, presently and continuously, in the posture of observing.*

So now read the whole inner movement of the ayah:

*I am one-who-sends a gift, and then I am one-who-watches with what the messengers return.*

Do you hear what she is doing?

She is not sending the gift to *bribe* Sulayman. She is sending the gift as *an instrument of observation*. The gift is the probe. The gift is the experiment. The gift is the thing she releases into the unknown so that *what comes back* can teach her something she could not have learned by sitting in her court.

This is one of the most beautiful descriptions in the Quran of how a sincere mind approaches a question it cannot yet answer.

She does not pretend to know. She does not pretend to *not* know. She designs a *test* — and then she does the hardest thing of all. She *waits*. She *watches*. *Fa-nāẓiratun*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Let me sit with this longer, because there is something here we modern people desperately need.

We live in a world that punishes uncertainty. Social pressure says: *take a side*. Algorithmic pressure says: *post your opinion*. Tribal pressure says: *which team are you on?* Career pressure says: *appear decisive*. We have built an entire civilization around looking certain.

And here, in the middle of a Quranic story, is a queen — at the height of political pressure, with her advisors begging her to fight, with a foreign king demanding her submission — who turns to her people and says, in effect:

*I do not yet know what to do. So I will design a careful experiment. And then I will watch what comes back.*

That is not weakness. That is not indecision. That is *wisdom*. That is what the Quran will later call *ḥikmah* — and we will see, in just a few ayahs, that her wise pause is precisely what allows her soul to find truth.

If she had attacked, she would have died in error. If she had surrendered, she would have surrendered to a man, not to Allah. Because she *paused* — because she *watched* — because she designed a probe and then let reality answer — she eventually arrived at the only words that matter: *"I have submitted with Sulayman to Allah, Lord of all the worlds."*

What this word reveals: **the sincere soul does not need to know the answer in advance. It needs only to be honest enough to admit it doesn't know — and patient enough to design a test and wait for reality to answer.**

---

### Bridge

Three words. *Mursilah, hadiyyah, nāẓirah.*

She becomes a *subject* — *mursilah*, one who acts, not merely reacts.

She sends a *probe* — *hadiyyah*, a directional gesture that holds her options open.

She enters the *posture of attention* — *nāẓirah*, the one-who-watches what reality returns.

This is the linguistic architecture of *istibṣār* — careful, watchful discernment. The grammar of someone *on the road to faith* but not yet at its destination.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about her words — but about how truth actually enters a human life.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Wise Pause — And Why It Is Not Weakness

Imagine a friend comes to you with a moral crisis. They say: *I don't know what to do.*

Watch what the world does to that admission. The internet says: *take a stand*. Their parents say: *make a decision*. Their boss says: *be decisive*. Their group chat says: *which side are you on?*

And the friend, terrified of looking weak, picks something. Anything. Because *not knowing* feels unbearable.

[PAUSE]

Now compare that to what the queen does in this ayah. She is in a high-pressure room. Her advisors have given her a recommendation. Her opponent has issued a demand. Every social force around her is pushing her to *decide right now*. And she does the unthinkable thing.

She says: *I will send a gift. And I will watch.*

She refuses the false choice. She refuses the demand for premature certainty. She *takes the third path* — the path of structured inquiry. She invents an experiment whose results will *help her decide*.

This is what the Quran is showing us. There is a kind of *paused action* that is the highest form of decisiveness, because it is the decisiveness of *not letting the situation force you into the wrong answer*.

Here is the principle, and it lives at the deepest level of how a believing mind should work: **the response to a hard question is not always an immediate answer. Sometimes the response is the design of the right test.**

The Quran will give us this same posture in other places. The Prophet ﷺ pausing during the migration. Mūsā being told *fa-stamiʿ li-mā yūḥā* — *listen to what is revealed*. Even the angels asking *atajʿalu fīhā man yufsidu fīhā* — *will You place therein one who corrupts?* — not as defiance, but as *inquiry before assent*.

The wise pause is not the *absence* of action. It is the *highest form* of action — because it refuses to let panic, social pressure, or premature certainty hijack the soul.

Now bring this home. What does this mean for you, tomorrow?

It means that when someone hands you a religious claim, a political position, a marriage proposal, a career decision, a contested verse, an angry comment, a viral outrage — you are allowed to be *nāẓirah*. You are allowed to send out a probe. You are allowed to *watch what comes back* before you commit. The Queen of Sheba, before she became Muslim, was already practicing the *epistemic posture* that submission to Allah requires.

[PAUSE]

Because here is the deeper truth: **you cannot submit to Allah if you have already been bullied into submitting to the loudest voice in the room.** Real *islām* — surrender — requires a soul that is *still capable of choosing*. The queen's pause is not a delay before her faith. The pause is *the very thing that makes her faith real when it arrives*.

#### What changes tomorrow

The next time you feel pressured into immediate certainty — whether by an argument online, a social demand, or your own fear of looking weak — pause. Ask yourself: *what is my hadiyyah here? What is the small, low-cost probe I can send into the situation that will teach me what I need to know?* And then *watch*. Reality is more patient than the internet. Truth answers — but it answers in its own time.

### Theme Two: How a Sincere Soul Walks Toward Truth

Let me tell you the most stunning thing about this story.

The queen sends a gift. Sulayman *refuses* it. He sends it back with a stern message — *"What Allah has given me is better than what He has given you."* By every diplomatic measure, her plan *failed*.

And it was the most important failure of her life.

Because here is what she had designed her test to *see*. She had assumed — reasonably, given everything she knew — that if Sulayman accepted the gift, he was a king like other kings. Greedy. Buyable. Status-seeking. And if he was that kind of king, she could *handle* him. She knew how to navigate that world.

But if he *refused* the gift — if he was *not* impressed by wealth — then he was something else. Something her categories did not yet contain. A *prophet*. A man whose authority did not run on the same fuel as every other king she had ever known.

She designed a test that could only return two answers. *He is a king like other kings* → she knew what to do. *He is something more* → she would have to investigate further.

And the gift came back. *Mā ātāniya-llāhu khayrun mimmā ātākum.* "What Allah has given me is better than what He has given you."

[PAUSE — long pause]

She got her answer. And the answer was: *go and look for yourself*.

This is how Allah brings sincere souls toward truth. He does not usually crush them with miracles. He does not usually drop the answer ready-made into their lap. He *meets their probe with a probe*. He answers their *hadiyyah* with a return-gift of *information*. He gives them just enough to *make them want to come closer*.

And she does. In the very next ayahs, she travels. She comes to Sulayman. She sees the throne. She enters the palace. She mistakes the smooth glass floor for water and lifts her dress — and at that moment, when her assumptions about *reality itself* are corrected (it looked like water, it was glass), her tongue finally moves:

*"My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulayman to Allah, Lord of all the worlds."*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the architecture? Her *nāẓirah* in ayah 35 — *I am one-who-watches* — is the seed of her *islām* in ayah 44. Her watching led to her travelling. Her travelling led to her seeing. Her seeing led to her submission. And every single step was *her own*. Allah did not drag her to truth. He let her *walk* there — *because she was looking*.

Here is the principle that should burn in our chest: **Allah honors the sincere watcher.** When a soul says *fa-nāẓiratun bi-mā yarjiʿu* — *I am watching to see what comes back* — Allah arranges reality so that *what comes back* is the next step on the road.

This is one of the most consoling truths in the Quran. You do not need to start with faith. You need to start with *honest watching*. You need to be the kind of person who designs a probe in good faith and then *actually looks at the answer*.

Because the people who do not arrive at truth are not usually the people who *doubted* — they are the people who *stopped watching*. They asked a question and walked away before the answer came. They sent the *hadiyyah* but they were not *nāẓirah*. They wanted certainty without the patience that certainty requires.

The queen is given an entire chapter of the Quran partly because she *kept her eyes open*. She did not flinch from what came back. She let reality re-educate her.

Now contrast her with Pharaoh in the next surah over. Mūsā sends Pharaoh a *probe* — sign after sign. Reality returns answer after answer. And Pharaoh *refuses to watch*. He sees the signs and calls them magic. He sees the answer and rejects the question. He has not been denied the data. He has *refused to be nāẓirah*.

The difference between Pharaoh and Bilqīs is not what they were told. It is *whether they kept looking*.

#### What changes tomorrow

You have, right now, questions you have not fully answered. Questions about your faith, your relationships, your purpose, your place. *Send the hadiyyah.* Design the small, honest test. Read the verse you have been avoiding. Have the hard conversation. Visit the place. Pray the prayer you've been postponing. *Watch what comes back.* Allah arranges return-shipments for sincere senders.

But you have to *actually look at the package when it arrives.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture beneath the ayah

Now look back at the nine words.

*Wa innī mursilatun ilayhim bi-hadiyyatin fa-nāẓiratun bima yarjiʿu-l-mursalūn.*

It begins with **mursilah** — I am one-who-sends.
It ends with **al-mursalūn** — the ones-who-are-sent.

The verb that opens the ayah and the noun that closes it are the *same root*. The grammar literally *sends and receives* — and the woman who began as the *sender* is, by the end of the ayah, *waiting for the senders to return*.

It is a ring. A circuit. A loop. The ayah is shaped like the very thing it describes: *something going out and coming back*. The sentence itself enacts the act of *hadiyyah*-and-*naẓar* in its own grammar.

[PAUSE]

And inside that ring lives the gift — *hadiyyah* — and the watching — *nāẓirah*. The probe and the gaze. The thing-she-sends and the posture-she-holds.

This is the entire psychology of a soul on the verge of belief, compressed into nine words.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are saying the same thing from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us: she *embodies* the role of sender (*mursilah*), she *gestures* a probe of inquiry (*hadiyyah*), she *holds the posture* of watching (*nāẓirah*).

The thematic side showed us: a wise soul *pauses before deciding*, sends a *probe into the unknown*, and *watches what reality returns*.

Both are saying: **the road to truth is walked by the one who refuses both panic and pretense, and who, instead, sends out a small honest gesture and then keeps her eyes open.**

### Questions to carry

1. What hard question are you avoiding right now because you are afraid you cannot answer it immediately?

2. What would be *your hadiyyah* — the small, honest, low-cost probe you could send into that question this week?

3. Where have you been *demanding immediate certainty* from yourself, when what you actually need is to be *nāẓirah* — to watch and wait?

4. Is there a return-shipment from a probe you already sent — an answer that has already come back — that you have been refusing to look at?

5. Whose voice in your life is pressuring you to skip the watching stage? What would it mean to politely send a gift instead — and then quietly observe?

### One-sentence distillation

**The Queen of Sheba's path to faith began not with belief but with the discipline of designing an honest test and keeping her eyes open for what reality would return — and this is how Allah brings sincere souls home.**

### The closing invitation

If you are not yet where you want to be in your faith — if you are full of questions, full of resistance, full of half-formed objections you do not even fully understand — you are not in a bad place. You are in *her* place. And she ended on the floor of Sulayman's palace, surrendering with Sulayman to the Lord of all the worlds.

She did not get there by pretending. She got there by *watching*.

Be the watcher. Send the probe. Stay honest. Stay attentive. Let what comes back, come back. Allah will arrange the return shipment.

### A brief du'a

*Yā Rabb, make us among the watchers — the ones who send honest probes into our own questions and keep our eyes open for what You return. Save us from the false certainty of those who refuse to look. And as You brought the queen from her throne in Sheba to the floor of the palace, bring us — through our own small, patient, watchful steps — to the only sentence worth ending on: that we have submitted, with all the prophets and the seekers before us, to You, Lord of all the worlds.*

*Āmīn.*
