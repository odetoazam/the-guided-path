# The Thing Already Riding Your Heels

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
قُلْ عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَكُونَ رَدِفَ لَكُم بَعْضُ ٱلَّذِى تَسْتَعْجِلُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Qul ʿasā an yakūna radifa lakum baʿḍu alladhī tastaʿjilūn.*

**Translation:**
*Say: "Perhaps some of what you are seeking to hasten has already drawn up close behind you."*

---

## Introduction: The Mockers' Question

Imagine you are standing in Makkah, somewhere in the late years of the Prophet's ﷺ mission. The opposition has hardened. The arguments are tired. The Quraysh have heard the warnings about a Day, about a reckoning, about a punishment that could come at any moment — and they have decided to weaponize the very mercy of its delay.

So they invent a new kind of attack. They turn waiting itself into a joke.

"When is this promise, if you are truthful?"

It's not a real question. They don't want a date. They want to use the *absence* of a date as proof that there is no date — to use Allah's restraint as evidence of Allah's nonexistence, His mercy as evidence of His indifference, His patience as evidence of His powerlessness.

And this becomes the cruelest tactic in the deniers' repertoire across the Quran. Because it does something subtle. It puts the believer on the defensive. It makes the *delay* of justice feel like a *failure* of justice. It says: *if the warning were real, it would be here already.*

Surah An-Naml has just been describing this — verse 71 has them sneering, "When will this promise come?" — and now, in verse 72, Allah teaches His Messenger ﷺ how to respond. Not with a date. Not with a threat. Not with a rebuke.

With nine words. One of which is a single small verb — *radifa* — that I want you to sit with for the next hour. Because that one verb does something to time, to space, and to the human heart that ordinary language cannot do.

**Here's the hook:** When someone mocks a warning, the natural human reply is to defend the *future*. Allah, instead, redefines the *present*. He doesn't tell them when the thing is coming. He tells them where it *already is*.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *ʿasā* — When Allah Says "Perhaps"

Let me start somewhere strange. Let me start with what looks like a problem.

The ayah begins: *"Qul ʿasā..."* — "Say: **Perhaps**..."

Stop there.

If you're paying attention, this should bother you. Because Allah — the One Who knows the seen and the unseen, the One Who decreed every moment before time began, the One in Whose hand is the Day itself — is telling His Messenger ﷺ to say to people who are demanding certainty: **"Perhaps."**

Why?

Before we go into the ayah, let me build the concept. In Arabic, the word *ʿasā* is what grammarians call a verb of *rajāʾ* — a verb of "hope" or "approximation." When a human being uses it, it expresses uncertainty. *Perhaps it will rain. Perhaps he will come. Perhaps the test went well.* The speaker doesn't know.

But the classical scholars of language faced a question that troubled them deeply: what happens to *ʿasā* when Allah Himself is the speaker? Allah cannot say "perhaps" in the human sense. Allah does not hope. Allah does not approximate. Allah knows.

And so the scholars arrived at a rule that they documented across the tafsir tradition: **when *ʿasā* comes from Allah, it does not mean "maybe." It means "certainly."** It becomes a word of guaranteed occurrence — but dressed in the language of restraint.

Think about that. Allah deliberately chooses a word that, in any other mouth, would signal uncertainty. He puts on the linguistic clothing of "perhaps."

Why?

Let's sit with this. There are two things happening at once.

The **first** is what we might call rhetorical mercy. The mockers asked: *When?* A direct answer — *"It will arrive at this moment, by this measure, on this day"* — would close the door. It would make the warning a sentence already passed. But *ʿasā* keeps the door of *tawbah*, of repentance, open. It says: *the thing is coming, but I am not going to nail it to a calendar your heart can grow numb to.* The veil of "perhaps" is the breathing room for return.

The **second** is more terrifying, and it's where most readers miss the rhetoric. When you say to someone, *"You will be hit by a car at 3:47pm on Tuesday,"* their fear is bounded. They can manage it. They know when to be afraid. But when you say, *"Perhaps you are about to be hit by a car,"* — and you mean it — every footstep, every street, every shadow becomes the moment. The "perhaps" doesn't reduce the threat. **It diffuses the threat into every instant of your remaining life.**

That is what Allah is doing here. The mockers wanted to weaponize the delay. Allah is weaponizing the *uncertainty about* the delay. He is telling them: *fine, you want a "when"? Here is your when. Every moment from now until your last breath is the when.*

Notice something extraordinary: a word that, in human speech, makes the future *less* present, in Allah's speech makes the future *infinitely more* present. Same word. Inverted force.

Here's what's remarkable about this rhetorical move: Allah does not raise His voice. He doesn't say, "It is coming!" He says, "Perhaps it is coming." And by lowering the volume, He raises the pressure.

[PAUSE]

One clean statement before we move on: *The "perhaps" of Allah is not Allah's uncertainty. It is the listener's.*

### Element Two: The Verb *radifa* — The Thing Riding Behind You

Now we come to the load-bearing word of the entire ayah. One verb. Three letters. *Rā-dāl-fā* — *radifa*.

Before I open the word, let me build the world it lives in.

In pre-Islamic Arabia, the camel was not just transportation. The camel was the architecture of survival. And on a journeying camel, there was a particular social arrangement that everyone in that world understood: the *ridf*. The *ridf* was the second rider — the person who mounted *behind* the primary rider, sitting on the camel's haunches, holding onto the rider in front, riding so close that they shared the same animal, the same motion, the same breath of dust.

The *ridf* wasn't a follower at a distance. The *ridf* wasn't someone coming "soon." The *ridf* was *already on the same beast as you.* You couldn't see them — they were behind you. But you could feel their weight. Every shift they made shifted the saddle. They were *behind your back, on your back, sharing your direction at your pace.*

That is the root meaning of *r-d-f*: to mount up behind, to ride pillion, to come immediately following in the same movement.

Now look at the ayah again:

*"Perhaps some of what you are seeking to hasten **has already mounted up behind you**."*

Stay with me here, because the metaphor is doing several things at once.

**First**: the punishment they are asking about is not described as approaching from the horizon. It is not "coming toward you." It is *already on your camel.* It is *already riding behind you.* The distance they assume — the safe distance between themselves and the consequence — is an illusion. There is no distance. The thing is on the same animal.

**Second**: notice that the *ridf* is behind. Out of sight. You cannot see what's riding behind you on a camel without turning your whole body. The deniers are looking forward, scanning the horizon for the punishment, demanding to know when it will appear on the road ahead. And Allah says: *you are looking in the wrong direction. It is not in front of you. It is behind you. It has already boarded.*

**Third** — and this is where the verb's grammar matters — *radifa* is in the **past tense**. *Has mounted.* Not "will mount," not "is mounting." **Has already mounted.** The action is complete. The thing they are asking *"when?"* about is described, grammatically, as having *already begun.*

This is one of the most devastating moves in the Quran. Past-tense language for a future event. Why? Because in the divine perspective, the matter is settled. The boarding has happened. The only thing left is for the rider in front — the heedless one looking at the road ahead — to *notice* what is already sharing his saddle.

[PAUSE]

Let me dwell here, because I want you to feel this and not just understand it.

We tend to live as if the consequences of our choices are far away — somewhere down the road, somewhere on the horizon of "later." We tell ourselves: *I'll deal with that decision later. The repercussion is far. The reckoning is distant.* And Allah, in one verb, dismantles that entire architecture of psychological distance.

He says: the thing you keep deferring is not deferred. *Radifa.* It mounted up behind you the moment you made the choice. It has been riding with you the whole time. You just haven't turned your head.

Notice something deeper still. The *ridf*, in the desert, didn't just ride along. The *ridf* shared the journey. The *ridf* arrived where the front-rider arrived — at the same moment, on the same animal. The destination of the *ridf* was the destination of the rider.

Which means: whatever the deniers are riding *toward* is the very thing the punishment is riding *with* them toward. Their motion forward in heedlessness is also the motion that brings the *ridf* closer to its arrival. **Their own travel is the engine of their own consequence.**

This is not a punishment chasing them. This is a punishment that has been *riding the same camel as them the entire time.*

[PAUSE]

One clean statement: *The thing you fear from the future is often already a passenger of your present.*

### Element Three: The Word *baʿḍu* — "Some Of"

Now I want you to notice something so small it almost slides past the ear. The ayah does not say *"what you are seeking to hasten has drawn close behind you."* It says: *"**some** of what you are seeking to hasten has drawn close behind you."*

*Baʿḍu alladhī tastaʿjilūn.* **Some** of what you are hastening.

This little word — *baʿḍ*, "some" or "a portion of" — is doing two enormous jobs.

Let me teach the concept first. In Arabic, *baʿḍ* explicitly signals partiality. *Some, but not all.* It's a word of partition. When you say *"baʿḍ al-nās"* — "some people" — you are deliberately *excluding* the rest. The word announces that what's named is a subset.

So when Allah says *"some of what you are seeking to hasten has already drawn close behind you,"* He is making a precise theological claim that almost every reader misses:

**The punishment they are asking about is not one thing. It is many things. And only some of them are riding behind. The others are still ahead.**

Sit with what this implies.

There are layers of consequence. There is what arrives in this world — the loss of community, the unraveling of trust, the rot of the heart, the worldly disasters that strike heedless societies, perhaps the eventual defeat of Quraysh at Badr. That is *some.* It is the *ridf* already on the camel.

And then there is everything else. The Day. The Reckoning. The standing. The questioning. The fire. The fixed and final consequence that no amount of looking over the shoulder could survey because it has not yet boarded — it is the final destination toward which both the rider and the *ridf* are traveling.

**By saying "some,"** Allah is doing something stunning: He is letting them know that what's already arrived is the *small portion*. The lesser shadow. The first installment. The thing they will experience in this life — *that's just the down payment on the warning.* The full sum is still to come.

You see what just happened? They mocked: "When will the promise come?" And Allah said: "Part of it is already here. The part you can't see is what's still on its way."

[PAUSE]

But there's a second job *baʿḍ* is doing, and this one is mercy.

If Allah had said, *"All of what you are hastening has drawn close behind you,"* — that would close the door. The full consequence would already be present, and *tawbah*, repentance, would be too late, because the thing has already arrived. The verdict has been carried out.

But "**some**" leaves a gap. Some has come. Some has not. And in that gap between "some" and "all," there is a corridor — a corridor in which return is still possible. The deniers can still turn. They can still see the *ridf* on their camel, recognize what it means, and dismount from the journey that's bringing the full sum closer.

So *baʿḍ* simultaneously **intensifies the threat** (this is only a portion) and **opens the door of mercy** (there is still room to return). It is rebuke and rescue in the same syllable.

One clean statement: *The word "some" tells the mocker that what he feels is the smallest fraction of what awaits — and the largest portion of what may still be averted.*

### Bridge

We have looked at three words. *ʿasā* — the "perhaps" that pressurizes every moment. *Radifa* — the rider already on the camel. *Baʿḍu* — the portion that hints at a fuller weight.

Together, they construct a single architecture: *the consequence is closer than you think, more partial than you think, and more total than you think — all at once.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Illusion of Distance

I want you to think about a feeling we've all had.

You owe someone an apology. You've owed it for months. Every morning, you wake up and the apology is still owed. And every morning, it feels a little less urgent — because it didn't arrive yesterday, so why would it arrive today? The longer you delay, the more the *delay itself* feels like a kind of resolution. You haven't fixed it, but you also haven't been forced to fix it, and over time, the not-fixing starts to feel almost as good as fixing it.

Or take something else. You know there's a medical test you should schedule. You've known for weeks. And each week the test doesn't happen, your fear of the test grows quieter, because the silence of the calendar starts to feel like the silence of the body. *If nothing has gone wrong yet, maybe nothing is wrong.*

This is one of the most reliable mechanisms of the human mind: **we mistake the delay of consequence for the absence of consequence.** We confuse "not yet" with "not real."

The mockers in this surah are not exotic villains. They are doing what every human being does when we don't want to face a thing. They are using the gap between the warning and its arrival as the very reason to dismiss the warning. *If it were going to come, it would have come.*

And the ayah's response is so quietly devastating because it doesn't argue with the assumption. It doesn't say: *"You're wrong, it will come!"* It says: *"You are looking the wrong way. The thing whose absence you take as proof of its non-existence is sitting behind you on the camel you're already riding."*

Here's what I want you to feel: in the desert, the *ridf* — the second rider — was not theoretical. He was felt. You felt his weight in every dune the camel climbed. You felt his shifting whenever you shifted. If you turned your head even slightly, you could see the edge of his shoulder. The only way to not know he was there was to commit, completely, to looking forward.

That is exactly what heedlessness is. Heedlessness is not the absence of evidence. Heedlessness is the *discipline* of looking forward at the road, when the thing you fear is on the saddle behind you.

[PAUSE]

And let me show you why this matters psychologically. The reason the mockers were mocking is that they had successfully constructed a model of reality in which the punishment was *somewhere else*. Somewhere ahead. Somewhere distant. Something they could prepare for, or scoff at, or schedule.

Allah's reply doesn't break their model by pointing at the future. He breaks their model by **collapsing the geometry**. There is no "out there." The thing is "in here." It is not on the horizon — it is on the haunches of the camel you have been riding all morning.

Notice what this does to the question "when?" The question becomes meaningless. You cannot ask "when?" about something that is already with you. You can only ask "have I noticed yet?"

This is the move I want you to carry into your own life. There is a temptation, when we hear about accountability and consequence, to ask *when?* — to negotiate with the timeline, to assume that whatever is going to happen will happen far enough away that we can postpone our response to it.

The ayah's invitation is to retire the question of *when*. The thing is not far. The shift in your saddle is the thing. The unease in your chest at this moment is the thing. The discomfort you feel reading these words is, perhaps, the small lift in the camel's gait as the *ridf* settles in for the journey.

**Practical application**: the next time you find yourself saying about anything — a sin you keep returning to, a duty you keep deferring, a relationship you keep avoiding — "I'll deal with it later," ask: *what if the thing I'm postponing is not in the future at all? What if it's already riding with me? What would it look like to turn my head?*

### Theme Two: Restraint as the Highest Form of Power

Let me ask you something. Imagine you have the power to destroy your enemy in an instant, and your enemy is, at this moment, openly mocking you, demanding that you prove your power by destroying them.

What would most of us do?

We would prove it. We would have to. The mockery itself would be unbearable.

But notice what Allah does — or rather, what Allah deliberately *does not* do — in this ayah.

He does not announce a date. He does not strike. He does not even raise the volume of the warning. He gives the Messenger ﷺ a reply that is, in its surface, almost gentle. *"Perhaps some of what you are seeking to hasten has drawn close behind you."* That's it. That's the response to mockery.

What is that?

I want to suggest that this is a window into something the Quran teaches relentlessly but that we, as creatures of impulse, almost never grasp: **the patience of Allah is not the absence of power. It is the highest exercise of power.**

Think about the Arabic word the mockers use — *tastaʿjilūn*, "you seek to hasten." Its root is *ʿ-j-l*, the same root as *ʿajalah* (haste), *ʿijl* (the calf — named for the speed of its growth). The deniers are framed by this word as people *in a hurry*. People who want speed. People who cannot wait.

And the structural irony of the ayah is breathtaking. The *ones in a hurry* are being told by *the One Who is never in a hurry* that the very thing they are rushing toward is already, quietly, traveling with them — and that He, the One in possession of the full timeline, will not be hurried by their mockery into bringing it sooner.

This is what *ḥilm* — divine forbearance — looks like in action. The mockery does not extract the strike. The pressure does not produce the impatience. Allah's restraint here is not weakness or hesitation. It is the calm of a power so complete that nothing outside it — no taunt, no demand, no clock — can pull at its rhythm.

[PAUSE]

And here is where this becomes a mirror for our own lives.

We live in a world that has trained us to mistake *responsiveness* for *power*. To prove ourselves, we are supposed to react. To answer every insult, return every slight, match every speed with our own speed. The cultural assumption is that if you don't strike back immediately, you are weak.

But look at the Quran's portrait of strength. The mocker shouts: *"When? When? When?"* And the All-Powerful answers, calmly, *"Perhaps already."* And then says no more.

That is the response of someone who does not need the mocker's approval, does not need the mocker's surrender, does not need to prove anything at the speed the mocker demands. **He answers in His own time, with His own measure, and lets the answer do its work.**

There is a station of the heart this points to. The believer who is mocked, attacked, dismissed, told *prove yourself* — that believer is being trained, by ayahs like this one, into a different relationship with urgency. The relationship of *I do not have to answer your speed with my speed. I do not have to defend my Lord on your clock. I can be still. Because the One I trust is not rattled by your taunts, and the consequences you mock are already on their journey — and they will arrive precisely when He has measured.*

**Practical application**: notice, this week, the moments when someone tries to drag you into their speed — to react quickly, to defend on demand, to prove on their schedule. And ask: *what would it look like to answer with the calm of "perhaps"? To respond in my own time, knowing that the unfolding of truth does not depend on the speed of my reply?*

The patience of Allah is not slow. It is **measured**. There is a difference. And learning that difference is one of the quiet works of a lifetime.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what these nine words have done.

The mockers came in with a weapon: *the delay proves nothing is coming.* And Allah's reply did not argue with the delay. It re-located the thing being delayed. The *ridf* — already on the camel. The portion — already arrived. The full weight — still ahead. The "perhaps" — pressurizing every breath. And underneath all of it, the calm sovereignty of the One who refuses to be rushed.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions. The words taught us that what we think is distant is near, and what we think is total is partial. The themes taught us that we are creatures who mistake delay for absence, and that the One who governs delay is not delayed in His knowledge — He is patient in His timing.

It is the same lesson: **proximity that we cannot see, and patience that we cannot rush.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life have I assumed is "far" because it hasn't yet announced itself — and what would it mean to turn my head and check the saddle?

2. Where am I being a *mockingly impatient* one — demanding that Allah prove Himself on my timeline, in my way, by my evidence?

3. What is the *ridf* on my camel right now? What consequence of my choices has been quietly riding with me, weight settling on the haunches of my days, that I have not yet turned to face?

4. When I am pressured to respond at someone else's speed, do I have the inner steadiness to answer like the ayah — calm, measured, not louder than needed?

5. If "some" of what I am owed has already arrived, what does that tell me about the proportion still ahead — and what, if I turned now, could still be averted?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The consequence you keep asking *when?* about is not on the horizon — it is on the same camel you have been riding, riding behind you, and the One who placed it there will not be hurried by your mockery into telling you so.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to fear this ayah. You have to *use* it.

The mockers in the surah heard it and grew harder. But the same ayah, in the heart of a believer, becomes one of the most merciful sentences in the Quran — because it does what no calendar can do. It collapses the lie of distance. It tells you that the moment of return is *now,* because the consequence is *here,* because the camel is *moving,* and because the journey toward the full sum has not yet ended.

You can still dismount. You can still turn. You can still notice.

That is the gift of *baʿḍ* — *some*. That word is the door.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You Who do not need our timelines and are not rattled by our mockery — let me not be among those who mistook Your patience for Your absence. Open my eyes to what already rides with me. Soften my heart to turn before the rest arrives. And let the "perhaps" in Your speech, which pressurizes every moment of the heedless, become for me the mercy that keeps every moment a door of return.
