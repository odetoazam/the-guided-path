# Dust, and the Tales of the Ancients

### An-Naml 67–68

> [ayah:27:67] وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ أَءِذَا كُنَّا تُرَٰبًا وَءَابَآؤُنَآ أَئِنَّا لَمُخْرَجُونَ

> [ayah:27:68] لَقَدْ وُعِدْنَا هَـٰذَا نَحْنُ وَءَابَآؤُنَا مِن قَبْلُ إِنْ هَـٰذَآ إِلَّآ أَسَـٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ

*Qāla lladhīna kafarū a-idhā kunnā turāban wa-ābā'unā a-innā la-mukhrajūn. Laqad wuʿidnā hādhā naḥnu wa-ābā'unā min qablu, in hādhā illā asāṭīru l-awwalīn.*

**"Those who disbelieve say: 'When we have become dust — and our fathers too — will we really be brought out again? We were promised this, we and our fathers, long before. This is nothing but the legends of the ancients.'"**

---

## Introduction: The Room Where This Was Said

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand in the room where these words were spoken.

We are in Mecca. The message has been arriving for years now. And by the time we reach this point in *Sūrat an-Naml*, something has been happening in the surah that most people rush past. Go back a few verses. Look at what Allah has just finished doing. He has just delivered one of the most relentless sequences in the entire Qur'an — a drumbeat of questions, each one beginning with the same word: *Amman* — **"Who is it that…?"**

Who is it that created the heavens and the earth and sends down rain, and We cause to grow gardens of beauty? Who is it that made the earth a stable place and set rivers running through it? Who is it that answers the desperate one when he calls, and removes the harm? Who is it that guides you in the darkness of land and sea?

Question after question. And each one has only one honest answer, an answer so obvious the deniers themselves cannot escape it: *Allah.* Then, at the summit of this sequence, comes the line that everything has been building toward:

**"Is not He who begins creation and then repeats it… better than what you associate with Him?"**

*Who begins creation and then repeats it.* Hold that phrase. Allah has just laid down, as the natural conclusion of everything the deniers already admit, that the One who started this can restart it. Origination, then repetition. It is the whole logic of resurrection, delivered not as a demand but as an obvious inference.

And now — right now, in verse 67 — we hear the answer come back from the crowd.

Notice the moment. This is not a random objection dropped into the surah. This is a **response**. Allah has just said *He begins creation then repeats it*, and the people turn and say: *when we are dust, will we really be brought out?* They are answering the exact claim just made. The surah has set up the logic, and the deniers walk straight into it and reject the one thing they've already conceded the premise for.

Here is what I want you to feel before we go a step further. These are not stupid people. These are not villains twirling their moustaches. These are people who have just heard an argument they cannot refute — *who made you the first time?* — and instead of following it where it leads, they reach for something. They reach for a shield. And the shield they reach for is one every human being has reached for at some point.

Here is the hook. In these two short verses, the deniers make one argument and hide two things. And the deepest wound in what they say is not the mockery you can hear — it's the word they use at the very end to dismiss the whole thing. Because that final word is going to turn around in the very next verse and become the thing that condemns them. They think they're closing the case. They're actually signing it.

Let's find out how.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — The Stutter of Disbelief

I want to teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because if you don't have this, you'll read right over what is arguably the loudest thing in the ayah.

In English, we mostly ask questions to get information. *What time is it? Did you eat?* We're missing something and we want it filled in. But questions can do other work. Think about how you'd say to someone who just told you something outrageous: *"You did* what*?"* You're not asking for information. You already heard them. The question is carrying your shock, your refusal to accept what you just heard. Arabic grammarians named this precisely. They call it *istifhām inkārī* — an interrogative that isn't seeking an answer, it's **denying** the very possibility of what it asks. And a close cousin, *istifhām istibʿād* — a question that pushes the thing away as far-fetched, absurd, beneath consideration.

Now hold that, and listen to what the deniers actually say. Not one question. **Two.** Stacked.

 The verse opens their objection with أَ — the interrogative *hamza* — fused onto إِذَا: **أَءِذَا** *(a-idhā)*, "when…?" And then, before the sentence even finishes, it happens *again*:  **أَئِنَّا** *(a-innā)*, "are we really…?"

<!-- morphology:27:67:6 pos=N --> أَءِذَا كُنَّا تُرَٰبًا …  أَئِنَّا لَمُخْرَجُونَ

Say it out loud. *A-idhā… a-innā.* Feel your mouth. Two glottal stops, two catches in the throat, the hamza breaking the flow twice. **The sentence itself stutters.** Notice something — the sound is doing the work of the meaning. This is not the smooth cadence of someone genuinely wondering. This is the choppy, doubled recoil of someone spitting the idea back out. The tongue trips on the same barrier twice, and each trip is a fresh refusal.

And there's a grammatical reason it's doubled that goes beyond sound. The first hamza attaches to *when* — questioning the **timing**, the circumstance: *when we've become dust?* The second attaches to *inna*, the particle of emphatic certainty, and questions the **outcome**: *are* we *— of all things — the ones to be brought out?* So they're not denying once. They're denying the setup and denying the conclusion. They've closed both doors. It's the difference between saying *"When it rains, will you come?"* and saying *"When* — *it* — *rains? Will* you *come?"* The second one isn't a question. It's a door slamming twice.

<!-- morphology:27:67:8 root=كون pos=V --><!-- morphology:27:67:10 root=ترب pos=N --> And what is it they cannot get past? **كُنَّا تُرَٰبًا** — *"we have become dust."* Turāb. Not ash, not bone — *dust.* The most scattered, the most anonymous, the most utterly-lost form a body can take. Dust has no shape, no name, no edge where one person ends and another begins. Mix a handful of graveyard dust and you cannot tell the king from the beggar. That is the image they hold up as the un-answerable objection: *once we are scattered into namelessness, who could possibly gather that back into* me *again?*

Here is what this single word reveals. Their disbelief is not primarily an argument. It is a *recoil*. The doubled hamza is the sound of a heart that has already decided, dressing itself up as a question. **They are not asking. They are refusing, and calling it asking.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two — The Two Hidden Doers

Now watch something they *don't* say. This is where reading absence matters as much as reading presence.

<!-- morphology:27:67:18 root=خرج pos=N --> Look at the word that ends verse 67: **لَمُخْرَجُونَ** *(la-mukhrajūn)*. The *lam* at the front is emphatic — *surely, really.* And **مُخْرَجُونَ**, from the root *kh-r-j*, "to come out," is not a simple form. It's Form IV — *akhraja*, "to bring out, to extract" — and it's in the **passive**. *Mukhrajūn* means not *"the ones who come out"* but *"the ones who are* brought *out."* Extracted. By someone. The verb demands a doer. The soil doesn't lift itself.

And who is that doer? The verse leaves the space **empty.** They will not put a name in it.

Then look at the very next word, opening verse 68: <!-- morphology:27:68:3 root=وعد pos=V --> **وُعِدْنَا** *(wuʿidnā)* — *"we were promised."* From *w-ʿ-d*, to promise. And again — **passive.** *We were promised.* By whom? The space is empty again. A promise, by definition, has a promiser. And they leave the chair vacant a second time.

Stay with me here, because this is remarkable. In the space of two words — the last of one verse, the first of the next — they use two passive verbs, and *both* of them have Allah as the necessary, invisible subject. Someone will bring them out. Someone made them a promise. And in both cases they perform a small act of grammatical avoidance: **they talk about His raising and His promising without ever letting Him into the sentence.**

Now, I want to be honest about the line between what the grammar says and what we're reading into it. That both verbs are passive — that is documented fact, plain morphology. That the *reason* is avoidance — that is one way to sit with it, an interpretation the structure invites but doesn't prove. So let me offer it as an invitation, not a claim: is this not exactly how the human heart handles a truth it doesn't want to face? We keep the subject vague. We use the passive voice of the soul. *"Mistakes were made." "It's said that…" "Supposedly there's a judgment coming."* We will discuss the thing as long as we never have to name the One standing behind it, because to name Him is to be answerable to Him. You can talk about "the universe" having a plan all day long. The moment you say *Allah* has a plan for you, the conversation has teeth.

The grammar catches them in the act. They cannot describe their own resurrection without brushing right up against the One who will raise them — and they flinch every time. **What this single feature reveals is that even the language of denial cannot escape Him. He is the unspoken subject of the sentence they built to push Him away.** [PAUSE]

### Element Three — The Fathers, and the Trap in the Last Word

Now we come to the word I promised you — the one they think closes the case.

But first, count something with me. How many times do they mention their fathers in these two short verses?  Once in verse 67 — **وَءَابَآؤُنَآ** *(wa-ābā'unā)*, "and our fathers."  And again in verse 68 — **وَءَابَآؤُنَا**, "we and our fathers." Twice. In a two-verse objection, the fathers come up *twice.* That is not incidental. That is the load-bearing wall of their entire position.

Watch how they use them. First: *when we and our fathers are dust, will we be raised?* The fathers are proof of the impossibility — *look how many generations have gone down into the ground and stayed there.* Second: *we and our fathers were promised this before.* Now the fathers are proof of the *staleness* — *this isn't new; our fathers heard the same threat and here we all still are, unraised.* The ancestors are doing double duty: they're the evidence that resurrection can't happen, and the evidence that the warning is old news.

And then comes the verdict, the last four words, the mic drop they think ends it:

<!-- morphology:27:68:12 pos=N --><!-- morphology:27:68:14 pos=P --><!-- morphology:27:68:17 root=سطر pos=N --><!-- morphology:27:68:16 pos=P --> **إِنْ هَٰذَآ إِلَّآ أَسَٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ** — *"This is nothing but the legends of the ancients."*

Look at the structure: *in… illā* — a negation followed by an exception. **Nothing except.** It is the most totalizing dismissal Arabic can build. *There is nothing here at all — except tales.* They don't argue it's *probably* false. They evacuate it entirely and leave only one thing standing: *asāṭīr.*

*Asāṭīr* — from the root *s-ṭ-r*, which means to write in lines, to rule out lines of script. *Asāṭīr* are the things written and lined up by earlier peoples — the old scribbled stories, the myths, the fables. And *al-awwalīn* — from *ʾ-w-l* — means **"the first ones, the former peoples, the ancients."** So the full charge: *this is just the fairy-tales the old-timers used to line up on the page.*

Now — here is the trap. Here is the thing they cannot see even as they say it.

Notice something about the two groups in these verses. They anchor their entire worldview to *al-awwalīn* — the ancestors, the fathers, *the first ones.* Twice they lean on the fathers as their authority: *our fathers, our fathers.* And then, in the same breath, they dismiss revelation because it belongs to… *al-awwalīn.* **The ancients.** The very same category.

Sit with that. They reject the message on the grounds that it's *old* — the tales of the former peoples — while grounding their own certainty in something *older still* — the ways of their fathers. Both hands are reaching backward. They use the ancestors as a pillar and as an insult in the space of two verses, and they never notice they've done it. *Our fathers* are wisdom. *The former peoples* are fools. But it is the same past. It is the same backward-facing gaze. **They have made antiquity a proof when it flatters them and a slur when it convicts them.**

What this final word reveals — and this is the whole engine of these two verses — is that the dismissal *"it's just old stories"* is not a conclusion they reached. It's a category they reached *for.* They didn't examine the claim and find it false. They found a shelf marked "myth" and shoved the claim onto it so they'd never have to open the box.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — When "This Is What We've Always Believed" Becomes a Wall

Let me start somewhere far from the Qur'an, in a room you've probably been in.

You're at a dinner table, and someone raises something — a habit in the family, a way money's always been handled, a grudge that's older than anyone sitting there, a belief everyone just carries. And you ask, honestly, *"but why do we do it this way?"* And the answer comes back, warm and final and completely closed: *"That's just how we've always done it. That's what your grandfather believed. Who are you to question it?"*

Feel what that sentence does. It isn't an argument. You can't argue with it, because it never made a claim — it just made a *wall*. The wall is built out of love, out of belonging, out of the very real fear that if you pull on this one thread, the whole warm blanket of who-we-are might unravel. And so the question dies. Not because it was answered. Because it was made to feel like a betrayal.

That wall has a name in psychology now. We call it the *appeal to tradition*, or its uglier cousin, *conformity bias* — the pull to believe a thing because the people around us and before us believed it. We even have a phrase for its mirror image: *chronological snobbery*, the assumption that whatever's newest is truest. And here's what's uncomfortable: we all think we're immune. We look at the man who says *"my ancestors did it, so it's right"* and we feel superior — until we notice we do the exact same thing, just pointing the other way. *"Nobody intelligent believes that anymore." "We're past that as a society." "It's the twenty-first century."* That's not an argument either. That's *"our fathers"* wearing a lab coat. It's the same wall, built by a different generation.

Now — *here is what these verses say about that.*

The deniers of Mecca reached for the oldest wall there is: *wa-ābā'unā, wa-ābā'unā* — *our fathers, our fathers.* When they could not answer *"who created you the first time?"*, they didn't answer it. They retreated behind the generations. *This is what we and our forefathers have always known.* And the Qur'an does something with this that I find almost merciful in its honesty: it doesn't rage at them. It simply **records** it. *Qāla lladhīna kafarū* — "those who disbelieve said." Allah enters their own words into evidence, exactly as spoken, and lets the words stand. Because the words indict themselves.

Watch the movement in it. A generation ago, the fathers heard this same message and reached for the same wall. Now the sons inherit not the *question* — they never engaged the question — they inherit the *wall.* Disbelief, it turns out, is often not a decision anyone actually made. It's a piece of furniture handed down. Nobody in the room ever sat with *"who made me from nothing?"* They just received, along with the family name and the family land, the family's way of *not asking.*

And this is the quiet horror of it, brought all the way home: you can go your entire life having never once chosen your deepest belief. You can inherit your certainty about the biggest question there is — *is there anything after this?* — the same unexamined way you inherited your accent. You didn't reason your way to it. You absorbed it at the dinner table. And on the Day it matters, *"that's what my family always thought"* will be worth exactly what it was: nothing. A wall with no foundation, in front of a question you were too comfortable to open.

So what does this change tomorrow? Here is the concrete thing. Take one belief you hold about God, about death, about what you're for — one of the big ones — and ask yourself with brutal honesty: *did I ever actually examine this, or did I inherit it and call it mine?* Not to throw it away. Maybe you'll find it's true and you'll now hold it with both hands instead of one. But a belief you've inherited and never examined isn't yet *yours.* It's your father's. And these verses are a warning about the difference.

### Theme Two — The Word "Just," and Everything We Hide Behind It

Let me give you the smallest, most dangerous word in the human language. It's the word *just.*

*"It's just a phase." "It's just a story." "It's just how I am." "Don't take it so seriously, it's just—"*

Watch what that word does. When something arrives at your door with a demand attached — a truth that, if real, would require you to *change* — the mind has a move. It doesn't usually argue the thing is false. That's expensive; it requires engagement. Instead it does something far cheaper and far more effective: it *reclassifies* it. It takes the thing off the shelf marked "things I must respond to" and quietly moves it to the shelf marked "things that are merely interesting" or "things that are just entertainment." And once it's on that shelf, you're free. You never have to deal with it. You didn't refute it. You *relabeled* it, and the label did the work of a refutation without any of the honesty.

We are experts at this. Someone confronts us with something hard about ourselves and we say *"you're being dramatic"* — reclassified, from *valid concern* to *overreaction*, and now we're off the hook. A civilization is confronted with a moral claim it doesn't want to meet and it says *"that's just old-fashioned"* — reclassified, and now the claim is a museum piece instead of a command. The most sophisticated defense the ego has is not the counter-argument. It's the *category shift.* It's the word *just.*

Now — *listen to what the deniers do.*

*In hādhā illā asāṭīru l-awwalīn* — **"this is nothing but the legends of the ancients."** Do you see it? They never engaged the argument they'd just been handed — *He begins creation and then repeats it.* They never said *"here is why that's false."* They performed the category shift. They took a claim that, if true, would restructure their entire lives — that they will be raised, gathered, and *asked* — and they filed it under *asāṭīr.* Fairy-tales. **They didn't defeat the truth. They relabeled it, and let the label do the killing.**

And the terror of it — the reason this isn't just their sin but ours — is how *painless* it is. Reclassifying a truth costs nothing. There's no argument to lose, no evidence to weigh, no sleepless night. You just… smile, and reach for the word *just*, and the demand evaporates. *"It's just religion." "It's just what people needed before science." "It's just a story people tell so they're not afraid of dying."* Each of those is *asāṭīru l-awwalīn* in a modern accent. And each one lets a human being walk past the single most important question of their existence without breaking stride, feeling not troubled but *clever.*

Let a paradox sit here, because I don't want to resolve it too fast: the most dangerous response to truth is not hatred. Hatred at least takes the thing seriously. The most dangerous response is the *shrug.* The person who hates the message is still in the room with it. The person who has filed it under *just a story* has left the building and locked the door, and he feels lighter for it.

So what does this change tomorrow? Here is the concrete thing, and it's uncomfortable. Catch yourself using the word *just* about something that scares you. *"It's just—"* — stop there. Ask: *am I answering this, or am I filing it away so I don't have to?* When a truth about your death, your accountability, your purpose comes near, notice whether your first move is to *engage* it or to *relabel* it. Because relabeling feels like thinking, and it is the exact opposite of thinking. It is the mind's way of running while sitting perfectly still.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture: A Verse Answered Before and After It Was Spoken

Now step back and look at where these two verses *sit*, because the surah has built something around them that they never saw coming.

Remember where we started — verse 64, just before: **"Is not He who begins creation and then repeats it…?"** That is the argument. Origination proves the possibility of repetition. The deniers' whole objection in 67 — *when we're dust, will we be raised?* — is answered *before they even open their mouths.* The premise they can't refute is already on the table: you were dust once before, and Someone made you. The impossibility they're leaning on was dismantled a sentence earlier.

That's the wall behind them. Now look at the wall in front of them — the very next verse, 69. They have just sneered, *"this is nothing but the legends of the ancients."* And Allah's response comes immediately:

**"Say: travel through the earth, then see how was the end of the criminals."**

Feel the precision of that. They said *asāṭīru l-awwalīn* — *tales of the former peoples.* And the answer is: *then go walk among the former peoples.* You call it old stories? The "stories" left **ruins.** The ancients you dismiss as fiction are lying in the ground you can walk across. *Go look.* The reply to *"it's just a myth"* is not another argument — it's an address. It's *"here is the museum; the exhibits are real; the doors are open; go."*

Now see the architecture whole. The deniers' objection is a single point — and it is enclosed on *both* sides. Before it: *the One who began you can repeat you.* After it: *the "tales" you dismiss left corpses in the earth — go verify.* Their objection didn't land in open air. It landed inside a sentence already spoken and a sentence about to be spoken, both of which answer it completely. **They thought they were closing a case. They were speaking into a trap that had already sprung.** That is not accident. That is design. The mountain was worth the climb because from this height you can see: the denial was surrounded before it was uttered.

### The Two Roads Were the Same Road

And now watch the linguistic discoveries and the themes fold into one thing.

The grammar showed us **two hidden doers** — the passives *mukhrajūn* and *wuʿidnā*, where they discussed His raising and His promising without ever naming Him. The theme showed us **the shrug** — reclassifying a demanding truth into a harmless one. These are the same move. To keep the Subject out of the sentence *is* to file Him under *just a concept.* Grammar and psychology are describing one act from two angles: the refusal to let the living God become the *You* you are answerable to.

And the grammar showed us the **fathers, twice** — antiquity as pillar. The theme showed us **inherited belief** — the wall you never chose. Same move again. To lean on *"our fathers"* while sneering at *"the ancients"* is to worship the past selectively, to bow to whichever tradition lets you keep sitting still.

Where we started: a crowd that thought it had a knock-down objection. Where we've arrived: two verses that reveal the objection was never an argument at all — it was a recoil, dressed as a question, propped up on inherited walls, and sealed with a label. The whole thing is the anatomy of how a heart says *no* to what it already knows is *yes.*

### Questions to Carry

Not rhetorical. Sit with these.

1. Which of your deepest beliefs — about God, about death, about what you're living for — did you actually *examine*, and which did you simply *inherit* and never open?
2. When a hard truth comes near you, what is your first move — do you engage it, or do you reach for the word *"just"* and file it away?
3. Where in your life are you keeping God as an *unnamed subject* — talking around Him, about "the universe," about "something bigger" — precisely so you never have to be answerable to *Him*?
4. If you were told *"travel and see the evidence,"* is there a truth you're avoiding not because you've looked and found it wanting, but because you've made sure never to look?

### One Sentence to Carry

**Disbelief is rarely a conclusion the mind reaches; it is usually a shelf the heart reaches for — so it never has to open the box.**

### The Invitation

Here is what I'd invite you to carry out of these two verses. Not fear — *honesty.* The people in verse 67 weren't destroyed by an argument they lost. They were undone by a question they refused to open. And the whole mercy of the Qur'an bringing us into that room is so that we, reading it, might do the one thing they wouldn't: *open the box.* Take the biggest claim — that you were made, that you'll be raised, that you'll be asked — and, just once, don't shove it onto the shelf marked *just.* Sit with it as though it might be true. That single act of not-flinching is the whole difference between the crowd in verse 67 and a heart that's alive.

### A Du'ā

O Allah, You who begin creation and then repeat it — do not let us be among those who talk around You so as never to face You. Save us from the shelf where we hide the truths that frighten us, and give us the courage to open the box and find You there. And when the earth gives up what it holds, gather us — the scattered dust of us — not among those who called Your promise a tale, but among those who waited for it with open hands. *Āmīn.*
