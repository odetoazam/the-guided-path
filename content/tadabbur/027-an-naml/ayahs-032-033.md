# The Queen Who Refused to Decide Alone

## Introduction — A Throne Room, A Letter, A Pause

Let me set the scene.

A letter has arrived. Not delivered by a courier, not stamped by a known kingdom. A bird carried it. A *hoopoe*. And the letter is sealed with words no one in this court has ever read before:

> **"In the name of Allah, the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful."**

The Queen of Sheba is holding it. Her name in tradition is Bilqis. She rules the southern Arabian kingdom of Saba — a kingdom Allah elsewhere calls **"a goodly land"** with two gardens, right and left (Saba' 15). Her people worship the sun. She herself sits on a throne the Quran describes as **ʿarshun ʿaẓīm** — a magnificent throne (27:23).

She is not a weak ruler. She is not a fool. She is, by every measure the world recognizes, *powerful*.

And a letter has just arrived telling her, essentially: *Submit. Come as muslims.*

[PAUSE]

Now — put yourself in her chair for a moment. You have armies. You have chiefs. You have a throne. And a foreign king is summoning you to surrender — but he is summoning you in the Name of a God you do not worship.

What do you do?

The instinct of power is to **assert**. To not look weak. To gather your generals and declare war, or to declare scorn, or to declare anything — because rulers who hesitate look like rulers who can be replaced.

But watch what Bilqis does. Watch carefully. Because what Allah preserves in these two ayahs — embedded in a pagan court, spoken by a sun-worshipping queen — is one of the most stunning portraits of mature leadership in the entire Quran.

She turns to her chiefs and she says:

> **"O chiefs, advise me in this matter of mine. I have never decided any matter until you bear witness with me."**

And they answer her:

> **"We are possessors of strength and possessors of severe might — but the command is yours. Consider what you will command."**

Two ayahs. A queen and her council. A question and an answer.

And inside this exchange is something so refined about how power should hold itself that Allah chose to preserve it — *before* she had even accepted Islam.

[PAUSE]

Here's the hook. Stay with me.

Bilqis has not yet become a believer. She is, at this moment in the surah, still the queen of a sun-worshipping kingdom. And yet Allah quotes her with approval. He gives her words a kind of grammatical weight that mirrors how He elsewhere describes the believers themselves.

Why?

Because there is something she is doing in *how she holds her power* that Allah wants us to see — that He wants kings and parents and managers and imams and presidents and committee chairs and anyone who has ever held a sliver of authority over another human being to see.

We're going to spend the next hour unpacking what that is.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Aftūnī* — "Give me a fatwa"

The first thing Bilqis says to her chiefs is a word that should make you sit up.

She says: **أَفْتُونِى فِى أَمْرِى** — *aftūnī fī amrī*.

Most translations render this as "advise me in my matter." That's accurate but flat. It misses what's happening.

Let's slow down.

The word *aftūnī* comes from the root **ف-ت-ي** (*f-t-y*). This is the same root from which we get *fatwā*. When a person asks a scholar *aftinī fī...* — "give me a *fatwā* on..." — they are not asking for a casual opinion. They are not asking, "Hey, what do you think?" They are asking for a considered, weighty, *binding-in-feel* judgment on a matter that has consequences.

Let me give you the everyday version of this distinction so you feel it.

There is a difference between asking your friend "what do you think of this email?" and asking your lawyer "do I have a case?" The first is opinion. The second is *fatwā*. The first you can ignore. The second changes what you do tomorrow.

The root *f-t-y* in classical Arabic carries a connection to *fatā* — a youthful, vigorous person — because a *fatwā* is meant to be a *fresh ruling, alive to the present moment*, not a recycled formula. To ask for a fatwā is to ask for someone's most considered judgment on a present, real, particular case.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Bilqis is doing.

She is the queen. She holds the power. She does not need her chiefs' permission. She could simply *decide*. She could announce. She could declare.

Instead, she turns to them and uses a word that places them, momentarily, in the position of *muftī* — the one whose ruling is sought. She elevates them. She treats their judgment as *weighty*.

Notice something. She doesn't say "tell me your opinions" — *qūlū ārāʾakum*. She doesn't say "what do you suggest" — *māḏā tarawn*. She uses a word with a *juridical* feel. A word that signals: *I am bringing this to you as something requiring real weight, not chitchat.*

Here's what's remarkable.

A leader who is genuinely powerful — who *knows* she is powerful — is the one who can afford to ask this way. Insecure power asserts. Secure power consults. And the deeper the consultation, the deeper the security.

[PAUSE]

And then she adds: **فِى أَمْرِى** — *fī amrī* — "in *my* matter."

She does not depersonalize the question. She does not say "in the matter of the kingdom" or "in the matter of Sulayman." She owns it. *My* matter. *I* am the one in front of this decision. The weight is *on me*.

So in one short phrase she has done two things at once that almost never coexist in human authority:

1. She has **elevated her chiefs** — *aftūnī*, give me your binding judgment.
2. She has **kept the weight on herself** — *fī amrī*, this is my matter.

This is what mature leadership sounds like. Not "you decide for me" — that's abdication. Not "I'll decide alone" — that's tyranny. But: *I will decide, and I need your weight to decide well.*

What this invites us to consider is that Allah is teaching us — through the mouth of a queen who is not yet Muslim — that the *shape* of how we ask for counsel reveals whether we actually intend to receive it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Mā Kuntu Qāṭiʿatan Amran* — "I have never been a cutter of matters"

Now look at the next sentence she speaks. It is one of the most psychologically precise self-descriptions any ruler has ever given.

> **مَا كُنتُ قَاطِعَةً أَمْرًا حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ**

"I have never been *qāṭiʿatan amran* until you bear witness with me."

The word at the center is **قَاطِعَة** — *qāṭiʿa*. From the root **ق-ط-ع** (*q-ṭ-ʿ*). Let me teach this root before we look at what she is saying about herself.

*Qaṭaʿa* in Arabic means *to cut*. A *qāṭiʿ* is a cutter. The root is used for:

- Cutting a rope (*qaṭaʿa al-ḥabl*)
- Cutting a journey short (*qaṭaʿa al-safar*)
- Cutting off relations (*qaṭīʿat al-raḥim* — severing kinship)
- A decisive, irrevocable proof (*dalīl qaṭʿī*)

The image is one of *severance*. A clean break. A finality. When you *qaṭaʿa* something, you sever it from what came before. There is no going back.

Now — here is the genius of how Arabic uses this word for *decision-making*.

When you make a decision, what are you doing? You are *cutting off* all the other possibilities you could have chosen. To decide is to *kill the alternatives*. The English word "decide" comes from the Latin *de-caedere* — *to cut off*. The same metaphor lives in *homicide*, *suicide*, *genocide* — all forms of *cutting off life*.

So when Bilqis says *mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amran* — "I have never been a *cutter* of any matter" — she is using a word that contains, inside its very letters, the *violence of premature decision*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. This is the door.

A decision is a small act of *killing*. You are killing all the futures that could have unfolded from the paths you didn't take. And the more powerful you are, the more futures you kill with a single word. A queen's "yes" kills a thousand "no"s. A queen's "war" kills a thousand peaces.

What Bilqis is saying is: *I have never been quick to cut.*

Notice the form she uses: **مَا كُنتُ** — *mā kuntu*. This is past continuous negation. "I was never in the habit of..." It is not "I am not going to" — that would be situational. It is **a description of her settled disposition**. Her *character*. Her *track record*.

She is telling her court, and Allah is telling us: this queen is, *by long habit*, slow to cut.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Look at how she pairs this with witness.

**حَتَّىٰ تَشْهَدُونِ** — "until you bear witness with me."

The word **تَشْهَدُونِ** is from **ش-ه-د** (*sh-h-d*). The root of *shahāda*. To witness, to testify, to be present-with.

She does not say "until you *advise* me" — *tushīrūna ʿalayya*.
She does not say "until you *agree* with me" — *tuwāfiqūnanī*.

She says: *until you bear witness with me.*

This is the language of *court*. Of *standing-with*. A witness is one who *was there*, who *saw*, who *will be present at the moment of judgment to verify what happened*. To say "I never cut a matter until you bear witness with me" is to say: *I never act so that I am the only one carrying the weight of what I did.*

Why does this matter? Because power tempts every ruler to become *the lone bearer*. The one who decided. The one whose name is on it. And when things go wrong, the one whose neck is on the line — alone.

Bilqis refuses this trap. Not out of cowardice. Out of *wisdom*. She knows that decisions made alone become *prisons* — because once you have decided alone, you have no one to think alongside you when reality turns out differently than you expected. The lone decider becomes the lone defender of a decision that may have been wrong.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is something Allah teaches in another place, to His own Prophet ﷺ:

> **وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِى ٱلْأَمْرِ** — "And consult them in the matter." (Aal-Imran 3:159)

The Prophet ﷺ — who received revelation — was *commanded* to consult. Why? Because *shūrā* is not for getting information you lacked. The Prophet ﷺ did not lack information. *Shūrā is for making sure the weight of a decision is held by the community that will carry its consequences.*

Bilqis, pagan queen of Saba, somehow already knew this. Allah preserves her words because the principle is older than her, older than Sulayman, older than any kingdom. It is woven into how human authority is *meant* to function.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Wal-Amru Ilayki* — Power Returned

Now flip to the chiefs' response. Watch what they do.

> **قَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أُو۟لُوا۟ قُوَّةٍ وَأُو۟لُوا۟ بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ وَٱلْأَمْرُ إِلَيْكِ فَٱنظُرِى مَاذَا تَأْمُرِينَ**

"We are *ulū quwwah* — possessors of strength — and *ulū baʾsin shadīd* — possessors of severe might. But the command is yours. Consider what you will command."

Three pieces here. Each one doing work.

**Piece one:** *ulū quwwah* — possessors of strength. The root **ق-و-ي** (*q-w-y*) is raw strength, capacity, force. They are saying: *we have the army. We have the men. We have the resources.*

**Piece two:** *ulū baʾsin shadīd* — possessors of *severe might*. Now this is different. *Baʾs* from **ب-أ-س** (*b-ʾ-s*) is not just strength — it is the strength *as experienced by the enemy*. It is the *fearsomeness* of a force. The word is used in the Quran for war (*al-baʾs* = combat) and for the dread that an opponent feels in the face of overwhelming force. *Shadīd* intensifies it: *severe, intense, unrelenting*.

So they are telling her two things in two phrases:
- We have the *capacity* (*quwwah*).
- We are *terrifying to face* (*baʾs shadīd*).

In other words: *we could win this war. Easily.*

[PAUSE]

Now — this is where the absence speaks.

Notice what they do *not* say.

They do not say: *so let us fight*.
They do not say: *send us, we will crush him*.
They do not say: *we recommend war*.

In a court of generals presented with a foreign king's demand for submission, the expected next words are *let us go to war*. That is what hawks do. That is what military advisors do across every kingdom in every age.

Their response is the *opposite*. They lay out their capacity — and then they hand it back.

**وَٱلْأَمْرُ إِلَيْكِ** — "And the command is yours."

Look at the grammar. *Al-amr* — the command, the matter, the affair. *Ilayki* — *to you*, second person feminine. The preposition *ilā* means *toward, returning to*. They are *returning* the *amr* to her.

It is as though they say: *Yes, we have the army. Yes, we could fight. But — the army is not the answer. The answer is your judgment. We are tools. You are the one who decides what tools to use.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what is stunning, and I want you to feel the weight of this.

A council of generals who have just declared they are *possessors of severe might* immediately, in the same breath, **subordinates that might to the queen's discernment**.

This is not weakness. This is the *opposite of weakness*. It is power that knows its place.

Strength without judgment is destruction. The chiefs of Saba — pagans, by the way — somehow understood that their *quwwah* and their *baʾs* were only as valuable as the *amr* they served. And the *amr* belonged to the one who had asked for *fatwā*, not to the ones who held the swords.

[PAUSE]

And then the closing phrase:

**فَٱنظُرِى مَاذَا تَأْمُرِينَ** — "So *look*, what will you command?"

The verb *unẓurī* — *look* — is from **ن-ظ-ر** (*n-ẓ-r*). Not just *see*. Not just *glance*. *Look carefully. Consider. Examine.* In Arabic, *naẓar* is the word for *contemplation*, for *reasoned consideration*. A *nāẓir* is a thoughtful observer.

So they tell her: *look carefully. Take your time. Consider. We are not rushing you. The military option is on the table — but only if you, after looking, command it.*

What this invites us to consider is the picture Allah is painting:

A queen who asks for *fatwā* and refuses to *cut*.
A council that *has* might and yet *returns* the decision.
And between them, the space — the *naẓar*, the considered looking — where wisdom can actually happen.

[PAUSE]

### The Sonic Texture

Before we leave the linguistic journey, listen to the *sound* of what's happening.

When Bilqis speaks — *aftūnī fī amrī, mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amran ḥattā tashhadūn* — there is a softness to the vowels. The repeated *ī* sounds. The flowing *m*s and *n*s. The closing *ūn* that draws her chiefs *into* her sentence.

When the chiefs speak — *naḥnu ulū quwwah wa ulū baʾsin shadīd* — there is a *thickening*. The hard *q* of *quwwah*. The compressed *ʾ* of *baʾs*. The intensifier *shadīd* with its doubled *d*. The mouth has to work harder. The sounds are armored.

And then — listen — the *softening* at the end. *Wal-amru ilayki* — flowing again. *Fa-nẓurī māḏā taʾmurīn* — the closing rhyme matches *tashhadūn* from her speech. They literally *return* to her cadence.

The sound carries the meaning. The chiefs' speech *hardens* when they describe their might, then *softens* back into her own music when they return the *amr*. The phonetics enact the politics: hardness offered, then dissolved into deference.

This is the texture of a mature court.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Person Who Decides Alone Is Already Lost

Let me start somewhere far from the ayah.

Most of us, when we picture *strong leadership*, picture a single figure in a quiet room making a hard call. The image of the lonely commander. The chief who *bears the weight alone*. The CEO who has to decide and live with it. Truman with the sign on his desk: *the buck stops here*.

There is something noble about this image. It captures something real — that ultimately, *somebody has to decide*, and that somebody carries the consequence.

But there's a shadow inside this image. The shadow is *isolation as identity*. The leader who comes to believe that being-alone-in-the-decision is what makes them a leader. The leader who stops asking *because asking would look weak*. The leader who, over time, becomes surrounded by people who have learned that their job is to confirm, not to advise.

[PAUSE]

You see this in companies. You see this in families. You see this in masjids. You see it in governments. A person rises into authority, and at some point along the way, they cross a line — and after that line, they no longer ask in a way that genuinely invites disagreement.

Their meetings become performances. Their councils become rubber stamps. Their advisors learn the shape of their preferences and feed it back. And the leader begins to make decisions in a hall of mirrors, mistaking the echo for verification.

When the decision turns out to be wrong, the leader has no one to think with. Because no one ever really got to think.

[PAUSE]

This is what Bilqis refuses.

When she says *mā kuntu qāṭiʿatan amran ḥattā tashhadūn*, she is describing not just a procedure but a *self*. She is telling her chiefs: *I have constructed myself, over years, as a queen who does not cut alone.* Which means — and this is the part we have to feel — she has constructed her court as a court in which *they have permission to actually disagree with her*.

Because if her chiefs knew that *aftūnī* was a performance — that whatever she actually wanted was what they were supposed to say — they would never have responded the way they did. They would have either said "fight!" to flatter her supposed strength, or "submit!" to flatter her supposed wisdom. They would have read her face for cues and given her back her own reflection.

Instead they did something almost unimaginable in a royal court: they declared their own power, and then *they handed it back*.

This only happens in a court where the queen has, over many small decisions, *proven* that asking is not a trap. That advisors who say uncomfortable things will not be punished. That the buck *stops with her* but the *thinking* doesn't.

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ built this kind of court.

After the loss at Uḥud — a loss that came partly because the Prophet ﷺ followed the council of the younger Companions who wanted to march out and meet the Quraysh outside Medina, against his own initial preference to stay and defend — Allah revealed:

> **وَشَاوِرْهُمْ فِى ٱلْأَمْرِ فَإِذَا عَزَمْتَ فَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ**
> "And consult them in the matter. Then when you have resolved, place your trust in Allah." (3:159)

Notice the structure. *Consult — then resolve — then trust.* The Prophet ﷺ was being told: keep consulting, even when consultation has just cost you a battle. Because the alternative — the lone decider — costs more in the long run than any single defeat ever could.

This is what Bilqis intuited as a pagan queen and what Allah commanded explicitly to the seal of the prophets ﷺ.

[PAUSE]

So ask yourself: in your own small kingdom — your team, your home, your project, your marriage — *do you decide alone?*

Not "are you the one with the final say" — that's fine, somebody has to be.

But: *when you ask for input, do you actually receive it? Or have you constructed a court of mirrors?*

The way to test: do people around you ever say "no" to you in ways that actually change what you do? When was the last time someone advised you against something and you *thanked them for it and changed course*?

If you cannot remember, you are not yet Bilqis. You are still the kind of ruler she refused to be.

And here is the practical movement: *the only way to build a court that will tell you the truth is to spend years rewarding people for telling you the truth, especially when it is the truth you didn't want to hear.* There is no shortcut. You build it slowly, decision by decision, *thank-you* by *thank-you*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Power That Knows Its Place

Now turn the camera. Look at the chiefs.

These are men with armies. *Ulū quwwah, ulū baʾsin shadīd*. They have spent their lives on horseback. They understand the calculus of force. They could, in twenty minutes around a map, explain exactly how to crush Sulayman's borderlands.

And when their queen turns to them — gives them an opening, asks for *fatwā*, places her decision on their counsel — what do they say?

They tell her *what they have*. And then they *hand it back*.

[PAUSE]

I want you to feel how rare this is.

Most people, when they are *consulted from above*, do one of two things. Either they *advocate* — they push for whatever expands their own role, their own department, their own importance. The military advisor pushes for war. The lawyer pushes for litigation. The marketer pushes for a campaign. Because in every consultation, what gets done validates *who got consulted*.

Or, they *defer entirely* — they say "whatever you think is best, your majesty" — which sounds humble but is actually a form of *withholding*. It refuses to share the weight. It puts the whole decision back on the leader and leaves the advisor with deniability.

The chiefs of Saba do neither.

They name their capacity *honestly* — we have the strength, we have the might, we are not bluffing — and then they *subordinate* it to the queen's *naẓar*, her considered looking. They are saying: *here is what we have to offer. Use it or don't. The decision needs to come from the seat that can see the whole picture, not from us who can only see our piece.*

This is power that *knows its place*.

[PAUSE]

There is a name for the failure mode they avoid. In English, we sometimes call it *the man with a hammer problem* — to a man with a hammer, every problem looks like a nail. To a general, every problem looks like a war. To a lawyer, every problem looks like a lawsuit. To a marketer, every problem looks like a campaign.

The chiefs of Saba had every reason to be men with hammers. War was their nail. They had *just been told* a foreign king was demanding submission. The frame *war* was practically pre-loaded into their mouths.

But they did not say *war*. They said: *the command is yours.*

[PAUSE]

What does this look like in your life?

You are a possessor of something. You have *quwwah* of some kind. Maybe it is expertise. Maybe it is money. Maybe it is voice. Maybe it is the trust of someone who listens to you. Maybe it is the loyalty of children who would follow you anywhere.

When you are *consulted* — when your spouse asks you, when your boss asks you, when your friend asks you, when your community asks you — *do you advocate for your hammer? Or do you name your capacity honestly and hand the decision back to the one who can see what you cannot?*

Most of us advocate. Most of us, when asked, use the consultation to push for what serves us. We dress it up as advice but it is sales.

The mature thing — the *Saba-chiefs thing* — is to say:

> *Here is what I can do. Here is the strength I have. But the decision is bigger than me. You can see what I cannot. So tell me what you want me to do, and I will do it.*

This is the posture of a soldier. The posture of a wife. The posture of a husband. The posture of an employee. The posture of a child to a parent and, sometimes, of a parent to a child. The posture of a citizen to a community.

It does not abandon your power. *Naḥnu ulū quwwah* — they *named* it. But it *places* your power inside something larger.

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ said: *"The strong one is not the one who overpowers people. The strong one is the one who controls himself when angry."* (Bukhari)

The chiefs of Saba demonstrate a sibling of this truth: *the strong one is not the one who pushes his strength forward whenever consulted. The strong one is the one who can name his strength and then place it under another's discernment.*

The practical movement here: next time you are asked for input, *try the Saba-chiefs structure*. Name your capacity. Name what you could do. *And then return the decision*. Say it out loud: *"but the call is yours."* Notice how different the conversation becomes when you stop advocating and start serving.

You will discover, over time, that you are trusted more — *because you were not selling*. People come back to advisors who refuse to push their own hammer. They stop coming back to advisors who always do.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Look at these two ayahs together now.

Notice the *symmetry* Allah has built.

**She** speaks. She asks for *fatwā*. She declines to cut. She invites their *witness*.
**They** speak. They name their *quwwah*. They return the *amr*. They invite her *naẓar*.

Her speech *elevates them*. Their speech *returns to her*. The motion in the passage is a *circle of deference*. Each side honoring the other. Each side refusing to grab what was theirs to grab.

And inside that circle — *that's where the decision will be made*. Not in her alone. Not in them alone. But in the space *between them* that her humility and their humility together create.

This is the geometry of *shūrā*. Allah preserved it inside a pagan court so that no Muslim could ever say *shūrā is a technique we invented*. No. *Shūrā is built into how human authority is supposed to work*, recognizable even to those who have not yet recognized the One who built it.

And it is precisely this *quality of soul* in Bilqis that prepares her, just a few ayahs later, to recognize the truth when she sees it. When she enters Sulayman's palace and sees the floor of glass that she mistakes for water — when she realizes her perception has been corrected — she says:

> **"My Lord, indeed I have wronged myself, and I submit with Sulayman to Allah, Lord of the worlds."** (27:44)

She does not resist. She does not double down. She *adjusts*. Because she had already, *as a queen*, built the muscle of not-cutting-too-fast. The same muscle that made her a good ruler made her, at the decisive moment, a *muslima*.

This is the deepest secret of the passage. *How you hold power on small matters is how you will hold yourself in front of the truth on the largest matter.* Bilqis was rehearsed in humility by the daily practice of consultation. So when revelation arrived, her soul already knew how to bow.

### Questions to Carry

- When you ask the people around you for advice, are you asking for *fatwā* — or are you asking for confirmation?
- When was the last time someone advised you against something you wanted, and you actually changed course?
- What is your *quwwah*? And when you are consulted, do you push your hammer — or do you name your capacity and return the decision?
- Is there a *matter* in your life right now that you are about to *cut* alone, when you should be calling your council together?
- Who in your life has earned the right to be your *shahīd* — to bear witness with you in your decisions? And who have you not yet honored that way?

### One-Sentence Distillation

A queen too wise to decide alone, and a council too mature to seize what she offered them — together they show what authority looks like when it knows that strength serves discernment, not the other way around.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need a kingdom for this to apply to you. You have a small kingdom already. A team. A family. A class. A circle. *Inside that kingdom, what kind of ruler are you?*

The invitation is not to abdicate. Bilqis did not abdicate. The *amr* remained *hers*. The invitation is to *hold your decisions like she held hers* — with your hand open enough that those around you can place their weight beside yours, and with your eyes considering enough that you *look* before you *cut*.

And — this is the harder half — if you are ever the one consulted, *be the chiefs of Saba*. Name what you have. Return the decision. Refuse to push your hammer. Trust that the seat above you can see what you cannot.

The world is not short of people who decide alone. The world is not short of advisors who advocate for themselves. The world is starving for *circles of deference* — for queens who ask, and chiefs who return.

Build one. Even if it is small. Even if it is only with one other person.
