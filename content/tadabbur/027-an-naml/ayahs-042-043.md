# The Throne She Almost Recognized

## Surah An-Naml, Ayahs 42–43

### Arabic

> فَلَمَّا جَآءَتْ قِيلَ أَهَٰكَذَا عَرْشُكِ ۖ قَالَتْ كَأَنَّهُۥ هُوَ ۚ وَأُوتِينَا ٱلْعِلْمَ مِن قَبْلِهَا وَكُنَّا مُسْلِمِينَ
>
> وَصَدَّهَا مَا كَانَت تَّعْبُدُ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ إِنَّهَا كَانَتْ مِن قَوْمٍ كَٰفِرِينَ

### Translation

> So when she arrived, it was said: *"Is your throne like this?"*
>
> She said: *"It is as though it were the very one."*
>
> *And we were given knowledge before her, and we were already in submission.*
>
> And what she used to worship besides Allah had diverted her. Indeed, she was from a disbelieving people.

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open these two ayahs, let's stand inside the moment.

A queen has been traveling. Not a queen of any small kingdom — the Quran has already told us about her in this surah. She had **a magnificent throne** (*ʿarshun ʿaẓīm*). She ruled a sophisticated civilization. She was politically shrewd — when Sulayman's letter arrived, she didn't lash out. She convened her council. She tested the messenger with gifts before committing to anything. She is, by every measure of the world, a person of standing.

And now she has chosen to come. She has decided that whatever this Sulayman is, he is not someone to fight; he is someone to investigate.

But before she arrives, something extraordinary happens behind the scenes. Sulayman has her throne — *her* throne, the seat of her sovereignty — transported across enormous distance and brought to his palace. Then he gives an instruction: *"Disguise her throne for her; we shall see whether she is guided or among those who are not guided."*

Stay with that for a moment. The throne is not displayed in triumph. It is **disguised**. *Nakkirū lahā ʿarshahā* — make it unrecognizable to her. Alter it just enough.

Then she arrives.

She walks into the court of Sulayman. She has prepared herself for diplomacy, for rivalry, perhaps for spectacle. She has not prepared herself for **her own throne, sitting there, slightly wrong**.

[PAUSE]

This is the moment our two ayahs open into.

And here is what we have to understand before we can hear what is being asked of her: this is not a riddle about a chair. The throne, in this surah, has been functioning as a symbol the entire time. It is the symbol of what she sits upon. The seat of her certainty. The source of her authority. The thing she returns to when the world becomes unstable.

Sulayman, by Allah's permission, has reached into her world and lifted up the very thing she trusts most — and placed it in front of her, altered.

And then asked her one question.

### The Hook

Watch what she says when the question comes. Watch the *exact* word she chooses. Because in Arabic, she does not say *"yes"* and she does not say *"no."* She says something far stranger — something that, if we do not slow down and look carefully, we will read right past.

She says a word that has built into it the quiet admission: *I almost recognize this. But not quite.*

And then, without a pause, before she has even finished speaking, **someone else's voice cuts in** — and tells us something about *them* that the queen herself has not yet earned.

Two voices in one ayah. One question. One almost-answer. And buried in the grammar — a diagnosis of what stands between any human being and the truth they are trying to reach.

Let's go inside.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element 1: *Ka-annahu huwa* — The Grammar of Almost

Before we touch her words, let's teach the structure first.

In Arabic, when you want to say *"it is X,"* the simplest way is to put the two nouns side by side: *huwa kitābun* — "it is a book." Direct. Identifying. No hesitation in the grammar.

If you want to say *"it is like X,"* you reach for one of the comparison particles. You can use *mithl* — "like" — or you can use the prefix *ka* — "as," "like." *Kal-kitāb* — "like a book." This is comparison, similitude, *tashbīh*. The thing is being likened to something else. It is not the thing itself.

There is a third construction. A more interesting one. You take *ka* — "as" — and you fuse it with *anna* — "indeed, that" — to form *ka-anna*. *Ka-anna* is what the grammarians call a particle of *tashbīh*. It does not just say *"like."* It says something closer to **"as if it were"** or **"as though it were."** It carries a heightened, almost contemplative quality of comparison. You use it when something *resembles* something else so closely that you are reaching for the language of *as-if-ness*.

Everyday example. A father walks into a kitchen. His teenage son is standing there with his back turned, just slightly taller than last week. For one half-second, the father almost says, *"Oh — sorry, I thought you were your uncle."* He doesn't say *"you are your uncle."* He doesn't say *"you look like your uncle."* He says **"it was as though you were him."** *Ka-annaka huwa.* That construction reaches for the sense of an identification that almost happened, that was *almost* certain, but that the speaker is just barely holding back from completing.

Now hold that, and listen to what she says.

Sulayman's people ask her: *a-hākadhā ʿarshuki?* — "Is your throne like this?"

She replies: **قَالَتْ كَأَنَّهُۥ هُوَ** — *qālat ka-annahu huwa.*

Notice something. She does *not* say *naʿam* — "yes." She does not say *huwa huwa* — "it is it." She does not even say *yushbihuhu* — "it resembles it." She reaches for **the most contemplative particle of comparison Arabic has** — *ka-anna* — and she pairs it with the bare pronoun *huwa* — "him," "it" — with no qualifier, no descriptor, no "like the one I had."

*Ka-annahu huwa.*

The most accurate English we have is something like: **"It is as though it were the very one."**

Stay with this. What is she actually doing in that moment?

She is standing in front of an object that has been deliberately altered. She has been asked a yes-or-no question. And she is refusing to give a yes-or-no answer. She will not commit to *"yes, this is mine"* — because something is off, and her intelligence is too sharp to ignore it. She will not commit to *"no, this is not mine"* — because the resemblance is too profound, the recognition too deep, to dismiss.

So she lands in the precise grammatical structure that means **"my recognition is real, but my certainty is suspended."**

Here's what's remarkable. The classical scholars noticed this. Some — like Al-Rāzī — read her response as evidence of extraordinary discernment: she will not be tricked, she will not be flattered into agreement, she holds her judgment in suspension when the evidence is incomplete. Other scholars read her caution as the very mark of a soul approaching truth — someone whose habit is not to leap to conclusions but to hold the question open until the question can be answered properly.

These are not competing readings. They are the same reality from two angles. The composite picture: **she is a sovereign of the intellect. She does not concede ground she has not verified. And precisely *because* she does not concede ground she has not verified, she is — without yet knowing it — already on the path.**

Notice what *ka-anna* does that *yes* could never do. *Yes* would have closed the moment. *No* would have closed it differently. *Ka-annahu huwa* keeps the moment **open**. She is leaving a door ajar.

[PAUSE]

This is the Quran giving us a verbal photograph of a soul on the threshold of recognition. Not yet across. Not yet able to say *huwa huwa*. But standing there — *ka-anna* — at the precise hinge where intellectual honesty becomes spiritual readiness.

One word. *Ka-annahu.* And in it, an entire psychology of the seeking soul.

### Element 2: The Voice That Interrupts — *Wa ūtīnā al-ʿilma min qablihā*

Now here is something that, the first time you see it, you may need to read three times to be sure you are reading it correctly.

She has just spoken. *Qālat ka-annahu huwa* — "She said, 'It is as though it were the very one.'"

The very next words in the ayah are:

> **وَأُوتِينَا ٱلْعِلْمَ مِن قَبْلِهَا وَكُنَّا مُسْلِمِينَ**
>
> *wa ūtīnā al-ʿilma min qablihā wa kunnā muslimīn*
>
> *"And we were given knowledge before her, and we were already in submission."*

Whose voice is this?

Let's teach the device first. In Arabic rhetoric there is a phenomenon called *iltifāt* — literally "the turn." It refers to a sudden shift in grammatical person within the same passage. The speaker turns. The "he" becomes "you." The "they" becomes "we." The voice pivots, and the listener has to pivot with it.

Here, the voice has shifted. She was just speaking. The grammatical "I" was hers. And then — *wa ūtīnā* — "and **we** were given." A first-person plural that is no longer her.

The classical commentators offer different camera angles on this *we*. Some read it as the voice of Sulayman and his people: *we* — Sulayman and the believers in his court — *were given knowledge before her, and we were already in submission to Allah, before she arrived at this moment.* Others read it as a quiet aside in the verse — the believing voice in the scene declaring its own state. Either way, the structural fact is the same: **someone else's voice has cut in**, and that voice has named two things that the queen, in this exact moment, does not yet possess.

Layer them like a composite sketch. The voice says:

1. *We were given knowledge* — *al-ʿilm*, the definite knowledge, with *alif-lām* — not "some knowledge" but **the** knowledge.
2. *Before her* — *min qablihā*. There is a precedence claimed. A timing established.
3. *And we had already been muslimīn* — already in a state of *islām*, of submission, before this moment ever arrived.

Now look at what is happening structurally. The queen has been given a test of recognition. She has *almost* recognized her throne. Her intellect is intact. Her honesty is intact. Her caution is intact. But standing right next to her — invisible to her, audible only to us, the readers — is a voice that says: *what she is about to step toward, we have already been standing in.*

Stay with the asymmetry. She is being shown her own throne, slightly altered, and asked to recognize it. They — Sulayman and his people — are already in submission to the One who *placed thrones in the world to begin with*. She is testing whether this object is hers. They have already settled the question of to Whom they themselves belong.

Here's what's remarkable about the placement. The Quran could have given us this information earlier. Allah could have said, *"And Sulayman and his people had been muslimīn from before."* That would be biographical context. Instead, this declaration is **embedded inside her moment of almost-recognition**. The two are placed side by side, in the same breath of the same ayah, with no transition.

What this invites us to consider is that her *ka-annahu huwa* — her *almost* — is being measured against an *already*. Her near-recognition of a physical throne is being framed against their settled submission to the Lord of every throne. The ayah is showing us, in a single grammatical movement, **the gap she has yet to cross**.

And notice this. The voice does not boast. It does not say *we are better*. It states a fact — *we were given knowledge*. *Ūtīnā* is a passive verb. **They were given.** The knowledge is not theirs by achievement; it is theirs by gift. The boast that could have been there is grammatically absent. They are not claiming superiority of effort. They are acknowledging precedence of grace.

[PAUSE]

So the placement of this voice does something we should not miss. It establishes — *for the listener, before she has even finished her sentence* — that what she is approaching is not a competition between civilizations. It is not a contest of thrones. It is one human being standing on the edge of a knowledge that others, by the gift of Allah, were already standing in.

Two voices. One ayah. The almost, and the already.

### Element 3: *Ṣaddahā* — The Grammar of Diversion

Now we move to ayah 43, and to a single word that diagnoses the entire situation.

**وَصَدَّهَا مَا كَانَت تَّعْبُدُ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ**

*wa ṣaddahā mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni-llāh*

*"And what she used to worship besides Allah had diverted her."*

Let's teach the verb first. The root *ṣ-d-d* in classical Arabic carries a cluster of meanings that the lexicons document carefully. To turn aside. To divert. To block. To prevent. *Ṣadd* is what a wall does to a flood. *Ṣadd* is what a sealed door does to a guest. *Ṣadd* is what a barrier does to a path. The verb is used elsewhere in the Quran for those who *yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli-llāh* — who turn people away from the path of Allah.

Now look at the construction here. The verb is in the past tense. *Ṣaddahā* — "it diverted her." The subject of the verb — the *thing* that did the diverting — is *mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni-llāh* — "what she used to worship besides Allah."

Notice something. The subject of *ṣaddahā* is **not Bilqis herself.** It is what she was worshipping.

Look how this is grammatically arranged. She is the *object* of *ṣadd*. The thing that diverted her is the agent. **In Arabic grammar, in this sentence, she is the one being acted upon.** She is, in a precise sense, **the one who has been turned away** — turned away from a path she might otherwise have walked, by an object of worship that intervened between her and that path.

This is one of the most precise diagnostic statements in the Quran about the mechanism of *shirk*.

Stay with what is being said. She is intelligent. She is honest. She is cautious in her judgments — we just saw *ka-annahu huwa* prove that. She is the kind of person who, given clear sight, would walk straight to truth.

So why has she not?

The Quran gives us the answer in one verb. **Something diverted her.** Not stupidity. Not malice. Not weak character. Something stood between her and the path. Something blocked her. *Ṣaddahā.*

And the *something* is named: *mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni-llāh* — "what she used to worship besides Allah."

Now look at the verb form *kānat taʿbudu*. This is *kāna* in the past tense plus *taʿbudu* in the present-tense form. Arabic uses this construction to express **continuous, habitual past action**. Not "what she once worshipped." Not "what she worshipped last Tuesday." But **what she had been habitually worshipping** — *the long arc of her worship life* — *the practice she had been embedded in.* The grammar slows the action down and stretches it across the whole biography of her soul before this moment.

Here's what's remarkable. The Quran does not say *kafarat* — "she disbelieved." It does not put the verb on her. It puts the verb on **what she was worshipping.** *That* is what blocked her. *That* is what diverted her. The grammatical structure is doing pastoral work — it is naming the obstruction without flattening her into the obstruction.

Now connect this to the previous ayah. Why was the test of the throne so precise? Because the throne is the symbol of *the thing she was sitting upon*. The test was designed to confront her with the question: *what is it that you actually trust? What is it that you actually rest upon?* The disguised throne is a parable — staged before her — of the very condition the next ayah will diagnose. The thing she relied upon was **not quite what she thought it was.**

She was diverted. Not by lack of intelligence. Not by lack of integrity. By **what she had been habitually worshipping**, which placed itself between her and the path.

[PAUSE]

And now look at the closing of ayah 43:

**إِنَّهَا كَانَتْ مِن قَوْمٍ كَٰفِرِينَ**

*innahā kānat min qawmin kāfirīn* — "Indeed, she was from a disbelieving people."

Notice the preposition. *Min qawm* — "from a people." She is not described as *al-kāfira* — "the disbelieving woman." She is described as *min qawmin kāfirīn* — *from* a disbelieving people. The grammar locates her **inside a context, not as the context itself**. She inherited a worship-environment. She was embedded in a community whose worship had drifted from the One. The diversion was not invented by her; it was the air she had been breathing.

And now look back at the *ṣaddahā* with this in view. **She was diverted by what she worshipped, and what she worshipped was inherited from the people she was from.** This is one of the most merciful diagnoses in the Quran. It does not dismiss her responsibility — she will, in the next verse, take that responsibility — but it names with surgical precision *where the obstruction came from.*

The intellect was hers. The intellect was working. The honesty was hers. The honesty was working. *Ka-annahu huwa* showed us both.

But standing between that working intellect and the truth was **a habituated worship inherited from a people**. And until that obstruction was named and removed, no amount of intellect was going to get her across.

### The Bridge

We have stood with three things.

*Ka-annahu huwa* — the grammar of almost. A soul refusing to commit to a yes it cannot verify and a no it cannot honestly give. The hinge of the seeking heart.

The interrupting voice — *wa ūtīnā al-ʿilma min qablihā wa kunnā muslimīn* — the unboasting reminder that what she approaches as a discovery, others were already standing in by gift.

*Ṣaddahā* — the diagnostic verb. Not "she failed." Not "she chose wrongly." But *something diverted her* — and that something was named with precision.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the journey of any soul moving toward the truth.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme 1: Why Intellect Alone Will Not Get You There

Think of someone you know who is brilliant. Someone whose mind is sharp. Who reads. Who debates. Who can dismantle a weak argument in three sentences.

Now think of how often that same person, in the matter of God, in the matter of meaning, in the matter of the one thing that would actually anchor a life — is *stuck*.

You have seen this. Maybe you *are* this. The intellect is not the problem. The intellect is functioning. The honesty is functioning. The caution is functioning. So why doesn't it move?

This ayah is doing something that almost no other passage does so cleanly. **It separates two things that we often confuse.** It separates *the working of the intellect* from *the obstruction of worship*.

Bilqis's mind is working perfectly. *Ka-annahu huwa* is the proof. She will not be flattered. She will not be manipulated. She will not commit to what she has not verified. By any standard of clear thinking, she is doing the right thing in that moment.

And yet — *wa ṣaddahā mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni-llāh*. Something has been diverting her. Not her thinking. Her *worshipping*.

Here is what this opens up. The soul does not move toward Allah on the strength of its intellect alone. The soul moves toward Allah by the orientation of its worship. **What you orient toward is what pulls you.** The direction of your worship is the direction of your motion. And if what you have been habitually worshipping — whether an idol of stone, or an idol of approval, or an idol of comfort, or an idol of the self — is pulling sideways, then no amount of intellectual horsepower will get you forward.

You can be *the smartest person in the room* and still be *ṣuddat* — diverted. Because the diversion does not happen at the level of thought. It happens at the level of *what you bow to*.

Stay with this. The verb the Quran uses is not "she misunderstood." It is "she was diverted." A person can be diverted by something they have not even examined. **What we worship habitually has a gravitational field, and it operates whether we are looking at it or not.** That is precisely why it is dangerous — and precisely why the Quran names it as the obstruction.

Now look at how the throne test was constructed. Sulayman did not stage a debate. He did not commission a treatise. He arranged an *encounter* — with the very symbol of what she sat upon, slightly altered. The test was designed not to inform her intellect but to **dislodge her from her seat**. To make the thing she trusted suddenly unfamiliar. To expose the gap between what she sat upon and what she should be sitting upon.

And it worked — *because the throne was not the point.* The throne was the lever. The point was: *what is it, actually, that you are resting upon? And is it as solid as you have been assuming?*

Here is the practical movement.

Tonight, before you sleep, do not ask yourself, *"What do I believe?"* Your beliefs may be fine. Your intellect may be working. Ask yourself instead: *"What have I been habitually worshipping?"* — meaning, what is the orientation that pulls me when no one is watching? What do I treat, in practice, as the thing I cannot lose? When my comfort is threatened, what do I run to? When my reputation is threatened, what do I defend? When my certainty is shaken, what do I sit back down upon?

Whatever that thing is — *that* is the candidate for *mā kānat taʿbudu min dūni-llāh* in your life. That is the thing that, while your intellect is functioning beautifully, is quietly turning the wheel sideways.

The Quran is not asking you to be smarter. **It is asking you to look at what you have been bowing to without noticing.**

### Theme 2: Submission Before Recognition

There is a sentence in the middle of these two ayahs that, if you let it, will rearrange the order in which you have been thinking about faith your whole life.

*Wa ūtīnā al-ʿilma min qablihā wa kunnā muslimīn.*

*"And we were given knowledge before her, and we were already in submission."*

Read that again. *We were given knowledge — and we were already in submission.* In the original Arabic, *muslimīn* — those who are in *islām*, in submission — sits at the end. It is the final word of the clause. It is the destination toward which the sentence moves.

Now here is what is strange, and what we usually miss. In the modern story we tell ourselves about faith, the sequence runs: **first I understand, then I submit.** First the proof, then the prostration. First I figure it out, then I commit.

This sentence reverses the order.

It does not say *we became muslimīn because we were given knowledge.* It says *we were given knowledge — and we were muslimīn.* The two are paired, but the *muslim* state is not described as a *consequence* of the knowledge. It is described as **already present**. *Kunnā muslimīn* — "we *were* muslimīn." The verb *kāna* sets the state in the past, established, prior.

And then — this is the part the Arabic does that the English cannot — the very next verse opens with the diagnosis of *her* state, in which the obstruction is named not as ignorance but as *what she was worshipping*. The contrast is structural. *Their* state was: knowledge given, submission already in place. *Her* state was: intellect working, worship misaligned.

What this reveals is that *islām* — submission — is not something you earn at the end of an intellectual journey. It is, in a deep sense, the **posture that makes the journey possible at all**. The scholars have a way of saying this: knowledge does not produce submission; submission opens the soul to receive knowledge. The order matters.

Think of it this way. Imagine you are trying to fill a cup with water. The water is the knowledge. The hand of submission is what holds the cup upright. If the cup is tipped sideways — if the orientation is wrong — you can pour a river into it and almost none will stay. The water of knowledge cannot fill a vessel that is not held in the orientation of receiving.

This is what was happening to Bilqis. The water of recognition was being poured. The throne was right there. The signs were all around her. But the cup was tipped sideways by *what she had been worshipping*. **Until that orientation was corrected, the water could not fill her.**

And look — within just one more ayah after these two, she will see the courtyard, mistake it for water, lift her dress, encounter the truth of it, and say:

*Rabbi innī ẓalamtu nafsī wa aslamtu maʿa Sulaymāna lillāhi rabbi-l-ʿālamīn.*

*"My Lord, I have wronged my own soul, and I submit, with Sulayman, to Allah, the Lord of the worlds."*

Notice the order in her own confession. **Acknowledgment of wrong first. Then *aslamtu* — I submit.** She does not say *"I now understand, therefore I submit."* She says *"I have wronged my soul, and I submit."* She corrects the orientation. *Then* the knowledge that was being poured can finally be received.

[PAUSE]

Here is the practical movement, and it may be uncomfortable.

If you are stuck in the matter of faith — if the intellect is firing and nothing is moving in your heart — the question to ask is not *what else do I need to learn?* The question is *what am I refusing to submit to?* Not in some grand theological sense. In a small, daily, specific sense.

Is there a command of Allah you have been intellectually circling for years, refusing to obey until you "fully understand"? The gift of *kunnā muslimīn* is that those who are already in submission *receive the understanding inside the submission, not before it.* The cup must be upright before the water can fill it.

Sometimes the way forward is not another book. It is the *fātiḥah* prayed with full presence tomorrow morning. It is the small act of obedience that has been on hold for a year. It is the lowering of one's gaze in a single moment when the eye wanted to roam. *That* is the cup being held upright.

And then — and only then — does what has been pouring all along begin to actually fill it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Look at what Allah has done in these two ayahs, side by side.

**Ayah 42** stages the *almost* — *ka-annahu huwa* — the soul on the threshold, with a working intellect and a still-misaligned worship. And it places, right next to that almost, the voice of those who were *already* in submission — *kunnā muslimīn*. The geometry is precise: her almost-recognition of a throne is set against their already-submission to the Lord of all thrones.

**Ayah 43** then diagnoses *why* she could only get to *almost*. *Ṣaddahā* — she was diverted. By what? By *what she had been worshipping besides Allah*. The obstruction is named not as a defect of mind but as a misorientation of worship.

So the two ayahs together form a single architecture. Ayah 42 *shows* the almost. Ayah 43 *names* what kept it as almost. The first is the symptom — caught with grammatical precision in one word. The second is the diagnosis — caught with grammatical precision in another.

And here is the proof that the architecture is intentional: the very next verse — 44 — gives us the resolution. The crossing. The confession. The water finally filling the cup. *I have wronged my own soul, and I submit.* The structure is *almost → diagnosis → submission → recognition*. In four ayahs, the entire arc of a soul.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed us *ka-annahu huwa* — a perfectly working intellect arriving at *almost*. The theme of intellect-and-worship explained *why* a perfectly working intellect cannot get past *almost* if the worship is misaligned.

The grammar showed us *kunnā muslimīn* placed before *al-ʿilm* arrives at her — submission preceding the gift of knowledge. The theme of submission-before-recognition explained *why* the order matters: the cup must be upright before the water can fill it.

The grammar showed us *ṣaddahā* — she was *diverted*, by something. The theme named what that something is for any one of us today: not a stone idol, but whatever orientation pulls our worship sideways without our examined consent.

### Questions to Carry

1. *What in my life have I been intellectually circling for years, refusing to obey until I "fully understand"? What if the understanding is on the other side of the obedience?*

2. *When the world is unstable, what do I sit back down upon? What is the throne in my life that, if Allah disguised it tomorrow, I would notice was almost — but not quite — what I thought?*

3. *Where is my own ka-annahu huwa? Where am I standing right now, almost recognizing a truth, refusing to commit because some inherited orientation is whispering, "not quite, not yet"?*

4. *What is the mā kānat taʿbudu in my life — the habitual orientation I have not even examined, that is quietly turning my path sideways?*

5. *Have I confused the working of my intellect with the readiness of my heart?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

A working intellect can carry you to *almost* — but only the correction of what you worship can carry you across.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this reflection asking yourself, *"What do I now believe?"* That is the question the modern world trained you to ask, and Bilqis shows us it is the wrong starting place.

Leave it asking, instead — *"What have I been bowing to, without noticing, that is keeping me at almost?"*

And then, tomorrow, in one small specific act — pray a prayer with full presence, lower the gaze in one moment, give the charity that has been waiting, perform the obedience that has been on hold — **hold the cup of your soul upright**. Not as a final answer. As an opening. As a turning. As an *aslamtu*.

The water has been pouring the whole time.

### Du'a

And pour into us, while we are upright before You, the knowledge by which we know You — until our own ka-annahu huwa becomes the steadying huwa-l-ḥaqq, and we say with her, "My Lord, I have wronged my soul, and I submit, with the believers, to You, the Lord of the worlds." Āmīn.*
