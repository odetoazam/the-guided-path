# The Question That Refuses to Look Away

## Surah An-Naml, Ayah 55

> [ayah:27:55] أَئِنَّكُمْ لَتَأْتُونَ ٱلرِّجَالَ شَهْوَةً مِّن دُونِ ٱلنِّسَآءِ ۚ بَلْ أَنتُمْ قَوْمٌ تَجْهَلُونَ

*A-innakum la-taʾtūna ar-rijāla shahwatan min dūni an-nisāʾ. Bal antum qawmun tajhalūn.*

**"Do you really approach men with desire instead of women? You are a people behaving ignorantly."**

---

## Introduction: The Surah of Seeing and Not Seeing

Before we open this ayah, we have to walk into the surah it lives in.

An-Naml — "The Ants" — is a surah obsessed with one question: **who sees and who refuses to see**. It opens with the words *hudan wa bushrā lil-muʾminīn* — guidance and good news for those who believe. It moves through Mūsā at the burning bush, where a man sees a fire in the distance and discovers it is light. It moves through Sulaymān, who understands the speech of ants and the speech of a bird, who reads a queen's letter and reads her people's idolatry beneath it. Sulaymān is a prophet of *perception* — he sees what others miss.

And then, in the middle of this gallery, we meet Lūṭ.

His people, the surah will tell us in the next ayah, respond to his preaching with one of the strangest sentences in the Quran: *"Expel the family of Lūṭ from your town — they are people who try to stay pure."* They literally use cleanliness as an accusation. Purity becomes the crime.

[PAUSE]

So when we arrive at ayah 55, we need to understand what kind of moment this is. Lūṭ is not introducing his people to a new piece of information. They are not standing there saying, *"We had no idea this was wrong."* They live in a society where the prophet's own family is being threatened with exile *because they refuse to participate*. The wrongness is already known. It is already organized into a social system that punishes those who name it.

Into this — Allah records a single sentence from Lūṭ. Not a sermon. Not a lecture. A question. One question, followed by one diagnosis.

**Here is what's stunning.** Allah does not give Lūṭ a long argument. He gives him a question that the people cannot answer — and a single word at the end that explains everything about how a community ends up here.

That word is *tajhalūn*. And by the time we get to it, we will see that it does not mean what most translations say it means.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to walk through three things in this ayah. A particle. A noun. And a verb. Each one, by itself, would justify a reflection. Together, they reveal the surgical precision of how Allah records prophetic speech.

### Element One: The Hamza That Refuses to Let You Look Away

The ayah opens with two letters that English cannot translate without losing them: **أَ** — the *hamza* of interrogation — attached to **إِنَّ** — a particle of emphasis.

Before we touch this ayah, let's understand what these particles do in Arabic, separately and together.

In Arabic, a question can be asked many ways. You can ask with *hal* — a neutral *"is it the case that...?"* You can ask with *a-* — the *hamza* — which is sharper, more pointed, more confrontational. And you can intensify either of them by adding *inna*, the particle of certainty.

Here's the everyday parallel. In English, there is a difference between:
- "Do you do this?"
- "Are you doing this?"
- *"Are you really doing this?"*
- *"Are you actually telling me you do this?"*

Each one tightens the screw. The last form is what grammarians call a rhetorical question — what classical Arabic calls *istifhām inkārī*, an interrogative not of inquiry but of denial. You are not asking because you don't know. You are asking because the listener needs to hear themselves answer.

Now look at what Lūṭ says: *a-innakum la-taʾtūna...*

**Three layers of emphasis stacked.** The questioning *hamza*. Then *inna*, "indeed." Then *la-*, the emphatic prefix on the verb — "you really do." A literal English approximation would not be "Do you approach men?" It would be something like: *"Is it truly, actually, really the case that you genuinely do this?"*

Let's sit with this.

Why does Lūṭ phrase it this way? Why three layers of emphasis on a question whose answer is obviously yes?

[PAUSE]

Because **the people are not denying it**. They are doing it openly. They have built it into the social fabric so completely that they will soon try to expel anyone who refuses to participate. So Lūṭ is not asking for information. He is doing something far harder — he is forcing them to *hear themselves admit it*.

This is what a rhetorical question does in Arabic when used by a prophet. It is a mirror. It takes the listener's behavior, holds it in front of their face, and says: *do you see this? Do you actually see what you are doing?*

Notice something. Lūṭ is not the one accusing. The construction makes *them* the ones who would have to confirm or deny. He has shifted the moral weight from his shoulders to theirs. He is not the angry preacher. He is the witness who keeps repeating the question until the answer becomes unbearable.

Here's what is remarkable about this. **A community can keep doing something wrong as long as it never has to name it aloud.** Silence is what gives sin its architecture. The moment someone forces the community to articulate what they are doing — *are you really, actually, genuinely doing this?* — the structure trembles. Not because new information has arrived. Because old information has been forced into the light.

This is what the triple-emphasis *hamza* does. It does not introduce a new fact. It collapses the gap between *doing* and *saying you do it*.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word That Is Missing

Now let me show you something you cannot see in any English translation.

The ayah says: *la-taʾtūna ar-rijāla shahwatan min dūni an-nisāʾ.*

The verb is *taʾtūna* — from the root *ʾ-t-y*, which means *to come to, to approach*. This is a careful word. The Quran uses it across many contexts: coming to a place, approaching a sign, coming to a person.

But the object of this approach is *ar-rijāl* — "the men." Definite. With the article *al-*. Not "men" generally. *The* men.

And what is *shahwa*? It is the noun form of desire, appetite, craving — a word documented across the Quran. The Quran uses it for any drive that pulls the self toward gratification, often when that drive has been allowed to lead.

So Lūṭ says: *you approach the men, in desire, instead of the women.*

**Now stay with me.** Look at the structure. He gives them *shahwa* — desire — when he speaks of how they approach men. But when he speaks of women, he does not say *"instead of women, in desire"* — he just says *"instead of the women."*

The word *shahwa* is mentioned once. Attached to one side of the comparison. Not the other.

Why?

This is what classical rhetoricians call asymmetric specification. Allah, in recording Lūṭ's speech, has placed a word on one side of a scale and deliberately left the other side bare. We are meant to feel the imbalance.

Listen to what this does. Lūṭ is not saying, *"You should redirect your desire from men to women."* That would be a sentence about object choice. He is saying something far more cutting: *"What you have done is taken what was meant to be a covenanted, ordered, sanctified meeting between man and woman — and you have stripped it down to bare appetite, then directed even that appetite away from where it was meant to flow."*

The asymmetry of *shahwa* tells us what the people have lost. Not just a partner. **They have lost the entire framework in which desire was supposed to live.** Desire was meant to be one element in a larger architecture — companionship, responsibility, lineage, the building of a household, the inheritance of children. By isolating *shahwa* and detaching it from that architecture, they have not just disordered their sexuality. They have flattened the human encounter into pure consumption.

[PAUSE]

The word *shahwa* sits in the ayah like a single brick lifted out of a wall. Once you remove it from its proper place and make it the whole structure, the wall is gone. There is no wall left. There is only the brick.

Notice something else. The phrase is *min dūni an-nisāʾ* — *instead of, in place of, to the exclusion of the women*. The particle *min dūni* in Arabic does not just mean "instead of." It carries the sense of *bypassing, going around, going beyond*. It is the same construction the Quran uses in *min dūni Allāh* — "besides Allah," meaning *bypassing Allah*.

So buried inside this ayah is a structural parallel. The deepest sin in the Quran — bypassing Allah for other objects of worship — is mirrored here in a smaller key: bypassing the natural order for another object of desire. The same word. The same architecture of substitution. **Going around what was placed in front of you to seek what was placed behind.**

This is one of those moments where the Quran's vocabulary does theological work without announcing it. The word *dūn* tells you that what is being described is not a different choice within a permitted range. It is a *bypassing* — a stepping around something that was meant to be approached directly.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Tajhalūn* — The Verb That Almost No Translation Renders Correctly

Now we come to the word at the end of the ayah, and this is where almost every English translation flattens something Allah made precise.

The ayah ends: *bal antum qawmun tajhalūn.*

Most translations: *"You are an ignorant people."*

This is technically not wrong. But it is severely incomplete. And the incompleteness is the whole point.

Let me show you the difference.

In Arabic, there is a word for being uninformed: *jāhil* in one of its senses can mean *not knowing*. But there is another, much heavier use of the root *j-h-l* — and the classical lexicons are explicit about this. *Jahl* in its primary classical sense is not the absence of knowledge. It is **the behavior of someone who acts as if they do not know — whether or not they do.** It is the opposite, in classical Arabic, not of *ʿilm* (knowledge), but of *ḥilm* — forbearance, self-mastery, the steadiness of someone who acts according to what they know.

The pre-Islamic Arabs called their era *al-Jāhiliyya* — not because they had no information. They had poetry, lineage, navigation, trade routes, oral memory that could recite ten thousand lines. They were called *jāhiliyya* because they *acted without self-mastery* — driven by tribal pride, blood vengeance, appetite, the immediate impulse.

So when Lūṭ ends with *bal antum qawmun tajhalūn*, he is not saying: *"You don't have the information."*

He is saying something far more devastating: **"You are a people who behave as though you do not know — even though you do."**

[PAUSE]

This is why the verb is in the present-continuous form. Not *jahiltum* — "you became ignorant," past tense. *Tajhalūn* — *you are, ongoing, behaving as ignorant ones*. It is a description of present-tense character, not a description of a knowledge state.

And look at the particle that introduces it: *bal*. In Arabic, *bal* is a particle of *idrāb* — a particle that contradicts what came before or escalates beyond it. The closest English equivalent is *"no, rather"* or *"on the contrary."*

Lūṭ has asked the question. The question itself was an indictment. And now *bal* introduces something that goes even further. He is not just saying *"you do this."* He is saying *"and the deeper truth — the diagnosis underneath the behavior — is this: you are a people whose entire mode of being is willful unknowing."*

This is what makes the ayah surgical.

Lūṭ does not say *fāsiqūn* — corrupted ones. He does not say *kāfirūn* — concealers of truth. He does not even say *mujrimūn* — criminals. He says *tajhalūn* — and this single word does work that those other words could not do.

Because what he is naming is the engine beneath the behavior. **You did not arrive here by being uninformed. You arrived here by treating what you know as though you do not know it.** You have made a community-level decision to act *as if*. As if this is fine. As if there is no inner voice telling you otherwise. As if the prophet standing in front of you is the problem and not the mirror.

[PAUSE — major]

Bringing the three elements together: a stacked rhetorical question that forces the people to hear themselves. A noun — *shahwa* — placed asymmetrically to expose what has been stripped from the human encounter. And a final verb — *tajhalūn* — that names the deepest layer: not that they don't know, but that they have organized their society around pretending they don't know.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about what happens to a heart.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Difference Between Not Knowing and Refusing to Know

Think for a moment about your own experience of doing something you knew was wrong.

Not someone else's wrong. Yours. Something small, even. The conversation you should not have had. The look you should not have taken. The lie that came out of your mouth because the truth was inconvenient. The moment you swiped past a notification you should have answered.

In every one of those moments, there is a strange thing that happens inside us. A voice rises — small, often, but distinct — and says *you know this isn't right*. And what we do next is one of the most fateful acts in the human interior.

We have a choice. We can let that voice speak and adjust. Or we can do something subtler. We can pretend we did not hear it. Then, next time, we can pretend a little harder. And next time, harder still. Until, eventually, the voice has not gone away — it has just been buried beneath so many layers of *acting-as-though-we-did-not-hear* that we genuinely believe we cannot hear it anymore.

This is what classical scholars call *ṭams al-fiṭra* — the gradual smothering of the inborn moral instinct. The fiṭra is never destroyed. It is *covered*. Each act of pretending-not-to-know is another layer of cloth thrown over a candle that still burns underneath.

[PAUSE]

This is *jahl* in its true Quranic sense.

And here is what should arrest us: Lūṭ does not say *you are individually ignorant*. He says *qawmun tajhalūn* — *a people* who behave-as-if-they-don't-know. The plural. The collective. Because here is one of the most uncomfortable truths the Quran teaches us about communities:

**A community can perform unknowing as a group.** It can build entire institutions — language, laws, customs, jokes, slogans — whose purpose is to make sure no individual member ever has to face the knowledge they all share.

Look at how this works in your own life. Have you ever sat in a room where everyone knows something is wrong — but no one says it? That silence is not random. That silence is being actively maintained by every person present. The community has agreed, without anyone saying so, that the knowledge will stay buried.

This is what Lūṭ's people had achieved. Not ignorance — *organized unknowing*.

And his question is the first crack in the wall. *A-innakum la-taʾtūna...* — *are you really doing this?* It is a sentence designed to force the unspoken into speech. Because once a thing is spoken, the community-level pretense begins to break.

This is why every prophet, in some form, ends up asking the question their people had decided never to ask.

[PAUSE]

What does this change for us, tomorrow?

It means we have to take seriously the small voice. The one that rises when we are about to do something we know we should not. We do not have a *jahl* problem because we lack information. We live in the most information-saturated moment in human history. We have a *jahl* problem because we have practiced not-listening for so long that we have forgotten the voice is still speaking.

The practical application is simple and difficult. **When the voice rises, pause for three seconds.** Just three seconds. Long enough to register: *yes, I heard that.* Long enough to refuse the act of pretending you did not hear. Even if you go on to do the thing anyway — refuse the pretense. Because the pretense is what builds the wall.

The act may be forgivable. The pretense is what hardens into character.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Compassion Hidden Inside a Confrontation

There is a question we have not yet asked.

If this ayah is so sharp — a triple-stacked rhetorical question, an asymmetric exposure, a diagnosis of willful unknowing — why does Lūṭ speak this way? Why does Allah record his speech in this form?

Because — and this is one of the most counterintuitive truths in the Quranic vision of prophethood — *this is what mercy sounds like when it has run out of softer options*.

Let me explain.

In modern speech, we often confuse compassion with comfort. We think the loving thing to say is the thing that does not disturb the listener. We have come to believe that the language of mercy is the language of affirmation — *you're fine, you're loved exactly as you are, no adjustment needed*.

But this is not the Quranic picture of mercy. The Quranic picture of mercy is a prophet who keeps speaking when staying silent would be easier. A prophet who delivers the uncomfortable sentence because his people's eternal welfare matters more to him than his own social standing.

Notice what Lūṭ is risking by speaking this way. He lives among these people. He has children. He has, the Quran will tell us in other places, daughters whose safety he will soon have to bargain for. He is not safe in this community. Every word of confrontation is a word that increases his exposure.

He could have softened it. He could have spoken in generalities. He could have hinted, gestured, suggested. He chose to ask the unaskable question with three layers of emphasis.

**Why?**

Because softening it would not have served them. It would have served *him*. The soft version preserves the prophet's safety at the cost of the people's clarity. The sharp version — the one Allah records — risks the prophet so that the people might wake.

[PAUSE]

This is what *naṣīḥa* means in its deepest form. *Naṣīḥa* — usually translated "sincere counsel" — comes from a root that means *to mend, to purify, to make seamless*. To give *naṣīḥa* is to give someone the words they need to mend themselves, even if those words cost you something to say.

Lūṭ's question is *naṣīḥa* in its purest form. He is mending them at his own expense.

And here is the part that should shake us. **The same voice that asks a hard question is the only voice that loves you enough to ask it.** The voice that affirms you when you are sliding — that is not the loving voice. That is the convenient voice, the voice that wants to keep your relationship intact. The loving voice is the one that risks your displeasure because your eternity matters more than your good mood.

Look at the people you have in your life. Who tells you the hard truth? Who asks you the question you have been organizing your life to avoid? That person — whoever they are — is operating in the prophetic mode. Not because they claim revelation. Because they are willing to be uncomfortable so that you might be clear.

And — let's be honest — most of us have such voices in our life and we have spent years pushing them to the margins. We surround ourselves with the affirmers. The ones who tell us we are fine. The ones who never ask *are you really doing this?*

This ayah is, among other things, an invitation to recover the value of the hard voice. To find, in our circles, the people who love us enough to confront us. And to become, for someone else, that voice.

[PAUSE]

What does this change for us, tomorrow?

It means we should ask ourselves a difficult question: *is there someone in my life whose hard counsel I have been avoiding?* Not the cruel voice. Not the contemptuous voice. The voice that is sharp precisely because it cares.

The practical application: pick up the phone. Or open the message. Or sit down with that person. And listen long enough to hear the question they have been trying to ask you. The one you have been organizing your life to never have to answer.

It will be uncomfortable. That is the point. *Naṣīḥa* is what mercy sounds like when comfort would have been a betrayal.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

A particle structure — *a-inna...la-* — that forces a community to hear itself name its own act. A noun — *shahwa* — placed asymmetrically to show that what was lost is not just a partner but an entire architecture of human meeting. A bypassing word — *min dūn* — that mirrors the deepest theological sin in a smaller key. And a final verb — *tajhalūn* — that names the engine beneath all of it: not absence of knowledge, but organized refusal to act on what is known.

Three linguistic discoveries.

And then, from the other direction, two themes. One: that the most dangerous form of ignorance is the *performed* kind — the kind a community builds institutions to maintain. Two: that the sharp prophetic question is not the opposite of mercy — it *is* mercy, in the form it must take when softer forms have failed.

These are the same insight from two directions. **The whole ayah is about what happens when knowledge and action are forced back into contact.** The linguistic devices are the surgical instruments. The themes are the surgery.

Lūṭ is not telling them something new. He is forcing them to *be present to what they have been pretending to forget*. That is what a prophet does. And that is what, every day, the inner voice in each of us is trying to do — if we will let it.

### Questions to Carry

1. What knowledge in your life have you been organizing yourself to act-as-if you do not have?
2. Whose hard question have you been avoiding — and what would happen if you finally let them ask it?
3. When the small voice rises in you before an act you know is wrong, what do you usually do with that three-second window?
4. Are you, for anyone in your life, the voice of *naṣīḥa* — or have you become only an affirmer?
5. If a prophet stood in front of you tomorrow and asked *a-innakum la-...* — what would he be pointing at?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The deepest sin is not the act itself but the community-wide decision to act as if we do not know — and the loving voice is the one that asks the question we have been organizing our lives to avoid.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before sleep, do one thing. Sit for one minute and let the question rise. Not the question someone else has asked you. *The* question. The one your fiṭra has been trying to articulate while you have been busy not hearing it. Let it have its three seconds. You do not have to act on it tonight. Just refuse the pretense that you did not hear it.

That is where the wall begins to come down.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who gave us a fiṭra that still speaks beneath every covering we have thrown over it — let us hear the voice we have been pretending not to hear. Send us, in this life, the *naṣīḥa* of those who love us enough to ask the hard question, and let us be that voice for someone else. Save us from being *qawmun tajhalūn* — a people who know and act as if we do not. Āmīn.
