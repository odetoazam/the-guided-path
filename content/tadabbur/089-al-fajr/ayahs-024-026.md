# The Life He Never Lived For

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Hear them in Arabic before we say anything about them.

> **يَقُولُ يَٰلَيْتَنِى قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِى**
>
> *Yaqūlu yā laytanī qaddamtu li-ḥayātī*
>
> "He will say: *Oh, how I wish I had sent ahead — for my life.*"

And then two verses fall like a door closing behind him:

> **فَيَوْمَئِذٍ لَّا يُعَذِّبُ عَذَابَهُۥٓ أَحَدٌ ۝ وَلَا يُوثِقُ وَثَاقَهُۥٓ أَحَدٌ**
>
> *Fa-yawma'idhin lā yuʿadhdhibu ʿadhābahu aḥad. Wa lā yūthiqu wathāqahu aḥad.*
>
> "So on that Day, no one will punish the way He punishes, and no one will bind the way He binds."

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand where this sentence stands.

### Rebuild the world

Surah Al-Fajr does not begin with a man's regret. It begins with the sky. It opens with oaths — by the dawn, by ten sacred nights, by the even and the odd, by the night *as it slips away*. There is something in those opening oaths already whispering about time: things that come, things that pass, things you cannot hold still.

Then the surah turns to history. It walks you past the graveyards of civilizations. ʿĀd — the people of Iram, *"the likes of whom were never created in the land."* Thamūd, who carved the rock. Pharaoh, the man of stakes and armies. Giants. Empires. *"Did you not see how your Lord dealt with them?"* And the answer hangs in the air: He poured upon them a scourge of punishment. The greatest powers the earth ever produced — gone, as if a hand had simply wiped the table clean.

And then — this is the genius of the surah — it stops talking about *them* and turns to look at *you*. It diagnoses the human heart with terrifying precision. *When his Lord tests him with honor and ease, he says: "My Lord has honored me."* And *when his Lord tests him by restricting his provision, he says: "My Lord has humiliated me."* Do you feel how exact that is? We read our bank balance as a verdict on our worth. Comfort, we hear as God's approval. Constraint, we hear as God's rejection. The surah says: **No. You have misread the entire test.** *You do not honor the orphan. You do not urge one another to feed the poor. You devour inheritance greedily. And you love wealth — with a love that has no floor to it.*

That is the man this surah has been building. Not a monster. A *normal* person. Comfortable. Acquisitive. Sure that his comfort meant heaven was pleased with him.

And now the surface of reality tears open. *"No! When the earth is pounded — pounded to powder. And your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank. And Hell, that Day, is brought near."* And in that instant — *yawma'idhin yatadhakkaru-l-insān* — **"that Day, the human being will remember."** He will remember everything. The whole forgotten file of his life opens at once. *"But how will the remembering help him now?"*

And out of that remembering comes one sentence — al-Jalālayn notes that he speaks it *maʿa tadhakkurihi,* "along with his remembering," so the sentence is not a reaction that follows the remembering but its very shape: the form the whole reopened file finally takes in his mouth. The sentence his entire life has compressed into. Eight syllables that he would trade the universe to unsay.

*Yā laytanī qaddamtu li-ḥayātī.*

### The hook

Here is what I need you to notice, and then I'm going to make you wait for it.

This man, in the most awake moment of his existence — standing in the full light of ultimate reality — uses a very strange phrase for where he is. He looks around at the Day of Judgment, the true and final reality, and he calls something **"my life."**

But he doesn't call *this* his life. [PAUSE]

He's looking back at the thing he just lost — and there is a word hiding in his sentence that reveals he understood, far too late, *which of his two lives was ever the real one.*

And there is a second strangeness. His wish has a hole in it. He says, "I wish I had sent ahead—" and then the sentence simply *opens*, with no object. Sent ahead *what?* Allah leaves the space blank. We are going to ask why that blank is the most generous — and most devastating — silence in the verse.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Layta* — the grammar of a wish that cannot come true

Let me teach you something about your own speech before we go to the Arabic.

In every language, we have two kinds of wishing. There is the wish you can still act on — *"I hope I can make it tomorrow."* The door is open. And there is the other kind. The wish for what is already sealed. *"I wish I had called him before he died." "I wish I had studied."* Notice that in English we shift the grammar itself — we say *"I wish I **had**,"* reaching backward into a past that has clicked shut. The very tense tells you: this door is closed.

Arabic has a dedicated tool for exactly this second kind of wish. It is the particle **لَيْتَ** — *layta.*

The grammarians call this **تَمَنّي**, *tamannī*. And here is the precise thing about *tamannī*: it is the longing for something **impossible, or beyond reach.** This is not *rajāʾ* — hope — which leans toward what's still attainable. *Layta* is the word you reach for when you are wishing against a locked reality. When you say *layta*, the Arabic ear already knows: **what is being wished for cannot be had.** The grammar is grief.

Now hear the man's first word of regret. Not just *layta.* He says:

**يَٰلَيْتَنِى** — *yā laytanī.*

There is a **يا** in front of it. *Yā.* The vocative particle. The "O—" that you put before someone you are *calling.* *Yā Allāh. Yā Rasūl Allāh.* You say *yā* when you are turning your face toward someone to call out to them.

Sit with this. [PAUSE]

He has placed a *call* — *yā* — in front of a wish that can never be answered. He is calling out... to whom? There is no one named after the *yā.* The grammarians note that here the *yā* is for *tanbīh*, for sheer intensification — it is the *yā* of a cry, not a conversation. It is the sound a person makes when the grief is too large to hold inside the sentence, so it spills out the front of it before the words even begin. **It is a call into a void.**

Think of what a human being does in the worst moment — the news at the door, the test result, the empty crib. The first sound is not a sentence. It is *"Oh—"*. A cry that reaches out for someone and finds no one. Allah recorded that exact sound. *Yā—.* The Quran did not give this man a tidy, grammatical regret. It gave him the raw vowel of someone whose whole life just came due at once.

And then, *laytanī.* A wish, by its very grammar, for the impossible. He is not strategizing. He is not repenting — repentance is a door, and *layta* is for when the door is gone. He is doing the most human and most useless thing a person can do: wishing backward. Al-Muyassar sharpens this to a point. He describes the man at this instant finally *taking heed* and even *turning to repent* — *yattaʿiẓu wa yatūb* — and then asks the question that closes every exit: how can heeding and repentance help him *now,* when he squandered both in the dunya and their time has already passed — *wa fāta awānuhumā?* He is reaching for the two things that could have saved him at the exact moment neither is available. *Layta* is the word left over when both of those doors have shut behind you.

**What this one word reveals:** there is a kind of remorse that comes too late to be repentance, and Allah has given it its own grammar. *Layta* is the sound of a soul that finally sees clearly — at the exact moment that seeing clearly can no longer change anything.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Qaddamtu* — and the hole where the deeds should be

Now the verb. **قَدَّمْتُ** — *qaddamtu.* "I sent ahead. I put forward. I advanced."

The root is **ق-د-م**, *q-d-m*, and it lives all around you. *Qadam* is a foot — the thing that goes *forward.* *Qadīm* is the ancient, what came *before.* *Taqaddum* is progress, moving *ahead.* The whole family of this root is about precedence — about something going on *ahead* of something else.

And the form he uses is **Form II** — *qaddama,* with that doubled, weighted middle letter. (The Leeds corpus confirms it: a Form II perfect verb.) Form II takes the bare idea of "coming before" and makes it deliberate, transitive, *done to something*: not merely *to be ahead*, but **to send something on ahead. To dispatch it forward. To make it precede you.**

Here is the picture buried in the word, and it is a picture the first listeners would have felt in their bodies. You are a traveler. You have a long, hard journey ahead across the desert, to a destination you cannot carry your whole household to at once. So what does a wise traveler do? He **sends provisions ahead.** He dispatches food, water, shelter to the waypoint *before* he arrives — so that when his exhausted body finally reaches that place, what he needs is already *there,* waiting for him. *Qaddama.* You send your provision forward to meet you at the destination.

That is what every deed is. This is the unseen reality the physical word is pointing at. A good deed is not consumed and gone when you do it. A good deed is *provision dispatched ahead* — sent forward down the road of your own journey, to be waiting for you at the place you are certainly going. *"And whatever good you send ahead for yourselves — qaddamtu li-anfusikum — you will find it with Allah"* (2:110). The deed travels. It arrives before you do. And on the Day you finally stagger into that destination, you discover whether you sent anything ahead — or arrived with empty hands at a place where nothing can be earned anymore.

Now. Here is the silence I promised you. [PAUSE]

He says: *qaddamtu* — "I sent ahead—". In Arabic, *qaddama* wants an object. You send ahead *something.* The sentence is grammatically reaching for a noun: I sent ahead *what?* My prayers? My charity? My faith?

**Allah leaves it blank.**

The object is simply not there. The man's mouth opens around the thing he should have sent, and the space is empty — which is precisely, exactly, what his hands are now. The grammar of the verse *enacts* the poverty of the man. He reaches for the word for what he sent ahead, and there is nothing to name.

Look at how the great commentators handled this blank. Al-Baghawī fills it: *"al-khayr wa-l-ʿamal al-ṣāliḥ"* — the good, the righteous deed. Al-Muyassar fills it: *"the deeds that would benefit me."* Al-Jalālayn fills it: *"the good, and faith."* Al-Qurṭubī fills it: *"some good, for my deliverance from the Fire."*

Do you see what just happened? Every single commentator had to *supply* the missing word — because Allah deliberately withheld it. And here is why that withholding is the most devastating mercy in the verse: **by leaving it blank, Allah made it mean everything.** If the verse had said "I wish I had sent ahead my prayers," the regret would be about prayer. By leaving the object out, the regret becomes about *all of it* — every unsaid *bismillāh*, every uncomforted orphan (and remember, this surah just named the orphan), every charity deferred, every dawn slept through. The empty slot in the sentence is large enough to hold every good thing he ever could have done and didn't. It is the exact size of a wasted life.

**What this word reveals:** your deeds are not behind you, finished. They are *ahead* of you, waiting — or absent. And the most terrible sentence a soul can speak is a verb of sending with nothing left to send.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Li-ḥayātī* — the word that betrays which life was real

We come to the strangest two words in the verse, and the heart of everything. **لِحَيَاتِى** — *li-ḥayātī.* "For my life."

The root is **ح-ي-ي**, *ḥ-y-y* — life itself. And there is a small preposition fused to the front: **لِ**, *li* — "for," "for the sake of."

Now I want you to feel the vertigo of this. *Where is this man standing when he says "my life"?*

He is standing **after death.** He has already died. He is on the Day of Judgment, the earth pounded to dust beneath him, the reality of the eternal abode unrolling in front of his eyes. By every reckoning he had while alive, *this* — where he now stands — would be "the afterlife," "death," "the end."

And he points *forward,* into this very reality, and calls it — **"my life."**

He does not call the seventy years he just spent on earth his life. He calls *this* his life. *Li-ḥayātī.*

Let the commentators widen the lens, because each gives you a different angle on the same staggering truth. Al-Baghawī: *"li-ḥayātī fi-l-ākhira — allatī lā mawta fīhā"* — "for my life in the Hereafter — the one **in which there is no death.**" Al-Jalālayn gives "the good life of the Hereafter" — but here he is careful, and honest, enough to record a *second* reading beside it: that *li-ḥayātī* may instead mean *"[during] the time of my life"* in the dunya — as though the cry were "I wish I had sent something ahead, *back when I was still alive.*" So there is a genuine *ikhtilāf* here, worth knowing: the classical scholars do not fully agree on which life the man is naming. Most — al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Jalālayn's own first reading — hear him naming the *Hereafter* as "my life," and that is the reading this whole reflection leans on. A minority reading hears him grieving the wasted hours of the *dunya.* And notice — this is the beautiful thing — the two do not fight. They are one regret seen from its two ends: either *"I never lived for the real life,"* or *"I squandered the only time I ever had to provision it."* Both leave the soul standing in the same empty-handed place. And al-Qurṭubī gives us the most haunting reading of all. He says the *lām* here can carry the meaning of *fī* — "in" — but then he opens a deeper door: he says the people of the Fire have *no real life at all* — their existence there is so wretched it does not deserve the name "life" — so the man's cry means: *"I wish I had sent ahead some good, so that I might be among those who actually have a life."*

Put those camera angles together and look at the single reality they're all circling:

This man spent his entire earthly existence calling the *dunya* "life" — *"and the life of this world is nothing but play and amusement."* He worked for it. He woke for it. He *loved wealth with a love that had no floor* — the surah told us so — because he was certain *this,* the cash and the comfort, *this* was living. And now, on the far side of death, the scales fall from his eyes and he sees the truth that was in front of him the whole time: **the thing he called "life" was never the life. It was the road. *This* — the eternal one, the one with no death in it — *this* was always "my life."** And he ran the entire race in the wrong direction, training for the warm-up and sleeping through the event.

[PAUSE]

There is one more turn of the knife, and it is in that little *li.* "For." *For* my life. He is saying: the dunya was supposed to be lived *for* this. The whole point of the temporary was to provision the permanent. The road exists *for* the destination. *Li-ḥayātī* — everything back there should have been spent *for the sake of* this life I am now standing in, empty-handed. He didn't just love the wrong thing. He inverted the entire relationship between his two existences. He treated the means as the end, and the end as something that would never come.

**What these two words reveal:** there are two lives, and only one of them deserves the name. The deepest tragedy is not that we die. It is that a person can live their whole "life" and discover, one second too late, that *it was never their life at all.*

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Layta* — the grammar of a wish that comes after the door has closed.
*Qaddamtu* — a verb of sending, with an empty space where the cargo should be.
*Li-ḥayātī* — a man calling the next world "my life," in the very breath that proves he never lived for it.

Three words. A wish that's too late, hands that are empty, and a man who finally knows which life was real. That is the whole anatomy of a soul's regret, compressed into eight syllables.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how *you and I* are living, today, with the door still open.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The deferred life — *the most expensive word in any language is "later"*

Let me start somewhere very ordinary. Somewhere you live.

There is a thing all of us do, and we do it so constantly we don't even hear ourselves do it. We say: *later.* *I'll start praying properly when the kids are older. I'll learn Quran when work calms down. I'll give regularly once I'm earning more. I'll fix my relationship with my mother — soon. I'll become the person I mean to be — once life settles.*

Psychologists have a clean name for this. They call it **"the planning fallacy of the self."** We imagine a future version of us — calmer, freer, more disciplined, more religious — and we quietly outsource all our hardest tasks to *that* person. The future self becomes a landfill where we dump everything we don't want to carry now. And the deep, unexamined assumption underneath it is: *there will be time. The future is a place I will definitely arrive at, with energy to spare.*

Now hold that ordinary human habit up against this verse. Because *yā laytanī qaddamtu* is the sound a person makes when the future self **never arrives** — when the landfill is opened on the Day of Judgment and every "later" he ever spoke is poured out in front of him at once.

Here is what I want you to see, and it is uncomfortable. The man in this verse is not being punished for a dramatic crime. Go back through the surah. His sins are *deferrals.* He *didn't* honor the orphan. He *didn't* urge feeding the poor. The greatest weight of his regret is not the evil he did — it is the good he kept meaning to get to. *Layta qaddamtu.* "I wish I had **sent ahead.**" His tragedy is a tragedy of *postponement.* This is exactly what al-Ṭabarī heard in the verse: he reads it as the sound of the son of Adam's *talahhuf* — his anguished grief — over his *tafrīṭ,* his *falling-short,* in the righteous deeds that would have *bequeathed him an eternal abiding.* Not a catalogue of crimes. A ledger of omissions. He didn't refuse God. He just... kept planning to start.

And this is the trap of "later" that makes it so deadly: **it never feels like a decision.** Refusing to pray feels like a sin. *Postponing* prayer doesn't — it feels like nothing at all, like a perfectly reasonable scheduling choice. You don't experience the closing of the door. You experience, every single day, a small, comfortable *not yet.* And then the accumulation of ten thousand "not yets" becomes a life. And the bill for all of them arrives in a single sentence: *yā laytanī.*

The Quran's answer to "later" is everywhere once you see it. *"O you who believe, fear Allah — and let every soul look at what it has **sent ahead** — qaddamat — for tomorrow"* (59:18). Same verb. *Qaddama.* Send it ahead. Today. *"Spend from what We have provided you before death comes to one of you and he says: My Lord, if only You would delay me a little, that I might give charity"* (63:10) — and there it is again, the same *layta*-shaped wish from the same too-late place.

[PAUSE]

So let me bring this all the way down to the ground, to tomorrow morning.

The practical shift this verse asks of you is brutally simple and it has nothing to do with grand transformation. It is this: **take one good thing you have been outsourcing to your future self — and do the smallest possible version of it before "later" can claim it.** Not "I'll become a person who gives charity." Give *something,* today, however small, so that something — *anything* — is already sent ahead. Not "I'll learn to pray with presence." Pray *one* prayer tomorrow as though it is the cargo you are dispatching to meet you. The man in the verse would trade the cosmos for a single deed in the "sent ahead" column. You have, right now, the one thing he is screaming for and cannot have: **a today in which the sending is still possible.** Don't hand it to a future self who may never arrive.

### Theme Two: The two lives, and the terror of getting the timeline wrong

Now the deeper current. Let me come at it through something you already know in your bones.

Imagine a man who spends thirty years and every penny he has building the most magnificent house you have ever seen — in a country he is about to be deported from, and to which he can never return. Marble. Gardens. A library. He pours his whole soul into it. And on the day it is finished, immigration officers arrive and put him on a plane to the land where he will actually spend the rest of his life — and in *that* land, he has nothing. A bare patch of dirt. Because for thirty years, he built lavishly in the country he was leaving, and sent *nothing* ahead to the country he was going to.

You would call that man a fool. Not because he built — but because he built *in the wrong country.* He got the timeline wrong. He invested everything in the temporary address and arrived at the permanent one destitute.

That is *li-ḥayātī.* That is the entire weight of "my life."

This man on the Day of Judgment is not regretting that he *lived.* He is regretting that he poured his living into the country he was always going to leave. *"My Lord has honored me,"* he used to say, when his wealth grew — reading every comfort as a sign he was building in the right place. The surah already told us his fatal misreading: he thought the dunya was the destination, when it was only ever the departure lounge.

And here is the part that should make all of us go quiet. [PAUSE]

The danger this verse is warning about is not the danger of being an obvious sinner. The man who is openly running from God at least *knows* which country he's neglecting. The danger this verse describes is far subtler and far closer to most of us: it is the danger of being **busy, comfortable, and pointed at the wrong life the entire time.** Of being a perfectly decent person who simply built in the departure lounge. Who confused the warm-up for the event. Who called the road "home" — for so long, and so sincerely, that he only learned the difference when the road ended.

Because both lives feel like life *while you are in the first one.* That is the trap. From inside the dunya, the dunya is overwhelmingly vivid — it is taxes and traffic and your child's fever and the deal closing Friday. And the *ākhira* feels like a rumor, a faint thing you'll attend to "later" (and now you see how the two themes are really one theme). The genius and the terror of *li-ḥayātī* is that it shows you the moment the vividness *flips* — when the dunya suddenly looks like the rumor and the ākhira becomes the only solid thing in the universe. The man isn't given new information on that Day. He is given *correct proportions.* For the first time, he sees the two lives at their true sizes. And the true size of the eternal one is so vast that he can only call it, simply, **"my life"** — as though, for the first time, he understands what the word was always supposed to mean.

And now — *now* — feel why verses 25 and 26 come where they come. He speaks his regret, and immediately:

*Fa-yawma'idhin lā yuʿadhdhibu ʿadhābahu aḥad. Wa lā yūthiqu wathāqahu aḥad.* "On that Day, no one punishes the way **He** punishes; no one binds the way **He** binds."

Notice the Arabic does something fierce here — it uses what the grammarians call the *cognate accusative*, the *mafʿūl muṭlaq:* *yuʿadhdhibu ʿadhābahu* — "punishes *His* punishing," *yūthiqu wathāqahu* — "binds *His* binding." The construction exists to do one thing: to make a thing utterly *incomparable.* No punishment is *like* His punishment. No binding is *like* His binding. (The reciters differ on these two verbs — most read them, with Ḥafṣ, as active: *none punishes as He punishes;* some read them passively: *none is punished as he is punished* — and both readings deepen the same dread: an incomparable accounting, falling on the one who sent nothing ahead.)

Why is *that* the answer to *li-ḥayātī?* Because the verse is teaching you the **stakes** of the timeline you choose. The man who built in the wrong country didn't just arrive poor — he arrived in a reality whose weight is beyond all comparison, with nothing in his hands to meet it. The incomparable life he ignored has an incomparable seriousness attached to it. You cannot treat the eternal as a rumor and then meet it on equal footing. *Li-ḥayātī* and *ʿadhābahu aḥad* are the two halves of one truth: get the life *right,* and you walk into the incomparable as a guest; get it *wrong,* and you meet the incomparable as a debtor.

[PAUSE]

So the practical turn here is not a task. It is a question you let reorganize your week. Before the big decisions — how you spend the windfall, where the next ten years of your energy go, what you are *actually* building — ask the *li-ḥayātī* question: **Which country am I building in right now? Am I sending this ahead, or am I sleeping in the departure lounge?** Not to make you despise the dunya — Islam never asks that. The traveler still needs provisions *for the road.* But the wise traveler never confuses the road with home, and never spends on the lounge what was meant to be sent ahead.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: a surah of two souls

Now let me show you something about *where* this verse sits, because the surah was built to make you feel it.

Surah Al-Fajr does not end with this man. It ends — just a few verses later — with the most tender address in the Quran. After the regret, after the incomparable punishment, the surah suddenly turns its face to a completely different soul:

*"O tranquil soul — yā ayyatuha-l-nafs al-muṭma'inna — return to your Lord, well-pleased and pleasing to Him. Enter among My servants. Enter My Garden."* (89:27-30)

Do you see the architecture? The surah gives you **two souls, side by side, at the same destination.** Same Day. Same pounded earth. Same Lord arriving. And two utterly different sentences come out of them. One soul says *yā laytanī* — "O, I wish—" — the cry into the void, the wish that comes too late. The other soul is *called:* *yā ayyatuhā* — "O you—" — and this time the *yā* is answered, because Someone is finally speaking *to* the soul, welcoming it home.

The regretful soul reached out with *yā* and found no one. The tranquil soul is reached *for,* with *yā,* by its Lord. The whole surah is the distance between those two calls.

And what made the difference between them? Everything in this verse. One soul *sent ahead.* One soul kept saying *later.* One built in the right country. One slept in the lounge. One understood, in time, which existence was *"my life."*

### Bringing it together

Look how the words and the themes turn out to be the same insight from two directions.

The **linguistic** journey gave us: *layta* — the wish that comes after the door shuts; *qaddamtu* with its empty object — hands with nothing to send; *li-ḥayātī* — a man who learns the name of real life one breath too late.

The **thematic** journey gave us: the deferred life, where "later" quietly eats a whole existence; and the two lives, where a decent, busy person can pour everything into the wrong country.

They are one truth. **The deeds are ahead of you, not behind you — and the door through which you send them is open only now.** *Layta* is what *now* turns into when you waste it. *Li-ḥayātī* is the name you will finally give to the life you either provisioned or abandoned. The grammar and the warning are the same hand, reaching for the same word, finding it either full or empty.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. Don't answer them quickly. Let them stay open.

1. If the whole of my life so far were compressed into one sentence spoken on that Day — would it sound more like *yā laytanī,* or like *yā ayyatuhā?*
2. What is the one good thing I am right now outsourcing to a future self who may never arrive?
3. Which country am I actually building in — and where would an honest audit say I've sent my treasure ahead?
4. When my wealth grows, do I still hear myself thinking *"my Lord has honored me"* — reading comfort as approval? And when it shrinks, *"my Lord has humiliated me"?* What would it mean to stop reading the test as the verdict?
5. If I truly believed the eternal one was *"my life,"* what is the very next thing I would do differently — today, while the sending is still possible?

### The one-sentence distillation

Your good is not behind you, finished — it is ahead of you, waiting; and the only word more expensive than "later" is the one this man finally spoke: *layta.*

### The closing invitation

I am not going to ask you to overhaul your life tonight. The man in this verse would tell you that grand resolutions are themselves a kind of "later." Instead, let one thing shift: **send something ahead before this day ends.** One deed. One prayer offered as cargo, not as chore. One small charity, dispatched down the road to meet you. One person from this surah's list — an orphan, a poor neighbor, a strained relationship — touched with good *now,* not *soon.* So that whatever your sentence sounds like on that Day, the object of *qaddamtu* is not blank. So that you have, in the one column that will matter, *something sent ahead — for your life.*

### A brief du'ā

*O Allah, You who let us send our deeds ahead to meet us — do not let us arrive to You empty-handed, speaking the word of regret after the door has shut. Teach us, while the door is still open, which of our two lives is truly "our life," and let us spend the road for the sake of the home. And when we stand before You on that Day, do not let us be among those who cry "yā laytanī" — but among the tranquil souls You call back to Yourself, well-pleased and pleasing to You.* Āmīn.
