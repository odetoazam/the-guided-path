# When He Comes and Everything Else Is Brought

## Introduction: Standing at the Edge of the Surah

Let me read these three verses to you. First in Arabic, then in sound, then in meaning. And I want you to feel where we are standing when we hear them.

> كَلَّآ إِذَا دُكَّتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ دَكًّا دَكًّا
> وَجَآءَ رَبُّكَ وَٱلْمَلَكُ صَفًّا صَفًّا
> وَجِا۟ىٓءَ يَوْمَئِذٍۭ بِجَهَنَّمَ ۚ يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَذَكَّرُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰ

*Kallā idhā dukkati-l-arḍu dakkan dakkā. Wa jāʾa rabbuka wal-malaku ṣaffan ṣaffā. Wa jīʾa yawmaʾidhin bi-jahannam, yawmaʾidhin yatadhakkaru-l-insānu wa annā lahu-dh-dhikrā.*

"Nay! When the earth is pounded and crushed, ground to powder — and your Lord comes, and the angels, rank upon rank — and Hell is brought near that Day — that Day the human being will remember. But how can remembrance avail him then?"

### Rebuild the World

To feel these verses, you have to know what the surah has just finished saying.

Al-Fajr begins with oaths — the dawn, ten nights, the even and the odd, the night as it departs. Then it walks through the ruins of nations that thought themselves untouchable: ʿĀd with their towering pillars, Thamūd who carved the rock, Pharaoh of the stakes. Civilizations that built and conquered and *vanished* — and Allah says of them, "Your Lord is ever watchful," *inna rabbaka labil-mirṣād* (89:14).

Then the surah turns the lens away from ancient empires and points it at *you*. At the ordinary human heart. And what it finds there is damning. When Allah tests a person with honor and ease, he says, "My Lord has honored me." When He tests him with a measured provision, he says, "My Lord has humiliated me." And then the verdict on how we actually live:

You do not honor the orphan. You do not urge the feeding of the poor. You devour inheritance with a devouring greed. You love wealth with an overwhelming love. (89:17-20)

That is the room we are standing in. The air is thick with the diagnosis of a heart that measured everything by what it could hold in its hands.

And then — into that silence — a single word lands like a gavel.

**كَلَّا.** *Kallā.* Nay. Not so. Stop.

### The Hook

Listen to what happens next, because the grammar is about to do something it almost never does so openly. In the space of three short verses, three enormous things happen — the earth is destroyed, the Lord arrives, the Fire is dragged forward. And if you listen to *who is doing what to whom*, you will hear a pattern so deliberate that, once you hear it, you will never read a scene of Judgment the same way again.

One of these three actors comes on His own. The other two are *brought*.

Stay with me. Let's find out which is which — and what that difference means for the day you will stand in this scene yourself.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word That Is Pounded From the Inside — *Dakkan Dakkā*

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning into *sound itself*.

In English, we have a few words that imitate what they describe — "crash," "thud," "boom." We call it onomatopoeia, and we treat it as a kind of party trick of language. In Arabic, this principle runs far deeper, down into the very *consonants* of a root. Some roots are built around what's called gemination — a doubled, stressed consonant, marked by a *shadda*, where the tongue strikes the same place twice in a row, hard. And again and again, the Arabic language attaches these doubled-consonant roots to meanings of *striking, breaking, splitting, pounding*.

Now look at the word.

**دَكَّ** — *dakka*. Root: د-ك-ك, *dāl-kāf-kāf*.

Do you see it? The root *itself* has the doubled *kāf*. The word for pounding is **pounded from the inside.** The shadda — that hard double-strike of the tongue — is the pestle hitting the mortar, inside the very body of the word. Allah did not just *describe* the crushing of the earth. He chose a word whose own structure *enacts* the crushing.

Now let's open what the word means. The classical commentators are precise here. Al-Qurtubī tells us *al-dakk* is "*al-kasr wad-daqq*" — breaking and pounding. He cites al-Zajjāj: the earth is convulsed until one part crushes against another. He cites al-Mubarrad with an even more vivid image: the earth is *flattened* — its elevation stripped away — and he reaches for the word *nāqa dakkāʾ*, a she-camel that has no hump. A landscape with its humps removed. Every mountain, every tower, every monument those vanished civilizations raised — *flattened to nothing*.

But Allah does not say *dakkā* once. He says **دَكًّا دَكًّا** — *dakkan dakkā*. He doubles it.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. We already have a doubled consonant *inside* the root. And now Allah doubles the *whole word* on top of it. Al-Baghawī reads this repetition as "*marratan baʿda marra*" — blow after blow after blow. This is not one earthquake. This is not a single impact. This is the wave that does not hit once and recede.

Think of the most violent thing you have ever felt the ground do — and then imagine it does not stop. It comes again. And again. Each blow finding what the last blow left standing and taking that too, until al-Baghawī's words come true: "*nothing remains on its surface*" — no mountain, no building, no tree. Al-Jalalayn glosses the shaking the same way: the earth is convulsed "*until every structure upon it is demolished and obliterated*." The earth that held every empire in this surah, every pillar of ʿĀd, every carved palace of Thamūd, **pounded to powder, blow upon blow.**

Here is what this single doubled word reveals: the solidity you are standing on right now is borrowed. The most permanent-feeling thing in your experience — the *ground* — is, on that Day, the first thing to dissolve.

### Element Two: The Asymmetry of Arrival — *Who Comes and Who Is Brought*

Now I want to teach you one grammatical distinction, and then I want to show you how Allah uses it to say something staggering without saying it out loud.

In Arabic, as in English, a verb can be *active* — the subject does the action — or *passive* — the subject has the action done *to* it. "He opened the door" versus "the door was opened." Active means agency. Passive means you are the object of someone else's agency. You are acted upon. You are *handled*.

Now hold that distinction in your mind and walk through these three verses with me, watching only one thing: who acts, and who is acted upon.

First: **دُكَّتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ** — *dukkati-l-arḍ.* "The earth **is pounded**." Passive. The earth does nothing. The earth is *done to*. It is the object.

Second: **وَجَآءَ رَبُّكَ** — *wa jāʾa rabbuka.* "And your Lord **comes**." *Jāʾa* — active, perfect tense. He, by His own majesty, **arrives**.

Third: **وَجِا۟ىٓءَ يَوْمَئِذٍۭ بِجَهَنَّمَ** — *wa jīʾa yawmaʾidhin bi-jahannam.* "And Hell **is brought**." And listen — it is the *same root* as the verb before it. *Jāʾa* — He came. *Jīʾa* — it was *made* to come. Hell does not arrive. **Hell is dragged.**

[PAUSE]

Do you hear the architecture now?

The earth — passive. Acted upon.
The Lord — active. The only one who *comes* of His own will.
Hell — passive. Acted upon. *Brought.*

The Prophet ﷺ made the dragging of the Fire even more explicit. Ibn Kathīr cites the ḥadīth reported by Muslim from Ibn Masʿūd: on that Day, Hell "*will be brought forth with seventy thousand reins, and with every rein seventy thousand angels dragging it.*" Picture the arithmetic of that image — a captive so vast it needs legions upon legions of handlers to haul it into place. Here in Al-Fajr the single passive verb *jīʾa* already tells you everything that image spells out: even Hell, the most terrifying reality in the entire scene, **has no agency of its own.** It is a creature. It is summoned. It comes only because it is brought.

There is only **one** active arrival in this entire passage. **وَجَآءَ رَبُّكَ.** Your Lord comes.

Let me sit with you in the weight of this. On a Day when the earth — the ground of all things — is reduced to a passive object, and when *Hell itself* is reduced to a passive object dragged into place, there is a single, sovereign, self-moved Agent in the entire cosmos. Everything that ever felt powerful to you — the planet, the forces of nature, the very fire you feared — all of it is revealed as object. Handled. Brought.

A note of care here, and it is a place where the classical scholars themselves diverge. Some — the approach of the early generations — affirm "your Lord comes" exactly as Allah affirmed it, without asking *how*, without imagining it in the shape of created coming; we affirm the majesty of the arrival and do not trespass into its *kayfiyya*, its modality. Others among the commentators read it with an implied word: al-Jalalayn glosses *jāʾa rabbuka* as "*His command comes*" — *jāʾa amru rabbika* — supplying a suppressed genitive to keep the sentence clear of any hint of bodily movement. Both readings are within the tradition, and it is worth knowing that the disagreement exists rather than pretending the verse yields only one gloss. And notice: on *either* reading, the grammar of the scene holds. Whether it is the Lord who comes or His command that comes, it is an *active* arrival — while the earth and the Fire are still only *brought*. Al-Muyassar frames the underlying purpose plainly: He comes "*li-faṣli-l-qaḍāʾ bayna khalqih*," to render the final judgment between His creation. That restraint before the *how*, whichever path a scholar took to it, is itself an act of worship.

And then — between two passives — the angels. **وَٱلْمَلَكُ صَفًّا صَفًّا** — "and the angels, **rank upon rank**." Al-Jalalayn notes the grammar: *ṣaffan ṣaffā* is a *ḥāl*, a circumstantial description — the angels arrive "*drawn up in ranks*," or, in his second reading, "*possessed of many ranks*," row behind row without number. And note the singular: Allah says *al-malak*, "the angel," using the singular as a collective for the entire host — one disciplined body, not a scattered crowd. Here is one more contrast Allah tucks into the scene. Below: the earth dissolving into formless powder, *dakkan dakkā*, chaos compounded. Above: the angels arriving *ṣaffan ṣaffā*, **order compounded** — line behind line behind line, in flawless formation. The created world that humans built collapses into disorder. The created world of obedient servants assembles into perfect order. The same repetition-structure — doubled words — used to paint the *opposite* of each other: chaos below, discipline above.

What this asymmetry reveals: on that Day you will finally *see* what was always true. That nothing you feared and nothing you trusted was ever an independent power. There was only ever One who acts — and everything else, including the things that terrified you, was always only ever *brought*.

### Element Three: The Pronoun That Should Not Be There — *Rabbuka*

I want you to notice a single attached pronoun. It is two letters. And it changes the temperature of the entire scene.

Allah does not say "and *Allah* comes." He does not say "and *the Lord*" or "and *the King* comes." He says **رَبُّكَ** — *Rabbuka.* "And **YOUR** Lord comes."

Two things are loaded into this word, and both of them matter.

First, the *name* chosen. Not *al-Qahhār* (the Overwhelming). Not *al-Jabbār* (the Compeller). Not *al-Muntaqim* (the Avenger). At the precise moment of maximum terror — earth pulverized, Fire dragged in — Allah names Himself **Rabb.** And *Rabb* is the name of nurture. The *Rabb* is the one who raises a thing gradually from nothing to completion, who sustains it, feeds it, tends it like a gardener tends a seedling. It is the most *intimate*, most *caring* of the great names. And it is the name He uses *here*, in the courtroom of the cosmos.

Second, the *pronoun* — "**your** Lord." Addressed to whom? Grammatically, to the Prophet ﷺ. But through him, to *you*, the one reading. In a scene where the human being is about to be reduced — where every false support is stripped away — Allah reaches in and reminds the listener: this terrifying arriving Sovereign is *yours*. The relationship was never severed. The One who comes to judge is the same One who nurtured you from a drop, who sustained you through every breath you took without thinking.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Why would Allah, at the apex of dread, choose the tenderest name and attach it to *you*? Because the terror of that Day is not meant to make Him a stranger to you. It is meant to make you run *toward* Him now, while running is still possible. The verse is frightening — and inside the frightening verse, hidden in two letters, is an open door. *Your* Lord. Still yours. For now.

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things. A word *pounded from the inside*, doubled until nothing stands. A scene where only One acts and everything else is *brought*. And a single pronoun that keeps the door of nearness open at the very edge of judgment.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the way you are living, today, right now.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Clarity That Arrives Too Late

Let me start where you already live.

Every one of us has had this experience. The exam where the answer surfaces in your mind *as the proctor says "pencils down."* The argument where the perfect, devastating, *true* thing to say arrives three hours later, in the shower, when the other person is long gone. The relationship where you finally understood what they needed from you — clearly, completely — the week after they stopped asking.

There is a specific, particular pain to this. It is not the pain of *not knowing*. It is worse. It is the pain of knowing **fully and exactly** — at the one moment when knowing can no longer do anything for you. The clarity comes. It just comes too late to spend.

Now hear the verse.

**يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَذَكَّرُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ** — *yawmaʾidhin yatadhakkaru-l-insān.* "That Day, the human being **remembers**."

And let me teach you the verb, because the *form* of it is the whole point. *Yatadhakkaru* is built on Form V of the root *dhāl-kāf-rāʾ* — the root of memory, of *dhikr*. Form V in Arabic — *tafaʿʿala* — carries the sense of an action done *to oneself*, gradually, with effort, a thing you *bring about in yourself* step by step. *Tadhakkur* is not a flash. It is the slow, effortful work of *making yourself remember* — of finally turning the whole soul around to face what it had been refusing to look at.

So picture it. On that Day, the human being finally does the work he would not do in this life. He finally turns and faces it. He sees, with total clarity, what he was, what mattered, what he traded it all for. The orphan he did not honor. The poor he did not feed. The wealth he loved with that overwhelming love. **He remembers everything. Perfectly.**

And then Allah closes the door in five words.

**وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰ** — *wa annā lahu-dh-dhikrā.* "But **how** can the remembrance avail him?" The word *annā* is not a simple "how." It is the *annā* of impossibility — "from *where*?" "by *what* path could it possibly?" It is a door that is not just closed but shown to have never had a key.

[PAUSE]

And here is the detail that should stop your heart. The root *dhikr* appears **twice** in this single moment. *Yatadhakkaru* — he remembers. *Al-dhikrā* — the remembrance. The same capacity, named twice, back to back. Because the tragedy is not that he *lacked* the ability to reflect. He *had* it. He had it the whole time. *Al-dhikrā* — remembrance, reflection, taking heed — was a faculty installed in him from the beginning. The Qur'ān itself is called *dhikrā*, a reminder. The reminders came. The faculty was always there.

He simply never *turned it on* until the moment it could no longer save him.

And the very next verse hands that remembrance its words. Ibn Kathīr reads straight on into what the human being *says* in that moment: **يَقُولُ يَـٰلَيْتَنِى قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِى** — "He will say, *Would that I had sent something ahead for my life!*" (89:24). Notice which life he calls "*my* life": not the one now ending, but the one now beginning — as if he only now grasps that *this*, the Hereafter, was the real life all along, and the years he treated as his life were merely the sending-ahead he squandered. The remembrance is not vague. It has a sentence. And the sentence is a wish pointed backward, at a door that has closed.

This is the deepest terror of the verse, and it is not fire. It is this: that the regret of that Day is not the regret of the *ignorant*. It is the regret of the one who **finally understands** — and whose understanding has become useless. The intellect gets, at last, everything it wanted. And it is worth nothing.

So what does this change about tomorrow? It means *tadhakkur* — that turning of the whole soul to face the truth — is a thing you can do **now**, in the one window where it still buys something. The same act that will be worthless on that Day is, today, the most valuable act available to you. Every honest moment of reflection you do in this life is *dhikrā* spent in the only market where it has currency. Do not save your remembering for the Day it cannot be spent.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Day Your Theory of Power Is Corrected

Let me start, again, where you live.

You operate, every day, on a private theory of what is powerful and what is not. You may not have written it down, but you live by it precisely. You fear certain things — losing your income, your status, someone's disapproval, the diagnosis, the market crash. You trust certain things — your savings, your competence, your relationships, your plans. This map of fears and trusts *runs your life.* It decides what you chase and what you avoid, what keeps you up at night and what lets you sleep.

And the indictment earlier in this surah was, at bottom, an indictment of a *false map*. The human who says "my Lord honored me" when he gets wealth and "my Lord humiliated me" when provision is narrowed — that is a man whose entire theory of power is *money*. He has mistaken the gift for the Giver, the object for the Agent.

Now bring back what the grammar showed us.

On that Day, the earth — the most solid thing you know — is revealed as a *passive object*, pounded to dust. Hell — the most terrifying thing you can imagine — is revealed as a *passive object*, dragged in by handlers. Every item on your map of fears, when the cover is finally pulled off reality, turns out to have been a *creature*. Brought. Handled. Object, never agent.

And the one thing that was *never* on most people's map of power — *your Lord* — is revealed as the only Agent there ever was.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the Day of Judgment actually *is*, underneath the terror? It is the day your false map is *corrected.* It is the day you finally see the agency of things as it truly is — and the heartbreak is that you see it when seeing changes nothing.

But notice the mercy folded into this. **You do not have to wait for that Day to correct the map.** Tawḥīd — the heart of this entire religion — is precisely the *early correction of your theory of power.* It is choosing, *now*, to see what the human in the verse only sees too late: that the things you fear are brought, that the things you trust are gifts, and that there is one Agent who acts, and He is *your Lord.*

The person who lives by tawḥīd is simply someone who got the map right *before* the earth was pounded. Who stopped fearing the object and started fearing — and loving — the Agent. Who feeds the orphan not because the orphan can repay him but because he already knows *who* actually holds the scales.

So what changes tomorrow? It is a single question you can hold over every fear and every craving: *Is this an agent, or is it brought?* The job, the money, the opinion of that person, the illness — *brought.* Object. Creature. And the One who decides whether it touches you at all — *your Lord.* Move your fear and your hope to where the agency actually is. That is the whole correction. Make it now.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how these three verses are built. They are a perfect rising structure — three "when" clauses crashing one after another toward a single human moment:

- **When** the earth is pounded — *dakkan dakkā* — chaos, doubled.
- And your Lord **comes** — *the one active arrival.*
- And the angels, rank upon rank — *ṣaffan ṣaffā* — order, doubled.
- And Hell is **brought** — *jīʾa* — the passive twin of *jāʾa.*

Three cosmic events. And they all funnel, like water down a drain, into **one** small word: *al-insān.* The human being. *That Day, the human remembers.* The entire universe is convulsing — earth, heaven, fire — and the camera, at the end, pushes in on a single human face, finally awake, finally understanding, finally too late.

The architecture *is* the argument. All of creation is mobilized, and it all converges on the worth of one human heart's *attention* — the attention it would not give while it could.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed you that only One *acts* and everything else is *brought*. The themes showed you that this is the very correction your soul most needs and most resists — to stop fearing the brought and to turn toward the One who acts.

The grammar showed you a word — *dhikrā* — appearing twice, a faculty you always had. The themes showed you that this faculty, *tadhakkur*, is worth everything today and nothing on that Day.

And holding the whole thing together is two letters: *rabbuka.* **Your** Lord. The terror was never meant to push you away. It was meant to make you turn — while turning still counts.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is on *your* private map of power — the things you truly fear, the things you truly trust? Which of them, when the cover is pulled off, will turn out to have been only *brought*?
2. You have the faculty of *dhikrā* right now. When did you last actually use it — sit and turn your whole soul to face what you are avoiding?
3. The human in this verse understood *everything* — too late. What do you already know, clearly, that you are simply declining to act on?
4. Why do you think Allah chose the name *Rabb* — the Nurturer — for the moment of judgment? What is He telling you about the door that is still open?
5. If you genuinely lived as though only *your Lord* is the Agent, what one fear would lose its grip on you this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day when the solid earth is pounded to dust and even Hell is dragged in as a helpless object, only your Lord arrives by His own will — and the human being, who could have turned his whole soul to face the truth while it still mattered, finally remembers everything at the one moment remembrance can no longer save him.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing about this verse is meant to leave you in dread. It is meant to move your remembering *forward in time* — out of that useless Day and into this usable one. The *tadhakkur* that will be worthless then is priceless now. The map you will be forced to correct then, you are *invited* to correct today, freely, gently, by your own choice. That is the whole mercy hidden inside the terror: *He told you in advance.* The reminder came while it could still be spent.

So do not read this and feel only fear. Read it and *turn* — even slightly, even now. Honor one orphan. Feed one hungry person. Move one fear off the throne it does not deserve. Let the One who *comes* be, today, the One you come *toward.*

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who are *our Rabb* — the One who nurtured us from nothing and sustains us in every breath — let us do our remembering *now*, while it still avails us, and not on the Day when the earth is pounded and the door is shut. Correct our map of fear and hope before You correct it for us. And on the Day You come to judge between Your creation, let us be among those who turned toward You while turning still counted. *Āmīn.*
