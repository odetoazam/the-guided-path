# Were We Wearied by the First? — The God Who Is Never at a Loss

## The Ayah

> أَفَعَيِينَا بِٱلْخَلْقِ ٱلْأَوَّلِ ۚ بَلْ هُمْ فِى لَبْسٍ مِّنْ خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ

*Afa-ʿayīnā bil-khalqi-l-awwal? Bal hum fī labsin min khalqin jadīd.*

**"Were We then wearied by the first creation? Nay — they are in confusion about a new creation."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me place you where this verse was first heard.

Picture Makkah, the early years. A man stands among his people and tells them something that strikes them as not merely false but *absurd*. He tells them that after they die — after the body has gone into the earth, after the flesh has returned to dust, after the bones have crumbled and scattered and become indistinguishable from the soil — they will be brought back. Reassembled. Stood up again, whole, to answer for their lives.

And the response, captured at the very opening of this surah, is not anger. It is something closer to a scoff. **بَلْ عَجِبُوٓا — "Rather, they marveled"** (50:2). They found it astonishing. And then they said the line that this whole surah is built to answer:

> ذَٰلِكَ رَجْعٌۢ بَعِيدٌ
>
> *"That is a far return."* (50:3)

*Baʿīd.* Far. Remote. Far-fetched. A long way off from anything believable. They held the idea of resurrection out at arm's length and said: *this is too much. The distance between a scattered corpse and a living, standing person — that gap is too wide for anyone to cross.*

Now I want you to feel the shape of their objection, because it is more sophisticated than it first appears. They are not stupid. They have buried their dead. They have seen what happens to a body in the ground. They know, with the hard certainty of people who live close to death, that what goes into the earth does not come back out. Their objection is grounded in observation. *We have never seen it. The thing has never been done. The gap is uncrossable.*

The surah answers them in waves. It points to the sky raised without pillars. It points to the earth spread out, the mountains set firm. And then — this is the one I want you to hold — it points to dead land:

> وَنَزَّلْنَا مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءً مُّبَٰرَكًا ... وَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِۦ بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ
>
> *"And We sent down blessed water from the sky... and We revived by it a dead land. Thus is the coming forth."* (50:9–11)

*Thus is the coming forth.* You have seen dead ground come back to life every single year. You call that ordinary. You call resurrection impossible. They are the same act.

And then — after recounting the nations destroyed before them, the peoples who also doubted and were swept away — comes our verse. One question. Seven words of Arabic. And it does something the previous arguments did not do.

The earlier arguments said: *look at what I have already done — the sky, the earth, the rain.* They argued from God's works.

This verse stops arguing from the works. It turns and looks straight at the doubter. And it asks a question so quiet and so devastating that, once you hear it correctly, the entire objection collapses — not because it is answered, but because it is *relocated.*

**Here is the hook.** The disbelievers said the problem was distance — *that is a far return.* They located the problem in the act: resurrection is too hard. This verse is going to tell them, in seven words, that they have misdiagnosed where the difficulty lives. The difficulty was never in the act. And by the end of the verse, you will see exactly where it actually is — and it is not a place any of us is comfortable looking.

Let us go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Is Not Asking — *Afa-ʿayīnā*

#### First, the concept

In every language, we have a kind of question that is not really asking for information. If a friend lets you down badly and you say, *"Is this how you treat people?"* — you are not requesting data about how they treat people. You already know. The question form is a vessel carrying something else: a rebuke, an astonishment, a denial. The grammar wears the clothes of a question, but underneath it is a statement, and usually a sharp one.

Arabic has a precise name for this. It is called **istifhām inkārī** — the *interrogative of denial*. The questioner asks a question whose only possible honest answer is *"No, of course not"* — and the asking itself is the argument. To even pose the question is to expose how absurd the opposite would be.

Hold that. Now look at how this verse opens.

#### The word

> أَفَعَيِينَا
>
> *Afa-ʿayīnā*

Three pieces fused into one word. The **أ** (*a-*) at the front is the hamza of interrogation — the question-mark, placed first. The **فـ** (*fa-*) is a connector — *then, so.* And the heart of it: **عَيِينَا** — *ʿayīnā* — a verb in the perfect tense, first person plural. *Did We...?*

Notice immediately *who* is being asked about. **We.** This is God speaking of Himself. The question is not "were *you* unable" — it is "were *We* unable." God is putting Himself in the dock of His own rhetorical question. And the only answer the grammar will permit is *No.* That is what istifhām inkārī does: it asks so that you will say no.

But the whole weight of this verse — its entire force — lives in that verb, **ʿayīnā.** And here is where we have to slow almost to a stop, because the English translation has been quietly lying to you. Not maliciously. But the standard rendering, *"Were We wearied,"* makes you picture the wrong thing.

#### Opening the word

Listen to the verb itself before we define it. *ʿA-yī-nā.* Say it. There is something almost laboured in the sound — the catch of the *ʿayn* in the throat, the long strained *ī*, the sound dragging slightly. This is a word from the root **ع-ي-ي** (*ʿayy*), and across the classical Arabic lexicon this root does *not* primarily mean the simple tiredness of a body that has worked hard. That meaning exists at the edges of it. But the core is something else, and it is far more interesting.

Let me give you al-Qurtubī's gloss directly, because it changes everything. He explains:

> *yuqālu: ʿayītu bil-amri idhā lam taʿrif wajhah*
>
> "One says: *I was* ʿayy *with the matter* — when you do not know the *way* of it, the *manner* of doing it, its *face*."

Stay with that. **ʿAyy is not running out of strength. It is not knowing how.** It is the state of a person who stands before a task and is *at a loss* — who cannot find the door, cannot see the method, is baffled by the thing and does not know where to begin. It is the failure not of muscle but of *capacity to find a way.*

We have all felt it. It is the feeling of being handed a problem so far beyond you that you do not even know which end to pick it up by. Not "I'm too tired to do this" — but "I have no idea *how* this could even be done." That helplessness, that being-stumped, that standing-baffled-before-an-impossible-task — *that* is ʿayy.

#### Deepening

Now reread the question with the real word installed:

**"Were We, then, at a loss with the first creation? Did the making of you the first time leave Us baffled, stumped, not knowing how?"**

[PAUSE]

Feel what that does. The disbelievers said resurrection was *baʿīd* — far, hard, beyond crossing. And God does not answer by saying *resurrection is easy.* He goes back behind resurrection, to a thing they have already conceded, and asks: when I made you *the first time* — out of nothing, when there was no precedent, no template, no prior human to copy, no existing model of an eye or a spine or a beating heart — **was I at a loss then?**

Because here is the buried logic, and al-Baghawī names it exactly. He calls this verse a **taqrīr** — an *establishing*, a pinning-down — and he explains why: *li-annahum-ʿtarafū bil-khalqi-l-awwali wa-ankarū-l-baʿth.* **"Because they acknowledged the first creation and denied the resurrection."** They already admit God made them the first time. They are not atheists; they grant a Creator. So the verse traps them inside their own concession. *You agree I did the harder thing — the thing with no precedent, the bringing-into-being from sheer nothing. And you doubt the easier thing — the re-bringing of what already once existed, whose pattern is already known, whose every atom I have already assembled once before?*

The first creation had no model. The second creation *is* the model, repeated. If He was not at a loss when there was nothing to work from, how could He be at a loss now that the work has already been done once?

al-Jalālayn compresses it to a single line: *lam naʿya bihi fa-lā naʿyā bil-iʿādah* — **"We were not at a loss with it, so We are not at a loss with the repetition."**

#### What this single word reveals

The disbelievers thought they were raising an objection about *power.* Can it be done? Is the gap crossable? And the verse reveals that they never understood the nature of the One they were questioning. They imported a human category — *getting stumped, being at a loss, not knowing how* — and projected it onto the Creator. They assumed that what is baffling to them must be baffling to Him.

And the istifhām inkārī demolishes it with a question that needs no answer. *Was I ever at a loss?* The very form of the question makes the absurdity audible. To picture God baffled is to picture a contradiction. The One who originated everything from nothing has never once stood before a task and not known the way.

### Element Two: The Two Creations, and the Tale Their Grammar Tells

#### The concept

In Arabic, as in English, a noun can be *definite* or *indefinite.* "The creation" versus "a creation." "The book" versus "a book." We mark the difference in English with *the* versus *a.* Arabic marks it with the prefix **ٱل** (*al-*) for the definite, and with a particular nunation — a final *-n* sound — for the indefinite.

This sounds like dry grammar. But definiteness carries *psychology.* When you say *"the"* thing, you are signalling: this is known, identified, settled, present to the mind. When you say *"a"* thing, you are signalling: this is unspecified, vague, one-of-many, not yet pinned down. The choice between *the* and *a* is the choice between *this settled reality* and *some uncertain something.*

Now watch the verse place two creations side by side and dress them in opposite clothing.

#### Opening the words

The verse names creation twice.

The first time:

> بِٱلْخَلْقِ ٱلْأَوَّلِ
>
> *bil-khalqi-l-awwal* — **"the first creation"**

Both words wear the **ٱل**. *Al-khalq, al-awwal.* The creation — definite. The first — definite. Doubly settled. This is a *known* thing, identified, fixed, present to everyone's mind. And rightly so: the first creation already happened. It is the most undeniable fact in the disbelievers' possession. They are *standing inside it.* Their own existence is the evidence. There is nothing more definite than the body you are living in.

The second time:

> خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ
>
> *khalqin jadīd* — **"a new creation"**

Both words now indefinite. *Khalq-in, jadīd-in* — hear the *-in* endings, the nunation. *A* creation. *A* new one. Unspecified. Vague. Held at a distance. One-of-some-possible-somethings, not yet real to them.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepening

Do you see what the grammar has just done?

The verse has dressed the **first creation as definite** — *the* creation, settled, real, owned. And it has dressed the **new creation as indefinite** — *a* creation, vague, distant, doubted.

**The grammar is wearing the disbelievers' own psychology.**

This is the genius of it. The verse is not merely *describing* their attitude — it is *enacting* it in the very case-endings. To the doubter, the past is *definite*: my first creation, the body I have, the existence I can touch — that is **the** real thing, beyond question. And the future, the resurrection, is *indefinite*: some vague new creation, off in the distance, a hazy *maybe.* They grant full reality to what already happened and only a flickering, indefinite half-reality to what is promised.

And here is the quiet devastation: **it is the same act.** *Khalq* the first time. *Khalq* the second time. The same word, the same root **خ-ل-ق**, the same Creator, the same making. The only difference between them is not in the act — the act is identical. The only difference is in the *grammar of the human heart*, which insists on calling one *"the"* and the other *"a"*. Which insists that the one already done is solid and the one yet to come is a rumour.

But logically — *theologically* — the indefinite one should be the *more* certain. The first creation was the genuinely improbable one, the one with no precedent. The "new" creation is merely the repeat of something already accomplished. If anything deserved to be held at arm's length as a vague *maybe*, it was the *first* creation, before it ever happened. Yet that is the one they call settled and real. They have it exactly backwards. They are certain of the harder thing because it already happened to them, and doubtful of the easier thing because it has not happened *yet.*

#### What the grammar reveals

The disbeliever's error is not in his reasoning. It is in his *calendar.* He treats *"already happened"* as the only category of *real*, and *"not yet"* as the category of *doubtful.* He has made his own experience of time into the measure of what God can do. The definite and the indefinite in this verse are a mirror held up to that error — and the moment you see that the act is the *same word* both times, the asymmetry of your own certainty becomes impossible to defend.

### Element Three: *Bal* — The Hinge That Moves the Problem

#### The concept

Some words are doors. Arabic has a small, powerful particle, **بَلْ** (*bal*), whose entire job is to *turn.* The grammarians call its function **iḍrāb** — a *turning away*, a retraction-and-redirection. *Bal* says: *set aside what just came before; the real matter is over here.* It is a hinge. Everything before it pivots, and the verse swings to face a new direction.

It matters enormously *where* a verse chooses to swing.

#### Opening the word

The first half of the verse was a question aimed *upward* — at God. *Were We at a loss?* It put the divine capacity in the frame. And then:

> بَلْ هُمْ فِى لَبْسٍ
>
> *bal hum fī labs* — **"Nay — they are in confusion."**

The hinge turns. And watch the direction it turns *toward.* The first half looked up at the heavens — *is the problem in God's power?* The word *bal* swings the whole verse around and points it **down, and inward, at the human being.** From *"Were We at a loss?"* to *"they are in confusion."*

#### Deepening

This is the structural heart of the entire verse — and of the surah's whole answer to *"that is a far return."*

The disbelievers located the problem of resurrection in the *act.* It is too hard, too far, *baʿīd.* They put the difficulty out there, in the doing of the thing, in God's capacity to cross the gap.

The word *bal* picks the problem up from where they put it — and sets it down somewhere else entirely.

**Not** "the act is hard." **Bal** — *rather* — "*they* are in confusion." The difficulty was never in the resurrection. The difficulty is in the *perceiver.* There is no obstacle in the heavens. The obstacle is in the human heart. The gap they called *baʿīd* — far — does not exist between the corpse and the standing person. It exists between the doubter and his own clarity.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how merciful and how merciless this is at once. Merciless, because it strips the doubter of his alibi: you cannot blame the difficulty of the thing; the thing is not difficult. Merciful, because it tells him exactly where to look if he wants to be free: not up at an impossible task, but in at a removable confusion. The whole obstacle is *inside*, which means the whole obstacle is *reachable.*

#### And now the word *labs* itself

The verse does not say *they doubt.* It says they are **فِى لَبْسٍ** — *in labs.* And this word, from the root **ل-ب-س**, is chosen with terrible precision.

al-Qurtubī defines it plainly: *fī ḥayratin min al-baʿth* — **"in bewilderment about the resurrection"** — and adds the lexical note: *yuqālu labasa ʿalayhi-l-amra yalbisuhu labsan* — "one says, *the matter was made confusing upon him,*" when truth and falsehood are mixed until you cannot tell them apart. *Labs* is the state where things are *mixed up*, where the true and the false have been stirred together until you can no longer separate them.

But hear what else lives in this root — because it is the same root, **ل-ب-س**, that gives the Arabic word **لِبَاس** (*libās*): *clothing, a garment.* The thing you *wear.* The thing that *covers.*

That is no accident of language. To be *in labs* is to be *clothed* in confusion — wrapped in it, covered by it, the way a garment covers the body. And the verse says they are **فِى** — *in* it. *Inside* it. Not glancing at confusion from the outside; *enveloped* by it, wearing it, living within it as you live within your clothes. It has become the medium they move through. It covers their sight.

#### What this reveals

Here is the full arc of the three words working together. The doubter accused God of *ʿayy* — of being at a loss, baffled, not knowing the way. And the verse, with one turn of *bal*, hands that very quality back to him, transfigured: *you* are the one in *labs* — *you* are the one baffled, the one for whom the matter is mixed up and the way is obscured. The incapacity you projected onto the heavens was a description of your own heart all along. He is not at a loss. *You* are — and you have wrapped the loss around yourself like a garment and called the resulting darkness *"a far return."*

### The Bridge

We have watched three things. A question that denies by asking — *were We ever at a loss?* — and answers itself: never. Two creations dressed in opposite grammar — the past made definite and owned, the future made indefinite and doubted — though the act is one and the same word. And a hinge, *bal*, that lifts the whole problem out of the heavens and sets it down inside the human chest, renaming it: not *a far act*, but *a near confusion*, worn like clothing.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the strange asymmetry in how you and I trust the past and doubt the future. And about what doubt actually *is* — whether it is a verdict we reach, or a garment we put on.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Improbable Thing You Already Survived

Begin not with resurrection. Begin with an ordinary Tuesday.

You woke up this morning inside a body of roughly thirty-seven trillion cells, each one running machinery so intricate that the smartest people alive have spent centuries only beginning to map it. A heart that has beaten, without your permission or attention, every second since before you were born. A brain reading these words by turning marks into meaning through a process no one fully understands. You did not build any of it. You did not ask for it. You woke up *inside the most improbable thing you will ever encounter*, and you found it so unremarkable that your first thought was probably about coffee.

Here is the strange thing about human beings. We treat *the thing that already happened to us* — our own staggering, improbable existence — as the baseline. The given. The boring. And we reserve our doubt for things that have *not yet* happened.

Think about how backwards that is. The genuinely astonishing event — that a smear of nothing became *you*, aware, alive, reading — that one already cleared. It happened. You are the proof. The odds against it were beyond counting, and yet here you are. That was the hard one. That was the one with no precedent, no template, the first-ever assembling of *you* from materials that had never been *you* before.

And what do we doubt? Not that. We doubt the *repeat.* We grant total certainty to the improbable thing because it already happened, and we withhold belief from the far easier thing — the doing-again of what has already been done once — purely because it sits in the future.

This is *exactly* the asymmetry the grammar of our verse exposed. **The first creation — definite, owned, real.** **A new creation — indefinite, vague, doubted.** And the quiet correction buried in the identical word *khalq* both times: *it is the same act.* You already survived the improbable version. The version you doubt is the sequel to a film you have already watched.

The Qur'an makes this argument again elsewhere, almost playfully. In Sūrah Yā Sīn, a man comes holding a rotted bone, crumbling it in his fingers, and demands: *"Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?"* And the answer:

> قُلْ يُحْيِيهَا ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنشَأَهَآ أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ
>
> *"Say: The One who produced them the first time will give them life."* (36:79)

*The One who produced them the first time.* It is the same argument as our verse, from the other side. There the focus is on God's "first time"; here it is on whether God was ever *at a loss* doing it. Both pin the doubter inside his own concession.

[PAUSE]

So let me bring it to your Tuesday. What would change if you genuinely held this — not as an argument you can win, but as a fact you live inside? If you treated your own existence as the *evidence* rather than the *background*?

Most of us doubt the future not because we have examined it and found it wanting, but because we have stopped *seeing* the past. We have let the most extraordinary fact about us — that we exist at all — fade into the wallpaper. And once the first creation becomes wallpaper, the second creation looks like fantasy. But the moment the first creation becomes *vivid* again — the moment you actually feel the improbability of the heartbeat you are not even noticing right now — the resurrection stops looking *baʿīd.* It starts looking like the most natural thing in the world: the Author finishing a sentence He has already begun.

**The practical turn:** Tonight, before sleep, do one small thing. Put your hand on your own chest and feel the beat you did not order and cannot stop. That is the first creation — definite, owned, undeniable, *the* creation. Let it be evidence again, just for a moment. The God who is doing *that*, right now, without your help, is not a God who will be *at a loss* to do it a second time.

### Theme Two: Doubt Is Not a Verdict. It Is a Garment.

We tend to think of doubt as a *conclusion.* As though the doubter is someone who weighed the evidence carefully, reasoned his way to the edge of belief, and stopped — honestly, intellectually — at *I'm not convinced.* Doubt as the responsible end-point of an investigation.

But our verse refuses that flattering picture. It does not say *they have reasoned and found resurrection wanting.* It says: **بَلْ هُمْ فِى لَبْسٍ** — they are *in* labs. They are *inside* confusion. And we saw what *labs* carries: the root of *libās*, clothing. Confusion not as a *verdict reached* but as a *garment worn.* A covering. Something they are wrapped in and moving through, which colors everything they see.

This is one of the most psychologically honest things in the Qur'an, and you have to be willing to look at your own life to feel it.

There is a difference between *not having the information* and *not wanting to see.* Between the doubt of the person who genuinely lacks the data, and the "doubt" of the person who has the data and finds it inconvenient — who has, quietly, *decided*, and then dressed the decision in the respectable clothing of *intellectual uncertainty.* The first is a problem to be solved. The second is a garment to be removed.

We all know the second kind from the inside, even if we would never admit it. There is a thing we suspect is true and do not want to be true, because if it were true we would have to *change.* And so we do not investigate it. We keep it vague. We keep it indefinite — *a* possibility, never *the* reality. We say *"it's complicated,"* *"who can really know,"* *"I have my doubts"* — and the doubt is not a finding. It is a coat we put on so we do not have to feel the cold of the truth.

Notice the verse's diagnosis is not *they are wrong.* It is *they are in labs* — they are in a *covered* state, a *mixed-up* state, where the true and the false have been stirred together precisely so that nothing has to be acted on. The garment of confusion is *useful.* It excuses. As long as the matter stays *mixed*, no decision is owed.

[PAUSE]

And here — return to the word *bal* one final time, because it carries the mercy. The verse could have left the disbelievers thinking the problem was *out there*, in the impossible distance of resurrection, in the limits of God's power. That would have been a *hopeless* place for the problem to live, because you cannot fix the heavens. Instead, *bal* relocates the problem to where it actually is: *inside them, in their labs.* And a problem that lives inside you is a problem you can *reach.* The garment that can be worn can be *taken off.*

This is the difference between *"resurrection is impossible"* — a wall you cannot move — and *"I am wrapped in a confusion I have chosen not to examine"* — a coat you can decide to remove. The verse is not condemning the doubter to his doubt. It is telling him, with surgical precision, that his doubt is *removable*, because it was never in the structure of reality. It was only ever in the structure of his looking.

**The practical turn:** Take the one thing you "have doubts" about — in your faith, in your direction, in some truth you keep at indefinite arm's length. And ask yourself the *labs* question honestly: *Is this a doubt of information, or a doubt of clothing? Am I uncertain because I lack the data — or have I kept it vague because certainty would cost me something?* You will usually know within seconds which one it is. And if it is the garment kind, the verse has already told you the good news hidden in the diagnosis: a garment can be removed. You are not stuck. You are dressed. Those are very different problems.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, look at how the seven words are built — because the structure *is* the argument.

The verse has two halves, and they mirror each other in perfect opposition:

**First half — aimed upward, at God:** *Were We at a loss?* — the verb *ʿayy*, being-baffled, **negated.** The question's only answer is *no.* So: **God is NOT at a loss.**

**Second half — turned by *bal*, aimed inward, at the human:** *they are in labs* — confusion, being-baffled, **affirmed.** So: **the human IS at a loss.**

Do you see it? The verse takes a single quality — *the state of being baffled, at a loss, not knowing the way* — and the first half **strips it off God** while the second half **fastens it onto the human being.** The doubters accused the Creator of incapacity. The verse takes that exact accusation, lifts it off the One it was falsely aimed at, and lays it down on the ones it was always actually describing. *He* is not the one who does not know the way. *You* are. The *ʿayy* you imagined in the heavens was a *labs* in your own chest the whole time.

And between the two halves stands the word *bal* — the hinge — performing the entire transfer in two letters. That is the architecture: an accusation, picked up off God and set down on the human, with one turn of a single word.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are one insight seen from different sides.

The grammar said: *the same act, the first creation dressed definite and the second dressed indefinite* — and the theme said: *you already survived the improbable thing; you only doubt its sequel because it sits in the future.* Same insight.

The grammar said: *bal relocates the problem from God's power to the human's labs, and labs is a garment* — and the theme said: *doubt is not a verdict you reached but a covering you wear, and a covering can be removed.* Same insight.

And the whole verse says: **the gap you called *baʿīd* — far — does not run between the corpse and the resurrection. It runs between you and your own clarity.** The distance was never in the act. It was in the looking. God was never at a loss. We were — and we called our own confusion the impossibility of God.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the most improbable thing that has *already happened* to you — and when did you last actually *see* it, instead of treating it as the boring background of your life?

2. Where in your life do you trust *"already happened"* as real and dismiss *"not yet"* as doubtful — and is that a law of reality, or just the shape of your own calendar?

3. Take the doubt you carry most honestly. Is it a doubt of *information* — or a doubt of *clothing*? Which would you rather it be, and why?

4. If God was never at a loss in making you the first time — out of nothing, with no model — what, exactly, are you imagining He would be unable to do?

5. The garment of confusion *excuses* you from acting. What, specifically, would you have to do differently the moment you took it off?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The One who made you from nothing is not the one who is at a loss — *you* are; resurrection was never a far act for God, only a near confusion you have wrapped around your own heart and mistaken for the limits of His power.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to win an argument tonight. You only have to do something quieter: stop treating your own existence as wallpaper, and stop treating your doubt as a verdict. Let the first creation become vivid again — the heartbeat, the breath, the trillion-celled improbability you woke up inside. And let the confusion become *visible as a garment* — something you are wearing, not something woven into reality. The God who was not at a loss the first time is not at a loss now. The only thing standing between you and that clarity is a coat. And coats come off.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, who originated us from nothing and was never once at a loss — let us not mistake our own confusion for the limit of Your power. Strip from our hearts the garment of *labs*, the covering we have worn so long we forgot it was not our skin, and let us see the first creation vividly enough to trust the second. You who finish what You begin — finish us well.

*Āmīn.*
