# Have You Been Filled?

## Introduction: The Day Hell Was Asked a Question

Let me first place the words in front of you, slowly.

> **يَوْمَ نَقُولُ لِجَهَنَّمَ هَلِ ٱمْتَلَأْتِ وَتَقُولُ هَلْ مِن مَّزِيدٍ**
>
> *Yawma naqūlu li-jahannama hali-mtala'ti wa taqūlu hal min mazīd.*
>
> "The Day We will say to Hell, **'Have you been filled?'** — and it will say, **'Is there any more?'**"

Nine words. One question asked, one question answered. And between those two questions sits one of the most disturbing exchanges in the entire Qur'an.

Notice, too, where the sentence begins: *"The Day…"* — *yawma.* The classical commentators (al-Muyassar makes it explicit) hear an unspoken command standing in front of it: *"Mention, O Messenger, to your people, the Day We say to Hell…"* The verse is not merely a report of a future scene. It is a *charge to recount it* — a warning to be carried, in the Prophet's own voice, into the hearts of those who laughed at the very idea of the Day. We are, in a sense, being handed the scene precisely so that we will speak it to ourselves.

[PAUSE]

I want you to rebuild the scene before we touch a single word of grammar.

Surah Qaf is a surah of *certainty arriving*. From its opening it has been pressing one truth against the chest of a people who laughed at it: *you are coming back*. You will be gathered. The earth that swallowed your body knows exactly what it took. The two angels are writing. And then the surah walks us, step by step, through that Day. The trumpet. The gathering. The book opened. The companion-angel who says, *here is the record, ready.* The witness. The verdict. The Garden, with its people brought near, settled, secure — *"enter it in peace; this is the Day of eternity."*

And just when the surah has shown us one house filled with light, it turns — and shows us the other house.

But notice *how* it shows it to us.

It does not describe the fire. It does not list the chains, the boiling water, the groaning. We have heard those descriptions elsewhere. Here, in Surah Qaf, at the climax of a surah about reckoning, Allah does something stranger and more piercing.

He *asks Hell a question.*

And Hell *answers back.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook I want you to carry into everything that follows. When Allah asks, *"Have you been filled?"* — there is only one answer that should make sense to us. After everything Surah Qaf has shown — the gathering of entire nations, the rejecters across every generation, the proud, the deniers, the tyrants of history poured into one place — surely the answer is: *"Yes. I am full. There is no more room."*

That is not the answer Hell gives.

Hell looks back and says: **"Is there any more?"**

Sit with the horror of that for a moment. The most capacious thing in all of creation, the thing built to hold the wreckage of every rebellion since Adam — and it is *still hungry.* It has not been satisfied. It wants more.

Why would Allah end the reckoning not with a verdict, but with an *appetite*? What is He showing us about Hell — and what, I will suggest before we are done, is He showing us about something much closer to home than Hell?

Let us go and see.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: "We Will Say" — When the King Speaks to the Fire

### First, the concept

In every language, there is a difference between *mentioning* something and *addressing* it.

If I say to you, "the door is heavy," I am mentioning the door. But if I turn to the door and say, *"Open"* — something shifts. To address a thing is to treat it as capable of being spoken *to*. We address people. We address God. We do not, in ordinary speech, address objects — and when we do, when a grieving man speaks to a grave or a sailor speaks to the sea, we understand that something extraordinary in the emotional register has happened. Address elevates. Address implies a listener.

Hold that. Because in this verse, Allah does not *mention* Hell. He *addresses* it.

### Now, the word

> **نَقُولُ لِجَهَنَّمَ** — *naqūlu li-jahannam* — "We will say *to* Hell"

The little preposition *li* — "to" — is doing enormous work. Allah is not narrating Hell. He is *speaking to* it. He turns to it as one turns to a listener, and He puts a question to it directly: *hali-mtala'ti* — "Have *you* been filled?" The verb carries the feminine "you" addressed straight at the fire.

Stay with this. In the world Surah Qaf has built, *everything answers to Allah directly.* The earth answered — it gave up what it held. The limbs will answer; elsewhere the Qur'an tells us the skin itself will speak. And here, the fire is spoken to as a *someone*, not a *something*.

[PAUSE]

### Notice the "We"

The verb is **نَقُولُ** — *naqūlu* — "**We** say." This is the *nūn al-ʿaẓama*, the plural of majesty. Not because Allah is many — the whole surah, the whole Qur'an, thunders His Oneness — but because this is the register of a King issuing a decree. When the sovereign speaks in the throne room, the language swells.

And here is a detail the classical reciters preserved for us. The companions of the Prophet ﷺ recited this in more than one way. Nāfiʿ and Abū Bakr read it *yaqūlu* — "**He** says," with the singular. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record this, noting the reading aligns with the earlier verse in the same surah, *"He will say: do not dispute before Me."* The majority read *naqūlu* — "**We** say" — the nūn of majesty, the King's voice.

Do not hear these as competing. Hear them as two camera angles on the same throne. One lens shows you *majesty* — the royal "We," the decree that fills the sky. The other lens shows you *intimacy of authority* — "He," the singular Lord, turning personally to address the fire. Together they tell you: this is not a mechanical event. This is the Sovereign, in His own voice, conducting the final accounting in person.

[PAUSE]

What does this single feature reveal? That on that Day, **nothing in creation stands outside the conversation with its Creator.** Not the proud who thought themselves unaccountable. Not the earth. Not even Hell. Everything is a listener. Everything answers when He speaks.

## Element Two: "Have You Been Filled?" — The Shape of the Verb That Fills From Within

### First, the concept

In Arabic, the *same root* can be poured into different molds, and each mold changes the meaning in a precise way. Think of a root as a raw material — say, the idea of "filling" — and the verb-forms as different machines that shape it. One machine makes it *I fill something* (I act on an object). Another machine makes it *it becomes filled* (the thing undergoes the filling within itself).

This matters enormously here, because Allah did not choose the simple form.

### The word

> **ٱمْتَلَأْتِ** — *imtala'ti* — "have you become filled / have you filled up?"

The root is *m-l-ʾ* (مـ-لـ-ء), the idea of fullness. The simplest form of this root is *malaʾa* — "he filled" — a transitive verb, one who fills a thing with another thing. *I filled the cup.*

But the verb in our verse is **Form VIII** — *iftaʿala* — *imtalaʾa.* The corpus confirms it: form VIII, an infixed *tāʾ* sitting inside the word. And Form VIII, classically, carries a reflexive, middle sense — the action turning back upon the subject, the subject *undergoing* the action within itself. *Imtalaʾa* does not mean "it was filled by someone." It means **"it filled up — it became full from within."**

Do you feel the difference? Allah does not ask, *"Has someone filled you?"* He asks, *"Have you become full?"* — as though the fullness is Hell's own interior state, something happening inside it, a vessel reporting on its own condition.

[PAUSE]

### Now hold this against another verse

Elsewhere, Allah makes a promise about this same fire. In Sūrah Hūd and in Sūrah al-Sajdah He swears: **لَأَمْلَأَنَّ جَهَنَّمَ مِنَ ٱلْجِنَّةِ وَٱلنَّاسِ أَجْمَعِينَ** — *la-amla'anna jahannama mina-l-jinnati wa-n-nāsi ajmaʿīn* — "I will **surely fill** Hell **with the jinn and mankind, all together**." That is the *simple* form, *malaʾa* — *I* fill it. The active hand of God, the King, pouring the consequence of rebellion into its place. And both al-Tabari and Ibn Kathīr fasten our verse directly to that oath: Hell is asked *"Have you been filled?"* — as al-Tabari puts it — *"on account of what had preceded: His promise to fill it with the jinn and mankind, all together."* Note what the oath specifies and what it does *not.* It names the *content* of the filling — jinn and mankind — but it sets no ceiling on the vessel. The promise was to fill it; it never said the fire would, by being filled, be *satisfied.*

So watch the two halves of the story fit together. In the promise: *I will fill it* — the Form I, the Lord as the one who fills. In our verse, on the Day the promise comes due: *have you become full?* — the Form VIII, the fire reporting its own inner state.

This is not an accident of grammar. This is the **fulfillment of a sworn oath being verified in person.** Al-Baghawī says exactly this: the question is asked *"because of what had preceded — His promise to it that He would fill it."* The King made a vow. Now, on the Day, He turns to the vessel and asks it to confirm: *Has My word come true in you? Have you become full?*

[PAUSE]

The verb-form is the hinge between promise and fulfillment. *He* fills (form I, the oath). *It* becomes full (form VIII, the verification). And the answer to whether the oath was kept — we are about to receive.

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: "Is There Any More?" — Two Words That Refuse to Sit Still

### First, the concept

Some sentences are closed. *"The room is full."* That sentence ends. It has a floor under it.

Other sentences are open at the bottom — they fall and keep falling. *"Is there any more?"* That sentence does not end. It reaches. And Arabic has a way of building a phrase so that it *stays* open, so that it cannot be sealed shut.

### The words

> **هَلْ مِن مَّزِيدٍ** — *hal min mazīd* — "Is there any more?"

Three pieces. *Hal* — the question particle, "is there…?" *Min* — here a particle of emphasis and generality, "any… at all." And *mazīd* — from the root *z-y-d*, increase — a noun left **indefinite**: not "the more," not "more of the jinn and mankind," just… *mazīd.* **Anything additional. At all. Of any kind.**

Notice the absence. Hell does not ask, *"is there more of the wrongdoers?"* It does not name what it wants. It leaves the object *unspecified, unbounded, open.* The grammar itself has no floor. *Is there… any… more.* The phrase reaches into empty space and finds no wall.

[PAUSE]

Listen, too, to the *sound* of these two key words side by side. *Imtala'ti* — heavy, clenched, the hamza catching in the throat, the closing *-ti* snapping shut: the sound of a thing sealed. And then *mazīd* — light, rising, the *zāy* buzzing forward, the long *-īd* opening outward into air. The question of fullness sounds *closed*; the answer sounds *open and ascending.* Even the phonetics enact the horror: you expect the door to shut, and instead it swings wide.

### Now — what does the answer *mean*?

Here the classical scholars give us not one reading but a composite, and each angle deepens the last. Al-Tabari records the disagreement plainly: *"the people of interpretation differed over its meaning."* This is a genuine *ikhtilāf* — not a single settled gloss — and both poles of it are worth holding.

**The first reading:** *istifhām inkārī* — a rhetorical question meaning a *denial.* On this reading, "Is there any more?" actually means: *"There is no more. I am full. Not a single space is left in me un-filled."* Al-Tabari reports this as the view of *some of* the people of interpretation — *"its meaning is: there is no more"* — and classical commentators name ʿAṭāʾ, Mujāhid, and Muqātil among them: *"I have become full, and no place remains that is not filled."* On this reading, Hell is confirming the oath kept — the King said *I will fill it*, and the fire answers, in effect, *You have; I can hold no more.*

**The second reading:** the question is *real* — *istifhām ḥaqīqī* — and Hell is genuinely *still asking.* Still hungry. Still reaching for more. Ibn Kathīr leans firmly here: he calls the genuine asking *"the apparent meaning of this Ayah,"* glossing the fire's words as *"Are there any more — any more whom You will provide me with?"* — and he notes it is *"supported by several hadiths."* Al-Muyassar reads the bare *mazīd* the same way, but restores its content by context: the fire is asking *"is there more — of the jinn and mankind?"* — reaching back, that is, for more of the exact thing it was filled with. Notice the loop this quietly closes. The oath in Sūrah Hūd and al-Sajdah named *jinn and mankind* as the filling; here the fire, filled with them, asks for *more of them still.* The word itself, as we saw, names nothing — *mazīd* leaves the object unwalled — yet the mufassir hears the fire straining specifically past even the oath's own measure: not content with what it was promised, it reaches beyond it. And this reading is anchored in the words of the Prophet ﷺ himself.

[PAUSE]

Here we must be precise, because this is a *marfūʿ* hadith — a narration traced through a sound chain to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ — recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The Prophet ﷺ said that Hell keeps saying *"Is there any more? Is there any more?"* — *until the Lord of Might places His Foot upon it* (or, in another wording, *His qadam*), and at that, Hell draws in on itself and says: *qaṭ, qaṭ* — *"Enough! Enough!"* Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar preserve a detail worth pausing on: when the limit is set, *"its parts fold in upon one another"* — *yanzawī baʿḍuhā ilā baʿḍ* — and it is *out of that constriction*, that being-pressed-in, that the word *qaṭ* is finally forced from it. Mark this precisely: the fire does not say *"enough"* because it has, at last, grown content. It says *"enough"* because it has been *crushed to its limit.* The only "enough" the fire ever speaks is one imposed from outside — never one it chose.

Two things must be said here, carefully. First, the status: this is the *speech of the Prophet ﷺ*, not merely a Companion's comment — it carries the weight of an authentic marfūʿ report. Second, the meaning: the *qadam* of Allah is among the texts that Ahl al-Sunna affirm **as it came, without asking "how," without likening it to creation, and without stripping it of meaning** — *bilā kayf.* We do not picture it; we do not deny it; we leave its reality with the One who said it. What the report establishes for our reflection is this: **Hell's hunger is not ended by Hell ever saying "enough." It is ended only when its Lord brings it to its limit.** Left to itself, the fire would never stop asking.

[PAUSE]

Do you see how the two readings, far from contradicting, complete each other? On one reading the fire is *full and confirms it.* On the other the fire is *full and still reaching.* And the second is the more terrible, because it tells us something about the *nature* of this hunger: **fullness and craving are not opposites here.** The fire can be filled to its utmost — and still ask for more. Satisfaction does not arrive with fullness. The appetite is not a function of emptiness that fullness would cure. The appetite is *what the thing is.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So what has the language shown us?

A King who *addresses* the fire as a listener. A verb-form that turns the fire into a vessel reporting its own inner fullness — the fulfillment of a sworn oath. And an answer with no floor under it: *is there any more*, reaching into open air, a hunger that fullness itself cannot close, stopped only when its Lord lays down the limit.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words on a Day yet to come — but about reality itself, and about a hunger that may be sitting inside your own chest as you listen.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Voice That Says "Is There Any More?" Is Already Speaking Inside You

### Begin where you live

Let me take you out of the next world and into a Tuesday afternoon.

You wanted a thing. A phone, a promotion, a number in an account, a follower count, a relationship, a degree, a house. You wanted it with your whole body. You told yourself — and you *believed* yourself — that when you had it, you would rest. *When I get this, I'll finally feel settled.*

And then you got it.

And do you remember the strangest part? Not that the satisfaction was small. That it was *brief.* Within days, sometimes within hours, the thing you had ached for slid quietly into the background of normal, and a new ache stood up in its place. Already your eye had moved to the next thing. Already a voice in you was saying — though you may never have heard it in words — *is there any more?*

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it the *hedonic treadmill.* You run and run, each acquisition lifting you for a moment, and then the floor rises to meet you and you are back where you started, running again. The economists of attention build entire industries on it. The feed never ends. The scroll has no bottom. *Hal min mazīd* is not a verse about a distant fire. **It is the slogan written across the operating system of the modern world.** *More. Is there more. Show me more.*

[PAUSE]

### Now let the verse illuminate it

Here is what the verse does that nothing else does. It takes that quiet, restless voice — *is there any more* — and it shows you where that voice leads when it is allowed to become the whole of a creature.

It becomes Hell.

I want to be very careful and very honest here. The verse is describing the literal fire on the literal Day. I am not dissolving that into a metaphor. But the Qur'an's images are *stepping stones* — Allah shows us the seen to point us toward the unseen. And He has placed, at the climax of His reckoning, a creature whose entire identity has collapsed into a single sentence: *is there any more.* No gratitude. No rest. No "enough." Just appetite, reaching into the dark.

And the terrifying gift of this verse is that **it lets you recognize the voice.** Because you have heard it. It has spoken in you. The difference between you and the fire is not that you lack the hunger. The difference is that you still have *time* — time to discover whether you will master that voice, or be mastered by it until it is *all that is left of you.*

[PAUSE]

### Follow it deeper

Why does fullness not silence the hunger? We saw it in the grammar: the fire is *filled* and still asks. The Qur'an diagnoses this exact disease in the human heart elsewhere. *"Competition for more"* — *al-takāthur* — *"distracts you, until you visit the graves."* More, until the grave. The hunger does not stop at fullness because the hunger was never really about the object. The phone was never the point. The promotion was never the point. The hunger is a shape the soul takes when it has turned away from the only One who fills.

And here is the mercy hidden in the horror. There is a hunger in you that *was* designed to be infinite — but it was designed to reach toward the Infinite. The heart that says *is there any more* was built to keep reaching past every created thing precisely because no created thing is its destination. Aimed at *dunyā*, that infinite reach becomes the fire's question — bottomless, tormenting, never closing. Aimed at Allah, that same infinite reach becomes *worship* — the soul forever ascending toward a Lord who is forever inexhaustible. The same engine. Two destinations. One of them is Hell, and one of them is the Garden.

[PAUSE]

### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical edge, and it is sharp. The next time you feel the pull — *when I get this, I'll finally rest* — I want you to recognize the voice for what it is. It is *hal min mazīd.* It is the question Hell asks. And you can answer it in this life, before it answers itself on that Day.

You answer it with one word that the fire could never say until its Lord forced the limit upon it: **"Enough."** *Qaṭ.* The discipline of saying, deliberately, *"This is enough. Alhamdulillah. I have what I need."* Of letting a desire arrive and *not* immediately reaching past it. Of practicing *qanāʿah* — contentment — as the daily training that teaches your soul to say "enough" now, by choice, so that it is not a word forced upon you later, in a place where it comes too late.

## Theme Two: A Universe Where Even the Fire Answers — and So Will You

### Begin where you live

There is a particular comfort the human heart secretly leans on, and almost no one admits it. It is the comfort of *not being addressed.*

We imagine that vast stretches of our lives are *unwitnessed* — that they happen in a kind of neutral space where no one is asking us anything. The private scroll at 2am. The word said about someone when they left the room. The small cruelty no one saw. The intention behind the generous-looking act. We move through these as if they fall outside the conversation — as if they are *mentioned* by no one, addressed by no one, asked about by no one. Unspoken to, and therefore safe.

[PAUSE]

### Now let the verse illuminate it

And then comes this verse, and it quietly demolishes the whole comfort.

Because look at *who* gets addressed on that Day. **Hell itself.** A created thing. And Allah turns to it — *naqūlu li-jahannam* — and puts a question to it, directly, and it *answers.* If the *fire* is brought into the conversation, if even Hell is asked and even Hell replies — then what in all of creation stands outside that conversation?

Surah Qaf has been preparing this the whole way. The earth was addressed and gave up its dead. The two angels write down *"not a word does he utter but there is a watcher ready"* — your very speech is being received, recorded, by a listener. The limbs will testify. And now the fire speaks. The universe of Surah Qaf is not a neutral space dotted with a few witnesses. **It is a universe that is entirely *responsive* — where every created thing is a listener, capable of being addressed and capable of answering.** There is no unwitnessed corner. There never was. The 2am scroll, the word in the empty room — these were never outside the conversation. They were always being heard.

[PAUSE]

### Follow it deeper

And see what this does to the *fear* of that Day, and then what it does to *hope.*

The fear first: if even the fire answers honestly — *I am full / is there more* — then your own faculties will answer honestly too. There will be no managing the narrative on that Day, no spin, no curated version. The fire does not lie about its state; it simply *reports* — *imtala'ti*, the vessel telling its own inner truth. So will your skin. So will your tongue. The most private truth of what you actually were will be *spoken*, plainly, into the throne room.

But now the hope — and this is where the personification turns from terror toward awe. The fact that Allah *speaks to* Hell, that He conducts even this in His own voice, addressing, asking, receiving the answer — tells you that **the entire event is personal and intentional, conducted by a Lord who is present, not by a blind machine.** And there is a thread in the grammar that makes this unmistakable. Al-Tabari, reading closely, notes that *"the Day We say to Hell"* hangs grammatically from the verse just before it — *"I am not unjust to the servants"* (*wa-mā ana bi-ẓallāmin lil-ʿabīd*, 50:29). The whole terrifying exchange with the fire is set, by the very syntax, *under the banner of divine justice.* The address to Hell is not the Lord venting wrath; it is the Lord demonstrating, in person, that every soul placed there was placed there *justly* — *"I am not unjust to the servants… on the Day We say to Hell, have you been filled?"* Justice on that Day is not a mechanism that grinds. It is a *King who attends to every detail in person* — who does not even let the fire be filled without turning to ask it directly. A Lord this attentive to the fire is a Lord attentive to *you.* The same voice that asks Hell *"have you been filled?"* is the voice that knows precisely what you carried, what you struggled against, what you whispered in the dark hoping He heard. He heard. He always hears. That is the terror — and for the one who turned to Him, it is the whole of the hope.

[PAUSE]

### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the second practical edge. *Live as one who is addressed.*

Not surveilled — *addressed.* There is a world of difference. The surveilled man hides. The addressed man *answers.* To know that you stand inside a conversation with your Lord at every moment — that your scroll, your word, your intention is not falling into neutral space but is being *received* by the One who will one day ask the fire itself to speak — this is not meant to crush you into paranoia. It is meant to lift the smallest private act into an act of *relationship.* The thing no one sees is the thing He sees. So make the unwitnessed moment your most honest worship: the quiet *dhikr* no one hears, the temptation refused when no one would have known, the intention purified in the dark. Live tomorrow as a creature who is *spoken to* — and answer well.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: Two Questions, Facing Each Other

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole verse, which we could not see until we had done the work.

The verse is built of **two questions that mirror and answer each other.**

The first question descends *from the King to the fire*: **"Have you been filled?"** — *hali-mtala'ti.* It is the voice of majesty, verifying a sworn oath, asking the vessel to report.

The second question rises *from the fire back toward the King*: **"Is there any more?"** — *hal min mazīd.* It is the voice of bottomless appetite, reaching into open air.

One question comes down with authority and closes — *the oath is kept, are you full.* The other rises up with hunger and refuses to close — *more, is there more.* And the entire reckoning of Surah Qaf comes to rest on the tension between them: the King who sets the limit, and the appetite that would never set its own.

[PAUSE]

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight all along, approached from two directions.

The *grammar* showed us a fire that is *filled* (form VIII, from within) and *still asks* (indefinite, floorless *mazīd*) — fullness and craving coexisting, the appetite that no amount of satisfaction can close. The *themes* showed us that this is not a fact about fire only. It is the diagnosis of the untrained human heart: *al-takāthur*, the hunger that runs past every acquisition straight to the grave — and the mercy that the very same infinite reach, turned toward Allah, becomes worship instead of torment.

The grammar showed us a King who *addresses* the fire and receives its answer. The themes showed us that this is a universe with no unwitnessed corner — where to be a creature is to be *spoken to*, and where the terror of being fully known is also, for the one who turned back, the whole of the hope.

One verse. The hunger that must learn to say "enough" — and the Lord to whom even the fire must answer.

## Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I said the word *"enough"* — and meant it — about something I wanted? When did I last let a desire arrive and *not* immediately reach past it?
2. What is the *mazīd* — the "more" — that I am currently telling myself will finally settle me? And do I actually believe, in honest moments, that it will?
3. Is my infinite reach aimed at the dunyā, where it becomes a fire's question — or at Allah, where it becomes worship that never needs to end?
4. Where in my life do I still behave as though I am *unaddressed* — in a neutral space, asked nothing by no one?
5. If even Hell reports its inner state truthfully on that Day, what is the inner state that my own faculties would report about me right now?

## One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day the King turns to the fire and asks if His oath is fulfilled, the fire — filled to its limit — still answers *"is there any more,"* revealing that an appetite which never learns to say *enough* does not end in satisfaction but in Hell, and that nothing in creation, not even the fire, stands outside its Lord's address.

## The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this verse thinking only about a fire far away. Leave it having heard the voice. *Hal min mazīd* — *is there any more* — is a voice you already know, because it has spoken in you, on a Tuesday afternoon, just after you got the thing you were sure would make you rest. The invitation of this verse is not fear. It is *time.* You still have the time the fire does not have — the time to teach your soul the one word that ends the hunger before the hunger ends you: *enough. Alhamdulillah. He is enough.*

And to live, from today, as one who is not surveilled but *addressed* — spoken to at every moment by the same Lord who speaks to the fire itself — and to answer Him well.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who fill Hell by Your word and set its limit by Your decree — set a limit upon the hunger in our hearts. Teach our souls to say "enough" while saying it still avails us, and turn our every reaching toward You, the only One who fills and is never exhausted. Make us, on the Day You address the fire, among those whom You address with peace. Āmīn.*
