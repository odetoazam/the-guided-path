# The God Who Never Tires: The Touch That Never Came

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the world they fell into.

> وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِن لُّغُوبٍ

*Wa laqad khalaqnā as-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa wa mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin wa mā massanā min lughūb.*

**"And certainly We created the heavens and the earth and all that is between them in six days — and no fatigue touched Us."**

Now. On the surface this reads like a verse about cosmology. Six days. The heavens, the earth, and everything in between. It sounds like the verse is telling us *how* the universe was made.

It isn't. Watch where the sentence actually lands. It builds the entire scale of creation — and then spends its final, heaviest word not on the creation at all, but on the **Creator's experience of making it.** And what does it say about that experience? Not that it was glorious. Not that it was vast. It says: *nothing touched Him.*

That is a strange way to end a verse about the universe. Why, after building everything that exists, would Allah's closing word be about what He did *not* feel?

Let me rebuild the room this verse was spoken into.

### The world this verse entered

Surah Qaf is, from its first breath, a surah about one wound in the human heart: the inability to believe we will be brought back. Look at how it opens. The disbelievers are astonished — not angry, *astonished* — "How can this be? When we have died and become dust, **that** is a far, far return" (50:3). A *baʿīd* return. A return too distant to imagine.

And so the whole surah becomes Allah's patient, almost tender response to that astonishment. He doesn't shout them down. He walks them outside. *Look up at the sky — have We not built it and adorned it without a single crack?* (50:6). *Look at the dead earth — We send down water and out come gardens and grain and tall palms heavy with fruit* (50:9–11). And then, the line that detonates the whole argument: **"كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ" — Thus is the coming forth"** (50:11). *That* — the green shoot out of dead ground — *that* is what your own resurrection looks like.

So by the time we arrive at our verse near the end of the surah, Allah has spent the whole surah making one case: *the One who gives life is not exhausted by giving it again.* And now He moves the argument all the way back to the beginning — to the first act, the creation of everything — to close the case for good.

But there's a second thing happening in this verse. A quieter thing. There was a claim circulating — recorded by the classical commentators, by al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī and others — a claim that God created the world in six days **and then rested.** That He needed the seventh day. That the act had cost Him something, and He had to recover.

This verse is the answer to *both* — to the one who cannot believe in resurrection, and to the one who imagined God catching His breath.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hunger for as we go in.

Allah does not simply say "We do not get tired." He says something far more precise, and far more astonishing. He chooses a word for the *lightest possible contact* — the faintest brush, a graze — and He chooses a word for the *heaviest, most bone-deep exhaustion* a body can know. And then He puts a tiny particle between them whose only job is to slam the door completely shut.

The lightest touch. The heaviest fatigue. And total denial that the two ever met.

[PAUSE]

By the time we are done, I want you to understand why the deepest form of idolatry the human heart commits is not bowing to a statue. It is something we *all* do, quietly, every day — and this verse is the cure for it.

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — مَسَّنَا: The verb of the faintest touch

Let me teach you something about how Arabic — and really, how every language — chooses verbs for *contact*, before we look at the word Allah chose.

Think about the difference between these English sentences. "The car *hit* me." "The car *struck* me." "The car *brushed* me." "The car *grazed* me." They describe the same category of event — contact — but they live on completely different points of a scale. *Hit* and *struck* are heavy, full, forceful. *Brushed* and *grazed* are the lightest end — barely contact at all, the kind of touch you might not even notice, a thing passing close enough to make the smallest contact and moving on.

Every language has a word that sits at that lightest end. The whisper of contact. The graze.

In Arabic, that word is from the root **م-س-س, *mass*.**

Now look at what Allah says: **وَمَا مَسَّنَا — *wa mā massanā* — "and there did not *touch* Us."**

Not "we were not exhausted." Not "we did not labor." The verb is *mass* — the verb of the faintest graze.

Let's sit with this, because the choice is deliberate and it is everywhere in the Quran. This is the same verb used when Ayyūb (Job), in the depth of his suffering, cries out: *"annī massaniya aḍ-ḍurr"* — "harm has *touched* me" (21:83). It's the verb used for the way temptation *touches* the God-conscious: *"idhā massahum ṭā'ifun mina ash-shayṭān"* (7:201). It's the verb for the lightest brush of something against you. *Mass* is intimate, minimal, a single point of contact.

So notice what Allah is doing. He is not saying, "the enormous labor of building galaxies did not exhaust Us." That would still leave you imagining that *some* effort registered, just not enough to break Him. No. He drops the claim all the way down to the faintest end of the scale. **Not even the lightest brush. Not even a graze. Not even the smallest particle of it ever made contact at all.**

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. When you want to deny something *completely*, you don't deny the big version of it — you deny the smallest version of it. If I want to tell you I am not in the slightest bit angry, I don't say "I am not furious" — because you could still imagine me mildly irritated. I say "I am not the *least* bit bothered." You negate the smallest unit, and the larger ones collapse automatically behind it.

That is exactly the architecture here. By denying that even the faintest *touch* of fatigue ever reached Him, Allah forecloses the entire scale. If the lightest graze never landed, then certainly nothing heavier did. The smallest denial is the most total denial.

One word — *massanā* — and the entire possibility of divine exhaustion, from the faintest flicker to total collapse, is swept off the table.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — مِن لُّغُوبٍ: The heaviest word, and the particle that seals it

Now we come to the word the whole verse has been walking toward. And we need to look at two things together: the word *lughūb*, and the tiny word that sits in front of it — *min*.

First, the heavy word. **لُّغُوب — *lughūb*.**

Let me teach the concept before we open it. Arabic, like every rich language, does not have one word for "tired." It has a graded vocabulary of exhaustion. There is the light tiredness of someone who could keep going. There is *taʿab*, ordinary fatigue. And then there is the word at the *bottom* of the well — the exhaustion that comes *after* exertion, the depletion where the body has nothing left, the collapse, the wiped-out, bone-deep weariness. The lexicographers and the classical mufassirūn are precise about this. Al-Qurṭubī glosses *lughūb* directly: **"*al-laghūb: at-taʿab wa-l-iʿyā'*"** — *lughūb* is exhaustion and the state of being utterly worn out. Al-Baghawī: *"iʿyā' wa taʿab."* It is not the tiredness you shake off. It is the exhaustion you have to *recover* from.

And now — say the word out loud. *Lughūb.* Feel it in your mouth. That heavy guttural غ — *ghayn* — rising from the back of the throat, and the dragging *ū*, and the closing *b*. The word is *heavy*. It is laborious to say. It sits in the mouth like a weight. **The word itself sounds like the thing it names.** A word for exhaustion that exhausts you slightly just to pronounce it.

So look at what Allah has assembled. He took the *lightest* verb in the language — *mass*, the graze — and aimed it at the *heaviest* noun for exhaustion — *lughūb*, the bone-deep collapse. The faintest touch of the deepest depletion. He is reaching for the two extreme ends of the scale at once and declaring that they never met.

[PAUSE]

Now — the tiny word. And this is where the grammar does something most translations cannot carry.

He does not say *wa mā massanā lughūb* — "no fatigue touched Us." He inserts a particle: **وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِن لُّغُوبٍ — *wa mā massanā* ***min*** *lughūb*.**

That *min* is doing something specific. In a sentence that is already negated — *mā massanā*, "there did not touch Us" — a *min* placed before the noun is what the grammarians call **مِن الزائدة, *min az-zā'idah*** — the so-called "extra" *min*. But "extra" is a terrible name for it, because it is not extra at all. Its entire function is **emphasis of total negation.** It takes the noun behind it and erases it down to its last conceivable particle.

Without the *min*: "no fatigue touched Us" — you could still picture *some* fatigue, just not enough to count.
With the *min*: **"there did not touch Us so much as a *particle* of fatigue — not the smallest unit of it, not an atom, nothing of it whatsoever."**

The *min* converts a general denial into an absolute, exhaustive, down-to-zero denial. It is the grammatical equivalent of saying not "I have no money" but "I do not have a single *cent*."

[PAUSE]

So now assemble the whole machine and watch it work:

- **مَا** — the negation: *not.*
- **مَسَّنَا** — the lightest verb of contact: *did so much as graze Us.*
- **مِن** — the particle of total exhaustion-of-the-category: *of even the smallest particle.*
- **لُّغُوبٍ** — the heaviest, most crushing noun for exhaustion: *of bone-deep, wiped-out collapse.*

Read it back the way the Arabic actually says it: **"There did not so much as graze Us the faintest particle of the most crushing exhaustion."**

Three separate intensifiers — the lightest verb, the totalizing particle, the heaviest noun — all stacked to do one job: to seal the door of divine fatigue so completely that not a crack remains. This is not a casual "God doesn't get tired." This is the most airtight denial the Arabic language is capable of constructing.

What this single phrase reveals: **the Creator's power has no relationship to the size of the task.** A thing that *costs* you leaves a trace — a touch, a graze, the smallest residue of effort. Allah declares there was *no trace at all.* And a power that leaves no trace, no matter the magnitude of what it produces, is a power of an entirely different kind than anything we have ever known.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three — The absence: what He created, and what He did *not* say

Now read the verse one more time and listen for what is *not* there.

> "We created the heavens and the earth **and all that is between them** in six days, and no fatigue touched Us."

Notice the scope first. He does not just say "the heavens and the earth." He adds **وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا — *wa mā baynahumā* — "and all that is between them."** This is the totalizing phrase. The two great poles of creation — the highest heavens, the lowest earth — and then *everything packed into the space between.* Every star, every ocean, every mountain, every cell, every breath of wind, every creature that has ever lived. Nothing is excluded. The phrase is built to leave nothing out.

So Allah maximizes the input. He makes the task as large as language can make it — *everything that exists.* And against that maximum input, He sets the output of effort at **zero.** The greater the work He names, the more staggering the *no fatigue* becomes. The two halves of the verse are deliberately weighted against each other: infinite production, zero cost.

But here is the absence I most want you to hear — and it lives in the historical moment we built at the start.

There was a claim, recorded by the commentators, that God created in six days **and then rested on the seventh.** Now watch what Allah does. He affirms the part of the picture that is true — *yes, six periods, the heavens and earth and all between.* He keeps the structure. And then, exactly where the false claim expected the next words to be — *"and then He rested"* — Allah puts instead: **"and no fatigue touched Us."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the precision of that? He answers the claim by *occupying its own sentence.* He lets you build the whole expectation — six days of labor, the work complete — and then at the precise hinge where a human story would say "and He rested," He says the opposite. *Rest implies prior fatigue. There was no fatigue. So there was nothing to rest from.* The al-Jalālayn commentary makes exactly this point: the verse came down rebutting the notion of divine rest, "because He is exalted far above the attributes of created things, and because there is no likeness between Him and anything else."

The *absence* of the word "rested" — in the one place the false story most expected it — is itself the argument. He didn't have to refute the claim with a separate sentence. He refuted it by what He *withheld* at the decisive moment.

This is why I told you at the start: read the verse for what it does *not* say. The whole rebuttal lives in a missing word.

### Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us.

The verb *mass* told us: not even the faintest brush. The particle *min* and the heavy word *lughūb* told us: not the smallest particle of the deepest exhaustion. And the missing word — *rested* — told us: the very category of recovery does not apply to Him, because the category of depletion never did.

But this is not finally a lesson about Arabic grammar. The grammar is the doorway. Let's now ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange thing the human heart does when it tries to imagine God.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — Everything you build, costs you. That is the thing you cannot un-feel.

Let me start where you actually live.

You know the feeling of the end of a long day. Not the pleasant tiredness of a good walk — the *other* one. The one where you've given everything you had to give and there is a kind of hollowed-out flatness behind your eyes. Maybe it was a project at work that took weeks and left you depleted in a way sleep didn't quite fix. Maybe it was caring for a sick child through the night, or sitting with a dying parent, or just the slow grind of holding a hard life together. There is a specific human truth underneath all of it:

**Everything you create, costs you something. Every act of making draws the maker down.**

This is so woven into our existence that we never even question it. The painter is spent after the canvas. The mother is depleted after the birth. The builder's back aches after the house. The more you pour into a thing, the more of *yourself* is gone when it's done. Effort and depletion are, for us, two names for the same event. To do is to be diminished by the doing. We have *never once* in our entire lives experienced an act of creation that did not subtract something from us.

And so — and this is the subtle thing — **we cannot imagine a power that doesn't work this way.** When we try to picture God making the universe, some hidden part of us pictures effort. Strain. A vast labor. We picture the *biggest possible version of ourselves* doing the biggest possible job. And a being who labors at a vast job would, surely, need to rest.

That is *exactly* the picture the verse reaches into and dismantles.

Let the ayah illuminate the experience now. *Wa mā massanā min lughūb.* Allah is telling you: the assumption you have never once questioned — that making costs the maker — **does not apply to Me at all.** Not partly. Not mostly. Not "I get tired but recover quickly." *Not the faintest particle of fatigue ever touched Me.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the first movement of this. We tend to measure God's greatness by the *size* of what He made. The vastness of the cosmos. The billions of galaxies. And that is good — but the verse is pointing at something deeper than size. **It is not the magnitude of the creation that reveals His power. It is the absence of cost.** Anyone powerful enough could, in principle, make a big thing at great effort. What is *unimaginable* — what has no parallel in our experience whatsoever — is making *everything* at *no* effort. The heavens and earth and all between them produced with less than the residue of a graze.

A god who made the universe and was tired afterward would be a *large* being. The God of this verse is not a large being. He is a being of an entirely different *category* — One for whom the relationship between effort and outcome, the iron law of our every waking moment, simply does not exist.

Second movement. Listen to what al-Muyassar says this verse is actually *for*: "In this immense power there is proof of His ability — *min bāb awlā* — all the more so — to bring the dead back to life." Do you see the argument the whole surah has been building toward? The people couldn't believe in resurrection because, in *their* economy, raising the dead would be an enormous second labor — and surely God, having spent Himself on the first creation, would be in no state for a second. The verse cuts the root of that thought. **The first creation cost Him nothing. So the second one — your resurrection — costs Him nothing either.** There is no "running low." There is no reserve being depleted. The One who built the cosmos without a graze of fatigue is not going to find *you* difficult to raise.

This is the consolation buried in a verse that looks like cosmology. **Your return is not a strain on the system.** You are not asking too much of God by hoping to be raised, remembered, restored, brought back to those you've lost. The God you are asking is not running out.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. When you pray — when you ask Him for the impossible thing, the healing that seems too far gone, the heart that seems too hardened, the situation that seems too tangled — notice the secret assumption that you are *asking a lot.* That somewhere in you, you are rationing your requests because surely there's a limit, surely you shouldn't ask for too much. That instinct is the instinct of a creature who knows that everything costs. **Allah is telling you it doesn't cost Him.** *Wa mā massanā min lughūb.* Ask for the large thing. The largeness is in your imagination, not in His capacity.

### Theme Two — The quietest idolatry: making God in our own image

Now let me take you to the deeper wound, the one I promised at the start.

We think of *shirk* — of idolatry — as a dramatic, ancient thing. Statues. Idols of stone. Something other people did, long ago, in places far from us. But there is a form of it so subtle that it lives inside the believing heart, and most of us never notice it our whole lives.

**It is the habit of imagining God in our own image — of projecting our limitations onto Him.**

Go back to the claim this verse answered. Where did the idea that "God rested on the seventh day" *come from?* It didn't come from observing God. It came from observing *ourselves.* A human being who worked six days would need the seventh to recover — so the human mind, reaching up to imagine the Creator, simply *enlarged the human picture* and handed God a day off. It took the most basic fact of *our* nature — we tire, we rest — and draped it over the Divine.

That is the move. That is the quiet idolatry. Not denying God — *shrinking* Him. Building a God in the shape of an exhausted laborer because that is the only kind of maker we have ever met.

[PAUSE]

And al-Jalālayn's commentary puts its finger precisely on the cure. Why is fatigue impossible for Him? **"Because He is exalted far above the attributes of created things — *li-tanazzuhihi taʿālā ʿan ṣifāt al-makhlūqīn* — and because there is no likeness between Him and anything else — *li-ʿadam al-mumāthalah baynahu wa bayna ghayrih*."** This is the great Quranic refrain: *laysa ka-mithlihī shay'* — "there is nothing like unto Him" (42:11). And our verse is one of its sharpest applications. The instant you attach *your* exhaustion to God, you have made Him *like* something — and the moment He is *like* something, He is no longer Allah. He is a projection. A mirror. A larger you.

Let me show you how alive this is, because the six-days-and-rest story is just the obvious version. The subtle version is *yours.* Watch how often we do it:

We assume God must be *fed up* with us — because *we* would be fed up with someone who failed this many times.
We assume He keeps a ledger of resentment — because *we* keep ledgers.
We assume He'll grow tired of our repeated repentance — because *our* patience runs out, so surely His does.
We assume that after a certain amount of sin, He's "done" with us — because *we* reach the end of our forgiveness, so we picture Him reaching the end of His.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? Every one of these is the *exact same error* as the six-days-and-rested error. It is taking a real feature of the *created* heart — its finiteness, its depletion, its limits — and projecting it up onto the One of whom it is said: *no fatigue touched Him.* If the bone-deep exhaustion of building the entire cosmos never *grazed* Him, then your hundredth return to Him does not deplete some reserve of divine patience. **There is no reserve. There is no running low. The One who does not tire from making galaxies does not tire of your coming back.**

This is the same attribute — the *no-fatigue* — turned from the cosmos toward your soul. *Lughūb* is not only about physical exhaustion. It is the name for *everything in us that wears out.* And the verse says: none of it, not a particle, touches Him.

Now connect this to the universal struggle. Every human being, in their relationship with God, is fighting a slow gravitational pull to drag Him down to human size — to make Him manageable, predictable, *like us.* Sometimes we shrink Him into a tyrant who is always disappointed. Sometimes into a vending machine who owes us. Sometimes into a tired old judge who has stopped caring. **All of these are the seventh-day God.** All of them are the laborer who needs to rest. And this verse, dropped into the middle of a surah about resurrection, quietly demolishes every one of them.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical work, and it is genuinely hard. The next time you feel that specific spiritual heaviness — *"surely He's tired of me by now," "surely I've used up my chances," "surely He's as fed up as I would be"* — I want you to catch it and name it for what it is. It is not humility. It *feels* like humility, but it is actually a small act of idolatry: you are dressing God in your own exhaustion. And then say the verse back to yourself: *wa mā massanā min lughūb.* The faintest particle of weariness never touched Him. Not at the creation of everything. And not at your return. **Let Him be God-sized. Stop handing Him your limits.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two journeys we took were the same journey from two directions.

The **linguistic** path took us *down* — from the lightest verb (*mass*, the graze), through the totalizing particle (*min*, not a single atom), to the heaviest noun (*lughūb*, bone-deep collapse) — and showed us a denial so complete that not a crack remained. And then it showed us the *missing word* — "rested" — withheld at the exact hinge where a human story demanded it.

The **thematic** path took us *up* — from the most basic fact of our existence (everything we make costs us, everything we are wears out) to the realization that we keep dressing God in that very fact, building a tired laborer who needs a day off, who runs out of patience, who gets fed up.

And the two paths meet at one point. **The grammar's airtight denial of divine fatigue is the exact cure for the heart's quiet habit of projecting human fatigue onto God.** The verse uses the most total negation the Arabic language can build — *because* the error it is healing is the most stubborn one the human heart commits. It needed that much sealing. The smallest crack — "well, maybe He gets *slightly* tired of me" — and the whole idol creeps back in. So Allah left no crack.

### The architecture

And now see the shape of the whole. The verse hands you the *maximum* of creation — heavens, earth, and all between, in six periods — and then sets against it the *zero* of cost. Infinite output. No trace of effort. That asymmetry *is* the verse. It is built like a scale with everything that exists piled on one side, and on the other side, nothing — not a graze. And the verse is telling you: that is what divine power looks like. Not a large effort. *No* effort. A different category of being entirely.

And remember *where* this sits — near the end of a surah whose deniers said resurrection was a "far return." The verse's final answer to them is its own architecture: if making *everything* cost Him nothing, then remaking *you* costs Him nothing. The far return was never far for Him. It was only far for a heart that measures God by its own exhaustion.

### Questions to carry

- Where in my life have I been *rationing* my requests to God — quietly assuming I shouldn't ask for too much, as though His capacity could run low?
- When I feel "He must be tired of me by now," whose exhaustion am I actually describing — His, or mine?
- What would change in how I pray if I truly believed that the largeness of my request is in my imagination and not in His capacity?
- In what ways have I, without noticing, built a "seventh-day God" — a Creator shaped like a tired, fed-up, limited version of a human being?
- If raising the entire dead earth into gardens "costs" Him nothing, what does that say about the thing in my life I have written off as too far gone?

### One-sentence distillation

**The One who made everything without the faintest trace of effort cannot run low on the power to raise you, forgive you, or bring you back — so stop measuring God by your own exhaustion.**

### The closing invitation

I'm not asking you to memorize a point of grammar. I'm inviting you to let one phrase stand guard over your heart. *Wa mā massanā min lughūb.* The next time the heaviness comes — the sense that you've worn out your welcome, used up your chances, asked too much, returned too often — let this verse rise up and answer it. He did not tire making the heavens and the earth and everything between them. He will not tire of you. Let Him be the God He actually is: the One whom nothing depletes, of whom there is nothing like.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, You created the heavens and the earth and all that lies between them, and not the faintest weariness ever touched You. We confess that we have so often dressed You in our own exhaustion — imagining You tired of us, fed up with us, run low on patience for us. Forgive us for shrinking You. Let our hearts believe, all the way down, that the One who never tired in making everything will never tire of our returning. And by that same power that cost You nothing, raise us, gather us, and bring us home. Āmīn.*
