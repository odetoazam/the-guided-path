# The Two Motions of a Saved Heart

## Surah Qaf, Ayah 32

> هَـٰذَا مَا تُوعَدُونَ لِكُلِّ أَوَّابٍ حَفِيظٍ

*Hādhā mā tūʿadūna li-kulli awwābin ḥafīẓ.*

**"This is what you were promised — for everyone who turns back again and again, and who keeps."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Inscription Over the Gate

I want you to imagine a door.

Not just any door. Imagine you have spent your whole life hearing about a place. People described it to you — its rivers, its shade, its peace. Some of them believed in it so completely that they reshaped their entire lives around it. Others laughed and said it was a story told to frightened people, a comfort for the weak.

And now imagine the moment when that place is no longer a description. It is in front of you. The distance has collapsed. What was always spoken of as *far* — *over there, someday, if* — is suddenly **near**.

That is exactly the moment Surah Qaf has brought us to.

The verse right before ours says it plainly: *wa uzlifati l-jannatu lil-muttaqīna ghayra baʿīd* — "And Paradise will be brought near to the God-conscious, not far off" (50:31). The whole surah has been an argument with people who said resurrection was impossible, who said *that's far-fetched, that's a distant return* (*dhālika rajʿun baʿīd*, 50:3). And now Allah answers their word "far" with His word "near." The garden is drawn close. It is right there.

And then — at the very threshold, in the moment the door appears — something is said.

Our verse.

Here is what I want you to feel before we go a single step further. When that garden is brought near, Allah does not simply say "enter." He first does something else. He **names the people it belongs to.** He writes, as it were, an inscription over the gate. And the inscription is only six words long. Six words that decide everything.

*Hādhā mā tūʿadūn* — "This is what you were promised."
*Li-kulli awwābin ḥafīẓ* — "for everyone who turns back again and again, and who keeps."

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question I want to plant in you, the question we will spend our whole time together answering. When you imagine the kind of person who gets *this* — the garden brought near, the promise fulfilled — what do you picture? Be honest. Most of us picture someone clean. Someone who got it right. Someone whose record is unblemished, who didn't struggle the way we struggle, who didn't fall the way we fall.

What if I told you that the very first word Allah uses to describe these people — *awwāb* — is a word that **only makes sense for someone who keeps falling?**

That the form of the word, its very shape in Arabic, requires a person who left, and came back, and left, and came back — over and over and over?

Stay with that. Because if it's true, then the inscription over the gate of Paradise is not what you feared it was. It is not a list of the people who never strayed. It is something else entirely. And we are going to read it carefully enough to see it.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Shape of a Word — *Awwāb* and the Grammar of Returning

Before we touch the word, let me teach you something about Arabic. Just one feature. But it changes how you'll hear the Quran forever.

In Arabic, you can take a simple action and **intensify its very shape**. You don't add the word "very" or "a lot." You change the architecture of the word itself, and the new shape *carries* the intensity inside it.

Here's an everyday example. The root in Arabic for "knowing" gives you a word for "one who knows" — *ʿālim*. Fine. A knower. But there is a heavier shape, a pattern the grammarians call *faʿʿāl*, where you double the middle sound and stretch the word out: *ʿallām*. And now it doesn't mean "one who knows." It means **one who knows deeply, vastly, again and again, by nature** — which is why Allah calls Himself *ʿallāmu l-ghuyūb*, the One who knows all the hidden things, completely and always.

Hold onto that pattern. *Faʿʿāl.* The doubled, stretched shape that means *not once, but as a way of being.* Repeatedly. Habitually. By character.

Now let's open our word.

> أَوَّاب — *awwāb*

It comes from the root *alif-wāw-bāʾ* (أ و ب), and the root means, very simply: **to return. To come back. To go back to where you belong.**

So the simple meaning would be "one who returns." A returner.

But look at the shape. It is not the simple shape. It is *awwāb* — the doubled, stretched *faʿʿāl* pattern. The intensive.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? The word is not "one who returned." It is not even "one who returns." It is the shape that means **one who returns, and returns, and returns again — the one whose entire character is returning.**

Let's sit with this, because everything hinges on it.

A word can only be built on the pattern of *repeated* action if the action is, in fact, repeatable — if it happens more than once. You cannot meaningfully call someone an *intensive, habitual returner* unless they have **left** more than once. The very form of the word *presupposes* a person who departs. Who slips. Who wanders off.

Think about what this means. Allah, describing the people to whom Paradise is brought near, does not choose a word that means "the one who never left." He chooses a word whose grammar *requires* leaving. The *awwāb* is not someone with a perfect record. The *awwāb* is someone with a perfect **reflex.** Something pulls him back. Every single time he drifts, something turns him around and brings him home.

And now listen to how the early scholars heard this exact word — because they heard the same thing, centuries ago, in the grammar.

Al-Qurṭubī records the explanation of al-Ḍaḥḥāk and others: *the awwāb is the one who returns to Allah from disobedience — then he sins, then he returns; he sins, then he returns. Like that.* Did you catch the rhythm in his very sentence? *Sins, then returns. Sins, then returns.* He is describing the doubled shape of the word with the doubled shape of his explanation.

Al-Baghawī preserves the words of Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyib, one of the great early teachers of Madinah, defining the *awwāb* with stunning plainness: *huwa lladhī yudhnibu thumma yatūb, thumma yudhnibu thumma yatūb* — **"he is the one who sins, then repents, then sins, then repents."**

[PAUSE]

I want you to hear who is saying this and where it is written. This is not a soft modern reading designed to make us comfortable. This is classical, sober tafsir, the inherited understanding, telling us that the word inscribed over the near-brought Paradise describes a person *defined by a cycle of falling and returning.*

So when your mind whispers — *but I keep failing, I keep coming back to the same sin, I repent and then I'm right back, what is the point* — I want you to understand that you have just described, almost word for word, the definition of the person Paradise is promised to. *Yudhnibu thumma yatūb, thumma yudhnibu thumma yatūb.* You thought that cycle was the proof of your distance. The grammar of this word says it may be the proof of your belonging — **as long as the return keeps happening.**

Because here is the hinge, and I won't soften it: the danger was never the falling. The danger is the day you fall and *don't turn around.* The day the reflex dies. The *awwāb* is not the one who never sins. The *awwāb* is the one in whom the return never dies.

[PAUSE]

And there is more in this word, a second layer the scholars give us, that lifts it higher still.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record a reading from Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿAṭāʾ: the *awwāb* is **the one who glorifies and praises Allah** — *al-musabbiḥ.* And they tie it to a haunting verse elsewhere, where Allah commands the mountains: *yā jibālu awwibī maʿah* — "O mountains, **return** [the glorification] with him" (34:10), spoken of Dāwūd, peace be upon him, whose praise of Allah was so alive that the mountains echoed it back.

Do you see how the two readings are one reality seen from two angles? In the first, the *awwāb* keeps returning *from* sin. In the second, the *awwāb* keeps returning *to* remembrance — his tongue keeps coming home to the praise of his Lord. And there's even a third angle: al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUtayba says the *awwāb* is *the one who remembers Allah in private, in solitude* — when no one is watching, his heart still turns home.

These are not three competing definitions you have to pick between. They are three photographs of one person from three sides. Here is the composite, the whole face: **the *awwāb* is the human being whose default direction is *back toward Allah*** — back from sin, back to praise, back in the secret hours when nobody could possibly see. Whatever pulls him away, the homing instinct fires, and he turns.

That is the first word over the gate. Not *sinless.* **Oriented.**

> **The first quality of the people of the near-brought Paradise is not a clean record. It is a return reflex that never stops firing.**

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Two: The Quiet Counterweight — *Ḥafīẓ* and the Other Direction

Now Allah pairs that first word with a second. And the pairing is the whole point — so let me first teach you to feel the *difference between two word-shapes,* because the shapes themselves are arguing with each other in a beautiful way.

We saw that *awwāb* is built on the shape of **repeated action** — doing, doing, doing again. It is a verb's energy frozen into a noun. Motion.

The second word is built differently.

> حَفِيظ — *ḥafīẓ*

It comes from the root *ḥāʾ-fāʾ-ẓāʾ* (ح ف ظ), and the root means **to guard, to keep, to preserve, to protect, to watch over.** It is the same root behind *ḥāfiẓ* — the one who has "preserved" the entire Quran inside his chest. The root is about *holding something safe and not letting it slip away.*

And the shape here is *faʿīl* — the pattern of a **settled, established, abiding quality.** Where *awwāb* is *motion repeated*, *ḥafīẓ* is *a state that holds steady.* One word vibrates with coming-and-going; the other stands still and keeps watch.

Now notice — Allah did not say *ḥāfiẓ*, "one who happens to guard." He said *ḥafīẓ*, the deeper, more constant form: **one for whom guarding is a fixed trait, a standing condition of the soul.** Not a guard who clocks in for a shift. A guardedness that is simply *who he is.*

And what does the *ḥafīẓ* guard? The scholars are precise here, and generous.

Al-Jalālayn: *ḥāfiẓun li-ḥudūdih* — "one who guards the limits of Allah." The boundaries. The lines you don't cross.

Al-Muyassar opens it wider and warmer: *ḥāfiẓun li-kulli mā qarrabahu ilā rabbihi mina l-farāʾiḍi wa-l-ṭāʿāt* — **"one who guards everything that brought him near to his Lord — the obligations, the acts of devotion."**

[PAUSE]

Stay with al-Muyassar's phrase, because it is exquisite. The *ḥafīẓ* guards *everything that brought him near.* He has noticed which acts draw him close to Allah — his prayer, his charity, his quiet remembrance, the company that lifts him — and he stands over them like a watchman, refusing to let them erode. He **protects his own nearness.**

Now hold the two words side by side and watch what Allah has built.

The *awwāb* is the one who, when he drifts away, **comes back.**
The *ḥafīẓ* is the one who, when he has come close, **does not let it slip.**

One is the motion of *repair.* The other is the discipline of *maintenance.*

Do you see why neither alone is enough? Here is what is remarkable about this pairing — and I want you to feel the danger of having only one.

Imagine a person who is all *awwāb* and no *ḥafīẓ.* He returns beautifully — every fall, he weeps, he repents, he comes rushing home. But he guards nothing. He preserves no boundary, builds no habit, defends no nearness. So he is forever sprinting back to a house he never bothers to keep, losing in the morning everything he won the night before. His life is all repair and no upkeep. Exhausting. And the ground never actually rises beneath him.

Now imagine the opposite. A person who is all *ḥafīẓ* and no *awwāb.* He guards the boundaries rigidly, keeps every rule, never lets a single duty slip. But he has no *return* in him — so the day he finally *does* slip, as every human must, he has no reflex to come back. His guarding hardens into pride, and his single fall, unhealed by any return, festers. He becomes brittle. The kind of person whose religion is fierce until the moment it cracks, and then it shatters, because he never learned how to come home.

[PAUSE]

Allah did not put these two words together by accident. He put them together because **a saved heart needs both motions.** It needs the humility to keep returning *and* the strength to keep guarding. The *awwāb* without the *ḥafīẓ* leaks. The *ḥafīẓ* without the *awwāb* breaks. The promise is for the one who is **both** — *awwābin ḥafīẓ* — the one who comes back, and then keeps watch over what the coming-back restored.

> **The promise belongs to the heart that knows both motions: the courage to return, and the vigilance to hold on.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Word That Looks Straight At You — *Tūʿadūn* and the Reach of *Kull*

There are two more small things in this verse — small in size, vast in weight — and they sit at its opening. Let me show you both.

First: *tūʿadūn.* "[What] you were promised."

Teach yourself this one feature of the Quran, and you will catch it everywhere: the Quran will sometimes be *talking about* people, and then, without warning, **turn and talk directly to them.** It swings from "they" to "you." The grammarians call this turning *iltifāt* — the "turn of the face." It is the difference between a teacher describing the prize to the class, and the teacher suddenly turning, looking you in the eye, and saying: *this one is yours.*

Now, the early reciters actually preserved this verse in two ways, and both are precious. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī record that Ibn Kathīr (the reciter) read it as *yūʿadūn* — "what **they** were promised," a report *about* the righteous. But the general reading, the *qirāʾat al-ʿāmma*, is *tūʿadūn* — "what **you** were promised." Direct address. Face to face.

Feel the force of the common reading. The garden has been brought near. And at that threshold, the voice does not describe the righteous in the third person, at a polite distance. It **turns to them and says, *you.*** *Hādhā mā tūʿadūn* — "This. Right here. Is what *you* were promised." Every patient prayer in the dark, every return after every fall, every duty you guarded when no one applauded — *this* is the thing it was all for, and Allah Himself turns to confirm it to your face.

And notice what the promise is *of.* Allah says *mā tūʿadūn* — "**what** you were promised." He uses the open word *mā*, "that which," and He does not enumerate it. He does not say "the rivers you were promised" or "the shade you were promised." He leaves it vast and unspecified — *what* you were promised, the whole of it, everything that was ever pledged to you across every verse and every prophet's tongue. The container is left open precisely so that nothing of the promise is left outside it.

[PAUSE]

Now the second small word, and perhaps the most merciful word in the verse:

> لِكُلِّ — *li-kull.* "For **every**."

Not *for the best of.* Not *for the elite among.* Not *for the few who.* **For every** *awwāb,* **for every** *ḥafīẓ.*

Sit with how wide that door is. Allah could have restricted it. He could have said "for the foremost," for the *sābiqūn,* for the spiritual aristocracy. Instead He throws it open to *every single soul* in whom these two qualities live — no matter their background, no matter the size of the file of their past, no matter how many times the cycle of *yudhnibu thumma yatūb* has turned. **If you are an *awwāb,* if you are a *ḥafīẓ,* the *kull* has your name in it.** The condition is not *who you were.* The condition is *what your heart does now* — does it return, and does it guard.

> **Allah turns His face to you, calls the whole promise yours, and opens it to everyone — every last one — in whom the return and the guard are alive.**

[PAUSE]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what these six words have shown us.

A word — *awwāb* — whose very shape requires a person who keeps falling and keeps coming back.
A word — *ḥafīẓ* — whose steady shape names the one who guards what his returning restored.
A turn of the face — *tūʿadūn* — that makes the promise land directly on *you.*
And a single syllable — *kull* — that refuses to lock anyone out.

We have been reading words. Now let's ask what these words reveal not just about Arabic — but about the nature of the human heart, and about what Allah is actually looking for in you.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Theology of Return — Why God Built You to Come Back, Not to Never Leave

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a trap almost every sincere person falls into, and it has a name in the modern world: **perfectionism.** Not the harmless "I like things neat" kind. The spiritual kind. The kind that says: *I have to get this right. If I fail, I have failed completely. One slip and the whole thing is contaminated.*

Watch what perfectionism does to a person trying to be good. They make a resolution — *I will never do this again.* They mean it with their whole heart. And then, two weeks later, three days later, that night — they do it again. And here is the lethal moment. The perfectionist doesn't just feel sad about the slip. They feel that the *entire project is now void.* "I said never. I did it again. So I'm a fraud. So what's the point. So I might as well stop pretending." And the shame doesn't pull them back toward God — it pushes them further away. One sin becomes ten, because the shame of the one made them abandon the whole road.

Psychologists who study this have a precise observation: it is almost never the first lapse that destroys a person's growth. **It is the second move — the response to the lapse.** The person who treats one slip as total failure relapses completely. The person who treats one slip as one slip, gets up, and re-engages — they recover. The difference between the two is not the falling. *Everyone falls.* The difference is entirely in **what they do in the thirty seconds after.**

[PAUSE]

Now bring back our word. *Awwāb.*

Do you see that Allah, fifteen centuries before any psychology lab, named the saved human being precisely by **the move after the fall?**

He did not build a religion for people who never leave the path. Read the grammar again — He *couldn't* have meant that, because the word He chose only works for people who keep leaving. He built His promise around the *return.* He looked at the human being He created — *and He created us, He knows exactly what we are* — and He did not pretend we would be sinless. The same surah, Qaf, just a few verses earlier, says *wa laqad khalaqnā l-insāna wa naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihi nafsuh* — "We created the human, and We know what his own soul whispers to him" (50:16). He knows the whisper. He knows the slip is coming. And knowing it, He did not demand a creature who never slips. He named, as the heir of Paradise, the creature who **never stops returning.**

This is the theology of return, and it runs through the whole Quran like a golden thread. When the believers are described at their best, in Āl ʿImrān, listen: *and those who, when they commit an immorality or wrong themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness for their sins...* (3:135). Not *those who never commit immorality.* Those who, *having* committed it, **remember and turn.** The Prophet ﷺ himself taught — and this is a sound, marfūʿ hadith — *"All the children of Ādam are constant sinners, and the best of the constant sinners are those who constantly repent"* (*kullu banī Ādama khaṭṭāʾ, wa khayru l-khaṭṭāʾīna l-tawwābūn*) — reported by al-Tirmidhī and others. Look at the construction even there: *khaṭṭāʾ,* the intensive — constant sinner — and *tawwāb,* the intensive — constant returner. The very same doubled shape as *awwāb.* The best of the ones who keep slipping are the ones who keep turning back.

[PAUSE]

So let me ask you the question this changes. When you fall — and you will — what is the story you tell yourself in the next thirty seconds?

If the story is *"I'm a fraud, I always do this, there's no point"* — that is not humility. That is the whisper of the one who wants the reflex to die, who wants you to mistake your fall for your identity, so that you'll stop returning. Because Shayṭān is not actually that interested in your sin. He's interested in what comes *after.* A sin followed by a return is a wound that heals stronger. A sin followed by despair is a wound that becomes a grave. He doesn't need you dirty. He needs you to **stop coming home.**

And if instead the story is *"That was a fall. I am an awwāb. I turn around. I'm getting up and going back, again, like I have a hundred times and will a thousand times more"* — then you have just done the single thing that Paradise was inscribed for.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** This: build a *return ritual.* Decide *now,* in calm, what you will do the moment you slip — before the shame can write its story. Maybe it is two units of prayer. Maybe it is a single sincere *astaghfirullah* and a refusal to spiral. Decide the return *before* you need it, so that when you fall, the reflex is already loaded. Make returning the most practiced motion in your spiritual life. Because that — not flawlessness — is the name written over the gate.

> The core insight: **Allah did not promise Paradise to the one who never strays. He promised it to the one whose strayings always end in return. Your falling is human; your returning is everything.**

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Repair and Maintenance — Tending the House You Keep Coming Back To

Now let me take you somewhere you also already live: a long relationship.

Anyone who has been in a marriage, a deep friendship, a bond that lasted years, knows there are two completely different skills involved, and that having one without the other quietly destroys the relationship.

The first skill is **repair.** When you've hurt each other, when there's been a rupture, a fight, a cold silence — repair is the motion of *turning back toward each other.* The apology. The reaching out. The "I'm sorry, let's not stay like this." A relationship without repair dies in its first serious conflict, because the rupture never closes.

But there is a second skill, and it's the one people forget. **Maintenance.** The daily, unglamorous tending. The small attentions when nothing is wrong. The checking-in, the keeping of promises, the protecting of the time you have together. And here is the thing every long-married person learns: a relationship that is *only* repair — only dramatic ruptures and dramatic reconciliations, over and over — is exhausting and eventually hollow. You can't build a life out of breaking and fixing. You also have to **keep.** To tend. To guard the thing when it's *not* broken, so it breaks less.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah placed in our verse. *Awwābin ḥafīẓ.* **Repair and maintenance.** The exact two skills of any lasting love — placed side by side, as the two requirements of a lasting relationship with *Him.*

The *awwāb* is your repair. Every rupture between you and Allah — every sin, every drifting away — the *awwāb* in you turns back and closes the gap. That is the dramatic motion, the return after the fall.

But the *ḥafīẓ* is your maintenance. Remember al-Muyassar's beautiful gloss: the *ḥafīẓ* guards *everything that brought him near* — the obligations, the acts of devotion. This is the quiet, daily tending of the relationship *when nothing is broken.* The prayer you guard not because you fell but because it keeps you from falling. The remembrance you protect, the company you keep clean, the boundary you don't let erode. It is love expressed not as reconciliation but as **upkeep.**

And now you understand, in the most human terms, why neither works alone.

A relationship to Allah that is *all repair* — all sinning and weeping and returning, with no daily maintenance in between — is the spiritual life of someone forever putting out fires they keep setting, never building anything that lasts. They are an *awwāb* who never became a *ḥafīẓ,* and they are exhausted, because they win the same ground every night and lose it every morning.

And a relationship that is *all maintenance* — all rigid rule-keeping with no living return — becomes a marriage of cold duty. Everything technically guarded, nothing actually warm. And on the day a wall finally cracks, as walls do, there is no repair reflex to fix it, so the whole thing collapses at once. They are a *ḥafīẓ* who never learned to be an *awwāb,* and their religion is strong right up until the moment it shatters.

[PAUSE]

What Allah is describing is a *complete* relationship. Return *and* keep. Repair *and* maintain. Come home when you've strayed — and guard the home when you haven't. The verse is, in its deepest sense, a description of **how to love Allah for a lifetime** rather than in dramatic, exhausting bursts.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** This: most of us are naturally strong in one motion and weak in the other. So name yours. If you are an *awwāb* — quick to repent, full of feeling, but your habits leak and your boundaries erode — then your work is *ḥafīẓ.* Pick *one* act that brings you near and build a wall around it. Same time, every day, non-negotiable. Guard it like it's the last thing you own. And if you are a *ḥafīẓ* — disciplined, consistent, never misses — but you've grown a little hard, a little proud, and you don't quite know how to come back warmly when you slip — then your work is *awwāb.* Practice the soft return. Let a fall make you tender instead of brittle. Learn to come home.

> The core insight: **A lasting love of Allah is built from two motions — the return that repairs and the vigilance that keeps. Grow the one you lack, and you will stop losing in the morning what you won the night before.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look back at where we started — at the door, the garden brought near, and the fear that the inscription over it would be a list of people you could never join: the sinless, the flawless, the ones who got it right.

And look at what the six words actually say.

*Hādhā mā tūʿadūn* — Allah turns His face to you, *you,* and says the whole vast promise is yours.
*Li-kull* — and He opens it to *every* soul who qualifies, locking no one out by their past.
And what is the qualification? *Awwāb* — the one whose very name requires that he kept falling and kept returning. *Ḥafīẓ* — the one who then guarded what the returning restored.

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes turn out to be the same insight seen from two directions. The *grammar* of *awwāb* — that doubled, repeated shape that only fits a person who leaves and comes back — *is* the theology of return: God built you to come home, not to never leave. And the *pairing* of *awwāb* with *ḥafīẓ* — motion beside steadiness, the verb's energy beside the settled state — *is* the wisdom of every lasting love: repair beside maintenance, returning beside keeping. The words were never decoration. They *are* the teaching.

The garden is brought near not for the ones who never wandered. It is brought near for the ones whose wandering always bent back toward home, and who, once home, kept watch.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you fall, what story do you tell yourself in the next thirty seconds — and does that story make you *return,* or make you *stop returning?*
2. Are you more naturally an *awwāb* or a *ḥafīẓ* — and what does the lopsidedness cost you?
3. Is there a sin you've stopped repenting from, not because you stopped doing it, but because you decided the repeated return was pointless? What would it mean to fire the reflex one more time?
4. What is the *one* thing that brings you near to Allah that you have been letting quietly erode? What would *guarding* it actually look like this week?
5. If Allah turned His face to you right now and said *hādhā mā tūʿadūn* — "this is what *you* were promised" — would you believe Him? And if not, whose voice taught you not to?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Paradise is promised not to the one who never falls, but to the one whose heart keeps two motions alive — the courage to keep returning, and the vigilance to keep guarding what the return restores.**

### The Closing Invitation

So here is the invitation — not the obligation, the invitation. Stop measuring your nearness to Allah by the cleanness of your record, and start measuring it by the liveliness of your return and the steadiness of your guard. The next time you fall, don't ask *"how could I, again?"* — ask *"am I still an awwāb? Does the reflex still fire?"* And if it does — get up, come home, and then build one wall around one good thing, and keep watch. That is the whole of it. That is the name over the gate.

### A Du'a From These Words

*O Allah, You named the heirs of Your nearness awwābīn — make our returning to You constant, and never let the reflex of our return grow cold, no matter how many times we fall. And You named them ḥafīẓīn — so make us guardians of everything that draws us near to You, that we may not lose by morning what Your mercy restored by night. Turn Your face to us on the day the garden is brought near, and let us be among the kull — every one — to whom You say: this is what you were promised. Āmīn.*
