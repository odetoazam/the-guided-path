# Before the Rising, Before the Setting

> [ayah:50:39] فَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ ٱلْغُرُوبِ

*Fa-ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wa sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika qabla ṭulūʿi-sh-shamsi wa qabla-l-ghurūb.*

**"So be patient over what they say, and glorify the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before the setting."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let's go back and stand where this ayah was first heard.

Surah Qaf is one long argument with a particular kind of person — not the violent enemy, not the schemer, but the one who simply finds the whole thing *unbelievable*. Listen to how the surah opens. After the lone letter **Qāf** and the oath by the glorious Qur'an, we hear them: *"They wonder that a warner has come to them from among themselves, and the deniers say, 'This is a strange thing. When we have died and become dust — that is a far return.'"*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that phrase. *That is a far return* — **ذَٰلِكَ رَجْعٌ بَعِيدٌ**. They are not angry. They are *amused*. The idea that scattered dust could be gathered back into a living, accountable human being strikes them as so far-fetched that it doesn't even merit fear. It's a joke. A *strange thing*.

And so the surah does not shout back. It walks them, patiently, through the evidence. The earth they stand on, brought to life after death by the rain. The date-palms with their stacked spathes. The construction of their own bodies. Then it brings them somewhere they cannot see but cannot escape — to the two angels seated at their right and left, writing, *"not a word does he utter but there is a watcher by him, ready."* It brings them to the stupor of death that comes *in truth*. To the Trumpet. To the Day of Emergence — **يَوْمُ ٱلْخُرُوجِ** — the day of coming *out*.

The entire surah is about *coming out* of the ground. About return. About the thing they laughed at.

And then — right near the end, after all of that — the surah turns away from the deniers and speaks to one man. The man who has been carrying their laughter on his shoulders. The man who has been told he is *strange*, that his message is a *strange thing*. Allah turns to His Messenger ﷺ and gives him something to do with all of it.

That is where our ayah lives.

**The Hook:** Here is what I want you to wonder about as we go. When someone wounds you with their words — mocks what is most sacred to you, calls your deepest truth a *joke* — what does your soul instinctively reach for? A retort. A defense. The last word. Allah's instruction to the most mocked human being who ever lived contains no retort at all. It contains two things: a binding, and a redirection. And tucked inside the redirection is a quiet, devastating answer to the very people who said there is no return — an answer written into the sky twice a day, every single day, that they had been ignoring their whole lives.

Let's find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: **فَٱصْبِرْ** — The Binding

Before we touch the word, let me teach you the idea underneath it.

In English, "patience" is a soft, passive word. It sounds like *waiting*. Like sitting in a doctor's office. Like enduring something until it's over. We imagine a patient person as someone *relaxed* — someone who has let go of tension.

The Arabic word is the opposite of relaxed.

The root here — **ص-ب-ر**, *ṣ-b-r* — does not originally mean "to wait." In the old language of the Arabs, *ṣabr* means **to bind, to confine, to restrain, to hold something firmly in place.** When the Arabs spoke of *qatlu ṣabran*, they meant killing someone who has been *bound* and held still — tied down. *Ṣabarat-hu* — I confined him, I held him fast. The root describes a force *holding back* something that desperately wants to break loose.

[PAUSE]

So *ṣabr* is not the absence of struggle. *Ṣabr is the struggle.* It is the act of taking a soul that is straining to react — to lash out, to collapse, to flee — and **tying it down**. Holding it in place by force of will.

Now look at the word Allah gives His Messenger:

**فَٱصْبِرْ** — *fa-ṣbir.*

It is a command. An imperative. *Bind yourself.* And notice the small letter at the front — the **fā**, *fa-*. This is what the grammarians call *fāʾ as-sababiyyah* or the *fā* of consequence — "so," "therefore." It ties this command directly to everything before it. *Because* of all this — because of the watching angels, because of the certainty of the Day of Emergence, because of the truth of what is coming — *therefore, bind yourself.*

The patience is not blind. It is *anchored* in certainty. He is not being asked to grit his teeth and hope. He is being asked to hold firm *because the outcome is already known.* The deniers laugh now; the surah has just spent thirty-eight ayahs showing exactly how that laughter ends. So: *fa-ṣbir.* Hold the line. You already know how this resolves.

Now notice the object of the patience: **عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ** — *ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* — "over **what they say**."

Stay with this. He is not told to be patient over what they *do* to him — the boycott, the stones, the exile. He is told to be patient over what they **say**. Their *words*. The root here is **ق-و-ل**, the simple root of speech, *qāla, yaqūlu.*

Why specify speech?

[PAUSE]

Because words are the wound this surah is treating. Go back to the opening: *the deniers said* — **قَالَ** — "this is a strange thing." Their weapon in Surah Qaf is not the sword. It is the *sentence*. The mockery. The casual dismissal. *That is a far return.* The thing crushing the Messenger's heart is not violence; it is the sound of sacred truth being called *ridiculous* by people who should know better.

And here is what's remarkable about the command. When someone strikes you, you can be patient by simply not striking back — by stillness. But when someone *speaks* against you, stillness is much harder, because speech demands speech. A word hangs in the air *asking* to be answered. The silence after an insult feels like defeat. Every fiber of the self wants to fill that silence with a reply.

So *fa-ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* is asking for the very hardest form of restraint: **to hear the words, and to bind the tongue that wants to answer them.**

And here the ayah does something extraordinary. It does not leave the mouth empty.

### Element Two: **وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ** — The Redirection

Watch the turn. *Be patient over what they say —* **and glorify the praise of your Lord.**

The mouth that has been forbidden to answer them is, in the very same breath, given something *else* to say. This is not suppression. This is **redirection**. The energy of the tongue is not killed; it is *re-aimed* — away from them, toward Him.

Let me teach you the word **سَبِّحْ** — *sabbiḥ.*

The root **س-ب-ح**, *s-b-ḥ*, in its physical origin means **to swim, to move swiftly and freely through a medium** — the way a swimmer cuts through water, the way the celestial bodies *swim* in their orbits (*kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn*). From this comes *tasbīḥ*: to move swiftly *away* — specifically, to declare Allah *far removed*, swept clean, utterly distant from every flaw and imperfection. *Subḥān Allāh* means: Allah is **free of** all that is unworthy of Him.

And notice the *form* of the word. This is not the simple *sabaḥa* (to swim). It is **sabbiḥ** — Form II, with the doubled middle letter, the *shadda* on the *bāʾ*. In Arabic, this doubling characteristically carries **intensity, repetition, deliberate and thorough doing.** Not a single glance toward His perfection — a *sustained, repeated, wholehearted* declaration of it.

Now — *tasbīḥ* is a negation. It clears away. It says: *not this flaw, not that limitation, not what they ascribe.* And here is the genius of the pairing. Allah does not say merely *sabbiḥ*. He says:

**وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ** — *sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika* — "glorify **with the praise** of your Lord."

The root **ح-م-د**, *ḥamd*, is the opposite movement. *Tasbīḥ* says what He is *not*; *ḥamd* says what He *is*. *Tasbīḥ* empties the false; *ḥamd* fills with the true. Together — *sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbik* — they form a complete act: **clear away every imperfection, and in the cleared space, affirm every perfection and praise Him for it.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened to the wound?

The deniers filled the air with *what they say* — flawed words, false ascriptions, the claim that this is a *strange thing*, that there is no return. Those are imperfections *spoken*. And the cure Allah prescribes is not to argue each claim. It is to drown the entire field of their false speech in *tasbīḥ* — the sweeping declaration that your Lord is **free** of everything their words imply — and then to fill it with *ḥamd*, His praise.

Their words said: *He cannot bring the dead back.* Your tongue, instead of debating, says: *Subḥān Allāh — far is my Lord above the limitation you just imagined for Him. Al-ḥamdu lillāh — and all praise belongs to the One who does exactly what you say cannot be done.*

The reactive retort would have met their words on *their* ground. *Tasbīḥ bi-ḥamd* lifts the whole conversation onto His ground. **It does not answer the mockery. It dissolves it.**

And notice the name He is told to praise by: **رَبِّكَ** — *rabbika* — "**your** Lord." Not "Allah," not "the Lord of the worlds" — but *your Lord*, with the intimate possessive *-ka* attached to it. The root **ر-ب-ب**, *rabb*, means the One who owns you, nurtures you, raises you stage by stage, and brings you to completion. In the moment of being mocked, when he feels most exposed and alone, the word given to him is the most intimate word of belonging: *your* Lord. The One whose project *you* are. Turn from their crowd, and toward the One to whom you personally belong.

### Element Three: **قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ ٱلْغُرُوبِ** — The Sun, and the Word That Vanishes

Now we come to *when*. And here lies the most subtle thing in the ayah — something you only see when you read what is **absent**.

Allah anchors the praise to two moments:

**قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ** — *qabla ṭulūʿi-sh-shams* — "**before the rising of the sun**"

**وَقَبْلَ ٱلْغُرُوبِ** — *wa qabla-l-ghurūb* — "**and before the setting**"

The classical commentators read these two windows as the bracketing prayers of the day — *qabla ṭulūʿ*, the dawn prayer offered before the sun breaks the horizon; *qabla al-ghurūb*, the afternoon worship offered before the sun goes down. Al-Baghawī reports both readings of the second window — the ʿAṣr prayer, and on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās, the Ẓuhr and ʿAṣr together — and the dawn for the first. So at the plainest level: *frame your day, at both ends, with turning to your Lord.* (Some of the earlier scholars, al-Qurṭubī notes, debated whether this command was later folded into the broader commands of the Sharīʿah; but its meaning — patience answered by praise, anchored at the day's two hinges — stands untouched.)

That is beautiful on its own. But now I want you to look at the *shape* of the two phrases. Look very carefully.

The first phrase names three things: *before* — *the rising* — *of the sun*. **قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ.** The act (rising), and the actor (the sun), both fully named.

The second phrase names... how many?

**وَقَبْلَ ٱلْغُرُوبِ** — *before* — *the setting.* That's it. **The setting** — *al-ghurūb*. And then nothing. **The sun is gone.**

[PAUSE]

The word *shams* — "sun" — appears at the rising. At the setting, it is not there. The setting stands alone, unattached to its actor. *Qabla ṭulūʿi-sh-shams*, but only *qabla-l-ghurūb*.

Now — is this just a stylistic shortcut? Let me show you it is not. There is a near-twin of this ayah in Sūrat Ṭā-Hā (20:130): *"and glorify the praise of your Lord before the rising of the sun and before its setting"* — there the second window is **قَبْلَ غُرُوبِهَا**, *qabla ghurūbihā*, "before **its** setting," with the little pronoun *-hā* reaching back to grab the sun and hold onto it. In Ṭā-Hā, even at sunset, the sun is still grammatically *present*, tethered by the pronoun.

But here, in Sūrat Qaf — the surah of *death and return* — Allah lets it go completely. No *shams*. No *-hā*. Just **al-ghurūb**. The setting, severed from the thing that sets.

Stay with me, because this is the heart of it.

What is *al-ghurūb*? The root **غ-ر-ب**, *gh-r-b*, is the root of the sun's disappearance — and from the very same root comes *gharīb*, the **stranger**; *ghurba*, **estrangement, exile, vanishing into a far place.** The same opening word the deniers used — *this is a strange thing*, *ʿajab* — names the same instinct that calls the unfamiliar *foreign*. The root of *ghurūb* is the root of *things going away and not being held onto.*

So watch what the grammar *enacts*. At the rising, everything is named, present, accounted for — *the sun is here.* At the setting, the very word for the sun *disappears from the sentence*, just as the sun itself disappears from the sky. **The form of the phrase performs the meaning of the phrase.** The sun *sets* out of the verse.

And that is precisely the experience the deniers built their whole denial on. They watched things *set* — watched the living go down into the ground like the sun going down past the horizon — and they concluded: *gone. Far return. A strange thing.* They saw the *ghurūb* and could not imagine the *ṭulūʿ*.

Which brings us to the quiet answer hidden in the order of the words.

---

**The bridge:** We have seen a soul commanded to *bind itself* against words that wound; a tongue redirected from retort to the twin act of clearing-and-praising; and a remembrance anchored to a sunrise that is fully named and a sunset where the sun slips away. Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about *words* — but about *reality itself*, and about the man, and us, who are asked to live this.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Sun Is the Argument

Let me start where you already live.

You have watched a sunset. Everyone has. There is a particular feeling to it — a kind of soft melancholy. The light goes amber, then red, then it drains out of the sky and the cold comes in. Something in us reads a sunset as a *small ending*. Poets across every culture have used the setting sun for death, for loss, for the closing of things. It is the most universal image of *gone*. The day dies.

And every single one of those endings is followed, without exception, by a rising.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to see. The deniers in this surah laughed at *one specific idea*: that what goes down into the ground can come back up. *When we have died and become dust — that is a far return.* And the surah's name for the Last Day is **يَوْمُ ٱلْخُرُوجِ**, the Day of *Coming Out* — the day the dead emerge from the earth the way a seed emerges, the way — *the way the sun emerges every morning from the place it set.*

The sun does the impossible thing every day. It *sets* — it goes into the *ghurūb*, the disappearance, the place of strangers — and the deniers, watching, would say if they were honest: *that is a far return; the sun is gone; how strange would it be for it to come back.* And then it comes back. **Ṭulūʿ.** Rising. Without fail. Without exception. Roughly twenty-five thousand times in a human lifetime, the universe rehearses resurrection in front of their eyes, and they call resurrection *a strange thing.*

So when Allah tells His Messenger to praise Him *before the rising of the sun and before the setting*, He is not only assigning prayer times. He is **placing His servant's worship at the exact two hinges where the cosmos itself preaches the sermon the deniers refused to hear.** Stand before Me, He says, at the moment of *ṭulūʿ* — the daily resurrection — and at the moment of *ghurūb* — the daily death. Let your praise sit on the two pivots of the proof.

And now the disappearing word lands with full weight. The sun *sets* — and even the word for it vanishes from the verse, *al-ghurūb*, alone, gone. The deniers see only that. They see the setting and call it the end. But the believer who prays *qabla ṭulūʿ* has already been promised the rising. He has been to the dawn prayer. He knows, *in his body*, in his daily rhythm, that *ghurūb* is never the last word — that every disappearance in this universe is the front half of a return.

[PAUSE]

Let this reorganize how you see your own losses. Every *ghurūb* in your life — the people who have gone down into the ground, the chapters that have closed, the version of yourself that has died — your soul, untrained, reads them the way the deniers read death: *gone. Far. Strange that it would come back.* The discipline of this ayah is to greet every setting already standing at the next sunrise. To let the *ṭulūʿ* you witness every morning teach your heart what to expect of every *ghurūb* you grieve.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Tomorrow morning, before the sun is fully up, you will have a choice you usually make unconsciously: to reach for your phone and let the world's words begin, or to stand for a moment at the day's first hinge and put your forehead down. Pray Fajr *as the answer to the deniers in your own chest* — the part of you that, at every loss, whispers *far return.* Let the sunrise you are praying before be your evidence against your own despair.

### Theme Two: The Cure for Being Mocked Is Not Winning the Argument

Now let me bring us to the human wound this ayah was sent to heal.

There is a specific kind of pain that has nothing to do with physical harm. It is the pain of being *not taken seriously*. Of having the thing you have given your life to — your faith, your work, your love — waved away as *a strange thing*, a joke, naive, embarrassing. We feel it as teenagers when our values are mocked. We feel it as adults online, where the most sacred thing can be reduced to a smirking comment in seconds. The Messenger ﷺ felt it from his own tribe, his own neighbors, the people whose respect should have meant something.

And here is what that pain does to us. It hijacks the tongue. It makes us *need* to respond. We lie awake composing the perfect retort. We re-enter the argument in our heads a hundred times. The mockery sets up residence in us and *demands* to be answered, and as long as we are trying to answer it, we are living on the mocker's territory, breathing the mocker's air, shaped entirely by *what they say.*

Look again at what Allah does *not* prescribe.

He does not say: *refute them.* He does not say: *prove the resurrection to them* — even though the surah just spent thirty-eight ayahs of proof. He does not even say: *defend yourself.* To the most articulate human being who ever lived, capable of silencing any of them, He says: **fa-ṣbir** — bind the tongue that wants to answer — **wa sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbik** — and give that same tongue something infinitely better to do.

[PAUSE]

This is the move our reactive hearts can never make on their own. We think the only two options are *strike back* or *swallow it.* Lash out, or repress. And we know repression is poison — swallowed anger rots. So we feel we *must* answer, or we'll be eaten alive.

The ayah reveals a third door. **Not repression — redirection.** The tongue is not silenced; it is *re-employed.* The energy that wanted to go down into their petty argument is lifted up into *tasbīḥ* and *ḥamd*. You don't swallow the wound. You *transmute* it — you turn the heat of the insult into fuel for praise.

And think about *why this actually heals*, where arguing never could. If you defeat the mocker, you are still defined by him — your victory is *about him*. But when you turn from him to *rabbika*, *your Lord*, you have left the entire arena. You are no longer a man being mocked; you are a servant standing before the One he belongs to. The mockery doesn't get answered. *It gets orphaned* — left behind, with no one standing on its field to receive it.

Notice the deep structure here, the same shape as *tasbīḥ* itself. *Tasbīḥ* clears away the false; *ḥamd* fills with the true. The wound of mockery fills your inner space with *their* false words. *Fa-ṣbir* clears them — binds them out. *Wa sabbiḥ bi-ḥamd* fills the cleared space with His praise. The ayah is not just telling you what to do; **its two halves are the two halves of every act of healing — empty out the lie, fill with the truth.**

[PAUSE]

And this is why the timing matters even here. *Before the rising, before the setting.* The cure is not a one-time grand gesture; it is a *rhythm*. You will be wounded again tomorrow. So the praise is built into the structure of every day — at both hinges — so that no matter what *they say* between sunrise and sunset, your soul begins and ends each day *off their field and on His.* The mockery is daily; so is the medicine.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** The next time someone's words lodge in you — the comment, the dismissal, the contempt — and you feel the familiar pull to compose the reply, catch it. Recognize that pull as the invitation to live on their ground. And instead of reaching for the retort, reach for the words Allah handed His own Messenger in that exact moment: *Subḥāna rabbiya — al-ḥamdu lillāh.* Let the wound become the trigger for praise. Watch what happens to the wound when you stop standing on its field to defend it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now step back and see the whole verse at once, because its pieces fit together with a precision that only appears after you've walked through them.

The ayah has two commands, and they answer two halves of a single problem.

The problem arrives as *speech*: **مَا يَقُولُونَ** — *what they say.* Words that deny, mock, and disappear the truth — words shaped, at root, by the same instinct as *ghurūb*: to watch a thing go down and call it *gone, strange, finished.*

The first command, **فَٱصْبِرْ**, addresses the *receiving* of those words: bind yourself; do not let their speech pull your speech onto their ground.

The second command, **وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ**, addresses the *replacing* of those words: clear the field with *tasbīḥ*, fill it with *ḥamd*, and aim it all at the One you belong to.

And the timing — **قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ ٱلْغُرُوبِ** — is not decoration on the end. It is the *proof* welded to the prescription. The deniers' whole error was reading *ghurūb* — the setting — as final. So Allah sets the believer's praise at the sunrise *and* the sunset, and then, in the grammar itself, lets the sun *vanish* from the sunset exactly as it vanishes from the sky — and trusts the worshipper, standing there at dawn, to *know what comes next.*

### Two Directions, One Insight

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey arrive at the same place from opposite sides.

From the **words**, we found: the cure for false speech is not counter-speech but *tasbīḥ bi-ḥamd* — emptying the lie, filling with truth, aimed at *your Lord.*

From the **sun**, we found: the cure for the despair of *ghurūb* — the conviction that what sets is gone — is the *ṭulūʿ* you witness every dawn, the daily rehearsal of the return they called a *far return.*

And they are one insight: **Do not let what disappears have the last word.** Not the mocker's sentence, which wants to define you — let it set, unanswered, while you turn to praise. Not your own losses, which feel final — let them set, knowing every setting is the front half of a rising. The deniers built their lives on *ghurūb*. The believer builds his on *ṭulūʿ.*

### Questions to Carry

- When someone's words wound me, where do I go — onto *their* ground to answer them, or onto His ground to praise Him? Which have I made my reflex?
- What in my life am I currently grieving as a *ghurūb* — a setting, a thing gone for good — that this ayah is asking me to greet as the front half of a return?
- My tongue runs all day. How much of it is *tasbīḥ bi-ḥamd*, and how much is answering people who are not worth answering?
- The sun has risen for me roughly every day of my life. Have I let it teach me anything about resurrection — or have I, like the deniers, watched the proof twice a day and called the conclusion *strange*?
- Do I stand at the two hinges of my day — dawn and the late afternoon — *off the world's field and on my Lord's*? Or does the world own both my sunrise and my sunset?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the world's words wound you, do not answer them in kind — bind your soul, and fill the same mouth with your Lord's praise, anchored at the two hinges of a day where the sun itself rehearses, every dawn, the very return the mockers call impossible.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to become thick-skinned, to stop feeling the sting. The Messenger ﷺ felt it; that is *why* the consolation was sent. It is inviting you to do something harder and lighter than enduring: to take the energy that the wound creates in you and *lift it* — out of the argument, off the mocker's ground, up into praise. And to let the most ordinary thing in your sky, the rising and setting you have ignored your whole life, become your daily evidence against your own despair. Begin tomorrow at the first hinge. Pray Fajr as the answer. And watch the sun come up like it always does — like everything that sets, eventually, comes back.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O my Lord — Rabbī — when their words crowd my chest, bind my soul before it answers them, and let my tongue rise instead to declare You far above every flaw they imagine, and to praise You for every perfection that is Yours. Teach my heart, at every setting it grieves, to wait for the rising the way the dawn always comes. Place me, at both hinges of my days, before You — and nowhere else.*

*Subḥāna rabbinā wa bi-ḥamdih. Āmīn.*
