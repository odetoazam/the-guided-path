# The Ceiling of Every Wish — and the Word That Waits Beyond It

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Six words. That's the whole verse.

[ayah:50:35] لَهُم مَّا يَشَآءُونَ فِيهَا وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ

*Lahum mā yashā'ūna fīhā wa-ladaynā mazīd.*

**"For them is whatever they wish therein, and with Us is more."**

Six words. And I want to tell you honestly — when I first sat with this verse, the first half almost slid past me. *For them is whatever they wish.* Of course. That's Paradise. That's what we've always been told it is — a place where you get what you want. The wish-machine. The garden where nothing is denied.

It was the last two words that stopped me. **وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ.** *And with Us is more.*

More?

More than *whatever they wish?*

[PAUSE]

Sit with the strangeness of that for a second. The verse has just told you that these people have **everything they could possibly want**. Whatever the soul reaches for — it's there. Whatever the heart names — granted. The first half of the ayah closes the door on lack completely. There is nothing left to desire, because desire itself is being fully met.

And then Allah adds a word. *And with Us is more.*

How do you have "more" than "everything you could wish for"? What is left, once every wish is filled? What could possibly sit in the space **beyond the ceiling of human desire** — a thing you would have wanted, except you never even knew it existed to want it?

That question is the door into this ayah. Hold onto it. We're going to walk all the way to the bottom of it.

### The world this verse is speaking into

Before we open the words, let's rebuild the room this verse was first heard in.

Surah Qaf is a Makkan surah, and it is relentless about one thing: **the Day is real, and Allah is near.** Earlier in this same surah comes one of the most intimate lines in the entire Qur'an — *"We created man, and We know what his soul whispers to him, and We are nearer to him than his jugular vein"* (50:16). The whole surah pulses with this theme of closeness. Allah closer than the vein in your own neck. Two angels seated, recording, on your right and your left. Nothing escaping. Nothing distant.

And then the surah marches you through the resurrection — the earth splitting, the trumpet, the soul arriving "with a driver and a witness," the books opened, the reckoning. Verse after verse of accountability, until we reach the people who feared the Most Merciful unseen, who turned to Him with a returning heart. And they are told, in verse 34: *"Enter it in peace. That is the Day of Eternity."*

Picture the original listener in Makkah. Someone poor. Someone mocked. Someone who had lost status, lost family, lost safety for the sake of saying *lā ilāha illā Allah.* Someone whose entire life had become a chronicle of things denied — denied a tribe, denied a home, denied even the right to pray in peace.

And into that life, this voice descends and says: there is coming a Day when you will have **whatever you wish.** Every door that was closed to you — open. Every "no" your life was made of — reversed.

That alone would be overwhelming. That alone would be enough to weep over.

[PAUSE]

But Allah does not stop at *enough.* He adds a word these listeners could not have asked for, because they did not yet know it was theirs to ask. **وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ.**

Here's what's remarkable, and it's the thing we'll spend our time on: that final word is not just *bigger Paradise.* It is not more rivers, more fruit, more thrones. The scholars who knew this verse best understood that the "more" is of a completely different kind from everything before it. The first half is about **the gift.** The last two words are about **the Giver.**

Let's earn that, slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three things to look at. None of them is decorative. Each one is doing structural work, and together they build the whole meaning of the verse.

First, a small, almost invisible word — **مَّا**, "whatever" — and the enormous silence Allah leaves around it.

Second, a quiet contrast hiding in plain sight, between two location words: **فِيهَا** ("therein") and **لَدَيْنَا** ("with Us").

Third, the final word itself — **مَزِيدٌ** — and the deliberate way it is left unfinished.

### Element One: مَّا — the object Allah refuses to name

Let me teach you something about how language works before we look at the word.

When I make you a promise, the *specificity* of the promise tells you its size. If I say, "I'll give you a sandwich," you know exactly what you're getting — and you know its limit. A sandwich. That's the whole of it. But if I say, "I'll give you whatever you want from the kitchen" — notice what just happened. I gave you *less* detail and *more* generosity. The vaguer the promise, the larger it became. By refusing to name the thing, I handed you everything.

In Arabic, there's a principle the grammarians lean on constantly: **the indefinite, the unspecified, in a context of giving, signals limitlessness.** A named gift has edges. An unnamed gift does not.

Now look at what Allah says.

He does not say, "for them are gardens." He does not say, "for them are rivers of milk and honey," though He says exactly that elsewhere. Here, at this summit, He uses one of the most open-ended words in the entire Arabic language:

**مَّا يَشَآءُونَ** — *"whatever they wish."*

**مَّا** — "whatever." It is a relative word with no edges. It does not name a single thing. It names the **entire category of the wishable**, and then leaves it completely blank for you to fill.

Let's sit with this. Allah is the One who knows what every soul whispers — He told us that nineteen verses earlier. He knows, precisely, every fruit and pleasure and reunion and relief that any one of these people could ever want. He could have listed them. He has the knowledge to make the most detailed promise ever spoken.

And instead He says: **مَّا يَشَآءُونَ.** Whatever. You name it. Whatever rises in you — it's yours.

Notice something about why this is more generous than any list. A list, no matter how long, is still a **boundary.** The moment Allah names ten thousand pleasures, your heart — being a heart — would whisper, *but what about the ten-thousand-and-first?* Any specified Paradise is a Paradise with a far wall. But **مَّا يَشَآءُونَ** has no far wall. It expands to the exact size of your wanting, whatever that turns out to be, forever.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing the absence is telling us. Allah left the object of the verb empty *on purpose.* In Arabic you expect, after "they wish," some object — they wish *for what?* The grammar leans forward, waiting for the thing. And Allah withholds it. That withholding is not a gap in the sentence. **The withholding is the gift.** The empty space where the object should be is precisely the room left for your soul to be infinite in its asking.

The scholar al-Qurtubi captures the human texture of it beautifully. He says **مَّا يَشَآءُونَ** means *"whatever their souls crave and their eyes delight in."* Not what someone decided was good for you. What *you* crave. The promise is fitted to the shape of the individual heart.

So already, before we even reach the famous part, the first half of the ayah has done something staggering: it has promised every human being a Paradise sized exactly to their own infinite capacity to want. It has closed the door on lack entirely.

Remember that. Because the whole shock of this verse is that **after closing that door, Allah opens another one.**

### Element Two: فِيهَا and لَدَيْنَا — where the gift is, and where the *more* is

This is the heart of the ayah, and almost everyone walks past it. Let me slow us all the way down.

The verse has two location words in it. Two little adverbs of place. And they are not the same — they are pointing in two completely different directions.

The first is **فِيهَا** — *"therein,"* "in it." In *what?* In the Garden. In Paradise, the place mentioned just before. So the first half of the ayah reads, fully: *"For them is whatever they wish — IN IT,"* in the Garden.

Their wishes are fulfilled **in the place.** Inside the Garden. The gifts have an address, and the address is Paradise.

Now watch where the second half locates its treasure. Allah does *not* say "and in it is more." He does not keep us in the Garden. He says:

**وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ** — *"and with US is more."*

**لَدَيْنَا.** "With Us." In Our presence. Near Us.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the location shift? Do you feel the camera move?

The first half of the verse, your eyes are *in the Garden* — fīhā — looking around at everything your heart could want. And then in the second half, Allah lifts your gaze off the scenery entirely and points somewhere else. Not to a better corner of the Garden. **To Himself.** *With Us.*

The wishes are fulfilled *fīhā* — in the place.
The "more" is found *ladaynā* — in the presence.

This is not an accident of phrasing. This is the entire theology of the verse compressed into a contrast between two prepositions. Allah is telling you, with the grammar itself, that there is a category of joy that the **Garden cannot hold** — because it is not located in the Garden at all. It is located in *Him.* It is "with Us."

And there's more buried in **لَدَيْنَا.** In Arabic, Allah doesn't just say "with Us" — He *fronts* it. He puts the "with Us" *before* the word "more." The natural, flat way to say it would be *"and for them is more."* Instead the structure is *"and — with Us — is more."* When Arabic moves a phrase to the front like this, ahead of where it would normally sit, it does two things at once: it **emphasizes** that phrase, and it hints at **exclusivity.** The placement is whispering: *this particular treasure exists only in one place. With Us. Nowhere else. It cannot be delivered to you in the Garden like fruit on a branch; it can only be had where We are.*

Let's stay with this, because it's the hinge of everything.

Why would Allah do this? Why fulfill every wish *in the Garden*, and then deliberately keep the greatest thing *with Himself?*

Because a wish, by its nature, is something you can picture. You can only wish for what your mind can already hold an image of. Everything *fīhā*, everything "in the Garden," is — by definition — a thing you were able to want, which means a thing you were able to imagine. The gifts of the Garden are the size of your imagination.

But there is a joy that no imagination can hold in advance, because it is not a *thing* at all. It is **nearness to the One who made you.** You cannot wish for it accurately, the way you cannot describe a color to someone born blind. It is not in the inventory of the wishable. So Allah doesn't put it *fīhā*, among the wishes. He keeps it *ladaynā*, with Himself — the one treasure you'll receive that you never knew enough to ask for.

[PAUSE — let that land]

The Garden gives you everything you can imagine. Allah keeps, for Himself to give, the thing you could not.

### Element Three: مَزِيدٌ — the word left deliberately open

Now the final word. **مَزِيدٌ.** "More." "An increase."

It comes from the root **زاد** (*zāda*) — to increase, to grow, to add beyond. From this same root come words you may know: *ziyāda*, an addition; *al-mazīd*, the surplus, the extra. The form **مَزِيد** carries the sense of *that which is added on* — a surplus laid on top of something already complete.

And here is the first thing to feel about it: **مَزِيد** is "more" added *on top of "everything you could wish."* Linger on the logic of that. The increase is not increasing a lack — there is no lack left; the first half abolished lack. The increase is increasing **fullness itself.** It is more poured onto a cup that was already, by every human measure, completely full. It is the answer to a longing you didn't know you still had, after every longing you knew about was already satisfied.

Now notice how Allah leaves the word. **مَزِيدٌ** — it is **indefinite.** No "the." No description after it. No clause explaining what the "more" consists of. The verse simply ends. *…and with Us is more.* — and stops.

Think about what that open ending does to you as a listener. The whole verse has been building, and you arrive at the last word *desperate* to know — *more of what?* And Allah closes the sentence and leaves you standing there, with the question ringing.

That unfinishedness is the same move as **مَّا** at the start. Just as Allah refused to name the object of your wishing — because naming it would shrink it — He refuses to define the **مَزِيد**, because to define it would be to give it edges, and this is precisely the gift that has no edges. It is left grammatically open because the reality it points to is **boundless** and **beyond conception.** Al-Baghawi puts it plainly: the *mazīd* is increase in bliss **"that never crossed their minds"** — *mā lam yakhṭur bi-bālihim.* A thing that never even occurred to them to imagine. You cannot describe what was never imagined. So the word is left open, like a door, and you are invited to walk through it for eternity.

[PAUSE]

But the tradition does not leave us *entirely* in the dark about what the "more" is. This is where the linguistic journey meets something extraordinary.

The Companions and the early scholars were asked: what is this **مَزِيد**? And a remarkable cluster of them — Anas ibn Mālik, Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh, and others — gave one answer. Al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi, and al-Muyassar all preserve it. The **مَزِيد**, they said, is:

**النَّظَر إلى وجه الله الكريم** — *the gaze upon the noble Face of Allah.*

And this was not mere opinion. Al-Qurtubi notes carefully that this understanding is anchored in *akhbār marfūʿa* — reports traced up to the Prophet ﷺ himself — connected to a parallel verse. In Sūrat Yūnus (10:26), Allah says: *"For those who do good is the best [reward] — and more"* — *al-ḥusnā wa ziyāda.* The same root. The same "more." *Ziyāda.* And in a sound, elevated tradition, the Prophet ﷺ explained that the *ziyāda* — the "more" — is **looking upon the Face of Allah.**

So when al-Muyassar glosses our verse, it says the *mazīd* is increase of bliss, and *"the greatest of it is the gaze upon the noble Face of Allah."*

[PAUSE]

Now bring the three pieces together and feel the architecture click.

— **مَّا يَشَآءُونَ**: everything you could *imagine* wanting. Limitless within the bounds of imagination.
— **فِيهَا**: and all of it is *in the Garden* — in the place, among the gifts.
— **وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ**: and beyond all of that, *with Us* — in the presence, not the place — is the one joy imagination could never reach: to **see Him.**

The grammar was telling us the whole time. The *mazīd* is "with Us," not "in it," because the *mazīd* **is** being with Us. The location word *was* the answer. The "more" is found *ladaynā* because the "more" is *Him.*

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the shape of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The ceiling you keep hitting — and why "everything you want" was never the answer

Let me start where you actually live, today, in this world.

There is an experience nearly every honest human being eventually has. You want something — a job, a person, a house, a number in a bank account, a level of recognition — and you want it with your whole chest. You're certain that *when I get this, I'll have arrived.* This is the thing. This is the door to the contentment I've been chasing.

And then you get it.

And there's a window — a week, a month, if you're lucky a year — where it's wonderful. And then, quietly, something you didn't expect happens. The wanting comes back. Not for that thing — you *have* that thing — but for a new thing, one notch further out. The horizon, which you were *sure* you had finally reached, has simply moved. You stand exactly where you dreamed of standing, and you feel the old, familiar pull toward somewhere else.

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it the **hedonic treadmill.** You run and run toward the things you desire, and the ground moves under you, and you end up roughly where you started — wanting. The set-point resets. The pleasures normalize. The ceiling you broke through becomes the floor you stand on, and a new ceiling appears above you.

Most people draw one of two conclusions from this. The cynic says: *desire is a trap, wanting is suffering, the answer is to stop wanting.* The consumer says: *I just haven't gotten the right thing yet — keep running.* Both are wrong, and this ayah tells us why.

Look again at the first half: **لَهُم مَّا يَشَآءُونَ فِيهَا.** *For them is whatever they wish, therein.*

Allah does **not** say the answer is to stop wanting. In Paradise, the people of Paradise still *wish* — *yashā'ūn,* present tense, ongoing — and their wishes are *met.* The treadmill is not abolished by killing desire. It is abolished by **a desire that is finally, fully, permanently satisfied.** Every wish met, with no reset, no normalization, no horizon sliding away. The thing this world could never give you — a wanting that actually arrives — is given completely.

[PAUSE]

So here is the first hard, beautiful truth: **your inability to be satisfied in this world is not a defect to be ashamed of. It is a piece of information.** The treadmill is not lying to you when it tells you "this isn't it." It is telling you the truth. *This* isn't it. No amount of this is it. You were built with a wanting too large for the world — and that is not a bug, it is a homing signal.

But now comes the part that should break us open.

Even *that* — even a Paradise where every wish is met forever — even **that** is not the final word of the ayah. Because after Allah grants the fully-satisfied desire — the thing we cannot even fathom from inside our restless world — He says: **وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ.** *And with Us is more.*

Stop and feel the vertigo of this.

We cannot, from down here, even *imagine* the contentment of a wish that never resets. That alone is beyond us. And Allah stations it as the **floor.** As the baseline. As the thing that comes *before* the word "more." The satisfaction of every desire you could ever have is, in the economy of this verse, **the setup** — and the "more" is what comes after.

What that tells you about the size of what you were made for is almost unbearable. Your heart is so vast that *even infinite wish-fulfillment does not fill it to the brim.* There is a chamber in you that the Garden, with all its gifts, leaves untouched. A chamber that only opens *ladaynā* — in His presence.

So when you feel, in this life, that nagging incompleteness even in your happiest moments — when you get the thing and the ache remains — do not despair, and do not numb it, and do not run faster. **Recognize it.** That ache is the shape of the chamber that only Allah fills. You have been carrying, your whole life, a hunger that is the exact size and shape of nearness to God. Nothing else fits the hole because nothing else was meant to.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time you catch yourself certain that *this one thing* will finally complete you — pause and tell yourself the truth this verse taught you. *Even if I get it, even if I get all of it, even if I get a Paradise of it — there is still a "more," and the "more" is Him.* Let that re-rank your wanting. Not by killing the small desires, but by refusing to let any of them sit in the seat that belongs only to nearness to Allah. Keep that seat empty for its rightful occupant.

### Theme Two: The gift and the Giver — and which one your heart is actually for

Here's a question to start with, and I want you to answer it honestly inside yourself.

When you make duʿā', when you raise your hands — what are you reaching for? Are you reaching for **what Allah can give you**, or are you reaching for **Allah**?

For most of us, most of the time — and there's no shame in admitting it — it's the first. We come to Allah the way a child comes to a parent's purse. We want the *out of* — the relief, the rizq, the cure, the spouse, the way out of the hard thing. We love the Giver, genuinely, but often we love Him *for* His gifts. The gifts are in the foreground; the Giver is the means to the gifts.

Now bring back the contrast we uncovered in the grammar. **فِيهَا** versus **لَدَيْنَا.** *Therein* versus *with Us.*

The gifts are *fīhā* — in the Garden.
The "more" — the greatest thing — is *ladaynā* — with Him.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah is doing? He is, with infinite gentleness, **reordering your love.** He is showing you that the entire created Paradise — every river, every fruit, every reunion, every pleasure that ever crossed a human mind — *all of it* belongs to one category: **the gift.** And then He points past all of it and says: the real treasure was never the gift. It was always **the Giver.** *With Us is more.*

There is a piercing way to feel this. Imagine the people of Paradise. They have **مَّا يَشَآءُونَ** — everything they could wish. They are, by definition, the most satisfied beings in existence. And then, the tradition tells us, the call comes — that they will look upon His Face. And every scholar who described that moment said the same thing: in that instant, **they forget the Garden.** Everything *fīhā* — all of it — pales. Not because the gifts became less, but because the Giver was always infinitely more, and now they can finally see it.

That is the test, isn't it? That is the whole test of the human heart, run forward to its conclusion. Will you, when surrounded by every gift, still turn toward the Giver? Will the One who gave still be more beloved to you than the things He gave?

And here is the mercy in it: **you can begin answering that question now.** You don't have to wait for the Garden. Every time you worship Allah not *for* something but simply because He is your Lord and you long for Him — you are training the muscle that will, in the end, turn your face from the gift to the Giver. Every time your duʿā' includes not just "give me X" but "and let me be near You" — you are reaching for the *mazīd* while still in this world.

[PAUSE]

And remember where this verse sits. This is Surah Qaf — the surah that told you, nineteen verses earlier, that Allah is **"nearer to him than his jugular vein"** (50:16). The surah opens by telling you how close Allah *already is* to you, right now, in your struggle, in the whispering of your own soul. And it closes the garden scene by telling you what the ultimate reward is: to be **with Him** — *ladaynā.*

Do you feel the arc? The nearness that is *hidden* now — He is closer than your vein, but you cannot see Him — becomes the nearness that is *unveiled* then. *With Us is more.* The whole surah is one long love letter about closeness: the closeness you cannot yet perceive, ripening into the closeness that is the highest joy of eternity.

The gift was always the road. The Giver was always the home.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Audit one duʿā'. Just one. Take a request you make often — and before you ask for the thing, ask for *Him.* "Yā Allah, before I ask You for this, I ask You for nearness to You. Let me be of those for whom there is *mazīd.*" Watch what it does to the rest of the duʿā'. Watch how the thing you were desperate for re-sizes itself once you've named the real desire underneath it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, now that we can see it

Let's stand back and look at the whole verse as one built thing, now that we've handled every stone.

**لَّهُم مَّا يَشَآءُونَ فِيهَا — وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ**

Two halves, and they are a deliberate ascent.

The **first half** is the world of the *gift*: *for them* — *whatever they wish* — *therein.* Everything is here: the recipients, the limitless wishing, the place. It is complete. It is a finished Paradise. If the verse had ended at *fīhā,* it would already be the most generous promise imaginable.

The **second half** lifts off from that completeness: *and with Us — is more.* Notice it mirrors and transcends the first. Where the first half located joy *fīhā* — in the place — the second locates it *ladaynā* — in the Presence. Where the first half named the wishers and their wishes, the second names only **Us** — Allah Himself — and leaves the "more" undefined, open, infinite.

The verse *moves you* from the gift to the Giver, from the place to the Presence, from what you can imagine to what you never could. The structure is not decoration. **The structure is the meaning.** Allah built the sentence to walk your heart from the Garden to its Lord.

### Bringing the two roads together

We came at this verse from two directions, and they arrive at the same point.

The **linguistic road** showed us: the object of "wish" is left empty (limitless desire), the joy is located *with Us* not *in it* (Presence over place), and the "more" is left undefined (boundless, beyond conception). Three open spaces in the grammar — and each one is Allah making room for something too large to name.

The **thematic road** showed us: the restless human heart hits a ceiling on every worldly desire (the treadmill), and even a Paradise of fulfilled desire leaves one chamber untouched — the chamber that only *nearness to Allah* can fill. The gift was always the road; the Giver was always the home.

They are the same insight from two sides. **The empty space in the grammar is the empty chamber in the heart.** Allah left the object of "wish" unnamed and the "more" undefined for the exact same reason your deepest longing has no name in any language: because it is not a *thing.* It is *Him.* And the only word large enough to hold it is the word He chose to leave open — *mazīd.*

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. When I imagine Paradise, am I imagining the *gifts* — or am I imagining *being near Allah?* Which one actually moves me? And what does my honest answer reveal about where my heart currently lives?

2. The ache I feel even in my happiest, most "I-got-what-I-wanted" moments — have I been treating it as a problem to fix, when it might be a signal to follow?

3. In my duʿā', what sits in the foreground — the Giver, or the gift? If I'm honest, would I still come to Him as eagerly if He offered only Himself, and nothing *fīhā?*

4. What would change in how I chase things this week if I truly believed that the seat at the center of my heart is reserved, and only One can fill it?

### One-sentence distillation

Allah promises the people of Paradise every joy their hearts can imagine — and then promises **more:** the one joy no heart could imagine, found not *in* the Garden but *with Him.*

### The closing invitation

You don't have to wait for the Garden to begin reaching for the *mazīd.* The whole point of the verse is that the greatest treasure was never the *stuff* — not here, not there, not ever. It was always nearness to the One who is *already* nearer to you than your jugular vein.

So let this verse re-rank your wanting. Keep wanting — Paradise itself is full of people who still wish. But keep one seat empty in the center of your desire, the seat that belongs to nearness to Allah, and refuse to let any gift sit down in it. Reach, in your worship and your duʿā', not just for what He can give, but for **Him.** That reaching is the muscle that turns a face from the gift to the Giver — and it is the only muscle that will matter on the Day the *mazīd* is unveiled.

### A brief du'a

> O Allah, You who are nearer to us than our own veins — do not let us be content with Your gifts while forgetting You, the Giver. Make us of those for whom there is *mazīd* — and let the *mazīd* You grant us be the gaze upon Your noble Face. We ask You, before we ask You for anything in the Garden, for nearness to You. *Āmīn.*
