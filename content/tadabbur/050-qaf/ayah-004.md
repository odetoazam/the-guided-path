# What the Earth Takes Is Already Counted

### Surah Qaf, Ayah 4

> قَدْ عَلِمْنَا مَا تَنقُصُ ٱلْأَرْضُ مِنْهُمْ ۖ وَعِندَنَا كِتَـٰبٌ حَفِيظٌ
>
> *Qad ʿalimnā mā tanquṣu-l-arḍu minhum, wa ʿindanā kitābun ḥafīẓ.*
>
> **"Certainly We know what the earth diminishes of them — and with Us is a guarded Book."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Objection Behind the Objection

I want you to begin not with the verse, but with the moment the verse is answering. Because this ayah is a reply. And you cannot feel the weight of an answer until you have stood inside the question.

Go back one breath, to ayah three. The people of Makkah are listening to a man tell them that the dead will rise. And they laugh. Not a cruel laugh, necessarily — more the laugh of someone who has just been told something physically absurd. And they say:

*"أَإِذَا مِتْنَا وَكُنَّا تُرَابًا ۖ ذَٰلِكَ رَجْعٌ بَعِيدٌ"* — **"When we have died and become dust — *that* is a far-fetched return."**

Sit inside their objection for a moment, because it is not a stupid one. It is, in fact, the most natural objection a human being has ever raised. Their logic runs like this: a body, once it dies, does not simply switch off like a lamp. It *disperses.* It is taken apart. The flesh returns to soil, the soil is blown across valleys, drawn up into plants, eaten, scattered, rained on, washed into rivers, pressed into the deep earth over centuries. The very atoms that were *you* are, after enough time, distributed across the whole face of the world. There is no "body" left to raise. There is no "you" left to find. You have not just died — you have been *un-assembled,* erased into the general dust of everything.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing I need you to notice. Their objection is not really about *power.* They are not saying "God is not strong enough to do this." They are saying something subtler and, honestly, more modern: they are saying *there is nothing left to act upon.* The information is gone. The record is lost. You cannot reassemble a book whose pages have been burned and the ashes scattered to the wind — not because you lack glue, but because the *thing itself* no longer exists to be reassembled.

This is the objection behind the objection. And it is *our* objection too. Every one of us, somewhere underneath, carries the quiet dread that death is not a door but a *dissolving* — that whatever made us *us* simply diffuses back into the indifferent matter of the universe and is gone. Forgotten. Untraceable.

Now — watch how Allah answers.

He does not, in this verse, talk about His power. He does not say "I am able." That comes later in the surah. Here, in the very first counter-stroke, He goes straight to the hidden premise. The deniers assumed that *dispersal equals loss.* That *scattered equals gone.* And Allah replies, with a precision that should stop your heart:

**"Certainly We know what the earth diminishes of them — and with Us is a guarded Book."**

[PAUSE]

He does not argue that He *can* rebuild you. He informs you that the demolition was *catalogued.* Every particle the earth has ever taken from a human body — *We knew it as it left.* And it is written down, in a record that loses nothing.

Here is the hook I want you to carry through everything that follows. We are about to discover that in this single short verse, Allah does something extraordinary: He turns the most terrifying image a human can imagine — the body coming apart in the dark of the earth — into an image of *safekeeping.* He takes the word for *loss* and reveals it was *accounting* all along.

How does He do it in nine words? Let us go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Qad ʿalimnā* — The Knowing That Came First

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because the whole emotional force of the opening hangs on a single small word that English simply throws away.

The verse opens: **قَدْ عَلِمْنَا** — *qad ʿalimnā.*

Two things are happening here. First, the verb *ʿalimnā,* "We knew," is in the **perfect tense** — the tense Arabic uses for what is *completed, settled, done.* Not "We are coming to know," not "We will know." **We *knew.*** Already. In the past. Finished.

And in front of it sits this little particle **قَدْ — *qad.*** Now, *qad* before a perfect-tense verb is what the grammarians call *ḥarf taḥqīq* — a particle of *certainty,* of *verification.* It takes something already stated as fact and presses down on it: *certainly, indeed, without question.* In everyday Arabic, if someone doubts you and you want to settle it, you put *qad* in front of the verb. It is the linguistic equivalent of looking someone in the eye and saying, "Yes. It is *already* done. This is not in dispute."

So before we even reach the content, the *form* has answered the deniers. They said, "That is a far-fetched return" — speaking in the future, speaking in the realm of *maybe, somehow, someday.* And the very first words of the reply come back not in the future but in the **completed past:** *We have* ***already*** *known.*

[PAUSE]

Now let me ask you to sit with what that timing means. *When* did Allah know what the earth would take from these bodies?

The grammar says: *already.* Before the question was asked. Before the body died. Before the body was even *born.* The knowing did not arrive in response to the decomposition. The knowing *preceded* the decomposition. The earth has not yet taken a single atom from you, and it is *already* counted.

Here is what is so striking. To the deniers, the sequence felt like this: *you live, you die, you scatter, and at the very end — far too late — God would somehow have to go hunting for your pieces.* As if reassembly were a frantic search through the rubble. And Allah dismantles that entire picture with one tense. The search never happens, because **nothing was ever lost to be searched for.** The knowledge was complete before the loss began.

Think of it this way. There is a difference between a man who watches his house burn down and *then* tries to remember what was inside, and a man who has, sitting in a vault across town, a perfect itemized inventory of every single object in that house, written *before the fire.* For the first man, the fire is a catastrophe of memory — things are gone that he will never recover even the *names* of. For the second man, the fire changes the *location* of his possessions, but not his *knowledge* of them. Every item still exists, fully, in the record.

[PAUSE]

*Qad ʿalimnā.* We *already* knew. You were inventoried before you were ever taken apart.

And notice — this is the answer to *our* dread, not just theirs. The fear that death dissolves the *information* of you, that whatever made you a specific, named, particular soul gets smeared back into anonymous matter — that fear assumes God is the *first* man, sifting the ashes after the fact. The verse tells you He is the second. The record exists *before* the fire. **You cannot be lost to the One who knew you before you were taken.**

Let that be the first thing we hold. Now the verse turns to the *taking* itself — and chooses a word that should surprise us.

---

### Element Two: *Mā tanquṣu-l-arḍ* — When "Loss" Is the Wrong Word

Here is the heart of the verse, and it turns on a single verb. Allah says the earth does something to the bodies of the dead, and the verb He chooses is **تَنقُصُ — *tanquṣu,*** from the root *n-q-ṣ.*

Now what does this root mean? In its plain, lexical sense, *naqaṣa* means **to diminish, to decrease, to reduce in number or amount.** It is a word of *subtraction.* When you have ten and you are left with nine, that is *naqs.* When the moon wanes from full, that is *naqs.* When a scale tips because something was taken off it, that is *naqs.* It is, at its core, a word about *quantity going down.*

I want you to feel how *cold* and *precise* this word is, and why that is the point.

Allah could have used other verbs for what the grave does to a body. There are richer, more visceral words in Arabic — words for *consuming,* for *devouring,* for *rotting,* for *destroying.* The classical commentators, in fact, *explain* this verse with exactly those words: al-Baghawī glosses it as the earth "eating from their flesh and their blood and their bones." Al-Qurṭubī says "what it eats of their bodies." The *reality* being described is decay, decomposition, the slow dissolution of a corpse — there is no avoiding that.

But Allah does not *say* "eats." He says ***tanquṣu*** — "diminishes," "subtracts," "reduces." Why choose the accountant's word for the gravedigger's work?

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the quiet genius of the verse.

*Naqs* — diminishment — is a word that only has meaning *against a known total.* You cannot say something has been "diminished" unless you know what the *full amount* was. To "subtract" presupposes that there was a sum, that the sum was counted, and that you are tracking what remains. The word *naqs* secretly *contains* the claim that someone is keeping the books. A pile of dust scattered by the wind is not "diminished" — it is just *gone,* because no one was counting it. But a quantity that is being *diminished* is a quantity that is being *watched.*

Do you see what He has done? He described decomposition — the most chaotic, uncontrolled, entropic process we can imagine, a body coming apart over centuries — and He labeled it with a word that implies **precise, ongoing accounting.** To the eye of the deniers, the grave is a place of *erasure.* To the language of Allah, the grave is a place of *bookkeeping.* The earth is not destroying the dead. The earth is making *withdrawals* — and every withdrawal is recorded against a balance that the Owner knows exactly.

[PAUSE]

And now look at the object of the verb. *What* does the earth diminish? The Arabic says: **مَا — *mā,*** "*what.*"

Not "their bodies." Not "their bones." Not "their flesh." Just — ***mā.*** "What the earth diminishes *of them.*"

This is an open, unspecified relative pronoun, and its openness is doing real work. Allah does not name the thing being taken, because to name it — "the flesh," "the bones" — would be to *limit* it. By leaving it as the bare *"what,"* the verse expands to cover **every single particle, of every kind, by every means, over all of time.** Whatever the earth takes — a fingernail dissolved in the first month, a sliver of bone worn down over a thousand years, a trace of you carried off in a root and a river — all of it falls under this one unspecified *"what."* The vagueness is not imprecision. It is *totality.* He left it open precisely so that *nothing* could fall outside it.

Notice the asymmetry, too. He specifies the *knowing* with total clarity — *qad ʿalimnā,* certainly, completely, We knew. But He leaves the *thing taken* deliberately unnamed. The knowledge is exact; the loss is unbounded. And that is exactly right: **His knowledge is precise enough to encompass a loss that is, from our side, beyond all naming.** You could not list everything the earth has taken from a human body across the centuries. He does not need you to. *Mā tanquṣu* — *whatever* it takes — is already inside *qad ʿalimnā.*

So here is what this single clause reveals, and I want to state it cleanly before we move to the last word. **What looks to us like dissolution is, to Allah, a tracked transaction. The grave does not erase you. It withdraws you — particle by particle — from a balance that He has never once stopped counting.**

[PAUSE]

And then, as if to seal it, comes the last word of the verse.

---

### Element Three: *Kitābun Ḥafīẓ* — The Record That Keeps and Is Kept

The verse closes: **وَعِندَنَا كِتَـٰبٌ حَفِيظٌ** — *wa ʿindanā kitābun ḥafīẓ* — "and with Us is a guarded Book."

Two things here deserve our full attention: the *placement* of "with Us," and the strange, double-edged word *ḥafīẓ.*

First, the placement. In Arabic, the normal order would be to mention the Book and then say where it is. But the verse *front-loads* the location: **عِندَنَا — *ʿindanā,* "with Us"** — comes *first,* before the word "Book." In Arabic rhetoric, moving a word forward out of its expected slot is called *taqdīm,* and it creates emphasis — it throws the weight of the sentence onto the thing brought forward. So the stress is not merely "there is a Book." The stress is: **with *Us* — right here, in Our keeping, near, present, and inaccessible to anyone else — is a Book.**

Feel the contrast the verse is building. The body is *out there* — scattered across the earth, dispersed into the soil and the wind, *far* from anyone's reach. But the record of it is *ʿindanā* — *here, with Us, close.* The pieces may be everywhere; the *ledger* is in one place, in the safest place that exists. Your scattered atoms have one fixed point they all answer to.

[PAUSE]

Now the word that crowns the entire verse: **حَفِيظ — *ḥafīẓ.*** Root *ḥ-f-ẓ:* to guard, to preserve, to protect, to keep. This is the root behind *al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ,* "the Preserved Tablet," and behind *ḥāfiẓ,* the one who has *preserved* the entire Qur'an in his heart.

Here is the beautiful difficulty. *Ḥafīẓ* sits on the morphological pattern *faʿīl* — and this pattern in Arabic is famously two-faced. It can carry a **passive** meaning — *that which is guarded, kept, preserved* (like *maḥfūẓ*). Or it can carry an **active,** intensive meaning — *the one who guards, who preserves, who keeps* (like *ḥāfiẓ*). And the classical scholars, reading this word, did not all land in the same place — which is itself a gift, because each reading is a different camera angle on the same truth.

Let me show you the composite they build together.

Al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar read it toward the **passive** sense: a Book that is itself *guarded* — *maḥfūẓ* — protected from any corruption, any alteration, any tampering. This is the Preserved Tablet, sealed against the meddling of devils, against being effaced or edited or lost. The record cannot be hacked. It cannot decay the way the body decays. While *your* atoms are subject to the wear of the world, the *account* of them is held in something that the world's entropy cannot touch.

Al-Baghawī, beautifully, holds *both* meanings up at once. He reports the passive reading — guarded from the devils and from being worn away and changed — *and* the active one: that *ḥafīẓ* here means *ḥāfiẓ,* "preserving" — actively *keeping* their number and their names. Not just a sealed archive, but a *vigilant* keeper that retains every count and every identity.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the *faʿīl* form lets the word do? It refuses to choose. It is a Book that is so perfectly *kept* that it also *keeps* — guarded and guarding in one breath. It is preserved from all loss, and *therefore* it preserves *you* without loss. The two meanings are not rivals; they are the same fact stated from two sides. **Because nothing can be taken *from* the record, nothing about you can be lost *in* it.**

And put it now beside everything that came before. The earth *subtracts* — *tanquṣu.* The Book *preserves* — *ḥafīẓ.* These are the two opposite motions of the whole verse, set side by side. Out there, in the realm of dust, the number goes *down.* Here, in the realm of *ʿindanā,* the number is *kept whole.* The verse holds *diminishment* and *preservation* in a single frame — and tells you, unmistakably, which one is the *reality.* The diminishing is the appearance. The preserving is the truth.

Al-Qurṭubī draws the conclusion out loud: *"so that nothing of us escapes Him — to the point that re-creating us could never be difficult for Him."* The whole point of telling you the record is perfect is to collapse the deniers' objection. There is nothing to *find,* because nothing was ever *lost.* The reassembly the deniers thought impossible turns out to be the easiest thing of all — you do not rebuild from rubble; you read back from a record that never let a single particle slip.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these nine words have built. A particle of certainty that puts the *knowing* before the *loss.* A verb of *subtraction* that secretly means *accounting.* An unnamed *"what"* that quietly encompasses everything. A location, *ʿindanā,* that gathers the scattered into one safe point. And a final word that is both *guarded* and *guarding,* sealing the whole thing shut.

The linguistic construction has shown us its architecture. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the two oldest fears in the human heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Terror of Dissolution, and the One Who Counts the Dust

Let me start where you actually live, not in a graveyard but in an ordinary, modern fear.

There is a particular dread that haunts the contemporary mind, and it is not the dread of *pain* or even, exactly, of death. It is the dread of *dissolution.* Of becoming *nothing-in-particular.* We feel it when we stand in front of an old photograph of a crowd and realize we cannot name a single face — people who laughed, who loved, who were the center of their own universe, now just *anonymous figures in a faded image.* We feel it at a vast cemetery, walking past worn stones whose names have weathered off entirely. We feel it when we read that the atoms in our bodies will, given enough time, be recycled into soil and sea and other living things, until there is no "us" left anywhere — just *matter,* redistributed.

This is the secular version of the deniers' objection, and it is everywhere. The fear is not "will I be punished?" The fear is "**will I simply be *erased*?** Will the specific, particular, named *me* — my inner life, my exact identity — diffuse back into the general stuff of the world until there is nothing left to even *refer to*?"

[PAUSE]

Now bring this verse to that fear, and watch what it does.

The deniers pointed at the most extreme case of dissolution — *"when we have become dust"* — as their *proof* that the self is lost. They picked the worst-case scenario, the body fully decomposed and scattered, *precisely because* it seemed like the clearest possible example of total erasure. And Allah takes their worst case and uses it as His *evidence in the opposite direction.* He says: yes. The earth diminishes you. Down to the last particle. *And I knew every particle as it left.* The very scattering you offer as proof of your erasure is, to Me, a *fully attended event.*

Here is the reframe, and I want you to feel how total it is. **You cannot dissolve out of God's knowledge.** Dissolution is a *physical* process; it operates on *matter;* it scatters *atoms.* But His knowledge is not a physical thing that the scattering can scatter. The atoms going their separate ways does nothing whatsoever to the *record* of them, because the record was never made of atoms. The earth can take the *substance* of you. It cannot reach the *knowledge* of you — which is *ʿindanā,* with Him, in a different order of being entirely, where *ḥafīẓ* means nothing is ever lost.

[PAUSE]

And there is a tenderness in this that I do not want us to rush past. We tend to imagine being "known by God" primarily as being *watched* — as surveillance, as accountability, sometimes as threat. But sit with what it means *here.* In the grave, when every human being who ever loved you has also died, when your name has worn off the stone, when not one living person on the earth remembers that you existed, when even your *atoms* have forgotten the shape they once held together — in *that* absolute abandonment, this verse says: **you are still being counted.** Someone still knows exactly what was taken. Someone still holds the complete record of the particular, specific, named soul that you are.

To be known like that is not surveillance. It is the opposite of being forgotten. It is the only thing in the universe that can survive the grave *with* you — the fact that the One who matters has *never once lost track.* The Prophet ﷺ told us, in a ḥadīth recorded in the authentic collections, that "all of the son of Adam will be consumed by the earth except the *ʿajb al-dhanab* — the tailbone — from it he was created and from it he will be reassembled." The earth takes nearly everything. But "nearly everything" is not "everything that *matters,*" because the part that matters was never the *atoms.* It was the *knowledge,* and the *record,* and the *seed* He keeps for the rebuilding.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time the fear of being forgotten touches you — the fear of being un-special, un-remembered, one anonymous face in the crowd of seven billion — answer it the way the verse answers it. You are not tracked by your fame, or your legacy, or whether anyone living remembers your name. You are tracked by the One for whom *mā tanquṣu* — *whatever* is taken — is already inside *qad ʿalimnā* — *already known.* Live as someone who is *counted,* not someone who is *crowd.* You have an audience of One who will never lose your file.

### Theme Two: The Anxiety of the Lost Record — and the Book That Keeps Everything

Let me bring you to the second fear, and it is the most *modern* fear of all, even though this verse is fourteen centuries old.

We are the people of the *lost file.* Think about how much of your anxiety, in this actual life, orbits around *records being lost.* The document you didn't save before the crash. The photographs trapped on a phone that died. The password you cannot recover, locking away years of your own life. The sick feeling when a hard drive fails and you realize that *memories you thought were permanent* are simply *gone* — not somewhere you can't reach, but *nowhere at all.* We have built an entire civilization on storing things, and we live in low-grade terror of *losing what we stored.* Backups of backups, and still the fear.

And underneath the practical fear is a deeper one: that *nothing is truly kept.* That every record decays. That memory — ours, our parents', humanity's — is a leaking vessel, and that everything pours out of it eventually. The historians cannot recover most of what happened. The families cannot recall their own great-grandparents' faces. *Even we* forget the texture of days we swore we'd never forget. The universe seems to be, at bottom, a machine for *losing information.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold that fear up against the last two words of this verse: ***kitābun ḥafīẓ.*** A Book. That is *ḥafīẓ.* Guarded, and guarding.

Everything you have ever known as a "record" shares one fatal property: it is made of *stuff,* and *stuff* degrades. Stone weathers. Paper burns. Ink fades. Drives fail. Neurons die. Every vessel we have ever used to *keep* something is itself subject to the same decay as the thing it keeps. That is precisely why the deniers were right to worry — in *their* world, in *our* world, all records are mortal.

And this is exactly the wall that the word *ḥafīẓ* breaks through. The whole force of choosing this word — guarded *from* corruption, sealed *against* alteration, as al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar read it — is to declare a record that does **not** share the mortality of what it records. The body decays; the Book does not. The world is an engine of *naqs,* of subtraction, of things going down — but *ʿindanā,* with Him, sits a Book that the engine cannot touch. For the first time, you are being told of a record made of *something other than perishable stuff* — a keeping that is not itself subject to being lost.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what that does to the *meaning* of everything you fear losing.

You think the danger is that things get *lost.* The verse quietly tells you: in the deepest reality, **nothing is.** Every moment of your life — the ones you saved and the ones you let slip, the deeds you remember and the ones you forgot the same evening, the kindness no one witnessed, the wrong no one caught, the prayer in the dark, the thought that crossed your mind and vanished — *all of it* is in a Book that is *ḥafīẓ.* Al-Muyassar says it plainly: the Book records *"all that befalls them in their lives and after their deaths."* Nothing falls out of it. There is no crash. There is no unsaved draft. There is no forgotten file.

And this is a double-edged comfort, and it *should* be — because a record that keeps *everything* is, for the human being, both the greatest reassurance and the most searching summons. Reassurance: not one atom of your good is lost; not one quiet act of faithfulness that the world failed to notice has slipped through. The same word, *ḥafīẓ,* that promises your scattered *body* is kept, promises your scattered *deeds* are kept. But also summons: if the Book keeps everything, then the way you live is not being *forgotten* as fast as you forget it. You are, right now, in this hour, *being recorded* into something that does not lose its contents. The grave is not the eraser. There is no eraser.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** stop living as though your unwitnessed moments are *unrecorded* moments. The kindness no one saw is not lost; it is *ʿindanā.* And the compromise you made when you were sure no one was watching is not lost either. Let the word *ḥafīẓ* do to your private life what it does to the buried body — let it tell you that *nothing is too small to be kept,* and live as someone whose every hour is being written into a Book that never, ever, loses a page.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look back now at how the two halves of the verse mirror each other, because the architecture is the proof of the whole thing.

The verse is built in two clauses, and they are *opposites that turn out to be one truth.* The first clause is about *the body, out there, going down:* **the earth diminishes** — *tanquṣu,* subtraction, dispersal, the realm of dust and distance. The second clause is about *the record, in here, kept whole:* **with Us is a guarded Book** — *ʿindanā... ḥafīẓ,* preservation, presence, the realm of the unlosable. Diminishment on the left. Preservation on the right. The scattering of the substance, and the keeping of the knowledge. And bridging them, holding both in His single grip, is the *qad ʿalimnā* at the very front — the *knowing that came first,* the certainty that makes the subtraction nothing more than a transaction recorded against a balance that was always complete.

That is the architecture: *loss* and *keeping,* set face to face, with *knowledge* standing over both — and the verse leaves you in no doubt which one is real.

And see how the linguistic discoveries and the human themes turn out to be the *same insight from two directions.* The word *naqs* — "diminishment" presupposing a known total — is, in lived terms, the answer to the *fear of dissolution:* you cannot be "subtracted" unless you are first *counted.* And the word *ḥafīẓ* — guarded and guarding — is, in lived terms, the answer to the *fear of the lost record:* a keeping that does not share the mortality of the kept. The grammar and the consolation are one thing. The earth's withdrawal is the deniers' *proof of erasure* and Allah's *proof of accounting* — the very same fact, read by two opposite hearts.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, in your life, are you most afraid of being *forgotten* — and what would change if you truly believed that the One who matters has *never once lost your file?*

2. You guard your photographs, your documents, your passwords with such care. With what care do you live, knowing that your *deeds* are being kept in a Book that is far more *ḥafīẓ* than any drive you own?

3. The deniers used the *worst case* — total decomposition — as proof against resurrection. What "worst cases" in your own life have you been reading as proof of God's *absence,* when they might be evidence of His *attention?*

4. If nothing you do is ever truly *lost* — not the kindness no one saw, not the compromise no one caught — how does that change the way you will spend the next *unwitnessed* hour?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Before the earth ever took a single particle of you, it was already counted, named, and kept — so the grave is not the place where you are erased, but the place where the safekeeping shows.**

### The Closing Invitation

You came into this verse, perhaps, carrying the oldest fear there is — that to die is to dissolve, to scatter, to become un-you. Let the invitation be this: to walk back out of it carrying the *counter-fact.* You are not loosely held by a forgetful universe. You are *known* — completely, already, in the finished past tense of *qad ʿalimnā* — and you are *kept,* in a Book that the world's decay cannot reach. Not to terrify you into watching your step, but to *steady* you. The most scattered thing in creation — a body returned to dust — is, in this verse, the most carefully accounted-for thing of all. If *that* is held so perfectly, then so is everything you are afraid of losing. Including you.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who knew us before the earth ever took a particle of our bodies — let our hearts rest in being known by You when no one else remembers our names. You with whom is a Book that is guarded and guarding — keep our good safe in it, and make us live as those who know that nothing we do is ever lost, and nothing of us is ever beyond Your reach. Āmīn.*
