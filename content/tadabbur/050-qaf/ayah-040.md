# A Portion of the Night, and the Trailing Edge of Prostration

> **وَمِنَ ٱلَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْهُ وَأَدْبَـٰرَ ٱلسُّجُودِ**
>
> *wa mina-l-layli fa-sabbiḥhu wa adbāra-s-sujūd*
>
> "And of the night, glorify Him — and in the trailing edges of prostration."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where we are standing

To feel this verse, you have to feel the surah it closes.

Surah Qaf is short, and it is relentless. From its opening it presses one question against the chest of the listener until they can barely breathe: *you really think this is the end?* The deniers had said it out loud — **"When we have died and become dust, that is a far return"** (50:3). Far. Impossible. Absurd. And the surah answers them not with one proof but with a flood: look at the sky built above you, look at the earth spread out, look at the rain that revives a dead land, look at the date palms rising with their clustered fruit. *Like that* — the verse says — *is the coming-forth.*

And then, in the middle of the surah, comes the line that has haunted hearts for fourteen centuries:

**"We are closer to him than his jugular vein."** (50:16)

This is a surah about *nearness*. The nearness of Allah, who knows what the soul whispers. The nearness of death, whose "stupor comes with the truth" (50:19). The nearness of the Hour, when the caller calls from a near place (50:41). Everything in Qaf is closing in.

And the deniers respond to all of it the way deniers always respond — with words. Mockery. Dismissal. *A far return.* So as the surah turns toward its end, Allah turns to His Messenger ﷺ, who is carrying the full weight of being disbelieved to his face, and He says:

**"So be patient over what they say"** (50:39) — *fa-ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn.*

And then He tells him what to do with that patience. Not how to argue back. Not how to win. He tells him to **glorify**. Before sunrise. Before sunset (50:39). And then our verse:

**"And of the night, glorify Him — and in the trailing edges of prostration."** (50:40)

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us.

A man is being called a liar. His message is being mocked as fantasy. The natural human response — the one wired into every nervous system — is to defend, to explain, to *fight the words with words.*

And Allah's instruction is to **turn away from the argument entirely** and pour himself into glorifying God — at the edges of the day, through a portion of the night, and in the strange, almost untranslatable moments that *trail behind* his prostration.

Why this? Why glorification as the answer to slander? And why does Allah measure it out the way He does — not "the night" but *of* the night; not "after the prayer" but *the backs, the trailing edges, of the prostration*?

There are five words here. Let us not leave a single one unopened.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The little word that changes everything: *min*

Let me start with the smallest word in the verse, because Arabic hides its largest meanings in its smallest words.

The verse does not say *wa-l-layla fa-sabbiḥhu* — "and **the night**, glorify Him." It says *wa **min** al-layli* — "and **of** the night."

That one letter — *min* — is a preposition. And in Arabic, *min* has a particular use that grammarians call *al-tabʿīḍ*: **the partitive**. It means *a part of, a portion of, some of.*

Let me teach this before we use it, because once you feel it you will hear it everywhere.

Imagine I hand you a plate of food and say, "Eat the food." That is a command over the whole. Now imagine I say, "Eat *of* the food" — *kul min aṭ-ṭaʿām.* Something has shifted. I am no longer commanding the whole plate. I am inviting you into a portion. *Some* of it. The word *of* carves out a piece.

Now hear the verse again: **"And *of* the night, glorify Him."**

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not command the whole night. He did not say, "spend the night standing." He carved out a portion. *Some* of the night.

Stay with this, because it is mercy hiding in grammar.

This is the same Lord who, in another surah revealed around the same period, says to this same beloved Messenger: **"Stand the night, except a little — half of it, or subtract a little from that, or add to it"** (73:2-4). Even when the command to the night-prayer was at its most demanding, it was *measured, portioned, humane.* The night is not a weight dropped on the worshipper to crush him. It is a field, and he is invited to take *a portion* of it.

And there is something tender in *which* portion is left unspecified. He did not say *the first third* or *the last third.* He said *of the night.* The portion is yours to find. The exhausted nurse coming off a shift, the mother whose sleep is broken by an infant, the student, the laborer — each finds *their* portion. The door is the same; the hour is yours.

Now look at something the grammarians noticed about the *order* of the words. The verse front-loads the time before the command: *wa min al-layli* comes **before** *fa-sabbiḥhu.* In Arabic, when you move a phrase to the front of its sentence — what is called *taqdīm* — you are throwing a spotlight on it. The night is pushed forward, emphasized, held up. As if to say: *of all the portions of time you could give Me, this one — the night — bring Me this one.*

Why the night?

Because the night is the one time no one is watching. The day's worship — the verse just before this one named it: *before the rising of the sun and before its setting* (50:39) — the day's worship happens in the seen world, among people, where prayer can be witnessed, where it can even be performed. But *of the night*? The portion you carve out of your own sleep, in the dark, when no human eye could confirm it and no human voice would praise you for it — **that** portion is the truest measure of who you are with your Lord when the audience is gone.

The deniers in this surah deal in the seen and the public: they *say* things, they mock in the open. And the answer Allah gives His Messenger is to go where they cannot follow — *into a portion of the night,* into the one space that belongs to God alone.

What this single word *min* reveals: **God does not ask for all your night. He asks for a portion — and He asks for it precisely where no one but Him can see you give it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Sabbiḥ*: the word that means to swim away

Now the command itself: **فَسَبِّحْهُ** — *fa-sabbiḥhu* — "so glorify Him."

We translate *tasbīḥ* as "glorify" or "exalt," and that is correct, but it is the dried flower of the word. Let me give you the living plant.

First, the grammar. *Sabbiḥ* is a verb on the pattern the grammarians call **Form II** — *faʿʿala* — built by doubling the middle letter (here the *b*). This is confirmed in the morphology of the verse. And Form II, characteristically, carries **intensity, repetition, thoroughness.** It is the difference between doing a thing and doing it *again and again, deeply, completely.* Not *praise once* — but *keep declaring, thoroughly and repeatedly.*

That is the form. Now the root, which is where the wonder lives.

The root is *s-b-ḥ* (س ب ح). And before it ever meant "to glorify," do you know what *sabaḥa* meant in the speech of the Arabs?

**To swim.** To glide. To move freely and swiftly through a medium — through water, through open space.

This is not my invention; it is the plain lexical meaning, and the Quran itself uses the root this way. Of the sun and moon and stars Allah says: **"each in an orbit *swimming*"** — *kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn* (21:33, 36:40). The celestial bodies *swim* through the heavens. And of the angels, or the souls at death, one surah opens: *wa-n-nāziʿāti... wa-s-sābiḥāti sabḥā* — those that *glide.*

So sit with this. The word for declaring God's transcendence is built from the word for **swimming, gliding, moving freely away.**

[PAUSE]

Why would *that* be the root of *glorification*?

Here is what it invites us to see. To make *tasbīḥ* of Allah is to declare Him *free of* — to move every imperfection, every limit, every unworthy thought *away* from Him. When you say *Subḥān Allāh,* you are not adding a decoration to God. You are *swimming Him clear* — gliding every deficiency away from His reality, declaring Him utterly beyond it, transcendent, far, unreachable by the smallness of what the deniers say.

Now feel why this is the *answer to slander.*

The deniers are throwing words at the Messenger ﷺ — and through him, at God: *a far return... a sorcerer... a liar... fantasy.* They are trying to drag the sacred down into the mud of their categories. And Allah's instruction is not "drag back." It is *sabbiḥ* — **swim Him clear of all of it.** Lift Him, in your heart and on your tongue, *above* and *away* from every small word they could ever say. They throw mud at the sky; you declare the sky too high to be reached.

This is why glorification, and not argument, is the answer. An argument stays down in the arena with the insult, trading blow for blow. *Tasbīḥ* refuses the arena. It says: *the One you are mocking is beyond your mockery; let me glide Him free of it, repeatedly, thoroughly* — that is what Form II demands — *until my own heart is at rest in His transcendence.*

And notice the mercy folded into this for the Messenger himself. When you are slandered, the wound is that the lie threatens to stick — to become how you are *defined.* The cure Allah prescribes is to spend a portion of the night declaring that *God* cannot be defined by His enemies' words. And the heart that learns to swim God clear of every insult slowly learns to be unbothered by the insults thrown at itself. You cannot spend the night exalting the One who is beyond all words, and walk out into the morning crushed by a few of them.

What *sabbiḥ* reveals: **Glorification is not flattery offered upward; it is the act of swimming God free of everything unworthy — and the soul that does it is itself set free.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — *Adbār al-sujūd*: the backs of the prostration

Now the last two words, and the strangest, most beautiful image in the verse: **وَأَدْبَـٰرَ ٱلسُّجُودِ** — *wa adbāra-s-sujūd.*

We rendered it "in the trailing edges of prostration." Let me show you why.

The root is *d-b-r* (د ب ر). Its core meaning is **the back, the rear, what is behind, what comes after.** The *dubur* of a thing is its hindmost part. To *adbara* is to turn your back and go, to recede, to be in the aftermath of something. And — hold onto this — from this very same root comes the word *tadabbur*: **reflection.** Because to *reflect* on a matter is to look at its *outcome,* its *back end,* what trails behind it. The root *d-b-r* lives in the space *after,* in the *consequence,* in the *trailing edge.*

So *adbār al-sujūd* — literally — is **"the backs of the prostration."** The trailing parts. The moments that come *after* you have put your forehead down and lifted it again. Not the prostration itself — its *wake.* What follows in its slipstream.

Now, there is a beautiful textual detail here that the scholars of recitation preserved. The word is read in two ways. The reciters of the Ḥijāz, and Ḥamza, read it *idbār* — with a kasra — which makes it a verbal noun, *the act of finishing, the receding itself.* The others read it *adbār* — with a fatḥa — the plural of *dubur,* **the backs, the ends.** This is reported by al-Baghawī. And as is so often the case with the two valid readings, they do not contradict — they layer. One reading gives you *the act of withdrawing from prostration;* the other gives you *the multiple trailing edges, the ends, of it.* Together: the receding moments, the after-glow of having been down before God.

And what *are* these trailing edges, concretely? Here the early community spoke. Among the narrations: al-Jalālayn and others take it as the **voluntary prayers that trail the obligatory ones** — the sunan performed in the wake of the farḍ. Al-Baghawī gathers a striking chain of names — ʿUmar, ʿAlī, al-Ḥasan, al-Shaʿbī, al-Nakhaʿī, al-Awzāʿī — who said *adbār al-sujūd* is **the two units of prayer after Maghrib.** Others, al-Qurtubī reports, heard in it the *tasbīḥ of the tongue* in those quiet seconds after you rise. These are not rival answers to be chosen between; they are camera angles on one reality: **whatever immediately follows in the wake of your prostration, fill it with Him.**

[PAUSE]

Now let me ask you to feel what this *image* is pointing toward — because remember, the physical is always a stepping stone.

Think about the trailing edge of any powerful experience. You finish a difficult conversation, and there is a *wake* to it — the minutes after, when its meaning is still settling. You wake from a vivid dream, and there is a trailing edge before the day reclaims you. The moment *after* the moment is when the moment is decided. It either lands in you, or it evaporates.

The prostration — *sujūd,* from the root *s-j-d,* the placing of the highest part of you, your face, on the lowest place, the ground — is the most intense act of nearness a body can perform. The Messenger ﷺ taught that the servant is *nearest to his Lord in prostration.* So what does Allah draw attention to here? Not only the prostration — but its **backs, its trailing edges.** The seconds *after* you lift your head.

Because that is exactly where most of us lose it. We come up from sujūd and we are already gone — already reaching for the phone, already back inside the argument with the deniers in our own lives, already in the morning's worry. The nearness we just touched evaporates in its own wake.

And Allah says: *no — the wake is sacred too. Glorify Him in the trailing edges.* Do not let the nearness drain out the instant your forehead leaves the ground. Stay in the slipstream. Let the prostration keep radiating into the minutes that follow it.

There is one more thing the root *d-b-r* is whispering. It is the root of *tadabbur,* reflection-on-outcomes. *Adbār al-sujūd* — the backs of prostration — is, by the very fiber of its root, an invitation to **reflect in the aftermath.** To sit in the wake of nearness and let it be *digested* rather than spilled. The verse names a moment and, in naming it with *this* root, tells you what to do with it: linger, reflect, let it settle into the back of you where it can change you.

What *adbār al-sujūd* reveals: **God lays claim not only to the peak of your nearness but to its wake — because what you do in the trailing seconds after worship decides whether the worship entered you or merely passed through you.**

[PAUSE — major]

### The bridge

Step back and look at what these five words have built.

*Min al-layl* — a **portion**, carved from the unwatched dark. *Sabbiḥ,* Form II — to **swim God clear**, intensely, repeatedly, of everything unworthy. *Adbār al-sujūd* — the **trailing edges**, the sacred wake of nearness. A portion of the hidden time; an act of lifting God beyond all words; and a tending of the moments *after* the peak.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human life is actually held together before God.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — Who you are in the unwatched hours

Let me begin where you live, not where the verse lives.

There is a concept in psychology, and you have felt it even if you never named it. Researchers talk about the gap between our **public self** and our **private self** — who we are when we are seen, versus who we are when the door closes and the audience disappears. And there is a near-universal human finding underneath it: *character is what you do when no one is keeping score.* The cashier who returns the extra change handed to them by mistake, in an empty store, with no camera — that act tells you more about the person than a thousand witnessed kindnesses.

We all know, somewhere, that the unwitnessed version of us is the *real* one. And we are all, somewhere, afraid of it. Because the unwitnessed self is where the slippage happens — the private scroll we would be ashamed of, the resentment we would never say aloud, the corner cut when the boss has gone home.

Now hear the verse again with this in your bones: **"And *of the night,* glorify Him."**

The day's worship — *before sunrise, before sunset* — is the public self. It can be seen. It can be praised. A person can perform it. But Allah does not stop at the public self. He reaches into the one place performance cannot follow him — *a portion of the night* — and He says: *here too. Especially here. Give Me the version of you that only I will ever see.*

Stay with this, because it reframes the whole instruction. The night prayer is not extra credit. It is the **calibration of the private self.** It is the soul saying: *I will not be one person in the masjid and another in the dark. I will be the same.* The integrity that the whole world chases — the seamlessness between the public and the private face — Allah builds it through *a portion of the night.* Because the soul that drags itself out of a warm bed to swim God clear of every imperfection, when not a single human being will ever know it happened, is a soul that has nothing left to perform. It has become *one thing,* all the way down.

And notice the gift inside the demand. The deniers can touch the public self — they can mock it, slander it, exhaust it. *That is the whole pressure of this surah.* But the night portion is **untouchable.** It is a room they cannot enter. When the world has had its say all day long, you have a sanctuary that opens only inward, where no word of theirs can reach, where you and your Lord settle who you really are. The night prayer is not only worship. It is *refuge* — the one self the world cannot get its hands on.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Not "pray the whole night." The verse already refused to ask that — *min al-layl,* a portion. Find *your* portion. It may be ten minutes before the rest of the house wakes. It may be the two units before you sleep. But let there be *something* — some worship that has no audience and earns you no praise — so that you know, with certainty, that your private self belongs to God. Guard one act that only He will ever see. That one act will quietly recalibrate everything the world *does* see.

### Theme Two — Tending the wake: what you do *after* the holy moment

Now the second theme, and again, let me start where you already are.

You have had the experience. A talk that moved you to tears. A funeral that shook you awake to your own mortality. A Ramadan night where, for once, the heart actually softened. A page of Quran that landed like a hand on the chest. In the moment, you thought: *I am changed. This time it is different.*

And then — what happened?

Most of the time, nothing. The moment had a *wake,* and we did not tend it. We walked out of the lecture and into the parking lot conversation. We left the graveside and turned on the radio. We finished the prayer and reached for the phone before our forehead's warmth had faded from the floor. The moment was real. But it **drained out in its own trailing edge,** because we treated the wake as empty space instead of sacred ground.

This is the most common way transformation dies. Not because the holy moment never comes — it comes to all of us — but because we abandon it the instant it peaks. We are present for the prostration and absent for *adbār al-sujūd.* And so the nearness we genuinely touched leaves no residue. We are moved a thousand times and changed by almost none of them.

Now hear Allah's instruction with this ache in view: **"and in the trailing edges of prostration."**

Allah is naming the exact place where you keep losing it — and telling you to *stay.*

The prostration is the peak; you were as near to Him as a body can be. And the verse does not say "and then return to your life." It says: *the backs of it. The wake. Glorify Him there.* Stay in the slipstream a moment longer. Do not let the nearness spill. The two units after Maghrib that ʿUmar and ʿAlī and al-Ḥasan tied to this verse — what are they but a *refusal to let the moment evaporate?* A deliberate lingering in the wake. A way of saying: *I will not touch nearness and immediately drop it.*

And remember what the root *d-b-r* was whispering — it is the root of *tadabbur,* reflection. The trailing edge of prostration is, by its very name, an invitation to **reflect in the aftermath** — to let what just happened be *digested.* This is the difference between a moment that passes *through* you and a moment that passes *into* you. The reflection in the wake is the digestion. Without it, even the holiest experience is a meal swallowed without chewing — it nourishes nothing.

[PAUSE]

Now lift this off the prayer mat, because the prayer is the training ground for the whole of a life.

Every grace in your life has a trailing edge. A recovery from illness has a wake — do you glorify Him in it, or forget Him the moment the fever breaks? A crisis survived, a child born safe, a danger passed — each has its *adbār,* its backs, its after-moments where the meaning is either harvested or wasted. The deniers in this surah are people who experienced rain reviving dead earth, date palms heavy with fruit, the whole reviving world — and felt *nothing in the wake of it.* The signs passed through them and left no mark, because they never lingered in the trailing edge to let the sign do its work. They saw resurrection rehearsed in front of them every spring and still called it *a far return.*

The verse is teaching the opposite reflex. *Tend the wake.* When God draws near — in prayer, in mercy, in fear, in the small reviving rains of an ordinary week — do not turn your back the instant it peaks. Linger in the trailing edge. Glorify Him there. Reflect there. Let it settle into the back of you, where it can actually become *who you are.*

So what does this change about tomorrow?

After the prayer, before you reach for the phone — *stay.* Even ten seconds. Three times *Subḥān Allāh,* slowly, swimming Him clear, while the floor is still warm. Let the prostration have its wake. Build the reflex of lingering, and watch how slowly it spreads — until you are a person who no longer lets the holy moments of your life drain out unused.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the three words and the two themes are one insight seen from different sides.

The **linguistic** journey gave us: a *portion* carved from the unwatched night; the act of *swimming God clear* of every unworthy word; and the *trailing edges* of prostration, the sacred wake.

The **thematic** depths gave us: the calibration of the *private self* in the hours no one sees; and the *tending of the wake* so that nearness is harvested instead of spilled.

And they meet in one place. This verse, closing a surah about deniers who deal in *public words* and *unfelt signs,* hands the believer the two things the deniers most lack: **a private self that belongs wholly to God** (the night portion), and **a capacity to be changed by what passes through them** (the tended wake). The deniers are all public surface and no private depth; all spectacle and no slipstream. The believer Allah is shaping here is the inverse — deepest where no one looks, and most changed in the quiet seconds after.

And the architecture of the two verses together completes the picture. Verse 39 wrapped the **day** — *before sunrise, before sunset,* the two bright edges. Verse 40 reaches into the **night** — its hidden portion — and then into the **wake of prostration** — the trailing edges of nearness itself. Day-edges, night-portion, and the slipstream of sujūd: between these two verses, *the entire cycle of a human day is laced shut with glorification.* There is no hour the deniers' words can find you in which God has not already given you a way to swim Him clear of them.

### Questions to carry

1. Is there a single act of worship in my life that *no one* will ever know about — a portion of my own night that belongs to God alone? If not, what does that say about which self is real?
2. When I rise from prostration, where does my mind go in the next three seconds — and what would change if I stayed?
3. How many holy moments have I genuinely had this year — and how many did I let drain out in their own wake, untended?
4. The slander, the worry, the words that get under my skin — do I argue back down in the mud, or do I *swim God clear* of all of it and let that lift me above the wound?
5. What grace in my life right now still has a trailing edge I have not yet harvested?

### One-sentence distillation

**Give God a portion of the hours no one sees, swim Him clear of everything unworthy, and stay in the sacred wake of your nearness long enough for it to change you.**

### The closing invitation

You will be misunderstood. You will be, at some point, called something you are not. The pressure of this surah is the pressure of a real life. And the answer offered here is not a better comeback. It is an inward turning so deep that the words can no longer reach you — *a portion of the night,* where you become one true thing; and *the trailing edge of prostration,* where you let nearness leave a mark. Not all the night. Just a portion. Not a performance. Just a wake, tended. Begin there — tonight, and in the ten seconds after tomorrow's prayer.

### A brief duʿā

*O Allah, who is nearer to us than our own jugular vein — give us a portion of the night that belongs to You alone, and teach us to swim You clear of every unworthy thought our hearts could hold. Do not let us rise from before You and walk away unchanged; let us linger in the wake of nearness until it settles into who we are. Subḥānaka — far are You, and high, above all that they say.*
