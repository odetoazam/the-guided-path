# The Faculties of Gratitude, Idle for Gratitude

## Surah Al-Mulk, Ayah 23

> قُلْ هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنشَأَكُمْ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ ۖ قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ

*Qul huwa alladhī anshaʾakum wa jaʿala lakumu as-samʿa wal-abṣāra wal-afʾidah. Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.*

**"Say: He is the One who brought you into being, and made for you the hearing, and the sight, and the hearts. How little are you grateful."**

---

## Introduction: The Inventory You Have Never Counted

Let me ask you to do something strange before we begin.

Right now, without moving, notice that you are hearing. There is sound reaching you — the room, your own breath, something distant. You did not switch it on. It has been running every second of your life, even in your sleep, even before you were born, in a chamber of fluid where you had not yet seen a single thing.

Now notice that you are seeing. Your eyes are moving across these words, adjusting, focusing, and you are not aware of issuing a single command.

And now — this is the harder one — notice that you are *understanding*. Something inside you is taking these black marks and turning them into meaning, into feeling, into a sense of being addressed. There is a place in you where all of this lands.

Three faculties. Hearing. Sight. The inner chamber where meaning arrives.

[PAUSE]

You have used all three, continuously, for as long as you have existed. And here is the strange thing: **you have almost never thanked anyone for them.** Not because you are ungrateful by nature. But because they are so constant, so silent, so always-already-there, that they have become invisible to you. You notice your sight the day you lose it. You notice your hearing the day it fades. You notice the inner chamber the day it floods with grief or goes numb.

This is the surah of *al-Mulk* — of dominion, of sovereignty, of the One in whose hand is all command. And in its closing movement, after challenging the human being to look up at a flawless sky and find a crack, after asking *who is this army of yours that will help you*, after asking *who will give you water if your water sinks into the earth* — after all of that, Allah turns inward. He stops pointing at the cosmos and points at the listener's own body.

He names the three faculties. And then He says something that should stop us cold.

*Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.* How little you thank.

The very instruments by which you could have known the Giver — He gave them to you. And those are precisely the ones lying idle for the purpose they were built for.

Stay with me. We are going to open this slowly.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Anshaʾakum* — You Were Not Manufactured. You Were Grown.

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the word.

In English, "to make" is flat. A factory makes a car. A baker makes bread. The verb does not tell you whether the thing was assembled in an instant or grown over time. But Arabic is more textured than that. It has a verb for stamping something into existence — *khalaqa*, to create. And it has a different verb for bringing something into being *and causing it to unfold, to develop, to grow up*.

That verb is from the root **ن-ش-أ** (*n-sh-ʾ*). And from the same root comes *nashʾah* — which means upbringing, the way a child is raised. And *nāshiʾ* — an adolescent, someone in the process of growing. The root does not describe a finished object. It describes a *becoming*.

Now look at the word Allah chooses:

> أَنشَأَكُمْ — *anshaʾakum*

Not *khalaqakum*, "He created you." He could have said that, and often does. Here He says *anshaʾakum*. The grammar tells us this is Form IV of that root — the form that makes a verb reach out and take an object, that turns "to grow" into "to bring into being and grow." So the meaning is not merely "He made you." It is closer to: **He originated you and brought you up. He grew you into being.**

Let's sit with this.

Think of the difference between a manufacturer and a gardener.

A manufacturer makes a thousand identical units and never thinks of one of them again. The relationship ends at the factory door. But a gardener — a gardener plants a seed, and then *tends* it. Waters it through stages you cannot see. Knows which one is struggling. Watches it unfold from a thing with no shape into a thing with a shape that was always coded inside it, waiting.

When Allah says *anshaʾakum*, He is not describing a manufacturer. The classical commentators gloss it simply — al-Muyassar says *awjadakum min al-ʿadam*, He brought you into existence from nothingness. But the root carries more than the gloss can hold. He brought you from a drop to a clot to a form to a child to whatever you are now reading this — **and at every stage, He was the One growing you.** You were never set down and forgotten.

[PAUSE]

And notice *when* this verb is placed. It comes right after ayah 22, where Allah paints two figures: one *mukibban ʿalā wajhihi* — fallen, stumbling, face pressed toward the ground, unable to see where he is going — and one walking *sawiyyan ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm*, upright on a straight path.

And then immediately: *He is the One who grew you, and gave you hearing, and sight, and hearts.*

Do you see what He is doing? **The faculties that let you walk upright instead of face-down — He is the One who grew them into you.** The very equipment for being guided was installed by the One you are being asked to recognize. You did not arrive in this world face-down. You were grown upright, with eyes that look ahead and ears that catch the warning and a heart that can understand the path. To stumble face-down after that is not a design flaw. It is a turning away from the design.

What this single word reveals: **you are not a product. You are a project He has never stopped tending.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: One Hearing, Many Sights — The Asymmetry You Were Never Meant to Miss

Now I want to show you something hidden in plain sight, something that runs through the entire Quran like a watermark, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

Look carefully at the three faculties, in Arabic, paying attention to *number* — singular or plural:

> ٱلسَّمْعَ — *as-samʿ* — **the hearing** (singular)
> وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ — *wal-abṣār* — **the sights** (plural)
> وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ — *wal-afʾidah* — **the hearts** (plural)

Hearing is singular. Sight is plural. Hearts are plural.

This is not a one-off. Across the Quran, with remarkable consistency, *samʿ* — hearing — arrives in the singular, while *abṣār* — sights — and *afʾidah* — hearts — arrive in the plural. The grammar books note it. The rhetoricians have written about it for centuries. It is a deliberate, stable pattern. And the question is: **why?**

Let me first teach the small grammatical key. *As-samʿ* is what Arabic calls a *maṣdar* — a verbal noun, the pure idea of "hearing" itself. A verbal noun does not need to be pluralized, because it names the *act*, not the *instances*. One word covers everyone's hearing at once, because hearing-as-such is one undivided thing.

But there is more here than grammar. Let's reflect on what hearing and sight actually *are*, as experiences.

Notice something about your hearing right now. **It has no direction.** Sound comes at you from behind, from the side, from above, from a room you cannot see. You cannot "close" your ears the way you close your eyes. You cannot point your hearing. It is *omnidirectional* and *always on*. It received the world before you were even born — the first faculty to switch on in the womb, the last to switch off as a person dies. Hearing is the great undivided receiver. One faculty, one channel, taking in everything, from everywhere, undirected. *Samʿ* — singular.

Now notice your sight. **Sight is a choice.** You aim it. You point your eyes at one thing and away from another. You can shut it instantly. Two people in the same room see two different scenes depending on where they turn. Sight is directional, selective, multiple, fragmented across angles and objects and viewers. *Abṣār* — plural.

Do you feel the precision of it? The single word for the faculty that receives everything indiscriminately. The plural word for the faculty that you aim and divide and direct.

[PAUSE]

And then the third — *afʾidah*, the hearts — also plural. Because nothing is more individual, more divided, more uniquely yours than what happens *inside*. Two people hear the same verse. Two people see the same sky. But what it does *in the heart* — that is as plural as there are human beings. No two hearts are kindled by the same thing in the same way.

So look at the architecture: **the faculty of pure reception is one; the faculties of selection and inner response are many.** The grammar is not decoration. It is a map of how a human being takes in the world — through one open door, and many aimed windows, and a private chamber that belongs to no one else.

And there is one more thing in the order itself. Watch the sequence: *samʿ* → *abṣār* → *afʾidah*. Hearing, then sight, then the heart. It moves from the most external and passive — sound simply arriving — to the directional — sight, aimed outward — and finally *inward*, to the chamber where it all becomes meaning. **It is the journey of a sign.** First you hear it. Then you see it. Then, if you let it, it reaches the heart and is understood. The verse names the faculties in the exact order that guidance travels through a person: in through the ear, confirmed through the eye, and home into the heart.

What this reveals: **Allah did not give you three random tools. He gave you a system — an intake, an aim, and a destination — and He described it in an order that traces the very path a truth must walk to reach you.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: *Al-Afʾidah* and *Qalīlan Mā Tashkurūn* — The Kindled Heart and the Vanishing Thanks

I left the third faculty for last, because its name carries a fire.

The commentators, when they reach *al-afʾidah*, gloss it plainly: *al-qulūb*, the hearts. Al-Jalalayn, al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi all say it — *yaʿnī al-qulūb*, meaning the hearts. And that is correct and sufficient. But the word Allah actually chose is not *qulūb*. It is *afʾidah*, the plural of *fuʾād*. And these two words for "heart" are not identical.

The root of *fuʾād* — ف-أ-د — in the lexicons carries a sense of *heat, kindling, burning*. The Arabs spoke of *fa'ada al-laḥm* — to roast meat over fire. The *fuʾād* is the heart not as a quiet organ but as a *kindled* thing — the heart in the moment it is lit, the seat of passion, of burning perception, of feeling that has caught flame. This is why some renderings of this very ayah translate *afʾidah* not as "hearts" but as "**feelings**" — the faculty of inner fire, the place where understanding arrives already warm.

So when Allah completes the triad, He does not name the heart as a still pool. He names it as a hearth. *Samʿ* receives, *abṣār* aim, and *afʾidah* — the kindled hearts — are where the received and the seen are meant to *ignite into recognition*.

Now hold all three faculties in your mind — the open ear, the aiming eye, the burning heart — and read what comes next:

> قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ — *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — "How little you thank."

Let me unfold this clause, because it is doing more than it appears.

First, the grammar. *Qalīlan* means "little," and it sits in front, in emphasis. The little particle *mā* the grammarians here read as *mā zāʾidah* — an emphatic particle, as al-Jalalayn notes — not adding new meaning but *intensifying*. And then the verb: *tashkurūn*, "you thank."

But here is the subtlety the classical scholars caught. Al-Qurtubi explains that when an Arab says *qallamā afʿalu kadhā* — "I seldom do such-and-such" — he very often means *lā afʿaluhu*, **I do not do it at all.** "How little you thank" is not measuring a small quantity of gratitude. It is a form of understatement that means *near-total absence*. The polite shape of the words — "a little" — hides a devastating content: almost none.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the weight settle.

Second, notice the *tense*. Look back at the verbs in this ayah. *Anshaʾakum* — He grew you — past tense, complete, done. *Jaʿala* — He made for you — past tense, complete, done. The divine acts are finished gifts, sealed and delivered. And then the human verb: *tashkurūn* — "you thank" — **present tense, ongoing, unfinished.** The shift is exact. His giving is a completed fact. Your thanking is a present, continuous, open failure — happening right now, as you read.

And third — this is the heart of it — *look at what the gifts and the failure have in common.*

He gave you hearing — the faculty by which you could hear His words, His signs, the call to recognize Him.
He gave you sight — the faculty by which you could see His signs written across the sky He just challenged you to inspect.
He gave you the kindled heart — the very faculty *in which gratitude is supposed to ignite*.

**The instruments of *shukr* are the exact three things He just named.** Hearing, to receive the reasons for gratitude. Sight, to witness them. The burning heart, to feel them and turn them into thanks.

And the verdict on those three instruments? *Qalīlan mā tashkurūn.* The tools of gratitude lie idle for gratitude.

Do you see the terrible precision of it? It is not that He gave us hearts and we used them for hatred instead of thanks. It is subtler and sadder: He gave us the faculty whose whole purpose is recognition, and we use it for everything *except* recognizing the One who gave it. The eye that could read a flawless sky scrolls past it. The ear that could catch the call lets it pass. The heart built to be kindled by its Maker is kindled by a thousand smaller fires and stays cold toward the One.

What this reveals: **the most underused capacity in the human being is the one he was most precisely designed for.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

We have seen three things in the words themselves. We have seen that you were *grown*, not manufactured — *anshaʾakum*. We have seen that your faculties form a designed system — one open ear, many aimed eyes, a private burning heart — described in the exact order that truth travels into a person. And we have seen that the closing verdict lands precisely on those faculties: the very instruments of gratitude, lying nearly silent.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we actually live. Because this ayah is not describing the disbelievers of Makkah at a safe distance. It is describing a pattern in the human heart that you can watch in yourself today.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Tragedy of the Always-On Gift

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a thing psychologists call *hedonic adaptation*. It works like this: when something good arrives in your life — a new home, a recovery from illness, a relationship, a comfort — there is a spike of joy. And then, with terrible reliability, the joy fades. Not because the good thing left. Because you *adapted* to it. It became the new baseline. The background. The thing you no longer notice.

We are built to notice *change*, not *constancy*. A new sound makes you turn your head; a sound that has run for an hour disappears from awareness entirely. This is efficient. It is also a spiritual catastrophe. **Because the greatest gifts in your life are the most constant ones.** And constancy is exactly what your attention is built to ignore.

Your hearing has been running for every second of your life. When did you last thank for it? Your sight has opened every morning without fail. When did it last cross your mind as a gift rather than a given? The very reliability of the blessing is what hides it. The gift that never fails becomes the gift you never see.

[PAUSE]

This is why *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* is not an insult flung at wicked people. It is a diagnosis of a healthy nervous system pointed in the wrong direction. We do not fail to thank because we are monsters. We fail to thank because the blessing is *too constant to register*. Hedonic adaptation is the machinery; ingratitude is the output; and the only thing that breaks the cycle is *deliberate noticing* — the conscious act of turning the faculty back toward its source.

Let the ayah illuminate this. Watch what Allah does. He does not introduce some new, exotic blessing to provoke your wonder. He reaches for the *most* adapted-to, most invisible, most background gifts you own — hearing, sight, the inner heart — and He *names them out loud*. Because the cure for invisibility is *naming*. The moment Allah says *as-samʿ*, your attention is forced back onto the hearing you had stopped noticing a lifetime ago. The ayah is performing the very act it is calling for. It is dragging the background into the foreground.

And here is the movement that should reorganize how you live: **gratitude is not a feeling that visits you. It is an attention you direct.** *Shukr* is not waiting for a wave of warmth toward your eyes. It is the decision, right now, to look at the fact that you can see, and to trace it back to the One who *grew* it into you. The faculty you stopped noticing — point it, once, at its Giver.

[PAUSE]

So here is the practical turn, the thing that can change tomorrow morning. When you wake, before you reach for the device that will hijack all three faculties into noise — pause for the length of a single breath. Notice that you can hear. Notice that you can see. Notice that there is a chamber in you that can understand what this day will mean. And say, just once, with intention: *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — for these three, before I aim them at anything else. You will have done, in ten seconds, the one thing this ayah says almost no one does.

## Theme Two: The One Who Grew You Once Can Grow You Again

Now let's return to that first word, *anshaʾakum*, because it opens onto something vast — and the surah itself points us there.

Read what comes in the *very next ayah*, 67:24:

> *Qul huwa alladhī dharaʾakum fil-arḍi wa ilayhi tuḥsharūn.*
> "Say: He is the One who multiplied you across the earth, and to Him you will be gathered."

Place 23 and 24 side by side. Ayah 23: *He is the One who grew you* — your first coming-into-being. Ayah 24: *to Him you will be gathered* — your final coming-into-being, the resurrection. The surah moves in a single breath from your origin to your return. And the bridge between them is that one verb: *anshaʾa*.

Here is why that matters. The Quran returns again and again to a single argument against the one who doubts resurrection: **the One who grew you the first time can grow you a second time.** The *nashʾah al-ūlā* — the first growing — is the proof of the *nashʾah al-ākhirah*, the final one. If you accept that you were once a drop of fluid that became a hearing, seeing, feeling person — and you cannot deny it, because you are reading these words — then on what grounds do you find the second growing impossible? The gardener who grew the garden once is not defeated by winter.

[PAUSE]

But I want to take you somewhere more intimate than the argument. Because *anshaʾakum* is not only a proof. It is a *relationship*.

Remember what we found in the word: not manufacture, but upbringing. Not a stamp, but a tending. To be *grown* by someone is to be *known* by them, at every stage, in a way the finished product is never known by the machine. The One who grew you watched the stages no human eye ever saw. He was there in the dark before your mother knew you existed. He set the hearing running before there was anything to hear. He was tending you when you were less than a whisper of a person.

And this is the One the ayah says we thank *so little*.

Sit with the asymmetry of it. Not the asymmetry of grammar this time — the asymmetry of the relationship. On one side: a Being who originated you, grew you through every unseen stage, installed in you a perfect system of hearing and sight and feeling, sustains every beat of it this very second, and will gather you back to Himself with the same ease He brought you the first time. On the other side: *qalīlan mā tashkurūn*. A little. Almost nothing.

[PAUSE]

And yet — feel the mercy buried in the complaint. He does not say *you do not thank* and close the door. He says *you thank little*. The little is still counted. The door is still open. A Being who wanted only to condemn would not bother phrasing the verdict so gently. The understatement that al-Qurtubi unpacked — "how little" meaning "almost none" — is also, read another way, an *invitation*: the gap between your little gratitude and the gratitude these gifts deserve is exactly the space He is inviting you to fill. He is not slamming a ledger shut. He is showing you how much room there is to grow.

Because you are still being grown. *Anshaʾakum* is past tense for your body. But for your soul, the growing is not finished. The One who tended you from a drop is still tending you — and every act of conscious gratitude is you, at last, cooperating with the growth He has never stopped working in you.

The practical turn here is different from the first. The first theme asks you to *notice* the gift. This one asks you to *trust the Giver*. The next time you face something that feels like an ending — a loss, a fear, the thought of death itself — return to this word. The One who grew you out of nothing, who knew you in the dark before you were anything, has not run out of power to bring you through. *Ilayhi tuḥsharūn.* You are not headed toward a void. You are headed back to the Gardener.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Now That We Can See It

Look back at the whole ayah now, with everything we have uncovered, and watch how tightly it is built.

It opens with *anshaʾakum* — **He grew you.** It then lists, in ascending order from outer to inner, the system He grew into you: *as-samʿ* (the one open ear) → *al-abṣār* (the many aimed eyes) → *al-afʾidah* (the many burning hearts). And it closes with the verdict that lands on exactly those faculties: *qalīlan mā tashkurūn* — **how little you thank.**

Origin. System. Verdict. The structure itself is an argument: *Here is how thoroughly you were made for recognition — and here is how far you stand from it.* The gifts named are precisely the instruments the verdict indicts. The architecture is the proof.

## Two Doors, One Room

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes turn out to be the same insight approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a *designed system* — one receiver, many aimers, a private hearth — described in the order truth travels. The themes showed us *why we miss it* — because constancy hides the gift, and because we have forgotten that the Designer is also the Gardener who grew us and will regrow us.

Put them together and you have the whole ayah in a single thought: **The One who grew you gave you the exact faculties needed to know Him — and the deepest tragedy of the human being is that those faculties, built for recognition, are the ones we point everywhere but home.**

## Questions to Carry

- The faculty you would grieve most to lose — when did you last thank for it while you still have it?
- If hearing is the open door and sight the aimed window, what have you been *aiming* your eyes and ears at — and is your heart catching fire from any of it, or from everything except its Maker?
- *Qalīlan* — "a little." Is your gratitude little because the gifts are small, or because the gifts are so constant you stopped seeing them?
- You accept that you were grown once from almost nothing. On what honest grounds do you find it hard to believe you will be grown again?
- What would change in a single ordinary morning if you treated your senses as a trust on loan rather than a possession you own?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The One who grew you into being and installed in you the very faculties of hearing, seeing, and feeling — the instruments by which you could have known and thanked Him — is met with the one response those faculties were never meant to give: almost no gratitude at all.

## The Closing Invitation

This ayah does not ask you to acquire something new. It asks you to *turn around* something you already have. You are already hearing. You are already seeing. There is already a chamber in you where meaning arrives warm. Nothing needs to be added. One faculty, just once a day, simply needs to be aimed back at the One who grew it into you. That is the whole invitation: not more, but *toward*.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who grew me from nothing and gave me hearing, sight, and a heart that can know You — forgive me for the years I aimed them everywhere but at You. Let me be of those who thank much and not little, and grow me, as You once grew me, until I am gathered back to You upright and grateful. Āmīn.*
