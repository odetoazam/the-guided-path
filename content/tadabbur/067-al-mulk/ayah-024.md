# Scattered Like Seed, Gathered to One Point

> قُلْ هُوَ ٱلَّذِى ذَرَأَكُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ
>
> *Qul huwa alladhī dhara'akum fī al-ardi wa ilayhi tuḥsharūn.*
>
> **"Say: He is the One who scattered you across the earth, and to Him you will be gathered."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Before we open a single word, I want you to feel where we are standing in this surah.

Surah Al-Mulk is the surah of *dominion*. From its opening — *Blessed is the One in whose hand is all dominion* — it has been doing one thing relentlessly: lifting your eyes. Look up at seven heavens with no flaw in them. Look again — do you see any crack? Look at the birds holding themselves in the air. Look at the water that could sink into the earth tomorrow and leave you with nothing to drink.

Up, up, up. The whole surah has been pulling your gaze toward the vastness above you, the sovereignty over you.

And then, just a few verses before ours, something shifts. The camera turns away from the heavens and points at *you*. Verse 23: **"Say: He is the One who brought you into being, and made for you the hearing, and the sight, and the hearts — little do you give thanks."**

For the first time, the surah stops talking about the sky and starts talking about the equipment of your own soul. Your ears. Your eyes. Your heart.

[PAUSE]

And then comes our verse. The very next breath.

He has just reminded you that He *built* you — gave you the instruments of perception. And now He tells you what He did with you after building you, and where it is all going.

Here is the world I want you to inhabit. Imagine a farmer at the edge of a vast, open field. He takes a handful of seed, and with one wide motion of his arm, he *throws* it — and the seed scatters, spinning out in every direction, landing far apart, each grain in its own patch of soil, kilometers of distance opening up between them. From the seed's point of view — if a seed could feel — it would feel like being flung into loneliness. Cast out. Separated from every other seed. Dropped somewhere random.

That word — *flung, scattered, cast across a surface* — is the word Allah chose for what He did with the human race.

Hold that image. Because here is the hook:

**The verse uses one verb for how you were spread out, and a completely different kind of verb for how you will be brought back. The first you did nothing to deserve and nothing to control. The second — you will do nothing to escape. And the entire weight of your life is suspended between those two verbs.**

Let's go and find what is buried in just eight words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: ذَرَأَ — The Verb of Scattered Seed

#### Teaching the idea first

In any language, we have many words for "to make." In English: to make, to create, to build, to produce, to fashion, to generate, to breed. They are not interchangeable. *To build* a house is not *to breed* cattle is not *to fashion* a ring. Each verb carries a hidden picture of *how* the making happens and *what shape* it leaves behind.

Arabic is exquisitely precise about this. The Qur'an has several verbs for divine creation — *khalaqa* (to create, to proportion), *bara'a* (to originate, to bring out from nothing), *fatara* (to split into being), *ansha'a* (to develop and raise up, the very verb in the verse just before ours).

So when Allah reaches past all of those familiar verbs and picks one rare, specific word, we must stop and ask: *why this one?*

#### Opening the word

The word is **ذَرَأَ** — *dhara'a*.

Listen to it. *Dha-ra-'a.* It ends on a hamza — that little catch in the throat, a glottal stop, like a tiny throw, a release.

This root, *dhāl–rā'–hamza*, is uncommon. And its core meaning, preserved in the classical lexicons, is not simply "to create." It is **to scatter, to disperse, to multiply by spreading.** The Arabs used a word from this same family for *scattering seed across the ground* — and for *salt scattered over food*, and for the way descendants multiply and spread out generation after generation. The very word for "offspring," *dhurriyya*, the seed of your seed spreading down through time, is woven from this same idea of dispersal-and-multiplication.

So *dhara'akum* does not just say *He created you.* It says: **He created you by scattering you — and in scattering you, He multiplied you — flung across the surface of the earth like seed cast across a field.**

#### Sitting with it

Let's sit with this. Notice that the classical commentators themselves felt the richness here and approached it from two angles. Al-Qurtubī preserves both: Ibn ʿAbbās reads *dhara'akum* simply as **"He created you."** And he reports another reading, from Ibn Shujara, that it means **"He spread you out and dispersed you across the face of the earth."**

These are not two competing claims fighting for the one true meaning. This is one reality seen from two camera angles. The first angle says: *the origin is from Him.* The second says: *the spreading is from Him too.* Put them together and you get the full picture the verb is painting: **the same act that brought you into existence is the act that scattered you.** Your creation and your dispersal were not two separate decisions. They were one motion of the divine hand — like the farmer, for whom *sowing* and *scattering* are not two acts but one.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because here is what is remarkable.

Think about how that reframes the distance you feel from other human beings. We tend to read the scattered-ness of humanity as a kind of accident, or as a sad fact — people drift apart, families spread across continents, you end up far from where you began, far from the people you love, in a corner of the earth you never chose.

But *dhara'akum* says the scattering was **deliberate.** It was *agriculture*, not *abandonment.* A farmer does not scatter seed to get rid of it. He scatters it precisely *because* the only way for a seed to become a harvest is for it to be sent away from the hand that holds it, buried alone in the dark, separated from every other seed. The distance is not the failure of the plan. **The distance is the plan.**

You were not dropped. You were *sown.*

And one statement of what this single word reveals: **the loneliness of your scattering is not evidence that you were forgotten — it is evidence that you were planted.**

### Element Two: The Two Verbs That Don't Match — ذَرَأَ vs. تُحْشَرُونَ

#### Teaching the idea first

Here is something Arabic does with extraordinary control, and which English readers usually miss entirely: **tense and voice.**

*Tense* is when something happens — past, or present-and-ongoing-and-future. In Arabic, the perfect tense (*al-māḍī*) is for the completed, the done, the sealed. The imperfect tense (*al-muḍāriʿ*) is for the unfolding, the ongoing, the still-to-come.

*Voice* is who does the doing. **Active voice:** the subject acts — *He scattered.* **Passive voice:** the subject is acted upon — *you are gathered.* In active voice, you are the one holding the verb. In passive voice, the verb is being done *to* you.

Now hold those two tools — tense and voice — and watch what happens across this single short verse.

#### Opening the feature

Look at the two verbs that carry the whole sentence:

**ذَرَأَكُمْ** — *dhara'akum* — "He scattered you."

**تُحْشَرُونَ** — *tuḥsharūn* — "you will be gathered."

Now look closely, because they do not match. They are deliberately built differently from each other.

**ذَرَأَ** is **perfect tense** — past, completed, *done.* And it is **active** — *He* is the explicit subject. *He scattered.*

**تُحْشَرُونَ** is **imperfect tense** — ongoing, still-to-come, *not yet finished.* And it is **passive** — *you* are not the doer; *you* are the ones the verb falls upon. *You will be gathered.*

#### Sitting with the first asymmetry: the tenses

Notice the first thing this does.

The scattering is in the *past.* It is **finished.** *Dhara'akum* — it already happened, the seed is already in the ground, the deed is sealed and behind you.

The gathering is in the *imperfect.* It is **coming.** *Tuḥsharūn* — still on its way, not yet arrived, as certain as a sunrise that has not yet broken the horizon.

So the verse positions you precisely. You are standing in the middle of an arc. Behind you: a completed act you cannot undo — your scattering, your being-here, the patch of earth you were sown in. Ahead of you: an unfinished act you cannot avoid — your gathering.

Your whole life is the space between a perfect verb and an imperfect one. Between *what was done to bring you here* and *what will be done to bring you back.*

[PAUSE]

#### Sitting with the second asymmetry: the voice

Now the deeper layer. The *voice.*

When Allah describes the scattering, He uses the **active** voice and names Himself the doer: *He* scattered you. The agency is announced loudly. There is no ambiguity about who did this.

But when He describes the gathering, He switches to the **passive**: *you will be gathered.* The verb falls on *you.* You are the object now.

Here's what's worth slowing down for. In the dispersal, the doer is made explicit — *He.* In the gathering, the doer is grammatically hidden, but everyone knows who it is, because the verse already told you in the middle: *wa ilayhi* — "and **to Him**." So the gatherer is the same as the scatterer. There is no second power involved. The hand that flung the seed is the hand that brings in the harvest.

But why phrase the gathering in the passive at all? Why not say, "and He will gather you"?

Because the passive does something the active cannot. The passive **erases your agency.** *Tuḥsharūn* — *you will be gathered* — there is nothing you contribute, nothing you initiate, no role for your will. You do not gather *yourself.* You do not walk to the gathering. You are *brought.*

[PAUSE]

Think about your whole life on this earth — the life lived in *fī al-ard*, "in the earth." Down here, in the scattered field, you experience yourself as an *agent.* You make choices. You move. You build, you travel, you decide where to live and whom to marry and what to chase. The entire texture of worldly life is the feeling of being the subject of your own verbs.

And then the verse tells you that the final motion of your existence — the one that matters most — is one in which you are entirely **passive.** You will not author it. You will not control its timing. You will not negotiate its terms. *Tuḥsharūn.* You will be gathered.

The scattering let you *feel* free across the surface of the earth. The gathering reveals who held the rope the entire time.

One statement of what these two verbs reveal together: **You spend your life as the subject of active verbs, and you will end it as the object of a passive one — and the lesson of the grammar is that your sense of total control was always a feature of the field, never a fact about your destiny.**

### Element Three: فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ versus إِلَيْهِ — Two Directions

#### Teaching the idea first

Prepositions seem like the smallest, most forgettable words in any language — *in, to, on, from.* But in the Qur'an they are often where the geometry of a verse is hidden. A preposition tells you the *shape* of a movement: is it spreading out, or converging in? Is it horizontal, or does it point somewhere?

Watch the two prepositional phrases in this verse sitting directly across from each other.

#### Opening the feature

The scattering happens **فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** — *fī al-ard* — "**in** the earth," across the earth, over its wide flat surface.

The gathering happens **إِلَيْهِ** — *ilayhi* — "**to Him**."

*Fī* is the preposition of *containment and spread* — within a space, dispersed across an expanse. *Ilā* is the preposition of *destination and direction* — toward a single point, arriving somewhere.

#### Sitting with it

Feel the geometry. *Fī al-ard* is **horizontal and plural.** A surface stretching in every direction. The seed went *that* way and *this* way and a thousand other ways — north, south, east, west, scattered wide, no two grains in the same place. It is the geometry of *manyness* and *distance.*

*Ilayhi* is **convergent and singular.** Not "to many places" — to *Him.* One point. One direction. Every scattered grain, no matter which corner of the field it landed in, is pulled back along a line that ends in the *same single place.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the image. Picture the seed thrown across a vast field — points of light spreading outward in all directions, the pattern of an explosion, everything flying apart. That is *fī al-ard.* That is human history: billions of lives flung across continents and centuries, each one feeling like its own separate story going its own separate way.

Now run the film backwards. Every one of those points of light reverses, and they all begin to travel inward, toward one center, converging, until the explosion becomes an *implosion*, and everything that was flung apart arrives at one point.

*Ilayhi.* To Him.

The same word, *ilayhi*, with the *ilā* of direction, tells you the scattering was never the final shape. The wideness of *fī al-ard* was temporary. The destination of *ilayhi* is permanent. **What looked like an explosion outward was always, secretly, the first half of a return.**

And notice the placement — Arabic put *ilayhi*, "to Him," *before* the verb *tuḥsharūn*, "you will be gathered." Word order in Arabic carries emphasis: what comes first is what's stressed. So the verse does not say "you will be gathered to Him" with Him as an afterthought. It says, in effect, "**to Him** — and to no one and nowhere else — you will be gathered." The destination is named, fronted, emphasized, *before* you even learn what will happen to you there.

One statement of what these two prepositions reveal: **the wide horizontal scattering of your life was always bracketed by a single vertical destination — and the One you are returning *to* is the very One who scattered you in the first place.**

### The Bridge

So look at what these eight words have shown us through their construction alone.

A rare verb — *dhara'a* — that turns your existence into *sown seed* rather than dropped litter. A pair of mismatched verbs — one perfect and active, one imperfect and passive — that locate your entire life in the gap between a finished scattering and a coming gathering, while quietly stripping away the illusion that you were ever in control of the ending. And two prepositions — *fī* and *ilā* — that draw the geometry of a life: flung wide across a surface, then drawn back to a single point.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of a human life, and the secret that the construction has been hiding inside it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Distance You Feel Is the Field You Were Planted In

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of loneliness that is very modern and very old at the same time. It is the feeling of *dispersal.* You look around at your life and you notice how *spread out* everything is. Your family is across the world — a parent in one country, a sibling in another, you in a third. The friends who once felt like a single warm cluster are now scattered dots on a map, a group chat that goes quiet. Even your own self can feel scattered — pulled across a dozen obligations, a dozen tabs, a dozen versions of you that never quite gather into one whole person.

We are, all of us, a scattered people. And the modern world has a word for the ache of it: *fragmentation.* The sense that things have come apart, that the center didn't hold, that you are a long way from some origin you can't quite name and can't get back to.

Now here is where the ayah walks into the room.

It does not deny the scattering. It does not tell you the distance is an illusion. It *names* it — *dhara'akum fī al-ard*, He scattered you across the earth — and then it tells you something about the scattering you did not know.

**It was done by a Gardener.**

[PAUSE]

A seed has no way of understanding its own burial. From inside the dark soil, separated from the hand that held it, surrounded by nothing but cold earth, the seed's entire experience is *loss.* It cannot see the field. It cannot see the other seeds. It certainly cannot see the harvest. All it knows is that it was thrown, and it landed alone, and now it is in the dark.

And it is *precisely right* about the facts — and *completely wrong* about their meaning.

Because the burial it experiences as abandonment is the only doorway to the only thing it was made for. A seed kept safe in the farmer's hand, never scattered, never buried, never sent into the lonely dark — that seed simply *stays a seed forever.* It never becomes the green thing it was carrying inside it the whole time. The scattering is not the interruption of its purpose. **The scattering is the activation of its purpose.**

So when you feel the distance — from your people, from your origin, from the gathered wholeness you long for — the ayah is not asking you to pretend you don't feel it. It is asking you to *reinterpret* it. The same logic that tells the seed it was abandoned is the logic that, from above the soil, looks exactly like *being planted.*

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means the scattered conditions of your life — the distance, the displacement, the sense of being far from where you belong — are not a detour from your spiritual life. They *are* your spiritual life. The specific patch of lonely soil you were sown into — this city, this family situation, this hard season — is not the obstacle to your growth. It is the medium of it. The very next verse before this one reminded you that He gave you *hearing, sight, and a heart.* Those faculties were not given to a seed kept in a jar. They were given to a seed in the ground — equipment for growing exactly where you were thrown.

So tomorrow, the practice is this: the next time you feel the ache of how scattered your life is, stop and ask the seed's better question. Not *"why was I abandoned here?"* but **"what was I planted to grow into, here, that I could not have grown anywhere else?"**

### Theme Two: You Are the Subject of Every Sentence Until the Last One

Now let's go to the second thing the grammar uncovered — the active *He scattered* against the passive *you will be gathered.*

Begin again where you live.

Modern life is, more than any life in human history, a project of *control.* We curate. We optimize. We have apps to manage our sleep, our money, our steps, our moods. We build careers as acts of authorship — *I made myself into this.* We post the gathered, edited highlight of a life and quietly bury the scattered, unedited rest of it. The deep assumption underneath all of it is that *I am the subject of my own verbs.* I act. I decide. I steer. The world is the object; I am the agent.

And on the surface of the earth — *fī al-ard* — that assumption *works.* It is even, in a limited way, true. You really do make real choices. You really do steer.

But the ayah quietly tells you something the curated life works very hard to forget. It tells you that there is one verb in your existence that you will never be the subject of.

*Tuḥsharūn.* **You will be gathered.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with how strange and total that is. Everything else about you is active. You will be *born* — well, that one's passive too, isn't it? You will eat, walk, work, love, build, fail, repent, age — verb after verb after verb where *you* are the one acting. A whole grammar of agency.

And then, at the end, the voice flips, and you become the object. You will not gather yourself. You will not decide when. You will not RSVP. You will not be consulted. The most important appointment of your entire existence is one to which you are *brought*, not one you *attend.*

Why does this matter for how you live tomorrow, and not just for how you die?

Because the people who live wisely are the people who have made friends with the passive verb *early.* Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing to dissolve it: **the more honestly you accept that the final verb is not yours, the more freely you can live the active verbs that are.**

Think about it. So much of our anxiety, our exhaustion, our frantic over-control, comes from quietly trying to be the subject of *tuḥsharūn* — trying to author an ending, to control outcomes that were never ours, to gather our own lives into a guaranteed shape. We are seeds trying to schedule the harvest. And it cannot be done, and the trying is what burns us out.

The believer who has internalized *tuḥsharūn* is released from that impossible job. *The gathering is His to do, not mine.* That doesn't make me passive about my choices — my choices are exactly the active verbs I *am* responsible for, and the gathering is precisely the moment I'll be asked about them. It makes me passive about the *outcome*, which was never mine to hold. I sow with everything I have. I do not pretend I command the harvest.

[PAUSE]

And notice the mercy hidden in the passive voice itself. *You will be gathered* — by whom? By the same One who scattered you. *Ilayhi.* To Him. The gathering is not a random reassembly by some indifferent force. It is a homecoming engineered by the very One who planted you, who gave you the ears and eyes and heart to grow with, who knew the exact patch of soil He threw you into and exactly what He expected it to yield.

The One gathering you is not a stranger to you. He is the most intimate fact of your existence — the One whose single act *created and scattered* you in the same motion. You are not being summoned by a judge who just met you. You are being brought home by the One who sowed you.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means you can put down the exhausting work of trying to control the ending. Tomorrow, when the anxiety rises — about your reputation, your future, your legacy, the outcome you can't force — you can say the truest sentence: *the harvest is not my verb.* Mine is to be a good seed in the soil I was thrown into. *Ilayhi tuḥsharūn* — the gathering belongs to Him, and He is the One who has wanted me back since before He scattered me.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now let me show you something we could not have seen at the beginning, but can see now.

Go back one verse, to 23, and read the two verses as the single unit they were revealed to be:

**Verse 23:** *Qul — huwa alladhī —* "Say: **He is the One who** brought you into being, and made for you the hearing and the sight and the hearts; little do you give thanks."

**Verse 24:** *Qul — huwa alladhī —* "Say: **He is the One who** scattered you across the earth, and to Him you will be gathered."

Two verses. The same opening: *Say: He is the One who…* It is a deliberate parallel, a matched pair. And watch what each half does.

Verse 23 is about your **equipment**: He produced you, and fitted you with hearing, sight, and a heart — the instruments of perception, the faculties of a moral being.

Verse 24 is about your **trajectory**: He scattered you into the field, and He will gather you back.

Put them together and the architecture is breathtaking. **He gave you faculties (23) — and the field you were scattered into (24) is where you use them — and the gathering you're returning for (24) is where you'll answer for how you used them.** The hearing, the sight, the heart of verse 23 are not decorations. They are exactly what will be asked about at the *tuḥsharūn* of verse 24. The gift and the accounting are bolted together. And both verses end on the same quiet warning note: verse 23 says *little do you thank*; verse 24 ends on *to Him you will be gathered* — and the very next verse has the disbelievers scoffing *when is this gathering, then?*

The seed was given everything it needed to grow (23). It was sown in the field (24). And the harvest is coming (24), whether it scoffs at the harvest or not (25).

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be a single insight approached from two sides.

The *grammar* told us: a finished active scattering, a coming passive gathering, bracketed by a single destination — *to Him.*

The *themes* told us: the distance you feel is a field you were planted in, and the control you crave over your ending was never yours to hold — because the ending belongs to the Gardener.

They are the same truth. **You were sown by a Hand, into a field, for a harvest — and the loneliness of the soil and the helplessness about the harvest are both dissolved by the same fact: the One gathering you in is the One who planted you out.** Your scattering was never abandonment. Your gathering is not erasure. Both are the love of a Gardener who does not throw away seed.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the specific "soil" I was scattered into — the conditions of my life I keep treating as obstacles — and what was I planted there to grow into that I could not grow anywhere else?

2. Where in my life am I exhausting myself trying to be the subject of a verb that was always going to be passive — trying to author an outcome that belongs to Him?

3. If I truly believed the One gathering me is the One who sowed me — not a stranger, but the most intimate fact of my existence — what fear would that quietly retire?

4. Verse 23 says I was given hearing, sight, and a heart. When I am gathered, these are exactly what I'll answer for. Which of the three have I been using least as it was meant to be used?

5. The seed cannot see the harvest from inside the soil. What in my life am I judging by the darkness of the soil, when I was never given the vantage point to see the field?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The same Hand that scattered you like seed across the earth is the One toward whom you will be gathered — so the distance you feel was planting, not abandonment, and the ending you cannot control was always a homecoming, not an erasure.**

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to leave this reflection with a new task to perform. You can leave it with a new way of seeing the field you're standing in. The invitation is simply this: stop reading your scattered-ness as a verdict against you, and start reading it as the evidence that you were planted with intention — by Someone who is, even now, waiting for the harvest, and who has wanted you *back to Him* since before He threw the seed.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, the One who scattered us across this earth and will gather us to Yourself — let us understand the soil You sowed us in, and grow in it the thing You planted us to become. We cannot author our own gathering; we ask only that when You bring us back, You bring us back as a harvest that pleases You, and not as seed that was given everything and yielded nothing. To You we were entrusted, and to You we return.*

*Āmīn.*
