# The Army That Was Never Yours

## Introduction: Two Questions You Have Never Actually Answered

Let me give you the words first, before we go anywhere with them.

> [ayah:67:20] أَمَّنْ هَـٰذَا ٱلَّذِى هُوَ جُندٌ لَّكُمْ يَنصُرُكُم مِّن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ۚ إِنِ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ إِلَّا فِى غُرُورٍ
>
> *Amman hādhā alladhī huwa jundun lakum yanṣurukum min dūni-r-Raḥmān; ini-l-kāfirūna illā fī ghurūr.*
>
> **"Or who is this — the one who would be an army for you, to help you, besides the Most Merciful? The disbelievers are in nothing but delusion."**

> [ayah:67:21] أَمَّنْ هَـٰذَا ٱلَّذِى يَرْزُقُكُمْ إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُۥ ۚ بَل لَّجُّوا۟ فِى عُتُوٍّ وَنُفُورٍ
>
> *Amman hādhā alladhī yarzuqukum in amsaka rizqahu; bal lajjū fī ʿutuwwin wa-nufūr.*
>
> **"Or who is this — the one who would provide for you, if He withheld His provision? Rather, they persist in insolence and aversion."**

Two questions. Notice they are *questions*. Not statements, not commands. Allah, who could simply declare the truth, instead chooses to *ask*.

### Let's rebuild the world these questions fell into.

You have to understand where we are in this surah. Al-Mulk opens like a coronation: *Tabāraka alladhī biyadihi al-mulk* — "Blessed is the One in whose hand is the dominion." From the first word, the whole surah is about one thing: who actually owns reality. And it does not argue this the way a philosopher would. It argues it the way a friend would, taking you by the shoulder and turning your face toward the sky.

*Look up,* the surah keeps saying. Look at the seven heavens — do you see a single crack, a single flaw? *Then look again. And again.* Your eye will come back to you exhausted, defeated, having found nothing to criticize. Look at the night and the lamps placed in it. And then — right before our two verses — look at the birds.

> "Have they not seen the birds above them, spreading their wings and folding them in? Nothing holds them up except the Most Merciful." (67:19)

Sit with that picture for a moment, because the verses we are reading were *built* on top of it. A bird is hanging in the open air. There is nothing under it. No wire, no shelf, no hand that you can see. By every intuition a heavy body in the air should fall — and it does not. What is holding it there? The surah answers: *nothing but ar-Raḥmān.* Nothing but the Most Merciful.

And the original listener — a person in Makkah who has watched hawks ride the desert wind their entire life — feels the floor of their certainty shift. They have *seen* this a thousand times and never once asked who was holding the bird up.

Now the surah turns. It has finished pointing at the bird. It turns and points at *you.*

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to feel before we begin. You are about to be asked two questions that you have, in some sense, been answering with your whole life — answering with your savings account, your career plans, your network of people who would catch you if you fell, your sense of *I've got this.* You have an answer to "who protects me?" and an answer to "who provides for me?" You live by those answers every single day.

[PAUSE]

These two verses are going to walk up to those answers — and ask you to *name one.* Name one soldier in your army. Name one source of your bread. And then watch what happens to the name when you say it out loud in the presence of the One who holds the bird in the sky.

Stay with me. This is going to be gentler, and more devastating, than you expect.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَمَّنْ — The Question That Already Contains Its Answer

Let me teach you something about questions before we look at this word.

There is a kind of question that is not really seeking information. You already know the answer. The questioner already knows the answer. The question exists to make you *say it,* or to make you *feel it.* A parent looks at the broken vase and the child standing next to it and asks, "And who did this?" The parent is not gathering data. The question is a mirror.

Arabic has a precise name for this. It is called **istifhām inkārī** — an interrogative of *denial.* It has the grammatical *shape* of a question, but its rhetorical *work* is negation. When Arabic asks a question in this mode, it is not opening a door — it is closing one. The expected answer is not a name. The expected answer is: *no one. nothing. there is no such thing.*

Now look at how our verse opens. **أَمَّنْ** — *am-man.*

This little word is actually two words pressed together: **أَمْ** (*am*), a connective particle that means roughly "or — rather," and **مَنْ** (*man*), the interrogative "who?" Put them together and you get something like: *"Or — who, exactly?"*

And here is the first thing to notice. That **أَمْ** at the front — that "or" — tells you this question is *connected* to something before it. It is not standing alone. It is reaching back to the bird in the sky. The flow is: *Nothing holds the bird up but the Most Merciful... OR — who is this supposed army of yours?* The "or" yokes the two together. The same Mercy that holds the bird is the Mercy this verse is asking about. The question is not a new subject. It is the bird's lesson, now aimed at you.

Both al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi name this directly. Al-Baghawi writes of these words: *istifhām inkār* — this is a question of *denial.* Al-Qurtubi says the meaning is: **"there is no army for you that can repel from you the punishment of Allah besides the Most Merciful."**

Do you feel what just happened? The verse asked, "Who is this army that helps you besides the Most Merciful?" — and the grammar itself has already answered: **there is none.** The question is shaped so that the only honest response is silence. You go looking for the name of your defender, and you come back with empty hands, the way your eye came back from the sky exhausted, having found no flaw.

[PAUSE]

This is mercy, by the way. I want you to see that. It would have been easy for the verse to simply say: *You have no army besides Me.* A flat declaration. Instead it asks you a question — *"who is this one?"* — and lets you do the searching yourself. Because a truth you *arrive at* changes you. A truth merely *handed to you* you can set down again. The question makes you walk through your own life, opening every door, looking for the army you were sure was in there somewhere. And finding every room empty. That walk is the gift.

### Element Two: جُندٌ — The Whole Army, In the Singular, Indefinite

Now look at the word for army. **جُندٌ** — *jundun.*

Two strange things about this word, and both of them are doing work.

The first: it is **singular.** Arabic has perfectly good plural words for troops and hosts and armies. But the verse uses *jund* — a collective singular, the kind of word that names a whole host as a single mass. Al-Qurtubi notices this and pauses on it: *"the word jund is kept singular"* — *wa lafẓ al-jund yūḥad.* The verse gathers up the entire imagined army — every soldier, every weapon, every wall — and refers to it as *one thing.* One unit. As if to say: bring all of it. Bring the whole host. Pile it into a single word, and let us weigh that single word against ar-Raḥmān.

The second strange thing: it is **indefinite.** Not *al-jund,* "the army" — but *jundun,* "an army," *any* army. The indefiniteness throws the net as wide as it will go. The verse is not asking about a *particular* defender. It is asking about *the very category.* Is there, anywhere in existence, *any* such thing — any force at all — that could function as a host for you against Him?

What did the early scholars understand this army to *be?* Here the companion Ibn ʿAbbās gives us a gloss that both al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi preserve. He reads *jund* as **ḥizb wa manʿa** — *a faction and a stronghold.* A party that fights for you, and a fortification that shelters you. So the word holds both meanings at once: the army that goes out and the wall that stands firm. Offense and defense. Everything a frightened human being could possibly want standing between themselves and harm.

And the verse takes all of it — the faction, the fortress, the entire single-worded host — and sets it beside one Name. And the host evaporates.

Let me bring this close to where you live.

You have an army. I mean it. Every one of us assembles one. It is made of the things we believe will catch us if we fall. For one person it is a number in a bank account — a number that feels like a moat around a castle. For another it is a profession, a set of credentials, the sense that "I am too skilled to ever truly be in danger." For another it is people — a parent who would never let me starve, a spouse, a network, a name that opens doors. For another it is the body itself: *I am healthy, I am strong, I have time.*

We don't usually *call* it an army. But watch what happens to a person when one soldier in that army falls — when the number drops, when the job disappears, when the protector dies, when the diagnosis comes. Watch the panic. That panic is the sound of someone discovering that what they thought was a stronghold was a tent. The panic reveals where the trust actually was.

[PAUSE]

The verse is not asking you to *get rid of* your savings or your skills or your people. It is asking you to *name* them out loud — *"who is this army of yours?"* — and to notice, as you name each one, that not a single soldier in it answers to anyone but ar-Raḥmān. Your wealth is His provision. Your health is His grace. The people who love you have hearts He turns. There is no soldier in your army who did not enlist under His command. You never had a force *besides* Him. You only had His mercy, dressed up in uniforms you mistook for your own.

### Element Three: مِّن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ — Why the Name of *Mercy* in a Verse of Threat?

Now I want to show you something I find almost unbearable in its tenderness, and you will only see it if we read an *absence* — a word that *could* have been here and is not.

The verse asks: who is your army to help you... **مِّن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ** — *besides ar-Raḥmān.* Besides the Most Merciful.

Stop on that name.

Think about what this verse is actually describing. It is describing a scenario in which you would *need* an army — a scenario in which God's power might come *against* you. Al-Qurtubi spells out the implied threat: an army "to repel from you *what He intends for you* if you disobey Him." Al-Baghawi: "to defend you from *His punishment.*" This is a verse about the possibility of divine displeasure. About being on the wrong side of the Owner of the dominion.

So which of His names would you expect here? In a verse about needing protection *from God,* you would expect a name of might. *Al-Jabbār* — the Compeller. *Al-Qahhār* — the Subduer. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty. *Al-Muntaqim* — the Avenger. Any of these would fit the logic of fear.

And instead the verse says: *there is no army for you besides* **ar-Raḥmān.** **The Most Merciful.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? Let it land slowly.

The One you imagine you might need defending *from* — is named here as **Mercy itself.** The verse is telling you, in the very grammar of the threat: *the Power you are afraid of is the Mercy you are made of.* There is no army against Him because there is no "against." The only force in existence that could ever stand over you is **Mercy** — and what kind of army do you raise against *Mercy?*

This is the verse dismantling fear with a single Name. It says: yes, He is the only One who could ever harm you — and that same only-One is *ar-Raḥmān.* The hand you might flinch from is the hand that holds the bird in the sky. So your terror was always misplaced. You were arming yourself against the only Mercy there is.

And now watch the second verse complete the picture — because it makes the *same* move from the opposite direction.

The first verse was about **protection** — warding off harm. The second is about **provision** — the giving of good. Together they cover the entire territory of human need. Everything you want from existence falls into one of two buckets: *keep the bad away from me,* and *bring the good toward me.* Defense and sustenance. The verse on the army handles the first. The verse on *rizq* handles the second. There is nothing left over. Between these two questions, your whole dependent life is accounted for.

And in the second verse the threat is even more intimate:

> **أَمَّنْ هَـٰذَا ٱلَّذِى يَرْزُقُكُمْ إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُۥ**
>
> "Who is this that would provide for you, **if He withheld His provision?**"

That word **أَمْسَكَ** — *amsaka,* "if He held it back, if He withheld." From the root that means to *grasp, to close the hand, to retain.* Picture an open hand that has been pouring, pouring, pouring without pause your entire life — every breath, every heartbeat, every mouthful, the sunlight, the rain on the crops you didn't grow, the function of organs you never think about — and the verse asks you to imagine, for *one instant,* that hand beginning to **close.** *In amsaka rizqahu.* If He should withhold.

And the question hangs there: *then who?* Who steps in? Name the backup provider. Name the second supplier. The grammar — istifhām inkārī again — has already answered: **no one.** There is no second hand. There never was.

Notice the same tender shock as the first verse. Whose provision is it? *Rizqahu* — **His** provision. Even the word makes the ownership total. It was never "my income that I generate" with God as a kind of silent investor. It is *His* provision, His to pour and His to hold, and you have been living, every day of your life, inside an open hand that has simply *never closed* — and you called that "being self-sufficient."

### Bridge: From Words to Reality Itself

So look at what these three features have shown us. The **question that denies** — *am-man* — empties your hands of the defender you were sure you had. The **single indefinite army** — *jund* — gathers every safety net you ever wove into one word and weighs it against a Name. And that **Name** — *ar-Raḥmān,* appearing exactly where you'd expect a name of wrath — tells you that the only Power over you is Mercy, both in what it withholds from harming you and in what it pours to sustain you.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way you are actually living, right now, today.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Backup Plan That Is Secretly a god

Let me start somewhere very ordinary.

You know the relief of a backup plan. You feel it physically. You're about to do something risky — quit the job, make the move, have the conversation — and your nervous system calms the moment you can say to yourself: *and if it doesn't work out, I still have X.* The savings. The fallback offer. The parents' house I could move back into. The relationship I could return to. X is the floor beneath the tightrope. And the existence of that floor is, for most of us, the difference between a peaceful night and a sleepless one.

There is nothing wrong with floors. Allah commands us to tie the camel, to take the means, to prepare. This is not a verse against planning.

But here is the psychological knife-edge that these verses press on. *Where does your peace actually come from?* Watch carefully. Does your peace come from God — *with* the backup plan as one of His gifts? Or does your peace come from the **backup plan itself** — with God as a kind of distant guarantor you rarely think about?

There is a test for this, and the verse hands it to you: ***in amsaka*** — *if He withheld.* If the floor were suddenly removed. If the savings evaporated, the fallback offer disappeared, the parents' house was no longer there, the protector died. Run the simulation honestly. Who is left?

For the person whose peace came from God, the floor was always *a* mercy, never *the* security. Its removal is painful, but the ground is still there beneath it, because the ground was never the floor — the ground was *ar-Raḥmān.* But for the person whose peace had quietly migrated into the backup plan, its removal is the end of the world. Total panic. Free-fall. Because that person had, without ever deciding to, made the floor into a god. A *jund min dūni-r-Raḥmān* — an army besides the Most Merciful.

[PAUSE]

This is what the verse calls, with surgical precision, **غُرُور** — *ghurūr.* Delusion. And here all four of our tafsir sources agree on something important about the *source* of this delusion. Al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi — all four say the *ghurūr* is **from Shaytan.** As al-Baghawi puts it, Shaytan "deceives them that the punishment will not befall them." The delusion is *whispered.* Someone is actively telling you: *the floor is solid. you've got this. you don't need to look up.*

And do you hear what *ghurūr* is, psychologically? It is not stupidity. It is not lack of intelligence. The disbelievers in this verse are not described as foolish — they are described as *deceived.* You can be brilliant and deceived. You can build a magnificent, sophisticated, diversified army of safety nets and be, the entire time, *fī ghurūr* — *inside* a delusion, living within it like a fish in water, unable to see the water at all. The most dangerous lie is not the one you're told. It's the one you're so surrounded by that it stops looking like a claim and starts looking like *the floor.*

So what changes tomorrow?

Here is the practical move, and it is small and it is daily. The next time you feel that backup-plan relief — that exhale of *"it's okay, I still have X"* — do not throw away X. Instead, in that exact moment, say something underneath it: *and X is from ar-Raḥmān, and if He held it back I would still be held, because He is the One who holds the bird in the sky.* You are not removing the floor. You are reattaching your peace to the ground beneath every floor. You are taking the trust that quietly leaked into the safety net and walking it back home. Done daily, this is the difference between a life of low-grade terror managed by accumulation, and a life of peace that no loss can fully reach.

### Theme Two: How Delusion Hardens Into Defiance — And the Mercy of Imagining the Hand Closing

I want to show you a progression hidden across these two verses, because it is one of the most honest descriptions of the human heart in the whole Qur'an.

Look at how the disbeliever is described at the end of verse 20, and then at the end of verse 21. Two different descriptions. And they are not the same.

At the end of verse 20: **إِنِ ٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ إِلَّا فِى غُرُورٍ** — "the disbelievers are in nothing but **delusion.**" Notice the posture here. It is *passive.* They are *in* something that is being done *to* them. They are *deceived.* There is almost — almost — a sympathy in it. They are victims of a whisper. Held inside an illusion they did not fully author.

At the end of verse 21: **بَل لَّجُّوا۟ فِى عُتُوٍّ وَنُفُورٍ** — "Rather, they **persist** in insolence and aversion." And now the posture has completely flipped. **لَّجُّوا** — *lajjū* — means they *persisted, dug in, obstinately refused to let go.* This is not something being done *to* them. This is them *gripping.* And what they grip is **عُتُوّ** — *ʿutuww,* insolent transgression, the arrogance that breaks all bounds — and **نُفُور** — *nufūr,* aversion, recoiling, *fleeing.* The word *nufūr* is the word you'd use for an animal that bolts, that shies violently away from something it should run *toward.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the arc? It is the whole tragedy of a hardening heart in two words. First you are *deceived* — *fī ghurūr.* That is forgivable; that is human; that is a whisper you could still wake up from. But if you refuse the wake-up, if when the truth is shown to you — *look at the bird, look at the sky, who is your army, who is your bread* — you turn away from it again and again, then the delusion *calcifies.* The soft *ghurūr* of being deceived hardens into the iron *ʿutuww* of choosing the deception. You move from *being lied to* to *defending the lie.* From victim to volunteer.

And feel the sound of these words. *Ghurūr. ʿutuww. nufūr.* Say them. They all end in that long, hollow, rolling sound — *ūr, uww, ūr* — like something receding into the distance, like a door swinging shut, like an echo in an empty room. The phonetics *are* the meaning. These are the sounds of a heart turning away, of mercy being declined, of a creature *bolting* from the very hand that feeds it. *Nufūr* especially — it is almost onomatopoeic for flight, for the spooked recoil. The verse doesn't just *describe* aversion. It makes you *hear* it.

Now — how does mercy break into a heart like this? How does Allah try to stop the hardening before it sets?

He does it with the most compassionate thought-experiment imaginable. **إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُۥ** — *if He withheld His provision.*

Here is the deepest thing in these verses, and I've been holding it for the end. Why does the verse make you *imagine* the hand closing? Why this hypothetical?

Because you cannot see what never stops. This is a law of human attention. We perceive things by their *edges,* by change, by contrast. You do not feel the clothing on your skin until you think about it. You do not hear the hum of the refrigerator until it switches off — and then the *silence* is loud. We are blind to constancy. And *rizq* — provision — is the most constant thing there is. The breath has not stopped since before you were born. The heartbeat has kept its rhythm through every night of your life without your permission or attention. Provision is the water, and you are the fish, and the very *constancy* of the mercy is what makes it *invisible* to you.

So how do you make someone *see* the water? You cannot point at it — they've stopped perceiving it. You can only ask them to imagine it *gone.* *In amsaka rizqahu* — *if the hand closed.* For one second, picture the breath not coming. Picture the next heartbeat simply... not arriving. Picture the sun withheld, the rain withheld, the function of one kidney withheld.

[PAUSE]

And in the horror of that imagined instant — the silence where the refrigerator's hum should be — you suddenly *hear* the mercy you'd gone deaf to. You realize that every unremarkable moment of your entire life has been a hand *deliberately kept open.* That you have been, this whole time, inside a continuous, uninterrupted, never-once-withheld outpouring — and you called it *normal.* You called it *Tuesday.* You called it *self-sufficiency.*

The hypothetical is not a threat. It is the only door through which the constancy of the mercy could be made visible to a heart gone numb. *In amsaka* — *if He withheld* — is Allah's way of switching off the refrigerator for one second, so that in the silence you finally hear the hum that has loved you your whole life.

What changes tomorrow? This one is even simpler than the first. Once today — just once — catch one piece of *rizq* in the act. One breath. One bite of food. The sight in your eyes as you read this. And instead of letting it pass as background, run the verse's experiment on it for one heartbeat: *if this were withheld... and it is not. The hand is open. Right now. It chose to stay open through this very moment.* That single act of catching the constant in motion — that is the cure for *ghurūr* before it ever hardens into *ʿutuww.* You cannot bolt from a hand you have actually felt feeding you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the shape of what we've read, because the two verses are built as a single, perfect structure — and you could only see it once we'd done the work.

Verse 20 asks: **who defends you?** It is about *keeping harm away.* Its threat is *His punishment.* Its answer is: no army but ar-Raḥmān. Its diagnosis of the deny-er is *passive* — *fī ghurūr,* deceived.

Verse 21 asks: **who provides for you?** It is about *bringing good toward you.* Its "threat" is *the withheld hand.* Its answer is, again, only the One who pours. Its diagnosis of the deny-er has *hardened* — *lajjū fī ʿutuww,* willfully persisting.

Two questions. *Am-man... am-man.* The same opening, twice, like two strikes of the same bell. Between them they enclose the *entire* surface area of human need — ward off the bad, bring me the good — and shut every exit. There is no third question, because there is no third kind of need. And both questions point at the same single Name: **ar-Raḥmān.** The Mercy that holds off the harm *is* the Mercy that pours the good. One hand. It defends and it feeds. The army you don't have and the provider you can't name are the *same* Mercy, seen from your two sides — the side that fears, and the side that hungers.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight approached from two directions.

The grammar — *istifhām inkārī,* the question that denies — emptied your hands of every defender and provider you thought you had. The themes showed you what those empty hands *feel* like in a real life: the backup plan you'd made into a quiet god, the constant mercy you'd gone blind to. And the single Name at the center of both verses — *ar-Raḥmān* where you expected a name of wrath — turns the whole thing from terror into homecoming. The questions are not meant to frighten you into submission. They are meant to *relieve* you of an exhausting illusion: the illusion that your safety was ever your own job, that the army was ever yours to muster, that the bread was ever yours to guarantee. *It never was.* And the One it always belonged to is **Mercy.**

### Questions to Carry

1. If I am honest — when I feel that exhale of relief from a "backup plan," is my peace resting on the plan, or on the One who gave it? What would the test of *in amsaka* — *if it were withheld* — reveal about where my trust actually lives?

2. What is one soldier in my personal "army" — one thing I treat as my real security — that I have never once consciously traced back to ar-Raḥmān?

3. When was the last time the truth was shown to me clearly and I turned away from it *again?* Am I still in the soft place of *ghurūr* — or have I started to *lajja,* to dig in and defend the turning-away?

4. What is one piece of constant *rizq* — so constant I have gone deaf to it — that I could "switch off" in my imagination for one second today, just to hear its hum?

5. The bird is held in the sky by nothing but the Most Merciful. What is holding *me* up right now — and have I ever actually looked up to see?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you imagine the one Source of all defense and all provision withdrawing for even a single instant, you discover that everything you ever called your own security was only His mercy wearing a disguise — and the only Power that was ever over you is Mercy itself.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to dismantle your life — to abandon your savings, your skills, your people, your plans. Keep tying the camel. The invitation is quieter than that. It is to let the trust that has quietly leaked out of you and into all those things come *home.* To keep the floor, but to know the ground beneath it. To hold the backup plan in an open hand, the way the open hand of ar-Raḥmān has been holding *you,* unremarked, since before you took your first breath. You were never the army. You were always the bird. And the One who holds you up is the Most Merciful.

### A Brief Du'ā

*Yā Raḥmān* — there is no army for me besides You, and no provision but from Your hand. Forgive me the long delusion in which I mistook Your gifts for my own strongholds, and went deaf to a mercy that never once stopped pouring. Keep my heart soft in the place of *ghurūr* before it hardens, and let me catch — just once today — the open hand that has been feeding me my whole life. *Āmīn.*
