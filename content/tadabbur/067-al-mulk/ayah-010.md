# Had We Only Listened — The Faculty That Was Never Broken

> [ayah:67:10] وَقَالُوا۟ لَوْ كُنَّا نَسْمَعُ أَوْ نَعْقِلُ مَا كُنَّا فِىٓ أَصْحَـٰبِ ٱلسَّعِيرِ
>
> *wa-qālū law kunnā nasmaʿu aw naʿqilu mā kunnā fī aṣḥābi-s-saʿīr*
>
> "And they will say, 'If only we had been listening, or reasoning, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION — The Scene at the Gate

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

Surah Al-Mulk opens with majesty. *Tabāraka-lladhī biyadihi-l-mulk* — blessed is the One in whose hand is all dominion. Then it moves quickly to the thing that gives dominion its weight: *the One who created death and life to test you — which of you is best in deed* (67:2). The whole surah is a meditation on a kingdom you live inside but did not build, ruled by a King who is watching what you do with the short lease you've been given.

And then, a few verses on, the surah does something startling. It takes us forward in time. Past death. Past the grave. To the lip of the Fire itself.

Picture it. Group after group is being driven toward *jahannam*. And as each group arrives, the keepers of the Fire — *khazana* — ask them a single question. *Alam ya'tikum nadhīr?* "Did no warner come to you?" (67:8).

Stay with that question for a moment, because it is the question the whole scene turns on. The keepers are not asking, "Were you good people?" They are not asking, "Did you pray enough?" They ask one thing: *Was the message delivered to you? Did someone come?*

And the answer comes back — *balā* — "Yes." (67:9). Yes, a warner came. *We denied him. We said: Allah has sent down nothing. You are only in great error.*

So the excuse is gone. The messenger came. The warning was delivered. The signal reached the receiver. There is no one left to blame.

And it is *into that silence* — the silence after every excuse has been stripped away — that verse 10 falls. Their own voice. Their own verdict on themselves.

*"If only we had been listening, or reasoning, we would not be among the companions of the Blaze."*

Here is the hook. Here is what I want you to feel the weight of before we go in.

This is the most lucid, the most logically perfect, the most clear-eyed sentence these people ever speak in the entire Qur'an. **Their reasoning finally works.** It works flawlessly. They diagnose their entire ruin in eleven words, and the diagnosis is exactly correct.

[PAUSE]

And it is being spoken in Hell.

What does it mean that the faculties switch on — perfectly — in the one place where they can no longer save you? What does it mean that the confession is *articulate*? And what exactly are they confessing they failed to do — because notice, they do not say "if only a messenger had come." They admit he came. They are confessing to something else entirely.

Let's go find it.

# PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Law* — The Particle of the Impossible Wish

Before we open the word, let me teach you the tool.

In English we have one word, "if," and it does a lot of jobs. "If it rains, I'll bring an umbrella" — that's a real, open possibility. But we also say, "If only I had studied," — and that "if only" means something completely different. It means: *I did not study. That door is shut. And I am aching over it.*

Arabic does not blur these. Arabic has a dedicated particle for the second kind. It is **لَوْ — *law***.

The grammarians have a precise description of *law*. They call it *ḥarf imtināʿ li-imtināʿ* — a particle that signals **the non-occurrence of one thing because of the non-occurrence of another**. In plain terms: *law* is the particle you use for a wish about a past that did not happen and now cannot. It carries grief inside its own grammar. The moment you hear *law*, you already know the thing being wished for did not occur.

Now look at how they open their confession:

> **لَوْ** كُنَّا نَسْمَعُ — ***law* kunnā nasmaʿ** — "*Had we* been listening…"

They do not say "if we listen" — an open door. They reach for *law* — the slammed door. With the very first word of their confession, they are telling you that the time for the listening they're describing is **over**. Gone. Unreachable.

Let's sit with what that means. *Law* is not the language of someone deciding. It is the language of someone *grieving a decision already made* — made over a whole lifetime, and now sealed. The grammar itself is weeping. Before they name a single faculty, the particle has already told you: *whatever we are about to wish for, we did not do it, and we can never go back and do it.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. *Law* is the word of the person standing at the bottom of a consequence, looking back up at the choice. It is the most human sentence-opener there is. *If only.* Every one of us has said it. And the Qur'an puts it in the mouths of the people of the Fire — because *if only* is the native tongue of regret, and regret is the permanent climate of that place.

One word in, and we already know the whole tragedy. The rest of the ayah just tells us *what* they would have done differently.

## Element Two: *Kunnā Nasmaʿu* — The Habit, Not the Moment

Here is the second tool, and it is subtle, so let me build it carefully.

In Arabic, when you take the verb *kāna* (to be, in the past) and you place after it a present-tense verb — what's called the *imperfect* — you do not get a single past action. You get a **past habit**. An ongoing, repeated, characteristic way of being.

An everyday example. If I say *katabtu* — "I wrote" — that's one finished act. A letter, written, done. But if I say *kuntu aktubu* — *kāna* plus the present "I write" — that means "I *used to* write," "I *would* write," "writing was something I did, again and again." It's not an event. It's a pattern. A texture of a life.

Now — watch the verbs they choose. The Leeds corpus confirms the shape exactly. *Kunnā* is past. But *nasmaʿu* — "we hear/listen" — and *naʿqilu* — "we reason" — are both in the **imperfect**. Present tense, sitting under a past *kāna*.

> لَوْ كُنَّا **نَسْمَعُ** أَوْ **نَعْقِلُ**
>
> "Had we *used to* listen, or *used to* reason…"

This is enormous. Let it land.

They are not standing there saying, "If only, on that one Tuesday, we'd listened to that one sermon." They are not confessing to a single missed moment. The grammar reaches across their **entire lives**. They are confessing that listening-to-understand was never their *habit*. Reasoning-toward-truth was never their *practice*. It wasn't a slip. It was a sustained way of being — a lifelong posture of the heart turned away.

Notice something almost unbearable. The very verbs of their confession are in the *present tense*. *Nasmaʿu. Naʿqilu.* "We hear. We reason." Right now — *in the Fire* — the hearing and the reasoning are switched on and running. The present tense is alive in their mouths. The faculties are working *beautifully*. They are listening to the keepers. They are reasoning out, with perfect logic, exactly why they're there.

[PAUSE]

The equipment was never broken. That is the horror buried in the tense. The samʿ works. The ʿaql works. We are watching them *use both, fluently,* to narrate their own destruction. They didn't lack the machinery. They simply never made it a *habit* to point it at the truth — and now the habit they spent a lifetime building has hardened into where they stand.

## Element Three: *Aw* — The "Or" That Doubles the Grief

Watch the conjunction between the two faculties. It would have been so natural to say "had we listened *and* reasoned" — *wa*. Both together. The full set.

But that is not what they say. They say:

> لَوْ كُنَّا نَسْمَعُ **أَوْ** نَعْقِلُ
>
> "Had we been listening **or** reasoning…"

***Aw*** — "or." Not "and." Not both. *Either.*

Teach yourself the difference and the grief opens up. "And" would mean: *we needed the whole set, and we were missing the whole set.* That's almost a softer confession — it would take both faculties, working together, to be saved, and we had neither.

But "or" says something far more cutting. "Or" means: **either one alone would have been enough.** Just the listening — without even the reasoning — would have saved us. Or just the reasoning — without even the warner's words reaching us — would have saved us. We did not need both. We needed *one*. We had *two*. And we engaged *neither*.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how that *aw* deepens the wound? It strips away the last shred of the "it was too hard" defence. They are not saying salvation required some rare double-gift they weren't given. They are saying: there were **two separate doors**, and walking through *either one* would have led them out — and they walked through neither. The "or" multiplies the regret. It turns one missed chance into two missed chances, each sufficient on its own.

And here the classical scholars help us see what the two doors actually were.

Imam al-Qurtubi and Imam al-Baghawi both explain *nasmaʿ* as hearing **from the warners — the messengers — what they brought.** That is the door of *reception*: the truth arriving from outside you, through revelation, through the one sent to you. And *naʿqil* — the great grammarian al-Zajjāj, as al-Baghawi reports him, glosses it beautifully: *the reasoning of one who discerns and considers* — *ʿaql man yumayyiz wa-yanẓur*. That is the door of *processing*: the truth you could have worked out, weighed, recognized from within.

So the two doors are: **let it in from outside, or work it out from inside.** Revelation received, or reason engaged. al-Imām al-Jalālayn pins it precisely — *samʿ* here is *samāʿ tafahhum*, the hearing that seeks to understand, and *ʿaql* is *ʿaql tafakkur*, the reasoning that reflects. And Ibn ʿAbbās — in a comment preserved by both al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi — frames it as hearing *the guidance* or reasoning it out so as to *act* on it.

Two roads to the same rescue. Either would have done it. They took neither.

## Bridge — From the Words to the Reality

Step back and see what these three small grammatical features have built, together.

*Law* told us: the door is shut, and they grieve. *Kunnā nasmaʿu* told us: this was a lifelong habit, not a single slip — and the faculties are working *right now*, perfectly, too late. *Aw* told us: either faculty alone would have saved them, and they engaged neither, so the regret is doubled.

Notice what is **completely absent** from their confession. There is no "if only the truth had reached us." No "if only a clearer messenger had come." No "if only we'd had more time." They named none of the things we usually reach for. The interrogation in verse 9 already closed those exits — *the warner came; we denied him.* All that is left to confess is the failure of *reception*. The signal arrived. The receivers were switched off — by choice, as a habit, on two separate channels either of which would have been enough.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About the faculties you are carrying right now, as you listen to this.

# PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Faculties Were Never the Problem

Start where you already live.

You have, on the device in your pocket, more access to guidance than every scholar of the first thousand years of Islam *combined* could have dreamed of. Every translation of the Qur'an. Every tafsīr. Ten thousand reminders, lectures, recitations — one tap away, free, instant, in your own language. The signal has never in human history been louder or more available than it is for you, right now.

And yet.

How many of those reminders have *moved* anything? How many lectures have you *heard* — sound entering the ear, words understood, even enjoyed — and then nothing changed? You closed the app. You felt a flicker. And the next morning you were exactly who you were the day before.

That gap — between sound *received* and truth *let in* — is the entire subject of this ayah.

Here is the thing the Arabic forces us to confront. The people of the Fire do not blame a lack of information. The information *came*. They blame *nasmaʿ* and *naʿqil* — the two faculties on the receiving end. And, as we saw, those faculties were never broken. They are using both, perfectly, as they speak.

So the diagnosis the ayah hands us is razor-precise, and it cuts against our deepest excuse. **It was never the equipment. It was never the access. It was the engagement.** *Samʿ*, in the Qur'an, is almost never about the ear's ability to detect sound — the deaf are not the ones condemned. *Samʿ* is the heart's willingness to *receive*. To let the words land somewhere that hurts and changes you. The disbelievers of the Fire could hear perfectly well in the worldly sense. Allah says of their kind elsewhere: *they have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, ears with which they do not hear* (7:179). The ears work. The hearing — the real hearing — was switched off.

[PAUSE]

Let me re-enter this through your own door, because it is far too easy to picture "those people" as some distant villains.

Think of the last reminder that genuinely *should* have changed something in you. You knew it. You felt the truth of it land. And then — the habit reasserted itself. *Kunnā nasmaʿu* — you *used to* hear, in the shallow way, sound passing through. But the deep *samʿ*, the receiving that reorganizes a life — that you did not make your habit.

This is the ayah's gift, and it is both terrifying and liberating. **Terrifying**, because it removes the comfortable excuse: you cannot say "no one told me," "I never had access," "the message was unclear." For you, of all generations, the warner has *more* than come. And **liberating**, because if the problem was never the equipment or the access — if you already possess, intact, the two faculties that either one of which is enough — then nothing external is missing. You are not waiting on a resource. You are waiting on a *turn of the heart toward what is already arriving.*

**What does this change tomorrow?** Here is one specific thing. The next time you hear a reminder — the next ayah, the next khuṭbah, the next time a verse stops you — do not let it stay at the level of *sound enjoyed* or *point understood*. Ask the one question that turns *samʿ* the shallow into *samʿ* the deep: *What is the one thing this asks me to change — and will I move on it before the feeling fades?* That single question is the difference between hearing and *listening*. It is the difference, the ayah tells us, between the two destinations.

## Theme Two: When Reason Finally Works — and It's Too Late

Now the harder, stranger truth — the one hiding in the *tense*.

Let's go back to where we began. This confession is the clearest thinking these people ever do. *Law kunnā nasmaʿ aw naʿqil* — they reason out their entire ruin with flawless logic. Cause and effect, perfectly traced. Premise, conclusion, verdict. If A, then not-B; we failed A; therefore B. It is *immaculate* reasoning.

And it is happening in Hell.

Here is the bridge to your own life, because you have felt this exact thing, and it has a flavor you know.

It is the 3 a.m. clarity — when you lie awake and suddenly see, with perfect precision, exactly where you went wrong, exactly what you should have said, exactly the choice that started the unraveling. The reasoning is *brilliant* at 3 a.m. It just arrives twelve hours, or twelve years, too late.

It is the clarity *after* the relationship ends — when you can finally articulate, with painful exactness, the thing you couldn't see while it was happening.

It is, the scholars tell us, the clarity of the deathbed and beyond. al-Qurtubi, at this very ayah, records a narration from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī — traced to the Prophet ﷺ — about the regret of the wicked on the Day of Resurrection. The text trails off in the report, but the point is unmistakable: regret, in that place, is *total* and *too late*. The faculties don't fail at the end. They sharpen. The seeing gets *clearer*. That is precisely what makes it Hell.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this paradox, because the ayah does not rush to resolve it, and neither should we. We tend to imagine the damned as *stupid* — as people who never figured it out. The grammar says the opposite. They figured it out *perfectly*. The *naʿqil* — the reasoning — is switched on and running in the present tense, in their mouths, in the Fire. **Reason is not salvation.** Reason that arrives after the door closes is not a rescue; it is the most exquisite torment, because now you can see, with total clarity, the door — and that it is shut, and that you shut it.

Why does it work so well *there* and so poorly *here*? Because here, in the world, reason is fighting a current — the current of desire, of distraction, of the habit we named earlier, of a thousand reasons to look away. The *ʿaql* is real but it is *resisted*. There, in the Fire, every veil that desire and distraction hung over the truth has been torn down. There is nothing left to want, nothing left to be distracted by. So the reasoning runs clean — uselessly, perfectly, clean.

Which means the work — the only work that matters — is to drag that 3 a.m. clarity *forward*. To do now, against the current, the reasoning they will do later in still water. The Qur'an is, in a sense, *engineered for exactly this*: it is the truth of the deathbed delivered to you while you still have time to act on it. Every warning ayah is a transcript of a future regret, handed to you *in advance* — so the *naʿqil* can fire while the door is still open.

There is a companion of the Prophet ﷺ, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who captured the whole of this theme in a single line: *Call yourselves to account before you are called to account.* That is nothing but the deliberate act of running the *naʿqil* now — holding the future confession in your hands today, reading it, and changing course while changing course still does something.

**What does this change tomorrow?** Here is the specific practice. Tonight, before you sleep, do the thing the people of the Fire will do too late: run the confession *forward*. Ask yourself the question the keepers will ask — *did the warning reach me, and what did I do with it?* — and then ask the question *they* asked themselves — *what one thing, if I had truly listened or truly reasoned, would I change?* And then, the part they could not do: **change it.** While the verb is still in the open present and the *law* of regret has not yet shut the door. That is the entire difference between this ayah being a description of someone else, and a warning that saved you.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture — A Confession Built in Reverse

Now that we've done the work, look at how the ayah is actually constructed, because its shape is its argument.

Read the logical chain it lays out:

> **Input** → *nasmaʿ* (hearing / reception from outside)
> **Processing** → *naʿqil* (reasoning / engagement from inside)
> **Outcome** → *mā kunnā fī aṣḥābi-s-saʿīr* (we would not be companions of the Blaze)

That is a complete causal chain: *receive the signal → engage the faculty → arrive at safety.* Input, processing, outcome. It is, in fact, the architecture of guidance itself, stated in miniature.

But notice — they speak it **in reverse, as a confession of absence.** *Had we received, had we engaged — we would not be here.* They are standing at the *outcome* — inside the Fire — and tracing the chain backwards to the two failures that put them there. The whole verse is a perfectly sound piece of reasoning about why their reasoning came too late. The form *is* the content. The architecture is the proof: the machinery of salvation was intact and available; they ran it, flawlessly, in the wrong direction of time.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are one insight seen from two sides.

The *grammar* told us: *law* shuts the door; *kunnā nasmaʿu* makes it a lifelong habit with the faculties still humming in the present; *aw* doubles the loss because either door alone was enough.

The *themes* told us: the equipment was never the problem (so your excuse of access is gone), and reason that arrives after the door closes is torment, not rescue (so the work is to reason *now*, against the current).

Put them together and you get the single, unified truth of the ayah:

**You already possess everything you need to be saved. Two faculties, either one sufficient, both working perfectly. The only question the universe is really asking you is whether you will turn them toward the truth *while the present tense is still open* — or save that perfect, clear-eyed reasoning for the one place where it can no longer do anything but grieve.**

## Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I truly *heard* a reminder — not just understood it, not just enjoyed it, but let it *change* something? And if I cannot remember, what does that say about my habit of *samʿ*?
2. What is the 3 a.m. clarity I already have — the thing I *know*, with perfect reasoning, I should change — that I am quietly saving for "later"? What would it cost to drag it into today?
3. The keepers ask, *did a warner come to you?* For me, the warner has more than come. What, then, will my honest answer be?
4. I have two doors — receiving the truth from outside, and reasoning my way to it from inside. Either one is enough. Which one is more open for me right now — and am I walking through it?
5. If I wrote my own confession in the grammar of *law* — "if only I had…" — what verb would I put in it? And what is stopping me from making that verb my habit *today*, while it is still in the present tense?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The people of the Fire confess, with flawless reasoning, that the faculties of hearing and reason — either one of which would have saved them — were never broken, only never turned toward the truth in time; and their perfectly lucid regret, spoken too late, is itself the warning handed to us early enough to act on.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need a new resource. You do not need clearer access, more proof, a better teacher, or more time than you'll be given. You are already carrying the two faculties intact. The invitation of this ayah is not "go acquire something." It is simply: *turn.* Turn the hearing you already have into listening that lands. Turn the reasoning you already have toward the one subject your *ʿaql* keeps avoiding. Do it now — in the open present tense — so that the only confession you ever speak in the grammar of *law* is one you spoke *to yourself*, in time, and then made unnecessary by changing.

## A Brief Du'ā'

*O Allah, You gave us hearing — make us of those who truly listen; and You gave us reason — make us of those who reason toward You while the door is still open. Do not let our faculties wait for the Fire to finally work. Let us call ourselves to account today, so that we are not among the companions of the Blaze tomorrow. Āmīn.*
