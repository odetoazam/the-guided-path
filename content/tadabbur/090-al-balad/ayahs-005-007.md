# The Man Who Counted Wrong
### A Tadabbur of Surah Al-Balad, Ayahs 5–7

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing in the City

Listen to the words first. Let them land before we touch them.

> [ayah:90:5] أَيَحْسَبُ أَن لَّن يَقْدِرَ عَلَيْهِ أَحَدٌ
> *a-yaḥsabu an lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad*
> **Does he think that no one has power over him?**

> [ayah:90:6] يَقُولُ أَهْلَكْتُ مَالًا لُّبَدًا
> *yaqūlu ahlaktu mālan lubadā*
> **He says, "I have squandered abundant wealth."**

> [ayah:90:7] أَيَحْسَبُ أَن لَّمْ يَرَهُۥٓ أَحَدٌ
> *a-yaḥsabu an lam yarahu aḥad*
> **Does he think that no one has seen him?**

Thirteen words. Three short breaths. And inside them, a whole human being — a recognizable one. You have met him. You may, on some afternoon you are not proud of, have *been* him.

Let me rebuild the ground beneath these words.

The surah begins with an oath. Allah swears **by the city** — *al-balad* — and the early listeners knew exactly which city: Mecca. The sanctuary. The place where, by ancient custom, no blood was shed and no creature was hunted, the one spot on earth where even an enemy was safe. And then Allah says something to His Prophet ﷺ that should stop us: *"and you are a free dweller in this city."* You, Muhammad — in the very city that is supposed to protect every soul — are being hunted. The sanctuary has stopped sanctifying. The rules have been suspended *for you.*

Why? Because of men. Specific men. Men with wealth, with standing, with the strength of a hundred hands and the confidence of someone who has never once been told *no* and meant it.

Then comes ayah 4, the thesis of the whole surah: **"We created the human being in *kabad*"** — in toil, in hardship, in a struggle baked into the very clay of him. Life is uphill. Everyone's life is uphill. That is the design.

And it is *right here*, against this backdrop of universal struggle, that Allah turns the camera onto one man. A man who has decided he is the exception. A man who looks at his pile of wealth and concludes that the uphill road is for other people — that he, personally, has bought his way onto level ground.

Here is the hook. I want you to hold two things in your mind, because the entire reflection lives in the tension between them.

This man asks himself a question — *does anyone have power over me? did anyone even see me?* — and he answers it with a confident **no.**

And the very words he uses to say *no* are built from the names of the One he is denying. [PAUSE]

He is not just wrong. He is wrong *in a language that is constantly correcting him.* Let us learn to hear it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb of the Reckoning — *yaḥsabu*

Before we open the word, let me give you a small everyday picture.

Imagine a man sitting at a desk with a calculator, working out his taxes. He runs the numbers, he is pleased, he leans back and says, *"There. I owe nothing."* He has done a calculation. He has reached a verdict. And the entire question — the only question that matters — is whether his calculation matches the ledger held by the authority that will actually audit him.

Hold that picture. Now look at how these ayahs begin. Twice:

> **أَيَحْسَبُ** — *a-yaḥsabu* — **"Does he reckon...? Does he calculate...?"**

The little hamza at the front, that *a-*, is the hamza of questioning — Arabic's way of raising an eyebrow. And the verb it sits on, *yaḥsabu*, comes from the root **ḥ-s-b (ḥasiba)**.

Now stay with me, because this root is doing something almost unbearable.

The root **ḥ-s-b** carries the sense of *counting, computing, reckoning, keeping accounts.* From this exact root comes one of the most important words in the entire Quran: **al-ḥisāb** — *the Reckoning.* The Day of Accounts. The audit at the end of all things, when every deed is counted and the ledger is read back to you. *Yawm al-ḥisāb.*

So look at what Allah has done in His choice of verb. The man is doing a *ḥisāb* — a private little reckoning in his own head — and the conclusion of his reckoning is *that there will be no Reckoning.* [PAUSE]

He uses the faculty of accounting to conclude that he will never be brought to account.

This is not a coincidence of translation. In Arabic the irony is *audible.* The word for his arrogant supposing and the word for the Day that will refute it grow from the same three letters. Every time he "reckons," the language itself is whispering the name of the audit he is dismissing.

Let's sit with what *yaḥsabu* means, exactly. It is not *yaʿlamu*, "he knows." It is not *yarā*, "he sees clearly." *Ḥasiba* is the verb of *supposing*, of *assuming*, of running an estimate and treating the estimate as fact. The classical commentators — al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurṭubī — all gloss it with **"a-yaẓunnu"**: *does he merely* ***think,*** *does he merely* ***presume?*** It is the verb of a man who has confused his confidence for knowledge.

Here is what is remarkable. Allah does not say *he is wrong.* He asks a question: *"Does he reckon...?"* And a question, when God asks it, is heavier than a statement. Because a statement can be argued with. A question hands the man a mirror and says: *go on — say it out loud. Say that you calculated your way out of being held accountable. Hear how it sounds when you say it.*

What does this single verb reveal? That arrogance is, at its root, a **bookkeeping error.** It is not strength. It is not even really defiance. It is a man at his desk, running the numbers wrong, mistaking the absence of an audit *so far* for the absence of an auditor.

### Element Two: The Power No One Has — *lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad*

Now the content of his miscalculation. What did he conclude?

> **أَن لَّن يَقْدِرَ عَلَيْهِ أَحَدٌ**
> *an lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad*
> **"...that no one will ever have power over him."**

Two words here carry the weight. Let's take them one at a time, and let's teach the grammar before we lean on it.

First, *lan*. In Arabic there are ordinary ways to say "not" — *lā*, *lam*. And then there is *lan*, which negates the future with emphasis: *never, not ever, it will not come to pass.* When the man reaches his verdict, he does not say "I don't think anyone has power over me." He says *lan* — **never.** He has closed the future. He has bolted the door on every tomorrow in which someone might prove stronger than him.

Now the verb under that *never*: **yaqdira**, from the root **q-d-r**.

This is one of the great roots of the Quran, so let me open it slowly. **Q-d-r** means *to have power, to have capacity, to be able* — and also *to measure, to determine, to apportion, to decree.* From this root comes **al-Qadīr** and **al-Qādir** — *the All-Powerful, the One with absolute capacity* — names of Allah Himself. From this root comes **al-qadar** — the divine decree. And from this root comes **Laylat al-Qadr** — the Night of Power, of decree, of measuring out.

So when the man declares *lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad* — "no one has *qudra* over me" — he is, without knowing it, reaching for the exact attribute that belongs to God and announcing its absence. He is standing in a room flooded with light and swearing there is no sun.

The classical tafsīr makes the unspoken referent explicit. Al-Jalālayn fills in the silence: he supposes *"that no one will have power over him — yet Allah is fully able over him."* Al-Muyassar reads it through his wealth: *"Does he think, because of the wealth he has piled up, that Allah will have no power over him?"* And al-Qurṭubī sharpens it to its consequence: *"Does the son of Adam think that Allah — mighty and majestic — will not* ***punish*** *him?"* Three angles, one image: a man who has mistaken *no one has stopped me yet* for *no one is able to.*

And now read the absence — because this is where the deepest thing is hiding.

He says **أَحَدٌ** — *aḥad* — **"any one."** No one. Not a single one. The word is indefinite, sweeping, total: he scans the whole field of existence and finds no one with power over him.

But listen to that word. **Aḥad.** [PAUSE]

It is the same word — the very same — that Allah uses to name Himself in the surah every child learns first: **قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ** — *"Say: He is Allah, the One — al-Aḥad."* The Utterly Singular. The One beside whom there is no second.

So hear the full sentence again, with both meanings ringing at once. The man says: *lan yaqdira ʿalayhi* ***aḥad*** — "no *aḥad* has power over me." And the answer, built into his own sentence, is: *the One with all power is precisely* ***al-Aḥad.*** The One he says does not exist *is* the meaning of the word he chose to say it. He reached for "anyone" to empty the universe of authority over him, and the word turned in his hand and pointed straight at God.

What does this reveal? That you cannot construct a sentence of pure self-sufficiency. The language will not let you. Try to say *no one rules me*, and the word for "no one" is shadowed by the Name of the One who does. Denial, in the end, has to borrow its vocabulary from the very truth it denies.

### Element Three: The Two Walls — Power and Sight

Now step back and look at the *shape* of the passage, because the architecture is itself an argument.

Notice that ayah 5 and ayah 7 are nearly twins:

> Ayah 5: **أَيَحْسَبُ أَن لَّن يَقْدِرَ عَلَيْهِ أَحَدٌ** — *Does he reckon that no one has* ***power*** *over him?*
> Ayah 7: **أَيَحْسَبُ أَن لَّمْ يَرَهُۥٓ أَحَدٌ** — *Does he reckon that no one has* ***seen*** *him?*

Same opening: *a-yaḥsabu* — "does he reckon." Same closing: *aḥad* — "any one." Identical frame. Only the middle changes. In ayah 5 it is **yaqdira** — *has power.* In ayah 7 it is **yarahu**, from the root **r-ʾ-y** — *has seen.*

**Power** and **sight.** Two walls. And between them — sandwiched, protected, displayed — sits ayah 6, the boast: *"I have squandered abundant wealth."*

Do you see the structure? The man builds his life like a vault. On one side, a wall: *no one is strong enough to call me to account.* On the other side, a wall: *no one even saw what I did.* And in the safe little chamber between the two walls, he stacks his treasure and admires it.

These are the two and only two things a guilty man needs in order to feel free. He needs to believe **no one can hold him accountable** (no power over him), and he needs to believe **no one witnessed him** (no sight of him). Remove either wall and the freedom collapses. If someone *saw*, then accountability has a witness. If someone *has power*, then being seen has a consequence.

And here is the quiet devastation of the passage: **Allah is the answer to both walls at once.** He is *al-Qadīr* — the wall of "no power over me" is His. And He is the One who sees — the wall of "no one saw me" is His. The man built two walls to keep one Reality out, and that one Reality is the foundation of both walls.

Now feel the genius of the verb in ayah 7 — **yarahu**, *"has seen him."* Because what comes *immediately after,* in ayah 8, is this:

> **أَلَمْ نَجْعَل لَّهُۥ عَيْنَيْنِ** — *"Did We not make for him two eyes?"*

The man asks: *did no one see me?* And Allah answers, in the very next breath: *the eyes you are seeing with right now — who do you think gave them to you?* You doubt that you were seen — *by the One who installed sight itself into your skull.* The faculty you used to scan the room and confirm you were alone is on loan from the Witness. **The bridge.** We have heard three things. We heard *yaḥsabu* — that arrogance is a miscalculation made with the very faculty of reckoning that the Reckoning will use against him. We heard *qudra* and *aḥad* — that the man's denial is spoken in borrowed words, that "no one" is haunted by *al-Aḥad*, that "no power" is the negation of *al-Qadīr.* And we saw the architecture — two walls, power and sight, both of them God's, with a hoard of wealth trembling in between.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about a man's words — but about the deepest machinery of the human heart. Why do we build these walls? And what is actually behind them?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Anatomy of "Getting Away With It"

Begin where you actually live.

There is a particular feeling — quiet, almost physical — that arrives the moment you realize you have *gotten away with something.* The lie that was never caught. The corner cut that no one inspected. The unkindness no witness recorded. The money moved where no eye followed it. There is a small loosening in the chest, a private exhale: *no one knows. No one can touch me for it.*

We live in the most-watched civilization in human history. Cameras on every corner, a phone tracking your steps, an algorithm that knows what you'll buy before you do. And yet — here is the strange thing — modern people feel *more* unwitnessed than ever, not less. Because we have learned that being *recorded* is not the same as being *seen.* The camera captures the act but not the heart. You can be on a thousand servers and still feel, in the only place that matters, *completely alone with what you did.*

That feeling — *no one really saw the real me do the real thing* — is ayah 7, word for word, fourteen centuries early. *A-yaḥsabu an lam yarahu aḥad.* Does he reckon no one saw him?

Now let the ayah illuminate the feeling. The man in these verses is not a cartoon villain. He is sophisticated. He has done the math. He looked at the evidence available to him — *I haven't been caught, no consequence has arrived, I am still standing* — and he drew the only conclusion the evidence seemed to support: *there is no one watching, and no one able.* His logic is not stupid. **His logic is incomplete.** He reasoned correctly from a sample that was missing one term.

This is the trap of every soul that grows comfortable in sin: we treat *delay* as *absence.* The audit has not come, therefore there is no auditor. The lightning has not struck, therefore there is no sky. But *lan* — "never" — is a claim no human being is ever in a position to make. To say *no one will* ***ever*** *have power over me* you would need to stand outside of time and see every tomorrow at once. The man says *lan* about a future he cannot see, using eyes that were given to him by the One who owns every future there is.

Let me push to the heart of it. Why does getting away with it feel so good? Not because of the thing gained. The man in ayah 6 isn't even *keeping* the wealth — he *destroyed* it, he says so. The pleasure is not in the prize. The pleasure is in the *invisibility* — in the fantasy that there exists a sealed room where you can be your worst self with no record kept. We don't actually crave the sin half so much as we crave the *unwatched* in which to commit it.

And that is the fantasy the Quran dismantles, not with a threat, but with a fact about reality: **there is no unwatched room.** The Seer does not blink. *"He knows the treachery of the eyes and what the breasts conceal"* (40:19). The sealed chamber the man built between his two walls was never sealed. It had no roof. It never did.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge, and it is gentler than you might expect. The Quran is not trying to make you paranoid, to feel hunted by a divine camera. It is offering you the opposite of loneliness. The same attribute that means *your worst moment was seen* also means **your most invisible good was seen too.** The kindness no one thanked you for. The tears you cried where no human eye reached. The night you almost gave up and didn't. *Yarahu aḥad* — Someone saw. The wall you feared is also the wall that holds you. To live before the Seer is to know that nothing true about you is ever wasted on an empty room.

Tomorrow, then: the next time you feel the small exhale of *no one knows* — pause on it. Ask which God you would rather have: the one who missed it, or the One who never misses anything, including the good in you that the world overlooks. The first leaves you alone. The second never will.

### Theme One Resolves Into Theme Two: The Boast That Confesses

Now go to the middle. The thing between the two walls. Ayah 6:

> **يَقُولُ أَهْلَكْتُ مَالًا لُّبَدًا**
> *yaqūlu ahlaktu mālan lubadā*
> **He says, "I have squandered abundant wealth."**

Begin, again, where you live. You know this man. He is the one at the table who tells you, unprompted, what the watch cost. He is the post about the trip, the car, the renovation — *look how much I can burn through.* In our age it has a name: the flex. The boast is never really about the object. It is about the *capacity* the object proves. *I can destroy this much money and not even feel it.* The waste is the point. The waste is the flex.

Now look at the verb the Quran chose to put in his mouth: **أَهْلَكْتُ** — *ahlaktu* — from the root **h-l-k**, *to destroy, to ruin, to cause to perish.* He does not say "I *spent* wealth" or "I *gave* wealth." He says **"I caused wealth to perish."** And he says it *proudly.* In his mouth, *ahlaktu* is a boast: *I have such abundance that I can annihilate it for sport.*

But listen to what the word actually carries. **H-l-k** is the root of *halāk* — utter ruin, destruction, perishing. It is the word for what happens to nations that are destroyed, to the soul that is lost. The man reaches for a word of *power* — "look what I can destroy" — and grabs, instead, a word of *doom.* He thinks he is describing his command over wealth. The language is describing his trajectory. [PAUSE]

He calls it destruction. And he is right — but not about the money. The thing being led to *halāk* is not the wealth. It is the man.

And the wealth itself — **مَالًا لُّبَدًا**, *mālan lubadā.* That word *lubad* is rich. The lexicographers tie it to things *heaped, matted, packed densely together* — wealth piled up like matted wool or the thick wadded hair on a beast's back, so abundant it clumps. It is the image of *so much you lose count.* It is also, of course, the perfect fuel for the miscalculation we already named: when you can no longer count it, you start to believe it cannot be counted *against* you. Abundance becomes its own kind of blindness. The heap is so high it blocks the view of the Reckoner on the other side.

Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing it. **His boast is a confession.** Read ayah 6 between the two walls again. He thinks the middle chamber is where he stores his glory — *look at my wealth, look at my power to destroy it.* But the surah has framed that chamber with two indictments. To his left: *does he think no one can hold him accountable?* To his right: *does he think no one saw?* And what is the thing they are asking about? **This very boast.** The wealth he is so proud of destroying is the exhibit. He thinks he is showing off his treasure. He is reading his own charge sheet aloud and mistaking it for applause.

Now connect it to the universal struggle, the *kabad* of ayah 4. Every human being was made for toil — the uphill road. And what comes a few verses later? *Al-ʿaqaba* — the steep ascent. And Allah tells us what climbing it looks like: *freeing a slave, feeding the orphan and the destitute on a day of hunger.* In other words: **the road up is paved with the exact wealth this man boasted of destroying.** He had *mālan lubadā* — heaped, matted abundance — the precise resource that buys passage up the steep path. And he *ahlaktahu* — he destroyed it. He burned the fuel of his own ascent to impress an audience he could not even prove was real.

That is the tragedy in full. It is not that he had nothing. It is that he had *everything he needed* to climb, and he set it on fire to feel powerful for an afternoon.

What does this change about tomorrow? It reframes every dirham, every dollar, every hour and ounce of capacity you have. The question the surah presses into your hand is not *how much can I spend* but *which road am I funding?* The same wealth is either fuel for the climb or kindling for the boast. Tonight, look at one thing you have "extra" — and ask whether it is currently being *spent on the ascent* or *destroyed for the display.* You do not need much. The surah's example is one slave freed, one hungry mouth fed on one hard day. The steep path is not bought in heaps. It is climbed in single, witnessed, deliberate steps.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we have done the work, stand back and see the whole building.

Three ayahs. A perfect frame:

- **Ayah 5** — the wall of *power*: *Does he reckon no one is able to hold him to account?* — and the word he denies, *aḥad*, is the Name of *al-Aḥad*; the power he denies, *qudra*, is the attribute of *al-Qadīr.*
- **Ayah 6** — the chamber between: *"I destroyed abundant wealth"* — a boast spoken in the vocabulary of doom, *ahlaktu*, the wealth that was his ladder turned to ash.
- **Ayah 7** — the wall of *sight*: *Does he reckon no one saw him?* — answered one breath later by *"Did We not make for him two eyes?"*

Two walls of denial, a hoard of misused wealth between them, and a single Reality — *al-Qadīr*, the Seer, *al-Aḥad* — who is the foundation of both walls, the witness in the sealed room, and the Reckoner the man's own verb keeps naming.

### The Two Roads Meet

The linguistic discovery and the human theme are the same truth from two directions. The language said: *you cannot deny God in words that do not borrow from Him* — "no one" is shadowed by *al-Aḥad*, "no power" by *al-Qadīr*, "I destroyed" by *halāk.* And the human heart said: *you cannot build a sealed room to sin in, because the walls you build are made of His attributes.* Every brick of the man's self-sufficiency was quarried from the very God he was walling out. His denial was always, secretly, a confession.

### Questions to Carry

- Where in my life have I quietly mistaken *delay* for *absence* — assumed that because the consequence has not come, the One who sends it is not there?
- What "sealed room" do I still believe I have — a space where I am alone with my worst self and no one is watching?
- The same Sight that worries me about my hidden faults also witnessed my hidden good. Which of those two truths do I let myself feel more?
- What is my *mālan lubadā* — my heaped, matted abundance? Not only money: time, talent, attention, strength. And right now, today — is it fuel for the ascent, or kindling for the display?
- If I said my boasts out loud in front of *al-Aḥad*, which of them would turn, in my mouth, into a confession?

### The One Sentence

*A man used the faculty of reckoning to conclude there would be no Reckoning, and built two walls — "no one can touch me, no one saw me" — out of the very attributes of the One who can, and did.*

### The Invitation

You do not have to be afraid of being seen. That is the strange gift hiding inside this stern little passage. The man feared a watching God and so he denied Him — and in denying Him, lost the only Witness who would ever value the good no human eye recorded. The invitation is to flip the wall into a window: to let *yarahu aḥad* — "Someone saw" — stop being a threat and start being the deepest companionship you have ever known. Not *I am being watched,* but *I am never, ever, in the worst room or the loneliest night, alone.* And then to take the wealth you were tempted to burn for applause, and spend it — quietly, witnessed only by Him — on a single step up the steep path.

### A Du'a

*O Allah, al-Aḥad, the One beside whom there is no other — there is no sealed room hidden from You, and there is no good of mine too small for You to see. Save me from the reckoning of a heart that mistakes Your patience for Your absence. Let the abundance You gave me become fuel for the climb and not kindling for my pride, and let me live every unwatched hour knowing that the One who gave me my eyes has never once looked away.* Āmīn.
