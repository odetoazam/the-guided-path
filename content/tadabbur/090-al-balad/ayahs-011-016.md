# The Steep Path: Why Allah Used a Warrior's Word for Feeding the Poor

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with their sound before you reach for their meaning.

> فَلَا ٱقْتَحَمَ ٱلْعَقَبَةَ ۝ وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْعَقَبَةُ ۝ فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ إِطْعَٰمٌ فِى يَوْمٍ ذِى مَسْغَبَةٍ ۝ يَتِيمًا ذَا مَقْرَبَةٍ ۝ أَوْ مِسْكِينًا ذَا مَتْرَبَةٍ

*Falā-qtaḥama-l-ʿaqabah. Wa mā adrāka ma-l-ʿaqabah. Fakku raqabah. Aw iṭʿāmun fī yawmin dhī masghabah. Yatīman dhā maqrabah. Aw miskīnan dhā matrabah.*

"But he has not attempted the steep ascent. And what can make you know what the steep ascent is? It is the freeing of a neck from bondage, or feeding on a day of severe hunger an orphan of near kinship, or a destitute man clinging to the dust."

### Rebuild the world

To feel where these words fall, you have to hear what came just before them.

A man is standing in Makkah. He has wealth, and he is proud of it — proud in a particular way that the surah captures with brutal economy. He says, earlier in this same surah, *ahlaktu mālan lubadā* — "I have destroyed wealth abundant." Notice the verb. Not *I spent*, not *I gave* — **I destroyed**. He is bragging about how much he burned through. He imagines, the surah tells us, that no one has any power over him, and that no one ever saw what he did.

So Allah answers him. And the answer is not a threat. The answer is an inventory. *Did We not make for him two eyes? And a tongue, and two lips? And did We not guide him to the najdayn* — the two raised highways, the road of good and the road of evil, both laid out plainly before him like two ridgelines on the horizon. Al-Ṭabarī draws out the sting in that inventory: the eyes were given *yubṣiru bihimā ḥujaja-Llāhi ʿalayhi* — "so that with them he might see the very proofs of Allah standing against him," the tongue so he could speak his own mind, all of it a *niʿmah*, a gift. The faculties handed to him as favors are the same faculties that will testify to what he did with them. Every gift is also a piece of evidence.

That is the moment we walk into. The man has been shown the map. He has two eyes that work, a tongue that speaks, and two clearly marked roads. He has every instrument he needs to climb.

And then — our ayah. *Falā-qtaḥama-l-ʿaqabah.* He didn't take the hard road. He saw the high pass and he turned away from it.

This is a Makkan surah. It is fierce, compressed, and addressed to a society where the gap between the man boasting about destroyed wealth and the man clinging to the dust was visible on every street. Keep both of those men in your mind. They are about to meet inside a single word.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I want you to let it bother you.

Allah is about to describe the single hardest, highest, most heroic thing a human being can do. He stops the entire recitation to ask you a question — *do you even know what this thing is?* — the way you'd grab someone by the shoulders before saying something enormous.

And then He tells you what it is. It is feeding a hungry child. It is unlocking a chain from a human neck.

Why does the most ordinary act of kindness get the most extraordinary build-up in the Qur'an? And why — this is the part that should keep you up — does Allah describe it using a word that Arab warriors used for **plunging headlong into the thick of battle**?

Stay with that. We're going to climb toward it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: ٱقْتَحَمَ — The Word for Throwing Yourself Into Danger

Let me teach you something about Arabic verb shapes before we touch this word, because the whole ayah turns on it.

Arabic verbs come in patterns, and the pattern is not decoration — it carries meaning. There is a plain pattern that just says a thing happened. And then there are intensified, reshaped patterns that add force, effort, repetition, or a turning of the action back onto the doer. Think of the difference in English between "to sit" and "to *throw* yourself down." Same general direction — but one is passive arrival and the other is a deliberate, forceful, whole-body act.

The verb in our ayah is **ٱقْتَحَمَ — iqtaḥama** — and it sits in the pattern the grammarians call **Form VIII** (the *iftiʿāl* pattern). This is documented; the Leeds Qur'anic corpus tags it precisely as Form VIII, from the root **ق-ح-م**, *q-ḥ-m*. The Form VIII shape characteristically pulls the action **back onto the doer with effort and involvement** — you don't just do it, you throw *yourself* into it.

Now the root itself. Listen to how the classical lexicographers and mufassirūn handle it, because they do not soften it.

Al-Qurṭubī, explaining this exact word, writes: *al-iqtiḥām: ar-ramyu bi-n-nafsi fī shayʾin min ghayri rawiyyah* — "al-iqtiḥām is **the hurling of oneself into something without hesitation, without pausing to calculate.**" He goes further and gives you the desert image behind it: you say it of a horseman, *qaḥama-l-farasu fārisahu* — when the horse pitches its rider forward, flings him. The word lives in the world of cavalry, of charges, of bodies thrown into the danger before the mind has time to object.

Al-Baghawī adds the other face of it: *al-iqtiḥām: ad-dukhūlu fī-l-amri-sh-shadīd* — "al-iqtiḥām is **plunging into the severe thing, the difficult thing.**"

Let's sit with this.

This is the vocabulary of recklessness. Of nerve. Of the soldier who doesn't count the enemy before he charges. Of someone who sees the gap and **leaps** without letting fear do its arithmetic.

[PAUSE]

And now remember who is being described. The same man who, a few lines earlier, boasted *ahlaktu mālan lubadā* — "I destroyed wealth abundant." Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both tell us the tradition here: this man was willing to **hurl his wealth** into things — al-Qurṭubī says he claimed to have poured it out *fī ʿadāwati Muḥammad*, in enmity, in opposition. He had the capacity to throw himself into something. He had the *iqtiḥām* in him.

Do you see what Allah is doing? **He is not telling this man to find a courage he lacks. He is telling him he already has it — and he aimed it at the wrong mountain.** He was willing to plunge his fortune into vanity and opposition. He simply would not aim that same nerve at the climb that mattered.

Here is what is remarkable. Generosity, in our imagination, is gentle. Soft. The quiet writing of a check. But Allah refuses that picture. He reaches into the lexicon of the battlefield and says: the act of freeing a slave, of feeding a starving child — that takes the nerve of a cavalry charge. It is something you must **throw yourself into** before the nafs has time to whisper its objections.

Because the nafs *always* has objections ready. *You worked for this. You'll need it later. He'll only waste it. Someone else will give.* The whole machinery of hesitation — *ar-rawiyyah*, the calculating that Qurṭubī says *iqtiḥām* overrides — is precisely the machinery that stops generosity. And Allah names the cure in a single verb: don't deliberate your way up this mountain. **Charge it.**

One word, and the entire emotional register of charity is rewritten. Not soft. Brave.

[PAUSE]

And before we move on — the particle in front of the verb. **فَلَا — falā.** On its surface a flat negation: "he did *not*." But the early mufassirūn, including the reading preserved in al-Jalalayn, hear in it the sense of **فهلا — fa-hallā**: "*why* did he not? If only he had!" It is the tone of reproach laced with grief — the way a father says *you didn't even try* to a child who had everything he needed to succeed. So even the negation is not cold. It carries a kind of ache. *You had the nerve. The road was marked. Why didn't you charge it?*

### Element Two: ٱلْعَقَبَة — The Steep Pass, and the Question That Stops the World

Now the mountain itself. **ٱلْعَقَبَة — al-ʿaqabah**, from the root **ع-ق-ب**, *ʿ-q-b*.

An *ʿaqabah* is not just any hill. In Arabic it is the **steep mountain pass** — the hard ascent in the road, the place where the path tilts up sharply and the climb becomes labor. It is the part of the journey where the camel strains, where you feel the pull of the earth in your legs and lungs. The travelers of Arabia knew exactly what an *ʿaqabah* was: it was the section of the route you dreaded, the place that separated those who would arrive from those who would turn back.

And notice — it is **definite**. Not "*a* steep pass." **ٱلْعَقَبَة — *the* steep pass.** As if it is already known. As if there is one specific, famous, unavoidable ascent on the road of every human life, and you know which one I mean.

Al-Baghawī tells us plainly that this is a *mathal* — a parable Allah strikes. *Dhikru-l-ʿaqabati hāhunā mathalun ḍarabahu-Llāhu li-mujāhadati-n-nafsi wa-l-hawā wa-sh-shayṭān* — "the mention of *the steep pass* here is a likeness Allah coined for **the struggle against the nafs, against desire, and against Shayṭān** in the works of righteousness." He continues: Allah made the doer of good *ka-lladhī yatakallafu ṣuʿūda-l-ʿaqabah* — "like one undertaking the grueling climb of the mountain pass."

So the physical image — a steep, dreaded ascent on a desert road — is the stepping stone. The destination is something you cannot see and cannot photograph: **the interior climb against your own gravity.**

And here the classical readings pull along two threads that are worth holding at once, because both are true. Al-Baghawī points the pass *inward* — the *ʿaqabah* is the struggle against the nafs, desire, and Shayṭān in the moment of giving. But al-Muyassar reads it looking *forward*: he glosses *falā-qtaḥama-l-ʿaqabah* as *fa-hallā tajāwaza mashaqqata-l-ākhirah bi-infāqi mālih* — "why did he not surmount the *hardship of the Hereafter* by spending his wealth, so that he might come through safe?" On that reading the steep pass is the obstacle waiting on the far side of death, and generosity now is the only thing that carries you over it later. Notice these two are not rivals. The interior climb *is* the crossing of the Hereafter's pass — the mountain you refuse to charge today is the same mountain that stands between you and safety on the Day you cannot avoid. One pass, seen from two ends of time.

Let's sit with why a *mountain* and not, say, a long flat road.

Because the defining feature of a slope is that it fights you. On flat ground, stopping costs nothing — you simply stand still. On a slope, the moment you stop pushing, **you don't stay where you are. You slide back.** Gravity is never neutral on a climb. This is the genius of the image. The spiritual life is not a plateau where you can coast once you've arrived. It is an *ʿaqabah*. The pull is constant and downward, and the only way to hold your place is to keep climbing.

[PAUSE]

Now watch what Allah does between naming the pass and defining it. He inserts an entire ayah whose only job is to stop you:

**وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْعَقَبَةُ — *And what can make you know what the steep ascent is?***

This construction — *wa mā adrāka* — is one of the most charged formulas in the entire Qur'an. Allah uses it to introduce realities that are too immense for the human mind to reach on its own. *Wa mā adrāka ma-l-Ḥāqqah* — what will make you know the Reality? *Wa mā adrāka mā Saqar* — what will make you know what Hellfire is? It is the divine equivalent of a sudden silence before the most important sentence — the speaker stops, looks you in the eye, and says: *you have no idea what I am about to tell you. You cannot guess it. Brace yourself.*

And this is not a reader's impression laid over the text — it is what the grammarians themselves say the sentence is *doing*. Al-Jalalayn notes that this whole ayah is an *iʿtirāḍ*, a parenthetical clause deliberately wedged between the naming of the pass and its definition, and that its purpose is *taʿẓīman li-shaʾnihā* — "to magnify the matter of it." The interruption is not incidental. It is a rhetorical device whose entire function is to swell the thing that follows, to make you brace before a weight is set on you.

This formula is reserved for the Day of Judgment. For the Fire. For cosmic, world-ending realities.

And here Allah deploys that same earth-stopping formula… and then tells you it means **feeding a hungry child.**

Here's what's remarkable. The mismatch is the message. By wrapping an ordinary act of mercy in the grammar He reserves for the Day of Reckoning, Allah is telling you that **you have catastrophically underestimated what kindness is.** You thought it was small. You thought it was the leftover act, the thing you do after the real worship is done. And Allah stops the entire recitation to say: *you have no idea what this is. This is the* ʿaqabah*. This is the summit. This is the thing your whole life is measured against.*

The thing you called minor, He called the mountain.

### Element Three: رَقَبَة and the Rhyme of the Dust

Now we arrive at the definition, and I want you to listen to it twice — once for what each word means, and once for how the words *sound*, because in this passage the sound is doing theological work.

The first item: **فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ — *fakku raqabah*** — "the unfastening of a neck."

Stop on the noun. Allah does not say "free a slave," *ḥarrir ʿabdan*. He says free a **رَقَبَة — a neck**, from the root *r-q-b*. Why name the whole human being by the neck?

Because the neck is where the bondage *sits*. The collar, the yoke, the rope, the chain — they all close around the neck. The neck is the precise anatomical point where one human being's ownership of another becomes visible and physical. By naming the neck, Allah makes you see the *shackle*. He doesn't give you the abstraction "slavery"; he gives you the iron on the throat. And the verb — *fakk* — is the word for **unfastening, unlocking, prying open** a bond. You can almost hear the lock give way. *Fakku raqabah.* The freeing of a throat from the thing closed around it.

The second item: feeding, *iṭʿām*, on a particular kind of day — **يَوْمٍ ذِى مَسْغَبَةٍ — a day possessed of *masghabah*.** That word, from the root *s-gh-b*, does not mean ordinary hunger. It means **severe hunger, the gnawing famine-hunger** of a day when food is scarce and the body is in real distress. Not "someone who'd like a meal" — someone whose hunger is an emergency.

And whom do you feed on that day? Here Allah gets specific in a way that should unsettle us:

**يَتِيمًا ذَا مَقْرَبَةٍ — an orphan possessed of *maqrabah*** — an orphan of **near kinship**, root *q-r-b*. A relative. Your own blood. The child in your extended family that it would be *easiest to overlook precisely because they are close* — because closeness breeds the assumption that someone else, some other relative, will handle it.

**أَوْ مِسْكِينًا ذَا مَتْرَبَةٍ — or a destitute man possessed of *matrabah*.** And this final word — sit with it. **مَتْرَبَة, matrabah,** comes from the root *t-r-b*, the root of **تُرَاب — turāb — dust, dry earth, the soil of the ground.** A *miskīn dhā matrabah* is the poor man so stripped of everything that he is **cleaving to the dust** — nothing between his skin and the bare earth, no bed, no barrier, no buffer. Poverty reduced to its final image: a human being and the dirt, with nothing in between.

Now listen to them in sequence. Hear the *sound*:

ٱلْعَقَبَة — *al-ʿaqabah*
مَسْغَبَة — *masghabah*
مَقْرَبَة — *maqrabah*
مَتْرَبَة — *matrabah*

[PAUSE]

Did you feel it? The whole passage **rhymes on the same heavy, closing syllable** — *-abah, -abah, -abah*. Four times the tongue climbs over a hard consonant — the *q*, the *gh*, the *r*, the *t* — and lands on that same weighted ending. The rhyme is not decoration. The rhyme is the **ascent.** Each word is a step, and each step lands with the same labored thud, the way your foot lands the same heavy way over and over when you are climbing a slope that will not end.

And notice *which* realities Allah chose to carry that climbing, effortful sound: the **pass** itself (*ʿaqabah*), the **famine-hunger** (*masghabah*), the **kinship** that obligates you (*maqrabah*), and the **dust-poverty** of the destitute (*matrabah*). The mountain you climb and the human conditions you climb toward are bound together by sound itself. Allah made the suffering of the poor *rhyme with the summit of the spiritual life* — because in His speech they are the same ascent.

That is the linguistic construction. A word for a reckless charge. A definite mountain pass that is really the interior struggle. A neck that means a shackle, a dust-word for the destitute, and a rhyme that climbs.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why Goodness Is Always Uphill

Let me start where you already live.

You have noticed, at some point in your life, that bad habits require no maintenance and good ones require constant effort. Nobody has to schedule time to waste an evening. Nobody sets a reminder to scroll. But to pray on time, to read, to exercise, to give — these need alarms, accountability, discipline, willpower. Left completely alone, a human life does not drift *upward*. It drifts down. Toward ease. Toward the couch. Toward the self.

We all feel this. And we tend to feel ashamed of it, as though it were a personal defect — *why is it so hard for me specifically to be good?*

Now hear the ayah answer you. Allah did not call the path of righteousness a road. He called it an **ʿaqabah** — a steep pass. And al-Baghawī told us what the steepness *is*: the struggle against the nafs, against desire, against Shayṭān.

Do you understand what that reframes? **The difficulty is not a sign that something is wrong with you. The difficulty is the design.** Goodness is uphill *for everyone*, by construction, because it runs directly against the gravity of the self. The pull you feel when you reach for your wallet and something in you tightens — that is not your failure. That is the slope. That is what an *ʿaqabah* feels like from the inside.

And here is what the image gives you that a flat "path" never could. On a slope, you remember, stillness is not neutral. **Stop climbing and you slide.** This is why you cannot bank your spiritual life. You cannot say "I was generous five years ago, I'm set." There is no plateau on a mountain. The person who gave generously and then stopped is not standing at their old height — they are already sliding back down the face of the pass, because the gravity never switched off.

[PAUSE]

But let me hold up the paradox the ayah refuses to resolve, because it matters.

If goodness is so against our nature — why are we *built* to be able to do it at all? Why give us the nerve, the *iqtiḥām*, if the whole thing is uphill?

Because the climb is not a punishment. **The climb is the point.** A reward earned on a slope means something a reward handed out on flat ground never could. Allah did not want you to arrive somewhere. He wanted you to *become* the kind of being who can charge a mountain against your own gravity — and there is no way to become that except by climbing. The steepness is not Allah making it hard for you. The steepness is Allah making the achievement *real*.

So what changes tomorrow?

The next time you feel that internal resistance — the tightening before you give, the heaviness before you rise to pray, the voice that says *not now, not this much, not for them* — I want you to **rename it**. That feeling is not evidence that you are bad at this. That feeling is the *ʿaqabah*. It is the slope reporting itself. And the correct response to a slope is not to feel ashamed that it is steep. The correct response is the one word Allah already gave you: **iqtaḥim** — throw yourself at it before the calculating starts. The resistance you feel is not the wall. It is the doorway.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Summit Is a Human Being

Here is something we do with religion, all of us, quietly.

We prefer the vertical to the horizontal. We would rather have a relationship with God that runs straight up — me and my prayer mat, me and my fasting, me and my dhikr — than one that runs sideways, into the messy, demanding, ungrateful lives of other people. The vertical feels clean. It is between me and Allah. No one disappoints me. No one needs more than I want to give.

And so we build a private spirituality and call it the summit. We assume that the highest thing, the *ʿaqabah*, must be some peak of personal devotion.

Now read what Allah actually put on the summit.

He stopped the recitation. He used the formula reserved for Judgment Day and the Fire. He grabbed you by the shoulders — *do you even know what this mountain is?* And then He defined it, and **not one item on the list is private.**

*Fakku raqabah* — go unlock a chain from another human being's neck. *Iṭʿām* — go put food in a starving mouth on the day the hunger is worst. The orphan who is your own relative. The destitute man lying in the dust. **Every single item is another person.** The summit of the spiritual life, in Allah's own definition, is not found on a prayer mat in a quiet room. It is found **out there, in the streets, kneeling next to someone whose neck is shackled or whose stomach is empty.**

[PAUSE]

And notice the precision of *whom*. Allah did not say "feed the poor" as a category. He said the orphan *of near kinship* and the destitute man *clinging to the dust*. He named the two people we are most expert at not seeing.

The relative — *dhā maqrabah* — is the one we overlook *because* they are close. Closeness manufactures excuses: someone else in the family will handle it, it's not specifically my responsibility, we don't really talk. Allah closes that exit. He says the orphan with a claim on you *by blood* is part of the climb, and your familiarity with them is no excuse for stepping over them.

And the *miskīn dhā matrabah* — the man in the dust — is the one we overlook because his poverty is *complete*. We can look at someone slightly down on their luck and imagine helping. But the person reduced all the way to the dirt, with nothing — that total destitution makes us look away, because it feels bottomless, because what could my one gift even do. Allah names *that* man specifically. The dust-poor. The one your eye slides past.

So the summit is not just "other people." It is precisely **the other people you have trained yourself not to notice.** The climb runs straight toward the faces you avoid.

Here is the human paradox, and I won't rush to dissolve it: the way *up* to God is *down* — down to the level of the orphan, down to the dust where the destitute lie. You ascend the *ʿaqabah* by bending low. The neck you free is below you; the mouth you feed is lower still; the dust you reach into is the ground itself. The summit is reached by stooping. **Every other mountain in the world you climb by going up. This one you climb by going down to the lowest among you.**

[PAUSE]

And this connects to the most universal struggle there is. Every human being wants to matter. We want our lives to have weight, to have counted for something. We chase it through achievement, through wealth — like the man at the start of the surah, boasting about the fortune he destroyed, desperate to prove he mattered. And Allah's verdict on that whole enterprise was: *falā-qtaḥama-l-ʿaqabah.* For all his wealth, **he never climbed the one mountain that confers real weight.** He spent his life accumulating altitude on a hill that doesn't count.

The weight you are looking for is not in what you accumulate. It is in what you *unfasten* from someone else's neck, what you put into someone else's hand on the day they are starving.

So what changes tomorrow?

Find your *dhā maqrabah*. The relative you've been letting the rest of the family handle. The cousin, the aunt, the orphaned child at the edge of your own bloodline whose need you've filed under *someone else's responsibility*. And find your *dhā matrabah* — the one whose poverty is so complete you usually look away. This week, before the calculating starts, before the nafs lines up its reasons, **charge that mountain.** One neck. One meal. One person you'd trained yourself not to see. That — Allah is telling you, with the grammar He saves for the Day of Judgment — is the summit.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now step back and see how the passage is built, because the structure is the proof.

Allah constructs these six ayat as an ascent in three deliberate movements:

**First, the verb of the climb** — *falā-qtaḥama-l-ʿaqabah* — He has not *thrown himself* at the steep pass. The nerve, and the mountain.

**Then, the divine interruption** — *wa mā adrāka ma-l-ʿaqabah* — the recitation stops, the Day-of-Judgment formula descends, and you are told you have no idea what this mountain really is.

**Then, the definition** — and it pours out in that climbing rhyme: *raqabah… masghabah… maqrabah… matrabah.* Neck, famine, kinship, dust. Four heavy steps landing on the same labored beat, each one a human being.

The shape itself enacts the meaning: a charge, a held breath, and then the long climb toward the faces of the poor. The form *is* the ascent.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight reached from two directions.

From the **words**, we found that Allah described charity with a warrior's verb (*iqtaḥama* — the reckless plunge), set it on a steep pass (*ʿaqabah* — the slope that fights you), and bound the suffering of the poor to the summit of the spiritual life through rhyme itself.

From the **themes**, we found that goodness is uphill by design — the slope is the point — and that the summit Allah set before us is not a private peak of devotion but a human being in chains or in hunger, and specifically the human beings we've trained ourselves not to see.

They are one truth: **The hardest, highest, bravest thing a soul can do is to bend down to the lowest person in front of it — and it takes the nerve of a cavalry charge, because every gram of your nature is pulling the other way.**

### Questions to Carry

1. The man in the surah had the nerve to *hurl* his wealth into things that didn't matter. Where in my life do I already have *iqtiḥām* — boldness, capacity to throw myself at something — that I've simply aimed at the wrong mountain?

2. When I feel that tightening before I give, have I been reading it as my failure — when Allah calls it the slope itself? What changes if the resistance is the doorway and not the wall?

3. Who is my *dhā maqrabah* — the relative whose need I've quietly assigned to someone else *because* they're close?

4. Who is my *dhā matrabah* — the person whose poverty is so complete that I look away because I can't imagine my gift mattering?

5. Have I built a spiritual life that runs only vertically — me and my worship — to avoid the harder, horizontal climb toward people who would actually cost me something?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The summit Allah set before you is not a ritual but a human being in chains or in hunger, and He chose a warrior's word — *throw yourself at it* — to tell you that bending down to the lowest person in front of you is the bravest climb a soul will ever make.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to feel the resistance disappear before you move — you never will, because the slope is permanent. You only need to do what the word commands: move *before* the calculating starts. This week, find one neck to unfasten, one mouth to feed, one overlooked face to finally see — and charge it. Not when it feels easy. It will not feel easy. That heaviness is just the mountain telling you you've finally found the right one.

### A Du'a

O Allah, You who named the climb and marked the road, You gave us eyes and a tongue and two lips and showed us the two highways — let us not be among those who saw the steep pass and turned away. Place in us the nerve to throw ourselves at the ascent before our own souls talk us out of it. And open our eyes to the orphan of our own blood and the poor one clinging to the dust, that we might climb toward them, and climbing, rise to You. Āmīn.
