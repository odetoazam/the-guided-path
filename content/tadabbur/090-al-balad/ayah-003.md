# The Oath by the Parent and the Child

### وَوَالِدٍ وَمَا وَلَدَ

*wa wālidin wa mā walad*

**"And [by] a begetter — and that which he begot."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: When Allah Stops to Swear

Let me begin by asking you to slow down at something we usually rush past.

When Allah is about to tell us something — and He is the One who created our hearing, who created the very capacity in us to receive truth — He sometimes pauses first. He swears an oath. He says, in effect: *before I tell you this, let me hold up something for you to look at.*

Now think about what that means. The One whose single word brings galaxies into being does not *need* to swear. An oath, for us, is how the weak borrow strength — we swear by something greater than ourselves to make our word believed. But Allah has nothing greater than Himself. So when Allah swears, He is not borrowing credibility. He is doing something else entirely. **He is taking your face in His hands and turning it toward something He wants you to see.** An oath in the Quran is a pointing finger. It says: *look here. Look here, because what I am about to tell you depends on you having seen this first.*

So now come with me to the opening of Surah Al-Balad.

The surah begins in a city. Allah swears — *lā uqsimu bi-hādhā al-balad* — by this city, this *balad*, which the early community knew immediately to be Mecca. The sacred precinct. The place of the Ka'ba, the place of refuge, the place where even the hunted animal is safe and the broken branch is not to be cut. And then a second oath, stranger than the first: *wa anta ḥillun bi-hādhā al-balad* — "and you" — the Prophet ﷺ himself — are *ḥill* in this city. A resident of it. Dwelling in the very sanctuary that his own people would soon make unsafe for him.

So we have a place. A sacred geography. The camera is wide, taking in a whole city, its hills, its sanctuary, its history.

And then — ayah three — the camera does something unexpected. It leaves the city entirely. It stops looking at stone and street and sanctuary. And it comes to rest on **a parent. And a child.**

*wa wālidin wa mā walad.*

[PAUSE]

From the largest sacred space the Arabs knew — to the smallest, most ordinary unit of human life. From a city to a family. From geography to genealogy.

Why?

Here is the hook I want you to carry through everything that follows. In one breath, Allah is about to deliver one of the most important and most difficult truths in the entire Quran about what it means to be human — that you were made *fī kabad*, in toil, in struggle, in hardship woven straight into your design. That truth is coming in the very next ayah. But before He says it, He swears. And of all the things in creation He could have sworn by — the sun and its brightness, the night when it covers, the heaven and the One who built it, as He does elsewhere — here, to prepare us for the truth about human struggle, **He swears by a parent and what that parent brought forth.**

Why this oath, for this truth?

By the end of our time together, I want that question to have answered itself inside you. Not because I told you. Because you saw it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Oath Itself — What It Means That Allah Swears *By* You

Let me first put something simple in your hands, because the whole verse turns on it.

In Arabic, one of the ways an oath is formed is with a single letter — the letter *wāw*, the *wāw al-qasam*, the "*wāw* of oath." It attaches to the front of a word. You have already met it in this very surah: *lā uqsimu bi-hādhā al-balad*. And here in ayah three, that opening *wa* — *wa wālidin* — is that same oath particle, carried over. Allah is still swearing. The oath that began with the city now extends to the parent. "And by a begetter, and by what he begot."

Now hold an ordinary human experience next to this. When you swear by something — "by my mother," "by God," "by everything I hold dear" — you are reaching for the thing you treasure most, the thing whose name carries weight, and you are placing your truth on top of it. **You only ever swear by what is precious to you.** The oath is a measuring instrument. It tells the listener: *this — this thing I am swearing by — is something I hold in honor.*

So sit with this. [PAUSE]

When Allah swears by the city of Mecca, He is telling us: *this place has honor.* We understand that. It is the sanctuary.

But when Allah swears by *a parent and what that parent begot* — He is telling us that **a human father and a human child carry enough honor to stand inside a divine oath.**

Do you feel the weight of that? You — an ordinary person, born of an ordinary parent, who will perhaps bring forth ordinary children — you are made of the same stuff that Allah holds up and swears *by*. The chain of human generation is not beneath His notice. It is, in this verse, lifted up to the level of the sacred city itself.

And this is exactly where the classical scholars take us. Listen to what al-Qurtubī preserves. He gathers the early authorities — Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Ḥasan, Abū Ṣāliḥ — who read *wālid* as Ādam, peace be upon him, the first father, and *mā walad* as his offspring, all of humanity descended from him. And then al-Qurtubī adds the reason these voices give for why Allah would swear by them: *li-annahum aʿjabu mā khalaqa Allāhu* — **because they are the most wondrous of what Allah created upon the face of the earth.** Because of what is in them — *al-bayān wa al-nuṭq wa al-tadbīr* — the capacity for clear expression, for speech, for reasoned planning. And because among them are the prophets, and the callers to Allah.

Stay with that phrase: *the most wondrous of what Allah created.* This is not flattery. This is a sober statement about a creature that can speak, that can reason, that can choose, that can carry revelation. The oath by the parent and child is an oath by the single most astonishing thing on this earth — and that astonishing thing is *you*.

Now let me show you how the great commentators see this scene, each from a slightly different angle — not as competing guesses, but as several cameras trained on one reality.

Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī both read it plainly: *wālid* is Ādam, *mā walad* is his progeny. Al-Muyassar widens the lens to the same vista — *an oath by the father of humanity, Ādam, and what issued forth from him as offspring.* And al-Qurtubī, having given us the majority view, then preserves two further angles: one reading takes the oath to be by Ādam and the *righteous* among his descendants specifically — as if the truly human ones, the ones who lived up to speech and reason and guidance, are the heart of the oath. And another voice, Abū ʿImrān al-Jawnī, hears *wālid* as Ibrāhīm, peace be upon him, and *mā walad* as his progeny.

Do you see how these are not contradictions? They are layers. The first father — Ādam. The father of prophets — Ibrāhīm. The righteous who fulfill what it means to be human. **And beneath all of them, the simple universal truth: a parent, and what a parent brings forth.** Every camera angle is pointed at the same astonishing creature, sworn-by by its Maker.

One word reveals itself here, and I want to state it clearly before we move on: **the oath tells you that the human being — the plain fact of a parent and a child — is precious enough to Allah to be held up as a witness to His truth.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Wālid* Without the "The" — A Parent, Any Parent, Every Parent

Now look closely at the word itself: *wālidin*.

In Arabic there is a small sound at the end of a word that carries enormous meaning — the *tanwīn*, the doubled vowel-ending you hear as "-in" here: *wālid-in*. That ending, generally, marks a noun as **indefinite**. Not *the* begetter — but *a* begetter. One among many. Unbounded.

Let me give you the everyday version of this so it sits in your hand. If I say "*the* teacher told me," your mind reaches for a specific, known person — *that* teacher, the one we both have in mind. But if I say "*a* teacher told me," I have thrown the doors open. Could be any of a thousand teachers. The indefinite word is a wide-open field; the definite word is a single marked spot.

Now — Allah does not say *al-wālid*, "the begetter," pointing to one known father. He says *wālidin* — **a begetter.** Indefinite. Open. And this is the very thing that lets all those scholarly readings live together. Because the word is left open, it can be Ādam, and Ibrāhīm, and the righteous, and — yes — *your* father, and *you* as a father or mother, and the nameless parent in a village ten centuries from now.

Here is what is remarkable. By choosing the indefinite, Allah swears not by *one* parent but by **the phenomenon of parenthood itself** — stretched across every generation that has ever lived and ever will. The oath does not land on a single point in history. It pours out across the entire human chain. Every threshold where one life brought forth another life — Allah has swept all of them into His oath.

Notice what this does. It means there is no one listening to this verse who stands outside it. You were begotten by *a* begetter. You are *mā walad* — what someone brought forth. The indefiniteness reaches down through the centuries and includes you by name without ever naming you. **You are inside this oath.**

Let's sit with one more thing here. The word *wālid* comes from the root *w-l-d* — the root of bearing, of bringing forth, of birth. And we are about to see that this very root is the hinge of the entire verse.

### Element Three: *Mā Walad* — "What He Begot," and the Object Left Open

Look at the second half: *wa mā walad* — "and what he begot."

Two features here, and both are doing real work.

First: Allah says *mā* — "what" — not *man*, "who." In Arabic, *man* is the word you use for persons — "the one *who*." *Mā* is the word you reach for with things, with the general, with the unspecified — "*what*." And the offspring of a parent are *persons*. So we might have expected *man walad* — "and whoever he begot." Instead: *mā walad* — "and *what* he begot."

The classical commentators noticed this immediately. Al-Jalālayn states it directly: here *mā* carries the meaning of *man* — "*wa mā bi-maʿná man*." So at the level of plain meaning, "what he begot" simply means "those whom he begot," the offspring. That is settled; do not let anyone tell you the verse is talking about objects rather than children.

But — and here the door opens to reflection — *why this word?* Why does Allah, who chose every syllable, reach for *mā*, the word of the general and the unbounded, when *man* was available? What this invites us to consider is the sheer **scope** of it. *Man* would have pointed at known persons. *Mā* throws the net wide — *whatever* came forth, *all* that issued from this parent, across all of it, named and unnamed, the ones we know and the uncountable ones we will never know. The word of generality matches a reality too vast to count: the entire descending river of humanity from one source.

Second feature — and read this absence as carefully as any presence. The verb *walad* — "he begot" — is a verb that normally wants an object. *He begot — whom? what?* And Allah gives it an object that is itself the most open word in the language: *mā*. He does not say "and his three sons." He does not say "and the believers among them." He says: **and what he begot.** The object is left maximally, deliberately, gloriously unspecified.

Stay with me, because this is the quiet heart of the verse. When a parent brings a child into the world, *they do not know what they have begotten.* They hold an infant — soft, unformed, unwritten. Will this be a prophet or a tyrant? A caller to Allah or one who turns away? A life of ease or — and now we hear the next ayah leaning in — a life *fī kabad*, in toil? The parent does not know. **"What he begot" is unknown even to the one who begot it.** The open object is the unwritten future of every human life.

[PAUSE]

And now — feel the root. *w-l-d.* It is in *wālid*, the one who brings forth. It is in *walad*, the act of bringing forth. The begetter and the begetting share a single root, sounded twice in five short words: *wa **wālid**in wa mā **walad**.* The morphology itself enacts the cycle. **The one who was begotten becomes the one who begets.** *Wālid* and *walad* are the same root wearing two faces — the parent's face and the child's face — and every child wearing the second face will, in time, turn and wear the first. The endless turning of the generations is not just *described* by this verse. It is *built into the very sound of it.*

One clear statement before we cross over: **Allah swears by the parent and by the boundless, unknown, ever-renewing river of what the parent brings forth — and He builds the cycle of generation into the root-music of the words themselves.**

### The Bridge

So look at what these few words have shown us. An oath — and so a declaration of honor — placed upon the human being. An *indefinite* parent, so that the oath includes every parent and every child who has ever lived, including you. And an *open, unspecified* object — "what he begot" — so that the entire unwritten future of humanity stands inside the oath, while the shared root *w-l-d* turns the whole thing into a wheel of generations.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. Because Allah did not swear this oath to admire human beings. He swore it to *prepare* us. The very next words are: *laqad khalaqnā al-insāna fī kabad* — "We have surely created the human being in toil." The oath by the parent and the child is the runway. The truth about struggle is the landing. Let us go and meet it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Are Glorious and You Are in Struggle — Both Are True

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a feeling almost every honest adult knows. It is the quiet collision between *how much we sense we are worth* and *how hard it is to simply be alive.* On the one hand, you know — somewhere deep — that you are not nothing. You can think, love, build, repent, pray, forgive. You ache toward meaning. No animal does this. On the other hand, life is *hard*. You are tired. The bills, the body that fails, the relationships that fray, the prayers that feel dry, the same struggle returning the morning after you thought you had beaten it. And a thought creeps in: *if I am so worthless that everything is this much of a fight — maybe I am not worth much at all. Maybe the struggle is the verdict. Maybe being in this much pain means something has gone wrong with me.*

Hold that thought against this verse. Because the Quran refuses to let those two things — your glory and your struggle — be separated. Watch how close together Allah places them.

In ayah three, He swears by the human being. He lifts you up. *The most wondrous of what Allah created*, as al-Qurtubī's authorities said — speech, reason, prophets among you. You are sworn-*by*. You are honored.

And in the very next breath, ayah four: *laqad khalaqnā al-insāna fī kabad.* We created this same human — *in toil.* In *kabad*, a word the lexicographers tie to hardship, to strain, to the difficulty that presses on the chest. The same creature that Allah swears by is the creature He made in struggle.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what this does to the lie? The lie says: *if you were truly worth something, life would not be this hard.* But the Quran puts your worth and your hardship in adjacent verses, sealed together by a divine oath. **The struggle is not the evidence against your glory. The struggle sits right beside your glory, and both come from the same Hand.** You were not made in toil *instead of* being honored. You were made in toil *as* one of the most honored things in all creation.

This is the dignity the verse hands you. When you are in *kabad* — when life is grinding and the weight is on your chest — that is not a sign that you have fallen out of Allah's regard. It is the predicted, sworn-over, designed condition of the very creature He held up as wondrous. The toil is in the blueprint, not in the error log.

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes the *meaning* you assign to your hardship. The next time the weight comes — and it will — you have a choice about what story you tell. The lie's story: *something is wrong with me.* The Quran's story: *I am the creature made in kabad, the one my Maker swore by. This weight is the texture of being human, not the proof that I have failed.* That single re-reading will not remove the weight. But it will remove the *second* weight — the shame stacked on top of the struggle — and that second weight is often the heavier of the two.

### Theme Two: The First Labor — Why Allah Reaches for Birth to Teach Struggle

Now I want to take you to the most ordinary place in the world. A room where a child is being born.

In English we have a word that has always struck me. The word *labor.* It means work — hard, draining, sweat-on-the-brow work. And it means, also, *the bringing forth of a child.* The same word. As if the language itself stumbled onto a truth: that to bring forth a human life is *work*, the hardest and most painful work, and that the human enters the world *through* that work.

Hold that next to our verse. Allah is about to tell us that the human being is made *fī kabad* — in toil. And what does He swear by, one breath before? *Wa wālidin wa mā walad.* By the one who brings forth, and what is brought forth. **By the very act of birth.**

This is the load-bearing connection, and I do not think the verse survives without it. Allah does not swear by struggle in the abstract. He reaches for the single most concrete image of struggle that every human being has in common: the labor of birth. Think of it. The mother in her pain — the Quran elsewhere names it, *ḥamalat-hu ummuhu wahnan ʿalā wahn*, "his mother carried him in weakness upon weakness." The child forced through the narrow passage into cold air and first breath, the first cry that is itself a cry of distress. **Before you did anything, before you sinned or struggled or strove — your very entrance into existence was through someone's toil, and into your own.** The first thing that ever happened to you was *kabad*.

So now hear the sequence as one motion. *By the parent — and what the parent brought forth* — *We created the human in toil.* The oath is not decoration before the truth. The oath *is* the truth, shown before it is said. Allah points to the parent and child — the labor and the labored-into — and then names what the pointing meant: **this is a creature whose very arrival is struggle, all the way down.**

[PAUSE]

And here is where it opens into something almost unbearably tender. That same root, *w-l-d*, that we traced through the words — the parent who labored to bring you forth was *themselves* once *mā walad*, themselves brought forth through another's labor. And you, who were labored-into the world, will labor in your turn — whether to raise children, or to carry burdens, or simply to live a life that is, as promised, *fī kabad*. The wheel of *w-l-d* is a wheel of labor turning through every generation. **No one is born outside the toil. No one is born without someone laboring. This is the human inheritance, sworn over by God.**

Let me close this theme by giving the struggle back its meaning. If birth — the most painful passage — is also the doorway to *life*, to speech and reason and worship and the chance to know your Lord, then *kabad* is not the opposite of the gift. *Kabad is the shape the gift arrives in.* The labor and the life are not two things. The pain of the passage and the glory of the creature are bound together exactly as ayah three is bound to ayah four.

What does this change about tomorrow? It asks you to stop waiting for the toil to end before you begin to live. You were not promised a life *after* the struggle; you were sworn over as a creature made *in* the struggle. So the worship happens *in* the toil. The patience happens *in* the toil. The gratitude — astonishingly — happens *in* the toil. And when you next watch someone labor — a mother, a worker, a soul carrying something heavy — let it return you to this oath, and to the One who swore by it, and remember that you, too, were once *mā walad*, brought forth in someone's pain into a world that your Maker promised would be hard, and held you precious anyway.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look now at how the two roads we walked turn out to be one road approached from two directions.

The **linguistic journey** showed us: an oath that *honors* the human being; an indefinite *wālid* that sweeps every parent and child into that honor; an open object — *mā walad* — that holds the entire unknown future of humanity; and a single root, *w-l-d*, turning the cycle of generations into the very sound of the verse.

The **thematic depths** showed us: that your glory and your struggle are sealed together, verse against verse, by a divine oath; and that Allah reaches for the image of *birth* — the first labor — to teach you that toil is the shape your life arrives in.

These are the same insight. The oath *honors* the human being (linguistic) *precisely as* the verse prepares to call that human being one made in toil (thematic). The honor and the toil are not in tension; they are spoken in one breath, by One who swore an oath to bind them. **To be human is to be sworn-by and to be in struggle, at the same moment, by the same Hand.** The grammar and the meaning say it together.

### The Architecture

And see the shape of the whole opening now. Allah swears by a *place* — the sacred city (90:1). He swears by a *person* in that place — the Prophet ﷺ dwelling there (90:2). And then He swears by *generation itself* — a parent and what was brought forth (90:3). Place, then person, then the chain of all persons. The oath narrows from a city to a man to a single act of birth — and then, having gathered all of that, it releases its truth: *We created the human in toil* (90:4). The whole structure is a hand drawing back a bow, three deliberate pulls, before letting the arrow fly into the heart of what it means to be alive.

### Questions to Carry

- When my life is hard, which story do I tell myself — *something is wrong with me*, or *I am the creature made in kabad, the one my Lord swore by*?
- Have I been waiting for the struggle to end before I truly live and worship — when the struggle is the very ground I was made to live and worship in?
- I am *mā walad* — what someone brought forth, into someone's labor. Do I honor the toil I was born through? Do I honor the toil of those who labor now?
- If Allah swears by the human being as among the most wondrous of His creation — do I treat my own soul, and the souls around me, as worth a divine oath?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah swears by the unbroken chain of parent and child — the most ordinary, most labored, most wondrous thing on earth — to tell you, before He says it, that the struggle you were born into is not the verdict against your worth but the very design of being human.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what might shift. Not that your life gets easier — the verse does not promise that, and I will not promise what Allah did not. But that the *meaning* of the difficulty changes. You walk out of this verse knowing that your Maker looked at the parent and the child — looked at *you* — and held you up in an oath, and in the same breath told you the truth about your toil. You are not a mistake having a hard time. You are a wonder, made in struggle, sworn over by God. Let that be the ground you stand on the next time the weight comes.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who swore by the parent and by what the parent brought forth, You who created us in *kabad* and called us wondrous in the same breath — teach our hearts to read our struggle the way You wrote it: not as Your turning away, but as the shape of the life You gave. Let us not despise the toil You designed, nor forget the honor You placed upon us within it. And as we were once brought forth in another's labor into a world You promised would be hard — bring us forth at last, through all our toil, into the ease of Your nearness. *Āmīn.*
