# The Ground Beneath the Climb

## Introduction: A Mountain You Already Know

Let me put you somewhere before we open a single word.

You are standing at the foot of a steep mountain pass. Not a gentle slope — a *pass*, the kind that cuts between two heights, where the only way through is up, and up is hard. Your legs already ache looking at it. In Arabic this is called **al-ʿaqaba** — the steep ascent, the obstacle you have to break through to get to the other side.

For six ayat now, Surah Al-Balad has been standing you in front of this mountain.

[PAUSE]

The surah opened with Allah swearing an oath by the city — *lā uqsimu bi-hādhā al-balad* — by Mecca itself, the sacred ground. It reminded the human being that We created him *fī kabad* — in toil, in struggle, in hardship. Life itself is uphill. And then it asked a piercing question: does he think no one sees him? Does he think his wealth, piled up and consumed, will save him?

And then it named the climb.

> *Fa-laqtaḥama al-ʿaqaba.* — But he did not break through the steep pass.
> *Wa mā adrāka mā al-ʿaqaba?* — And what will make you understand what the steep pass even is?

Then Allah tells us what the mountain is made of. It is **fakku raqaba** — the freeing of a neck, the liberation of a human being held in bondage. It is **iṭʿāmun fī yawmin dhī masghaba** — feeding, on a day of biting hunger, an orphan who is family, a destitute person crushed into the dust of poverty.

These are not small deeds. These are the *hardest* deeds. To free a slave costs you wealth and standing. To feed others on the day *you yourself* are hungry costs you your own mouth. This is the summit. This is the steep pass that the human being, left to himself, will not climb.

[PAUSE]

So here is the moment we arrive at. The deeds have been named. The mountain has been described. You would think the next word is the reward. You would think Allah now says: *and whoever does this is saved.*

He does not.

Instead He says one word — *thumma*, "then" — and turns away from the deeds entirely. He turns to something underneath them. Something the climber must already *be* before the climb means anything at all.

**Why, after listing the very highest deeds a person can perform, does Allah pivot — and tell us about something else? What could possibly rank above freeing a slave and feeding the starving?**

Stay with that question. The answer is the whole ayah.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Small Word That Re-Ranks Everything — *Thumma* (ثُمَّ)

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah.

In English, when we want to put things in order, we usually only have one kind of "then." *I woke up, then I ate, then I left.* It means time. One thing after another on the clock.

Arabic is more precise. It has a word, *thumma*, that can mean "then" in time — but it carries a second, subtler power. Grammarians describe a use of *thumma* called **al-tartīb al-dhikrī** — ordering by *mention*, ordering by *rank* — not by the clock. In this use, *thumma* does not say "this happened later." It says: *now I am going to raise you to something higher, something weightier in the telling.*

Think of how you might say to someone: "He's a good worker. He's reliable. He shows up early. And **then** — above all that — he's honest." That last "then" is not about time. He didn't become honest *after* he became reliable. You are using "then" to *elevate*. To say: now I name the thing that matters most.

That is the *thumma* we are standing in front of.

[PAUSE]

Here is the ayah's opening:

> **ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟**
> *Thumma kāna mina alladhīna āmanū*
> *Then he was of those who believed.*

Now notice the problem this *thumma* solves — and it is a real problem the early commentators wrestled with directly.

If *thumma* here meant time — "then, *afterward*, he believed" — it would mean a person could free the slave, feed the orphan, climb the entire mountain of righteous deeds, *and only then* come to faith. And that the faith arriving last is what counts.

The classical commentators tell us: **that is not the meaning.** Al-Jalālayn states it plainly — *thumma* here is *li'l-tartīb al-dhikrī*, ordering by mention, not by time; the sense is that *at the time of the climbing*, he was already among those who believed. Al-Qurṭubī is even more direct: a person does not break through the steep pass by freeing a neck or feeding the hungry **until** he is among those who believe — because the condition for the acceptance of righteous deeds is faith. Faith arriving *after* the spending does not retroactively rescue the deed. The deed must be carried *on* faith, the whole way up.

So what is *thumma* doing? It is **re-ranking**.

Allah lists the towering deeds — and then uses *thumma* to say: *and above all of these, beneath all of these, holding all of these up — he was a believer.* The "then" does not push faith to the end of the timeline. It pushes faith to the *top of the ranking.*

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete, because it changes how you read your own life.

Imagine the most generous, most self-sacrificing person you know who has no faith in God. They give their wealth. They feed the poor. They free people from cruelty. By the standard of the deeds in ayat 13 to 16, they have climbed the mountain.

This one word, *thumma*, is the quiet, devastating answer to "is that enough?" Al-Qurṭubī reaches for the Quran's own words about the hypocrites: *nothing prevented their spending from being accepted except that they disbelieved in Allah and His Messenger.* The hands did the work. But there was no ground beneath the hands.

The deed is the building. **Faith is the bedrock.** And *thumma* is Allah lifting your eyes from the height of the building to the depth of what it is standing on.

What this single word reveals: **the climb is never the first thing. The ground you climb on is.**

## Element Two: He Did Not "Believe" — He "Was Among Those Who Believed" (كَانَ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ)

Now look at *how* Allah says he believed. Because He could have said it in one verb.

In Arabic, Allah could simply have said *thumma āmana* — "then he believed." Clean. Short. One man, one verb, one act of belief.

That is not what He said.

> **ثُمَّ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟**
> *Thumma kāna **mina alladhīna** āmanū*
> *Then he **was of those who** believed.*

He used the verb *kāna* — "he was" — and then the phrase *min alladhīna* — "of those who," "from among the ones who." Allah did not describe an isolated act. He described a **membership**. A belonging. The man is not standing alone on the mountain holding his private faith. He is *one of a people.* He is *from* a body.

Let me slow down on the verb *kāna* for a moment, because it is doing quiet work. *Kāna* is the verb of *being*, of established, settled existence. It is not the verb of a flash — *he believed once.* It is the verb of a *state* — *he was, and continued to be, a person of this kind.* Faith here is not an event he had. It is a category he belonged to.

And then *min alladhīna* — "of those who." This is the language of inclusion in a group. You are not the believer. You are *a* believer, one of *the* believers, a single thread in a fabric.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter so much that Allah chose the longer phrasing over the shorter one?

Here is what I want you to notice. Everything *before* this ayah was an individual, vertical act. *You* free the slave. *You* feed the orphan. It flows downward — from the one who has to the one who lacks. The giver gives; the receiver receives. These are real, towering deeds, but they are things one person does *to* or *for* another.

The moment we cross into this ayah, Allah does not say "and then this lone hero also believed." He says: *he was woven into a people.* The language itself stops being about a solitary climber and starts being about belonging to a body.

Hold that thread. Because in the very next words, the verb is going to turn — from something one person does, into something people can only do *to each other.*

What this phrase reveals: **faith in the Quran's grammar is not a private possession. It is a belonging. You are not saved as an individual climbing alone — you are saved as one of a people.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Verb That Cannot Be Done Alone — *Tawāṣaw* (تَوَاصَوْا)

This is the heart of the ayah. Let me teach you the grammar first, because once you see it, the ayah opens like a door.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — *forms* — and each form bends the basic meaning in a specific direction. There is a form, **Form VI** (the pattern *tafāʿala*), whose entire job is to express **reciprocity** — an action that goes *back and forth between people.* It is the form of mutual exchange.

You already know this shape if you know a few Arabic words. *Taʿāwana* — to cooperate, to help *one another.* *Tarāsala* — to correspond, to write back *and forth.* *Tashāwara* — to consult *one another.* The Form VI shape itself means: *this cannot be done by one person. It requires at least two, facing each other, passing something between them.*

Now hold that, and read:

> **وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِٱلصَّبْرِ وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِٱلْمَرْحَمَةِ**
> *wa **tawāṣaw** bi'ṣ-ṣabri wa **tawāṣaw** bi'l-marḥama*
> *and they **enjoined one another** to patience, and they **enjoined one another** to compassion.*

*Tawāṣaw* is from the root *waṣṣā* — to counsel, to urge, to enjoin earnestly, the way a dying person leaves a *waṣiyya*, a last and weighty instruction, to those they love. It is not casual advice. It is the urging of something that *matters*, passed deliberately from one heart to another.

And it is in Form VI. Reciprocal. *They enjoined **one another.***

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the shift?

The deeds before were vertical — the strong lifting the weak. *Tawāṣaw* is **horizontal**. It is shoulder to shoulder. It is not "he counseled them." It is not "they were counseled." It is *they counseled each other* — back and forth, mutually, each one both the giver and the receiver of the urging.

You cannot do *tawāṣaw* alone. By the very grammar of the word, it is impossible. A solitary believer on a mountaintop cannot *tawāṣā.* There is no one to turn to, no one to turn back. The word requires a *community.*

This is why the previous element mattered — *kāna mina alladhīna*, "he was of those who." Allah established the *people* first, and now He tells us what the people *do for each other.* The membership in element two and the reciprocity in element three are the same truth said twice.

[PAUSE — stay here]

Now notice something you could easily read past. Allah did not say *tawāṣaw bi'ṣ-ṣabri wa'l-marḥama* — "they enjoined one another to patience *and* compassion," joining the two under one verb. That would have been shorter, lighter, the natural economical phrasing.

Instead He **repeated the verb**:

*tawāṣaw bi'ṣ-ṣabr* — and again — *tawāṣaw bi'l-marḥama.*

He spent the word twice. In a Book where not a syllable is wasted, the repetition is a spotlight. It says: *these are two separate acts of mutual urging, each weighty enough to stand on its own.* Patience is its own discipline that the community must hand each other. Mercy is its own discipline that the community must hand each other. Neither is a footnote to the other. Each gets its own verb, its own breath, its own command.

And listen to the two things they pass between them — listen to the very *sound* of them.

**Aṣ-ṣabr.** The *ṣād*, the heavy, blocked, emphatic letter; the *bā*; the closing *rā*. The word is *hard.* It clenches. It holds. *Ṣabr* is the root of restraint, of binding the self, of holding firm under weight — Lisān al-ʿArab connects it to confinement, to *holding something back.* The word sounds like teeth set against a hard thing.

**Al-marḥama.** From *raḥima* — the root of *raḥma*, mercy; the same root as *raḥim*, the womb. The word *flows* — *mar-ḥa-ma* — soft consonants, open vowels, no clench. It sounds like opening. Like tenderness. Like the warmth a mother's body makes for a child it has not yet met.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has placed in your hands?

He gives the believing community **two opposite-feeling gifts** to pass between each other. *Ṣabr* — the hard, firm, clenched strength of endurance: *hold on, don't break, stay.* And *marḥama* — the soft, flowing, open warmth of mercy: *be gentle, be tender, have compassion.* One braces the bones. The other warms the heart. A community that only had *ṣabr* would be hard and cold — enduring, but merciless. A community that only had *marḥama* would be warm but weak — tender, but unable to hold under pressure.

The believer hands his brother *both.* Firmness for the self. Softness for others. The spine and the heart, passed back and forth, until the whole body can climb.

What these words reveal: **faith completes itself not in what you do for the weak below you, but in what you and your people hand to each other, side by side — the hard gift of endurance and the soft gift of mercy.**

## Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three small features have shown us, standing together.

*Thumma* lifted our eyes from the deeds to the ground beneath them — faith. *Kāna mina alladhīna* told us that faith is not a solitary possession but a belonging to a people. And *tawāṣaw*, repeated, in the form of reciprocity, told us what that people does: they hand each other, again and again, the hard strength to endure and the soft warmth to be merciful.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being is actually saved. About reality itself.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Foundation You Cannot See Is Holding Up Everything You Can

Let me begin where you already live.

You have walked past tall buildings your whole life. You have never once thought about their foundations. You admire the glass, the height, the way the tower catches the light. The foundation is underground. Invisible. Nobody photographs it. Nobody praises it. And yet — pull it out, and the entire gleaming tower is a pile of rubble in an afternoon.

We are wired to admire the *visible* part. The achievement. The deed you can point to. The tower.

[PAUSE]

This is exactly the trap the surah has been walking the human being toward — and *thumma* is where it springs the truth.

The deeds in ayat 13 to 16 are the tower. They are visible, magnificent, photographable. *Look — he freed a slave. Look — he fed the orphan on the day of hunger.* The world sees those. The world praises those. And it is *right* to praise them; they are the steep mountain pass, the real thing.

But then Allah says *thumma* — and points underground. To faith. To the foundation no one photographs.

Here is what the commentators were protecting when they insisted *thumma* is about rank, not time. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar — they converge on one truth from different angles, like cameras circling the same building: **these noble acts only benefit the one who does them when they rest on faith.** Al-Baghawī puts it simply: *Allah clarified that these acts of nearness only profit alongside belief.* The deed without the foundation is a tower built on sand. It looks identical from the outside. It will not stand on the day the ground is shaken.

[PAUSE]

Now turn this on yourself — gently, but honestly.

How much of your spiritual life is *tower* — visible, performable, the part others can see and praise? The prayer others watch. The charity others know about. The good name. And how much of it is *foundation* — the invisible reality of *why*? The actual faith in your chest when no one is looking, the conviction that you are doing this *for Allah* and not for the eyes of the city?

The terror and the mercy of this ayah is the same fact: **Allah is not impressed by the tower. He is examining the foundation.**

And here is the mercy in it — because it cuts the other way too. The believer whose tower is *small*, who could not free a slave because she had no wealth, who could not feed the orphan because she was the orphan — but who carried real faith, the foundation, all the way through her small and difficult life? *Thumma kāna mina alladhīna āmanū.* She was of those who believed. The foundation was sound. And Allah, in the very next ayah, calls such people *aṣḥāb al-maymana* — the companions of the right hand.

What this changes about tomorrow: before you measure your day by what you *did* — the visible tower — ask the *thumma* question first. *What was underneath it? Was I, in this, of those who believe? Was the ground sound?* Build the foundation in private, every day, where no camera reaches. It is the only part that holds.

## Theme Two: No One Climbs the Steep Pass Alone

Let me put you back at the foot of the mountain — the *ʿaqaba* — but this time, look around you.

We have a myth in our age. The myth of the self-made climber. The lone hero who, by sheer willpower, hauls himself up the mountain of his own betterment. We build entire industries on it: *you* can fix yourself, *you* can discipline yourself, *you* can transform yourself — alone, with the right mindset, by yourself, for yourself.

And then you try it. You decide, alone, to become more patient. To hold your tongue, to endure the hard thing, to not break. And for three days, by sheer will, you climb. And on the fourth day, alone, with no one beside you, no one urging you, no one to catch you when your grip slips — **you fall back down the mountain.**

You know this feeling. We all know this feeling. The private resolution that dies in private.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again, and hear what it refuses to say.

It does not say *he was patient.* It does not say *he was merciful.* It says **they enjoined one another** to patience. **They enjoined one another** to compassion. *Tawāṣaw.* The reciprocal form. The verb that, by its very grammar, cannot be performed by a person standing alone.

Allah is telling you something about the structure of the human being that your loneliness has been trying to teach you for years: **patience is not, finally, a solo discipline. Mercy is not a solo discipline. They live and die in community.**

Let me sit with *ṣabr* first. Why would patience need *other people*? Because patience is endurance over *time*, and over time the self erodes. The voice in your head that said "hold on" on day one goes quiet by day ten. And this is precisely where *tawāṣaw bi'ṣ-ṣabr* enters — *they urged one another to hold on.* When your own voice fails, your brother's voice carries you. When his fails, yours carries him. The grip that slips alone is held by the rope that ties you to others. You do not climb the *ʿaqaba* on your own two legs. You climb it roped to a people who, every time one of you weakens, *enjoin one another* — *hold on, stay, don't let go.*

[PAUSE]

And then — *tawāṣaw bi'l-marḥama.* They urged one another to mercy.

Here is the movement that should stop you. Why would a community of strivers, climbing the hard mountain of endurance, need to be *reminded to be merciful — to each other?*

Because *ṣabr* has a shadow. People who are hard on themselves, who endure and grit and discipline and *hold the line* — those very people grow hard on *everyone else.* The most disciplined person in the room is often the least forgiving. The one who pushed himself up the mountain by sheer endurance looks back down at the one still struggling at the bottom — and feels contempt, not mercy.

So Allah, in the same breath, with the same reciprocal verb, hands the climbing community the antidote to their own strength: *and they urged one another to compassion.* Stay soft. As you climb, stay soft toward the ones still climbing. Endure — *and have mercy.* Be firm with yourself — *and be gentle with your brother.* The womb-warmth, the *marḥama*, passed hand to hand alongside the iron of *ṣabr*, so the community does not become a brotherhood of the hard-hearted.

[PAUSE]

Feel the paradox the ayah is holding without resolving: you must be *hard* (ṣabr) and *soft* (marḥama) at once, and you cannot sustain either one alone. The hardness without the softness curdles into cruelty. The softness without the hardness dissolves into weakness. And both of them, left to a solitary self, simply do not last. The only place a human being can hold both, over a lifetime, is *roped to other believers who keep handing them back to him* — *tawāṣaw, tawāṣaw* — back and forth, patience and mercy, until the whole people reaches the top together.

This is the universal struggle and the universal answer. You were never meant to climb alone. The lone hero on the mountain is a myth, and it is a myth that leaves you broken at the bottom. The believer climbs roped to a people.

What this changes about tomorrow: stop trying to fix yourself alone. Find the people who will *tawāṣā* with you — who will urge you to endure when you are weak, and urge you to be gentle when you are growing hard — and *be* that person for them. Patience and mercy are not downloads you install in private. They are gifts you and your people hand each other, every single day, all the way up the pass.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Look at how the two journeys — through the words and through the themes — are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The linguistic journey showed us: *thumma* points beneath the deeds to the foundation of faith; *kāna mina alladhīna* makes that faith a belonging to a people, not a private act; and *tawāṣaw*, repeated and reciprocal, shows the people handing each other the hard gift of patience and the soft gift of mercy.

The thematic journey showed us the same architecture from the inside: the invisible foundation holds up the visible tower, and no one climbs the steep pass alone — endurance and mercy survive only in community, passed back and forth.

Now see how the ayah is *built*. It moves in a perfect descent-into-depth:

- **The deeds** (ayat before) — the visible tower.
- ***Thumma*** — the turn downward, to the ground.
- ***Kāna mina alladhīna āmanū*** — the foundation: faith, held as a belonging.
- ***Tawāṣaw bi'ṣ-ṣabr*** — the firm beam: endurance, handed across the community.
- ***Tawāṣaw bi'l-marḥama*** — the soft beam: mercy, handed across the community.

Faith is the bedrock. Patience is the structure that holds under weight. Mercy is the warmth that makes the structure a *home* and not a prison. And every piece after *thumma* is shared — belonged-to, handed back and forth — because a single believer cannot be a *people*, and it is the people, roped together, who break through the steep pass.

And then — *aṣḥāb al-maymana.* The companions of the right hand. Not he. *They.* They climbed together, so they are named together.

## Questions to Carry

1. When I measure a good day, do I count the tower — what I *did* — or do I ever inspect the foundation: *why* I did it, and whether faith was truly underneath it?
2. Is there anyone in my life I *tawāṣā* with — who urges me to endure when I weaken, and to soften when I harden? If not, what does my loneliness on the mountain cost me?
3. Has my discipline, my *ṣabr*, quietly curdled into contempt for those still struggling at the bottom? Where do I need to be handed *marḥama* — and to whom do I owe it?
4. What private resolutions have died in me precisely because I tried to keep them alone?
5. If faith is a *belonging* and not just a possession — am I living as *one of* a people, or as a solitary believer who thinks he can climb the pass by himself?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The steep climb of righteous deeds only counts when it rests on the hidden bedrock of faith and is lived out roped to a people who hand each other, again and again, the iron of patience and the warmth of mercy.

## The Closing Invitation

You are still standing at the foot of the *ʿaqaba.* You always will be — life is *kabad*, uphill, until the end. But this ayah has changed two things about the climb. First: check the ground before you admire the height. Build, in private, the faith that no one sees, because it is the only thing that will still be standing when everything shakes. And second: stop climbing alone. Find your people. Rope yourself to them. Hand them your patience and your mercy, and let them hand you theirs — *tawāṣaw, tawāṣaw* — until the day you are named, not alone, but among *aṣḥāb al-maymana*, the companions of the right.

## A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, lay the bedrock of true faith beneath every deed of ours, so that nothing we build is built on sand. Tie us to a people who urge us to endure when we are weak and to be merciful when we grow hard — and make us that mercy for them. And by the patience and the compassion we hand each other, carry us together through the steep pass, until You name us among the companions of the right.
