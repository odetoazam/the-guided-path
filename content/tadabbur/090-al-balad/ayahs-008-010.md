# The Two Eyes, the One Tongue, and the Uphill Road

### Sūrat al-Balad 8–10

> أَلَمْ نَجْعَل لَّهُۥ عَيْنَيْنِ • وَلِسَانًۭا وَشَفَتَيْنِ • وَهَدَيْنَٰهُ ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ
>
> *Alam najʿal lahu ʿaynayn • wa lisānan wa shafatayn • wa hadaynāhu al-najdayn*
>
> **"Have We not made for him two eyes, and a tongue and two lips, and shown him the two heights?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Man Who Thought No One Was Watching

Let me put you somewhere first. Before we touch a single word, I want you to feel the ground these verses are standing on.

You are in Makkah. Not the Makkah of pilgrims and minarets — the older, harder Makkah. A valley of black rock with almost nothing growing in it. A trading town where status is everything and the strong eat the weak as a matter of course. And in this town there is a certain kind of man. He has wealth. He has standing. And he has a way of talking about himself that the surah is about to quote directly.

Because just before our verses, this surah does something remarkable. It opens by swearing an oath — **by this city** — and then makes a flat, almost brutal statement about what a human being actually is:

**لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ فِى كَبَدٍ** — *"We certainly created the human being in kabad."* In toil. In hardship. In a kind of structural struggle, built into the design. Life as labor. Life as something you have to push against.

And then the surah shows us the boaster. The man who imagines **"none has power over me."** The man who says **"I have squandered wealth abundant"** — *ahlaktu mālan lubadā* — actually proud of how much he has burned through, as if that were a monument. And then the verse that stings: **"Does he think no one saw him?"** *Ayaḥsabu an lam yarahu aḥad.*

[PAUSE]

That is the moment our three verses arrive. A man, puffed up, convinced of his own self-sufficiency, convinced he is unobserved.

And Allah does not answer him with thunder. He does not begin by describing Hellfire. He answers him by turning the man around to face **his own body.**

*Did We not make for him two eyes?*

Think about how disarming that is. The man is boasting about his wealth, his power, his invisibility — and the response is to point quietly at his own face. *Where did you get the eyes you're looking at me with? Whose tongue is forming this boast? Who showed you the road you're walking down?*

Here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows. When Allah wanted to humble the proudest man in the valley, He did not reach for something far away and terrifying. He reached for the three or four most ordinary things about him — two eyes, a tongue, two lips, a road — things so familiar the man had stopped noticing them entirely.

Why those? And why in *that* order? And why does the very next verse, the moment after this list ends, accuse him of failing to **climb a mountain?**

Stay with me. There is an entire vision of what a human life is buried in eleven words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The Question That Is Actually an Answer

Let me teach you something small before we use it, because it changes how the whole passage sounds.

In every language, we have a trick where we ask a question that isn't really a question. If a friend has done something extraordinary for you and you turn to them and say, *"Didn't I always tell you I'd be there for you?"* — you are not asking. You are not waiting to find out the answer. You are using the *shape* of a question to make the other person say "yes" inside themselves. You are walking them into an admission they cannot escape.

Arabic has a formal name for this. The grammarians call it **istifhām al-taqrīr** — the interrogative of affirmation. A question whose only purpose is to force agreement. And this is precisely how the classical commentators read the opening here. Al-Jalālayn says it plainly: *«ألم نجعل» استفهام تقرير* — *"'Did We not make' is an interrogative of affirmation."* Meaning: *We certainly made.*

So look again at the opening particle: **أَلَمْ** — *a-lam.* It is two tiny pieces fused together. A questioning *hamza*, and the negation *lam*. Literally: *"Did We not…?"*

[PAUSE]

Now notice what this does to the boaster. He cannot argue. He has two eyes — he is, at this very second, using them. The question is constructed so that the only honest response his own body can give is *yes.* Yes, You made them. Yes, they are Yours. He came in boasting about what *he* had accumulated, and he is walked, word by word, into confessing what was *given* to him.

And here is the deeper thing. Allah does not say *"I made for you two eyes"* as a statement. A statement, you can hear and move past. A statement about a gift, the proud heart files away and forgets. But a *question* — a question demands a response. A question reaches into you and makes *you* supply the truth. The grammar itself refuses to let you stay passive. You have to answer. And the moment you answer "yes," you have admitted you are not self-made and not unobserved.

The man said, "Does he think no one saw him?" — and now Allah says, *"Did We not give you the very eyes you see with?"* The One who gave the seeing is, of course, the One who has been doing the watching.

What this single word **أَلَمْ** reveals: this is not a lecture being delivered *at* the human. It is a cross-examination, gentle and inescapable, in which the witness against the accused is his own face.

And al-Ṭabarī sharpens that point to a fine edge. When he explains *why* Allah gave this man eyes, he does not say "to see the world." He says the eyes were given so that *yubṣiru bihimā ḥujaja Allāhi ʿalayh* — "he sees with them the proofs of Allah against him." Read that again. The eyes were not issued as neutral windows. They were issued as instruments for perceiving God's evidence — the signs written across the valley, the sky, the caravan, his own aging hands. Which is exactly the double edge this reflection is named for: the very faculty given to him so that he might *see the truth* becomes, the moment he refuses to look, the faculty that *testifies against him.* He was handed eyes to read the case for God. If he will not read it, those same eyes are entered as the case against himself.

### Element Two — Two Eyes, One Tongue, Two Lips: The Arithmetic of the Body

Now I want to do something slow with the *numbers* in this list, because the numbers are not decoration. They are the message.

Let me give you the grammar first. Arabic, beautifully, has a separate form for *exactly two* of something. Not singular, not plural — a special **dual**, marked by the ending *-ayn* or *-ān*. English lost this; we just say "eyes" whether we mean two or two hundred. Arabic refuses that vagueness. When it wants two, it builds the two into the very shape of the word.

So listen to the list as Arabic hears it:

- **عَيْنَيْنِ** — *ʿaynayn* — "two eyes." Dual.
- **وَلِسَانًۭا** — *wa lisānan* — "and a tongue." **Singular.**
- **وَشَفَتَيْنِ** — *wa shafatayn* — "and two lips." Dual.

[PAUSE]

Do you hear it? Two — one — two. The list is symmetrical, and right in the center, flanked on both sides, sits a single thing. **One tongue.** And what surrounds it, what stands on guard on either side of it? **Two lips.**

Stay with this, because the early scholars certainly did. Of all the organs in this list, the tongue is the one given alone — and the one given a *double gate.* Two eyes, yes, but the eyes are simply two. The tongue is *one weapon behind two doors.*

Why would that be? What is the one faculty in the human being that needs guarding more than any other?

You already know. The tongue is the fastest part of you. It moves before the mind catches up. It can build a person in a sentence and destroy them in a sentence. It speaks the *shahāda* — the testimony of faith, the most weighty words a human can utter — and it speaks the lie, the slander, the boast. *"I have squandered wealth abundant"* — that boast a few verses earlier — came out of exactly this organ. The tongue is where faith becomes audible and where the ego becomes audible, and they come out of the *same door.*

This is precisely how al-Ṭabarī glosses the gift of the tongue: it was given *yuʿabbiru bihi ʿan nafsihi mā arād* — "so that he expresses by it, of himself, whatever he wills." Notice his phrasing. Not "so that he speaks the truth" — but "so that he expresses *of himself* whatever he wills." The tongue is the organ through which the self is *projected outward,* and it will carry whatever the self hands it, faith or boast alike. That is why, of everything on this list, it alone was walled.

And so Allah did not leave it open. He gave it two lips. A gate you can close. Notice the eyes were not given a second set of anything — they were simply made. But the tongue, the dangerous one, the one through which both your salvation and your ruin will pass — that one He hedged.

[PAUSE]

I want to draw out one more layer here, and this is where I'll mark the line between what the grammar *says* and what it *invites.* The grammar gives us, as documented fact: two eyes, one tongue, two lips — dual, singular, dual. That structure is simply there in the words. What the structure *invites us to consider* — and this is the reflection of many of those who pondered these verses — is that the body itself is teaching you a discipline. The eyes: take in much. The tongue: release little, and only through a gate. There is a reason you have two instruments of *receiving* the world bracketing one instrument of *projecting* yourself onto it. You were built to absorb more than you emit.

Now lift your eyes from the anatomy to the *sequence.* Look at the order Allah chose: first the eyes — the organ of **perceiving** the world. Then the tongue and lips — the organs of **declaring** yourself into the world. Perception, then expression. First you see; then you speak. The body is laid out in the exact order of the moral life: take the world in truthfully, then put yourself out truthfully.

And once Allah has given you the instrument to *see* and the instrument to *speak* — what is the one thing left? A place to walk. A road to take. Which is exactly where the next verse goes.

### Element Three — *Al-Najdayn*: Why the Road Is a Mountain

Here is the word I have been waiting to give you. The last word of our passage, and the one that re-reads everything before it.

[ayah:90:10] وَهَدَيْنَـٰهُ ٱلنَّجْدَيْنِ

Now what does *al-Muyassar* tell us *najdayn* means? *Sabīlay al-khayr wa al-sharr* — **"the two paths of good and evil."** That is the dominant, settled reading: Allah laid before the human the two ways, the way of obedience and the way of rebellion, and made both *clear* to him. Good. Hold that.

And notice how al-Muyassar phrases the *showing.* It does not say merely "He guided him," but *bayyannā lahu* — "We made it **clear** to him," We explained it, We laid it out in the open. Guidance here is disclosure; the two ways were exposed to view, not buried in a fog. Hold onto that word *clear* — it will matter in a moment.

One more thing, and it is quietly telling. When al-Muyassar reaches for a word to *explain* najdayn, it does not keep the word for height. It substitutes *sabīl* — "path." The commentator translates the elevation-word into path-language, because the meaning being pointed at really is the moral road: the way of good and the way of evil. But set that next to what the Qur'ān actually said. Allah did *not* use *sabīl.* He kept the climb-word. The interpreter tells you *where* the two ways lead; the revealed word insists on *what they cost.* And that gap — "path" in the explanation, "height" in the revelation — is the whole reason the next question is worth asking.

But now I have to ask you the question that the translation hides. If Allah simply meant "the two paths," there is a perfectly ordinary Arabic word for path — *sabīl*, *ṭarīq*. He used those words constantly elsewhere. So why, *here*, did He not say *al-sabīlayn*, "the two roads"? Why did He reach for this other word, **najd?**

[PAUSE]

Because *najd* does not mean "road." *Najd*, in the living Arabic of the desert, means **high ground.** Elevated, open, raised terrain. It is the word inside the place-name Najd — the highland plateau of Arabia. A *najd* is land that rises. Land you can see from far off because it stands up out of the plain — but also land you have to *climb* to reach. The root carries both senses at once: **conspicuous** and **uphill.**

Sit with what that does to the verse.

When Allah says He showed the human the *najdayn*, He is not describing two flat roads running off into the distance, where you might stroll down either one with equal ease. He is describing **two heights.** Two ascents. Two paths that both go *up.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the first thing the elevation gives us: a *najd* is conspicuous. You cannot miss high ground; it stands up against the sky. So the choice of word reinforces the meaning of "showed" — *hadaynāhu* — and reinforces, too, al-Muyassar's *bayyannā*, "We made clear." Allah did not hide the two ways in a fog and dare you to find them. He raised them up where they could not be missed. The good and the evil are not subtle. They stand up out of the landscape of your life like two hills you can see from anywhere. **You were never left guessing where they are.** That is mercy folded inside the word.

But the elevation gives us a second thing, and this is the one that detonates.

Both heights are *uphill.* And remember — *remember* — how this surah began. **"We created the human in kabad."** In toil. In a life that pushes back. Now we understand what kind of toil. The human was made for a terrain that *rises.* He was set down at the foot of two slopes and told: there is no flat road here. There is only the climb up toward good, or the climb up toward ruin. Both cost you. Both are *najd.*

[PAUSE]

And now — *now* — read the verse that comes immediately after our passage. The very next breath of the surah:

**فَلَا ٱقْتَحَمَ ٱلْعَقَبَةَ** — *"Yet he did not attempt the ʿaqaba"* — the steep mountain pass.

Do you see what Allah has done? He told you the two ways are *heights.* And then He says: the human, given the eyes to see the heights and shown plainly where they are — **did not climb.** He stood at the bottom of the slope that leads to good, looked up at it, and would not begin the ascent. The word *najdayn* in verse 10 was a setup. It planted the mountain in your mind precisely so that the failure to climb it in verse 11 would land with full weight.

What *al-najdayn* reveals: guidance is not a flat invitation. The good is not downhill. Allah owed you clarity — and He gave it; the heights are conspicuous, unmissable, raised against the sky. But He did not owe you the climb. **The climb is yours.** And the whole tragedy of the boaster is that he was given perfect sight of a mountain he refused to set one foot upon.

### Bridge

So look at what these eleven words have shown us as a construction. A question you cannot escape answering. A body laid out in the exact order of the moral life — perceive, then declare — with its most dangerous instrument set behind a double gate. And two ways, raised up so they cannot be missed, both of them uphill. And al-Ṭabarī closes his comment on this whole list with a single word that gathers all of it — *niʿmatan minnā bi-dhālika ʿalayh*, "as a favor from Us to him." Every item on it — the eyes that see God's proofs, the tongue that projects the self, the lips that guard it, the two clear heights — is entered into the record not as a neutral fact of anatomy but as a *gift,* a favor pressed into the hands of a man who was busy congratulating himself on what he thought he had made.
