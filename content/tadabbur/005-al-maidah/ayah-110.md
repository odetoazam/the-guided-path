# Al-Ma'idah 5:110 — The Phrase That Reclaims the Miracle

## Introduction: The Scene Before the Words

We are at the end of Al-Ma'idah. The surah has been about covenants — the covenant of the believers in its opening, the covenant of Bani Isra'il in its middle, and now, as it draws to its close, the covenant with the followers of Jesus.

And the surah does something almost no other passage in the Quran does. It stages a scene from the Day of Judgment — not the scene of believers entering paradise, not the scene of the deniers being interrogated, but a quiet, devastating scene between Allah and one of His messengers.

In 5:109, Allah gathers the messengers and asks them: *"What response did you receive?"* And they answer with a phrase of staggering humility — *"We have no knowledge. You are the Knower of all that is unseen."*

Then, out of all of them, the divine voice singles out one. Not Muhammad ﷺ. Not Musa. Not Ibrahim.

ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.

Why him? Al-Tabari notes that the grammatical anchor connecting this address to the prior scene — the *idh* in *"idh qāla Allāhu"* — ties the divine address to Jesus directly back to the question posed to the messengers in 5:109: what did your communities respond to you? The *idh* is not marking a separate scene. In al-Tabari's reading, it points to the content of the answer: *this* is how Jesus's community answered him. Of all the messengers, it is Jesus's community whose response is the most historically consequential, the most in need of clarification before all of creation. So the divine voice addresses it directly, at length, in the hearing of every witness assembled on that Day.

Because of all the messengers, his name had been used to construct a theology that he himself never taught. His miracles — real, undeniable, witnessed — had been read as proof of something the Quran insists they were never proof of.

So Allah, on the Day of Judgment, takes the very list of miracles that has been used as evidence of divinity, and recounts them Himself, in His own voice, with one small phrase repeated like a hammer striking the same nail again and again.

[PAUSE]

Listen for the phrase. It comes four times. *Bi-idhnī.* By My permission. By My leave.

Every miracle is real. Every miracle is preserved. Every miracle is claimed.

And every single one is qualified.

This is one of the most extraordinary acts of language in the entire Quran. The correction works at the level of **grammar** — not by denying the miracles or constructing a counter-argument, but by qualifying every verb at the moment of attribution, binding the proof to its disclaimer inside the same breath.

## Rebuilding the World

Before we open the words, we have to stand in the moment.

Imagine you are someone who, in this life, was given a gift no one else received. You held in your hands the power to make clay into a creature. You spoke to people while still nursing. You touched the eyes of someone who had never seen, and the world arrived in them. You stood beside a corpse and called it back into breath.

If you had not given that gift to yourself — and you knew it — what would your relationship be to those gifts as you carried them? Would you walk through your life as their owner? Or as their custodian?

Now imagine that long after you have left the world, people who never met you have built a temple around the gifts. They have used the gifts to claim things about you that you yourself never claimed. They have, in fact, made the gifts into a wall between themselves and the One who gave them.

What would you want, on the Day of Judgment, more than anything?

You would want — desperately — for the One who gave the gifts to **say so**. Out loud. In front of everyone.

This ayah is Allah, on the Day of Judgment, saying so.

Not to convince Jesus. Jesus already knows. The recounting is for everyone *else.*

Notice the verb that opens it: *"O Jesus, son of Mary — **udhkur** — **remember** My favor upon you."*

The mercy in that command is almost too much to hold. Allah doesn't ask Jesus to defend himself. He doesn't ask him to confess. He gives him the gift of being able to **recount** — to be present at the unmasking of his own legacy and to participate in the truth-telling.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We will sit with three things in this ayah.

The first is small enough to hold in two syllables.
The second is one word that almost made me stop reading the first time I noticed it.
The third is the architecture that holds the dual — Jesus and his mother — at the very entrance to the passage.

### Element One: *Bi-idhnī* — The Two-Syllable Theology

Let's start where the ayah's repetition forces us to start.

Allah lists the miracles. Shaping clay into the form of a bird. Breathing into it. The clay becoming a bird. Healing the one born blind. Healing the leper. Bringing forth the dead.

And after almost every one — *bi-idhnī.*

By My permission.

[PAUSE]

Before we go into what this phrase does, let's understand what *idhn* actually means in Arabic.

*Idhn* is from the root *adhina* — to give permission, to grant leave, to authorize. But it carries a flavor that the English word "permission" almost loses. In Arabic, *idhn* is what a host gives a guest at the door. It is what a sovereign gives a vassal before the vassal acts in the sovereign's name. It is the kind of permission where the act, once permitted, still belongs to the one who permitted it.

When a king's seal is given to a messenger, and the messenger acts with the king's seal — the action is the messenger's hand, but the **authority** is the king's. If you ask whose decree it is, the answer is not the messenger. The answer is the king.

That is *idhn.*

Now hear the verses. *"You shape from clay the form of a bird **bi-idhnī** — then you breathe into it, and it becomes a bird **bi-idhnī.** And you heal the one born blind and the leper **bi-idhnī.** And you bring forth the dead **bi-idhnī.**"*

Notice something. Allah does not say "*I* shape from clay through you." He doesn't strip Jesus of the verb. The verb is preserved — *takhluqu*, *tanfukhu*, *tubri'u*, *tukhriju* — *you* shape, *you* breathe, *you* heal, *you* bring forth. The action is given to Jesus.

But the **authorization** is reclaimed.

This is the precise theological move the Quran is making. The miracle is not denied. The agent of the action is not erased. What is corrected is the **source of authority**. The claim being deconstructed is not "Jesus performed miracles." The claim being deconstructed is "Jesus performed miracles **on his own authority.**"

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the heart of how the Quran handles Christology, and it is one of the most refined moments of theological language in the entire Book.

A blunt argument would have said: "Jesus did not heal the blind." That argument denies the data — and anyone who actually believed they had witnessed the miracle would have rejected the denial.

A different blunt argument would have said: "Allah healed the blind, Jesus had nothing to do with it." That argument erases Jesus from his own life — and any honest reader would have felt the historical record being violated.

The Quran does neither. The Quran preserves the entire account. Yes, you shaped the clay. Yes, you breathed. Yes, the bird flew. Yes, the blind saw. Yes, the leper was healed. Yes, the dead came forth.

And then, after every act, two syllables: *bi-idhnī.*

The miracle is preserved. The miracle's **theological meaning** is corrected.

### Why Repeat It Four Times?

Now ask the obvious question. Why does Allah repeat *bi-idhnī* four times? Once would have been grammatically sufficient. Twice would have been emphatic. Four times begins to feel like something else.

Listen to the rhythm.

*Takhluqu mina-ṭ-ṭīni ka-hay'ati-ṭ-ṭayri **bi-idhnī**, fa-tanfukhu fīhā fa-takūnu ṭayran **bi-idhnī**, wa-tubri'u-l-akmaha wa-l-abraṣa **bi-idhnī**, wa-idh tukhriju-l-mawtā **bi-idhnī**.*

It is not just emphasis. It is **rhythm**. Each miracle has its own *bi-idhnī*. The phrase is being attached to each act individually — as if Allah is going down the list, miracle by miracle, and stamping each one.

Imagine an heir going through a deceased parent's house, and on every painting, every piece of furniture, every book, taping a label that says "On loan." Not "the house is on loan" — that would be too general. Each item, separately, gets its own label. The repetition is not redundancy. It is **comprehensive attribution.**

Bird? Bi-idhnī. Healing the blind? Bi-idhnī. Healing the leper? Bi-idhnī. Raising the dead? Bi-idhnī.

The phrase becomes a kind of seal. A divine watermark on every miracle in the gospel.

And here's what's remarkable. Anyone who tries to lift one of these miracles out of the passage and use it as proof of Jesus's divinity has to lift the *bi-idhnī* with it. The Quran has bound the proof to the disclaimer.

You cannot quote the miracle without quoting the attribution. They are linguistically welded together.

[PAUSE]

This is what classical scholars meant when they said the Quran does not just contain truth — it contains truth **structured against distortion**. Built into the very form of the verse is a defense against its own misuse.

### Element Two: *Takhluqu* — The Boldest Word in the Verse

Now sit with what is, to me, the single most astonishing word choice in this ayah.

When Allah describes Jesus shaping the clay, He does not use a soft verb. He doesn't say *taṣnaʿu* — you make. He doesn't say *tashkulu* — you form. He doesn't say *tarsumu* — you sculpt.

He says **تَخْلُقُ — takhluqu.**

You **create.**

The same root — *kh-l-q* — that is used everywhere in the Quran for Allah's act of creation. *Khalaqa-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ.* He created the heavens and the earth. *Khalaqa-l-insāna min ṭīn.* He created the human being from clay.

This verb, in this root, is so powerful that classical theologians wrestled with whether any creature can ever truly be its subject. The Quran's general rule is that *al-Khāliq* is one of Allah's names, and the verb *khalaqa* — when it carries the full weight of bringing something into being from nothing — belongs to Him alone.

And yet here, in this ayah, Allah Himself uses the verb of Jesus.

*"And when you create from clay the form of a bird."*

Why?

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because the answer is not what you might first think.

A weaker text would have avoided the word *takhluqu* altogether. It would have used a safer verb so as not to risk any confusion about the boundary between creature and Creator. But the Quran does not retreat from the bold word. The Quran uses the word — and then **constrains** it.

Notice what comes immediately after. *Takhluqu mina-ṭ-ṭīni* — you create **from the clay**. Not *ex nihilo*. From a substance Allah already made.

And then the object: *ka-hay'ati-ṭ-ṭayri* — **the form of** a bird.

Here is where the precision of Quranic Arabic becomes almost surgical. Allah does not say *takhluqu ṭayran* — "you create a bird." He says *takhluqu... ka-hay'ati-ṭ-ṭayri* — "you shape **the form** of a bird."

*Hay'ah* in classical Arabic means the configuration, the appearance, the outward shape. It is the contour of a thing without its essence. A wax figure has the *hay'ah* of a person without being a person. A statue has the *hay'ah* of a king without being a king.

What Jesus shapes is the *hay'ah*. The form. The outward shape. The clay-figure of a bird.

What Jesus does **not** do — and what the verse will soon tell us — is bring the bird to life. He shapes the form. *Then* he breathes. And then — *fa-takūnu ṭayran bi-idhnī* — *it becomes a bird by My permission.*

[PAUSE]

Notice the precise division.

The shaping — Jesus.
The breathing — Jesus.
The **becoming a living bird** — Allah's permission.

The clay's *form* is in Jesus's hands. The clay's *life* is not.

This is why Allah was willing to use the word *takhluqu*. Because the verse immediately tells us what kind of *khalq* it is. It is the *khalq* of a sculptor. It is the shaping of a substance Allah made, into a form Allah designed, that becomes living through a power Allah alone holds.

Allah uses the boldest word. And then the verse itself, line by line, defines exactly what that word does and does not mean.

[PAUSE]

This is so important.

The Quran is not afraid of language that, in lesser hands, might seem to grant too much. The Quran trusts itself to use a strong word and then refine its scope. It is willing to honor the reality of what Jesus did — *yes, this was a kind of creating* — and at the same time absolutely refuse to let that reality slip into divinity.

If you read this ayah as a Christian who has been told that Jesus "co-creates" with the Father, the Quran is telling you something almost stunning: yes, there is a sense in which he creates. But the verse itself draws the lines. He shapes — Allah animates. He sculpts the *form* — Allah grants the *being*. He breathes — Allah permits the breath to become life.

The distinction, in other words, runs not outside the miracle but through its very center — along the seam between form and life, between shaping and animating, between the human hand and the divine permission. The Quran does not refuse the bold word. It illuminates what the word actually means.

### Element Three: *Wālidatik* — The One Through Whom He Came

We have been focused on the miracles. But the sentence opens before the miracles, with a dual that most readings pass through too quickly.

*"Udhkur niʿmatī ʿalayka wa-ʿalā wālidatik."*

Remember My favor upon you — and upon your **mother.**

Before the clay bird. Before the opened eyes. Before the raised dead. The sentence places two people in its opening breath: Jesus and his mother. The entire listing of miracles that follows is cast as a double gift — to him, and to the one through whom he came.

[PAUSE]

Why *wālida* and not *umm*?

Both words can mean mother in Arabic. But they are not identical. *Umm* is the relational title — the position, the role. *Wālida* is the active participle of *walada* — to give birth. She is not just "his mother" in the abstract sense. She is specifically *the one who bore him*, the one through whose body he entered the world. The word carries the act of birth within it. In a verse that will spend most of its length on what Jesus did, the opening gift is framed in this intimate, physical, relational way: you, and the one through whose body you were born.

Classical commentators noted that the favor to Maryam was specific and identifiable. Al-Muyassar states that among the gifts Allah recounts to Jesus is that He had chosen his mother above the women of all the worlds — *iṣṭafaytuhā ʿalā nisāʾi-l-ʿālamīn* — an election that echoes what the angels said to her in 3:42. And He had cleared her of the accusations that had been laid against her. Ibn Kathir identifies the precise instrument of that clearing: it was the infant Jesus himself, testifying to his mother's chastity from the cradle, who absolved her.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment.

The first miracle in the list — speech in the cradle — was not primarily a demonstration of Jesus's prophetic status. In the classical reading, it was for Maryam.

She had returned to her people carrying this child, unmarried, with no father to name. Whatever she tried to say, the words of a woman defending herself in that context would have fallen short. So Allah arranged something else entirely: before her son could walk, before he could be weaned, before he could do anything a human infant normally does, he spoke — in full sentences, with the articulation of a prophet — and the first thing he said was that he was a servant of Allah, and that he had been made blessed wherever he was.

The cradle speech was his first act of prophecy. It was also his first act of love.

This is what the dual at the opening of the verse is holding. The favor to Jesus and the favor to his mother are not two separate categories listed side by side. They are the same gift, seen from two angles. His miracles were given to him — and some of those miracles were, in their first occurrence, given *for her.*

### *Rūḥ al-Qudus* — The Support That Never Wavered

The ayah says Allah strengthened Jesus — *ayyadtuka* — with the Holy Spirit, *rūḥ al-qudus.*

Al-Muyassar identifies this as Jibreel, reading the verse as: He strengthened and assisted Jesus through Jibreel throughout his mission. The verb *ayyada* carries within it the sense of sustained, active fortification — not a one-time endowment but ongoing support. The same Jibreel who descended with revelation, who is described elsewhere in the Quran as *al-rūḥ al-amīn*, the trustworthy spirit, accompanied Jesus from his earliest moments through his entire prophetic life.

What is remarkable about how the verse frames this is that it is listed among the **favors** — the *niʿam*. Not conditions of prophethood. Not instruments taken for granted. Favors.

The presence of Jibreel with a prophet was itself a gift the prophet could not summon, could not command, could not manufacture. It was sent. It was given. And here, on the Day of Judgment, Allah counts it among the things He wants Jesus to remember: *I gave you this. I did not leave you to face your mission alone.*

[PAUSE]

Then comes the phrase: *tukallim al-nāsa fī-l-mahdi wa-kahlā* — you spoke to people in the cradle and in maturity.

The speech in the cradle has already been discussed. But notice the second term: *kahlan* — as a mature man. The word *kahl* designates a person past the flush of youth, settled into the full strength of their adult years. It is the age of established speech, established judgment, established message.

Al-Muyassar says that in his maturity, Jesus called people to Allah through *tawhid* — the divine unity that was revealed to him. Not to himself. Not to any compound conception of divinity. To the One.

This is the Quran quietly holding counter-testimony. The grown Jesus, the Jesus of his years of active prophecy, the Jesus who taught in the markets and the hillsides — his message, in the classical Islamic reading, was tawhid. In a single word — *kahlan* — and in al-Muyassar's gloss on what that mature speech contained, the ayah answers one of the most contested claims of Christology: ask what the mature Jesus actually said. He called people to Allah.

## Part 2: The Voice Behind Every Gift

The list of favors in this ayah is not random. Read it in sequence.

The miraculous birth. The support of Jibreel. Speech in the cradle — clearing his mother's name before she could clear it herself. Teaching him writing and wisdom and the Book and the Torah and the Gospel. The bird from clay. The healing of the one born blind and the leper. The raising of the dead. The restraining of Bani Isra'il from him when they sought to destroy him.

Each gift is larger than the previous one.

The birth is personal — a sign of divine power over the conditions of human beginning. The support of Jibreel is prophetic — the infrastructure of revelation throughout a life. The cradle speech is public — the first miracle witnessed by others, and tied to his mother's honor. The knowledge given is intellectual — writing, wisdom, every scripture. Then come the physical miracles, each one escalating: healing a condition present since birth, healing disease, reversing death itself. And then the largest gift of all, the one that required no action from Jesus — Allah directly restraining the collective force of Bani Isra'il from destroying him.

By the time we reach *kafafa ʿanka banī isrāʾīl*, the scale of divine involvement in Jesus's life has become staggering. His protection was personal, ongoing, active, and at its peak, an act of divine intervention against an entire nation.

[PAUSE]

Then the verse pivots to its final, devastating line.

*"When you came to them with the **bayyinat** — and those of them who disbelieved said: this is nothing but **siḥrun mubīn** — plain magic."*

Listen to the roots.

*Bayyinat* — from *b-y-n*, to be clear, to be manifest, to be unmistakable. Signs that admitted no ambiguity.

*Mubīn* — also from *b-y-n*. Plain. Evident. Clear.

They were shown what was *bayyinah* — clear. They called it *sihr mubīn* — clear sorcery. The same root, the same quality of clarity — inverted. The certainty of the divine sign was taken, and its unmistakability was turned into the language of rejection: *this is obviously, plainly, evidently, not what it claims to be.*

[PAUSE]

The ayah preserves this response. It does not suppress it or editorialize around it. It holds the disbelievers' words alongside the divine recounting — the bi-idhnī stamps, the favors, the gifts — and lets the two stand in the same verse.

On one side: every miracle, clearly given, clearly authorized, each one sealed *bi-idhnī*.

On the other side: the human refusal to receive it as given. *Siḥrun mubīn.* Plain magic.

Both are part of the record. Both are recounted on the Day of Judgment. Not to shame Jesus — he did not fail — but so that the full truth of what happened to him is visible to every witness present at that recounting.

The phrase *"fa-kafara alladhīna kafarū minhum"* — and those who disbelieved among them, disbelieved — has a deliberate repetition in the Arabic. Those who disbelieved, disbelieved. The structure is not accidental. It is saying: the disbelief was not a response to insufficient evidence. The bayyinat were plain. What the disbelievers brought to them was already a settled posture, and the signs were received into that posture, not the reverse.

## The Listing as an Act of Love

There is something I have been circling around and want to name directly.

Allah does not need to recount these gifts to Jesus. Jesus knows every miracle he was given better than any recounting could convey. He was there. He felt the clay in his hands. He heard the bird's first sound. He saw the eyes open that had never seen before. He watched a man who had been dead walk back toward his family.

The recounting is not information. It is something else.

In Arabic, when someone you love has been wronged, and you want to make their honor visible again, you speak of what they did and what was done to them — not to inform but to restore. To say, in public, in the presence of witnesses: *I know what happened. I saw it. And here it is, said properly, once, finally, in the form it deserves.*

This is Allah, on the Day of Judgment, doing that for Jesus.

Not just correcting the theological record. **Honoring him.**

*"Remember My favor upon you."* Not: account for yourself. Not: explain your followers. *Remember My favor.*

And then the full list. The birth. The mother. Jibreel at his side through his entire mission. The cradle speech that defended his mother before he could walk. The knowledge poured into him. The miracles, each one stamped. The protection from those who sought to destroy him. And finally, preserved alongside everything else — the moment his enemies called what he did plain sorcery.

All of it is held in the divine recounting. The gifts and the denials. The bi-idhnī and the sihr mubīn. Together.

Because that is what it actually was. That is the full truth of what happened to ʿĪsā ibn Maryam — prophet, messenger, servant of Allah, son of Maryam.

He was given everything. He held everything as custodian, not owner. And some of those who witnessed what he was given could only see sorcery.

[PAUSE]

And here, on the Day of Judgment, the One who gave the gifts says: *I remember. I counted. And I am saying it now, with everyone listening.*

## Closing

What do we take from this into our own lives?

Most of us will never hold clay in our hands and have it become a living thing. Most of us will never stand at the foot of a grave and call someone back. But every one of us has been given something.

Skills that arrived before we could account for them. Moments when a door opened that we did not open. Words that came out of us that surprised even us. Relationships that arrived like gifts we didn't earn. Understanding that dropped into us when we hadn't worked hard enough to deserve it.

The question the ayah quietly poses is not: *have you been given gifts?* The question is: what is your relationship to the authorization?

Do you hold what you have been given as the holder? Or as the custodian?

The *bi-idhnī* is not just a theological qualifier on Jesus's miracles. It is the grammar of every true gift. Every capacity, every insight, every ability to do something good in this world — *bi-idhnī*. By His permission. By a leave you did not grant yourself.

To live with that awareness is not false self-deprecation. It is accurate self-location. Jesus shaped the clay. He breathed into it. He did the thing. The actions were his. The authorization was not. And because he knew that — truly, completely knew it — when the gifts became distorted by others into a claim of divinity, they couldn't actually touch him. The distortion didn't require him to correct it. It required the One who gave the gifts to correct it.

[PAUSE]

And on the Day of Judgment, He did.

May Allah make us among those who receive what we are given with the clarity that it was given — not generated, not owned, not earned beyond what He enabled us to earn. May He give us the vision to see the *bi-idhnī* woven through every good thing we have ever touched. May He honor the mothers through whom our lives arrived. And may He, when our names are mentioned in whatever context they will be mentioned, say of us what He knows is true.
