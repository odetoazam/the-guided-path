# The Mercy of What Allah Did Not Legislate

## The Ayahs

[ayah:5:101] يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تَسْـَٔلُوا۟ عَنْ أَشْيَآءَ إِن تُبْدَ لَكُمْ تَسُؤْكُمْ وَإِن تَسْـَٔلُوا۟ عَنْهَا حِينَ يُنَزَّلُ ٱلْقُرْءَانُ تُبْدَ لَكُمْ عَفَا ٱللَّهُ عَنْهَا ۗ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

[ayah:5:102] قَدْ سَأَلَهَا قَوْمٌ مِّن قَبْلِكُمْ ثُمَّ أَصْبَحُوا۟ بِهَا كَـٰفِرِينَ

*Yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū lā tas'alū ʿan ashyā'a in tubda lakum tasu'kum wa-in tas'alū ʿanhā ḥīna yunazzalu l-Qur'ānu tubda lakum. ʿAfā Llāhu ʿanhā wa-Llāhu Ghafūrun Ḥalīm. Qad sa'alahā qawmun min qablikum thumma aṣbaḥū bihā kāfirīn.*

> O you who have believed, do not ask about things which, if shown to you, would distress you. And if you ask about them while the Qur'an is being sent down, they will be shown to you. Allah has pardoned that — and Allah is Forgiving, Forbearing. A people before you asked them; then they became, because of them, disbelievers.

---

## Rebuilding the World

Imagine the early Madinan community. They are not a people who have just stumbled onto Islam. They are people who have left tribes, abandoned commerce, buried family across battle lines, and tied their entire interior life to a slow drip of revelation arriving — sometimes weekly, sometimes after long silences — through a man they love.

They are also new. The architecture of the dīn is being built around them in real time. A verse arrives, and a hundred questions follow it. Is this for everyone or only the Prophet? Does it apply once or always? What about the edge case I'm thinking of? What about *my* situation?

Some of these questions are genuine. Some of them are anxiety wearing the costume of piety. And some — and this is the dangerous category — are the questions of a person who senses that more law would *trap* them, and so cannot resist asking, the way a tongue cannot stop returning to the loose tooth.

Picture this concretely. The hadith corpus records the moment that triggered this passage. The Prophet ﷺ stands among them and announces: *Hajj has been made obligatory upon you.* A man stands and asks: *Every year, O Messenger of Allah?* The Prophet is silent. The man asks again. Again, silence. He asks a third time. The Prophet ﷺ — and this matters — shows visible distress, and then says, *"If I had said yes, it would have been made obligatory upon you. And you would not have been able to bear it."*

[PAUSE]

Sit with what just happened. The questioner was not malicious. He may have thought he was being thorough. But his question was capable of *manufacturing the very weight he was anxious about*. The silence the Prophet was holding was the *space in which the obligation remained light*. The question — once asked while revelation was active — had the power to collapse that space.

This is the world this ayah arrives into. A community is being told, gently and seriously, that **the size of the dīn that lands on their shoulders is partly a function of what they do not ask**.

That is a strange and almost unbearable thing to learn. It cuts against everything we are taught about curiosity. And we have not even opened the verse yet.

**The hook**: There is a category of mercy in this dīn that is not given by what Allah said. It is given by what Allah *withheld saying*. And the verse is about to teach us that the silence is not absence. The silence is a gift. And it can be revoked.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### One: The deliberate vagueness of *ashyā'*

The word Allah uses for the things you should not ask about is **أَشْيَآءَ** — *ashyā'*. *Things.* Plural. Indefinite. Unspecified.

Now stay with this for a moment, because it is doing more than it looks.

In Arabic, when a writer wants to be specific, the language has every tool available. *Hādhā l-amr* — this matter. *Tilka l-masā'il* — those issues. *Al-aḥkām allatī* — the rulings which… The Qur'an deploys precision constantly. When Allah names the four invented sacred animals just two ayahs later — *baḥīrah, sā'ibah, waṣīlah, ḥām* — the precision is surgical.

So here, when Allah is telling believers what *not* to ask about, He has every option to specify. He could have said: do not ask about the rulings of pilgrimage. Do not ask about the categories of inheritance. Do not ask about the timing of fasts. He chose none of those.

He said *things*.

[PAUSE]

Here is what's remarkable. The vagueness is not laziness. The vagueness *is the ruling*. If Allah had specified, the prohibition would only protect believers from one set of questions. By leaving it as *things*, He has handed every believer a private mirror. Each of us, reading this, knows which questions in *our* life this ayah is naming. The ones we asked anyway and regretted. The ones we sense we should not ask but cannot stop circling. The "what if" we keep returning to in our prayer mat at night, looking for permission to escape.

The indefiniteness is the device that makes the verse continue to land fourteen centuries later. *Ashyā'* is your specific things. And mine. And the next person's. Allah did not legislate the list because **the list was never the point**. The disposition was.

This is what the linguistic tradition calls *al-tankīr li-l-tafkhīm* in one register, but here something quieter: *tankīr li-l-shumūl* — indefiniteness for inclusion. The unspecified does not narrow the verse. It widens it to include everyone who reads it.

### Two: The double *if*, and the trap inside it

Now look at the sentence's spine. It is built on two conditionals — two *if*s — sitting in parallel:

**إِن تُبْدَ لَكُمْ تَسُؤْكُمْ** — *if they are shown to you, they will distress you.*

**وَإِن تَسْـَٔلُوا۟ عَنْهَا حِينَ يُنَزَّلُ ٱلْقُرْءَانُ تُبْدَ لَكُمْ** — *and if you ask about them while the Qur'an is being sent down, they will be shown to you.*

Read them as a unit and you see what Allah has actually constructed. He has placed the listener inside a closed loop and named the door.

Loop: the things you are asking about, *should* they be revealed, will harm you. So far, mercy — the harm is being kept from you. Now the door: but if you ask while the Qur'an is being revealed, the answer will come. **You will pry open the very door whose closure was protecting you.**

The verse is not just saying "don't ask." It is showing us the mechanism. Your question is the key that unlocks the burden. The burden was withheld *for you*. Your insistence is what breaks the seal.

[PAUSE]

And notice the precise temporal phrase: **حِينَ يُنَزَّلُ ٱلْقُرْءَانُ** — *while the Qur'an is being sent down*. The verb here is *yunazzalu* — Form II passive, present-progressive. Form II of *nazala* carries gradual, repeated descent. The picture is not *the* Qur'an as a single completed book. It is revelation as an *active process*, still descending, still arriving.

This is a window verse. It is naming a specific moment in history when questions could pull rulings into the world that were not yet here. The window has, in one sense, closed — revelation is complete. But in a deeper sense, the principle has not closed. There is still a category of *things* whose answers are beyond the closed canon, things scholars must reason about, things our hearts must wrestle with — and the same dynamic applies. **Press too hard on what was not given, and the burden of an answer is still capable of arriving in your life.** Just not from the same source.

What this invites us to consider — and I am marking this clearly as interpretive extension, not linguistic fact — is that the *ḥīna yunazzalu* phrase is teaching us about *active windows of mercy* in any believer's life. There are seasons in which Allah is being merciful by holding something back from your knowing. The wrong question, asked with the wrong heart, in the wrong window, can collapse that mercy. Not because Allah is petty. Because **mercy that depends on a veil cannot survive the tearing of the veil**.

### Three: *ʿAfā Llāhu ʿanhā* — the silence is the pardon

Here is where the verse does something the casual reader will skip past, and which is the structural heart of the entire passage.

After warning against the question, Allah says:

**عَفَا ٱللَّهُ عَنْهَا**

*Allah has pardoned them.*

Read that one more time. Not: Allah has not yet ruled on them. Not: Allah is silent on these matters. **Allah has pardoned them.** The verb *ʿafā* is the same root used for the pardoning of sin. *Al-ʿAfū* is one of His Names — the One who effaces, who erases, who lifts.

[PAUSE]

Catch what just happened. Allah is using the language of *forgiveness* for things that **were never even legislated**. There was no command for you to violate. No prohibition for you to breach. The matter never entered the legal world at all. And yet Allah calls His withholding of a ruling on it a *pardon*.

This is one of the most theologically extraordinary moves in the Qur'an, and it is doing something specific. It is teaching us that **the absence of a ruling is not legal vacuum. It is divine generosity.** The space is not empty. The space is filled with mercy.

Imam al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir both pause on this and converge on the same image: Allah has *graced* you with a category of life that He chose not to bind. He did not forget those matters. He did not overlook them. He looked at them, considered the weight they would place on you, and *withheld the verse*. And He calls that withholding *ʿafw*.

Here is what this asks of us. The next time you find yourself uneasy about something the Qur'an does not address — the next time you feel that gnawing "but where is the verse on *this*?" — pause and recognize what Allah has just told you about that gap. **It is not a gap. It is a gift.** The thing you wanted spelled out is precisely the thing whose unspecification is your protection.

The classical scholars then differ on the exact referent of *ʿanhā* — about *what* exactly Allah pardoned. Al-Tabari leans toward: those specific question-categories that, had they been ruled on, would have constrained you. Ibn Kathir layers in: the matters Allah deliberately left silent in the law. Al-Jalalayn extends: every matter on which a ruling has not been revealed. They are not contradicting one another. They are three angles on the same generosity. **The dīn that Allah gave you is the dīn He intended you to carry. And the dīn He did not give is the load He intended to spare you.**

### Four: *Ghafūr Ḥalīm* — the closing seal

The closing pair of Names is doing precise work. **غَفُورٌ حَلِيمٌ** — Forgiving, Forbearing.

Notice He did not say *Ghafūr Raḥīm* — Forgiving, Merciful — which would have been the more common pairing. He chose *Ḥalīm*. Why?

*Ḥilm* in Arabic is a specific thing. It is not generic mercy. It is the deliberate withholding of a deserved response. It is the king who *could* punish, *could* answer, *could* respond — and instead, in measured restraint, holds back. *Ḥilm* is power that refuses to express itself the moment it is provoked.

Now apply that to the verse. You asked — not yet, but Allah is anticipating that you might. The pattern of unhelpful questions has just been named. *Ghafūr* covers what is past: the questioning that already happened gets forgiven. **And *Ḥalīm* covers what is coming**: the next time you reach for that question, Allah is going to be *forbearing* with you — not crashing the burden onto your head, even when you are walking toward it.

This pairing is the verse's quiet hand on your shoulder. It is saying: I am not lying in wait for your next intrusive question to punish you. I am holding back. I am giving you time. **But the warning that follows, in the next ayah, is what happens when *ḥilm* is exhausted.**

### Five: *Aṣbaḥū bihā kāfirīn* — the daybreak of disbelief

 *Qad sa'alahā qawmun min qablikum thumma aṣbaḥū bihā kāfirīn.*

A people before you asked them. Then they became, because of them, disbelievers.

Two words here are worth slowing down on.

**أَصْبَحُوا۟** — *aṣbaḥū*. From *ṣabāḥ* — morning. The verb literally means *they entered the morning as*. In Arabic, the verbs *aṣbaḥa, amsā, baita, ẓalla* are time-of-day verbs that describe a state of being in a particular moment. *Aṣbaḥa* says: they woke up *as*. They became *overnight*.

The image is precise and devastating. The classical commentators read it this way: a community asked, was answered, **and the morning after the answer, they were disbelievers**. The transition was not slow. It was a daybreak. The light came up and they were on the other side. The question was an evening act. The kufr was a morning state.

[PAUSE]

How does that happen? How does asking become disbelieving? Look at the second word: **بِهَا** — *bihā*, "by them" or "in them." The grammar of the *bā'* preposition here is doing two things at once that we have to feel separately and then hold together.

Read one: *bā' al-sababiyyah* — causal *bā'*. They became disbelievers *because of* the questions. The questions were the cause.

Read two: *bā' al-musāḥabah* — accompanying *bā'*. They became disbelievers *in* the answers — they rejected the very rulings their questions had pulled into the world.

The two readings are not in tension. They are the full picture. Their questioning *caused* the answer to descend, and then they *disbelieved in* the answer that descended. **Their own question was the door. The answer was the test. They failed the test their question manufactured.**

This is one of the most subtle warnings in the Qur'an about the inner life of the believer. There are doors in revelation that, if you open them yourself, you will not be able to walk through with faith intact. **The grace was in the door staying closed for you. Your own hand was the threat to that grace.**

[PAUSE]

Bridge to the themes: We have heard *ashyā'* widen the verse to include every reader's private list. We have seen the double conditional close into a trap whose key is the questioner's own tongue. We have learned that Allah's silence on a matter has a name — *ʿafw*, pardon. We have heard *Ghafūr Ḥalīm* extend a hand. And we have heard *aṣbaḥū bihā kāfirīn* warn us that this hand can be exhausted.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how revelation actually meets the human heart.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The mercy of what was withheld

Picture a different mercy than the one we usually picture.

We are trained to recognize Allah's mercy in what He *gives*. The food on the table. The healing that arrives. The verse that lands on the wound. The community we did not deserve. We say *Alhamdulillāh* for what came.

But there is another category of mercy, and most of us go through life unable to see it, because it leaves no fingerprint. It is the mercy of **what was withheld**. The friendship that was supposed to enter your life and didn't, because it would have unmade you. The job that you prayed for and lost, because the version of you that took it would not have survived. The knowledge you wanted and did not receive, because the burden of knowing would have crushed the version of you that asked.

Modern psychology has, almost by accident, started to name this. There is a body of research now on *information aversion* — the documented finding that, on certain matters, people who choose not to know live happier, more functional lives than people who insist on knowing. Genetic risk for incurable disease. The exact words said about you behind your back. The thing your spouse thought twenty years ago that they have, in the meantime, transcended. Sometimes the truth is correct, available, **and ruinous**.

A therapist will tell you, gently: not every truth is meant to live in you. Some truths are meant to live in the universe and not in your nervous system. Trauma psychology in particular has had to learn this the hard way — not every memory excavated heals. Some excavations re-injure.

What the secular world has had to discover through three generations of clinical observation, this verse said in three Arabic words. **ʿAfā Llāhu ʿanhā.** Allah pardoned that.

Now hear the depth of it. When the therapist tells you "you don't need to know that," they are giving you a permission. When *Allah* withholds a ruling and calls the withholding *pardon*, He is doing something more. He is **declaring the silence to be sacred**. Not a coincidence. Not an oversight. A deliberate act of love.

This rewires what we do with the gaps in revelation. The believer who looks at an unaddressed corner of life and says *but I want a ruling* is asking, without realizing it, for the lifting of a mercy. The mature believer learns to look at the gap and say: *Allah has pardoned that. I will live in the space He left for me.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change in your life tomorrow? Concretely:

You stop pressuring scholars for fatāwā on edge cases your life has not actually arrived at. The hypothetical you keep circling — the "what if my future spouse does X," the "what if I'm in this exact situation that has not yet happened" — recognize the loop. The version of revelation that would answer it is the version that would also bind it onto you.

You stop demanding that your dīn explain itself for every silence. *Why doesn't the Qur'an address this directly?* Sometimes the answer is: because it loves you enough not to.

You make peace with not-knowing as a category of worship. *Lā adrī* — "I do not know" — was, the early jurists said, half of knowledge. It is also half of trust. To say *I do not know, and that is fine, because Allah did not require me to know* is itself an act of *ʿibādah*.

### Theme Two: The questions we ask, and the people they make us

Now sit with the second movement, because this is the harder one.

The verse is not naming questions in general as the problem. The Qur'an is not anti-question. The same surah elsewhere encourages believers to ask the people of knowledge. Ibrahim *ʿalayhi al-salām* asked Allah how He gives life to the dead. The mother of Maryam asked. Mūsā asked. The Qur'an is full of holy questions.

So what kind of question is being warned against here?

The classical commentators converge on this: it is the question that **does not arise from the believer's actual life, but from a desire to test the limits of revelation, to extract certainty for its own sake, to hedge every possible scenario, or — most dangerously — to find a reason to disqualify oneself**.

Modern life has industrialized this category. Watch yourself for a week and you will see it everywhere. The relentless searching for *the answer* on a forum, on a podcast, in a thread, when no actual decision in your life depends on having that answer right now. The need to know the ruling on every scenario before any scenario has materialized. The mind that turns every minor act of worship into a tribunal of conditionals.

There is a name for this in the tradition: *waswās al-fiqh* — the Satanic whispering that disguises itself as legal precision. It looks like piety. It feels like thoroughness. It is in fact a slow paralysis. And the one who lives in it eventually arrives, exhausted, at one of two places: a kind of religious exhaustion that quietly disengages, or a brittle certainty that breaks the first time real life tests it.

This is what happened to the people of 5:102. The commentators read their story as a parable of this exact disposition. They asked. The answer came. They could not bear the answer. **And the disbelief was not despite their seriousness — it was caused by it**. They had taken seriousness one click past the point at which seriousness becomes its own enemy.

[PAUSE]

There is an analogy here that might land. Imagine someone you love. You have a relationship with them — there is trust, there is a long history of patience, there are unspoken understandings about what they will and won't tolerate, what they need from you and what they don't. The relationship is *alive*. It is held together by knowing, but also by the parts you have *agreed not to interrogate*. Every long marriage knows this. Every deep friendship knows this. **Some of the love is in the questions you have learned not to ask.**

Now imagine that same person, and imagine you decide: I am going to know everything. I am going to extract every truth. I am going to corner every silence. I am going to make them account for every ambiguity in their interior life from the past twenty years. *I am only being thorough.*

You will end the relationship. Not because the truth was fatal — though some of it might be. You will end it because **you have refused the form love took**. Love took the form of certain pardoned silences. You unmade the silences. You unmade the love.

This is what the verse is saying about your relationship with the dīn. There are silences in revelation that are part of the *shape* of your relationship with Allah. To pry every one of them open in the name of "I just want to be sure" is to refuse the form Allah's mercy took. **And mercy whose form is refused is mercy that withdraws.**

What does this change tomorrow?

You start to notice the texture of your own questions. Not all questions. *Your* questions. The ones you keep returning to. Ask honestly: is this question coming from a real decision in my life? From a desire to draw closer? Or is it coming from a need to corner the dīn into accounting for itself?

If it is the first — ask. The dīn is wide and the scholars are willing.

If it is the second — *practice the silence*. Sit with the not-knowing. Let it be uncomfortable. Recognize that the discomfort is the price of a mercy you would otherwise destroy. **And let *ʿafā Llāhu ʿanhā* become a verse you whisper to your own restless mind**.

There is a kind of believer this verse is trying to make. Not the credulous believer who never asks. Not the relentless interrogator who asks everything. Someone in between: a believer with a *trained tongue*, who has learned to feel the difference between a question that is a door to closeness and a question that is a door to exhaustion. The first kind, ask. The second kind — *Allah has pardoned that*.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture

Look at how the passage has been built.

It opens with an address: *yā ayyuhā lladhīna āmanū* — believers, I am speaking to you specifically.

It names a behavior: *lā tas'alū* — do not ask.

It explains the trap: *if shown, would distress you.*

It shows the mechanism: *if you ask while revelation is descending, you will be shown.*

It places — at the structural center — the silence: *ʿafā Llāhu ʿanhā*. Allah has pardoned that.

It seals with two Names that hold the past and the future: *Ghafūr Ḥalīm* — Forgiving, Forbearing.

And then, in 5:102, it shows the one historical example needed: a people who broke through that mercy, and woke up the next morning on the other side of faith.

The architecture is a **sanctuary verse**. The center of the passage is the silence. Everything before it builds toward it. Everything after it protects it. This is not a verse against questions. It is a verse that has placed Allah's silence in the middle of a structure and surrounded it with warnings to keep it intact.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes have been the same insight from two directions. The linguistic side: Allah used vagueness, used silence, used a verb of pardon for unspecified things, used a Name (*Ḥalīm*) of restrained power. The thematic side: there is a category of mercy that is given through withholding, and there is a category of question that is the great threat to that mercy. They are one truth.

### Questions to carry

- Where in your life right now are you pressing for a ruling on something that has not yet entered your life as an actual decision? What would change if you said: *Allah has pardoned that — I do not need to know yet*?

- What silence in revelation, in your own knowledge of the dīn, in your understanding of why something painful happened — what silence have you been treating as a problem to solve, that the verse is asking you to treat as a mercy to receive?

- Is there a question you keep returning to, in prayer or out of it, that you sense is not opening you but tightening you? What would it mean to put it down — not as defeat, but as worship?

- When you imagine the people of 5:102 — who asked, were answered, and woke as deniers — can you feel any pattern in your own restlessness that runs in that direction? Where might you be one or two questions away from a burden that would not survive contact with you?

- What if the maturity of your faith were measured not by what you know, but by what you have learned not to demand to know?

### The one sentence to carry

**The dīn that Allah gave you is the dīn He intended you to carry — and the dīn He did not give is the load He intended to spare you.**

### The closing invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, take one question — one — that you have been circling for weeks or months without resolution. Hold it for a moment. Then say to yourself, slowly: *ʿafā Llāhu ʿanhā*. Allah has pardoned that. Not as a way of refusing the question forever. As a way of releasing it for now. As an act of trust that the silence around it is not Allah's neglect but Allah's restraint. **Sleep, and see if you wake up lighter.**

That is what this verse is for. Not to shut the believer's mouth. To teach the believer's heart which questions are doors that open onto Allah, and which questions are doors that open onto the burden Allah was holding for you.

*Yā Ghafūr, Yā Ḥalīm — teach us the silence You placed in our dīn as Your mercy. Hold back from us the answers our hearts cannot yet carry. Let our questions be doors of nearness, not doors of burden. And keep us, by Your forbearance, from being people who ask, are answered, and wake on the wrong side of faith. Āmīn.*
