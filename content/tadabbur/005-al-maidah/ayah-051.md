# What *Awliyāʾ* Actually Means

## Surah Al-Ma'idah (5:51)

[ayah:5:51] ۞ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تَتَّخِذُوا۟ ٱلْيَهُودَ وَٱلنَّصَـٰرَىٰٓ أَوْلِيَآءَ ۘ بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍ ۚ وَمَن يَتَوَلَّهُم مِّنكُمْ فَإِنَّهُۥ مِنْهُمْ ۗ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ

*Yā ayyuha alladhīna āmanū, lā tattakhidhū al-yahūda wa-l-naṣārā awliyāʾ. Baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ. Wa man yatawallahum minkum fa-innahu minhum. Inna Allāha lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn.*

## Introduction

There is no ayah in the Quran more frequently quoted *out* of its grammar than this one.

It appears in the comment threads of every interfaith story. It appears in extremist recruitment material flattened to *do not befriend the disbelievers.* It appears in critical writings about Islam offered as proof that the Quran prohibits ordinary kindness across religious lines. It appears in the mouths of people who have read it once, in translation, and decided it means exactly what they wanted it to mean — for or against.

What it does *not* often appear inside of is the specific Arabic word at its center.

That word is *awliyāʾ.*

And the entire moral weight of the verse turns on whether that word has been read precisely or replaced — by the reader, the translator, the polemicist, the would-be defender — with a word it does not actually mean.

This reflection is therefore unusual. Most tadabbur opens by building the world the listener must inhabit. This one has to first **un-build** a world the listener has likely already inherited. We have to take a few minutes to remove an English word that has been quietly substituted into the Arabic — and then return to what the Arabic actually says.

One more thing before we begin, and it matters. This reflection is not going to tell you that the classical commentators all agreed and all agreed with the comfortable reading. They did not. Some of them read this ayah narrowly and occasionally; at least one of them read it broadly and sharply. Being honest about that disagreement is not a weakness in the defence of the verse. It *is* the defence of the verse — because a reading that has to hide part of the tradition to survive was never solid to begin with.

The ayah is precise. The flattening is the problem.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. *Walāʾ* — The Word That Has Been Lost in Translation

In English, *awliyāʾ* is usually translated as *friends,* sometimes as *allies.*

The first is genuinely wrong. The second is not wrong — it is thin, and it is the thinness that gets exploited.

Start with the honest half. When *al-Tafsīr al-Muyassar* paraphrases this very ayah in plain Arabic, it renders *awliyāʾ* as *ḥulafāʾ wa-anṣār* — **confederates and backers.** Those are the words for a treaty partner and a battlefield supporter. So anyone who tells you flatly that "allies is a mistranslation" is overcorrecting. It is in the classical gloss. But notice the phrase al-Muyassar attaches to it, the one that falls off in almost every quotation of this verse in either direction: *ʿalā ahl al-īmān* — confederates and backers **against the people of faith.** The gloss is not naming a category of person. It is naming a *direction of force.*

Now go under the gloss to the root. The Arabic root is و-ل-ي. The classical lexicons — *Lisān al-ʿArab*, *al-Mufradāt fī Gharīb al-Qurʾān* — gather around a single core meaning: **closeness so close that one party takes responsibility for the other.** The Arabic word for "to be next to" — *yalī* — comes from this root. *Mawlā* — guardian, master, freed slave's sponsor — comes from this root. *Walī al-amr* — the one with binding authority — comes from this root. The *walī* of an orphan is not their friend; he is the man into whose protective hand the orphan's life and property has been placed. The *walī* of a bride at her marriage contract is not her friend; he is the one whose consent is structurally required for the marriage to be valid.

Al-Tabari confirms the same centre of gravity when he reaches 5:55 four ayahs later. Commenting on *innamā waliyyukum Allāhu wa rasūluh*, he does not gloss *walī* with a word for affection. He glosses it with **nāṣir** — you have no *helper, no backer* except Allah, His Messenger, and the believers. Helper. Backer. Not confidant.

*Walāʾ,* then, is **structural protective intimacy.** It is the relationship of someone whose loyalty has been transferred so completely that another's interest is now defended *as if it were one's own.*

Now hear what the ayah is forbidding.

It is not forbidding friendship. Arabic has other vocabulary for that, and so does the Quran. *Ṣadāqa* exists — but it would overstate things to call it *the* Quranic word for friendship, because the Quran reaches more often for *khalīl*, the intimate companion: Ibrāhīm is named *khalīl Allāh* (4:125), and the sharpest regret voiced on the Day of Judgment is over a *khalīl* wrongly chosen (25:28). The point is not which word wins. The point is that the Quran *has* a vocabulary of companionship, uses it elsewhere, and does not use it here.

It is not forbidding *birr* either — and *birr* is a much wider word than "kindness." It carries righteousness, moral uprightness, and the dutiful goodness owed to those who have a claim on you; the same root names a child's duty to his parents. Allah commands it explicitly toward non-Muslims who are not at war with the believers in 60:8: *Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you on account of religion or expel you from your homes — that you should treat them with birr (tabarrūhum) and act justly toward them.* Al-Tabari, glossing that exact phrase, spells out what it obliges: that you deal equitably with them **by doing good to them (iḥsān) and by your *birr* toward them.** Al-Muyassar renders it more plainly still — that you honour them with good and act with justice toward them.

And it is not forbidding ordinary human warmth. There is a separate word for that too — *mawadda.*

What it *is* forbidding is the transfer of *walāʾ* — the transfer of *structural protective intimacy* — across a specific line. **Allies in the modern political sense, sponsors in the legal-protective sense, guardians in the binding-authority sense.** The kind of relationship where, when conflict comes, you are now defending their interest as if it were your own.

That is what *awliyāʾ* means. That is the unit being prohibited.

### 2. *Baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* — The Diagnostic That Names the Pattern

The ayah does not stop at the prohibition. It immediately gives the *reason*:

**بَعْضُهُمْ أَوْلِيَآءُ بَعْضٍۢ.**

*They are awliyāʾ of one another.*

Stay with this clause. It is the key that unlocks the whole sentence.

The clause names a **structure**, not a set of individuals. There exists, in the historical moment Allah is speaking into, a *walāʾ network* — a system of protective alliance. The believers are being told: **do not insert yourselves into that network as a third member.**

It is worth noticing how the classical paraphrase parses it, because it is more precise than most English renderings. *Al-Muyassar* reads the clause **distributively**: the Jews give *walāʾ* to one another, and likewise the Christians to one another — two internally-bonded blocs, not one merged alliance — and both, in that moment, converging in hostility toward you. That is a harder reading than the softened version usually offered, and it deserves to be stated rather than smoothed over. But look what al-Muyassar does with it next, because this is the sentence that never gets quoted: *wa-antum, ayyuhā al-muʾminūn, ajdaru bi-an yanṣura baʿḍukum baʿḍā* — **"and you, O believers, are more deserving that you should back one another."** The clause is not primarily an accusation aimed outward. It is a diagnosis of the believers' own failure to hold their own line: *they* close ranks; *you*, who have more reason to, do not.

Why does it matter? Because *walāʾ* is not portable across opposing structural commitments. You cannot, in a moment of active conflict, be inside the protective-alliance network of one side and the protective-alliance network of the other. The geometry does not work. *Baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* names the existing network. The prohibition is about not crossing into it.

Here is where the classical commentators genuinely diverge, and the divergence should be named plainly rather than papered over.

**Al-Tabari** (d. 310 AH) opens his commentary on this ayah with a sentence that is itself a lesson in method: *ikhtalafa ahl al-taʾwīl fī al-maʿniyy bi-hādhihi al-āya* — **"the people of interpretation differed over who is meant by this ayah."** He then reports the view that it concerns ʿUbāda ibn al-Ṣāmit, who declared himself free of his old *ḥilf* (treaty-alliance) with the Jewish tribe, and ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, who clung to his after their hostility to Allah and His Messenger had become open. But al-Tabari immediately adds a qualifier that the contextual reading is often quoted without: *wa-in kāna maʾmūran bi-dhālika jamīʿ al-muʾminīn* — **"even though all the believers are commanded by it."** So even the most occasion-anchored of the four does not treat the specific occasion as a shrinking device. The occasion explains the ruling; it does not confine it.

**Al-Qurṭubī** (d. 671 AH), in *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān*, reads the prohibition as bearing on *muwālāt* — the binding alliance relationship — and distinguishes it from ordinary social interaction.

**Ibn Kathīr** (d. 774 AH) does something different, and honesty requires saying so. He does not open with an occasion at all. He opens with a standing, general prohibition, framed around enmity as an ongoing condition: Allah forbids His believing servants from taking Jews and Christians as *awliyāʾ*, "because they are the enemies of Islam and its people." His register is polemical — he attaches an imprecation against them — and he supports the ruling not with a Madinan battle-report but with a transmitted administrative report from ʿUmar, recorded by Ibn Abī Ḥātim. This is a universalist reading, and it belongs to the tradition as much as al-Tabari's does. Any presentation of this ayah that quietly folds Ibn Kathīr into the contextual camp is not defending the Quran; it is editing the tafsir library.

**Al-Rāzī** (d. 606 AH), in *Mafātīḥ al-Ghayb*, frames it as a prohibition against any *walāʾ* that would compromise the structural integrity of the believing community under hostility.

So there is real *ikhtilāf* here, and it runs deeper than scope. It runs to method: whether the ayah's operative force tracks a described *situation* (active hostility, treaty-alliance, wartime backing) or tracks a named *category* (the two communities as such). The contextual reading — al-Tabari's occasion, al-Qurṭubī's *muwālāt*, al-Rāzī's structural-integrity framing — is the better-supported one and the one this reflection follows, for reasons the surah itself supplies and we will come to. But it is a reading held *against* another reading, not a fact everyone agreed on.

What the disagreement is *not* about is the semantic field of the word. Across all four, and across al-Muyassar's plain-Arabic paraphrase, *walāʾ* is glossed in the register of alliance, backing, protection and support — *ḥilf, ḥulafāʾ, anṣār, nuṣra, wilāya.* Not one of them glosses it with the vocabulary of ordinary companionship. When the standard English edition of Ibn Kathīr renders *awliyāʾ* as "friends" — as it does, in the section heading itself — that is a translator's choice made in English, sitting on top of an Arabic word that never carried it. And that single translation choice is the pipe through which most of the modern misreading, in both directions, actually travels.

### 3. *Yatawallahum* — The Verb of Internal Allegiance Transfer

Now hear the threat clause.

**وَمَن يَتَوَلَّهُم مِّنكُمْ فَإِنَّهُۥ مِنْهُمْ.**

*Whoever turns to them in walāʾ is of them.*

A note on the morphology, because it is routinely garbled. *Yatawallā* is **Form V of the root و-ل-ي** — the very same three letters as *awliyāʾ.* There is no separate root ت-و-ل-ي; the *tāʾ* at the front is the Form V prefix, not a radical. This is not pedantry. It is the whole argument in miniature: the noun the ayah forbids taking and the verb the ayah warns against *are the same word*, one nominal and one verbal. Whatever *awliyāʾ* means in the first clause, *yatawallā* is the act of doing it in the third.

Form V in Arabic is the reflexive — the form of *taking on a state for oneself.* *Tawallā* is not just *to ally with*; it is **to make someone one's own walī, internally and bindingly.** It is the act of placing one's protective allegiance in another's hands.

And the consequence is severe: *fa-innahu minhum* — *he is of them.*

This is the line that gets quoted with the most heat. **It is also the line where the precision of the prior linguistic work matters most.** Allah is not saying that anyone who is friends with a Christian becomes a Christian. He is saying that anyone who **transfers their protective allegiance** — their structural identity-bearing loyalty — across the line, has crossed the line. *Walāʾ* is identity-defining. You become what you give your *walāʾ* to. That is not a metaphor. It is the structural logic of *walāʾ* in classical Arabic. Al-Muyassar states the consequence in exactly juridical terms: whoever gives them *walāʾ* becomes counted among them, *wa-ḥukmuhu ḥukmuhum* — **his ruling is their ruling.** It is a statement about which side of a line a person now stands on, not about the contents of his heart.

If you want to see what the forbidden act actually looked like in practice, the Quran itself supplies a worked example — and it is not a friendship. In 60:1, the same prohibition appears with the same verb, and Ibn Kathīr identifies its occasion: the case of Ḥāṭib ibn Abī Baltaʿah, an early emigrant and a veteran of Badr, who was not of Quraysh but whose children and property remained back in Mecca. The ayah describes the offence as *tulqūna ilayhim bi-l-mawadda* — conveying *mawadda* to them — and al-Muyassar spells out concretely what that conveying consisted of: **passing them news of the Messenger and of the Muslims.** That is the paradigm case. Not affection. Not a shared meal. The transfer of information to a party in an active state of war. When the Quran wants to show you what crossing the *walāʾ* line looks like, it shows you intelligence reaching an enemy — from a man of unquestioned sincerity and battlefield history, which is precisely why the case is instructive.

This is also why the same word, in this surah a few ayahs later, is used to describe the *only* legitimate *walāʾ* the believer is permitted to take: **innamā waliyyukum Allāhu wa rasūluhu wa-lladhīna āmanū** (5:55). *Your walī is only Allah, His Messenger, and the believers.* The ayah we are reading is the negative shape of that positive instruction. The believer's *walāʾ* — the deepest layer of protective allegiance — is reserved. It is not, in classical fiqh, distributable across the line of structural opposition.

But again — note what is *not* being said. The Quran does not prohibit *muʿāmala* (transactional dealing). It does not prohibit *birr* — righteousness and dutiful goodness — which 60:8 explicitly commands. It does not prohibit *qisṭ* (fairness). It does not prohibit *mawadda* (affection) toward those not at war; indeed 60:7, commenting on which Ibn Kathīr glosses *mawadda* as **"affection after animosity, tenderness after coldness, coming together after parting,"** holds out the express hope that Allah will place exactly that between the believers and those they currently hold as enemies. Nor does it prohibit friendship as such. It reserves one specific category — the category of structural identity-bearing protective allegiance — and tells the believer: keep this one inside.

Sit with the sequencing in Al-Mumtaḥanah for a moment. The same short surah forbids *walāʾ* with an enemy at war (60:1), anticipates *mawadda* with that very enemy (60:7), and commands *birr* and *qisṭ* toward everyone not fighting you (60:8). If *walāʾ* and *mawadda* meant the same thing, that surah would contradict itself inside nine ayahs. They do not. That is the proof, and it is internal to the Quran.

### 4. The Closing — *Allāha lā yahdī al-qawma al-ẓālimīn*

The ayah closes with a divine name and a moral category.

**إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ.**

*Indeed, Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.*

Notice what word closes this ayah. *Ẓālimīn.* Not *kāfirīn.* Not *fāsiqīn.* **Ẓulm** — the displacement of something from its rightful place.

This is grammatically significant. Allah is not saying that those who misplace their *walāʾ* are unbelievers. He is saying that **what they have done is misplace something — they have moved a thing from its rightful place to a wrong place.** *Walāʾ* belongs in one location; they have put it in another. That is the precise definition of *ẓulm.*

And what He says is not that He has cursed them. He says He **does not guide them** — meaning He withdraws the guidance that would correct the misplacement. Al-Muyassar's paraphrase catches the register exactly: *inna Allāha lā yuwaffiqu al-ẓālimīn* — **Allah does not grant *tawfīq*** to them, does not grant the enabling success that makes a person's steps land where they should. Not a curse. A withheld alignment. The displacement of *walāʾ* misroutes the heart's compass; once that happens, the navigation goes wrong, and Allah does not interfere with that gone-wrongness until the misplacement is corrected.

This is not vindictive. It is mechanical. *Walāʾ* misplaced is the heart's compass misaligned. A misaligned compass cannot be guided unless first corrected. The ayah closes with the natural physics of this: misplaced *walāʾ* is a self-perpetuating wrongness, and divine guidance flows around it rather than through it, until the displacement is undone.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depth

### What This Means for Us

We live in a moment in which this ayah is misused in two opposite directions.

**One direction is extremist.** The ayah is flattened to *do not befriend non-Muslims*, ripped from its grammar, divorced from 60:8 and 5:5 and the seerah's overflowing record of the Prophet ﷺ in close, warm, contractual, and deeply protective relationships with non-Muslim individuals — Christian, Jewish, polytheist, treaty-bound. This flattening produces a Muslim who treats the colleague at work, the neighbor across the street, the parent of the kid in his daughter's class, as someone to be held at distance because *Allah said.* This is a misreading. It is also a *ẓulm* — it displaces the ayah from its place and puts it where it does not belong. The ayah's closing line lands on this flattening as much as it lands on its original target.

And it is refuted from inside this very surah. Forty-three ayahs earlier, Al-Ma'idah has already said: *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū kūnū qawwāmīna lillāhi shuhadāʾa bi-l-qisṭ, wa lā yajrimannakum shanaʾānu qawmin ʿalā allā taʿdilū* (5:8) — do not let hatred of a people carry you into injustice. Al-Tabari's gloss on that ayah is worth hearing beside 5:51, because he uses the *same word*: be upright for Allah, bearing witness with justice, **"toward your *awliyāʾ* and toward your enemies alike"** — do not exceed the limits set for you in dealing with your enemies because of their enmity, and do not fall short of them in dealing with your own because of their alliance with you. Al-Muyassar puts it in one line: *iʿdilū bayna al-aʿdāʾ wa-l-aḥbāb ʿalā darajatin sawāʾ* — **be just between enemies and loved ones at exactly the same level.** The surah that restricts where your *walāʾ* may go is the same surah that forbids letting that restriction bend your justice by a degree.

**The other direction is critical.** The ayah is quoted as proof that the Quran prohibits ordinary social relationships across religious lines and is therefore inherently hostile to pluralism. This too is a misreading. Anyone who has read the surrounding ayahs — in this very surah, 5:5: *and the food of those who were given the Book is permitted to you, and your food is permitted to them, and chaste women from among the believers and chaste women from among those who were given the Book before you* — is reading a text that explicitly authorizes *eating with* and *marrying into* the People of the Book. The same surah. Within forty ayahs of each other. **No coherent reading of the prohibition in 5:51 can extend it to the social warmth and contractual integration that 5:5 explicitly permits.** A verse cannot forbid you a category of person the same surah lets you marry.

But the honest version of this response has to concede something first. A critic who quotes Ibn Kathīr's opening line at you is quoting him accurately. He did write it, and his register is hard. The answer is not to pretend the sentence is not there. The answer is to place it: it is one reading inside a tradition that argued with itself, written by a man in the fourteenth Christian century in a specific political world; it stands beside al-Tabari's explicit *ikhtalafa ahl al-taʾwīl* and al-Qurṭubī's careful narrowing to *muwālāt*; and none of it can override 5:5 and 5:8 in the same surah. A tradition that records its own disagreements in the commentary itself is not a tradition that hides behind consensus. Pointing at the disagreement is not an embarrassment. It is the receipt.

What both flattenings share is **a refusal to read the word *walāʾ* in Arabic.** They both substitute an English word — *friends* — into an Arabic slot that does not contain it.

What is asked of the serious reader is therefore simple, and humbling. **Read the actual word.** *Walāʾ* is structural protective allegiance. The ayah forbids transferring that one specific category across a line that, in the historical moment of revelation, was the line of active conflict. The classical commentators apply that prohibition with care, with ikhtilāf about both scope and method, with explicit attention to context. The ayah is a fiqh-laden instruction, not a slogan.

And the fiqh is precise enough to allow — and the rest of the Qur'an commands — kindness, fairness, marriage, shared meals, neighborly affection, business partnership, and the full range of human relationships that any decent life requires.

What it reserves is one thing: **the deepest layer of protective allegiance, the layer that defines who you are when conflict comes.** That layer, the believer is told, is reserved for Allah, His Messenger, and the community of those who have given themselves to Him.

### What to Carry from This

If you have ever felt the ayah sit awkwardly in your chest — as if it were demanding a coldness from you that you knew, in the deeper places of your conscience, was not what God could be asking — that conscience was reading correctly. The ayah does not ask coldness. It asks **clarity about where the deepest layer of your allegiance lives.** Outside that one reserved category, the warmth is not only permitted; it is commanded.

If you have ever felt the ayah weaponized — by a stranger online, by a hostile commentator, by a flattening voice trying to convince you Islam is something it is not — you now have the linguistic ground to stand on. *Walāʾ* is not friendship. The ayah is precise. The flattening is the violence.

And remember al-Muyassar's overlooked half-sentence: *you are more deserving that you should back one another.* The clause read as an accusation about others was, in its own commentary tradition, first heard as a summons to the believers about themselves.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Distillation

The whole ayah, in one sentence:

**The deepest layer of a believer's protective allegiance is reserved — not because warmth across the line is forbidden, but because that one inner allegiance is what makes the believer a believer at all.**

### Questions to Carry

What word have you been substituting for *awliyāʾ* when you read this ayah — *friends, allies, partners* — and how does the actual Arabic change what is being asked?

Where in your own life is *walāʾ* — the deepest, structurally identity-bearing layer — actually resting? On Allah and His Messenger, or quietly elsewhere?

When you have heard this ayah quoted hostilely — by a flattener or by a critic — were you able to respond from inside the Arabic, or did you only have the translation to defend?

How does 5:5 (eating with, marrying among) sit alongside 5:51 in your understanding now? And how does 5:8 — justice unbent by hatred — sit alongside both?

Al-Muyassar heard *baʿḍuhum awliyāʾu baʿḍ* and immediately asked the believers why they were not backing one another. When you hear this ayah, whose failure do you find yourself examining first?

### The Closing Invitation

There is an honesty this ayah asks for, and it is harder than the slogan version. **It asks the believer to know precisely where the inner core of his loyalty lives — and to be unembarrassedly warm to everyone outside that core.** Both halves are required. Either alone fails.

May Allah place the deepest layer where it belongs, and free the rest of the soul to love widely.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who are *al-Walī* — the truest *walī* — anchor the deepest layer of our allegiance in You. Free us from misreadings that make us cold where You command warmth, and from misreadings that hollow out the inner allegiance that makes us Yours. Teach us the precision of Your words, and protect us from those who would wield them in flattened form, in either direction.

Āmīn.
