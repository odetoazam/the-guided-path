# The Forgotten Portion: When a Community Is Punished by Its Own Discord

## Surah 5 — Al-Ma'idah, Ayah 14

> وَمِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا نَصَـٰرَىٰٓ أَخَذْنَا مِيثَـٰقَهُمْ فَنَسُوا۟ حَظًّۭا مِّمَّا ذُكِّرُوا۟ بِهِۦ فَأَغْرَيْنَا بَيْنَهُمُ ٱلْعَدَاوَةَ وَٱلْبَغْضَآءَ إِلَىٰ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ ۚ وَسَوْفَ يُنَبِّئُهُمُ ٱللَّهُ بِمَا كَانُوا۟ يَصْنَعُونَ

*Wa mina lladhīna qālū innā naṣārā akhadhnā mīthāqahum fa-nasū ḥaẓẓan mimmā dhukkirū bihi fa-aghraynā baynahumu l-ʿadāwata wal-baghḍāʾa ilā yawmi l-qiyāmah, wa sawfa yunabbiʾuhumu llāhu bimā kānū yaṣnaʿūn.*

> "And from those who said, 'We are Christians' — We took their covenant. But they forgot a portion of what they were reminded of. So We stirred up among them enmity and hatred until the Day of Resurrection. And Allah will inform them of what they used to craft."

---

## Introduction — A Diptych About Forgetting

We are in the middle stretch of Surah Al-Ma'idah, where Allah is opening one covenant after another like a forensic file. The previous ayah, 5:13, named the cracks that opened in the covenant with Banī Isrā'īl — hardness of heart, distortion of words from their places, treachery that the Prophet ﷺ would keep on discovering.

Now the camera turns. Same architecture, new community.

Picture the original audience. Madinah, mid-revelation. The believers have just heard the indictment against one community — and it landed, perhaps, with that small comfort an indictment of someone else can give. *That happened to them, not us.* And then, before the comfort has time to settle, Allah moves on. *And from those who said, 'We are Christians'...*

The community changes. The pattern doesn't.

This is one of the most sobering moments in the whole surah. Because if you read 5:13 and 5:14 side by side, you find a phrase that appears in both. Word for word. The same phrase. Said about two different peoples.

> *fa-nasū ḥaẓẓan mimmā dhukkirū bih*
> "They forgot a portion of what they were reminded of."

The same diagnosis. Twice in two ayahs. About two different covenants, separated by centuries.

[PAUSE]

But here's what's strange. The diagnosis is the same — and the consequence Allah names is **completely different**.

For Banī Isrā'īl: hearts made hard, ongoing treachery.
For the community of Jesus: enmity and hatred **among themselves**. Until the Day of Resurrection.

Why the difference? Why does the same sin produce a different wound in a different community?

And there's a second question waiting underneath. Allah did not say "they forgot the religion." He did not say "they abandoned the message." He said *they forgot a portion*. **A piece.** Just a share of it.

How can forgetting a piece of something you still have — most of it — be so catastrophic that the consequence reaches all the way to the Day of Resurrection?

Let's sit with this together.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: حَظًّۭا — The Indefinite Portion

Begin with a word so small it can be missed: **ḥaẓẓan**. *A portion. A share. A lot.*

In the lexicons, ح-ظ-ظ (ḥ-ẓ-ẓ) carries the sense of *one's assigned share* — your portion, your fortune, your allotment. When the Arabs spoke of someone's *ḥaẓẓ*, they meant the piece that was theirs by entitlement. The slice on your plate. The inheritance reserved for your name.

So when Allah says they forgot **ḥaẓẓan** of what they were reminded of, He is not saying they forgot some abstract content. He is saying they lost their **share**. The piece that had been entrusted to them — assigned to them — they let it slip from their hands.

But notice something even more careful. The word is **indefinite**. *Ḥaẓẓan* — not *al-ḥaẓẓ*. Not "the portion." A portion. Some portion. A share — but Allah does not specify which.

Before we go deeper, let's teach the grammar. In Arabic, when you want to point at a specific thing your audience can identify, you use the definite article *al-* — *al-kitāb* (the book), *al-ḥaẓẓ* (the portion). When you leave a noun indefinite — *kitāb* (a book), *ḥaẓẓ* (some portion) — one of the things you can be doing is **leaving the identity unspecified on purpose**. The vagueness is doing work. You are saying: *some piece — and I'm not going to tell you which*.

Now sit with this in the ayah.

Allah is naming a community that broke its covenant — and refusing to name what piece they dropped.

[PAUSE]

Why?

Here is what this invites us to consider. If Allah had said *they forgot **the** portion* — defined, identified — the listener would file it away. *Ah, that part. The Christians forgot that one specific thing.* It would be about them. Diagnostic of their case alone.

But because the portion is indefinite, the warning is **portable**. It travels. *A* portion was forgotten. *Some* piece. Which means the question turns on every reader who comes after: *which piece have we forgotten?* The unspecified portion is a mirror. Anyone holding a religion can ask: *what did I let slip without noticing?*

And there is a second thing the indefinite is doing. It is a small word for a small loss. Not the whole. Not even most. **A portion.** Allah is making the loss sound minor — and then He will show, by the consequence, that the minor loss was catastrophic.

This is the geometry of selective forgetting. We will return to it.

But sit with the word itself for a moment. *Ḥaẓẓan*. A share. Their share. Their assigned, entrusted portion — and they forgot it. Some of it. Not all of it.

Most of the building still stands. Just one wall is missing.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: ذُكِّرُوا۟ بِهِۦ — Reminded, Not Taught

The phrase continues: *mimmā **dhukkirū** bih* — "of what they were **reminded** of."

Notice what Allah did not say. He did not say *mimmā ʿullimū bih* — "of what they were taught." He did not say *mimmā uʿṭū* — "of what they were given." He chose a different verb.

**Dhukkirū.** They were reminded.

Let's open the morphology. The root is ذ-ك-ر (dh-k-r) — the same root as *dhikr*, remembrance. The root meaning carries *to mention, to remember, to call to mind*. When you put this root into Form II of the Arabic verb — *dhakkara* — the pattern adds an intensive, causative meaning. The Form II verb means *to make someone remember*. To trigger recall in them. To activate something that was already there.

This is exactly what we mean in English by *to remind*. You remind someone of something they already know but have momentarily lost grip on. You don't teach a stranger; you remind a friend who forgot. The person being reminded had the information. Something had blurred it. The reminder pulls it back into focus.

Now here it is in the passive — *dhukkirū bih*. **They were reminded of it.** Allah is saying: this content was not new to them. Not unfamiliar. Not strange. They had already received it. They already knew it. The Messengers had already brought it. Their own scripture, when it was sound, had already named it.

What they forgot was something that had been **placed in their hands and reactivated again and again**.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because it changes the moral weight of the ayah completely.

If Allah had said *they forgot what they were taught*, the failure would still be serious. But it would be the failure of distant content slipping away. *Yes, they were taught it once, long ago. Time passed. They lost it.*

But Allah did not say that. He said *they forgot what they were **reminded** of*. They forgot the very thing that had been continually pulled back into their attention. The thing the Messengers came to **re-place** in front of them, again and again. The thing every reminder was designed to keep alive.

And they let that piece slip.

This is the most damning kind of forgetting. Not forgetting the obscure. Forgetting the obvious. Forgetting the thing you have been told a hundred times. The piece that every prophet, every reminder, every gentle nudge from heaven was working to keep present.

Here is what this invites us to consider. There are two kinds of religious forgetting. There is the forgetting of what we never quite grasped — the verses we never opened, the laws we never studied, the doctrines we never stress-tested. That forgetting is one kind of failure.

And there is the forgetting of what we were **reminded** of. The thing every Friday khutba named. The thing our parents told us. The thing the Quran circles back to in every juzʾ. The thing that came at us so often we stopped hearing it. *That* forgetting — the forgetting of what was constantly being re-placed in our hands — is the failure Allah is naming here.

The ayah does not diagnose people who never knew. It diagnoses people who had been told, and reminded, and told again — and let the piece slip anyway.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: بَيْنَهُمُ — The Punishment Located Inside

Now we come to the word that does the most diagnostic work in the whole ayah.

*Fa-aghraynā **baynahumu** l-ʿadāwata wal-baghḍāʾa* — "So We stirred up enmity and hatred **among them**."

Look at the preposition. **Baynahum.** Among them. Between them.

Before we open the verb *aghraynā* — which is itself heavy — pause on this little locator first. Because *baynahum* is the word that turns this ayah from a generic warning into a precise diagnosis.

In Arabic, *bayna* names location *between* parties. It is the word you use for what is in the space between two people, two groups, two parties. So when Allah says the enmity was stirred up *baynahum* — between them, among them — He is naming **where** the punishment lives.

It does not live outside the community. It is not enemies coming from the borders. It is not foreign powers descending. It is not famine, not plague, not military defeat.

It is **inside**.

The community that forgot a portion of what it was reminded of — the punishment Allah names for that community is *that the community itself begins to break against itself*. The wound is internal. The fight is between brothers. The hatred runs along seams that should have been sutures.

[PAUSE]

Now the verb. *Fa-**aghraynā***.

The root is غ-ر-ي (gh-r-y). In Form IV — *aghrā* — the lexicons document a striking image. *Aghrā* is most precisely the verb for **inciting** — spurring something on toward a target. Classical Arabic usage records *aghrā al-kalba bil-ṣayd*: to set the hound on the quarry. What matters in this image is not the release alone — it is the command, the direction, the will behind the motion. The leash is not simply dropped; the hound is *aimed*. The verb names deliberate divine **instigation**: not merely permission for discord to arise, but the active goading of it, purposive and directed.

Listen to the sound. The *ghayn* in *aghraynā* is one of the deepest, throatiest sounds Arabic has. It catches in the back of the throat. There is friction in the very pronunciation. The word **sounds harsh** because it names a harsh act.

So Allah is not saying *the discord drifted into them*. He is not saying *enmity arose by accident among them*. He is using a verb that means **We incited it among them** — aimed it, sent it — as a consequence that was *directed* at the space between people who had once shared a covenant.

[PAUSE]

And then the two nouns: *al-ʿadāwah* and *al-baghḍāʾ*. Both definite. Both heavy. Listen to them sounded together: *al-ʿadāwata wal-baghḍāʾ*. The *ʿayn*, the *ḍād*, the *ghayn*, the *bāʾ* — these are the heaviest letters in Arabic, and Allah has stacked them. The phonetic texture is itself ugly. Difficult. Choking. The sounds match the realities.

In the lexicons, the two words are not synonyms. *ʿAdāwah* is the root for *ʿaduww* — enemy. It names enmity that is **enacted** — hostility that shows up in behavior, in posture, in action. *Baghḍāʾ* is the root for *bughḍ* — hatred. It names enmity that is **felt** — the loathing in the heart, before the action.

So Allah names both layers. The hatred in the heart. The enmity in the behavior. The full machinery of communal poisoning, top to bottom.

And the terminus: *ilā yawmi l-qiyāmah*. **Until the Day of Resurrection.**

This is not a phase. Not a chapter that closes when the next century opens. The discord stretches all the way to eschatology. The community will not, in time, knit itself back together. Only the Day of Resurrection breaks this open. Only when the world ends does this end.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Four: يَصْنَعُونَ — What They Used to Craft

The ayah does not close with the image of enmity. It closes with a future reckoning — and with a deliberate choice of word.

*Wa sawfa yunabbiʾuhumu llāhu bimā kānū **yaṣnaʿūn*** — "And Allah will inform them of what they used to **craft**."

The root is ص-ن-ع (ṣ-n-ʿ). In classical Arabic, *ṣanaʿa* is the verb for *purposive, skilled making* — craftsmanship, fashioning, the deliberate construction of something. It is distinguished from *ʿamila* (to do, to work) precisely in this: *ʿamila* names general action; *ṣanaʿa* implies a thing *fashioned with intention and effort*. The smith who works iron *ṣanaʿa*. The one who shapes clay *ṣanaʿa*. Classical commentators on this word consistently read it as referring not to incidental deeds but to their *worked* behaviors — the deliberate construction of departures from the covenant.

Allah does not say *bimā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "what they used to do." He says *yaṣnaʿūn* — what they used to **craft**. What they were *making*, piece by piece, deliberately, over time.

Sit with the implication. The forgetting that opened this ayah could be read as passive — a slipping, a drift, a slow erosion. Forgetting can feel like something that *happens to you* rather than something you *do*. But the ayah closes by revealing that the forgetting was not purely passive. The deeds that surrounded it, that emerged from it, that entrenched it — those were *ṣunʿ*. Crafted. Built with intention.

A community does not drift into permanent enmity. It builds it. Sermon by sermon. Schism by schism. The refusal that becomes a position. The position that becomes a doctrine. The doctrine that becomes a wall. Each act of construction — *yaṣnaʿūn* — will be laid before them.

And Allah will *yunabbiʾ* them — acquaint them fully, inform them completely — with every piece of what they made.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So here is what the linguistic construction has shown us. A community lost a small portion — not the whole — of something they had been reminded of constantly. The consequence Allah named for them was not external defeat. It was internal fracture. Enmity and hatred, actively incited among them by divine purposiveness, running until the Day of Resurrection. And the deeds that surrounded the forgetting were not passive drift — they were *ṣunʿ*, deliberate construction, which will be accounted for in full.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about how communities live and die.

## PART 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Geometry of Selective Amnesia

Let's start where you live.

You have a recipe your grandmother gave you. You make it. You think you remember it. You produce a dish that is almost what she made — almost — but something is off. You can't tell what. You have the ingredients. You have the steps. You followed the technique. The dish looks right. It even smells close. But something is not right. One element — a spice you quietly stopped using because you were never quite sure it belonged — is gone. And its absence has changed the character of the whole thing. Not dramatically. The dish is still recognizable. But it is not the same dish.

This is the geometry of selective amnesia. You keep most of something and lose a piece. The piece you lose is not labeled "important." It often looks — to the eye that has already let it slip — like a peripheral detail. A nuance. Something on the edges. But the edges of a teaching are often where the teaching's integration lives. The piece on the margins is often the piece that holds the center in place.

[PAUSE]

For a community, the geometry works the same way. A community can keep the architecture of its tradition — the prayers, the rites, the vocabulary, the buildings, the festivals — while one piece has gone quiet. The piece does not disappear with a sound. There is no announcement. There is no day on which someone decrees: *we are dropping this*. It simply fades. It is talked about less. It is no longer emphasized. It is retained in the texts but no longer activated in the hearts. Gradually, a generation grows up that has never felt it, even if they can recite it.

And what happens when that piece — the binding piece — is gone?

What Allah names in this ayah is not theological failure in the abstract. It is structural. When the piece that held the community's unity was the piece forgotten, the community loses its capacity to cohere. Not dramatically. Not all at once. But the hairline fracture becomes a crack, and the crack becomes a fissure, and the fissure becomes the ground they live on: the enmity between denominations, between sects, between the ones who emphasize *this* teaching and the ones who insist on *that* one. The argument is always, ostensibly, about theology. But underneath the argument is the missing piece — the thing that once said: *despite your differences, here is what you share*.

The missing piece was not the whole of the religion. It was the *ḥaẓẓ* — the particular portion — that made the whole integrate.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Those Who Said They Were Christians

Here is a precision in the Arabic that cannot pass without notice.

Allah does not say *the Christians*. He does not say *Banī ʿĪsā* — the people of Jesus. He says **al-ladhīna qālū innā naṣārā** — *those who **said**: We are Christians*.

*Qālū.* They said it. The declaration is foregrounded, and it is expressed as speech — as something they articulated about themselves — before anything they lived.

Why does this matter?

The Quran uses *qāla* (he said / they said) with great precision. When Allah wants to describe a community by what they profess but not necessarily by what they embody, the verb of speech (*qāla*) appears in a different register than the verb of being (*kāna*). *Those who said they were Christians* is not the same construction as *the Christians*.

The construction invites a question the ayah never explicitly answers: were they what they said they were?

Not as a sweeping judgment — the Quran is not here reducing all Christians to this critique, as the same surah will shortly note (5:82) that among those closest in love to the believers are those who say *innā naṣārā*. The diversity within any named community is real. But the phrasing here, in this specific moment of covenant-failure, is *those who said*. The self-declaration is intact. The community still calls itself by its name. Still wears the identity. Still performs the markers.

The covenant is what fractured.

[PAUSE]

This is, perhaps, the most uncomfortable part of the ayah for any believing community to sit with. Because the mirror is direct. The test is not: *do you call yourself Muslim?* The test is: *does the covenant that names what a Muslim is — the mīthāq — remain whole in you?* Or is there a *ḥaẓẓ*, some assigned portion, that has quietly been dropped while the label has been faithfully retained?

The label and the reality can diverge. This is not a warning about other people. It is a question about every generation that inherits a name.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Three: The Eschatological Horizon

Look at the timestamp on the consequence: *ilā yawmi l-qiyāmah*. **Until the Day of Resurrection.**

We have already spent time on the heaviness of the enmity itself — the *ʿadāwah*, the *baghḍāʾ*, the *aghraynā*. But the temporal horizon deserves its own pause.

Until the Day of Resurrection is not a long time. It is **the end of time**. It means: for as long as this world exists.

What Allah is saying is not *there will be a bad patch*. He is not saying *they will struggle for a century and then recover*. He is saying: the fracture that entered when the portion was forgotten — that fracture is now permanent within this world's timeline. It will not be healed by reformation, by revival, by councils of unity, by dialogue. Communities will reach toward each other. They will have moments of closeness. And the enmity will return. Because the enmity is not a misunderstanding to be resolved. It is a consequence that has been *incited into place*.

[PAUSE]

But — and this is important — the verse does not end at *qiyāmah*. After naming the perpetual enmity, Allah adds: *wa sawfa yunabbiʾuhumu llāhu bimā kānū yaṣnaʿūn*. **And Allah will inform them of what they used to craft.**

The *wa sawfa* — "and indeed" — is the verse's turn toward accountability. The enmity in the world is the consequence within time. The informing — the reckoning — is the consequence beyond time. Both are named. Both are real.

The enmity runs until the Day of Resurrection. The Day of Resurrection is when it ends — and when everything they *ṣanaʿū*, everything they built and fashioned and crafted in the space between people who once shared a covenant, is laid before them.

The timing of God's accounting is not the timing of human amnesia. They forgot. He did not.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Four: A Mirror for the Listening Community

Now we return to the diptych.

The surah has offered this pattern twice, in deliberate sequence. Banī Isrā'īl forgot — and their consequence was hardness of heart, treachery, distortion. The community of Jesus forgot — and their consequence was internal enmity until the end of time. Two communities. The same phrase. Two different wounds.

The Madinan audience hearing this was the third community in the surah's frame — the one that held the covenant at that moment. The two panels before them were not history for its own sake. They were offered as what we might call case studies in covenant failure.

And the question left hanging — not stated, not named, but vibrating in the structure — is: *what happens when the third community forgets its portion?*

The surah does not answer that question here. It doesn't need to. The architecture has already taught the principle: covenant broken → piece forgotten → consequence attached. The principle is not community-specific. It is how the order of things works.

[PAUSE]

What piece is each generation at risk of forgetting?

The answer will differ by era. There are portions of the reminder that every age tends to drop — the inconvenient ones, the ones that cost something, the ones that interfere with comfort or power or the desire to fit in with whatever world surrounds the community. The portions that are *ḥaẓẓ* — assigned, entrusted, belonging to us by covenant — are the ones that require the most care to keep. They are the ones that take effort to remember.

The indefinite noun in the ayah — *ḥaẓẓan*, some portion — was not vague by accident. It was vague by design. A mirror aimed at no one in particular, and therefore capable of reflecting everyone.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Closing — The Accountability That Doesn't Forget

The ayah ends on a verb in the imperfect — *yaṣnaʿūn*. Present-tense action, framed in the past by *kānū*: what they *used to be crafting*, continuously, as a habit. The tense is a reminder that deeds accumulate. Every act of constructing the enmity, every sermon that deepened the schism, every refusal that hardened into doctrine — these were not lost. They were *ṣunʿ*. They were made. And they will be shown.

*Wa sawfa yunabbiʾuhumu llāhu.* **And Allah will acquaint them with it in full.**

*Yunabbiʾ* is Form IV of the root ن-ب-أ — to give news, to acquaint, to bring to knowledge. The Form IV intensifies the completeness of the informing. Not a partial account. Not a summary. The full picture of what was made.

There is something almost merciful in this. Because the enmity that runs until the Day of Resurrection is not the end of the story. It is the end of one chapter. The next chapter begins with *yunabbiʾ* — a disclosure, a laying-bare of the full record of what was crafted in the space between people who once shared a covenant.

And in that disclosure is the implicit invitation, still extended to the living: *before the acquainting comes the remembering*.

The portion can still be recalled. The *dhikr* is still offered. The reminder is still available. The question is whether the community — any community, this community, each of us as a node within it — will reach back for what was let slip.

[PAUSE]

The ayah makes no promise about what happens if we do. It was not given to reassure us. It was given to wake us. The geometry is laid bare: a piece forgotten, a community fractured, a consequence that does not end until the world does.

We are the ones reading this now.

*Which piece have we forgotten?*

---

*May Allah make us of those who guard their covenant — who hold the whole of what they were reminded of — and who meet the Day of Resurrection as people who remembered, even when forgetting was easy.*
