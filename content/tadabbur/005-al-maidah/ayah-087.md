# When Refusal Becomes Transgression
## Surah Al-Ma'idah, Ayah 87

---

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تُحَرِّمُوا۟ طَيِّبَٰتِ مَآ أَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَلَا تَعْتَدُوٓا۟ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُعْتَدِينَ

**Transliteration:**
> Yā ayyuhā 'lladhīna āmanū lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla 'llāhu lakum wa-lā taʿtadū. Inna 'llāha lā yuḥibbu 'l-muʿtadīn.

**Translation:**
> O you who have believed — do not declare forbidden the good things that Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not love the transgressors.

---

## Introduction: The Companions Who Tried to Out-Worship Each Other

Step back into Madinah, into a particular night that the early commentators on this surah preserved for us. A group of companions are sitting together. They have just heard a discourse on the Day of Judgment — vivid, shaking, the kind of reminder that leaves a person sleepless.

They start talking. Each one realises, with genuine fear, that he is not doing enough. The Hereafter is real. The reckoning is real. What kind of worship, they ask each other, would actually be worthy of what is coming?

So they make pacts.

One says: I will fast every day for the rest of my life — no more breaking fast.

Another says: I will pray every night and never sleep.

Another says: I will never eat meat again.

Another says: I will leave my wife. I will live alone. I will wear only the roughest cloth. I will never enter a comfortable home. The dunya will not touch me.

Each of them is sincere. Each of them is afraid. Each of them is reaching for what they believe is closeness to Allah. And each of them is reaching by **taking something away from themselves**. By forbidding to themselves what Allah had not forbidden.

Word reaches the Prophet ﷺ.

He gathers them. And what he says is one of the most famous corrections in the Sunnah: *I fast and I break my fast. I pray at night and I sleep. I marry women. So whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me.*

[PAUSE]

It is in the wake of this moment — this very specific spiritual mistake — that this ayah is revealed.

> *O you who have believed — do not declare forbidden the good things that Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not love the transgressors.*

Now hold this carefully. Because something is about to happen here that almost nobody expects.

The men who took the vow had no intention of disobedience. They wanted *more* obedience. They were trying to become *more* religious, not less. They were not violating a command of Allah — they were inventing additional commands and imposing them on themselves.

And Allah calls what they did **transgression**.

Not weakness. Not error. Not enthusiasm gone slightly too far.

**Transgression.** The same word the Quran uses for trespass against the sacred limit.

[PAUSE]

This is the puzzle we are going to sit with. How is it that adding more restriction to oneself — out of fear of Allah, out of love for Allah, out of a desire to draw nearer — could possibly count as crossing a line *against* Allah?

By the end of this reflection, you will see why. And it may rearrange how you have been thinking about your own religion.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Tuḥarrimū* — The Verb That Reveals Who Thinks They Are God

The first word of the prohibition is — itself — a word about prohibitions.

> **لَا تُحَرِّمُوا** — *lā tuḥarrimū* — "Do not declare forbidden."

Before we open this word, let me teach a feature of Arabic that we will need.

Arabic verbs come in patterns called **forms**. The base form (Form I) is the simple action. *Ḥaruma* in Form I means "to be forbidden" — passive, descriptive, just the state of being haram. But Arabic has a Form II — the doubled middle radical — and Form II does something specific. It takes the basic meaning and turns it into an act of *making* or *causing* or *declaring*.

If Form I means "to be clean," Form II means "to make clean."

If Form I means "to know," Form II means "to teach" — to *cause* someone else to know.

So when we move from Form I (*ḥaruma* — to be forbidden) into Form II (*ḥarrama* — what Allah does in "*aḥalla*'s" mirror twin), the verb stops being descriptive and becomes **declarative**. **Legislative**. To do *taḥrīm* is not just to avoid something. It is to issue the verdict that this thing IS forbidden.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the ayah again.

> *Lā **tuḥarrimū** ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla 'llāhu lakum.*

It does not say "do not avoid." It does not say "do not abstain from." It does not say "do not refrain from." Allah could have used a dozen verbs that would have meant simple non-engagement.

He chose the verb of **legislation**.

Sit with this. The companions in our story did not write a fatwa. They did not stand up and tell the ummah, "henceforth meat is haram." They simply made a private vow about themselves. And yet the verb Allah uses to describe what they did is *taḥrīm* — the verb of *issuing the verdict of forbiddenness*.

Here is what this is exposing. **The moment you treat something Allah made lawful as something off-limits — even privately, even just for yourself, even sincerely — you have done an act that belongs to Allah.** You have acted as if a verdict has been issued. The fact that you only issued it to yourself does not change the structure of the act. *Taḥrīm* is *taḥrīm*.

This is why the verb stings. It is naming the thing nobody wanted to admit they were doing. They thought they were being humble. They were, in fact, legislating.

Here's what is remarkable. The very next word — the verb describing what Allah has done — is *aḥalla*. Form IV. **Causative.** "*To make halal.*" The same morphological pattern. Form II for what they were doing, Form IV for what Allah did. Look at the sentence:

> *Do not **make-haram** what Allah has **made-halal**.*

It is exactly parallel. Two verbs of legislation, set against each other. And the ayah is making the point so quietly you might miss it: **there is one Legislator in this matter.** When you do *taḥrīm* of something He did *iḥlāl* of, you are not adding to His religion. You are setting yourself up as a counter-legislator.

[PAUSE]

This is why what looks like extra worship is, in the verdict of the Quran, transgression. Because the structure of the act is not "doing more of what He commanded." The structure is "issuing commands He did not issue." And there is no version of that act, no matter how piously framed, that is not a quiet usurpation.

What this single verb form reveals is that the boundary between obedience and transgression does not run where most people think it runs. It does not run between *much* and *little*. It runs between *what He said* and *what you said*. To go past what He said — even in the direction of more restriction — is to cross out of His law into your own.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Ṭayyibāt* — The Word Allah Did Not Need to Use

Now look at the noun that comes next.

> *Lā tuḥarrimū **ṭayyibāti** mā aḥalla 'llāhu lakum.*

The grammatical object of *tuḥarrimū* is *ṭayyibāt* — "the good things." And it is connected by *iḍāfa* to *mā aḥalla 'llāhu lakum* — "what Allah has made lawful for you."

Notice something. The sentence would have been complete without the word *ṭayyibāt*. Allah could have said:

> *Do not declare forbidden what Allah has made lawful for you.*

That would have been a clean, complete sentence. Grammatically sufficient. Theologically sufficient. The point would have been made.

But Allah inserted an extra word in the middle. Not "what Allah made lawful." **The good things of what Allah made lawful.** The *ṭayyibāt*.

This is not redundant. It is doing surgical work.

Let's stay with what *ṭayyib* means. The root ط-ي-ب carries pleasantness, wholesomeness, sweetness, fragrance, goodness. *Ṭayyib* is the opposite of *khabīth* — the foul, the loathsome. When you call something *ṭayyib*, you are saying: this is something a healthy soul recognises as *good*. The food smells good. The rest is restful. The companionship is warm. The marriage bed is sweet.

Now reread the ayah with this in mind. **Do not declare forbidden the GOOD things that Allah has made lawful for you.**

Allah is not just saying "what I permitted." He is going out of His way to insist that **what He permitted is, in fact, good.** Not neutral. Not tolerated. Not begrudgingly allowed because you are too weak to do without it.

**Good.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the spiritual error this single word destroys. There is a way of being religious that secretly believes: *the truly holy thing would be to refuse all of this. Allah only made it permissible because we are too weak to live the higher life. The food, the marriage, the rest, the beauty — these are concessions. The real saints are the ones who don't need them.*

This way of thinking is everywhere. It runs through the monastic traditions. It runs through certain forms of late-Christian mysticism. It runs through corners of the Muslim community too — every era produces its own version of "the true believer is the one who has the least."

And the word *ṭayyibāt* cuts straight through it. **Allah is calling what He permitted *good*.** He is not making a concession to human weakness. He is offering a gift. He is saying: I made these things, and I made them good, and I made them for you. Their goodness is not in spite of their lawfulness. Their goodness is *the reason* for their lawfulness.

Notice something else. There is a small word at the end of the phrase: **لَكُمْ** — *lakum* — "for you."

Not "what Allah made lawful." **What Allah made lawful *for you*.**

This is the hand on the shoulder. The pronoun makes it personal. The food, the rest, the marriage — these were not legislated abstractly into existence. They were legislated *for you*. Specifically. Tailored. Allah, who knows what your soul actually needs, opened these particular things and said: *take them. They are for you.*

To refuse them is not just to refuse a category of permission. It is to refuse a gift that has your name on it.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what is happening here. We came in with a story about men who wanted to be more devoted. They thought the path to Allah was through deprivation. The ayah is telling them — telling us — something they could not have anticipated. **The thing you are pushing away was a gift. From the One you were trying to reach.**

This is what *ṭayyibāt* is doing in the ayah. It is naming the gift. Refusing to let us pretend we were only refusing a "thing." We were refusing **the good thing.** The one He gave. To us. Personally.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Lā Taʿtadū* — Why Restriction Is Called *Crossing a Line*

The third element is the most surprising. Look at what comes immediately after the prohibition:

> *Lā tuḥarrimū ṭayyibāti mā aḥalla 'llāhu lakum **wa-lā taʿtadū**.*

> *Do not declare forbidden the good things that Allah has made lawful for you, **and do not transgress**.*

The verb is *taʿtadū*, from the root ع-د-و. The Form VIII. *Iʿtadā*. The lexical core of this root is **going past a limit, crossing over a boundary, exceeding what was set**. The active participle, *muʿtadī*, is "the one who crosses the line." It is the same root that gives us *ʿaduww* — enemy. Because the enemy is the one who has crossed a boundary into territory not theirs.

Now think about what we expect. If Allah were going to issue a *second* command after "do not forbid the good things," we would expect Him to broaden the warning. *And do not be ungrateful.* Or: *and do not despise My provision.* Or: *and do not innovate in My religion.* Any of those would fit naturally.

He says something different. He says: **and do not cross the line.**

The word is the language of *boundaries*. Of going *past where you were supposed to stop*.

Sit with this for a moment. Because it is doing something specific. The classical commentators read the *wāw* connecting these two prohibitions as **explanatory**. *Wa-lā taʿtadū* is not a separate command bolted onto the first. It is **the same command, named again, with its true name exposed**.

> *Do not declare forbidden the good things that Allah made lawful for you. (And let me tell you what that act actually IS:) Do not cross the line.*

Now feel the force of this.

The men in our opening story would have described their vows as *holding back*. *Pulling away from the world.* *Drawing in.* *Restricting themselves.*

The Quran reaches into their description and replaces every word of it with one word: **trespass.**

You thought you were retreating. You were advancing — onto territory that was not yours.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this exposes. We assume that **transgression** has a single direction. That you cross the line by doing what was forbidden — going past a "stop" sign. But the ayah is telling us that the line can also be crossed in the other direction. By forbidding what was permitted, you are **going past the boundary Allah set in the other direction**. There were two limits, not one. Allah set the line of what is forbidden. He also set the line of what is permitted. And both lines are sacred. Both lines are His. To override the second line is structurally identical to overriding the first. It is *iʿtidāʾ* — trespass — in both directions.

This is why the closing of the ayah is so chilling.

> **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَا يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُعْتَدِينَ**
> *Indeed, Allah does not love the transgressors.*

The active participle, *al-muʿtadīn* — "the ones who keep crossing lines." It is from the same root as the verb. So the warning is not "Allah does not love when you transgress." It is **"Allah does not love those who have made a habit of trespass."** The participle names a *type of person*.

And here is the sting.

The men in the story were trying to become a different *type of person* — the type who has reached a higher station. They were spiritually self-conceiving. *We will be the people who don't need food. The people who don't need wives. The people who don't sleep.*

The ayah hands them a different identity. **You are not becoming "the people of high station." You are becoming "the people who trespass."**

The very project of self-elevation through self-restriction has been renamed. From "ascent" to "transgression." From "drawing near" to "crossing the line." From a story they were telling themselves about their own piety to a name Allah is giving them in His Book.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Take a breath. Look at what the language has shown us.

The first word — *tuḥarrimū* — exposed the act as **legislation** when its actors thought it was just devotion.

The middle word — *ṭayyibāt* — exposed what they were rejecting as **good** when they had assumed it was beneath them.

The closing prohibition — *lā taʿtadū* — exposed the entire posture as **trespass** when they had imagined it as ascent.

Three words have completely rebuilt the moral picture. What looked like piety from the inside is named, in Allah's vocabulary, as legislation, ingratitude, and trespass.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about a moment in seventh-century Madinah — but about a permanent feature of the religious heart.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Quiet Disease of Religious Overcompensation

Let me start somewhere outside the religious frame, because what this ayah is treating is something every human being knows from inside.

There is a particular emotional logic that anyone who has ever felt inadequate has met. It goes like this: *I have failed. I have fallen short. I have done less than I should have. So now, to make up for it, I will impose on myself a punishment that exceeds the crime.*

The teenager who got a B on a test and decides she will not eat dessert for a month. The man who lost his temper with his wife and decides he will not speak for a day. The recovering addict who, beyond what the program asks, vows never again to enter any restaurant where alcohol is served. The athlete who missed a workout and adds three the next day, then four the next, then collapses.

Notice the structure of this pattern. **The discipline is not calibrated to the offense. It is calibrated to the guilt.** And because guilt has no natural ceiling, the discipline keeps escalating. Each new restriction is the price of admission to feeling clean again. And when the clean feeling fades — and it always fades — the price has to go up.

Therapists who treat eating disorders, exercise compulsion, and certain anxiety conditions have a name for this. They call it the **purity trap**. The sufferer is not really trying to be healthy. They are trying to feel clean. And because cleanness is never achieved through restriction — it is only chased — the restrictions multiply.

Now bring this back to the ayah.

The companions who took the vow were not bad men. They were good men, terrified men, men who felt they were not enough. And they reached for the only tool the inadequate heart knows: *deny myself more*. The fasting that the religion asked of them did not feel like enough — so they would fast every day. The prayer that the religion asked of them did not feel like enough — so they would pray all night. The companionship and rest and food that the religion permitted did not feel like enough of a sacrifice — so they would refuse them.

And the Quran, with surgical love, names this for what it is. **You are not getting closer. You are crossing a line.** The disease that runs through every culture's spiritual hospitals — the conviction that more deprivation equals more devotion — has just been diagnosed and rejected by Allah Himself.

[PAUSE]

This is a stunning thing to admit out loud. **Allah does not want your religion to look like an eating disorder.** He does not want a closeness to Him that is built on self-punishment. He does not want a worship that is essentially a performance of unworthiness.

Why? Because that worship is no longer about Him. It is about the ego's relationship to its own guilt. It is about the self trying to manage the self. The deity, in that whole transaction, is functionally absent. The deprivation is a private deal between the worshipper and the worshipper's shame.

The ayah breaks the deal. It says: **the path back to Me does not run through more punishment of yourself. It runs through accepting what I gave you, with thanks, and using it as I prescribed.**

And here is the practical edge. When you feel, after a sin, the urge to invent a punishment — to deny yourself a meal, to refuse a comfort, to add a vow that nobody asked you to take — this ayah is whispering to you: *that is not the path*. The path is **istighfār**, the prescribed acts of expiation if any apply, and a return to the regular life of the believer. Eat the food. Sleep in the bed. Be with your spouse. Pray the prayers that were prescribed. The thing your guilt wants you to do — the extra deprivation, the unauthorised abstention — is not climbing toward Allah. It is, in His own word, *iʿtidāʾ*. Trespass.

What changes tomorrow if you let this in? You stop secretly admiring the part of yourself that wants to be more austere than the religion requires. You begin to recognise that ascetic impulse, when it goes past what was legislated, as *guilt mismanaged*. And you let yourself receive the gift again — not because you have earned it, but because He gave it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Theology of Receiving

Now let me draw out the deeper theology under all of this.

There is a way of relating to a giver that the giver experiences as honour, and there is a way that the giver experiences as insult — and they can look almost identical from the outside.

Imagine a friend who has worked for weeks on a meal for you. They have chosen the ingredients with care. They have set the table. They have lit candles. They have thought, all the way through, about what you would like. You arrive. You sit down. And then, before tasting anything, you push the plate away and say, *I am not worthy of this. I will eat plain bread instead. Out of respect for you.*

What has just happened? You did not insult the cook by complaining about the food. You did not throw it on the floor. By the bare description of your behaviour, you were *humble*. You were *deferential*. You took less than you were offered.

But anyone who has ever cooked a meal for someone they love knows what your friend is feeling right now. **They are not honoured. They are wounded.** Because the gift was not given to be refused. The gift was given to be *received*. And to refuse it, in the name of unworthiness, is to make the gift about you. About *your* worthiness. About *your* humility. The cook prepared the meal in love. You converted it into a stage for your own self-effacement.

This is the structure the ayah is exposing.

When Allah, in the Quran, runs through the long catalogue of His provisions — the cattle He sent down for you, the seas He made to flow with what you can eat from them, the spouses from your own selves to find rest in — He is not listing background data. He is **naming His gifts.** Each one of those mentions is a hand extended.

To take a gift Allah has named and *forbid it to yourself* is not humility. It is the same wound as the meal pushed away. It is treating the gift as a stage on which to perform your own asceticism.

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox at the heart of this.

**The most spiritually advanced posture is not the rejection of the gifts. It is the gracious receiving of them.**

Because rejecting the gift centres the self. *Look at what I can do without. Look how little I need. Look how spiritual I am.*

Receiving the gift centres the Giver. *I did not earn this. I did not invent this. I did not produce this. He sent it. He named it good. He named it for me. Bismillāh.*

Notice which one of these postures requires a constant awareness of Allah. It is not the rejecting one. The rejecting one only requires awareness of the self. The receiving one requires Him in every bite, every breath, every embrace. *Alḥamdulillāh*. The cup of water is His. The soft bed is His. The arm of the spouse around your shoulder is His. To use these things, in His name, with thanks, is a more sustained form of worship than refusing them ever could be — because it makes every ordinary moment a moment of *dhikr*.

This is why the very next ayah — 5:88 — completes the instruction:

> *Wa kulū mimmā razaqakumu 'llāhu ḥalālan ṭayyiban, wa-'ttaqū 'llāha 'lladhī antum bihi muʾminūn.*
> *And eat from what Allah has provided you — lawful and good — and have taqwa of Allah, in whom you are believers.*

5:87 says: do not declare it forbidden. 5:88 says: now actually eat it.

The pair is a complete instruction. Don't refuse the gift, **and** actually receive the gift. Because only receiving completes the loop. The gift unopened is still, in some sense, a gift refused. The believer is being commanded into the world — not out of it.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how we live tomorrow?

Stop apologising for the *ṭayyibāt* of your life. The good food on your table — receive it with His name. The marriage you are in — be present in it without guilt that it is too pleasant. The home that is comfortable — do not feel that you are betraying the Hereafter by living in a place that is warm and clean. None of these things are concessions to your weakness. They are gifts. He named them good. He named them for you.

The ascetic story — that the truly close-to-Allah person sleeps on a stone, eats one date, refuses the comforts of life — is a story this ayah does not endorse. The Sunnah does not endorse it. The Prophet ﷺ slept on a bed, ate good food when it was offered, loved his wives, wore the best garment he had on Friday. The high station of the believer is not the highest level of self-deprivation. It is the highest level of *gratitude inside the gifts.*

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic and the thematic have converged on the same single insight from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us three words that completely rebuilt our picture:

- *Tuḥarrimū* exposed self-imposed restriction as **legislation** in the place of Allah.
- *Ṭayyibāt* exposed what was being rejected as a **gift** the Giver had personally named good.
- *Lā taʿtadū* exposed the entire act as **trespass** rather than ascent.

The thematic side approached the same point through the human heart:

- The first theme — religious overcompensation — showed us that the impulse toward extra deprivation is usually *guilt mismanaged*, not closeness pursued.
- The second theme — the theology of receiving — showed us that *gracious acceptance* of His gifts is a higher worship than performative refusal of them.

These are the same point. From the outside (the linguistic verdict): adding to His legislation is trespass. From the inside (the human heart): adding to His legislation is the ego trying to use religion to manage its own shame. Same act. Two views.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry With You

1. Where in your religious life are you imposing on yourself things Allah did not impose? What story are you telling yourself about why those impositions make you closer to Him?

2. When you feel guilt, what is your first instinct? Is it to seek forgiveness — or to invent a punishment that nobody asked you to take?

3. Are there *ṭayyibāt* in your life that you have learned to feel quietly ashamed of receiving? A meal too pleasant? A home too comfortable? A spouse too beloved? What would change if you let yourself name them as His named gifts?

4. Have you ever admired, in yourself or in someone else, an austerity that was not actually prescribed? What was the admiration really about?

5. If someone watched the way you receive Allah's bounty — would they see a person honouring the Giver, or a person performing their own unworthiness?

### The Ayah in One Sentence

**The believer's path to Allah does not run through more deprivation than He prescribed; it runs through gracious acceptance of the goods He named, used as He directed, with the thanks of someone who knows the gift had their name on it.**

### The Closing Invitation

The next time you sit down to a meal — let this ayah sit at the table with you. Notice the food. Notice that He named it good. Notice that the *lakum* — the *for you* — is not poetic. He really did mean *for you*. Not abstractly. Not for humanity in general. **For you, specifically, today, this meal.**

And let yourself receive it as a gift, not as a concession to your weakness.

That alone, repeated three times a day, is a quiet revolution. It rebuilds the relationship from the inside. It moves the centre from your worthiness to His generosity. It turns ordinary life into a sustained *dhikr*.

This is the religion the ayah is calling us back to. Not less than what He prescribed. Not more than what He prescribed. Exactly what He prescribed — received with hands open.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, you have called what You permitted us "good," and You have called us — by name — the ones it is good for. Forgive us for the times we pushed Your gifts away in the name of a piety You never asked of us. Teach our hearts the worship of grateful receiving. And let us never trespass — not even in the direction we mistook for ascent.*

*Āmīn.*
