# Turning Back as a State, Not a Moment

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 31

> **مُنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ وَاتَّقُوهُ وَأَقِيمُوا الصَّلَاةَ وَلَا تَكُونُوا مِنَ الْمُشْرِكِينَ**
>
> *Munībīna ilayhi wattaqūhu wa-aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāta wa-lā takūnū mina-l-mushrikīn*
>
> *"Turning back to Him — and be conscious of Him, and establish the prayer, and do not be among those who associate partners with Him."*

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Ayah

Imagine standing on a hillside outside Makkah, around the year 615 CE.

The news from the north is the only conversation that matters. The Persian Empire, the great fire-worshipping superpower of the age, has just shattered the Byzantines. Jerusalem has fallen. The cross has been carried away. Every coffee fire and caravan stop hums with the same question: *who will rule the world now?*

The Quraysh — the polytheists of Makkah — are quietly delighted. The fire-worshippers, more like them, have crushed the people of the book. To them, this is a sign. *The world bends toward our way.*

And into this geopolitical moment — this moment when everyone is asking who is up and who is down, who is rising and who is falling — Allah reveals Surah Ar-Rum. He opens it with something staggering: *the Byzantines will return*. Within a few years, the defeated will be victorious. Then He pivots from empires to the earth itself — how it dies and returns to life. And then He pivots again, from earth to the human soul — and says: *set your face toward the religion as a ḥanīf, the fitrah of Allah upon which He created people.*

Cycles. Return. Coming back to what is original.

And then, right after the fitrah verse, comes our ayah.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Most people read this ayah and see a list — four religious instructions. Repent. Have taqwa. Pray. Don't commit shirk. A checklist of obligations.

But Allah did not write a checklist. He wrote something far stranger and far more beautiful.

The first word — *munībīn* — is not a command at all. It is not a verb you do. It is a **state you exist in**. And from that state of being, three commands flow.

You are not told to *become* a person of return. You are told that you already are one — and *therefore*, certain things must follow.

What if the first lesson of this ayah is that obedience does not produce identity? **Identity produces obedience.**

Let's slow down and see what Allah placed in these nine words.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Munībīn* — The Word That Is Not a Command

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about Arabic grammar.

In Arabic, there is a category of words called **ḥāl** — circumstantial accusatives. A ḥāl describes the *state* a person is in *while* they do something. In English we use phrases like "smiling" or "walking slowly" or "in tears." We say: "She entered the room **smiling**." The smiling isn't a separate action — it describes the state she was in while entering.

In Arabic, ḥāl is even more powerful. It locks a quality onto the subject. It says: *this is the condition you are in. From within this condition, the action happens.*

Now look at the ayah.

*Munībīna* — "turning back" — sits at the very front of the sentence, but it is **not the main verb**. The main verbs come next: *ittaqūhu* (be conscious of Him), *aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāh* (establish the prayer), *lā takūnū* (do not be). Those are commands. But *munībīn* is grammatically a **ḥāl** — a description of the state the addressees are in *while* they receive these commands.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that.

Allah is not saying: "First, repent. Then have taqwa. Then pray." He is saying: "**As people who are already turning back to Me** — have taqwa, pray, do not associate partners."

The return is the *foundation*. The taqwa, the salah, the rejection of shirk — these are what *grow out of* a soul that has already oriented itself homeward.

This is everything.

Notice something. We tend to think of repentance as an *event*. Something we do. A moment. *I did wrong. I repented. Now I move on.* The English word "repent" carries this feeling — a discrete act, performed and completed.

But *munīb* — from the root *n-w-b* — does not work that way. The classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī) explain that *nāba* means to return repeatedly — to come back to a place again and again. *Inābah* — the form-IV verbal noun from which *munīb* comes — means a sustained, habitual turning. Not a single U-turn. A continuous orbit.

A *munīb* is not someone who returned once. A *munīb* is someone whose **default orientation** is return. Whose face is always tilted homeward, even when their feet wander.

[PAUSE]

Here is the analogy that does the structural work.

Think of a compass needle. You can pick up a compass and shake it. You can spin it. You can hold it sideways or upside down. You can carry it through magnetic fields and into caves. And in every single one of those moments, *the needle is still pointing north*. Not because you keep telling it to. Not because it just remembered. But because that is **what kind of object it is**.

The needle does not *go* north. The needle *is* north-pointing.

A munīb is a compass-soul.

Allah is not commanding you to perform an act of return as a one-time correction. He is describing what kind of object the believing heart actually is. It is a compass. It will drift. It will spin. But it is constituted toward Him.

And **from within that constitution** — three things follow.

Notice now how perfectly this lands on top of the previous ayah. Ayah 30 just told us about the fitrah — the original disposition Allah created humans upon. Ayah 31 describes us as *munībīn* — those who return. Return to what? Return to the fitrah. Return to the original orientation. The *munīb* is not inventing a new direction; they are coming back to where they started.

This is why the word is plural — *munībīn*, not the singular *munīb*. Allah is addressing a community of compass-souls. The fitrah is not a private possession; it is the shared substrate of humanity. We are *together* a people of return.

What this single word reveals: **repentance is not an act you complete. It is a posture you inhabit.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Ittaqūhu* — The Pronoun That Refuses to Let Go

Now move to the second word in the sequence: *wa-ttaqūhu*.

The root is *w-q-y*, which classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt) explain as "to shield, to guard, to put a barrier between yourself and harm." The form-VIII verb *ittaqā* means to actively place yourself behind a protection. *Taqwa* — the famous noun from this root — is the state of being shielded.

Most translations render *ittaqūhu* as "fear Him." But that is not quite what the verb means. *Ittaqūhu* means: "shield yourselves with respect to Him" — meaning, shield yourselves from doing what would displease Him, from being on the wrong side of Him.

That is already worth a lifetime. But that is not what I want to draw your attention to.

Look at the pronoun.

*Ittaqū-**hu*** — "shield yourselves [with respect to] **Him**."

In Arabic, the *-hu* is the attached pronoun for "him." It is fused to the verb. You cannot pry it off without breaking the word.

Now ask yourself: why didn't Allah say *ittaqū Allāh* — "have taqwa of Allah"? Why didn't He name Himself? Why the pronoun?

Because the pronoun *refers back*. A pronoun has no meaning by itself — it depends on its **antecedent**, the noun it points to. And the antecedent here is **the entire preceding ayah** — the fitrah verse — which ended with the divine name and the description of Allah as the One upon whose nature humans were created.

Allah is grammatically refusing to let you forget what you just heard.

It is as if He is saying: *I am not going to name Myself again. You already know who I am. You just heard who I am. I am the One who fashioned you. I am the One whose fitrah you carry. **That** is the One you shield yourselves regarding.*

[PAUSE]

The pronoun is a thread. It stitches ayah 31 to ayah 30 so tightly that you cannot separate them.

And there is something deeper still.

Notice the order. The ayah opens with *munībīna **ilayhi*** — "turning back to **Him**" — and then *ittaqū**hu*** — "shield yourselves regarding **Him**." Two pronouns, back to back, both pointing at the same Person.

Allah is, in nine words, three times turning your face toward Him without using His name. *Ilayhi… hu… hu…* The pronoun keeps tapping. *To Him. From Him. About Him.*

Here is the analogy.

Imagine a child has wandered into a crowd at a marketplace. They are lost. Their parent is nearby. The parent does not call the child by shouting their own name — *"It is Father here! It is Father!"* No. The parent grabs the child's shoulders, turns the face, and says: *"Here. Here. Look. It's me."*

The pronoun is intimacy. The pronoun says: *we both already know who this is*.

To use the divine name here would have been to introduce someone. To use the pronoun is to say: *you and I both already know exactly who I am talking about*.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing. *Taqwa* is sometimes described in classical works using the image of someone walking through a path lined with thorns — lifting their robe carefully, watching where they step. Imagine walking that path. Now imagine walking it *as a munīb* — as someone whose entire body is oriented toward a destination. The taqwa is not paranoid; it is purposeful. You are careful with thorns because you are *going somewhere*.

The state of return makes the carefulness make sense.

What this word reveals: **taqwa is not free-floating anxiety about a distant deity. It is the careful walk of someone who already knows whose home they are heading to.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Aqīmū-ṣ-Ṣalāh* — The Verb That Does Not Mean "Pray"

Now the third element, and we will go more carefully here, because this phrase is often translated quickly.

*Aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāh* — usually rendered "establish the prayer" or "perform the prayer."

The verb is *aqīmū*, the plural command form of *aqāma*, the form-IV verb from the root *q-w-m*. The root *q-w-m* means "to stand." From it we get *qiyām* — standing. From it we get *qāʾim* — one who is standing. And from it we get *aqāma* — to **cause** to stand, to make something stand up straight.

Notice what Allah did not say.

He did not say *ṣallū* — "pray." That verb exists. It is used elsewhere in the Quran. The simple, straightforward command "pray" was available.

He chose *aqīmū* instead.

[PAUSE]

What is the difference?

*Ṣallū* would describe the action — the bowing, the prostrating, the recitation. *Aqīmū* describes something else: making the prayer **stand**. Setting it upright. Causing it to have presence in the world.

Think about what it takes to make something stand. A flag does not stand on its own — someone hoists it, anchors it, fixes it in place. A house does not stand on its own — someone builds its foundations, raises its walls, props it up. *Iqāmah* is structural work. It is the work of giving something a posture in reality.

So when Allah commands *aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāh*, He is not commanding the performance of prayer. He is commanding that prayer be **made to stand** in your life. That it have structure. That it be fixed. That it not collapse.

[PAUSE]

Here is the analogy.

There is a difference between *having* a body and *carrying yourself well*. Two people can have the same bone structure. One slouches; one stands. The one who stands has done something with the body that the one who slouches has not — they have *erected* it. They have given it posture.

You can perform prayer without establishing it. You can move through the motions while the salah itself slouches in your life — collapsed against the wall of habit, propped up by guilt, doing none of the structural work it was designed to do.

To *establish* the prayer is to make it stand inside you with such posture that it organizes everything around it. The day bends around the prayer, not the prayer around the day. The prayer is the load-bearing wall, not the decorative trim.

And notice — this is the **only** specifically commanded act of worship in this ayah. Allah did not list fasting, zakah, hajj, dhikr. He named one: *salah*. Why?

Because salah is the rehearsal of return. It is *inābah* given a body. Five times a day, the compass-soul performs an enactment of what they already are. The standing, the bowing, the prostrating — all of it is the body publicly performing what the heart is silently always doing: **coming back**.

Salah is the visible architecture of an invisible orientation.

What this word reveals: **prayer is not a performance you put on. It is a structure you make stand. And what is being made to stand is the inner orientation of return itself.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Step back and look at what these three linguistic features have shown us.

*Munībīn* — return is not an event; it is the state we exist in.

*Ittaqūhu* — the divine reference uses a pronoun, not a name, because the One we are returning to is already intimately known.

*Aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāh* — the act of worship is not performed but *erected*; salah is the structural enactment of return.

These are not three separate commands. They are three windows onto a single architecture: **the soul as a compass-needle, oriented homeward, with prayer as the visible scaffolding that holds the orientation upright.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Difference Between Doing and Being

Here is something every honest person eventually discovers about themselves.

You can do all the right things and still not be the right kind of person.

You can show up on time, return the phone calls, send the cards, perform the rituals of decency — and still be hollow. The behavior is correct. The behavior is even praiseworthy. And yet you know, somewhere in the marrow, that you are *performing* a goodness you do not *embody*.

Modern psychology has a name for this. It calls it the difference between **state** and **trait**. A state is something passing through you — a mood, an action, a momentary condition. A trait is something constitutive — a stable feature of who you are. You can have a state of kindness without being a kind person. You can have a state of honesty in this particular moment while being, in your deeper grain, deceitful.

The reason a state without a trait feels hollow is because we know that one is a costume and the other is a body. The costume can be taken off. The body cannot.

[PAUSE]

Now look back at our ayah.

Allah did not say "do these four things." He said: *being people of return — do these three things*.

He grounded the entire instruction in a **trait**. Not a state. Not a passing condition. A constitutive feature of the soul.

Why?

Because Allah knows something about the human heart that we often forget. **Behavior without identity is unsustainable.** You can force yourself to pray for a while. You can grit your teeth through taqwa for a while. You can avoid shirk by sheer willpower for a while. But the gritting and forcing and willing always run out. The reservoir empties.

What does not run out is *who you are*. If you are a compass-soul — if your fundamental orientation is homeward — then the prayer is not something you have to muster. It is something that *expresses* what you already are.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ said that the deeds most beloved to Allah are those that are *consistent, even if small* (al-Bukhārī). Consistency is not a feature of effort — it is a feature of identity. You consistently do what you consistently *are*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the human door back in.

Have you ever tried to change a behavior — and the change held for a few weeks and then collapsed? Have you ever resolved to pray on time, and managed it for a while, and then slipped back? Have you ever felt that exhausting loop — *I'll start again, I'll really commit this time, I mean it now* — and the cycle never stops?

There is a reason. You were trying to install a behavior on top of an identity that did not match it. The behavior was sitting on the surface, not anchored in the depth. Like a flag stuck in sand — every wind blew it over.

What this ayah teaches is that the work is **at the level of identity, not behavior**. Become a munīb. *Be* a person of return. And then the prayer, the taqwa, the rejection of shirk — these are not heavy lifts. They are exhalations.

The mystics of the tradition called this *istiqāmah* — uprightness as a constitutive condition. It is the same root, *q-w-m*, that gives us *aqīmū* in this very ayah. To establish prayer and to be upright are linguistically the same act, performed in different domains. Inside, you stand. Outside, the prayer stands. Because they are *one standing*.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means: stop trying to fix your behavior first. Fix your **orientation** first. Before you wake up tomorrow and force yourself through another set of obligations, sit for a moment and ask: *am I a person of return? Is my compass pointing home?* If it is, the rest will flow. If it isn't, the rest is going to keep collapsing no matter how hard you push it.

Practically: when you fall short — when you miss a prayer, when you slip — do not first ask *what did I do?* Ask *what am I oriented toward right now?* And if the answer is something other than Him, do not first try to change the behavior. Turn the compass. The behavior will follow the needle.

This is not lowering the bar. This is finding the **load-bearing wall**.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Negative Command and the Shape of a Whole Life

The ayah ends with something easy to skim past: *wa lā takūnū mina-l-mushrikīn* — "and do not be among those who associate partners with Him."

Notice how it is phrased.

Allah did not say *lā tushrikū* — "do not commit shirk." He said *lā takūnū mina-l-mushrikīn* — "**do not be of** those who commit shirk."

This is significant. The whole ayah has been about *being*, not just *doing*. *Munībīn* described a state of being. And here, at the close, Allah mirrors that structure on the negative side. Don't just *avoid the act*; **don't belong to the category**.

[PAUSE]

Here is what's happening. The ayah is bracketed.

On the front end: *munībīn* — a positive state of being you are *in*.

On the back end: *mushrikīn* — a negative state of being you must *not be in*.

Between the two brackets sit the three actions: taqwa, salah, no shirk in act. But the ayah architecturally communicates that the **actions are not the point**. The *category you belong to* is the point. The actions are how the category expresses itself.

You are not being asked to perform monotheism while remaining a polytheist at heart. You are being asked to **not belong to the polytheist class** — to not be of them, with them, among them, identified with them.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this gets uncomfortable.

Most of us think of shirk as a primitive sin — the worship of idols, the praying to statues, the bowing before stones. Things *other people* do. Things we have safely outgrown. We are sophisticated. We are monotheists. We have never bowed to a stone in our lives.

But the Quran's diagnosis of shirk is far more searching than that. Shirk is the giving of ultimate weight to anything other than Allah. It is the soul orienting around something that is not Him. It is treating a created thing as if it had the authority of the Creator.

And by *that* definition — which is the Quranic definition — who among us is sure we are not *of* the mushrikīn?

The status that organizes your decisions. The career that determines your worth. The relationship that becomes your unmoved center of gravity. The opinion of the crowd that bends your truth. The fear of an outcome that makes you betray a principle. The image of yourself that you protect at all costs.

The classical scholars called this *shirk khafī* — hidden shirk. Not the worship of stones. The worship of *significances*.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the ayah's architecture again.

*Munībīn* — your face is turned to Him.

*Mushrikīn* — those whose faces are turned to many.

This is **directionality**, not theology. Shirk is what happens when the compass needle no longer points one way. It points everywhere at once. It oscillates. It cannot settle. And so the mushrik can never have *iqāmat al-salah* — because *iqāmah* requires a single direction to stand toward. A compass that points everywhere stands nowhere.

The reason Allah linked *aqīmū-ṣ-ṣalāh* and *lā takūnū mina-l-mushrikīn* directly is not arbitrary. They are opposites. To establish the prayer is to give the soul one direction. To be of the mushrikīn is to deny the soul any single direction. **You cannot establish anything on a compass that will not settle.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the analogy.

Think of a sundial. A sundial works because it has *one* fixed direction to align with — true north. You set it once. From then on, every shadow it casts is meaningful — it tells time. The whole information system of the sundial depends on its singular alignment.

Now imagine a sundial that you have set to two norths. Or three. Or seven. It can no longer tell time. The shadows it casts mean nothing. The whole instrument is broken — not because its parts are damaged, but because its **orientation has been multiplied**.

A polytheist soul is a broken sundial. Not because it is doing nothing — it is casting shadows constantly. But the shadows mean nothing. The actions have no readable meaning. Because the orientation has been multiplied.

And this is why the ayah closes here. Because everything that came before — the return, the taqwa, the establishing — *all of it depends on the singularity of the direction*. The moment that singularity breaks, the whole architecture collapses.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means asking, gently and often: *what else is competing for the position of true north in me right now?* Not in theory — in practice. In the actual decisions I make, what is the gravitational center?

If something other than Him is bending my choices, distorting my speech, organizing my fears — I have not necessarily *done* shirk in the formal sense. But I may already be *of* the mushrikīn in the sense the ayah cares about. And the path back is not a new performance. It is a returned compass.

Become a munīb. Re-singularize the direction. The shadows of your life will start telling time again.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Look now at the ayah whole.

It opens with a **state**: *munībīn* — turning back.
It closes with a **state**: *mina-l-mushrikīn* — of the associators.

In between, three **commands** — taqwa, salah, and the negation of shirk-act.

The architecture is a ring. The states on the outside; the actions on the inside. And the entire ring is a single message: **the actions are downstream of the orientation**.

You cannot have taqwa without return.
You cannot establish prayer without return.
You cannot escape shirk without return.

Return — *inābah* — is the door. Everything else is a room inside.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us that *munībīn* is a state, that the pronoun *-hu* refuses to release its grip on the previous ayah's intimate naming, and that *aqīmū* is structural rather than performative.

The thematic side showed us that identity precedes behavior, and that direction is the substrate of every act.

These are the same insight. **Allah does not ask you to perform a religion. He asks you to *be* a certain kind of soul, and to let the religion be the natural shape that soul takes in the world.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When I think of repentance, do I think of it as an event I performed or as a state I inhabit?

2. What is the actual antecedent of "him" in my own life — what am I actually shielding myself regarding, when I am being careful?

3. Does my prayer *stand* in my life, or does it slouch — propped up by guilt, decorating the day rather than structuring it?

4. If shirk is multiplication of direction, what else has been quietly added as a second north in my decision-making?

5. Am I trying to fix behaviors, when the real work is at the level of orientation?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**You were not created to perform return as an act; you were created to be a soul whose default direction is home — and from that direction, prayer rises like a flag and shirk falls away like a shadow that has lost its sun.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow, before you do anything religious — before you pray, before you read, before you remember — sit for thirty seconds and ask yourself a single question: *am I turned toward Him?*

That is the work. Not the doing. The turning.

If the needle is pointing right, the rest of the day will be downstream of that needle. If the needle is off, no amount of forcing the right behaviors will fix what is fundamentally a direction problem.

Become a munīb. And then let everything you do be the shape that a munīb naturally takes.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who fashioned us upon the fitrah and called us back to it — make us *munībīn*, not by our striving but by Your turning of our hearts. Let our prayer stand because our orientation toward You stands. And keep us from being **of** any class but Yours, in act and in identity, until we return to You as we were first sent forth.

Āmīn.
