# The Beginning That Returns: Creation as a Round Trip

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 11

> ٱللَّهُ يَبْدَؤُا۟ ٱلْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُۥ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ
>
> *Allāhu yabda'u-l-khalqa thumma yu'īduhu thumma ilayhi turja'ūn*
>
> **Allah begins the creation, then He repeats it, then to Him you will be returned.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

To hear this ayah the way the first listeners heard it, you have to step backward into the moment. Surah Ar-Rum opens with one of the most strange and tender prophecies in the Quran. Rome — the Christian empire to the north — had been crushed by Persia. The Muslims in Makkah, a tiny minority being persecuted, were quietly hoping the Romans would recover, because at least the Romans believed in one God and a Day of Judgment. The Persians worshipped fire.

The disbelievers of Quraysh mocked them: *Your Romans are finished. Your God is finished. Your message is finished.*

And then comes this revelation: **the Romans have been defeated, but in a few years they will be victorious.** The very thing that looks finished is going to come back. The very civilization the Persians believed they had ended is going to rise again.

Then, after that opening, the surah quietly does something extraordinary. It takes that small earthly cycle — defeat that returns as victory, ending that turns out to be a beginning — and it lifts it. It widens it. It says: *what you just saw happen between empires? That is a tiny picture of what is true about everything.*

And in that widening moment, this ayah arrives.

### The Hook

There are seven words in this verse. Three verbs. And those three verbs hold the entire shape of your existence — past, present, and future — in a single arc.

But here is what I want you to feel before we open the words: in the Arabic, the *first* verb and the *third* verb are not parallel. Allah is the active subject of beginning. Allah is the active subject of repeating. But when we reach the third movement — *the return* — something shifts. The grammar tilts.

[PAUSE]

We will sit with that tilt. Because what Allah does with that final verb is the most merciful and the most terrifying thing in the ayah.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yabda'u* — The Verb That Means "To Start Something Brand New"

Before we open the Arabic, let me give you an everyday picture.

In English, we have two different kinds of "starting." If I say *I started the car*, I mean I turned on something that already existed. The car was sitting in the driveway. I just activated it. But if I say *I started a company*, I mean something different. I mean: before me, this thing did not exist. I brought it into being.

Arabic distinguishes these two kinds of starting with completely different roots. The root for "activating something pre-existing" is different from the root for "originating something brand new."

The word Allah uses here is **يَبْدَؤُا۟** — *yabda'u* — from the root **ب-د-أ**. And this root specifically carries the second meaning. It is not about activating. It is about origination. It is the kind of beginning where, before the beginning, there was nothing of this thing to point to.

Now, notice the tense. *Yabda'u* is present tense — what grammarians call *al-mudāriʿ*. In Arabic, present tense doesn't only mean "right now." It carries an ongoing, habitual, repeated quality. The most accurate translation is closer to: **Allah originates the creation.** Not did. Not will. *Does.*

Let's sit with this for a moment.

The Quraysh standing in front of the Prophet ﷺ would have heard the past tense if Allah meant a one-time event long ago. *Allah originated the creation* — back then, finished business, mountains in place, stars hung, move on. But that's not the verb form Allah chose.

He chose the form that says: **this is happening. Right now. As you breathe.**

Every cell dividing in your body. Every star being born in a nebula you cannot see. Every seed cracking under the soil in a field on the other side of the world. Every thought forming in the mind of a child who will be born tomorrow. The origination is not a single ancient event. It is a continuous outpouring.

Here's what is remarkable: many people imagine Allah's relationship to creation the way you might imagine a watchmaker. He built the watch, wound it, set it down, and now He is somewhere else while the watch ticks on its own. The verb *yabda'u* — present tense — closes that picture down completely. The watch is not ticking on its own. The ticking *is* Him originating it. The moment He withholds, there is no next tick.

[PAUSE]

So when we read *Allah begins the creation*, hear it correctly: **Allah is, this very second, beginning a new creation that did not exist a heartbeat ago.**

That's the first verb.

### Element Two: *Yuʿīduhu* — The Word That Carries the Idea of *Eid*

Now we come to the second verb: **ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُۥ** — *thumma yuʿīduhu* — "then He repeats it."

The root here is **ع-و-د**. And here's where I want you to slow down, because this root is everywhere in your Muslim life and you may never have made the connection.

This is the root of the word **ʿīd**. As in Eid al-Fitr. Eid al-Adha.

In classical Arabic lexicons, the root carries a beautifully precise meaning: **to return to a thing after having departed from it**. Not just to come back — but to come back to *something that was already there*. The departure was real. The return is real. And the thing being returned to has continuity with the thing that was left.

That's why Eid is called Eid. It's not just a holiday. It's a *return* — a return of a sacred state, a sacred day, the cyclical re-arrival of something we had departed from.

Now apply that to the verse. *Thumma yuʿīduhu* — "then He brings it back."

Notice something extraordinary. Allah is not saying: *I will originate a new creation later.* If He wanted to say that, He would have used *yabda'u* again. He would have said: *Allah originates the creation, then He originates another one.*

But He didn't. He used *yuʿīduhu* — He **returns** it. He brings *the same thing* back.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the deepest answers to the disbeliever's mockery in the Quran. The pagans of Makkah used to scoff: *When we are bones and dust, who will bring us back?* They were not denying that God could make new things. Their question was sharper: *how can the specific me — this particular nose, this particular memory of my grandmother, these particular tears I cried in this particular year — come back, when it has scattered into atoms?*

And the verb *yuʿīduhu* is Allah's quiet answer. **It will not be a new creation. It will be a return.** The same *you* that left the world will be the *you* that returns to Allah. Not a copy. Not a reconstruction. A return — in the deepest sense the root allows.

Let's stay with this. Because this is also why the morphology matters. Notice the form of the verb: *yuʿīdu*. This is **Form IV** — the *afʿala* pattern in Arabic, which characteristically carries *causation*. The root *ʿ-w-d* in its basic form means "to return" (intransitive — the thing returns on its own). But Form IV adds a causative layer: **to cause to return**.

In other words, you cannot return yourself. The departure was real enough that nothing in you has the power to retrace its steps. The return is something done *to* you. Allah causes the return.

And here is something to notice — a small gift inside the word. The same root that gives us Eid, the day we return to joy after a long discipline. The same root that gives us *ʿāda* — to revisit a sick person. The same root that gives us habitual practice — *ʿādah* — the patterns we return to over and over.

The whole semantic field around this root is gentle. It is not the language of catastrophe. It is the language of homecoming.

[PAUSE]

The disbelievers asked the question as if resurrection were a horror — a violent reconstruction of scattered debris. Allah answers them with the same root from which He named our holidays.

### Element Three: The Tilt — From Active to Passive

Now we come to the third verb, and this is where I need you to lean in.

The first two verbs are *active*. **Allah** begins. **Allah** repeats. Allah is the visible, named, foregrounded subject.

But the third verb — **ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ** — *thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn* — translates literally as: "then to Him **you are returned**."

*Turjaʿūn* is **passive voice**. You are the object of the verb. You are being acted upon. The active agent — the One doing the returning — is now grammatically hidden behind the verb. He is mentioned only through the prepositional phrase *ilayhi* ("to Him"), which is fronted before the verb for emphasis.

Why?

Let's teach the concept first. In Arabic rhetoric, the choice between active and passive voice is never accidental. Active voice foregrounds the *doer*. Passive voice foregrounds the *receiver* — or the *destination* — and lets the doer recede.

So in the first two clauses, Allah wants us to see Him as the One who begins and repeats. He is the artist; we are watching Him work. But in the third clause — the return — He shifts the camera. Suddenly, *we* are the foregrounded ones. *We* are the ones being acted upon. *We* are the ones with no power over our own motion.

And notice the word order in Arabic: *ilayhi turjaʿūn* — **to Him you are returned.** The destination comes *before* the verb. In Arabic grammar, fronting like this carries the force of restriction or emphasis. The most natural English equivalent is: **It is to Him — and to no one else — that you are being returned.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the moment the whole ayah opens.

In the first half of the verse, the universe is the subject. *Allah begins the creation, then He repeats it.* It's cosmic. It's about everything — galaxies, atoms, civilizations, ecosystems.

Then the camera pivots. Suddenly the universe disappears and there is only **you**. *Then to Him you are returned.* Not "it" — not the creation, not the cosmos. *You.* The plural *-ūn* is direct address. Allah is now looking at the listener and saying: *and you, specifically, are part of this pattern.*

The passive voice tells you something about your situation that you may not want to hear. You are not the one navigating yourself home. You don't get to choose whether to return. You don't get to schedule the return. You don't get to set the terms. The verb is passive because **the return is happening to you whether you cooperate or not.**

There is something terrifying in that — and something also unbelievably reassuring. Terrifying, because it means you are not in control of the most important journey of your existence. Reassuring, because it means the return does not depend on your strength, your wisdom, your spiritual fitness, or your ability to find your way. You will arrive. The current is taking you there. The only question is what condition you arrive in.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Look at what these three verbs have done together. *Yabda'u* — Allah originates. Active. Cosmic. *Yuʿīduhu* — Allah returns it. Active. Cosmic. *Turjaʿūn* — you are returned. Passive. Personal.

The first two are about the universe. The third is about you. And the grammar moves you from observer to participant before you have time to brace.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of your life.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Pattern of the World Is a Round Trip, Not a Straight Line

Modern people — and this is something deep in the bones of post-Enlightenment culture — tend to think of time as an **arrow**. A line. Things start, develop, progress, end. There's a beginning point and an endpoint, and the line between them never crosses back.

If you ask someone what their life looks like, they will draw a line. Birth on the left. Death on the right. A line in the middle that hopefully bends upward — career, family, accomplishments, legacy. Then the line ends. The end.

This is the deep imagination of the secular world. A straight line, going nowhere in particular.

Now let me show you what the ayah is doing.

It says: **Allah begins the creation, then He returns it.** That is not a line. That is a **circle**. Or more precisely — because Islamic cosmology is not actually circular, it is *spiral* — a journey that goes out and then returns to its origin, but the returning version is not identical to the leaving version. Something has happened to it in the meantime.

Think of breath. You exhale. The air leaves your body. You inhale. The air — *that same air* — returns. But the body that exhaled is not the body that inhaled. Oxygen was absorbed. Carbon dioxide was released. The return was real. The transformation was also real.

This is the shape Allah is sketching for the entire universe in seven words. Out from Him. Back to Him. *Inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi rajiʿun* — "to Allah we belong and to Him we return" — is not a funeral cliché. It is a description of the actual physics of existence.

Here is why this matters for how you live tomorrow.

If life is a line, then everything is a one-way departure. Every loss is a deletion. Every ending is a termination. Every dead person is genuinely gone. Every wronged person whose case was never heard in court is permanently un-heard. Every patient you nursed for years who slipped away anyway — slipped away into nothing. This is the spiritual shape of secular grief, and it is genuinely unbearable. People numb themselves with everything they can find because they cannot stand to feel the weight of a world where everything that begins simply ends.

If life is a return — a round trip — none of that is true. The loss is real, but it is not final. The ending is real, but it is the first half of a sentence. The wronged person whose case was never heard? They have a court date. The patient who slipped away? They slipped *toward* something, not into nothing.

[PAUSE]

The shape of the universe Allah is sketching here is the only shape that makes grief bearable without numbing it. You can feel the full weight of every ending because you know it is not the whole sentence.

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes what you call "the end." It changes what you call "wasted." That long phone call with your dying grandmother — if life is a line, it was a goodbye. If life is a round trip, it was a *prepayment*. You were investing in a relationship that has not actually ended; it has only paused. The patience you showed to the difficult parent who never thanked you — if life is a line, you wasted it. If life is a round trip, it was deposited in an account that pays out on the other end.

The ayah is not asking you to think about the afterlife as a far-off abstraction. It is asking you to re-shape how you understand *time itself* — and through that, how you understand every action you take today.

### Theme Two: You Are Carried, Not Steering

Now let's pick up the second theme, and I want to open it with something everyone has experienced.

Have you ever been on an airplane during turbulence? Real turbulence — the kind where the plane drops several hundred feet, the overhead bins rattle, and even the flight attendants look uncertain? What you feel in those moments is something very specific. It is the feeling of being **carried by something you cannot steer**.

You did not build the plane. You did not check its engines this morning. You do not know the captain. You don't know what altitude you're at, what speed you're going, or what weather is ahead. You are sitting in a metal tube being flung through the sky, and you have exactly zero input into the outcome. If the plane lands, it lands. If it doesn't, it doesn't. Your seat-clutching has no effect on anything.

Most people find this terrifying. But underneath the terror, there is something else: a strange, almost forbidden relief. *I am not in charge of this. I do not have to fix this. Whatever happens, happens to me — not because of me.*

Now hear the verb again. **Turjaʿūn**. *You are returned.* Passive voice. The captain of this flight is not you.

[PAUSE]

Modern Western life has trained us into one of the most exhausting illusions in human history: the illusion that **we are the active agent of our own existence**. That we are steering. That every outcome traces back to our choices. That if we just optimize harder, plan better, manage our brand more carefully, hustle longer, we will arrive at the destination we engineered for ourselves.

This is the prevailing psychological architecture of the modern person. It is also a primary cause of the anxiety epidemic. Because if you are the captain, every turbulence is your failure. Every loss is your miscalculation. Every illness is something you should have prevented. Every disappointment is evidence that you didn't try hard enough.

And then the ayah arrives, in passive voice, and says: **you are being returned.**

You did not begin yourself. (The first verb belongs to Allah.) You do not maintain yourself in being. (The second verb also belongs to Allah.) You do not navigate your way back. (The third verb is passive — it is happening *to* you.)

You are not the captain. You never were.

This is one of the hardest things in Islam for modern people to internalize, and one of the most liberating once they do. The Quran does not flatter our agency. It does not tell us we are the architects of our destiny. It tells us we are *carried* — that the most important motion in our existence is one we did not initiate and cannot stop.

Now hear me carefully, because this is where it would be easy to misunderstand. Allah is not saying you have no responsibility. The Quran is full of *commands* — do this, avoid that — and those commands are real. But the *motion home* is not yours. Your responsibility is what you do *while you are being carried.* It is what you pack. Who you forgive. Who you visit. What you read. How you pray. Those are the contents of your carry-on bag. But the plane is going where the plane is going, and you are on it whether you like it or not.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes the *texture* of your striving. The same prayer, prayed from "I am the captain and I must earn my arrival," produces exhaustion and despair. The same prayer, prayed from "I am being carried home and this prayer is how I prepare," produces peace and stamina. The actions can look identical from the outside. The internal experience is completely different.

It also changes how you handle the moments when life happens *to* you — the diagnosis, the loss, the door that closed, the rizq that didn't come. If you are the captain, those are crash landings. If you are being carried, those are the captain's adjustments to a flight path you don't see.

The passive voice in the third verb is one of the great mercies of this ayah. Allah is telling you: **you don't have to figure out how to get back to Me. I am bringing you back.** Your job is to be ready when you arrive.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the structure of the ayah and see what Allah has built.

**Allāhu** — *yabda'u-l-khalqa* — *thumma yuʿīduhu* — *thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn*

There are three movements. The first names **Allah** as the active originator. The second names **Allah** as the active restorer. The third names **you** as the one being brought. The frame begins with Allah's name and ends with the pronoun *you*. Between them, two **thumma**s — "then" — that don't just mark sequence in time but also mark a *rising stakes*: cosmic, cosmic, **personal**.

The ayah is shaped like a funnel. It opens wide — *Allah originates the creation*, the entire universe in scope. It narrows — *He brings it back*, focusing on the act of return. And then it closes around a single point: **you**. By the end of seven words, the cosmos has been pulled down to the listener's chest.

This is not accidental architecture. The grammar is the message. The ayah is *enacting* what it is *describing*. It is performing the return. It begins in vastness and ends in your face.

### Bringing the Discoveries Together

Look at what we found.

We found that *yabda'u* is present tense — Allah is originating creation right now, this second, not in some distant past. We found that *yuʿīduhu* shares its root with *Eid* — the return is a homecoming, not a horror. We found that the final verb tilted from active to passive — Allah is the One doing the returning, and you are the one being returned.

We then found two themes underneath: that the universe is a round trip rather than a line, and that you are carried rather than steering.

These are not two findings. They are one finding seen from two angles. The linguistic structure — present-tense beginning, *Eid*-rooted return, passive arrival — is exactly the same insight as the thematic structure — life as circle rather than line, life as being-carried rather than self-driving.

The grammar and the meaning are the same shape. That is what tadabbur is: the moment you realize the architecture of the Arabic and the architecture of reality are one architecture.

### Questions to Carry

1. If everything you began in this life will be returned to its source — including the version of you who began it — what part of what you are doing today are you confident will survive the return trip?

2. Where in your life are you exhausting yourself trying to be the captain of a flight you were never actually flying?

3. When was the last time you grieved something — a person, a relationship, a chapter of your life — and what would change if you began grieving it as a *pause* rather than a deletion?

4. The ayah names *yuʿīduhu* — the return — using the same root as the word for our holidays. What would it mean to begin imagining the Day of Judgment less as a courtroom and more as an Eid?

5. The grammar made you the object, not the subject, of the final verb. Are you living as if you are the verb's subject? What would surrender to being the verb's object actually look like, tomorrow morning, in your first decision?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Every breath you draw is Allah beginning you again, and every breath releases you a little further along a journey home that you are not steering and cannot stop — so the only question is what you are carrying when you arrive.**

### The Closing Invitation

You did not need to be told that you will die. Everyone knows that. What this ayah offered is something subtler — a re-shaping of the *shape* of your existence. From line to round trip. From captain to passenger. From "the end" to "the second *thumma*."

If something has shifted in you while listening, the invitation is small: let the shift outlive this reflection. Let it touch your next prayer. Let it touch the next time you feel the panic of not-being-in-control. Let it touch the next time you grieve something. Don't try to memorize the insight. Let it work on you the way a verse worked on the first Muslims — quietly, over days, until the shape of your inner life had reorganized around it.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who originate the creation moment by moment and return it to Yourself, return us to You while we are recognizable to ourselves and acceptable to You. Let us live as those who know they are being carried, and let our striving be the striving of guests preparing to meet a Host, not captains pretending to steer a sky we did not build. And when our return comes — *thumma ilayka nurjaʿ* — make it an Eid, not a reckoning we could not bear.

Āmīn.
