# The Joy That Belongs to Allah Before and After

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
فِى بِضْعِ سِنِينَ ۗ لِلَّهِ ٱلْأَمْرُ مِن قَبْلُ وَمِنۢ بَعْدُ ۚ وَيَوْمَئِذٍ يَفْرَحُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ بِنَصْرِ ٱللَّهِ

**Transliteration:**
*Fī biḍʿi sinīn. Lillāhi'l-amru min qablu wa min baʿd. Wa yawma'idhin yafraḥu'l-muʾminūna bi-naṣri'llāh.*

**Translation:**
"Within a few years. To Allah belongs the command before and after. And on that day the believers will rejoice in the help of Allah."

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Prophecy

To stand inside this ayah, you have to first stand on a dusty corner in Makkah, somewhere around the year 615 of the Common Era, and feel how the air tastes.

The Romans — Christians, People of the Book, monotheists in the eyes of the Quraysh — have just been defeated. Crushed. Their armies overrun, Jerusalem fallen, their churches burned. The Persians — Zoroastrian fire-worshippers, polytheists in the eyes of the Arabs — have swept through Syria, through Palestine, through Egypt. The greatest Christian power on earth looks finished.

And the Quraysh, who have been hunting and harassing this small community of believers in Makkah, find their argument ready-made. They walk up to the Muslims and they smirk. *Look. The People of the Book — your "cousins in monotheism" — have been crushed by the idol-worshippers. The fire-people have beaten the book-people. And we will crush you too, just as the Persians crushed the Romans. History is on our side.*

Imagine being a Muslim in that moment. You believe in One God. You believe revelation is real. And the news comes in: the great kingdom of monotheism has fallen. The geopolitical map confirms what your enemies say — that idolatry wins.

Then Allah opens this surah. *Alif Lām Mīm. The Romans have been defeated, in the nearer land. And they, after their defeat, will be victorious — within a few years.* [PAUSE]

Now stay with me. Because the ayah we are sitting with today — ayah 4 — is the hinge. It is where Allah does three things at once that the Quraysh could not see coming. He fixes a timeline. He claims the entire flow of history as His personal possession. And then He shifts — quietly, almost in passing — to the emotional life of a believer when news from the unseen finally lands in the seen.

There is something stunning hidden inside the smallest word of this ayah. There is something even more stunning hidden in what Allah does *not* say. And there is a relocation of joy happening here that, if you really catch it, will change how you respond to every headline you read for the rest of your life.

Let's sit with it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Biḍʿ* — When Allah Specifies Without Specifying

Before we open this word, let me ask you to picture something ordinary.

A friend asks you, *"When will the renovation be done?"* You could say *"in five months."* You could say *"in a year."* But often what we actually say is, *"in a few months."* That little phrase — *a few* — is not vague because we don't know. It is a specific kind of unspecific. *A few* means *more than two, less than ten.* It is a window. A bounded uncertainty.

Now hold that in your mind, and let's enter the ayah.

Allah says: **فِى بِضْعِ سِنِينَ** — *fī biḍʿi sinīn* — *within biḍʿ of years.*

The word **بِضْع** — *biḍʿ* — comes from a root meaning a piece, a portion, a part broken off from a larger whole. In classical Arabic lexicons it carries a specific range when used with numbers. *Biḍʿ* covers the numbers from three to nine. More than two. Less than ten.

Now notice something. [PAUSE]

Allah did not say *"in years"* — which would have been vague, open-ended, escape-hatched. The Quraysh could have waited fifty years and said *"see, nothing happened."* And Allah did not say *"in seven years"* — which would have pinned everything to a single date, and made the prophecy hostage to an exact calendar that even the believers might fail to track precisely.

Instead He chose the one word in the Arabic language that is **specific enough to be testable, and humble enough to honour the texture of how history actually unfolds.**

Here's what's remarkable. The believers now have a window. Three to nine years. Not vague enough to be unfalsifiable. Not narrow enough to be brittle. And when the Romans did defeat the Persians at the Battle of Issus in roughly 622 CE — within that window — the Quraysh could not say *"that's not what was promised."* The word *biḍʿ* had already drawn the perimeter.

But this is not just rhetorical precision. Stay with the meaning of the root — *a piece broken off from a larger whole.* What does that tell us?

It tells us that time, in this ayah, is not being chopped up arbitrarily. It is being **measured out from a larger whole that Allah already holds.** The few years are not a guess. They are a portion of His knowledge being released into history. *Biḍʿ* is the slice He chose to disclose. The rest of the timeline — the exact day, the exact season, the exact battle — He kept.

[PAUSE]

This is the texture of divine speech. Specific enough to commit. Unspecific enough to remind us who owns the calendar.

And it is the first answer to the Quraysh's mockery. *You think we don't know what we're saying? Here is a window. Watch it close.*

### Element Two: *Lillāhi'l-Amr* — The Sentence Structure of Total Ownership

Now we come to a phrase that, if you only read the translation, looks unremarkable. *To Allah belongs the command.* Fine. We've heard that before.

But the Arabic does something the English cannot replicate. And it does it with **word order.**

Let me teach the concept first, because this is one of the most important rhetorical features in the Quran and it shows up again and again.

In Arabic, the normal order of a sentence with *belonging* is: *the thing belongs to the owner.* Like saying, *the book belongs to Zayd.* But when Arabic wants to **restrict** the belonging — when it wants to say *the book belongs to Zayd and no one else* — it flips the order. It puts the owner first. *To Zayd belongs the book.* That fronting of the owner, in classical Arabic balāghah, carries the force of **exclusivity**. Grammarians call this *taqdīm mā ḥaqquhu't-taʾkhīr* — the fronting of what would normally come later — and it produces a meaning very close to the English *only.*

Now look at what Allah says.

He does not say *al-amru lillāh* — *the command belongs to Allah.* He says **لِلَّهِ ٱلْأَمْرُ** — *Lillāhi'l-amr* — *to Allah belongs the command.*

The owner is fronted. The structure is restrictive. And the meaning is: **the command belongs to Allah and to no one else.**

That alone would be powerful. But notice what is sitting next to the fronted owner. The word **ٱلْأَمْر** — *al-amr* — *the command* — carries the definite article **al-**. In Arabic, when *al-* attaches to a noun like this — what grammarians call *al* of *istighrāq*, the *al* of comprehensiveness — it can mean *the entirety of the genus.* Not *a command.* Not *some commands.* **The command** — meaning the whole category, every command, every decree, every directive.

Put it together. *To Allah — and to no one else — belongs the entirety of all command, all decree, all the unfolding of events.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is where it lands. Look at what surrounds this phrase: **مِن قَبْلُ وَمِنۢ بَعْدُ** — *min qablu wa min baʿd* — *from before and from after.*

And notice — Allah does not specify **before what** and **after what.**

Let's sit with this absence. Because this is a textbook case of what we should always do with the Quran: read absence as carefully as presence.

The mufassirūn offer several readings of *before* and *after*, and instead of choosing one, let me layer them, because each is a camera angle on the same reality:

- Before the Roman defeat, and after their victory.
- Before the Persians won, and after Allah will give victory to whomever He wills.
- Before any event in history, and after any event in history.
- Before this world, and after this world.

The fact that Allah left it open — *before* and *after* without a specified anchor — means the statement expands to fill every possible reading. The command was Allah's before the Romans fell. It is Allah's after they rise again. It was Allah's before the Persians ever sharpened a sword. It will be Allah's after every empire that has ever existed has turned to dust.

The Quraysh looked at the geopolitical map and said *the Persians are in charge now.* Allah is saying: *No power was ever in charge. The map you are reading was always Mine.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the staggering implication for you, sitting wherever you are sitting right now. Every news cycle that has ever frightened you. Every empire that has ever looked unstoppable. Every illness, every loss, every shift in your life that felt like control had slipped away from you — *lillāhi'l-amru min qablu wa min baʿd.* The command belonged to Him before it happened. The command belongs to Him after it happens. There was no moment, however brief, in which the wheel was being turned by another hand.

This is not a comforting platitude. This is a **claim about the architecture of reality.** And Allah locates it in the exact middle of the ayah — flanked by the timeline on one side and the believer's joy on the other — because everything before it and everything after it depends on it being true.

### Element Three: *Yafraḥu'l-Muʾminūn* — The Surgical Relocation of Joy

Now we come to the part of the ayah that, on first reading, sounds like a small emotional postscript. *And on that day the believers will rejoice in the help of Allah.* Sweet. Pious. Easy to move past.

Do not move past it.

Let me teach the device first. In classical Arabic balāghah there is a phenomenon called **iltifāt** — the *turn.* It is when the speaker, mid-flow, suddenly shifts perspective, or tense, or addressee, in a way that the listener feels almost like a camera move in a film. The Quran uses iltifāt constantly, and it is never decorative. The turn is always pointing at something.

Look at what happens across this ayah. We begin with **timing** — *within a few years.* We move to **sovereignty** — *to Allah belongs the command before and after.* And then suddenly, without warning, we are inside **the emotional life of a specific group of human beings** — *and on that day the believers will rejoice.*

The camera has zoomed. We were in the throne room of decree. Now we are in the chest of a believer. [PAUSE]

And here is where you have to look very carefully, because Allah is doing something with this word *yafraḥ* — *will rejoice* — that recurs all over the Quran but never more sharply than here.

The root **ف-ر-ح** — *farah* — means joy, rejoicing, gladness. It shows up in the Quran in two very different lights. Sometimes it is condemned — *innallāha lā yuḥibbu'l-fariḥīn* — *Allah does not love those who exult* (Quran 28:76, about Qarun). Sometimes it is commanded — *bi-fadli'llāhi wa bi-raḥmatihi fa-bi-dhālika fa'l-yafraḥū* — *in the bounty of Allah and in His mercy — in that let them rejoice* (Quran 10:58).

So what makes farah praiseworthy and what makes it blameworthy? The answer is always in what comes after the preposition **bi-** — *in.* What are you rejoicing *in*?

Qarun rejoiced in his wealth. Condemned. The believers rejoice in Allah's bounty. Praised.

Now look at our ayah. **يَفْرَحُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ بِنَصْرِ ٱللَّهِ** — *the believers will rejoice in the help of Allah.*

Notice what Allah does **not** say. He does not say *the believers will rejoice in the Roman victory.* He does not say *the believers will rejoice in the defeat of the Persians.* He does not say *the believers will rejoice that history vindicated their reading of geopolitics.*

Stay with this absence. Because everything in the moment was set up for the believers to rejoice in the Romans. The Quraysh had made it about the Romans. The whole argument was about the Romans. The whole timeline — *fī biḍʿi sinīn* — was the timeline of the Romans. The natural emotional response of any group whose enemies have been embarrassed is to celebrate the embarrassment.

Allah surgically removes that from them.

The believers will not rejoice in the Romans. The believers will rejoice in **the help of Allah** — *naṣri'llāh* — and again notice, the genitive structure here links the help directly to Allah Himself. It is not *naṣrun* (some help). It is not *naṣrun mina'llāh* (help from Allah, which would emphasise the source as separate). It is *naṣru'llāh* — **Allah's help**, as if the help and its Owner are inseparable, as if you cannot point at the help without your finger being on Him.

[PAUSE]

This is the relocation. The Roman victory is the **occasion**. The believer's joy is in something else entirely. The believer's joy is in Allah Himself — that Allah spoke, that Allah promised, that Allah delivered, that Allah is the kind of God whose word becomes history.

And here is what this means for us. The believers in Madinah on the day the news of the Roman victory reached them — and many narrations tell us this news arrived on the very day of Badr — did not celebrate that some Byzantine general had outmaneuvered some Persian general. They wept and rejoiced that **Allah had told them this would happen, and it happened.** The geopolitics was the stage. The drama was Allah's truthfulness.

Now contrast this with how we live. We rejoice in the win of our team. The defeat of our political opponent. The collapse of someone who insulted us. The downfall of a company we hate. Allah is here teaching the believer to **keep the occasion but transfer the joy.** Yes, the Romans won. But what you celebrate is not the Romans. What you celebrate is Allah.

This is one of the deepest disciplines of the heart in the entire Quran. And it is hidden inside one preposition — **bi-** — and one absent specification.

---

### Linguistic Bridge

We have seen three things. A word — *biḍʿ* — that holds time in Allah's hand and releases just enough into history to make the prophecy testable. A sentence structure — *lillāhi'l-amr* — that puts every event before and after under His exclusive ownership. And a verb — *yafraḥu* — whose object has been surgically redirected from the visible victory to the Sovereign behind it.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a believer is meant to receive any news at all, including the news arriving on your screen tomorrow morning.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The News Test — How a Heart Reads History

Picture how news enters your life today.

You wake up. You reach for the phone. There is something — a headline, a notification, a video clip. Somewhere a regime fell. Somewhere a war began. Somewhere a market crashed. Somewhere a celebrity disgraced themselves. Somewhere your team won. Somewhere your opponent lost.

What happens in your chest in the second after you read it? [PAUSE]

If you are like most people — including most believers — what happens is an immediate emotional reaction that fuses three things together. The **event**, the **meaning** we assign to it, and **who we credit or blame.** The reaction comes so fast that we don't even notice it is composite. We just feel relief, or fear, or vindication, or rage.

The Quraysh in Makkah were doing exactly this. The news arrived: *Persians beat Romans.* Their reaction fused immediately. *Idolatry wins. The People of the Book are weak. Our enemies — the Muslims — are next. History is on our side. The gods of fire are stronger than the God of the book.* All of that compressed into a single emotional package, delivered in a smirk.

Now look at what Allah does with the believers in the same news environment. He pulls the package apart.

First, He **slows the event down.** *Within a few years.* He removes the urgency. The news cycle insists on instant interpretation. Allah inserts time. *Don't read this moment as final. The frame is wider than the headline.*

Second, He **claims the meaning.** *To Allah belongs the command before and after.* The Quraysh thought the meaning of the news was *idolatry is winning.* Allah says, *the meaning of the news is Mine. Before it happened, and after it happened, I owned what it means.*

Third, He **redirects the emotion.** *The believers will rejoice in the help of Allah.* Not in the event. In Allah behind the event.

This is the news test. Every time something happens in the world, and you feel something rising in your chest — slow it down. Re-anchor the meaning. Redirect the emotion.

Try this with your own week. Something happened to you. Someone got the promotion you wanted. Someone you love got the diagnosis you feared. A friend's marriage broke. A family member came back to the deen. The moment the news lands, three things happen in you at lightning speed: you assign a meaning, you assign a cause, you feel a feeling. Almost always, the meaning is too small, the cause is too horizontal, and the feeling is misdirected.

Ar-Rum is teaching you to interrupt all three. The meaning is His. The cause is His. And if you feel joy, **feel it in Him** — that He is the kind of Lord who is doing this. If you feel grief, **feel it before Him** — that He is the kind of Lord whose wisdom holds this. The event is the same. The relationship to the event is everything.

What this changes in how you live tomorrow: **for one day, when news lands, pause for three seconds before reacting.** In those three seconds, ask: *who owned this before it happened? Who owns it after?* That single discipline, repeated, will slowly turn your nervous system into a believer's nervous system.

### Theme Two: The Architecture of Promise — Faith as Trust in the Word That Has Not Yet Closed

Here is something most of us have never thought about. Imagine you are a Muslim in Makkah, somewhere between the year 615 and the year 622. The ayah has been revealed. *Within a few years.* You believed the Prophet ﷺ. You repeated the prophecy to your family. You may even have gone to a few Quraysh and said, *watch — the Romans will win.* And then you waited.

Year one. Nothing. Year two. Persians still advancing. Year three. Romans still humiliated. Year four. The Quraysh laughing harder. Year five. Some believers wavering. *Did we hear it right? Is it taking too long?*

The fascinating thing about *biḍʿ* — three to nine years — is that it forced the believers into a **protracted exercise of waiting on Allah's word.** They could not say *it's only year two, who cares.* And they could not say *it's year ten, the promise has failed.* They had to live, every single day for almost a decade, inside an unclosed prophecy.

This is what īmān actually is. Not the easy faith of *I believed yesterday and the news today confirms it.* But the heavy, slow, patient faith of *I believed yesterday, and the news today is silent, and I am still believing.*

There is a Companion-era narration that on the day the Romans finally defeated the Persians — when the news arrived in Madinah — it coincided with the day of Badr. The believers received both pieces of news together: their own military victory, and the confirmation of the prophecy from years before. Allah does not just close the prophecy. He **stacks the joys**, so that you feel the weight of everything He has been carrying for you in the unseen all at once.

Now let me bring this home. You are also living inside unclosed promises. Allah has promised you that with hardship comes ease. He has promised you that He does not waste the deeds of the doers. He has promised you that whoever fears Him, He will make for him a way out. He has promised you Jannah. He has promised you reunion with those of your loved ones who believed. He has promised you that He will answer when you call.

Some of those promises closed quickly in your life. Most of them are still open. You are in your *biḍʿ.*

The question Ar-Rum asks you is: **what is your relationship to an unclosed promise?**

The Quraysh's answer was: *if it hasn't closed yet, it won't.* The believer's answer is: *it hasn't closed yet, and the One who promised owns the closing.* *Lillāhi'l-amru min qablu wa min baʿd.* The command was His before He spoke the promise. The command is His while you wait. The command is His when He closes the promise. There is no moment in which the wheel is turning without His hand.

And then — when He closes the promise — *yawma'idhin yafraḥu'l-muʾminūn.* On that day, the believers will rejoice. Not in the closing. In **Him** who closed it.

What this changes in how you live tomorrow: pick one promise of Allah's that has not yet closed in your life. Maybe it's the easing of a hardship. Maybe it's a child returning. Maybe it's healing. Maybe it's Jannah itself. **Name it.** And then, every time the waiting gets heavy, repeat these six words: *lillāhi'l-amru min qablu wa min baʿd.* The command was His before. The command is His after. You are inside the *biḍʿ*. The closing will come. And on that day, your joy will not be in the closing. It will be in Him.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Look back now at the ayah and notice its shape.

It opens with **time** — *within a few years* — the narrow slice Allah disclosed.

It centres on **sovereignty** — *to Allah belongs the command before and after* — the infinite frame around the slice.

It closes with **emotion** — *the believers will rejoice in the help of Allah* — the proper human response to time held inside sovereignty.

Time is bounded by sovereignty. Sovereignty produces joy. The ayah's architecture mirrors the architecture of a believer's life: every moment you live is a *biḍʿ*, a slice of a larger whole, held by a Lord who owns what was before and what comes after, and the only emotion that fits this structure is joy in Him.

### Bringing It Together

The three linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight from two sides.

*Biḍʿ* says: *time is a piece, not the whole.* Theme one says: *don't react to the slice as if it is the entire story.*

*Lillāhi'l-amr* says: *the command belongs only to Allah, before and after.* Theme two says: *trust the One who owns the closing of every promise you are still waiting on.*

*Yafraḥu'l-muʾminūna bi-naṣri'llāh* says: *joy is relocated from the event to the Lord of the event.* Both themes say: *your nervous system was made to land on Him, not on the news.*

### Questions to Carry

1. When news landed on you this week, what did you feel — and *what was the preposition* of your feeling? Were you rejoicing **in** the event, or **in** Allah behind the event?

2. Which of Allah's promises to you is currently still open? What year of *biḍʿ* are you in?

3. If *to Allah belongs the command before and after*, what does that mean about the moment in your life you most regret, and the moment you are most afraid of?

4. When was the last time you felt joy *in Allah Himself* — not in something He gave you, but in the fact that He is who He is?

5. The Quraysh read the geopolitical map and concluded *idolatry wins.* What map are you reading right now whose conclusion you should not trust until you've held it against *lillāhi'l-amru min qablu wa min baʿd*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The believer lives inside a slice of time owned entirely by a Lord whose word always closes — and when it closes, the believer's joy is not in the event, but in Him.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow morning, before you check your phone, sit for thirty seconds and say to yourself: *Lillāhi'l-amru min qablu wa min baʿd.* Then read the news. Watch what happens to your chest. The events will be the same. The frame will be different.

This is not optimism. This is not denial. This is the *farah* of the believer — a joy that does not depend on which empire rises today, because the One who raises and lowers empires has already told you He will close every promise He has made to you, and in the closing, you will rejoice in Him.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who own the command before every event and after every event — anchor our hearts in the slice of time You have given us, and let our waiting on Your promises be a worship of trust. Teach us to rejoice not in what happens but in the fact that **You** are the One who causes it to happen. And on the day You close every promise You have made to us, let our farah be in You, in Your help, in Your name, in You alone. Āmīn.
