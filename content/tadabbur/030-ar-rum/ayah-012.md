# When the Hour Stands and the Criminals Go Silent: The State of Iblīs

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 12

> وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ يُبْلِسُ ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ
>
> *wa-yawma taqūmu l-sāʿatu yublisu l-mujrimūn*
>
> **And the Day the Hour stands, the criminals will be struck into a silence of despair.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

To feel the weight of this ayah, you have to step back into the world that produced it.

Surah Ar-Rum opens with a prophecy so unlikely it sounds like provocation. The Byzantine Empire — Rome — has been crushed by the Persian forces. The Muslims in Makkah, a minority being persecuted in their own city, had been quietly hoping for Rome's recovery. At least the Byzantines believed in God and in a Day of Judgment. The Persians worshipped fire.

When Rome fell, the Quraysh mocked the believers. *Your Romans are finished. Your God is finished. Your message is finished.*

And into that mockery, the Quran says: the Romans have been defeated — but in a few years, they will be victorious. The thing that looks ended is not ended.

This is the surah's founding premise. Don't trust appearances. Don't read endings as endings or silence as emptiness. Look past what you can see to what the signs are actually pointing toward.

Then the surah widens. It takes the pattern — defeat that returns as victory, ending that turns out to be a beginning — and lifts it into something cosmic. What you just saw happen between empires is a small picture of what is true about everything. Creation begins and is repeated. Peoples rise and fall. Dead land is revived by rain. Sleep ends in waking. History is not chaos — it is signs, stacked on signs, all pointing in the same direction.

And in the middle of that widening, after establishing in ayah 11 that Allah begins creation and then returns it — after that pivot from history to cosmos — comes this ayah.

It arrives like a stamp being pressed into the argument: And on the Day the Hour stands — the criminals will be struck dumb.

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah that shares its root with the name of Iblīs himself. And that is not an accident.

When Allah says يُبْلِسُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ — "the criminals will be struck dumb" — He is choosing a verb whose root is the root from which classical scholars trace the very name of Satan. The criminals will do what Iblīs did. They will reach the state Iblīs reached.

[PAUSE]

But what is that state exactly? What does it mean to enter it? And what path leads there?

We are going to sit with this word until we see what it contains. And then we are going to ask the harder question: where in my own life is that path already underway?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### يُبْلِسُ — The Word That Names a State

Before we open this word, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work — because it changes everything about what يُبْلِسُ means.

Arabic verbs exist in forms. Think of them like gears. Form I is the base: he walked, he spoke, he sat. Form II intensifies: he walked intensely, he made someone speak. Form IV does something specific — it drives an action or a state to its logical and total completion. When the gear shifts to Form IV, there is no halfway. Whatever is happening, happens completely.

يُبْلِسُ is Form IV. From the root ب-ل-س.

Say it: yub-li-su.

Notice what happens in your mouth. The lips come together for the bā (ب). Then the tongue rises for the lām (ل). Then the sibilant exhales for the sīn (س) — and trails off. By the time the word is finished, something has closed. The word phonetically sounds like a door shutting in silence.

Ibn Manzur in Lisān al-ʿArab defines the state with striking precision:

*"أَبْلَسَ الرَّجُلُ إِذَا انْقَطَعَتْ حُجَّتُهُ وَتَحَيَّرَ لَا يَدْرِي مَا يَصْنَعُ"*

"A man has entered iblās when his argument is cut off and he is bewildered — not knowing what to do."

Three things in that single sentence. Three things arriving at once:

His **argument is cut off**. Not defeated in a debate. Not answered and refuted. Cut off. As if it was never real enough to survive the moment.

He is **bewildered**. Confounded. The ground he was standing on is not there anymore.

He **does not know what to do**. There is no action available. No next move. No strategy.

That is iblās. Not just sadness. Not just loss. All three — simultaneously — and permanently.

The classical scholars approach this word from slightly different angles, and each angle illuminates a different facet. Ibn Kathir emphasizes the **despair**: the criminals will despair of any mercy, any good, any relief. Al-Qurtubi emphasizes the **confoundedness**: they will have no excuse, no argument, no way forward. Al-Tabari emphasizes the **silence**: they will fall mute from grief, having absolutely nothing to say.

These are not three competing opinions. They are three cameras pointing at different parts of the same reality. Put the cameras together: **the criminals will be struck into a state where they despair of mercy, have no argument left, and fall completely silent. All at once. Forever.**

Let's sit with that for a moment.

[PAUSE]

Now. The connection you cannot unsee.

Al-Raghib al-Asfahani — the great classical lexicographer whose *Mufradāt* is the definitive dictionary of Quranic vocabulary — connects the name إِبْلِيس directly to this root. Iblīs is the one who entered iblās. The one whose argument was cut off. The one who was bewildered and left with nothing to do.

Think about what Iblīs did. He did not simply rebel in silence. He built a case.

He had a logical argument. He said: أَنَا خَيْرٌ مِّنْهُ — "I am better than him." He presented evidence: خَلَقْتَنِي مِن نَّارٍ وَخَلَقْتَهُ مِن طِينٍ — "You created me from fire and created him from clay." Fire is more active than clay. Fire rises; clay falls. His ontological reasoning was internally consistent.

And then — iblās.

Not because Allah needed to answer the argument. But because the argument was built over a gap — the gap between logic deployed to justify a refusal, and actual truth. When the structure was removed, there was nothing beneath it. No foundation. And he was left with nothing to say.

On that Day: يُبْلِسُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ. The criminals reach the same state.

This is not the surah calling them "like Satan" as an insult. It is the surah describing the natural destination of a specific road. If you spend a lifetime building arguments against the signs — weaving narratives to justify what you already know to be wrong — there is a place that path ends. It ends in iblās. It ends in Iblīs.

**The Form IV tells us there is no partial version of this. When the argument is cut off, it is cut off completely. When the confoundedness arrives, it arrives entirely.**

[PAUSE — a long pause here]

### تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ — The Hour That Stands

Now let's look at the other linguistic decision Allah makes in this ayah, one that sits at the other end of the sentence.

وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ — "And the Day the Hour stands."

The root ق-و-م is one of the most saturated roots in the Quran.

قَامَ: he stood, he rose, he was established.
يُقِيمُ الصَّلَاةَ: he establishes the prayer — makes it stand upright before Allah.
الْمَقَامُ الْمَحْمُودُ: the Praised Station — the place where the Prophet ﷺ will stand on that Day.
يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ: the Day of the Rising — the Day when everything stands before its Lord.

Standing, in Arabic, is not neutral. Standing is decisive. When a judge stands to pronounce a verdict, the courtroom goes silent — the standing is the announcement that something final has been said. When a witness stands to testify, it means: now the real account begins.

This is what تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ means. The Hour does not "arrive." The Hour does not "happen." The Hour **stands up**.

Like a witness who has been seated throughout history and now rises to give testimony.
Like a judge who stands, and everything in the universe falls silent.

Notice: Allah does not say يَوْمَ يَجِيءُ — "the Day it comes." He doesn't say يَوْمَ يَكُونُ — "the Day it is." He says يَوْمَ تَقُومُ — "the Day it stands." And that vocabulary — qāma — carries within it the entire weight of al-qiyāmah, the Day of Standing, the Day everything is brought upright before its Lord.

The Hour stands, and in its standing is an implicit command: everything else must respond.

And then: يُبْلِسُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ.

The Hour rises with authority. The criminals fall into silence.

**One standing. One falling. In a single breath.**

The symmetry is precise and devastating. The Hour stands — a word of authority, establishment, declaration. The criminals are struck into iblās — a word of collapse, bewilderment, the cutting off of all argument. These are not two separate events. They are one motion, described from two directions.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the linguistic construction has shown us. The Hour does not merely arrive — it stands, with all the decisive authority that word carries in Arabic. And the criminals do not merely despair — they are driven into a complete, total, Form IV state of confounded silence, the same state that is written into the very name of Iblīs.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### The Making of Iblīs

Have you ever been in an argument with yourself?

Not a philosophical debate. The internal argument — the one you conduct when you've done something you know to be wrong and you need to explain it to yourself. You build a case. The circumstances were unusual. Anyone would have done the same. The other person's behavior had provoked it. You're not that kind of person.

And for a while, the case holds. You walk through your days and it feels solid. The argument is accessible, well-structured, internally consistent.

But there are moments — they might be rare, they might arrive at 3am or in a flash of stillness — when the case goes quiet. When you can suddenly see through it. You can see that you built it out of the need to not see what you already knew.

That's a small iblās. A preview. A taste of what happens when the argument is cut off.

The key phrase in Ibn Manzur's definition is worth lingering on: انْقَطَعَتْ حُجَّتُهُ — "his argument is cut off." Not "his argument is defeated." Not "his argument is answered." **Cut off.** As if it never had roots. Because an argument built over a gap — over something you already know but have decided not to see — doesn't have roots. It has scaffolding.

And scaffolding requires constant maintenance. You run the argument again. You find new supports. You avoid the questions that would expose the gap. This is not unique to dramatic evil. It is the ordinary texture of how human beings manage the distance between what we know and what we do.

Iblīs was not performing this calculation cynically. He believed his argument. He brought it before Allah with full sincerity: fire is genuinely superior to clay, ontologically. He had thought it through. The problem was not the quality of his reasoning. The problem was what the reasoning was built to protect: a fundamental refusal to recognize the reality of what Allah had placed in Adam — a reality his hierarchy of elements could not account for.

When that structure was removed, there was nothing beneath it. And the bewilderment that followed was total.

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox worth sitting with: Iblīs did not fall permanently silent after his iblās. He kept speaking. He said: لَأُغْوِيَنَّهُمْ أَجْمَعِينَ — "I will mislead them all." He has been active throughout human history. So iblās is not the silence of all speech. It is the silence of one very specific kind of speech.

The speech of self-justification.

The speech that says: *I am actually fine.*

That speech stops. Because the argument it was built on — the scaffolding over the gap — has been removed. What remains is just the gap itself. The thing that was always known. The refusal that was always there beneath the reasoning.

On the Day the Hour stands, the mujrimūn can still cry. They can still beg. They can still say يَا لَيْتَنَا — "if only we had..." What they cannot say is: *I had good reasons.* That sentence — the sentence of self-justification — ends on that Day. Not because it is silenced from outside. But because the scaffolding that made it possible collapses from within.

And this is why the Quran does not describe the Day of Judgment as an arbitrary punishment raining down on people from outside. It describes it as the logical completion of paths that are already underway. The mujrimūn يُبْلِسُ because iblās is where their road was always going. They were getting there. The Day just makes it final.

Stay with me, because this is the part that actually matters for us — the ones who are still alive, still in the space where the door is open.

Every time we reinforce the internal argument — every time we run the narrative again, every time we avoid the stillness that would expose the gap — we are not just committing a single act. We are practicing a habit. We are building a pattern. The argument becomes more elaborate. The gap beneath it becomes deeper. And the heart becomes less capable of hearing the things that would stop the practice.

The Quran calls this rān — a rusting, a sealing, that accumulates. The mujrimūn did not arrive at their state on that Day. They arrived at it across a lifetime of small, quiet choices to not see what they already knew.

Which means the warning here is not about that Day. **The warning is about this one.**

What argument am I maintaining right now? What structure am I carefully keeping intact over a gap I already know is there?

[PAUSE — and truly let that question sit]

### From Voices to Silence

Surah Ar-Rum is, in the deepest sense, a surah about a world that is speaking.

It begins with a historical voice: Rome is defeated, but it will rise. Read the pattern, not just the moment. History itself is a sign.

And then, as the surah unfolds, the signs multiply in waves. Allah keeps saying وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ — "and among His signs." It becomes a rhythm, a pulse:

Among His signs is that He created you from dust.
Among His signs is that He created between you love and mercy.
Among His signs is the diversity of your languages and your colors.
Among His signs are your sleep and your seeking of His bounty.
Among His signs: the lightning showing you fear and hope; the rain descending and the dead earth reviving.
Among His signs: the sky and earth standing by His command.

Every dead field that comes back to life after rain is articulate. It says something. Every bolt of lightning that strikes while you are watching says something. Every human who falls asleep — entering what the Quran elsewhere calls "the lesser death" — and then wakes again is moving through a statement.

The world in this surah is a world that does not stop speaking.

And the mujrimūn moved through that world and chose — quietly, habitually, across years — not to listen.

This is what we need to feel. Not a dramatic, visible rejection. The refusal to listen is almost never loud. It lives in the decision to let wonder be brief. To encounter something astonishing — the precision of the cosmos, the complexity of a single cell, the fact that a human being can fall asleep and wake up again with their self intact — and to let it register as "interesting" without letting it become a question you follow.

We live in the most sign-saturated era in human history. The instruments we now possess to read creation are incomparably more powerful than anything available to any previous generation. We know that the observable universe contains more galaxies than grains of sand on every beach on Earth. We know the molecular machinery inside a single living cell contains more information than a library of books. We know that the physical constants governing the universe are calibrated with a precision so extreme that even tiny deviations would make life — any life — impossible.

These are signs. Not metaphors. Signs. Precise, articulate, unambiguous voices.

And for many people, including many of us, they produce not wonder but a kind of numbness. The information arrives and is filed. The heart processes it as data and moves on. Because somewhere along the way, a quiet decision was made: these things will be interesting, but I will not let them change the direction of my walking.

That is the condition the surah is describing. Not atheism as a formal position. Not an angry rejection. Just the long, accumulated habit of treating the signs as scenery rather than speech.

[PAUSE]

On the Day the Hour stands: the reversal completes.

The signs — the thunder, the rain, the lightning, the dead earth revived, the diversity of human faces — they have been speaking for 70 or 80 years of a person's life. They spoke without pause. They fulfilled their role completely. They pointed, and pointed, and pointed.

On that Day, they are not present to point again. The verdict is in. The testimony is complete. Everything the signs were gesturing toward is now present in its full reality — no longer signs needed, because the destination has been reached.

And the mujrimūn — who spent a lifetime not following the signs to where they pointed — discover, in that moment, that they have nothing to say about the reality they find themselves in.

يُبْلِسُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ.

**The world spoke. They did not listen. And in the end, they cannot speak.**

There is something in this symmetry that is not cruel — it is just. And it is not imposed from outside. The ear that refused to hear; the heart that refused to receive; the tongue that spent its life saying "not enough evidence" — these are the same faculties that, in the end, have no case to make.

The iblās of that Day is not a punishment added onto a crime. It is the crime completing itself.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Surah's Architecture

I want you to see where this ayah sits.

Surah Ar-Rum opens with an apparent defeat — Rome is crushed. Everything visible says: it's over. And Allah says: wait. Watch what actually happens.

The surah's entire argument rests on this: appearances are not the final reality. What looks finished may be gathering. What looks victorious may be collapsing. There is a pattern running beneath the surface of history, and the signs are there for those willing to read them.

The mujrimūn — in every era — are defined by this: they bet on appearances. They read the world at the surface and built their lives on what they could immediately see, without following the signs beneath the surface to where they were pointing. They looked at the dead earth and said: dead. They looked at their arguments and said: solid. They looked at the present world and said: this is what's real.

وَيَوْمَ تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ يُبْلِسُ ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ.

On the Day the Hour stands — appearances end. The thing the signs were always pointing to is now present. The surah's founding premise — don't trust appearances, follow the signs to where they lead — arrives at its destination.

And those who bet everything on what was always temporary find themselves in the state of the one who pioneered that bet.

### Bringing It Together

The two linguistic discoveries are one insight seen from two directions.

The Hour stands — تَقُومُ ٱلسَّاعَةُ — with the authority and decisiveness of a final declaration. It rises the way a judge rises, the way the Day of Standing is named after this very rising.

And the criminals are driven into iblās — يُبْلِسُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ — the complete, Form IV confoundedness in which the argument is cut off, the bewilderment is total, and there is nothing left to do. The state named after Iblīs. The destination of the road Iblīs pioneered.

One rising. One falling. Two directions of a single motion on that Day.

And both of them are already underway — in us, in the world around us — before that Day ever arrives.

### Questions to Carry

These are not rhetorical. They are genuine invitations. Take them into your day.

1. What internal argument am I currently maintaining — about myself, about a choice I have made, about why I haven't changed something I know I should change — that I could not sustain if I sat with it in silence for ten minutes?

2. Where in my life am I moving past signs without letting them speak? Not because the signs are unclear — but because I have decided, somewhere, not to follow them to where they point?

3. Is there something in me that is practicing iblās? Not all at once — but gradually, across small and repeated choices, building the habit of self-justification that, left unchallenged, becomes the only speech I know?

4. What does it tell me about Allah that He encoded a warning about the endpoint of Iblīs's path inside a verb describing the criminals — using the same root — so that anyone who reads the Arabic can see: this is where that road ends?

5. The Day the Hour stands, everything the signs were pointing toward will be present. Am I living as though that Day is real — or as though it is a distant metaphor that doesn't touch where I actually am?

### One Sentence

The Day the Hour rises with its final authority, those who spent their lives building arguments against the signs will be driven into the state of Iblīs — argument cut off, bewildered, with nothing left to say.

---

### The Closing Invitation

Iblīs reached his state, and the door behind him closed.

We have not reached ours yet.

The signs are still speaking — the rain, the complexity of what you are made of, the precision required for you to be here at all. The gap between where your argument starts to run thin and where you already know the truth is still crossable. The hearing that the mujrimūn chose, over years, not to do — you can still do it. Right now.

Not out of performance. Not purely out of fear of what that courtroom holds.

But because what the signs are pointing to is genuinely worth seeing. And because before the Hour stands, there is still this: the moment of stillness in which you choose to listen.

### Du'a

يَا رَبَّنَا — You surrounded us with signs that never stop speaking: in the rain, in the thunder, in the diversity of our faces, in every sleep and every waking. Do not let our hearts become so thick with argument that we can no longer hear what Your creation is saying.

Save us from the iblās of that Day — and whenever our arguments run thin in this life, let what remains be not bewilderment but surrender, not the silence of having nothing to say, but the stillness of one who has finally arrived at the truth they always knew.

Let us be among those who come before You on the Day the Hour stands — not speechless, but saying: رَبَّنَا اغْفِرْ لَنَا — Our Lord, forgive us. And You are the Most Merciful of those who show mercy.
