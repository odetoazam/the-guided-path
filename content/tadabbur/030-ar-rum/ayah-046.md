# The Winds That Carry Mercy

## On Reading the Signs Before the Rain

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to stand somewhere. We have to feel the wind on our face the way the first listeners did.

Picture this. You are standing in the outskirts of Madinah, or maybe on a road outside Makkah. The earth beneath you is dry. It has been dry for weeks. The crops you planted are leaning toward the sky, waiting. Your animals are restless. Your well is lower than it was last month. You don't have a forecast on a phone. You don't have a satellite tracking pressure systems. You have your eyes, your skin, and the wind.

And then one afternoon, the wind shifts. It comes from a different direction. It carries a smell — wet earth from somewhere far away. It carries a coolness it didn't have an hour ago. And your whole body knows, before your mind catches up: **rain is coming**.

That feeling — that the universe just whispered something to you — is what the original listeners felt when they heard this ayah. The wind, in their world, was not background. The wind was a messenger.

[PAUSE]

Now widen the frame. Surah Ar-Rum opens with one of the most astonishing political prophecies in all of revelation: the Romans have been defeated, but they *will* be victorious within a few years. A defeated empire restored. A reversal nobody could see coming. This is a surah about **hidden futures becoming visible**.

And then, throughout this surah, Allah keeps returning to the same phrase: *wa min āyātihi* — "and among His signs..." He points to your sleep. To your spouse. To the languages and colors of humankind. To the lightning. To the standing of the heavens by His command.

And here, in ayah 46, He points to the wind.

Why the wind? Of all the things in creation, why does the wind become a sign?

That is what we are here to discover.

**The Hook:** There is a word in this ayah that almost no English translation can carry. Allah does not say "that He may *give* you of His mercy." He says something stranger, more intimate. He says that He may let you *taste* it. And once you see what tasting means here — once you see why the wind is the perfect vehicle for that tasting — this ayah will never sound the same again.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Min Āyātihi* — The Grammar of Pointing

Let's start with the opening: *wa min āyātihi* — "And among His signs..."

Before we examine the phrase, let's teach a small grammatical concept. In Arabic, the preposition *min* often functions to mean "from" or "of" — but in a partitive sense. It means **"some of"** a larger whole. When you say *min al-mā'*, you don't mean "all the water." You mean "some of the water." A portion. A sample.

So when Allah says *min āyātihi* — "from among His signs" — He is not saying *these* are His signs, full stop. He is saying: **these are a sample**. A few drops from an ocean of signs.

Notice something. He does not say *hādhihi āyātuhu* — "these are His signs." That would be a closed list. He says *min āyātihi* — "from among His signs." That is an open list. Infinite.

Stay with this. The grammar itself is telling you that **what you are about to read is a window, not the whole house**. The wind is a sign. But so is everything else. The grammar is gently teaching you to walk through the world with your eyes open.

And then — *āyātihi*. His signs. The word *āyah* in Arabic does not mean "miracle" in the sense of magic. It means a marker. A sign on the road. A pointer that directs you elsewhere. The verses of the Quran are called *āyāt*. The phenomena of creation are also called *āyāt*. The grammar is making a quiet equivalence: **the wind on your face and the verse on your tongue are the same kind of thing**. Both are signs. Both point beyond themselves.

[PAUSE]

Here's what this opens up. Most of us, when we walk outside on a windy day, see only the wind. We see the wind as a fact. The Quran is asking us to see the wind as a **sentence**. To hear it as speech. Not the wind itself — but the One the wind points to.

What this invites us to consider: how much of creation are we treating as mere fact, when Allah has placed it in the world as a sentence to be read?

### Element Two: *Mubashshirāt* — Glad Tidings on the Wind

Now we come to the word that, in my view, is the heart of this ayah. The winds are sent as *mubashshirāt*.

Let's slow down. The root is *b-sh-r* — to bring glad tidings. From this root we get *bushrā* (good news), *bashīr* (a bringer of good news), and *bashar* (the human being, possibly because the human face is where joy first shows). This is a root of **announcement** — specifically, announcement of something good that has not yet arrived.

Now look at the morphological form: *mubashshirāt*. This is the feminine plural of an active participle from Form II — *bashshara*. Form II in Arabic carries intensification, repetition, or the doing of an action thoroughly. So *mubashshirāt* doesn't just mean "winds that bring good news." It means winds that are **announcing**, **proclaiming**, **heralding** — actively, intensively, in plural.

Picture what this is saying. The winds are heralds. They are running ahead of the rain like messengers running ahead of a king. They are saying: *something is coming*. Get ready. Open your hands. Open your fields. Open your hearts.

[PAUSE]

But here is where it deepens.

Notice what the wind is doing. The wind is **not** the rain. The wind comes *before* the rain. The wind is the announcement of mercy — not the mercy itself.

Stay with this. Why does Allah, in this ayah, focus on the *announcer* and not the *announcement*? Why not say "and among His signs is the rain that gives you life"? He could have. He says it in other ayahs. Here, in this one, He chooses the wind — the thing that comes *before*. The thing that says: *get ready*.

Because the wind is teaching you something the rain cannot.

The wind is teaching you **the spiritual posture of anticipation**. The wind says: mercy is coming, and you have a chance to prepare for it. The rain just arrives. You have nothing to do with it. But the wind invites you in. The wind gives you the gift of **expectation**.

Here's what's remarkable. The wind is mercy *announcing itself before it arrives*. Which means — if you train your senses — you can feel mercy on its way before you feel it land.

And that opens something staggering. **Every blessing in your life had a wind before it.** Every rain in your life — every relief, every opening, every answered prayer — had signs that arrived before the substance. The question is whether you read the wind or only noticed the rain.

What this invites us to consider: how many *mubashshirāt* are blowing through our lives right now — small announcements that something good is on the way — that we are missing because we only believe in mercy once it has soaked us?

### Element Three: *Wa-li-yudhīqakum* — That He May Let You *Taste* His Mercy

Now we reach the word I promised you in the hook. Allah does not say He will *give* you mercy. He says *li-yudhīqakum min raḥmatihi* — "so that He may let you **taste** of His mercy."

The root is *dh-w-q* — to taste. *Dhā'iqah* is the sense of taste. *Idhāqah* — the form used here — is the causative: to cause someone to taste, to give them a taste of something.

Now stop and feel this word. *Taste*. Not "give." Not "shower." Not "drench." **Taste.**

What does tasting imply, that giving does not?

First, tasting is **intimate**. You can be given a thing without ever experiencing it. A package on your doorstep. You can be drenched in something without registering it. But you cannot taste something without your body being involved. Tasting requires you to be present. Tasting requires the mercy to *enter you*.

Second, tasting is **partial**. A taste is not a feast. A taste is a sample. *Min raḥmatihi* — *of* His mercy. Not all of it. A portion. The grammar is whispering again: what you are about to receive is a drop from an ocean. The full ocean of His mercy is in the next life. What you taste here is the appetizer.

Third — and this is the heart — tasting is **incomplete on purpose**. A taste is designed to leave you wanting more. It's designed to point you toward the fullness it came from. The rain that falls on your field is not the whole mercy of Allah. It is a *taste* of His mercy — engineered to make you long for the Source.

[PAUSE]

Notice the asymmetry. The winds are sent as full *mubashshirāt* — heralds proclaiming intensively, repeatedly, in plural. But the mercy itself is just a *taste*. The herald is loud. The substance is a sip.

Why?

Because if Allah gave you the fullness of His mercy in this life, you would forget the Source. You would think the gift was the whole point. He gives you a taste — enough to know it is real, not enough to mistake it for everything — precisely so that your heart keeps turning back toward Him.

The dunyā is not a banquet. The dunyā is a tasting menu. And every taste is engineered to make you ask: *what is the chef like, that He made even this small thing this good?*

What this invites us to consider: what if every joy you have ever known — every taste of rain after drought, every moment of relief, every wave of love — was deliberately incomplete, so that your soul would keep moving toward the only place where the meal is finished?

---

### Bridge

So we have three discoveries. *Min āyātihi* — the wind is a sentence, one of an infinite library. *Mubashshirāt* — the wind is a herald that announces mercy before mercy arrives, teaching you anticipation. *Li-yudhīqakum* — the mercy itself is a taste, deliberately incomplete, designed to point beyond itself.

Three words. Three movements. Each one teaching you to read creation as speech and to receive blessing as invitation.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Spiritual Skill of Reading the Wind

Let me start somewhere you live.

You know the feeling, I think, when something good is on its way but hasn't arrived yet. You haven't gotten the call. You haven't seen the result. But something in you knows. A door creaks open in a way it didn't before. A conversation lands differently. A face softens that hadn't softened in a year. You can't name it. But your soul knows: **something is shifting**.

Most of us dismiss that feeling. We tell ourselves we're being optimistic. We're imagining things. We don't want to jinx it. So we keep our heads down and refuse to feel hope until the rain has actually fallen on our skin.

And then the rain falls — the good news comes, the relief arrives, the door opens — and we are *grateful*, but we are also *surprised*. As if we hadn't been warned. As if the wind hadn't been blowing for days.

This ayah is naming a skill we have forgotten. Allah is saying: **I sent the winds first**. The winds are not random. The winds are *mubashshirāt* — heralds I commissioned. Every mercy in your life had a wind. The question is whether you learned to read it.

[PAUSE]

What does it look like, in practice, to read the wind?

It looks like noticing when your heart softens toward a person you had hardened against — and recognizing that as a sign Allah is preparing reconciliation. It looks like noticing when a worry that used to consume you suddenly loses its grip — and recognizing that as a sign Allah is preparing relief. It looks like noticing when an idea you have been chasing suddenly arrives unbidden in the shower — and recognizing that as a sign Allah is preparing opening.

The wind is **internal** as much as it is external. The wind blows through your nafs. And if you train your senses, you can feel mercy on its way.

This is what the people of *taqwā* learn to do. They live with their face turned slightly toward the horizon. They are not anxious — anxiety is the wrong posture. They are *attentive*. They are reading.

Here is what's deeper. The wind is sent so that **mercy does not arrive as a shock**. Allah is so gentle with His servants that He does not want even His mercy to startle them. He sends a herald. He gives you days, sometimes years, to soften your hands for what is coming.

But this means something hard, too. If we have been missing the winds, we have been receiving His mercies un-prepared. We have been getting rain on hard ground. The water has been running off because we did not till the soil. The wind was there. We just didn't read it.

**Application:** Tonight, before sleep, ask yourself one question. *What winds did Allah send me today that I almost missed?* Don't ask what happened. Ask what was being announced. The thought that came at the right moment. The person who said the right thing without knowing why. The verse that fell open. These are not coincidences. These are *mubashshirāt*. The mercy will follow. The question is whether you will be ready when it comes.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Gratitude as the Architecture of Tasting

Now look at how this ayah ends. Allah lists four purposes for sending the winds: *li-yudhīqakum min raḥmatihi* (that He may let you taste His mercy), *wa li-tajriya al-fulk bi-amrihi* (that ships may sail by His command), *wa li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi* (that you may seek of His bounty), *wa la'allakum tashkurūn* (and that you may give thanks).

Four purposes. And the last one — *la'allakum tashkurūn* — is gratitude.

Now notice something. The first three purposes are *experiential*. You taste mercy. Ships sail. You go out and seek bounty. These are all things happening *to* you and *for* you. But the fourth purpose is something different. It is something that is supposed to happen *in* you.

The architecture of the ayah is this: Allah gives you tastings, voyages, and provision — *so that* you give thanks. The receiving and the gratitude are not two separate events. **The gratitude is the point of the receiving.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Why is *shukr* the final purpose?

Because *shukr* is not just saying "alhamdulillah" after the rain. *Shukr* is the **mechanism by which mercy reproduces itself**. When you taste mercy and respond with gratitude, you make yourself a receptacle that can receive more. When you taste mercy and respond with entitlement, you make yourself a sieve that loses what it was given.

Look at the asymmetry one more time. He gives you a *taste*. He asks you for *shukr*. The taste is small. The thanks should be full. Why?

Because the *taste* is not the gift. The taste is the *invitation* to the gift. The real gift is the relationship the taste opens — the chance to turn back to the Giver and say: *I see You. I see what You sent. I see who sent it.* That turning-back is the actual mercy. The rain on the field is just the occasion for it.

This is why ungrateful people stay thirsty even when they have everything. They have the rain but not the relationship. They have the substance but they missed the sentence. They got the package and threw away the letter.

And grateful people stay full even when they have little. Because every taste, however small, opens the door to the Source. They are drinking from the well behind the well.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this changes about how you live tomorrow.

Most of us treat gratitude as a *response* to mercy. Mercy comes; we say thanks. Linear. Transactional. This ayah is reversing the order. Allah is saying: I send the wind — so that you anticipate. I give the taste — so that you turn. **The gratitude is not the response to the mercy. The gratitude is what the mercy was for.**

Which means: if you do not practice *shukr*, you have not actually received the mercy. The package arrived. You signed for it. But you never opened the letter inside.

**Application:** Tomorrow, when something small lands — a meal that tastes good, a breeze that cools you, a message from someone you love — pause for one full breath before moving on. In that breath, do not just feel grateful. Do the *turning*. Recognize who sent it. The breath is the *shukr*. The breath is what the mercy was for. Without it, the rain ran off.

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Look back at the ayah now. *Wa min āyātihi an yursila al-riyāḥa mubashshirātin wa li-yudhīqakum min raḥmatihi wa li-tajriya al-fulk bi-amrihi wa li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa la'allakum tashkurūn.*

See how it moves. It begins with **sign**. It ends with **gratitude**. Between them: wind, tasting, ships, provision. The arc of the ayah is the arc of a soul. You see a sign. You read it as an announcement. You taste what it pointed to. You move out into the world. And you return, full, to the One who started the wind.

The ayah itself is a *mubashshirah*. The whole verse is wind. It is announcing that this is what your life is supposed to look like. Sign → taste → seeking → thanks.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Min āyātihi* taught us the world is a sentence. *Reading the wind* is what it looks like to live as if that is true.

*Mubashshirāt* taught us that mercy announces itself before it arrives. The *anticipation posture* is what it looks like to receive announcements with open hands.

*Li-yudhīqakum* taught us that the taste is deliberately incomplete. *Gratitude as architecture* is what makes the incompleteness into a doorway instead of a disappointment.

The wind is not just a meteorological event. The wind is **the way Allah does mercy**. He never gives without announcing. He never lets mercy strike a cold heart. He blows first. He gives you time. He gives you a taste. He asks for your thanks. And the cycle begins again.

### Questions to Carry

1. What winds is Allah sending through your life right now that you are dismissing as coincidence?
2. When the last mercy arrived in your life, did you receive it as rain on prepared soil, or as rain on hard ground?
3. If every joy you have known is only a *taste*, what does that mean about the meal that is coming?
4. Are you living with your face turned toward the horizon — or with your head down, refusing to hope until the rain has soaked you?
5. When something small lands in your day, do you take the breath of *shukr*, or do you move on and let the rain run off?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The wind is a sign because mercy announces itself before it arrives, and the taste is incomplete because the real gift is the turning-back to the One who sent it.

### The Closing Invitation

This is the shift. Stop waiting for the rain to be sure the mercy is real. Learn to read the wind. And when the taste comes — however small — do not swallow it without turning. The breath of *shukr* is what the whole verse was written for. Without that breath, the wind blew for nothing.

You are not living in a world of facts. You are living in a world of sentences. Allah is speaking through every breeze, every relief, every taste. The question is not whether He is speaking. The question is whether you have learned to listen.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who send the winds before the rain — train our senses to read Your announcements before Your mercies arrive. Let no taste of Your kindness pass through us without our turning back to thank You. And from the small tastings of this life, make us thirsty for the full meal of the next. *Āmīn.*
