# The Lightning That Splits Fear and Longing

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 24

> وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ يُرِيكُمُ الْبَرْقَ خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا وَيُنَزِّلُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَيُحْيِي بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا ۚ إِنَّ فِي ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَاتٍ لِّقَوْمٍ يَعْقِلُونَ

> *Wa min āyātihi yurīkumu-l-barqa khawfan wa ṭamaʿan wa yunazzilu mina-s-samāʾi māʾan fa-yuḥyī bihi-l-arḍa baʿda mawtihā. Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-qawmin yaʿqilūn.*

> "And among His signs is that He shows you the lightning, causing fear and hope, and He sends down from the sky water, then revives the earth by it after its death. Indeed in that are signs for a people who reason."

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Surah Inside a Storm

Before we open this ayah, we have to remember where we are in this surah.

Surah Ar-Rum begins with a defeat. The Romans — Byzantines — have lost a major battle to the Persians. The Quraysh in Mecca, watching from far away, are mocking the Muslims: *your fellow monotheists got crushed; your God's people lose; we side with the fire-worshippers who win.* And then Allah does something audacious. He says — in just a few years, the Romans will win. He stakes the credibility of revelation on a prediction about a war the Muslims have no involvement in.

That is how the surah opens. With a promise about reversal. About something dead coming back. About defeat turning into victory in the space of a few years.

And then, after that opening, Allah pivots. He stops talking about the war. He starts walking us through signs. Six of them, strung together like beads, each beginning with the same phrase: **وَمِنْ آيَاتِهِ** — *"And among His signs..."*

[PAUSE]

Now — pay attention to the *pattern* of the six:

Sign 1: He created you from dust.
Sign 2: He created mates for you, and placed between you love and mercy.
Sign 3: The diversity of your languages and colors.
Sign 4: Your sleep by night and your seeking by day.
**Sign 5: The lightning — fear and hope — and rain that revives dead earth.**
Sign 6: The heaven and earth standing by His command, and the call that will summon you out of the ground.

Notice what's happening. The first four signs are *inside* — inside your body, inside your marriage, inside your tongue, inside your sleep. They're intimate. They're domestic.

Then — sign five lifts your gaze up. The lightning. The sky.

Then sign six expands to the cosmos and ends with resurrection.

The surah is building. From dust → to your spouse → to your community → to your sleep → **to the sky** → to the standing-up of the dead. And the ayah we're sitting with today is the *hinge*. It's the moment the surah lifts your face from your own body to the heavens — and uses a single flash of light to teach you something about what it means to be alive in the world.

So here's what we have to be ready for. Allah is going to take one ordinary phenomenon — lightning — and use it to expose something about the human heart that you probably haven't noticed before. And He's going to bundle it with rain. And He's going to end with a word that should stop us cold: *for a people who reason*.

[PAUSE]

The hook is this: **Allah is about to describe one single thing that produces, at the same instant, in the same chest, two emotions that should not be able to exist at the same time.** And He's going to tell us this is one of His signs. Not a sign in spite of the contradiction — a sign *because* of it.

Let's open it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yurīkumu* — "He Shows You"

Before we get to the lightning, we have to slow down at the verb that precedes it.

In Arabic, there's a difference between *seeing* and *being shown*. The verb for "to see" is **ra'ā**. Simple. You looked, you saw. That's form one.

But this ayah doesn't use form one. It uses **yurīkum** — which is form four, the causative form. The shape of the word literally means: *He causes you to see.* He makes seeing happen to you. The subject of the verb is Allah; the seeing is something He does *to* you.

Let me teach this construction first, because it matters everywhere in the Quran.

In English, when I say "I see the tree," I am the active one. I have eyes; I direct them; I receive the image. Seeing is something I do.

In Arabic form-four causative, that whole architecture flips. The seer becomes the receiver. The shower becomes the actor. **"He shows you"** means: the lightning didn't just happen to enter your eyes; it was placed in front of your eyes by the One who made eyes and made lightning and made the alignment between them.

Now look at the ayah again:

> **يُرِيكُمُ الْبَرْقَ**
> *"He shows you the lightning..."*

Allah did not say: *"And among His signs is the lightning."* That would have been a statement about the *thing*. He said: *"And among His signs is that **He shows you** the lightning."* The sign is not the lightning itself. **The sign is the showing.**

Sit with this.

The sign is not in the sky. **The sign is in the relationship between the sky and your eye.**

If the lightning had flashed and there were no eyes to receive it — would it still be a sign? In the cold sense, yes; it would still exist. But Allah is not pointing at the existence of the lightning. He is pointing at the *event of you witnessing it*. He is saying: *I am the one who arranged for that flash, at that distance, with that timing, to enter your particular consciousness right now.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. We tend to read signs in the Quran as if Allah is gesturing at objects in the world like a tour guide — *look at this mountain, look at that river.* But the verb here corrects us. He is not pointing at objects. He is pointing at *the showing of objects*. The sign includes you. The sign is not complete without the seer.

Which means — if you walk through your life and never notice the lightning, the sign that was prepared for you has not arrived. It happened in the world, but it didn't happen to you. The verb is generous: *yurīkum* — He keeps showing you. But the showing has to land somewhere.

This is the first thing the ayah teaches before it teaches anything else. **The signs of Allah are not deposits in the world waiting to be discovered. They are encounters being arranged.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: *Khawfan wa Ṭamaʿan* — Fear and Longing, Side by Side

Now the lightning itself. And the two words that follow it.

> **خَوْفًا وَطَمَعًا**
> *"...fear and hope (longing)..."*

Two nouns, both indefinite, both in the accusative (*manṣūb*) — which in Arabic grammar typically functions as what's called a *maf'ūl li-ajlihi*, an "object of purpose" or "object of cause." The translation that most preserves the grammar is: *"He shows you the lightning **for the sake of** fear and longing"* — or — *"such that it produces in you fear and longing."*

Let me teach what these two words actually carry.

**Khawf** is fear. But classical Arabic distinguishes *khawf* from a related word, *khashyah*. *Khashyah* is fear that comes from knowing the greatness of what you fear — the fear of the knowing person. *Khawf* is more elemental. It is the body's fear. The fear that makes you flinch. The fear of being struck.

**Ṭamaʿ** is harder to translate. English versions often render it "hope." But *ṭamaʿ* is not the polite, distant hope of "I hope it works out." *Ṭamaʿ* in classical Arabic is **eager longing for something you actually expect to receive**. It's the leaning-forward of a hungry person when they smell food being prepared. It is appetite plus expectation. In some contexts it shades toward greed; in this context it is the holy, healthy form of the same root — the active reaching of a soul that wants the rain it can already smell coming.

Now hold both in your mouth at once. *Khawf wa ṭamaʿ*. Fear and reaching-longing.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want you to sit with. **Have you ever felt those two things at exactly the same time, in the exact same instant, about the exact same event?**

Think about it. Most of the emotions we live in are sequential. We are afraid, then relieved. We are excited, then disappointed. The clock of our inner life ticks from one mood to the next.

But lightning, in the desert, was different. Lightning meant a thunderstorm was coming. And a thunderstorm in the Arabian desert was the difference between dying of thirst and the land bursting back to life. It was the most needed thing in the world.

*And* — lightning could kill you. It could strike your tent. It could spook your camels. It could set fire to your stores. The same flash that meant water meant possible death.

So when the Bedouin saw lightning, what happened inside them was not "first fear, then hope." It was both, at once, undivided. The chest pulled in two directions in a single beat of the heart.

This is what Allah is naming. He is naming **the simultaneity**.

And notice — He didn't have to. He could have written: *"He shows you the lightning, sometimes to frighten you, sometimes to give you hope."* Sequential. Conditional. Clean. Instead He bundled them into one prepositional phrase with a single conjunction: *khawfan wa ṭamaʿan*. Fear-and-longing. As if they are one compound emotion. One state.

[PAUSE]

Now — why would Allah point this out? Why is the *simultaneity* the sign?

Here is what this invites us to consider. The relationship between a servant and his Lord, in the Quran, is described again and again with these same two words. Look at how the believers are described in Surah As-Sajdah (32:16): *"They call upon their Lord in fear (khawfan) and hope (ṭamaʿan)."* Look at Surah Al-A'raf (7:56): *"And call upon Him in fear and hope (khawfan wa ṭamaʿan)."* The exact same pairing. The exact same construction.

The lightning in the sky is teaching you what your prayer is supposed to feel like.

When you stand before Allah — you should be afraid. You should know what He can do. The same Lord who can answer your prayer can also withhold it. The same Lord who can forgive can also withhold forgiveness. The lightning that brings the rain can also strike. That is the *khawf*.

And — you should be leaning forward. You should be reaching. You should expect the rain. You should believe it is coming. The hand should be open. That is the *ṭamaʿ*.

A worshipper who is only afraid prays from despair. A worshipper who is only hopeful prays from entitlement. **The worshipper Allah is forming in this ayah holds both, at the same instant, in the same chest, about the same Lord.**

The lightning is rehearsal. Every flash is asking you: *Can you hold both? Can your heart contain fear and longing at the same time? Because the standing in prayer requires it.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: *Fa-yuḥyī bihi-l-arḍa baʿda mawtihā* — "Then He Revives the Earth After Its Death"

The third movement of the ayah is the rain — and the verb that does the work.

> **وَيُنَزِّلُ مِنَ السَّمَاءِ مَاءً فَيُحْيِي بِهِ الْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا**
> *"And He sends down from the sky water, then revives the earth by it after its death."*

Two verbs to notice here. **Yunazzilu** — "He sends down," in form II, which classical Arabic grammarians associate with *gradualness* or *intensification* (as distinct from form IV *anzala*, which can connote a single dispatch). The form-II verb often gets used in the Quran for revelation, rain, and tranquility — things that come down in stages, soaking in.

And then — **fa-yuḥyī**. "Then He revives." The *fā'* of sequence: rain comes, *then* — immediately, as a consequence — life.

And the noun that completes the picture: **baʿda mawtihā** — *after its death.*

Allah did not have to call dry land *dead*. He could have called it dormant. He could have called it dry, parched, thirsty. Arabic has words for all of those. He chose **mawt** — death. The same word used for human death. The same word that names what we fear most.

The earth was dead. Then the rain came. Then the earth was alive.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what this ayah has done in just three movements:

It showed us a flash in the sky.
It taught us the simultaneity of fear and longing.
And now it shows us a resurrection.

Because that's what this is. The earth dies. The earth comes back. Allah uses the language of death and revival — not metaphor, not approximation — to describe what rain does to dry ground.

The whole surah, remember, opens with the promise that Rome will rise again after defeat. The whole surah closes (we'll get there in verse 50, but the seed is here) with the explicit statement that *the One who revives the earth after its death will revive you the same way*.

So this ayah is doing something subtle. It's hiding resurrection inside a weather report. And it's telling you that the *yaqilūn* — the people who reason — are the ones who can see the resurrection hiding inside the rain.

The sign is not just that rain revives earth. The sign is that **the same Lord, the same act, the same pattern is happening to you.** You have been dead before — spiritually, in some seasons. You will be dead literally. And the One who sends the rain has a pattern. The pattern is: *death is not the end. The water always comes.*

[PAUSE]

There's one more thing in this verb to notice. *Fa-yuḥyī* is in the present-imperfect tense — ongoing, continuous, repeating. He doesn't just *revive*; He *keeps reviving*. Every spring. Every year. Every storm season. The resurrection is being staged for you, again and again, in slow motion, all around you. **The proof of the final resurrection is happening once a year outside your window.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So look at what the linguistic construction has given us so far. *Yurīkum* — the sign is the showing, the encounter, you are part of the sign. *Khawfan wa ṭamaʿan* — the lightning teaches you the dual emotion that prayer requires. *Fa-yuḥyī baʿda mawtihā* — the rain is a slow-motion resurrection rehearsed in the soil every year.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the chest that's supposed to receive it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Heart Was Built to Hold Two Things at Once

I want to begin somewhere outside the ayah.

Think of a parent watching their child leave for college. Or for their first day of school. Or step onto a plane alone for the first time. What is the parent feeling?

If you ask the parent, they will say something like: *"I'm so happy for them. And I'm terrified."* And if you press them — *which one is the real feeling?* — they won't be able to tell you. Because both are real. At the same time. In the same chest. Watching the same back walk away.

Or think of falling in love. The longing to be close to someone. And — woven into the longing — the fear of what it would mean to lose them, the fear that is born the moment love is born, the fear that exists *because* the love exists. You cannot have one without the other. The size of the fear is calibrated to the size of the love.

Or think of the morning before a major exam, or a major surgery, or a major decision. The reaching of the heart toward the outcome you want. And the dread of the outcome you don't. Both in your chest. At the same time. Pulling you in opposite directions and somehow holding you still.

**Here is what the modern world keeps telling us, and what this ayah quietly contradicts: that contradictory emotions are a sign of dysfunction.** Therapy culture, optimization culture, productivity culture — they all want you to "process" your emotions, "resolve" your ambivalence, achieve clarity. Pick a feeling. Commit to it.

This ayah says: **no.** The lightning is one of Allah's signs *precisely because* it shows you that the most truthful response to reality is dual. The heart was not built to feel one thing at a time. The heart was built — wide enough, deep enough — to feel two opposing things at once, and to know that both are right.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back to Allah.

The most common spiritual mistake is collapsing into one side. The person whose religion is only *khawf* — only fear — eventually breaks. They stop praying because they are sure their prayers are not accepted. They despair. The Prophet ﷺ said despair of Allah's mercy is among the gravest sins.

The person whose religion is only *ṭamaʿ* — only easy hope, only "Allah will forgive, don't worry about it" — eventually stops praying for a different reason. They never felt the weight that would make them stand up.

Both extremes end in not-praying. **The middle is where the believer lives. And the middle is not the average of the two. The middle is holding both at full intensity at the same time.**

That's why the ayah doesn't say *khawfan aw ṭamaʿan* — "fear or hope." It says *khawfan wa ṭamaʿan*. Fear *and* longing. *Wa*. The conjunction that joins. The conjunction that refuses to choose.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you sit in your own prayer. The next time you stand for ṣalāh — notice. Are you only afraid? Then you've forgotten the rain that always follows the flash. Are you only relaxed? Then you've forgotten the strike. Try, just for the duration of one ṣalāh, to hold both. To stand the way the Bedouin stood when he saw lightning. *I do not know if this flash brings the rain that saves me or the bolt that ends me. I am afraid. I am reaching. I am both. And I am praying anyway.*

That is the *ṣalāh* of the *yaqilūn* — the people who reason.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Quran Is Teaching You to Read the Sky as a Text

Now I want to turn to something even bigger.

There's a concept in classical Islamic thought called the *ayāt* — signs. The same Arabic word is used for two different things, and that's not an accident. *Ayāt* are the verses of the Quran. And *ayāt* are also the phenomena of the natural world. The same word. The same noun.

Why?

Because — in the Islamic worldview — both are *texts* by the same Author. The Quran is the verbal text. The cosmos is the visual text. Both are speaking. Both have meaning. Both are addressed to you.

[PAUSE]

This is what Allah is teaching us in this passage of Surah Ar-Rum. He is not just listing wonders. He is teaching us how to *read*. He is showing us that the sky is not decoration. The sky is sentences. The earth is sentences. Your spouse is a sentence. Your sleep is a sentence. The lightning is a sentence — and the punctuation is fear-and-longing — and the meaning is *I am the One who revives after death.*

The reason the ayah ends with **li-qawmin yaʿqilūn** — *for a people who reason* — is because reading takes work. The text is not hidden, but it is also not automatic. You have to apply your *ʿaql* — your reasoning faculty — to extract the meaning from the phenomenon.

This is what *istidlāl* means in classical Islamic thought — using the seen to reason toward the unseen. Allah does not say *anyone* benefits from these signs. He says *qawmin yaʿqilūn* — a *people* (a category, a community) *who reason*. The signs are placed for those who do the work of reading them.

[PAUSE]

Here is the modern bridge.

We live in an age of more visual stimulation than any generation in human history. We see more images in a single hour than our great-grandparents saw in a year. Lightning, rain, storms, sunsets, animals, mountains — all of it, on demand, in 4K.

**And we read almost none of it.**

We don't read the sunset as a sign of Allah's tenderness. We read it as content. We don't read the storm as a rehearsal of resurrection. We read it as weather, or as an inconvenience, or as material for the photo we will post. The phenomenon arrives. The sign-content of the phenomenon does not arrive.

The ayah is calling us back. It is saying: *the sky is a verse. Read it. Use your reason. The flash is grammar. The rain is conclusion. The death of the earth is the premise. The revival is the proof. The whole storm is a syllogism, and the syllogism's conclusion is — I will raise you up the same way.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

The next time it rains where you live — pause for sixty seconds. Just sixty. Stand by the window. Don't pull out your phone. Don't narrate. Look at the water coming down. Remember that the earth under that water was dead, and is about to be alive. Remember that you are made of the same matter as that earth. Remember that the One who is sending this rain has a pattern, and you are part of the pattern.

Sixty seconds. Once. That's the beginning of being one of the *yaqilūn*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Underneath

Look at what we've uncovered. Three linguistic discoveries — *yurīkum* (the sign is the showing), *khawfan wa ṭamaʿan* (the dual emotion held at once), *fa-yuḥyī baʿda mawtihā* (the rain as slow-motion resurrection). Two themes — that the heart was built to hold two things at once, and that the cosmos is a text we have stopped reading.

Now stand back. The architecture is this:

The ayah moves from **sight** (lightning) → to **emotion** (fear and longing) → to **transformation** (dead earth becomes living earth) → to **reason** (for a people who think).

That's the full arc of how a sign is supposed to work in a human being. **Sight begets emotion. Emotion is the doorway. Reason walks through the doorway and arrives at transformation.**

The lightning enters your eyes. *Khawf* and *ṭamaʿ* enter your chest. The rain enters the earth, and the earth — which was dead — begins to live again. And the people who reason watch this whole sequence and understand: this is what is being done to me. *I* am the earth. The lightning of Allah's word, the fear-and-longing of standing before Him, the rain of His mercy — and then, suddenly, I am alive after I had been dead.

The whole ayah is autobiography for anyone who has ever been brought back.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you encounter Allah in prayer, which of the two — fear or longing — is harder for you to hold? What would it mean for you to deliberately practice the other side this week?

2. Think of a season in your life when you were spiritually dead. What was the *rain* that revived you? Have you thanked the One who sent it?

3. When was the last time you watched a natural phenomenon — lightning, rain, a sunset, snow — and *read* it instead of just seeing it? What would change if you treated the sky as a text?

4. Is your relationship with Allah currently sequential (sometimes I fear Him, sometimes I hope from Him) or simultaneous (I hold both at once)? Which one is the *ṣalāh* of the *yaqilūn*?

5. If the lightning is rehearsal for the resurrection, what is being rehearsed inside you when you witness it?

### One Sentence

**The lightning is Allah's lesson that the human chest was made wide enough to hold fear and longing at the same instant — because that is how the believer is supposed to stand before Him, and that is how the earth is supposed to receive the rain.**

### The Invitation

The next storm that passes over you — don't let it pass. Stand at the window. Feel both. Let the *khawf* arrive without trying to push it away. Let the *ṭamaʿ* arrive without feeling guilty for hoping. Let them sit in your chest together, the way they were designed to. And then watch the rain fall. And know that the same Lord who is reviving the earth outside your window is reviving the parts of you that have been dead. The pattern is the same. The Author is the same. The text is being read to you in real time.

You are one of the *yaqilūn* the moment you start to read it.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, you who arrange the lightning to be seen by me — let me see it. You who placed fear and longing side by side in my chest — let me hold both, the way You designed. You who revive the earth after its death — revive in me what has died. And count me among the people who reason, and act on what they have reasoned, before the flash becomes the call.*

Āmīn.
