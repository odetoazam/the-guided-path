# When the Dead Cannot Hear: The Limits of Calling

## The Ayat

**Arabic:**
فَإِنَّكَ لَا تُسْمِعُ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ وَلَا تُسْمِعُ ٱلصُّمَّ ٱلدُّعَآءَ إِذَا وَلَّوْا۟ مُدْبِرِينَ ۝ وَمَآ أَنتَ بِهَـٰدِ ٱلْعُمْىِ عَن ضَلَـٰلَتِهِمْ ۖ إِن تُسْمِعُ إِلَّا مَن يُؤْمِنُ بِـَٔايَـٰتِنَا فَهُمْ مُسْلِمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
*Fa-innaka lā tusmiʿu l-mawtā wa-lā tusmiʿu ṣ-ṣumma d-duʿāʾa idhā wallaw mudbirīn. Wa-mā anta bi-hādī l-ʿumyi ʿan ḍalālatihim. In tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā fa-hum muslimūn.*

**Translation:**
So indeed, you cannot make the dead hear, nor can you make the deaf hear the call when they have turned their backs, retreating. And you are not the guide of the blind out of their misguidance. You cannot make hear except those who believe in Our signs — they are the ones who submit.

## Introduction: The Weight a Prophet Carries

Picture the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah. He has been calling for years. He has watched his uncle Abu Talib — the man who protected him, fed him, defended him against an entire tribe — die without saying the words. He has seen people he grew up with, people he loves, sit across from him with their arms folded and their faces set, and walk away. He has heard the mockery in the marketplace. He has felt the dust thrown on his shoulders.

And he is grieving.

Not the grief of a man whose feelings are hurt. The grief of a doctor watching a patient he loves refuse the medicine that would save them. The grief of someone who can *see* what's coming and cannot make the people he loves see it too.

Surah Ar-Rum has been doing something extraordinary leading up to this moment. It has been spreading out signs like a feast at a table. The shifting of empires. The alternation of night and day. The languages of humankind. The colors of skin. The mercy placed between spouses. The rain that revives dead earth. The ant-line of pattern after pattern after pattern, each one labeled: *a sign for those who hear, for those who think, for those who know.*

And then — these two ayat.

After all that — after Allah has poured signs into the world like light through every available window — He turns to His Prophet and says something that should stop us cold:

*Some of them are still dead. Some of them are still deaf. Some of them are still blind.*

[PAUSE]

What is this? After every sign has been laid out, why does Allah end this stretch by telling the Prophet ﷺ what he *cannot* do?

Stay with that question. Because what's about to unfold is one of the most tender and one of the most difficult consolations in the entire Quran.

## PART 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Verb That Allah Refuses To Let the Prophet Own

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we open this word.

Arabic has these things called *forms* — patterns that take a basic verb and twist its meaning. Form I is the base. Form IV — called *afʿala* — typically does something specific: it makes someone or something do the action. If *ḍ-ḥ-k* means "to laugh" in Form I, then *aḍḥaka* in Form IV means "to *make* someone laugh."

Form IV is the **causative** form. The form of *making it happen.*

Now look at the verb at the heart of these two ayat: *tusmiʿu*. From the root *s-m-ʿ*, hearing.

This is not "you do not hear." That would be *tasmaʿu* — Form I. The Prophet ﷺ obviously hears. He is the one *speaking*.

This is Form IV. *Tusmiʿu* — "you cannot **make** them hear."

[PAUSE]

Feel what just happened. Allah did not say to His Prophet: *you cannot speak to them*. He did not say: *you cannot reach them*. He did not even say: *you cannot teach them*.

He said: *you cannot make them hear.*

There is a wall in the architecture of the human soul that even a prophet cannot climb over. The Prophet ﷺ can deliver the message with the most beautiful tongue Allah ever placed in a human mouth. He can recite the Quran in a voice that froze hardened warriors mid-step. He can sit in front of you and weep for your salvation.

But the act of *hearing* — the act of the message actually arriving inside you and lodging there — that is not in the messenger's hands.

The Arabic distinguishes precisely between *samiʿa* (to hear, to receive) and *asmaʿa* (to cause hearing). It distinguishes between *the ear receiving sound* and *the soul receiving meaning*. The first happens automatically. The second is a separate act, and that act belongs to a different actor.

Notice something. The Prophet ﷺ is being relieved of a burden he was never meant to carry.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this for a moment. Because in our own lives we make the same mistake the Prophet ﷺ would have been forgiven for making. We think that if we just say it the right way, with the right words, in the right tone, at the right time — they will hear. We think the hearing is downstream of our skill at speaking.

And when they don't hear, we blame ourselves. *I should have said it differently. I should have waited. I should have been gentler. I should have been firmer.*

Allah is saying to His Prophet — and through him to every one of us who has ever tried to wake someone — *the causation of hearing was never your department.*

Your job is the *call*. Their hearing belongs to the One who made ears.

### Element Two: The Triple Diagnosis — Dead, Deaf, Blind

Now look at how Allah describes the people who do not respond. Not one word. Three.

*Al-mawtā* — the dead.
*Aṣ-ṣumm* — the deaf.
*Al-ʿumy* — the blind.

Why three? Why not one comprehensive word? When the Quran piles up descriptions like this, each one is doing different work. Let's open them one at a time.

**The dead.** *Al-mawtā.* Root *m-w-t*.

The dead are not refusing to hear. The dead *cannot* hear. There is no instrument left for hearing. The Quran is using death here as the deepest possible image of disconnection — not a person who *won't* engage but a person whose capacity for engagement has been extinguished entirely. The heart, the Quran teaches us elsewhere, can die while the body lives. A walking corpse with breath in its lungs and no soul reaching toward its Lord.

**The deaf.** *Aṣ-ṣumm.* Root *ṣ-m-m*.

Listen to that root. *Ṣ-m-m.* The Arabs used this root to describe a rock with no cavity in it — solid, sealed, no opening for sound to enter. *Ḥajar aṣamm* — a stone that cannot ring. The deaf are different from the dead. The dead have no instrument. The deaf *have* the instrument but it is closed shut. There is something there — there *should* be hearing — but the entry has been sealed.

**The blind.** *Al-ʿumy.* Root *ʿ-m-y*.

Different organ. Different problem. The deaf cannot receive what they *would* have heard. The blind cannot perceive what they *would* have seen. The signs are out there — but the eye that should catch them is dimmed.

Now stay with me. Watch what Allah is doing. He is not insulting these people. He is *describing* three distinct conditions of the soul's failure:

A person who has lost the capacity entirely.
A person who has the capacity but it is sealed.
A person who is wandering — has eyes, but cannot see what's in front of them.

Three failures of three different organs of the heart. And the Prophet ﷺ — the most powerful caller Allah ever sent — cannot reach any of them.

[PAUSE]

But notice something more. Notice the asymmetry.

For the dead, Allah does not add a condition. Just: you cannot make the dead hear. Full stop.

For the deaf, He adds something extraordinary: *idhā wallaw mudbirīn* — "when they have turned their backs, retreating."

Here's what is remarkable. Allah is not saying the deaf are unreachable in absolute terms. He is saying the deaf are unreachable *when they have turned and walked away.*

### Element Three: The Two Movements of the Walk-Away

This phrase — *wallaw mudbirīn* — is one of the most psychologically precise descriptions in the Quran. Let's slow down.

*Wallaw* comes from the root *w-l-y*. The base meaning is to turn — specifically, to turn so that your back is now facing what was in front of you. To give your back to something. This is the *internal* turn. The decision in the heart. The moment when someone you were facing becomes someone you are pivoting away from.

*Mudbirīn* comes from the root *d-b-r*. *Dubr* in Arabic means the back, the rear, what is behind. *Mudbir* is the active participle — someone who is in the process of moving with their back to you. This is the *external* walk. The legs are now carrying out what the heart already decided.

Do you see what Allah is showing us?

There are two movements in turning away from truth. First, the inner pivot — *wallaw*. The heart decides. Second, the outer departure — *mudbirīn*. The body executes. And Allah piles them on top of each other like two locks on the same door.

The deaf person — the one whose ear is sealed — is unreachable *when both locks are engaged*. When the heart has decided to turn, *and* when the body is now walking out, **then** even a prophet's voice cannot summon them back.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because there is something almost unbearably tender hidden inside it.

Allah did not say the deaf are unreachable absolutely. He said they are unreachable when they have turned and walked away. Which means — implicitly — that as long as someone is still facing you, even if their hearing is poor, even if they seem far away, the door is not yet sealed. The despair is not absolute. The closure happens at a *specific moment* — the moment of the turn plus the walk.

This is not a sentence of doom on every distant heart. This is a description of the exact threshold past which the messenger's voice no longer enters.

There is a final word of weight here: *ad-duʿāʾ* — "the call."

From the root *d-ʿ-w*. The same root as *daʿwah*. *Duʿāʾ* is not just speech. It is *calling* — calling toward, summoning, invitation. The Prophet ﷺ is not a lecturer. He is a *caller*. The whole project of revelation is *daʿwah* — an invitation extended outward, hoping to be received.

But a call that lands in the back of someone who is walking away — that call simply does not arrive. The ears are not facing the sound. The body language has already cast a vote. The call goes out and bounces off the receding form.

[PAUSE]

Bring it together. What has the linguistic construction shown us?

That Allah uses Form IV to release the Prophet ﷺ from a burden he was never meant to carry. That He diagnoses three distinct failures of the soul — total loss, sealed receptivity, wandering perception. And that He pinpoints the exact moment of unreachability — not as an absolute, but as a specific configuration: heart turned, back walking, call landing on no one.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Mercy of Being Released From Outcomes You Were Never Going to Author Anyway

Imagine you are a surgeon. You have been training your whole life. You walk into an operating room and the patient is on the table. You have the skill, the instruments, the training, the team. You do everything perfectly.

And the patient dies.

If you are wise, you eventually come to understand that the life of the patient was never in your hands. Your hands were in your hands. The decision to enter the operating room, the steadiness of your wrist, the cleanness of your cut — those belonged to you. But the *life* — the actual continuation of breath in a body — that belonged to the One who placed breath there in the first place.

A bad surgeon believes their skill *causes* survival. They take credit when patients live and they shatter when patients die. A wise surgeon does the work as if everything depends on it and surrenders the outcome as if nothing of it ever belonged to them.

Now apply this to the soul.

You have someone you love. A parent. A child. A sibling. A friend. You can see, with clarity, that they are walking toward something that will harm them — spiritually, eternally. You speak to them. You pray for them. You weep in the third part of the night for them.

And they keep walking.

If you are like most of us, you do one of two things. Either you blame yourself — *I must not have said it right, I must not have lived it right, I must not have prayed enough.* Or you blame them — bitterness creeps in, anger, a kind of contempt that hardens your own heart in the process.

Both of these responses share a hidden mistake. They both assume the hearing was supposed to happen as a function of *your* calling.

What these two ayat are saying — and notice that Allah is saying them to **His Prophet**, the greatest caller in human history — is: *the hearing was never yours to produce.*

[PAUSE]

The Prophet ﷺ is being given a mercy. He is being told that his grief over his uncle, his grief over the tribes who would not listen, his grief over the cousin who would not say the words — that grief belongs in Allah's hands now, not his. The Prophet ﷺ did everything. The call went out. The signs were displayed. The receipt of the call was always going to be processed in a department to which the Prophet ﷺ does not hold the keys.

You have to *feel* what this is doing to him. In the next surah Allah will say to him with even more directness: *innaka lā tahdī man aḥbabt* — "you cannot guide the one you love" (28:56). This is *that* same conversation, in a slightly different register. The Prophet ﷺ keeps trying to do something that is not his to do. And Allah keeps gently, repeatedly, lovingly relieving him of it.

Now here is the application. What in your life are you currently grieving as if the outcome belonged to you?

That family member who left the deen. That marriage that ended. That friend who turned cold. That child who is making choices you cannot bear to watch. You have been carrying the weight of their hearing as if you were the one who installed the ear.

These ayat are saying: *do the calling. Then let go of the hearing.*

This does not mean stop caring. The Prophet ﷺ never stopped caring. It means stop holding yourself responsible for an outcome that was never going to flow from your skill or effort or sincerity alone. The skill, effort, and sincerity were always yours. The reception belongs to Allah and to the heart He has either softened or sealed.

There is a deep, deep mercy in this. The mercy of being released from a burden that was crushing you because it was never yours to lift in the first place.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Final Sentence That Re-Opens Every Door

Look at how the second ayah ends.

*In tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā fa-hum muslimūn.*

"You cannot make hear except those who believe in Our signs — so they are the ones who submit."

If you read quickly, you miss the structure. Allah has just spent two ayat closing doors. The dead cannot hear. The deaf cannot hear when they walk away. The blind cannot be guided out of their misguidance. *Doors closing, doors closing, doors closing.*

And then He opens one.

*Except those who believe in Our signs.*

Watch carefully. The exception is not "the geniuses." Not "the eloquent." Not "the people lucky enough to be born into the right family." The exception is — *anyone who believes in Our signs.* Anyone who turns *toward* instead of *away*. Anyone whose heart has not yet turned its back.

And then notice the consequence: *fa-hum muslimūn.* "*So* they are the ones who submit." That *fa* — that little Arabic word — means "and as a result." The relationship between believing in the signs and being a *muslim* — one who submits — is causal. The belief produces the submission. The hearing produces the surrendering.

But the verbs here are in the present tense. *Yuʾminu* — "is believing." *Muslimūn* — "are submitting." This is not a one-time event in the past. This is a living, continuous orientation. *Those who are believing.* *Those who are submitting.*

Bring this back to a real human life. To yours.

The terrifying part of the first ayah is the thought: *what if I am one of the deaf? What if I am one of the blind?* And you would not be the first to have that thought. The companions had it. They wept in fear of being among those whose hearts were sealed.

But this final clause is a door. Listen to what it is actually saying. *You cannot make hear except those who believe in Our signs.* The path back from deafness, the path back from blindness, the path out of the walk-away — is to turn back, to face the signs, to receive them with belief.

[PAUSE]

The mercy here is staggering. Allah did not say "except those born into the right tribe." He did not say "except those who never sinned." He did not say "except those with a certain IQ or a certain education." He said *anyone who believes in Our signs.*

The signs are everywhere. The surah just spent dozens of ayat cataloging them. The rain on dead earth. The night and day. The mercy between spouses. The diversity of human language and color. The collapse of empires. *Look up. Look around. Look in.* The signs are saturating every direction. The only thing being asked is that you not turn your back.

This is what makes these ayat so radically *opening* rather than closing. The surface reading is: "some are unreachable." The deeper reading is: "the unreachability is precisely located, and the door is precisely defined, and the door is always — *always* — open to anyone facing toward it."

The threshold of being-unreachable is *wallaw mudbirīn* — the heart turned, the back walking. The threshold of being-reachable is *yuʾminu bi-āyātinā* — actively, continuously believing in His signs.

The first is a posture of departure. The second is a posture of arrival.

And in the time it takes to read this sentence, a heart can pivot from one to the other.

[PAUSE]

So here is the application — and it is for two different people.

For the caller — the one trying to wake someone they love — your job is to keep making the call. Beautifully, patiently, with mercy. But your job ends at the calling. The hearing belongs to Allah. Release yourself.

For the one being called — the one who suspects they might be one of the deaf or the blind — your work is the smallest, simplest thing in the world and the most consequential. *Do not turn your back.* That is the whole of it. Stay facing the signs. Stay reachable. Even if you are not sure you can hear yet. Even if the meaning has not yet landed. Just do not walk away.

Because the moment your face is turned, the call is still going out. And the door, in the same instant, is still open.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Look back at the two ayat now, with everything we have walked through.

The first ayah uses a verb (*tusmiʿu*) that releases the Prophet ﷺ from causation. The second ayah uses a verb (*hādī* — guide) that releases him from outcome. Together they form a complete release: *you cannot cause hearing, and you cannot author guidance.*

The first ayah describes the soul in two modes — totally lost (*dead*) and sealed-when-walking (*deaf*). The second ayah describes the soul in one mode — wandering (*blind*). Three diagnoses, mapped to the organs of receptivity: heart, ear, eye.

And then the final clause cuts across all of it: *in tusmiʿu illā man yuʾminu bi-āyātinā.* The exception that re-opens every door.

The architecture is: *release the caller. Diagnose the failures. Open the door.*

That is the entire shape of these two ayat. A consolation, a diagnosis, and an invitation — folded into one breath.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discovery — that Form IV verbs name a causation the messenger does not own — and the thematic discovery — that we are released from outcomes we were never going to author — are the same insight from two directions. One arrives through the architecture of Arabic verbs. The other arrives through the architecture of a grieving heart. They meet in the same place. The same release. The same mercy.

### Questions to Carry

1. Who in your life have you been holding yourself responsible for — as if their hearing were a function of your speaking?

2. Are there moments in your day when you sense your own heart performing the *wallaw mudbirīn* — the inner pivot followed by the outer walk-away — from a sign Allah has placed in front of you?

3. The signs in Surah Ar-Rum are everywhere — in the empires, the seasons, the spouse, the rain, the languages. When was the last time you stopped and let one of them actually land?

4. If "do not turn your back" is the smallest possible commitment a soul can make, what specifically does that look like in your week ahead?

5. What would change in your relationships if you fully internalized that your job is the *call* and the *hearing* belongs to Allah?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Your task is to call beautifully and stay facing Him; the hearing of others, and even your own, belongs to the One who made ears.

### The Closing Invitation

Let these two ayat lift something from your shoulders today. The weight of producing other people's transformation was never a weight you were assigned. Speak. Pray. Live the message. Then breathe. The hearing is in better hands than yours.

And for yourself — make the smallest commitment in the world, the one this ayah leaves open as the entire path home: *do not turn your back.* Stay facing the signs. The rest will come.

### Du'aʾ

O Allah, You who taught Your Prophet that the hearing of hearts belongs to You alone — release us from the burden of outcomes we were never meant to carry. Keep our faces turned toward Your signs even when our hearing is dim, and do not let us be among those who turn their backs and walk away. Make us, in every moment of our remaining lives, *muslimūn* — those who submit — through the door You have left open for any heart that believes in Your signs. Āmīn.
