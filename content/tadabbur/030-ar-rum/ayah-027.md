# And It Is Easier for Him

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 27

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:30:27] وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى يَبْدَؤُا۟ ٱلْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُۥ وَهُوَ أَهْوَنُ عَلَيْهِ ۚ وَلَهُ ٱلْمَثَلُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa huwa alladhī yabda'u al-khalqa thumma yuʿīduhu wa huwa ahwanu ʿalayh. Wa lahu al-mathalu al-aʿlā fī as-samāwāti wa al-arḍ. Wa huwa al-ʿAzīzu al-Ḥakīm.*

**Translation:**
> "And it is He who originates the creation, then repeats it — and that is easier for Him. To Him belongs the highest description in the heavens and the earth. And He is the Mighty, the Wise."

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, I want you to feel the room it was first spoken into.

Picture Makkah in those middle years. The Prophet ﷺ standing in the marketplace, in the courtyard of the Kaʿbah, on the path between the houses. And what is the loudest objection coming back at him? It is not, "Who is your God?" The Quraysh knew Allah. They swore by Him. The objection is something else, something more visceral, something that escapes their mouths in disbelief and laughter.

*"You are telling us that after we die — after we become bones, after we become dust, after the wind carries us across the desert and we are scattered into nothing — we will be put back together? **That**?"*

[PAUSE]

This is the scandal of the Quranic message in Makkah. Not monotheism. Resurrection. The Quraysh could imagine a high God. They could not imagine themselves reassembled.

And the Quran does something patient with this objection. It does not shout it down. It does not call them stupid. It walks them, again and again, through the world they already see. It says: look at the rain that descends on dead earth and makes it green. Look at the seed that splits into a tree. Look at the night that swallows the day and gives it back. Look at your own body, that was a drop, that became a clot, that became flesh, that became you.

The whole surah of Ar-Rum is built on this rhythm. Cycles. Returns. The rise of an empire, the fall of an empire, the rise again. *Wa min āyātih* — and among His signs — repeated like a heartbeat. Ar-Rum is the surah of *the second time.*

And right at the structural center of the surah, Allah drops this single sentence. A sentence the Quraysh would have heard and frozen at. A sentence that takes their objection — *bringing dust back to life is hard* — and answers it not by denying the difficulty, but by reframing the entire scale.

What if I told you that one word in this ayah is so grammatically strange that it has occupied scholars for fourteen centuries? That a single morphological choice — a single letter pattern — opens a door into how Allah wants you to think about His power, His mercy, and the second life you have been promised?

Stay with me. Let's enter slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to open three doors in this ayah. Each one is small. Each one is load-bearing. Together, they hold up the whole roof.

### Element One: The Verbs of Beginning and Returning

Look at the first part of the ayah:

> *yabda'u al-khalqa thumma yuʿīduhu*
> "He originates the creation, then repeats it"

Two verbs. *Yabda'u* — from the root *b-d-'*, to begin, to originate, to bring forth from nothing. And *yuʿīdu* — from the root *ʿ-w-d*, to return, to repeat, to bring back.

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we go further. In English, when we say "I begin" and "I begin again," we use the same verb with an added word. Arabic doesn't do that. Arabic gives you two completely different verbal architectures. One verb for the first time. A different verb for the return. The language itself **marks the two events as different in kind.**

Now notice something — both verbs are in the present tense, the *muḍāriʿ*. Not "He originated" and "He will return." But *He originates, He returns.* Present, ongoing, continuous.

Why does this matter?

Let's sit with this. If Allah had said "He originated the creation in the past, and He will resurrect it in the future" — the Quraysh could have said, *prove it.* The past is gone. The future hasn't happened. Both are out of reach.

But the present tense places this in a different register. It is not a one-time event in history. It is **what Allah is doing right now.** At this moment, as you breathe — He is originating. A child is being formed. A seed is splitting. A galaxy is condensing. The first creation is not behind us. **It is around us, continuously, every second.**

And if origination is something you can see happening right now, then the *return* is not an alien category. It is the same verb's natural partner. He who is originating — that One returns.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The Quraysh thought of creation as a finished event in the past. The Quran reframes it as a continuous present. And once you see it that way, resurrection stops being a strange, future, supernatural intervention. It becomes the natural completion of something Allah has been doing all along.

### Element Two: The Word That Stopped the Scholars — *Ahwan*

Now we come to the heart of the ayah. The phrase that has occupied tafsir for fourteen centuries:

> *wa huwa ahwanu ʿalayh*
> "and that is easier for Him"

The word *ahwanu* is what's called in Arabic an *ism al-tafḍīl* — a comparative or superlative form. It comes from the root *h-w-n*, which carries the meaning of ease, lightness, of something being slight or easy to bear. The form *afʿalu* — *ahwanu* — means **"more X than"** or **"easiest."**

Let me teach you what this form does. In English we say "easy / easier / easiest." Arabic compresses two of those into one form. *Ahwan* means: *easier than something else.*

So Allah is saying: *and it — the returning, the resurrection — is **easier for Him.***

[PAUSE]

Now stop. Sit with this. Because if you are paying attention, this sentence should ring a strange bell in your mind.

**Easier than what?**

Easier than the first creation. That's the comparison the grammar is forcing.

And immediately the theological mind protests. Wait. Is anything **harder** for Allah than anything else? Is there a scale on which some divine actions cost Him more than others? Surely not. Allah does not strain. The Quran tells us repeatedly that for Him to create is to simply say *kun* — *be.* There is no difficulty for Allah.

So why does He say *easier*?

This is exactly where the classical scholars stopped and looked at each other. And what they offered is not a list of competing answers. It is a single composite picture, drawn from multiple angles.

**First angle.** *Ahwan* here is spoken in a register the human mind can receive. Allah is not describing His own internal experience — He is meeting you in the only logic you have. **By your measure**, if originating something from nothing was already possible, then re-assembling something whose pattern already existed is, by every standard of human reasoning, the smaller task. The ayah is saying: *judge by your own scale. Even on your scale, the second is easier. So why is it the second that you find impossible?*

**Second angle.** The classical lexicographers note that *ahwan* doesn't only mean "easier." The root *h-w-n* also carries the meaning of *something being slight, light, not weighing on the doer.* In some usages it doesn't mean "less effort than" — it simply means "light, trivial, of no consequence." So the ayah can be read: *and it is utterly trivial for Him.* Not a comparison at all. A description of how slight this return is from the divine vantage point.

**Third angle.** Some scholars point out that *ahwan* is placed here as a rhetorical concession to the listener — *what you find hardest to imagine is, even by your logic, the easier of the two divine acts.* It is an argument, not a description of God's effort.

Hold all three angles together. They are not three options. They are three cameras pointed at the same truth: **the return that the Quraysh found impossible was, by every measure that exists — human or divine — the smaller act.**

Here is what the ayah is doing to its listener. It is not winning a theological argument. It is dismantling a psychological barrier. The barrier was: *resurrection is too hard to believe.* The ayah replies: *easier than what you already believe.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Mathal al-Aʿlā — The Highest Description

Now look at the next clause:

> *wa lahu al-mathalu al-aʿlā fī as-samāwāti wa al-arḍ*
> "And to Him belongs the highest *mathal* in the heavens and the earth."

The word *mathal* is usually translated "likeness" or "parable" or "example." From the root *m-th-l*, which carries the meaning of similarity, resemblance, comparison. And *al-aʿlā* — from *ʿ-l-w*, height, elevation — means **the highest, the most exalted.**

Now this clause sits here for a reason. We just spent a whole conversation about a *comparison* — easier than the first creation. We were *just* operating on a scale, measuring one act against another. And the moment that comparison closes, Allah turns and says:

*And to Him belongs the highest mathal.*

[PAUSE]

Let me teach you what is happening grammatically. Earlier the ayah used a comparison the human mind could process. Now the ayah closes the door on comparison altogether. *Al-mathalu al-aʿlā* means: **the highest description, the most exalted likeness, the attribute beyond which no attribute climbs.** It is not saying Allah has the *best* example among examples — as if He is the top of a list. It is saying His description belongs to a height that the list cannot reach.

This is a deliberate architectural move. The ayah just used a comparative — *ahwan*, easier than — to meet the listener at their level. And then immediately afterward, it withdraws the comparison and reminds you: *do not think I have actually been compared. I lent you a comparison because you needed one. My reality is above comparison.*

Notice the geography of the clause: *fī as-samāwāti wa al-arḍ* — *in the heavens and the earth.* Not just on earth. Not just in heaven. Across the entire architecture of existence — wherever you look, the highest description belongs to Him.

What is the relationship between this clause and the one before it? The previous clause said: even by your logic, the return is easier. This clause says: *and don't think your logic measures Me.* I came down to your scale so you could see the answer. But I am not on a scale.

This is one of the most beautiful pivots in the Quran. Allah condescends to argue with you in your terms — and then, before you mistake His condescension for His nature, He reminds you that His nature is higher than any term.

### The Bridge

So look at what these three elements have shown us:

The two verbs — *yabda'u* and *yuʿīdu* — placed the first creation and the return in the same continuous present. Origination is not a finished past event. It is happening now. Therefore the return is not strange.

The word *ahwan* — *easier* — met you on your scale and showed you that even by human reckoning, the return is the smaller of the two acts.

And the phrase *al-mathalu al-aʿlā* — *the highest description* — caught the argument before it became reduction, and lifted Allah back above every scale.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Asymmetry Between What Is Hard for You and What Is Hard for Allah

Let me start somewhere you live.

You have a friend who is a master craftsman. A carpenter, say. You watch him take a raw plank of oak — rough, splintered, no shape — and over weeks he turns it into a chair. He measures, he cuts, he sands, he joins, he stains. At the end there is a chair where there had been a plank.

Now imagine that chair, finished, is broken. It cracks at the joint, the leg snaps, the seat splits. You bring it back to him. He looks at it. **And what does he say?**

He says: *give me an afternoon.*

Why? Because the hard thing was never the wood. The hard thing was the **knowing.** Once he knew the chair — once it had existed once, once its pattern lived in him — putting it back together is not a fraction of the original work. He doesn't have to re-design it. He doesn't have to discover its proportions. He has only to **return to what he already knows.**

[PAUSE]

This is, dimly, what the ayah is doing. By a comparison your mind can actually hold.

The first time, Allah brought you from nothing. Not from a worse version of you. From **nothing.** No template, no precedent in your case, no prior version. Just *kun.*

The second time, the pattern of you already exists. Your soul has been recorded, your form has been known, your name has been written. The "return" is not a new construction. It is the meeting of two halves that are already in His knowledge.

So the ayah is asking you a question, gently. **Which of these two would you, with your craftsman's mind, find easier? The first or the second?**

The Quraysh, when they laughed at resurrection, were saying: *the second is impossible.* The ayah replies: *by every logic you use to evaluate any maker, the second is the smaller task.*

[PAUSE]

But there is a second movement here, and it goes deeper. Notice what the ayah is doing to your conception of what is "hard."

In your life, what feels hard? Healing from a wound. Repairing a broken relationship. Coming back to prayer after years away. Returning to yourself after losing yourself. **Returning.** That is what you find hard.

Because for *you*, returning is harder than beginning. A fresh start is easier than going back to fix what broke. You'd rather move to a new city than reconcile in the old one. You'd rather start a new religion than re-enter the one you abandoned.

The ayah holds up a mirror. **The thing you find hardest — return — is the thing that is, for Allah, easier than the original creation.**

What does that mean for you?

It means: the door you think is closed because you have already passed through it once and broken it — is the *easier* door for Allah to reopen. The relationship you think is impossible to repair, because too much has happened — is, in His logic, the smaller task. The version of yourself you think is gone forever — is the one He can return to you with less effort than He used to make you the first time.

This is the gift of the ayah, hidden beneath its argument about bodies. **Return is easier for the One whose attribute is return.** And you, the human, who feels that the second chance must be harder than the first — you are reading the universe backwards. With Allah, **the second is the easier one.**

What does this change about how you live tomorrow? It means that the prayer you have not prayed in a year, when you finally pray it, is not a heavier prayer because of the distance. The distance is yours. For Him, your return is *ahwan*. It is the lighter thing. It is the one He is, in some sense, *already doing for you* — because origination and return are one continuous verb in the present tense.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Two-Step Argument and the Mercy of Condescension

Now let's look at the **shape** of the ayah, not just its content. Because the shape itself is a teaching.

The ayah does two things in sequence that, on the surface, contradict each other.

**Step one:** It uses a comparison. *Ahwan* — easier than. It places Allah's two acts on a scale and says one is heavier, the other is lighter. This is the language of measurement, of relative weight, of the human mind that needs to compare things to understand them.

**Step two:** Immediately after, it says *lahu al-mathalu al-aʿlā* — *to Him belongs the highest description.* Which means: He is not, in fact, on the scale at all. The comparison just used was a translation, a courtesy, a stooping. The reality is above measurement.

Now think about this carefully. What kind of teacher does this?

I'll tell you what kind. A teacher who knows the student must use a tool to understand — and who lets them use the tool — but who, the moment the student grasps the lesson, takes the tool back so the student doesn't mistake the tool for the truth.

[PAUSE]

When a parent teaches a child what numbers are, they put apples on the table. Three apples plus two apples is five apples. The child grasps it. But a great teacher does not let the child grow up thinking that **numbers are apples.** At some point they say: the apples were a help. Numbers themselves are higher than apples. They live in a different place.

The ayah is doing precisely this. It says: *here is the apple* — *ahwan, easier.* Now you understand. Now look up: *the highest description belongs to Him.* I am not actually an easier-and-harder God. I am above ease and difficulty. But you needed the apple to see the lesson, so I gave it to you.

This is the mercy of the Quran. It speaks your language. It uses the categories of your mind. It compares, weighs, illustrates, narrates, exemplifies — all to meet you where you are. And then, again and again, it reminds you that the *language is a stair, not the destination.*

What does this mean for how you read the Quran the rest of your life?

It means every image, every parable, every comparison Allah uses about Himself in this book — sitting on a throne, having a hand, being closer to you than your jugular vein, descending in the last third of the night — every one of these is a courtesy. A scaffold. A way of saying: *I will speak in your dialect so you can find Me.* And every one of these is, simultaneously, surrounded by the warning of *al-mathal al-aʿlā* — *the highest description is Mine, beyond every dialect.*

This is how the Quran trains your heart. To use the comparison without trapping Him in it. To enter through the door of the image and then walk into the room of the unimagined.

And the practical application: when you read of Allah's mercy and you picture it as a vast ocean — use the picture. It is a gift. But know that the ocean is the apple. The mercy itself belongs to *al-mathal al-aʿlā* and exceeds every ocean your mind can contain. Your hope in His mercy should always be larger than the largest image you have of it. Because **the image is the lower mathal. He is the higher.**

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Look back now at the structure of this single ayah.

It opens with two verbs in the present tense — *yabda'u, yuʿīdu* — establishing creation and return as one continuous divine action. It centers on the word *ahwan* — placing those two acts on a scale and showing that, even by human logic, the return is the lighter. It closes with two divine names — *al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm* — **the Mighty, the Wise.** Mighty: the power to do it. Wise: the reason for doing it. Power without wisdom is tyranny. Wisdom without power is helplessness. Allah holds both.

And right between the comparison and the two names — *al-mathal al-aʿlā.* The hinge. The reminder that the entire comparative argument was a courtesy, and that His true description is higher than any scale.

The ayah's shape is its meaning. It descends to meet you, rises to remind you, and lands on a Name that holds both power and purpose.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes converge here:

- The continuous present of *yabda'u* and *yuʿīdu* showed you that **origination is happening now**, which means the return is not strange.
- The word *ahwan* showed you that, on every scale the human mind can use, **return is the smaller act.**
- The phrase *al-mathal al-aʿlā* showed you that Allah's reality is **above the scale altogether** — He stooped to speak in scales only as a gift to you.

The theme of asymmetry between what is hard for you and what is hard for Him took the word *ahwan* and pressed it into your own life: the return you find impossible is the return He finds easy.

The theme of the two-step argument took the structure of the ayah itself and showed you how the Quran teaches — using your categories without trapping Him in them.

It is one insight, seen from two directions: **what you cannot do, He does easily, because He is above the very logic by which you measure difficulty.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the return you have been telling yourself is too hard? The prayer, the relationship, the discipline, the self you abandoned? On whose scale is that return *too hard* — yours, or His?

2. When you imagine Allah's mercy, His patience, His attention to you — what image do you use? And do you allow that image to be the **apple**, while reaching for the higher *mathal* beyond it?

3. The Quraysh found resurrection impossible because they thought their body was the hard part. What in your life are you treating as the hard part — when the hard part has already happened, and what remains is the easier return?

4. Allah speaks in your dialect as a mercy. Are you mistaking the dialect for the truth — collapsing Him into images you can hold? Or are you using the images and then looking past them?

5. If origination and return are one continuous verb in the present tense — what is Allah originating in you, **right now**, that you have not yet noticed? And what is He returning to you, right now, that you have been calling lost?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The One who is originating you at this moment is the same One returning you at the next — and the return, by every measure your mind can use, is the easier of the two; though in truth, His reality is higher than ease and difficulty altogether.

### The Closing Invitation

Walk away from this ayah with one shift, if you can. Stop calling your return *too late.* Stop calling your second chance *too hard.* You are using a scale that does not apply.

The God who began you is the God who returns you. And the return — the small return of your heart to prayer tonight, the larger return of your body on the Day — is in His logic the lighter act.

So begin. The door you are afraid is too heavy for you to push back open is, on the other side, being held lightly by a Hand that calls reopening *ahwan.*
