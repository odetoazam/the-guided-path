# The Ingratitude That Comes After Rescue

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 34

> [ayah:30:34] لِيَكْفُرُوا۟ بِمَآ ءَاتَيْنَـٰهُمْ ۚ فَتَمَتَّعُوا۟ فَسَوْفَ تَعْلَمُونَ
>
> *Li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum — fa-tamatta'ū fa-sawfa ta'lamūn*
>
> **"So as to deny what We have given them. So enjoy yourselves — soon you will know."**

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Words

Before we step into this ayah, we have to walk into the moment it answers. Because this verse — only seven words long in Arabic — is not the beginning of a thought. It is the verdict at the end of one.

Read the two verses before it, and listen to the human pattern Allah is tracing.

In verse 33, Allah says: *"And when harm touches people, they call upon their Lord, turning to Him in repentance. Then when He gives them a taste of mercy from Himself, a portion of them associate others with their Lord."*

So picture it. A person is broken. The diagnosis comes back. The marriage collapses. The business goes under. The plane shakes. The child stops breathing. And in that moment — *that* moment — something happens that they did not choose and could not have manufactured. Their hands lift. Their heart turns. They cry out, not to a list of names, but to **their Lord**. Stripped of pretense. Stripped of partners. One soul, one Master.

Then Allah lifts the harm. The diagnosis was wrong. The marriage holds. The plane lands. The child breathes.

And the very same hands that lifted in pure tawhid — those same hands — go back to building partners next to Allah. Back to giving credit to the doctor, the spouse, the pilot, the luck. Back to dividing the heart that had, for one shining moment, been completely whole.

That is the moment this verse responds to.

This is a surah named **Ar-Rum** — "The Romans." A surah whose opening promises that defeat will turn to victory, that what looks lost will be returned. A surah obsessed with the **turn**, with how Allah reverses conditions. And right here, in the middle of it, Allah turns His attention to the turn that happens *inside* a human heart — and how that turn so often goes the wrong way.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. The verse we are about to open is not a description. It is a **purpose clause** — Allah is telling us *why* this person reverts. And then, having told us why, He says something that should make every one of us go quiet: **"So enjoy yourselves. Soon you will know."**

Allah does not stop them. He lets them go. And He lets them go *in a particular tone of voice*.

What is that tone? And why is the most frightening thing in this verse the fact that Allah does not raise His? That is what we are here to sit with.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element 1: The Lām That Reveals a Purpose Hidden From the Person Themselves

Let me teach you a small grammatical thing before we step into the words. In Arabic, there is a particle called the **lām** — just a single letter, لِ — that attaches to the front of a verb. Depending on context, it can mean *"in order to..."* or *"so that..."* or *"with the result that..."*

Think of how we use this in English. *"I picked up the phone to call my mother."* The *"to"* there explains my purpose. It tells you what I was aiming at. The whole previous action makes sense in light of that *"to."*

Now there is a special use of this lām that the classical Arabic grammarians named carefully. They called it **lām al-ʿāqibah** — the *lām of consequence* or the *lām of outcome*. It looks identical to the lām of purpose, but it does something different. It marks an outcome the actor never consciously intended, but which their behavior reveals as the true direction they were heading all along.

A modern example. Imagine a parent who indulges their child in every craving, every tantrum, every demand. If we said, *"They spoiled the child to ruin them,"* no one would say the parent *meant* to ruin them. But the outcome reveals what the behavior was actually aimed at, even if the actor would deny it. The behavior *functioned as if* ruin were the goal.

That is lām al-ʿāqibah. The grammar of revealed direction.

Now look at this verse. After Allah describes the person crying out in distress, being rescued, and then turning back to associating partners with Him, He says:

> **لِيَكْفُرُوا بِمَآ ءَاتَيْنَـٰهُمْ**
>
> *Li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum* — *"so as to deny / so that they deny what We have given them."*

That **li-** at the front of *yakfurū* — that is the particle we just described.

Let's sit with this. Did the person, when they were rescued, consciously say to themselves, *"My goal here is to deny what God just gave me"*? Of course not. They probably said, *"What a relief, I got lucky, the doctor was great, my own effort paid off."*

But Allah, who sees the architecture of the human soul from above, is saying something staggering. **Whether or not they articulated it, the structure of their behavior was aimed at this.** The functional purpose of returning to shirk after being rescued *is* to bury the gift.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah is not just describing what they did. He is reading their action back to them at a depth they themselves cannot see. He is saying: *I know what your behavior was actually for, even if you don't.*

[PAUSE]

Now let me give you the second layer.

The verb here is *yakfurū* — from the root **k-f-r**. We translate it loosely as "to disbelieve" or "to deny." But the classical lexicons preserve the original physical meaning. **K-f-r originally meant *to cover, to bury, to conceal.*** A farmer who covers a seed with soil is called a *kāfir* in old Arabic — not as an insult, but as a description. He buries the seed.

So when Allah says *li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum*, the picture in the original Arabic is not abstract intellectual denial. The picture is: **so as to bury what We have given them.**

Stay with me. Allah gave them a gift — *bimā ātaynāhum*. The relief. The rescue. The lifted harm. And the person, by returning to shirk, is taking that gift and **pushing soil over it.** Like burying a body. Like hiding evidence.

Why would anyone bury a gift?

Because the gift is a *witness*. When Allah lifts your harm in that moment of total tawhid, the gift is also a piece of testimony. It says: *"He answered. There is no one else."* To continue living as if there are partners, you must first **bury the witness.** You must cover the moment with enough noise — enough other explanations, enough coincidences, enough I-handled-it — that the testimony stops accusing you.

Kufr, in this verse, is not a position taken at a podium. It is a **shovel** in the dark, pressed against a small, bright thing that was given to you, pushing it down until you cannot see its light anymore.

That is what Allah says the structure of their behavior was aimed at — whether they knew it or not.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element 2: The Word That Sounds Like Indulgence — *tamatta'ū*

Now look at what comes next.

> **فَتَمَتَّعُوا۟**
>
> *Fa-tamatta'ū* — *"So enjoy yourselves."*

Before we open this word, I want to teach you something about Arabic verb forms, because this word's shape is doing real work.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns. The simplest pattern — Form I — describes the basic action. *Kataba* means "he wrote." But when you double the middle letter, you move into Form II, which often intensifies the action. *Kattaba* means "he made someone write" or "he wrote extensively."

There is another form — Form V — built by adding a *ta-* at the front *and* doubling the middle letter. Form V often carries the meaning of **doing something to yourself, for yourself, indulgently, reflexively.** It is the grammar of self-direction.

Form I of this root, *mata'a*, means simply "to enjoy, to benefit." But the word in this ayah is Form V: **ta-matta'ū**. Doubled middle. Reflexive prefix. The meaning shifts. Now it carries the sense of *steep yourself in enjoyment, indulge yourselves, take your pleasure.*

Notice the sound of the word: *ta-MAT-ta-'ū*. Listen to it. There is a doubling, a sticky quality — the tongue catches on the doubled *tt*, like fingers stuck in honey. The phonetics of the word *feel* like overindulgence. The sound is doing what the meaning is doing.

Now — here is the question.

Why does Allah, in a verse condemning ingratitude, suddenly switch and say: *"So enjoy yourselves"*?

Is this an invitation? A blessing? Permission?

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. In Arabic rhetoric there is a device called **al-amr al-tahdīdī** — the **command of threat.** It looks grammatically like an order, but it functions as a warning. We have it in English too. A parent says to a child climbing dangerously, *"Go ahead. Keep climbing. We'll see what happens."* The grammar is permission. The meaning is the opposite.

That is what Allah is doing here. *Fa-tamatta'ū* — "So go on, enjoy yourselves" — is not permission. It is something far worse. It is **withdrawal of argument.** Allah is saying: *I will no longer dispute with you. I will no longer pursue the case. Take your enjoyment. Wrap yourself in it.*

And here is what should make us tremble. When a parent stops arguing, sometimes it's because they've given up the lesson. When **Allah** stops arguing with someone in this life — that is one of the most fearful conditions a soul can be in. It is the condition the Quran elsewhere calls *istidrāj* — being drawn in slowly by good things, so that the person mistakes their drift for blessing.

Notice the structure here. The same person who, in verse 33, was crying out to *their Lord* in distress, is now being told *"enjoy yourselves."* The intimacy of *Rabbahum* (their Lord) is gone. The pronoun has gone cold. Allah was so close — close enough to be cried to — and now He stands back and says, *"Go ahead."*

What did they bury? The witness of His closeness. So He withdraws the closeness.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing in this little word.

The root **m-t-ʿ** in classical Arabic does not just mean "enjoyment." Lexicographers note that *matāʿ* refers specifically to something **temporary, portable, finite** — provisions you carry on a journey, household goods you take from place to place. The Quran uses *matāʿ* repeatedly to describe the *dunya* — "*matāʿu'l-ghurūr*" — the temporary provision of delusion.

So when Allah says *tamatta'ū*, He is not even using the word for *real* joy or sustained delight. He is using the word for **road snacks.** Provisions on a trip. Things you carry that will run out before you reach the destination.

Allah is saying: *Indulge in your road snacks. The journey ends. We'll see what you have at the end.*

The word does not contain dignity. It contains the smell of something that is about to be over.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element 3: The Two Words That Are Not Finished — *fa-sawfa ta'lamūn*

> **فَسَوْفَ تَعْلَمُونَ**
>
> *Fa-sawfa ta'lamūn* — *"Soon you will know."*

Three words in English. In Arabic, two words plus a particle: *fa* (so), *sawfa* (will, soon), *ta'lamūn* (you will know).

Let me teach you something about that particle *sawfa*. In Arabic, there are two ways to put a verb into the future. You can attach a small *sa-* to the verb — *sa-ya'lamūn*, "they will know shortly." Or you can use the longer particle *sawfa* — *sawfa ya'lamūn*, "they will know — eventually, certainly, in the fullness of time."

Classical grammarians distinguished these two. *Sa-* is **near future, almost immediate.** *Sawfa* is **extended future, drawn out, but certain.** Both promise the event. *Sawfa* promises it *with a sense of held breath* — it gives the future time to arrive.

Why does that matter here?

Because the person being addressed is *enjoying themselves*. They are inside their road snacks. They are wrapped in their indulgence. They cannot see beyond it. And Allah's threat is not, "*I will strike you down today.*" That would be easier to feel. The threat is, **"You will know."**

You will not always be inside this enjoyment. The pleasure has a horizon. There is a knowing on the other side of it. And that knowing will arrive, with certainty, *eventually* — through illness, through age, through the moment your eyes close, through the standing before Him.

[PAUSE]

Now notice what Allah does *not* say.

He does not say *what* they will know. The verb *ta'lamūn* has no object. The sentence is grammatically incomplete on purpose.

Stay with this. In Arabic, when a transitive verb is used without specifying its object, classical rhetoricians treat that absence as a deliberate vacuum. The mind of the listener is forced to **fill it in for themselves.** And by leaving the object out, Allah multiplies the threat infinitely.

What will they know?

- They will know the truth they buried.
- They will know who they cried out to.
- They will know that what they enjoyed was nothing.
- They will know what they could have had.
- They will know the One they ignored.
- They will know themselves — finally, without the noise.

Every reader fills in the silence with their own deepest fear. That is the rhetorical genius of the unstated object. **Allah's threat is custom-made to each listener** because each listener completes the verb with the thing they most do not want to know.

And here's what's chilling. There is also an asymmetry in the address. In the earlier part of the verse, Allah was speaking *about* them in third person — *li-yakfurū* (so they bury), *ātaynāhum* (We have given them). Then suddenly the address flips. *Fa-tamatta'ū* (you all, enjoy yourselves). *Fa-sawfa ta'lamūn* (soon **you** will know).

This is the device of **iltifāt** — the turn of address. Allah was speaking to the audience *about* these people. And then, mid-verse, He turns His face toward them — toward *us* — and speaks directly. The judgment that was a description becomes a finger pointed at the listener.

We thought we were hearing about someone else. We discover, in two words, that we were being warned the whole time.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So we have walked through three small structures. A particle of consequence that reveals where the heart was actually heading. A verb form whose sound is overripe and whose root means *provisions about to run out*. And a future-tense vow with an empty object and a turned face.

Three pieces of grammar.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Pattern of the Foxhole and the Forgetting

Let's start where you live.

Every one of us has had a *foxhole moment.* Maybe you don't call it that. The phrase comes from soldiers in trenches — the saying *"there are no atheists in foxholes"* — describing how, under fire, even people who have spent years denying God find themselves whispering His name.

But you don't need a war. You've had the moment.

It is 3 a.m. and the test results haven't come back. It is the second hour of turbulence. It is your child not waking up. It is the moment after the phone rings and before the voice on the other end speaks. In that moment — the most honest moment in your life — *something happens to your prayers.* They strip themselves of decoration. There is no rhetoric. There is no "if it be Your will, and if the conditions are met, and if it is good for me in this world and the next." There is only: *"Please. You. Save this."*

Allah, in verse 33 just before our ayah, describes this exactly. *"They call upon their Lord, turning to Him."* The word He uses, *munīb*, comes from a root meaning *to keep returning, like a homing pigeon.* In the foxhole, every soul becomes a homing pigeon to its real direction.

Then the harm is lifted.

And here is where this surah looks into you. Allah does not say "**they** associate partners again." He says *"a portion of them*** — fariqun minhum — associate partners again." Some do. Some don't. Which side of that line you fall on is not determined by the foxhole. It is determined by **what you do with the lifting.**

[PAUSE]

Think about the structure of your last rescue.

Did you, in the days after, name it? Did you sit and say to yourself, *"That was Him. He did that. He answered the prayer I was too desperate to compose properly"*? Or did the explanation immediately migrate? *"Well, the doctor was actually really skilled."* *"The market was bound to recover."* *"My therapy is finally paying off."* *"I just got lucky."*

Notice what is happening. The explanations are not *wrong* — Allah does work through doctors, markets, therapists, luck. The shift is not in the facts. The shift is in **what stands in the foreground of your gratitude.** The doctor walks in front of Allah. Your effort walks in front of Allah. Luck walks in front of Allah. Eventually, Allah is so far behind the parade of secondary causes that you can't see Him anymore.

That is the burial. **You did not become an atheist. You buried Him under attribution.**

And here is what this ayah is teaching us about ourselves. The pattern is so reliable that Allah uses a *purpose clause* to describe it. *Li-yakfurū* — "as if their behavior were aimed at burying." The Quran is saying: this is not an occasional human glitch. This is a **default direction of the unprocessed nafs**. When relief comes, the soul left to itself will reach for the shovel.

So what stops it?

One thing. *Dhikr.* The conscious naming of the gift. The deliberate sentence said aloud or in the heart: *"Ya Allah, that was You. I see You in this. I will not let this rescue lose its name."*

The reason the Quran is so saturated with the language of remembrance — *udhkurū ni'matī* (remember My favor), *fa-dhkurūnī adhkurkum* (so remember Me, I will remember you) — is because Allah knows the default. Without active remembrance, every rescue dissolves into "luck" within seventy-two hours. The gift gets *buried*, not by a person who hates God, but by a person who simply forgot to name what just happened.

What does this change tomorrow? **When relief comes — even small relief — pause and name the giver out loud.** Out loud. Even if you whisper. The naming is the difference between the gift becoming a witness and the gift becoming a corpse you walk over.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme 2: When God Stops Arguing

There is a stage of any difficult relationship that everyone who has lived has encountered. It is the stage where the other person — a parent, a spouse, a friend — finally stops fighting with you.

You feel it before you understand it. The arguments used to be loud. Now they are short. Then they are absent. The person who used to push back, correct you, reach for you, *stops reaching.* They become polite. They say, "Sure, do what you want." They say, "Okay, whatever you think."

If you have ever been on the receiving end of this, you know it is *worse than the fighting.* The fighting meant they were still inside the relationship with you. The silence means they have moved their heart somewhere you cannot reach.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the second half of this ayah again.

> *Fa-tamatta'ū fa-sawfa ta'lamūn*
> *"So enjoy yourselves. Soon you will know."*

This is Allah, after building the entire case in the previous verses — after describing how He lifted the harm, after describing how they returned to shirk, after revealing the buried purpose of their behavior — choosing to **stop arguing.**

He does not say, "Repent." He does not say, "Come back." He does not say, "Don't you see what I just did for you?" He says, **"Enjoy yourselves."**

This is the most frightening sentence in the surah. Because Allah has not stopped loving the listener — He is still speaking, He is still warning — but He has stopped *disputing with the buried ones.* He is letting them have their road snacks. He is letting them go where the burial is pulling them.

The Prophet ﷺ described this state. He said: *"When you see Allah giving His servant the dunya he loves while persisting in disobedience, then know that this is istidrāj"* — Allah drawing him in slowly. The blessing keeps coming. The career keeps rising. The body keeps holding up. And the person, wrapped in their *tamatta'ū*, mistakes the silence for approval.

It is not approval. It is **withheld correction.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox this ayah asks us to hold. The same Allah who is *al-Ḥalīm* — the Forbearing, the One who delays consequence to allow space for return — is *also* the One whose forbearance, if you sleep through it, becomes the very rope that runs out at the end of the journey. The kindness of the delay is the kindness of His giving you time. The horror of the delay is that you can mistake it for permission.

How do we tell the difference between *delay-as-mercy* and *delay-as-withdrawal* in our own lives?

There is a sign in this very surah. The person being warned in verse 34 is the person who **was just rescued and forgot.** If your last rescue produced gratitude, named the Giver, deepened your prayer, drew you closer — the delay you are experiencing now is mercy giving you time to do more of that.

If your last rescue produced amnesia, drifted you into pleasure, made you confident in *yourself* — the same delay is not mercy in the same way. It is the *fa-tamatta'ū* moment. Allah has stopped arguing for now. And the longer you stay in that silence without breaking it yourself — without crying out the way you cried out *before the rescue* — the closer the *fa-sawfa ta'lamūn* gets.

What does this change tomorrow? **Don't wait for the next foxhole to learn how to cry out.** The whole structure of this ayah is the structure of someone who only knows how to call on Allah when they are about to lose everything. The cure is to call on Him when you are not losing anything. To make the foxhole prayer your morning prayer. To name Him *before* the crisis, so that when the crisis comes, you are not meeting a stranger in the dark.

The person who keeps the relationship alive in peacetime is the person who, when the harm lifts, doesn't have to *return* to Allah — because they never left.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two directions.

Grammatically, Allah did three things in seven words. He used a particle that revealed a buried purpose the person could not see. He used a verb whose sound is sticky and whose meaning is *temporary provisions*. He used a future verb with no object, addressed in the second person, with His face turned toward us.

Thematically, those three moves correspond exactly to three movements of the human soul. The shovel that buries the witness of rescue. The honey of distraction we wrap ourselves in afterward. The arrival of knowing — eventually, inevitably, custom-fitted to whatever we most refused to face.

The grammar *is* the psychology. Allah did not just describe the disease. He encoded it into the very shape of His sentence.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I was rescued — even from something small — and did I name the Rescuer, or did I let the rescue dissolve into "luck" or "my own effort"?
2. Is there a current ease in my life that I have been quietly treating as permission, when it might in fact be Allah's silence?
3. What would I *not* want to know on the other side of *fa-sawfa ta'lamūn*? What is the object I would fill into that empty verb?
4. Have I learned to call on Allah only in foxholes, or have I built the habit of crying out when nothing is wrong?
5. What gift in my life right now is at risk of being buried — not by my unbelief, but by my forgetting to name it?

### The Distillation

In one sentence: **Ingratitude is not a position; it is a slow burial of every witness God has placed in your life, and Allah's most fearful response to it is not to strike — it is to stop arguing.**

### The Closing Invitation

After this verse, do not let your next relief — small or large — go unnamed. When something is lifted, when something is given, when you arrive home safely, when the result comes back clean, when the meeting ends well — pause. Even for three seconds. And say, in your heart or aloud: *"Ya Allah, that was You. I see You. I will not bury this."*

That sentence is the shovel laid down. That sentence keeps the gift breathing. That sentence is the difference between being the one in verse 33 who cried out — and being the one in verse 34 who is told, *"Enjoy yourselves."*

### Du'a

Allahumma, do not let us be among those whose rescues become silent in our mouths. Teach us to name You while the gift is still warm in our hands, before the soil of forgetfulness reaches it. And if You have, in Your forbearance, stopped arguing with us — break Your silence in our hearts before *fa-sawfa ta'lamūn* arrives, so that we know You now, on our knees, and not later, when knowing is no longer a choice.
