# The Ruins That Speak: When the Earth Itself Becomes a Witness

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:30:9] أَوَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَيَنظُرُوا۟ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَـٰقِبَةُ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ ۚ كَانُوٓا۟ أَشَدَّ مِنْهُمْ قُوَّةً وَأَثَارُوا۟ ٱلْأَرْضَ وَعَمَرُوهَآ أَكْثَرَ مِمَّا عَمَرُوهَا وَجَآءَتْهُمْ رُسُلُهُم بِٱلْبَيِّنَـٰتِ ۖ فَمَا كَانَ ٱللَّهُ لِيَظْلِمَهُمْ وَلَـٰكِن كَانُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُمْ يَظْلِمُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> *Awa-lam yasīrū fī al-arḍi fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu alladhīna min qablihim? Kānū ashadda minhum quwwatan wa athārū al-arḍa wa ʿamarūhā akthara mimmā ʿamarūhā wa jāʾat-hum rusuluhum bi-l-bayyināt. Fa-mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum wa lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn.*

**Translation:**
> "Have they not traveled through the earth and observed how was the end of those before them? They were greater than them in strength, and they tilled the earth and built it up more than they have built it up, and their messengers came to them with clear proofs. And Allah was not to wrong them, but they were wronging themselves."

---

### Rebuilding the World

Let's place ourselves where the first listeners stood.

The year is somewhere around 615 CE. The people hearing this for the first time are the early Muslims of Makkah — a small, persecuted community living in a city that thinks of itself as eternal. Around them, the great powers of the world are at war. The Byzantines have just lost Jerusalem to the Persians. To anyone watching, the world is being rearranged by armies, by emperors, by the kind of power that builds aqueducts and writes laws and stamps its face on coins.

And here is this tiny community in Makkah being told two things at once.

First, in the opening of this very surah: *the Byzantines, who just lost, will win again within a few years*. A prophecy about empires from a band of refugees.

And then, a few ayahs later, this:

> *Have they not traveled through the earth?*

Because the Quraysh — the merchant aristocracy of Makkah — *did* travel. Their entire wealth came from the two caravan routes Allah mentions in another surah: the journey of winter and the journey of summer. They traveled north into the territory of fallen empires. They traveled south past the ruins of the dam of Marib. They literally walked past the bones of civilizations every year of their trading lives.

[PAUSE]

And the Quran asks: *did you actually look?*

Not "did you travel." You traveled. You always travel. That's not the question.

**Did you see what was right in front of your eyes?**

### The Strand

This ayah is part of a strand that runs through the entire Quran — a strand we might call *the rhetoric of ruins*. Allah does not merely tell us about destroyed peoples. He tells us their physical traces are still on the earth, and He commands us to go and *see* them. The ʿĀd. The Thamūd. The people of Lūṭ. The people of Madyan. Pharaoh's Egypt. Their cities did not vanish — they remained, hollowed out, as a kind of permanent open-air classroom.

The surah we are in, Ar-Rum, is named after a living empire — Rome. By the time you finish reading the surah, you understand that Rome itself, the greatest superpower the early Muslims knew, is being placed in the same category as those ancient ruins. Not yet fallen. But operating under the same law.

### The Hook

Here is what is stunning about this ayah, and what we are going to spend the next hour unpacking.

The people Allah describes — those before us — were *stronger* than us. They built *more* than we built. They worked the earth *deeper* than we have worked it. And yet they ended.

And the explanation Allah gives is not a single thing. It is a strange, almost paradoxical pair of statements:

> *Allah was not going to wrong them — but they were wronging themselves.*

Two statements about ẓulm in one breath. One denied of Allah. One affirmed of them, with a strange grammatical twist that the translations cannot fully carry.

Stay with that. We will come back to it. Because the deepest meaning of this ayah lives not in the ruins it describes, but in those final five words — five words that explain why every civilization that ever fell, fell.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to walk through three linguistic features in this ayah. Each one is a key that unlocks a different door into the same room.

---

### Element One: *Yasīrū* and *Yanẓurū* — The Verbs of Looking

Let me start with something simple, almost too simple.

In English, when we say "see," we have one word. We see. That's it. But Arabic, like many older languages, distinguishes between different kinds of looking. There is *raʾā* — to see in the most basic sense, vision happens to you, light hits your eye. There is *naẓara* — to direct your gaze, to look at something on purpose. There is *abṣara* — to perceive, to register. And there is *baṣīrah* — inner sight, insight, the seeing of the heart.

Now look at the verb Allah chooses here.

> *Fa-yanẓurū* — "so that they may look."

From the root **ن-ظ-ر** (*n-ẓ-r*). This is not the verb of accidental seeing. This is the verb of *directed attention*. It's the same root from which we get *naẓar* — a considered look, a study, a contemplation. When you *tanẓur* at something, you are not just having photons enter your eye. You are turning your attention toward it.

Notice that Allah did not say *fa-yarawhum* — "so that they may see them." He said *fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu* — "so that they may direct their gaze at how the end was."

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The ayah opens with *yasīrū* — "travel." Travel is passive in a sense. You can travel through a place and notice nothing. The Quraysh traveled their whole lives. The verb *sār* — to move through space — is one of the most ordinary verbs in Arabic. But the verb that follows it, *yanẓurū*, is anything but ordinary. It demands you to stop, turn your head, focus.

So the structure of the ayah is:

**Movement → Attention**

You can have all the movement in the world and gather nothing. You can travel through a hundred cities and remain blind. The ayah is not commanding you to travel — it's reminding you that you *already* travel, and asking why your travel produced no *naẓar*.

Now layer this against ayah 7 of the same surah, just two ayahs back:

> *They know only the outer surface of the worldly life, and of the Hereafter they are heedless.*

The word for "outer surface" there is *ẓāhir* — the apparent, the visible skin of things. So the diagnosis was: you only see *ẓāhir*. The prescription, two ayahs later, is: practice *naẓar*. Don't just register the skin of what you see — direct your attention beneath it.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is that the Quran is not asking us to acquire new information. It is asking us to *change the quality of our attention*. The data is already in front of you. The ruins are already on your trade route. The signs are already on the horizon. What is missing is not facts.

What is missing is the willingness to *look*.

---

### Element Two: *Athārū al-Arḍ* — The Shocking Verb

This is the verb I want you to sit with the longest. Because it is one of the most extraordinary word-choices in the entire surah.

Allah says about the previous civilizations:

> *Wa athārū al-arḍ wa ʿamarūhā* — "And they tilled the earth, and they built it up."

That word — *athārū* — is from the root **ث-و-ر** (*th-w-r*).

Now, before we get to what it means here, let me show you where this root lives elsewhere in Arabic. The same root gives us the word *thawrah* — which today is the Arabic word for *revolution*. It gives us *thawr* — a bull, the animal that snorts and charges. It gives us *istathāra* — to stir something up, to provoke it, to make it rise in agitation.

The core meaning of the root is **to stir, to agitate, to make rise**. To take something that was settled and disturb it. To turn something over so that what was buried comes up.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says these previous people *athārū al-arḍ*, the translations usually give us "they tilled the earth" or "they cultivated the earth." And that is not wrong — agriculture is one of its meanings. To plow is, after all, to turn the soil over.

But notice the *force* of this word.

Allah did not say *zaraʿū al-arḍ* — "they planted the earth," which is the gentler agricultural verb. He did not say *ḥarathū al-arḍ* — "they cultivated the earth," which is the standard farming word. He chose *athārū* — the verb of *agitating*, of *stirring violently*, of *making the earth rise up*.

Let's sit with this. What does it mean to "agitate the earth"?

It means much more than farming. It means: they didn't just live on the earth — they *churned* it. They turned it over. They dug into it. They extracted from it. They mined it. They engineered it. They reshaped its surface.

Think about what this verb evokes if you read it with the imagination it invites. A civilization that *agitates the earth* is a civilization that does not leave the earth alone. It quarries. It builds. It excavates. It rearranges rivers. It carves roads through mountains. It does not just exist on the surface — it *intervenes* in the substance of the planet.

[PAUSE]

Now think about the people Allah is describing. The ʿĀd, with their pillared cities. The Thamūd, who carved their homes directly into the cliffs of Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ — you can still go and see them, geological-scale architecture cut into living rock. The Egyptians, who moved blocks of stone the size of trucks across a desert. The Persians, who turned earth into terraced gardens. The Romans, who flattened hills, drained marshes, laid 250,000 miles of stone road.

*Athārū al-arḍ.* They didn't farm. They *agitated the planet*.

And here is the bite of the ayah:

> *Akthara mimmā ʿamarūhā* — "more than these people have built it up."

More than you. More than the Quraysh who hear this ayah. The merchants of Makkah, who at most have a few stone buildings around the Kaʿbah, are being told: the people you walked past in those ruins — they built more than you can imagine.

And they still ended.

[PAUSE]

What this opens up for us is something almost dizzying. *Athārū* sits in this ayah like a mirror held up to every civilization that has ever congratulated itself on its mastery of the physical world. The verb does not condemn building. It does not condemn engineering. It simply notes — with terrifying neutrality — that civilizations have agitated the earth before, on scales we cannot match, and that did not save them.

Notice the absence here. Allah does not say *what* they built. He does not name a single building. He could have. He could have said "they built towers." "They built temples." "They built pyramids." Instead, the buildings are erased — only the *act* of building remains. The architecture is gone. The act is what He preserves.

[PAUSE]

It's as if Allah is saying: the buildings themselves don't even merit description. What matters is the *posture* — the disposition of a civilization that thought churning the earth would secure it. That posture, He keeps. The product, He doesn't bother to name.

---

### Element Three: *Anfusahum Yaẓlimūn* — The Inverted Ending

And now we come to the heart of this ayah. The five words that change everything.

> *Wa lākin kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn.*
> "But they were — themselves — wronging."

Let me read this slowly in the Arabic, because what Allah does here cannot survive translation. In English, we read "they were wronging themselves" — and it sounds like one phrase, almost an idiom. In Arabic, Allah does something with the word order that is grammatically *startling*.

The normal Arabic sentence order for "they were wronging themselves" would be:

> *Kānū yaẓlimūna anfusahum.*

Subject — verb — object. They were — wronging — themselves. That's the natural flow.

But Allah does not say that. He says:

> *Kānū anfusahum yaẓlimūn.*

He puts *anfusahum* — *themselves* — **before** the verb. He moves the object in front of the action.

In Arabic grammar, this is a feature called *taqdīm* — the fronting of a word, pulling it forward, out of its normal position. And taqdīm carries a specific rhetorical force: **restriction**, **emphasis**, sometimes **exclusivity**.

So Allah is not just saying "they were wronging themselves." He is saying something closer to:

> ***Themselves*** *— that is who they were wronging.*

Or even more pointed:

> *It was* ***themselves*** *— and themselves alone — whom they were wronging.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Allah just spent the first half of the ayah describing massive civilizations. Strength. Tilling. Building. The agitation of the earth itself. And then He says — *for all that scale, the only thing they ever actually damaged was themselves*.

The whole machinery of empire — the armies, the buildings, the agricultural revolutions, the engineered landscapes — and the net effect was self-inflicted wound.

Notice the contrast with the previous clause:

> *Fa-mā kāna Allāhu li-yaẓlimahum* — "Allah was not going to wrong them."

The negation here is also rhetorically intensified. The construction *mā kāna li-* is not just "He did not" — it carries the sense "it was not for Him to," "it does not befit Him," "it is not in His nature to." Some classical grammarians describe this as a denial that reaches into the realm of *possibility itself*. It is not merely that Allah did not wrong them — it is that wronging them was *unbecoming of Him*, *outside His way*.

[PAUSE]

So now read the two halves together:

> *It was not in Allah's nature to wrong them — but themselves, themselves they were wronging.*

The architecture of this ending is breathtaking. Two verbs of *ẓulm*. One negated of Allah with maximum force. One affirmed of them with fronted emphasis. The whole moral universe of the ayah collapses into this final symmetry: **the thing Allah refuses to do, they did to themselves.**

[PAUSE]

And there is something even subtler. The word *anfusahum* — "their selves" — is the plural of *nafs*, the self, the soul. In Arabic, the *nafs* is not merely the body. It is the inner person — the seat of will, of desire, of character. When Allah says they were wronging their *nafs*, He is not saying they damaged their bodies. He is saying they damaged the very interiority of themselves.

The cities they built fell. But what truly fell was *themselves*. And the cities are mentioned only as ironic evidence of how much they could have built, of themselves, if they had pointed their efforts inward instead of churning the dirt.

---

### Bridge

Three linguistic discoveries. Let me name them before we move on.

First — Allah is not asking for new information. He is asking for a new *quality of attention*. The data is already there. *Yanẓurū* is the missing verb in most lives.

Second — civilizations that *agitate* the earth at planetary scale are not new. The deepest pyramids, the most carved cliffs, the largest empires — Allah preserves the verb of their making and erases the buildings themselves. The act of building remains, the buildings do not.

Third — when Allah explains the fall, He does it through a fronted, restricted, grammatically intensified statement: *it was themselves they were wronging.* Not Allah. Not bad luck. Not history. Themselves.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: Why Empires Don't See Themselves in Ruins

Let me ask you to do something. Picture yourself walking past an abandoned building. Maybe a factory. Maybe a school that closed years ago. Maybe an old mall, the kind that used to be the center of a town and is now a hollow shell with cracked tile and grass coming through the parking lot.

What do you feel when you walk past it?

For most of us, the honest answer is: very little. We register it. We might think "that's sad," or "that's eerie," or "I wonder what happened." And then we keep walking. Because in our minds, *that* place ended. *Our* place is fine.

This is what the Quran is naming. The Quraysh walked past the ruins of empires every trading season. They had to. Their caravans literally moved through the corpses of civilizations. And they walked past those ruins the same way we walk past abandoned malls. *That place ended. Our place is fine.*

[PAUSE]

There's a name in modern thought for this — the *normalcy bias*. Psychologists who study disaster response noticed something strange: when a fire alarm goes off in a building, most people don't run. They stand still. They look around. They wait for someone else to react. They tell themselves, *probably nothing*. Even in actual emergencies — even with smoke filling the room — people often continue what they were doing for surprisingly long, because the brain has a default assumption that *today will be like yesterday*.

This is the cognitive default of every civilization. It is what makes Pompeii so haunting. The casts in the ash show people who died mid-task — mid-meal, mid-bread-making, mid-embrace. They did not believe, until it was too late, that *their* day could be different from yesterday.

This is exactly what Surah Ar-Rum is diagnosing two ayahs earlier when it says they *know only the outer surface* — *ẓāhir* — *of worldly life*. Surface-vision is the cognitive engine of the normalcy bias. If you only see what's in front of you today, then today is the only data you have. And today, the lights are still on. Today, the mall is full. Today, the empire stands.

[PAUSE]

The Quran's prescription is not to fearmonger. It is not to make you panic about collapse. It is something gentler and far more demanding: *go and look at the places where today eventually ended*.

Not because you are supposed to despair. But because the ruins are doing something the present cannot do. The ruins are showing you that *time is real*. That what feels permanent is not. That what you assume is the default state of the world — power, comfort, your civilization's continued existence — was once also someone else's assumption, and they were wrong.

[PAUSE]

And here is where this gets devastating on a personal level. Because the ayah is not really about civilizations.

It is about *you*.

Every one of us carries a small empire in our chest. A self that thinks of itself as permanent. A career that feels established. A family configuration that feels eternal. A body that, despite all evidence, we relate to as if it will not age. A reputation we have spent years building. A self-image we *athārnā* — we agitated, we worked, we churned soil to build.

And the same surface-vision the Quraysh had about Makkah, we have about our own selves.

The traveling through the earth that Allah commands is not just geographic. It is also temporal and biographical. Go look at versions of yourself that have already ended. The teenager you were. The 23-year-old whose certainties seem laughable now. The career path you were sure of in 2015. The relationships you assumed were permanent. The fears that consumed you for two years and now you cannot remember why they mattered.

You have ruins in your own past.

*Have you not traveled and looked at them?*

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Look once at something you currently treat as permanent — a job, a friendship, a financial situation, a state of health — and ask honestly: *what is the version of me five years from now likely to say about how I'm relating to this right now?* If a future version of you would gently shake their head at your current certainty, that is a piece of *naẓar* — directed attention into the ruins of selves you haven't yet become. Let it loosen, just slightly, the grip of the present moment on your sense of what is permanent.

---

### Theme Two: The Strange Mercy of Self-Wronging

Now let's go to the closing of the ayah, because this is where the entire reflection lands.

> *Allah was not going to wrong them — but themselves, themselves they were wronging.*

In most religious traditions, when a civilization falls, the explanation given is some version of *the gods were angry*. The volcano erupted because Zeus was offended. The flood came because the deity was displeased. The empire fell because divine wrath was poured out.

The Quran is doing something completely different here. And it is so subtle it can be easy to miss.

Allah, at the moment of explaining catastrophic civilizational collapse, *removes Himself as the active agent of the harm*. He goes out of His way to say — and grammatically intensifies the saying of — *this was not Me. It would not befit Me. It is not in My way to wrong them.*

[PAUSE]

Why does He do this?

Here is the bridge into modern life. Think about how we relate to consequences.

When something bad happens to us — and we are spiritually undeveloped — there is a default narration our nafs reaches for: *this happened to me*. The passive voice. *I was wronged. The world is against me. Allah is testing me unfairly. Why me?*

Notice that this narration locates the cause *outside the self*. Something happened *to* me. Some force did this *to* me. The self is the victim of a story it did not write.

And then, slowly, in some lives — through age, through pain honestly faced, through enough rounds of blaming others and finding the same problems recurring — something shifts. We start to notice a pattern. The bad job. The failed relationships. The financial crises. The conflicts that follow us. And one day we whisper to ourselves the most uncomfortable sentence a human can say:

*Maybe... I had something to do with this.*

[PAUSE]

That sentence — that whispered, painful, terrifying sentence — is the doorway into the second half of this ayah.

> *They were themselves wronging.*

And here's what's strange. That sentence sounds at first like an accusation. Like Allah is pointing the finger. But sit with it longer. Read it again with different ears.

If the harm came from outside us — if Allah was the one who did it, if fate did it, if the world did it — then we are powerless. The story ends there. We are the recipients of forces we cannot affect.

But if the harm came from *ourselves* — then we have something almost unimaginable in our hands.

**We have agency.**

[PAUSE]

This is the strange mercy hidden inside the second half of the ayah. By insisting that the wronging is *self-wronging*, Allah is not just laying blame. He is preserving for us the only thing that makes change possible: the recognition that the lever is *in our hand*. That if it was the self that broke the self, then the self can also turn back. The self that wronged itself can also seek forgiveness. The self that built ruin can also build differently next time.

If Allah had been the agent of their fall — there would be nothing to do. You cannot negotiate with an unstoppable force. But because the agent was *themselves*, the door of return is open. *Tawbah* — repentance, turning back — is only possible if you are the one who turned away in the first place.

[PAUSE]

This is why the Quran is full of *tawbah*. It is why every chapter opens with *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*. Because the entire architecture of the Quranic vision of the human being depends on this: **you are not the victim of your fall. You are the author of it. Which means you are also the author of your return.**

That is not a curse. That is the most dignifying sentence anyone has ever spoken about the human being.

[PAUSE]

And now look at how the ayah holds these two together:

> *It was not in Allah's nature to wrong them — but they themselves they were wronging.*

The first half preserves Allah's justice. The second half preserves your agency. The first half says: nothing that happens to you, at the deepest level, is divine cruelty. The second half says: nothing that happens to you, at the deepest level, is outside your reach to address.

Civilizations forget this. They blame the gods, blame the climate, blame the barbarians at the gate. The Quran insists, with the gentleness of its grammar, that the gates were broken from the inside.

[PAUSE]

And one final thing to notice. Look at the messengers in the ayah:

> *And their messengers came to them with clear proofs.* — *bi-l-bayyināt.*

This is the missing piece. Civilizations do not fall in silence. They fall *after warning*. Messengers came — with *bayyināt*, with clear, unambiguous proofs. The fall was not random. It was preceded by an invitation to course-correct that was refused.

In your own life, the *messengers* may not be prophets. They may be the small voice of conscience at 2 AM. The friend who tells you a hard truth. The body that gives you the first signal that something is wrong. The pattern that keeps recurring and is trying to tell you something. The verse that crosses your screen on the day you needed exactly that verse. These are the *bayyināt* of your own life. The clear signs.

*Their messengers came to them.*

The question of the ayah is not whether the messengers came. They always come. The question is whether you *yanẓur* — whether you direct your attention to them, or walk past them the way the Quraysh walked past the ruins.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** When you find yourself in a familiar form of suffering — a familiar conflict, a familiar frustration, a familiar dead-end — instead of asking *why is this happening to me*, try once asking the more uncomfortable question: *what messenger have I been walking past?* What signal, what conscience-prick, what trusted person's hard truth have I been declining to *yanẓur* toward? That question is the doorway into the second half of the ayah — the doorway that turns self-wronging into self-return.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we've done the work, look at the shape of what Allah built.

The ayah opens with a *question* — *awa-lam yasīrū* — "have they not traveled." It ends with a *statement* — *anfusahum yaẓlimūn* — "they were themselves wronging."

In between, there is a journey. Movement. Observation. Then the *evidence*: strength, agitation of the earth, more building than yours. Then the *witness*: messengers came with proofs. Then the *verdict*, in two clauses, perfectly balanced:

- Allah was not going to wrong them.
- They were themselves wronging.

The ayah is shaped like a courtroom. The earth is the evidence. The ruins are the exhibits. The messengers are the witnesses. The verdict has two halves: divine justice preserved, human agency affirmed.

And the listener — you, the one being asked *have you not traveled* — is not the judge. You are the next defendant in the same case, being shown what was decided last time, being asked whether you want to walk into the same room with the same plea.

### Bringing It Together

Our three linguistic discoveries — *naẓar* (directed attention), *athārū* (the agitation of the earth), and the fronted *anfusahum* (themselves emphasized) — are not three separate insights. They are one insight from three angles.

The civilizations that fell were not blind in the eyes. They were blind in the *naẓar*. They could *see* the ruins of those before them. They simply did not *look*. They were not weak — they *athārū al-arḍ*, they out-built us. But their building was all directed outward, into the dirt, while the only construction project that ultimately mattered — the building of the *nafs* — they neglected. And so when the verdict came, the grammar of the verdict pointed back at them. *Anfusahum*. Fronted. Emphasized. *Themselves*. The only target they ever actually hit.

The whole ayah, in one architectural sweep: *they built everything outside themselves and nothing inside themselves, and so they were the only thing they ever damaged.*

---

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life have you been traveling without *naẓar* — moving through circumstances, conversations, relationships, years, without ever actually directing your attention to what they were showing you?

2. What have you been *agitating the earth* for — what external project, achievement, possession, or status have you been churning soil to build — that, if you fell tomorrow, would be revealed as having taken more of you than it gave back?

3. What is the version of yourself five years ago that, if you visited its ruins today, would teach you something you have not yet learned about your current certainties?

4. Where in your current suffering have you been narrating *this is happening to me*, when an honest second look would show you *I have had something to do with this*?

5. What *bayyināt* — what clear signs, what trusted-voice warnings, what conscience-pricks, what recurring patterns — have been knocking at your life, and which one have you been declining to *yanẓur* toward?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> *Allah commands us to walk through the ruins of those who built more than we ever will, so that we learn — before our own ruin teaches us — that the only structure that ever truly falls is the one we refused to build inside ourselves.*

---

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking for a posture of fear. It is not asking us to live as if collapse is around every corner.

It is asking for *attention*. For a slightly more honest *naẓar* into the moments of our own lives. For the willingness, just sometimes, to walk past the abandoned places of our own past selves and let them tell us something. For the courage to read our own suffering not as something happening *to* us, but as something we have, in some measure, been authoring — because the moment we accept that authorship, we receive back the pen.

What is being invited is not despair at how every civilization falls. It is dignity — the dignity of being told *the lever is in your hand*. The dignity of *anfusahum yaẓlimūn* — which sounds like accusation but is, at its root, a statement of how much power Allah has placed inside the human being.

You are not the wreckage of forces outside you.

You are the author of your own returning.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who do not wrong — give us the attention that travels through life and actually looks. Show us the ruins in our own pasts before our present becomes a ruin. And in every place where we have wronged ourselves, open for us the door of return that You have left open in this very ayah — the door that exists only because You wrote the verdict in the second person plural, and not in Yours.*

*Āmīn.*
