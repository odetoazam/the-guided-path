# The Forgetting That Follows Relief

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 33

### Arabic

> وَإِذَا مَسَّ ٱلنَّاسَ ضُرٌّ دَعَوْا۟ رَبَّهُم مُّنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَآ أَذَاقَهُم مِّنْهُ رَحْمَةً إِذَا فَرِيقٌ مِّنْهُم بِرَبِّهِمْ يُشْرِكُونَ

### Transliteration

> Wa idhā massa an-nāsa ḍurrun daʿaw rabbahum munībīna ilayh, thumma idhā adhāqahum minhu raḥmatan idhā farīqun minhum bi-rabbihim yushrikūn.

### Translation

> And when harm touches people, they call upon their Lord, turning back to Him in repentance. Then when He gives them a taste of mercy from Himself, behold — a faction of them associates partners with their Lord.

---

## Introduction: The World Beneath the Verse

Before we open this ayah, I want you to stand somewhere you've already stood.

You've been in the hospital waiting room. Or beside someone in surgery. Or in a car that just lost control on wet asphalt and somehow stopped before the guardrail. Or on a plane through turbulence so violent that the cabin went silent — that particular silence that isn't quiet at all, because every passenger is praying.

Notice what happens in those moments. People who haven't prayed in years pray. People who claim no religion whisper *please, God.* People who have spent a lifetime arguing against Him address Him with a fluency they didn't know they had. The fitrah — the original orientation of the soul toward its Creator — surfaces. Not because the person has been argued into it. Because the pretense of self-sufficiency has been stripped away by a force greater than the ego's resistance.

Now imagine the turbulence ends. The plane lands. The surgeon comes out smiling. The car is towed and you're fine. Walk forward forty-eight hours. The cabin prayer is gone. The hospital vow is half-remembered, slightly embarrassing. The hand that was raised has gone back into the pocket.

[PAUSE]

This is the ayah.

Surah Ar-Rum is not lecturing the disbeliever from the outside. It is describing a movement that lives inside almost every human heart — including the believer's. The surah moves through cosmic signs, agricultural signs, marital signs, historical signs — and right in the middle of that majestic tour, Allah pauses to hold up a mirror. A small, devastating mirror.

The hook is this: Allah does not say *all of them* return to shirk. He says *a faction.* And He does not say He *gave* them mercy. He says He let them *taste* it. And He does not say they were *struck* by harm. He says it merely *touched* them.

Every one of those choices is doing work. By the end of this reflection, those three words — *touch, taste, faction* — will have told you something about your own heart that you cannot un-see.

# Part One: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to enter this ayah through three doors. Three words. Each door, once opened, reframes the rest.

## 1. *Massa* — When Harm Merely *Touches*

Let me teach you a small piece of Arabic before we go in.

Arabic, like many languages, has a spectrum of physical-contact verbs. There is a verb for striking. A verb for crushing. A verb for grasping firmly. A verb for piercing. And then, at the gentlest end of that spectrum, there is a verb for *touch* — for the lightest possible contact, the brush of a fingertip across a surface. That verb is **مَسَّ — *massa.***

When Arabs wanted to say *the disease destroyed him,* they had verbs for that. When they wanted to say *the disease afflicted him,* they had verbs for that. When they wanted to say *the disease merely brushed him — grazed him — passed its fingers across him,* they reached for *massa.*

Now look at how Allah phrases this:

> **وَإِذَا مَسَّ ٱلنَّاسَ ضُرٌّ** — *And when harm **touches** people…*

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Allah did not say *and when harm strikes people.* He did not say *and when harm devastates people.* He did not say *and when harm overwhelms people.* He chose the lightest verb in the lexicon of pain.

Why does that matter?

Because the rest of the ayah describes a profound spiritual movement — a calling upon the Lord, a turning back in repentance, a returning of the heart to its origin. We would expect that kind of movement to require some massive shock. A catastrophe. A near-death.

And Allah is telling us: *No. Sometimes it only takes a touch.*

Let's sit with this. Human beings are so finely tuned to vulnerability that the gentlest brush of harm — a fever, a flat tire, a financial scare, an ache that turns out to be nothing — is enough to surface the prayer that's been hiding under the surface of our days. We do not need to be destroyed to remember Him. We need to be *touched.*

Here is what is remarkable, and slightly painful. If the lightest contact is enough to make the soul cry out — what does it mean that, on most days, we are *not* crying out? It means we have been arranging our lives in such a way that *even the touch* is filtered out. Insurance, comfort, distraction, schedule. Layer upon layer. Until the fingertip of harm cannot quite reach the skin.

And then, when at last it does, the prayer that emerges is not invented. It was always there. We just couldn't feel it through the padding.

[PAUSE]

Notice also the grammatical subject: *ḍurr* — *harm* — is the actor. Not *the people.* Harm comes and touches *them.* They are passive in the encounter. Allah is showing us something the modern self does not like to admit: we are not the authors of our own waking. Something has to come and find us. Reality has to reach in.

That single verb — *massa* — has already reframed the entire ayah. The point is not how bad the suffering was. The point is how easily, how cheaply, the soul gives up its illusion of self-sufficiency the moment reality grazes it.

## 2. *Munībīna* — The Word That Means "Coming Home"

The next word is one of the most beautiful in the Quranic vocabulary of repentance.

> **دَعَوْا۟ رَبَّهُم مُّنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ** — *they call upon their Lord, **munībīna** to Him.*

We translate *munībīna* as "turning back in repentance." That translation is correct, but it doesn't carry the weight of what the word does in Arabic.

Let me teach you the concept first. In English, we have several words for spiritual return: *repent, return, come back, turn around.* In Arabic, the Quran has a layered vocabulary for this — *tawba, rujūʿ, ināba* — and each one occupies a slightly different region of the soul.

*Ināba,* the verbal noun behind *munībīna,* carries the meaning of repeatedly returning home. The root suggests a turning that is not one-time. It is the motion of someone who, having been away, comes back — and having come back, makes that returning the *pattern* of their life. It is the homing instinct of the bird. The lexicons gloss it as *raj'ʿ ilā Allāh marratan baʿda ukhrā* — returning to Allah time after time.

Notice the implication. To *return* implies you came from somewhere. *Ināba* is not the soul going to a stranger. It is the soul going *home.*

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says *the people call upon Him munībīn,* He is not saying *they decided in that moment of crisis to become spiritual.* He is saying: *the harm peeled back what was covering the original direction of their souls, and they began moving — by reflex — toward the One they had always belonged to.*

This is enormous. It means that what we call "finding God in a foxhole" is not a manufactured response to fear. It is the ordinary direction of the heart becoming visible when the noise stops. The disbeliever, in the moment of distress, does not invent a new orientation. He *remembers* one.

And there is another layer in the form of the word. *Munībīna* is in the plural, masculine, accusative — a *ḥāl,* a circumstantial state. It describes *the manner in which* they call. They don't just call. They call *while in the state of returning.* The supplication and the homecoming are happening simultaneously. The mouth says *please,* and the soul, at the same moment, is already walking back across the threshold.

[PAUSE]

Here's what this opens up for us. Sometimes we suspect, in moments of distress, that our turning to Allah is not "sincere" because it was triggered by need. Allah is doing something extraordinary here. He is *not* condemning the need-driven prayer. He is describing it with the most dignified verb in the language of return. *Munībīn.* Coming home.

He could have said *daʿaw rabbahum khā'ifīn* — "they called upon their Lord, afraid." He could have said *muḍṭarrīn* — "compelled, desperate." He chose neither. He chose the word that means: *coming back to where you belong.*

Allah is honoring even the prayer that begins in panic. He is naming it homecoming.

The reproach of this ayah is not going to fall on the panicked prayer. It is going to fall on what happens *after.*

## 3. *Adhāqahum* — When Allah Lets Them *Taste*

Now we come to the verb that will undo us.

> **ثُمَّ إِذَآ أَذَاقَهُم مِّنْهُ رَحْمَةً** — *Then when He **lets them taste** of mercy from Himself…*

The verb is *adhāqa* — from the root **ذ-و-ق** — *dhāqa,* to taste. The form *adhāqa* is Form IV, the causative: *to cause to taste, to give a taste of.*

Let me teach you the concept of "tasting" in Arabic before we proceed.

In Arabic, *dhawq* — taste — has a very particular semantic field. To taste something is *not* to consume it. It is to bring it briefly to the tongue, to register its flavor, and then — to either swallow or set down. A taste is, by its very nature, *a small portion.* A sample. The first sip from a glass, not the glass itself.

When Allah uses *adhāqa* — *He let them taste* — He is not saying *He drowned them in mercy.* He is not saying *He overwhelmed them with mercy.* He is saying *He let a flavor of His mercy reach their tongue.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold this against the first verb. When harm came, it was *massa* — the lightest touch. When mercy came, it was *adhāqa* — the smallest taste. Allah is being precise about the *scale* of His dealings here. He is describing the most minimal possible interaction in both directions.

And yet look at the asymmetry of the human response.

A *touch* of harm produced *full prayer, full return, full homecoming.*

A *taste* of mercy produced *full shirk.*

The minimum dose of pain pulled them all the way back to Him. The minimum dose of relief pulled some of them all the way away.

[PAUSE]

There is something else hidden in *adhāqahum.* Allah says **مِّنْهُ** — *minhu* — "from Him." The mercy is *attributed.* Allah is making absolutely sure, grammatically, that the taste of relief is traceable to its source. The food on the tongue has a sender. He is not letting them eat anonymously.

And that is exactly what makes the next clause so painful. The shirk that follows is not a misunderstanding of where the mercy came from. The mercy was *labeled.* It said *from Me* on the wrapper. They tasted it, they knew the sender, and *still* a faction turned to associate partners with Him.

[PAUSE]

Let's pause and let this land. Allah is showing us the structure of ingratitude with surgical precision. The shirk that emerges after relief is not innocent. It is not a forgetting. It is a recognition that has been actively pushed aside.

Now let me show you one more linguistic feature before we leave Part One. Look at the word **فَرِيقٌ** — *farīq* — "a faction."

Allah does not say *they* all returned to shirk. He says *a faction of them.* The Arabic word *farīq* denotes a *part* of a larger group — a portion, not the whole. Why is this so important?

Because it tells us that the human response to relief is *not* uniform. Some, when they taste mercy, deepen their gratitude. Some, when they taste mercy, drift. Some, when they taste mercy, turn fully away. Allah is not painting humanity with a single brush. He is naming a sub-pattern within humanity.

Which means the ayah is not really a description of "those people over there." It is a question, addressed to each one of us, about which faction we belong to *this week* — because the test of relief recurs. Every relief is a new fork in the road.

[PAUSE]

So three words have done all this work:

- *Massa* — the touch — has told us how little it takes to surface our true orientation.
- *Munībīna* — homecoming — has told us that the soul's reflex direction is toward Allah.
- *Adhāqa* — the taste — has told us that even the smallest mercy is sufficient to produce ingratitude, when ingratitude is what we have prepared in ourselves.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# Part Two: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Strange Geometry of Human Memory

Begin here, where the listener already lives.

Have you noticed that pain is sticky and relief is slippery?

When you are sick, you cannot imagine being well. The illness fills the entire frame of consciousness. You make promises to yourself: *when I get better, I will never take my health for granted again. I will be different. I will live differently.*

Then you get better. And within seventy-two hours, the wellness is invisible to you. The body is doing exactly what you prayed for it to do — breathing, walking, sleeping without pain — and you cannot feel it. You are already complaining about traffic.

This is not a moral failure unique to you. This is a structural feature of human cognition. Psychologists call it the *hedonic treadmill* or *hedonic adaptation.* Whatever you have, you cease to notice. Whatever you lack, you notice constantly. Loss is loud. Possession is silent.

Now here is what is stunning. Allah is not just describing this phenomenon. He is naming its *spiritual cost.*

The ayah is telling us: the speed at which mercy becomes invisible is the speed at which shirk creeps back in. Not because we consciously choose another god. But because, the moment relief becomes background, we begin attributing it to *causes* — to our own effort, to the doctor's skill, to good luck, to the strength of our planning. The mercy that arrived from *Him* now arrives, in our internal narrative, *from everywhere but Him.* That redistribution of credit is the seedbed of shirk.

[PAUSE]

Watch how subtle this is. The person who, in the hospital, said *Yā Rabb, please* — and three days later said *thank God my doctor was good* — has not, in their own mind, betrayed anyone. They have simply *attributed.* The doctor was good. The treatment worked. The body responded.

But notice what has dropped out of the sentence: *who made the doctor's skill exist, who held the treatment in being, who designed the body to respond.* The chain has been cut at the second link. And in cutting the chain, *farīqun minhum bi-rabbihim yushrikūn* — a faction has begun associating partners with their Lord.

This is the diagnosis. Shirk in this ayah is not idol-worship. It is *credit-misattribution.* The slow, automatic, almost involuntary handing-over of thanks to the secondary causes — and the silent removal of the One who is the cause of every cause.

The question the ayah leaves you with is not *do you worship an idol?* The question is: *the last time something painful resolved, where did the thanks go?*

[PAUSE]

What changes about tomorrow if this lands?

One small practice: when relief arrives — even small relief — make the attribution explicit before the moment passes. Out loud, if you can. *Alhamdulillāh, this is from Him.* You are not being performative. You are training your nervous system to keep the chain of causation intact. You are refusing to cut it at the second link.

Because the geometry of memory will, on its own, route gratitude away from Him. The geometry has to be actively bent back. That bending — that *act of attribution* — is the daily, hourly *ināba.* The continuous coming-home.

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: Why Allah Tests with Mercy, Not Just with Pain

Here is something that may surprise you, and I want to take it slowly.

We tend to think of trials as the painful things. The illness. The loss. The fear. We say someone is "being tested" when their life is hard.

But this ayah names a different kind of test, and it may be the harder one.

Look again at the structure. Harm comes. People pray. Then mercy comes. And it is *the mercy* that produces the spiritual failure. The shirk does not erupt during the suffering. It erupts during the relief.

This means relief itself is a test — and a more dangerous one than suffering, because we don't perceive it as a test. We perceive it as a reward. As a confirmation that we are okay. As a green light to relax our vigilance. The very framing of relief as *the end of the test* is what allows the deeper test to begin without us noticing.

[PAUSE]

Imagine a teacher who gives a difficult exam. Most students assume the exam is the test. But this teacher has a second test, hidden inside the moment when the grades come back. The students who scored well — *what do they do with the success?* Do they study harder, or do they coast? Do they thank the teacher, or do they congratulate themselves?

That second test is the real one, because it reveals not what they know but who they are.

Mercy from Allah is exactly that second test. The first test — the suffering — measures whether you will turn to Him. Most people pass that test on reflex; the fitrah does the work for them. The second test — the mercy — measures whether you will *stay turned* after the pressure is off. That test, almost no one passes on reflex. It requires character that has been built deliberately, over time.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ would, after every relief, increase his worship rather than decrease it. After Badr — not relax, but more prayer. After Mecca's conquest — not celebration, but *Subḥānaka Rabbanā wa bi-ḥamdik, astaghfiruka wa atūbu ilayk.* He understood, more than anyone, that mercy is the dangerous test. He answered the test by leaning harder into ināba *precisely* at the moments when others would have leaned back.

[PAUSE]

So here is the practical reframe.

Most of us pray hardest when we are in trouble. That is not wrong — it is human. But the ayah is teaching us that the *more spiritually consequential* prayer is the one we make when we are not in trouble. The prayer of the well person. The prayer after the surgery succeeded. The prayer after the paycheck cleared. The prayer when the relationship is good.

If your spiritual life looks like a heart-rate monitor — spiking during pain, flatlining during peace — you are passing the easy test and failing the hard one.

The work, then, is to make peacetime prayer feel as natural as crisis prayer. To pray when nothing is wrong with the same urgency you'd pray if something were. Not because you are manufacturing emergency. But because the absence of emergency is *itself* the moment Allah is watching most closely.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

One specific application: tie one prayer per day — one *dhikr,* one short sūrah, one moment of acknowledgment — to a moment of ease, not a moment of stress. When the coffee tastes right. When the child laughs. When the traffic clears. *That* is the moment Allah is testing. Meet Him there.

Because the people in this ayah were not bad people. They were people who only knew how to find Him through pain. And when pain ended, the path ended.

The Muslim who keeps the path open *through* the relief is the Muslim who has understood Surah Ar-Rum.

# Closing Synthesis

## Bringing It Together

Look at what these three words have shown us, working together.

*Massa* told us how *little* it takes to surface our true direction. The brush of a fingertip of harm is enough.

*Munībīna* told us where that direction *points.* Home. To Him. Always.

*Adhāqa* told us how *little* mercy it takes for that direction to be lost again — if our hearts have not been trained to hold gratitude.

And *farīq* told us that this is not a sentence on humanity. It is a sorting. Some pass the test of mercy. Some don't. The ayah is asking us, each time we taste relief, which faction we are choosing to join *this time.*

The architecture of the ayah, now that we can see it, is breathtaking. Allah deliberately matched the *minimum* of pain against the *minimum* of mercy, and then exposed the *maximum* asymmetry of the human response. He used the gentlest verbs in the language to show us the harshest truth about ourselves.

He did not need to describe a great catastrophe to make us see this. He chose *touch* and *taste* on purpose, because if those are enough to swing the soul in both directions, then we cannot blame the size of the suffering or the smallness of the relief for our spiritual movement. The movement is in us.

## Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. The last time something painful in your life resolved — where did your thanks actually go? To Him, by name? To the doctor? To luck? To your own effort?

2. What is the ratio between your crisis prayers and your peacetime prayers? Be honest. What does that ratio say about which test you are passing?

3. When relief comes, what is the very first sentence that forms in your mind? Whose name is in it?

4. Is there a layer of comfort in your life so thick that even the *touch* of harm — the lightest reminder — has stopped reaching you? What might it cost you to thin that layer deliberately?

5. Allah honored the panic-prayer with the word *munībīn* — homecoming. What is stopping you from offering Him the same prayer *before* the panic arrives?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The smallest touch of pain is enough to bring the soul home, and the smallest taste of mercy is enough to send a part of it away — unless the heart has been trained, in peacetime, to keep returning.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need a catastrophe to begin praying as you would in one. You do not need to wait for the hospital, the turbulence, the diagnosis, the loss. The same fitrah that would surface in those moments is alive in you right now, this minute, sitting in the ordinary safety of your ordinary day. It is just covered.

Uncover a little of it tonight. Pray once — even briefly — *as if* the touch had already come. Address Him by His name. Attribute one thing — one small good thing about today — to Him explicitly, out loud. Refuse to let the chain be cut at the second link.

That is the path of the ones who, when mercy was tasted, did not slip into the faction Allah named here. That is the path of the ones who stayed *munībīn* even when nothing was wrong.

## Du'a

> Yā Rabb, You know that the touch of harm makes us call You, and the taste of mercy makes us forget You. Do not leave us in that pattern. Train our tongues to thank You in ease as urgently as we beg You in difficulty. Keep the chain of attribution unbroken in our hearts, so that when relief comes, we recognize the Sender — and come home to You before the panic, and not only because of it.
>
> *Āmīn.*
