# The Right Upon Us: When Mercy Becomes Obligation

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:30:47] وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ رُسُلًا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِمْ فَجَآءُوهُم بِٱلْبَيِّنَـٰتِ فَٱنتَقَمْنَا مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا۟ ۖ وَكَانَ حَقًّا عَلَيْنَا نَصْرُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

**Transliteration:**
*Wa laqad arsalnā min qablika rusulan ilā qawmihim fa-jā'ūhum bil-bayyināt, fantaqamnā mina alladhīna ajramū, wa kāna ḥaqqan ʿalaynā naṣru-l-mu'minīn.*

**Translation:**
"And We had certainly sent before you messengers to their peoples, and they came to them with clear proofs; then We took retribution from those who committed crimes, and incumbent upon Us was the support of the believers."

---

### Rebuilding the World

To feel this ayah, you have to step inside a particular kind of waiting.

Imagine the small community in Makkah. They are not yet a state. They are not yet protected. They are mocked in the marketplace. Some are dragged across hot sand. Some lose their families. Some lose their lives. They pray quietly, and they listen to verses descend that promise — what, exactly? Sometimes the promises seem to point to a future they cannot see. The Persians and the Romans, who fill the opening of this very surah, are vast powers in the distance. The believers in Makkah are perhaps a few dozen, perhaps a few hundred. They look small. They feel small.

And the surah they are reading — Ar-Rum — has been pulling them into a vast lens. It opened with empires. It moved them through cosmic signs: the night, the day, the wind, the rain, the dead earth that becomes alive again. It is teaching them to read history the way you read weather — not as random noise, but as a pattern with an Author.

And now, right at this point in the surah, the lens narrows. From empires and clouds and rain — down to a single phrase, slipped in at the end of an ayah, almost in passing:

*"...and incumbent upon Us was the support of the believers."*

[PAUSE]

You have to understand: there is a particular kind of word in Arabic that is so weighty, so legally and morally heavy, that when it is used about Allah it should stop you. *Ḥaqq* — a right, a due, a binding claim. And here Allah uses it not in the way we usually expect — not "Allah has a *ḥaqq* over you" — but the other direction. **Something has become a *ḥaqq* upon Him. Self-imposed. Self-binding.**

This is what we are about to sit with.

---

### The Hook

The strangeness is this: Allah, who needs nothing, who is bound by no one, who is the source of every obligation — is here telling you that He has placed something as a *ḥaqq* upon Himself. He could have said "We help the believers." He could have said "We will support them." He says something far stranger. He says it is a *right owed*. **A debt He has written on His own ledger.**

What kind of love is that? And — listen carefully — *who exactly are these "believers" who can call in such a debt?*

By the end of this ayah, we will see that Allah has done something with the word *naṣr* — "support" — that He withheld from the word "messengers" in the same sentence. And in that withholding, He has invited every one of us into the promise.

But we are not ready to see that yet. Let us build.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa laqad* — The Oath Without the Oath

#### The concept first

In Arabic, there is a way of speaking that carries the weight of an oath without ever using the word "I swear." It is a construction so distinctive that classical grammarians gave it a name: *lām al-qasam* (the *lām* of swearing) combined with *qad* (the particle of certainty/realization).

In English, if I want to swear without swearing, I might say: "I'm telling you — this happened." That little phrase "I'm telling you" carries oath-weight even without an oath. Arabic does the same thing, but with one short syllable: *laqad*.

Whenever you see *laqad* opening a statement, Arabic ears hear: *"By God — this is certain. This is not opinion. This is not possibility. This is sealed."*

#### Opening the word

The ayah opens: **وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا** — *Wa laqad arsalnā*. "And — by oath — We had certainly sent..."

Notice something. Allah is not introducing a controversial claim. Everyone in Makkah, even the Quraysh, knew that messengers had been sent before. The Prophet (ﷺ) had already been talking about Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb, Musa. **This is not new information.** So why the oath-weight? Why *laqad*?

Let's sit with this. When Allah swears about something already known, He is not trying to prove it to you. He is trying to **make you feel it**. He is using the oath to reach past your intellect, which already concedes the point, and to grip your heart, which has not yet been moved by what the point implies.

Here is what He is making you feel: *this pattern is real. It is not metaphor. It is not poetic exaggeration. Messengers were sent. Their people responded in specific ways. There were specific consequences. And I am about to tell you that the same pattern is operating around you right now.*

[PAUSE]

#### The forward pull

*Laqad* is the oath. *Arsalnā* — "We sent" — is the divine "We," the royal plural that classical scholars call the *nūn al-'aẓamah*, the *nūn* of majesty. Whenever Allah uses this plural, He is not multiplying Himself. He is signaling the **vastness of the doing**. Not "I sent a messenger" — that would sound personal and small. But "We sent" — with all that "We" implies of cosmic dispatch, angelic accompaniment, scripture revealed, miracles distributed, hearts prepared, peoples warned.

This is a *production*, not a memo. And the oath is telling you: it really happened. It is still happening.

#### The conclusion of this element

So before we have read three words, Allah has done two things: He has sworn (without using the word "swear"), and He has reminded you that whatever follows is not a small private affair. It is a divine pattern He is making structurally binding upon Himself.

The grammar has already started preparing us for the *ḥaqq* that is coming.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Intaqamnā mina alladhīna ajramū* — Retribution Against "Those Who Committed Crimes"

#### The concept first

In Arabic, when you want to identify a group of people, you have many choices. You can call them by their tribe. By their nation. By their religion. By their disbelief. By a specific action. The choice of *which* descriptor to use is theology.

Watch: in this ayah, when Allah describes the people the messengers were sent to, He calls them — at first — simply *qawmihim*, "their peoples." Neutral. No judgment. Just the human group the messenger belonged to. But then, when retribution falls, Allah does not say "We took retribution from *them*" (the same peoples). He shifts the description. He says:

**فَٱنتَقَمْنَا مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا۟** — *fa-ntaqamnā mina alladhīna ajramū* — "then We took retribution from **those who committed crimes**."

#### Opening the word

Stay with me. This is one of the most merciful pivots in the Quran, and it is easy to miss.

The root of *ajramū* is *j-r-m*, which classically carries the meaning of cutting, severing, and from there: the deliberate act of severing what should not be severed. A *mujrim* — a "criminal" in Quranic usage — is not someone who made a mistake. He is someone who, knowingly, cut himself off from what was offered to him. The *bayyināt* — the clear proofs — had come. The path was visible. And he severed himself from it deliberately.

Here is what Allah is doing with this word choice. The messenger was sent to *qawmihim* — to his **people**, broadly, all of them. But the punishment did not fall on the *qawm*. It fell on a subset: *alladhīna ajramū*, "those who committed crimes." The group Allah punishes is **narrower** than the group the messenger was sent to.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with that. The mercy in this is enormous. Allah is telling you: I do not punish a *people*. I punish *those who, within a people, severed themselves deliberately*. The category of those-who-are-destroyed is always defined by their action, never by their birth, never by their tribe, never by their lineage.

#### Deepening

There is more. Notice the verb *intaqamnā* — "We took retribution." The classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib) preserve a precise meaning: *intiqām* in Quranic usage is not the hot, impulsive revenge that the modern English word "vengeance" conjures. It is the **measured response of a sovereign to a deliberate violation**. It is judicial. It is proportionate. It comes *after* the *bayyināt* came. It comes *after* the severing.

In other words: the order of the ayah is itself a courtroom. **Messengers → clear proofs → deliberate severing → measured retribution.** Every step is named. No step is skipped. There is no divine arbitrariness here. There is the patient unrolling of a process that the people inside it could have exited at any moment.

#### The conclusion of this element

So when Allah promises, at the end of this ayah, to support the believers — He is not describing a partisan deity who picks favorites. He is describing a moral architecture. The architecture has two halves. One half: *those who deliberately sever themselves from what is clear* face a measured response. The other half — and we are about to enter it — *those who do not sever themselves* have something owed to them.

The first half is what Allah does in the world. The second half is what He has bound Himself to.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa kāna ḥaqqan ʿalaynā* — "And it was a right upon Us"

This is the center of the ayah. Everything before it has been preparing us to read this.

#### The concept first

In Arabic legal and moral vocabulary, *ḥaqq* is one of the heaviest words a language can carry. It is the word for **a right, a due, a binding claim that exists in reality whether or not it is acknowledged**. When Arabs said *li-fulānin ʿalayya ḥaqq* — "so-and-so has a *ḥaqq* upon me" — they meant: there is a debt of obligation between us, real and structural, that I am bound to fulfill.

The opposite of *ḥaqq* is *bāṭil* — falsehood, voidness, that which is structurally empty. *Ḥaqq* is what has weight in reality.

Now: in normal religious speech, we expect to hear *ḥaqq Allāhi ʿalā al-ʿibād* — "Allah's *ḥaqq* upon the servants." We owe Him. That is the natural direction of the moral universe.

#### Opening the phrase

But this ayah reverses the arrow. It says: **وَكَانَ حَقًّا عَلَيْنَا** — *wa kāna ḥaqqan ʿalaynā* — "and it was a *ḥaqq* upon Us."

Upon **Us**.

Read it again. Slowly. *Allah is saying that something is a binding right upon Himself.*

[PAUSE — long pause here]

Let's sit with this. In Surah Al-An'am (6:12, 6:54), Allah says *kataba ʿalā nafsihi al-raḥmah* — "He has written mercy upon Himself." That is the same structural move. Allah, who is bound by no external party, who answers to no one, who is the source of every obligation in existence — has chosen to bind Himself. Not because anyone could have bound Him. But because His love for His servants takes the form of **self-imposed obligation**.

Think about what that means in a human relationship. The difference between someone who says "I'll be there if I can" and someone who says "you can count on me — I owe you this" is the difference between possibility and *ḥaqq*. The second person has stepped into a different kind of relationship with you. They have made themselves bound.

Allah — the Unbound — has made Himself bound to something. To what?

#### Deepening: the brilliance of *kāna*

Notice the verb: *kāna*. "Was." Past tense. Not "is." Not "will be." **"Was."**

In Arabic, when *kāna* is used about Allah, it does not function the way past tense functions in English. It does not mean "this used to be true." Classical grammarians explain it: when *kāna* is predicated of Allah, it points to a **timeless reality that has always already been the case**. "It *was* a *ḥaqq* upon Us" means: this obligation did not begin when this ayah was revealed. It did not begin when the messengers were sent. **It has always been so.** Before the messengers, before the peoples, before time itself — supporting the believers was already a *ḥaqq* upon Allah.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight of that? The structure of mercy you are inside of, right now — it predates you. It predates the world. It is woven into reality before reality began.

#### The asymmetric absence — *the* deepest move in the ayah

Now we come to what may be the most extraordinary thing in this entire verse. And we have to read it through what is *not* said.

The ayah names two specific groups:
- *alladhīna ajramū* — those who committed crimes (the destroyed)
- *al-mu'minīn* — the believers (the supported)

And it names one group it does *not* identify by name: **the messengers' followers from the past peoples.**

In other ayahs of this exact pattern — for example, Surah Yunus 10:103 — Allah ends with: *thumma nunajjī rusulanā wa alladhīna āmanū* — "Then We deliver Our messengers and those who believed." There, the deliverance is tied to the **specific historical messengers and their specific followers**. The promise is locked to the past.

But here, in Ar-Rum 47, Allah does something different. He does not say "We saved the messengers and their believers." He says: **"incumbent upon Us was the support of *al-mu'minīn*"** — "the believers."

Definite. Open. Unqualified by time or people. **Not "those believers." Not "their believers." Just — the believers.**

Let's sit with what this means. Allah took the historical pattern — messengers, proofs, severing, destruction, deliverance — and at the moment of stating the promise, He **opened the noun**. He did not lock it to the past. He did not say "the believers of Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb." He said: **al-mu'minīn**. The believers. As a category. **You. Anyone. Whoever steps into that name.**

[PAUSE]

The grammatical absence — the *missing* historical qualifier — is the door He has left open. He could have closed it. He chose not to. And in the choosing, He has bound Himself to support **anyone, in any era, who lives inside the category of *īmān*.**

This is what I told you we were not ready to see at the beginning. We are ready now.

#### The conclusion of this element

So the phrase *wa kāna ḥaqqan ʿalaynā naṣru-l-mu'minīn* contains four moves stacked on top of each other:

1. *Ḥaqq* — a right, the heaviest word for binding obligation
2. *ʿAlaynā* — *upon Us*, reversing the natural direction of divine-human obligation
3. *Kāna* — a timeless "was" that means *always already*
4. *Al-mu'minīn* — the believers, definite and open, with no historical qualifier locking it shut

Allah has made the support of the believers a timeless, self-imposed, structurally binding obligation upon Himself, defined not by birth or era but by the single question: do you stand inside *īmān*?

[PAUSE — long pause]

### Bridge to Part 2

We have seen what the linguistic construction does. An oath that grips the heart. A verb of retribution that is judicial, not impulsive. A pivot in description that narrows the destroyed and broadens the saved. And a final phrase — *ḥaqqan ʿalaynā* — in which Allah binds Himself to a promise that is timeless, definite, and addressed to *anyone* who steps into the name of believer.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The God Who Owes You Something He Could Have Refused

#### Start where you live

Think about the most binding word in your own relational vocabulary. "I promise." It carries weight. But you and I both know — promises can be broken. We can offer excuses. We can renegotiate.

Now think about a stronger word: "I owe you." When you say *I owe you* to someone, you are no longer in the territory of intention. You are in the territory of debt. The relationship has a structure now. There is a ledger. You are bound until you pay.

And there is one step further. The strongest possible version of this in human language is something like: **"I have written this into law for myself."** A king who issues a decree binding upon himself. A judge who recuses himself in advance of a case. A parent who signs over an irrevocable trust. The point of these acts is that the person *who had no obligation* has chosen to manufacture an obligation, *on themselves*, that no one can revoke — including, sometimes, themselves.

This last category — the **self-imposed binding obligation** — is the closest human analogy to *ḥaqqan ʿalaynā*.

#### Now let the ayah illuminate

Here is what is staggering. Allah, who is *al-Ghaniyy* — the One Free of All Need — who could have created a universe in which He owed His creatures nothing, who could have offered support as pure favor revocable at any moment — has instead **structured the universe so that supporting the believers is a debt on His own ledger.**

He did not have to do this. He chose to.

Think about why. In a universe where Allah's support of the believers is *raḥmah* alone — pure unmerited mercy — the believer lives in hope. He hopes Allah will help him. He prays Allah will help him. But there is no claim. There is only request.

In a universe where Allah's support of the believers is *ḥaqq* — written, structurally binding — the believer lives in something different. He lives in **certainty**. Not certainty that he is righteous (he never knows that). Not certainty that help comes the way he imagines (it almost never does). But certainty that **the architecture of reality itself has been bent toward him by the One who bent it**. The arc is real. The ledger is real. The debt is real. The believer does not need to convince Allah to help him. He needs to **stay inside the category** to which help has already been promised.

[PAUSE]

Do you understand what this changes? It changes the inner posture of the believer entirely. The believer is not a beggar at the door of a king who might or might not respond. The believer is — and this is shocking language but it is the language Allah used — **a creditor.** Allah has written something on His own ledger. The believer's job is not to earn the help. The help has been written into the structure of being. The believer's job is to *be* a believer.

#### Building toward the harder question

But here is where this gets difficult. Because if the help is a *ḥaqq* — a binding obligation upon Allah — then **why do believers suffer?** Why are they killed? Why are they driven from their homes? Why do they pray for years for things that never come?

Sit with this question. Do not rush past it. The ayah was revealed to people who were being beaten in the streets of Makkah. They heard *ḥaqqan ʿalaynā naṣru-l-mu'minīn* — "the support of the believers is a right upon Us" — and they walked out of the gathering and were spit on. Some of them died before the *naṣr* came visibly. Bilal under the rock. Sumayyah pierced through. Yasir tortured to death.

So what kind of *ḥaqq* is this?

Here is what we have to grasp. *Naṣr* in the Quran does not mean "Allah will arrange your life to look the way you wanted it to look." That is not what the word means. *Naṣr* — from the root *n-ṣ-r* — means **the granting of victory, the giving of decisive support against an opposing force**. And the opposing force, in the deepest reading, is not always the Quraysh. The opposing force is **whatever pulls a soul out of *īmān*.**

The *naṣr* Allah owes is the *naṣr* that keeps you a believer. It is the *naṣr* that, on the day you are spit on, holds your heart together. It is the *naṣr* that, on the day you bury someone you loved, opens your tongue to say *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*. It is the *naṣr* that, on the day your prayers seem to have produced nothing, lets you pray again the next morning. **Allah has bound Himself to keep you a believer through whatever comes.** That is the *ḥaqq*. Everything visible is downstream of that.

Bilal under the rock said *aḥad, aḥad* — "One, One." That was the *naṣr*. That was Allah keeping His ledger.

The believers of Makkah who were eventually delivered — they received another layer of *naṣr*. The believers of Uhud who were martyred — they received another. The believers in eras after the Prophet (ﷺ) who never saw a visible victory — they too received the *naṣr* that mattered, because they died as believers.

**The promise is not that your life will be easy. The promise is that you will not lose your *īmān*.** And nothing — *nothing* — is bigger than that.

#### What this changes about tomorrow

If you take this seriously, here is what shifts. When you pray and the answer does not come, you stop asking the wrong question. The wrong question is: *did Allah hear me?* He always hears. The wrong question is: *am I doing something wrong?* Maybe, maybe not — but that is a separate examination. The right question, the one this ayah teaches, is: **am I still a believer?**

If yes, the *ḥaqq* is being paid. The *naṣr* is happening right now, in the very fact that you are still standing inside the name. The visible thing you asked for is downstream. It may come. It may not come. It may come transformed into something you did not recognize as the answer. But the **structural support — the *ḥaqq* upon Allah** — is being paid every breath you stay a believer.

This is the inner posture the surah is trying to build in us. We are not orphans waiting for rescue. **We are creditors of a debt that has already been written.**

### Theme Two: The Door He Left Open

#### Start where you live

Think about an inheritance. Imagine a family wealth, accumulated over generations, that is held in a trust. Trusts have specific language. The trust can say: "for my children." That is one kind of trust. It is closed. It names a specific people. When those people die, the trust dies.

Or the trust can say: "for any descendant of my line." That is a different kind of trust. It is **structural**. It does not close. As long as the line continues, the trust continues to pay out.

The first kind of trust is locked to a generation. The second kind is locked to a **category**.

#### Now let the ayah illuminate

We saw, in Part 1, that Allah did something extraordinary with the closing phrase of this ayah. He had every grammatical option available to lock the promise to the past. He could have said: *We delivered Our messengers and the believers among them.* He has said exactly that elsewhere in the Quran. But here, in Ar-Rum 47, He **opened the noun**. He said: *al-mu'minīn*. The believers. Definite, but open. Not theirs. Not yours. *The* believers.

Let's stay inside this for a moment. Allah, in the middle of describing a historical pattern about past peoples, took the moment of promise and **detached it from history**. He attached it instead to a category. And the category is, in principle, **enterable by anyone, in any era**.

This is the door He left open.

#### Why this matters

I want you to feel why this is so generous. Consider: the easiest thing for a religion to do is to lock its promises to its founding generation. Most religions, in their late forms, drift toward a kind of nostalgia — "the saints were the early ones; we today live in the shadow." There is a sense that the spiritual structure was complete then and is now being dispensed in smaller and smaller measures.

Allah did not allow that drift to take place in the Quran's promise architecture. By using *al-mu'minīn* — the believers, as a definite open category — He flattened the timeline. The believer of the 7th century, the believer of the 14th century, the believer of the 21st century, the believer who will exist in the 30th century — all of them stand on **equal structural ground** in this promise. The *naṣr* is owed to the category. The category is enterable now.

[PAUSE]

This is why the Prophet (ﷺ) said in a famous hadith: *the strangers* — *al-ghurabā'* — *will have a portion like that of the earliest companions*, because they held to *īmān* in an age when holding to it was strange. The mechanism behind that statement is exactly this ayah's grammar. The promise is structural, not historical. It pays out to anyone in the category.

You are in an age, perhaps, when *īmān* feels strange. When the surrounding culture does not reinforce it. When prayer feels swimming upstream. **The ayah is for you.** Allah did not address it to a vanished people. He addressed it to a category, and you can step into it. You can stay in it. The *ḥaqq* is operating now.

#### Building deeper

But here is the second layer, and it is harder. The door is open — but it is open into a category, not into a name. *Al-mu'minīn* is not a tribe. It is not a passport. It is not a certificate of membership. It is a *state of soul* that must be **renewed**.

The classical scholars distinguished between *islām* (the outer submission, the visible posture) and *īmān* (the inner state, what is actually inside the chest). *Al-mu'minīn* is the second kind. You are in the category when your heart is genuinely turned to Allah. You are out of it when your heart turns away.

This is sobering. The *ḥaqq* is on Allah, yes. But it is owed to *al-mu'minīn*, and *being* among them is not a one-time event. It is the **continuous orientation of the heart**.

So the promise is structural and timeless and addressed to anyone — *and* it requires you to keep showing up as the one to whom it is addressed. The door is open. You have to keep walking through it.

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Two things shift. First — never again let yourself think the promise has aged. The believer of 2026 stands exactly where the believer of 626 stood, structurally. The *ḥaqq* is on Allah. The category is open. Your era is not a late era. **Your era is the era you are in, and the promise is in it with you.**

Second — be jealous about your *īmān*. The door is open, but the door opens onto a category that requires inhabitance. Do not let your heart drift out of the category for convenience. Do not let cynicism eat the certainty that the promise is real. Do not let visible difficulty convince you that the structural support is not happening. The *ḥaqq* is being paid. Stay in the category to whom it is being paid.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at the ayah whole.

> *And We had certainly sent before you messengers to their peoples, and they came to them with clear proofs; then We took retribution from those who committed crimes, and incumbent upon Us was the support of the believers.*

Notice the structure now:

- **Oath-weight at the start**: *wa laqad*. This is sealed.
- **A pattern in history**: messengers → peoples → clear proofs.
- **A pivot in description**: the destroyed are not the peoples; they are *those who deliberately severed themselves*.
- **A reversal of obligation**: *ḥaqqan ʿalaynā* — upon Us.
- **A timeless verb**: *kāna* — always already.
- **An open category**: *al-mu'minīn* — the believers, anywhere, anyone.

The ayah builds a structure: it sets the historical scene, it narrows the punished, it broadens the saved, and it ends — not with a description but with a **divine self-binding** that has no time stamp on it.

What the linguistic discoveries reveal and what the thematic depths reveal are the **same insight from two directions**. Linguistically, Allah used grammar to encode a promise that is heavier than promise (*ḥaqq*), older than time (*kāna*), and open to anyone (*al-mu'minīn*). Thematically, this means: the believer lives inside a structure of binding divine obligation that predates the universe and pays out to anyone who stays in the name.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time you prayed for something specific and didn't get it the way you asked? What if the *naṣr* you actually received that day was the very fact that you kept believing?

2. Have you been treating Allah's help like a favor you must beg for — or like a *ḥaqq* He has written upon Himself? What changes in your prayer if you carry the second posture into the next *du'ā*?

3. When you read about the early believers — the suffering, the migrations, the martyrdoms — do you feel separated from them by time? What does it mean that this ayah refused to separate you?

4. *Īmān* is the category to whom the *ḥaqq* is owed. What in your life right now is most likely to pull your heart out of that category? And what would it look like to be jealous about staying in?

5. Sit with this: Allah, who needed nothing, chose to make Himself bound to you. What is the appropriate response to that kind of love?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah has bound Himself, in a debt timeless and structural, to keep you a believer through whatever comes — and the door into that promise is open, in your century, to anyone who walks in.

---

### The Closing Invitation

Let something shift here. Not a new piece of information — a new posture. The next time you pray, the next time you face a difficulty, the next time you feel the small voice that says *maybe I am alone in this* — remember:

There is a ledger. It is in Allah's hand. He wrote it. He could have refused to write it. He wrote it anyway. Your name, the moment you became a believer, was inscribed on the side that is owed. Not the side that owes. **The side that is owed.**

Stay in the category. The rest is His.

---

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, who has made the support of the believers a *ḥaqq* upon Yourself — keep us in the category to whom the *ḥaqq* is owed. When visible help is slow, keep our hearts inside the name. When prayers seem unanswered, let us remember that the deepest *naṣr* is that we are still standing inside *īmān*. And on the day we meet You, let it be said of us: these were among *al-mu'minīn*.

Āmīn.
