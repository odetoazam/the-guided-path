# Every Faction Rejoicing in What It Has

## Surah Ar-Rum, Ayah 32

> مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ فَرَّقُوا۟ دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُوا۟ شِيَعًا ۖ كُلُّ حِزْبٍۭ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ

> *Mina-lladhīna farraqū dīnahum wa kānū shiya'an — kullu ḥizbin bimā ladayhim fariḥūn.*

> "[Be not] of those who fractured their religion and became sects — every faction rejoicing in what it has."

---

## Introduction — The World Before the Ayah

I want you to picture something before we open this ayah.

You're standing at the edge of a great forest. You can see the whole canopy from where you stand. From above, it looks like one living thing — one shade of green, one ecosystem, one continuous lung breathing in and out with the seasons.

Now you walk inside.

And suddenly the forest is not one thing anymore. There's a group of people gathered around one particular tree, swearing the whole forest grows from its roots. There's another group around a different tree, certain *theirs* is the source. Each cluster has its own fire, its own songs, its own enemies. They can no longer see the canopy. They can barely see the next clearing.

And here's the strange part — every group is happy.

Every group is sure.

Every group is at peace with *its tree*.

[PAUSE]

That is the world this ayah is describing. And it sits at a very specific place in the surah. Two ayahs earlier, Allah has just delivered one of the most famous declarations in the Quran — the ayah of *fiṭrah*. **"Set your face toward the religion, inclining to truth — the fiṭrah of Allah upon which He has created humanity. No alteration to the creation of Allah. That is the upright religion."**

Allah has just said: there is one design. One nature. One pull at the bottom of every human being.

Then ayah 31: turn back to Him. Establish prayer. **Do not be of those who associate partners with Allah** — the *mushrikīn*.

Then ayah 32 — and this is what we'll spend our time inside — Allah specifies a particular kind of *shirk* most people don't recognize as *shirk* at all.

The people who took the one religion and **fractured** it. Who became sects. Each sect hugging its piece, calling that piece "the truth," and rejoicing.

[PAUSE]

Here's the hook I want you to carry into this reflection: the people Allah is describing in this ayah do not think they are doing anything wrong. They are not malicious. They are not consciously deviant. They are *rejoicing*. They are at peace. They feel they are on the haqq.

And Allah is warning *you*, the believer, **do not be of them**.

Which means it is possible to be of them without knowing it.

That is the question this ayah is going to put to us. Not "are those *other* people sectarian?" — but "could that quiet satisfaction I feel about *my* group, *my* shaykh, *my* madhhab, *my* circle, *my* understanding — could that be the very thing Allah is naming here?"

Let's open the words and find out.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The verb *farraqū* — to shatter, not just to separate

Before we look at the word itself, let me teach you something about Arabic that the listener of the Quran would have felt instinctively.

In Arabic, many verbs come in different "forms" — patterns that intensify, redirect, or transform the basic meaning. The simplest form is just the bare root: *faraqa* — to separate, to distinguish between two things. Clean. Neutral. You separate the wheat from the chaff. You distinguish truth from falsehood.

But then there is a second form — *farraqa* — which doubles the middle letter. And in Arabic, doubling the middle letter typically signals **intensification** or **causing the action to happen repeatedly, on many objects, with force.**

Think of the difference in English between "to cut" and "to *shred*." Both involve separation. But "shred" tells you something happened over and over, in many directions, until what was once whole is no longer recognizable as a single thing.

That second form — the *shredding* form — is what Allah uses here.

**فَرَّقُوا دِينَهُمْ.**

Not *faraqū dīnahum* — "they separated their religion" — as if they made one clean distinction. **Farraqū.** They *shattered* it. They *fragmented* it. They took one whole, living thing and they broke it into pieces, and broke the pieces into pieces, and broke those into pieces.

Now notice something else — and this is where the grammar starts whispering.

The verb is active. *They* did this. The religion did not break. The religion did not split itself. The religion did not, on its own, develop differences. **Human beings actively fragmented it.**

And what did they fragment? **Dīnahum.** *Their* religion. The pronoun suffix is critical. It's not "the religion" as some abstract idea floating in the air. It's the religion that belonged to them. The dīn that was already, by ayah 30, the fiṭrah upon which they were created. The one given thing. Their own inheritance.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

Allah is not saying these people abandoned the religion. He is not saying they rejected it. He is saying they took something whole — something that was *given* to them whole — and they shredded it themselves. Then each one walked away holding a fragment.

And here's the part that should unsettle us. A person holding a fragment **still feels they have the religion**. They are not standing there with empty hands. They are holding *something*. They look down at their piece and they say, "I have the dīn." They are not lying. They genuinely believe it. They have *a piece* of it — and they have mistaken the piece for the whole.

Notice what Allah is *not* saying. He is not saying "those who rejected their religion." He is not saying "those who left their religion." He is saying something far more uncomfortable: **those who kept their religion, but only a piece of it, and forgot it used to be more.**

There is a kind of person who walks away from faith entirely. The Quran addresses that person elsewhere. But this ayah is not about that person. This ayah is about the person who is *still inside the religion* — still praying, still claiming the dīn — and is *unaware* that the dīn they are holding has been shredded down to one corner of itself.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this single grammatical form has just revealed: **fragmentation is not the opposite of faith. Fragmentation is a particular shape that faith takes when human ego gets its hands on something whole.**

---

### Element Two: *Shiya'an* and *ḥizb* — the two words for "group"

Allah could have stopped at *farraqū dīnahum*. The picture would already be complete: they shredded their religion.

But He doesn't stop. He adds: **wa kānū shiya'an** — *and they became sects*. And then He adds again: **kullu ḥizbin** — *every faction*.

Two different words for "group" in a single ayah of ten words. That is not redundancy. That is precision.

Let's teach the words.

**Shī'a** — from the root *sh-y-ʿ* — at its base means "to follow." A *shī'a* of someone is the group of his followers. The Quran uses this word throughout for **factions defined by who they follow**. Mūsā walks into a city and finds two men fighting — one *min shī'atihi*, of his faction. Pharaoh divides his people into *shiya'*, factions. The word carries the flavor of **partisanship**. People grouped together because of allegiance to a person, a leader, a banner.

**Ḥizb** — from the root *ḥ-z-b* — at its base means a group that has bound itself together. *Ḥizb* in modern Arabic is the word for "political party." Same root. A *ḥizb* is more solidified than a *shī'a*. It has structure, identity, hardness. A *ḥizb* knows itself as a *ḥizb* and defines itself against the other *ḥizb*.

So watch what Allah has done in the architecture of this short ayah:

First: they fragmented the religion. (The action.)
Then: they became *shiya'* — partisans, followers of this leader and that leader. (The intermediate stage.)
Then: each one became a *ḥizb* — a hardened, defined faction. (The final stage.)

There is a **progression** here. Religion does not shatter all at once into bricks. It softens into partisanship first. Then partisanship hardens into faction. The shī'a becomes the ḥizb.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the word **kullu** — "every." *Every faction.* Not "some factions." Not "a few." Allah is making a universal statement about the psychology of every single one of these hardened groups.

And here is where the analogy becomes load-bearing.

Think about how an organism becomes cancer. Cancer is not a foreign invader. It is your own cells. Your own tissue. But at some point, a cluster of cells stops *recognizing* itself as part of the larger body. It starts replicating only for itself, defending only its own borders, fed by the same blood as the rest of the body but unaware of the rest of the body. The body keeps trying to send it signals — *you are part of me* — and the cluster keeps ignoring them. The cluster does not know it is doing anything wrong. As far as the cluster is concerned, it is *thriving*.

That is what a *ḥizb* is in this ayah. A cluster of believers who used to be part of one body, who at some point stopped recognizing themselves as part of the larger body, and who are now thriving inside their own walls, fed by the same blood as the rest of the Ummah, oblivious that anything is wrong.

And the body keeps trying to signal. *Inna hādhihi ummatukum ummatan wāḥidah.* "Indeed this Ummah of yours is one Ummah." (21:92) The signal is sent again and again across the Quran. But the *ḥizb* doesn't hear it. Because the *ḥizb* is busy *rejoicing*.

Which brings us to the heart of the ayah.

---

### Element Three: *Bimā ladayhim fariḥūn* — and the absence that should terrify us

**Kullu ḥizbin bimā ladayhim fariḥūn.**

"Every faction — with what is in their possession — is rejoicing."

I want you to look very carefully at what Allah did *not* say.

He did not say: *kullu ḥizbin bil-ḥaqqi-lladhī ladayhim fariḥūn* — "every faction rejoicing in the truth they have."

He did not say: *bimā uʿṭū min al-ḥaqq* — "in what they were given of truth."

He did not even say: *bimā ẓannū annahu ḥaqq* — "in what they thought was the truth."

He said only this: **bimā ladayhim.** With **what is with them.** With *what they have in their hands.* The phrase is stripped naked of any qualifier of truth or correctness. It is purely about *possession*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that absence. Because the absence is the entire point.

If Allah had qualified it — "rejoicing in the truth they have" — the ayah would be praising them. If He had said "rejoicing in the falsehood they have," the ayah would be condemning a specific error. But by leaving the object **completely unqualified**, Allah has revealed something far more devastating about the psychology of the *ḥizb*:

**The faction does not rejoice because what it has is true. The faction rejoices because what it has is theirs.**

The criterion has shifted. Truth used to be the standard, and you would only rejoice if what you had matched the truth. But in the world of the *ḥizb*, the standard has become *ownership*. *Whatever* is in our hands — by virtue of being in *our* hands — is the thing to rejoice in.

This is the deepest psychological diagnosis in the entire ayah. The *ḥizb* is not characterized by what it holds. The *ḥizb* is characterized by *the fact that it holds*. The contents are interchangeable. What is permanent is the joy of *being the group that has it*.

[PAUSE]

Now let's open the last word — **fariḥūn.**

The root *f-r-ḥ* means joy, rejoicing, exultation. Sounds positive. And in some places in the Quran it is. But notice how the Quran tends to use this word when it stands alone, without a *good* object attached to it.

Qārūn, the man whose wealth swallowed him — his people warned him, **"Do not exult (lā tafraḥ); indeed, Allah does not love the *fariḥīn*."** (28:76)

Or in Surah Al-Hadid: do not grieve over what passes you by and do not exult over what comes to you — "Allah does not love every conceited *fakhūr*." (57:23)

*Faraḥ*, when it stands without a worthy object, becomes the joy of self-satisfaction. The joy of having gotten something. The joy of being on the winning side. It is the smile of the man who looks at his pile and forgets where the pile came from.

That is the smile on the face of every *ḥizb* in this ayah.

**They are happy.** Notice that. They are not anxious. They are not searching. They are not in the posture of someone who is still working out the truth. They have arrived. They are content. They are *fariḥūn*.

And here is the chilling part — Allah is using their happiness as evidence *against* them.

In most contexts, peace and contentment are signs of being close to Allah. But in this ayah, the contentment is precisely what reveals the spiritual sickness. Because **a believer who has glimpsed the wholeness of the religion can never be fully at peace inside one fragment.** There will always be a humility in them — a sense that what they hold is partial, that there is more to learn, that other Muslims may have caught something they missed.

The *ḥizb* does not have that humility. The *ḥizb* is **done**. The *ḥizb* has the answer. The *ḥizb* is rejoicing.

[PAUSE]

---

### The Linguistic Bridge

Step back with me and look at what these three elements have built.

First — *farraqū* — they actively shredded what was given to them whole.

Then — *shiya'an*, *ḥizbin* — the shredding produced partisans, then partisans hardened into factions.

Then — *bimā ladayhim fariḥūn* — and inside the factions, the standard quietly shifted from truth to possession, and the people inside became *happy*.

That is the anatomy of sectarianism as Allah diagnoses it. Not a sudden departure from the faith. A slow shredding, a hardening into camps, and a final state where the camp itself becomes the object of joy.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## PART 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: When the group becomes the truth

Let me start somewhere you already live.

Think of the last time you scrolled social media and felt a small, sharp pleasure when someone you disagree with got publicly humiliated by someone on *your* side. Not a victory for truth. A victory for *your team*.

That little pulse of joy — that's the pulse this ayah is about.

Modern psychology has a name for what Allah is describing fourteen centuries ago. It is sometimes called **identity-protective cognition**. Researchers have shown — over and over — that when a piece of information *threatens our group identity*, our brains process it differently than when the same information threatens only an opinion. We don't engage with it. We don't weigh it. We *reject it before it can reach the part of us that thinks*.

We are wired this way. Tribal psychology runs deeper than reason. It runs deeper than scripture. It will, if we are not careful, run deeper than faith itself.

That is what the ayah is naming. The moment your group identity becomes the lens through which truth must pass before it can reach you — the moment a hadith that troubles *your madhhab* is dismissed before it is examined, the moment a scholar from *the other side* is presumed wrong before you've read him — at that moment, **the truth is no longer the standard. The group is the standard.** And the group has quietly become an idol.

This is why ayah 32 follows ayah 31. Ayah 31 said: **do not be of the mushrikīn.** Then ayah 32 immediately specifies one form of that *shirk*: dividing the religion into sects, every faction rejoicing in what *it* has.

Sectarianism is named in the Quran as a form of *shirk*. Because what is *shirk*? *Shirk* is taking something other than Allah and making it the standard against which Allah Himself must be measured. The *ḥizb* does exactly this. The *ḥizb* takes its own group and makes the group the standard against which the dīn itself is measured. If the dīn fits the group, the dīn is welcome. If the dīn challenges the group, the dīn must be reinterpreted to fit. The group has been placed *above* the religion.

[PAUSE]

Now let's get personal. Because it is easy to imagine this ayah is about *other* Muslims. The deviant ones. The ones with the wrong manhaj. The ones in the other corner of the masjid.

But here is the question the ayah is putting to *you*:

When was the last time you encountered a truth — a hadith, an ayah, a scholarly position — that genuinely challenged the assumptions of the group you most identify with? And when you encountered it, did you sit with it? Did you let it work on you? Or did your mind immediately go to *who said it* and *what camp they are in*?

If your reaction was the second one, you were not measuring the truth. You were measuring the source against your group. And the standard had already quietly shifted.

[PAUSE]

The hardest practical implication of this ayah is this: **a person who is loyal to the truth will sometimes look disloyal to their group.** Because the truth will eventually require them to break ranks. To agree with someone on the "other side." To question their own teacher. To admit that the group they grew up in does not have the whole picture.

A person who is loyal to the *ḥizb* never has this experience, because their loyalty allows them no room for it. They are always vindicated. They are always rejoicing.

The litmus test of which one you are is not what you say at a lecture. It is what happens, alone in your room, when you read something that contradicts your camp. Does it disturb you enough to make you investigate? Or do you scroll past, comforted that you already know it must be wrong?

**Practical application:** This week, find one position — one — held by Muslims you usually dismiss, and read its strongest articulation. Not to convert. To honor the fact that *Allah did not put the whole truth inside the walls of your camp*. The first step out of the *ḥizb* is the moment you sit with a piece of the dīn you used to refuse to touch because the wrong people were holding it.

### Theme Two: The fitrah was one — fragmentation is anti-fiṭrah

We cannot read ayah 32 without ayah 30. They are speaking to each other.

Ayah 30 says: there is *one* fiṭrah. *One* original design. *One* upright religion. No alteration in what Allah has created.

Ayah 32 says: some people *fragmented their religion* and became sects.

Do you see what is happening structurally? The ayah is describing the **un-doing** of the fiṭrah. The fiṭrah is wholeness. Sectarianism is *de-wholing*. The fiṭrah is one. Sectarianism shatters the one. Sectarianism is, in its most precise definition, **anti-fiṭrah**.

[PAUSE]

This is theologically enormous and I want you to feel it.

When Allah created the human soul, He created it with a *single* longing — to know Him, to return to Him, to be in submission to Him. That longing is the dīn al-qayyim, the upright religion. It is not invented; it is *uncovered*. It was placed in you before you were placed in this world.

So what does it mean to "shatter your religion"? It means to take that single, original longing — and break it into pieces. To say: the part of the dīn that is loving Allah is enough for me, the rest I do not need. Or: the part of the dīn that is following the law is enough for me, the love is decorative. Or: the part that is purifying my heart is enough, the community is optional. Or: the part that is fighting for the community is enough, the heart can wait.

Each fragment is real. Each fragment is part of the dīn. But none of them is the dīn. The dīn was given whole. To hold only a piece is to be holding *part* of the answer to a longing that was given to you *whole*.

And here is what the ayah is whispering: **the joy of the faction is the joy of a soul that has settled for a fragment of itself.**

[PAUSE]

Think about what this means for how you walk around in the world.

You meet a Muslim who is intensely focused on, say, intellectual proof. They love kalām. They love debate. Every conversation is about evidence. *That's a piece of the dīn.* You meet another Muslim who is intensely focused on ihsān, on heart-work, on dhikr. *That's a piece.* You meet another who is intensely focused on community, on activism, on standing for the oppressed. *That's a piece.*

None of these people are wrong about what they hold. They are wrong about the *shape* of what they hold — they think they are holding the whole when they are holding a piece. And the proof that they think they are holding the whole is that they are **happy** with only that piece. They are *fariḥūn*. They have stopped looking.

A soul that has glimpsed the wholeness of the dīn is *never* fully satisfied inside one fragment. There is always a hunger for the other dimensions. The scholar of law longs for the dhikr. The contemplative longs for engagement with the world. The activist longs for stillness. **This longing is the signature of a soul that remembers it was given the dīn whole.**

If you ever feel that longing — that suspicion that there is more to your religion than the corner of it you live in — that longing is the fiṭrah trying to reach you through the cracks of your *ḥizb*. Do not silence it. That ache is the most spiritually healthy thing in you right now.

[PAUSE]

**Practical application:** Identify the fragment of the dīn you are most fluent in. Then identify the fragment you neglect most — the one that the Muslims you find most irritating tend to emphasize. This month, take *one* concrete step into that neglected fragment. Read a book from that tradition. Sit in that majlis. Pray *behind* that imām. Not to abandon your own corner. To re-member — *re-member* — the dīn that was once whole.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and look at what these three ayahs do together, because the structure is breathtaking.

**Ayah 30:** *The fiṭrah is one.*

**Ayah 31:** *Turn back to Him. Establish prayer. Do not be of those who associate partners with Allah.*

**Ayah 32:** *— those who fragmented their religion and became sects, every faction rejoicing in what it has.*

The passage moves from **the original wholeness**, to **the warning against shirk**, to **the most subtle form of shirk** — taking the whole religion and breaking it into tribal pieces, then worshipping the piece by mistaking it for the whole.

The architecture is telling you: *sectarianism is the everyday shape of shirk for people who would never bow to an idol*. They will never bow to a stone. But they will bow, daily, to their group. And they will call that loyalty piety.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey showed us a verb of shredding, a progression from partisanship to faction, and an unqualified joy that revealed possession had replaced truth as the standard.

The thematic journey showed us why: the group becomes an idol when its standard supersedes Allah's standard, and the dīn is given whole — to settle for a fragment is to undo the very fiṭrah that defines our humanity.

These are the same insight from two directions. *The shredder of religion* and *the soul that settled for a fragment of itself* are the same person. The one is the action; the other is the inner state. Both end in *faraḥ* — the smile of a person who has gotten what they wanted and stopped looking.

### Questions to carry

1. When was the last time I changed my mind on a religious question because the evidence demanded it — and not because someone in my own camp gave me permission to change it?

2. If a Muslim I dismiss as "deviant" said something true about Allah tomorrow, would I be able to hear it? Or would my ears already be closed by the source?

3. Which piece of the dīn am I most comfortable in — and which piece am I avoiding because it would require me to sit next to Muslims I find uncomfortable?

4. Is my contentment with my religious identity the contentment of someone who has arrived at Allah — or the contentment of someone who has arrived at *the group that talks about Allah a certain way*?

5. If Allah were to whisper to me, *"The dīn I gave you is bigger than the corner you live in,"* would that feel like good news, or like a threat?

### The one-sentence distillation

**The deepest sectarianism is not the one you fight — it is the one you do not notice, because the group has become the lens through which even Allah's words must pass before they are allowed to reach you.**

### The closing invitation

This ayah is not asking you to abandon your shaykh, your madhhab, or your community. It is asking you to remember that *they are not the dīn*. They are doors into the dīn. And the moment a door starts to behave as if it is the whole house, it has stopped being a door.

Hold what you have — but hold it the way a person holds a fragment they know to be a fragment. With humility. With curiosity about the other fragments. With the awareness that other believers are holding pieces you do not yet hold, and that the whole of the dīn was once, and remains, *one*.

Do not be a *ḥizb*. Be a seeker who happens to live in a particular room of a house with many rooms — and who knows, every time he prays, that the house is bigger than the room.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You gave us the dīn whole. Forgive us for the times we shredded it down to the corner of it we found most comfortable. Let our loyalty be to the truth You revealed, not to the camp that revealed it to us. And when we encounter a piece of Your religion in the hands of a Muslim we did not expect to be holding it — give us the humility to receive it from them, that we may begin again to remember what once was one.

Āmīn.
