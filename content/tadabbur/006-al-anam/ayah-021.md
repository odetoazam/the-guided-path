# Who Is More Unjust — The Two Directions of a Single Lie

> <!-- ayah:6:21 -->     وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا أَوْ كَذَّبَ بِـَٔايَـٰتِهِۦٓ ۗ إِنَّهُۥ لَا يُفْلِحُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ
>
> *Wa man aẓlamu mimmani-ftarā ʿala-llāhi kadhiban aw kadhdhaba bi-āyātih; innahu lā yufliḥu-ẓ-ẓālimūn.*
>
> **"And who is more unjust than one who invents a lie about Allah, or denies His signs? Indeed, the wrongdoers will not succeed."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The room this verse was spoken into

Before you hear a single word of analysis, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

Mecca. Not the Mecca of pilgrims and prayer that we picture — the Mecca *before*. A city built around a house that Ibrahim raised for the One God, and which, over centuries, had been slowly refurnished. Not emptied of God — that is the thing to understand. **Filled.** Filled with three hundred and sixty stone forms, each with a name, each with a story, each with a claim on the human heart. The people of that city were not atheists. They would have found atheism absurd. They believed in Allah — ask them who made the heavens and the earth and they would say *Allah*, and the Qur'an itself records that they said it. But between themselves and that overwhelming Reality they had installed a crowd of go-betweens, intercessors, daughters-of-God, patrons — a whole bureaucracy of the sacred. And they had built stories to hold it all up. *This is what our fathers did. This is what the tribe does. This god brings rain, that one brings sons.*

Now feel the pressure of the surah that carries our verse. Al-An'am was revealed, the reports say, in a single descent — one long, unbroken argument, so heavy that it is said seventy thousand angels accompanied it. It is not a scattering of topics. It is a *case being made*, from its opening word to its close: that there is One, that He alone is the Origin, and that everything humans have inserted between themselves and Him is a fabrication laid over the truth.

By the time we reach verse 21, the surah has already established the courtroom. A few verses earlier Allah asks: *what thing is greatest as a witness?* And answers: *Allah is witness between me and you.* The scene is judicial. Testimony is being taken. And into that courtroom, verse 21 delivers what sounds like a prosecutor's question — except that no prosecutor on earth could ask it, because it reaches beyond every human court into the structure of reality itself.

### The hook

Here is what I need you to notice, and then not let go of for the next hour.

The verse asks: *who is more unjust?* — and then it names not one crime but **two**. One person **adds** something to God that was never there. Another **subtracts** something God gave. Opposite motions. A fabricator and a denier. A man who builds a false wall and a man who tears down a true one.

And the Qur'an files them **under the same charge.**

Why? Why would inventing a lie *about* Allah and rejecting the truth *from* Allah be the *same* injustice — the injustice than which there is no greater? What is the single thing both of these people are doing, underneath the opposite things they appear to be doing?

Stay with that question. Everything is inside it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *aẓlamu*: the question that cannot be answered, and the word that means "out of place"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because if I don't, the first word will slide past you as ordinary.

In Arabic there is a form called *ismu-t-tafḍīl* — the form of preference, of comparison. You take a quality and you bend the word into a shape that means *more* of it, or *most* of it. From *kabīr* (big) you get *akbar* (bigger, biggest). From *karīm* (generous) you get *akram*. The shape itself — *af'al* — carries the comparison built in. You don't need extra words. The mold does the work.

Now, there is a particular way the Qur'an uses this mold that you have to hear as an Arab would. When you say *"who is more X than the one who does Y?"* — *man aẓlamu mimman...* — you are not actually asking a question you expect answered. You are using the shape of a question to slam a door. It is what the grammarians call *istifhām inkārī* — an interrogative that denies. The meaning is not *"I wonder who is more unjust."* The meaning is: **"There is no one. Nobody. This is the floor of injustice. You cannot dig lower than this."**

*Now* watch what happens when Allah pours this mold.

The word is **أَظْلَمُ**, *aẓlamu* — "more unjust," from the root *ẓ-l-m*. And here is the thing about that root that changes everything. Before *ẓulm* ever meant "oppression" or "injustice" in the moral sense we reach for, it meant something startlingly concrete. The Arabs used *ẓalama* to mean **to put a thing in the place that is not its place.** To pour water into a vessel meant for wine. To bury a seed in barren rock. To slaughter a young animal before its time. And from the same root comes *ẓalām* — **darkness** — because darkness is what happens when light is *displaced*, when things are no longer where the eye can find them.

Sit with this. *Ẓulm* is not first about cruelty. It is about **misplacement.** Injustice, in the Qur'anic imagination, is fundamentally a *geography* problem — a thing has been moved out of its rightful location, and the whole map has gone dark as a result.

So when Allah asks *"who is more misplacing than the one who...?"* — and the mold tells us the answer is *no one* — He is telling us that the crime named next is the **absolute maximum of things-out-of-place.** The most dislocated a human being can ever make reality.

And what is that crime? To take a lie — a thing that has no existence — and set it *upon Allah*. To take the truth God sent — a thing that is real — and shove it *out of its place*. The largest misplacement conceivable, because it is a misplacement about the One in reference to Whom everything *else* finds its place.

[PAUSE]

Notice something that the grammar quietly refuses to do. Everywhere else, injustice has an *object* — you oppress *someone*, you wrong *a person*. Here the mold gives us "more unjust" and then does not point at a victim first. It points at an *act performed toward Allah*. And Allah cannot be harmed. So who exactly is being wronged, if the target of the lie is beyond all harm?

Hold that. The answer is coming, and it is not who you think.

### Element Two — *iftarā* and *kadhdhaba*: two hands, one theft

Here is the heart of the verse, and here is where the Arabic does something you could translate a hundred times and never see in English.

The verse names two crimes. Listen to the two verbs.

The first: **افْتَرَىٰ**, *iftarā*. This is Form VIII of the root *f-r-y*. And the root, in its bare physical sense, is a leather-worker's word. *Farā* meant **to cut a hide, to slit it, to work it into shape** — to *fabricate* in the most literal sense, the way a craftsman cuts and stitches a thing into existence that did not exist before. When Arabic bends it into Form VIII — *iftarā* — it takes on the sense of *deliberately manufacturing*, sewing together, constructing a falsehood with care and intention, the way a forger doesn't stumble into a fake document but *builds* one. *Iftirāʾ* is a lie you had to *work at*. You cut the pieces. You stitched the seams. You made a thing and then dressed it up as real.

And notice the *sound* of it. *If-ta-rā* — it opens outward, it flows, it ends on that long soft *ā* like a hand smoothing the leather flat. It is a *pretty* word for an ugly act. It *sounds* like construction, like something being made — which is exactly what the fabricator wants you to feel: that his invention is seamless, finished, believable.

Now the second verb: **كَذَّبَ**, *kadhdhaba*. Root *k-dh-b*, "to lie" — but this is Form II, and Form II in Arabic is the form of **intensification and repetition.** You take the simple act and you *hammer* it. And *hear* the word — *kadh-dha-ba* — that doubled *dh*, that *shadda* sitting on the middle letter like a fist coming down twice. Where *iftarā* flowed, *kadhdhaba* **strikes.** It is a choppy, percussive, closed-mouthed word. And its meaning is not "to lie" in the sense of making something up — *kadhdhaba* means **to actively, repeatedly, forcefully declare something true to be false.** To call the truth a liar. To take what God actually sent and hammer *"fake, fake, fake"* onto it.

So look at what we have. Two verbs, two sounds, two directions:

- *iftarā* — the smooth motion of **adding.** Building a false thing and attaching it to God. *Here is a truth about Allah* — except it isn't.
- *kadhdhaba* — the hammering motion of **subtracting.** Taking a real thing from God and driving it out. *That is not from Allah* — except it is.

One man walks up to reality and **glues on** what was never there. The other man walks up to reality and **rips off** what was always there. Fabrication and denial. Addition and subtraction. And the sounds of the two words *enact* the two crimes — one flows on like a seamless forgery, the other pounds like a fist rejecting a gift.

[PAUSE]

And now — here is what I promised you at the start.

Look at the *root* underneath *kadhdhaba*: **k-dh-b.** Now look back at the *object* of the first crime: *iftarā ʿala-llāhi* **كَذِبًا** — *kadhiban*, "a lie." Same root. *K-dh-b* again.

The fabricator invents a *kadhib*. The denier commits *takdhīb*. **It is the same root standing on both sides of the verse.** The Qur'an is not describing two unrelated sins that happen to be listed together. It is showing you, at the level of the letters themselves, that these are **two faces of one lie.** The man who adds and the man who subtracts are doing — etymologically, spiritually, exactly — *the same thing*. They are both falsifying the relationship between a human being and God. One does it by inflation, one by erasure. But it is one act of *kadhib*, performed with two different hands.

*This* is why the verse can file them under a single charge and call it the maximum of injustice. Because *ẓulm* is misplacement, and both men have misplaced the single most important thing there is: **the truth about who God is and what He said.** One buried it under an addition. One buried it under a denial. Same darkness. Same *ẓalām*. Same crime.

This is not my invention. It is sitting in the roots. Allah wrote the connection into the consonants and trusted us to find it.

### Element Three — the pronoun that isn't pointing where you think: *innahu*

Now the closing clause, and one last piece of Arabic that most translations quietly smooth over.

*Innahu lā yufliḥu-ẓ-ẓālimūn.* "Indeed, the wrongdoers will not succeed."

Read it slowly and something should snag. *Inna-**hu*** — "indeed *he*." A **singular** pronoun, masculine, "he" or "it." And then the sentence finishes with *aẓ-ẓālimūn* — "the wrongdoers," **plural.** Grammatically that is a bump. If the *hu* referred to the wrongdoer, you would expect the verb and subject to stay singular to match. Instead we get "indeed-**he**... the **wrongdoers** do not succeed." Singular pronoun, plural subject. Something is going on.

Let me teach you the device. In Arabic there is a construction the grammarians call *ḍamīr aš-šaʾn* — the **"pronoun of the matter,"** sometimes called the pronoun of the situation or of dignity. It is a "he/it" that does not point at any person at all. It points at **the entire state of affairs about to be described.** The closest English can come is a heavy, formal *"The truth of the matter is this:"* or *"Here is how reality itself stands:"* — and then the full statement lands.

So *innahu* here is not saying "indeed *that liar*." It is saying: ***"The reality — the fixed, standing truth of the cosmos — is this:"*** and *then* the verdict falls: *the wrongdoers do not succeed.*

Feel the difference this makes. If the verse said "indeed *he* will not succeed," it would be a threat aimed at one man. But the *ḍamīr aš-šaʾn* lifts the statement clean off the individual and turns it into a **law of the universe.** It is no longer "this particular fabricator will fail." It is "**Reality is built such that misplacers do not flourish — full stop, as a structural fact, about all of them, forever.**"

And now watch the number do its final work. The question opened with a **singular** — *man aẓlamu*, "who — one person — is more unjust?" It moves through two **singular** verbs — *iftarā*, *kadhdhaba* — one man fabricating, one man denying. And it closes on a **plural** — *aẓ-ẓālimūn*, "the wrongdoers," all of them, the whole category. The verse **widens** as it goes. It starts by isolating a single soul in the dock and ends by pronouncing on an entire species of behavior across all of time. The individual dissolves into the law. You thought this was about *that* man. It was about the *shape of reality* the whole time.

[PAUSE]

**The bridge.** So the linguistic architecture has shown us three things, and they are really one thing. *Ẓulm* is misplacement — reality shoved out of true. The two verbs, sharing one root, are the two hands with which a human being misplaces the truth about God — by adding and by subtracting. And the closing clause lifts the whole matter off the individual and files it as a law: *reality does not let the misplacer flourish.*

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about what it means to live a human life. Because you and I are not fabricators of idols in seventh-century Mecca. Or are we?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — the age of "my truth," and a God you cannot edit

Let me start where you actually live, not where the verse seems to.

There is a phrase that has quietly become the water we swim in. *My truth.* You hear it everywhere now, and it almost always arrives dressed as courage. *I'm just living my truth. That may be true for you, but it's not my truth. You have to speak your truth.* And there is something real and good being reached for in it — the refusal to let someone else narrate your inner life, the dignity of your own experience. I don't want to flatten that.

But sit with the *architecture* of the phrase for a second. *My* truth. It makes truth a possessive. It makes reality a thing you *own* and therefore a thing you can *furnish* — you decorate it to taste, you add the parts that flatter you, you quietly remove the parts that indict you. And the deepest version of this isn't even about pronouns or identity debates. It is the ordinary, daily, human project of **building a self-flattering account of reality and then living inside it as though it were the house God built.**

We all do this. You construct a version of yourself that adds virtues you don't quite have — *iftirāʾ*, a little forgery, a stitched-together self. And you quietly deny the parts of the truth that would cost you something — *takdhīb*, a small hammer, *that verse doesn't really mean that, that obligation is really cultural, that sin isn't really mine.* Adding and subtracting. Building a curated reality about who you are and what is required of you.

Now bring the verse's word back. *Ẓulm.* Misplacement. **Putting a thing where it does not belong.**

Here is what the verse says to the age of *my truth*, and it is devastating precisely because it is so quiet: **there is exactly one Reality about whom you get no editorial rights at all.** You may curate your résumé, your feed, your reputation, your self-image — the world lets you, mostly. But when your editing turns toward *God* — when you start adding to Him what He did not say and subtracting from Him what He did — you have not exercised freedom. You have committed the largest *misplacement* there is, because you have moved *the one fixed point* by reference to which everything else knows where it stands.

Think of what a fixed point is *for*. A sailor lost at sea doesn't resent the North Star for being un-editable. Its refusal to move is the entire reason it can save him. The moment the star becomes *"my* star," bending to wherever I'd like it to be, it stops being able to tell me where I am — and I am, at that exact moment, most thoroughly lost while feeling most thoroughly free. **A God you can edit is a God who can no longer locate you.** That is the terror hidden inside the comfort. The un-editability of God is not His constraint on your freedom. It is the only thing that can tell you where you actually are.

So the question the verse plants in you is not *"do I invent lies about Allah?"* — because you'll say no. The question is: **where, in the quiet furniture-moving of my daily self-account, have I been treating the truth about God as *my* truth — something to decorate and edit — rather than the fixed star I take my bearings from?**

And here is the mercy folded inside the severity — because I don't want to leave you only with the weight. The reason misplacement is a catastrophe is that there *is* a right place. The verse only makes sense in a universe where things *have* true locations. To be told "you have put the truth out of its place" is also to be told, in the same breath, **"there is a place for it, and you can put it back."** That is what *tawba* is. It is not self-improvement. It is *re-placement.* Moving the truth about God back to where it belongs, and discovering that when it returns to its spot, so, quietly, do you.

**What changes tomorrow:** Tonight, find one place where you have edited God to fit your life — one obligation you've quietly reclassified as optional, one attribute of His you've quietly muted because it disturbs you. Don't fix your whole self. Just move *that one thing* back to its place, and notice whether the room gets less dark.

### Theme Two — *falāḥ*: why the farmer, and not the merchant, is the image of success

Now the last word of the verse, and I want to open it not with theology but with dirt.

Picture a farmer. Not a metaphor yet — an actual man, at the actual end of an actual winter. He took good seed — food he could have eaten — and he did the strangest thing a hungry person can do with food: he *buried* it. He put it in the dark, in the cold, under the ground, and then he walked away and *waited.* For months he had nothing to show. No leaf, no proof, no return. If you'd photographed his field in February you'd have called him a failure — a man who threw away good grain into the mud.

And then the earth *splits.* The seed cracks its own shell open in the dark, sends a green blade up through soil that looks like a wound in the ground, and by summer the man is standing in more food than he buried. That is the picture. Now here is the word.

The Arabic for "success" in this verse is **يُفْلِحُ**, *yufliḥu*, from the root *f-l-ḥ*. And do you know what *falaḥa* meant, in the mouths of the Arabs, before it ever meant "to succeed"? **It meant to split the earth. To cleave the soil open for planting.** The *fallāḥ* — same root — is literally the **farmer**, the one who splits the ground. *Falāḥ*, the word the Qur'an chooses for the highest human success, for the thing the call to prayer summons you toward five times a day — *ḥayya ʿala-l-falāḥ*, "come to *falāḥ*" — that word is, at its root, **the farmer's word.**

Now sit inside what Allah has *chosen* by choosing this word.

He did not choose the merchant's word for success — the language of the quick transaction, buy low, sell high, profit today. He did not choose the conqueror's word — seize, take, win now. He chose the *farmer's* word. And the farmer's word carries an entire theory of what success *is*, buried right in the root:

Success is **something you plant, not something you grab.** It requires **burial before it shows** — a season of looking like loss. It works **in the dark, underground, invisibly,** long before there is anything to photograph. And it demands the one thing our entire age has been engineered to destroy in us: **the willingness to wait, and to keep tending what shows no result.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel the *negation* land. *Lā yufliḥu-ẓ-ẓālimūn* — "the misplacers **do not** *yufliḥ*." They do not split-and-grow. And you see now that this is not a curse arbitrarily attached to them from outside — a God in a bad mood withholding a prize. It is a **description of what they made themselves.** The misplacer, the one who fabricates and denies, is the man who refuses to bury the seed. He wants the harvest *now*, self-declared, on his own terms, out of its proper place and season. He wants *falāḥ* without the *falaḥa* — the success without the splitting, the crop without the burial, the standing-in-the-summer-field without the walking-away-in-February. And reality — the *ḍamīr aš-šaʾn*, the standing law of the cosmos — simply does not run that way. **You cannot harvest what you refused to plant.** The wrongdoer does not "fail" as a punishment. He fails the way an un-sown field fails to yield: structurally, quietly, because nothing was ever put in the ground.

And here is where it comes home to you and me, in an age of the instant. We are the most *harvest-now* generation that has ever lived. We refresh for the number. We want the transformation without the season in the dark. We want to be *someone* without the buried, unglamorous, unwitnessed months of becoming that someone — the prayers no one saw, the sin left un-committed with no applause, the small faithfulness in February. And the verse leans in and says, gently and without cruelty: *that is not how falāḥ works. It never was. It is the farmer's word.* The green blade only comes up through soil that looks, for a long time, like a grave.

**What changes tomorrow:** Choose one buried, un-photographable act of obedience this week — one that will show no result, that no one will see, that you cannot post. Plant it and walk away from the field. You are not failing to see fruit. You are learning the actual grammar of the word we ask God for five times a day.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now that we have climbed, look back down at the shape of the whole, because the verse is built with a symmetry you could not have seen from the bottom.

It **opens** with a category — *aẓlamu*, the maximum of *ẓulm*, misplacement. It **closes** with the same category — *aẓ-ẓālimūn*, the misplacers. The root *ẓ-l-m* is the two pillars holding up the doorway, and *everything else stands between them.* And what stands between them? The two verbs — *iftarā* and *kadhdhaba* — sharing the single root *k-dh-b*, the twin hands of the one lie. So the verse is a perfect frame: **misplacement** on the left post, **misplacement** on the right post, and in the center, the **two-handed lie** by which the misplacement is done.

*Ẓulm ... [the lie, added and subtracted] ... ẓulm.* The crime is named, enacted, and named again — and it is the *same word* both times, so that when you reach the end you realize the verse has closed a circle on you. It began by asking "who is the most misplaced?" and ended by telling you what happens to *the misplacers* — and the space in between showed you *exactly how* the misplacing is done, with both hands, by one root. You did not travel from a question to a different answer. You traveled around a ring and arrived back at *ẓulm*, now understanding it from the inside. That is not accidental design. Human speech does not do that by luck.

### The two roads were one road

And see now that the linguistic journey and the two themes were never separate.

The grammar showed us that *iftirāʾ* and *takdhīb* — adding to God and subtracting from Him — are one crime of *kadhib*, misplacing the truth. The *first theme* was that same insight walked into your bedroom: the daily editing of God to fit "my truth," adding the flattering, subtracting the costly. The *second theme* was the same insight shown from its far side: the misplacer wants *falāḥ* without burial, harvest out of its season — success itself put in the wrong place, out of its proper time. **Misplacement in the mind about God; misplacement in the life about success.** The whole verse, from *aẓlamu* to *lā yufliḥu*, is one teaching seen from three windows: *put the truth back where it belongs, and you begin — slowly, in the dark, like a seed — to grow.*

We started in a Meccan courtroom, watching other people's idols. We end standing in our own field in February, asking what we have actually planted.

### Questions to carry

- When you tell the story of your own life — to yourself, at night — where do you *add* a virtue you don't have, and where do you *subtract* a truth that would cost you? Which hand do you use more?
- Is there an attribute of Allah, or a command He gave, that you have quietly *edited* to fit the life you'd rather live? Can you name it?
- What in your life are you demanding as a *harvest* right now, that you never *buried* as a seed?
- If *falāḥ* is the farmer's word — is there a single act of obedience you could plant this week and then *walk away from,* expecting nothing visible in return?
- Where is the truth about God sitting *out of its place* in you — and are you willing to move it back tonight?

### One sentence to keep

**Injustice is not first cruelty — it is putting reality out of its place; and the largest misplacement a soul can commit is to edit the truth about God, adding what He never said or erasing what He did, and then to wonder why nothing in the field ever grows.**

### The invitation

You are not being asked to be afraid tonight. You are being invited to *re-place* one thing. The verse is severe only because it is true, and it is true only in a universe where things have real locations — which means the same verse that says "you have misplaced the truth" is also promising, in the same breath, that **there is a right place, and it will receive the truth back the moment you carry it home.** That is not a threat. That is a map for the lost. Carry one thing back to its place tonight, and bury one seed you'll never photograph, and let the standing law of the cosmos — the one that does not let misplacers flourish — begin, quietly, to work *for* you instead of against you.

### A du'a from this verse

O Allah, You are the One whom no soul can edit and no hand can misplace — keep me from adding to You what You never said, and from denying what You did. Teach me that *falāḥ* is Your farmer's word: let me bury good seed in the dark and trust You with the season, and do not let me be among the *ẓālimīn* who demand the harvest they never planted. Put the truth about You back in its place within me, and let my heart find its bearings there. Āmīn.
