# The Mathematics of Turning Away

## Surah Al-An'am, Ayah 4

> [ayah:6:4] وَمَا تَأْتِيهِم مِّنْ ءَايَةٍ مِّنْ ءَايَـٰتِ رَبِّهِمْ إِلَّا كَانُوا۟ عَنْهَا مُعْرِضِينَ
>
> *Wa mā taʾtīhim min āyatin min āyāti rabbihim illā kānū ʿanhā muʿriḍīn*
>
> *"And no sign comes to them from the signs of their Lord except that they turn away from it."*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

The surah has just opened with three ayahs that build a cathedral around the listener.

*"All praise belongs to Allah who created the heavens and the earth and made the darknesses and the light."*

*"He is the One who created you from clay, then decreed a term — a term named with Him..."*

*"And He is Allah in the heavens and in the earth — He knows your secret and your declared, and He knows what you earn."*

Three ayahs. The cosmos is named. Time is named. The inner life of the human — the secret thought, the hidden gesture — is named. By the end of ayah three, the listener is standing inside a universe in which there is no place to hide and no moment that escapes notice. The Lord of this universe knows the whisper inside the chest.

And then ayah four arrives.

After establishing all of this — the creating, the timing, the seeing, the knowing — Allah does not turn next to a command. He does not turn next to a promise. He does not turn next to a threat.

He turns to a diagnosis.

He says: *here is what humans do with all of this.*

### The Hook

Allah is about to describe a pattern. Not an exception. Not a tendency. Not "many people sometimes." A pattern so total that He will frame it with one of the most absolute constructions in the Arabic language — a construction that means, **mathematically, without exception**.

He is going to say that every single sign — every single one without exception — meets the same response.

And the word He chooses for that response is not the word for *rejection*. It is not the word for *denial*. It is not the word for *anger*. It is something quieter than all of those. Something more polite. Something more devastating.

Stay with me. Because this ayah is naming something that lives inside every one of us — and once you see it, you will not be able to unsee it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Construction That Means *Without Exception*

Before we open the words, let me teach you a structure. Once you see this structure in the Quran, you start seeing it everywhere — and it changes how you read.

In Arabic, when you want to say "no X happens," you can simply say *mā* (not) followed by the verb. *Mā jāʾa* — "he did not come."

But there is a stronger construction. You take that negation, and then — instead of stopping there — you add the word *illā* — "except." Negation, plus exception.

In English, this same structure exists, but we use it loosely. *"Nothing happens around here except trouble."* The exception is just commentary; we don't really mean it mathematically.

In classical Arabic rhetoric, this construction — *mā ... illā* — is called *al-ḥaṣr*, restriction. And when it's used precisely, it functions like a logical claim. It means: **the entirety of category X falls into condition Y. Without remainder. Without exception.**

It is the language of mathematical totality.

Now look at the ayah:

> *Wa **mā** taʾtīhim min āyatin min āyāti rabbihim **illā** kānū ʿanhā muʿriḍīn*

The whole ayah is sandwiched between *mā* and *illā*. Allah is not making an observation. He is making a structural claim about the totality of a relationship.

Let me show you what this means. He is not saying:

*"Most signs that come to them, they turn away from."*

He is not saying:

*"Many signs are met with turning away."*

He is not even saying:

*"Almost every sign meets refusal."*

He is saying — and I want you to hear this with the precision the Arabic actually carries — **there does not arrive at them a single sign, from among the signs of their Lord, except that they are, with respect to that sign, in a continuous state of turning away.**

[PAUSE]

Notice what this construction does to the listener. It removes the possibility of self-exception. You cannot read this and say, "ah, those people, who refused those signs." Because the *mā ... illā* is total. Every sign. Every time. The mathematics close the door behind you as you walk through.

There is no escape hatch labeled "but I am one of the ones who responds." Not in this construction. The pattern is universal until something disrupts it. And the question the ayah is silently placing on your chest is: *has anything disrupted it in you?*

### Element Two: The Doubled Word — *Min Āyatin Min Āyāti Rabbihim*

Now I want to draw your attention to something almost no one notices on a first reading.

Allah uses the word *āyah* — sign — twice in a row. First in the singular: *min āyatin* — "of any sign." Then in the plural: *min āyāti rabbihim* — "from the signs of their Lord."

In ordinary speech, you would say one or the other. *"No sign comes to them..."* Or: *"None of the signs of their Lord comes to them..."* You would not normally pile both forms on top of each other.

So why does Allah?

Let me explain what each *min* is doing, because they are doing different work.

The first *min* — *min āyatin* — is what grammarians call *min al-tabʿīḍiyyah* combined with the indefinite. It functions to universalize the negation. *"There does not come to them anything that could even be called a sign."* The smallest sign. The faintest sign. The sign so small it barely registers.

The second *min* — *min āyāti rabbihim* — is partitive in a different sense. It locates that sign within a vast collection: *"from among the signs of their Lord."* It tells you that the singular sign you just zoomed in on is one drop in an ocean of such signs.

Put both halves together, and you have something extraordinary. Allah is simultaneously:

1. Zooming in to the smallest, faintest possible sign — *any* sign at all.
2. Pulling the camera back to show that this small sign is one of countless signs, all coming from one source.

It is as if He is saying: *Picture the tiniest leaf falling. Now picture that this leaf is one of a forest's worth of leaves, all falling. Every single one ignored.*

[PAUSE]

The doubled word creates the architecture of the indictment. Without it, you would think of a few miraculous signs — a parted sea, a staff that becomes a serpent — and the ayah would be about those moments. With it, the ayah is about the **constant, uninterrupted rain** of signs that the human walks through every day, paying attention to none of them.

The sunrise is a sign. It is one of the signs of their Lord. Turned away from.

The body that woke up still working is a sign. One of the signs of their Lord. Turned away from.

The verse just heard is a sign. One of the signs. Turned away from.

The construction is doing the work of multiplying the indictment until you can no longer count what has been ignored.

### Element Three: The Word for *Turning Away* — *Muʿriḍīn*

Now we come to the word that, to me, is the heart of this ayah.

Allah does not say *kāfirīn* — disbelievers. He does not say *jāḥidīn* — deniers, those who openly reject. He does not say *mukadhdhibīn* — falsifiers, those who call truth a lie.

He says *muʿriḍīn*.

This word comes from the root *ʿ-r-ḍ*. And the root meaning of *ʿarḍ* in classical Arabic is **the side**. The flank. The breadth of something — its width, its lateral surface.

When you hear *ʿarḍ al-jabal* in classical poetry, it means the side of the mountain. *ʿArḍ al-baḥr* — the breadth of the sea, the side you don't traverse, the lateral expanse.

Now the verb *aʿraḍa* — Form IV, which gives us the active participle *muʿriḍ* — literally means **to present the side**. To turn the side toward something. To show your flank to it.

Think about what that physically looks like.

When you reject something with your full self, you turn your back. That is *idbār* — back-turning. It is open, definite, declared. The other person sees the back of your head. There is no ambiguity.

When you embrace something, you turn your face toward it. *Iqbāl* — face-turning. Your eyes meet it. Your attention is engaged.

But what is *iʿrāḍ*? It is **neither**. It is the half-turn. It is the gesture you make at a party when someone you don't want to talk to comes near — you don't turn your back (that would be rude); you don't turn your face (that would invite conversation); you turn your **side**. You drift. Your shoulder becomes the wall between you and them. You remain technically present, but you have made yourself unreachable.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this.

Allah is not saying these people scream at His signs. He is not saying they raise their fists at His messengers. He is saying something far quieter. He is saying that when His signs come — the sunrise, the verse, the ayah, the quiet pull of conscience, the moment of clarity — they **drift**. They show the side. They become unreachable without the courtesy of declaring it.

This is not the sin of the loud rebel. This is the sin of the polite avoider.

And here is what makes the word so devastating: *iʿrāḍ* does not require you to disagree with the sign. It does not require you to argue with it. It does not require you to think badly of it. It only requires you to **not let it land**.

You can affirm a sign with your tongue and still be *muʿriḍ* with your side. You can nod along to a verse and still be *muʿriḍ* with your life. The body language is the betrayal. Not the words.

This is the concept many of us live inside without having a name for. The Quran has a name for it. *Iʿrāḍ*. The polite half-turn from what you cannot afford to face.

### The Ongoing State — *Kānū ... Muʿriḍīn*

There is one more linguistic feature, and then we move on.

Allah does not say *aʿraḍū* — "they turned away" — a single past-tense action. He says *kānū ... muʿriḍīn* — "they were ... turning-away ones."

This is the construction of *kāna* (was) plus the active participle (the one-who-is-doing). And what it produces in Arabic is the sense of **a continuous, ongoing, habitual state**. Not a moment. A condition.

The English equivalent would be: not "they turned away" but "they were, the whole time, in a state of being turners-away."

[PAUSE]

What this means is that the *iʿrāḍ* is not a reaction. It is a **disposition**. The signs do not arrive at a neutral person who then chooses to turn away. The signs arrive at a person whose side is already presented, whose flank is already showing, whose body is already drifting — and the sign passes through that pre-existing posture without making contact.

The turning away is not the response to the sign. The turning away is the **state into which the sign arrives**. The sign was always going to be missed, because the receiver was already half-turned before the sign came.

This is one of the most uncomfortable diagnoses in the Quran. Because it means the problem is not in the moment of meeting the sign. The problem is in the posture you are carrying *before* the sign arrives.

### Bridge

Look at what the linguistic construction has built.

A mathematical totality — every sign without exception. A doubled scope — both the smallest sign and the entirety of the rain of signs. A word for turning away that is not refusal but drift. And a verbal form that turns the drift from an action into a posture.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the spiritual reality of being human under signs.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Architecture of Heedlessness

Imagine you live in a city where, for some reason, the air carries trace amounts of gold. Every breath you take, you are inhaling something precious. The gold is so finely dispersed that no single breath feels different — but cumulatively, you are receiving a fortune with every minute of being alive.

You would not feel rich. You would feel ordinary. Because that is what abundance always becomes — when it is constant, it becomes invisible. The mind cannot register what does not change.

This is the first thing the ayah is naming. Not malice. Not rebellion. Just the simple fact that **the constant becomes the ignored**.

The signs of Allah are like that gold-laden air. They are constant. They saturate the day. The fact that the lungs work, that the eyes open, that the heart beats, that the sun rises in the same direction it rose yesterday, that water still extinguishes fire, that the body of someone you love is still warm when you touch it — every one of these is a sign. *Min āyāti rabbihim*. From the signs of their Lord.

And the human walks through this rain of signs, and *kānū ʿanhā muʿriḍīn* — they are, the whole time, in a state of side-presentation. Not because they have decided against the signs. Because their attention was already elsewhere when the signs came.

[PAUSE]

Where is their attention? On the same things our attention is on. The next thing. The notification. The grievance. The plan. The replay of the past conversation. The rehearsal of the future one. The thousand small fires the *nafs* tends.

The Quran is not describing some special class of denier. It is describing the default cognitive posture of the human under abundance. And the diagnosis is brutal because it implicates everyone who is not actively, deliberately fighting it.

This is why the great scholars of *tazkiyah* — the purification of the heart — taught that the first work of the spiritual path is not adding worship. It is **dismantling the *iʿrāḍ***. Because if you are still in the half-turned posture, no amount of worship will register. The prayer itself will become one more sign you are turned away from.

What does this change about how I live tomorrow? It means the question is not *did I do enough religious things today?* The question is: *was there any moment today where my side was not presented? Was there any moment where I let a sign land?*

A sunrise that you actually saw. A verse that actually entered. A person whose face you actually noticed. A blessing you actually counted. One. Just one. Because the problem the ayah is naming is not the absence of signs. **The signs are coming. The signs do not stop coming.** The problem is the posture of the receiver.

### Theme Two: The Wound of *Rabbihim*

The second theme is hidden in a single pronoun, and if you miss it, you miss the entire emotional register of the ayah.

Allah does not say *min āyāti Allāh* — "the signs of Allah." He does not even say *min āyātinā* — "Our signs," which would have been the standard construction. He says *min āyāti **rabbihim*** — "from the signs of **their Lord**."

The word *rabb* is not the word for distant deity. It is the word for the One whose ongoing care made you. The One who is nurturing you in this very moment. The One whose relationship to you is not abstract sovereignty but lived, intimate, sustaining provision. *Rabb* is the word a child might use of a guardian — the one who feeds, raises, protects, returns to.

And the suffix *-him* — "their" — pins this *rabb* to the very people who are turning away. He is **their** Lord. The Lord of the very ones who are presenting their side. The relationship is not severed by the *iʿrāḍ*. The signs keep coming. The provision keeps flowing. The *rabb* keeps being *rabb*.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this does emotionally.

If the ayah had said *the signs of God*, it would be a metaphysical observation. People ignore God. Fine. We've heard that.

But *the signs of **their Lord*** — that is the language of betrayal inside an ongoing relationship. It is the parent who has been raising you your entire life, whose hand is still on your back, whose food is still on your plate — and you cannot bear to meet their eyes when they walk into the room.

This is what the ayah is describing. Not atheists in the abstract. The human inside the relationship with the *rabb*, who keeps receiving from that *rabb* in every breath, and whose side stays politely presented to every reminder of the One whose hand is still feeding them.

The *iʿrāḍ* is not a metaphysical posture. It is a **relational wound**. And the wound is mostly inside the one doing the turning.

Because here is the structural truth: the *rabb* does not need the face of the servant. The signs are not for Him. The signs are the language of the relationship He is offering. When the side is presented, what is being refused is not Allah's need to be acknowledged. What is being refused is the human's own opportunity to be **inside the relationship that is already feeding them**.

[PAUSE]

The hadith of the Prophet ﷺ tells us that Allah says: "I am with My servant according to his thought of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me. If he remembers Me in himself, I remember him in Myself..."

The signs are the invitation into that closeness. To be *muʿriḍ* is not to refuse God. It is to refuse the feast that has already been laid for you, while continuing to eat the appetizers of His provision and never lifting your eyes to meet the host.

What does this change about how I live tomorrow? It changes the emotional weight of inattention. *Iʿrāḍ* is not just a cognitive failure. It is a **kind of unkindness toward the One whose kindness has not stopped for a single second of your life.** Once you feel that, the half-turn becomes harder to sustain. The relational pressure is too great. You find yourself, almost involuntarily, beginning to turn your face — not because of obligation, but because the politeness of the half-turn has become unbearable in the presence of so much grace.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic construction and the themes are saying the same thing.

The mathematical totality of *mā ... illā* shows you that the *iʿrāḍ* is not a special failing of bad people. It is the default human posture under the saturation of signs. The doubled word *min āyatin min āyāti rabbihim* shows you that the signs are constant — both vast and minute, both rare miracles and the daily breath. The word *muʿriḍīn* shows you that the failure is not loud refusal but quiet drift. And the *kāna* + active participle shows you that the drift is a posture, not a moment.

The themes simply translate this into the heart's language. Heedlessness is not malice. It is the architecture of attention under abundance. And the deepest cut of the ayah is the word *rabbihim* — because the One being half-turned-from is the One whose hand has not, for a single instant, stopped giving.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I let a sign actually land — not nodded through, not affirmed verbally and dropped, but *received*?

2. What is my body doing during dhikr, during salāh, during the recitation? Is my face turned, or is my side presented?

3. If I were to track the things my attention does instinctively reach for through the day — would those be the things being treated as the signs of my Lord, or the things being treated as the signs that matter?

4. The ayah says *kānū* — "they were continuously" turning. What if the issue in my life is not this or that decision, but a posture I have been carrying for years that no decision has yet disrupted?

5. What would it cost me to fully turn my face to one sign today? Why does even imagining that feel like a kind of loss?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Every sign of your Lord is arriving — the question is not whether they come, but whether your side is still politely presented when they do.**

### The Closing Invitation

This is not an ayah that wants to make you feel bad. The Quran does not name *iʿrāḍ* to convict — it names it so that you can finally **see** it. Because what is invisible cannot be turned from. The half-turn works precisely because we don't notice ourselves doing it. The whole architecture of heedlessness depends on its own invisibility.

The ayah hands you the word. *Muʿriḍ.* Now you have a name for the posture. And once you have the name, the posture loses some of its power to stay invisible.

Let the invitation be: tomorrow, just once, **catch the side-turn as it begins**. Not to fix everything. Not to become a saint. Just to notice — *that was iʿrāḍ. A sign just came, and my body half-turned, and I was about to drift past it.* The noticing is the first thing the ayah is asking from you. Not the fixing. The seeing.

Because the One whose signs are arriving has not stopped sending them. He is *rabbukum*. The signs will come tomorrow. They came this morning before you opened this. The only question — the only one this ayah is whispering — is whether anything has changed in the posture of the one to whom they are arriving.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You are our Lord. The signs You send have not stopped — and we have not been the receivers we should have been. Disrupt the posture in us before it hardens further. Let one sign land today. And let the landing of that one sign be the beginning of a face that no longer half-turns from the One whose hand has never half-turned from us.*

*Āmīn.*
