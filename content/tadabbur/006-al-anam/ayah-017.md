# When Harm Touches You and When Good Does

## The Ayah

> وَإِن يَمْسَسْكَ ٱللَّهُ بِضُرٍّ فَلَا كَاشِفَ لَهُۥٓ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ وَإِن يَمْسَسْكَ بِخَيْرٍۢ فَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ قَدِيرٌۭ

> *Wa in yamsaska Allāhu bi-ḍurrin fa-lā kāshifa lahu illā huwa. Wa in yamsaska bi-khayrin fa-huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr.*

> **"And if Allah touches you with harm, there is none who can lift it except Him. And if He touches you with good — then He is over all things capable."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

Surah Al-An'am descends in Makkah, late in the Prophet's ﷺ time there. The audience is a society that does not deny Allah — they affirm Him as Creator (you'll see this several ayahs later, in 6:25 and beyond) — but they have surrounded Him with intermediaries. Idols at the Ka'bah, daughters they attribute to Him, intercessors they imagine standing between themselves and Him. When something goes wrong, they call on these intermediaries. When something goes right, they thank them.

The Surah is dismantling that architecture. Brick by brick. Ayah by ayah.

In the lines just before this one, Allah has said: *To Him belongs what is in the night and the day* (6:13). *Should I take other than Allah as a protector?* (6:14). *I fear, if I disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous Day* (6:15). And then this ayah comes — almost like the practical application after a long theological lecture. **Not just "Allah owns everything." But: when something happens to you — when life lands on you — what does that ownership actually mean for you, in that moment, in that body, with that fear?**

The ayah answers that question. But the way it answers — the architecture of *how* it answers — is what we'll spend our time with today.

### The hook

Here is what should stop us before we even begin:

This ayah has two halves. They look symmetrical. *If harm touches you... if good touches you...* The setup is mirror-image. **But the two responses are not mirror-image at all.** When harm comes, Allah names Himself the *only one who can lift it.* When good comes, Allah does not say "no giver except Him." He says something different. He says, "He is over all things capable."

Why?

Why doesn't Allah just say "no giver except Him" to match the first half? Why the change of vocabulary? Why does the heart need a different sentence when good arrives than when harm does?

By the end, we will see: **the asymmetry is not a stylistic accident. It is a diagnosis of the human heart. Allah is naming exactly what we fear in each situation — and answering each fear in its own language.**

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb *Yamsas* — "Touch"

#### Teaching the concept first

In every language, there is a difference between **grabbing** and **brushing against**. If I tell you "a stranger grabbed my arm in the market," your body tightens. If I tell you "a stranger brushed against me in the market," your body relaxes — that's normal, crowds do that.

The verb is the same category — physical contact between two bodies — but the *intensity* is doing huge work. In Arabic, this distinction exists very precisely.

For seizing, gripping, encompassing — Arabic has *akhadha*, *qabaḍa*, *aḥāṭa*. These are the verbs of force.

For light contact, brushing, touching the surface — Arabic has the root **م-س-س** (*m-s-s*). This is the root of the word *mass* — which is what Allah uses here.

#### Opening the word

> **يَمْسَسْكَ** — *yamsaska* — "He touches you"

Notice: Allah does not say *yuṣībuka* (He strikes you). He does not say *yansiluka* (He deals you a blow). He does not say *yaqbiḍu ʿalayka* (He grips you). He says *yamsaska* — He *touches* you.

Let's sit with this.

The classical lexicographers are unified on the texture of this verb. *Mass* is the lightest possible contact. Al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* notes that *mass* is what happens between two surfaces meeting — without grip, without grasp. It is the same verb the Quran uses for the *touch* of fire on skin (*massat-hu al-nār*), the *touch* of shayṭān on the heart, the *touch* of poverty (*massa-hum al-ba'sā'*). It is contact at the surface.

Now look at what *mass* is paired with in our ayah: **ḍurr.** Harm. Affliction. The kind of thing that — if you are inside it — feels like it is *crushing* you. Cancer. A child's diagnosis. Bankruptcy. The phone call at 2 a.m.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The thing that, *to you*, feels like the universe sitting on your chest — Allah describes from His vantage as a **touch.** Not a grip. Not an encompassment. A surface contact, calibrated, deliberate, finite.

This is not minimizing your pain. The same Quran tells you *fa-la-tublawunna fī amwālikum wa anfusikum* — *you will surely be tested in your wealth and yourselves* (3:186). It does not pretend the test is light. **What this word does is reframe the geometry of the test.** It tells you: from where you stand, this looks like an ocean. From where Allah stands, this is a fingertip. And the fingertip belongs to the One who has written mercy upon Himself.

There's a second thing. *Mass* implies *temporariness*. When two surfaces touch, they don't fuse. The contact has a beginning and an end. Built into the verb itself is the message that **this is not permanent.** Even if it is long. Even if it lasts decades. The grammar of the affliction is the grammar of a touch — something that, by its nature, will lift.

And notice: the same verb is used for *good* in the second half. *Wa in yamsaska bi-khayrin* — *and if He touches you with good*. The good is also a touch. Surface contact. Not your possession. Not fused to you. It came; one day, it will go; both of these are happening within the geometry of His fingertip.

What this single word reveals: **everything that lands on you in this life — the worst and the best — has the same texture from Allah's vantage. A touch. Calibrated. Contained. Coming, going.**

### Element Two: The Asymmetric Response — *Kāshif* vs. *Qadīr*

This is the heart of the ayah. Stay with me.

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, when you want to say "*nothing* X *except* Y" with maximum force, the structure is: a **negation particle** followed by a **noun**, then **illā** (except), then the only thing that breaks the negation. *Lā ilāha illā Allāh.* No deity, except Him. This construction is called *al-istithnā' al-mufarragh* — the "emptied exception" — and it is the most absolute restriction the Arabic language has. It says: I have searched the entire universe for an exception, and there is one, and only one, and here is its name.

Allah uses exactly this construction in the first half of our ayah. **And then refuses to use it in the second half.** That refusal is the entire teaching.

#### Opening the asymmetry

Look at the two halves laid side by side:

| First half (harm) | Second half (good) |
|---|---|
| وَإِن يَمْسَسْكَ ٱللَّهُ بِضُرٍّ | وَإِن يَمْسَسْكَ بِخَيْرٍ |
| *and if Allah touches you with harm* | *and if He touches you with good* |
| فَلَا كَاشِفَ لَهُۥٓ إِلَّا هُوَ | فَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ قَدِيرٌۭ |
| *no remover for it except Him* | *He is over all things capable* |

The setup is parallel. The response is not.

Notice what Allah *did not* say in the second half. He did not say *fa-lā mu'ṭiya lahu illā huwa* — "no giver except Him." That would have been the perfect symmetric mirror. He did not write the mirror. He wrote something *different*.

Let's sit with this.

#### Why the change?

Here is the question we need to answer: when something difficult lands on a human being, what is the *first thing* the heart asks? And when something good lands, what is the *first thing* the heart asks?

When affliction hits, the question that screams in the chest is: **"Who can take this off me?"** That is the entire emotional content of the moment. You are not theorizing about cosmology. You are not pondering divine attributes. You are a body in pain looking for the only door. So Allah, in the first half, reaches directly into that scream and answers the *exact* question being asked. He uses the noun *kāshif* — "remover," "lifter," from the root *k-sh-f*, which means to uncover, to pull aside, like pulling away a veil that was covering something. *No lifter except Him.* And He uses the most absolute restriction in the language: *fa-lā kāshifa lahu illā huwa.* Searched the universe. One door. Here.

Now what happens when good lands? Does the heart ask "who lifted this onto me?" — the parallel question?

Almost never.

When good comes, the heart asks something else. It asks: **"Will it last? Can I keep it? What happens to it now? Can it be taken? Can it grow? Is it real?"**

The fear is no longer about *removal*. It is about *the future.*

[PAUSE]

And so Allah answers a different question, because *we are asking a different question*. He says: **fa-huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr** — *He is over all things capable.*

Notice the scope: *kulli shay'* — *all things*. Not just "this good." All things. Including:
- Sustaining this good
- Increasing this good
- Removing this good if He wishes
- Replacing it with something better
- Making it a reason for your nearness or your distance from Him

The response to good is not "no giver except Him" because the anxiety of the moment is not about the source. The anxiety is about **the future of the gift in His hand.** And so Allah says: relax — *every possible future* of this good is within the scope of My power. There is no scenario you can imagine for it that is not in Mine.

Here is the second layer. There is a documented observation in classical tafsir — al-Rāzī notes it, and Ibn ʿĀshūr develops it — that **the second half is broader than the first.** *Lā kāshifa lahu illā huwa* speaks to a single situation: lifting *this* harm. *Huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr* speaks to *all things*. Why widen at the moment of good?

Because at the moment of good, **the temptation is to narrow.** To grasp. To say: "this gift is mine; this category of good is what I want; let it stay this way forever." The good narrows the heart's vision onto the gift itself. So Allah counters with the widest possible attribute — *over all things capable* — saying: do not narrow your gaze onto this gift. Lift your gaze to the One who is capable over *everything*. The gift is small. He is vast. The gift may go; He stays.

[PAUSE]

#### A third layer: what *qadīr* does that *kāshif* does not

*Kāshif* is an active participle — "remover." It names a specific action.

*Qadīr* is an intensified attribute (*ṣīghat al-mubālaghah*) on the pattern *faʿīl* — but it does more than name an action. It describes a **standing reality** of capability. *Qadīr* is not "He removed this" or "He gave this." It is "He is, in His essence, the one over whom no possibility falls outside His reach."

So when Allah pivots from *kāshif* to *qadīr*, He pivots from **a verb-like attribute** ("the lifter of *this*") to **an essence-like attribute** ("the one whose capability covers *all*"). The first half handles your immediate crisis. The second half hands you the entire universe.

What this asymmetry reveals: **Allah does not give the heart a one-size-fits-all theology of His action. He answers the question the heart is actually asking. Harm produces one cry; He answers it. Good produces a different cry; He answers that. Both cries are answered by Him, but in their own grammar.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Conditional *In* — "If"

#### Teaching the concept first

Arabic has two main words for "if": *idhā* and *in*. They are not interchangeable.

*Idhā* is used for things expected to happen — almost certainly. *Idhā jā'a naṣr Allāh* — *when the help of Allah comes.* The "if" is functionally a "when."

*In* is used for things whose occurrence is *uncertain*, possible, or in the speaker's hands to assume hypothetically. It is the more conditional of the two.

#### Opening the word

Allah uses *in*. Twice. *Wa **in** yamsaska Allāhu bi-ḍurr.* *Wa **in** yamsaska bi-khayr.*

If we translated word-for-word: "And **if at all** Allah touches you with harm... and **if at all** He touches you with good..."

This is small but tender. Allah is not promising you that harm will come. He is not promising you that good will come either. He is naming what is *possible* — and telling you what to know *if* it happens.

This is the grammar of a guide, not a sentence. It says: *life will hold both. I am not telling you which arrives next, or how much. I am telling you that whichever lands, here is the door.*

There is a second thing in *in*. Because it is the conditional of *possibility*, it implicitly acknowledges that there are people in your life right now to whom neither has yet arrived in the way you imagine. The person without major harm yet. The person without major good yet. The ayah is not addressed only to those in the storm or those in the sun. It is addressed to everyone, in advance.

What this word reveals: **the ayah is preparation, not just consolation. It is teaching you the grammar before life writes the sentence.**

---

### Bridge from linguistic to thematic

We have spent time with three elements:
- The verb *yamsas* — that what feels like a crushing weight is, from Allah's vantage, a touch.
- The asymmetric response — that harm and good produce different questions in the heart, and Allah answers each in its own language.
- The conditional *in* — that this is preparation for a life that will hold both.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart, and what it means to actually *live* this ayah.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Where the Heart Looks When Harm Strikes

Sit, for a moment, in a memory. The last time something hard landed on you. A diagnosis. A loss. A betrayal. The phone call. The email. The realization at 3 a.m.

What did your heart do, in the first hour?

If you watch yourself carefully, you will notice it ran. It ran *somewhere*. Maybe to the doctor whose number you searched for. Maybe to the friend whose voice you needed. Maybe to your own mind — bargaining, calculating, building escape plans. Maybe to a substance that would mute it for a few hours.

The heart, under affliction, will look for a *kāshif* — a remover. This is not a flaw. **This is how Allah designed you.** When a body is in pain, it cries for relief. When a soul is in pain, it cries for the same.

The question is not *whether* you will look for a remover. The question is *whose face* your heart sees when it looks.

[PAUSE]

The pre-Islamic Arabs Allah is addressing in this surah did not deny that Allah was *capable* of removing harm. They affirmed it. Their problem was that, in the moment of pain, they looked first to other doors. The intermediary. The idol. The intercessor. They believed Allah was up there. But in the cry-out moment, they cried out *here*. To the closer thing.

We are not so different. Our intermediaries have changed. We do not bow to stones. We bow to algorithms, to financial planners, to therapists who become priests, to networks of people we hope can save us, to our own competence which we treat as a god small enough to fit in our chest. Allah is not denying that these are *means*. He is naming what they are *not*. **They are not kāshif.** None of them, in the deepest sense, can lift what Allah has placed.

The doctor cannot lift cancer. The doctor can be Allah's hand. There is a difference. The financial planner cannot lift a recession. The friend cannot lift grief — they can sit with you in it, and Allah blesses that, but they cannot pull the veil off your chest. The veil is held in one Hand.

Now hear the gift in this. **If only one Hand can lift it, then only one Hand needs to be sought.** The whole exhausting search across faces — that search ends here. There is one door. Knock on it.

This is the practical core of *tawakkul*. Not that you don't try. The Prophet ﷺ said *iʿqil wa tawakkal* — *tie your camel and trust* (Tirmidhī). You take the means. You go to the doctor. You make the call. You write the email. **But your heart is not handing the camel its identity.** Your heart, the entire time, knows that the rope is being held above by a different Hand than the one tying it below.

Here is the question to carry: in the last hard thing that landed on you, **whose face did your heart see in the first hour?** If it wasn't His — that is your invitation to learn this ayah by heart. Not as words. As a reflex.

The practical application: when affliction next lands, before you reach for the phone, before you reach for the search bar, before you reach for the bottle — **say His name first.** Even one word. *Yā Allāh.* Then reach for the means. The order matters. The order trains the heart.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Anxiety of Good — The Fear We Don't Talk About

There is a fear we rarely name out loud, because we are embarrassed by it. The fear of losing what we have.

A new marriage. A new child. A new job. A health that has held. A peace in the home. A relationship that has finally settled. The good has arrived, and almost the moment it arrives, the heart begins to *guard* it — and the guarding is its own kind of torment.

Have you noticed?

You can be more anxious *during* good times than during hard ones. In hard times, the worst has already happened; the heart has nothing more to lose. In good times, the heart has *everything* to lose — and so it scans the horizon for the storm. It tightens. It builds defenses. It cannot rest.

This is a kind of suffering that hides behind the language of gratitude. We call it "being careful." "Being protective." "Being mature." But underneath, often, there is a knot.

The classical mufassirūn observed something quietly devastating about this ayah. They noticed that the heart, when good arrives, does not feel the need to ask "who gave this?" the way it asks "who can lift this?" when harm arrives. **The heart of harm searches for a Lord. The heart of good often forgets there is a Lord.** Good can be more spiritually dangerous than hardship, because hardship drives you to His door, and good can lull you into thinking the door isn't needed today.

This is why Allah does not say *fa-lā muʿṭiya lahu illā huwa* — "no giver except Him" — in the second half. That phrasing would have answered a question about the *past*. Who gave this? But the heart in receipt of good is not, in its anxious moments, asking about the past. **It is asking about the future.** Will it stay? Can I keep it? What if it goes?

So Allah answers the future. *Huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr.* He is over *all things* capable. Every future of this gift sits inside His hand.

[PAUSE]

Stay with what this does to the anxiety.

If every possible future of the good — its keeping, its losing, its growing, its replacing — sits in *one* hand, then the guarding of it stops being your job. **You cannot guard against Him. And He is not your adversary.** He is the same one who has written mercy upon Himself, two ayahs earlier in this very surah. The hand holding the gift's future is the hand of the Most Merciful.

This unlocks something the heart desperately needs. It unlocks the ability to **enjoy good without flinching.** To hold the child without the inner script of *what if I lose him.* To accept the love without the inner script of *what if it ends.* To walk in the health without the inner script of *what if it breaks.* Not because those endings are impossible — Allah said *if* — but because their possibility no longer terrorizes you, since the One holding all possibilities is the One who has already said *I have written mercy upon Myself for you.*

Sit with this. **You were not given the good to guard it. You were given the good to use it.** Use it for Him. Pray with it. Love with it. Build with it. Spend it on the people He has placed near you. Whatever you spend rightly, He keeps in a different account, on a different server, that no future can corrupt. *Mā ʿindakum yanfadu wa mā ʿindAllāhi bāq* — *what is with you runs out, and what is with Allah remains* (16:96).

There is one more layer. Look again at *qadīr*. The attribute is *over all things*. Including: **He is capable of giving you good you have not yet seen.** The good you currently hold is not the ceiling. He is over *all* good, not just *this* good. So if this good leaves — and one day it will, because every *mass* is a touch and every touch ends — the same Hand that held this good can place a different good in its place. Often a better one. The widow Allah married to His Messenger ﷺ had buried three husbands before. The good was not over because the previous good had ended. The good was just shifting hands within the same Hand.

The practical application: when you next notice yourself *guarding* a good — tightening around the spouse, the child, the health, the home — pause and say, *fa-huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr.* Loosen the grip. **You were never the holder.** You were given a touch. Enjoy the touch. The Holder is the one over all things capable, and He has written mercy upon Himself for you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

We have walked through three linguistic discoveries and two themes. Let us see how they were always one insight from different angles.

The verb *yamsas* — *touch* — established the **scale**: every harm and every good is a contained, calibrated, surface contact from a vantage where Allah stands above, not within.

The asymmetric response — *kāshif* for harm, *qadīr* for good — established the **diagnosis**: Allah knows the heart asks a different question in each case, and He answers each in its own language.

The conditional *in* established the **frame**: this is not a description of one event, it is a grammar for a whole life.

And the two themes — where the heart looks under harm, and where the heart looks under good — were the same insight in lived form. The question of harm is *who lifts?* The question of good is *who holds the future?* The answer in both directions is one Name.

### Architectural reveal

Now look at the shape of the ayah:

**A. If Allah touches you with harm — no lifter except Him**
**B. If He touches you with good — He is over all things capable**

The architecture is not symmetric. **It is *converging*.** The first half names *Him.* The second half names *Him.* Both halves end with the third-person pronoun *huwa*. The ayah opens with the divine name — *Allāh* — at the start, and pivots through *huwa* twice. Every door in this ayah opens onto the same Face.

**The architecture *is* the message.** Whatever happens to you — whichever side of the *if* arrives — the geometry of your gaze is the same. Up. To Him. Always Him.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these in the days after listening. Not for instant answers. For honest examination.

1. **In the last hard thing that touched you, whose face did your heart see in the first hour?** Not the third hour, after you'd had time to compose yourself. The first.

2. **What good are you currently guarding?** And what would change in your body if you released the guarding to Him — not the gift, the *guarding*?

3. **When you say *Yā Allāh*, do you mean it as a first reflex or a last resort?** What would have to be true in your inner life for it to become the first?

4. **What good have you lost in the past that, looking back, you can now see was the necessary opening for a different good He brought?** Where does the evidence in your own life point about His handling of *kulli shay'*?

5. **If you knew — knew with the kind of knowing that lives in the body, not just the mind — that every future of every good in your life sat inside one Merciful Hand, what is one knot in your chest that would untie?**

### One-sentence distillation

> **Whatever the touch — harm or good — there is one Hand, one door, and one Face; harm has no lifter except Him, and good has no future outside His capability.**

### The closing invitation

This is not an ayah to memorize and move on from. It is an ayah to *live inside.*

The next time difficulty lands — even small difficulty, even a stuck traffic light, even a hard email, all the way up to the worst — practice saying His Name first. Before the search, before the call, before the bargain. *Yā Allāh.* One word. Then take your means.

The next time good lands — a moment of unexpected ease, a child's laugh, a meal you enjoyed, a body that worked, a love that held — practice loosening your grip. Not letting go of the gift. Letting go of the *guarding.* Whisper, even silently, *fa-huwa ʿalā kulli shay'in qadīr*. Hand the future back to its Owner.

Do this for forty days. Watch what happens to the inside of your chest.

### Du'ā

> *Yā Allāh — we ask You by Your name al-Kāshif, the Lifter, that whenever harm touches us, You are the first door our heart finds. And we ask You by Your standing as al-Qadīr over all things, that whenever good touches us, You teach our heart to enjoy without grasping, knowing that every future of every good rests in Your hand. Make our reflex toward You. Make our gaze upward. Make us, in harm and in good, people of one door and one Face. Āmīn.*
