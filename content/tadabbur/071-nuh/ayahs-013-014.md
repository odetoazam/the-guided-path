# The Weight You Refuse to Give

## Surah Nuh 71:13–14

> **مَّا لَكُمْ لَا تَرْجُونَ لِلَّهِ وَقَارًا ۝ وَقَدْ خَلَقَكُمْ أَطْوَارًا**
>
> *Mā lakum lā tarjūna lillāhi waqārā — wa qad khalaqakum aṭwārā*
>
> "What is the matter with you that you do not regard Allah with the weight He deserves — when He has created you in stages?"

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Man Who Preached for Nine Centuries

Before we open a single word, I want you to feel the room these two short verses are spoken into.

Imagine standing in front of a people for nine hundred and fifty years. Not nine hundred and fifty days. Not a long career. **Centuries.** Generation after generation, fathers handing down their refusal to their sons like an inheritance. Nuh — peace be upon him — tells Allah elsewhere in this very surah that he called them *by night and by day*, that he called them *openly* and then *in secret*, that every time he called them they *put their fingers in their ears and pulled their garments over their heads* (71:5–7).

Picture that gesture. A grown man, when the truth comes near, physically stuffing his fingers into his ears and pulling his cloak over his face — like a child hiding from a sound. That is the audience of these two verses.

And notice what Nuh has *just* finished saying. In the verses immediately before these — 71:10 to 71:12 — he is not threatening them. He is almost pleading with sweetness. *Ask forgiveness of your Lord; indeed He is ever-Forgiving. He will send the sky upon you in showers, and increase you in wealth and children, and make for you gardens, and make for you rivers.* Rain. Money. Children. Orchards. Flowing water. He lays the whole catalogue of what a desert people would die for at their feet, and says: all of this — just turn back. Al-Ṭabarī notices something easy to skip past: Nuh did not name these gifts at random. He chose *wealth* and *children* and *gardens* precisely because, as al-Ṭabarī puts it, *they were a people who loved wealth* — he aimed the appeal straight at what their hearts already prized, meeting them at the exact altar where they worshipped. And still they would not move.

[PAUSE]

And then, in verse 13, the tone breaks. Something shifts in the voice. After all the gifts have been named, a question rises up — and it is not really a question for information. It is the question of a man who cannot believe what he is seeing:

*What is the matter with you?*

What is **wrong** with you — that you will not give Allah His weight?

Here is the hook. Hold onto it for the next hour. Nuh does not ask, "Why don't you believe?" He does not ask, "Why don't you obey?" He reaches for a stranger, deeper word — a word that means *weight, gravity, heaviness, the seriousness a thing carries* — and he says: the problem with you is that **Allah weighs nothing to you.** He is light to you. He does not land.

And then, before they can answer, he closes the door behind them with five words that turn their own bodies into a witness for the prosecution.

Let's go find out what he saw in those eight words — words so few you could say them in a single breath, and so heavy you could spend a lifetime under them.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تَرْجُونَ — When the Word for "Hope" Means "Fear"

Let me teach you something about language before we touch the verse.

In every language we have, there are words that seem to mean one thing on the surface but carry their opposite hidden inside them. Think of the English word "awesome." We use it now to mean "great, fun, nice" — *that's awesome.* But underneath it is **awe** — the older, heavier thing — the feeling that makes your knees weak, the trembling at something vast. "Awesome" once meant *terrifying in its grandeur.* The fear and the wonder were the same feeling. Over time we hollowed it out and kept only the light part.

Hold that idea. Because the same thing is alive in the heart of this verse.

The word Allah uses is **تَرْجُونَ** — *tarjūna* — from the root **ر-ج-و** (*r-j-w*). Now, if you have ever studied any Arabic at all, you know this root. It is the root of **رَجَاء** — *rajāʾ* — **hope.** It is one of the most beautiful and gentle words in the entire spiritual vocabulary of Islam. Rajāʾ is the wing of hope, paired with khawf, fear, the two wings the believer flies with. Tarjūna, on its face, simply means *you hope, you expect, you look forward to.*

So read the verse with that plain meaning: *What is the matter with you that you do not **hope** for Allah a weight?*

That sounds almost strange in English. And here is where the verse opens.

[PAUSE]

When the classical scholars came to this exact word in this exact place, something remarkable happened. They did not all reach for "hope." Listen to what al-Qurtubī records. He says plainly: **"الرجاء هنا بمعنى الخوف"** — *rajāʾ here carries the meaning of khawf — fear.* The word for hope is being used to mean its companion, fear. *What is wrong with you that you do not **fear** for Allah a greatness, a power over you to punish?*

And he does not say this alone. He stacks the witnesses. He brings Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Abū al-ʿĀliya, and ʿAṭāʾ ibn Abī Rabāḥ — that you do not *hope for His reward nor fear His punishment.* He brings a narration through Ibn ʿAbbās — that you do not *dread His punishment while you hope for His reward.* Al-Baghawī adds al-Kalbī — *you do not fear Allah as His greatness deserves* — and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī — *you neither know Allah's right over you nor give thanks for His blessing.* And al-Muyassar renders the whole line in the plainest terms: *what is wrong with you that you do not fear the greatness of Allah and His dominion* — pairing His *ʿaẓama,* His greatness, with His *sulṭān,* His sovereign authority over you. It is not merely that He is vast; it is that His vastness *holds power over you.*

There is even a reading that keeps *hope* fully intact and simply turns the verse on a different hinge. Al-Jalālayn glosses it this way: *what is the matter with you that you do not look forward to — do not hope for — Allah's* waqār *toward* you, *His granting you weight and honor, by your coming to believe?* On this reading the *waqār* is not the gravity you withhold from Him but the gravity He is offering to bestow on *you* — that to take Him seriously is to be made weighty yourself, lifted into dignity in His sight. The two readings pull in opposite directions — reverence you owe Him, versus honor He would give you — yet they close on the very same diagnosis: here is a people for whom neither Allah nor anything Allah offers carries the slightest weight.

Now stay with me, because here is what is so easy to miss. We could treat these as a list of competing opinions — *did it mean hope, or did it mean fear?* — and try to pick a winner. **Don't.** That flattens the miracle. Look at what the word is actually doing.

Allah chose the word for **hope** — *rajāʾ* — to deliver a meaning that includes **fear.** He did not say *takhāfūn* (you fear). He did not say *tarhabūn* (you dread). He reached for the gentlest word in the spiritual dictionary and let the heavy meaning live inside it.

Why?

[PAUSE]

Here is what this invites us to consider. To truly *hope* in someone, you must first take them seriously. You cannot hope in something you consider weightless. A child does not *hope* in a stranger on the street; he hopes in his father — because the father is *real* to him, weighty, capable, someone whose response actually matters. **Hope and fear are not opposites. They are two faces of the same thing: taking someone seriously enough that what they do matters to you.**

The person who neither hopes in Allah nor fears Him is not "in between." He is suffering from one single disease with two symptoms: **Allah does not weigh anything to him.** He does not hope, *because* Allah is not real enough to hope in. He does not fear, *because* Allah is not real enough to fear. The absence of hope and the absence of fear come from the very same hole in the heart.

And so the word *tarjūna* — "you hope" — used in the negative, with this meaning, diagnoses the deepest possible illness in one stroke: not that you fear too little, not that you hope too little, but that **Allah does not register on you at all.**

That is what this single word reveals. The disease is not in the emotions. It is upstream of the emotions. It is in the *weight*.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: وَقَارًا — The Root That Means "Heavy"

Which brings us to the word the whole verse is built around. The word Nuh reached for when he could have reached for a dozen others.

**وَقَارًا** — *waqārā.*

Let me teach you the root first, the way a desert listener would have heard it, before any theology was attached to it.

The root is **و-ق-ر** (*w-q-r*). And at its base, in the oldest layer of the language, this root means one thing: **weight. Heaviness. A heavy load.** The Arabs would say *waqr* for a heavy burden a camel carries. And — this is the detail I want to burn into your memory — they used this exact root for the ear. **وَقْرٌ فِي الْأُذُن** — *waqr fī al-udhun* — means a *heaviness in the ear,* a deafness. In fact, the Qur'an itself uses this root for exactly that: *and in their ears is waqr* — a heaviness, a deafness (as in 41:44 and elsewhere). The same root.

So pause and feel the physical image at the root: **weight.** A load on the back of a camel. A heaviness in the ear that stops sound from passing.

Now — *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs.* Allah is taking something you can lift with your hands, something heavy, and pointing you toward something you cannot see at all. From this root of *physical weight* grows the spiritual word **waqār** — and the scholars are unanimous on what it means here. Al-Baghawī states it directly: **"الوقار: العظمة"** — *al-waqār is greatness, majesty* — and he tells us its grammatical pedigree: it is *ism min al-tawqīr,* a noun built from *tawqīr,* which is **taʿẓīm** — to magnify, to revere, to treat as great. Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, as al-Baghawī reports: *you do not see for Allah any greatness.* Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: *you do not magnify Allah as He truly deserves to be magnified.*

[PAUSE]

Now bring the two layers together, and watch the verse detonate.

To give Allah **waqār** is to give Him **weight.** And the people who refuse it are described, by the very next breath of the language, with a root that *also* means **deafness — a heaviness in the ear that blocks sound.**

Do you feel that? Nuh's whole complaint earlier in the surah was that *they put their fingers in their ears.* And the word for the reverence they withhold from Allah shares its root with the word for the *deafness they have chosen.* They have a *waqr* — a heaviness — in the wrong place. They put the weight in their ears, plugging out the call, instead of putting the weight on their hearts, where Allah belongs.

**The weight has to go somewhere.** Either you carry Allah as the heavy, central, gravitational fact of your life — or that same heaviness settles into your ears and makes you deaf to Him. The root is telling you: there is no weightless option. You will either revere, or you will go deaf.

[PAUSE]

And one more thing the grammar quietly does. Notice the word is **وَقَارًا** — *waqārā* — **indefinite.** Not *al-waqār,* "the reverence." Just *waqārā* — *a* weight, *any* weight, the smallest measure of gravity. Nuh is not even asking for full reverence. He is saying: *you will not grant Him even an ounce. Not a grain of weight. Not the slightest seriousness.* The indefinite form makes the accusation more devastating, not less. It is one thing to fail to revere fully. It is another to give *nothing at all* — to treat the Creator of the heavens as weighing precisely zero.

And listen to the sound of the word itself. **Wa-qā-rā.** That **qāf** — that deep, heavy, guttural consonant struck from the very back of the throat — is itself a heavy sound. The word *sounds* like what it means. It lands with weight. Say it slowly: *waqār.* You cannot say it lightly. The mouth itself has to do the work of lifting something heavy.

This is what one word reveals: **the disease of the human heart is not usually denial. It is weightlessness.** Most people who drift from Allah never decide He doesn't exist. They simply let Him become light — one consideration among a thousand, a small thing, a Sunday thing, a funeral thing. Nuh's diagnosis is sharper than atheism. *You have made the Real into the weightless.*

[PAUSE — let this sit]

### Element Three: أَطْوَارًا — The Body Brought as Evidence

Now Nuh closes the trap. And he closes it not with a threat, but with a fact so close to them they had never thought to look at it: their own bodies.

[ayah:71:14] وَقَدْ خَلَقَكُمْ أَطْوَارًا

First, feel the force of that little particle **قَدْ** — *qad.* In Arabic, when *qad* comes before a completed-tense verb like this, it does one job: it asserts the fact as **certain, settled, already done, undeniable.** It is the Arabic equivalent of saying, "and the fact is —" or "when, beyond any argument —." Nuh is not floating a theory. He is placing on the table a fact they cannot dispute, because they are made of it. *And He has ALREADY created you — there is no arguing this — in stages.*

Then the word: **أَطْوَارًا** — *aṭwārā.* The root is **ط-و-ر** (*ṭ-w-r*), and it means a **stage, a phase, a state, a condition** that something passes through. *Aṭwār* is the plural — *stages, phases, one condition after another.* The single *ṭawr* is one stage; *aṭwār* is the whole staircase.

What stages? The mufassirūn fill it in from the rest of the Qur'an. Al-Muyassar lays them out exactly as they appear in Surah al-Muʾminūn (23:12–14): **a drop, then a clinging clot, then a lump of flesh, then bones, then the bones clothed in flesh.** You were a *nuṭfah,* then *ʿalaqah,* then *muḍghah,* then *ʿiẓām,* then *laḥm.* Stage. After stage. After stage.

[PAUSE]

Now feel why this is the perfect, devastating answer to verse 13.

In verse 13 Nuh accused them: *you give Allah no weight.* And the unspoken question hanging in the air is — *why should we? Where is the evidence that He deserves this weight you demand?*

Verse 14 answers without raising its voice: **look at yourself.**

You are standing here, refusing to take Allah seriously, *with a body that He built in deliberate, measured, sequenced stages.* You did not assemble yourself. You were not present for a single stage of your own construction. You have no memory of being a drop, no memory of becoming a clot, no memory of your bones forming and then being wrapped in flesh. **Every stage of you happened to you.** You were pure receiver. Someone, with overwhelming knowledge and power and patience, fashioned you slowly, stage by stage, while you contributed *nothing* and *knew nothing.*

And *that* Being — the One who did all of that to you, in you, for you — *that* is the One you say weighs nothing?

[PAUSE]

This is *istidlāl bil-maḥsūs* at its most intimate. Allah does not point Nuh's people to the stars first (that comes in the next verses — the seven heavens, the moon as light, the sun as a lamp). He points them to the *nearest* thing. The thing they can never put down or walk away from. **Their own flesh.** The argument is not out there in the cosmos. It is *inside the very mouth* that refuses to give Him weight.

And notice the asymmetry the language sets up — read the absence. Verse 13 is full of *you*: *what is the matter with **you,** **you** do not give weight.* The human is the loud, active, refusing subject. But verse 14 quietly removes you from the throne: *He created **you.*** Suddenly you are the object, not the subject. You are the thing that was made, in stages, while He was the One making. The grammar performs the very humbling the verse is arguing for. In thirteen, you are the one standing in judgment of Allah's worth. In fourteen, you are revealed as the handiwork standing in the workshop, foolishly questioning the Craftsman.

That is what these three words reveal: **the proof you demanded that Allah deserves weight is the body you are standing in while you demand it.**

---

### Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. *Tarjūna* — a word for hope that carries fear, diagnosing not a missing emotion but a missing *seriousness.* *Waqārā* — a word for reverence rooted in *physical weight,* whose own root warns that the weight will become deafness if it does not become devotion. And *aṭwārā* — the staged body brought as silent, irrefutable evidence.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about how a human heart actually drifts, and how it finds its way back.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Crisis of Weightlessness

Let me start where you actually live, not where Nuh's people lived.

You wake up and reach for your phone. In the first ninety seconds of consciousness, before you have even prayed, before you have spoken to a single human being, your eyes pass over: a war, a recipe, a death, a comedian, a verse of Qur'an, an advertisement, a friend's wedding, a genocide, a sale on shoes. **They scroll past at the same speed.** They occupy the same size on the screen. Your thumb moves them all upward at the same velocity. The atrocity and the advertisement are given the *exact same weight* — which is to say, almost none.

This is the disease of our age, and it has a name now that Nuh named it fourteen centuries before the screen existed. **Weightlessness.** We have built a world in which *nothing is allowed to be heavy.* Everything is content. Everything is light, swipeable, disposable, equal. The flattening is total. And a heart trained for years to give everything the same near-zero weight slowly loses the *ability* to give anything its true weight.

Do you see why this is the most dangerous spiritual environment imaginable? It is not that we are being argued out of revering Allah. Nobody is mounting a case against Him in our feeds. It is far quieter and far worse: **He is being placed on the same conveyor belt as everything else, moving at the same speed, given the same flick of the thumb.** Not denied. Just *lightened.* Made into one more piece of content between the recipe and the shoes.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing about *waqār* that should stop us cold. The root means *weight.* And weight is what makes you **slow down.** A heavy object cannot be moved quickly. You cannot swipe past a boulder. The presence of true weight in your life is what *resists* the scroll, what makes you stop, sit, and stay.

So the restoration of *waqār* is not, at first, about emotion. You cannot manufacture awe by trying to feel awed. The restoration begins with a far simpler, more physical act: **you let something be heavy enough to make you slow down.** You let one ayah stop you for ten minutes instead of ten seconds. You let one prayer be heavy enough that you do not rush through it like a man late for a train. You restore weight by restoring *time* — because time spent is the truest currency of seriousness. We give weight to whatever we are willing to be slow for.

Think about what you are slow for. Think about what you let be heavy. That list — not your stated beliefs, but that list of what you slow down for — *that* is the real measure of what carries waqār in your life.

[PAUSE]

And notice the mercy buried in Nuh's diagnosis. He does not say *what is the matter with you, you wicked people.* He says *mā lakum* — *what is the matter with you* — the way you would speak to someone you love who is harming themselves. *What's wrong? This isn't like you. Why are you doing this to yourselves?* The accusation is wrapped in bewildered care. Because to live weightlessly is not freedom — it is its own punishment. A life in which nothing is heavy is a life in which nothing is *real.* The person who reveres nothing is not liberated; he is *starving,* because the human heart was built to carry weight, and a heart with nothing heavy in it does not float — it withers.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Pick one thing — your fajr, one page of Qur'an, the moment you say *Allāhu akbar* — and refuse to let it be light. Be slow for it. Let it be the boulder in your day that the scroll cannot move past. You are not trying to feel something. You are restoring *weight.* The feeling follows the weight; it never precedes it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: You Are the Argument You're Ignoring

Now the second theme, and it begins with a strange fact about being human: **we are profoundly estranged from our own making.**

You have never met the most important construction project of your life. You were assembled in total darkness, in stages, over months — *aṭwār* — and you were not invited. You have no memory of it. You cannot recall becoming. You woke up, years into being alive, already finished, already walking around, and you have spent your whole life treating this body as a *given* — as the floor you stand on rather than the miracle you are made of.

This is the genius of verse 14. Nuh does not send his people on a journey to find evidence of God. He says: *the evidence is the thing you are standing in.* You are the proof you keep saying you don't have.

Let me make this concrete, because it is easy to nod at and miss. Modern people will travel to the edge of the universe with their telescopes looking for a sign of the Creator — and they are right to look, the heavens are full of signs. But Nuh's argument is sharper and closer. He says: *you don't need the telescope. You ARE the data.* Before you ever looked up at a single star, **you were a drop that became a clot that became a lump that became bones that were wrapped in flesh** — and you have no idea how, and you did not do it, and you could not undo it or redo it if your life depended on it.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to make in your own chest.

Most of us locate the question of God *outside* ourselves. *Out there* — in the cosmos, in philosophy, in the arguments. And so the question always feels distant, debatable, something you can shelve. But verse 14 relocates the entire question to *inside your own skin.* It says: the One whose weight you are deciding *built the deciding-machine.* He made the very brain you are using to weigh Him. He staged the very tongue you are using to dismiss Him. He sequenced the very heart you are using to feel nothing toward Him.

And once you really see that, the refusal of *waqār* becomes almost absurd. It is the painting arguing that the painter weighs nothing. It is the letter claiming the writer was never serious. **You are walking around inside the most intimate, irrefutable argument for taking Allah seriously that has ever existed — and you carry it everywhere, and you ignore it every day.**

Why do we ignore it? Because it is *too* close. We cannot see what we never look away from. A man searches the house for his glasses while wearing them. We overlook the body precisely because we have never once been without it. Familiarity has dissolved the weight. The *aṭwār* — the staged miracle — has become "just me," background, furniture.

[PAUSE]

And there is a tenderness here too, the same tenderness as in the gifts of verses 10 to 12. Notice that Allah did not create you in *one* stage. He did not speak you into existence finished, the way He could have. He took His time. *Aṭwār* — stage, then stage, then stage. There is a patience in staged creation, a deliberate slowness, a craftsmanship. The same Lord who, three verses earlier, promised *gradual* rain and *growing* gardens and *increasing* children, also made *you* gradually. He is, in His dealing with you, a God of stages — patient, sequential, unhurried. The very people refusing to give Him weight were *built* by a patience they have never once extended to Him.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tonight, before you sleep, put your hand on your own chest and feel it rise and fall — a stage of breathing you did not author. Remember that you were once a drop in the dark, and that you were carried, in stages, by a knowledge and a patience that asked nothing of you. Let *that* be the place reverence starts — not in an argument you read, but in the body you are lying in. You don't have to go looking for the sign. You're sleeping inside it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Question That Is Its Own Answer

Now stand back and see what these two verses do together, because the structure is the proof.

Verse 13 is a **question.** *Mā lakum lā tarjūna lillāhi waqārā* — what is the matter with you that you give Allah no weight?

Verse 14 is **not a separate point.** It is bound to verse 13 by that single particle *qad* — *and He has, in fact, already created you in stages.* It is the **answer folded into the question.** The "why should we revere Him?" is rebutted before it can even be spoken aloud. You ask where the weight comes from? *You are the weight. You were made in stages by the One you refuse to weigh.*

Nuh did not need a second argument. He let the question and the body answer each other. The most efficient sermon in the world: *Why won't you take Him seriously? — Look at how seriously He took you.*

### Bringing It Together

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The **root of waqār is weight** — and the **crisis of the human heart is weightlessness.** Same truth.

The **root of waqr is also deafness** — and the people Nuh describes *plugged their own ears.* The weight you refuse to give Allah does not disappear; it lodges in your ears as deafness. Same truth.

And the answer to weightlessness is not an emotion you summon — it is the **staged body** you already inhabit, the *aṭwār* that argues, silently and constantly, for the One who made it. The proof and the disease are housed in the same flesh. Same truth, from two directions.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. What, in my actual life — not my stated beliefs — am I willing to be *slow* for? And what does that list reveal about where Allah's weight truly sits in me?
2. Where have I placed the *waqr* — the heaviness? On my heart, where Allah belongs? Or in my ears, deaf to the call while plugged with everything else?
3. When did I last look at my own body — my breath, my hands, the fact that I was assembled in stages I cannot remember — as an *argument* rather than as furniture?
4. Have I been searching "out there" for a seriousness about Allah that He already wove into the very flesh I'm searching with?
5. If hope and fear are both just *taking Allah seriously* — does Allah register on me at all? Or have I made the Real into the weightless?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The deepest sickness of the heart is not denying Allah but weighing Him as nothing — and the cure was sewn into your body, stage by stage, before you were ever old enough to forget it.**

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked, today, to manufacture awe. You cannot squeeze a feeling out of yourself, and Nuh never demands one. You are being invited to do something quieter and far more possible: **to let one thing be heavy again.** To slow down for it. To stop swiping the Real past at the speed of everything else. To let your own breath, your own staged and unremembered making, become the small boulder that the scroll of your day cannot move past. The weight will go somewhere. Let it, even once, settle where it belongs.

### A Closing Du'ā

*O Allah — You who fashioned me in stages I never saw and cannot recall, forgive me for the days I have weighed You as nothing while standing inside the proof of Your majesty. Restore to my heart the* waqār *it was built to carry. Do not let the heaviness settle in my ears as deafness; let it settle on my heart as reverence. And make me, even now, slow enough to feel the weight of You.*

*Āmīn.*
