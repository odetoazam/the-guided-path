## INTRODUCTION

---

Nine hundred and fifty years.

Let that number sit. Not nine hundred and fifty days. Not nine hundred and fifty months. Years. A prophet of Allah calling his people to the truth for nearly a millennium, watching generation after generation arrive, refuse, and pass away. His da'wah outlasted empires. It outlasted the lifespans of ten generations. By any human measure, it should have broken him.

And yet the Quran does not give us a picture of Nuh defeated. It gives us a record of what he said. Allah, in this surah, preserves the content of Nuh's sermon — so that every person who recites these words until the end of time can witness the methodology of a prophet who refused to stop pointing.

What did he point to?

First, he pointed upward. To the cosmos. Seven heavens laid one atop another. The moon pouring out cool light. The sun blazing like a lamp. Then to rain pouring down from above. Rivers cut through valleys. The whole cosmic order, from the farthest reaches of the sky down through clouds and rain to the rivers at your feet.

And then — here, in ayahs 19 and 20 — he arrives at something so close, so present, so beneath notice that most of us have forgotten it was ever a gift at all.

The ground under your feet.

[ayah:71:19] وَٱللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ بِسَاطًا
[ayah:71:20] لِّتَسْلُكُوا۟ مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا

*And Allah made the earth for you a spread-out carpet — so that you may traverse it on wide, open paths.*

Two short sentences. The whole world in two short sentences.

Stay with me. Because in these nine words is a claim about what the earth IS, what it was made TO DO, and what YOU were built to become — that I think most of us have never fully encountered.

## THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### I. The Verb That Changes Everything

Before we can reach the word for carpet, we need to sit with the verb that carries the whole sentence.

**جَعَلَ** — ja'ala.

Let me explain something about Arabic grammar that will unlock everything here.

In Arabic, there are several verbs that describe what Allah does in relation to creation. The most fundamental is **خَلَقَ** (khalaqa) — to create, to originate, to bring into being from nothing. When Allah says *He created you*, He is speaking about ontological origination. The fact that you exist at all. That there is something rather than nothing.

But جَعَلَ is doing something different.

When ja'ala appears in a double-object construction — **جَعَلَ شَيْئًا شَيْئًا** (ja'ala X Y) — it means to *render* something as something else. To *appoint* it to be a certain thing. To *transform how something stands in relation to you*. It is not the act of bringing something into existence. It is the act of *setting that thing into a particular role, for a particular person*.

Here is the sentence: **وَٱللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ بِسَاطًا**

Allah rendered — appointed — *made* — the earth, for you, a bisāṭ.

The earth already existed long before Nuh's people walked on it. But the earth being a bisāṭ *for you* — that is a second, distinct act. A deliberate act of rendering. Of appointment. Of direction.

Think of it this way. A room exists. A host can take that room and *set it* for guests. The act of setting — of laying a cloth, of arranging chairs, of making it ready — is not the same as constructing the room. It is the act of *rendering* the room into a place of welcome. The room becomes something different when someone has prepared it for someone else.

جَعَلَ is that act.

Allah did not merely create an earth that happens to be flat enough to walk on. He *made* it — appointed it, rendered it — as a bisāṭ. As a prepared spread. And He did this *for you*. لَكُمُ — for you plural, for humanity.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the sentence. It means the earth is not merely a physical fact you happen to be living on. It is the product of an intentional act of preparation directed specifically at you. Before you arrived, the carpet was already laid.

And look at the word order. Classical Arabic grammarians note that when the prepositional phrase precedes the object in this kind of construction, it carries emphasis. Allah could have said **جَعَلَ الأرضَ لكم** — "He made the earth for you." He didn't. He said **جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الأرضَ** — "He made **for you** — the earth." You are foregrounded. You are the intended recipient. The earth comes after you in the sentence because you come first in the intention.

[PAUSE]

This is Nuh's argument to his people — not a philosophical abstraction but a direct address. Look at what has been arranged for you. Look at how you were *anticipated* before you arrived.

---

### II. The Word That Holds a World — بِسَاطًا

Now we reach the word itself.

**بِسَاطًا** — bisāṭan.

The root is **ب-س-ط** (b-s-ṭ). To spread. To lay out flat. To extend across a surface.

From this root, Arabic gives us **بَسَطَ** (to spread out), and the divine name **الْبَاسِطُ** — al-Bāsiṭ, one of the ninety-nine names of Allah, meaning the One who extends, who opens wide, who spreads out His bounty.

But **بِسَاط** as a noun means a carpet. A spread cloth. Something deliberately laid flat for a purpose.

Al-Jalalayn glosses it simply: *mabsūṭah* — spread out.
Al-Qurtubi, the same: *mabsūṭah*.
Al-Baghawi goes a step further: "He spread it and laid it out for you" — *farashahā wa basaṭahā lakum*.

The scholars are anchored on the same image: something deliberately flattened and prepared and *laid down*.

And al-Tabari adds a dimension the single word *carpet* can hide. He glosses the verse: *tastaqirrūna ʿalayhā wa tamtahidūnahā* — you settle and come to rest upon it, and you find it smoothed out beneath you. That second verb shares its root with **مَهْد** (mahd) — a cradle, an infant's bed — the very word the Quran reaches for when describing the earth elsewhere (*ٱلَّذِى جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ مَهْدًا*, 20:53; *أَلَمْ نَجْعَلِ ٱلْأَرْضَ مِهَٰدًا*, 78:6). Al-Muyassar reads the bisāṭ the same way: the earth was made *mumahhadah ka-l-bisāṭ* — leveled and made ready like a carpet. So before the earth is a road to be traversed, it is firm enough to be rested upon. The stability comes first. A carpet you cannot stand on steadily is no carpet at all — and a journey is only ever possible from solid ground.

Now I want you to hold the image of a bisāṭ — a carpet, a spread — for just a moment.

When you lay a carpet, you are doing several things at once. You are making a surface comfortable to walk on. You are defining a space. And you are making an implicit statement: *this is for someone*. Carpets are not for empty rooms. You don't lay a bisāṭ for no one. The act of spreading it presupposes a guest.

Nuh is telling his people: *you are the guest the carpet was laid for.*

[PAUSE]

The noun form **بِسَاط** follows the Arabic pattern فِعَال (fi'āl). This is a pattern that often describes something constituted by its action — the thing defined by what was done to it. A bisāṭ is not merely anything flat. It is *the thing that has been spread*. It carries in its very form the mark of preparation.

This means the earth, in Allah's rendering of it, is not incidentally accommodating — it is *constitutively prepared*. It is what it is because someone made it ready.

There is a word in English I want you to think about: *arranged*.

When something is arranged, it implies a mind behind it. Things don't arrange themselves. An arrangement has an arranger. And what Nuh is inviting his people to see — what Allah placed in Nuh's sermon and preserved for all time — is that the earth under your feet is not a coincidence. It is arranged. It is a bisāṭ. It has been set out.

[PAUSE — major pause]

We live in a time when we have a thousand explanations for *how* the earth came to be the way it is — plate tectonics, gravitational physics, billions of years of geological formation. And those explanations are real. But none of them answers the question Nuh was asking.

*For whom?*

The bisāṭ answers that question. For you. لَكُمُ. You specifically, placed at the front of the sentence.

### III. سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا — The Paths That Were Already Wide

Now we arrive at ayah 20, and everything snaps into clarity.

**لِّتَسْلُكُوا۟ مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا**

So that you may traverse — through it — paths wide and open.

Three things deserve our full attention here.

**First: the purpose particle لِ** — lām al-ta'līl, the lām of cause and purpose. This single letter is doing enormous work. It tells us *why* the earth was made a bisāṭ. The bisāṭ was not a decorative gesture. It had a purpose. The carpet was laid so that you could move.

Which means: you were created to move. You were *expected* to journey. The ground was prepared in anticipation of your traversal.

**Second: تَسْلُكُوا** — taslukū. Root: **س-ل-ك** (s-l-k). To enter a path. To pass through. To thread through a space. The word carries the image of threading — like a needle drawing thread through cloth. You don't merely appear on a path. You *enter into it*, you *thread yourself through it*, you commit to it with intention.

Notice too that this is addressed to you plural. All of humanity. The wide paths are for all of you, from wherever you stand.

**Third, and most striking: سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا** — subulan fijājan.

**سُبُل** (subul) is the broken plural of **سَبِيل** (sabīl) — a path, a way, a road that is well-worn through use. But the plural here is indefinite and unnumbered. Not "the seven paths." Not "three roads." Just: paths. Many. Open. Their number unspecified because the generosity is too wide to count.

And then **فِجَاجًا** — the word that opens the sentence like a breath of open air.

**فِجَاج** (fijāj) is the plural of **فَجّ** (fajj). A fajj is a wide mountain pass. A gap carved between peaks. A route that opens unexpectedly — where the mountain that seemed like a wall turns out to have a way through. Classical Arabic used fajj specifically for a road wide enough to accommodate caravans, armies, the great movements of peoples. Not merely passable — *spacious*.

And Allah gave you فِجَاجًا — plural. Many wide passes. Many spacious routes. The same word appears in Surah al-Hajj (22:27), where people are described coming to the pilgrimage *from every deep mountain pass* — **مِن كُلِّ فَجٍّ عَمِيقٍ** — arriving from the world's every wide road toward the House of Allah. The fajj is where you travel when the destination is sacred.

But here the classical commentators are not entirely of one voice, and the disagreement is worth hearing rather than smoothing over. Al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar gloss *fijājan* simply as *wāsiʿah* — wide, spacious — the reading we have been following. Al-Tabari, however, renders the whole phrase *ṭuruqan ṣiʿāban mutafarriqah* — paths that are **rugged and divergent**, scattering off in many directions. Not only broad and easy, but demanding; not one smooth avenue, but many routes that branch apart, some of them hard going. Hold both readings together, because together they tell the truer thing. The paths Allah opened are *wide* — there is room — but width is not the same as ease. A fajj is, after all, a gap cut through a mountain. To pass through it is to climb. The breadth is the mercy; the ruggedness is simply the nature of a real journey. Allah did not promise a level corridor. He promised an opening through the mountain — and openings through mountains are walked, not glided.

[PAUSE]

Let's read the whole picture together now.

The earth was set out as a carpet — deliberately, as an act of divine preparation, arranged for you specifically — so that you could thread your way through it on paths, not one path but many, not narrow passages but wide, spacious, open mountain passes.

This is not a verse about geography. Or rather — it is about geography the way the Quran always uses physical reality: as a stepping stone to a larger truth.

The earth's quality as bisāṭ — its flat spread-out readiness — exists so that you can move. The paths you move through are fijāj: wide, with room to breathe, room to choose, room to change course and find another way. And the movement has a purpose: **لِ** — so that. Not random movement. Intentional traversal.

[PAUSE — major pause]

What Nuh is giving his people here — what Allah is giving us through Nuh's recorded sermon — is a theology of human movement. You are not stationary. You were not made to be planted in one place and stagnate. The earth was prepared as a bisāṭ not so you could stand on it but so you could *traverse* it. لِتَسْلُكُوا — so that you could thread your way through.

And the paths are many and wide. Which means you have choice. Which means this life is not a corridor but an open landscape with countless passes and routes. The question is not whether paths exist — they do, they are fijāj, they are spacious — the question is whether you are actually journeying.

---

**The Bridge**

What the linguistic construction has shown us across these two verses is this: there is a gift, deliberately made and directed at you (bisāṭ, rendered for you by ja'ala); there is a purpose named explicitly (lam al-ta'līl, *so that*); and there is a quality of abundance built into the arrangement (subulan fijājan — plural, wide, unnumbered).

Gift. Purpose. Abundance.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### I. The Gift You Stopped Seeing

I want to start somewhere that might feel distant from Surah Nuh.

Psychologists use the term *hedonic adaptation* to describe something most of us have experienced without having a name for it. When something new and wonderful enters your life — a house, a job, a relationship, a sensation — your nervous system registers it intensely at first. Then, as it becomes familiar, the registration fades. What was extraordinary becomes background. The brain essentially says: *this is baseline now, no need to keep flagging it*.

Hedonic adaptation is why new shoes feel wonderful for a week and then just normal. Why the view from your window that moved you on day one becomes something you stop seeing by month three.

It is also why the earth — the most present, most intimate physical reality of your entire life — has become invisible to you.

You have walked on the earth every single day of your existence. It has met your foot every time. Not once has it failed you. And with almost complete consistency, you have not noticed.

[PAUSE]

This is precisely the condition Nuh was confronting. Not malice, initially. Not sophisticated theological argument or reasoned rejection of evidence. Just — the loss of the capacity to see.

His people, when described in 71:7, plugged their fingers in their ears, wrapped their garments around their heads, and persisted in their arrogance. But that deep, deliberate refusal came after something earlier — the simpler failure to notice. To notice that the earth was a bisāṭ. That it had been arranged for them.

Allah, in preserving Nuh's sermon, is teaching every reciter of this book that the da'wah to the human heart begins with the most immediate, most overlooked, most intimate reality. Not with the abstract heavens — though those matter. But ultimately with the ground. The thing right here. The thing meeting your foot right now.

The ground under your foot was prepared for you.

[PAUSE]

And there is a depth to that preparation that the surrounding verses make explicit. Al-Muyassar, commenting on 71:17-18, notes that the earth is where your origin lies (human beings made from its substance), where your body returns (burial), and from where you will be raised on the Day of Resurrection. The bisāṭ is not just the stage for your life's journey. It is the material of your beginning, the ground of your traversal, and the keeper of your body in the interval.

The bisāṭ holds you in every phase of existence.

[PAUSE — major pause]

What would it take to actually *see* the ground under your feet?

Not as a thought experiment. Not philosophically. But right now, concretely — to notice that what has been meeting your foot every morning of your life is not a cosmic coincidence but a prepared surface. A bisāṭ. Something set out with you specifically in mind.

Gratitude researchers have found that the most transformative gratitude practice is not cataloguing blessings — it is *noticing what has been arranged*. The difference is subtle but it changes everything. Blessings can feel like luck, like randomness, like good fortune that happened to fall your way. But an arrangement implies an arranger. Someone who thought of you *before* you arrived. Someone who set things out in anticipation of your coming.

Nuh was trying to restore his people's perception of the Host.

He had been at it for 950 years. He had not run out of things to point to. He kept finding new angles — cosmic, meteorological, geographical, and now this: the most immediate gift of all. *Look at the ground. It was laid for you. You didn't fall through it this morning. It held you. It holds you now. When did you last notice?*

The practical question this leaves us with: what in my life has become so constant that I've stopped registering it as gift? Not just the earth — but what else was arranged before I arrived that I now walk across without noticing?

---

### II. You Were Made to Move

Now the second theme — and this one cuts closer to the bone.

**لِّتَسْلُكُوا۟ مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا**

So that you may traverse it on wide, open paths.

This purpose clause is telling us something about what human beings are *for*.

Let me start somewhere familiar. Most of us, at some point in life, have felt stuck. Not stuck in the sense of minor inconvenience — but the deep, disorienting sense that life is not moving. That you have been in the same place for too long. That the paths have narrowed, or disappeared, or that you cannot find them at all.

It is one of the most common human experiences. The feeling of spiritual and psychological stagnation. Of being planted when you were built to walk.

What this verse says — quietly, definitionally — is that that feeling of stagnation is a kind of exile from your purpose.

The earth was made a bisāṭ *so that* you could traverse it. The traversal is the point. **لِتَسْلُكُوا** — "so that you may thread your way through." You were *anticipated* as a mover. As a traveler. As someone with paths to walk and distances to cover.

[PAUSE]

The word **تَسْلُكُوا** — taslukū — is worth dwelling in for a moment. The root s-l-k carries this image of threading through. Of entering a space with intention. You don't just appear on a path; you enter it, you pass through it, you draw yourself into it like a thread pulled through cloth. It is not passive. It is not drift. It is intentional entry.

The wide paths are already there. They exist. They are fijāj — spacious, already open. But they require that you *enter* them. The fijājan are Allah's generosity. Your entering them is your response.

And recall al-Tabari's reading — that these passes are *ṣiʿāb*, rugged as well as wide. That, too, is mercy once it is understood rightly. If the path you are on is demanding — if it climbs, if it costs you something — do not assume you have wandered off the route. The fajj was always going to be steep in places. The Quran nowhere describes the journey toward Allah as a downhill drift. It describes it as a passage carved through mountains: wide enough to be walked, hard enough to mean something.

[PAUSE]

There is a verse in Surah al-Mulk (67:15) that rhymes with what we are reading:

**هُوَ ٱلَّذِى جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ ذَلُولًا فَٱمْشُوا۟ فِى مَنَاكِبِهَا**

*He is the One who made the earth submissive for you — so walk in its open expanse.*

Notice: the same **جَعَلَ**. The same **لَكُمُ**. The same earth. But in al-Mulk, the adjective is **ذَلُولًا** — tamed, made submissive — and the verb is **فامشوا**: *walk!* A command. An imperative. An invitation that presses.

Here in Surah Nuh, the adjective is **بِسَاطًا** — a prepared spread — and the form is a purpose clause: *so that you may traverse*. Same logic, different register. Al-Mulk says: the earth was tamed, so go! Surah Nuh says: the earth was prepared as a carpet *for this very purpose* — that you move.

Two surahs, same divine statement, arrived at from different angles: the earth has been prepared. The remaining question is your movement.

[PAUSE — major pause]

Now here is where this becomes personally demanding.

The fijāj — the wide mountain passes — are plural and open. Allah did not give one path. He gave many, unnumbered, each spacious. This is a profound mercy: you have room to choose, room to err and correct, room to find a different route when one closes, room to arrive from wherever you are. The spaciousness is intentional.

But wide paths can also produce paralysis. When everything is open, when there is no single obvious road, the soul can mistake abundance for absence of direction. The fijājan can feel overwhelming rather than liberating. And in that overwhelm, some people stop moving altogether — sitting in the middle of the bisāṭ, staring at the open paths, choosing none of them.

Here is what Nuh's sermon, taken as a whole, provides: the paths are wide, but you need a *destination*. The wide passes of the Hajj — فِجَاجٍ عَمِيقٍ in 22:27, using this same word — were spacious so that people could come from every far place. But they were not wandering. They were traveling toward the House of Allah. The fajj was wide so the journey was possible; the destination gave the journey its meaning.

The bisāṭ without a destination is just a field. The fijāj without a purpose is just wandering.

Nuh's entire sermon — the pointing to heavens, moon, sun, rain, rivers, and earth — is pointing toward one thing: the recognition of the One who arranged all of this. The journey for which the wide paths were opened is not merely geographical. It is the journey toward the One who laid the carpet.

[PAUSE]

The theological claim embedded in these two verses is this:

*The earth was prepared so that humanity could move through it. And the movement was intended to have a direction — toward the recognition of its Arranger.*

This is the journey Nuh spent 950 years inviting his people to begin. He kept pointing at the bisāṭ. He kept gesturing at the fijāj. He kept saying: you were made to move, and I am telling you toward what.

And they covered their ears.

[PAUSE — major pause]

What does this mean for us?

The wide paths are still there. The fijāj have not narrowed. The bisāṭ is still spread. The invitation Nuh carried has not been rescinded.

The question every person who recites these verses must carry is: *Am I actually moving?* Not busyness — busyness is not the same as traversal. Not activity — activity is not the same as intentional threading through a path with a destination. Am I journeying, in the sense these verses mean? Is my life oriented as a traveler, or have I settled into the bisāṭ as though it were my home rather than my route?

The Arabic word for this life — **الدنيا** (al-dunyā) — means "the near, the lower, the present." The Quran consistently describes it as a pass-through, a waypoint, a road that is covered and then left behind. The bisāṭ is set out. The paths are wide. The traveler is expected to pass through.

The question is not whether you are moving. The question is whether you know where you are going.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

---

### The Architecture

Step back and look at what these two verses do together.

**Ayah 19**: وَٱللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ بِسَاطًا — The gift. The preparation. The carpet laid out for you before you arrived.

**Ayah 20**: لِّتَسْلُكُوا۟ مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا — The purpose. The movement. The wide paths you were meant to thread through.

Gift → Purpose. Preparation → Traversal. Receiving → Moving.

The two verses have a built-in motion. The first is static: something has been laid out. The second is dynamic: now go through it. And the whole of Nuh's sermon shares this shape — he names the gifts, then the gifts implicitly name the Giver, and the recognition of the Giver is itself the beginning of the journey.

The bisāṭ is where you stand. The fijāj are where you walk. But the direction — toward Allah — is what transforms movement into meaning.

### Where the Linguistic and Thematic Insights Converge

The verb **جَعَلَ** showed us that Allah's act here is not creation-from-nothing but *preparation-for-you*. The theme of gratitude confirmed: you can only perceive a bisāṭ as a bisāṭ when you recognize the Host who laid it. Creation without the recognition of the Creator is just furniture — present, used, never truly seen.

The word **بِسَاطًا** showed us that the earth carries a quality of intentional preparation — it is defined by what was done to it. The theme of journey confirmed: a bisāṭ is laid for traversal, not for settling. It is a road, not a home.

The words **سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا** showed us abundance of routes — plural, unnumbered, wide. The theme of purposeful movement confirmed: the abundance is not a sign that direction doesn't matter. It is a mercy that makes the journey possible from wherever you happen to be standing.

**The same insight arrives from two directions:** You were arranged for, and you were made to move. These two truths together constitute something the Quran returns to again and again — the human being as a purposeful journeyer on an earth prepared for that very traversal.

### Questions to Carry

1. The earth has met my foot every morning of my life without once failing. When did I last notice? What would it mean to notice it tomorrow?

2. Am I actually moving — traversing, threading through — or have I confused the comfort of the bisāṭ with the destination?

3. The fijāj are wide and plural, which means I have genuine choices in how I journey. Am I making those choices with intention, or drifting?

4. Nuh pointed to this gift for 950 years and his people covered their ears. What have I been covering my ears to — and why?

5. If the earth was prepared for me before I arrived — if the carpet was laid in anticipation of my coming — what does that say about how anticipated I am by the One who prepared it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah set out the earth like a carpet before you arrived and gave you wide, open paths to walk through — the only question remaining is whether you are actually journeying, and toward what.**

### The Closing Invitation

These two verses are not asking for a grand spiritual breakthrough. They are asking something simpler and far harder: to look down.

The next time you walk outside, notice the ground meeting your foot. Just once, for a single moment — notice it as bisāṭ. As something arranged for you, set out in advance, prepared. Feel the solidity of it. Remember that it has met your foot every morning for however many years you have been alive, without once failing.

And then ask: where am I walking? The paths are wide — they were made wide, deliberately, with your traversal in mind. But threading through a fijāj requires that you enter it with intention. That you move toward something.

Nuh gave his people 950 years of this invitation. He pointed to the cosmos, the rain, the rivers, the ground. He was tireless, tender, and clear.

They covered their ears.

We have been given this recitation instead. The carpet is still spread. The paths are still wide. The invitation has reached us across fourteen centuries of faithful transmission.

What do we do with it?

### Du'ā'

يَا بَاسِطُ — O You who spreads out —

You laid the earth as a bisāṭ before we arrived. You carved wide passes through mountains so we would have room to journey from wherever we stand. You preserved the words of a prophet who spent 950 years pointing us back to what was always right beneath our feet.

Open our eyes to what has been arranged for us.
Make us people who actually move — who thread through the wide paths toward You, and do not mistake the road for the destination.
And when we are tempted to cover our ears as those before us did, remind us of the carpet under our feet, and the One who laid it.
