# The Prayer With No Past Tense

## Introduction: The Last Words of the Longest Life

Let me read you the words first. In Arabic, then slowly in English, and then I want us to do something we almost never do — I want us to sit with *who* is speaking before we touch *what* he says.

> وَقَالَ نُوحٌ رَّبِّ لَا تَذَرْ عَلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ دَيَّارًا
>
> *Wa qāla Nūḥun rabbi lā tadhar ʿalā l-arḍi mina l-kāfirīna dayyārā*
>
> "And Nuh said: My Lord, do not leave upon the earth a single dweller from among the disbelievers."

> إِنَّكَ إِن تَذَرْهُمْ يُضِلُّوا۟ عِبَادَكَ وَلَا يَلِدُوٓا۟ إِلَّا فَاجِرًا كَفَّارًا
>
> *Innaka in tadhar-hum yuḍillū ʿibādaka wa lā yalidū illā fājiran kaffārā*
>
> "Indeed, if You leave them, they will mislead Your servants and will not beget except a wicked, deeply ungrateful one."

> رَّبِّ ٱغْفِرْ لِى وَلِوَٰلِدَىَّ وَلِمَن دَخَلَ بَيْتِىَ مُؤْمِنًا وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتِ وَلَا تَزِدِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا
>
> *Rabbi-ghfir lī wa li-wālidayya wa li-man dakhala baytiya mu'minan wa lil-mu'minīna wa lil-mu'mināti, wa lā tazidi ẓ-ẓālimīna illā tabārā*
>
> "My Lord, forgive me and my parents and whoever enters my house as a believer, and the believing men and the believing women — and do not increase the wrongdoers except in ruin."

### Rebuild the world

Now. Hold those words for a moment, because we cannot understand them until we feel the weight of the man behind them.

Somewhere else in the Qur'an, Allah tells us how long Nuh stood among his people: *a thousand years less fifty* (29:14). Nine hundred and fifty years of calling. Try to make that number real. Not a career. Not a lifetime. **Generations born, grown old, and buried** — while one man kept saying the same words. Earlier in this very surah Nuh describes the texture of those centuries to his Lord: *I called my people night and day* (71:5). *I called them publicly, then I spoke to them privately* (71:9). He tells Allah that when he called them, *they put their fingers in their ears and wrapped themselves in their garments* (71:7) — they covered their ears and pulled their cloaks over their heads so they would not have to see his face or hear his voice.

This is not a man who gave up early. This is the most patient caller in human history. He tried the loud way and the quiet way. He tried mercy. He tried warning. He offered them rain and wealth and gardens and rivers if only they would turn (71:11-12).

And now — at the very end of the surah — *this* is what comes out of his mouth. Not "give them more time." Not "soften their hearts." But: **"My Lord, do not leave a single one of them on the earth."**

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is the question I cannot get past, and I want it to bother you the way it bothers me.

How does the gentlest man become the one who prays for the end of his entire people? How is *that* prayer righteous? And then — in the very same breath, the very next sentence — how does that same heart turn around and open into the widest circle of forgiveness in the whole surah?

There is something hidden in the grammar of these three verses that answers both questions at once. There is a verb here that, in the Arabic language itself, **has no past tense**. There is a word for "a single dweller" that rhymes, deliberately, with a word for "total ruin." And there is an asymmetry in his final prayer that, once you see it, you will never read a du'a the same way again.

Let's go slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: تَذَرْ — The Verb That Only Looks Forward

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because without it the verse stays flat.

In any language, most verbs are *complete* — they can travel through time. "I walk, I walked, I will walk." Past, present, future — the verb stretches across all three. We take this for granted. A verb, we assume, can always look backward.

But there is a small, strange family of Arabic verbs that **cannot look backward**. The Arabs, the classical lexicographers tell us, simply *let the past tense die*. Ibn Manẓūr records it plainly in *Lisān al-ʿArab*: «أماتت العرب ماضي يَذَرُ ويَدَعُ» — *"the Arabs put to death the past tense of yadharu and yadaʿu."* These two verbs of "leaving, abandoning, letting be" survive only in the present and the future and the command. If you want to say "I left it" in the past, you have to borrow a different word entirely — *taraka*. The verb of abandonment was itself, in a sense, abandoned by its own past.

Now look at the word Nuh chooses:

> لَا **تَذَرْ** عَلَى ٱلْأَرْضِ مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ دَيَّارًا
>
> "Do not **leave** upon the earth a single dweller."

*Tadhar.* From that very root — wāw, dhāl, rā' — the root the Leeds corpus confirms for this word, the root that, in the living Arabic of the Qur'an, **points only forward and never back.**

Let's sit with this. Why does it matter that Nuh's verb for "leave" has no past?

Because Nuh's prayer is not about the past. [PAUSE]

Think about what he could have been praying. After nine hundred and fifty years of being mocked, ignored, cloaked-against — a human being would be drowning in the past. "My Lord, look at what they did to me. Look at the centuries. Repay them for the ears they covered." That would be a prayer *backward* — a prayer of grievance, of settling accounts, of the wound.

But the very verb he reaches for cannot go there. **It can only face the future.** And when you read the next verse, you discover that the future is *exactly* and *only* what his prayer is about:

> إِنَّكَ إِن تَذَرْهُمْ يُضِلُّوا۟ عِبَادَكَ
>
> "Indeed, if You leave them, **they will mislead** Your servants."

Notice — not *they have* misled. *They will.* The whole logic is forward-facing. He is not asking God to punish them for yesterday. He is telling God what tomorrow will hold if they remain. The grammar and the argument are the same shape. A verb that cannot look back, attached to a prayer that refuses to look back.

This is what this single word reveals: **Nuh's imprecation is not vengeance.** Vengeance lives in the past tense. Nuh's prayer lives entirely in a verb that has no past — it is concern for what is *coming*, not payment for what is *gone*.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: دَيَّارًا — Not a Single Circling Soul

Now the next word. Hold the two together, because they are a pair:

> لَا تَذَرْ ... مِنَ ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ **دَيَّارًا**

First, the concept. In Arabic there are certain words that exist almost *only inside negation* — words designed to slam a door completely shut. In English we have a faint echo of this: "there wasn't a *living soul* in sight." You would never walk into a full room and say "there is a living soul here." The phrase exists to express *total absence*. *Dayyār* is one of those words. The grammarians and the mufassirūn note that you essentially only encounter it after a negation — it is built to mean *not even one*.

But where does it come from? Here the classical scholars give us two camera angles on the same reality, and we should hold both.

Al-Baghawī, drawing on one reading, traces *dayyār* to the root of **dawr — to circle, to turn, to go round** — the root d-w-r that Leeds confirms. On this reading, *dayyār* is "one who moves about on the earth," one who comes and goes, who *circulates*. "Do not leave on the earth a single soul that still walks and turns and moves."

Ibn Qutayba, as al-Baghawī also reports, traces it to **dār — a dwelling, a home.** On this reading *dayyār* is *nāzilu dār*, "an inhabitant of a house," a settler, a dweller. This is the reading al-Jalālayn gives directly: *nāzilu dār*, and the meaning, he says, is simply *aḥadan* — *anyone*.

Do not choose between these. Let them layer. *Dayyār* is the one who **moves** across the earth *and* the one who **settles** upon it. The wanderer and the homeowner. The nomad and the resident. **Every mode of human presence on the land** — the one passing through and the one staying put — gathered into a single word and then negated.

Here is what is remarkable. Nuh does not say "destroy the disbelievers." He says "do not *leave* a single *dayyār*." The framing is not about killing — it is about **emptiness afterward**. Picture the earth he is asking for: not a battlefield strewn with the fallen, but a land *swept smooth* — no one moving across it, no one dwelling on it, not a single curl of smoke from a single home. A planet returned to silence.

And why such totality? Verse 27 tells us, and it lands like a hammer:

> وَلَا يَلِدُوٓا۟ إِلَّا فَاجِرًا كَفَّارًا
>
> "and they will not beget except a wicked one, **kaffār** — deeply, repeatedly ungrateful."

That word *kaffār* — root k-f-r, confirmed in our morphology — is not the plain *kāfir*. It is the **intensive** form, the *faʿʿāl* pattern, the form Arabic reserves for something done deeply, habitually, to the bone. *Kaffār* is not "a disbeliever." It is "a relentless rejecter of grace." Nuh is saying: the very wombs of this people now produce only this. The corruption has reached the genetic line of the soul. Leave one *dayyār*, one circling, dwelling soul, and from it will come not a mixed harvest but *only* the relentless rejecter.

[PAUSE]

And this is why we must not flinch at the prayer. Nuh did not arrive at this conclusion from rage. He arrived at it from **revelation**. Elsewhere Allah had already told him: *None of your people will believe except those who have already believed* (11:36). The door was shut by God first; Nuh's prayer only names what God had already disclosed. Al-Qurṭubī preserves exactly this from Qatāda: Nuh prayed against them *after Allah revealed to him that none would believe but those who already had.* This is not a man losing his temper. This is a man who has been shown the future and is asking, in grief, that it not be prolonged.

What this word reveals: when Nuh asks for *not a single dweller*, he is not asking for cruelty — he is asking for a **clean line under a chapter God Himself has closed.**

### Element Three: دَيَّارًا and تَبَارًا — Two Words That Rhyme, Two Destinies That Don't

Now I want you to do something with your ears.

Listen to the last word of verse 26: **dayyārā.**
Now listen to the last word of verse 28: **tabārā.**

*Dayyārā... tabārā.*

[PAUSE]

Do you hear it? The Qur'an has bracketed these three verses between two words that **rhyme almost perfectly** — same rhythm, same ending, same closing breath. And yet they are opposites in meaning. *Dayyār* is the living dweller, the soul that moves and settles. *Tabār* — from the root t-b-r (ت-ب-ر), documented in the lexicons as **ruin, perishing, annihilation** — is the end of all dwelling.

The whole passage runs from *dayyār* to *tabār*. From "a single dweller" to "nothing but ruin." The sound carries you from presence to erasure. The two words are twins to the ear and enemies to the heart. This is not accident — this is the architecture of divine speech, where even the *music* of the verse enacts its meaning. You begin with the image of one soul still circling the earth, and the sound itself dissolves, three verses later, into ruin.

Notice the verb that governs *tabār*:

> وَلَا **تَزِدِ** ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا
>
> "and do not **increase** the wrongdoers except in ruin."

*Do not increase them except in ruin.* This is one of the most chilling turns in the Qur'an. He does not say "ruin them." He says "do not let any increase come to them — except an increase *of* ruin." If they gain wealth, let it be ruin. If they gain years, let it be ruin. If they gain children — and remember verse 27, *they beget only the kaffār* — let even that be ruin. Every line of increase that life offers is sealed off, and only one channel of increase is left open: the channel down into *tabār*.

So we have travelled, in sound and in sense, from the verb that only faces forward (*tadhar*), through the single circling soul (*dayyār*), to the only future left for those who chose rejection (*tabār*).

[PAUSE]

The linguistic construction has shown us this much: that Nuh's prayer is forward-facing, total, and divinely-informed — and that the very *sound* of the passage carries us from a living, moving humanity to its erasure. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about despair, about justice, and about the strange, enormous mercy hidden in the last verse.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Despair That Is Not a Sin

Let me start where you live, not where Nuh lived.

You have, at some point, given up on a person. Maybe a parent you could never reach. Maybe a sibling spiralling. Maybe yourself, on a bad night. And you know the guilt that comes with giving up — because we are taught, rightly, that despair is dangerous. *Do not despair of the mercy of Allah* (39:53). So when the heart finally says "this will not change," a second voice immediately says: *you are not allowed to feel that. Hope is obligatory.*

So here is the knot, and Nuh sits right in the center of it. **The most patient prophet in history prayed a prayer of total finality.** "Do not leave a single one." If despair were always a sin, this verse could not exist in the Qur'an as a *praiseworthy* act. And yet Allah preserves it, honours it, and — verse after verse — *answers it*.

How do we resolve this?

The resolution is in one distinction, and it is the whole theme. There is a despair that comes from **the smallness of your own hope**, and there is a clarity that comes from **knowing what God has already decided.** The first is a sin. The second is *submission*.

Nuh did not give up because he was tired — though God knows he had earned tiredness over nine and a half centuries. He "gave up" only *after* Allah told him: *none will believe except those who already have* (11:36). His despair was not a failure of hope. It was an *agreement with God's own disclosure*. He stopped hoping for their faith at the exact moment God told him there was no more faith to hope for — and not a moment before.

Stay with this, because it reorders something inside us. The question is never "am I allowed to stop hoping?" The question is "**on what is my hopelessness based?**" Is it based on my exhaustion, my mood, my wound, my limited reading of a situation? Then it is forbidden — because my reading is small and God's mercy is vast. But is it based on something I genuinely know to be true and final? Then accepting it is not despair — it is *realism in the hands of God.*

For most of us, almost always, we are in the first case. We have not been given *waḥy*. We do not know the end of anyone's story. The person we have written off may be one night of grief away from turning back. **We are not Nuh, and we have not been told what Nuh was told.** Which means the door we are tempted to shut, God has very likely left open.

What does this change about tomorrow? It means that when you feel the pull to close the file on a human soul — your own included — you should ask one honest question: *Has God actually told me this is finished, or am I just tired?* And nine times out of ten, the answer will send you back to the prayer you almost stopped making.

### Theme Two: The Heart That Calls for Ruin and Forgiveness in One Breath

Now the part that breaks me open every time.

Look at the structure of Nuh's final words — really look at it. He has just called for the removal of every disbeliever on earth. The next thing out of his mouth is verse 28. And watch how *long* it is, how it *expands*:

> رَّبِّ ٱغْفِرْ
> **لِى** — for me
> **وَلِوَٰلِدَىَّ** — and for my two parents
> **وَلِمَن دَخَلَ بَيْتِىَ مُؤْمِنًا** — and for whoever entered my house as a believer
> **وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ** — and for the believing men
> **وَلِلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتِ** — and for the believing women

Count the circles. Himself. His parents. His household — but conditioned, precisely, on faith: *whoever entered my house as a believer.* Then outward past his household, past his lifetime, to **all believing men and all believing women** — a category with no border in space or time. He is praying for believers who would not be born for thousands of years. He is praying, in this moment, *for us.*

Now here is the asymmetry, and it is everything. Read the very next clause, and feel how it *contracts*:

> وَلَا تَزِدِ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا
>
> "and do not increase the wrongdoers except in ruin."

Look at what just happened. For the believers: **five expanding clauses**, names piling on names, circle widening past circle, mercy enumerated lovingly, item by item. For the wrongdoers: **one short, terse line.** No names. No detail. No lingering. Just: *only ruin.*

This is the read-the-absence moment of the whole passage. **Where Allah lets Nuh be expansive, he is expansive about mercy. Where the subject is destruction, the language goes cold and brief.** The heart of the believer, even at the very edge of calling down judgment, *dwells* in forgiveness and merely *passes through* condemnation. Mercy is where he wants to linger; ruin is something he names quickly and leaves.

Let me bring this home to you. We tend to do the exact opposite. When we have been wronged, our hearts compose *long* prayers against the one who hurt us — detailed, itemized, lovingly cruel. And our prayers for the people we love come out short, generic, rushed: "Allah forgive the believers," tossed off at the end. Nuh inverts our instinct entirely. His list of mercy is long and tender and specific. His word of judgment is a single syllable of ruin, *tabār*, and then silence.

And notice *who* he begins with. Before his parents, before his household, before the believers of all time — **rabbi-ghfir lī.** "My Lord, forgive *me.*" The prophet who preached for 950 years, who endured what no one has endured, who is about to be vindicated by a flood that reshapes the earth — *he asks for forgiveness for himself first.* No part of him stands on the success of his mission as a credit. At the summit of vindication, he is a servant asking to be forgiven.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing, and it ties the two themes together. Look again at the household: *whoever entered my house **as a believer.*** Nuh does not pray blanket forgiveness over everyone under his roof. We know why — his own son drowned outside the ark, refusing to board (11:42-43). His own wife was among those who betrayed (66:10). Nuh has learned, in the most personal way a human can, that **the line is not blood; the line is faith.** So even his prayer for his house is conditioned: *the one who entered believing.* The same clarity that let him call for the removal of the *kāfirīn* makes him precise even in mercy — he asks forgiveness for the believing soul wherever it is, and withholds the claim of mere relation.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is a discipline you can practice in your very next du'a. Notice the length of your lists. Are you more detailed in mercy or in grievance? When you raise your hands, let the part that asks for forgiveness — for yourself, your parents, the believers living and unborn — be the *long* part. Let any part that names harm be brief, named, and released. Build your prayers in the proportions of Nuh: an ocean of mercy, a single drop of judgment, and *yourself first* at the door of forgiveness.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Now Revealed

Step back and see the whole shape, because now the pieces lock together.

These three verses are built as a single arc, bracketed by two rhyming words that are opposites:

- It opens on **دَيَّارًا — dayyārā** — the single living, circling, dwelling soul.
- It closes on **تَبَارًا — tabārā** — total ruin.

Between those two twinned sounds, the passage moves through a precise logic:

1. A verb of leaving that **has no past tense** (*tadhar*) — so the entire prayer faces only forward.
2. A future justification — *if You leave them they will mislead, and beget only the kaffār* — built entirely in the tense the verb demands.
3. A final prayer that **expands into five circles of mercy** and **contracts into one line of ruin** — the asymmetry that reveals the believer's heart.

The forward-facing verb, the all-encompassing *dayyār*, the cold brevity of *tabār*, and the lavish breadth of the forgiveness — they are not four observations. They are **one truth seen from four sides:** that true justice, in the hand of a servant of God, is never vengeance. It looks forward, not back. It rests on what God has disclosed, not on personal wound. And it is always, *always* dwarfed by the mercy in the same heart.

### Bringing It Together

The grammar told us his imprecation was not revenge — the verb cannot look back.
The themes told us his despair was not a sin — it rested on God's own word.
And the architecture told us that even his harshest prayer was wrapped inside the widest mercy he could reach for.

The man who prayed for the emptiest earth in human history prayed, in the same breath, for every believer who would ever live. **Both of those are the same heart.** That is the lesson the language was protecting all along.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I give up on someone, is it because God has shown me the end — or because I am simply tired and wounded?
2. If I wrote out my du'a as Nuh's is written, which list would be longer: my mercy or my grievance?
3. Do I, like Nuh, ask forgiveness for *myself first* — or do I quietly assume my own standing is secure?
4. Whom in my life have I made a *kāfir* in my heart, closing a door that God has, in fact, left open?
5. Is there a "house" I am praying over by blood, when the Qur'an asks me to pray over it by *faith*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Even at the moment a servant of God calls for the end of those who reject Him, his prayer faces forward not backward, rests on God's word not his own wound, and is wrapped — always — inside a mercy far larger than the judgment.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not be Nuh. You will almost never be given the certainty that lets a heart close a door justly. So take from him not the imprecation but the *proportion*: a heart that lingers in mercy and only passes through judgment, that asks forgiveness for itself first and for the believers of all time after, and that — when it must finally let something go — lets it go forward, with no past tense, no wound, no revenge. Let your prayers be shaped like his.

### A Brief Du'a

> O Allah, You who taught Nuh to ask forgiveness for himself before all others — *rabbi-ghfir lī wa li-wālidayya* — forgive me, and my parents, and every believing soul living and yet to be born. Make the part of my heart that asks for mercy vast, and the part that asks for ruin small and rare. And do not let me close a door on any soul that You, in Your knowledge, have left open. Āmīn.
