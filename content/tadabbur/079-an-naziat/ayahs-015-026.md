# Would You Be Willing to Become Pure?

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first to the sound of it, before we ever touch the meaning.

> **هَلْ أَتَىٰكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰ ۝ إِذْ نَادَىٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ بِٱلْوَادِ ٱلْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى**
>
> *Hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā. Idh nādāhu Rabbuhu bil-wādil-muqaddasi Ṭuwā.*
>
> "Has the story of Musa reached you? When his Lord called him in the sacred valley of Tuwa…"

And it keeps going, and every single line lands on the same open sound:

*Mūsā… Ṭuwā… ṭaghā… tazakkā… takhshā… al-kubrā… ʿaṣā… yasʿā… nādā… al-aʿlā… al-ūlā… yakhshā.*

Twelve verses. One breath. The whole passage exhales on the long, open *ā* — the sound a human being makes when they are wounded, when they sigh, when they finally let something go. We will come back to this. Hold onto it.

**Let me rebuild the room you are standing in.**

This is Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ is being mocked in the streets of his own city. The people who watched him grow up — who called him *al-Amīn*, the trustworthy — now spit accusations. He is one man. The power is entirely on the other side: the chiefs of Quraysh, the wealth, the social weight, the sneering certainty that this will all blow over.

And into that exact moment, Allah does not send a battle plan. He does not send reinforcements. He sends a **story**. "Has the story of Musa reached you?"

Why a story? Because the chiefs of Mecca thought they were strong. So Allah reaches back across the centuries and shows His Messenger a tyrant mightier than any of them — a man who didn't just *have* power but who stood in front of a nation and said, *I am your lord, the most high* — and shows him exactly how that strength ended. Al-Qurtubi names this plainly: this is **tasliya**, consolation. *Pharaoh was mightier than the disbelievers of your age — and then We seized him. So too these.*

**Here is the hook.** Allah is about to send one of the gentlest, most tender invitations in the whole Quran. And He is going to send it to a man the tradition holds up as the very archetype of the tyrant — mightier, al-Qurtubi says, than any disbeliever of the Prophet's own age. The cruelty of Pharaoh and the softness of the words that reach him are placed side by side on purpose. [PAUSE]

And there is something hidden in this passage about *who the story is even for* — a single word at the very end that quietly disqualifies most people from receiving it. We'll get there. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ṭaghā* — The Water That Forgot Its Banks

Let me teach you a word before we use it. In Arabic, when a river swells past its edges in a flood — when water rises beyond the level it was meant to hold and pours destruction over everything — the verb the Arabs reach for is from the root **ṭ-gh-y**. *Ṭaghā al-māʾ* — "the water transgressed." It overflowed. It exceeded the boundary set for it.

This is a *physical* picture. Water has a natural level. A vessel has a capacity. A riverbed has banks. And *ṭughyān* is what happens when the thing inside refuses the limit drawn around it and spills out, ruining everything downstream.

Now hear the ayah:

> **ٱذْهَبْ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ**
>
> "Go to Pharaoh — indeed, *ṭaghā*."

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say "he disbelieved." He did not say "he is cruel," or "he is unjust," though all of that is true. He named the **disease beneath the symptoms**: this man overflowed his banks. He was given a level — the level of a created, dependent, mortal being — and he refused it. He rose past it. And like floodwater, everything downstream of him drowned: the Israelites enslaved, the infants slaughtered, a whole nation held underwater.

And the classical commentators reach for the very same boundary-language to explain the word. Al-Jalalayn glosses *ṭaghā* in a single phrase — *tajāwaza al-ḥadd*, "he crossed the limit" — and al-Muyassar with *afraṭa*, "he ran to excess." They fill in *what* he overflowed into — disbelief, defiance — but the picture is theirs too: a bound was set, and a man poured past it.

Stay with this image, because the Quran is not really talking about water. It never was. The water is the stepping stone. The destination is a truth about **the human soul**: that every one of us is a vessel with a level, a created thing with a boundary, and the entire spiritual drama of a life is whether we will stay within our banks — *or rise.*

Here is what is remarkable. The passage ends, twelve verses later, with Pharaoh's own voice:

> **أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ** — "I am your lord, *the most high*."

*Al-aʿlā.* The highest. Look at what *ṭughyān* actually is, traced to its end: it is the swollen water reaching for the *highest* point, the ego rising until it claims the one position that belongs to no creature. Allah said at the start, "he overflowed." Pharaoh, at the climax, *tells you exactly how high the water rose.* The diagnosis in verse 17 and the confession in verse 24 are the same flood, photographed at its source and at its crest. [PAUSE]

That is what this single word reveals: **transgression is not first an act. It is a level.** The deeds come downstream.

### Element Two: *Hal laka ilā an tazakkā* — The Tyrant Approached With a Question

Now I want to slow everything down, because what comes next should not be possible.

Allah has just called Pharaoh a transgressor, a flood, a man drowning nations. And now He instructs Musa in the *exact words* to use. Brace yourself for the harshness this monster deserves. Here is what Allah sends:

> **فَقُلْ هَل لَّكَ إِلَىٰٓ أَن تَزَكَّىٰ**
>
> *Fa-qul: hal laka ilā an tazakkā.*
>
> "So say: Would you be willing to purify yourself?"

Let me teach you the structure, because it is everything. **هَل لَّكَ** — *hal laka* — is, in Arabic, the syntax of a gentle offer. It is not a command. It is not "purify yourself!" It is closer to: *"Is there, for you, an inclination toward…?" "Would you care to…?" "Is this something you might want?"* It is the phrasing you use when you are placing a door in front of someone and leaving them entirely free to walk through it. It hands the dignity of the choice back to the listener.

To **the archetype of the tyrant,** Allah sends a question. An invitation. A door held open with both hands.

And this is not an isolated reading. In Surah Ṭā Hā, describing this very mission, Allah commands Musa and Harun: **فَقُولَا لَهُۥ قَوْلًا لَّيِّنًا** — "speak to him a *soft* word" (20:44). The two passages are the same instruction from two angles. The Lord of the worlds, addressing the man who would soon say "I am your lord the most high," chooses *softness*.

Sit with what this does to you. [PAUSE]

If Allah commands gentleness toward *Pharaoh* — if the door is held open even for *him* — then on what grounds do you and I slam it shut on the people in our lives we have privately decided are beyond saving? The brother who left the prayer. The relative who mocks the dīn. The version of *ourselves* we've written off. The methodology of Heaven, demonstrated on the hardest case imaginable, is: *hold the door open. Ask. Invite. Leave them free.*

And notice *where* this invitation is born. The call that carries it came, verse 16, *bil-wādil-muqaddas* — in the valley al-Tabari and al-Muyassar gloss as *al-muṭahhar al-mubārak*, "the purified and blessed." Al-Jalalayn adds that the valley bears a name, *Ṭuwā*, one the early reciters passed down two ways — with the final *tanwīn* and without it — and al-Tabari records that the commentators themselves differed over what that name signifies. But however they read it, the point holds: the command to purify — *tazakkā* — is handed to Musa on ground that has *already* been made pure. The medicine is dispatched from the very condition it is meant to produce.

Now look at the word inside the invitation: **تَزَكَّىٰ** — *tazakkā.* This is a verb of a particular shape — what grammarians call Form V, *tafaʿʿala*. Two things about this form matter enormously here.

First, the root is **z-k-w**, which carries two married meanings at once: *purification* and *growth*. The same root gives us *zakāt* — the wealth you give away that somehow makes the rest *grow*. To be *zakī* is to be both cleansed *and* increased. Purity, in the Quranic imagination, is never subtraction alone — it is the pruning that produces fruit.

And the mufassirūn make the *content* of this purity startlingly specific. Al-Muyassar reads *tazakkā* as *to cleanse your self of its defects and adorn it with faith*; al-Jalalayn, noting the recitation that doubles the *zāy*, glosses it as *to purify yourself of shirk by bearing witness that there is no god but Allah.* So the door Musa is told to open is not vague self-betterment. It is the precise invitation to wash off the one stain of setting up rivals to God — and to be clothed in *tawḥīd* instead. Hold that, because it sharpens everything that follows: the exact filth Pharaoh is asked to rinse away is the thing he will, five verses later, wrap himself in — *I am your lord, the most high.*

Second — and feel the weight of this — Form V is **reflexive**. The action turns back on the one who does it. *Tazakkā* does not mean "be purified by me." It means **"purify your own self."** Allah, through Musa, is not offering to scrub Pharaoh clean from the outside. He is offering Pharaoh the chance to *do it himself* — to participate, to turn the key from the inside.

This is staggering when you hold it next to *ṭaghā.* The flood rose *by itself* — *ṭughyān* is the self swelling past its banks under its own pressure. And the cure is offered in the *same reflexive grammar*: *tazakkā* — bring your *own* self back down to its level. The disease was self-inflicted; the door to the cure is self-opened. Allah honors Pharaoh's agency in the sickness *and* in the medicine. [PAUSE]

What this reveals: **Allah does not purify people over their own heads.** He offers. He guides. But the turning of the key — *tazakkā* — He leaves in the hand of the human being. Even Pharaoh's.

### Element Three: The Two Callings and the Echo of *Rabb*

Now let me show you the architecture that holds this whole passage together — and you could read it a hundred times and not see it until you listen for one repeated verb.

The verb is **نَادَى** — *nādā* — Form III, "to call out to someone." It appears twice. Watch where.

The *first* call, verse 16:

> **إِذْ نَادَىٰهُ رَبُّهُ** — "When his **Lord called** him…"

Allah calls Musa. In a sacred valley. By night. The call that lifts a shepherd into a prophet. A call *downward* — from the Lord to His servant — that *raises* the servant up.

The *second* call, verse 23 — and now it is Pharaoh:

> **فَحَشَرَ فَنَادَىٰ** — "So he gathered [the people] and **called out**…"

> **فَقَالَ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ** — "saying, 'I am your **lord**, the most high.'"

Two callings. Do you feel the mirror? Allah *calls* Musa to His service — and Musa rises. Pharaoh *calls* his people to *himself* — and Pharaoh is about to fall. One voice calls a man toward the Lord; the other voice calls a nation toward an impostor.

And now the deepest echo. Listen to the word *Rabb* — Lord — trace its way through the passage.

- Verse 16: **رَبُّهُ** — *Rabbuhu*, "**His** Lord" called Musa.
- Verse 19: Musa is to invite Pharaoh — **وَأَهْدِيَكَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ** — "that I guide you to **your** Lord."
- Verse 24: Pharaoh answers — **أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ** — "I am **your** lord."

Do you see the theft? Musa says: *let me guide you to* **rabbika** — *your* Lord, the One above you who made you. And Pharaoh's reply is to *seize the very word* and turn it on his people: *I* am **rabbukum** — *your* lord. He hears the offer of guidance *toward* the Rabb, and he responds by *appointing himself* the Rabb. [PAUSE]

The invitation in verse 19 was: *come up toward your Lord and feel awe.* Pharaoh's counter-move in verse 24 was: *no — everyone come up toward me.* The same preposition of ascent, *ilā* (toward), runs under both: Musa says ascend *toward* your Lord; Pharaoh demands the ascent terminate at himself.

This is the whole disease of *ṭughyān* in one stolen word. The transgressor does not merely refuse his level — he tries to **occupy God's.** He hears "your Lord" and cannot tolerate that the word points anywhere but at him.

---

**Let me gather what the words have shown us.** A flood that forgot its banks. A door held open with a question, even to a tyrant. A purity you must turn the key on yourself. Two callings — one that raises, one that ruins. And a single word, *Rabb*, that one man offered to point upward and another man stole to point at himself.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The "I Am the Most High" That Lives in Smaller Letters

Let me start where you actually live, not in ancient Egypt.

You have never stood before a nation and declared yourself a god. Good. But notice what happens inside you in the small moments. Someone corrects you in a meeting and your face goes hot — not because they're wrong, but because *they* corrected *you*. Someone merges into your lane and a flash of pure fury, disproportionate, animal, rises up. Your sibling gets the praise at the family gathering and something sours in your chest that you would be ashamed to name out loud.

What is that? Trace it honestly. It is a small voice — very quiet, very fast — that says: *I should be higher than this. I should not be subject to correction, to inconvenience, to being second.* It is *al-aʿlā* — "the most high" — whispered in letters too small to hear.

Pharaoh is not a monster from another species. Pharaoh is what happens when that whisper is given *unlimited power and zero resistance for forty years.* The Quran shows you the flood at full height precisely so you can recognize the trickle at its source — in yourself. *Ṭughyān* begins as a single drop over the line. The atheism of Pharaoh and the irritation of being corrected are the *same root*, **ṭ-gh-y**, at different volumes.

And here is the movement that should reorder how you see your own anger. The opposite of Pharaoh's "I am the most high" is not "I am the lowest." It is *tazakkā* — return to your **level**. Not self-loathing. Self-*correction* back to your true size: a creature, dependent, loved, in awe. The vessel holding exactly what it was made to hold. [PAUSE]

The cure was never to crush yourself. Floodwater that drops back to its level isn't destroyed — it becomes the river again. It becomes useful. It nourishes. The same self that swelled into transgression, brought back to its banks, becomes the servant who irrigates everything around them.

**What does this change tomorrow?** The next time the heat rises in your chest — the correction, the merge, the unfair praise — name it precisely: *that is the water testing its banks.* Don't hate yourself for it. Just say, quietly, the reflexive verb: *tazakkā* — come back down to your level. Five seconds. It is the entire difference between Musa and Pharaoh, rehearsed in miniature, many times a day.

### Theme Two: He Saw the Greatest Sign — And It Did Nothing

Now I want to confront something that should genuinely disturb you.

> **فَأَرَىٰهُ ٱلْـَٔايَةَ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ ۝ فَكَذَّبَ وَعَصَىٰ**
>
> "So Musa showed him **the greatest sign.** But he denied it and disobeyed."

*Al-āya al-kubrā.* Not *a* sign. **The greatest** sign. The staff becomes a living serpent. The hand draws out radiant white. Reality itself bends in front of Pharaoh's open eyes — and the very next word is **fa-kadhdhaba**: he cried that it was a lie.

We tell ourselves a comfortable story: *if only I had proof. If I could just see a miracle, I would believe completely, I would change everything.* The Quran demolishes this fantasy in two verses. Pharaoh got the proof. He got *the greatest proof in the visible world.* And it changed **nothing**, because the problem was never a shortage of evidence in his *eyes.* The problem was *ṭughyān* in his *heart.* [PAUSE]

A swollen heart can watch a serpent rise from a staff and call it a magic trick. Information enters the eyes. It does not enter the soul. *Fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā* — and then, *thumma adbara yasʿā* — "then he turned his back, *striving hard.*" Look at that. He didn't just walk away. The verb *yasʿā* is exertion, hustle, effort. He turned his back and *worked at it* — gathered his people, built his counter-narrative, organized his denial into a project. The mufassirūn split on what exactly the striving *was*: al-Muyassar reads it as exertion *to oppose Musa*, and al-Jalalayn as *spreading corruption through the land* — *yasʿā fī al-arḍ bil-fasād.* Either way the motion is identical: energetic flight from the truth, dressed up as work. The transgressor doesn't drift from the truth. He *labors* away from it.

So if the greatest sign couldn't open Pharaoh's heart, what could? The passage already told us, two verses earlier. The invitation was: *"that I guide you to your Lord, **fa-takhshā** — so that you would stand in awe."*

**Khashya.** Awe. The trembling of a heart that grasps Who it stands before. *That* was the missing ingredient — not more evidence, but the soft, fearing, receptive heart that lets evidence *land.* Pharaoh was offered awe (*fa-takhshā*) and chose the flood instead.

And now — the word I promised you at the very beginning. The single word at the end that quietly decides who this whole story is even *for.*

> **إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً لِّمَن يَخْشَىٰ**
>
> "Indeed, in that is a lesson **for whoever stands in awe** — *li-man yakhshā*."

Feel the asymmetry. Pharaoh was *invited* to *khashya* — *fa-takhshā* — and refused. And the entire story, twelve verses of one of the mightiest lessons in the Quran, is now handed *only* to **man yakhshā** — the one who *already* trembles. [PAUSE]

The story is wasted on the hard heart. Pharaoh stood inside the greatest sign and took nothing. The story only *yields* its treasure to a heart soft enough to receive it. Awe is not the *reward* at the end of understanding — **awe is the organ you understand with.** No *khashya*, no *ʿibra.* You can read this passage with a swollen heart and walk away with a fact about ancient Egypt. You read it with a trembling heart and walk away changed.

Now hear that word **ʿibra.** Its root is **ʿ-b-r** — *to cross over.* The same root gives *ʿubūr*, the crossing of a river; the same root names the *interpretation of a dream* — *taʿbīr* — because to interpret is to *cross over* from the image to the meaning. An *ʿibra* is not a "lesson" you memorize. It is a **crossing-place** — a ford in the river — where you pass from the *event you can see* to the *truth you cannot.* From the drowned king to your own swelling heart. From the staff-serpent to the trembling that lets you believe. [PAUSE]

And who can make that crossing? Only *man yakhshā.* The one in awe. The fearing heart is the only foot that finds the ford. The hard heart stands on the bank forever, looking at the same river, and never gets across.

**What does this change tomorrow?** Stop waiting for a sign. You will not be saved by more evidence; Pharaoh had the most. Start tending the *organ that receives.* Before you open the Qur'an, before you stand in prayer, take ten seconds to soften — to remember *Whose* words these are, *Who* you are standing before. Ask for *khashya* the way you'd ask for eyesight before reading. Because without it, the greatest signs in existence will pass right through you, and with it, even a small ayah becomes a crossing-place into a different life.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now That We Can See It

Read the passage as a single shape and it falls into a perfect mirror, hinged in the middle on the choice of one man:

- **The call that raises** (v.16): Allah *calls* Musa — *nādā* — and a shepherd becomes a prophet.
- **The open door** (v.18–19): the invitation — *would you be willing to purify yourself,* and be guided *to your Lord,* so that *you would feel awe.* Three gifts offered: purity, guidance, awe.
- **THE HINGE** (v.20–21): the greatest sign is shown. *Fa-kadhdhaba wa-ʿaṣā.* He chose the flood.
- **The descent** (v.22–23): he turns his back, *labors* away, gathers, and *calls* — *nādā* — but now the call drags a nation down with him.
- **The stolen word** (v.24): *I am your lord* — *rabbukum* — the very word Musa offered to point him upward, seized to point at himself.
- **The seizure** (v.25): *fa-akhadhahu-llāh* — Allah took him, with *nakāl*, exemplary punishment, *nakāl al-ākhira wal-ūlā.* The commentators read that closing phrase two ways, and both are worth carrying. Al-Muyassar takes it as punishment *of the Hereafter and of this first life* — drowned in the world, and worse beyond it. Al-Tabari and al-Jalalayn read *al-ākhira* and *al-ūlā* instead as his two *words*: *al-ūlā*, his first claim to godhood years earlier — *"I know of no god for you other than me"* — and *al-ākhira*, his last — *"I am your lord, the most high."* Al-Jalalayn notes that between those two sentences lay **forty years.** Forty years of invitation, of signs, of the door held open — and when the seizure came, it answered not one outburst but the whole slow arc of a life spent climbing toward the place that is God's alone.
- **The crossing** (v.26): and the whole thing becomes an *ʿibra* — a ford — *for whoever stands in awe.*

Two callings frame it. Two uses of the word *Rabb* hinge it. And the same *khashya* that was *offered* in verse 19 is what *qualifies* you to receive the lesson in verse 26. The passage tests you with the exact thing it describes.

### Two Directions, One Insight

The words showed us a flood that forgot its banks, and a cure written in reflexive grammar: *bring your own self back down.* The themes showed us that the swelling lives in our small irritations, and that the trembling heart — not the seeing eye — is what crosses over into meaning.

They are the same truth from two sides: **the self that rises cannot receive, and the self that bows cannot help but receive.** Pharaoh rose and went blind to a serpent of light. The one who fears bows, and an ancient story becomes a door into his own chest.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, specifically, is the water testing its banks in me this week — which small irritation is really *al-aʿlā* whispered in letters too small to hear?
2. Who have I quietly decided is beyond the door — and would I still hold it shut if I remembered the door was held open for Pharaoh?
3. Do I keep waiting for a "sign," when the thing I actually lack is *khashya* — the softness that would let the signs I already have finally land?
4. When I read the Qur'an, do I come as *man yakhshā* — ready to cross over — or as a tourist on the bank, collecting facts and never getting wet?
5. Is the key of *tazakkā* — the one Allah leaves in *my* hand — turning, or rusting?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The same awe that Pharaoh was *offered and refused* is the one organ that lets a human being *cross over* from a story they merely hear into a life they actually change.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not become Pharaoh by accident — but you can drift toward his *root* a hundred small times a day, every time the self swells past its banks and refuses to come back down. The invitation of this passage is not to fear becoming a tyrant. It is to keep your hand on the key. To soften the heart *on purpose,* before it hardens by default. To stop standing on the bank waiting for a bigger sign, and to wade, trembling, into the crossing that is already in front of you.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who held the door open even for the one who said "I am the most high" — hold it open for me, and give me the humility to walk through it. Do not let me stand inside Your signs and take nothing. Soften my heart with khashya until it can cross over, and bring my swelling self gently back to its level, that I may be of the water that nourishes and not the flood that drowns.* Ameen.
