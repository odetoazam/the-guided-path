# When Does the Ship Drop Anchor?
### A Reflection on Surah An-Nazi'at, 42–46

---

## INTRODUCTION — The Question Behind the Question

Let me give you the words first, in three forms, before we go anywhere near what is hidden inside them.

The Arabic:

> [ayah:79:42] يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلسَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَىٰهَا
> [ayah:79:43] فِيمَ أَنتَ مِن ذِكْرَىٰهَآ
> [ayah:79:44] إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنتَهَىٰهَآ
> [ayah:79:45] إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرُ مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا
> [ayah:79:46] كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا

The transliteration, so the sound is in your mouth:

> *Yas'alūnaka ʿani s-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā.*
> *Fīma anta min dhikrāhā.*
> *Ilā rabbika muntahāhā.*
> *Innamā anta mundhiru man yakhshāhā.*
> *Ka'annahum yawma yarawnahā lam yalbathū illā ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā.*

And the meaning, in plain English:

> "They ask you about the Hour — when is its arrival?
> In what are you, that you should mention it?
> To your Lord is its final end.
> You are only a warner for whoever fears it.
> On the Day they see it, it will be as though they had lingered no longer than an evening, or its morning."

[PAUSE]

### Rebuild the world

Picture Makkah. The surah you are standing at the end of has been a storm. It opened with oaths so violent the early listeners would have flinched — *by those who tear out, drowning the soul; by those who draw out gently* — angels at the deathbed. Then a sudden cut to Musa walking into the court of the most powerful man on earth, the man who said *anā rabbukum al-aʿlā*, "I am your lord, the highest." Then a sweep upward: who built the sky, who raised its ceiling, who darkened its night and brought out its morning. Then down to the earth spread out, the water and pasture brought from it — and the mountains. *Wa-l-jibāla arsāhā.* "And the mountains — He anchored them."

Hold onto that word. *Arsāhā.* He anchored them. We will come back to it, and when we do, the floor is going to move under you.

All of this — every oath, every scene, every mountain — is one sustained argument. The argument is: *the One who did all of this can raise you again, and there is a Day coming when He will.*

And how does the audience answer an argument that monumental?

With a smirk. With a single word disguised as a question.

*Mata?* When?

That is the climate these five verses land into. Not honest inquiry. The classical commentators are direct about it — al-Jalālayn names them plainly as *kuffār Makka*, the deniers of Makkah, and al-Qurtubī preserves a tradition on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās that they asked *istihzāʾan* — mockingly. *"When is this Hour of yours? Give us a date. Put it on the calendar."* It is the oldest trick there is: when you cannot refute a claim, demand a detail it was never about, and treat the silence as a victory.

### The Hook

Now here is what should keep you in your seat.

Allah does not answer the question.

He does something stranger and far more devastating. He takes the question — *when?* — and He turns it around in His hand, and He hands it back pointed at the questioner's own heart. He answers a question about a *date* with an answer about a *self*. And buried inside the very word they used to mock — the word for the Hour's "arrival" — is an image so precise that once you see it, you will never hear this verse the same way again.

They thought they were asking about a clock.

Allah answers them with a ship.

Let us go and find it.

## PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مُرْسَىٰهَا — The Ship That Drops Anchor

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning, because without it this word is just a sound.

In Arabic, almost every word grows from a three-letter root, like a seed. From that one seed, the language grows a whole tree of related words — and crucially, the original, physical, concrete meaning of the seed never fully leaves. It clings to every branch. So when Allah chooses a word, He is not only choosing its dictionary definition; He is choosing everything the root has ever meant. The native ear hears the whole tree at once.

Keep that in mind, and now look at the last word of verse 42:

> أَيَّانَ مُرْسَىٰهَا
> *ayyāna mursāhā* — "when is its *mursā*?"

Translations render *mursāhā* as "its arrival," "its coming to pass," "its occurrence." Perfectly fine. But notice — that smooth English hides the seed.

The root is **r-s-w** (ر-س-و). And the documented, foundational meaning of this root in the classical lexicons is not "to happen" or "to occur."

It is **to anchor. To moor. To come to a standstill and stay there.**

[PAUSE]

It is the word for a ship.

Listen to al-Farrāʾ, the early grammarian, quoted directly by al-Qurtubī in his commentary on this very verse. He says: *rusūhā: qiyāmuhā ka-rusū al-safīna* — "its *rusū* means its standing-fast, like the **anchoring of a ship**." This is not a poetic flourish I am importing. This is the root's own meaning, recorded by the grammarians, sitting in the tafsīr.

And watch — even the most stripped-down commentary keeps the "standing" alive. Al-Jalālayn, famous for compressing each phrase into the fewest possible words, does not gloss *mursāhā* with a single verb. It reaches for two: *matā wuqūʿuhā wa-qiyāmuhā* — "when is its *happening* and its *standing-fast*." That second word, *qiyām*, is the same one al-Farrāʾ used for the ship. The plainest tafsīr in the tradition could not say "occurrence" and leave it there; it had to add the *standing*. The anchoring is not something a reader has to dig for. It is sitting right on the surface of the word, refusing to be smoothed away.

So sit with what they actually asked.

A ship is out on the open sea. It is moving, it is somewhere out beyond the horizon, you cannot see it yet but it is coming in. And the question the people of Makkah threw at the Prophet ﷺ — the question dressed up as mockery — was, in its deepest grammar:

***"When does it drop anchor?"***

The Hour is not a date on a calendar. The Hour is a vessel already underway, already crossing the water toward the harbor that is your life, and the only thing in question is the moment its anchor chain finally runs out and it comes to rest — *here.*

Notice what that image does that the word "arrival" could never do. "Arrival" is neutral; a letter arrives, a guest arrives. But a ship dropping anchor is **the end of a voyage that was always going to end.** The ship was never not coming. Its motion was never in doubt. The deniers asked "when will it anchor?" as if the answer might be *never* — and the very word they used betrayed them, because you do not ask the anchoring-time of a ship that isn't sailing.

[PAUSE]

Now I promised you the floor would move. Here it is.

Go back twelve verses, to verse 32 of this same surah. Allah, listing the proofs of His power, says:

> وَٱلْجِبَالَ أَرْسَىٰهَا
> *wa-l-jibāla **arsāhā*** — "and the mountains — He anchored them."

The same root. **R-s-w.** The same Form. (This is what grammarians call Form IV — *afʿala* — the form that takes a root and makes it *do* something, makes it firm, drives it home; *arsā* means "He made [them] anchor, He fixed [them] fast.") The mountains, Allah says, *I anchored.* I dropped them into the earth like anchors so the ground would hold still beneath your feet. They are the most permanent, immovable, unshakeable thing you know. When you want a word for *forever*, you point at a mountain.

And then, twelve verses later, the deniers ask about the *mursā* — the anchoring — of the Hour.

Do you feel it? The same hand that **anchored** the mountains so firmly that you built your whole sense of "permanent" on them — that same hand holds the anchor chain of the Hour. The Hour is as certain to drop anchor in your harbor as the mountains were certain to be driven into the earth. The deniers picked up Allah's own word — *arsā*, the mountains stand fast — and Allah let it ring back at them: *mursāhā*, and so will the Hour.

The mountains were anchored down. The Hour is sailing in. The same verb. The same certainty. The same Hand.

[PAUSE — let this one sit]

So this single word reveals the first thing: **the Hour is not a possibility you can negotiate the timing of. It is a vessel already at sea, and asking "when does it anchor?" only proves you've already conceded that it sails.**

### Element Two: فِيمَ أَنتَ — The Question Turned Back

Now watch what Allah does with the answer. It is one of the most surgical redirections in the entire Qurʾān, and it happens across two short verses.

First, let me teach the device. In Arabic, as in English, you can ask a question not to get information but to *dismiss* a premise. If a child demands to know exactly what time the sun will burn out, an adult might say: "What have *you* got to do with that?" That is not cruelty. It is a redirection. It says: *you are asking about something that is (a) not yours to know and (b) not the thing you should be worried about.* The question itself is the correction.

Now read verse 43:

> فِيمَ أَنتَ مِن ذِكْرَىٰهَا
> *fīma anta min dhikrāhā* — literally, "*In what* are you, regarding the mention of it?"

The phrasing is strange even in Arabic, and the strangeness is the point. *Fīma* — "in what?" — is an interrogative of negation and amazement. It means, roughly: *In what position are you, that the mentioning of its date should be among your tasks at all?* What relation do you have to *announcing the time* of this thing?

And here is what should stop you cold: **this is said to the Prophet ﷺ himself.**

This is not Allah scolding the deniers. The grammatical address — *anta*, "you" — is the Messenger. The most beloved of creation, the one carried up beyond the Lote Tree, the one who knew more of the unseen than any human who ever lived — *even he* is told: this is not yours. The when of the Hour was placed in a vault that the Messenger of Allah does not hold the key to.

And notice how the classical reading sharpens this. Al-Muyassar paraphrases the verse, speaking on Allah's behalf to the Messenger, with a single devastating clause: *lasta fī shayʾin min ʿilmihā* — "you are in **nothing** of its knowledge." Not merely *you are not the one to announce the date* — but *the knowledge of it was never deposited with you at all.* The redirection runs deeper than the question assumed. The deniers thought they were demanding a fact the Prophet ﷺ was withholding. The verse answers that there is no withholding, because there is nothing in his hands to withhold. You cannot accuse a man of hiding a key he was never given.

Read that as an absence, the way we are taught to read absences. What is *withheld* here, and from *whom*? Knowledge of the Hour's timing — withheld even from the Prophet ﷺ. And if it is sealed away from *him*, then notice the quiet devastation of the original question: the people of Makkah were demanding from a human messenger a piece of knowledge that Allah did not even deposit *with that messenger.* They were not asking a hard question. They were asking an impossible one — and pretending its impossibility was his failure.

Then comes verse 44, and the door that was closed in 43 is now sealed shut:

> إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنتَهَىٰهَآ
> *ilā rabbika muntahāhā* — "**To your Lord** is its final end."

Here is a second device, and it is everywhere in the Qurʾān once you learn to see it. In Arabic, the normal word order puts the subject and its predicate in a settled sequence. But when you *move a piece to the front* — when you front it, out of its expected place — that fronting carries the force of **restriction**. It means *only.* It means *this and nothing else.*

The natural order would be *muntahāhā ilā rabbika* — "its end is to your Lord." Instead Allah fronts the phrase: *ilā rabbika* — "**to your Lord** — its end." And the fronting slams a wall around it. It does not merely say its knowledge is with your Lord. It says its knowledge is with your Lord **and absolutely nowhere else.** Not with an angel, not with a prophet, not with a star chart, not with the most pious saint, not with you. *Muntahā* — from the root meaning "to reach the utmost limit, the terminus, the place beyond which there is no further" — its terminus runs back to one place and stops there. The buck stops at the Throne.

[PAUSE]

And there is a tenderness folded inside the severity. Notice the word: *ilā **rabbika***. "To **your Lord**." Not "to Allah," not "to the All-Knowing" — to *your* Lord, the one who nurtures *you*, O Muhammad. The same verse that takes the knowledge out of his hands reassures him whose hands they belong in. *You don't carry this. The One who has carried you your whole life carries this.* The restriction is not abandonment. It is relief.

So this second element reveals: **the obsession with "when" is not just unanswerable — it is being deliberately refused, and the refusal is itself the teaching. The timing is sealed in the one place that is pure mercy: with your Lord. You were never meant to hold it.**

### Element Three: إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرُ مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا — Only, and Only For

If verses 43 and 44 take something *away* from the Prophet ﷺ, verse 45 gives him back something far greater — and in doing so, it tells *us* exactly what to do with our anxious "when."

> إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرُ مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا
> *innamā anta mundhiru man yakhshāhā* — "You are **only** a warner for whoever fears it."

Two restrictions live in this one short line, and we have to take them one at a time, because each is doing heavy lifting.

The first word is *innamā.* This is the great particle of restriction in Arabic — the word that means "nothing but," "exclusively," "the whole of the matter is just this." When Allah says *innamā anta*, He is saying: *the entire scope of your role, the complete and total job description, is this one thing.*

And what is that one thing? *Mundhir.* A warner.

Stay with the contrast. They wanted him to be an *astronomer* — give us the date. Allah says: you are not a teller of times; *innamā anta mundhir* — you are only and entirely a giver of warning. The deniers tried to shrink him into a fortune-teller who had failed his test. Allah refuses the whole frame. *Your job was never to tell them when. Your job is to wake them up before it's too late.* A man who knows the storm is coming does not waste his breath calculating the precise minute of landfall. He bangs on doors. The value of a warner is not in his precision about the clock; it is in the urgency of his knock.

(And note — *mundhir* itself is from the root *n-dh-r*, in the same Form IV we met with the mountains and the Hour: *andhara*, to deliver a warning that demands a response. It is an *active* word. A warner is not a commentator. He is someone trying to *move* you before the door closes.)

Now the second restriction — and this one is the most piercing absence in the whole passage.

*Mundhiru **man yakhshāhā***. "A warner **for whoever fears it.**"

Read that slowly. The warning has an *audience boundary built into it.* He is a warner — but a warner *to whom?* Not to everyone, in the sense of who will *benefit*. He is functionally a warner *for the one who fears it* — *man yakhshāhā*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the asymmetry. Allah does not say "a warner for those who ask about it." He does not say "a warner for the clever, the curious, the ones who want a date." He says: a warner for the one in whom there is *khashya* — that particular fear that is reverent, that trembles, that takes the thing seriously.

And do you see what that does to the people who started this whole exchange? They came demanding a date. They had *curiosity* about the Hour. But they had no *khashya* of it. And the verse quietly steps right past them. The warning does not even fully land on the merely curious. It lands on the heart that already fears.

This is one of the most important things the passage teaches, and it is hidden in who is *left out.* **Information is not the problem. The deniers had access to all the information — they stood inside the storm of this whole surah. What they lacked was not data about the Hour. It was *khashya* of it.** And so even a perfect answer to "when?" would have changed nothing in them. You cannot warn someone into fear. The warning only completes itself in a heart that was already willing to tremble.

The question "when?" comes from curiosity.
The transformation comes from *khashya.*
And the gap between those two is the gap between the people in this verse and the people Allah actually addresses through it.

### The Bridge

Step back and look at what these three discoveries have shown us, just at the level of language.

The deniers asked *when* — and the word they used (*mursā*) revealed that the Hour is a ship already sailing, anchored toward them as surely as Allah anchored the mountains.

They demanded the timing from the Prophet ﷺ — and Allah sealed that timing away (*ilā rabbika muntahāhā*), even from him, in the one place that is pure mercy.

And then Allah redefined the whole point: not *when*, but *who fears.* The Messenger is *innamā mundhir* — only a warner — and the warning is for *man yakhshāhā*, the heart that trembles.

Three linguistic moves. One single redirection: **away from the clock, and into the chest.**

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we are living, right now, today.

## PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The "When" That Is Really a Way of Hiding

Begin where you already live.

You know the feeling of refreshing a screen for news that hasn't changed. You know the strange comfort of looking up *exactly* how bad something is going to be — the storm tracker, the diagnosis statistics, the market forecast — as though knowing the precise hour of the bad thing would somehow tame it. We are a species addicted to *when.* When will the economy turn. When will I get sick. When will the world end. We pour enormous energy into pinning down the *timing* of things we cannot control.

And here is the secret about that addiction, the thing the therapist eventually says out loud: **demanding to know exactly when something will happen is very often a way of avoiding the thing you should actually be doing about it.**

The man who spends three hours researching the precise statistics of his illness, and zero minutes changing how he lives — he is using *when* as an escape hatch. The "when" question *feels* like engagement. It feels like taking the thing seriously. But it is frequently the opposite. It is a way to stand at a safe analytical distance from a reality that, if you actually let it in, would demand that you *change.*

This is precisely what the people of Makkah were doing. *"When is this Hour?"* sounds like engagement with the message. It is actually a flight from it. As long as the conversation is about the *date*, it is not about *me.* As long as I'm interrogating the timing, I never have to face the standing.

[PAUSE]

And now watch the genius of Allah's response, because it is the exact move of the wisest counsel you could ever receive. He does not take the bait. He does not get drawn into the *when.* He reaches past the question — *fīma anta min dhikrāhā* — and He relocates the entire conversation from the calendar to the conscience. From *when will it come* to *who is ready when it does.*

He is teaching us how to handle our own anxious "whens." When you find yourself obsessively trying to time the uncontrollable — the death, the loss, the reckoning — the redirection is not "here is the date that will calm you." There is no date that will calm you; *ilā rabbika muntahāhā*, the date belongs to Him alone, and He is not telling. The redirection is: **stop asking when the ship arrives, and ask whether you are the kind of person who is ready for any ship at any hour.**

Because here is the thing about a warner versus a timekeeper. If you knew the date, you would procrastinate up to it. We always do. Every student given a deadline does the work the night before. **The mercy of the sealed date is that it forbids procrastination.** A Hour that could arrive *this evening* leaves no room for "I'll get serious later." The hiddenness of the *when* is not Allah withholding comfort. It is Allah refusing to let you delay.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is this: the next time you catch yourself anxiously trying to *time* a thing you cannot control — to know exactly when the bad news lands — let that anxiety become a trigger, a doorbell. Let it remind you: *the timing was never mine. The readiness always was.* And turn the energy you were spending on the clock toward the only question that was ever yours to answer — *am I among those who fear it, and live like it?*

### Theme Two: An Evening, or a Morning

Now the final verse, which I have saved for last because it is where the whole passage detonates.

> كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا
> *Ka'annahum yawma yarawnahā lam yalbathū illā ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā.*
> "On the Day they see it, it will be as though they had lingered no longer than an evening — or its morning."

Begin again where you live.

Think of the longest stretch of your life that, from where you stand now, has utterly collapsed. Childhood — those endless summers that felt like they would never end while you were inside them, and now amount to a handful of bright images you could describe in a minute. Or this: ask anyone who has buried a parent how long the parent's whole life felt, at the funeral. *It went so fast.* Eighty years. Gone like an afternoon. We all know, in our bones, that **time felt from the inside and time measured from the end are two completely different quantities.** Lived forward, a life is vast. Looked back upon, it shrinks to almost nothing.

The Qurʾān takes that experience — which you already have, in miniature, every time you say "where did the years go?" — and stretches it to its absolute limit.

On the Day they finally *see* the Hour they kept demanding the timing of — the entire span of worldly life, every year of it, every long afternoon and sleepless night and decade of striving — will feel like *no more than an evening, or the morning that followed it.* *ʿAshiyya* — the falling-time of a single day. *Ḍuḥā* — the bright early forenoon. A few hours. Less than one full day.

And here the classical commentary adds something our "where did the years go?" experience does not quite reach on its own. Al-Muyassar names the *cause* of the collapse: it is *li-hawl al-sāʿa* — "because of the terror of the Hour." The years do not merely shrink the way childhood shrinks in fond memory. They are *swallowed* — eclipsed by a terror so total that everything standing before it is flattened into nothing. It is the way a single overwhelming present can erase whole years behind it: when the thing in front of you is enormous enough, the entire road that led there contracts to a step. The whole of worldly life, measured against the sheer weight of that Day, will not even register as time worth counting.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the merciless irony, and let it complete the circle of all five verses.

They spent so much energy asking *when.* When, when, when — as though the *duration* until the Hour was the most important thing in the world, as though there were vast stretches of time to be measured and planned around and delayed within. And Allah's final word to them is: **on the Day it comes, you will look back at all that time you were so busy counting — and it will feel like one afternoon.**

The very thing they were trying to *time* will obliterate their whole sense of time. All that worldly life they thought they had so much of — *lam yalbathū illā ʿashiyya* — they will swear they barely lingered at all. (The classical commentaries dwell on the felt smallness of it — al-Muyassar glosses it as the span from noon to sunset, or from sunrise to midday — a fraction of a single day, the whole of a human lifetime.)

So consider what this does to the opening question. *"When is the Hour?"* The question assumes time is long, that there is a comfortable distance to be measured, that the "when" is far. The final verse pulls the rug: from the vantage point of eternity, the distance you were so worried about timing is *nothing.* You will not experience your seventy years as seventy years. You will experience them as the brief dusk before the door opened.

[PAUSE]

And here is the turn, because this is not meant to crush you. It is meant to *clarify* you.

If your whole life is going to feel, in the end, like a single evening — then how should you spend an evening you know is short?

You do not spend a short evening obsessing over what time it is. You spend it doing what matters, with the people who matter, in the way you would not regret. The collapse of time at the end is not a threat; it is a *lens.* It tells you what your life already is, seen truly: brief, precious, and almost over. Not "almost over" in years — maybe you have many. "Almost over" in *weight.* Against the scale of what comes after, the whole thing is one *ʿashiyya.*

What does this change tomorrow? It is the oldest and simplest medicine, and it is exactly what these verses prescribe. Tonight, before you sleep, look back on this single day as though it were the whole of your life seen from the Day they see it. Because in miniature, it is. If today were the *ʿashiyya* you barely lingered in — were you a person of *khashya*, or a person counting the clock? You do not need to know *when* the ship anchors. You need only to live each evening as though the harbor were already in sight. And it is.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now look at the shape of the whole, which we could not see until we had walked through it.

Five verses. Watch how they move:

- **42 — the human question:** *when?* (the clock)
- **43 — the redirection:** not yours to announce (away from the Prophet)
- **44 — the restriction:** *to your Lord alone* (the sealed center — the pivot)
- **45 — the re-direction:** *only a warner, for the one who fears* (back toward the human heart)
- **46 — the answer that was never about timing:** all of time will feel like an evening (the collapse of the clock entirely)

Do you see it? It opens with a question about *time* and closes with the *annihilation of time.* It begins in the deniers' false confidence that there is a long "when" to measure, and it ends by dissolving that "when" into a single afternoon. And right at the center — verse 44, the pivot of the whole structure — sits the one fixed point: *ilā rabbika.* To your Lord. Everything orbits that. The clock at the start is an illusion; the Lord at the center is the only reality. The passage is built so that you fall, line by line, *out of the clock and into the Throne.*

And listen — say them aloud again and you will hear it — every single verse ends on the same soft, falling sound: *mursā**hā**, dhikrā**hā**, muntahā**hā**, yakhshā**hā**, ḍuḥā**hā**.* Five times, the long *-ā* and then *-hā*: "it… its… it." The whole passage rhymes on a pronoun whose owner is never renamed — *the Hour.* Every line bends back to the same unspoken center, the way every life, however it is spent, bends back to the same appointment. The very sound of the verses is doing what the verses say: everything returns to the one thing.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same truth approached from two sides.

The word *mursā* showed us a ship already sailing. Theme two showed us that when it anchors, all our time will have felt like an evening. **The ship was always close; we only thought the voyage was long.**

The restriction *ilā rabbika* showed us the timing is sealed away. Theme one showed us *why that is mercy* — because a known date breeds delay, and a hidden one demands we be ready now.

The restriction *innamā… man yakhshāhā* showed us the warning lands only on the heart that trembles. And the whole passage exists to *make us tremble* — not with the data they demanded, but with the *khashya* they lacked.

### Questions to Carry

- When I find myself anxious to *know exactly when* a hard thing will come, what am I avoiding *doing* by focusing on the clock?
- Is the Hour, for me, a piece of information — or an object of *khashya*? What is the honest difference in how I live between those two?
- If my whole life is going to feel, on that Day, like a single evening — would I be content with how I am spending *this* evening?
- The timing was sealed away even from the Prophet ﷺ. What does it mean that I keep wanting to control a "when" that he himself was told to let go of?
- Who in my life is *banging on the door* — warning me — and have I been demanding dates from them instead of waking up?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The Hour you keep asking the time of is a ship already sailing toward you, its anchor-chain held in your Lord's hand alone — so stop counting the distance, because when it finally drops anchor your whole life will have felt like a single afternoon, and the only question that was ever yours was whether you were among those who feared it.

### The Closing Invitation

You came, perhaps, the way they came — wanting to *know.* Wanting the date, the certainty, the timeline that would let you plan and, quietly, delay. Let this passage do to you what it did to that question. Let it lift your eyes off the clock. The invitation is not to find out when. It is to become the kind of soul for whom the *when* no longer matters — because every evening is lived as though the harbor were already in view. Not in dread. In readiness. The ship is not your enemy. It is only carrying you home.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who anchored the mountains and hold the anchor-chain of the Hour — to You alone belongs its time, and I let go of needing to know. Place in my heart the khashya that the merely curious never find. And let me live this short evening of my life as one who saw the harbor and was not afraid.*

Āmīn.
