# The Hands That Take You: The Five Oaths of the Extractors

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic, then in sound, then in a meaning you can hold.

> وَالنَّازِعَاتِ غَرْقًا • وَالنَّاشِطَاتِ نَشْطًا • وَالسَّابِحَاتِ سَبْحًا • فَالسَّابِقَاتِ سَبْقًا • فَالْمُدَبِّرَاتِ أَمْرًا

*Wan-nāziʿāti gharqā. Wan-nāshiṭāti nashṭā. Was-sābiḥāti sabḥā. Fas-sābiqāti sabqā. Fal-mudabbirāti amrā.*

"By those who wrench out, drowning-deep. And by those who draw out, gently. And by those who glide, swimming. Then those who race ahead, racing. Then those who arrange the whole affair."

Now I want you to notice your own confusion. Because if you don't know this surah, you have just read five lines and you do not know what a single one of them is *about*. Who wrenches? Who draws out? Who glides? Allah has not told you. He swears by five somethings, and He names none of them. He gives you only what they *do*.

[PAUSE]

Sit in Makkah for a moment. This is an early surah, revealed into a city that did not believe the dead would rise. To them resurrection was the one absurd claim — the punchline. *You mean these crumbled bones, scattered in the valley, will stand again?* They could accept a God. What they laughed at was the idea that death was not the end of the story.

And here is how Allah chooses to answer that laughter. Not with a syllogism. Not yet. He answers with an **oath**. Five oaths, fired off in a rhythm so tight and so fast that the original listener would have felt it in the chest before the mind caught up — *nāziʿāti gharqā, nāshiṭāti nashṭā, sābiḥāti sabḥā* — like a drumbeat, like hooves, like something rushing toward you that you cannot see.

When a person swears by something, they are pointing at it and saying: *look at this — this is so real, so weighty, that I stake my word on it.* So the first question of this surah is not "will you be resurrected?" The first question is: **what are these things so enormous that the answer to death itself is sworn upon them?**

The Companions — Ali, Ibn Masʿud, Ibn ʿAbbas, and those who learned from them — answered with near one voice: these are **the angels**. And the very first of them is the one whose hand is already, right now, assigned to the moment of your death.

Here is what I want you to feel by the end of our time together. Most of us live as though death is a rumor — something that happens to other people, somewhere off the edge of the map. This passage opens by swearing that it is not off the map at all. There are hands already at work. The question the surah will not let you avoid is simply: **when those hands reach you, what will the taking feel like?**

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: غَرْقًا — the word that should not be there

Let me teach you a small piece of Arabic before we touch the ayah, because once you see it you will never read the Quran the same way.

In English, if I want to intensify a verb, I reach for an adverb. *He ran — quickly. She wept — bitterly.* Arabic has a far more powerful tool. It can take the verb and then repeat its own meaning back as a noun, slammed down right beside it in the accusative case. Grammarians call this the **maf'ul mutlaq** — the "absolute object." We might call it the echo-word.

So you don't say "he was grateful." You say *shakara shukrā* — "he was-grateful with a gratefulness." The echo doubles the action onto itself. It is the difference between saying "I love you" and "I love you with a *loving*." The word turns back on itself and deepens.

Now look at the first oath:

> [ayah:79:1] وَٱلنَّـٰزِعَـٰتِ غَرْقًا

The first word, **النازعات** — *an-nāziʿāt* — comes from the root *nazaʿa* (ن-ز-ع), and the lexicons are blunt about it: it means to pull out, to wrench, to tear something away by force, to rip out by the roots. It is the word for drawing an arrow back against the full tension of the bow. It is the word for pulling a stubborn stake out of hard ground. There is nothing gentle in it. It is extraction against resistance.

So we expect the echo-word. We expect *an-nāziʿāti nazʿā* — "those who wrench with a wrenching." The grammar is leaning toward it. The rhythm is leaning toward it.

And Allah does not give it.

He gives a **different word entirely.** *Gharqā* — غرقًا — from the root غ-ر-ق, which has nothing to do with pulling. *Gharaq* is the word for **drowning.** For sinking. For being completely submerged until the water closes over your head.

[PAUSE]

Stop here. This is the door.

Why would Allah, describing an act of *pulling*, reach for the word for *drowning*? Al-Baghawi and the classical commentators noticed this immediately. They explain that *gharqan* here stands in the place of *ighrāqan* — and *ighrāq* is what an archer does when he draws the bowstring back **to its absolute limit**, until the arrowhead almost touches his hand, until there is no further the bow can be pulled without breaking. To pull to the point of total submersion. To pull *all the way.*

So now hear the two words together. *An-nāziʿāt* — the ones who wrench out. *Gharqā* — drowning-deep, to the very limit, all the way down. The pulling is not a tug. It is a total, engulfing, no-resistance-left extraction. Ibn Masʿud described it in terms that make you wince: the soul of the one who has rejected his Lord drawn out *from beneath every hair, from beneath the nails, from the soles of the feet* — like a barbed skewer dragged out of wet wool, catching, tearing, taking everything with it.

And listen to the **sound** of the word. *Gharqā.* That opening غ — *ghayn* — is a sound made deep in the throat, gargling, guttural, heavy. Then the *r*, then the hard stop of the *q* — *qāf* — slammed from the very back of the mouth. The word itself catches in your throat the way water catches in the throat of the drowning. The sound of the word **is** the experience of the word. Allah did not only describe a violent death. He made you choke a little when you say it.

This is the physical image — *drowning* — and it is a stepping stone. It is not really about water. It is about a soul that has spent a whole life clinging to a body, clinging to dunya, sunk into it up to the eyes, and now must be pulled out of everything it loved all at once. For such a soul, death is not a doorway. It is **drowning in reverse** — being dragged up and out of the only water it ever knew how to breathe.

One word. *Gharqā.* And it has already told you that there is a way to die that feels like the sea closing over you.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: نَشْطًا — and the mercy hidden in a second word

Now stay close, because Allah does something here that He does not have to do, and the *doing* of it is the whole point.

The second oath:

> [ayah:79:2] وَٱلنَّـٰشِطَـٰتِ نَشْطًا

**الناشطات** — *an-nāshiṭāt* — from the root ن-ش-ط. And here, finally, Allah gives the echo-word: *nashṭā*. "Those who draw out, with a drawing-out." The grammar settles into its natural shape. After the disruption of *gharqā*, the rhythm comes home.

But what does *nashṭ* mean? The lexicons tell us it is the word for **untying a knot** — specifically, for pulling the loose end of a rope so that the whole knot slips open *easily*, in one smooth motion. It is also the root from which Arabic gets the word for **briskness, liveliness, energy** — *nashīṭ*, an active and willing person. It is the opposite of dragging. It is the rope that comes free when you pull the right end.

So look at what has just happened across two lines.

In the first oath: a soul *wrenched* out, *drowning-deep*, torn from beneath the nails, against everything in it that resists.

In the second oath: a soul *drawn* out the way you slip a knot loose — *briskly, willingly, in one clean motion.* The commentators are explicit: these are the souls of the believers, taken with energy and ease, gliding free the way a rope comes untied when you know exactly where to pull.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has built here. **The same act. Two completely different experiences.**

It is the *same* angels, the *same* unseen hands, performing the *same* function — the removal of a soul from a body — and Allah has split the description in two. For one, *gharqā* — drowning. For the other, *nashṭā* — untying. The act does not change. **What changes is the one being acted upon.**

This is an asymmetry you must read carefully, because it is the entire moral architecture of the passage. Allah is not describing two kinds of death. He is describing two kinds of *soul* meeting the one event of death. The difference is not in the hand that takes. The difference is in what the soul has spent its life becoming — whether it sank itself into this world until it must be torn loose, or held it lightly enough that it slips free at the first pull.

And here is the absence I want you to hear. Allah does not say *why* one is drowned and the other is loosed. He does not, in these two lines, explain the criteria. He simply lays the two experiences side by side — *gharqā… nashṭā* — and lets the gap between them ring. The silence is the question. **Which knot are you tying around your own soul, right now, with every attachment you refuse to loosen?**

### Element Three: the و that becomes ف — and the doer who is never named

Two more things in these five lines, and then I want to show you how the whole structure moves.

First, look at the connecting letters — and I promise this is not dry grammar, it is the hidden machinery of the passage.

The first three oaths are joined by **wāw** (و): *wan-nāziʿāt… wan-nāshiṭāt… was-sābiḥāt.* The wāw is the simplest conjunction — "and." It lists. It sets things side by side without claiming any order between them. These are three descriptions held up together.

But the fourth and fifth oaths switch:

> **فَ**السَّابِقَاتِ سَبْقًا • **فَ**الْمُدَبِّرَاتِ أَمْرًا

That is no longer wāw. That is **fā'** (ف) — and *fā'* does not merely list. *Fā'* means *then, and so, as a consequence.* It signals **sequence and result.** One thing follows from another.

So watch the shape of the five. The first three are laid out together — the wrenching, the loosing, the gliding. Then, with the *fā'*, the passage tips into motion: *and so they race ahead… and so they arrange the affair.* The grammar itself accelerates. The wāw is standing still and describing. The *fā'* breaks into a run.

And what are these latter words? **السابحات** (*as-sābiḥāt*, root س-ب-ح) — those who *swim, glide, float* — the angels coursing down from heaven and back up, moving through the unseen the way a swimmer moves through water, no friction, no effort. Then **السابقات** (*as-sābiqāt*, root س-ب-ق) — those who *outstrip, race ahead, get there first* — pure forward urgency. And finally **المدبرات** (*al-mudabbirāt*, root د-ب-ر) — those who *manage, arrange, direct the affair.*

That last word deserves a beat. *Al-mudabbirāt* is built on Form II of the verb — *dabbara* — and the root د-ب-ر literally points to the *back, the rear, the consequence* of a thing. To do *tadbīr* is to manage something by looking at where it ends up — to arrange the back-end, to administer with the outcome in view. This Form II is not a mere "causing"; it is **deliberate, ongoing, careful management.** The angels who run the affair. *Amrā* — "the affair, the command" — left wide open: which affair? All of it. The cosmos. The order. The decree being carried out.

[PAUSE]

Now stand back and look at the trajectory the five words trace:

**نزع** — wrench out (the deathbed) → **نشط** — draw out gently (the believing soul released) → **سبح** — glide upward through the heavens (the soul carried) → **سبق** — race ahead (rushing to their stations) → **دبر** — arrange the whole affair (the cosmic order administered).

Do you see it? The passage **begins at your deathbed and ends at the management of the universe.** It starts with a single soul being pulled from a single body — the most intimate, most terrifying private moment a human will ever have — and within five short lines it has zoomed all the way out to the entire affair of creation being run by these same unseen hands. The *fā'* is the camera pulling back. Your death is line one. The administration of everything is line five. And they are the **same continuous motion** — which means your private death is not an isolated event off in a corner. It is woven into the running of the whole order.

And through all of it — here is the final absence, the largest one — **the doer is never named.** Five participles, all on the same feminine-plural pattern (*fāʿilāt*), all describing, none identifying. Allah swears not by named beings but by *functions* — by what is being done. He withholds the subject entirely. We say "the angels" because the Companions taught us so, but the *text* gives us only the actions, relentless and anonymous.

Why withhold the name? Because a name would let you file it away — *oh, the angels, I know about them.* But a bare verb gives you nothing to file. It leaves you with only the **act, still happening.** The wrenching. The loosing. The racing. Not "there exist beings called angels" but "*right now, something is wrenching, something is loosing, something is racing.*" The grammar refuses to let it become a settled, distant fact. It keeps it live.

---

**So what have these words shown us?** That Allah opened His answer to death with five anonymous, hammering oaths; that He chose a drowning-word to make us feel a violent death and a knot-loosing word to make us feel a gentle one; that He split one identical act into two opposite experiences and left the reason ringing in the silence; and that He traced a single line from one human deathbed all the way to the running of the cosmos.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the life you are living today.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The certainty you have organized your whole life around forgetting

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a calendar. It has appointments in it for next week, next month, maybe a trip in the fall. You have plans. You have a sense, mostly unspoken, that life is a long road stretching out ahead of you, and that the serious business of preparing for the end of it is something you will get to — later. When things settle down. When the kids are older. When the work is less. There is always a *later* in which you will finally turn and face it.

This is not a character flaw. It is how the *nafs* protects itself. The mind cannot hold the certainty of its own death and function at the same time, so it does something clever: it converts the most *certain* fact in your entire life into the most *abstract* one. Everyone dies — yes, obviously, in general, in theory. But not as a thing with a date. Not as a thing with a *hand assigned to it.*

And then you open Surah An-Nazi'at, and the very first thing Allah does — before any argument, before any threat, before any promise — is **swear by that hand.**

[PAUSE]

Think about what it means that *this* is the opening oath. Of everything in creation Allah could have sworn by to establish that the dead will rise — the sun, the mountains, the seas, all of which He swears by elsewhere — He opens *this* surah by swearing by **the act of taking your soul.** As if to say: you want proof that the affair will be completed? Look at the first stage of it, which is *already in motion.* The hands that will take you are not waiting in some distant future. They are deployed. They are part of the running order of the universe — *al-mudabbirāt amrā* — as constant and as operational as gravity.

Your death is not an event that *might* happen. It is an assignment that has *already been given.* The only variable left is the date, and the date is not yours to know.

Here is the shift this asks of you. Not panic — the Quran is not trying to frighten you into paralysis. The shift is from treating death as a rumor to treating it as an **appointment on the calendar you cannot see.** Because that is exactly what it is. You schedule your life around the appointments you *can* see. What would change if you lived around the one you can't — knowing only that it is real, that it is set, and that a hand has been assigned?

The Companions, who learned this surah, were not morbid people. They were the most alive people who ever walked. But they lived with death close, not far — close enough to keep them honest, close enough that nothing trivial could masquerade as urgent. They had simply accepted what most of us spend enormous energy denying: that *al-nāziʿāt* is not a metaphor.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Once, before you sleep tonight — sleep being the small death, the daily rehearsal — let the thought come fully, without flinching from it: *there is a hand assigned to me, and I do not know the hour.* Not to torment yourself. To get honest. The things that felt urgent today — feel them again with that fact in the room. Watch how many of them quietly let go of their grip. That loosening is itself a mercy. It is *nashṭ* practiced in advance.

### Theme Two: The same wave — and why it drowns one and carries another

Now the harder, more beautiful thing. Let me give you a picture.

Imagine two people in the same ocean, in the same swell, at the same moment. A large wave rises and comes down over both of them. For the first — someone who has spent the whole time fighting the water, thrashing, clenching every muscle, terrified, refusing to let the sea hold them — the wave is a catastrophe. It drives them down, fills their lungs, drowns them. For the second — someone who has learned to go loose in the water, to let the wave lift and pass — the *same wave* simply raises them up and sets them down again. Same ocean. Same wave. Same physics. **Two opposite fates, decided entirely by what each body had become before the wave arrived.**

That is *gharqā* and *nashṭā.*

[PAUSE]

We tend to imagine that death will be made easy or hard *by God's decision in the moment* — as if at the deathbed the verdict is handed down and the experience assigned. But the passage tells a subtler truth. The hand is the *same* hand. The act is the *same* act. The wrenching and the loosing are not two different policies; they are **one event meeting two different souls.** What determines whether your soul drowns or slips free is not negotiated at the end. It is *built* — quietly, daily, over a whole life — by how deeply you let yourself sink into this world.

Think about what *gharqā* — drowning — actually requires. To drown in something, you have to be *in* it, fully submerged, dependent on it for the very air you breathe. The soul that is "drowned" out of the body at death is the soul that had made dunya its oxygen — that breathed status, breathed wealth, breathed the approval of others, breathed its appetites, until being pulled out of all that at once was literal suffocation in reverse. Of course it is torn from beneath every hair. It had grown roots into everything.

And *nashṭ* — the loosened knot — requires the opposite. Not a soul that hated the world or refused it, but a soul that held it the way you hold a rope you know you'll have to let go of: with a real grip, but a *releasable* one. Tie a knot you intend to untie later, and you tie it differently. You leave the end loose. The believer lives in the world with the loose end always within reach — *I am here, I am grateful, I am working, and I can let this go the moment I am asked to.* For that soul, the angel does not have to wrench. It just pulls the loose end, and the whole life comes free in one clean motion.

[PAUSE]

This reframes the entire spiritual project. We often think of *taqwa*, of righteousness, as a list of things done and avoided — a ledger. But this passage reveals it as something more like **the practice of tying releasable knots.** Every attachment you form in this life, you are tying a knot around your own soul. The question is never *should I be attached to nothing* — Allah gave you family and provision and beauty to love. The question is: *am I tying these knots so tight that they will have to be torn out of me — or am I leaving the loose end where I can find it?*

And the way you learn to leave the loose end is not by some grand renunciation. It is small and repeated. Every time you give something away that you wanted to keep — you loosen a knot. Every time you let go of a grudge you had every right to hold — you loosen a knot. Every prayer where you set the whole world down for a few minutes and stand before Allah with empty hands — that is a *rehearsal* of *nashṭ*. You are teaching your soul the motion of release while the stakes are still low, so that when the great release comes, your soul already knows the movement. It comes free, *briskly, willingly* — *nashṭā* — because it has done this thousands of times before.

The drowning soul never practiced letting go. So when the moment comes that *everything* must be let go at once, it has no skill for it, and the letting-go becomes a tearing.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Find one knot this week. One thing you are gripping too tightly — a possession, a resentment, a need to be right, a fear of losing something. And practice the loose end. Give a portion of it away. Loosen your hold by one degree, on purpose, while you still can. You are not just doing a good deed. You are training your soul in the one motion it will need most, on the one day it cannot rehearse.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, now that we can see it

Look back at the five lines as a single shape, because the structure is the proof.

| | Word | Action | Where we are |
|---|------|--------|-------------|
| 1 | النازعات غرقا | wrenching, drowning-deep | the rejecter's deathbed |
| 2 | الناشطات نشطا | loosing, gently | the believer's deathbed |
| 3 | السابحات سبحا | gliding, swimming | the soul carried through the heavens |
[ayah:79:4] فَٱلسَّـٰبِقَـٰتِ سَبْقًا
[ayah:79:5] فَٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰتِ أَمْرًا

The first two lines, joined by *wāw*, are the **fork in the road** — the same act splitting into two destinies. The last two lines, joined by *fā'*, are the **forward rush** — sequence, consequence, the affair being carried out. And the whole thing moves from the most intimate point imaginable — a single soul leaving a single body — outward and upward until it reaches *the administration of everything that exists.*

That movement is the argument. The Makkans said resurrection was impossible — too big, too cosmic, too far. And Allah answers: the same hands that perform the most enormous task in the universe, *running the entire affair*, are the hands at your bedside performing the smallest, most personal one. **The cosmic and the intimate are one continuous motion.** If you doubt the affair can be completed, look — its first stage is already being performed on every human who has ever died. The machinery is not theoretical. It is running.

### The two discoveries are one discovery

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths have arrived at the same place from two directions.

From the *words*, we found that Allah split one act — *gharqā* / *nashṭā* — and left the reason silent.

From the *themes*, we found that the reason is not silent at all; it is written in advance, by you, in the knots you tie and loosen across a lifetime.

The grammar gave us the gap. The reflection filled it. The drowning and the loosing are the same wave; what differs is the swimmer. And the swimmer is being made — right now — by every grip you tighten or release.

### Questions to carry

- If a hand is assigned to me and the hour is hidden, what on today's calendar would I treat differently?
- Which attachments in my life am I tying as knots to be *torn out* of me — and which am I leaving with a loose end?
- When was the last time I genuinely *let go* of something I wanted to keep? Is my soul practiced in that motion, or out of practice?
- Do I live with death close enough to keep me honest, or have I pushed it off the edge of the map?
- If death is one continuous motion with the running of the whole order, what does it mean that my death is *not* a private accident but a thread in something vast?

### The one sentence

Allah opens His answer to death by swearing on the unseen hands that take every soul — wrenching the one that drowned itself in this world, loosing the one that held it with a free hand — so that you would know your death is not chaos at the edge of life, but the first move of an order already in motion, and that whether the taking drowns you or carries you is being decided, quietly, by how you live today.

### The invitation

You cannot change that the hand is coming. That is not on offer. What is on offer — what this passage lays at your feet — is the *kind* of soul that hand will meet. Not through fear, and not through fleeing the world, but through learning, in a thousand small daily releases, the motion of the loosened knot. Begin tonight. Begin with one grip. Let your soul start to learn the movement of *nashṭ* while the stakes are still small, so that when the great wave comes, it does not find you thrashing — it finds you ready to be lifted.

### Du'a

*Allah, You who assigned the hand and hid the hour — let me not be among those whose souls must be torn from this world drowning. Teach my heart the motion of the loosened knot, that I may hold Your gifts with gratitude and a free hand. And when the appointed one comes for me, draw my soul out gently — nashṭā — and let it glide home to You.*

*Ameen.*
