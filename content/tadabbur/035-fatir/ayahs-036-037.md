# The Mercy That Is Withheld
### Surah Fatir 35:36–37

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> [ayah:35:36] وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لَهُمْ نَارُ جَهَنَّمَ لَا يُقْضَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ فَيَمُوتُوا۟ وَلَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُم مِّنْ عَذَابِهَا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى كُلَّ كَفُورٍ

*Wa-lladhīna kafarū lahum nāru jahannam. Lā yuqḍā ʿalayhim fa-yamūtū wa-lā yukhaffafu ʿanhum min ʿadhābihā. Kadhālika najzī kulla kafūr.*

And then, the next breath:

> وَهُمْ يَصْطَرِخُونَ فِيهَا رَبَّنَآ أَخْرِجْنَا نَعْمَلْ صَـٰلِحًا غَيْرَ ٱلَّذِى كُنَّا نَعْمَلُ

*Wa-hum yaṣṭarikhūna fīhā: Rabbanā akhrijnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan ghayra alladhī kunnā naʿmal.*

In English, plainly:

*"And those who disbelieved — for them is the Fire of Hell. Death is not decreed for them, so that they might die; nor is anything lightened for them of its torment. Thus do We recompense every ingrate. And there they cry out: 'Our Lord, take us out — we will do righteousness, other than what we used to do.'"*

### Rebuild the world

To feel where these words land, you have to feel what came just before them.

The surah has been describing the other group. The people who inherited the Book. And listen to the very first thing that leaves their mouths when they enter the Garden — verse 34, just two ayahs earlier:

*"Praise belongs to Allah, who has removed all grief from us."*

Sit with that for a second. The first word of the saved is *al-ḥamd* — gratitude. The first instinct of the people of Paradise is to **thank**.

Now the camera turns. *Wa-lladhīna kafarū* — "And as for those who disbelieved." And we are shown the opposite group, and we are about to hear the first thing *they* say too. Hold that — because the contrast between the first words of the grateful and the first words of the ingrate is the entire engine of this passage.

The original listeners in Makkah knew suffering as something that *ends*. Every pain they had ever known had a ceiling. The worst illness ended — in healing or in death. The worst grief faded. The worst night gave way to morning. The human nervous system is built on this one quiet certainty: **nothing lasts.** Even agony is on a timer.

These two ayahs were revealed to a people who carried that certainty in their bones. And they reach into that certainty — and remove it.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to watch for. There is a thing every human being fears more than almost anything else. We organize our entire lives around avoiding it. We build medicine around delaying it, religion around surviving it, psychology around not thinking about it.

And in these two ayahs, that very thing — the thing we flee with everything in us — becomes the thing that is **longed for, begged for, and refused.**

What is it? And why would Allah frame the withholding of *that* as the heart of the punishment?

Let's find out slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The Grammar of a Denied Exit: *lā yuqḍā ʿalayhim fa-yamūtū*

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you one small piece of Arabic, because the whole weight of this verse hangs on it.

In Arabic there is a construction the grammarians call the **fa of causation** — *fāʾ al-sababiyya*. You take an action, you put *fa* in front of the next verb, and you bend that second verb into a special form (the subjunctive). What it produces is a *result* relationship: "this happens — **so that** that would follow."

Everyday example. You say in Arabic, the equivalent of: "Visit me, *fa-* I-would-be-happy." It doesn't mean "visit me and I am happy." It means "visit me **so that** my happiness would result *from* the visit." The first thing is the cause; the second is what flows out of it.

Now — here is the move that almost no English translation can carry. What happens when you put that construction **after a negation**? When you negate the cause, you don't just delete the first thing. You delete the *result that would have flowed from it.*

Hold that, and let's open the ayah.

> لَا يُقْضَىٰ عَلَيْهِمْ فَيَمُوتُوا۟
> *Lā yuqḍā ʿalayhim fa-yamūtū.*

The word *yuqḍā* is from the root *q-ḍ-y* — to decree, to decide, to bring a matter to its conclusion, to *finish* it. It is a verb of completion. And notice it is **passive** — *yuqḍā*, "it is not decreed." Allah does not say "We do not let them die." He removes the agent from view entirely. There is simply a decree that **does not come.** A gavel that does not fall. Somewhere there is a sentence that would end the matter, and it is never read out.

And then: *fa-yamūtū* — "so that they would die." There it is. The fa of causation, after a negation.

Let's sit with what that grammar is actually doing.

It is **not** saying "they do not die." That would be a flat fact. What it is saying is deeper and far more cruel: the *thing that would cause death is never issued* — **so that the dying that would have flowed from it never arrives.** Death is sitting just on the other side of a door, and the door is the *decree*, and the decree never comes. The result is denied at its root.

Notice something. *Q-ḍ-y* is the root of *finishing*. The cruelty here is the withholding of **the end itself.** Not the withholding of comfort — the withholding of **conclusion**. In every other arena of existence, the merciful thing buried inside all suffering is that *it concludes*. Here, the very faculty of conclusion is suspended.

Al-Baghawī, in his commentary, pauses on exactly this. He links it to another scene in the Qur'an — Surah al-Zukhruf — where the people of the Fire call out: *"Yā Mālik, let your Lord finish us off"* (*li-yaqḍi ʿalaynā rabbuk*, 43:77). Same root. *Yaqḍi.* "Let Him **conclude** us." Al-Baghawī glosses it with devastating simplicity: *ay li-yaqḍi ʿalaynā al-mawt fa-nastarīḥ* — "meaning: let Him decree death upon us, **so that we may rest.**"

[PAUSE]

Read that gloss again, because it contains the whole secret. *Fa-nastarīḥ* — "so that we may **rest**." For the people of the Fire, **death has become the word for rest.** The thing the living spend their lives fleeing has become, on the other side, the only word they have left for peace. And it is the one word that gets no answer.

Al-Qurṭubī sets this verse beside its twin in Surah Ṭā Hā: *lā yamūtu fīhā wa-lā yaḥyā* — "he neither dies therein nor lives" (20:74). Neither dies — so there is no exit. Nor lives — so there is no life worth the name. Suspended exactly between the two doors, with both nailed shut.

So what has this single piece of grammar revealed? That the architecture of this punishment is not built primarily out of *fire*. It is built out of a **removed certainty** — the one certainty the whole human psyche quietly leans on: *that everything ends.* The grammar of *lā yuqḍā ʿalayhim fa-yamūtū* is the grammar of an exit that is shown to you and then sealed.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — Not Even a Lightening: *wa-lā yukhaffafu*

The ayah does not stop at sealing the exit. It anticipates the next thing a desperate mind reaches for.

When you cannot escape pain, what is the very last hope you cling to? Not escape — you've given that up. The last hope is smaller. It is: *let it just ease, even a little. Let it dip, even for one breath.* Anyone who has sat with chronic pain knows this prayer. Not "take it away." Just — *"let there be one moment where it lets go of me."*

The ayah closes that door too.

> وَلَا يُخَفَّفُ عَنْهُم مِّنْ عَذَابِهَا
> *wa-lā yukhaffafu ʿanhum min ʿadhābihā.*

The verb is *yukhaffafu*, from *kh-f-f* — lightness. But look at the **shape** of the word. The middle letter is doubled — *khaffafa*. This is what Arabic calls **Form II**, and one of its core functions is *factitive*: to take a quality and turn it into the *act of making something have that quality.* *Khaffa* means "to be light." *Khaffafa* means "to **make** light," to lighten, to reduce a weight.

So the verb itself is already the verb of *easing a burden*. And it arrives, again, **negated** and **passive**: "**nor is it made-light for them.**" No hand reaches in to lift even an ounce of the load. And there is a small word doing enormous work here: *min*. *Min ʿadhābihā* — "**of** its torment." In Arabic this *min* is partitive; it means "**a part** of." The denial is not "the torment is not lightened." It is sharper: "**not even a portion** of it is lightened." Not a fraction. Not one degree.

Stay with the precision of that. The ayah has now sealed two doors in sequence:

- *Lā yuqḍā ʿalayhim fa-yamūtū* — you cannot get **out**.
- *Wa-lā yukhaffafu ʿanhum* — and it will not get **lighter**.

No exit through the wall, and no give in the ceiling. Al-Qurṭubī pairs this with the verse: *"every time their skins are roasted through, We replace them with fresh skins, that they may taste the punishment"* (4:56) — a renewal that exists precisely to defeat the body's one mercy, which is that nerves eventually go numb. Even *numbness* — the body's built-in mercy — is overridden.

Here is what I want you to feel about Element One and Element Two together. They are a closing of every standard human strategy for surviving pain. We survive pain in this life through three doors: *it will end* (death), *it will ease* (relief), or *I will go numb* (habituation). The ayah names all three and bolts each one.

[PAUSE]

And now — having shown us the shape of the punishment — the ayah turns and tells us **what it is a punishment for.** And this is where the deepest word waits.

### Element Three — The Word That Closes the Verse: *kulla kafūr*

The ayah ends:

> كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى كُلَّ كَفُورٍ
> *Kadhālika najzī kulla kafūr.*

"Thus do We recompense every *kafūr*."

Now I need you to notice something about how this single ayah is *built*, because Allah has done something exquisite with one root from one end of the verse to the other.

Look at how the ayah **opened**: *wa-lladhīna **kafarū*** — "those who **disbelieved**." That is a **verb**. From the root *k-f-r*. An action they did.

Look at how the ayah **closes**: *kulla **kafūr*** — "every **kafūr**." Same root. *K-f-r.* But it is no longer a verb. It is now a **noun of character**, and not the ordinary one.

The ordinary word would have been *kāfir* — "a disbeliever," one who does the act. Allah does not use it. He uses *kafūr* — a form the Arabs call **mubālagha**, the form of *intensification*, the *faʿūl* pattern. It does not describe someone who *did* a thing once. It describes someone in whom the thing has become a **trait, a saturation, a defining grain.**

And here is the secret the lexicon hands us. The root *k-f-r* does not first mean "disbelief." Its oldest, most physical meaning is **to cover, to bury, to conceal.** The farmer who buries a seed in the soil is, in classical Arabic, a *kāfir* — because he *covers* the seed. From that comes the whole family of meanings. To *kufr* a blessing is to **bury it** — to cover over a gift so completely that no trace of acknowledgment shows on the surface.

So *kafūr* — the intensive — is not primarily "the great unbeliever." Its taproot is **the great ingrate.** The one who takes gift after gift after gift and *buries* every one. Whose entire orientation is the covering-over of what he was given.

[PAUSE]

Feel the arc Allah has built inside one ayah. He opens with the *act* — *kafarū*, they disbelieved. He closes with the *essence* — *kafūr*, the saturated ingrate. The verse begins with a verb and ends with a soul. It begins with what they *did* and ends with what they *became*.

And notice what He chose to put on the scale at the moment of recompense. He did not say "thus do We recompense every *kāfir*" — every denier of doctrine. He said **"thus do We recompense every *kafūr*"** — every *ingrate*. At the precise instant of justice, the Qur'an renames the crime. The thing being weighed is not, at bottom, an intellectual error. It is **a relationship to gift.** It is the burying of what you were given.

Hold that word — *ingratitude* — because the next ayah is about to tell us *exactly which gift* was buried.

### Bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three things in sequence. It has shown us an **exit sealed** — death decreed never to come. It has shown us a **ceiling that will not give** — not a portion of the torment lightened. And it has shown us, in one root traveling from verb to essence, that the thing on trial is not a debate that was lost but a **gift that was buried** — *kafūr*, the ingrate.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About death. About time. About the strange thing we are doing right now with the one life we were given.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — When the Thing We Flee Becomes the Thing We Beg For

Let me start where you actually live.

Psychologists have a name for one of the deepest engines of human behavior. They call it **terror management.** The theory, plainly, is this: underneath an enormous amount of what human beings do all day — the achieving, the distracting, the building, the scrolling — is a quiet, ceaseless work of *not thinking about death.* We are the one creature that knows it will die, and most of the machinery of a human life is, in some part, a machine for keeping that knowledge at arm's length.

You don't need a study to confirm it. Notice your own flinch when a near-miss happens on the road. Notice how the body floods with chemicals whose entire purpose is *away from death, at any cost.* Every cell you have was shaped by billions of years of one instruction: **survive.** Death is the thing the whole organism is built to refuse.

Now hold that lifelong, full-body refusal — and read the cry in verse 37.

> رَبَّنَآ أَخْرِجْنَا
> *Rabbanā akhrijnā* — "Our Lord, take us **out.**"

And read what al-Baghawī told us they are *really* asking in its sister-verse: *li-yaqḍi ʿalaynā rabbuk* — *"let your Lord finish us off."* **They are begging to die.**

[PAUSE]

Let that inversion land with its full weight. The same beings who, in this life, would have done *anything* to add one more day — who feared the grave above all things — are now, on the other side, using their voices to **beg for the grave** and being refused.

This is the istidlāl, the pointing, that Allah is doing. He takes the one experience *every* human shares — the bone-deep terror of death — and He inverts it to show you something you could never see from the inside of this life: that **death was a mercy.** That the timer on suffering, the conclusion built into every pain, the fact that everything *ends* — that was not a flaw in the world. It was a **kindness** woven into the fabric of the creation. We spent our whole lives fleeing the very thing that was, quietly, protecting us — the exit. And the punishment of the Fire is, at its core, *the removal of that kindness.* The world without its mercy of endings.

Here is the movement I want you to feel: the people of the Fire have not been given a *new* kind of pain humans never knew. They have been given an *ordinary* pain — but stripped of the three mercies that, in this life, surround all pain like a cushion: *it will end, it will ease, I will adjust.* Hell is not the invention of a new agony. **Hell is the subtraction of the mercies we never noticed were holding us.**

And that should do something to how you walk through *this* day. Because right now you are living inside those mercies. Right now, every hard thing you carry has a ceiling. Every grief is on a timer. Every pain you have will, by the design of the One who made the design, *end.* The people of the Fire would give the whole world for one of the small mercies you are, at this moment, taking entirely for granted.

What changes tomorrow? This: the next time a hardship presses on you, and some part of you cries *"this is unbearable, this will never end"* — catch it. It **will** end. The endedness of your suffering is itself a gift, and a sign, and a foretaste of mercy. Let the existence of *exits* in your life become a thing you actively thank God for. That is the opposite of *kafūr.* That is the burying-of-gifts run in reverse.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two — The Gift That Was Buried Was Time

We left Element Three with a question: the crime being weighed is *ingratitude* — the burying of a gift. **Which gift?**

Verse 37 answers it directly. After their plea to be let out, Allah responds — and His response is not a description of their sins. It is a single, quiet, devastating question:

> أَوَلَمْ نُعَمِّرْكُم مَّا يَتَذَكَّرُ فِيهِ مَن تَذَكَّرَ
> *A-wa-lam nuʿammirkum mā yatadhakkaru fīhi man tadhakkar?*
> "Did We not give you a life **long enough** for whoever would take heed to take heed?"

The verb *nuʿammirkum* is from *ʿumr* — a lifespan, the length of one's days. And again it is built on that doubled, **Form II** shape — *ʿammara* — the verb of *granting* a length of life, of *extending* someone's days. "Did We not **grant you the years**?"

Start where you live again. You know the experience of being given *enough time* and wasting it. The exam you had a whole semester to prepare for. The relationship you had years to tend and let go cold. The conversation with someone now gone that you kept meaning to have. The particular flavor of that regret is unlike any other — because it is not the regret of *"I never had the chance."* It is the far heavier regret of *"I had nothing but chance, and I buried it."*

[PAUSE]

That is the regret Allah names here. Notice the dignity and the terror of the question all at once. He does not say "you ran out of time." He says: *"Did We not give you a life long enough for **whoever would take heed** to take heed?"* The clause is exquisite. *Man tadhakkar* — "whoever would take heed." The verb *tadhakkara* is a **Form V** verb, the reflexive shape — it is not just to *remember a fact*, it is to *take something into yourself*, to let it turn you. And the ayah's claim is breathtaking in its fairness: the lifespan you were given was *calibrated.* It was **long enough that anyone who was ever going to wake up, would have had the time to wake up.** The gift was not stingy. The window was not narrow. *Whoever would take heed had time to take heed.*

And then the second gift, named in the same breath:

> وَجَآءَكُمُ ٱلنَّذِيرُ
> *Wa-jāʾakumu al-nadhīr* — "And the warner **came** to you."

So it was not even time alone. It was time *plus* a voice. The years to wake up, **and** someone sent to wake you. The lamp was given, and the hand to light it was sent.

This is why the verse closes the way it does — and why the earlier word *kafūr* was so exactly chosen. Look at the recompense again now that you know what the gift was:

> فَذُوقُوا۟ فَمَا لِلظَّـٰلِمِينَ مِن نَّصِيرٍ
> *Fa-dhūqū — fa-mā li-ẓ-ẓālimīna min naṣīr.*
> "So **taste** — for the wrongdoers there is no helper."

*Taste.* The word of one who is *experiencing the consequence of his own action.* The Fire is framed, start to finish, not as an arbitrary cruelty imposed from outside, but as the **recompense of an ingrate** — *najzī kulla kafūr* — one who was handed a calibrated lifespan and a warner to walk beside it, and **buried** both. Covered them over. Lived as though neither had been given.

[PAUSE]

Now turn it on yourself, gently, because this is the whole point of the ayah. You are, right now, *inside* the gift the people of the Fire are weeping for. You have *ʿumr* — a lifespan still running. You have *al-nadhīr* — the warning has reached you; you are reading the very words of it this moment. Everything the people of the Fire are begging to be sent back to have — **you have it.** This day is the exact thing they would trade the universe for. *Rabbanā akhrijnā naʿmal ṣāliḥan* — "send us back, we will act righteously." You have not been sent back. You were **never taken away.** You are *still here.*

What changes tomorrow? Make it concrete, because *kafūr* is defeated only in the concrete. Take one gift you have been burying — one relationship you've let go cold, one act of worship you keep deferring, one wrong you keep meaning to put right, one person you keep meaning to forgive — and **uncover it.** Today. Not as a grand reformation. As one small, deliberate refusal to be the one who buries what he was given. That single act is you standing on the right side of this verse while the standing is still possible.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Watch how the two halves of this passage are the same truth approached from two directions.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a punishment built by *subtraction* — an exit decreed never to come, a torment of which not a portion is lightened, and a root, *k-f-r*, traveling from the *act* of disbelief to the *essence* of the ingrate. The crime, at the moment of the scale, is named **the burying of gifts.**

The **thematic journey** then named the two buried gifts exactly: the **mercy of endings** — death, the timer on all suffering, that we spend our lives fleeing and will one day beg for — and the **gift of time-plus-a-warner**, a calibrated lifespan long enough for anyone who would wake to wake, walked beside by a voice sent to wake them.

And here is where the two directions meet. **The punishment is the gifts inverted.** In the Fire, *endings* are withheld — the very mercy of death that the ingrate never thanked God for is now the thing refused to him. And the cry to be *given more time* — *akhrijnā* — is met with the reminder that he was already given exactly enough. The thing he buried in life is the precise thing denied to him in death. The justice is not arbitrary. **It is the shape of his own ingratitude handed back to him.**

That is why the surah set this scene against verse 34. The people of the Garden walk in saying *al-ḥamd* — **thank You.** The people of the Fire are defined as *kafūr* — those who **buried** what there was to thank Him for. The entire distance between the two eternities is the distance between the one who *uncovered* the gifts and the one who *buried* them.

### Questions to carry

- When something hard presses on me and I think *"this will never end"* — can I learn to feel the *endedness* of my suffering as itself a mercy I've been refusing to thank Him for?
- What gift, right now, am I *burying* — covering over, living as though it were not given?
- The people of the Fire are begging for one ordinary day exactly like the one I am about to waste. What would I do with this day if I truly believed that?
- I have been given *ʿumr* and *al-nadhīr* — a lifespan and the warning. Which one am I quietly assuming I'll always have more of?
- Is my first instinct, when I receive something, closer to *al-ḥamd* — or closer to burying it and moving on?

### One-sentence distillation

The torment of the Fire is the mercies of this life subtracted — the exit of death and the gift of time withheld from the one who, when he had them, buried them instead of giving thanks.

### The closing invitation

You are not being asked to fear the Fire into paralysis. You are being invited into the *opposite* of *kafūr* — into the uncovering of gifts. The simplest, truest response to these two ayahs is not dread. It is to look up from these words and **notice what you are holding** — the breath, the time, the warning that just reached you — and to let one small act of gratitude, one buried thing brought back into the light, mark you as someone standing on the right side of this verse while the standing is still possible.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who decreed an end to every pain as a mercy and gave us years long enough to wake — do not let us be among those who bury Your gifts. Make our first instinct *al-ḥamd*, and let the warning that has reached us tonight be one we *took heed of*, while we are still here to take heed. Āmīn.
