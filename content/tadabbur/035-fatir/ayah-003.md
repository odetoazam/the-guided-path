# Is There Any Other? The Creator Who Never Stops Feeding You

## Surah Fatir, Ayah 3

> [ayah:35:3] يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ ٱذْكُرُوا۟ نِعْمَتَ ٱللَّهِ عَلَيْكُمْ ۚ هَلْ مِنْ خَـٰلِقٍ غَيْرُ ٱللَّهِ يَرْزُقُكُم مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ لَآ إِلَـٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ ۖ فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ

*Yā ayyuhā an-nās, udhkurū niʿmata-llāhi ʿalaykum. Hal min khāliqin ghayru-llāhi yarzuqukum mina-s-samāʾi wa-l-ard. Lā ilāha illā huw, fa-annā tuʾfakūn.*

**O mankind! Remember the favor of Allah upon you. Is there any creator other than Allah who provides for you from the sky and the earth? There is no god but He. Then how are you deluded?**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question No One Can Answer

I want to begin not with the ayah, but with a strange fact about the people this ayah was first recited to.

Picture the streets of Mecca. The merchants who passed the Prophet ﷺ in the marketplace, the men who sat by the Kaʿbah, the families who would later march out to fight him at Badr. If you had stopped one of them — even the most hardened opponent of the message — and asked him a single question: *Who created the heavens and the earth?* — do you know what he would have said?

He would have said: **Allah.**

The Quran tells us this directly elsewhere. *"And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will surely say: Allah."* (29:61) *"Say: Who provides for you from the sky and the earth... they will say: Allah."* (10:31) The mushrikūn of Mecca were not atheists. They were not confused about who made the world. They knew. They would tell you, without hesitation, that there was one Creator, one Originator, one Provider.

[PAUSE]

And yet — they bowed to stones. They circled idols. They poured their hopes and fears and sacrifices into objects they had carved with their own hands.

So here is the puzzle that haunts the background of this ayah, and I want you to feel its weight before we go a single word further. **How does a person admit, with full clarity, that there is only one Creator — and then live as though there were many gods?** How do you hold the truth in your head and miss it with your entire life?

That is not an ancient problem. That is the most modern problem there is. And this ayah — twenty-one words — walks straight up to that puzzle, names it, and then ends with a question so sharp it has never stopped echoing: ***fa-annā tuʾfakūn*** — *then how are you deluded? How are you turned away? What is twisting you off the path you already know is true?*

Before we open it, let me build the room we need to stand in.

### The world of Surah Fatir

This surah begins with one of the most majestic openings in the Quran. *Al-hamdu lillāhi fātiri-s-samāwāti wa-l-ard* — "All praise belongs to Allah, the **Fātir** of the heavens and the earth." The word *Fātir* comes from a root meaning to split, to cleave, to break something open into existence for the first time — the way a seed splits, the way dawn cracks the dark. Allah is the One who tore the cosmos open into being from nothing.

Then ayah 2 makes a claim about that same God's mercy: *whatever Allah opens to mankind of mercy, none can withhold it; and whatever He withholds, none can release it after Him.* The hand that opens, the hand that closes — and no other hand in the universe can override either one.

So by the time we reach ayah 3, two things have been established: Allah originated everything, and Allah alone controls the flow of every mercy. And now — [PAUSE] — the surah turns. It stops describing God to us and turns to *face* us. **"Yā ayyuhā an-nās"** — O mankind. Not "O you who believe." Not the Meccans only. All of humanity. Every single person who would ever hear these words, including you, tonight.

And to all of us, it issues a command, asks a question, states a truth, and then asks one final question that we are meant to carry out of the room and into the rest of our lives.

Let's earn it, word by word.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: "Remember" — and the secret meaning the scholars found inside it

The ayah opens its command with one word: **ٱذْكُرُوا۟** — *udhkurū* — "Remember."

Now, before we touch what it means here, let me teach you something about how this word works in Arabic, because it will change what you hear.

The root is **dh-k-r** (ذكر). In ordinary use, it covers two things that English splits apart: to *remember* something internally — to bring it back to mind — and to *mention* something out loud, to speak it. One root, both meanings. To remember and to mention are, in the Arabic imagination, almost the same act: the truth that lives in the heart wanting to come out on the tongue.

So when Allah says *udhkurū niʿmata-llāhi* — "remember the favor of Allah" — at the simplest level He is telling us: bring the blessing back to mind. Don't let it slip into the background. Notice it.

But now sit with something the great commentator **al-Qurtubi** observed about this exact word in this exact ayah. He wrote that the meaning of this *dhikr* — this "remembering" — is **shukr**. *Gratitude.* "Remember the favor of Allah" means: **be thankful for it.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land, because it is doing real work. Why would "remember" mean "be grateful"? Because Allah is telling us something profound about the architecture of the human heart: **gratitude begins with attention.** You cannot thank for what you have not noticed. The reason we are ungrateful is almost never that we have decided the blessings don't matter. It is that we have *stopped seeing them.* They have faded into the wallpaper of our lives.

And so the command is not first "feel thankful" — you cannot order a feeling into existence. The command is *remember*. *Notice.* Bring it back into focus. And gratitude follows attention the way warmth follows a fire. Al-Qurtubi saw that the one word contains the whole sequence: notice it → and thanks will come.

And **al-Muyassar** widens it for us beautifully — *remember the favor of Allah upon you with your hearts, your tongues, and your limbs.* Three registers. The heart that recognizes. The tongue that says it aloud — *al-hamdu lillāh*. And the limbs that obey, that bow, that give, that live as though the blessing were real. The remembering that this ayah wants is not a passing thought. It is a thought that travels from the heart, into speech, and out into a changed life.

So already, before we have left the first verb, we have a complete diagnosis and prescription: the disease is forgetting; the cure is remembering; and the proof of the cure is gratitude lived in three places at once.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: "Is there ANY creator?" — the tiny letter that slams the door

Now we come to the heart of the ayah, and to a feature of Arabic so small you would walk right past it in translation — but the classical scholars built towers on it.

The ayah asks: **هَلْ مِنْ خَـٰلِقٍ غَيْرُ ٱللَّهِ** — *hal min khāliqin ghayru-llāh* — "Is there **any** creator other than Allah?"

Let me teach you the grammar first, gently.

In Arabic there is a tiny word, **مِنْ** (*min*), that usually means "from." But sometimes it appears in a sentence where it does not mean "from" at all — where, grammatically, you could lift it out and the sentence would still stand. Scholars call this **min zāʾidah** — a *min* that is "extra." But — and this is the crucial part — "extra" does not mean *useless.* It is the opposite. In Arabic, when a particle is grammatically unnecessary and Allah includes it anyway, it is there for **emphasis**. It is a thickening of meaning.

Here is an everyday way to feel it. There is a difference between asking *"Is there a problem?"* and asking *"Is there **any** problem **at all**?"* The second version isn't just longer — it sweeps wider. It reaches into every corner. It dares you to find even one exception.

That is exactly what this *min* does. Both **al-Jalalayn** and **al-Baghawi** identify it explicitly: *"min" here is zāʾidah* — extra, for emphasis. So the question is not merely "Is there a creator besides Allah?" It is **"Is there any creator whatsoever, of any kind, anywhere, other than Allah?"** The little word throws the net over the entire universe.

And now the second half of the grammar. This is a question — *hal*, "is there?" — but al-Jalalayn and al-Baghawi both tell us it is a specific kind of question: an **istifhām taqrīrī**. A *rhetorical question asked not to seek information, but to force an admission.* Al-Baghawi spells out what it really means: *"ka-annahu qāla: lā khāliqa ghayru-llāh"* — **it is as if He said: there is no creator other than Allah.**

[PAUSE]

Feel what just happened. Allah did not *tell* them "there is no other creator." He **asked** them — and built the question so that the only honest answer is the one He wants. Because remember who is being asked: people who already admit Allah is the Creator. He has trapped them inside their own knowledge. He hands them the question and says, in effect: *you answer it. Go ahead. Name one other creator. Find me a single one.*

And there is silence. There has always been silence. No one, in fourteen centuries, has stepped forward to fill that blank.

This is why the rhetorical question is so much more powerful than a statement. A statement you can argue with. A statement comes from outside you and you can push back against it. But this question makes you reach into your *own* mind, pull out the answer that is already sitting there, and say it yourself. **The conviction it produces is not borrowed. It is yours.** Allah does not impose tawhid on the mushrik here — He makes the mushrik convict himself with his own admission.

### The two readings — one truth from two angles

There is one more layer here, and it is a place where the early reciters of the Quran read the word two slightly different ways — and both are preserved, and both are true.

The word **غَيْرُ** (*ghayr*) — "other than." Al-Baghawi reports that **Hamza and al-Kisāʾī** read it as *ghayri* (with a kasra, the *jarr* case), while the other reciters read *ghayru* (with a damma, the *raf* case). Al-Qurtubi unpacks the grammar: in the *raf* reading, *ghayru* can carry the sense of "Is there any creator **except** Allah?" — treating it almost like the word "except," the exception that proves there is only One. In the other case-reading, *ghayri* functions as a description attached to "creator."

Now — do not hear these as two competing meanings fighting for the prize. Hear them the way the tradition hears them: as **two camera angles on one reality.** One angle frames it as exception — *no creator stands outside of Him.* The other frames it as description — *there is no creator who could be described as "other than Him."* Both arrive at the identical destination: the Creator is One, and the word, read either way, closes every exit. The Quran's own recitation traditions surround the truth from two sides so that no reading leaves a gap.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: From "Creator" to "provides" — the shift from a noun to a living verb

Now watch something in the structure of the sentence that, once you see it, you cannot un-see.

The ayah says: *hal min **khāliqin**... **yarzuqukum***. "Is there any **creator**... who **provides** for you."

Look carefully at those two words. **خَـٰلِقٍ** — *khāliq*, "creator" — is a **noun.** Specifically, it is an *ism fāʿil*, a noun of the doer — like saying "a maker," "a builder." It names an *identity.* It is static. It sits still. And **يَرْزُقُكُم** — *yarzuqukum*, "provides for you" — is a **verb**, and not just any verb: it is in the *muḍāriʿ*, the **present-ongoing tense.** The tense of action happening *now*, and now, and still now — renewing in every moment.

Why does this matter? Let me teach you the difference these two grammatical forms carry.

A noun, in Arabic, suggests permanence, fixedness, a settled fact. A present-tense verb suggests motion, renewal, something being done continuously. So when Allah calls Himself "**Creator**" with a noun, He is naming a *finished identity* — creation, in one sense, is a done thing. You were created once. The act is complete; the title is permanent. But when He describes provision with a *verb in the present tense* — *yarzuqukum, He provides for you* — He is describing something that **never stops happening.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that contrast. Your creation was a single event. Your provision is a ceaseless act. Right now, as you hear these words, your lungs are being given air you did not manufacture. Your heart is being given the next beat. The sun is being held at the exact distance that keeps you alive. The earth beneath you is being held together. **The verb is in the present tense because the giving is in the present tense — it has not paused for a single second of your life.**

The grammar is preaching. "Creator" tells you *who* He is. "Provides" tells you what He is *doing for you, this instant.* He did not make you and walk away. The relationship did not end at your creation — it is happening live, in the tense that never closes.

### "From the sky and the earth"

And where does this ceaseless provision come from? **مِّنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ** — "from the sky and the earth."

The commentators fill this in concretely. Al-Jalalayn: *from the sky — the rain; and from the earth — the plants.* Al-Muyassar widens it: *from the sky, rain; and from the earth, water and minerals and more.* Rain descending from above; the green and the gold rising up from below.

But now lift your eyes from the literal image to what it is *pointing at* — because this is a stepping stone, not the destination. Picture where you actually stand in this ayah. You are suspended **between two sources of life, and you command neither one.** Above you is a sky you cannot reach — you cannot order the rain, cannot summon a single cloud. Below you is an earth whose fertility you did not write into the seed — you can plant, but you cannot make it grow; you bury a seed in the dark and something you do not control decides whether it lives.

Provision comes down at you from a height you cannot climb to, and up at you from a depth you cannot author. You are *encircled* by a giving that surrounds you on every axis — above and below — and originates entirely outside your power.

[PAUSE]

That is the unseen reality the physical image was carrying all along: **total, inescapable dependence.** Not dependence as an idea you nod at, but dependence you are physically standing inside of, right now, hemmed in by gifts from a sky and an earth that answer to Someone other than you. The ayah does not argue that you are dependent. It *places you* between the two and lets you feel the walls.

### The last word: *tuʾfakūn* — the word that sounds like what it does

And then the ayah ends with a question that twists like the thing it describes: **فَأَنَّىٰ تُؤْفَكُونَ** — *fa-annā tuʾfakūn* — "Then how are you deluded? How are you turned away?"

Two things about this closing word.

First, its **sound.** Say it: *tuʾfakūn.* There is a catch in the throat at the hamza, then the hard *f-k* — a sound that jolts, that turns sharply. The word does not flow smoothly off the tongue; it *twists.* And that is fitting, because the root **A-f-k** (أفك) means precisely to turn something away from its proper face, to overturn it, to flip a thing upside-down from what it should be. It is the same root as **ifk** — a slander, a lie — because a lie is exactly that: the truth *turned over,* flipped onto its back. The word sounds like a wrenching, because it names a wrenching: the soul wrenched off the obvious path of truth.

Second — and notice this carefully — the verb is **passive.** Not "how do you *turn away*," but "how *are you turned* away." *Tuʾfakūn.* And Allah does not name who is doing the turning. The agent is hidden. [PAUSE] And this absence speaks. It leaves the question hanging open over your own life: *what is it that turns you?* What unseen pressure pulls a person who knows the truth off the path of the truth? Is it desire? Habit? The crowd? A whisper? The ayah refuses to fill in the blank, because the blank is meant for you to fill — by looking honestly at what, in your own case, keeps turning you.

Al-Jalalayn captures the full force: *fa-annā tuʾfakūn* — "from where are you turned away from His tawhīd, **while you yourselves admit that He is the Creator, the Provider?**" That is the sting. The question is not asked of someone in the dark. It is asked of someone holding the light and walking away from it anyway.

### Bridge

So look at what these twenty-one words have built with their grammar alone. A command that means "notice, and thanks will follow." A tiny "extra" letter that sweeps the whole universe for a rival and finds none. A rhetorical question that makes you convict yourself with your own knowledge. A noun for a finished creation and a living present-tense verb for a giving that never stops. A sky and an earth you stand helpless between. And a final twisting, passive, agentless word that turns the whole ayah around to face *you* and asks: so what is turning you?

Now let us ask what all this reveals not just about words — but about the strange wiring of the human heart.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Why we stop seeing the gift — and how remembering rescues us

Let me start where you already live.

There is a phenomenon that psychologists have a name for: **hedonic adaptation.** It is the well-documented tendency of human beings to return to a baseline of feeling no matter what happens to them. The new car that thrilled you for a week becomes, by the second month, just *the car.* The raise you celebrated becomes the salary you grumble about. The health you prayed for when you were sick becomes invisible the moment you are well. We adapt. The extraordinary becomes ordinary. The gift becomes the floor.

This is not a moral failing in the simple sense. It is closer to a feature of perception. Your nervous system is built to notice *change* and to filter out the constant. The refrigerator's hum disappears from your hearing within minutes — not because it stopped, but because your brain decided it was not worth flagging anymore. **And the very things most essential to your life are the most constant — so they are exactly the things you are wired to stop perceiving.** Your breath. Your heartbeat. The sun. The fact that you woke up this morning with a working mind.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again, and hear how precisely it is built for this exact flaw in us. It does not begin "Be grateful," because gratitude cannot be commanded directly into a heart that has gone blind to its blessings. It begins **"udhkurū"** — *remember. Notice. Bring it back into view.* Allah is not naive about how we work. He knows the gift has faded into the wallpaper. So He does not scold us for not feeling thankful — He commands the one act that *makes* thankfulness possible: He commands us to *look again.*

This is why al-Qurtubi could say the *dhikr* here means *shukr.* Because for a human being, attention is the doorway and gratitude is the room. You do not feel grateful and then notice. You notice — really notice, with the heart, the tongue, and the limbs that al-Muyassar named — and the gratitude floods in behind it. The ayah is a prescription written by the One who designed the patient.

And watch how the rest of the ayah is engineered to *break the spell of the ordinary.* Look at what it points you to: not some rare, dramatic blessing — but the most constant, most invisible thing of all. *Provision from the sky and the earth.* Rain. Food. The most adapted-to, taken-for-granted facts of your existence. Allah deliberately aims your attention at the blessings you have stopped seeing precisely *because* you have them every single day. He is reaching into the wallpaper and pulling the gift back out and holding it in front of your face: *look — this. The food on your plate that you barely glance at. Who is doing that, right now, in the present tense?*

[PAUSE]

So here is what this changes about tomorrow, concretely. Gratitude is not a mood you wait to feel. It is a *practice of re-noticing,* and the ayah commands it as an action. Tomorrow morning, before the day swallows you, take one constant thing you have completely stopped seeing — the first breath, the working of your eyes, the water from the tap — and *remember* it on purpose. Name it on the tongue: *al-hamdu lillāh.* That is not a small piety. That is you obeying *udhkurū.* That is you defeating the very flaw in your perception that the ayah was sent to heal. Remembering is the rescue. And the strange mercy of it is this: the more you remember, the more you have, because a blessing you have stopped seeing is, to your heart, a blessing you do not really possess.

## Theme Two: The man who knows the truth and lives as if he didn't

Now we come back to the puzzle I opened with — and it is the deepest thing in this ayah.

Let me bring it into our world first.

Think of how many things you *know* and do not *live.* You know that scrolling late at night wrecks your sleep — and your thumb is on the screen at midnight anyway. You know that the relationships matter more than the work — and the work eats the relationships alive. You know, in some clear chamber of your mind, exactly what is good for you, and you watch yourself walk in the opposite direction. There is a gap in the human being — a real, structural gap — between **what we know to be true** and **what we actually do.** Knowledge sits in one room of the soul. Action lives in another. And they are not always on speaking terms.

[PAUSE]

This is the gap the ayah walks straight into. Because the Meccans were not people who lacked the information. I told you at the start: ask them who created the sky and the earth, and they say *Allah.* The knowledge was intact. The doctrine, in their heads, was almost correct. And yet their *lives* — their bowing, their sacrifices, their fears, their hopes — were poured out to idols. **They affirmed one Creator with their minds and served many gods with their bodies.** The gap, in their case, ran straight down the middle of the soul.

And this is why the ayah ends not with an argument but with that wounded, almost incredulous question: ***fa-annā tuʾfakūn*** — *then how are you turned away?* Read the tone. It is not the tone of someone presenting evidence to a skeptic. It is the tone of someone watching a person walk off a cliff with the map open in his hand. *You know. You've already admitted it. There is no other creator — you said so yourself. So what on earth is turning you?* The astonishment in the question is the whole point: the tragedy is not ignorance. **The tragedy is knowing and being turned anyway.**

And here is where it stops being about seventh-century Mecca and becomes about us — because we are *experts* at this gap. The believer who knows, with total certainty, that Allah is the only Provider — *yarzuqukum,* He provides for you, in the present tense, ceaselessly — and who then lies awake sick with anxiety over money, as though the rizq depended on his own trembling effort and on no One else. **That is the ifk in modern dress.** That is being *turned.* You have the tawhid in your creed and a functional polytheism in your nervous system — a heart that bows to the job, to the bank balance, to the approval of people, while the tongue recites *lā ilāha illā huw.* The Meccan bowed to a stone idol. We have subtler ones. But the geometry of the turning is identical.

[PAUSE]

Notice, again, that the verb is **passive and agentless** — *tuʾfakūn,* "you *are* turned." The ayah will not name the force for you, and that is a mercy, because the force is different for each of us. For one person it is desire. For another, the crowd — the terror of being the odd one out. For another, simple habit, the path worn so deep that the soul rolls down it without choosing. The ayah hands you the blank and asks you to fill it honestly: *what, specifically, turns me? What is the idol that my creed denies but my calendar confesses?*

And the cure is woven right back into the start of the ayah. How do you close the gap between knowing and living? The same way you close the gap between having and being grateful: ***udhkurū.*** *Remember.* The reason knowledge fails to move us is that it goes cold — it sits in the mind, un-revisited, un-felt, until it has all the force of a fact about a country we will never visit. The remedy is to *keep bringing it back to the heart* until it is warm again, until it is not just known but *felt,* until *yarzuqukum* — He provides for you, right now — stops being a sentence you believe and becomes the air you breathe. Knowledge that is constantly remembered becomes conviction. Conviction is what finally closes the gap and stops the turning.

So what changes tomorrow? This: when you catch yourself anxious, or chasing, or bowing inwardly to something small — pause and *name what you already know.* "There is no creator but Allah. He is providing for me from the sky and the earth, right now, in the present tense. So why am I turned toward this?" You are not learning something new in that moment. You are *remembering* something true — and dragging your living, finally, back into line with your knowing.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The architecture: a circle that closes on you

Step back now and see the whole shape of these twenty-one words, because they are built as a perfect circle.

It **opens** with a command about the human interior: *remember.* It **moves** to the unanswerable question that sweeps the universe: *is there any creator other than Allah?* — and finds none. It **arrives** at the bedrock declaration: *lā ilāha illā huw* — there is no god but He. And then it **closes** by turning back to the human interior it began with: *so how are you turned away?*

Remember → recognize → affirm → and then the demand that you live it. The ayah begins inside you and ends inside you, and in between it walks you through the entire universe and back, so that when the final question lands — *fa-annā tuʾfakūn* — you have no exit left. Every door has been closed by your own admissions. The circle has no gap in it. It closes on you.

## The two discoveries are one discovery

And see now that the linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same truth approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a **noun** for creation and a **living present-tense verb** for provision — *He provides, now, ceaselessly.* And the themes showed us a human heart that **stops seeing** the constant gift and **fails to live** the truth it knows. Put them together and the whole ayah snaps into focus: **the giving never stops — but the noticing does.** The Provider is in the present tense; our gratitude is stuck in the past. The truth is permanent; our living of it flickers. The disease, in both halves, is the same — *forgetting* — and the cure, in both halves, is the same single word the ayah opens with: **udhkurū.** *Remember.* Remember, and the gratitude returns. Remember, and the turning stops.

## Questions to carry

- What constant, daily blessing have I completely stopped seeing — and what would it do to my heart to *remember* it on purpose tomorrow?
- I affirm "there is no god but He" with my tongue. What does my anxiety, my chasing, my fear of people's opinions confess that I actually bow to?
- The verb was passive — *you are turned.* In my own life, what specifically is the force that turns me off the path I already know is true?
- His provision is in the present tense, ceaseless, this very second. Do I *live* as though that were true — or only believe it as a fact filed away in my mind?
- If gratitude begins with attention, where is my attention actually going all day — and is it pointed at the Giver, or only at the gifts, or at neither?

## One-sentence distillation

The One who created you once and feeds you ceaselessly — in the present tense, from a sky you cannot reach and an earth you cannot command — is the only One there is to worship, and you already know it; so the only real question left is *what keeps turning you away.*

## The closing invitation

This ayah does not ask you to believe something new. It asks you to *stop forgetting* something you already hold. You do not need to be convinced that Allah is the Creator — like the Meccans, some part of you already knows. The invitation is gentler and harder than belief: it is to *remember,* daily, deliberately, until the truth in your head finally reaches your hands and your fears and your calendar — until *yarzuqukum* is not a creed you recite but a peace you live inside. Let one taken-for-granted blessing be re-seen tomorrow. Let one anxious moment be met with "there is no god but He, and He is providing for me right now." That is the turning *back.* That is the whole ayah, obeyed.

## A brief duʿā

O Allah, *Fātir* of the heavens and the earth, You who provide for me from a sky I cannot reach and an earth I cannot command — keep me from forgetting. Let me see the gifts I have stopped seeing, and thank You with my heart, my tongue, and my limbs. And when something rises to turn me off the path I know is true, remind me, before I take a single step, that there is no god but You. *Āmīn.*
