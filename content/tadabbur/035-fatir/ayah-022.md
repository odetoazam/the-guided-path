# The Living and the Dead: When the Heart Cannot Hear

## INTRODUCTION

Let's begin by hearing the ayah.

> **وَمَا يَسْتَوِي ٱلْأَحْيَآءُ وَلَا ٱلْأَمْوَٰتُ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُسْمِعُ مَن يَشَآءُ ۖ وَمَآ أَنتَ بِمُسْمِعٍ مَّن فِى ٱلْقُبُورِ**

*Wa mā yastawī al-aḥyāʾu wa lā al-amwāt. Inna Allāha yusmiʿu man yashāʾ, wa mā anta bi-musmiʿin man fī al-qubūr.*

**"Nor are the living and the dead alike. Indeed, Allah causes to hear whom He wills, and you cannot make those in the graves hear."**

[PAUSE]

### The world this ayah enters

Picture this. A man has been speaking for over a decade. He has spoken in the heat of the day and in the cold of the night. He has spoken to family who turned away, to neighbors who threw stones, to chieftains who laughed, to slaves who wept and believed. He has buried his wife. He has buried his uncle. He has walked back from Ṭāʾif with blood in his sandals. And still — *still* — he keeps speaking, because the people in front of him are sliding off a cliff and he can see it and they cannot.

This is the Prophet ﷺ in the late Makkan period. Surah Fāṭir comes down to a man whose grief over his people has become a physical weight. We know it has, because two surahs earlier Allah will say to him: *fa-laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka* — "are you going to destroy yourself, grieving after them?" (18:6). The same theme runs through 26:3 — *laʿallaka bākhiʿun nafsaka allā yakūnū muʾminīn*. The Prophet ﷺ is not a man casually delivering a message. He is a man whose chest is being compressed by the weight of watching people refuse what he knows would save them.

Now look at the four ayahs immediately before ours. Allah builds a staircase of contrasts:

- The blind and the seeing — *not equal* (35:19)
- The darknesses and the light — *not equal* (35:20)
- The shade and the scorching wind — *not equal* (35:21)
- And then ours: the living and the dead — *not equal* (35:22)

Each contrast is sharper than the last. Each one moves from the merely visible — eyes that see, a sky that's bright — to something more interior, until we arrive at the most absolute contrast a human being can imagine. A body that breathes. And a body that does not.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

But here's what I want you to notice before we even begin. In every one of those four contrasts, Allah is not really talking about eyes. Or light. Or shade. Or breath.

He is talking about something invisible.

And by the time we arrive at this ayah — the living and the dead — He is going to do something that should stop us in our tracks. He is going to tell His Messenger ﷺ that there is a kind of death that has *nothing to do with the body*. That there are people walking around, eating, laughing, arguing, raising children, and they are — in the only sense that matters — already in their graves.

And then He is going to say something to His most beloved creation that, if we hear it correctly, will reshape what we think a human being is even capable of doing for another human being.

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yastawī* — the verb of standing-level

Look at the second word of the ayah: **يَسْتَوِي** — *yastawī*.

Before we open it, let me teach you the shape of this verb.

In Arabic, verbs come in **forms**. There are ten of them in common use, and each form is like a different lens that takes a basic root and tilts it slightly. The root here is س-و-ي (*s-w-y*), which means at its most basic: to be straight, to be even, to be level. From this root you get *sawāʾ* — a flat surface. You get *istiwāʾ* — being settled at the same level.

Now, this particular word — *yastawī* — is in what's called **Form VIII**. Form VIII has a distinctive shape: you take the root and put a *tā* (ت) right inside it. So س-و-ي becomes *ista-wā*. Form VIII often does something specific to a verb. It often turns an action into a *state that the subject undertakes for itself*. It is reflexive — it folds the action back onto the doer.

If the basic verb *sawā* means "to make level," then Form VIII *istawā* means "to come to be level *of one's own accord*, to stand at the same level."

Why does this matter?

Because Allah is not saying "the living and the dead are not the same." He is saying something more precise. He is saying: **they do not stand at the same level.** They do not occupy the same plane of reality. Even if you put them next to each other, even if a living person and a dead person were placed side by side, they could never be brought into equivalence. The gap between them is not a gap of degree. It is a gap of *kind*.

Stay with this for a moment. The English verb "to equal" is mathematical. Two plus two equals four. It implies that two different things, when added together, produce the same value. But *yastawī* is not mathematical. It is dimensional. It is the verb you would use to ask: are these two things even in the same world?

[PAUSE]

And notice — the verb is in the present tense, the *muḍāriʿ*. Allah does not say "the living and the dead *were not* equal" or "*will not be* equal." He says they are *not now and not ever* equal. This is a permanent ontological fact about reality. Wherever you look, whenever you look, in this life or the next — these two categories do not meet.

So Allah has chosen a verb that does three things at once:

1. It speaks of *level*, not just *value*.
2. It is reflexive — it tells us they cannot bring themselves to the same level no matter what you do to them.
3. It is timeless — this is not a temporary distinction.

And He repeats this same verb in all four contrasts before this one. *Wa mā yastawī.* And not equal. And not equal. And not equal. And finally — and not equal.

Each "not equal" is a hammer falling on the same nail. By the fourth fall, the listener cannot escape: there is something Allah wants us to understand about *categories of being* that we keep wanting to treat as if they were on a spectrum.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Lā* — the doubled negation

Now look at this construction: **وَمَا يَسْتَوِي ٱلْأَحْيَآءُ وَلَا ٱلْأَمْوَٰتُ**.

Translate it literally. *And not equal the living and-not the dead.*

The "not" appears twice. The first time as *mā*. The second time, in the middle of the sentence — right between "the living" and "the dead" — as *wa lā*. "And not the dead."

In English, this sounds redundant. We would say "the living and the dead are not equal." We would not say "the living and-not the dead are equal." But Arabic is doing something here that the Imam Jalāl al-Dīn al-Maḥallī flagged centuries ago in his commentary. He says: **زيادة لا في الثلاثة تأكيد** — *the addition of "lā" in the three [contrasts] is for emphasis.*

What kind of emphasis?

Imagine I told you: "Apples and oranges are not the same." That's a clean statement. But now imagine I said: "Apples are not, and oranges are not, the same." I have negated each side individually. I have stopped to point at apples and say "this one is not." Then I have stopped to point at oranges and say "this one is not."

This is what the *wa lā* is doing. Allah is not saying "the living and the dead collectively fail to be equal." He is saying: **the living are not — and the dead are not — equal to each other.** Each side gets its own negation. Each side is held up separately and inspected and pronounced *distinct*.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. This grammatical move *protects* the two categories from collapsing into each other. In a world where someone might say "well, the living and the dead are roughly the same, they're both human, they're both bodies," the *wa lā* steps in and says: *no — not the living, and not the dead.* Each one is fenced off from the other.

Which is precisely what classical commentators say this ayah is doing on the spiritual level. Al-Qurṭubī cites Ibn Qutaybah and Qatāda: the living are the *ʿuqalāʾ* — those whose hearts comprehend. The dead are the *juhhāl* — those whose hearts do not. And what the *wa lā* is doing grammatically is what Allah is doing existentially: He is refusing to allow these two categories to be treated as variations of the same thing.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Yusmiʿu* and *bi-musmiʿ* — the verb of making-hear

Now we come to the part of the ayah that will change everything.

Look at the two verbs in the second half:

**إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُسْمِعُ مَن يَشَآءُ** — *Indeed Allah yusmiʿu whom He wills.*

**وَمَآ أَنتَ بِمُسْمِعٍ مَّن فِى ٱلْقُبُورِ** — *And you are not a musmiʿ of those in the graves.*

Both verbs come from the root س-م-ع (*s-m-ʿ*), to hear. But neither of them is the basic verb "to hear." Look at the shape.

The basic verb is *samiʿa* — he heard. That's Form I. But these two words are in **Form IV** — *afʿala*, which here gives us *asmaʿa*. The active participle of Form IV is *musmiʿ*.

What does Form IV do here? In this specific lexical context — confirmed across the classical lexicons — *asmaʿa* means *to make someone hear*. To deliver hearing to another. To bridge the gap between a sound and an ear that receives it.

This is not the same as *speaking*. You can speak to someone all day. You can shout. You can read in their language. The word *asmaʿa* describes the moment when your speaking *arrives* — when it actually lands inside the listener and registers.

Now look at what Allah does with this verb.

**Allah** does the *yusmiʿu* — He makes hear.

But **the Prophet ﷺ** — *mā anta bi-musmiʿ* — *you are not a musmiʿ.* You are not one who makes hear.

[PAUSE]

This is staggering. Stay with me.

Allah is telling His Messenger ﷺ — the most eloquent human being who has ever lived, the man whose recitation made Quraysh hide behind walls so they wouldn't hear it, the man whose voice changed the trajectory of human history — Allah is telling *him*: you cannot deliver this hearing. You can deliver the *speaking*. You cannot deliver the *hearing*.

The speaking is yours, O Muḥammad ﷺ. The hearing is Mine.

[PAUSE]

And notice the precise grammar of the negation: **مَآ أَنتَ بِمُسْمِعٍ** — *mā anta bi-musmiʿ*. There's a *bā* (ب) attached to *musmiʿ*. In Arabic, when you negate the subject of a sentence with *mā* and then attach a *bā* to the predicate, you are not just saying "you are not." You are emphasizing it absolutely. The *bā* here is what grammarians call *al-bāʾ az-zāʾida* — the additional *bā* that adds rhetorical weight. It is the difference between "you are not a hearer-maker" and "you are *in no way whatsoever* a hearer-maker."

Allah did not soften this. He did not say "you may not be able to." He did not say "it is difficult for you." He said: it is categorically, absolutely, definitionally outside of what you can do.

[PAUSE]

### Bridging the linguistic to the real

Look at what these three elements together have shown us.

The verb *yastawī* told us there are categories of being that cannot be brought into the same plane. The doubled negation *wa lā* told us those categories must be held apart from each other, refused to be collapsed. And the Form IV verbs *yusmiʿu* and *musmiʿ* told us that there is a kind of hearing that no human being — not even the Prophet ﷺ — can produce in another human being.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The death you can be walking around inside

Let me ask you something.

Have you ever tried — *really* tried — to wake someone up to something true?

Maybe it was a parent stuck in a pattern that's destroying them. Maybe it was a friend in a relationship that everyone except them can see is wrong. Maybe it was a sibling spiraling into something dark. You sat them down. You said the words. You used every ounce of love you had. You crafted the sentence carefully. You used examples. You cried, even.

And they nodded. And they smiled politely. And nothing — *nothing* — moved behind their eyes.

You walked away and you felt the most particular kind of loneliness. Not the loneliness of being unloved, but the loneliness of speaking into a room where no one is home.

[PAUSE]

That feeling — that exact feeling — is what this ayah is about.

Because here is what Allah is doing in this verse. He is telling the Prophet ﷺ, and through him telling every one of us who has ever loved a person who refuses to be reached: the failure to land is not your failure. It is not a failure at all. It is the encounter with a wall that does not yield to speech, because the people you are speaking to are, in the most precise sense, **in their graves**.

Listen to how the classical commentators read this. Al-Baghawī says: *shabbahahum bi-l-amwāti fī al-qubūr* — *He likened them to the dead in their graves.* Al-Maḥallī adds: *aḥyāhum lam yujībū* — *they did not respond.* Al-Muyassar puts it most directly: **أحياء القلوب بالإيمان، وأموات القلوب بالكفر** — *those whose hearts are alive through faith, and those whose hearts are dead through denial.*

There is a death of the body. We all know it. We have all stood next to it. We will all undergo it.

But there is a death the Qur'an names that is far more terrifying, because you cannot see it. The person is moving. The person is breathing. The person is having opinions about politics and texting their friends and going on vacation. And yet — *the chamber inside them where meaning lands* — that chamber is silent. The walls are closed. The light does not enter.

[PAUSE]

Now, why is this a *mercy* to know?

Because there is no burden in human life heavier than thinking you are responsible for outcomes you cannot produce. The parent who blames themselves for a child who left the faith. The daʿwah worker who blames themselves for a friend who walked away. The teacher who blames themselves for the student who refused to learn.

Allah is taking this burden — gently, firmly — off the chest of His Messenger ﷺ. He is saying: you carry the *speaking*. I carry the *hearing*. The two are different. You have done your part the moment the words leave your mouth. What happens after that is a transaction between Me and the soul I have created.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ was given this ayah. Because a man whose grief over his people was about to destroy him needed to be told: *you are not failing. You are speaking to graves. The graves are not your responsibility.*

[PAUSE]

### Practical application of Theme One

Tonight, or tomorrow, you will speak to someone you love. And you will, at some point, find yourself saying something that you wish they would absorb. And they will not absorb it.

This ayah asks you to do something very specific in that moment. To remember that the door between your speech and their understanding is not a door you possess the key to. That door belongs to Allah alone. Your job ends with sincerity in the speech. Their hearing — that is a transaction you are not part of.

The minute you accept this, two things change. First, you stop carrying a weight that was never yours to carry. Second — and this is the harder one — you stop *resenting* the person for not hearing you. Because resentment requires you to believe they could have heard if they had only tried harder. This ayah tells you something different: that without Allah's *yusmiʿu*, even the speech of the most beloved human being in history did not land.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The asymmetry that protects compassion

Now look at what's missing.

I want you to read the ayah again, slowly. Notice what Allah did *not* say.

He said: **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُسْمِعُ مَن يَشَآءُ** — *Allah causes to hear whom He wills.*

He did not say: *Allah causes to hear whom He wills, and refuses to make hear whom He wills.*

He named only the positive side. He named only the people Allah *grants* hearing to. The negative side — the people Allah *withholds* hearing from — is not stated. It is left silent.

Stay with this.

Then in the next clause He says: **وَمَآ أَنتَ بِمُسْمِعٍ مَّن فِى ٱلْقُبُورِ** — *and you cannot make hear those in the graves.*

Here, He names the negative. He says: there is a category of people — those in the graves — who will not hear.

So look at the asymmetry. When Allah speaks of His own action, He emphasizes who He *gives* to. When He speaks of the limit on the Prophet's ﷺ action, He emphasizes who *cannot be reached*. Allah's side of the equation is framed by generosity. The Prophet's ﷺ side is framed by realism.

Why?

[PAUSE]

Because Allah is teaching the Prophet ﷺ — and through him us — how to hold two truths simultaneously without despairing.

**Truth one:** Some hearts are sealed. They are in their graves. You cannot reach them.

**Truth two:** You will never know which heart is which.

The ayah is structured so that Allah never lets the Prophet ﷺ — or you — look at a specific human being and say *that one is dead*. Why? Because Allah's *mashīʾah* — His willing — is hidden. He says *yusmiʿu man yashāʾ.* He does not list the names. He does not tell you which heart, on which day, in which moment, will be granted hearing.

So here is the most beautiful thing about this ayah. It tells you that some people are unreachable. And it refuses to tell you who they are.

[PAUSE]

Which means what?

Which means you must continue to speak. To everyone. As if every soul in front of you is the one Allah is about to grant hearing to. Because *you do not know.*

The Prophet ﷺ kept walking to Ṭāʾif. The Prophet ﷺ kept reciting to Quraysh. The Prophet ﷺ kept inviting his uncle. Right up to Abū Ṭālib's deathbed, he kept asking — because he did not know, and could not know, which heart was alive and which was already buried.

This ayah does not give you a reason to stop. It gives you a reason to keep going *without breaking*. You speak because you don't know who is alive. You don't despair because you know your job ended at the speaking.

[PAUSE]

### Practical application of Theme Two

There is a person in your life right now who you have given up on.

I don't know who they are. But you do.

Maybe a family member. Maybe a former friend. Maybe a child who has gone in a direction that breaks your heart. Maybe a parent who refused to see you. You have, on some level, written them off — not consciously perhaps, but in that quiet interior way where you have stopped praying for them as if they could change. You have stopped imagining them as someone Allah might *yusmiʿu*.

This ayah asks: who told you to stop? Allah didn't. He kept the curtain drawn over which hearts are alive precisely so that you would not give up on any of them. He hid His *mashīʾah* in mercy — so that the door of duʿāʾ for every soul stays open until the moment of their death.

Tonight, when you make duʿāʾ, name one person you have stopped naming. Say their name out loud. And ask Allah to be the One who delivers the hearing you cannot.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Look back at what we found.

The verb *yastawī* established that some categories of being are dimensionally apart — they do not stand at the same level. The doubled *wa lā* held those categories rigorously separate, refusing to let them blur. The Form IV verbs *yusmiʿu* and *musmiʿ* showed that delivering *hearing* is a different act from speaking — that one belongs to Allah, the other to His messengers.

And then the asymmetric absence — Allah naming the positive of His will and the negative of the Prophet's ﷺ limit — completed the picture. The structure of the ayah is the structure of the truth it teaches: that you and Allah operate on different planes of agency, and the boundary between those planes is the boundary that protects you from despair.

The four contrasts that led up to this ayah — the blind and the seeing, the darknesses and the light, the shade and the scorching wind, the living and the dead — were not redundant. They were a staircase descending from the outer world to the inner world. By the time we arrive at this ayah, we are no longer talking about eyes or weather. We are talking about *what is happening inside a chest*.

### The two discoveries are one

The linguistic and the thematic meet here:

When Form IV *asmaʿa* is given to Allah but denied to the Prophet ﷺ, what is being communicated is the same thing the asymmetric absence communicates: that the burden of changing a soul is not on the speaker. The grammar enforces the theology. The negation protects the heart.

### Questions to carry

1. Who am I speaking to as if they could hear, when I have already, in my own mind, buried them?

2. Where in my life am I carrying responsibility for outcomes that belong to Allah's *yashāʾ*, not to my speech?

3. When someone I love refuses to be reached, do I treat that refusal as my failure — or as the encounter with a wall that Allah alone can open?

4. Whose name have I stopped saying in duʿāʾ because I have decided in my heart that they are unreachable?

5. If Allah deliberately hides which hearts are alive — what does it mean about how I should treat *every* person I speak to?

### One-sentence distillation

*The living and the dead cannot be brought to the same plane — and your job is to speak as if every soul might be among the living, because the One who delivers the hearing has kept that knowledge to Himself.*

### The closing invitation

What might shift, after sitting with this ayah?

A small thing, perhaps. The next time you speak to someone and the words seem to slide off them — the next time you watch a person you love walk in a direction that breaks you — this ayah invites you to take a breath, and to remember that the door between your speech and their soul is not a door you possess. That the *failure to land* is not failure at all. It is the encounter with a *mashīʾah* greater than yours.

And from this — from accepting this — comes a strange and beautiful freedom. The freedom to keep speaking without resenting. The freedom to keep inviting without despairing. The freedom to make duʿāʾ for the people you had given up on, because Allah has refused to tell you who is in their graves.

### A duʿāʾ

*O Allah — You are the One who causes hearing, and we are not. Be for those we love the One who delivers what our speech cannot deliver. Open the chambers we cannot open. And do not let us mistake our limit for Your limit, or treat any soul as buried when You alone know who is alive. Āmīn.*
