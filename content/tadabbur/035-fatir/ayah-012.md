# The Two Waters That Will Not Merge — and Both Feed You

## Surah Fatir, Ayah 12

> **وَمَا يَسْتَوِي الْبَحْرَانِ هَٰذَا عَذْبٌ فُرَاتٌ سَائِغٌ شَرَابُهُ وَهَٰذَا مِلْحٌ أُجَاجٌ ۖ وَمِن كُلٍّ تَأْكُلُونَ لَحْمًا طَرِيًّا وَتَسْتَخْرِجُونَ حِلْيَةً تَلْبَسُونَهَا ۖ وَتَرَى الْفُلْكَ فِيهِ مَوَاخِرَ لِتَبْتَغُوا مِن فَضْلِهِ وَلَعَلَّكُمْ تَشْكُرُونَ**

*Wa mā yastawī al-baḥrān — hādhā ʿadhbun furātun sā'ighun sharābuhū wa hādhā milḥun ujājun. Wa min kullin ta'kulūna laḥman ṭariyyan wa tastakhrijūna ḥilyatan talbasūnahā. Wa tarā al-fulka fīhi mawākhira li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi wa laʿallakum tashkurūn.*

"And the two bodies of water are not alike — this one fresh, sweet, pleasant to drink, and this one salty, bitter. Yet from each you eat tender meat and extract ornament that you wear. And you see the ships plowing through it, so that you may seek of His bounty — and that perhaps you may be grateful."

---

## Rebuilding the World

Before we step inside this ayah, sit for a moment with the listener it first arrived to.

Picture a man in seventh-century Makkah. His world is not a world of plenty. He lives in a basin where water is *the* question. The wells of his town — Zamzam, the wells of the surrounding valley — are sweet, and they are life. They are also fragile. They run low. They draw lines on the map of who can survive and who cannot.

He has heard of the sea. Maybe he has seen it. Caravans come from the south speaking of the Red Sea and its strange waters. You cannot drink the sea. If you tried, you would die faster than the man who drank nothing. The same liquid that looks like the water in your well will kill you if it crosses your lips.

This is his lived sense of "the two waters." One pulls you back from death. The other, despite looking the same, drags you toward it.

Now — into this world — Allah speaks an ayah that does something very strange. He puts the two waters side by side. He underlines that they are **opposite**. He does not soften the contrast. He intensifies it: *ʿadhbun **furātun*** — sweet, *intensely* sweet. *Milḥun **ujājun*** — salty, *bitterly* salty. He makes the gap as wide as possible.

And then He says: from **each** of them, you eat the same fresh fish flesh. From each comes ornament. Across the surface of this same water you cannot trust to drink, ships move and carry your trade.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is the middle of a chain. Look one ayah up — 35:11 — and Allah is talking about the human pair: dust, sperm, male and female. Look one ayah down — 35:13 — and Allah is talking about the day and the night, the sun and the moon. Three ayāt, three pairs of opposites. The human pair. The water pair. The light pair.

He is building an argument. And the argument is not yet visible. It will surface in 35:14, when He turns to the people who worship besides Him and exposes how those false gods own none of this. But before the argument lands, He wants you to *see the pattern*. He wants you to walk into His creation and notice: opposites are not accidents. Opposites are designed.

The hook is this: Allah does not say "and one is better than the other." He does not say "one is for you and one is not." He says they are *not alike* — and from each of them, He feeds you. What does it mean that the sweet and the bitter are both productive? What does it mean that the very things in our lives that refuse to merge are both, somehow, where the sustenance is coming from?

Stay with that question. Let it pull you into the ayah.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### First: *wa mā yastawī al-baḥrān* — "the two waters are not equal"

Before we open the words, let's teach a small grammatical point that the rest will rest on.

In Arabic, there is a verb form called Form VIII — the *iftaʿala* pattern. One of the things Form VIII often does is express a **reflexive** or **middle** voice. The verb turns back on itself. Where the simple form would say "he made X equal," the Form VIII version says "X made itself equal to something else." It is the action turning inward.

Think about an English analogue. There's a difference between "I balanced the two scales" and "the two scales balanced themselves." The first puts an outside hand on the action. The second describes a state that the things settle into on their own.

Now, the verb in our ayah — *yastawī* — is Form VIII of the root *s-w-y*, the root that gives us *sawāʾ* (equal), *istiwāʾ* (being level, being settled). The Leeds morphological corpus confirms this is Form VIII. *Yastawī* literally means: "to make itself equal." "To level itself out." "To settle into equivalence."

And what does Allah do? He places *mā* in front of it — the particle of negation. *Wa mā yastawī*.

Listen to what this actually says:

> "And the two bodies of water — they do not make themselves equal."

Notice something. Allah is not saying *I have not made them equal*. He is saying *they do not become equal*. The verb is reflexive. The non-equality is in the very nature of what they are. They cannot, on their own, settle into sameness. The barrier between them is not something Allah has to keep policing. It is built into their being.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The next word — *al-baḥrān* — is in the **dual**. Not "the seas." Not "all bodies of water." Two. Specifically two. Arabic, as you may know, has three numbers: singular, dual, and plural. When Arabic wants to point at *a pair*, it has a special grammatical form for it. Allah did not need to use the dual. He could have said "the waters do not become equal." He chose the dual. He chose to highlight *twoness*.

Stay with me. The dual is doing something here. Allah is not making a general claim about all water. He is putting **two and only two** waters next to each other, and He is saying these two — *these* two — do not collapse into one. This is the same surah, one ayah earlier, that pointed to another pair: male and female. It is the same surah, one ayah later, that points to another pair: day and night. The grammatical *dual* is the architecture of the whole passage. Allah is teaching you to read creation in pairs.

So in five Arabic words — *wa mā yastawī al-baḥrān* — He has done three things at once:

He has negated the equality.
He has placed the negation inside a reflexive verb, so the non-equality is intrinsic, not external.
And He has used the dual, so you know we are looking at a designed pair, not a random instance.

Five words. Three layers of precision. And we have not even reached the description of the waters yet.

[PAUSE — major]

### Second: *sāʾighun sharābuhu* against *milḥun ujāj* — when the sound becomes the meaning

Now look at how Allah describes each water.

The sweet one: *ʿadhbun furātun sāʾighun sharābuhu*. Four words. Sweet, intensely-sweet, easily-going-down, its drink.

The salt one: *milḥun ujājun*. Two words. Salty, bitterly-salty.

Already there's an asymmetry — the sweet water gets four words of description, the salt water gets two. Allah lingers on the sweetness. He hurries past the bitterness. You can hear in the very *length* of the description which one is gift and which one is reminder.

But the deepest thing is in the sound.

*Sāʾigh* — from the root *s-w-gh* — means "going down smoothly, easily swallowed." Say it slowly: *sā-ʾi-ghun*. The vowels glide. The *sīn* is soft. The *ghayn* at the end is a kind of throat-purr. The mouth opens, settles, releases. The word itself is *swallowable*. Saying it feels like sipping water.

Now say *ujāj*. *U-jāj*. The *jīm* is sharp. The doubled vowel after the *jīm* makes the throat grind. The word does not glide. It catches. It scrapes.

Allah is doing something the Arabs called *muhākāt al-maʿnā bi-l-lafẓ* — the imitation of meaning in the very texture of the word. The word for "smooth-going-down" goes down smoothly. The word for "bitter-on-the-tongue" sticks on the tongue. The Quran is not just *describing* the two waters. It is *enacting* them. Read this ayah aloud and your mouth literally tastes the difference.

[PAUSE]

This is not decoration. Think about why Allah does this.

The ayah is an argument about creation as a sign. It is saying: the very things in front of you that you take for granted carry signatures of design. Even the *sound of the word for the thing* carries the signature. The phonetic shape of the language matches the physical shape of the reality. Sweetness is built to slide. Bitterness is built to catch.

And then notice — Allah does not stop at the description. He doesn't say "and the sweet one feeds you." He says *wa min kullin ta'kulūna laḥman ṭariyyā* — "and from **each** you eat tender meat."

The sweet water does not have a monopoly on nourishment. The bitter water is not banished from the table. The same fish flesh, the same tenderness, the same *ṭariyy* — fresh, soft, alive — emerges from both.

The mouth of the listener has just tasted, in language, the difference between the waters. And in the same breath he is told: *the difference does not appear in what feeds you.*

That is a stunning thing to do. The very sound architecture of the verse maximizes the contrast, and then the meaning of the verse collapses the contrast in what it produces. Allah lets you taste the gap and then He shows you the gap doesn't determine the gift.

### Third: *tastakhrijūn* — the verb of going after

Now move to the next clause: *wa tastakhrijūna ḥilyatan talbasūnahā*. "And you extract ornament that you wear."

There is one word here that the Leeds corpus flags, and that flag opens up the whole ayah. The word is *tastakhrijūna*. It is Form X — *istafʿala* — of the root *kh-r-j*.

Teach yourself the form first. The simple verb, *kharaja*, means "to come out, to exit." It is intransitive — the thing exits on its own. *Akhraja* (Form IV) means "to bring out" — someone else brings the thing out. *Istakhraja* (Form X) means something different again. Form X usually carries the sense of **seeking, requesting, extracting** — going *after* something with effort.

If *kharaja* is "the pearl came out," and *akhraja* is "he brought the pearl out," then *istakhraja* is **"he went down after the pearl and pulled it out."**

[PAUSE]

Now stack the two verbs Allah used in this ayah:

For the fish: *ta'kulūna* — "you eat." Plain present-tense Form I. The fish is taken, prepared, eaten. The verb assumes the gift is on the surface, available, ready.

For the ornament: *tastakhrijūna* — "you extract." Form X. The pearl is *not* on the surface. You must dive. You must hold your breath. You must risk. You must come up and try again. The verb itself contains the labor.

Allah uses two different verbs for two different kinds of bounty from the same water.

There is a kind of gift in creation that swims to you — fish on a line, food in season, sustenance you can catch with a net. *Ta'kulūn.*

And there is a kind of gift that will not come unless you go after it. It is in the depths. It is hidden under bitter water. It exists. It is real. But it requires *istikhrāj* — extraction, the deliberate effort of going down to bring it up. *Tastakhrijūn.*

And here is the small detail you would miss if you weren't watching the grammar: what is *istakhraja*-d? *Ḥilyah*. **Ornament.** Beauty. Adornment. The thing you wear. The fish keeps you alive. The pearl makes you beautiful. The fish is necessity. The pearl is *fadl* — extra, surplus, grace beyond what you needed to survive.

So even within the bounty of the sea, Allah is teaching a hierarchy of how His gifts come. The basic — eaten. The exquisite — extracted. The surface — received. The depth — sought.

And one more thing, because the same Form pattern appears at the end of the ayah: *li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi*. *Tabtaghī* is Form VIII of *b-gh-y* — "to seek." Form VIII intensification. Not just *bagha* (to want) but *ibtaghā* (to *actively pursue, deliberately seek*). The ships do not drift; they *plow* — *mawākhir*, cleavers of the water — so that *you may actively, deliberately seek* of His bounty.

Two verbs of seeking. *Tastakhrijūn* — extracting from below. *Tabtaghūn* — pursuing across the surface. Both intensive forms. Both pointed at *fadl* — bounty beyond bare need.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge — what the words have shown us

Pause and look at what the language has done.

Allah has placed before us two waters that **cannot make themselves equal**. He has put the contrast in the *sound* of the words themselves, so that your mouth tastes the difference before your mind explains it. And then He has told you that from each of these opposite waters comes the same tender flesh — and that beyond the flesh, there are pearls, and the pearls require you to dive.

Three linguistic gestures. One picture: opposites in creation are designed; they will not merge; and the gifts within them come at different depths.

Now we ask what this has been pointing at all along. The ayah is talking about water. The ayah has never *only* been about water.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Opposites in Your Life That Will Not Merge

Start where you actually live.

There is something in modern life — call it the integration impulse — that tells you the goal of growth is to resolve your contradictions. Bring the warring parts of yourself into one room and get them to sit together. Heal the split between work and meaning. Merge faith and reason. Reconcile the sweet seasons and the bitter seasons of your life into one continuous story of "and that made me who I am today."

There is something true in that impulse. We do need integration. We do need our parts to talk.

But there is also something it cannot do. There are pairs in our lives that are not *meant* to merge. There are losses that do not become gains. There are relationships that do not heal. There are bitter waters in your past that, no matter how many years you pour over them, will not turn sweet. And the modern instinct — to keep working on them until they merge with the sweet — sometimes becomes its own kind of suffering. You sit in the bitter water trying to make it taste sweet. It will not.

[PAUSE]

Now read what Allah has just said.

*Wa mā yastawī al-baḥrān.* The two waters do not make themselves equal. The reflexive verb. Built into their nature. The bitter does not become sweet. The sweet does not absorb the bitter. They sit next to each other and remain what they are. Allah did not design them to merge. He designed them to remain distinct.

And from **each** comes meat.

Sit with that. The bitter season of your life is not destined to become a sweet season. It may never become sweet. The verse does not promise that. What the verse promises is that from each — even from the bitter — there will be *laḥman ṭariyyā*. Tender, fresh, alive flesh. Sustenance for the body. Nourishment that is exactly the same kind of nourishment the sweet sea produces.

The mistake — the modern mistake — is thinking that if a season cannot be made sweet, it cannot feed you. The Quranic mind says: the sweet feeds you and the bitter feeds you, and they will not become each other, and that is *the design*.

The Companions lived this. The first generation of Muslims had brothers who died at Uhud and brothers who died on their beds. They had years of triumph and years of grief. They did not flatten the seasons into one story. They named them. They wept in the bitter and praised in the sweet. And they trusted that both seas were carrying them somewhere.

Bring this home. There is, in your life right now, almost certainly a sea you wish were sweet that is not. A relationship that did not heal. A loss that does not soften. A season you cannot integrate. The Quranic invitation is not "keep working until it merges." The Quranic invitation is "stop trying to merge it. Eat from it."

What does it mean to eat from a bitter sea? It means asking the bitter season: what is it *yielding*? What capacity has it grown in me? What dependence on Allah did it teach? What sin did it expose? What people did it bring close? What people did it strip away that needed to be stripped? The flesh is in there. The verse promises it. *Wa min kullin ta'kulūn.* From **each** you eat.

You do not have to make the bitter sweet to be fed by it. You only have to be the kind of person who knows how to fish.

[PAUSE]

And then notice the second movement. The fish is the bare nourishment. But Allah goes further. He says you also *extract* ornament. *Tastakhrijūn ḥilyatan talbasūnahā.* You go down into the water and bring up beauty.

This is where it gets remarkable. Classical commentators — al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and others — observed that pearls in the ordinary world come from the salt sea, not the sweet one. Some held that the verse's "from each" applies only to the eating; the ornament is specifically from the bitter water. Others held that pearls can be attributed to both because freshwater springs run beneath salt seas. The classical reading is contested.

But hold the first opinion in your mind for a moment, the one al-Baghawī gives weight to: the pearl, the *ḥilyah*, the thing of beauty that you wear, **comes from the bitter sea**. The salt water that you cannot drink. The water that, on the surface, looks only like death — that is where the pearl is.

What kind of life lesson is the ayah offering, if you read it that way?

That the most beautiful things you will ever extract from your life are going to come, disproportionately, from the seasons you would never have chosen. The depth that you wear as ornament — the wisdom in your face, the gentleness in your voice, the way you sit with another person's grief — was extracted from water you could not drink. Not from the sweet seasons. The sweet seasons fed you. The bitter seasons adorned you.

And what is the verb again? *Istakhraja*. Form X. You go after it. You do not wait for the pearl to surface. You dive. You hold your breath. You enter the very water that, on the surface, kills you. And from inside it, you bring up something you could not have made.

[PAUSE]

This is the practical hinge of the theme. Tomorrow, the bitter sea in your life — name it — will still be bitter. Do not waste your years trying to turn it sweet. Cast a net into it. Eat the flesh it gives. And then, when you are ready, dive into it. Go down into the season, the loss, the failure, the silence — go down with your breath held — and ask: *what is the pearl Allah hid here?* Not "why did this happen." Pearls. What did this teach me that I would wear for the rest of my life and into the next?

The sea will not change. *Mā yastawī al-baḥrān.* But you, having dived, will come up changed.

### Theme Two: Two Kinds of Receiving — and the Posture for Each

Stay inside the ayah and look at how it teaches you to *receive*.

The ayah uses six second-person plural verbs in a single sentence. *Ta'kulūn* (you eat), *tastakhrijūn* (you extract), *talbasūn* (you wear), *tarā* (you see — singular here, but still addressing the human), *tabtaghū* (you seek), *tashkurūn* (you give thanks). Six. Allah is teaching the human being the *posture of the recipient*.

And the postures are not all the same.

In modern life, we have one dominant model of receiving: **consumption**. The thing is presented; we take it. The food arrives; we eat it. The notification appears; we read it. The content scrolls; we scroll back. Almost every "gift" the modern world offers is delivered to the surface. Convenience is the underlying virtue. The pearl that requires diving has been almost engineered out of our daily life.

This ayah refuses that model.

It says: there is a kind of gift you eat — *ta'kulūn*. The fish swims toward your nets. You do not have to be elaborate. Be available. Be present. Receive.

But there is another kind of gift you must **extract** — *tastakhrijūn*. The pearl will not come unless you go after it. The Form X verb is a posture. It is the posture of someone who knows the surface is not all of it. Someone who has learned that the easy gifts are real, and they are not enough.

And then there is a third movement, the ships: *tarā al-fulka fīhi mawākhira li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi*. You *see* the ships plowing through it, so that you may *seek* of His bounty. *Mawākhir* — plural of *mākhirah*, from the root *m-kh-r* — means cleavers, splitters. The ship is not floating. It is *cleaving* the water. It is doing work against resistance. And the purpose clause — *li-tabtaghū* — is Form VIII intensification of "to seek." Not passive movement. *Active pursuit.*

So the ayah lays out three levels of receiving:

You eat. (The gift comes to the surface.)
You extract. (The gift is in the depths and requires you to go down.)
You cleave. (The gift is across the ocean and requires you to cross.)

Three depths of effort, three kinds of *faḍl* — bounty.

[PAUSE]

Bring this to the way you live with the Quran. With your prayer. With your relationship to Allah.

There are some gifts in this religion that come to the surface. You hear an ayah and it lands. You enter the prayer and you feel held. A friend brings a reminder at exactly the moment you needed it. These are real. These are *ta'kulūn* gifts. They feed the soul like fish feeds the body. You did not engineer them. They came.

But there is also *ḥilyah* — ornament. The deep beauty of this faith. The pearls that the seekers have always written about: presence with Allah in the night prayer, the heart that has tasted *ḥubb*, the patience that holds steady when everything is being stripped. These do not come to the surface. They are *istakhraja*-d. You go after them. You dive. The verb is built that way for a reason.

And then there are the ships — *li-tabtaghū min faḍlihi*. There are kinds of bounty that require a journey. Years of seeking knowledge. The slow work of building a household on faith. The decades of teaching your children. These are the ships that cleave. You do not arrive at them by accident. You set out.

Three postures. The ayah is not asking you to be one. It is asking you to recognize which gift is which, and bring the right posture to each. Do not stand on the shore expecting pearls to wash up. Do not dive for fish. Do not refuse to set sail because the sea is wide.

And then — at the very end — *wa laʿallakum tashkurūn*. "That perhaps you may be grateful."

Notice what gratitude is being asked for. Not just the fish. Not just the pearl. The whole architecture. The fact that there *are* two waters that do not merge. The fact that the bitter still feeds. The fact that some gifts require diving. The fact that ships can cross. The whole design — the entire system of designed opposites that 35:11–13 lays out — is *the* thing to be grateful for.

The shukr is not "thank you for the sweet water." That is too small. The shukr is "thank you that You designed reality with bitter and sweet, with surface and depth, with shore and ocean — and made me a creature who can eat, extract, and cleave through all of it."

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now step back and look at where this ayah sits.

35:11 — *Wallāhu khalaqakum min turābin thumma min nuṭfatin thumma jaʿalakum azwājā.* Allah created you from dust, then from a drop, then He made you **pairs** — male and female.

35:12 — The two **waters**. Sweet and salt. Pairs that do not merge.

35:13 — *Yūlij al-layla fī al-nahār wa yūlij al-nahār fī al-layl wa sakhkhara al-shamsa wa al-qamar.* He merges the night into the day and the day into the night, and harnessed the **sun and the moon**. Pairs in light.

Three ayāt. Three pairs. The human pair. The water pair. The light pair.

Allah is showing you that pair-structure runs through reality at every level. The human is a pair. The water is a pair. The light is a pair. And every one of these pairs is *designed* — none of them is an accident, none of them is two gods, none of them is a contest. They are complementary, and they all point at one Designer.

This is why ayah 14 — the very next move in the surah — turns immediately to those who worship others besides Allah. *Tadʿūhum lā yasmaʿū duʿāʾakum* — "those you call upon do not hear your call." The argument has now landed. After showing you that all the pairs in creation are designed by *one*, He confronts the absurdity of dividing your worship among many.

The two waters are not just an image of bounty. They are a piece of evidence. Each pair Allah names in 11–13 builds the case. By the time He turns to false gods in 14, you have already conceded — at the level of the eyes and the tongue and the swallow — that one mind designed all of this.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar said: *mā yastawī* is reflexive. The two waters cannot make themselves equal. The non-equality is intrinsic.

The theme said: the bitter seasons of your life are not designed to become sweet. Stop trying to make them merge.

The grammar said: *tastakhrijūn* is Form X. The pearl is extracted, not received. The seeking is in the verb.

The theme said: the deepest beauty in your life will be dived for, not delivered. Bring the right posture to the right gift.

The grammar said: Allah described the sweet water in four words and the bitter in two — *and then said "from each you eat."* The sound enacted the gap; the meaning closed it.

The theme said: the bitter does not become sweet, and yet it still feeds. The gift is not in the merging; it is in your capacity to be fed by both.

### Questions to Carry

What is the bitter sea in my life that I have been trying to make sweet — and what would change if I stopped trying to merge it and started fishing in it?

Where have I been waiting for pearls to wash up on the shore, when the verse says they must be *extracted*?

Of the three postures — eating, extracting, cleaving — which one have I been refusing? Have I been refusing to dive? Refusing to set sail? Or refusing to receive what is already on the surface?

When I read 35:11–13 as a single argument, what does the architecture of *designed pairs* in creation say about the contradictions I am holding in my own life right now?

What would *shukr* look like — not for one gift — but for the whole system that Allah built, including the bitter waters?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah designed the bitter and the sweet to never merge — and from **each** He feeds you tender flesh, and from the depths of each He hides pearls for the ones willing to dive.

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this is small and large at once.

Small: tomorrow, when you sit with a bitter season in your life — a grief, a loss, a relationship that will not heal — do not ask Allah to turn it sweet. Ask Him what He has placed in it for you to eat, and what He has hidden in its depths for you to extract.

Large: read all of creation — including your own life — as a system of designed pairs. Stop reading opposites as enemies. Read them as architecture. The two waters do not fight. They sit side by side. They do not merge. And both of them, by His decree, carry you.

### Du'a

Yā Allah — You who placed two waters side by side and would not let them merge, and yet fed us from each — teach us how to eat from the bitter seasons of our lives without demanding that they become sweet. Give us the breath to dive when the pearl is in the deep. Give us the ships when the bounty is across the ocean. And when we have eaten and extracted and cleaved, do not let us forget the One who designed the sea. *Wa laʿallanā nashkur.* Make us, perhaps, of the grateful.
