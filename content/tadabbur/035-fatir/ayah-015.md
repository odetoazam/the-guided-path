# Introduction

Let's begin with the ayah.

[ayah:35:15] ۞ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ ۖ وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ

*Yā ayyuhā an-nāsu antumu al-fuqarā'u ilā Allāh, wa-Allāhu huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd.*

A common translation: *"O mankind, you are in need of Allah, while Allah is the Free of Need, the Praiseworthy."*

Hold that translation lightly for now. We're going to find that the English is doing something gentler than what the Arabic is actually doing.

## The world this ayah enters

We are in Surah Fatir — *the Originator*. The surah opens by introducing Allah as the One who splits the heavens and the earth into existence, who sends angels with wings, who opens mercy that no one can close and closes mercy that no one can open (35:1-2). Then it begins to confront the human heart. It speaks of Shaytan as an open enemy. It warns those who plot, those who turn away, those who insist they don't need to listen.

And then, in the middle of all this warning and invitation, the surah does something striking. It steps back. It addresses not the believers, not the disbelievers, not the people of the Book — but *all of mankind*. **Yā ayyuhā an-nās.** O humanity.

It addresses you and me at the level of *being human*, not at the level of belief or unbelief.

And it says: before we go any further, before any of this warning lands or any of this invitation reaches you — here is the actual structure of reality. Here is what is true about you. Here is what is true about Him.

The ayahs that come immediately after (16-17) make the stakes brutally clear: *If He willed, He could remove you and bring a new creation. And that is not difficult for Allah.* But before that warning, comes this naming. This diagnosis. This X-ray of the human condition.

## The hook

Here is what we are going to discover, and I want you to feel the weight of it before we begin.

The ayah does not say *"you need Allah."* It says something stranger, and infinitely heavier. It does not describe a state you are in. It names an identity you cannot escape. The translation softens it. The Arabic does not.

And then, having named what you are, the ayah names what He is — with two attributes paired in a way that, if you let them land, will turn your entire understanding of wealth and worth inside out.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: *Antumu al-fuqarā'* — "You ARE the poor ones"

### Teaching the concept first

In every human language, there is a difference between saying *"you are hungry"* and *"you are the hungry one."* The first describes a state. The second names an identity. The first comes and goes — you ate breakfast, now you're not hungry anymore. The second is who you are — perhaps you are the one in the room whose hunger defines you, perhaps you are the figure of hunger itself.

Arabic, like English, marks this distinction. And Arabic has another tool English doesn't quite have in the same way: the definite article *al-*. When you place *al-* on a noun, you don't just say "a poor person." You can say *"the poor one"* — or even, in a particular construction, *"the genus of the poor."* The class itself. The category. The very embodiment of the thing.

Hold that distinction in your mind: state versus identity. Description versus essence.

### Opening the word

Now look at what Allah says.

He does not say *antum fuqarā'* — "you are poor." He says **أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ** — *antumu al-fuqarā'* — "you are *the* poor ones."

The definite article is sitting there, doing tremendous work. And the classical commentators noticed.

Al-Qurtubi, in his tafsir, quotes a question raised by al-Zamakhshari: *fa-in qulta lima ʿurrifa al-fuqarā'?* — "If you ask: why is *al-fuqarā'* made definite?"

His answer, in essence: Allah is showing them that *because of the intensity of their need for Him, they are the very genus of the poor.* Other things are poor too — every created thing is poor toward Allah — but humans are being addressed as the paradigmatic example. The textbook case. The dictionary entry under "poverty."

### Let's sit with this

Notice what has just been said about you.

You are not someone who *experiences* poverty. You are not someone who *occasionally needs* something. You are not a being who is mostly self-sufficient but reaches out for divine help in times of crisis.

You are *the fuqarā'*. The poor ones. By identity. By definition.

Stay with me — because we have not yet looked at the word itself.

### The word *fuqarā'*

The root is **ف-ق-ر** — *f-q-r*. And in Arabic, this is not a polite or gentle word for need. The root literally relates to the *spine* — *faqār* is the vertebrae. And the classical lexicons explain that the *faqīr* is one whose spine is broken by his need. One who cannot stand up under what he lacks.

Lisān al-ʿArab and the *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib both preserve this image. *Faqr* is not the polite English "need." It is the destitution of someone who has been bent over by their lack. Someone whose back cannot hold them.

And the plural form here — **فُقَرَاء**, *fuqarā'* — comes in the pattern **فُعَلَاء**. This plural pattern in Arabic tends to attach to *fixed* qualities of a person, qualities that define them: *shuʿarā'* (poets), *karamā'* (the generous), *ʿulamā'* (the learned), *ḥukamā'* (the wise). These are not transient states. They are identities. The person who has the trait so deeply that the trait is *who they are*.

So when Allah uses *fuqarā'* in this pattern, with the definite article, He is not saying: "you have some neediness in you." He is saying: **your poverty is your defining trait. It is what you are made of. You belong to the category of beings whose entire identity is "broken-backed need."**

### Here's what's remarkable

The English translation reads: *"you are in need of Allah."*

Now hear what the Arabic actually says: *"you are the broken-backed destitute, by identity, in your essence — directed toward Allah."*

Those are not the same sentence.

The English describes a circumstance. The Arabic names an ontology — a statement about what you *are*.

You did not become poor when you ran out of money. You did not become poor when illness struck. You did not become poor when relationships failed. Those events did not make you poor. **They simply revealed what was always true.** Your bank account did not change your status before Allah by even an atom's weight. Your health did not change it. Your relationships did not change it. You were always the *faqīr*. The wealthy person and the homeless person — before Allah, both are *al-fuqarā'*. They differ only in how aware they are of what they have always been.

[PAUSE]

### One small word — *ilā*

There is one more piece to this element, and then we move on.

The ayah says: *al-fuqarā'u **ilā** Allāh* — the poor ones **toward** Allah.

Not *min* — "from." Not *ʿinda* — "with." But *ilā* — *toward*. A preposition of direction. Of orientation. Of motion toward.

What does that change?

It means your poverty is not just a condition you sit in. It is *directional*. Your need has a destination. The hollowness in you is not a hollowness that goes nowhere — it is a hollowness that, by its very shape, points. Like a compass needle that cannot help but turn north, the *faqr* in you is oriented toward Him. You are not just empty. You are empty *toward Allah*. Your need is a vector. Your lack has a true north.

This is why even the atheist who denies Him cannot escape Him. Because the orientation is not in the believing — it is in the being. You can refuse to face north, but you cannot make north move. The needle of your *faqr* still points where it points, regardless of what your tongue says.

What this single word *al-fuqarā'u **ilā** Allāh* reveals: you are not a self-sufficient being who occasionally needs help. You are a structure of oriented emptiness, made to be filled only by the One toward whom that emptiness is shaped.

## Element Two: *Wa Allāhu **huwa** al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd* — the pronoun that restricts

### Teaching the concept

In Arabic grammar, there is a small but powerful feature called the **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — the "pronoun of separation." It is a pronoun placed between a subject and a predicate when, grammatically, the sentence could function without it.

Listen to the difference in English: *"Allah is the Self-Sufficient"* versus *"Allah — He is the Self-Sufficient."* That extra "He" doesn't have to be there. It adds nothing to the basic information. But it adds something else: emphasis, restriction, exclusivity. It says: *He, and only He.* It draws a circle around the subject and says: this attribute belongs here and nowhere else.

That is what the *ḍamīr al-faṣl* does in Arabic. The grammarians call it a particle of *qaṣr* — restriction.

### Opening the word

Look at the second half of the ayah: **وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ** — *wa-Allāhu **huwa** al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd*.

Allah could have said *wa-Allāhu al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd* — "And Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy." The sentence would be complete. The meaning would be conveyed.

But He places *huwa* — "He" — in the middle. And that small pronoun does enormous work. It says: **He, and only He.** The genuine, exclusive, undiluted possessor of these two names.

### Let's sit with this

We live in a world full of *relative ghina* — relative wealth, relative self-sufficiency. The wealthy person seems self-sufficient compared to the poor. The healthy person seems self-sufficient compared to the sick. The corporation seems self-sufficient compared to the start-up. There is a constant illusion that *someone* in the picture has it together, has enough, doesn't need.

The pronoun *huwa* sweeps the entire field. **No.** Look again. Of all things — created, uncreated, rich, poor, kings, beggars, civilizations, planets, galaxies — only One is Self-Sufficient. **Huwa.** Him. Not "Him among others." Not "Him to a greater degree." Only Him, full stop.

Which means: **every other apparent self-sufficiency is borrowed.** Every wealthy person is wealthy by His allowance. Every strong person is strong by His giving. Every nation that thinks it stands by its own strength stands only by His withholding of withdrawal. The pronoun *huwa* doesn't just elevate Him; it dethrones every false claimant to self-sufficiency.

### The pairing — *al-Ghaniyy* with *al-Ḥamīd*

And now, the deepest thing.

The two names are paired: *al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd* — the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy. These two names appear together not just here but in many places in the Quran (you'll find the same pairing in 14:8, 31:12, 31:26, 64:6, and others). The pairing is not accidental. It is structural.

Why these two together?

Here is what we are invited to consider. In the human world, *ghina* — self-sufficiency, wealth, not-needing — has a problem. The rich person who needs no one often *gives* to no one. The self-sufficient man becomes the withdrawn man. The one who has everything has no use for you. We have all met that wealth — the wealth that has hardened into a wall. The wealth that says: *I don't need you, and I owe you nothing.* That wealth is *ghaniyy*. It is not *ḥamīd*.

Praise — *ḥamd* — is reserved in Arabic for goodness that *flows*. *Ḥamd* is not just any praise; it is the praise given for qualities that are intrinsically beautiful *and* extended in benefit. You praise someone for their generosity, their wisdom, their kindness — the goodness in them that reaches you.

So here is the marvel: Allah is *al-Ghaniyy* — He has no need of you whatsoever, in any direction, in any way. And He is *al-Ḥamīd* — and yet His self-sufficiency does not become withdrawal. His wealth does not become a wall. His not-needing does not become not-giving. He needs nothing from creation, and pours everything onto creation. That is why He is *al-Ḥamīd*.

[PAUSE]

The Muyassar tafsir captures this beautifully: *al-Ḥamīd fī dhātihi wa asmā'ihi wa ṣifātihi, al-maḥmūd ʿalā niʿamihi* — "the Praiseworthy in His essence and His names and His attributes, the One praised for His favors. For every blessing on people comes from Him; so to Him belongs all praise and gratitude in every state."

He needs nothing from you. He owes you nothing. And yet — every breath you take is His giving. Every cell in your body that functions, every neuron that fires, every moment of consciousness, every moment of love, every moment of beauty — His. Not because He needs to. Because He *is* what He is. *Al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd.*

### What this pairing reveals

This is the kind of wealth that exists nowhere in the created order.

Every human wealth either *needs* (and therefore takes) or *withdraws* (and therefore stops giving). Wealth that genuinely needs nothing and yet genuinely keeps giving — there is no such wealth in this world. It exists only with Him.

And the pairing teaches you something about what you were *made for*. You were made *al-faqīr* — by identity — toward One who is *al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd* — by identity. Your emptiness was shaped to be filled by a wealth that does not require you to pay it back, does not extract from you, does not need you in any way. The fit is perfect. You are the cup with the broken bottom; He is the spring that never stops flowing. He does not flow because He needs the cup. He flows because that is what He is.

[PAUSE]

### Bridging to the themes

We have now seen two things. We have seen that *you* are not someone who has need — you are *the* needy ones, by identity, oriented toward Him. And we have seen that *He* is not someone who happens to be self-sufficient — He is the Only Self-Sufficient, and His self-sufficiency does not withdraw but pours.

Now let's ask what this means not just about words — but about how we are actually living.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The illusion that you are not poor

Let's start where you actually live.

You woke up this morning. You probably did not think: *I am the broken-backed destitute, by my very nature, dependent for every breath on a Sustainer.* You probably thought: *I have a meeting at 10. I need to answer that email. The kids need lunch. I should go to the gym.*

The architecture of modern life is built to obscure the fact of your *faqr* from you. You have a refrigerator full of food, so you do not feel the hunger that would remind you that you do not provide your own sustenance. You have a heart beating without your input, so you do not feel the dependency that every beat represents. You have a roof over your head, a job, a calendar, a sense of control. You are organized. You are functional. You are — by the standards of your culture — *fine*.

The deepest assumption of late modernity, articulated by everyone from your therapist to your favorite influencer to the founding documents of your nation, is this: **you are an autonomous self. You are the agent of your own becoming. You are the captain of your soul. You do not, fundamentally, need anyone.** Independence is the highest virtue. Self-reliance is the highest dignity. Needing someone else — really needing them — is weakness, codependency, lack of growth.

And then the Quran walks into the room and says: *Yā ayyuhā an-nās. Antumu al-fuqarā'.*

It is not a suggestion. It is not a corrective for unbelievers only. It is addressed to *al-nās* — all of humanity, including you, right now. And it says: every story you have been told about your autonomy is a story told by someone who needed you to keep buying it. The story is not true. **You are the poor ones.** Broken-backed. By identity. Oriented toward Him whether you face Him or not.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does, when you really let it in.

If your poverty is *circumstantial* — if it is something that happens to you sometimes — then you will spend your life trying to escape it. You will build walls against it. Money is one wall. Status is another. Relationships, accomplishments, plans, control, insurance policies, savings accounts, retirement plans — every one of these is partly an attempt to engineer your way out of *faqr*. To not be poor anymore. To finally arrive at self-sufficiency.

But if your poverty is *ontological* — if you *are* the *faqīr* — then no wall can hold against it. No amount of money will move the needle. You will still be the broken-backed one, just better dressed. You will still be oriented toward Him, just with a more impressive distraction in the way. The walls don't make you not-poor. They just make you *unaware* of your poverty.

And being unaware of your poverty is not a blessing. It is a tragedy. Because the person who knows they are poor toward Allah turns to Him. The person who does not know — turns to anyone, anything, everything *but* Him. And finds, eventually, that none of it fills the shape that was made to be filled by Him alone.

### The mercy hidden inside this naming

Here is something that should change how you hear this ayah.

It feels, at first, like a humiliation. *You are the poor ones.* That sounds like a rebuke. Like Allah is putting you in your place. Like a reminder of your smallness designed to crush you.

It is not.

Stay with this.

When Allah names something about you, the naming is a *mercy*. Because the alternative — the alternative to being told what you are — is to live your whole life *guessing* what you are. Inventing identities. Constructing selves out of jobs and roles and accomplishments and other people's opinions. Spending decades on constructions that the next gust of wind blows down. And then dying without ever having met yourself.

When Allah says *antumu al-fuqarā'*, He is handing you the key to your own identity. He is saying: *stop guessing. Here is what you are. You are the one made empty toward Me. Now you can stop building selves that don't fit, and start being the self you actually are.*

The diagnosis is the beginning of the healing. The naming is the door to peace. Because the moment you stop pretending to be self-sufficient, the moment you accept that you are *al-faqīr* — the entire crushing project of *becoming enough on your own* collapses. And in its place opens up a different kind of life. A life where you do not have to be the source. You do not have to be the foundation. You do not have to be the wealth. You can simply be the cup, and let the Spring be the Spring.

### What this changes about tomorrow

Tomorrow morning, when you wake up, try this one thing.

Before you reach for your phone, before the day's tasks crowd in, place your hand on your chest and feel your heartbeat. You did not start it. You cannot stop it. You did not choose its rhythm. Whatever you accomplish today, it accomplishes itself, second by second, without your permission.

That heartbeat is the ayah, made flesh in you. *Antumu al-fuqarā' ilā Allāh.* You are the poor ones, oriented toward Allah. Every beat is given. Every breath is given. You are not the source of yourself. You never were.

And in that moment of remembering, something becomes possible that was not possible before: you can begin the day with *thanks* instead of with *demand*. You can move through the day as a receiver instead of as a producer. You can let your *faqr* be the door it was always meant to be — the door through which His wealth enters.

## Theme Two: The wealth that does not need you, and gives anyway

Now let's go to the other side of the ayah.

We talked about your *faqr*. Let's talk about His *ghina*.

Everyone you have ever known is one of two things. Either they need you in some way — and so their giving to you is, in part, a transaction — or they have decided they do not need you, and so their giving has dried up. There is no third option in the human world. Even the most generous parent needs their child in some way: needs to be needed, needs the role of parent, needs the meaning that loving the child provides. The most generous friend needs the friendship in some way. The most selfless teacher needs the act of teaching. *We do not give without needing.*

This is not a moral failure. It is the structure of being created. Every creature is itself *al-faqīr*. Every creature needs. So every creature's giving is intertwined with its needing.

This means that, in your entire life as a human being, you have never received a gift that was not, in some sense, a transaction. You have never been loved by someone who did not need your love back, at least a little. You have never been helped by someone for whom the helping was not also, somehow, theirs.

[PAUSE]

And now, Allah names Himself.

**Wa-Allāhu huwa al-ghaniyyu al-ḥamīd.**

He is the Self-Sufficient. He needs nothing. Not your worship, not your gratitude, not your existence, not your love, not your obedience. The Prophet ﷺ is reported in the qudsī narration to convey: *"If the first of you and the last of you, the human and the jinn, were as pious as the most pious heart among you, it would not increase My dominion in the slightest. And if the first of you and the last of you were as wicked as the most wicked heart among you, it would not decrease My dominion in the slightest."*

He does not need you.

And yet — *al-Ḥamīd*. Praiseworthy. Because His not-needing does not become withdrawal. Because the wealth that needs nothing keeps pouring anyway. Because the One who gains nothing from your worship gives you the capacity to worship. Because the One who loses nothing from your sin gives you the breath in which to repent.

[PAUSE]

### Where in the human world does this exist?

It doesn't.

Find me one being in creation whose generosity does not depend, in some hidden corner, on a need. You will not find one. Because need-free giving is not within the capacity of created beings.

Which means: when Allah pairs *al-Ghaniyy* with *al-Ḥamīd*, He is naming a quality that exists nowhere else in existence. A wealth you cannot imagine because you have never seen its analog. A generosity that has no transactional residue. A giving that does not extract from the giver and does not place the receiver in debt that warps the relationship.

When you give someone a thousand dollars, you have less. When you give your time, you have less time. When you give your love, you may feel it expand, but you also experience the cost. Every act of giving in creation involves the giver becoming, in some sense, smaller.

Allah gives. And nothing in Him decreases. *Al-Ghaniyy.* Self-Sufficient before the giving. Self-Sufficient during the giving. Self-Sufficient after the giving. Your existence does not subtract from Him. Your forgiveness does not subtract from Him. The mercy that fills your life is not pulled from a finite reservoir. It is the overflow of an infinite One whose overflowing is not loss.

### The freedom this gives you

Stay with what this means for your relationship with Him.

In every human relationship, there is a hidden ledger. *I did this for you; what will you do for me?* Even the relationships we call most pure are not fully free of this. Friendships break when one side feels the ledger has tilted. Marriages strain when the accounting feels unjust. Family bonds fray under accumulated debts.

You cannot owe Allah in this way. You cannot. Because *He needs nothing from you*. Your obedience does not pay Him back, because there is no debt in His ledger that needs paying. Your worship does not enrich Him. Your gratitude does not increase Him. The relationship has no transactional dimension on His side.

This means: when you turn to Him, you are not entering into a contract. You are not building a balance you must maintain. You are not at risk of being cut off because you stopped delivering value. **He does not need your value.** He never did.

So why does He call you? Why the worship, why the prayer, why the Quran, why the entire architecture of religion?

Because *you* need it. Not Him. *You* are the *faqīr*. *You* are the one who shrivels without orientation toward Him. *You* are the one whose being needs to be in motion toward its true north or it collapses into incoherence. He called you to prayer not because His glory required your bowing — His glory is intact whether you bow or not — but because *your* spine cannot hold its own weight, and bowing toward Him is what straightens it.

Every command in this religion, every prohibition, every act of worship, every story in the Quran, every prophet sent — none of it was for Him. He needs none of it. All of it was for you. For the cup, not the Spring. The Spring needs no one to drink. The cup needs the Spring or it is just a hollow object.

[PAUSE]

### What this changes about tomorrow

Tomorrow, when you pray — and I hope you do — try entering the prayer with this awareness.

You are not doing Allah a favor by praying. You are not earning credits in a divine ledger. You are not topping up an account that will be drawn down later. **You are a cup, going to the Spring, because that is the only place the cup makes sense.**

And try, in the prayer, to feel what it is to stand before the One who needs nothing from you. There is no transaction. There is no exchange. There is nothing you can offer that He needs and nothing you can withhold that He misses. There is only the cup, and the Spring, and the act of being filled — not because the Spring requires your drinking, but because *you cannot live without the drinking, and the Spring, in its infinite generosity, simply pours.*

That is what *al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd* invites you into. A relationship with no ledger. The first relationship in your entire life that is genuinely, completely, eternally free of debt.

# Closing Synthesis

## The two halves are one truth

Look back now at the structure of the ayah. It is built in two halves, mirroring each other.

**أَنتُمُ ٱلْفُقَرَآءُ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ** — *You are the poor ones, toward Allah.*
**وَٱللَّهُ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ** — *And Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy.*

In the first half: an identity (the poor ones), with a direction (toward Allah).
In the second half: an identity (the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy), with an exclusivity (He alone).

The first half names you. The second half names Him. And the two halves are not two pieces of information. They are one truth, seen from two sides. Because the very meaning of *al-faqīr* requires *al-Ghaniyy*. The cup only makes sense in relation to the Spring. The orientation only makes sense if there is something to be oriented toward. The emptiness only has a shape because the One who would fill it has a shape.

And that is the marvel of this short ayah. In one breath, Allah tells you what you are *and* what He is, and shows that the two are perfectly fitted to each other. Your poverty is exactly shaped for His wealth. His giving is exactly shaped for your need. The architecture of reality is the architecture of this ayah: *al-fuqarā'* oriented *ilā Allāh*, and *Allāh huwa al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd*.

1. What walls have I built to avoid feeling my own *faqr*? And what would happen if, just for a day, I let those walls down?

2. Whose face do I turn to when I feel the first hint of need — and is that face *al-Ghaniyy*, or is it someone or something that is itself *al-faqīr*?

3. In my worship, am I trying to *give* Allah something? And if I let that go — if I came to Him as a cup goes to the Spring, with nothing to offer — would I be able to pray?

4. The pairing *al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd* says that true wealth is wealth that *gives*. Where in my own life am I withholding because I think I "have," and where am I giving because I have understood that I have nothing?

5. If my *faqr* is permanent — if I will never not be the *faqīr* — what does it mean to live this whole life as a being who does not need to escape that fact, but who can rest in it as the very door to the relationship I was made for?

## The one-sentence distillation

**Allah does not say you are needy — He says you ARE need itself, oriented toward Him; and He alone is the Self-Sufficient whose self-sufficiency, miraculously, does not stop giving.**

## The invitation

What might shift after this is not an action. It is a stance.

Stop carrying the weight of being your own source. Stop performing the autonomy that was never yours. Stop trying to be *al-Ghaniyy* in your own small way — in your finances, in your relationships, in your spiritual practice, in your sense of self.

You are *al-faqīr*. You always were. And the moment you stop pretending otherwise, the moment you let your broken back bend, the moment you let your emptiness be empty and turn it toward the One toward whom it is already pointed — *that* is the moment you start to live.

The cup does not have to become the Spring. It just has to come to the Spring.

## A brief du'a

*Yā Allāh, You are al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd, and we are al-fuqarā' toward You.*

*Strip from us the illusion that we are anything else. Let us not be ashamed of our poverty before You; let us not perform a self-sufficiency that was never ours. Make us cups that come to the Spring without pretense, and let us drink from a wealth that needs nothing from us and yet, by Your eternal mercy, pours and pours and pours.*

*Āmīn.*
