# The Ruins of the Strong

### Surah Fatir 35:44

> أَوَلَمْ يَسِيرُوا۟ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ فَيَنظُرُوا۟ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَـٰقِبَةُ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ وَكَانُوٓا۟ أَشَدَّ مِنْهُمْ قُوَّةًۚ وَمَا كَانَ ٱللَّهُ لِيُعْجِزَهُۥ مِن شَىْءٍ فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَلَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ عَلِيمًا قَدِيرًا

*Awalam yasīrū fi-l-arḍi fa-yanẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu-lladhīna min qablihim wa kānū ashadda minhum quwwah. Wa mā kāna-llāhu li-yuʿjizahu min shayʾin fi-s-samāwāti wa lā fi-l-arḍ. Innahu kāna ʿalīman qadīrā.*

**"Have they not traveled through the land and seen how was the end of those before them? And they were greater than them in power. But nothing in the heavens or on the earth can ever frustrate Allah. Indeed, He has ever been All-Knowing, All-Powerful."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Road Outside Mecca

I want you to begin not with the verse, but with a road.

Picture the trade routes that ran out of Mecca. North toward Syria, the caravans of the Quraysh moved twice a year — the journey of winter and the journey of summer, the one the Qur'an itself mentions. These were not casual travelers. These were merchants who knew the desert the way you know your commute. And along those roads, they passed something.

They passed **ruins.**

The shattered dwellings of ʿĀd in the south. The carved-out mountain homes of Thamūd at al-Ḥijr, where Ṣāliḥ had warned a people who hewed palaces from the living rock and thought themselves untouchable. The wreckage of the cities of Lūṭ's people, by the Dead Sea. The Quraysh did not read about these places in a book. They *drove past them.* Twice a year. Their camels' hooves kicked dust off the graves of civilizations that had been incomparably mightier than the small merchant city of Mecca.

And here is the thing that should stop you. They saw the ruins — and felt nothing.

This is the world you need to inhabit before this ayah can land. Just before this verse, in 35:43, Allah has said something stark: that the deniers had sworn solemn oaths that *if* a warner ever came to them, they would be more guided than any nation before. The warner came. And they were worse. So Allah closes that passage with a sentence that hangs in the air like a verdict over all of human history:

*"You will never find any change in the way of Allah. You will never find any alteration in the way of Allah."*

The law does not bend. The pattern does not make exceptions.

And then — *then* — comes our verse. It is the **evidence** offered for that unchanging law. It does not argue. It does not theorize. It points out the window of the moving caravan and says: *look.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Two things in this verse should unsettle you and I want you to carry the questions, not the answers, into what follows.

First: Allah does not say these dead nations were *like* the deniers in power. He says they were **stronger.** More powerful. The lesson is not "the weak were destroyed." The lesson is "*the strong* were destroyed — the ones who looked exactly like you look to yourself: solid, established, in control."

Second: after sending them to look at the wreckage, the verse seems to change the subject entirely. Suddenly we are not talking about ruins at all. We are talking about the heavens and the earth, and a single word — *nothing* — that can escape Him. Why? What does an empire crumbling in the sand have to do with the architecture of the cosmos?

Let's walk the road and find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Two Verbs — *yasīrū* and *fa-yanẓurū* — Walking Is Not Seeing

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because it changes everything about how this opening reads.

In Arabic, there is a small letter — *fā'* (فَ) — that connects two actions. It is not the neutral "and" (*wa*) that simply lists things side by side. The *fā'* carries **sequence and consequence.** It means *and so*, *and thereby*, *which then leads to*. If I say in Arabic "he struck him *fa* he died," I am not listing two events. I am telling you the second flowed out of the first. The blow *produced* the death.

Now hold that, and look at what Allah pairs.

Two verbs. **يَسِيرُوا** — *yasīrū*, "they travel" — from the root *s-y-r*, the root of physical movement across the earth, the same root behind *sayyārah*, the thing that moves, a caravan, and in modern Arabic, a car. And then, joined by that consequential *fā'*: **فَيَنظُرُوا** — *fa-yanẓurū*, "and so they see," from *n-ẓ-r*, not the passive seeing of the eye, but *naẓar* — the look that considers, that examines, that turns a thing over.

[PAUSE]

Notice what the grammar is quietly insisting on. Allah does **not** say *"have they not traveled and been destroyed,"* nor *"have they not traveled through the ruins."* He says: have they not traveled **so that they would then look.**

The travel is not the point. The travel is supposed to *produce* something. The *fā'* is the hinge on which the whole rebuke turns — because the Quraysh *had* done the first verb. They traveled. Constantly. They were the most well-traveled people in Arabia. What they had not done was let the travel cross the threshold into the second verb. The motion never became sight.

Let's sit with this, because it is devastatingly modern.

You have walked past more wreckage than the Quraysh ever did. Not ruins of stone — ruins of people. The relative who built an empire and died estranged from his children. The famous name from a decade ago you cannot now remember. The obituaries you scroll past. The collapsed company, the fallen leader, the friend's marriage that everyone thought was unshakeable. You *travel through the land.* Your feed is a caravan route lined with the ʿĀd and Thamūd of our age — the strong who fell.

And here is the indictment hidden in a single letter: **movement past the lesson is not the lesson.** The eye registering the ruin is not *naẓar*. You can drive past the car wreck on the highway — and everyone does slow down to look, the whole road slows — and then accelerate away having changed nothing about how you drive. You *saw*. You did not *see*.

The classical commentators are unanimous and unhurried about this. Al-Qurtubi explains the verse as a command to *tadabbur* — to reflect — through two channels at once: through their own eyes, looking upon the dwellings and the houses of the destroyed, *and* through what reached them by mass-transmitted report. He says, in effect: is there not in this an *ʿibrah*, a crossing-over lesson, for them? They are no better than those people were, and no stronger.

That word *ʿibrah* is itself beautiful — it comes from the root that means *to cross over a river.* A lesson is something you are supposed to *cross over on* — from one bank of your life to another. The ruins are a bridge. The Quraysh stood on the bridge and never crossed.

[PAUSE]

So the first thing this verse reveals, before we have even reached its main subject, is this: **Allah holds you responsible not for what passes before your eyes, but for what you do with it.** The data is not the wisdom. The travel is not the sight. And the most traveled person can be the most blind.

### Element Two: *ʿāqibah* — The Thing That Comes Behind

Now to the word they were supposed to look *at.* **عَاقِبَةُ** — *ʿāqibah.* The translations say "the end" or "the outcome." Both are true, but both are thin, and the root is where the weight lives.

The root is *ʿ-q-b.* And here you should know that this is the same root family that gives us *ʿaqib* — the **heel**, the back of the foot. It gives us *ʿuqbā*, the final return. It gives us, sobering to note, *ʿuqūbah* — **punishment**, the consequence that comes walking up behind a deed. The whole root circles one image: **that which follows behind, that which comes at the heel of a thing.**

So *ʿāqibah* is not merely "the end" the way a clock runs out. It is the **trailing consequence** — what your actions drag along behind them, the wake a ship leaves, the part of the story that catches up to you when you thought you had walked on past it.

Think about how a film works. You sit through two hours and you think you understand the characters — this one is the hero, that one got away with it, this empire is glorious. And then the final scene reveals what it all *meant*, and you suddenly re-read everything that came before in its light. The *ʿāqibah* is the final scene. It is the moment when the meaning of the whole life, the whole nation, the whole arc of arrogance, finally arrives — from *behind*, at the heel.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable, and you have to hear the grammar to feel it. The verse does not say "look at the *ruins* of those before them." It says "look at **how was the** *ʿāqibah* — how was the *trailing consequence* — of those before them." *Kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu...* — "*how was* the end."

It directs your gaze not to the rubble as a tourist photograph, but to the **causation.** *How* did it come to this? What walked up behind them? The ruin is not the lesson; the ruin is the evidence of a law. Al-Muyassar's plain reading captures it: how was the end of those before them — like ʿĀd and Thamūd and their like — and the destruction that befell them, the desolation of their dwellings, *when they denied the messengers.* The *when* is the heel. The denial walked behind them the whole time, and at the *ʿāqibah*, it caught up.

So when you stand — really stand, in *naẓar* — before any fallen strength in your own world, the question the word *ʿāqibah* trains you to ask is not "what happened?" It is: *"what was walking behind them the entire time that I should check is not walking behind me?"*

Because the consequence is patient. It is already on the road. The root tells you so: it travels at the heel.

### Element Three: *li-yuʿjizahu* — The Word That Seals Every Exit

And now we arrive at the strangest turn in the verse, the place where it seems to abandon the ruins entirely. After sending the deniers to the graves of the strong, Allah says:

**وَمَا كَانَ ٱللَّهُ لِيُعْجِزَهُۥ مِن شَىْءٍ** — *wa mā kāna-llāhu li-yuʿjizahu min shayʾ* — "and nothing whatsoever can frustrate Allah."

Let me build this carefully, because three pieces of grammar are working together and each one tightens the seal.

**The verb.** *Li-yuʿjizahu* comes from the root *ʿ-j-z.* The bare root means *to be incapable, to be weak, to fall short* — *ʿajz* is the very state of powerlessness, of trying and failing. Now it has been put into **Form IV** — the form *afʿala*. Here Form IV makes the verb transitive and outward-directed: not "to be weak" but **to render the other party incapable of reaching you — to outstrip, to elude, to thwart, to escape such that the pursuer is left helpless.** This is the *iʿjāz* of one who slips a grasp. It is the fugitive who gets away; the prey that outruns the hunter so that the hunter, panting, is *brought to ʿajz* — reduced to incapacity. Al-Baghawi glosses it with one transparent word: *li-yafūta* — "that anything should *escape* Him, slip past Him, be missed by Him."

So the picture inside the verb is not abstract metaphysics. It is a **chase.** It is the deep, animal human fantasy that you might run fast enough, far enough, clever enough, that the One pursuing is left grasping at air.

**The negation.** Now look at the little word stack: *mā kāna... li-yuʿjizahu.* This is a specific, emphatic construction the grammarians call the *lām al-juḥūd* — the **lām of utter denial.** When Arabic puts *mā kāna* ("it never was the case that...") and then a *lām* before the verb, it does not merely say "this does not happen." It says: **this is not in the nature of things to ever happen. It is categorically impossible. It was never so and the very possibility is foreclosed.** It is the difference between "he doesn't lie" and "he was *never one to* lie — it is not in him." Allah does not say "nothing escapes Him." He says it was **never even a possibility** that anything *could.*

**The scope.** And then, having sealed the *kind* of impossibility, the verse seals the *space* of it: *min shayʾin fi-s-samāwāti wa lā fi-l-arḍ* — "of any thing, in the heavens, *nor* in the earth." Not one realm. Both. Up and down. The entire coordinate system of existence. There is no third place.

[PAUSE]

Now — *now* — do you see why the verse left the ruins?

It did not change the subject. It **answered the fugitive's last hope.**

Because when a person looks at the destroyed nations, the strong who fell, the heart's final defense is not "it won't happen to me." The heart's final defense is deeper and more desperate: *"and even if it comes for me — I will not be there when it arrives. I will be somewhere else. I will have moved on, slipped out, gotten away."* This is the universal instinct of the one who fears a reckoning: not to deny the reckoning, but to plan an exit from it.

And the verse closes the exit. There is no *somewhere else.* The Form IV verb says you cannot outrun Him. The *lām al-juḥūd* says it was never even possible to. And *the heavens nor the earth* says there is no jurisdiction outside His, no border you can cross, no room in the house that is not in the house.

Think of the child in hide-and-seek who covers his own eyes and believes he has disappeared. We smile at it because the child has confused *not seeing* with *not being seen.* The whole edifice of human denial is that child grown tall. We build elaborate ways of covering our own eyes — distraction, postponement, the sheer momentum of *yasīrū* without *fa-yanẓurū* — and we mistake our blindness for our safety.

The verse uncovers the eyes. Gently, terribly: *there is nowhere.*

[PAUSE]

**The bridge.** So watch what the linguistic architecture has built, three words laid in sequence. *Yasīrū / fa-yanẓurū* told us we are responsible for turning motion into sight. *ʿĀqibah* told us the consequence travels patiently at the heel, and that the strong are not exempt. And *li-yuʿjizahu* told us that the one place we imagine we could flee to — the elsewhere, the later, the exit — does not exist.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about the two oldest lies the human heart tells, and the two divine names at the end of the verse that answer them.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lie of the Exception — Why Allah Sends You to Walk Among the Strong

Let me start where you actually live, with something psychologists have a name for.

It is called **survivorship bias** — but I want a sharper version of it. Imagine you study only the billionaires who dropped out of college and you conclude that dropping out makes you rich. You never counted the millions who dropped out and vanished, because the vanished leave no memoirs. We reason from what is loud and present, and we are blind to what is silent and gone.

Now flip it. The deeper human disease is not just statistical. It is *personal exceptionalism.* Every empire that ever collapsed believed itself the exception to the rule that empires collapse. Rome believed it. Every civilization in the museum believed it. And — here is the uncomfortable part — *you* believe it about yourself. Not consciously. But in the quiet operating system underneath, you run on the assumption that the rules that broke everyone else have, in your case, made some allowance. The diagnoses, the declines, the reckonings — those are for *the data.* You are not the data. You are *you.*

This is precisely the disease the verse is treating. And notice the medicine Allah chooses.

He does not send the Quraysh to look at the ruins of the *weak.* That would teach the wrong lesson — it would let them say *"of course the weak fell; we are not weak."* Instead, the verse insists: **وَكَانُوٓا۟ أَشَدَّ مِنْهُمْ قُوَّةً** — *"and they were stronger than them in power."*

Sit inside that for a moment. The destroyed were the *strong.* The ones in the rubble were not the cautionary failures; they were the success stories — the ʿĀd whom the Qur'an elsewhere describes building monuments on every height, the Thamūd who carved mansions into mountains. They were the people other people envied. They were the ones who looked, to themselves, exactly the way you look to yourself: stable, capable, in control, *the exception.*

[PAUSE]

This is why the lesson had to be *walked* and not merely *told.* You can argue with a sentence. You cannot argue with a mountain that someone carved a palace into, standing empty, with the wind moving through it. The strength is still visible in the stone. *That* is what makes it unbearable — and curative. The strength remained; the people did not. The carving outlasted the carver. Al-Qurtubi presses exactly this nail: they are *not better than those people, nor stronger.* The strongest argument against your sense of exception is the bones of someone stronger than you.

Now let the human movement build. Why do we cling so hard to the lie of the exception? Because the alternative feels like despair. If even the strong fall, what hope is there? And here the verse performs a quiet reversal that I do not want you to miss.

The destruction of the strong is not, in the end, bad news for you. It is the *most level ground there is.* If strength could buy exemption, then you and I — ordinary, unmighty — would have no chance at all; we would be crushed simply for lacking power. But the verse reveals a universe where power buys **nothing** before Allah. Which means the currency that *does* count is not power at all. It is the thing the messengers came carrying, the thing ʿĀd and Thamūd threw away: response. Humility. The turning of the heart.

The fall of the strong is the abolition of the only ranking that would have doomed the weak. The graveyard of empires is, strangely, the leveling that makes mercy possible for the small.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the specific practice. The next time your feed or your life shows you a fallen strength — a collapsed reputation, a sudden diagnosis, an empire of a person brought low — stop the scroll. Do not perform the second verb's failure. Do not just *travel past.* Ask the *ʿāqibah* question out loud: *"What was walking behind them — and is it walking behind me?"* You are not gloating; you are crossing the bridge. You are letting their ruin be your *ʿibrah*, the river-crossing it was placed there to be. One conscious *naẓar* a day. That is the discipline this verse is asking for.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Lie of the Exit — and the Two Names That Close Both Doors

The first lie is *"it won't happen to me."* When that lie finally breaks — and it does break, for everyone, eventually — the heart does not surrender. It retreats to a second, more cunning lie: *"fine, it happens — but I will not be caught when it does. I will have gotten away."*

This is the lie of the **exit.** And it is everywhere in how we actually live.

It is the person who knows the lifestyle is unsustainable but assumes *some future version of me will fix it before the bill comes due.* It is the deferral of repentance — *I'll become serious about my prayer, my honesty, my heart, later, when things settle.* It is, at its root, the belief that the gap between the deed and the *ʿāqibah* is a gap you can *escape through* — a door, not just a delay. We treat the patience of consequence as if it were the *absence* of consequence. We mistake the heel that has not yet caught up for a heel that never will.

The fugitive's psychology is profound here. A person can fully believe in justice in the abstract and still, in their body, run from it. They are not denying the law. They are betting on *evasion.* And Allah, in this verse, does not argue against the existence of the reckoning — He has already shown it, in the ruins. He argues against the *evasion.* *Wa mā kāna-llāhu li-yuʿjizahu min shayʾ* — nothing can ever slip His grasp.

And then He gives you the two names. And you must hear them as the closing of *two specific doors*, because that is exactly what they are.

[PAUSE]

**إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ عَلِيمًا قَدِيرًا** — *"Indeed, He has ever been ʿAlīman, Qadīran"* — All-Knowing, All-Powerful.

Why these two? Why not Merciful, Forgiving, here? Because to *escape* a pursuer, you need only one of two things to fail in him. Either he must not **know** where you are — or, knowing, he must not be **able** to reach you. Every escape in the history of the world has worked through one of those two gaps. The fugitive hides where he cannot be found (a failure of the pursuer's knowledge), or he runs where he cannot be caught (a failure of the pursuer's power).

So the verse closes both. *ʿAlīm* — All-Knowing — slams the first door: there is no *where* He does not know. You cannot hide; the child's covered eyes do nothing. *Qadīr* — All-Powerful — slams the second: there is no *reach* He does not have. You cannot run; there is no distance that is far enough, no strength that is strong enough, and we have just stood among the bones of the strongest to prove it.

Knowledge seals the hiding. Power seals the running. Together, they leave the heart with **no exit at all.** This is the precise theological reason the verse pairs *exactly* these two attributes and not others — al-Muyassar names it plainly: *ʿalīman* with their deeds, *qadīran* over their destruction. He knows *what.* He is able *to.* The two halves of every escape, both foreclosed.

[PAUSE]

And now I want to turn this — because everything in the Qur'an that closes a door for the runner *opens* a door for the returner, and we must not leave you in fear when the verse intends transformation.

There is no exit. Yes. But read it the other way: **there is no need for one.** The entire desperate project of the human life — the hiding, the running, the deferral, the exhausting maintenance of the exit strategy — is unnecessary, because it was always impossible. You were never going to get away. So you can *stop running.* The one from whom there is no escape is the same one who said, in this very surah, that He is *al-Ghafūr ash-Shakūr* — the Forgiving, the Appreciative. The pursuer you cannot elude is not a bounty hunter. He is the One who, the moment you stop running and turn, was already nearer than you imagined.

The fugitive who finally stops is not captured. He is *received.*

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the practice. Find the thing you are currently planning to deal with *later* — the deferred repentance, the postponed honesty, the reckoning you are betting you will exit before it arrives. Name it. And then apply the verse to it directly: *there is no later that is an exit; there is only a later that is a longer run.* Then do the small first thing now — the one prayer, the one apology, the one honesty — not out of terror, but because you have finally understood you were never going to outrun Him, and the running was the only thing keeping you from the receiving.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: The Walk and the Wall

Now stand back and look at how this verse is built, because the structure is itself the argument.

The verse has two movements, and they are joined like a corridor that ends in a wall.

**The walk:** *Have they not traveled... and seen... how was the end of those before them — and they were stronger.* This is horizontal. It moves you *outward and backward* — out across the land, back into history, through the ruins of the strong. It is the journey of *sayr* and *naẓar*, the corridor lined with the wreckage of empires.

**The wall:** *But nothing in the heavens or the earth can ever frustrate Him. He has ever been All-Knowing, All-Powerful.* This is vertical and total. It moves you *upward and inward* — up to the heavens, down to the earth, and into the two attributes that admit no gap. It is the wall at the end of the corridor.

And here is the architecture's quiet genius. The walk is the *natural* part — anyone can travel, anyone can see ruins. But the walk alone leaves you an exit: *that was them, this is me; even if not, I'll get away.* So the verse does not let you end at the ruins. It marches you down the whole corridor of history and then sets you against a wall with no door in it. The *ʿāqibah* of the strong (the walk) is sealed by the *iʿjāz* that is impossible (the wall). You cannot dismiss the ruins as someone else's story, because the same Power that wrote their ending holds every coordinate of yours.

### The Two Directions, One Insight

Look how the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the same truth seen from two sides.

The *fā'* of *fa-yanẓurū* taught us that motion must become sight — and the **lie of the exception** is precisely the failure to let it: traveling past the lesson without crossing the bridge. They are one thing.

And the verb *li-yuʿjizahu*, with its impossible escape sealed in the *lām al-juḥūd*, taught us there is no elsewhere — and the **lie of the exit** is precisely the bet that there is. They are one thing.

So the whole verse, from its first letter to its last name, is doing a single work: it is taking the two doors the human heart keeps cracked open — *"not me"* and *"not caught"* — and closing them, gently, completely, so that the heart, finding itself with nowhere left to run, might at last do the one thing it was made for, and **turn.**

### The Distillation

> **The strongest who ever lived are now only dust to be looked at — and since nothing in all the heavens and earth can hide from the One who knows or outrun the One who is able, the only exit was never an exit at all, but a turning toward the One you were exhausting yourself to flee.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I performing the first verb — *traveling, scrolling, moving* — past a lesson I have refused to let become *naẓar*, real sight?

2. When I see a strong person or thing fall, do I quietly file it under "them," or do I ask the *ʿāqibah* question: *what is walking behind me at the heel?*

3. What am I currently betting I will *exit before it arrives* — and what would change if I truly believed there is no door in that wall?

4. Which of the two attributes do I most secretly hope has a gap — that He does not *know*, or that He cannot *reach*? What does my answer reveal about where I am hiding?

5. If I finally stopped running — not from despair, but from understanding — what is the single small thing I would do today?

### The Closing Invitation

You have been on a road this whole time — the caravan route out of Mecca, lined with the bones of the strong. The verse never wanted you to fear the road. It wanted you to *finally see it* — and then to notice that the running was always the heaviest thing you carried, and that you were running from the only One who was never going to let you fall through a gap, because for you He had a different ending in mind than the one in the ruins. Let the walk become sight. Let the sight stop the running. Let the wall, with no door in it, turn you around toward the door that was behind you the whole time — wide open.

### A Du'ā from These Words

*O Allah — ʿAlīm who knows where I have hidden, Qadīr from whom nothing has ever escaped — I am tired of the running, and there was never anywhere to run. Let my traveling become true sight, and let me read the ruins of the strong before I become one of them. And since there is no exit from You, let there be, for me, a turning toward You — and let me find that You were never the pursuer I feared, but the One who was waiting to receive me the moment I stopped.* Āmīn.
