# The Painter of Mountains
## What Color Teaches About Allah

---

## INTRODUCTION

**The Arabic:**

> أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ أَنزَلَ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءً فَأَخْرَجْنَا بِهِۦ ثَمَرَٰتٍ مُّخْتَلِفًا أَلْوَٰنُهَا ۚ وَمِنَ ٱلْجِبَالِ جُدَدٌۢ بِيضٌ وَحُمْرٌ مُّخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَٰنُهَا وَغَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ

**Transliteration:**

*Alam tara anna-Llāha anzala mina s-samāʾi māʾan fa-akhrajnā bihi thamarātin mukhtalifan alwānuhā; wa mina l-jibāli judadun bīḍun wa ḥumrun mukhtalifun alwānuhā wa gharābību sūd.*

**Translation:**

> "Have you not seen that Allah sends down water from the sky, and We bring forth thereby fruits of various colors? And in the mountains are tracts — white and red of various shades — and others raven-black."

---

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, I want you to stand somewhere with me.

Stand on the edge of the Arabian peninsula. Look east toward the mountains of the Hijaz. Look at the volcanic ridges that rise from the desert floor — and notice what you see. You see black mountains. You see red mountains. You see white limestone cliffs. You see ridges that run like veins of color across stone, *paths* of pigment carved into rock that has stood there longer than memory.

The first audience of this ayah lived among these mountains. They walked past them daily. They knew the black ones were called *gharābīb*, so black they were named after the raven. They knew that after rain, fruit emerged from the same brown earth in greens and yellows and reds and purples. They knew all of this.

And yet — they didn't *see* it.

That is the heart of where we are in Surah Fatir. This surah has been speaking, for twenty-six ayahs now, to a people surrounded by mercy and refusing to register it. Allah has called Himself *Fāṭir as-samāwāti wal-arḍ* — the One who originated the heavens and the earth — and the surah has been steadily turning the listener's gaze: look here, look here, look here. But the people keep looking *past* the evidence.

So in ayah 27, Allah does something different.

He doesn't add more evidence. He asks: **have you not seen?**

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

What if I told you that this ayah — about rain and fruit and colored mountains — is not really about rain. Not really about fruit. Not really about mountains.

What if I told you that the next ayah, 35:28, contains one of the most consequential sentences in the entire Quran — the sentence that defines who actually fears Allah — and that ayah 27 is the ramp you must climb before you are allowed to read that sentence?

This ayah is doing something specific. It is teaching us how to *see*. And it is teaching us that the difference between someone who sees and someone who doesn't is not the eyes — it is the heart that's behind them.

Let's earn this slowly.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will sit with three things in this ayah. One question. One shift. One word so strange that classical grammarians wrote pages trying to figure out why Allah arranged it the way He did.

---

### ELEMENT ONE: *Alam tara* — The Question That Isn't a Question

The ayah opens with two small words: **أَلَمْ تَرَ** — *alam tara* — "Have you not seen?"

Before we open what this means, let me ask you something from your own life.

When a parent says to a child, *"Did you not see the cup on the counter?"* — they are not asking for information. They are not waiting for the child to say yes or no. They are pointing. They are saying: *the thing was right there. How did you miss it?*

This is a rhetorical construction. In Arabic grammar it's called an *istifhām taqrīrī* — an interrogative meant to *establish* something, not to ask it. The question expects no answer. It expects recognition.

Now let's open the words themselves.

**أَلَمْ** — *alam* — is the negative particle *lam* with the interrogative *hamza* attached. *Lam* negates a verb in the past sense even though the verb attached to it is in present-tense form. So *alam tara* is literally: *did you not see?* — pointing to the past, to something that has already happened, that should already be in your memory.

**تَرَ** — *tara* — is from the root **ر-أ-ي** (*r-ʾ-y*), the root for sight. But here's what's beautiful: this root in Arabic does not only mean physical seeing. It carries within it *seeing with the heart*, *seeing with the mind*, *understanding*. Imam al-Qurtubi notes on this very ayah: **هذه الرؤية رؤية القلب والعلم** — *this seeing is the seeing of the heart and of knowledge.*

Sit with that for a moment.

Allah is not asking: *did your eyes register photons of light bouncing off colored fruit and colored mountains?* Of course they did. He is asking something else entirely.

He is asking: **did you SEE?**

[PAUSE]

There's a phenomenon psychologists call *inattentional blindness*. The famous experiment: subjects are told to watch a basketball game and count the passes. While they're counting, a person in a gorilla suit walks across the court, stops, beats their chest, and walks off. Roughly half the subjects don't notice the gorilla. Their eyes received the image perfectly. Their brain refused to process it because they weren't *looking* for it.

That is the human condition the Quran is naming.

The mountains were always colored. The fruit was always varied. The rain was always falling. The data was always arriving at the eye. **But the heart was somewhere else.**

So *alam tara* is not just *did you not see*. It is closer to: **how did you miss this? It was right in front of you.**

And notice something — Allah does not say *alam yaraw* — *have they not seen* — in the third person. He addresses *you*, singular. The listener. Each individual reader. He is closing the distance. He is not talking about other people who failed to see. He is asking *you* whether *you* see.

This is the first thing the ayah does. Before it shows us a single mountain or a single fruit, it positions us — not as observers of the world, but as people whose seeing is itself the subject of the question.

---

### ELEMENT TWO: *Anzala* → *Fa-akhrajnā* — The Shift Mid-Sentence

Now we move to something the classical grammarians treasured. Read these two phrases slowly:

> **أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ أَنزَلَ** *— that Allah sent down*
>
> **فَأَخْرَجْنَا** *— so We brought forth*

Did you catch what happened in the middle of that sentence?

The first phrase speaks of Allah in the third person. *Allah sent down.* The second phrase — referring to the very same Allah — switches to the first person plural. *We brought forth.* In a single breath, the speaker has moved from talking *about* Allah to Allah speaking *as* Allah.

This is not a slip. It is not bad grammar. It is one of the most refined devices in the Quran's literary architecture, and it has a name.

Before we name it, let me show you what it does in ordinary speech.

Imagine your grandfather is telling a story about himself. He says: *"And then your grandfather climbed up onto the roof…"* It feels distant. Formal. Then suddenly he shifts: *"…and I sat there until the sun came up."* In that instant, the story collapses the distance. The narrator and the actor become one. You feel him closer.

That is what Arabic rhetoricians call **iltifāt** — *the turning of the face*. The Quran uses it more than any other text in the Arabic language, and it is one of the signatures of divine speech. The address turns toward you. The voice steps closer.

Here in 35:27, the iltifāt accomplishes something specific. Listen.

The first phrase establishes that **Allah** — the One distant, the One transcendent, the One we are required to speak of with reverence — sent the water. Then immediately: **We brought forth.** The very act of bringing fruit out of the soaked ground is presented in the first person, as if Allah is leaning into the sentence.

Why?

Because *anzala* — sending down — is something we can imagine impersonally. Rain falls. It happens. We've seen it.

But *akhrajnā* — *We brought forth* — is intimate. It is a *making*. It is taking something hidden and pulling it into visibility. The Arabic root **خ-ر-ج** (*kh-r-j*) carries the sense of emergence, of being drawn out from concealment. A baby being delivered. A secret being revealed. A treasure being unearthed.

Allah is saying: the rain is My sending. **But the fruit is My making.** And I am not stepping back from the making. I am *here*, in the soil, in the seed, in the splitting of the green shoot from the husk — I am the One pulling color into existence from water that has none.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentator al-Jalalayn notes this iltifāt explicitly — **فيه التفات عن الغيبة** — *in it is a turning away from the absent address.*

And once you see it, you cannot unsee it. The whole ayah becomes more intimate. The mountains become more present. The colors become not facts on a page but Allah's own pigment, dripped from His own hand.

This is the second thing the ayah does. It refuses to let you observe Allah's work from a distance.

---

### ELEMENT THREE: *Gharābību sūd* — The Word That Should Be Wrong

Now we arrive at the strangest piece of language in the ayah. Read these two words slowly:

> **وَغَرَابِيبُ سُودٌ** *— wa gharābību sūd*

The translation often gives us: *"and intensely black."* Or *"raven-black."* But these translations hide what's grammatically strange about it.

In Arabic, the normal way to say *deep black* would be **أَسْوَدُ غِرْبِيبٌ** — *aswadu ghirbīb*. *Black, raven-like.* The base color comes first, then the intensifier. Just like in English: *bright red*, not *red bright*. The qualifier follows the noun it qualifies.

But Allah, in this ayah, reverses the order. He places **غَرَابِيبُ** — *gharābību* — before **سُودٌ** — *sūd*. The intensifier comes before the color. As if to say: *raven-black… black.*

Al-Baghawi, looking at this exact construction, notes that the natural Arabic would be **سود غرابيب على التقديم والتأخير** — that this is a case of *taqdīm wa taʾkhīr*, fronting and delaying. Allah has *moved* the intensifier to the front. He has prioritized it.

Now: why?

Let me ask you something. When you describe a color, what do you usually emphasize?

Most of the time, you emphasize the color itself. "Red roses." "Blue sky." The color is the noun. Whatever modifies it is secondary.

But sometimes — when something is *so* of one color that the color itself becomes inadequate — language strains to keep up. You don't say "red." You say "blood red." You don't say "white." You say "blinding white." The intensifier becomes more important than the color it modifies, because the *intensity* is the real subject.

That is what *gharābību sūd* does. By putting *gharābību* first, the ayah is saying: **the intensity is the point**. The blackness here is not a quality these mountains happen to have. The blackness is so absolute that it has become an event, a presence, a thing that demands its own word before the color word even arrives.

And the word itself — *gharābību* — is from the root **غ-ر-ب** (*gh-r-b*), the same root as **غُرَاب** (*ghurāb*) — the raven. These are mountains so black that the language of *raven* has been pulled in to describe them.

[PAUSE]

But here is where it goes deeper.

Look at the whole list of colors in the ayah:
- Fruits of **various** colors (*mukhtalifan alwānuhā*)
- White (*bīḍ*)
- Red (*ḥumr*)
- Various shades (*mukhtalifun alwānuhā*)
- Raven-black (*gharābību sūd*)

Notice the structure. Allah names *various* colors twice. He gives us *white* and *red* — the colors of life, the colors of soft mountain rock catching sunlight. And then, at the very end, almost as a coda — **and raven-black**.

The black mountains are not lumped in with the others. They are pulled out and given their own word, their own emphasis, their own grammatical privilege. The intensifier is moved forward to make sure we cannot pass over them quickly.

Why?

Because the ayah is not just teaching us that creation has variety. It is teaching us that **the variety includes extremes**. That Allah, the Painter of mountains, does not only paint pastel. He paints with white, with red, with grades in between — and He paints with absolute, unyielding, raven-black.

The same Hand that made the fruit *soft and varied* made the mountains *uncompromising and black*.

Hold onto that. We will come back to it.

---

### Bridge

So we have three things now.

A question that is not a question — *alam tara* — pulling us into seeing with the heart, not just the eye.

A grammatical shift — *anzala → akhrajnā* — collapsing the distance between Allah and His work, making Him present in the soil.

And a word so emphatic that grammar itself had to rearrange around it — *gharābību sūd* — telling us that creation includes not only variety but absolute extremes.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### THEME ONE: The Difference Between Looking and Seeing

I want to start here, far from the ayah, in a place you already live.

You have walked past your front door a thousand times. Could you, right now, describe the doorknob? The exact color of the door? The pattern of the wood grain, the small chip on the lower left, the way the hinges have aged?

For most of us, the answer is no. We have *looked* at our front door thousands of times. We have not *seen* it.

This is the deepest human pathology the Quran names. It is not that we are blind. It is that we are looking at the wrong layer of reality. We are processing the world as a backdrop — as the scenery against which our actual lives play out — instead of as the *signal* it is.

The ayah uses a specific Arabic verb for this: *tara*. And it situates the verb in the negative-interrogative form: *alam tara*. *Have you not seen?* The construction implies that seeing was always possible. The data was always there. The failure is not perceptual. **The failure is attentional.**

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back into the ayah. Look at what Allah lists.

Rain. Fruit. Mountains. Colors.

These are not exotic. These are not signs you have to travel to find. These are the most mundane facts of human existence. Every culture in human history has known rain. Every human who has eaten has known fruit of different colors. Every person who has lived near land has known that mountains have shades.

And that is the point.

Allah is not pointing to the spectacular. He is pointing to the *unremarkable*. He is saying: the most ordinary things in your visual field are, in fact, evidence. You have been walking past evidence your entire life and calling it *scenery*.

The mountain was always colored. The fruit was always varied. The rain was always falling. **You were just somewhere else in your head.**

This is what the Quran calls *ghaflah* — heedlessness. Not active denial. Not aggressive rejection. Just… being elsewhere when reality is speaking.

So when Allah asks *alam tara*, He is not shaming us. He is *interrupting* us. He is breaking the trance. He is doing what a kind teacher does when a student has been staring at a math problem for an hour without seeing the answer: *"Look. Look right here."*

[PAUSE]

And here is what is staggering. The next ayah, 35:28, ends with the sentence:

> **إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى ٱللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْعُلَمَٰٓؤُاْ**
>
> *"Only those who know — *al-ʿulamāʾ* — fear Allah from among His servants."*

This is one of the most consequential definitions in the Quran. And the seeing of 35:27 is the *prerequisite* for the knowing of 35:28. The *ʿālim* in the Quran is not someone who has memorized facts about Allah. The *ʿālim* is someone whose *seeing has been activated*. Someone who looks at a fruit and registers that Allah brought it forth. Someone who looks at a black mountain and feels the weight of the One who painted it.

The pathway is: *seeing → knowing → fearing.* And the fearing here is not terror. It is *khashyah* — the awe that grips a person who has finally understood what they are standing in front of.

So what does this ayah change about how I live tomorrow?

**Tomorrow, when you eat fruit — stop.** Just for a second. Notice that the strawberry is red and the banana is yellow and the blueberry is blue and they all came from the same water and the same soil. Let the question Allah asks land: *alam tara?* — *have you not seen?* Let the answer be: *no, but I see now.*

That is the practice. Not new information. New seeing.

---

### THEME TWO: The God Who Paints With Black

Now we turn to the second theme, which is harder, and which I think is the deeper one.

Why does the ayah end the way it ends?

After listing the soft variety — fruits, whites, reds, gradations — the ayah closes with *gharābību sūd*. **Raven-black.** Allah's chosen final image is not the pastoral pastel of orchards. It is the dark stone of volcanic mountains.

We have to ask: why?

Let me bring in an analogy from somewhere unexpected.

When you look at a painter's palette — a real painter, not a beginner — you will see something specific. You will see not just bright colors but *deep* colors. Not just pigments that catch light but pigments that *absorb* it. Black, in particular, has a strange power in painting. A painting without black can feel washed out. A painting with black has *depth*, *contrast*, *weight*. The black is what makes the colors land.

What if creation works the same way?

What if Allah is teaching us, in this ayah, that the world He has made is not a place of soft sameness — but a place where extremes exist, and the extremes are not mistakes? What if the raven-black mountain is not a contrast *to* mercy, but part of mercy's full palette?

[PAUSE]

Consider the structure again. The ayah moves:

1. **Rain** — soft, life-giving, descending mercy.
2. **Fruit** — variety, color, sweetness.
3. **Mountains** — white and red, the gentler tones, the ones we associate with beauty.
4. **And then** — *gharābību sūd*. The pure, absolute, unyielding black.

Allah does not flinch at the end of the list. He does not soften it. He does not bury it. He brings it forward — using the very grammar of *taqdīm wa taʾkhīr* — to ensure we cannot pass over it.

What this is showing us, I believe, is that **the world Allah has made includes hardness, includes darkness, includes the uncompromising — and these are also His**. He did not paint a world of only fruit and only white mountains. He painted a world that also contains raven-black stone that has stood unmoving since before you were born and will stand unmoving long after you are gone.

This matters because we live in a moment that wants only the white and red of life. We want soft fruits. We want rain. We do not want the black mountains.

But the black mountains are part of the painting.

[PAUSE]

Bring this back to your own life. Think of someone you love who has gone through something *hard*. Something that did not soften. Something that did not yield. A loss that did not dissolve into a lesson. A grief that did not become a story.

That hardness — that *gharībiyyah*, that absolute unyielding quality — is also in the painting. It is not outside Allah's design. It is one of His pigments.

This is not a comfortable theology. The ayah does not pretend that everything is soft. But it does something more important than comforting us: it tells us that the One who made the black mountain is the same One who made the fruit. The same Hand. The same Painter. The same *We brought forth*.

When the iltifāt of the ayah collapses the distance — *We brought forth* — it does not only collapse the distance for the sweet fruit. It collapses the distance for the black mountain too. Allah is *as close* to the dark stone as He is to the strawberry.

[PAUSE]

And here is the application — and it is hard.

The work of *seeing*, in this ayah, includes seeing what we do not want to see. The believer is not someone who looks past the black mountain. The believer is someone who looks *at* it, and registers: this too is from the same Hand. This too has a maker. This too, somehow, is in the palette.

Tomorrow, when you encounter the hard thing — the unyielding grief, the loss that does not soften, the answer that does not come — do not pretend it is fruit. It is not fruit. It is the black mountain. But the same God made it. And the same God is leaning into the sentence when He says *We*.

That is what *gharābību sūd* gives us. Not the lie that everything is soft. The truth that the hardness is also signed.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now let me show you something I held back at the beginning.

This ayah is structured as a triad. Look:

1. **Water from the sky** — descending, sourced from above.
2. **Fruit from the earth** — varied, emerging from below.
3. **Mountains** — standing between, the meeting of sky and earth.

And inside the mountain image is its own triad: **white, red, and raven-black**.

The architecture is layered: the macro-triad of cosmic location (sky / earth / between), and inside it a micro-triad of intensity (gentle / medium / extreme). Allah has built a small cosmos inside a single ayah.

And then — and this is the part that, for me, made everything click — He moves directly into 35:28, which begins: **وَمِنَ ٱلنَّاسِ وَٱلدَّوَآبِّ وَٱلْأَنْعَٰمِ مُخْتَلِفٌ أَلْوَٰنُهُۥ كَذَٰلِكَ** — *"And among people and animals and livestock there are also various colors, just like that."*

Notice what just happened. The ayah we have been reflecting on — about fruit and mountains — turns out to be a ramp. Allah has been teaching us to see color in the natural world precisely so that He can ask us, in the very next breath, to see color in *human beings*. The varied colors of fruit and stone are the rehearsal. The varied colors of humanity are the lesson.

And then, only after both — *only after we have learned to see* — comes the verdict of 35:28: *innamā yakhsha-Llāha min ʿibādihi l-ʿulamāʾ*. *Only those who know fear Allah.*

The whole architecture is: **see the world → see humanity → know Allah → fear Allah.** And our ayah, 35:27, is the first step on the staircase.

---

### Bringing It Together

What we found in the language and what we found in the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The language taught us that Allah is asking us to *see*, that He has stepped close to His own work, and that His palette includes absolute black.

The themes taught us that the difference between heedlessness and faith is attention, that the world is signed by the same Hand whether soft or hard, and that the black mountains are part of the mercy.

These are not two messages. They are one message: **reality is full of Allah, and the only thing missing is your seeing.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is in your visual field right now that you have walked past a thousand times without seeing? What would change if Allah's hand were the first thing you registered?

2. Where in your life is there a black mountain — an unyielding hardness — that you have been pretending is not there? What would it mean to register it as *also from the same Painter*?

3. When was the last time you saw the color of a fruit and *registered* that Allah brought it forth from water that had no color?

4. If 35:28 says that those who *know* are those who *fear*, and 35:27 teaches us that knowing begins with *seeing* — where in your daily life is your seeing being trained? And where is it being dulled?

5. The ayah asks *alam tara* — *have you not seen?* What would you say to Allah today if He asked you that?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah is asking you whether you have seen what He has been showing you your entire life — and the seeing is the prerequisite for everything else.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to learn something new. It is asking you to *look at what is already there*.

The fruit is already colored. The mountains are already painted. The rain has already fallen. **The only variable is you.**

Tomorrow, when you walk past a tree, or eat something, or look at a mountain on a drive, or notice the strange variety of human faces around you — let the ayah's question land:

*alam tara?*

*Have you not seen?*

And then, gently, let your answer be: *I am beginning to.*

---

### Du'a

O Allah, You who sent down water and brought forth color from the soil — open our seeing. Let our eyes not pass over Your signs as if they were scenery. And when we meet the raven-black mountains in our lives, let us not flinch from them, but recognize Your hand in them as we recognize Your hand in the fruit.

Make us, by Your mercy, among those who *see* — so that, by Your mercy, we may be among those who *know* — so that, by Your mercy, we may be among those who *fear You with awe*.
