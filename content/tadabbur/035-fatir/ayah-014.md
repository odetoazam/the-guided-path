# The Gods That Cannot Hear: On Calling Into Silence

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse three times, so it settles in three places — your ear, your tongue, and your understanding.

The Arabic:

> إِن تَدْعُوهُمْ لَا يَسْمَعُوا۟ دُعَآءَكُمْ وَلَوْ سَمِعُوا۟ مَا ٱسْتَجَابُوا۟ لَكُمْ ۖ وَيَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ يَكْفُرُونَ بِشِرْكِكُمْ ۚ وَلَا يُنَبِّئُكَ مِثْلُ خَبِيرٍۭ

The sound of it:

> *In tadʿūhum lā yasmaʿū duʿāʾakum, wa law samiʿū mā istajābū lakum, wa yawma-l-qiyāmati yakfurūna bi-shirkikum, wa lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīr.*

And a plain English rendering:

> "If you call upon them, they do not hear your call; and were they to hear, they could not respond to you. And on the Day of Resurrection they will disown your association of them. And none can inform you like the One who is All-Aware."

### Rebuild the world

To feel what this verse is doing, you have to understand where you are standing in the surah.

Surah Fatir is a surah about *ownership*. About who actually holds the keys. Page after page, Allah lays out His sovereignty with an almost overwhelming generosity of detail. He is the One who splits the seed and the date-stone open into life. He merges the night into the day and the day into the night. He has pressed the sun and the moon into service, each running toward a named end. He sends the winds that lift the heavy clouds. He knows the burden of every womb and what every womb sheds.

And then, right before our verse, Allah turns to the things people set up beside Him — the carved stones, the venerated dead, the imagined intercessors — and He measures their ownership. Do you know how much He says they own?

The **qiṭmīr**. The qiṭmīr is the thin, papery white skin that wraps around a date-pit. That filament. *Those you call upon besides Him do not own even the membrane on a date-stone.*

[PAUSE]

So the original listener arrives at our verse having just been told: the things you turn to own *nothing*. And the natural human reply is the one we always make. We say: "But ownership isn't the point. I don't go to them because they own things. I go to them because they *hear* me. Because in the night, when I am desperate, I feel that something is listening."

And it is precisely *that* — the feeling that something is listening — that this verse walks up to, and takes apart, one stair at a time.

### The hook

There is a staircase hidden in this verse. Three steps going down, into a basement most people never let themselves enter. And then, at the very bottom — after the silence, after the betrayal — Allah does something with the grammar that turns the whole thing around and points it, gently, at one single person. Watch for the moment the verse stops saying "you" to a crowd and starts saying "you" to a heart.

Let us walk down the staircase first.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Three-Step Descent — *lā yasmaʿū* → *law samiʿū mā istajābū* → *yakfurūna bi-shirkikum*

Before we touch the Arabic, let me teach you a structure, because the entire first half of this verse is built on it, and if you see the structure you will never read this verse flat again.

Imagine you are trying to convince someone that the ladder they are about to climb cannot hold them. You could just say, "It's broken." But a truly devastating argument doesn't stop at the first failure. It removes *every possible* place the person could retreat to. You say: "First — the bottom rung is rotten, so you can't even start." And before they answer, you add: "And even if the bottom rung held — the middle is rusted through, so you'd fall there." And before they answer *that*, you say: "And even if you somehow reached the top — the whole thing isn't bolted to anything. It will turn on you and drop you."

Notice what that does. It doesn't just say "no." It chases the listener up the ladder and kicks out every rung *in advance*. By the end there is nowhere left to stand.

That is the architecture of this verse. The Arabic builds a three-stage demolition, and each stage is introduced in a way that says: *I am not even granting you the previous step, but watch — even if I did, the next one fails too.*

**Step one.** Allah says:

> إِن تَدْعُوهُمْ لَا يَسْمَعُوا۟ دُعَآءَكُمْ
> *In tadʿūhum lā yasmaʿū duʿāʾakum* — "If you call them, they do not hear your call."

Sit with the sound for a moment. The root د-ع-و, *daʿā*, "to call," appears twice in a breath — once as the verb *tadʿū*, "you call," and once as the noun *duʿāʾ*, "your calling." The act of calling echoes in the very phonetics. *Tadʿūhum... duʿāʾakum.* You can almost hear a voice going out and out — and landing on nothing. The verse makes you *hear* the call so that it can show you there is no ear to receive it.

Imam al-Qurṭubī notes the bluntest reason: *li-annahā jamādāt* — because these objects are inert matter. They do not see and they do not hear. There is no apparatus there. You are not speaking to a quiet listener who chooses not to answer. You are speaking to stone.

That is step one: **they cannot even receive the call.**

**Step two.** Now watch the move:

> وَلَوْ سَمِعُوا۟ مَا ٱسْتَجَابُوا۟ لَكُمْ
> *wa law samiʿū mā istajābū lakum* — "and were they to hear, they could not respond to you."

The word لَوْ, *law*, is one of the most important small words in the Quran. It is the particle of the *impossible hypothetical* — "if, contrary to fact." Allah is not conceding that they hear. He has just told you they don't. *Law* says: "Let us grant, for the sake of argument, the very thing I just denied — and even *then*, the result is failure."

And look at the verb He chooses for the failure: *mā istajābū* — from the root ج-و-ب, in Form X, *istajāba*. Form X often carries the sense of seeking out and fully answering — the verb you use for the response that actually *arrives*, that meets the need. Allah negates exactly that. Even granting hearing, the *complete answer never comes*.

The classical commentators open this second step into several facets, and I want you to hold them all at once, because they are camera angles on a single reality. Qatāda reads it: even if they heard, *it would not benefit you* — hearing without power is useless. Another reading reported by al-Qurṭubī is almost unbearable in its irony: *if* We had given these idols intellect and life so that they truly heard you — they would have been *more obedient to Allah than you are*. They would have turned to their Lord and abandoned you on the spot.

Do you feel the trap closing? Step one: they can't hear. Step two: and even if they could, the hearing buys you nothing — at best a deaf silence, at worst a creature that, the moment it came alive, would side with Allah against you.

That is step two: **even granted the impossible, no help arrives.**

**Step three.** And now the verse does not merely withdraw help. It reverses the relationship entirely:

> وَيَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ يَكْفُرُونَ بِشِرْكِكُمْ
> *wa yawma-l-qiyāmati yakfurūna bi-shirkikum* — "and on the Day of Resurrection they will disown your association of them."

The verb يَكْفُرُونَ, *yakfurūna*, from ك-ف-ر, here followed by the preposition *bi-*, does not mean "they will disbelieve" in the abstract. *Kafara bi-* means **to repudiate, to reject, to disown** — to publicly cut yourself off from something and declare you have nothing to do with it. Al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn both gloss it the same way: *yatabarraʾūna minkum* — "they will declare themselves innocent of you," they will say, in the words al-Baghawī supplies, *mā kuntum iyyānā taʿbudūn* — "you were never really worshipping *us*."

[PAUSE]

So here is the full descent. You called — and there was no ear. You imagined an ear — and there was no answer. And on the Day when everything is uncovered, the thing you served will stand up before the Judge and *renounce you*. The relationship you spent your devotion building will be denied from the other side. They will not even grant you the dignity of having been their worshipper.

Three steps. They cannot hear you. They cannot help you. And in the end they cannot *stand* you. Every rung kicked out — going down.

That is what this single verse-half reveals: **the false object of reliance is not a weak helper. It is an absent one, an indifferent one, and finally a hostile one — and the verse proves all three before you can retreat to any of them.**

[PAUSE — let that settle]

### Element Two: *bi-shirkikum* — "*Your* Association"

Now I want to slow down on one word inside that third step, because Arabic has placed a piece of information in it so quietly that in English it almost always falls out.

When Allah says they will disown *bi-shirkikum*, look at the ending: *-kum*. "Your." The shirk — the act of association, of pairing-up partners with Allah — is grammatically attached to **you**, the worshipper. Not to them.

Here is why that is extraordinary. Teach yourself a small grammatical habit: in Arabic, when a noun is bound to a pronoun like this — *shirk* + *kum* — that pronoun marks **possession or origin**. *Shirkikum* literally means "*your* association," the associating *that you did*.

So on the Day of Resurrection, the idols do not say, "we are sorry we misled them." They say, in effect, "this *shirk* was never ours. It was always *yours*." The verse refuses to let the worshipper offload responsibility onto the worshipped.

Stay with this, because it overturns something we all secretly believe. We assume that the thing we worship is the active partner — that it pulled us in, that it bears some of the weight of what we did with it. Money. Status. A person we orbited. The image of ourselves we kept polishing. We tell ourselves: *it had a hold on me.* And the grammar of this verse says: no. The pit was always inert. The qiṭmīr never reached out for you. The shirk had only one author, and the pronoun is pointing at you.

Notice the terrible symmetry with step two. There, the idols — if they had life — would have *obeyed Allah*, not you. Here, the idols disown *your* shirk as something they never shared. Both halves are saying the same thing from two sides: **the partner you imagined was never a partner at all.** You were alone in the relationship the entire time. The devotion went out; nothing was ever on the other end except your own projection.

This is what the word *bi-shirkikum* reveals, hidden in a single suffix: the loneliness of false worship is total. Not only is no one listening — no one ever even agreed to be worshipped.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *wa lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīr* — The Seal, and the Turn

Now we reach the bottom of the staircase, and Allah does the thing I promised you to watch for.

The verse ends:

> وَلَا يُنَبِّئُكَ مِثْلُ خَبِيرٍۭ
> *wa lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīr* — "and none can inform you like the One who is All-Aware."

First, the verb. يُنَبِّئُكَ, *yunabbiʾuka*, comes from the root ن-ب-أ, in Form II. Let me teach you that root before we use it. The noun *nabaʾ* does not mean ordinary news — *khabar* is ordinary news. *Nabaʾ* is **weighty news, news of consequence, the kind of report that changes things.** It is the root from which we get *nabī*, a Prophet — one who carries news of ultimate consequence. So *yunabbiʾuka* is not "no one will *tell* you." It is: **no one will bring you the deep, decisive, consequential truth — the inside report on reality — like…** like whom?

And here Allah does not say "like Me." He says مِثْلُ خَبِيرٍ — "like *a* Khabīr," like *an* All-Aware One. Indefinite. Listen for a moment to how strange that is. The whole Quran is the speech of Allah, and at the climax of His own argument He refers to Himself not as "I, your Lord," but with this almost veiled, third-person, indefinite phrase: "no one informs you like *one who is All-Aware*."

The commentators are unanimous on the referent. Al-Jalālayn: *huwa-Llāhu taʿālā* — He is Allah, exalted. Al-Baghawī, even more intimately: *yaʿnī nafsahu* — He means *Himself*; "no one informs you like Me, the Khabīr, the One who knows the realities of all things." So the meaning is settled. But *why the veil?* Why "an All-Aware One" instead of "I"?

Let me offer this as something the construction invites us to consider. By stepping back into the indefinite, the verse turns the claim from a personal boast into a *standing law of reality*: in all of existence, there is a category called "the One who truly knows the inside of things," and that category has exactly one occupant — and **the report you just received, this entire staircase of truth about your false gods, comes from that single source.** You were not given an opinion. You were given the *nabaʾ*, the consequential inside-report, from the only One positioned to deliver it. Everything you believed about the listening idol was a guess. This is the only sentence in the matter that comes from Knowledge itself.

[PAUSE]

And now — the turn I told you to watch for.

Run back through the whole verse and listen to *who* is being addressed.

"If **you** call them" — *tadʿūhum* — plural. "They do not hear **your** call" — *duʿāʾakum* — plural. "They could not respond to **you**" — *lakum* — plural. "They will disown **your** association" — *shirkikum* — plural. The entire argument is delivered to a crowd. To *you all*, the nations who set up partners.

And then the final word: *yunabbiʾu**ka***. *None can inform **you*** — *kāf*, singular. **One** person.

[PAUSE]

The verse has shifted its address. This sudden change of pronoun mid-verse is a known feature of Quranic style — the scholars call it *iltifāt*, the "turn" — and it is never decorative. Here, at the very end, the wide public argument against false worship narrows to a single heart. Classically, that singular "you" is the Prophet ﷺ — the verse seals its case by certifying it to him: *all of that — and no one could have brought you this report but the All-Aware.* But the *kāf* is also the door through which the verse reaches *you*, the one reading it now. After all the plurals, after the crowd, the last word leans in and says: **and none can inform *you* — you, personally, the one who has been calling into the dark — like the One who actually knows.**

That is what this closing clause reveals. It is not a footnote. It is the verse stepping out of the courtroom of all nations and sitting down beside one person to say: *now that you have seen the silence on the other end of your call — here is who has been speaking to you the whole time.*

### Bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three things. A staircase that kicks out every rung — they can't hear, can't help, will disown. A single suffix that proves the worshipper was always alone in the relationship. And a final pronoun that turns the whole public verdict into a private word, sourced from the only One who knows.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about idols of stone — but about the way every human heart attaches itself to things that cannot hold it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Deafness of What We Worship

Let me begin where you already live, far from any carved idol.

Think about the last time you were truly afraid in the middle of the night. Not annoyed — afraid. A diagnosis. A marriage cracking. A child you cannot reach. Money running out. And notice what the mind does in that moment. It *reaches*. It grabs for something to hold the fear. And for most of us, what it grabs is not Allah, at least not first. It grabs the phone, to check a number. It grabs the plan, to run it again. It grabs the person — the one whose approval has become our weather. It grabs the substance, the scroll, the fantasy of the future where everything is fine.

We are *calling*. We just don't use the word. Every one of those reaches is a *duʿāʾ* — a call sent out toward something we are trusting to hold us.

And here is what this verse does that nothing else does. It does not say those things are *evil*. It says something quieter and far more disturbing. It says: **they cannot hear you.** *In tadʿūhum lā yasmaʿū duʿāʾakum.* The number in your bank account does not know your name. The person whose approval you are dying for cannot actually carry your fear; they have their own. The scroll receives your night and gives you nothing back. You have been speaking into devices, into people, into images — and the verse, with terrible gentleness, points at the silence on the other end.

Let the ayah illuminate the experience, movement by movement.

First movement: *they do not hear.* You think your exhaustion is because the thing is hard to obtain. The verse says no — your exhaustion is because you are calling toward something with no ear. You can pour devotion into a career, a relationship, an image of yourself, for *years*, and feel the strange emptiness that no amount of success closes. That emptiness is the verse. It is the sound of a call with no receiver.

Second movement: *and even if they heard — no answer that reaches.* This is the cruelest comfort we cling to. We say: "but sometimes it *does* work. The promotion came. The person did call back." And the verse, with its *law samiʿū mā istajābū*, says: even when the thing seems to answer, it cannot give you *what you were actually asking for.* You were never really asking for the promotion. You were asking to feel *safe*, to feel *enough*. And no created thing can deposit that into a human heart. So the "answer" arrives and the hunger remains, and you conclude you need *more* of the thing — when the truth is the thing was never able to feed that hunger at all.

Third movement: *and on the Day, it disowns you.* This is the long view. Everything you built your safety on will, at the end, refuse to vouch for you. The career will not stand beside you in the grave. The approval of people will evaporate the instant the One whose approval is real takes His seat. *Yakfurūna bi-shirkikum.* They will say: *we never asked for this.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel the paradox, and do not rush past it. The verse is, on its surface, one of the most desolating in the Quran. *Everything you reach for is deaf.* And yet — read it again and it is one of the most *freeing*. Because if the things you have been straining toward were never able to hear you, then all that wasted, exhausting devotion was *misdelivered mail*. It was always going to come back marked "no such recipient." And that means the energy of your worship — your longing, your fear, your reaching — is not the problem. It was only ever pointed at the wrong address.

There *is* an ear. The verse ends by telling you so. *Lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīr.* The same God who tells you the idols are deaf is, in the act of telling you, *demonstrating that He hears* — because He answered the question you were too afraid to ask out loud: *is anyone actually listening?*

So what changes tomorrow? This: catch yourself in the reach. The next time fear sends you grabbing for the phone, the plan, the person, the substance — pause for one breath and name it. "I am calling. Who am I calling?" That single second of awareness is the difference between sending your duʿāʾ to the qiṭmīr and sending it to the Khabīr. You do not have to manufacture new longing. You already have all the longing you will ever need. You only have to redirect the call.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Betrayal of the Idol — and the One Witness Who Will Not Turn

Let me start with something you have probably felt and never had words for: the loneliness of being disowned by something you gave yourself to.

You have seen this in human form. The friend you defended to everyone, who, when it cost something, said they barely knew you. The institution you bled for, that quietly replaced you and acted as though you were never there. The cause, the group, the relationship you organized your identity around — and the moment it was advantageous, it cut you loose and denied you were ever truly part of it. There is a specific agony in that: not just the loss, but the *erasure* — the sense that the very thing you devoted yourself to is now testifying that the devotion never happened.

Hold that feeling. Because the Quran takes it and projects it onto the largest possible screen — the Day of Resurrection — and shows you that this is the *final form* of all false attachment. *Wa yawma-l-qiyāmati yakfurūna bi-shirkikum.* Everything a human being worships besides Allah will, on that Day, perform exactly that erasure. It will stand up and say: *I never asked for your worship. This was yours, not mine.*

Let the ayah open across a few movements.

First, see the asymmetry the verse insists on. You *claimed* the idol. You said "this is mine, this is what I serve, this is what I trust." But the claim was never mutual. *Shirkikum* — "*your* association." The relationship existed entirely on your side. This is the truth about every counterfeit god: **the devotion is always one-directional.** The money never loved you back. The fame never knew you. The person you made into your sun was just another person, equally lost, who never agreed to be your god. On the Day, that one-directionality becomes visible — and it is unbearable, because you discover you spent your one life in a relationship the other side never even registered.

Second, contrast this with what Allah does in the *same verse*. The idols disown you. And Allah — *seals the truth to you.* *Lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīr.* While everything false is busy renouncing its worshippers, the One who is truly worthy of worship is leaning in to *inform* you, to give you the inside-report, to make sure you are not deceived. Place the two side by side: the false gods take your devotion and, at the end, deny they ever knew you. The true God takes nothing from you that you need, and at the end *testifies on your behalf if you turned to Him.* One relationship is a mirage that evaporates under examination. The other is the only one that holds *weight* on the Day when weight is all that matters — because it is anchored in *Knowledge*, in the *Khabīr* who knew the whole time exactly what was in your heart.

Third — and this is the mercy hidden in the warning — the verse is telling you the ending *before you reach it*. The disownership scene of *yakfurūna bi-shirkikum* is not given to frighten you for its own sake. It is given the way a friend who can see around a corner warns you: *that road dead-ends at a cliff. The thing you are running toward will drop you. I am telling you now, while you can still turn.* The whole verse is a *nabaʾ* — a consequential report delivered *in time to act on it.* That is why it ends with *Khabīr* and not with the scene of betrayal. The last word is not the silence of the idol. The last word is the One who knew, and cared enough to tell you, and is close enough to be told.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the paradox here too. This is a verse about being *betrayed* — and yet it leaves you more *secure* than when you started. Because once you truly see that everything created will, in the end, disown the worship you give it, a strange peace arrives: you stop demanding that created things be your god. You stop putting the infinite weight of your soul on people who were never built to carry it. You let your spouse be a spouse, not a savior. You let your work be work, not a verdict on your worth. You let your reputation be what people happen to say, not the foundation you stand on. And you take the full weight — the longing, the trust, the fear, the *worship* — and you set it down in the one place that will never, on any day, turn and say "I never knew you."

What changes tomorrow? This: identify the one attachment you are most afraid to lose — the person, the position, the image — and ask yourself honestly, *am I treating this as something that can hear me, answer me, and vouch for me on the Day?* If the honest answer is yes, you have found your idol, and the verse has found you. The practice is not to destroy the attachment. It is to *demote* it — to move it from the throne where only the Khabīr belongs, back down to its proper place as a created thing you are grateful for but do not worship. Demote it before the Day demotes it for you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and look at how the verse is built, because the structure is itself the argument.

The verse is a descent and a single rescue. Watch the shape:

- **Down one:** *lā yasmaʿū* — they cannot hear. (No reception.)
- **Down two:** *law samiʿū mā istajābū* — even granted hearing, no answer. (No help.)
- **Down three:** *yakfurūna bi-shirkikum* — they disown you. (No loyalty.)
- **And then — up, and inward:** *lā yunabbiʾuka mithlu khabīr* — but One has been informing you all along. (The only Ear, the only Answer, the only Witness.)

Three descending steps in the **plural** — addressed to all the nations, building a public, airtight case. And then a single ascending word in the **singular** — *yunabbiʾuka* — that lifts one person out of the crowd and hands the whole verdict to their heart. The grammar *is* the meaning: the case against false worship is universal, but the invitation to true worship is personal. The verse argues to everyone and then speaks to *you*.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **staircase** of *can't-hear, can't-help, will-disown* is the grammar of every counterfeit god you have ever served — that is Theme One and Two read off the syntax.

The **suffix** *-kum* in *shirkikum*, proving you were always alone in the relationship, is the loneliness of false worship made into a single letter.

And the **turn** to the singular *kāf* in *yunabbiʾuka*, sourced from an indefinite, veiled *Khabīr*, is the entire mercy of the verse: the same speech that empties out every false listener reveals, in the act of speaking, the True One who was listening the whole time.

One verse. It takes away every deaf god and, in the same breath, gives you the One who hears.

### Questions to carry

- When I am most afraid, what do I reach for *first* — and have I ever asked whether it can actually hear me?
- Is there a person, a position, or an image in my life that I have quietly placed on a throne where only Allah belongs?
- If everything I rely on were to *disown* me on the Day — which of my attachments would survive that, and which would evaporate?
- Have I been pouring real, exhausting devotion into an address that keeps returning the mail marked "no such recipient"?
- The verse ends by telling me Someone has been informing me all along. Do I live as though I am being spoken to — or as though the line is dead?

### One-sentence distillation

Everything you call upon besides Allah is deaf to your call, useless if it heard, and will disown you in the end — and the One who tells you this is the only One who was ever truly listening.

### The closing invitation

You do not need to become a more devoted person. You already pour out devotion every day — into screens, into approval, into plans, into fear. The verse is not asking you to *generate* worship. It is asking you to *redirect* it. To take the call you are already making into the dark, and turn it, by one degree, toward the only Ear in existence that receives it. Let the false gods be demoted gently — not in self-hatred, but in relief. You were never alone because you reached out. You were alone because you reached toward the deaf. Reach toward the One who hears, and the loneliness ends in the first word.

### A du'a born of these words

O Allah, *Khabīr* — the One who knows the inside of all things — I have spent so much of my calling on what could not hear me. Turn my voice back toward You before the Day turns it for me. Let everything I have wrongly trusted be demoted from its throne in my heart, gently, and let You alone sit there. And on the Day when every false thing disowns its people, let me be among those You inform, You answer, and You do not turn away. Āmīn.
