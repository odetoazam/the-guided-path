# What Cannot Be Made Equal

## Surah Fatir 35:19-21

> وَمَا يَسْتَوِى ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ وَٱلْبَصِيرُ ۝ وَلَا ٱلظُّلُمَـٰتُ وَلَا ٱلنُّورُ ۝ وَلَا ٱلظِّلُّ وَلَا ٱلْحَرُورُ
>
> *Wamā yastawi-l-aʿmā wal-baṣīr. Wa lā-ẓ-ẓulumātu wa lā-n-nūr. Wa lā-ẓ-ẓillu wa lā-l-ḥarūr.*
>
> **"Not equal are the blind and the seeing; nor are the depths of darkness and the light; nor are the shade and the scorching heat."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

Imagine standing in Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ has been calling people for years. They have heard the message clearly. They are not confused about what is being asked of them.

They are doing something else.

They are **flattening the categories**.

They are saying — in different words across different conversations — *"What's the difference, really? Our fathers worshipped these idols and lived good lives. You worship one God and live a good life. We are all reaching for the same thing. Why should we choose? Why should anything change?"*

They have built a worldview where every path is roughly the same path, every state of the soul roughly the same state, every destination roughly the same destination. The Prophet ﷺ is asking them to stand up and *choose*, and they are responding by quietly insisting there is nothing real to choose between.

This is not ancient. This is the air we breathe.

We live in a moment that has been carefully taught to flatten every distinction — to say all worldviews are equally valid, all spiritual paths equally pointed at the divine, all inner states roughly comparable. The most refined version of this isn't aggressive atheism. It's a gentle relativism that smiles and nods and says "whatever works for you."

And this is the exact disease Surah Fatir is treating.

### Where these three ayahs sit

Look at the architecture around them. Just one ayah earlier, in 35:18, Allah destroyed the idea that one person can carry another person's burden. *"No bearer of burdens shall bear another's burden... And even if a heavily laden soul should call for its load, nothing of it would be carried — even if he should be a close relative."*

That ayah dismantled the first false equivalence: that we can outsource our accountability.

Then immediately — these three ayahs.

And the ayah right after, 35:22, will land the final blow: *"And not equal are the living and the dead."*

So the passage is a sustained, methodical refusal. Verse by verse, Allah is taking apart the human mind's habit of treating opposites as interchangeable.

### The hook

Here is what is stunning about what you are about to read.

Allah does not say, *"the believer and the disbeliever are not equal."*

He could have. It would have been true. It would have been clear.

But He doesn't.

Instead, He chooses **three physical images** that no one — believer, disbeliever, child, philosopher — could possibly disagree with. He picks pairs so undeniable that the moment you hear them, your whole being agrees.

[PAUSE]

And once your whole being has agreed, He has you. Because what He's really pointing at was never the physical pair. It was something inside you the entire time.

Stay with me. We are about to watch the most elegant argument in scripture unfold in fifteen words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Yastawī*: The verb that does its work in silence

Before we open the word, let's teach the structure.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns called **forms**, numbered roughly Form I through Form X. Form I is the bare root — the basic action. *Kataba* means "he wrote." That's Form I.

Then Arabic does something English cannot easily do. It takes that same root and slots it into other patterns, and each pattern changes the *flavor* of the action. Form II often intensifies it — *kattaba* means "he made someone write," or "he wrote a lot." Form IV often makes it causative — *aktaba* means "he caused someone to write." Form VII often makes it passive or reflexive — *inkataba* means "it got written, of itself."

Each form is a *fingerprint* on the action. It tells you not just *what* happened but *how* it happened — whether it was active or reflexive, intensified or simple, caused by an outside agent or arising from within.

The verb at the heart of our first ayah is *yastawī* — "they are not equal" — and it sits in **Form VIII** of the root س-و-ي *(s-w-y)*.

The root itself, *sawā*, means "to be equal, even, balanced." Form I would be a flat statement: *they are equal.*

But Allah does not use Form I.

He reaches for Form VIII — *istawā / yastawī* — which carries a particular weight. Form VIII of this root means **"to become equal of themselves; to even out; to level into one another."**

This is the **middle voice**. The reflexive sense. It's not that someone makes them equal. It's that they *equalize themselves* into one undifferentiated thing.

Now hold the negation against it.

*Wa mā yastawī.*

**"They do not equalize themselves."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. Allah is not saying *"I do not consider them equal."* He is not saying *"in our judgment they differ."* He is saying something far stronger — that **the blind and the seeing are not equal by their own structure**. There is no operation in the universe by which they could become so. The non-equivalence is not in the observer's mind. It is built into the things themselves.

Here is what is remarkable. The classical commentators — al-Jalalayn states it plainly, al-Qurtubi reaches for it, al-Baghawi develops it — all read this as a pointer toward the disbeliever and the believer. The blind one here is the one blind to guidance; the seeing one is the one whose inner eye has opened.

Al-Tabari is more precise still. He reads the blindness not as a general spiritual condition but as blindness *specifically to the dīn of Allah* — "blind to the religion of Allah which He sent His Prophet Muhammad ﷺ with." And the seeing one is he who "has seen guidance in it and followed Muhammad and believed him." The contrast is not abstract. It is the specific human being who has heard this message and chosen to look away, set against the one who has heard and chosen to see.

Now lay the linguistic discovery on top of the tafsir.

Allah is not telling the disbeliever, *"in my view, you are less than the believer."* He is saying something the disbeliever cannot escape from: **"You and the one you mock — you do not equalize. Reality itself refuses it. The fabric of being will not allow it."**

This is the structural impossibility of false equivalence. Not a verdict. A description of how the universe is built.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two — One verb, three negations, and a verb that disappears

Look at the three ayahs together one more time. Notice what happens to the verb.

**35:19:** *Wa mā **yastawī** al-aʿmā wal-baṣīr* — "And **not equal** are the blind and the seeing."

**35:20:** *Wa lā-ẓ-ẓulumātu wa lā-n-nūr* — "And nor the darknesses, and nor the light."

**35:21:** *Wa lā-ẓ-ẓillu wa lā-l-ḥarūr* — "And nor the shade, and nor the scorching heat."

The verb *yastawī* appears once. Then it vanishes.

In Arabic, this is called **ellipsis** — *ḥadhf* — and it is one of the most powerful rhetorical devices in the language. A word that is grammatically expected is deliberately removed, and the listener's mind fills it back in with even greater intensity than if it had been said.

Why does this matter? Because something happens in your mind when you hear the second and third ayahs. The verb *yastawī* — "they do not equalize" — is supposed to be there. Your brain knows it. Your ear waits for it. And it doesn't come.

So your mind supplies it. And then your mind supplies it again. And by the third pair, you are no longer reading — you are *participating* in the negation. The grammar has dragged you into the act of saying it yourself.

Notice something else. The first ayah uses *mā* — a flat negative. The next two use *lā* — a softer, more flowing negative. *Mā... wa lā... wa lā...* The first negation is the hammer-blow. The next two are the echoes that confirm it.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this construction does. It builds a **cascade**. One negation pours into the next into the next. The listener is not given the chance to disagree, because by the time the second pair arrives, the structure of refusal has already been internalized.

This is what Allah is doing to the listener who has been quietly insisting that all spiritual states are roughly equivalent. He is not arguing with them. He is *changing the architecture of their mind* through grammar itself.

By the end of three ayahs, the listener who entered believing all distinctions could be flattened is now actively, internally, repeating: *they do not equalize... they do not equalize... they do not equalize.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three — *Aẓ-ẓulumāt* and *an-nūr*: One light, many darknesses

Now we move to the second pair. And here Arabic teaches us something theologically enormous through the simplest possible move.

In the pair *al-aʿmā wal-baṣīr*, both nouns are singular. The blind one — singular. The seeing one — singular. Symmetric.

But look at the second pair.

*Aẓ-ẓulumāt* — "the darknesses" — is **plural feminine**.

*An-nūr* — "the light" — is **singular masculine**.

This asymmetry is not accidental. It runs throughout the entire Quran. Every single time Allah pairs darkness and light, *ẓulumāt* is plural and *nūr* is singular. Open Surah al-Anʿam (6:1). Open Surah Ibrahim (14:1, 14:5). Open Surah az-Zumar (39:6). The pattern is unbroken.

So what is Allah saying through this grammatical asymmetry?

Let's sit with it.

There is **one light**. One. Singular. Undivided.

There are **many darknesses**. Plural. Multiple. Branching.

You can be lost in a thousand different ways. There is the darkness of disbelief, the darkness of hypocrisy, the darkness of shirk, the darkness of arrogance, the darkness of distraction, the darkness of despair, the darkness of the world weighing on you, the darkness of your own heart turned against you. Endless darknesses.

But there is **one** light. *Nūr Allah.* One source. One direction. One way home.

Here is why this matters for the argument Allah is building. The disbeliever in Makkah was insisting — and the secular relativist today insists — that there are *many valid paths*. Many lights. Many truths.

And Allah's grammar quietly answers: **no.**

You have it backwards. The many is the darkness. The one is the light.

[PAUSE]

Plurality of paths is not a sign of richness. It is, in the Quranic architecture, the *signature* of being lost. Where there are many directions, none of them is the way. Where there is *one* direction, that is the way.

This is why the *ṣirāṭ* — the Path — in the Quran is always singular. *Iḥdinā-ṣ-ṣirāṭa-l-mustaqīm.* Not paths. *The* path. One.

When you hear a culture say *all paths lead to the same place*, the Quran's grammatical architecture is the precise counter-image: in reality, only one path leads anywhere, and the multiplicity of paths people imagine they are walking is the very texture of their being lost.

And note what classical tafsir does here. Al-Tabari names them without pause: *ẓulumāt al-kufr wa nūr al-īmān* — the darknesses of disbelief and the light of faith. Al-Muyassar follows the same exact formulation. The many darknesses are the many faces of refusing the truth; the singular light is the singular truth that is being refused. It is not that the scholars are imposing a meaning — it is that the grammar itself insists on this reading, and the scholars are only reading what the structure already says.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

We have spent time on three things — the structural impossibility hidden inside *yastawī*, the elliptical cascade that drags the listener into the negation, and the singular light against the plural darknesses.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The collapse of false equivalence

Before we open the ayah on this theme, let's start where you actually live.

You have been raised — whether you noticed or not — inside a culture that has invested enormous effort in teaching you that **distinctions are dangerous**.

Distinctions are how the cruel ones divide people. Distinctions are how the bigots categorize. Distinctions are what we used to make before we became enlightened. Now we know better. Now we know that nothing is really opposite to anything else, that every position is just another perspective, that to firmly insist *this is true and that is false* is itself a kind of violence.

This is the sea you swim in. You did not choose it. You inherited it.

And on the surface, it sounds compassionate. Generous. Open-minded. Who would want to live in a world of harsh black-and-white judgments?

But sit with what it actually does to you.

It means that when the Prophet ﷺ — peace and blessings upon him — stood in Makkah and said *there is no god but Allah*, the modern descendent of that audience hears it as a kind of marketing claim. *His path. One path among many.* And so the claim slides off, because the architecture for hearing it as ultimate truth has been dismantled in advance.

It means that when you read in the Quran that there is a heaven and a hell, your mind subtly translates it into *one possibility and another possibility* — both of them held at equal distance, neither of them quite real.

It means that you can read about *the believers* and *the disbelievers* in scripture and feel a vague embarrassment, because surely no real difference can be drawn between human beings — surely we are all, fundamentally, the same.

[PAUSE]

This is what Allah is naming.

He is not arguing with someone who disagrees with Islam. He is treating a sickness in the apparatus of perception itself — the modern (and ancient) tendency to *flatten*, *blur*, *equate*, until no distinction feels worth insisting on, and no call to commitment can land with any weight.

And the medicine He administers is not a theological argument.

It is three images you cannot argue with.

[PAUSE]

Can you look at a person who is blind and a person who can see and declare them *equivalent*? Not morally. Not in worth. But in what they *can access*? Can the blind man see the road ahead? Can the sighted man pretend the road isn't there?

You know the answer. Everyone knows the answer. You cannot flatten this.

Now: can you look at the darknesses of the night and the light of midday and say they are roughly the same thing? You can call them both "states of illumination on a spectrum." You can try. But you know that when you are lost at night and the dawn breaks, something real has happened. Not a shift on a spectrum. A rescue.

Now: can you look at someone sitting in the cool shade of a tree and someone standing in a *ḥarūr* — a scorching, driving heat that finds you wherever you stand — and say there is no meaningful difference between them?

Three times. Three images. The same answer every time.

And now Allah has you.

Because you just agreed — deeply, physically, in your whole body — that these pairs are not equal. And what He was pointing at through the images was something you were resisting the moment before you opened the Quran. He has used the undeniable to unlock the deniable. He has used your body to bypass your ideology.

This is *istidlāl al-maḥsūs* — **argument from the perceivable** — one of the Quran's most characteristic methods. The sensory image to reach the supra-sensory truth. The undeniable physical pair to establish the contested spiritual one.

Ibn Kathir reads the entire sequence — blind/seeing, darkness/light, shade/heat — as a single sustained *mathal*, a parable Allah makes of the believers and the disbelievers, building toward the deepest statement of all in 35:22: *"And not equal are the living and the dead."* Each pair is not an independent image. Each one is a step, a rung, descending toward that final identification. Which means that when you agreed, three times, that these physical pairs are incomparable — you were, step by step, agreeing to the conclusion you had been avoiding.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two — The shade and the hot wind

Now let's slow down on the third pair, because it carries a dimension the translation can almost miss.

*Wa lā-ẓ-ẓillu wa lā-l-ḥarūr.*

"And not equal are the shade and the *ḥarūr*."

What is a *ḥarūr*?

Most translations render it as "scorching heat" or "the sun's blazing heat." That is not wrong. But al-Muyassar's paraphrase of this exact ayah reads: *wa lā-ẓ-ẓillu wa lā-r-rīḥu-l-ḥārrah* — "and the shade and the **hot wind**."

A hot *wind*.

This changes the image.

When you are in a desert, the sun beats down from one direction. You can find shade from the sun — a rock, a wall, a tree, an overhang. The sun has a fixed position; you can put something between yourself and it.

But a *ḥarūr* is a wind. It moves. It finds you. It comes around the corner of the rock you thought would shelter you. It slips under the cloth you raised against it. You can shade yourself from light, but you cannot simply shade yourself from a searching wind.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this does to the third pair.

The *ẓill* — the shade — is not merely coolness. It is a *refuge that holds*. It is shelter that the heat cannot breach. You enter it, and you are safe from what was pursuing you.

The *ḥarūr* is not merely heat. It is heat that is **active, mobile, searching**. It is the heat that finds you in the place where you thought you were safe.

And notice: Allah chose the *ẓill* and the *ḥarūr* — not the *ẓill* and *ash-shams*, the sun. He did not say "the shade and the sun are not equal," which would have been the simpler, more obvious pairing. He said the shade and the *ḥarūr*.

Because the *ḥarūr* is what the ẓill specifically protects you *from*. The sun is what you see. The ḥarūr is what you *feel bearing down on you*. The sun is the source; the ḥarūr is the experience of having no shelter from that source's reach. The choice Allah is pointing at is not between proximity and distance from a fixed light. It is between being sheltered and being *found*.

[PAUSE]

The spiritual image is enormous. The person in guidance is not merely comfortable — they are *protected*. There is something between them and the force that would otherwise consume them. And the person outside of guidance is not merely in an unpleasant warmth. They are in something that pursues. This is why the Quran elsewhere speaks of disbelief as layered darknesses — not one darkness, but many, each one closing behind you as you go further in. Not a static condition. A dynamic one. A going-further-from.

And the ẓill is not a condition you are born into. It is a location you enter. It is a shelter with a threshold. Classical commentators understood this physical image in its full weight: you can be steps away from the shade while standing in the ḥarūr. The distance between protection and exposure, in the desert, is sometimes measured in feet.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Three — The heart that cannot hear

The three pairs in 35:19-21 are all, al-Muyassar tells us, building toward a single identification: "the living of hearts through faith, and the dead of hearts through disbelief." The disbeliever is not merely one who sees poorly or lives in the wrong environment. In this Quranic portrait, something is amiss at the level of *life itself* — the heart's capacity to be awakened, moved, transformed by what it hears.

And then something quietly stunning happens in the very next verse.

The Prophet ﷺ — the most eloquent human being who ever lived, the one who carried the words of the Most High in his chest — is told by his Lord: *"And you, O Messenger, cannot make those in the graves hear."*

Al-Muyassar notes this connection explicitly: just as the dead in their graves cannot be made to hear, so too these disbelievers — because of the death of their hearts — cannot be made to hear. Not because the words are insufficient. Not because the argument is weak. Because the capacity to receive guidance must itself be alive.

[PAUSE]

This is the most sober moment in the entire passage.

It is not a condemnation of the disbeliever. It is a *diagnosis*. And the diagnosis is this: the heart that has closed itself to guidance has, by that very closing, lost something of its capacity to be opened again. Not irreversibly — Allah can reverse it; this is the meaning of *ihdināṣ-ṣirāṭ*, the ongoing prayer for guidance. But not by more argument. Not by better rhetoric. Not by the Prophet ﷺ simply speaking more persuasively.

Only Allah can make the dead heart hear.

Ibn Kathir connects this directly to the ayah in Surah al-Anʿam: *"Is he who was dead and We gave him life, and made for him a light by which to walk among the people — like one who is in darknesses, never to emerge from them?"* The believer is precisely one whose deadness was reversed by Allah. The light they now walk by is not something they generated. It is something that was given — given to a heart that was, before the giving, incapable of generating it on its own.

[PAUSE]

What does this mean for you?

It means that the most important prayer you can make — more important than asking for rizq, for health, for ease — is the prayer that opens every rakʿah you will ever pray: *Iḥdinā-ṣ-ṣirāṭa-l-mustaqīm*. **Guide us** — present tense, continuous, ongoing. Not "thank You that You already guided us." Guide us. *Still*. *Now*. Because the one thing that cannot be relied upon is a heart that has stopped asking to be kept alive.

The three pairs in 35:19-21 are not a description of two types of other people. They are a mirror. The mirror shows you what you are when you are in guidance, and what you become, incrementally, in the moments you are not. And the mirror is not there to shame. It is there so that you choose, clearly, with your eyes open, which side of each pair you are standing on right now.

[PAUSE — major]

## PART 3: THE APPLICATION

### What the three pairs ask of you

You have now walked through the architecture of these fifteen words. Let's name what they are asking.

The first pair — the blind and the seeing — asks you: *which one are you?*

Not as a judgment you render on yourself from the outside. But as an honest examination. Is there something in your life that you can see — a truth you have recognized, a reality you have looked at squarely — that you are now choosing, quietly, to look away from? Because the ayah is not about permanent, fixed blindness. It is about the act of seeing, and the act of turning from what you see.

Al-Tabari's reading anchors this precisely: the seeing one is not merely the one who understood intellectually. It is the one who "saw guidance and *followed it*." Seeing, in the Quran, is directional. It moves toward what it sees. Understanding that does not produce following is, in this framework, a form of the blindness it resembles.

[PAUSE]

The second pair — the many darknesses and the singular light — asks you: *where are you looking for light?*

Not whether you have found beauty in other things. Not whether the world contains genuine goodness outside of faith. It does. But: *what is the source you return to when you are lost?* Because the singular light and the plural darknesses are not just metaphysical facts about the cosmos. They are realities that shape the one who orients toward them. The person who keeps returning to the one light finds, over time, that the many darknesses lose their grip — not because they disappear, but because the eye has learned what to follow.

And the person who keeps sampling many lights finds, over time, that the act of sampling has itself become the orientation. That is, itself, a darkness.

[PAUSE]

The third pair — the shade and the searching wind — asks you: *have you entered the shelter?*

Not: do you believe in its existence. Not: do you admire its architecture from the outside and intend someday to enter. Have you *entered* it? Because ẓill is not knowledge about shade. It is the experience of being *inside* the cool, on the other side of the threshold. The ḥarūr finds everyone who is not sheltered. It does not make exceptions for those who meant to enter and simply hadn't gotten around to it yet.

The shelter is not abstract piety. It is the practice of *dhikr*, the daily return to prayer, the continuity of relationship with the One whose shade does not shift with the hour of the day. Classical commentators understood this physical image in its full weight: the ẓill is a place you *return to*. Protection, in this framework, is not a permanent state you achieve once. It is a location you inhabit, daily, by crossing its threshold again.

[PAUSE — major]

### The question you cannot un-hear

Here is what makes this passage unreasonable to ignore.

Allah has not said: *"believe in the Prophet or be eternally condemned."* He has not issued a threat. He has not even made a direct claim.

He has simply asked you to look at what you already know.

You know that the blind and the seeing are not equal. You know this in your cells.

You know that the darknesses and the light are not equal. You have lived this.

You know that the shade and the *ḥarūr* — the shelter that holds and the heat that searches — are not equal. You have felt this.

And then, silently, without stating it, through the structure of a three-part cascade followed by ellipsis, He asks: *and the believer and the disbeliever? And the guided and the lost? And the heart kept alive by faith and the heart that has closed?*

You have already answered.

The grammar made you answer. Three times, without pause, you agreed. And now the agreement you made about the physical world stands in the same room as the question about the spiritual world, and they are looking at each other.

This is why fifteen words is enough.

This is why the cascade needed no conclusion.

You already know the conclusion. You supplied it yourself.

[PAUSE — major]

## DUʿĀʾ

*Yā Allah —*

*You know how thoroughly I have been taught to flatten the differences. How many times I have told myself that one choice is as good as another, that one direction is roughly equivalent to another, that the state of my heart is nobody's business — not even mine.*

*I have treated the seeing and the blind as if they were the same.*

*I have treated the light and the darkness as interchangeable.*

*Forgive me for the moments I stood in the ḥarūr and called it shade, because admitting the difference would have required me to move.*

*Keep my heart alive. Not just functional — alive. The kind of alive that can hear. The kind of alive that, when it sees guidance, follows.*

*Do not let me be among those who heard and responded by flattening what they heard into something they could safely ignore.*

*I know I cannot make myself hear. Only You can do that. So I ask You — now, in this reading, in this moment — to make me hear.*

*Make me one who sees.*

*Āmīn.*
