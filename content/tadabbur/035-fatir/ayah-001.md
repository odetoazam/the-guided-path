# Fatir 35:1 — The Originator of a Creation That Stays Open

> ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ فَاطِرِ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ جَاعِلِ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ رُسُلًا أُو۟لِىٓ أَجْنِحَةٍۢ مَّثْنَىٰ وَثُلَـٰثَ وَرُبَـٰعَ ۚ يَزِيدُ فِى ٱلْخَلْقِ مَا يَشَآءُ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۢ قَدِيرٌ

**Transliteration:** *Al-ḥamdu lillāhi fāṭiri's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, jāʿili'l-malāʾikati rusulan ulī ajniḥatin mathnā wa thulātha wa rubāʿ — yazīdu fi'l-khalqi mā yashāʾ — inna'llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr.*

**Translation:** All praise belongs to Allah — Originator of the heavens and the earth, Appointer of the angels as messengers, possessors of wings, in pairs and threes and fours. He adds to the creation whatever He wills. Indeed, Allah has power over every single thing.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah walks into

To feel what this opening does, you have to know what came right before it.

The surah immediately preceding this one is Sabaʾ. Both Sabaʾ and Fatir open with *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — they are a consecutive hamd-pair. Sabaʾ opens: *al-ḥamdu lillāhi alladhī lahu mā fi's-samāwāti wa mā fi'l-arḍ* — "All praise belongs to Allah, to whom belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth." And then Fatir opens. The very first word — again — *al-ḥamdu lillāh*. Two adjacent surahs, both thresholds of the same hall of praise. The curtain between them does not close the hamd. It opens it again.

Five surahs in the entire Quran open with *al-ḥamdu lillāh*. Al-Fatiha. Al-Anʿam. Al-Kahf. Sabaʾ. Fatir. Two of them — Sabaʾ and Fatir — are placed adjacent to each other. That is not a coincidence of compilation. That is two doors of the same hall.

Now place yourself in the Makkan moment when this was first heard. The community Muhammad ﷺ was addressing was, by and large, not praising Allah. They were praising other beings, attributing power to other agencies, refusing to acknowledge the One who originated them. Into that climate of refused praise, Allah opens a whole surah by **praising Himself**. The first speaker of hamd in this ayah is not the believer. It is Allah Himself, declaring what is owed to Him whether anyone on earth offers it or not.

Classical commentators noticed this immediately. Al-Ṭabarī reads *al-ḥamd* here as *al-shukru'l-kāmil li'l-maʿbūd alladhī lā taṣluḥu'l-ʿibādatu illā lah* — the complete, undiminished praise-gratitude owed to the One deserving of worship, to the exclusion of all others. Not partial acknowledgment, not polite recognition. *Complete.* The praise does not wait for our willingness. It is already happening at the highest possible voice. We are invited to join it.

### The hook

Now look at the ayah again. It moves through four moves so quickly you can miss what just happened:

Allah is praised. Then He is called *Fatir* — and we will see this is not the ordinary word for "creator." Then He is called the One who *appoints* angels as messengers — and we will see this is not the ordinary word for "make." Then He describes their wings — two, three, four. Then He says — almost in passing — *yazīdu fi'l-khalqi mā yashāʾ*. He adds to the creation whatever He wills.

Stay with that last clause for a moment. He **adds** to creation. Whatever He wills.

[PAUSE]

That single half-sentence quietly demolishes the picture most of us carry of creation as a finished object. The Quran is telling you, on the threshold of this surah, that the universe is not a closed list. It is still being added to. And the One adding is the same One being praised. The praise and the openness are the same fact.

That is what we are going to walk into.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will sit with three things in the words of this ayah. None of them is a footnote. Each of them is doing structural work.

### Element 1 — *Fāṭir*: the word that means "split open"

#### Teaching the concept first

In English we have one main word for bringing something into existence: *create*. We use it for almost everything. God creates the world. An artist creates a painting. A chef creates a dish. A child creates a mess. One word, very many uses, very flat.

Arabic does not work this way. Arabic has at least seven distinct words for what we lump together as "create." Each one is doing different work. *Khalaqa* is to proportion, to bring into being with measured structure. *Ṣawwara* is to shape, to give form. *Badaʿa* is to originate without precedent. *Baraʾa* is to bring forth distinct from a substrate. *Anshaʾa* is to grow up, to raise into being. *Sawwā* is to perfect, to even out. And *faṭara* — the word in our ayah — is different from all of these.

So when Allah chooses *fāṭir* here, instead of *khāliq* — which He also could have said — the choice is loaded. He is not just informing you that He made the heavens and the earth. He is telling you **how**. He is telling you the manner.

#### Opening the word

*Fāṭir.* Active participle. Root ف-ط-ر. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib, Tāj al-ʿArūs — converge on a startling root meaning.

*Faṭara* means: **to split open, to cleave, to crack apart.**

The Arabs used this verb for the cracking of a sprout through soil. The splitting of the gum of a baby's mouth as the first tooth pushes through. The cracking of a date pit as the new shoot breaks out. The first fast-breaking of Ramadan — *al-iftār* — comes from the same root: it is the splitting open of the closed fast.

Notice something. Every one of those usages involves **something that was sealed, and is now cracking open into existence.** A seed sealed in the earth — split. A gum sealed around a tooth — split. A pit sealed around a shoot — split. The fast sealed around the mouth — split.

There is a remarkable narration from Ibn ʿAbbas, the Prophet's ﷺ cousin and the most authoritative Quranic interpreter of the first generation, on precisely this word. He said: "I did not know what *Fāṭir al-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* meant — until two Bedouins came to me disputing over a well. One of them said to his companion: *Ana fataruha* — I started it. I was the first to dig it." The word that unlocked a divine name for Ibn ʿAbbas was not found in a library. It came from two Bedouins arguing over water in the desert. *Faṭara* carries both the sense of *splitting* and *initiating* — the Bedouin's claim was: I opened this ground first; there was no well before me. Both senses belong to the divine name. Allah split open the seam where nothing was, and He was first — uncaused, unbegun, with no one who dug before Him.

#### Let us sit with this

When Allah calls Himself *Fāṭir of the heavens and the earth*, He is not simply saying "I made them." He is saying: **I split open the seam where nothing was, and brought them through.**

This is the Quran's word for what theologians call *creatio ex nihilo* — creation from nothing — but it is more vivid than the theological phrase. The Arabic gives you a picture. There was no sky. There was no earth. There was no seam, even. And He split open the absence and brought them out.

This is why, when Ibrahim عليه السلام rejects the worship of the moon and the stars and finally turns his face wholly to Allah, the word he uses is the same one: *innī wajjahtu wajhiya li'lladhī faṭara's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — "I have turned my face toward the One who split open the heavens and the earth" (6:79). Ibrahim is not making a generic statement about a creator deity. He is saying: I worship the One whose hand was on the seam.

And this is why your own original disposition — the readiness placed inside you to recognize Allah — has the same word for its name. *Fiṭra.* From the same root. Because your *fiṭra* is the way you were **split open into being**, before anything was added on top. It is the original crack of you. The form you were brought through.

#### Going one level further

Stay with me here. There is something even more remarkable in placing *Fāṭir* at the head of this surah.

What is the surah about? Read on past this ayah and you will find: it is about people whose hearts have become sealed. Sealed against the message. Sealed against praise. Sealed against the messengers Allah keeps sending. The surah will speak repeatedly about deaf hearing and blind seeing and dead hearts (*wa mā yastawi'l-aḥyāʾu wa lā'l-amwāt*, 35:22). It is about closure. About refusal.

And what is the opening name of Allah in this surah?

The One who **splits open what is sealed**.

[PAUSE]

The argument of the whole surah is hidden in the first divine attribute. Your heart feels sealed? Your circumstance feels sealed? The world feels sealed against the truth? Look at the One you are addressing. *Fāṭir.* Splitting open is what He **does**. It is His mode. He has been doing it since before the heavens existed.

This is the first thing the ayah is asking you to see.

### Element 2 — *Jāʿil*: the appointment that is not a making

#### Teaching the concept first

In English, when we say "God made the angels messengers," we hear one verb, *made*, doing two possible jobs. It could mean He brought them into existence as messengers (production). Or it could mean He assigned them to that role (appointment). The English collapses both.

Arabic has separate words for these two ideas. *Khalaqa* is to bring into existence. *Jaʿala* is to make-into, to designate, to appoint, to confer a status, to set something into a function.

When the Quran says *innī jāʿiluka li'n-nāsi imāmā* — "I am appointing you as a leader for humanity" (2:124) — to Ibrahim, the verb is not *khalaqa*. Ibrahim already exists. Allah is **designating** him. The verb does the appointing.

When the Quran says *jaʿalnākum khalāʾifa fi'l-arḍ* — "We appointed you as successors on earth" (10:14) — again the verb is not *khalaqa*. The humans already exist. The verb does the **assigning of role**.

This distinction is going to matter a great deal here.

#### Opening the word

Look at our ayah again. Allah is *Fāṭir* of the heavens and the earth — He splits them open from nothing. Then immediately He is *Jāʿil* of the angels as messengers. **Two different verbs back-to-back.**

He could have said *Fāṭir* of the heavens and the earth and the angels. That would have grouped them all under one act of origination. But He did not. He chose a different verb for the angels. He did not just *originate* the angels; He **assigned** them. He gave them a role. He set them into a function.

And the function? *Rusulan.* Messengers.

#### Notice something

The angels are not merely the staff of the heavens. They are not just glorified ornaments arranged in a celestial court. They have a job. They have an appointment. They are *rusul* — the same word the Quran uses for the human messengers Allah sent to humanity. Nuh is a *rasūl*. Musa is a *rasūl*. Muhammad ﷺ is a *rasūl*. And here, in this ayah, the **angels are rusul.**

What does that do?

It tells you that the communication between heaven and earth is not occasional. It is structural. There is a permanent messengerial apparatus running through unseen reality, and Allah Himself appointed it. The angels are not a metaphor for divine action. They are a **delivery system** with assigned tasks.

Both al-Ṭabarī and al-Muyassar specify the scope of this appointment with care: the angels carry their missions *ilā man yashāʾu min ʿibādih, wa fīmā shāʾa min amrihī wa nahyih* — to whomever He wills of His servants, with whatever He wills of His commands and prohibitions. Not a fixed address, not a closed list of recipients. An ongoing flow — commands going out, messages finding their appointed destinations, the whole apparatus in motion at all times.

And the wings — the two, three, four — are the instrument of this conveyance. Al-Muyassar is direct about it: the wings exist *li-tablīghi mā amara'llāhu bih* — to fly with what Allah has commanded. The variety is not decorative. It is the graded capacity of a working delivery system, scaled to the urgency and weight of what it carries. And this variety itself — the fact that some angels have two wings, some three, some four, and the verse's own silence after *four* suggests what lies beyond — is named by the classical commentators as *min ʿaẓīmi qudrati'llāh*: from the immensity of Allah's power. The wings are an argument, not an ornament. They point not to angelic glory but to divine capacity.

#### Here is what is remarkable

The very next surah in the Quran's order — surah Yāsīn, which comes right after this one — opens with the famous oath about Muhammad ﷺ: *innaka la-mina'l-mursalīn* — "you are surely from the messengers" (36:3). And here in the opening of Fatir, the **angels** are also from the messengers.

The Quran is quietly building a picture across these adjacent surahs of two parallel messenger orders. The angels on the unseen side. The prophets on the seen side. Both are *rusul*. Both are appointed. Both are delivering the same message from the same Source.

And you — sitting here, reading this — are at the destination of both lines. The angels carried the message. The prophet ﷺ received and transmitted it. It came to you. The whole apparatus, from the unseen down to the page in your hand, is one *risāla* — one delivered message — set in motion by the *Jāʿil* who chose to **appoint** rather than merely create.

When you read this ayah and feel its weight, you are at the bottom of a chain of appointments that begins in the highest heaven.

### Element 3 — *Yazīdu fi'l-khalqi mā yashāʾ*: the open-ended universe

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, there is a subtle but very powerful device: leaving an object **unspecified**. When you say "He adds *what He wills*," the listener's mind asks: adds *what*? And the grammar refuses to tell you. It says: whatever. Anything. Unlimited.

This is called *taʿmīm* — generalization through omission. The unspecified object is doing the work that a long list would do. The list is left blank precisely so the listener fills it with *everything possible.*

Compare two sentences. "He gives charity to the poor of his neighborhood." Bounded. Specific. Closeable. "He gives." Just that. No object, no recipient, no amount. The unbounded version is **larger** than any specific version, because the imagination is forced to extend it.

#### Opening the phrase

*Yazīdu fi'l-khalqi mā yashāʾ.* He adds to the creation **whatever He wills.**

Look at how it is built. *Yazīdu* — He increases, He adds. *Fi'l-khalq* — in the creation. *Mā yashāʾ* — what He wills. No object named. No domain specified. No ceiling placed on the adding. The grammar does the work of infinity: it leaves the question open by refusing to close it.

Now notice the tense. *Yazīdu* is the *muḍāriʿ* — the present-ongoing form in Arabic, which carries the sense of continuous, unfinished action. Not *zāda* — He added, once, at some point in the past. *He is adding.* Right now. The act is open-ended in grammar because it is open-ended in fact.

[PAUSE]

The universe you are living inside is not a finished product. It is a work in ongoing progress, and the Worker has not put down His tools.

Some of the early commentators connected *yazīdu fi'l-khalqi* specifically to the angels mentioned just before it — perhaps Allah can give them more than four wings, as the grammar places no upper ceiling on the wing-count. That reading is coherent with the immediate context. But the classical tradition at large leaves the statement broad, and the breadth appears to be the deliberate point. Al-Muyassar reads it as a general declaration: *yazīdu'llāh fī khalqihī mā yashāʾ, lā yastaʿṣī ʿalayhi shayʾ* — Allah adds to His creation whatever He wills; nothing resists Him. The specific occasion of the wings is what prompted the clause; the principle behind it is universal.

#### The closing credential

The ayah ends: *inna'llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr* — Indeed, Allah has power over every single thing.

*Qadīr* is from the root ق-د-ر. Precise power. Measured capability. Not brute force but the ability to bring about exactly what one intends, in exactly the manner one intends.

Look at the architecture of the whole ayah now. It opens with *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — praise is declared. It moves to *Fāṭir* — the One who split open what had never existed. It passes through *Jāʿil* — the One who appoints an entire unseen delivery system. It carries through *Yazīdu* — the One who is still adding. And it closes with *Qadīr* — because only a Being of perfect, unbounded power could have done all of that, and could be continuing to do it.

The praise at the opening and the power at the closing are the two pillars of the ayah. What stands between them — the splitting, the appointing, the ongoing adding — is the evidence for why both pillars belong.

[PAUSE]

This is not coincidental architecture. The surah's Makkan audience was being told that the universe is governed by One who has no partners, no helpers, no co-creators. The opening ayah answers not by arguing but by **showing**. Here is the act: *Fāṭir*. Here is the structure: *rusul*. Here is the ongoing motion: *yazīdu*. Here is the credential: *qadīr*. Everything you doubted about who made this, who runs it, who is still adding to it — the answer is the same One. And He is praising Himself, because He is the One to whom the praise flows whether you consent to send it or not.

## CLOSING

There is a pattern woven through these three elements that is worth naming before we stop.

*Fāṭir* tells you that sealed things can be split open. The very mode of origination in this surah is a splitting. Whatever feels sealed against you — sealed heart, sealed circumstance, sealed door — you are calling on the One whose mode has always been *opening*. He did it at the beginning of time. He has been doing it since. Ibn ʿAbbas did not understand the name until he heard it in the desert. The man who dug the well first said: *ana fataruha*. There was nothing here before me. Allah said the same thing about the heavens and the earth, before there was anything at all to say it about.

*Jāʿil* tells you that the communication never stopped. The apparatus of messengerial appointment is structural, not occasional. A permanent, graded delivery system — wings and all — carrying what Allah has commanded to whoever He wills. And this message reached you. That reaching is itself part of the appointment. You are at the destination of a chain that began in the highest heaven, passed through the unseen order of the angels, came down through the prophet ﷺ, and arrived here.

*Yazīdu* tells you that you are not reading a final edition. The universe you live in is still being written. He is still adding. What feels like the last page of your situation may not be. The present-tense grammar is doing theological work: the Creator is not in the past tense.

The surah Fatir will go on to describe a Makkan audience that had closed itself against all of this. It will mourn the sealing of eyes, the stopping of ears, the death of hearts. But it opens — *opens* — with the name of the One who splits sealed things open.

He was doing it before the heavens existed.

He is doing it now.

*Al-ḥamdu lillāh.*
