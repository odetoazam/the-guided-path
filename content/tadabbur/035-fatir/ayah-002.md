# The Door No Hand Can Close
### A Reflection on Surah Fatir, Ayah 2

---

## INTRODUCTION

**The ayah:**

> مَّا يَفْتَحِ ٱللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِن رَّحْمَةٍ فَلَا مُمْسِكَ لَهَا ۖ وَمَا يُمْسِكْ فَلَا مُرْسِلَ لَهُۥ مِنۢ بَعْدِهِۦ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*Mā yaftaḥi-llāhu lin-nāsi min raḥmatin falā mumsika lahā, wa mā yumsik falā mursila lahu min baʿdih, wa huwa-l-ʿazīzu-l-ḥakīm.*

**"Whatever mercy Allah opens for people, none can withhold it; and whatever He withholds, none can release it after Him. And He is the Almighty, the All-Wise."**

---

### Rebuilding the world

Before we open this ayah, let us stand for a moment where its first listeners stood.

Imagine a city built around scarcity. Mecca is a valley of stone. Nothing grows there on its own. Every drop of water, every caravan of grain, every season of trade is watched, prayed over, and feared for. And in this world of thin margins, the people had filled the gaps in their security with names. Names carved in stone and wood. Around the Kaʿba stood idols, and each idol was, in the imagination of its worshippers, a kind of *manager* — a being who could pull a string, open a channel, tilt fortune your way if you fed it the right devotion. A gatekeeper of rain. A patron of trade. An intercessor who might, if you flattered him, get your request through to a higher office.

This is not as foreign to us as it sounds. We will come back to that.

Now hear how the surah begins. The very first word the listener meets — after the praise — is a name of Allah they had perhaps never weighed: **Fāṭir**. *Fāṭir as-samāwāti wal-arḍ.* The One who tore the heavens and the earth open into existence. The One who originated them where there was nothing. And then, in that same opening verse: *He made the angels messengers with wings — two, three, four — and He adds to His creation whatever He wills.*

So the surah opens with a single, overwhelming image: a Hand that opens what was closed, that brings forth what was not, that *adds* and *opens* at will, with no committee, no rival, no permission sought.

And then comes our ayah. Verse two. It takes that cosmic Hand — the Hand that split open the skies — and it brings it down into your kitchen, your bank account, your lungs, your unanswered prayer. It says: that same opening — the one that made galaxies — is the opening that decides whether mercy reaches *you* today.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

There is a word in this ayah that we expect — and it is missing. And there is a single letter, a pronoun so small you would never see it in translation, that quietly changes gender halfway through the verse. Most people recite this ayah their whole lives and never notice either one.

But Allah does not waste a letter. And when you see what He named, and what He refused to name — when you see *which* word He let slip into the feminine and which He left cold and abstract — you will understand that this verse is not a verse about logistics. It is not merely telling you *who controls the supply chain.*

It is leaning, with its entire grammatical weight, toward mercy.

Let us see how.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — He does not *give* mercy. He *opens* it.

Let us begin with the verb, because the verb sets the entire stage.

First, a small idea we all already know. There is a difference between *giving* you something and *opening* something for you. If I *give* you water, I am handing you a cup — a single, measured portion, and then my hand is empty. But if I *open* a tap for you, or unlock a door to a reservoir, the act is different in kind. Opening implies there was a vast store, sealed, held back behind a barrier — and the only thing standing between you and it was the *opening*. Opening is not the manufacture of the thing. It is the removal of what kept the thing from reaching you.

Hold that distinction. Now look at the word Allah chose.

> مَّا **يَفْتَحِ** ٱللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِن رَّحْمَةٍ

The verb is **yaftaḥ**, from the root **ف-ت-ح (f-t-ḥ)**, *fatḥ* — to open. This is a Form I verb, the plain, foundational form of the root, and its core lexical meaning is exactly what you think: to open a door, to unlock, to break a seal, to part what was shut.

He did not say *yuʿṭī* — "He gives." He did not say *yarzuq* — "He provides." He said **yaftaḥ** — *He opens.*

Notice something. The same root, *fatḥ*, is the word the Quran uses for *victory*, for *conquest*, for the breakthrough that ends a long siege. *Innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā* — "We have opened for you a clear opening." When a besieged people are finally relieved, that is *fatḥ*. When a locked situation finally breaks open, that is *fatḥ*.

So when Allah says He **opens** mercy for people, the image underneath the word is this: **mercy is not scarce. Mercy is not manufactured drop by drop. Mercy already exists, in oceans, in treasuries, sealed — and the entire question of your life is whether the door is opened.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it quietly demolishes the entire mental world of the first listeners — and ours.

The idol-worshipper imagines mercy as a *thin* thing. A trickle that must be coaxed, bargained for, competed over, because there isn't enough to go around. That is why you need a patron — to get *your* share before someone else gets theirs. Scarcity is the engine of every false god.

But *yaftaḥ* says: the constraint was never supply. Elsewhere the Quran makes the underground reservoir explicit — *"With Him are the treasuries of everything"* (15:21), *"Or do they possess the treasuries of the mercy of your Lord, the Almighty, the Bestower?"* (38:9). The mercy is already there, in unimaginable abundance. There is only ever one variable: **is the door open, or closed?** And there is only ever one Hand on that door.

So the physical image — a door, a seal, a tap, a treasury opened — is the stepping stone. The unseen reality it points to is this: **your scarcity was never a scarcity of mercy. It was a question of an opening. And the opening belongs to One.**

That is what a single verb did. *He opens.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — the word He named, and the word He buried

Now we come to the heart of the verse — and to the missing word I promised you, and the pronoun that changes gender.

I need to teach you one small thing about Arabic first, and then this will open like a flower.

In Arabic, the word **مَا (mā)** — "what, whatever" — is grammatically treated as *masculine*. It has no gender of its own really, but when a pronoun refers back to it, the default is the masculine pronoun, *hu* ("it/him"). However — and this is the key — when *mā* clearly stands for a specific noun that *has* a gender, the speaker has a choice: he can match the pronoun to the bare word *mā* (masculine), or he can match it to the *meaning*, to the actual named thing.

Keep that choice in your mind: **match the word, or match the meaning.** It is a small fork in the road, and Allah takes a different turn on each side of this verse. Watch.

**The first half:**

> مَّا يَفْتَحِ ٱللَّهُ لِلنَّاسِ مِن **رَّحْمَةٍ** فَلَا مُمْسِكَ **لَهَا**

"Whatever Allah opens for people **of mercy** — there is no withholder **for it.**"

Here Allah *names the thing*. He says *min raḥmah* — "of mercy." The word **raḥmah** (root **ر-ح-م**, *r-ḥ-m* — womb, mercy, tenderness) is grammatically **feminine**. And so when the pronoun comes — *falā mumsika **lahā*** — Allah chooses to **match the meaning.** *Lahā.* "For *her*, for *it*" — feminine, agreeing with *raḥmah*. The mercy is named, and the pronoun reaches back and embraces it by name.

Now hold your breath for the second half:

> وَمَا يُمْسِكْ فَلَا مُرْسِلَ **لَهُۥ** مِنۢ بَعْدِهِۦ

"And whatever He withholds — there is no releaser **for it** — after Him."

Two things just happened, and almost no one notices either.

**First: the word "mercy" is gone.** In the giving, He said "of mercy." In the withholding, He does not say "and whatever He withholds *of mercy*." He simply says *wa mā yumsik* — "and whatever He withholds." The noun is dropped. The thing held back is left **unnamed.**

**Second: the pronoun changes gender.** It is no longer *lahā* (feminine). It is **lahu** — masculine. Because there is no longer a named feminine noun to agree with. With "mercy" removed, the pronoun falls back to the bare, abstract, *masculine* default of *mā*.

This is not my invention. The grammarians, as reported by **al-Qurtubi** in his commentary, discuss this very fork: they note that one could, *in language outside the Quran*, have said *falā mumsika lahu* in the first half (matching the bare word) or *falā mursila lahā* in the second (matching the implied mercy) — but the Quran's chosen wording is precisely this asymmetry: **feminine, by the named meaning, for the mercy that is opened — and masculine, by the bare word, for the thing that is withheld and left unnamed.**

[PAUSE]

Now — why? Why name the mercy on the way in, and leave the withholding nameless on the way out?

Let us sit with this, because this is where the verse reveals its heart.

When mercy comes, Allah *dignifies it with its name.* He calls it what it is — *raḥmah* — and lets the grammar lean toward it, embrace it, follow it home in the feminine, the gender of the *womb*, of tenderness, of the thing that nurtures. The mercy is foregrounded, spotlit, named, cherished by the very syntax.

But when something is *withheld* — Allah does not say "and whatever **mercy** He withholds." He will not put the word "mercy" next to the word "withhold." He leaves the withheld thing in shadow, abstract, generic, *un-named.* As if the verse itself recoils from labeling the holding-back, refuses to dress it in the same beautiful word.

Here is what this invites us to consider. **The withholding is real — but the verse will not let it stand on equal footing with the giving.** The mercy is named and embraced; the withholding is acknowledged and then quietly hurried past, nameless, unadorned. The grammar is not neutral. The grammar has a tilt. And the tilt is toward mercy.

This is the same God who said in the sacred hadith, *"My mercy overcomes My wrath."* Here you can *see* it in the architecture of a single sentence: mercy gets its name and its tender feminine pronoun; the withholding gets neither.

[PAUSE — let that settle]

And note the small, devastating phrase tacked onto the withholding: *min baʿdih* — **"after Him."** As al-Jalalayn and al-Baghawi explain, this means *baʿda imsākih* — "after His withholding." It seals the door. Once He has held something back, there is no later authority, no appeal court, no second office down the hall, no *after* in which someone else might overturn it. The decision is not just unchallenged in the present — it is **final in time.** There is no "after Him."

So even here, in the hardest clause — the clause about denial — the verse is doing two things at once: it refuses to name the withheld thing "mercy," *and* it tells you the withholding is utterly final. Both of those, strangely, are kindnesses. We will see why in Part Two.

### Element Three — the sound of a closed fist and an open hand

One more thing, and this one you can *hear* before you can explain it.

Listen to the two opposed actions in this verse, in Arabic:

- To withhold: **مُمْسِكَ / يُمْسِكْ** — *mumsik / yumsik.*
- To release: **مُرْسِلَ** — *mursil.*

Say them slowly. *Mum-sik.* Feel what your mouth does. The lips press shut on the **m**, twice, and then the **s-k** clamps down — *sik* — a hard, closed, gripping cluster at the back of the mouth. The word **mumsik** physically *closes.* Your mouth makes a fist.

The root is **م-س-ك (m-s-k)**, which means exactly that — to grasp, to grip, to clutch, to hold fast. *Amsaka* (the Form IV verb here) is the deliberate act of *holding back* — and the word's very sound is a hand closing.

Now: *mur-sil.* The lips open. The **r** rolls forward, the **l** glides off the tongue and *releases* — there is nothing to clamp, nothing to grip, the sound flows out and away. The root **ر-س-ل (r-s-l)** means to send, to dispatch, to *let flow* — it is the root of *rasūl*, messenger, the one sent out, and of *irsāl*, the loosing of something to run free. The word **mursil** physically *opens and flows.*

[PAUSE]

So embedded in the very *sound* of these two words is the entire drama of the verse: a **closed fist** (*mumsik*) and an **open, flowing hand** (*mursil*). The Arabic does not merely *describe* withholding and releasing. It *performs* them in your mouth. When you recite this ayah, your own lips clench and then open, clench and then open — you are, without knowing it, miming the two states of the one Hand the whole verse is about.

And here is the structural beauty: the verse crosses them.

- What He **opens** (*yaftaḥ*) → no one can **close/grip** (*mumsik*).
- What He **grips/closes** (*yumsik*) → no one can **open/release** (*mursil*).

The open is met by the impossibility of closing; the closed is met by the impossibility of opening. It is a perfect lock. Two hands reaching for the same door — His, and everyone else's — and the verse calmly tells you that every hand but His finds the door already decided. *Falā mumsika lahā. Falā mursila lahu.*

And both negations begin with that little particle: **فَلَا (falā)** — *lā an-nāfiya lil-jins*, the *lā* that negates the **entire genus.** This is not "few can withhold it" or "it is hard to withhold it." Grammatically, *falā mumsika* empties the *entire category* of withholders out of existence. It says: **there does not exist, anywhere in creation, a single member of the class "withholder of what Allah opened."** The category is swept clean. Zero. And again for the releaser: zero.

That is the linguistic verdict. Not that His rivals are weak. That **His rivals do not exist as a category at all.**

---

### Bridge

So look at what these words have shown us, before we say a single thing about "lessons."

A verb — *yaftaḥ* — that reframes mercy not as a scarce trickle but as an ocean behind a door.

A buried noun and a shifting pronoun — *lahā* then *lahu* — that secretly tilt the whole sentence toward mercy, naming and embracing what is given, refusing to dignify what is withheld.

And two words whose very sound is a fist and an open palm, locked across the verse so that every hand but One finds the door already decided — and the category of rivals emptied to zero.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we are to *live*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — the relief of the single door

Let me start where you actually live.

You have spent your life, whether you've noticed it or not, learning to read *gatekeepers.* The manager who decides your raise. The admissions officer. The loan officer. The visa official behind the glass. The algorithm that decides whether your work is seen. The specialist whose calendar is full for three months. The landlord. The committee. You have learned, as we all have, a kind of anxious fluency in the faces of the people who hold the doors — when to push, when to flatter, when to wait, when to fear.

And here is the quiet exhaustion of modern life: **there are so many doors, and so many hands on them, and none of them are reliable.** The manager is moody. The market is irrational. The official is corrupt. The algorithm is a black box. We live scattered across a hundred gatekeepers, and our hearts are torn into a hundred pieces of hope and fear, distributed among people who, frankly, are as helpless as we are.

Now hear the ayah again, but this time as the relief it was sent to be.

*Whatever Allah opens of mercy — there is no withholder for it. And whatever He withholds — there is no releaser for it after Him.*

[PAUSE]

What this verse does, with surgical calm, is **collapse all your gatekeepers into one.**

It does not say the manager has no power *in himself* — it goes further. It says: **whatever Allah opened, no manager can close. Whatever Allah closed, no manager can open.** The manager who gave you the raise was a *door* — but the opening behind that door was Allah's. The manager who denied you was a *door* — but he could not have held back one atom that Allah had decided to open. He was never the source. He was a hinge. And the hand on the hinge was always, only, One.

Think about what this does to fear. The Quran says it plainly elsewhere — *"If Allah touches you with harm, none can remove it but He; and if He intends good for you, none can repel His bounty"* (10:107). And in our surah's own family of verses — *"Who is it that could provide for you if He withheld His provision?"* (67:21). Every false god in your life — every person you have over-feared and over-flattered — is exposed as a doorknob mistaking itself for a treasury.

[PAUSE]

There is a particular freedom in *monopoly* — and only here is monopoly a mercy.

If a hundred independent people each held a veto over your wellbeing, you would have to please a hundred masters, and any one of them could ruin you. That is **shirk** as a *lived emotional state* — the heart enslaved to many. But if **One** holds it all, then you have only one Face to turn to, one Door to knock on, and — crucially — that One cannot be outbid, bypassed, lobbied, or overruled by any of the others, *because there are no others.* Remember the grammar: the category of rivals was emptied to zero. *Falā mumsik. Falā mursil.*

So what changes tomorrow?

Tomorrow, when you walk into the room where the gatekeeper sits — the interview, the clinic, the office, the glass window — you do the *means* fully and excellently, because doing the means is itself worship. But your **heart** is not on the gatekeeper. Your heart has already gone past him, through the wall, to the One whose opening the gatekeeper merely transmits. You stop auditioning for doorknobs. You knock, instead, on the one Door behind all doors. And you walk in with the strange calm of a person who knows that the verdict was never the official's to give.

That is the first gift of the verse: **it gathers your scattered fear and hope into one place, and that place is safe.**

### Theme Two — when the door does not open

But we must be honest, because you are honest, and you have not forgotten the second half of the verse.

*And whatever He withholds — there is no releaser for it after Him.*

It is easy to love a verse about mercy *opening.* But what about the mercy that *doesn't* open? What about the duʿā you have made for years that has not been answered? The healing that did not come. The marriage that did not happen. The child. The relief. The door you have stood before, knocking until your knuckles bled, and it has not opened — *and now this verse tells you that since Allah is the one holding it shut, no power in existence can open it.*

That could sound like a sentence of despair. Let us see why it is the opposite.

[PAUSE]

Start with the human experience. There is a unique torture in *not knowing whom to blame, or whom to ask.* When a door won't open and you don't know who holds it, you exhaust yourself running between offices. *Maybe it's this one. Maybe if I'd done that. Maybe someone else can override it.* You are tormented not just by the closed door but by the *fantasy of the override* — the belief that somewhere there is another hand that could have opened it, if only you'd found it.

Now read the verse's hardest phrase again — the one we flagged in Part One: *falā mursila lahu **min baʿdih**.* "None can release it **after Him.**" There is **no after.** No second office. No override.

And feel, strangely, the *rest* in that.

The verse is not slamming a door in your face. It is ending your exhausting tour of the building. It is saying: **stop running between hands. There was only ever One. The door that did not open was held by the same Hand that opens every door that opens — and you do not need to find a different hand, because there isn't one, and the one that exists loves you.**

[PAUSE]

But now the deepest turn — and it lives in two places we have already touched.

**First, remember what the grammar refused to do.** When the verse spoke of withholding, it would *not* attach the word "mercy" to it — it left the withheld thing unnamed, masculine, abstract. We read that, in Part One, as the verse tilting toward mercy. But there is a second reading the classical scholars draw out, and it is breathtaking. Look at how al-Muyassar and the mufassirūn explain the *withholding itself*: that Allah *"sends mercy and withholds it according to His wisdom."* The withholding is not the *opposite* of mercy. **The withholding is itself an act of the same merciful, wise Hand.**

The verse declined to call the withholding "mercy" out loud — but it never called it anything else either. It left a silence. And into that silence the *next words* speak.

**Second — the two names that close the verse:**

> وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*And He is **al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm.* The Almighty, the All-Wise.*

Do not rush past these. This is the whole verse's resolution, and it is built from two roots in perfect tension.

**Al-ʿAzīz** (root **ع-ز-ز**, *ʿ-z-z*) — the Mighty, the Dominant, the Inaccessible One whom nothing can overpower or reach to pressure. This is the name of His *power.* It is the name that *guarantees* the rest of the verse: of course no one can override His opening or closing — He is *al-ʿAzīz*, the Unoverpowerable.

But power alone would be *terrifying.* A being of absolute, unchallengeable power, who opens and closes doors on you with no one able to appeal — if that were *all*, it would be tyranny on a cosmic scale.

So He does not stop at *al-ʿAzīz.* He says **al-Ḥakīm** (root **ح-ك-م**, *ḥ-k-m*) — the All-Wise. And the same root, *ḥ-k-m*, is the root of *ḥikmah* (wisdom) and of *ḥukm* (judgment) — but also, tellingly, of the **bridle**, the *ḥakamah*, the rein that *restrains a horse and directs it.* Wisdom, in Arabic, is the faculty that *holds power back and steers it toward the right end.* Al-Ḥakīm is not just "the One who knows what's wise." He is the One whose power is *reined to wisdom*, never random, never cruel, never wasted.

[PAUSE]

Now put them together, because the whole consolation of the verse is in their pairing.

**Power without wisdom is tyranny. Wisdom without power is helplessness.** A wise friend who cannot help you is heartbreaking. A powerful ruler who is not wise is terrifying. But the One who holds your closed door is **both at once** — *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm.* He has the power that means no one can overturn His decision — *and* the wisdom that means the decision was never careless, never blind, never against you.

So the door that did not open was not jammed. It was not forgotten. It was not held by a tyrant. **It was held shut by Wisdom that also had the Power to open it — and chose, for a reason you may not see, not to.** The same Hand that you trust to open is the Hand that, this time, held. And it is the *same Hand.* You cannot trust the opening and distrust the holding, because there is only One Hand, and it is named, in the same breath, Mighty *and* Wise.

This is why the verse left the withholding unnamed. It was waiting for these two names to speak for it. The withholding looks like absence — but it is signed, at the bottom of the verse, *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm.* And that signature changes everything.

[PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow, for the prayer that has not been answered?

You keep asking — because asking is worship, and the door is His, and He loves to be asked. But the *quality* of your waiting changes. You are no longer waiting at a random, indifferent door, tormented by the fantasy that some other hand could open it. You are waiting before *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm.* You say: *You opened so much for me that no one could close. So I trust that what You are holding, You are holding with the same hand and the same heart. Not against me. For a wisdom. And there is no "after You" — so I will stop looking for another door, and I will rest here, at Yours.*

That is not resignation. That is the deepest form of trust available to a human being: to let the closed door be as much a mercy as the open one, *because the Hand is the same, and the Hand is Wise.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now that we have done the work, look at how perfectly this verse is built. It is a chiasm — a mirror — and the mirror is the proof.

> **A** — What Allah **opens** (*yaftaḥ*) of **mercy** *(named, feminine, embraced: lahā)*
>   **B** — no one can **withhold** it *(mumsik — the closed fist)*
>   **B′** — and what He **withholds** *(yumsik — the closed fist again)* *(unnamed, masculine, abstract: lahu)*
> **A′** — no one can **release** it *(mursil — the open hand)* — **after Him** *(no appeal, final)*
>
> **Signature:** *And He is al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — Mighty, so it cannot be overturned; Wise, so it should not be feared.

See it now? The verb of opening (A) is answered by the impossibility of closing (B). The verb of closing (B′) is answered by the impossibility of opening (A′). Open against closed, closed against open, locked in a perfect X — and the *sound* of the words performs it: fist, open hand, fist, open hand. Then the two Names descend like a seal on a contract: **Power** that makes the lock unbreakable, **Wisdom** that makes the lock trustworthy.

The architecture *is* the argument. You did not need to be told to trust Him. The shape of the sentence trusted Him for you.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the lived themes turn out to be a single insight seen from two sides.

The **verb** *yaftaḥ* told us mercy is an ocean behind a door, not a trickle — and that became the **relief of the single door**: you have only One to ask, and He is not running low.

The **buried noun and the two Names** told us the verse tilts toward mercy and signs even its silences with Wisdom — and that became the **consolation of the closed door**: what is withheld is withheld by the same Mighty, Wise Hand, with no "after" to torment you and no carelessness to fear.

One Hand. On every door. Mighty enough that no one can overrule it. Wise enough that you would not want to.

### Questions to carry

1. Whose face have I been reading — whose approval, whose veto — as though *they* held the door, when they were only ever a hinge?
2. Is there an "open" in my life — a mercy that came — that I credited to a person, a stroke of luck, my own cleverness, and never traced back to the Hand that *opened* it where no one could close it?
3. Is there a closed door I am still touring the building to override — still half-believing some *other* hand might open what He has held?
4. Can I say, about the specific thing being withheld from me right now, the words *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — and mean them? What would change in my chest if I could?
5. If His mercy is an ocean He opens, and the only variable is the door — what in *me* am I treating as the lock, when the key was never mine to hold?

### One-sentence distillation

Mercy is an ocean Allah opens that no hand can close, and what He holds back no hand can open — but the Hand that holds is named, in the very same breath, both Almighty and All-Wise, so that even the closed door is signed by the One whose opening you trust.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to carry a hundred gatekeepers in your chest anymore. The invitation of this verse is to *consolidate your heart* — to take all the scattered hope and fear you have distributed among people who cannot help you, and gather it into the one place it was always meant to rest. To do your means with excellence, and then let your heart walk past every doorknob to the Door behind the doors. And when a door does not open, to stop running the building looking for an override — and to sit down, instead, before *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*, and trust the silence, because it is signed.

### A brief du'a

*Yā Fāṭir, yā ʿAzīz, yā Ḥakīm — what You have opened for us of mercy, let no fear of people make us forget it came from You; and what You have withheld, give us the heart to trust that the Hand which held it is the same Hand we love, Mighty and Wise. Pull our scattered hope back from every helpless door, and rest it at Yours alone, the only Door, after which there is no other.*

*Āmīn.*
