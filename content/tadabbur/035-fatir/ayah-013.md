# He Owns the Dominion, They Don't Own a Date-Skin

## Surah Fatir, Ayah 13

> [ayah:35:13] يُولِجُ ٱلَّيْلَ فِى ٱلنَّهَارِ وَيُولِجُ ٱلنَّهَارَ فِى ٱلَّيْلِ وَسَخَّرَ ٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ كُلٌّ يَجْرِى لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ رَبُّكُمْ لَهُ ٱلْمُلْكُ ۚ وَٱلَّذِينَ تَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِهِۦ مَا يَمْلِكُونَ مِن قِطْمِيرٍ

*Yūliju al-layla fī an-nahāri wa-yūliju an-nahāra fī al-layli wa-sakhkhara ash-shamsa wa-l-qamara kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā. Dhālikumu Allāhu rabbukum lahu al-mulk. Wa-lladhīna tadʿūna min dūnihi mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīr.*

**He causes the night to enter into the day, and He causes the day to enter into the night, and He has harnessed the sun and the moon — each running for an appointed term. That is Allah, your Lord; to Him belongs the dominion. And those whom you call upon besides Him do not possess even the thin skin on a date-stone.**

---

## Introduction: Standing in Two Worlds at Once

Before we open this ayah, sit with the world it was first heard in.

The Makkan Quraysh did not deny that Allah existed. They didn't deny that Allah created the heavens, the earth, the sun, the moon. They didn't deny that Allah sent rain or stretched the seas. The Quran tells us this in many places — ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will say *Allah*. Ask them who sends down rain, they will say *Allah*.

Their problem was somewhere else.

Their problem was that **they couldn't bring themselves to depend on Him alone.** They believed He was up there, vast, distant, busy. So for the small things — the harvest, the journey, the marriage, the child, the illness, the harvest, the rivalry between tribes — they turned to smaller gods. Closer gods. Specialized gods. Manageable gods. Stones and trees and figures they had carved themselves, who they thought could intercede, intervene, deliver a small favor.

It was a theology of **outsourcing**. The big things to Allah. The everyday things to local powers.

So Surah Fatir spends ayah after ayah doing one thing: **showing them that the same Allah who runs the cosmos is the One they should be running to for the qiṭmīr-sized things in their lives.** That He is not too vast to handle their small concerns. That the smaller powers they turn to handle nothing at all.

This ayah is the climax of that argument. It begins with the largest things in the visible universe — the sun, the moon, the alternation of night and day. It ends with the smallest thing the speaker can think of: the membrane on the inside of a date-stone.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Notice what Allah does in this ayah. He places the cosmic and the negligible side by side. He gives Himself all the dominion. He gives the false gods *less than a date-skin.* He doesn't just say they own less than Him. He chooses the smallest, most worthless thing He can find and says — **not even that.**

Why? What is He preparing you to see about the architecture of your own worship?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb يُولِجُ — Not "Alternates," But "Inserts"

Let me start with a familiar phenomenon and ask you to look at it differently.

When you describe day turning into night, what verb comes to mind? *Becomes. Turns into. Gives way to. Cycles.* In English we picture a switch — light, then dark. A handover. A rotation.

Now imagine instead the moment of sunset. The actual moment, slowed down. The light doesn't end. The dark doesn't begin. There is this long, drawn-out gradient where the two are *inside each other*. Blue inside gold. Gold inside indigo. The night is not waiting for its turn — **the night is already inside the day, slowly being drawn out.**

This is the verb Allah uses.

The word is يُولِجُ — *yūliju.* Its root is و-ل-ج (w-l-j), and the basic root meaning is **to enter into**. Not to pass into. Not to alternate with. **To enter into.** The same root gives us وَلَجَ — to go inside something, to penetrate it, to be lodged within it.

But Allah doesn't use the simple form. He uses **Form IV** — *aflaʿa*, which here makes the verb transitive: *yūliju* means **He causes (something) to enter into** (something else). He inserts. He lodges. He makes the one penetrate the other.

So the literal sense of يُولِجُ ٱلَّيْلَ فِى ٱلنَّهَارِ is not *"He alternates night and day."* It is:

**He inserts the night into the day. He inserts the day into the night.**

Stay with that image. The night is not waiting outside the door of the day for its shift to start. The night is being **slid into** the day — and the day, in return, is being **slid into** the night. They penetrate each other. They are not switching off; they are interleaving.

Classical commentators caught this. Al-Jalālayn glosses *yūliju* as *yudkhilu* — "He causes to enter." Al-Muyassar elaborates: He takes from the hours of the night and inserts them into the day, so the day grows by exactly what the night lost — and vice versa. The two times are not separate compartments. They are one continuous quantity, with Allah moving the boundary, hour by hour, day by day.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something else. The verb is **imperfect** — *yūliju*, in the ongoing present tense. Not "He inserted." Not "He will insert." **He inserts. Right now.** As you read this line, He is doing it. The boundary is moving as we speak.

Here is what this prepares us to see.

When we think about Allah's tadbīr — His management of our affairs — we tend to imagine clean breaks. A door closes. A door opens. A chapter ends. A new one begins. We expect transitions to be obvious, signposted, switch-like.

But this ayah is teaching us a different physics. Allah's transitions are not switches. **They are insertions.** The new is already being slid into the old long before you can see it. The relief is already entering the hardship. The ease is already inside the difficulty. The night is already inside the brightest noon.

What this invites us to consider: when you find yourself in a season that feels totally one thing — totally dark, or totally bright — the architecture of reality says you are wrong. **The other is already being inserted.** You just cannot see the boundary moving.

### Element Two: The Tense Shift — سَخَّرَ vs. يَجْرِى

Now look at the next two verbs. Something has changed.

The first half of the ayah was imperfect — *yūliju, yūliju*. He inserts, He inserts. Ongoing. Right now.

Then suddenly: وَسَخَّرَ ٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ — *and He has harnessed the sun and the moon.* The verb is **perfect tense.** Completed action. Already done.

And the verb itself is loaded. سَخَّرَ — *sakhkhara* — is **Form II** of the root s-kh-r. Form II in Arabic typically intensifies the meaning of the root. The basic root carries the sense of subjection, harnessing, putting something to service. Form II makes it heavier: **thoroughly harnessed, fully subjugated, decisively put to service.** It is the same verb the Quran uses elsewhere for how Allah has subjected ships to the sea, cattle to humanity, the rivers, the winds.

So Allah is saying: **the sun and the moon have been thoroughly, decisively harnessed.** Past tense. Settled fact. They are not still being persuaded. They are not still being negotiated with. They are already bound to their service.

And then comes the third verb: كُلٌّ يَجْرِى — *each runs.*

Back to imperfect. Ongoing. **Each one of them is running, right now, as you read this.**

[PAUSE]

Notice what this sequence does. It gives you a complete picture of authority across time:

- The **insertion** of night into day is happening *now* — ongoing, continuous.
- The **harnessing** of sun and moon is a *completed fact* — settled in the past, no longer in question.
- Their **running** is happening *now* — they are still in motion, obeying that completed decree.

This is the architecture of how Allah rules. Some of His decisions are already final. Some of His acts are perpetually unfolding. The completed and the ongoing braid together.

Al-Qurtubi, in his treatment, points to the parallel use of these phrases in earlier surahs — Āl ʿImrān and Luqmān — and reads this verse as a kind of cumulative reminder: *this is the One whose creative and sustaining acts you keep encountering across the Book.* The sun's orbit is not something Allah is still deciding. **It was harnessed. Now it runs.**

And the running is *li-ajalin musammā* — for an **appointed term**. *Musammā* — from the root س-م-و / س-م-ي — means **named.** Not just decided. **Named.** The end-time of the sun's running, the end-time of the moon's running, the end-time of the night-day rhythm — each of these has a specific name attached to it. A specific moment. Al-Jalālayn glosses *al-ajal al-musammā* here as the Day of Resurrection — the moment when the running stops, the harness is released, the insertion of night into day finally pauses because there will be no more day, no more night.

Here is what this invites us to consider.

Everything that is currently running has already been *named* with an end. The most reliable thing you can see in the sky — the sun, the moon, the cycle of light and dark — has an expiration date that has already been written down with a specific name. **Your own running has one too.** Your morning routine. Your career trajectory. Your relationships. Each is jāriy — running — *li-ajalin musammā.* For a named term.

This is not threat. This is **architecture.** When you understand that every running thing has a named end, you stop confusing motion with permanence.

### Element Three: The Hapax — قِطْمِيرٍ

Now we arrive at one of the most extraordinary words in the entire Quran.

The word is قِطْمِير — *qiṭmīr.*

Listen to it. *Qiṭ-mīr.* Notice the phonetic texture — the emphatic ط, the sharp consonant cluster, the way it ends with that thin, brittle *-īr*. It is a tiny, sharp-sounding word.

It occurs **only in this verse.** Nowhere else in the Quran. A hapax legomenon — a word used a single time in the entire revelation. Allah reaches into the Arabic lexicon, pulls out this one specific noun, uses it exactly once, and then never again. That alone should slow you down. *Why this word? Why here? Why only here?*

What does it mean?

The classical lexicographers are remarkably specific. Al-Jalālayn says it is *lifāqat al-nawāh* — **the husk of a date-stone.** Al-Muyassar is even more precise: *al-qishrah ar-raqīqah al-bayḍāʾ takūn ʿalā an-nawāh* — **the thin white skin that covers the date-stone.** Al-Baghawi: *lifāfat an-nawāh — al-qishrah ar-raqīqah allatī takūn ʿalā an-nawāh.* The thin wrapping on the seed.

Picture it. Take a date. Eat the flesh. You're left with the hard pit — the *nawāh*. Now look at the pit closely. There is a thin, papery, translucent skin clinging to it. Sometimes you can peel it off with your fingernail. It is almost nothing. It tears. It has no weight. It has no use.

**That** is qiṭmīr.

Now read what Allah says: *wa-lladhīna tadʿūna min dūnihi mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīr* — **And those whom you call on besides Him do not possess even a qiṭmīr.**

The negation is doubled for intensity. *Mā* (not) + *min* (here partitive, meaning "any amount of"). Not — any — qiṭmīr. **Not even that much.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. Allah has just spent the first half of the ayah listing the largest, most majestic phenomena available to human perception — the alternation of night and day, the harnessing of the sun, the moon orbiting on its track. He has just declared *lahu al-mulk* — to Him belongs **al-mulk**, the dominion, the kingdom, **all of it.** Then He turns to what people invoke besides Him, and instead of saying *they have nothing,* He chooses **the smallest specific object His listener can imagine** and says: not even that.

This is not random. This is **rhetorical precision at the level of theology.**

Look at the linguistic play. **The dominion** is *al-mulk* — root م-ل-ك. **They do not possess** is *mā yamlikūn* — also root م-ل-ك. The same root, used twice, in the same verse, on opposite sides of the comparison:

- Allah → *lahu al-mulk* → to Him belongs THE dominion.
- The idols → *mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīr* → they do not possess so much as a date-skin.

The verse is built around this single root. **Allah owns mulk. They don't yamliku qiṭmīr.** All of it on one side. Less than a peelable membrane on the other.

And then the sonic dimension. *Qiṭmīr* sounds like what it is. The hard, abrupt *qiṭ-* — a sharp, breakable thing. The thin *-mīr* trailing off, papery, weightless. The word **collapses in your mouth**, the way the thing it names crumbles between your fingers. Allah didn't say "atom" or "speck" or "grain." He picked a word whose phonetics enact its meaning.

What this invites us to consider:

When you decide to depend on something other than Allah — whether that something is wealth, status, a person, a system, a possession, an image of yourself — Allah is not contesting whether that thing has *any* power. He is making a sharper claim. **It does not own even a qiṭmīr.** It cannot deliver to you the equivalent of a peelable skin on a seed.

You are placing the weight of your hope on something that cannot bear a date-husk's worth of it.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

So we have three things. We have a God who *inserts* night into day, gradually, continuously, right now. We have sun and moon already *harnessed*, still *running*, toward a *named* end. We have a single word — *qiṭmīr* — that exposes the absurdity of where we place our trust.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words, but about how we are actually living.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: What You Actually Worship Owns Less Than a Date-Skin

Let me start somewhere very concrete. Not with the Makkans. With you.

Think about a recent moment when something you cared about was at stake. A job interview. A child's health. A conversation you were dreading. A medical result. A financial decision. A look from someone whose approval you wanted.

In that moment, **where did your mind go?**

Not in your formal prayer. In the running monologue of your inner life. What were you reaching toward to make it okay? What did you believe — if you got *that*, the anxiety would settle?

For most of us, the honest answer is: **we reached for something specific.** A particular outcome. A particular person's response. A particular number on a screen. A particular feeling we wanted to receive back.

And we invested in that thing with enormous emotional weight. We refreshed the inbox. We rehearsed the conversation. We performed the version of ourselves we thought would secure the result. We organized our day around it. Our breathing changed when we thought about it.

This is what the Quran calls *duʿāʾ* — calling on something. Not just verbal supplication. **The orientation of your hope.** The thing you turn toward when you need to be saved. The Makkan was kneeling in front of a stone. You and I are checking a phone. The mechanism is identical: *here is the thing I am trusting to deliver me from my current discomfort.*

Now hear Allah's verdict on that thing — whatever yours is. *Mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīr.*

**It does not possess a date-skin's worth of what you are asking it to deliver.**

[PAUSE]

This is not a moral attack. This is a **diagnostic**. Allah is showing you the disproportion. He is showing you the gap between the size of your hope and the size of what is holding it.

You are asking that conversation to give you your worth. It cannot deliver a qiṭmīr of worth.
You are asking that bank balance to give you safety. It cannot deliver a qiṭmīr of safety.
You are asking that person's affection to settle the question of whether you matter. They cannot deliver a qiṭmīr of that answer.

This is not because those things are bad. They are not. A conversation may go well. A balance may grow. A person may love you. But **none of them owns the thing you are trying to extract from them.** They are receivers, like you. They do not possess. They are possessed. They do not deliver. They themselves are being delivered.

Meanwhile — and this is what the first half of the ayah is for — the One who *does* own all of it is **right there.** Inserting night into day right now. Holding the sun on its track right now. Naming the appointed term right now. He has *al-mulk*. Full ownership. Of the whole field within which your situation is unfolding.

And here is where the disproportion becomes almost unbearable: the same God who runs the orbit of the sun is also the One who can settle the question your heart is asking. **He has both.** The cosmic AND the qiṭmīr-sized. The orbit of the moon AND the outcome of your meeting tomorrow. It is the *same authority* underneath both.

So the question this ayah forces you to ask is not, "Do I believe Allah exists?" The Makkan believed He existed. The question is:

**When my qiṭmīr-sized worry comes up, do I turn toward Him, or do I turn toward something that cannot hold it?**

The practical shift this invites: the next time you notice your mind reaching for *a specific thing* to settle *a specific anxiety* — a refresh, a check, a text, a substance, a fantasy — pause. Name the thing in your mind. Then ask, honestly: *does this object actually possess what I am asking it to give me, or am I asking a qiṭmīr to carry the weight of an entire dominion?*

You will find, when you look closely, that you have been outsourcing infinite needs to finite objects. The relief of that recognition is enormous. **You finally know why nothing has been working.** You were asking the wrong owner.

### Theme Two: He Inserts, He Does Not Switch

Let me move to the other end of the ayah and stay there for a while.

We talked about *yūliju* — *He inserts.* Not *He switches.* Not *He alternates.* **He slides one inside the other.**

Now let me name something most of us know in our bodies but rarely in our theology.

When a hard season is happening to you, you experience it as total. **Totalizing.** As if there is no other state. As if the dark has always been here and will always be here. The pain you are in this week feels like a complete fact, not a phase. The grief feels seamless. The fear feels uninterruptible. The doubt feels permanent.

This is the part of the human nafs that cannot perceive gradients. We see only states. We see *day* or we see *night*. We don't see the boundary moving.

But the ayah is telling you that **the boundary is always moving.**

He inserts the night into the day, even at noon. He inserts the day into the night, even at midnight. The very darkest hour you have ever lived through was not pure night — it had a sliver of dawn already being inserted into it. Right then. While you couldn't see it. The whole physics of the universe Allah built is structured around **interpenetration**, not switches.

Stay with me on this, because it matters.

Why did Allah choose this verb? Why not just say *yu-ʿāqib bayna al-layli wa-l-nahār* — He alternates between night and day? That phrase exists in Arabic. That phrase exists elsewhere in the Quran. Here He didn't choose it. He chose **yūliju.** *Inserts.*

Because alternation would suggest that the two states are separate, that one ends and then another begins. **Insertion** says they are not separate at all. They are one continuous reality, with Allah moving the proportion. Always. Every moment.

Apply this to your life.

What you experience as "this season of difficulty" is not a closed compartment that will one day end so that "the season of ease" can begin. That is not how Allah works. The season of ease is **already being inserted into** the season of difficulty. Right now. Hour by hour. You are not waiting for the door to open. **The door has been ajar this whole time.** The light is already entering. You cannot see the boundary because the boundary is moving too slowly to perceive — but the same God who moves the line between night and day, hour by hour, across the entire visible sky, is moving the line through your life with the same precision.

[PAUSE]

This is why patience in the Quran is not the same as endurance.

Endurance is gritting your teeth and waiting for the night to end. Patience — *ṣabr* — in the Quranic sense is **the recognition that the day is already being inserted into your night.** You don't have to manufacture it. You don't have to escape the night to find it. The same God who runs the boundary in the sky is running it through your circumstance. *Yūliju.* He inserts. **Right now.**

And there is one more layer. *Yūliju* is in the *imperfect* tense — ongoing. *Sakhkhara* — the harnessing of sun and moon — is in the *perfect* tense, completed. Some things Allah has finished. Some things He is still doing. The cosmos is held in place by the **already-done.** Your present moment is held in place by the **still-being-done.** Both are His.

So when you feel like your life is in flux, that nothing is settled, that the ground keeps moving — read the verbs again. *Sakhkhara* is the floor under your feet. It is not in question. The sun's harness is a settled fact. The basic architecture of your existence — that you have a Lord, that you have a soul, that you have an appointed term — *sakhkhara*. Done. **It is the running on top of that floor that is in motion.** *Yajrī.* You're running. The day-night line is moving through your hours. But the floor is fixed.

The practical shift this invites: when you find yourself thinking *this will never end* or *nothing is moving*, return to this ayah's grammar. **He inserts. Right now. Ongoing.** The ease is being lodged inside the difficulty as you read this sentence. Your job is not to manufacture the dawn. Your job is to trust the *yūliju* — to trust that the boundary is moving even when you cannot perceive it. Then to ask Him directly, not the qiṭmīr-sized things you usually ask.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Look at the verse now as a whole, with everything we have unpacked in view.

The structure is climbing, and then suddenly inverting.

First climb — **the cosmic insertions:**
- The night being slid into the day, hour by hour.
- The day being slid into the night, hour by hour.

Second climb — **the harnessed luminaries:**
- The sun, sakhkhara, already bound to its track.
- The moon, sakhkhara, already bound to its track.
- Each *running* — yajrī — toward a named end.

Then the pivot — **the direct address:**
- *Dhālikumu Allāhu rabbukum* — That is Allah, your Lord. (Notice: the demonstrative is *dhālikum* with the second-person plural — *that, for all of you.* Allah turns from the cosmos and points at the listener.)
- *Lahu al-mulk* — To Him belongs the dominion. Fronted prepositional phrase, restrictive: **to Him alone.**

Then the collapse — **the contrast:**
- And those whom you call on besides Him — *mā yamlikūna min qiṭmīr.* They do not own a date-skin.

The whole verse is built on **the same root used twice**: م-ل-ك. *Al-mulk* for Allah. *Yamlikūna* for the false gods. The verse is a balance scale. On one side: the harnessed cosmos, the named appointed terms, ownership of all dominion. On the other side: not a qiṭmīr.

This is not Allah arguing that He is *bigger.* He is. But the rhetorical move is sharper than that. The verse exposes the **absurdity of the proportion**. You have placed the weight of your life on something with less mass than a peelable skin.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are saying the same thing from two directions.

From the words: *yūliju* tells us Allah inserts, doesn't switch. *Sakhkhara* tells us the architecture is settled. *Yajrī* tells us we are running on top of that architecture. *Qiṭmīr* tells us what we are *actually* trusting weighs nothing.

From the themes: every difficulty in your life has its relief already being inserted into it by the same God who is inserting dawn into your darkest night. And whatever you are turning toward instead of Him cannot hold a date-skin's worth of what you need.

Both directions arrive at the same place. **There is one Owner. Everything else is qiṭmīr.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When my heart contracts with anxiety, what specific object does my mind reach for to make it okay? If I named that object honestly, would I still call it "a qiṭmīr" — or would I have to admit I have been treating it as a god?

2. In what part of my current life am I demanding a clean switch — *let this season be over* — when Allah has only ever promised an *insertion*? Where am I refusing to trust the gradient?

3. The sun and the moon are *running for an appointed term*. So am I. If today's running has a *named* end, am I running it the way I would run it if I knew the name?

4. The verse uses the same root (م-ل-ك) for what Allah owns and what the false gods don't own. If I traced the root through my own life — what do I treat as if I own it? And of the things I treat as mine, which would survive a qiṭmīr test?

5. What would shift if, the very next time my heart reaches for a finite object to settle an infinite need, I paused and said the word *qiṭmīr* — and then turned, instead, to the One who inserts dawn into midnight?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The God who inserts night into day, harnesses the sun, and names the moon's last hour owns every dominion — and the things you are asking to save you don't own a date-skin's worth of what you need.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not let this ayah pass you as a sermon about idol-worshippers. The idol-worshippers in this ayah are doing exactly what you and I do — outsourcing infinite needs to finite holders. The stones they bowed to and the screens we check are the same gesture in different costumes.

The invitation is not to feel ashamed. It is to *recalibrate the weight*. Take the load you have been placing on the qiṭmīr-sized things in your life and notice that those things cannot hold it. They were never built to. Then turn the same load — the same weight, exactly — toward the One who holds the sun on its harness and inserts dawn into midnight.

You will find that He has been there the whole time, while you were checking the date-skin to see if it would carry you.

### Du'a

اللهم يا من يولج الليل في النهار، أَوْلِجْ يقينك في قلوبنا حتى لا نطلب عند سواك ما لا يملكه

*O Allah, the One who inserts night into day — insert Your certainty into our hearts, until we stop asking from anyone besides You what no one besides You owns.*

*O Allah, the One who harnessed the sun and the moon, harness our reaching. Pull our hearts off every qiṭmīr we have mistaken for a god, and bind them to the only Owner of the dominion.*

*And when the appointed term of our own running is named, let us be found turning toward You.*

آمين.
