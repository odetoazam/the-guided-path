# When It Comes: The Victory Spoken in the Past Tense

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with their sound before we touch their meaning.

> [ayah:110:1] إِذَا جَآءَ نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ وَٱلْفَتْحُ
>
> *Idhā jāʾa naṣru-llāhi wa-l-fatḥ*
>
> *When the help of Allah comes, and the victory —*

Five words. That's all. A conditional clause that doesn't even finish a thought — it leans forward, waiting for what comes next. *When* this happens... then what? The sentence is built like a held breath.

Now let me rebuild the room you need to be standing in for this to land.

Imagine twenty-three years. Twenty-three years that began with a man alone in a cave, shaking, wrapped in a cloak, telling his wife *cover me, cover me*. Twenty-three years of being called a liar by his own tribe. Of watching the weakest of his followers tortured on burning sand. Of burying children. Of fleeing the city of his birth in the dark of night with a price on his head. Of standing at Uḥud with blood on his face and a tooth knocked out. Of digging a trench around the city while tying stones to his stomach against the hunger.

And then — slowly, impossibly — the tide turns. The city that exiled him opens its gates without a battle. The people who hunted him stand before him as he enters, and he asks them: *What do you think I will do with you?* And he forgives. All of it. The whole arc of suffering bends, finally, toward a morning of total victory.

You would expect the verse that captures this moment to *roar*. You would expect trumpets. You would expect *We gave you the victory! Look what you have achieved! Rejoice!*

[PAUSE]

That is not what Allah sends down.

What He sends down is a quiet conditional clause — *when the help of Allah comes* — and the tradition tells us something that should stop us cold. This was among the **last** things revealed. Ibn ʿAbbās reported, in a narration preserved by Muslim, that Sūrat An-Naṣr was the last complete sūrah to come down. And when it came, some of the Companions celebrated — *victory! the mission is complete!* — but Ibn ʿAbbās, and ʿUmar with him, understood something else underneath the words. In the narration preserved by al-Bukhārī, Ibn ʿAbbās explains that this sūrah was an announcement of the Prophet's own approaching death. The victory and the farewell arrived in the **same** five words.

So here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. **How do you speak about the greatest triumph of a life, when that triumph is also the sign that the life is ending?** What kind of language can hold victory and departure at once, without flinching toward either celebration or grief?

The answer is hidden in the grammar. Let's go and find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Refuses to Say "If"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse.

In English, we have one word for the front of a conditional sentence: *if*. "If it rains, I'll stay home." That single word carries everything — the doubt, the maybe, the hedged bet.

Arabic refuses that poverty. Arabic has (at least) two doors into a conditional, and choosing between them is a theological act.

There is **إِنْ** (*in*) — and there is **إِذَا** (*idhā*).

*In* is the word of genuine uncertainty. *If* it happens — and it might not. "*In* you study, you may pass." The outcome hangs in doubt. You use *in* for the thing you cannot count on.

*Idhā* is different. *Idhā* is the word you use for what is coming whether you like it or not. You don't say "*in* the sun rises tomorrow." You say "*idhā* the sun rises" — *when* it rises. *Idhā* is reserved, in the deep grammar of the language, for the appointed, the certain, the scheduled. It is the *when* of sunrise, not the *if* of a gamble.

Now look at what Allah chose.

> **إِذَا** جَآءَ نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ
>
> ***When*** the help of Allah comes —

Not *in*. Not *if*. **Idhā.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the moment this was revealed. The Muslims were not yet in possession of Mecca when these words came. The victory was, by every worldly measure, still in the realm of the *not-yet*. The natural human word for a future you are hoping toward is *if* — *if* Allah grants us victory, *if* the doors open.

But Allah does not speak the language of hope. Hope is a posture for those who do not know the outcome. Allah knows the outcome. So He uses the word reserved for the sunrise. The victory is not a thing that *might* come. It is a thing on a schedule, as fixed as dawn.

Notice what this does to the believer standing in the uncertain middle. You are told, in the very grammar, that your striving is not a gamble. The outcome is not in doubt — only its **timing** is hidden from you. The *idhā* says: *it is coming. Your only question is when.*

What this single word reveals: **the believer's relationship to the promise of Allah is not the relationship of a gambler to a bet. It is the relationship of a watchman to the dawn.** And no watchman despairs that the sun will fail to rise. He only waits.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Future That Already Happened

Stay with me, because the grammar has a second gift, and it sits right beside the first.

Let me teach the concept first. In every language, we mark *time* on our verbs. *Comes. Came. Will come.* These tenses sort the world into past, present, and not-yet. When you want to talk about something that hasn't happened, you reach for the future — *it will come.* That is the honest tense for an unrealized event.

Now look at the verb Allah chose for the victory that had not yet occurred:

> إِذَا **جَآءَ** نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ
>
> When the help of Allah **came** —

**جَآءَ** — *jāʾa* — is the *perfect* tense. The completed tense. The past. Literally, *it came. It has come. Done.*

Read it plainly and it makes no sense. The conquest of Mecca had not happened. Why describe a future event with the verb of a finished one?

This is one of the most breathtaking devices in the entire Qur'an. The scholars of balāghah call it the **prophetic perfect** — *al-māḍī al-muḥaqqaq al-wuqūʿ* — the past tense of the absolutely-certain-to-occur. Allah speaks of a future event in the past tense **because, to the One who holds all of time, it has already happened.** Its occurrence is so guaranteed that the future tense would actually *understate* it. To say "it *will* come" leaves a sliver of distance, a not-yet. Allah closes the gap entirely: *it came.*

[PAUSE]

Let me make this real for you. Think of how you speak about something you are dreading versus something you are certain of. When your friend is in surgery, you say "*I hope* she'll be okay." The trembling future. But when you have already seen the surgeon walk out smiling, you say — with your whole chest — "*she's fine.*" Past tense. Settled. The verb itself carries your certainty.

Allah is speaking about the conquest of Mecca the way the surgeon's smile lets you speak about your friend. From inside human time, it is the terrifying future. From where Allah stands, **it is already done.** And He lends the believers His vantage point for a moment, by handing them the past tense for a thing they have not yet seen.

Now feel the two devices working together. The **idhā** tells you the victory is as certain as sunrise. The **jāʾa** tells you it is, in the divine reckoning, *already accomplished.* One word locks the certainty; the other collapses the distance. Between them, there is no room left for doubt to breathe.

This is what it means to trust a promise from Allah. You are not being asked to believe in something that *might* happen. You are being invited to live now, in the uncertain middle, on the strength of an event your Lord describes in the **past tense.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: Whose Help Is It? The Two Words That Erase the Hero

Now we arrive at the heart of the line, and here the grammar performs its quietest, most devastating move.

Two small structural choices. Watch them both.

**First — the construction نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ.**

In Arabic, when you place two nouns side by side like this — *naṣru-llāh*, the help *of Allah* — you form what is called **iḍāfa**, a possessive bond. It welds the two words together. The *naṣr* is not floating free; it belongs, by the grammar itself, to **Allah**. Not the help that *the Muslims achieved.* Not the victory *the army won.* **The help of Allah.** The grammar hands the entire triumph upward, away from every human hand that fought for it.

Think about who is being addressed here. The man who endured twenty-three years. The Companions who bled at Badr and Uḥud and the Trench. By every human measure, this victory was *earned* — earned in blood, in patience, in exile and hunger. If anyone in history had a claim to say *we did this*, it was them.

And the grammar removes the claim. Gently, completely. **نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ.** The help is His.

[PAUSE]

**Second — and don't miss this — وَٱلْفَتْحُ. *The* victory.**

The word *fatḥ* arrives with the definite article — *al-fatḥ*. Not *a* victory, one among many. **THE** victory. The one you already know. The famous one. The one every listener's heart leaps to without being told its name. The commentators — al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, and the rest — are unanimous: *al-fatḥ* here is the opening of Mecca.

But notice the word itself. *Fatḥ* does not first mean "conquest" the way a sword conquers. Its root, **ف-ت-ح**, means *to open.* A door. A lock. A closed thing made open. Mecca was not so much *taken* as it was *opened* — its gates swinging wide, almost without a battle, the way a door opens to someone who was always meant to come home.

And here is the lexical jewel that al-Qurṭubī hands us. He traces *naṣr* — the very word for "help" — back to a usage in the speech of the Arabs: *naṣara al-ghaythu al-arḍ* — **the rain came to the aid of the land.** When parched, cracked, dead earth is revived by water falling from above, the Arabs called that *naṣr.* The rain *helps* the land.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The image beneath *naṣr* is **rain.** And rain is the one thing the soil can never produce for itself. The earth cannot strive its way to water. It cannot earn the cloud. The land may be perfectly prepared — plowed, seeded, ready — and still it must *wait*, face turned upward, for what only the sky can send down.

That is the picture buried in the word. The Muslims were the prepared field. Twenty-three years of striving was the plowing and the planting. But the *naṣr* — the thing that finally made the dead land of Mecca bloom green — that came **down.** From above. From Allah. Sent, not seized.

This is the *istidlāl* the verse is making through a physical image: victory is not a thing you *take* like a fortress. It is a thing that *descends* on you like rain on a field you could never have watered yourself. And the only honest response of the soil, when the rain comes, is not pride. It is **gratitude turned upward.**

Which is *exactly* where the next two verses of the sūrah go — to *tasbīḥ* and *istighfār*, glorification and the seeking of forgiveness. But that is for another sitting. For now, hold this:

**The grammar of this verse spends every device it has — the certainty of *idhā*, the doneness of *jāʾa*, the upward bond of *iḍāfa*, the rain hidden in *naṣr* — to make absolutely sure that when the victory finally comes, no human being mistakes it for their own.**

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Living on a Promise Written in the Past Tense

Let me begin where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes not from work, but from **waiting**. You have done the right thing for a long time. You have prayed, you have been patient, you have held the line — and the result has not come. The marriage you're praying for. The healing. The child. The relief from the debt. The day your effort finally *means* something. And the cruelest part is not the work. It's the **uncertainty.** The voice that whispers: *what if it never comes? What if all of this was for nothing?*

We live, most of us, in the trembling future tense. *Maybe. If. We'll see.*

Now hear what this verse does to that voice.

Allah did not say to the believers, in their uncertain middle, *if* the help comes. He said *idhā* — **when.** He did not say *the help will come.* He said *jāʾa* — **it came.** He reached into the future they were straining toward and handed it back to them as a *finished fact.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to understand. The believer is asked to do something that, from the outside, looks impossible: to find rest **before** the resolution arrives. To carry the calm of an ending in the middle of the story. And the verse shows us how this is even possible — not through wishful thinking, not through "positive vibes," but through a specific relationship to a specific Promiser.

Your striving is not a gamble whose outcome is unknown. The outcome of every sincere effort placed in Allah's hands is *known* — it is written, in His knowledge, in the **past tense.** What is hidden from you is only the *timing*. The *idhā* hides the *when*, never the *whether*.

Think again of the watchman waiting for dawn. He stands in total darkness. By the evidence of his eyes, the night is permanent — there has never been more darkness than right before the sun. But he does not despair. Why? Because the darkness is not evidence against the dawn. **The darkness is the appointed place where dawns are waited for.** His certainty does not come from the horizon. It comes from his knowledge of how the world is ordered by the One who set the sun on its course.

This is the shift the verse asks of you. Stop reading your darkness as evidence. The absence of the result is not proof that it isn't coming. It is simply the *idhā* — the not-yet-arrived *when* — of something already written as *jāʾa*, already done.

**What changes tomorrow:** The next time you are in the long middle of a sincere effort and the despair-voice asks *what if it never comes*, answer it in the grammar of this verse. Not *I hope it comes* — that is the trembling *in*. But: *it is coming, the way dawn comes to a watchman; my only work is to keep my post until the light I have been promised arrives.* Then go back to your post. That is the whole of the believer's labor: not to manufacture the dawn, but to keep watch faithfully until it breaks.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Trap at the Top of the Mountain

Now let me take you somewhere else entirely — to the most dangerous place a human soul can stand.

Not the valley of failure. The **summit of success.**

We assume the soul is most in danger when it is suffering. But watch yourself honestly. When you fail, you turn to Allah — failure drives you to your knees, and the knees are a safe place to be. The truly perilous moment is the one *right after the victory.* The moment the deal closes. The moment the diagnosis comes back clear. The moment the thing you worked years for finally lands in your hands. In *that* moment, something in the heart rises up and says one small, poisonous sentence:

***I did this.***

[PAUSE]

This is the oldest spiritual disease there is. It is the exact sentence that ruined Iblīs — *I am better, I was made from fire.* It is the sentence of Qārūn, who was given treasure and said, *I was only given this because of knowledge I have.* It is the sentence that turns a blessing into a curse, because the moment you say *I did this*, you have quietly removed Allah from your story and installed yourself at the center.

And it is *strongest* at the summit. Suffering humbles; success inflates. The higher the victory, the louder the *I*.

Now look at where this verse lands the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every one of us.

He is at the *absolute peak.* The single greatest day of vindication in his entire mission. The city that broke his heart is opening its gates. If ever a human being had earned the right to feel the swell of *I did this* — to feel the twenty-three years and say *look what I built* — it is this man, on this day.

And the verse, at that exact summit, hands him not a trophy but a **redirection.** *Idhā jāʾa* — when there comes — *naṣru-llāh* — the help **of Allah.** Not your help. His. The grammar reaches into the most triumphant moment of a human life and quietly turns the heart's face upward, before the *I* can even form on the lips.

[PAUSE]

And remember the rain. *Naṣara al-ghaythu al-arḍ* — the rain helped the land. The field, when the harvest finally comes in golden and full, has a choice about what story to tell. It can say *look what I grew.* Or it can remember that it never made a single drop of the rain that saved it. The field did real work — it was plowed, it held the seed, it endured the drought. None of that is erased. But the field did not water itself. **When the green finally comes, the honest field looks up.**

This is the antidote the verse is placing in your hands for the most dangerous moment of your life. Not *don't succeed.* Not *don't strive.* But: **when the success comes, catch the *I* before it forms, and look up.** Attribute the rain to the sky. This is precisely why the very next breath of the sūrah is *fa-sabbiḥ* — *so glorify* — and *wa-staghfir-hu* — *and seek His forgiveness.* At the summit, where every other soul plants a flag of *I*, the believer is taught to glorify Allah and to ask forgiveness. Forgiveness — at the moment of *victory.* Because the believer knows that even in his triumph, his hand was not as clean, his striving not as pure, as the One who sent the rain.

**What changes tomorrow:** Pick the next small victory that comes your way this week — and one will come, even a tiny one: a task completed, a problem solved, a kind word that landed, a prayer answered. At the very instant you feel the warm rise of *I did that*, train yourself to complete the sentence the way this verse does: *...by the help of Allah.* Say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* out loud and mean it as a **correction**, not a reflex — as the field looking up at the sky that sent the rain. Build this muscle on the small summits, so that when the great summit comes, your heart already knows which way to turn its face.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found, and watch how the two paths arrive at the same door.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a victory described as *certain* (idhā), *already accomplished* (jāʾa), *owned by Allah* (the iḍāfa of naṣru-llāh), and *sent down like rain* (the lexical root of naṣr). Four devices, one purpose: to make the victory both **unshakably sure** and **entirely His.**

The **thematic journey** showed us two human beings inside that same verse. The one in the *valley*, waiting, exhausted, tempted to despair — and told: *it is coming, in the past tense, keep your post.* And the one on the *summit*, victorious, tempted to claim it — and told: *look up, the rain was never yours.*

And here is where the two become one. **The same grammar that comforts the waiting soul humbles the triumphant one.** *Naṣru-llāh* — "the help *of Allah*" — is at once the most *reassuring* phrase in the world (because if the help is His, it cannot fail) and the most *humbling* (because if the help is His, it was never yours to boast of). The very words that say *you can count on it* also say *you cannot claim it.* Certainty and humility, it turns out, are carved from the same five words. To truly trust that the victory is Allah's is, in the same breath, to know it was never your own.

### The Architecture of a Farewell

And now, having done the work, we can finally see why the tradition heard a *farewell* in a verse of *victory.*

A whole life's mission, arriving at its summit — and at that summit, the language turns the Prophet's heart away from himself and toward his Lord, toward glorification and the seeking of forgiveness. That is not the posture of a man *beginning* something. It is the posture of a man **completing** something, and getting ready to return. The victory was the sign that the work was *done.* And a worker whose work is done is a worker about to go home. Ibn ʿAbbās did not read grief into a verse of joy out of pessimism. He read it because he understood the grammar of *completion.* When Allah hands you the whole harvest, it is because the season is ending.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I living in the trembling *if* — when Allah may be inviting me to live in the certain *when*?
2. What victory am I secretly calling *mine* — and what would change if I returned it, fully, to the One who sent it down like rain?
3. When the result I am praying for is delayed, do I read the darkness as evidence against the dawn — or as the appointed place where dawns are awaited?
4. Do I turn to Allah *more* after my failures or after my successes — and what does my honest answer reveal about where my heart locates the *I*?
5. If the arrival of my greatest victory were also the sign that my work here was nearly done — would I have lived these days any differently?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**At the summit of every triumph, Allah's grammar is already turning your face upward — because the victory was always coming, and it was never yours.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to wait for a conquest of Mecca to live inside this verse. Every life has its small summits and its long valleys. The invitation is simply this: in the valley, trade your trembling *if* for the watchman's *when* — and keep your post. And on the summit, catch the *I* before it forms, and look up like a field that knows it never made the rain. Let the same five words steady you when you are waiting and humble you when you have arrived. That is not two practices. It is one heart, oriented one way: toward the One whose help it always was.

### A Closing Du'ā

*O Allah, the *naṣr* is Yours and the *fatḥ* is Yours — never ours. Teach our hearts to wait for Your promise the way the watchman waits for dawn, certain it is coming, faithful at our post. And when You send Your help down upon us like rain on dead land, keep the word "I" from our lips, and turn our faces upward in glory and gratitude to You. Let us not be told of our victory only when our work is done — but let us live every day already turned toward home.* Āmīn.
