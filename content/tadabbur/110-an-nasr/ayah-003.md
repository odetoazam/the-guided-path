# The Summit Is for Seeking Forgiveness

### Surah An-Nasr, Ayah 3

> فَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ وَٱسْتَغْفِرْهُ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ تَوَّابًۭا
>
> *Fa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika wa-staghfirhu — innahu kāna tawwābā.*
>
> "So glorify your Lord with His praise, and seek His forgiveness. Indeed, He has always been the One who turns back, again and again, in mercy."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World You Have to Stand Inside

Before we touch a single word of this ayah, I want you to stand somewhere with me.

Picture the longest road a person ever walked. Twenty-three years. It began with one man, alone, in a cave on the edge of a city that would soon try to kill him. It passed through ridicule, through a boycott that starved his family, through the loss of the wife who believed in him first and the uncle who shielded him. It passed through a night flight out of his own city, through the graves of Uhud, through a trench dug in frozen panic around a town under siege. Twenty-three years of being called a liar, a madman, a sorcerer, by the very people he was sent to save.

And then — the road arrives.

The Prophet ﷺ returns to Mecca. The city that expelled him opens its gates. There is almost no blood. The man who was driven out as a fugitive walks back in as the one to whom the whole peninsula will turn. And then something happens that he had longed for across two decades: the tribes of Arabia begin to arrive. Not in trickles. In **crowds**. Delegation after delegation, streaming in to enter the religion of Allah — أَفْوَاجًا, in waves.

This is the moment the surah describes. النصر — the help. الفتح — the opening. الناس... أفواجا — the people, in crowds.

Now. Put yourself inside the heart of a human being at that exact instant. The mission of your entire life has just succeeded beyond anything you dared imagine. Every betrayal is behind you. The scoreboard is final and you have won.

What does a human heart want to do here?

It wants to **savor**. It wants to look back at everyone who doubted and feel the warmth of vindication. It wants to plant a flag on the summit and breathe.

[PAUSE]

And here is where Allah speaks. At the precise summit of the most successful life ever lived, the command that comes down is not *rest*. It is not *celebrate*. It is not even *give thanks and move on*.

It is: **glorify, praise, and ask Me to forgive you.**

Ask forgiveness — at the peak.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows: *What does a person at the absolute summit of victory have to seek forgiveness for?* Hold that question. Do not let me answer it too early. Because the answer is going to rearrange how you understand success, endings, and the strange direction that a heart is supposed to face when everything finally goes right.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: سَبِّحْ — The Word That Pushes God Away From Every Flaw

Let me teach you something before we open the word.

In Arabic, there is a family of word-shapes called *forms* — patterns you pour a root into, and the pattern itself changes the meaning. Take a simple root and you have a plain action. Pour it into the second form — double the middle letter — and very often the meaning becomes **intensive**: to do something thoroughly, repeatedly, to *declare* it, to *make* it so.

Think of it in English-ish terms. There's *to be clean*. And then there's *to cleanse* — to actively, deliberately purify something, scrubbing until nothing impure remains. That extra muscle, that deliberateness — that's the flavor the second form adds.

Now the word.

> فَسَبِّحْ — *fa-sabbiḥ.*

We translate it softly: "glorify." But sit with the root. The root س-ب-ح (s-b-ḥ) in its plain sense describes something that **swims, glides, moves swiftly and far through a medium** — a swimmer cutting through water, a star moving through its orbit. The Quran uses it that way elsewhere: the celestial bodies that *swim* (يَسْبَحُونَ).

So what is *tasbīḥ*, the second-form word built on "to glide far away"?

It is the act of **declaring Allah far** — moving Him, in your conception, far away from every flaw, every deficiency, every limitation, every unworthy thought you could possibly attach to Him. Tasbīḥ is not first a song of praise. It is first a *cleansing*. It is your tongue and your heart sweeping away anything that does not befit Him — and pushing Him, in your awareness, beyond the reach of all of it.

Notice something. The form is the intensive, declarative form. This is not "feel that God is great." It is **declare it. Pronounce it. Make the statement and let it stand.** سُبْحَانَ — *how utterly far He is* from what they ascribe to Him.

Now feel why this word — *this* word — arrives at the summit of victory.

Stay with me. When a human being wins, the most dangerous thing that can happen is not in the world outside. It is a quiet shift inside. A subtle creep of *I*. I did this. I endured. I deserve. The self begins, almost without permission, to swell into the space that the victory opened up.

And the very first word Allah sends down into that swelling space is the word that pushes the self **out** and pushes Him **far**. سَبِّحْ. Before you say a word about what *you* accomplished — declare Him free of every imperfection, and in the same breath you have declared that the perfection was never yours.

[PAUSE]

The word that means "glide far away" is given to the man at the top of the mountain. Because the top of the mountain is exactly where a soul can forget how far God is above the one who climbed.

## Element Two: بِحَمْدِ — The Two Wings of How a Heart Approaches God

Here is a piece of architecture so quiet you could read past it a thousand times.

> فَسَبِّحْ **بِحَمْدِ** رَبِّكَ — "so glorify your Lord **with His praise.**"

That little particle, the بِ at the front of *bi-ḥamdi*, is the *bā'* of accompaniment. It means *along with*, *carrying*, *together with*. So the instruction is not merely *glorify*. It is *glorify carrying praise* — do the two things fused into one motion.

Why does that matter? Because *tasbīḥ* and *ḥamd* are two opposite directions, and Allah just told you to face both at once.

Let me show you the two directions.

**Tasbīḥ** — as we just saw — is **negation**. It is *removing*. It declares what Allah is **not**: not deficient, not limited, not like anything, not in need. It is the heart saying *no* — no flaw touches Him.

**Ḥamd** — praise — is the opposite motion. It is **affirmation**. It declares what Allah **is**: the source of every good, every perfection, every gift, worthy of love for who He is and gratitude for what He gives. It is the heart saying *yes* — all goodness is His.

Now look at what you've been handed. The complete posture of a heart before God is **both wings at once**: pushing away everything unworthy of Him *and* affirming everything true of Him. *No* and *yes*, in a single breath. تَنْزِيه and *thanā'*. Transcendence and intimacy. You hold Him far above all flaw — and you draw near to Him in love and thanks.

A bird does not fly on one wing.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's remarkable about the placement. Imagine you had only been told *ḥamd* — only praise — at the moment of victory. Pure celebration could curdle. Praise alone, at the summit, can slide so easily into self-congratulation: *thank God for what a good job I did.* The *tasbīḥ* is the corrective wing. It keeps the praise clean. It says: *yes, affirm all the good — but first declare that none of the deficiency, none of the smallness, none of the ego, belongs anywhere near Him or near how you understand this gift.*

And imagine you had only been told *tasbīḥ* — only transcendence. That can drift the other way, into a cold, distant God you exalt but do not love. The *ḥamd* is *that* corrective wing. It pulls the exalted One close again — *and He is the source of every sweetness you have ever tasted.*

So the two words, fused by that one little بِ, give you the whole of how a servant stands before his Lord: **far enough to revere Him, near enough to love Him.** At the exact moment your heart was most at risk of standing wrong, you are taught to stand right.

## Element Three: وَٱسْتَغْفِرْهُ — Seeking Forgiveness on the Day You Won

Now we arrive at the word that should stop you cold.

Let me first teach the shape. There is a form in Arabic — the tenth form, the *istifʿāl* pattern — and its core meaning is **to seek, to request, to ask for** the thing the root names. Take the root for "forgiveness," غ-ف-ر, and pour it into this seeking-pattern, and you get *istighfār*: **to actively seek to be covered, to ask for forgiveness.**

And the root itself, غ-ف-ر, is beautiful. Its base sense is **to cover, to veil, to wrap over.** The Arabs called the helmet *al-mighfar* — the thing that covers and protects the head in battle. So *maghfira* — forgiveness — is not merely the erasing of a sin. It is Allah throwing a **covering** over it: shielding you from its consequence, veiling it from exposure, protecting you from what it earned.

> وَٱسْتَغْفِرْهُ — "and seek His covering. Ask Him to forgive."

Now. Feel the placement, because this is the heart of the whole surah.

This command does not come after a sin. It does not come after a defeat, a failure, a moment of shame where seeking forgiveness would feel natural and obvious. It comes after **victory**. After the conquest. After the crowds streaming in. At the highest, cleanest, most triumphant moment of the most sinless human life.

And there is something the Arabic does that I need you to notice — an **absence**. Read the word again: *wa-staghfirhu* — "and seek His forgiveness." Seek forgiveness **for what?**

The verse names no object. No sin is specified. No fault is pointed to. The grammar leaves the space deliberately, conspicuously **empty.**

[PAUSE]

Stay in that empty space, because it is doing enormous work.

If the verse had said "seek forgiveness for *this particular wrong*," it would be a verse about a moment. A one-time correction. Instead, by leaving the object unspecified, the instruction becomes total and permanent. It is no longer *atone for X*. It becomes a **posture** — the posture of a servant who, even at his most victorious, even when no fault can be named, stands before his Lord with his palms open asking to be covered.

Why would the victor do that?

The classical commentators open several windows onto this, and each is a camera angle on one reality. One reading — reported from Ibn ʿAbbās — is that *sabbiḥ* here means *pray*, and the instruction is to abound in worship now that the goal is reached. Another reading sits in the *istighfār* itself: that no human effort, however magnificent, is free of shortfall — and so even your worship, even your striving across twenty-three years, even *this very act of thanking Him*, falls short of what He deserves, and needs covering. You climbed the mountain — and the climbing itself was imperfect, and you ask Him to veil its imperfection.

And there is the deepest reading of all, which we will come to fully in a moment: that the command to seek forgiveness *at the summit* was understood by the most insightful of the Companions as a sign that the summit was also the **ending** — that when a believer's work is complete, the soul is turned to face its Lord and ask to be covered before the meeting.

But hold all of that. For now, simply let the structure land: **the place to seek forgiveness is not only the valley of your failure. It is also the summit of your success.** The empty object in *wa-staghfirhu* is not empty because there is nothing to ask for. It is empty because there is *always* something to ask for — even here, especially here.

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words have built, in sequence, at the peak of a life's triumph:

**سَبِّحْ** — push the self out and push Him far above every flaw.
**بِحَمْدِ** — and in the same motion, affirm that every good was His and draw near in love.
**وَٱسْتَغْفِرْهُ** — and then, standing at the very top, lower your palms and ask to be covered.

Glorify. Praise. Seek forgiveness. That is the entire interior architecture of a soul at the summit. Not a flag planted. A forehead lowered.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Summit Is the Most Dangerous Ground

Let me begin where you already live.

There is an experience almost everyone has had, in some scale. You wanted something badly. You worked, you waited, you sacrificed. The exam, the job, the wedding, the diagnosis that came back clear, the project you shipped, the goal you finally reached after years. And then — you got it.

And do you remember the strange thing that happened next?

For a moment, yes, there was joy. And then, often within days, sometimes within hours, a quiet hollowness crept in. Psychologists have a name for it — the *arrival fallacy*. We believe the summit will hold a fullness that, when we actually reach it, isn't there. The mountain we climbed for years turns out to be a small flat patch of rock, and a cold wind, and the disorienting question: *is this it?*

Why does the summit feel so empty so fast?

Here is one answer the Quran presses on us. The summit is empty when we expected the **summit itself** to fill us — when the achievement was supposed to be the destination. And it never can be, because no created thing was built to be a destination. It was built to be a **sign pointing past itself.**

Now watch what this ayah does to the summit.

The Prophet ﷺ reaches the highest victory in human history — and Allah does not let him stand on it as a destination at all. The instant the foot touches the peak, the soul is **turned around** to face the only true destination: *fa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika*. Glorify your *Lord*. The victory was never the top of the mountain. **Allah** is the top of the mountain, and the victory was just the last switchback in the path to Him.

[PAUSE]

Feel what that does to the hollowness.

The arrival fallacy says: you reached the thing and it was empty, so despair. The ayah says: you reached the thing and of course it was empty *as a destination* — because it was never the destination. Turn from it. Face Him. And the emptiness fills, because now you are standing in front of the One every gift was pointing to all along.

But there is a sharper edge here, and I don't want to soften it.

The summit is not merely *empty*. The summit is **dangerous**. It is the single most spiritually perilous ground a human being ever stands on — more dangerous than failure. Because failure keeps you humble; failure drives you to your knees and to your Lord almost automatically. But success? Success whispers. Success is when the ego, which knew to stay quiet during the hard climb, suddenly finds room to stretch. *Look what I did. Look who doubted me. Look what I deserve now.* The crowds are streaming in — أفواجا — and the self begins to believe the crowds came for **it**.

This is why the command at the summit is *sabbiḥ* and *istaghfir* — the two words that most decisively dethrone the self. Declare Him far above all flaw; admit your own need for covering. The most successful man who ever lived is taught, at his most successful moment, to do the two things that make it impossible for the ego to claim the victory.

So here is the practical edge — the thing that should change about tomorrow.

We have it exactly backwards. We reach for *istighfār* in our valleys, when we've sinned and we feel low, and we forget it entirely on our good days. But your good days are when you need it most. **The day you succeed is the day to seek forgiveness** — not because you did something wrong, but because success is the moment your heart is in the most danger of going wrong.

Tomorrow, when something goes *right* — a win at work, a kindness praised, a goal reached — let that be your cue. Not to celebrate first. To turn first. *Subḥāna-llāhi wa bi-ḥamdih, astaghfiru-llāh.* Push the self out, push Him far, draw near in thanks, and ask to be covered. Let your victories drive you to Him the way you usually only let your failures do. That single reflex, built into your good days, is the whole teaching of this ayah lived out.

## Theme Two: تَوَّابًا — The One Who Was Always Already Turning Toward You

Now I want to take you to the last word, because it changes the emotional color of everything before it.

After the command — glorify, praise, seek forgiveness — comes the reason. The *because*. And the *because* is a single name of Allah:

> إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ تَوَّابًۭا — "Indeed, He has always been *tawwāb*."

Let me build this word for you, because it is extraordinary.

The root is ت-و-ب (t-w-b). Its core meaning is **to return, to turn back, to come back to a place.** *Tawba* — what we translate as "repentance" — is, at its root, simply **turning back.** The sinner who repents is one who *turned back* toward Allah.

But here is the thing almost everyone misses: in the Quran, this turning-back goes in **both directions.** The servant turns back to Allah — that's our *tawba*. And Allah turns back to the servant — *that's* His *tawba* upon us. The same root. The same motion, from both sides. We turn to Him; He turns to us.

Now feel the form of the word. *Tawwāb* is not the plain "one who turns." Arabic has a special shape for **intensity and repetition** — the *faʿʿāl* pattern, with that doubled, drumming middle letter. It is the shape you use for someone who does a thing *constantly, habitually, overwhelmingly, again and again.* The *khabbāz* is not someone who once baked bread; he is the baker — bread is what he *does*. The *ghaffār* is not someone who once forgave; forgiving is His very occupation.

So *tawwāb* is not "the One who turns back once." It is **the One who turns back, and turns back, and turns back** — endlessly, habitually, as His very nature. You turn away a hundred times; a hundred times — and the hundred-and-first — He is already turning toward you.

[PAUSE]

And now the word that I almost cannot speak without trembling: كَانَ — *kāna*.

It's the past-tense verb "to be," and it sits right before *tawwāb*. *Innahu* **kāna** *tawwābā* — "Indeed He **has [always] been** the One who turns back, again and again."

Why the past tense? Why "has been"?

Because this *kāna*, in the divine context, points to something with no beginning. It is not saying "He became forgiving when you asked." It is saying His turning-toward-you is **eternal, permanent, established before you ever existed.** Before you climbed the mountain. Before you sinned. Before the first delegation arrived in crowds. Before time itself, He *was* — and is, and always will be — the One who turns back toward His servants.

Stay with what this means at the summit.

You have just been told: at the peak of your victory, seek My forgiveness. And a part of the heart flinches — *will He receive me, here, now, with my flawed worship and my swelling self?* And the ayah closes the door on that fear before it can finish forming. *Innahu kāna tawwābā.* Do not wonder whether He will turn to you. **He was already turning before you turned.** Your *istighfār* is not a knock on a closed door, hoping it opens. It is you finally facing a door that has stood open from before the beginning of time, with the Host already leaning toward you, waiting.

Let me bring this all the way into your chest with one picture.

You know the experience of having drifted from someone you love. The longer you stay away, the higher the wall feels. You imagine the awkwardness, the cold reception, the *where have you been.* The wall is built almost entirely of your own fear of how they'll receive you. And then one day you finally go — and they are not cold at all. They were waiting. They had been waiting the whole time. The wall was never on their side. It was only ever in your imagination of them.

*Tawwāb* is Allah telling you: **the wall was never on My side.** It never was. *Kāna* — I have always, eternally, been turned toward you, again and again and again. The only distance was the one you imagined.

Here is the practical weight of it. The reason we delay returning to Allah — after a sin, after a season of neglect, after years away — is almost never that we think He *can't* forgive. It's that some quiet voice says *not yet, not like this, clean yourself up first, He's tired of you.* This ayah, sealed with *kāna tawwābā*, dismantles that voice at the root. He does not get tired. The doubled letter says He turns back **habitually, by nature, without limit.** The *kāna* says He was doing it before you were born. So the turn you've been postponing — there is nothing on His side to wait for. Make it. Tonight. He has been facing you the entire time.

[PAUSE]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole surah breathe, because its shape is the proof of its meaning.

An-Nasr moves in two halves. The first two ayāt are pure description of triumph: the help of Allah arrives, the conquest comes, and the people enter the religion of Allah **in crowds.** Up, up, up — the arc rises to the absolute peak of worldly success.

And then the third ayah — our ayah — does not continue *up*. It turns the entire motion **inward and downward**, not in defeat, but in worship. *Glorify. Praise. Seek forgiveness.* The surah climbs to the summit of victory and then, at the top, **bows.**

That is the architecture. A surah about the greatest victory in history teaches you that the correct response to victory is not to stand taller but to bow lower. The structure *is* the lesson: the higher the climb, the deeper the prostration.

And the Companions saw something even more in this turn — and here I want to be careful with the sources. It is reported in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, in the narration of Ibn ʿAbbās, that ʿUmar would bring him into the council of the elders of Badr, and one day asked them all what they understood from this surah. Some said it commands us to praise Allah and seek His forgiveness when His help and victory come. But Ibn ʿAbbās said: it is the announcement of the term of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ — *Allah was telling him his time was near.* And ʿUmar said: "I do not know of it except what you know." When your work is complete, when the mission has reached its summit and the crowds are streaming in — that completion is itself the sign that the meeting with your Lord is near. So prepare for Him: glorify, praise, and seek to be covered. And we know — reported in the authentic narration of ʿĀ'isha — that after this surah came down, the Prophet ﷺ would frequently say in his bowing and prostration: *Subḥānaka-llāhumma rabbanā wa bi-ḥamdik, allāhumma-ghfir lī* — living this very ayah in his prayer until he met his Lord.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes are the same insight, seen from two directions.

The **words** taught us a posture: push the self out (*sabbiḥ*), affirm Him in love (*bi-ḥamd*), lower the palms and ask to be covered (*istaghfir*) — all sealed by the One who was eternally already turning toward you (*kāna tawwābā*).

The **themes** taught us *when* that posture matters most: at the summit, where the soul is in the greatest danger and the ego is most likely to seize the victory — and the assurance that the moment you turn, you find Him already turned toward you, as He has been from before time began.

One is the *how*. The other is the *when* and the *why*. Together they say one thing: **the proof that you understood your victory is that it bent your neck instead of lifting your chin.**

## Questions to Carry

- When something finally goes *right* in my life — what does my heart reach for first? Celebration, vindication, the eyes of those who doubted me — or my Lord?
- I run to *istighfār* on my worst days. Why have I never thought to seek forgiveness on my best ones?
- Is there a return to Allah I have been postponing — and is the thing holding me back something on *His* side, or only a wall I built in my own imagination of how He'll receive me?
- What "summit" am I standing on right now without realizing it is also a sign to prepare to meet Him?
- If *kāna tawwābā* means He has been turned toward me this entire time — what, exactly, am I waiting for?

## One-Sentence Distillation

At the summit of your greatest victory, the truest thing you can do is push the self aside, declare Him far above all flaw and near in all love, and lower your palms to seek the covering of a Lord who has been turned toward you, again and again, since before you ever existed.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to wait for a sin to seek forgiveness, and you do not have to wait for a valley to turn back to Allah. Let this ayah rewire one reflex: when life goes *right*, turn first. Let your victories bow you the way your failures do. And when you finally turn — after the success, or after the long drift, or after years — turn without the dread, because there is no wall on His side. He was *tawwāb* before the worlds were made. He is leaning toward you as you read this.

## A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, *Subḥānaka wa bi-ḥamdik* — we declare You far above every flaw, and we praise You as the source of every good we have ever been given. We have failed even in our thanking of You, so cover us, *ighfir lanā*. And since You have **always** been *Tawwāb* — the One who turns to us again and again — turn us toward You now, at our heights and in our depths, and keep us turned toward You until the day we meet You. Āmīn.
