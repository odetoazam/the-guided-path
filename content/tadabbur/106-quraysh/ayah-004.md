# The Two Hungers
### A Reflection on Surah Quraysh, Verse 4

> ٱلَّذِىٓ أَطْعَمَهُم مِّن جُوعٍ وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنْ خَوْفٍۭ
>
> *alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa-āmanahum min khawf*
>
> **"The One who fed them against hunger, and gave them security against fear."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Before we open this verse, I want you to stand in a particular place at a particular time.

You are standing in a valley with no river. No crops grow here. The Quran itself will later describe this valley through the mouth of the Prophet Ibrāhīm: *"a valley without cultivation"* — بِوَادٍ غَيْرِ ذِي زَرْعٍ. Nothing grows. The mountains around you are bare rock. There is a stone structure at the center of the valley — the House, the Kaʿbah — and around that House, a town has grown up. Mecca.

Now ask yourself the obvious question: **how does anyone survive here?**

There is no agriculture. There is no natural abundance. A town in a barren valley should not exist. And yet it does, and not just barely — it thrives. People come from across Arabia. Caravans arrive and depart. There is wealth here.

How?

The answer is in the verses just before this one. The people of this town, Quraysh, had two great journeys — *rihlata-sh-shitāʾi wa-ṣ-ṣayf* — the journey of winter and the journey of summer. South to Yemen when the cold came, north to Syria when the warmth returned. Two caravans a year, carrying trade, returning with food. That is how a town in a valley with no crops eats.

But here is what I want you to feel before we go further. **Those journeys were terrifying.**

The Arabian desert between Mecca and Syria was not a highway. It was raided. Tribes preyed on tribes. The classical commentator al-Qurtubi preserves the words of Ibn Zayd, who said: *"The Arabs used to raid one another, and capture one another."* A caravan crossing open desert was a slow-moving target — months on the road, loaded with goods, vulnerable every single night.

So picture the life of these people honestly. Two things should have destroyed them:

The **hunger** of a barren valley.

And the **fear** of a deadly road.

Two emptinesses. One in the stomach, one in the heart. And yet — they ate, and they traveled, and they came home. Generation after generation.

[PAUSE]

The surah has just commanded them, in the verse before: *"So let them worship the Lord of this House."* Worship Him. And the human heart, even when commanded, quietly asks: *why Him?* Why this Lord and not the three hundred and sixty idols standing around the very House you just named?

This final verse is the answer. It does not argue. It does not threaten. It simply describes the Lord by what He has *done* — and in seven words, it names the two exact places where these people were most broken, and tells them: that was Me.

Here is the hook. I want you to notice something and hold the question through everything that follows. Allah does not say He gave them *food*. He does not say He gave them *safety*. He says something stranger, something with a small word in it — مِّن, *min*, "against" — that turns this entire verse from a list of gifts into something far more intimate. Stay with that small word. We are going to spend a long time on it, because it is doing more work than almost any word in the surah.

Let us begin.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Twin Verbs — أَطْعَمَ and ءَامَنَ

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse.

In Arabic, verbs come in *forms* — patterns laid over a three-letter root that change the meaning in predictable ways. The simplest form just states a bare action. But there is a form, called **Form IV**, built on the pattern *afʿala*, which takes a root and makes someone *do* it *to* someone else. It reaches out. It is a verb with a hand extended toward an object.

Think of the difference in English between "to be safe" and "to make someone safe." The first is a state. The second is an act done *to* a person by an actor. Form IV is that second thing — an actor, deliberately, reaching out to change the condition of another.

Now look at our verse. There are two verbs in it, and I want you to see them side by side:

**أَطْعَمَهُم** — *aṭʿama-hum* — "He fed them."

**وَءَامَنَهُم** — *wa-āmana-hum* — "and He secured them."

Both are Form IV. Both are perfect tense — completed, done, accomplished, not hoped-for. And look at the endings: *-hum*, *-hum*. "Them." "Them." Each verb has the people attached directly to it, with no gap, no intermediary, no preposition standing between the action and the people.

Let's sit with this structure, because the shape itself is the meaning.

Notice what is *not* here. Allah does not say "food came to them." He does not say "safety was available to them." He does not say "the caravans provided for them." Every one of those would have placed something — a circumstance, a system, a cause — between Allah and the people. Instead, twice, the verb reaches directly from the Actor to the people. *He* fed *them*. *He* secured *them*. The hand of provision and the hand of protection both come from the same source, with nothing in between.

Here's what's remarkable. The people of Quraysh would have explained their survival through *means*. We eat because of the caravans. We are safe because of the sanctuary, the Ḥaram, the respect tribes gave to people of the House. Both true! The commentators mention both — al-Baghawi notes the food came *"by bringing the grain-supply to Mecca,"* and the security came *"through the sanctuary."* The means were real.

But the verse lifts the eye above the means to the One behind them. The caravan is a hand. The sanctuary is a hand. But whose hand moves the hand? *aṭʿama-hum.* *He* fed them. [PAUSE]

And now notice something you might walk right past. The second verb — **ءَامَنَ**, *āmana*, "He secured them" — comes from the root **ʾ-m-n**, the root *Amn*.

Say the related words slowly. *Amn* — security. *Amān* — safety, a guarantee of protection. *Amāna* — a trust. And **ʾīmān** — *faith itself.*

They are all one root.

In Arabic, *to believe* and *to be secure* grow from the same three letters. The mu'min — the believer — is, at the deepest level of the word, *the one who has been made safe.* When Allah says here *āmana-hum*, "He secured them against fear," He is using the very verb that, in another form, means "He gave them faith." The verse is speaking about caravans and desert raids. But the word it reaches for to describe their safety is the word that, at its root, *is* faith.

Hold that. We will return to it, because it is the hinge of the whole verse.

For now, the first discovery: two verbs, both reaching directly from Allah to the people, telling them that the food in their mouths and the calm in their chests came not from a system but from a Sustainer — and that the word for their safety is woven from the same threads as the word for belief.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Small Word مِّن — "Against," Not "From"

Now to that small word I asked you to hold.

After each verb comes the preposition **مِّن**, *min*. We meet it twice:

*aṭʿamahum **min** jūʿin* — and — *āmanahum **min** khawfin*.

In a beginner's Arabic class, *min* means "from." And you could read the verse that way: "He fed them from hunger, He secured them from fear." But that reading is thin, and the commentators do not stop there. They open the word.

Let me show you the two meanings the classical scholars draw out, because together they form a single, fuller picture — not two competing translations but two camera angles on one reality.

The first reading: **min meaning "after."** Al-Baghawi states it plainly — *aṭʿamahum min jūʿin* means *"He fed them **after** hunger."* Al-Qurtubi says the same: *"after hunger."* On this reading, the verse is telling a story in time. There *was* hunger. There *was* fear. These were not hypothetical — al-Qurtubi cites Ibn ʿAbbas describing a people who *"were in hardship and famine"* until they were gathered onto the two journeys. The hunger was real and it came *first.* Then came the feeding. *Min* marks the *from-which-they-emerged*: He brought them up and out of an actual pit of hunger into fullness.

The second reading: **min meaning "against" — protective.** On this reading, *aṭʿamahum min jūʿin* means He fed them *so as to guard them against* hunger — He stood the feeding between them and the starvation that was always reaching for them. The feeding is a shield. The security is a shield. *Min* names the enemy each gift was sent to repel.

Now — don't choose between these. Hold them together, and feel what they do as one image.

There was a real hunger; He brought you out of it. And there is a standing threat of hunger; He keeps you guarded from it. Both. The God of this verse is not only the One who *rescued* you from the pit you were in — He is the One who *stands between you and the pit* every day you are not in it.

[PAUSE]

This is the difference between gratitude for a past event and gratitude for a present, continuous reality. Most of us, if we thank God at all, thank Him for the rescue we remember — the illness we recovered from, the crisis we escaped. The *min* of this verse asks for something deeper. It asks you to be grateful not only for the hunger you came out of, but for the hunger you are *being held away from right now*, the one you cannot see because His provision is standing in front of it, blocking your view.

You do not feel the famine. That is not the absence of His gift. **That is the gift.** The not-feeling-it is precisely the *āmana* — the security — in action. Security is the most invisible blessing there is, because what it gives you is the experience of *nothing happening.*

Stay with that, because it is one of the hardest truths about being grateful: the greatest mercies are the ones that announce themselves as the absence of disaster. And the *min* — "against" — is the word that makes the invisible disaster visible, just for a moment, so you can see what you were spared.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Two Nouns Left Bare — جُوعٍ and خَوْفٍ

Now read the verse's two enemies. Look at how they are written.

**جُوعٍ** — *jūʿin* — "hunger."
**خَوْفٍۭ** — *khawfin* — "fear."

Notice — and this is the absence I want you to read as carefully as any presence in the verse — that both nouns are **indefinite.** Not *al-jūʿ*, "the hunger." Not *al-khawf*, "the fear." Just *jūʿin* — *a* hunger. *Khawfin* — *a* fear. Unbounded. Unspecified. Open.

Why does this matter? Because in Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite in a context like this opens it up to *generality and magnitude.* It is not pointing at one specific famine on one specific date. It is gesturing at hunger *as such* — hunger in all its kinds, of any size. Al-Muyassar, the contemporary plain-language tafsir, reads exactly this magnitude into it: *jūʿin shadīd* — *severe* hunger — and *khawfin ʿaẓīm* — *immense* fear. The indefinite form lets the words breathe outward to fill their largest meaning.

And here is where the physical verse points beyond itself. Stay with me, because this is the destination the stepping-stones were built toward.

The verse is literally about a barren valley and a dangerous road. *jūʿ* — the empty stomach. *khawf* — the raided caravan. Concrete. Historical. Real.

But ask: what *are* hunger and fear, underneath the specific famine and the specific road?

**Hunger is the body's signal that it lacks what it needs to continue.** It is need, made physical. It is the felt experience of *I am not enough on my own; something outside me must come in, or I end.*

**Fear is the heart's signal that something it depends on is threatened.** It is the felt experience of *what I have could be taken; I am not secure on my own.*

Do you see what these two words are? Strip away the famine and the desert road, and *jūʿ* and *khawf* are the **two fundamental forms of human insecurity.** The lack of what sustains the body. The threat to what sustains the heart. Between them, they cover the entire territory of human vulnerability. There is no anxiety you have ever felt that does not reduce, finally, to one of these two: *I might not have enough,* or *I might lose what I have.* Hunger and fear. Need and threat.

[PAUSE]

And notice the sound of the two words. *jūʿin… khawfin.* Both end on that same soft, open, breathy *-in*, the indefinite tail — a kind of exhaled vowel, *-ooh-in… -aw-fin* — almost like two sighs. Two sighs of the soul, set side by side and rhymed, so that hunger and fear become twins in your ear before you've even worked out that they are twins in your life.

The verse names the two sighs. And then — this is the architecture, and we will return to it at the end — the verse has *already*, in its first half, set a divine action against each one. *aṭʿama* against *jūʿ*. *āmana* against *khawf*. Every emptiness named in the second half has a hand of God placed in front of it in the first half. The verse is built as a wall: behind it, the two great fears of being alive; in front of it, the two great mercies of God, standing guard.

That is the linguistic skeleton. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality, and about you.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Hungers — and the God Who Meets You at the Bottom of Both

Let me start where you already live.

In 1943, a psychologist named Abraham Maslow drew a pyramid that has since been taught in nearly every classroom in the world. At the very bottom, the foundation everything else rests on, he put two things. The first tier: *physiological needs* — food, water, the body's survival. And directly above it, the second tier: *safety needs* — security, protection, freedom from fear.

Food and safety. The base of the entire human pyramid. Maslow's claim was that until these two are met, a human being cannot reliably reach for anything higher — not love, not esteem, not meaning, not self-actualization. Strip a person of food and safety and watch every higher aspiration collapse back down into those two desperate needs. The hungry, frightened human cannot philosophize. He can only look for bread and a wall to put his back against.

I bring you Maslow because he was *describing* something fourteen centuries after this verse *named* it. Look again at what Allah lists: *jūʿ* — the failure of the physiological. *khawf* — the failure of safety. The base of Maslow's pyramid, in two words, in the exact order, food before fear. The verse identifies the precise floor of human existence — the two needs so foundational that nothing else stands without them — and it says: **at that floor, at the very bottom of you, where you are most an animal and least a philosopher, that is where I met your forefathers. That is the level at which I provide.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is where the verse goes somewhere Maslow never could.

For Maslow, the bottom of the pyramid is just *the bottom* — the brute, embarrassing, biological floor you have to clear so you can get up to the *interesting* human stuff higher up. We treat it the same way. We are a little ashamed of our hunger and our fear. We think the spiritual life happens *up there* — in the contemplation, the worship, the higher meaning — and that food and safety are mere logistics to be handled so the real work can begin.

The verse refuses that. The verse takes the two needs we are most ashamed of — the most basic, most animal, most un-spiritual needs we have — and it makes *them* the evidence for God. It does not say: *I gave your ancestors profound spiritual experiences, therefore worship Me.* It says: *I fed their stomachs and I calmed their fear, therefore worship Me.* [PAUSE]

Sit with what that does. It means the most ordinary facts of your day — that you ate today, that you were not attacked today, that your children slept safely last night — are not beneath the spiritual life. They *are* the spiritual life. They are the daily, repeated, almost-invisible argument for the existence and care of your Lord. The full stomach is a verse. The unremarkable safe evening is a verse. You have been reading divine signs all day and calling them "just normal life."

And do you see the cruelty we do to ourselves? We wait for the dramatic blessing to feel grateful. We want the miracle, the rescue, the answered desperate prayer. Meanwhile Allah points at the two most constant facts of our existence — *you ate, you were safe* — and says: *this. This was Me. This is Me, every single day, standing between you and a hunger and a fear you have the luxury of not even imagining.*

What does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the one practical thing. Tomorrow, when you eat your first meal — not a special meal, the ordinary one — pause for the length of one breath before the first bite and let the word *aṭʿamahum* sit in your mouth alongside the food. *He fed them.* He is feeding me. And tomorrow night, when you lie down and nothing has gone wrong — no catastrophe, no attack, an utterly unremarkable day — before you sleep, name it: *āmana-hum min khawf.* He secured them against a fear I never had to feel. Let the absence of disaster become, for one breath, a presence of gratitude. That is the whole practice. The verse is asking you to learn to see the gift that disguises itself as *nothing happening.*

### Theme Two: ʾĪmān and Amn — Why the One Who Quiets Your Fear Is the Only One Worth Worshipping

Now we return to the thread I asked you to hold from the very first element.

*āmana-hum min khawf.* "He secured them against fear." And the verb — *āmana* — shares its root, ʾ-m-n, with *ʾīmān*, faith itself. To make-safe and to believe are the same three letters.

Let me show you why that is not a coincidence of language but a map of the human heart.

Think about what fear actually is, underneath. Fear is the experience of depending on something you cannot control. You fear for your health because you cannot control your body. You fear for your income because you cannot control the economy. You fear for the people you love because you cannot control what happens to them. Every fear is a place where your need exceeds your control. Fear is, precisely, *the gap between how much you depend on something and how little power you have over it.*

So what would it mean to be *secured* against fear — *āmana min khawf?* It cannot mean the removal of everything dangerous; the desert road was still dangerous, the caravans still set out, raiders still existed. The security was not the absence of danger. **The security was the presence of a Protector over the danger.** Quraysh did not travel a safe road. They traveled a dangerous road under the protection of the Lord of the House — and *that* was their *amn.* Not safety from the world. Safety in the hand of the One who holds the world.

[PAUSE]

Now you see why the root is the same. *ʾĪmān* — faith — *is* the resolution of *khawf* — fear. Because faith is exactly this: the transfer of your dependence from the thing you cannot control to the One who controls all things. Fear says: *I depend on what I cannot hold.* Faith says: *I depend on the One who holds it.* The frightened heart and the believing heart are looking at the same dangerous world — but the believing heart has moved its weight onto a foundation that cannot be raided. *Amn* is what *ʾīmān* feels like from the inside.

This is why the surah's logic is airtight, and why it does not need to argue. Go back to verse three: *worship the Lord of this House.* Why Him? The frightened human heart is *always* worshipping something — always leaning its weight on something it hopes will keep it safe. Quraysh leaned on idols. We lean on savings, on status, on the illusion of control, on the people we love, on our own competence. Every one of these is a god of safety we have appointed. And every one of them can be raided. Every one is itself subject to hunger and fear. You cannot be made *secure* by something that is not itself secure.

And so the verse closes the surah by naming the only Lord who can actually end fear — the One who is Himself beyond need and beyond threat, the One who *fed* and *secured* rather than needing to be fed and secured. Worship *that* One. Not because you are commanded to from the outside, but because He is the only place your fear can finally lay its weight down and *rest.* Every other god you appoint will, in the end, need you to protect *it.* This is the only One who protects *you.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practical anchor for this theme. The next time you feel that specific tightness — the anxiety, the *khawf*, the gap between what you depend on and what you can control — do not first try to fix the situation. First, locate the god. Ask: *what am I leaning my safety on right now? Whose hand am I trusting to keep me from falling?* And when you find it — the savings, the relationship, the plan, the control — ask the harder question: *can this thing itself be raided?* It always can. And then, deliberately, move your weight. Say the word the verse gives you: *āmana-hum min khawf.* Transfer the dependence. Not to deny the danger — the road is real — but to put the danger back under the only hand that was ever actually holding it. That movement, repeated, *is* ʾīmān. It is faith as a verb. It is what this verse is teaching you to do with your fear.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, look at the whole verse at once and watch it click into place.

> ٱلَّذِىٓ **أَطْعَمَهُم** ‖ مِّن **جُوعٍ** ‖ وَ**ءَامَنَهُم** ‖ مِّنْ **خَوْفٍۭ**

It is built as two perfectly matched halves, and the matching *is* the message:

| The Divine Hand (Form IV verb) | The Human Emptiness (indefinite noun) |
|---|---|
| **أَطْعَمَ** — He fed | **جُوعٍ** — hunger *(the body's lack)* |
| **ءَامَنَ** — He secured | **خَوْفٍ** — fear *(the heart's lack)* |

Two needs that cover the entire surface of human vulnerability — the lack that threatens the body and the threat that haunts the heart. And in front of each, the verse places a hand of God, reaching directly — *Form IV, perfect tense, attached pronoun, no intermediary.* The structure is a wall standing between the human being and the two abysses of existence. That is not decoration. That *is* the theology: wherever you are most empty, He has already placed Himself.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The grammar showed us *two verbs reaching directly* — and the theme of the two hungers showed us *why it matters that nothing stands between you and your Provider:* because the means (the caravan, the sanctuary, the savings, the plan) are real but borrowed; the Hand behind them is the only thing that does not need a hand behind it.

The grammar showed us *one root* — *āmana* and *ʾīmān*, security and faith — and the second theme showed us *why that root is one:* because faith simply *is* the act of moving your fear onto the only foundation that cannot be raided.

The whole surah was a single argument, and this verse is its conclusion: *the God who meets you at the absolute bottom of yourself — in your hunger and in your fear — is the only One with any right to the top of yourself, your worship.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the hunger I am being held away from *right now* — the disaster I cannot see because His provision is standing in front of it, blocking my view? Have I ever once thanked Him for a famine I never had to feel?
2. When I felt fear this week, *whose hand* was I actually trusting to keep me safe? And could that thing itself be raided?
3. I wait for dramatic blessings to feel grateful. What would change if I treated the full stomach and the safe, uneventful night as the divine signs the verse says they are?
4. Where in my life am I protecting a god that was supposed to be protecting me?
5. If faith and security share one root — if *ʾīmān* is where fear finally lays its weight down — then how much of my anxiety is, underneath, simply weight resting on the wrong foundation?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The God who fills the two emptiest places in you — the hunger of the body and the fear of the heart — is the only One worthy of your worship, because He alone needs neither, and stands, unseen, between you and both.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this verse asks you to manufacture a feeling. It asks you only to *see what is already there* — that you ate today, that you were safe today, and that neither was an accident. The invitation is simply this: let the ordinary become visible. Let the full stomach and the quiet night stop being the background of your life and become, even for one breath a day, the foreground — the daily, repeated proof that the Lord of the House is feeding you against a hunger and securing you against a fear you have been given the mercy of never having to imagine.

### A Brief Duʿā'

O You who fed them against hunger and secured them against fear — let me see Your hand in the bread I take for granted and in the night that passes without disaster. Move the weight of my fear off everything that can be raided, and let it rest on You alone, for You are the only security that was never itself in need of securing. Make my *amn* into *ʾīmān*, and my full stomach into gratitude. Āmīn.
