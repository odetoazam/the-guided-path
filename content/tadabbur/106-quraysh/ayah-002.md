# The Two Journeys: When the Rhythm You Stopped Noticing Is the Gift Itself

> **إِۦلَـٰفِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ ٱلشِّتَآءِ وَٱلصَّيْفِ**
>
> *īlāfihim riḥlata-sh-shitāʾi waṣ-ṣayf*
>
> "Their familiarity — the journey of winter and summer —"

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Surah That Begins in the Middle of a Sentence

I want you to notice something strange before we even begin.

This ayah does not stand on its own. It cannot. It opens with a word — *īlāfihim*, "their familiarity" — that is grammatically incomplete without the verse before it. Surah Quraysh begins: *li-īlāfi Quraysh* — "For the bonding of Quraysh." And then, immediately, this ayah leans forward and says it again: *īlāfihim* — "their bonding, their being-made-familiar." The surah opens by repeating itself. It says the same word twice before it tells you anything else.

Hold that. We will come back to it, because that repetition is not an accident. It is the surah pressing its thumb down on something it desperately wants you to see.

**Let me rebuild the world.**

Imagine Mecca fourteen centuries ago. A valley with no river. No fields. The Quran itself will later have Ibrahim describe this place as *wādin ghayri dhī zarʿ* — "a valley without any cultivation" (14:37). Nothing grows here. By every law of geography, no city should survive in this place. A settlement needs water, soil, crops. Mecca had a well and some stones.

And yet a tribe lived there — Quraysh — and not merely survived but prospered. How?

Because they were merchants. Twice a year, the great caravans set out. In the cold months, they journeyed south — most reports say to Yemen, where the climate was warmer. In the hot months, they journeyed north — to Syria, *al-Shām*, where the air was cooler and the markets were rich. Winter caravan. Summer caravan. The whole survival of the city hung on these two journeys.

Now — and this is what you must feel to understand the ayah — **a caravan is a fragile thing.** Picture it. Hundreds of camels strung across open desert for weeks. No walls. No army marching alongside. You pass through the territory of a dozen tribes, any one of which could fall on you, rob you, kill you, and vanish into the dunes. In pre-Islamic Arabia, raiding caravans was not a crime. It was an industry. A merchant tribe crossing that much hostile ground twice a year, every year, should have been bled dry within a generation.

But Quraysh moved through it all — safely. Year after year. The tribes that would have torn anyone else apart let Quraysh pass. Why? Because Quraysh were the keepers of the Kaʿba, the guardians of the House that all of Arabia revered. To touch them was to touch the sanctuary. So they walked through the most dangerous land on earth wrapped in an invisible garment of safety, and they grew rich doing it.

[PAUSE]

And here is the hook. Here is the thing I need you to be hungry for.

By the time this surah was revealed, **Quraysh had completely stopped noticing.** The safety had become so normal, so woven into the fabric of ordinary life, that no one looked up anymore and said: *why are we safe?* The two journeys had become routine — as invisible to them as the air they breathed. They thought it was just how the world worked. They thought it was their cleverness, their lineage, their gods.

So Allah revealed a surah whose entire purpose is to make them *see the water they were swimming in.* And He does it not by pointing at the Kaʿba, not by pointing at their wealth — but by pointing at the most boring, most taken-for-granted thing in their lives:

**The fact that they travel in winter and they travel in summer.**

The routine itself. The rhythm they stopped noticing. *That* is what this ayah holds up to the light. And by the time we are finished, I want you to understand that this surah was never really about Quraysh. It is about the safe, ordinary rhythm of *your* life — the one you also stopped noticing.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Īlāf* — The Word for a Gift So Deep You Forget It Was Given

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because without it the word will stay flat.

In Arabic, a simple verb can be lifted into a new "form" that changes its meaning in a precise, predictable way. The root here is **أ-ل-ف** (*a-l-f*). In its simplest form, *alifa* (form I), it means *to be familiar with something, to be at ease with it, to be accustomed.* It is intransitive — it just describes a state. You *are* familiar. A bird *is* at home in its nest.

Now lift that verb into what grammarians call Form IV — *ālafa*. Form IV characteristically takes that simple state and makes someone *cause* it, *grant* it, *bring it about* in another. So *alifa* — "to be familiar" — becomes *ālafa* — "**to make** familiar, to bond two things together, to knit something into a state of ease." The intransitive state becomes a deliberate act done *to* someone *by* someone.

And the word in our ayah, *īlāf*, is the verbal noun of that Form IV. It is not "familiarity" as a flat fact. It is **the act of having-been-made-familiar.** The act of being bonded. Of being granted ease.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with what that does to the ayah.

The verse does not say "Quraysh's *habit*" as though it were a thing Quraysh built. The grammar of Form IV smuggles in a hidden agent. Someone *made* them accustomed. Someone *knit* them into safety. Someone *bonded* them to this rhythm until it felt like the natural order of things.

Who?

The surah will not name Him until ayah 3. But the verb form has already pointed the finger. *Īlāf* is not something you do to yourself. It is something done *to* you. Every time you feel "this is just normal, this is just how my life is" — the grammar of this word is whispering: *no. Someone settled you into that normal. Someone is the cause of the ease you mistake for the natural state of things.*

[PAUSE]

But there is something even deeper inside this root, and I do not want you to miss it.

The same root, أ-ل-ف, gives Arabic the word for **intimacy and affection between hearts** — *ulfa*. It is the word for the bond between two friends, between spouses, between a person and a home they love. It is the warmth of belonging. When the Quran elsewhere speaks of Allah joining the hearts of the believers, it uses this family of meaning — *allafa bayna qulūbihim*, "He bonded their hearts together" (paraphrasing 8:63).

So *īlāf* is not cold. It is not merely "you got used to it." It carries the warmth of *being at home.* Allah did not just keep Quraysh alive — He gave them the felt experience of *belonging* to their land, their rhythm, their journeys. He made the dangerous feel safe. He made the foreign road feel like a familiar path home.

**That** is the gift this single word names: not just provision, not just safety — but the *feeling* of safety. The interior sense of ease that lets a human being stop bracing and simply live.

And here is the terrible irony the word exposes: the deeper a gift sinks into your sense of "normal," the *less* you thank the Giver for it. The gifts we notice are the dramatic ones — the rescue, the recovery, the windfall. The gifts we never notice are the ones woven so completely into the fabric of every single day that we mistake them for the floor we stand on. *Īlāf* is the gift that, by its very perfection, makes itself invisible.

One word. And it has already told us: your safety was granted, your ease was caused, and the very smoothness of it is what blinds you to the Hand behind it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Repetition — When the Quran Says It Twice

Now let's return to the strange thing I asked you to hold at the very beginning.

This word — *īlāf* — appears in ayah 1: *li-īlāfi Quraysh.* And then it appears again, immediately, in ayah 2: *īlāfihim.* The surah says it, and then says it again, almost in the same breath.

The classical commentators noticed this at once. Al-Jalālayn calls the second *īlāf* a **taʾkīd** — an emphatic repetition, a deliberate doubling-down. Al-Baghawī describes the second as a **badal** — a substitution, where the second phrase circles back and unpacks the first, defining it more precisely.

Let me teach you why a language repeats a word, because we do this too.

When you say to someone, "I'm telling you — *I'm telling you* — listen to me," you do not repeat "I'm telling you" because you forgot you already said it. You repeat it because the first time did not land, and you need them to *stop and register* what you are about to say. Repetition in speech is the verbal equivalent of taking someone by the shoulders.

That is what this surah does. It names the gift — *īlāf* — and then, before moving on, it grips the word again — *īlāfihim*, "*their* bonding, *theirs*" — and only now, having seized your attention twice, does it tell you what the gift actually consists of: *riḥlata-sh-shitāʾi waṣ-ṣayf*, "the journey of winter and summer."

[PAUSE]

Notice the rhetorical architecture. The surah refuses to tell you *what* the gift is until it has made you feel *that* it is a gift. First: the bonding, the ease, the safety (ayah 1). Again: the bonding, theirs, specifically (ayah 2). And *then*, finally: oh — it was the two journeys. It was the boring caravan you take every winter and every summer.

Do you see the strategy? If Allah had simply said, "Remember that you travel in winter and summer," Quraysh would have shrugged. *Yes, we know, we're merchants, so what?* But by framing the journeys first as *īlāf* — a granted, caused, intimate safety — and emphasizing it twice before naming it, the surah forces them to look at the most ordinary fact of their lives and suddenly see it glowing. The repetition is the surah saying: *do not let your eyes slide past this. The thing you find boring is the thing I want you to worship Me for.*

[PAUSE]

There is a tender detail in the variant readings the commentators preserve, and it deepens this. Al-Qurṭubī records that this word was recited in several ways across the early reading traditions — some read *īlāf*, some read *ilf*, some with two hamzas, *iʾlāf*. He even notes a report that the Prophet ﷺ himself was heard reciting the shorter form *ilf*. (These are documented differences in *qirāʾāt*, the recognized recitation traditions — I mention them as a composite witness, not to rank one over another.) What every one of these readings shares is the same root, the same insistence: *bonding, familiarity, being-made-at-ease.* The whole tradition of reciters, across cities and generations, kept circling this one idea. Even the *way* the word was passed down emphasizes it. The community could not stop repeating the word for the thing the community had stopped noticing.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: One Journey, Two Seasons — The Word *Riḥla* and What the Ayah Refuses to Say

Look closely at the words for the journeys: *riḥlata-sh-shitāʾi waṣ-ṣayf.*

The root of *riḥla* is **ر-ح-ل**, which carries the image of *saddling a mount and departing* — packing up, loading the camel, setting out. A *riḥla* is not a stroll. It is an expedition, a committed departure with everything you own loaded onto an animal's back. It is labor. It is risk. It is leaving the safety of home for the open road.

Now notice something the Arabic does that the English translation hides.

The word *riḥla* — "journey" — is **singular.** One journey. But it governs *two* seasons: *al-shitāʾ* (winter) *waṣ-ṣayf* (and summer). The ayah does not say "the two journeys" (*riḥlatay*). It says *the journey* — singular — *of winter and summer.*

Sit with that. Why one word for two trips going in opposite directions, in opposite seasons, to opposite ends of Arabia?

Here is what this invites us to consider. The commentators describe the two routes — and they differ on the exact destinations, which I'll come to. But the surah binds them into a single grammatical unit: *one* journey, with two faces. As if to say: this is not two separate strokes of luck. The winter caravan and the summer caravan are not two coincidences that happened to both go well. They are **one continuous, single act of care** — one unbroken rhythm of provision that simply wears the clothing of two seasons.

Think of how we describe a reliable thing in our own lives. We don't say "the seventeen separate sunrises this week." We say "the sunrise" — singular — because its reliability has fused all the instances into one trustworthy reality. The ayah does the same with Quraysh's provision. By making the journey singular, it tells them: *the safety you experienced in winter and the safety you experienced in summer are the same safety, from the same Source, with no gap in between.* You were never, for one season, outside of His care.

[PAUSE]

And now — read the absence. Read what this ayah does *not* say.

It tells you *when* they journey: winter and summer. It does **not** tell you *where.* It does **not** tell you *why.* It does **not** tell you *what they brought back.*

This is striking, because the destinations were the whole point of the trade. The commentators rush to fill the gap — al-Jalālayn says winter to Yemen, summer to al-Shām; al-Baghawī preserves a difference of opinion, reporting one tradition from Ibn ʿAbbās that they wintered in Mecca and summered in Ṭāʾif, and another that they journeyed to Yemen and Syria for trade. (Notice the honest *ikhtilāf* here — the early community held more than one account of the precise routes, and the surah's silence is exactly why.) The point for us is this: **the ayah does not care about the destination.** It strips the journeys of their commercial detail entirely.

Why? Because Quraysh's eyes were *fixed* on the destinations. On the markets. On the profit. On Yemen's goods and Syria's silver. That is where their hearts lived — in the *where* and the *what-did-we-earn.* So the ayah surgically removes all of it. It does not mention Yemen. It does not mention Syria. It does not mention the gold. It leaves only the bare rhythm — *winter and summer* — and the safety that made it possible.

It is as if Allah is saying: *you think the journey is about where you're going and what you're bringing back. Let Me show you the journey with all of that erased. Look at what remains. What remains is that you went, and you came home, and you were safe. That — the part you ignored — is My gift. The part you obsessed over was never the point.*

The ayah withholds exactly what Quraysh worshipped, so that they would finally see what they overlooked.

### Bridge: From the Words to the World

Look at what these four words have shown us.

*Īlāf* told us: your safety was *granted and caused* — and the deeper it sinks into "normal," the more invisibly the Giver works. The **repetition** told us: the Quran will physically grip you and say the word twice, because it knows your eyes slide off your own blessings. And the **single journey of two seasons**, with its destinations erased, told us: the rhythm you find boring *is* the gift, and the rewards you chase were never the point.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About *your* winters and *your* summers.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of "Normal" — Why You Cannot Feel Your Greatest Blessings

Let me start where you actually live.

Psychologists have a name for one of the strangest features of the human mind. They call it **hedonic adaptation.** Here is what it means. When something good happens to you — a raise, a new home, a recovery from illness, a relationship — your happiness spikes. And then, with almost mechanical reliability, it sinks back down to where it was before. Within weeks, sometimes days, the new good thing becomes the baseline. It becomes *normal.* And once it is normal, you stop feeling it.

This is why the car you dreamed about for two years is, eighteen months later, just "the car." It is why the apartment you were so grateful to get is now just "home," and you mostly notice what's wrong with it. It is why the people who love you and who are *alive and well in your house right now* are the people you are least likely to feel grateful for — precisely because they are always there.

Your mind treats stability as silence. It only sends a signal when something *changes.* Constant blessings produce no signal at all. They go dark. They become the floor.

[PAUSE]

This is the exact spiritual disease this surah was sent to treat.

Quraysh were not ungrateful in the way a thief is ungrateful. They were ungrateful in the way *all* of us are ungrateful — by adaptation. The safety of their caravans had been constant for so long that it had gone dark. It produced no signal. They genuinely could not feel it anymore, the way you cannot feel your own heartbeat until something goes wrong with it.

And notice the precise tool Allah chose to break the spell. He did not point them to something rare. He did not say, "Remember the year of the famine," or "Remember the elephant army." He pointed them at the *most repetitive* thing in their lives — *winter and summer, winter and summer, every year* — the very repetition that had numbed them. He reached into the heart of the numbness and switched the light back on.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel about your own life.

The greatest gifts in your life right now are, by mathematical certainty, the ones you cannot feel. Because the ones you *can* feel are the recent or the threatened ones. The blessings that are stable, daily, and reliable have gone dark in your mind — not because they are small, but because they are *constant.* Your eyesight. The fact that you woke up without pain this morning. The fact that you know where your next meal is coming from. The people who are simply, undramatically *there.*

Quraysh's "winter and summer" is your morning and your evening. Your commute that ends safely. Your routine that nobody attacked. The Quran is doing to you exactly what it did to them: it is taking the most boring, repetitive fact of your life and saying — *stop. Look at it. Someone caused that. Someone bonded you into that ease. It was never the floor. It was always a gift held up by a Hand you stopped seeing.*

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is one concrete practice, drawn straight from the surah's method. The surah does not ask Quraysh to feel grateful for something extraordinary. It asks them to feel it for the *ordinary.* So tomorrow, find your "winter and summer" — the single most repetitive, taken-for-granted rhythm in your day — and name it out loud as a gift, with its Giver attached. Not "I'm grateful in general." Specifically: *the fact that I made this same drive and arrived safely again — You kept me, and I almost didn't notice.* You are switching the light back on in the exact room the surah pointed to.

### Theme Two: The Caravan and the Kaʿba — Where Your Provision Actually Comes From

Now let me take you into the deeper anxiety this ayah touches — the one underneath the gratitude. The anxiety of *provision* itself. Of *rizq.* Of "will I be okay?"

Every working human being lives with a quiet, low-grade fear: *what if the income stops? What if the journey doesn't come back this time?* For Quraysh, this was not abstract. Their entire survival rode on two caravans crossing hostile desert. Everything they had — their food, their wealth, their children's future — was loaded onto camels and sent out into the open. Imagine the temptation to believe that your survival depends on *the strength of the caravan.* On the skill of the merchants. On the quality of the guards. On the cleverness of the trade.

And this is the lie that provision always tells us. It says: *your security comes from the mechanism.* From your job. From your savings. From your competence. From the thing you can see and touch and control.

[PAUSE]

Watch what the surah does to that lie.

It places the *cause* of the journeys not in the caravan, but in *īlāf* — a granted safety. And then, when it finally reveals the destination of all this reasoning in ayahs 3 and 4, it does not point to the markets of Yemen or the silver of Syria. It points to a building. *Falyaʿbudū rabba hādha-l-bayt* — "So let them worship the Lord of *this House.*"

Sit with that connection, because it reorganizes the entire economy of the soul.

The caravans went *out* — to Yemen, to Syria, to the markets, to the source of the goods. Quraysh believed that *out there* was where the provision lived. But the surah quietly reverses the arrow. The reason the caravans could go out and come back safely at all was *this House* — the Kaʿba — sitting motionless in the center of Mecca, the sanctuary that wrapped Quraysh in a safety no army could buy. They thought their wealth came from the journey *outward.* In reality it flowed from the still point at the *center* — the House and its Lord.

[PAUSE]

Remember the surah before this one — al-Fil. An entire army with elephants marched on Mecca to destroy this very House, and Allah annihilated them with birds and stones, *with no caravan, no merchant, no guard lifting a finger.* The same Quraysh who would soon credit their safety to their trade had just watched their safety preserved by something they did absolutely nothing to earn. The two surahs together make one argument: *the protection you think you produce by your effort is actually produced by your Lord, around a House you did not build, for reasons you did not earn.*

This is the answer to the anxiety of provision. Not that you stop working — Quraysh still loaded the camels, still made the journey, still did the trade. The surah never tells them to abandon the caravan. **The effort is real.** But the *security* underneath the effort was never *in* the effort. The journey was the *means.* The Lord of the House was the *Source.* And the difference between knowing that and not knowing that is the difference between a heart that travels in peace and a heart that travels in terror.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this concrete for your own life, because the same two arrows live in you.

You also have an "outward journey" — the thing you believe your survival depends on. Your job. Your skill. Your hustle. And you also have a "House" — the still center, the Lord who is the actual Source of every safe arrival you've ever had. The disease is to pour all your trust into the journey and forget the House. To wake up anxious because you've located your security entirely in the mechanism — which can break, which can be raided, which can fail.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** When the fear of provision rises — and it will — do what the surah does. Keep loading the camel. Keep doing the work; the effort is part of the worship. But move the *weight of your trust* off the caravan and onto the Lord of the House. Before the work, before the journey, anchor at the center: *the One who brought every other journey home will bring this one too.* You travel exactly as hard — but you travel as someone who knows where the safety actually comes from. That is the difference between Quraysh-before-the-surah and Quraysh-after.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape, because the surah is built with breathtaking economy.

It opens by naming a gift twice — *īlāf*, *īlāfihim* — gripping you by the shoulders before it will tell you what the gift is. Then, in our ayah, it finally names the gift: the single journey of winter and summer — and it deliberately *erases* the destinations and the profit, leaving only the bare, safe rhythm. And then, in the two ayahs that follow, it delivers the entire purpose: *so let them worship the Lord of this House, who fed them against hunger and made them safe from fear.*

Do you see it? The surah moves from **the gift** (the safe rhythm) → to **the Giver** (the Lord of the House) → to **the response** (worship). Four ayahs. One unbroken line from a numb, overlooked routine all the way to the foot of the One who authored it. The boring caravan is the *first step* on a staircase that ends in prostration.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The grammar of *īlāf* told us the safety was *caused* by a hidden Hand — and the theme of "normal" told us *why we can't feel it*: because that Hand works so reliably it goes dark. The single *journey* of two seasons told us the provision was *one unbroken act of care* — and the theme of the caravan and the House told us *where that care actually originates*: not in the mechanism we trust, but in the Lord we forget. The words and the life say one thing:

**The most ordinary, repeated, taken-for-granted rhythm of your life is not the floor you stand on. It is a gift, deliberately woven, and its very smoothness is the veil over the One who weaves it.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is *my* "winter and summer" — the safe, repeated rhythm so constant that I have completely stopped feeling it as a gift?
2. Which of my blessings have gone *dark* in my mind simply because they never changed and never threatened to leave?
3. Where have I located my security — in the *caravan* (my effort, my job, my competence) or in the *Lord of the House*? And how would tomorrow feel different if I moved the weight?
4. The surah had to say the word *twice* before Quraysh would look. What is Allah repeating in my life that I keep sliding my eyes past?
5. If everything I chase — the "destinations" — were erased like they are in this ayah, would I be able to see the safety that remains? And would I thank Him for it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The safe, unremarkable rhythm of your coming and going — the part of life you stopped noticing — is itself a deliberate gift Allah wove into your days, and it was given for one reason: to bend your heart back to the One who gave it.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need a dramatic rescue to feel gratitude. That was never the lesson of this surah. The invitation is gentler and harder than that: to walk back into the most boring room of your life — the routine, the commute, the daily safety, the people who are simply *there* — and switch the light back on. To see the *īlāf*. To feel, for the first time in a long time, the safety you swim in. And then to do what ayah 3 asks: to let that seeing *move* you — toward the Lord of the House, who has been holding all of it the entire time you weren't looking.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, Lord of the House, who knit us into a safety we stopped noticing and carried us through journeys we never thanked You for — open our eyes to the gifts that have gone dark in our hearts. Let us feel the *īlāf* You wrapped around our ordinary days, and let that feeling bend us, every winter and every summer, back to You. *Āmīn.*
