# When the Example Was Struck: Laughter as a Refuge from Truth

## Introduction

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single word.

> **وَلَمَّا ضُرِبَ ٱبْنُ مَرْيَمَ مَثَلًا إِذَا قَوْمُكَ مِنْهُ يَصِدُّونَ**
>
> *wa-lammā ḍuriba bnu Maryama mathalan idhā qawmuka minhu yaṣiddūn*
>
> "And when the son of Maryam was presented as an example — at once, your people erupted in clamor."

Nine words. One sentence. And in the middle of it, something happens so fast that the grammar itself has to scramble to keep up.

Let me rebuild the room for you.

The surah is called *Az-Zukhruf* — "The Gold Ornament," "The Gilding." It is a surah about surfaces. About people who are mesmerized by the glitter of a thing and never reach its meaning. Earlier in the surah, Allah says that if it weren't for the danger of all humanity becoming disbelievers, He would have given the rejectors of faith *houses with roofs of silver and staircases of gold* — because that glitter is worth so little to Him (43:33-35). The whole surah is teaching us to look past the gold leaf to what is underneath.

And now we come to a scene. A real one. The classical commentators — al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, and most of the mufassirūn — locate this in a live argument. When the ayah was revealed, *"Indeed, you and what you worship besides Allah are the fuel of Hell"* (21:98), one of the Quraysh — the narrations name ʿAbdullāh ibn al-Zibaʿrā — saw an opening. A debating point. "Then," he said, "what about ʿĪsā? The Christians worship him. Is he in the Fire too?" And he turned to the crowd, pleased with himself.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that picture. A sacred name has just been raised — *ʿĪsā ibn Maryam*, one of the mightiest of the prophets, a word and a spirit from Allah. He has been raised in the middle of a room. And what does the room do?

It doesn't go quiet. It doesn't lean in. It doesn't think.

It erupts.

Here is the hook. The entire moral weight of this ayah is not carried by a noun, or a verb, or even by the name of the Messiah. It is carried by **one small word that means "suddenly."** A word that captures the gap between hearing the truth and reacting to it — and tells us that, for this crowd, the gap was *zero.* There was no space at all between the stimulus and the laughter.

We are going to find out why Allah built this entire scene around the grammar of *suddenness*. And by the end, I want you to ask yourself a frightening question: **when truth is raised in front of me, how long is my pause before I react?**

Let's begin.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Ḍuriba* — Why an Example Is "Struck"

Let me teach you something before we use it.

In English, when we offer an illustration, we *give* an example. We *make* a comparison. We *draw* an analogy. Notice that all of our verbs are soft — giving, making, drawing. Gentle, constructive verbs.

Arabic does not do this. In Arabic, you do not *give* an example. You **strike** it.

The phrase here is **ضُرِبَ مَثَلًا** — *ḍuriba mathalan*. The root of *ḍuriba* is **ḍād-rāʾ-bāʾ (Ḍrb)**, and its core, physical, first meaning is *to strike, to hit, to beat.* The same root used for striking with a hand, striking a path, striking a tent into the ground. And from this root, Arabic builds its standard idiom for presenting an example: *ḍaraba mathalan* — literally, *to strike an example.*

Sit with that image. Why *strike*?

Think about how a coin is made. You do not *pour* a coin into existence gently. You take a blank disc of metal, you lay it on a die, and you **strike** it — one hard, decisive blow — and in that single strike the whole face of the coin appears at once. The image is driven into the metal. It is fixed. It cannot be wiped away.

That is what an example does. A *mathal* takes an abstract, slippery truth — something hard to hold — and **strikes it into a concrete image** so that it lands, so that it fixes, so that you cannot un-see it. Allah uses this very word for His own parables throughout the Quran: *"Allah strikes an example..."* (2:26, and many others). A struck example is meant to *land like a hammer-blow* and stay.

Now here is the first thing I want you to notice. [PAUSE]

The verb is **passive**. *Ḍuriba* — "it *was* struck." Not "they struck," not "he struck." The one who struck the example is grammatically erased. Allah does not name the striker in the verb itself.

Why does that matter?

Because in the live scene, the one who "struck" the example was the *opponent* — the man who raised ʿĪsā as a gotcha. But by leaving the verb passive, Allah lifts the moment out of that one petty argument and makes it universal. The point is no longer *who* raised the example. The point is: **a true and weighty thing was set down in front of these people, struck into a concrete image, made impossible to miss.** The truth landed. It was driven in like a coin.

And what did they do with a truth that had just been hammered into the ground in front of them?

Stay with that question. We're about to meet the word that answers it.

What this single passive verb reveals: **a real truth was decisively placed before them — not whispered, not hinted, but struck into the world like a stamp into metal.** Which makes everything that follows inexcusable.

### Element Two: *Idhā* — The Grammar of the Reflex

Here is the heart of the ayah. And to feel it, you need to learn one small piece of Arabic — one of the most beautiful, most psychologically precise tools in the entire language.

Let me teach the concept first, with something from your own life.

Imagine you write: *"When I opened the door, I greeted my guests."* That is a normal, planned sequence. You opened the door, and then — as you intended — you greeted them. There is a *you* in control, moving from one deliberate act to the next.

Now imagine instead: *"I opened the door — and there was a snake on the floor."*

Feel the difference? The second sentence has no plan in it. There is no deliberate *you* doing the second thing. There is just: the door opens, and **suddenly** — *there it is.* Reality ambushes you. The thing is just *there*, before you had any say in it.

Arabic has a dedicated grammatical particle for exactly that second kind of sentence. It is called **إِذَا الفُجائِيَّة** — *idhā al-fujāʾiyya*, the *idhā of suddenness*, the *idhā of ambush.* It means something like *"and lo!"* — *"and at once!"* — *"and there, suddenly—!"* It is the grammar of a reaction that arrives **before thought, before will, before any deliberate decision.** A reflex. A flinch.

Now look at the ayah:

> *wa-lammā ḍuriba bnu Maryama mathalan* — **idhā** *qawmuka minhu yaṣiddūn*
>
> "And when the son of Maryam was struck as an example — **suddenly!** — your people are clamoring at it."

Do you see what Allah has done? [PAUSE]

He could have said *fa-yaṣiddu qawmuka* — "*and then* your people clamored." A normal sequence: the example was given, *and then* they responded. That would imply some space. Some processing. A beat in which a person hears, considers, and *then* chooses how to react.

But Allah does not give them that beat. He uses **idhā al-fujāʾiyya.** And what that particle tells you, at the level of grammar itself, is that **there was no gap.** The example landed and the laughter erupted in the *same instant.* No pause. No reflection. No weighing. The truth was struck into the room — and before it had even finished landing, the room was already convulsing.

Let me say this as plainly as I can, because it is the whole ayah:

**The distance between the truth arriving and them rejecting it was zero.**

[PAUSE]

This is what makes *idhā al-fujāʾiyya* so devastating here. A sacred name — *ʿĪsā ibn Maryam* — has been raised. A genuine theological matter is on the table. And the grammar tells us their response had nothing to do with the *content.* They didn't engage it. They didn't lose to it in argument. They didn't even hear it out. The reaction fired off like a reflex — like a knee jerking when the doctor taps it. Pure, thoughtless, automatic.

And here is the thing about a reflex: a reflex is not an answer. **A reflex is what a person does instead of answering.**

Notice the absence here, too — and absence in the Quran always speaks. In this whole scene there is *no* verb of thinking. No *yatafakkarūn* (they reflect). No *yaʿqilūn* (they reason). No *yanẓurūn* (they consider). The space where a thoughtful human response should sit is simply *empty* — and into that empty space Allah drops the *idhā* of ambush. The grammar has a hole in it exactly where the deliberation should be. That hole *is* the indictment.

What this one particle reveals: **the crowd's rejection was not a conclusion they reached. It was a reflex that protected them from ever having to reach one.**

### Element Three: *Yaṣiddūn* — The Sound of Turning Away

Now the final word. And it carries a secret that even fluent readers can miss.

The word is **يَصِدُّونَ** — *yaṣiddūn.* Root: **ṣād-dāl-dāl (Ṣdd).** And the early reciters of the Quran read it in *two different ways* — a documented difference in the recitations (*qirāʾāt*) preserved from the earliest generations. Al-Baghawī notes it explicitly in his commentary on this very ayah.

- Read one way — *yaṣuddūn*, with a *ḍamma* on the ṣād — the root **Ṣdd** carries the meaning *to turn away, to avert, to obstruct.* (Think of *yaṣuddūna ʿan sabīli-llāh* — "they turn away from the path of Allah.")
- Read the other way — *yaṣiddūn*, with a *kasra* — the same root carries the meaning *to clamor, to shout, to raise a commotion, to laugh and shriek in uproar.*

Now — when the classical scholars meet a difference in recitation like this, they do not treat it as "either/or," as if we have to pick the right one and discard the other. They treat it as **both being revealed, both being true, each adding a dimension the other cannot hold alone.** Two camera angles on one moment.

So let us hold both at once. Watch what happens.

The same crowd, in the same instant, is doing **two things that should be opposites:**

They are **turning away** — *yaṣuddūn.* Disengaging. Refusing to face it. Putting their backs to the truth.

And they are **clamoring** — *yaṣiddūn.* Erupting in noise. Shrieking with laughter. Making a commotion.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the paradox? You would think turning away is *quiet* — a cold shoulder, a silent dismissal. And you would think clamoring is *engagement* — loud, present, involved. But Allah, through these two readings, shows you a single posture that is *both at once:* **loud disengagement. Noisy avoidance. A commotion whose entire purpose is to not deal with the thing.**

And isn't that *exactly* what mockery is?

Mockery looks like engagement — it's loud, it's animated, it's full of energy aimed at the target. But mockery is actually the loudest possible way of *not* engaging. When you laugh something off, you are making a great deal of noise *precisely so that you never have to answer it.* The laughter is a wall. The commotion is the turning-away. They are the same act.

Now listen to the *sound* of the word itself. **يَصِدُّونَ.** The hard, hissing **ṣād** — a heavy, grating letter. The doubled, clenched **dāl** — *ṣid-dūn,* the tongue striking twice, hard. The word *grates.* It does not flow; it scrapes. Read it aloud and your mouth makes an ugly, harsh, buzzing sound — the very texture of a jeering crowd, of a sneer, of derision. Allah chose a word whose *sound is the sound of the thing it describes.* The phonetics *are* the mockery.

And remember where the *idhā* put us. This grating, doubled, turning-away clamor erupts **with no gap at all** after the truth was struck. So the full picture assembles:

A weighty truth is *struck* into the room like a coin into metal — *ḍuriba.*
**And at once** — *idhā* —
before a single thought can form,
the crowd is *grating, jeering, turning its back while making noise* — *yaṣiddūn.*

What this final word reveals: **their laughter was not joy. It was a wall built at the speed of reflex — loud on the outside, empty on the inside — to keep a truth from ever getting in.**

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have built, together.

*Ḍuriba* told us the truth **landed** — decisively, undeniably, struck into the world.

*Idhā* told us the rejection was **instant** — a reflex with no thought in it.

*Yaṣiddūn* told us the rejection took the form of **loud avoidance** — a commotion that exists precisely to avoid the thing.

Three words, one anatomy: *a truth arrives, and a defense fires before the mind can engage.*

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about an argument in Mecca fourteen centuries ago, but about reality itself. About what happens inside a human being when something true threatens something we love.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Clever Argument as an Escape Hatch

Let me start where you live.

You have been in this conversation. Maybe you've been on *both* sides of it.

Someone makes a point that lands. A real point. The kind that, if you actually sat with it, might cost you something — might require you to change your mind, admit you were wrong, give up a position you'd grown comfortable in. And what does the other person do? They don't engage the point. They reach, fast, for a *counter-point.* A "but what about—." A clever reversal. A gotcha. And the moment they find it, you can see the relief wash over their face. They are *off the hook.* They never had to deal with what you actually said.

We have a modern name for the engine underneath this. Psychologists call it **motivated reasoning.** It's the discovery — and it is one of the most robust findings in all of psychology — that when a conclusion threatens us, we don't reason *toward* the truth. We reason *toward the exit.* Our intelligence stops being a searchlight looking for what's real and becomes a *lawyer* hired to defend what we already wanted. The smarter you are, the *better* the lawyer — which means, terrifyingly, the more capable you are of building an airtight case for staying exactly where you are.

Now go back to the room in Mecca.

The truth that had landed was *"you and what you worship besides Allah are fuel for the Fire."* That is a hammer-blow to a man who has built his whole life, his ancestry, his social standing on those idols. It *demands* a response. It demands change.

And what does the opponent reach for? Not an engagement with the truth. A *clever counter.* "Ah — but if everything worshipped besides Allah is in the Fire, then what about ʿĪsā, whom the Christians worship? Got you."

It is a brilliant little move. It feels like a checkmate. And the crowd *loves* it — *idhā qawmuka minhu yaṣiddūn* — they erupt, because their man has found them all an exit. He has handed the entire room permission to *not deal with* the thing that was demanding they change.

[PAUSE]

But here is what the very next ayah does. Allah does not even dignify the argument as a serious question. He says: *mā ḍarabūhu laka illā jadalan — bal hum qawmun khaṣimūn* — "They only raised it to *you* as a dispute. Rather, they are a contentious people" (43:58).

Listen to that diagnosis. *Jadalan* — as *dispute*, as *wrangling.* And *khaṣimūn* — a *contentious people,* people whose nature is to *contend.* Allah is naming the disease underneath the clever argument. The argument was never a sincere question — because a sincere question *wants an answer.* This was a weapon. Its purpose was not to *find* truth but to *escape* it. (And the answer to it is trivial, as the surah goes on to show: ʿĪsā never asked to be worshipped, was a servant whom Allah favored, and the rule is about false objects of worship, not about a righteous prophet wrongly worshipped by others. The "gotcha" was empty. But they never wanted the answer.)

Here is the unsettling turn, and I want you to take it personally, because I have to take it personally too.

The danger here is not *being stupid.* It is being **clever.** The faster your mind is, the faster it can hand you an escape hatch from a truth you don't want to face. Every one of us has a brilliant internal lawyer, on retainer, ready at a moment's notice to find the "but what about—" that lets us off the hook. Ready to find the one flaw in the messenger so we can ignore the message. Ready to change the subject to *someone else's* sin so we never have to look at our own.

So the practical question — the thing to carry into tomorrow — is this. **When someone says something true that costs you something, notice your first internal move.** Is it *"let me understand that"*? Or is it *"let me find the counter"*? Because the speed with which you reach for the counter-argument is the exact measure of how much the truth threatened you. The clever rebuttal that arrives *instantly* is almost never wisdom. It is, far more often, the lawyer rising to his feet before the witness has even finished speaking.

The sincere person, hearing a hard truth, gets *slower.* The evasive person gets *faster.* That's the tell.

### Theme Two: Laughter as a Wall

Let me take you somewhere you'll recognize from the inside.

Have you ever told someone something serious — something that mattered to you, something vulnerable — and they *laughed?* Not a cruel laugh, necessarily. Sometimes a nervous one. And you felt the conversation die. You felt the shutter come down. The laugh wasn't an *attack,* exactly. It was a *door closing.*

Here is something psychologists understand well: laughter is not only joy. Laughter is also one of our most powerful **defense mechanisms.** When something gets too close — too threatening, too heavy, too demanding — we laugh, and the laugh discharges the tension *without us ever having to deal with what caused it.* The nervous giggle at a funeral. The joke cracked in the middle of a serious accusation. The whole room laughing so that nobody has to sit in the discomfort of a truth that just entered.

This is *exactly* what the word *yaṣiddūn* captured for us. Remember — loud avoidance. A commotion whose entire function is to *not deal with the thing.* The crowd's laughter in this ayah is not the laughter of people who find something funny. It is the laughter of people who find something **threatening** — and who have discovered that if you laugh loudly enough, fast enough, *together* enough, you never have to feel the threat.

And notice that word — *together.* This is the third movement, and it matters. *Qawmuka* — "your *people.*" A *crowd.* This is not one man laughing alone. It is a *collective* eruption. And there is a terrible safety in that.

[PAUSE]

Truth is hard to face *alone.* But it is almost impossible to face in a *crowd that has already decided to laugh.* When everyone around you is shrieking with derision, the social cost of being the one person who goes quiet and says *"wait... is that actually true?"* becomes enormous. The laughter of the group is a kind of gravity. It pulls every individual into the same reflex. Nobody has to take responsibility, because *everybody* is doing it. The crowd laughs as one organism, and inside that organism, no single conscience ever has to wake up.

This is why mockery is the favored weapon against prophets throughout the Quran. *"And never did a messenger come to them but that they ridiculed him"* (15:11, 36:30, and elsewhere). Why ridicule, of all things? Why not argument? Because **ridicule is the one response that requires no thought and grants total immunity.** To argue with a prophet, you'd have to engage his claim — and engaging is dangerous, because the claim might be true. But to *laugh* at him? Laughter needs no premises. It builds a wall that no argument can climb. And it lets the whole crowd feel *superior* to the very thing they are too afraid to examine.

[PAUSE]

Now turn this inward, gently, because this is where it touches us.

We don't laugh at prophets. But we have our own versions of the wall. When the reminder comes — from a friend, from a khuṭbah, from a verse, from that quiet voice at 3am — and it asks something real of us, do we *sit with it?* Or do we reach for the deflection? "That's a bit extreme." "Easy for them to say." A dismissive little smile. A change of subject. A scroll to the next thing. These are all *small versions of yaṣiddūn* — small commotions we make so that the truth, which had landed, never gets to do its work.

The truth came *to do something to you.* That's what a struck example is for — to land, to fix, to change the metal. The wall of laughter — or dismissal, or distraction — exists to make sure it *bounces off* instead.

So here is the practice, and it is simple but it is not easy. **When something true makes you want to laugh it off, deflect it, or scroll past it — stop. Stay. Feel the discomfort instead of discharging it.** That discomfort is not your enemy. The discomfort is the truth *trying to land.* The whole spiritual game, in that one moment, is whether you let it land or build a wall against it. Don't be the crowd that erupts. Be the one person who goes quiet and says, *"wait."*

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this reflection are the same insight seen from two directions.

The *linguistic* journey showed us the **anatomy** of the moment: a truth *struck* into the world (*ḍuriba*), a reaction firing with *zero delay* (*idhā*), taking the form of *loud avoidance* (*yaṣiddūn*).

The *thematic* journey showed us the **meaning** of that anatomy: the clever argument that exists to *escape* truth rather than find it, and the laughter that builds a *wall* so the truth can never land.

They are the same thing. The *idhā* — the no-gap, the instant reflex — *is* motivated reasoning at the speed of the nervous system. The *yaṣiddūn* — the loud turning-away — *is* laughter-as-a-wall. The grammar and the psychology are describing one human reality: **the defense that fires before the mind can engage.**

And see how perfectly this sits inside *Az-Zukhruf,* the surah of ornament. This whole surah is about being dazzled by the surface and missing the substance. And what is mockery? Mockery is the ultimate *surface* response — all glitter, all noise, all show, with *nothing underneath.* The crowd's laughter is the gold leaf over an empty argument. It *looks* like victory. It *sounds* like confidence. But strike it, and it's hollow.

### The Architecture

Notice now the shape of the ayah itself, which we can finally see clearly:

The sentence is built around a *collision.* On one side: **وَلَمَّا ضُرِبَ ... مَثَلًا** — the slow, deliberate, weighty act of a truth being *struck and set in place.* On the other side: **إِذَا ... يَصِدُّونَ** — the instant, thoughtless, grating eruption. And the hinge between them — the single word on which the whole verse turns — is **إِذَا.** *Suddenly.*

The entire moral universe of this ayah lives in that hinge. On one side of *idhā,* a truth that took eternity to prepare and was driven home with care. On the other side, a reaction that took *no time at all.* The verse is built to make you feel the obscenity of that mismatch: *how could something so weighty be met by something so instant?*

That mismatch is the warning. That mismatch is us, more often than we'd like to admit.

### Questions to Carry

1. When someone tells me a hard truth, what is my *first* internal move — to understand it, or to defeat it? And how fast does that move arrive?
2. Where in my life do I use a clever argument as an *escape hatch* — a "but what about—" that lets me avoid changing?
3. What truths have I *laughed off,* deflected, or scrolled past — not because they were false, but because they were *threatening?*
4. When the crowd around me erupts in dismissal, am I the one organism in the body, or the one conscience that goes quiet and says *"wait"?*
5. The next time the truth tries to land in me — will I let it strike the metal, or will I build a wall at the speed of reflex?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When a truth was driven home with the weight of a hammer-blow, they answered it not with a thought but with a reflex — proving that the loudest laughter is often only the fastest wall.

### The Closing Invitation

I am not asking you to never laugh, or to take every clever argument as malice, or to treat your own quick mind as an enemy. I am inviting you to *watch the gap.* To notice the space — or the absence of space — between the moment a truth arrives and the moment you react to it. Because in that gap is your freedom. The crowd in this ayah had no gap; their reaction owned them. But you can *widen the gap.* You can learn to pause where they erupted. You can become the kind of person who, when a hard truth lands, gets *slower* — who lets it strike, and stay, and do its work. That pause is the whole of the spiritual life, lived in a single second.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You who strike the example so that the truth may land — do not let me be among those who meet Your truth with a reflex. Slow my heart at the moment of hearing. Widen the space between the truth and my response, and fill that space with You. And when everything in me wants to laugh a hard truth away, give me the courage to go quiet, and stay, and let it land. Āmīn.
