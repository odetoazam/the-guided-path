# God in the Heaven, God in the Earth: The One Allegiance for a Divided Self

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by simply placing the words in front of you.

> وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى فِى ٱلسَّمَآءِ إِلَٰهٌ وَفِى ٱلْأَرْضِ إِلَٰهٌ ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْحَكِيمُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ

*Wa huwa alladhī fī-s-samāʾi ilāhun wa fī-l-arḍi ilāh. Wa huwa-l-Ḥakīmu-l-ʿAlīm.*

"And it is He who is God in the heaven and God in the earth, and He is the All-Wise, the All-Knowing."

Eleven words. Read it once and it can pass right by you, because the mind hears it as something it already agrees with — *of course God is everywhere, who's arguing?* And that agreement is exactly the danger. When the intellect nods too quickly, the heart never has to move. So let me slow this down, because there is something inside this sentence that the people who first heard it found genuinely scandalous. It overturned the way they had organized the entire universe.

### Rebuild the world

To feel what this ayah did, you have to step out of your inherited monotheism for a moment and into the imagination of the Meccan listener.

For that listener, the divine was not one thing. It was a *bureaucracy*. There was a high god, yes — even the idolaters of Mecca affirmed Allah as the supreme Creator. But beneath Him stretched a whole administration of intermediate powers. There were the angels, whom they called His daughters. There were the tribal idols, each with its own jurisdiction — a god you went to for rain, a god you went to for fertility, a god of this valley, a god of that trade route. The sacred was *zoned*. Each power had a territory, a department, a desk. You worshipped locally. You worshipped by domain.

And this whole surah, Az-Zukhruf — the surah named after *gold ornament*, after the glittering surface-things people mistake for substance — has been patiently dismantling that bureaucracy, claim by claim. It has refuted the idea that the angels are daughters. It has refuted the blind loyalty to *what we found our fathers doing*. It has just finished addressing the figure of Jesus, lest he too be slotted into the administration as another deity.

And now, near the end, the surah delivers the line that collapses the whole structure at once.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I want you to sit with the discomfort of not yet having the answer.

The verse says God is *ilāh* — an object of worship — **in the heaven**. And then it says it again: God is *ilāh* — an object of worship — **in the earth**. The same word, twice. One realm, then the other.

Why say it twice? If you wanted to express that God is everywhere, the efficient sentence is *"He is God everywhere,"* or simply *"He is the only God."* Instead the Quran splits the cosmos into two and plants the same word in each half. **That repetition is not filler. It is the entire argument.** And to understand what it's arguing against — and what it's quietly doing to your own divided heart — we have to walk into the grammar.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word *ilāh* — and why it is *not* the word for "God-the-Creator"

Let me teach you something about the Arabic before we touch the verse, because if you don't have this distinction, the verse will say far less to you than it actually says.

In Arabic there is a difference between *describing what something is* and *describing what something is to you*. Take the English word "ruler." I can say "Elizabeth was a ruler" — a fact about her. Or I can say "Elizabeth was my ruler" — a statement about a *relationship of allegiance*, about who I obeyed. The second is not really about her at all. It's about me. It tells you where my loyalty was directed.

Arabic has a word like that for the divine. The word **إِلَٰه** — *ilāh* — comes from a root the lexicographers connect with turning toward something in longing, taking refuge in it, being utterly devoted to it. An *ilāh* is not merely *a being who exists out there*. An *ilāh* is **a thing that is worshipped, a thing the heart bends toward, a thing you organize your life around.** Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt* both anchor the root in this sense of the one *adored* and *turned to*.

Now hold that, because here is the move the verse makes.

This same surah already conceded that the idolaters affirmed Allah as Creator — they would have told you He made the heavens. So the verse does **not** say, "He is the One who *created* the heaven and *created* the earth." That they already accepted, and it changed nothing in how they lived. Instead it says He is *ilāh* in the heaven and *ilāh* in the earth — **the One worshipped here, the One worshipped there.**

Do you feel the shift? Al-Baghawi transmits the reading of Qatāda in exactly these terms: *yuʿbadu fī-s-samāʾi wa fī-l-arḍ* — "He is *worshipped* in the heaven and in the earth." Not merely present. Not merely powerful. *Worshipped.* The verse is not making a claim about cosmic real estate. It is making a claim about **allegiance** — about where every heart in both realms is meant to bend.

Al-Muyassar sharpens this with a phrase worth carrying: *المعبود بحق* — not merely *the one who is worshipped* as a descriptive observation, but *the one with the rightful claim to worship*. There is a *ḥaqq* — a right, a truth, a legitimate entitlement — in this particular devotion that belongs to no other object of the heart. The verse is not observing that some people do in fact direct worship toward God in heaven and earth. It is declaring that He alone *deserves* it in both realms — that any heart redirecting its allegiance elsewhere is, in some precise sense, placing something without a rightful claim in the position of the One who possesses it absolutely.

[PAUSE]

And that is a far more demanding statement than "God exists everywhere." A deist can say God exists everywhere and sleep soundly. This verse says God is *the One worth turning to* everywhere — which means there is no zone of your life, no department, no jurisdiction, where some *other* thing is allowed to sit on the throne of your devotion.

That is what *ilāh* costs.

### Element Two: The repetition that splits the cosmos in two

Now we return to the thing that stopped us at the start. The word *ilāh* appears **twice**, once for the heaven, once for the earth.

Let me teach the device first. In rhetoric, when you split a single reality into two named halves and then assert the same thing of both, you are doing something specific: you are pre-empting the listener's instinct to *separate* them. If I tell you "I'll love you in the good times and I'll love you in the hard times," I have not given you new information about two kinds of love. I have *blocked* the assumption that the hard times are an exception. The split exists only so that the sameness can be hammered across it.

Watch what the verse does with that.

The Meccan mind had already split the cosmos. The heaven was the domain of the high celestial powers — the angels, the stars, the lofty. The earth was the domain of the local idols — the stones, the shrines, the tribal patrons. Two zones, two pantheons, two registers of worship. The verse takes that exact division — السَّمَاء *and* الْأَرْض, heaven *and* earth — accepts the listener's own map, and then drops the **same single word** into both squares: *ilāh… ilāh.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The verse doesn't argue against their map. It *uses* their map against them. It says, in effect: *you who have carved reality into upper and lower jurisdictions — fine. Now look at who actually fills each one. Up there: God. Down here: the same God. There is no second desk. There is no local authority. The administration you imagined is empty in every office but One.*

Al-Qurtubi reads the verse precisely as a refutation — *takdhīb*, a giving-the-lie — to the claim that Allah has a partner or a child. The doubling is the instrument of that refutation. Every place you might have hidden a rival deity, the verse has already arrived first and written *ilāh* — the *same* ilāh — over the door.

And here is a detail the scholars preserve that shows how hard this point was being pressed. Al-Qurtubi reports a non-canonical reading — *contrary to the written muṣḥaf*, he is careful to say, and attributed to ʿUmar and to Ibn Masʿūd — in which the phrasing runs even more starkly. The very existence of such transmitted glosses tells you that the early community felt the weight here: the entire force of the verse hangs on the listener understanding that the One in the heaven and the One in the earth are **not two**. (We hold to the muṣḥaf reading; I mention the gloss only as evidence of how intently the meaning was being guarded.)

So the repetition is not redundancy. **The repetition is the demolition of polytheism's floor plan.** One word, written twice, erasing an entire imagined hierarchy.

### Element Three: The seal — *al-Ḥakīm, al-ʿAlīm*

The verse could have stopped at "God in the heaven, God in the earth." The tawhid is complete. But it doesn't stop. It closes with two names: **ٱلْحَكِيمُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ** — *al-Ḥakīm*, the All-Wise; *al-ʿAlīm*, the All-Knowing.

First, a note on their form, because the shape of these words carries meaning. Both follow the Arabic pattern *faʿīl* — the pattern of *ḥakīm* and *ʿalīm*. This is not the pattern of a passing action; it is the pattern of a settled, intrinsic, permanent quality. The difference is like the gap in English between "she knew that" and "she is *knowing*" — between an event and a *nature*. These names don't tell you God did something wise once. They tell you wisdom and knowledge are **what He is**, woven into Him, not switched on for the occasion.

Now — why *these* two names, here, as the seal of a verse about oneness?

Let me reach into your own life to answer it, because this is where the verse stops being theology and starts being personal.

When a human heart resists surrendering to a single authority, it is almost always for one of two reasons. Listen to them.

The first fear: *"What if the One in charge is not actually wise? What if behind it all there is no plan — just randomness, just a roll of dice, and my obedience is being poured into a void?"* That is the fear of a meaningless universe.

To that fear the verse answers: **al-Ḥakīm.** The One you are bending toward governs with wisdom — al-Jalalayn and al-Baghawi both gloss it here as wisdom *fī tadbīri khalqih*, in the masterful governing of His creation. Nothing in His handling of you is arbitrary. There is a *tadbīr*, a design, beneath what looks like chaos.

Al-Muyassar extends the scope of this wisdom in a direction worth noting: the Ḥakīm is the one who perfected His creation (*aḥkama khalqahu*) and equally perfected His **revealed law** (*atqana sharʿahu*). The same intelligence that orders the cosmos has also ordered the guidance addressed to you. The Quran you are reading, the sharīʿa you are being invited to live within — these are not separate from the creative wisdom being claimed here. They are that wisdom, addressed to human beings who can hear it. God's wisdom reaches the universe silently, through natural order. It reaches human beings explicitly, through speech. This very sentence is an instance of that: wisdom speaking into human language so that a divided heart might be shown what one allegiance looks like.

The second fear is quieter and more intimate: *"Even if there is a plan — what if I am not in it? What if I am too small, too obscure, my particular situation too tangled for the One in charge to actually see?"* That is the fear of being unseen.

To that fear the verse answers: **al-ʿAlīm.** The same scholars gloss it *biMaṣāliḥihim* — the One who knows His creation down to their *interests*, their needs, the specific good of each one. He doesn't govern the universe in general while losing you in the crowd. The wisdom that runs the cosmos is the same knowledge that has counted you.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what these two names do, standing together at the end? They remove the two escape hatches the heart uses to avoid surrender. You cannot say "I won't trust Him because it's all random" — *al-Ḥakīm* closes that door. You cannot say "I won't trust Him because He's too vast to notice me" — *al-ʿAlīm* closes that one. **Wise enough to have a plan; knowing enough to have put you in it.** The verse establishes the one God in both realms, and then, before you can flinch away, it tells you that this God is precisely the kind of God you can afford to hand yourself over to.

### The bridge

Look at what the words have shown us. *Ilāh* told us this is about worship, not mere existence. The doubled *ilāh* told us no realm, no zone, no jurisdiction holds a rival. And *al-Ḥakīm al-ʿAlīm* told us the One who fills every realm is wise enough and knowing enough to be trusted with all of it.

Before we cross into what this demands of our actual lives, one detail from the surrounding verses deserves a moment. The instruction to the Prophet immediately before this ayah — *fā-dhar-hum*, *leave them to their plunging and their play until they meet their promised Day* — is followed, without pause, by the declaration of absolute divine sovereignty. Al-Tabari's treatment draws the sequence out: first, confident disengagement from the argument; then, from the stillness that follows, the serene naming of what is simply true. The claim that God fills every realm is not made while locked in debate, while trying to out-argue opponents. It is made from the posture of one who has no need to prove it, only to state it.

That quality — tawhid spoken from stillness rather than grasped at in argument — will matter when we ask what this verse asks of us.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange, divided way most of us actually live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The God of every room — healing the partitioned self

Let me start where you actually live, not in seventh-century Mecca.

You are not one self. You are several, and you change between them as you move through your day. There is the self that shows up at work — competent, performing, careful about what it reveals. There is the self at home with family — softer, or sharper, depending. There is the self online, curating. There is the self alone at 2 a.m. with the screen lighting your face. There is even a "mosque self," a "prayer-mat self," that puts on a particular voice. We don't usually notice it, but most of us keep these selves in **separate rooms.** And — here is the uncomfortable part — we keep a different god in each room.

What do I mean? In the room called *career*, the thing we actually bend toward, organize our anxiety around, sacrifice for, is success. That's the *ilāh* of that room. In the room called *social life*, the thing we fear and serve is the opinion of people — their approval is the deity we offer worship to there. In the room called *desire*, late at night, something else sits on the throne. We are, quietly, polytheists of architecture. We have zoned our own hearts the way the Meccans zoned their cosmos: a different power ruling each jurisdiction.

[PAUSE]

And we tell ourselves this is normal, even healthy — *compartmentalization*, the psychologists call it. Keep work at work. Keep faith in its lane. Don't let the rooms bleed into each other.

Now hear the verse land on exactly this.

*"God in the heaven, and God in the earth."*

The verse refuses the partition. It says the same single word — *ilāh* — over the upper realm and the lower realm, and what it is really declaring is that **there are not supposed to be separate rooms with separate gods.** The One you turn to in the heaven of your prayer is the One you are expected to turn to in the earth of your work, your desire, your buried grief, your 2 a.m. self. There is no carve-out in the grammar. No zone where the verse permits a different word to occupy the same position. *Ilāh* goes where *ilāh* goes — in the heaven and in the earth — and its referent does not change between them.

[PAUSE]

This is what makes *tawhīd al-ulūhiyya* — the oneness of worship — categorically different from the mere affirmation that God exists uniquely. The Meccan polytheists largely accepted God's unrivaled existence as the supreme creative power. What they could not bring themselves to do was *worship uniquely* — to carry one allegiance intact across every domain of their lives rather than parceling out devotion to different authorities by jurisdiction. The verse targets that failure precisely. It does not argue about who created what. It asks who has the rightful claim on your heart, in every room, at every hour. And it gives a single answer.

Think about the practical shape of the polytheism the verse was addressing. Each idol answered a specific anxiety: rain for the farmer, protection for the traveler, fertility for the family. The shrines were not philosophical positions; they were *anxiety-management strategies*, each imagined power handling a particular territory of fear. The Quran's insistence on a single *ilāh* in every realm means you can no longer patch. You have to integrate. You have to carry the same One into the room of financial dread, the room of relational failure, the room of ambition, the room of desire. And you have to ask honestly: is it the same One in there? Or have you, quietly, installed a substitute?

Integration is not a spiritual refinement for advanced practitioners. It is, the verse insists, the basic shape of a life that is actually oriented correctly.

### Theme Two: A truth that does not need to be argued

Having established what the verse says, let me stay with something about *how* it says it — because the manner carries its own lesson.

The instruction to the Prophet in the preceding verse is *fā-dhar-hum*: *leave them*. Step back from the theological dispute. Let the idolaters plunge and play. And then, in the very next breath — or perhaps more accurately, from the stillness produced by that disengagement — comes the declaration: *He is the God of heaven and God of the earth.* The claim of absolute sovereignty is not asserted in the middle of the fight. It arrives after the argument has been set aside.

[PAUSE]

There is a posture encoded in this sequencing. Tawhid, as this verse frames it, does not need to be defended with anxious urgency. It is not a fragile thesis that requires constant reinforcement against all comers. The person who genuinely inhabits this conviction — that God alone has the rightful claim on every realm — does not need to win every argument about it to remain grounded in it. The truth does not depend on the opponents' concession. It is prior to their opinions, and it will be what it is regardless of the outcome of the debate.

Al-Muyassar's *المعبود بحق* points in the same direction. The *ḥaqq* — the legitimacy, the rightness — of this worship is not established by winning disputes. It is the prior truth that disputes are *about*. God's rightful claim on every heart in both realms does not wait for human acknowledgment to become real. It simply is, whether or not the Meccans agreed, whether or not our own distracted selves remember it from hour to hour.

There is a quiet form of fragmentation — not theological but psychological — that many believers live inside without naming it: the need to constantly defend the faith that is supposed to be their anchor. We argue for tawhid with a desperation that implies the faith depends on winning the argument. This verse models the alternative. *Leave them to their play.* And then, from stillness: *He is the God of heaven and earth.* That sequence is itself instruction — not just about the content of tawhid, but about the posture from which it is inhabited.

### Theme Three: The seal is a personal address

The two names that close the verse — *al-Ḥakīm al-ʿAlīm* — have been unpacked in their linguistic form and their theological function. Let me bring them down one final level, because this is where the verse stops being cosmology and becomes intimate.

Most of us, in our honest moments, hold the idea of surrendering to a single divine authority at arm's length. Not because we have thought it through philosophically and rejected it. But because two quieter fears sit in the way — fears we may never have named.

The first: *What if the One in charge is not actually wise? What if what looks like design is really drift — randomness dressed up in significance, suffering without meaning, goodness without reason?* If that is true, then directing all of your allegiance toward one point is the most expensive bet you can make in a universe that is not listening.

To that fear the verse says: *al-Ḥakīm.* The one whose wisdom, as al-Muyassar preserves it, perfected creation and perfected the revealed guidance both. Nothing in the ordering of the world, and nothing in the guidance addressed to you, was haphazard. There is craft here — *tadbīr* — even where you cannot yet read it.

The second fear is quieter and more personal: *Even if there is a plan — am I in it? Am I too specific, too small, my situation too tangled and ordinary for the One who governs galaxies to have actually accounted for me?*

To that fear the verse says: *al-ʿAlīm.* The one who knows, as al-Jalalayn's gloss preserves it, *biMaṣāliḥihim* — the interests, the welfare, the particular good of each one. He does not govern the universe in general while losing the individuals to administrative overwhelm. The same knowing that holds the cosmos holds you, down to the specific texture of your situation, the unnamed question you carry, the grief that has not found a word yet.

[PAUSE]

These two names are not decorative bookends. They are the precise answer to the two precise fears that keep a divided heart from coming home. The verse has told you where to bring your allegiance — to the One who fills every realm, leaving no jurisdiction to a rival. And before you can raise the objection that seals most people's hesitation, the verse has already closed the door: the God who fills both realms is wise enough to have a plan and knowing enough to have placed you in it.

**Wise enough. Knowing enough.** Two words, placed at the end of eleven, doing the final work a divided heart needs done before it can afford to integrate.

## CLOSING

We began by noting the particular danger of the verse that gets agreed with too quickly. When the intellect nods, the heart never has to move.

Let me name the movement this verse is actually asking for.

It asks you to inventory your rooms — not to collapse the functional distinctions of your life into a blur, but to notice who sits on the throne in each. What is the thing you organize your anxiety around in each domain of your existence? What, if it were threatened, would feel like the floor giving way? *That* is the functional *ilāh* of that room.

Then it asks a single question: is it the same One?

The verse has already told you what the answer is meant to look like. *Ilāh* in the heaven. The same *ilāh* in the earth. One word, two realms, no exception written in. And the God who is being named in both is — precisely — *al-Ḥakīm al-ʿAlīm*: wise enough to have ordered all of this, knowing enough to have included you in it.

The surrender this verse invites is not into an indifferent vastness. It is into the widest, most attentive, most precisely ordered knowing there is.

Eleven words. Enough to overturn a cosmology — and, held long enough, a life.
