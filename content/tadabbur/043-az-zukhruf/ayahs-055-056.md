# When They Grieved Us
### Surah Az-Zukhruf 43:55–56

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read the words first, slowly, and then let us spend the next hour discovering what is buried inside them.

> **فَلَمَّآ ءَاسَفُونَا ٱنتَقَمۡنَا مِنۡهُمۡ فَأَغۡرَقۡنَٰهُمۡ أَجۡمَعِينَ ۝ فَجَعَلۡنَٰهُمۡ سَلَفࣰا وَمَثَلࣰا لِّلۡءَاخِرِينَ**

*Fa-lammā āsafūnā, intaqamnā minhum fa-aghraqnāhum ajmaʿīn. Fa-jaʿalnāhum salafan wa-mathalan lil-ākhirīn.*

"So when they grieved and angered Us, We took retribution from them, and We drowned them all. And We made them a precedent and an example for those who came after."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel these words, you have to stand where the first listeners stood — inside the long argument that fills the verses just before this one.

Pharaoh has been talking. For nine verses he has been talking. He stands before his court and he gestures at the land beneath his feet. *Is not the kingdom of Egypt mine?* he says. *And these rivers that flow beneath me?* (43:51). He looks at Moses — a man with a speech impediment, a man without gold bracelets, a man without an escort of angels marching in formation — and he sneers. *Am I not better than this despicable creature who can barely make himself clear?* (43:52). And the verse that comes right before ours tells us the most chilling detail of all: **فَٱسۡتَخَفَّ قَوۡمَهُۥ فَأَطَاعُوهُ** — *he made light of his people, and they obeyed him* (43:54). He played them. He worked them like an instrument. And they let him.

So picture it. A civilization at the height of its confidence. An empire of stone and river and gold. A ruler so secure that he treats prophecy itself as a comedy routine for his courtiers. There is no thunderclap in the air. There is no sign that anything is about to change. The mockery is flowing freely and the laughter is real.

And then — into that laughter — comes verse 55.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, the thing that should stop you at the doorway and not let you pass too quickly.

When Allah finally describes the moment the sword falls, He does not say *when they disbelieved.* He does not say *when they rejected Moses.* He does not say *when they enslaved the children of Israel* or *when they killed the newborns* or *when they crossed every line.*

He says: **فَلَمَّآ ءَاسَفُونَا** — *when they grieved Us.*

And the word He chooses — *āsafūnā* — is, in the Arabic ear, not first and foremost the word for rage. It is the word a father uses when he weeps. It is the word on the lips of Yaʿqūb when his heart breaks over a lost son.

Why would the destruction of Pharaoh — the most spectacular act of divine retribution in the entire Quran — be introduced with the vocabulary of *grief*?

Stay with me. That question is the whole reflection.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Āsafūnā* — the word that holds two feelings at once

Before we open this word, I want to teach you something about how Arabic roots work, because without it the word will pass you by.

In English, our words tend to mean one thing and carry one emotional temperature. "Grief" is cold and heavy. "Anger" is hot and sharp. They live in different rooms of the heart. But Arabic has a category of words that refuse to be split — single roots that hold two emotional states *together*, as if the language knows something our categories miss: that some feelings arrive fused, and pulling them apart falsifies them.

Hold that idea. Now let us open the word.

The verb is **ءَاسَفُونَا** — *āsafūnā* — and it comes from the root **أسف** (*a-s-f*). This is a Form IV verb, and grammatically Form IV here is causative-transitive: it means *they caused Us to experience asaf.* They did something *to* a feeling. The "Us" is the object. They reached in and produced a state.

So everything turns on one question: **what is *asaf*?**

Let's go to the lexicon — to where the word actually lives in the Arabic language, not where we wish it lived.

The root *asaf* carries, by the testimony of the classical lexicographers, **two meanings at once: grief and anger.** Al-Rāghib al-Aṣfahānī, in his Mufradāt, gathers them under one definition — *asaf* is sorrow and anger *together.* Not one or the other depending on the sentence. Both, braided.

Now watch what the Quran does with this single root, because this is where it becomes breathtaking.

Take the same root and listen to where else it appears.

When Yaʿqūb — Jacob — has lost Yūsuf, and the years have piled up, and his eyes have whitened from weeping, what does he cry? **يَا أَسَفَىٰ عَلَىٰ يُوسُفَ** — *Yā asafā ʿalā Yūsuf!* (12:84). "Oh, my **asaf** over Yusuf!" That is pure grief. That is a father's broken heart given a name.

Now take Mūsā — Moses — coming down the mountain to find his people worshipping a calf. How does the Quran describe his face? **غَضۡبَٰنَ أَسِفࣰا** — *ghaḍbāna asifan* (7:150, 20:86). "Angry and grieving." Here the root tilts toward heat — the anger of a leader betrayed — but the grief is right there beside it, named in the same breath.

[PAUSE]

Now come back to our verse and feel the weight of what Allah has done.

The very same root — the root of Jacob's tears, the root of Moses's grieving fury — is the root Allah selects to describe what Pharaoh's people provoked in their Lord. **آسَفُونَا.** *They produced asaf in Us.*

Let's sit with this. Allah could have used a hundred words for displeasure. The Arabic of the Quran is not short of vocabulary for wrath — *ghaḍab*, *sakhaṭ*, *maqt*, *ghayẓ*. Al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, and al-Tabari all gloss *āsafūnā* with the plain word **أغضبونا** (*aghḍabūnā*), "they angered Us," and that is correct; that is the operative sense in this context. Al-Muyassar sharpens it further, adding the specific cause: *they angered Us through their disobedience, and their rejection of Moses and the signs he brought.* But notice that they are *translating* a word that the Arabic ear hears as something richer than bare anger. They render the meaning; the root keeps its color.

And the color is this: **this is not the anger of someone who flew off the handle. It is the anger that has grief inside it.** It is the displeasure of one who *waited.* Who was patient for a long, long time. Who sent a messenger, and signs, and more signs — the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood (7:133) — reprieve after reprieve. This is not a short fuse. This is the response that comes only *after* the long fuse has burned all the way down.

Here's what's remarkable. In our world, when we say someone "lost their temper," we mean they lost control — something snapped, reason left, and rage took the wheel. The word *asaf* points us toward the precise opposite. This is displeasure that arrives *with full knowledge*, weighed, grieved over, withheld as long as withholding was just. The grief in the word is the fingerprint of the patience that came before it.

#### A necessary caution — handling this word with *tanzīh*

Now I have to slow us down and be careful, because we are talking about Allah, and the Arabic word carries human emotional texture, and we must not drag the Creator down into the limits of the creation.

The classical scholars saw this immediately. Al-Qurṭubī, commenting on this very verse, records the discussion. He cites Ibn ʿAbbās — through al-Ḍaḥḥāk — that *āsafūnā* means *they enraged Us, they provoked Us* (ghāẓūnā wa-aghḍabūnā), and through another narration that it means *they angered Us* (askhaṭūnā). And then he raises the theological question directly: anger, attributed to Allah, cannot mean what it means in us — a churning of the blood, a loss of composure, a change in the self. Al-Qurṭubī, drawing on al-Qushayrī, lays out the two ways the tradition understands it: either Allah's "anger" here means **His will to punish** (*irādat al-ʿuqūba*) — which is an attribute of His essence — or it means **the punishment itself** (*ʿayn al-ʿuqūba*) — which is an attribute of His act. Either way, it is not an emotional disturbance in God. It is not God *losing* anything.

So when I say the word carries grief, I do not mean Allah is wounded the way Yaʿqūb was wounded. Allah is beyond need, beyond loss, beyond change. What I mean is subtler and, I think, more beautiful: **Allah chose to describe His response to Pharaoh in language that the human heart reads as the displeasure-after-patience, the anger that is the opposite of careless rage.** The word is a window He opened *for us*, in vocabulary we can feel, so that we would never imagine His justice as cold, mechanical, or hasty. He named it with a word that, in our own mouths, means a heart that grieved before it acted.

What does this single word reveal? **That divine retribution, when it finally comes, is never the first thing tried. It is the last.**

### Element Two: *Intaqamnā* — and the timing word that governs everything

Now look at what follows: **ٱنتَقَمۡنَا مِنۡهُمۡ** — *intaqamnā minhum* — "We took retribution from them."

The verb is from the root **نقم** (*n-q-m*), in Form VIII — *intaqama.* And I want to be precise about what this word means in Arabic, because in English "vengeance" sounds petty and personal, like a grudge being settled. That is not the Arabic sense. *Naqama* is to find something blameworthy, to disapprove of a wrong, and then to requite it. *Intiqām* is requital *for an offense* — it is justice with a moral cause underneath it, not appetite. It answers a *wrong*; it does not indulge a *want*.

Al-Muyassar adds a texture worth pausing over: the retribution arrived as *ʿājil al-ʿadhāb* — *the hastened punishment,* the swift punishment that was brought forward quickly. That detail changes the picture slightly. The patience was long; the punishment was fast. Allah does not prolong the moment of consequence. When the threshold is reached, the retribution arrives without delay. There is mercy even in that swiftness — the drawn-out punishment is not the style of the Most Merciful.

But the word I really want you to put your finger on is the small one at the front of verse 55, the hinge the whole sentence swings on: **فَلَمَّا** — *fa-lammā* — "so when."

*Lammā* is a word of timing. It means "at the point when," "the moment that." It builds a threshold. It says: *up until here, this; and then, at this line, that.*

Do you see what it does to the verse? It draws a line in time. On one side of the line: *āsafūnā* — the long provocation, the years of it, the grief accumulating. On the other side of the line: *intaqamnā* — the response. And the *fa-* attached to the front — "so," "then" — tells you the second thing follows *immediately* once the threshold is crossed.

Here is what this construction quietly teaches. **The retribution was instant, but the threshold was not.** The drowning happened in a single morning. But the line it crossed had been approached, slowly, over an entire lifetime of Pharaoh's defiance. The speed of the punishment can fool you into thinking God is quick to anger. The *lammā* corrects you: the punishment was quick; the *patience* was long. What looks sudden from the outside was the end of something that had been building for years.

Notice something else, an absence worth reading. Allah does not list their crimes here. He does not say "when they killed the prophets and enslaved the believers and claimed divinity, *then* We took retribution." He compresses an entire dossier of tyranny into one verb — *āsafūnā* — and moves straight to the consequence. Why? Because by this point in the surah, the crimes have already been shown. The mockery, the boasting, the manipulation of the masses — we just watched it. The verse does not need to re-prosecute the case. It only needs to mark the moment the case *closed.*

What does this word reveal, set beside the last one? **That God's justice has a threshold, and the threshold is real.** Patience is not permission. Delay is not approval. The fuse is long — genuinely, mercifully long — but it is a fuse, and it has an end.

### Element Three: *Ajmaʿīn* — the totality, and *salafan wa-mathalan* — the sentence after the sentence

Two more words, then two more, because they complete the picture.

First: **فَأَغۡرَقۡنَٰهُمۡ أَجۡمَعِينَ** — *fa-aghraqnāhum ajmaʿīn* — "and We drowned them, **all of them.**"

The verb *aghraqnā* is Form IV from *gharaq*, the causative — "We caused them to drown." But it is *ajmaʿīn* — "all," "every last one," "in their entirety" — that I want you to hear. It is a word of completeness. Not most of them. Not the leadership while the soldiers scattered. **All.** The same army that crossed the parted sea chasing the believers — the whole apparatus of the empire that thought itself eternal — closed over and gone in a single tide.

There is a terrible symmetry the original listener would have caught. The very water Pharaoh boasted of — *these rivers that flow beneath me* (43:51) — the water that was his pride, his proof of dominion. Water became the instrument. The thing he pointed to as evidence that he was god closed over his head as evidence that he never was. The Nile he claimed turned into the sea that claimed him.

Now the second sentence, verse 56 — and this is the part people skip, but it may be the most haunting line in the passage: **فَجَعَلۡنَٰهُمۡ سَلَفࣰا وَمَثَلࣰا لِّلۡءَاخِرِينَ** — *fa-jaʿalnāhum salafan wa-mathalan lil-ākhirīn* — "And We made them a **precedent** and an **example** for those who came after."

Two words here, both load-bearing.

**سَلَفًا** — *salafan.* The root *s-l-f* means *that which has gone ahead, that which precedes.* A *salaf* is a predecessor, a forerunner, a vanguard. We use the word *al-salaf* honorably for the righteous early generations — those who went ahead of us in faith. But here the word has been stripped of its honorific color and assigned its most sobering semantic: they are made *salaf* in reckoning, not in righteousness.

Al-Tabari records a subtle textual disagreement about this very word. Most Kufan readers — other than ʿĀṣim — read it as **سُلُفًا** (*sulufan*), with ḍamma on both letters, treating it as a plural of *sālif* on the pattern of *khādim* giving *khadam* — "one who goes before a people, who marches out in front." The reading carried by the reciters of Medina, Basra, and ʿĀṣim — and confirmed by al-Jalālayn — is **سَلَفًا** (*salafan*), with fatḥa: the same morphological family, the same image. Both readings converge on one picture: they have been sent ahead as the vanguard of a particular outcome.

And al-Muyassar makes the force of that outcome explicit in a way that stops you cold: they became *salaf* not merely as historical predecessors but as *predecessors for those who would do as they did — deserving punishment as they deserved it.* The phrase he uses is precise: *liman yaʿmalu mithla ʿamalihim mimman yaʾtī baʿdahum fī istiḥqāq al-ʿadhāb* — for those who come after them and act as they acted, in the deserving of punishment. The word is not passive chronology. Pharaoh's people did not merely happen to come first. They now *head a category.* Whoever walks their road inherits their station on the ledger.

This is the inverse of the salaf we honor. The righteous salaf went before us in faith, and we follow them toward mercy. These went before in defiance, and the Quran preserves them as a warning to everyone who would follow that road instead.

And then **مَثَلًا** — *mathalan.* The root *m-th-l*: likeness, similitude, parable. A *mathal* in Arabic is not just an example — it is a *living parable*, something whose story has been compressed into a form that others can carry and repeat. Al-Muyassar renders it directly: *ʿibra wa-ʿiẓa* — lesson and admonition — for all who come after. They became a *mathal* so that whoever hears their story might learn *before* reaching their end.

Here is what verse 56 quietly reveals about power and permanence. Pharaoh built in stone. He carved his name into monuments designed to outlast centuries. He literally shaped the landscape of his civilization. And none of that — not the stone, not the rivers, not the gold, not the name on the walls — is what made him eternal. What made him eternal was this verse. What made his end travel through every generation that came after was that Allah compressed his story into two lines of His own Speech.

The Quran is the most durable monument ever made. The stones of ancient Egypt still stand; but the lesson inside them, the meaning of what those stones mean, comes to us through revelation. Without this verse, Pharaoh is an artifact. *With* this verse, he is a *mathal* — a living parable, still speaking.

What do these final words reveal? **That to become a *mathal* in Allah's speech is to become permanent in a way no civilization ever planned and never wanted.** No empire builds monuments hoping to be remembered as a warning. But here Pharaoh's empire is precisely that — and the warning is inseparable from the mercy it contains, because a *mathal* is only issued to a people who still have time to hear it.

## PART 2: THE WORLD BEHIND THE WORDS

### What kind of God does this language reveal?

I want to step back now from the roots and the lexicons and ask the question that has been silently running underneath everything we have read.

What does this passage, this language, reveal about *who Allah is*?

There is a portrait that emerges if you read these two verses against the long surah they close. And the portrait is nothing like what people imagine when they think of divine punishment.

**The God who waits.** Read the Pharaoh passage from verse 46 onward. Moses arrives. Signs come. Pharaoh does not just reject them — he performs the rejection, staging it for an audience, building a cultural consensus against the prophet. He had help: the verse just before ours tells us *he made his people light* (*istaakhaffa qawmahu*) — he worked them, manipulated their self-regard, shaped public opinion. This is not a single act of impiety. This is a sustained, organized, deliberate campaign against revelation. And throughout all of it, the divine response is: *not yet.*

**The God whose patience is not absence.** One of the most dangerous misreadings of forbearance is to hear God's silence as approval. When Pharaoh boasts — when he points at the rivers and calls them his — there is no thunderclap. No immediate correction. And it would be very easy for Pharaoh to read that silence as confirmation: *if this were wrong, something would have happened by now.* But the word *āsafūnā* reveals what that silence actually was. It was not approval. It was not the absence of notice. It was grief being held. A threshold not yet crossed. Patience that, like all patience with a moral foundation, had an end.

**The God who grieves before He acts.** This is, I think, the most intimate and theologically careful thing these verses say. Allah does not describe Pharaoh's destruction in the language of cold cosmic machinery — a law violated, a consequence automatically triggered. He describes it in the language of *āsaf*: grief-anger, the displeasure that has sorrow braided into it. And the tradition immediately wraps that language in *tanzīh* — in the necessary reminder that Allah's "anger" is not the anger of a creature. But the *choice* of that word — when a hundred other words were available — tells us something. Allah wanted us to understand His justice not as mechanistic but as *felt*. Not as law executing itself, but as a response, *from Someone*, to a wrong *done to something real.*

This changes how we hear divine judgment, if we let it. The destruction of Pharaoh is not a fact about physics. It is a statement about *moral weight* — about what it means to repeatedly, systematically, publicly mock a messenger of God, manipulate a whole people into complicity, and refuse every offered exit.

### The complicity of the people — a detail al-Tabari does not let pass

Al-Tabari, in his commentary on the verse just before ours — *fa-istaakhaffa qawmahu fa-aṭāʿūhu* — pauses on a question that the surface reading might skip. Why did the people obey? Was it simply that Pharaoh was a persuasive tyrant?

His answer is more searching than that. He notes that they obeyed *because they were already a people departed from Allah's guidance — their hearts sealed against it.* The phrase he uses is precise: *ṭabaʿa ʿalā qulūbihim* — He had sealed upon their hearts. Pharaoh exploited a susceptibility that his people had cultivated themselves through their own prior rejection. He made them light (*istaakhaffahu*) because they had already chosen to be light. The tyrant found ready material.

This is a sobering detail that the two verses we are reading do not contradict — they confirm it. When Allah says *fa-aṭāʿūhu* — "and they obeyed him" — the record does not exonerate the mass of the people by pointing only at the leader. The *fa-* links obedience to lightness as cause to effect. They were moved because they were moveable. And they were moveable because of their own prior spiritual erosion.

In any age, in any scale, this is the more uncomfortable question the passage quietly asks: not only *who is playing the role of Pharaoh?* but also *what in me has been made light, and by whom, and did I notice?*

### The asymmetry of legacy

Here is a meditation I cannot get out of my mind when I read verse 56.

Pharaoh wanted to be remembered. That is evident from everything about him — the boasting in verse 51, the need for the courtiers to validate his superiority over Moses, the public performance of scorn. He needed an audience. He needed history.

He got it. But not the history he was building.

There is a crushing asymmetry in the way the Quran frames legacy. Pharaoh's legacy, as *he* understood it, was power demonstrated: rivers, armies, monuments, the sneering of a god-king at a stammering prophet. He thought he was building the argument *against* Moses with every speech he gave. What he was actually building — unknowingly, inevitably — was the material that Allah would use to make him a *mathal*. Every boast became evidence. Every performance of superiority became a frame for the fall. The very specificity of his arrogance — *these rivers beneath me, am I not better than this despicable one?* — is preserved in the Quran precisely so that the contrast with his end is complete.

This is what the word *salafan* points at in its deepest register. He went first — and everything he did was recorded not in his own monuments but in a text that would be recited five times a day in every generation and every tongue until the end of time. He wanted permanence. He received it in the form he least intended: as the opening entry in a category of those who deserved what he received.

## PART 3: WHAT THIS MEANS FOR US

### Sitting with this as a believer

Let me bring this down from the scope of civilizations and into something smaller and more personal.

The pattern these two verses encode is not only about Pharaoh. The Quran does not preserve Pharaoh's story as ancient history. It preserves it as *mathal* — as parable — precisely because the pattern repeats. The structure of the thing — *extended arrogance, extended patience, a threshold, swift consequence* — is a structure the Quran asks us to recognize.

What does that recognition ask of us?

First: **it asks us to take divine forbearance seriously, not casually.** The long fuse should not be read as a short one. When consequence does not arrive immediately after wrongdoing, the human instinct is one of two things — despair ("nothing matters") or complacency ("I got away with it"). Both are misreadings. The word *āsafūnā* with its *fa-lammā* — "so when" — tells us that the fuse burns whether or not we see it burning. The threshold is real. The timing is not ours to determine.

Second: **it asks us to be honest about complicity.** Al-Tabari's reading of *istaakhaffa qawmahu* cuts in both directions. There is a Pharaoh in the story. But there is also a people whose prior spiritual erosion made them moveable. The verse does not let the people off by blaming only the tyrant. They *obeyed.* The text records it plainly: *fa-aṭāʿūhu.* The question is not only: *who is playing the role of Pharaoh?* The question is also: *am I cultivating the lightness that can be made lighter?*

Third: **it asks us to think about what kind of legacy we are building.** The *mathal* of Pharaoh is still being recited. His empire is rubble. His name survives only as a title for oppressors. Whatever he thought he was building — that is not what survived. What survived is the meaning his story carries in divine speech, and he had no say in that.

The believer's version of this question is: *what is the story my life is building?* Not the one we narrate to ourselves, but the one that would be visible from outside — the one where the arc can be seen. The Quran invites us to read the *mathal* of Pharaoh not only as a warning against Pharaonic arrogance but as a prompt to audit our own arc — to ask whether the patience we are relying on is patience with us *toward* something better, or patience with us *toward* a threshold we have not noticed approaching.

### The mercy inside the warning

I want to end on something that I think often gets lost in passages about divine punishment.

A *mathal* is only issued to people who are alive and can hear it. Allah could have told the story of Pharaoh's destruction to no one, or recorded it only as dry historical fact. He preserved it as *mathal* — as a living lesson still being spoken — because there are always people on this side of the *lammā* for whom the parable is still useful. The warning is wrapped in a mercy: **you are receiving it, which means you are still before your own threshold.**

The verse does not say "We made them an example because there is nothing to learn." It says "We made them an example *for those who came after*" — *lil-ākhirīn* — for the ones who follow in time, the ones who still have time to follow differently. The last word of this surah passage is a word pointing forward. Not to Pharaoh. Not even to Moses. To *us*.

This is what tadabbur of these two verses leaves with me. Not a comfortable feeling. Not an easy peace. Something sharper and, in the end, more generous: the knowledge that divine justice is not hasty, not petty, not cold — that it grieves before it moves, and when it moves, it moves for something real. And the knowledge that I am reading this passage — that it reached me — means there is still time for whatever question these words were sent to ask.
