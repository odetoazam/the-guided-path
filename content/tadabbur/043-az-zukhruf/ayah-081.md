# If He Had a Son, I Would Be First to Worship Him

### قُلْ إِن كَانَ لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ وَلَدٌ فَأَنَا۠ أَوَّلُ ٱلْعَـٰبِدِينَ

*Qul in kāna lir-Raḥmāni waladun fa-anā awwalu-l-ʿābidīn*

**"Say: If the Most Merciful had a son, then I would be the first of the worshippers."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Let's go back to Mecca.

Not the Mecca of pilgrims and crowds you might picture today. A smaller, harder place. A city of clans, of trade caravans, of a sanctuary surrounded by stone figures. And inside that city, a theology — old, inherited, rarely examined — that the angels were the **daughters of God**.

Sit with how strange that belief was, even by its own logic. Because this same society despised daughters. We learn it elsewhere in the Quran: when one of them was told his wife had given birth to a girl, *his face would darken, and he would hide from his people in shame* (16:58). A daughter was, to them, a quiet humiliation. Something to be buried, in the worst cases, in the sand.

And yet — these were the beings they assigned to Allah. The very thing they found beneath their own dignity, they handed up to the Most High. Keep the sons for ourselves. Give God the daughters.

Surah Az-Zukhruf has been pulling at this thread for many verses. It is a surah about **gold ornaments** — *zukhruf* means the gilding, the decoration, the surface shine that dazzles the eye and distracts from substance. And one of the most ornamented, most decorated lies a human being can tell is the lie about God: that the Infinite needs to reproduce. That the Self-Sufficient requires an heir.

The surah has already shamed the inconsistency. It has already exposed the projection. And now, at this late point in the argument, you might expect the volume to rise. You might expect: *"There is no son! How dare you! The matter is closed!"*

Instead, Allah hands His Messenger something far more disarming.

He tells him to say: *If the Most Merciful had a son — then I would be the very first to worship him.*

[PAUSE]

Read that again and feel how unexpected it is. The single most committed monotheist who ever lived. The man who stood alone against an entire city for the oneness of God. And he is commanded to say: *if you were right, I would be first in line.*

Why would the defender of God's oneness be told to say he'd be the first to worship a son?

What kind of argument is this? And why does it land harder than any shout ever could?

That is what we are going to sit with today.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Smallest Word Carrying the Whole Verse — إِن

Let me teach you something before we touch the ayah.

In English, we have the word "if." It does one job. It opens a condition. *If it rains, I'll stay home.* Simple.

Arabic is more textured here. There is a particle, **إِن** *(in)*, that most often means exactly that — "if," the opener of a condition. But the very same letters, in certain contexts, function as something completely different. The grammarians call it **إِن النافية** — *in* the **negating** particle. In that mode, إِن no longer means "if." It means "**not**." It becomes a flat denial. The same way *ما* (mā) negates.

So picture two doors built into one word. Through one door, إِن means *"if — suppose, hypothetically."* Through the other door, the identical word means *"there is no such thing."*

Now look at our ayah.

**قُلْ إِن كَانَ لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ وَلَدٌ**

Here is what is remarkable, and I want you to slow down for it. The classical scholars did not agree on which door إِن walks through in this verse — **and the disagreement is not a problem to be solved. It is a gift, because both readings deliver the same truth from two directions.**

Let's open the first door.

**Reading one — إِن as "if."** Al-Baghawi and al-Suddī take it this way: *"If the Most Merciful had a son — on your claim, by your assumption — then I would be the first to worship him."* This is a conditional. And it's not an idle one. In Arabic rhetoric, you can grant your opponent's premise *for the sake of argument* precisely in order to expose where it leads. The Prophet ﷺ is saying: *let's say, hypothetically, your claim were true. Watch what I would do. I would not resist it. I would not cling to my position out of pride. I would be first — first — to fall down in worship of that son.* The hypothetical is offered, and then — by the surrounding verses and by reason itself — the premise collapses. There is no son. So the worship never follows. As al-Jalālayn puts it with surgical brevity: it is established that He has no child, *fa-intafat ʿibādatuhu* — so the worship of any such child falls away entirely.

Now the second door.

**Reading two — إِن as "not."** Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, and al-Suddī (in another narration) read إِن here as ما — negation. The meaning becomes: *"The Most Merciful never had a son — and I am the first of the worshippers,"* meaning the first of those who declare His absolute oneness, the first of the true monotheists among the people of Mecca. Al-Qurṭubī notes that on this reading, the sentence is complete at *"there is no son,"* and then a new statement begins: *and I am foremost among those who worship Him alone.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened?

Two readings. Two doors. And **they arrive at the same destination.** Whether you read it as *"if He had a son I would be first to worship him — but He doesn't,"* or as *"He never had a son, and I am first to worship Him alone"* — both leave you standing in the same place: **a single God, no offspring, and a Messenger who is foremost in devotion.**

This is what it means for a word to be load-bearing. The entire theology of the verse is balanced on one two-letter particle, and Allah chose a word that holds the truth no matter which way you turn it. The architecture is built so that you cannot misread it into error. Every door opens onto *tawḥīd*.

What this single word reveals: **the truth of God's oneness is not fragile. It does not depend on you reading the sentence the "right" way. It is woven so deeply into the speech that even the ambiguity confirms it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: Why *This* Name — لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ

Now I want you to notice something you might have walked straight past.

Allah has ninety-nine names. The verse is about *denying that God has a child*. So of all the names available — **al-Qahhār** (the Subduer), **al-ʿAzīz** (the Almighty), **al-Wāḥid** (the One) — which name would you expect in a verse slamming the door on the idea of divine offspring?

You might expect a name of power. A name of severity. A name that says *don't you dare.*

Instead, look at what He chose:

**لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ** — *for the Most Merciful.*

*Ar-Raḥmān.* From the root **rḥm** — the same root as *raḥim*, the **womb**. The name that describes mercy so vast, so overflowing, so all-encompassing that it covers everything in existence before anything is even asked of it.

Stay with this, because it is exquisite.

Why do human beings have children? Strip it down. Underneath every reason is one ache: **we are not enough on our own.** We have children because we will die, and we want something of us to continue. We have children because we are lonely, and we want to be loved. We have children because we are incomplete, and we hope a child will complete us. A child, for a human being, is the answer to a *lack*.

Now hear the name again. *Ar-Raḥmān.* The one whose mercy is so total it has no edge, no shortage, no gap. The one who needs nothing to continue, because He does not end. The one who lacks no love, because He is the source of all love. The one who is not lonely, because all companionship flows from Him.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the argument being made *just by the choice of name*? You don't even need the rest of the sentence. The moment Allah says *"a son for **Ar-Raḥmān**,"* the contradiction is already exposed. **A son is what fills an emptiness. There is no emptiness in the Infinitely Merciful.** To claim He has a son is to claim the Self-Sufficient has a need. The name itself refutes the lie before the verb arrives.

And there is a deeper tenderness here. He could have answered their offensive claim with a name of wrath. Instead, even while correcting the most serious error a human can make about Him, He names Himself by mercy. As if to say: *even now, even as you slander Me with offspring I neither have nor need, I am still Ar-Raḥmān to you.*

What this single name reveals: **the denial of a son is not a denial of warmth. It is the protection of a mercy so complete that adding anything to it — even a beloved child — would be to imagine it incomplete.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: أَوَّلُ ٱلْعَـٰبِدِينَ — "The First of the Worshippers"

Let's look now at the climax of the verse. **فَأَنَا۠ أَوَّلُ ٱلْعَـٰبِدِينَ** — *"then I would be the first of the worshippers."*

Two words I want to open: **أَوَّلُ** *(awwal)*, "first," and **ٱلْعَـٰبِدِينَ** *(al-ʿābidīn)*, "the worshippers," from the root **ʿbd**.

Start with *first*. Not *"I would be among the worshippers."* Not *"I would worship him too."* **First.** Foremost. At the front of the line. The Messenger is told to claim the *front position* in a devotion he is simultaneously proving impossible.

Why "first"? Because it does something an ordinary denial cannot. It removes every possible accusation of bias.

Imagine a person who refuses to believe a claim. The easiest thing to say about them is: *you're just being stubborn. You have a prior commitment. Of course you reject it — you were never going to accept it anyway.* Motivated reasoning. We'll come back to this, because it lives in all of us.

Now imagine that same person says: *"If this turned out to be true, I would not merely accept it — I would be the very first to embrace it, ahead of everyone, with my whole self."* Suddenly the accusation of stubbornness dissolves. You cannot say *"you just don't want it to be true"* to someone who has volunteered to be first in line if it were. Their rejection is revealed to be about **truth**, not about **preference**.

[PAUSE]

Now the second word — **ٱلْعَـٰبِدِينَ**, the worshippers. The root *ʿbd* is the root of *ʿabd*, the servant, the slave — total belonging, total submission. To be an *ʿābid* is to give yourself completely.

And here is where the classical readings shimmer again, the way they did with إِن. On the conditional reading, *al-ʿābidīn* means exactly what it says: **the worshippers of that son** — *I would be first to worship him.* But al-Baghawī preserves a striking minority lexical reading, that *al-ʿābidīn* here carries the sense of **al-ānifīn** — those who *disdain*, who *recoil from*, who *refuse* the claim. On that reading: *I would be the first to reject and repudiate it.*

Notice — I want to be precise here — this second sense is a contested lexical extension, not the dominant meaning, and most authorities read it as "worshippers." I name it not to settle it but because, once again, **the verse refuses to break.** Read it as *"first to worship him"* (within a hypothesis that then collapses), or read it as *"first to reject the claim"* — and you land, again, in the identical place: a Messenger absolutely committed to the truth about God, whichever way the grammar turns.

Al-Ṭabarī, in his comprehensive treatment of the verse, records a third shimmer in the chain of interpretation. He preserves a reading in which *al-ʿābidīn* is understood as *al-muʾminīna billāhi fī takdhībikum* — the believers in God who, by the very act of their worship, repudiate what the polytheists have claimed. On this reading, *"I am the first of the worshippers"* means precisely: *I am the first of those whose worship of God alone already calls your claim a lie.* The *ʿibāda* (worship) and the *takdhīb* (rejection of falsehood) are not two separate acts. To fall in total submission before the One God is, by that very act, to declare every rival claim false. The devotion carries the refutation inside it.

Which means: every time you pray, every time you bow, every time you prostrate — you are already speaking this verse. The *sujūd* is a sentence.

[PAUSE]

What these words reveal: **devotion at its purest is not loyalty to a position. It is loyalty to what is real — so total that it would race to the front of the line for the truth, even a truth it does not want, even a truth that would cost it everything it had argued for. And that same devotion, by being what it is, carries in it the negation of every false addition to the One it serves.**

---

### The Bridge

So look at what the language has built.

A two-letter word, إِن, that holds the truth through either door. A divine name, *Ar-Raḥmān*, that refutes the lie by its very meaning. And a phrase, *awwalu-l-ʿābidīn*, that strips away every charge of bias and reveals a heart whose worship is itself the rejection of every false claim — loyal only to what is real.

Three pieces. One message, machined so precisely that you cannot turn it the wrong way.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how *we* hold the truth, and about the God who needs nothing from us at all.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Honesty That Cannot Be Accused of Bias

Let me start where you already live.

You have had an argument with someone — maybe recently — where you could feel, halfway through, that they were no longer listening to *you*. They were defending a position. Every fact you offered, they bent. Every point you scored, they explained away. And you realized, with a sinking feeling, that there was nothing you could say. Because they had decided the conclusion before the conversation began, and now they were just reverse-engineering reasons.

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it **motivated reasoning** — the mind working backward from the answer it wants. And here is the uncomfortable part: it is not a flaw of *other* people. It is the default setting of the human mind. We do not believe things because they are true and then defend them. We *want* them to be true, and then we go hunting for the evidence. We are lawyers for our own desires, dressed up as judges.

Now hold that mirror up to faith.

The Meccans would have loved to dismiss the Prophet ﷺ exactly this way. *He just wants to be the only authority. He just opposes our gods because they threaten his message. His monotheism is self-interest in disguise.* The oldest move in the book: discredit the person so you never have to answer the argument. *Of course the Prophet rejects our theology — it threatens his position.* And this move, had it landed, would have been devastating. Not because it was true, but because once you've successfully framed someone's truth-telling as self-interest, the audience stops listening to the evidence. The messenger becomes the subject. The claim dies unexamined.

[PAUSE]

Now watch what this ayah does to that move.

The Prophet ﷺ is told to say: *"If He had a son — I would be first to worship him."* Not: *"he has no son, and I will never worship a son."* But: *"hypothetically, if your claim were true, I would race to the front of the line."*

The moment those words are spoken, the accusation of self-interest becomes impossible. You cannot say *"he's only saying this to protect his position"* to someone who has publicly stated he would abandon his position if the evidence demanded it. You cannot call it bias when the allegedly biased person has committed, in advance, to following the truth even against themselves.

What the Prophet ﷺ has done — and Allah, by commanding this statement, has given him the words to do it — is what we might call **epistemic transparency**. He has shown the architecture of his reasoning. He is not saying *"I have a conclusion and I'm defending it."* He is saying *"I am following a logic, and if the premises were different, the conclusion would be different, and I would be the first to change my conclusion."*

This is a devastatingly rare thing to encounter. Most of us, when we hold a position, build a fortress around it. Every counterargument strengthens the walls. Every challenge makes us grip the position tighter. We mistake commitment for integrity, and stubbornness for faith.

[PAUSE]

But true faith — real *ʿibāda*, the kind that comes from the root *ʿbd*, total submission — is not the same as being attached to your theological positions. The Prophet ﷺ is not defending *his monotheism*. He is pointing to *the actual truth*, and saying: wherever it leads, I will follow it there. If it led to a son of God, I would worship that son first. It doesn't. So I worship only Allah.

Sit with what that means.

Faith is not about having decided, and now defending the decision. Faith is about being genuinely oriented toward the real — and allowing what is real to determine everything else. The Prophet ﷺ's monotheism is not stubborn. It is completely open. It has already answered the question *"what would change your mind?"* in public, under pressure, with specificity. *This would. Here is what I would do.* And then: the condition doesn't hold. So here is what I do instead.

This, too, is a mirror worth holding. What do you believe that you have never once asked *"what would change my mind?"* Which of your convictions are so locked that you would never, under any circumstance, follow the truth the other way? Are those locked convictions genuine faith — or something else? Are they love of God — or love of the position?

[PAUSE]

The ayah is not asking you to doubt your faith. It is asking you to hold it the way the Prophet held his: openly, transparently, with your hands unclenched. *If I am wrong, I will be the first to say so.* And because he was not wrong — because the Most Merciful truly has no son, needs no son, is not diminished by having no son — the hypothetical dissolves before it becomes real, and the worship flows toward the only One who exists to receive it.

### Theme Two: The God Who Lacks Nothing

There is a question underneath this verse that does not get asked often enough, and I want to ask it plainly:

*Why would a god ever need a son?*

Not *did* God have a son, or *could* God have a son — those questions are already answered. But: what need would having a child fill, if a God had one?

[PAUSE]

Because that is the question this verse is quietly asking. Not by posing it explicitly, but by choosing the name it chose.

*Ar-Raḥmān.* We explored this in the linguistic section — the one whose mercy overflows from no shortage. But let's stay here longer, because this is where theology lives.

Think about what children are, at the most basic level, for human beings. We have children because we will die, and we want something of us to persist. We have them because we are lonely, and need someone to return to. We have them because we feel incomplete, and hope they will complete us. Every child, however beloved, answers some need in the parent. Even in the most selfless parenting — and parenting can be extraordinarily selfless — there is a structure of *needing* underneath it: *I brought you into the world because the world felt incomplete without you.*

Now apply that to *Ar-Raḥmān*.

The self-sufficient one. The one whose mercy has no floor, no ceiling, no drain. The one who does not age. The one who cannot be diminished. The one who experiences no loneliness because all companionship derives from Him. The one who does not fear ending because He is the only thing that cannot end.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar, commenting on this verse, says it plainly: *lam yakun wa-lā yakūn* — it has not been, and it will never be. Notice the tense. Not only *"it has not happened."* But also: *"it cannot happen, ever, in any future."* The denial is not merely historical — as though God simply chose not to, in this particular universe. It is ontological. The reason He has no son is not that He chose against it. It is that the *very concept* of a son — a completion, a continuation, an answer to a lack — cannot find any hook to hang on in the One who lacks nothing.

A son fills an emptiness. There is no emptiness in *Ar-Raḥmān*.

[PAUSE]

And here is something the classical commentators understood well, even when they said it briefly: the denial of offspring is not a *cold* denial. It is not a wall. The same verse that says *there is no child* names the Lord as *the Most Merciful*. You could have answered the claim of divine offspring with a name of power — *"the Almighty has no son"* — and the correction would be complete. Instead: *"the Most Merciful has no son."*

Why does that matter? Because it means the correction is offered in the spirit of the very thing it is protecting. **God's transcendence is a mercy.** The fact that He does not need us — does not need our worship, does not need our companionship, does not need us to extend His existence into the next generation — is not a distancing. It is what makes His drawing-near to us so astonishing. *Ar-Raḥmān* does not love you because He needs you. He loves you from completeness. From overflow. From a surplus of being that is simply, unconditionally there — not earned, not desperate, not contingent on what you give back.

That is a different kind of love than any we have tasted as human beings. And this verse is, quietly, an invitation to receive it.

[PAUSE]

And then — this is the moment I want you to feel — after the hypothetical has been posed and dissolved, after the name *Ar-Raḥmān* has done its work, after the condition collapses because it cannot stand — the Quran does not linger in argument. The very next verse does not extend the logic. It does not say *"and therefore you should accept this."*

It says: **سُبْحَانَ رَبِّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ** — *Glorified is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, Lord of the Throne, above all that they ascribe* (43:82).

*Subḥān.* The word that means transcendence so pure it cannot be fully articulated — only proclaimed. Al-Muyassar reads the transition exactly this way: *tanzīhan wa-taqdīsan* — a declaration of utter holiness, a stripping away of every false attribute. After the conditional is posed, and the premise dissolves, what remains is not the satisfaction of having won a debate. What remains is *glorification*. The argument was always in service of the *tasbīḥ*. The logic was clearing the ground for the prostration.

[PAUSE]

This is significant. The whole verse — the conditional, the hypothetical worship, the dissolution of the premise — was never primarily an intellectual exercise. It was the clearing of a space so that worship could arrive unobstructed. When you truly understand that He has no son — not just as a theological correction but as a felt reality about who He is — the natural response is not *"I have won the argument."* It is *"how can I not glorify this?"* The mind that truly grasps the self-sufficiency of *Ar-Raḥmān* does not file the thought away as doctrine. It erupts into *tasbīḥ*. It wants to say *subḥānAllāh* not because it has been told to, but because what it has understood is too large to hold quietly.

The theology and the devotion are one motion. The verse proves it — and the verse that immediately follows seals it.

### Closing: What This Verse Asks of You

We have come a long way from that smaller, harder Mecca.

We have sat with a two-letter word that holds the truth through either door. We have lingered with a name that carries an entire argument about divine self-sufficiency inside its syllables. We have watched a phrase — *"I would be first"* — strip away every possible accusation of bias and reveal a mind loyal only to what is real. We have understood that the act of worship is not separate from the act of bearing witness — that to bow before the One is already to call false every claim about any other.

And now, at the end of it, what does this verse ask?

Not belief. Not a conversion. Not an intellectual concession.

It asks something harder: **transparency about your own foundations.**

The Prophet ﷺ was commanded to say *what would change my mind* in public, under pressure, before the people who hated what he stood for. What would change yours? Is there anything you believe about God that you have never once examined — that you hold not because you have thought it through but because letting go would be too costly, too frightening, too lonely?

The verse does not ask you to doubt. It asks you to be the kind of person who *could* be moved by the truth — even against your own position. Because that is what *ʿibāda* actually is, at its most essential. Not the posture of prayer. Not the words you have memorized. But the total orientation of the self toward reality — toward what is actually true — with your hands open and your preferences set aside.

[PAUSE]

*Ar-Raḥmān* chose this name for this verse. Not because He needs your worship — not to fill a lack, not to complete a story, not because something in Him aches for acknowledgment. He is *lam yakun wa-lā yakūn* from every such need: past, present, future, ontologically beyond every kind of wanting.

He named Himself here by mercy because what He is offering you — in this verse, in this conditional, in this strange and beautiful honesty — is the chance to encounter Him as He actually is. Not the constructed God of motivated reasoning. Not the convenient God who happens to align with what you already wanted. But the self-sufficient, utterly complete, endlessly merciful One who named Himself *Ar-Raḥmān* even while correcting your deepest error about Him.

Even in the correction: mercy.

[PAUSE]

That is the God you are being invited toward.

And the Prophet ﷺ, standing at the front of the line, arms open, already said what he would do if it turned out differently.

It didn't.

And so the worship goes where it always was going — toward the One who lacks nothing, and gives everything.

**سُبْحَانَ رَبِّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ**

*Glorified is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, Lord of the Throne, above all they ascribe.*
