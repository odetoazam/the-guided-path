# Can You Make the Deaf Hear?
### The Weight You Were Never Asked to Carry

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first, in three forms, and then let's build the world around it before we touch a single word.

**The Arabic:**

> أَفَأَنتَ تُسْمِعُ ٱلصُّمَّ أَوْ تَهْدِى ٱلْعُمْىَ وَمَن كَانَ فِى ضَلَـٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ

**The transliteration:**

> *Afa-anta tusmiʿu ṣ-ṣumma aw tahdi l-ʿumya wa man kāna fī ḍalālin mubīn.*

**The accessible English:**

> "Then can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind and whoever is in clear error?"

Now. Before we go anywhere near the grammar, I want you to stand where the first listener stood.

### Rebuild the world

Picture a man. He has been speaking for years. Not selling anything, not asking for money, not seeking a following for his own name — he is offering the most important news a human being could ever receive: *you were made by Someone, you are returning to Someone, and here is the path home.* He says it gently. He says it with arguments. He says it with patience that outlasts insult. He says it to his own uncle, to his own neighbours, to the people who watched him grow up and called him *al-Amīn*, the trustworthy, before he ever spoke a word of this.

And they turn away.

Not because they have a better answer. Not because they've examined his case and found a flaw. They turn away the way a man turns his shoulder to a sound he has decided in advance he will not hear.

And it is *eating him alive.*

We know this, because Allah Himself describes it elsewhere — that the Prophet ﷺ was on the verge of destroying himself with grief over their refusal: *"Perhaps you would kill yourself with grief that they will not be believers"* (26:3). *"Perhaps you would kill yourself in sorrow over their footsteps, if they will not believe in this message"* (18:6).

This is a man carrying the salvation of people who keep dropping it.

So now look at where this ayah sits. The verses just before it (43:36–39) describe exactly those people — the one who turns blind to the Reminder of Ar-Rahman and is given a shaytan for a companion, the one who, on the Day of Judgement, wishes the distance between himself and that companion were the distance of the two horizons. Allah has just painted the rejecter in full.

And then — [PAUSE] — He turns.

He turns away from the rejecter, mid-passage, and He speaks directly to the one who has been straining to save them. He asks him a question. A strange question. A question that, on its surface, sounds almost like a rebuke — but is, in fact, one of the most merciful sentences ever spoken to a human being.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to sit with for the next hour. This ayah is built as a question — *"Can YOU make the deaf hear?"* — and every one of us instinctively hears it as being about *them.* The deaf. The blind. The lost.

But it isn't aimed at them. It's aimed at *you* — at the one doing the calling.

And tucked inside its grammar is a release so complete that, if it ever truly lands in your chest, you will put down a weight you have been dragging for years without knowing you were allowed to set it down.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word *afa-anta* — and the strange spotlight on "YOU"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open this word, because if I don't, you'll walk right past the most important thing in the ayah.

In Arabic, as in English, there's a default order to a sentence. If I want to ask "Do you hear?" the natural, unmarked way is to put the verb first and let the "you" ride quietly inside it. Arabic can bury the subject inside the verb entirely — *tasmaʿ* already means "you hear." You don't *need* a separate word for "you" at all.

So when a speaker stops, pulls the pronoun *out* of the verb, and sets it down *in front* — **"Is it YOU who hears?"** — that is never accidental. In English we'd do it with our voice: *"Are YOU going to fix this?"* That stress on the *you* changes everything. It's no longer a neutral question about an action. It's a question about a *person's capacity.*

Now look at what Allah actually said.

He did not say *a-fa-tusmiʿu ṣ-ṣumma* — "Do you, then, make the deaf hear?" — which would have been the smooth, expected phrasing. He said:

> **أَفَأَنتَ تُسْمِعُ** — *afa-anta tusmiʿu*

The interrogative *hamza* (أ), then *anta* — **"you"** — lifted out and placed *before* the verb. The spotlight swings off the action and lands, hard, on the person. **"Is it YOU — you, specifically — who can make the deaf hear?"**

Stay with this. Because there's a second thing happening, and the scholars of balāghah have a name for it.

This is not a question that wants information. The Prophet ﷺ already knows the answer. Allah already knows the answer. This is what's called *istifhām inkārī* — a question whose entire purpose is to *deny.* It is shaped like a question but it lands like a "no." When you say to someone, *"Can you bring the dead back to life?"* — you are not requesting data. You are saying: *you cannot, and we both know it.*

So the literal force of *afa-anta tusmiʿu ṣ-ṣumma* is: **"You cannot make the deaf hear. That was never within your power."**

Now here is where the heart has to slow down. [PAUSE]

Who decided to read this as a *rebuke*? We did. Because we're used to questions that begin with "can you" being challenges — *can't you do anything right?* But listen to what al-Qurtubi says this verse actually *is.* He calls it **تسلية للنبي** — *tasliyah lil-nabī* — a **consolation** for the Prophet ﷺ. He says its meaning is: *"this is not yours to do — so let not your chest be constricted if they disbelieve."*

Feel the turn. The fronting of *anta* — *"is it YOU?"* — which sounded like it was tightening a screw, is in fact *loosening* one. The spotlight on "you" isn't there to expose your failure. It's there to **redraw the border of your responsibility.** Allah is standing the Prophet ﷺ at the edge of his own jurisdiction and saying: *here. This far. Not one inch further. The hearing was never on your side of the line.*

The grammar that looks like it's pressing down is actually lifting off.

What this single construction reveals: **the most crushing weight a caller carries is a weight that was never assigned to him — and Allah removes it not by softening the demand, but by correcting the address.** *You* were never the one who makes them hear.

### Element Two: Two verbs that don't match — *tusmiʿu* and *tahdi*

Now look at the two things Allah says the Prophet ﷺ cannot do, and notice that they are built differently. This is subtle, and it's beautiful.

> **تُسْمِعُ ٱلصُّمَّ** — *tusmiʿu ṣ-ṣumma* — "make the deaf hear"
> **تَهْدِى ٱلْعُمْىَ** — *tahdi l-ʿumya* — "guide the blind"

Here's the grammar. *Tahdi* — "you guide" — is a plain Form I verb. Simple, direct. You guide.

But *tusmiʿu* is not Form I. It's **Form IV** — the pattern *afʿala* — and Form IV does a specific kind of work here: it makes the verb *causative.* The root س-م-ع (s-m-ʿ) is about *hearing.* Form I, *samiʿa*, means "he heard." But *asmaʿa / tusmiʿu* means **"to cause hearing to happen in someone else."** Not "you hear" — but "*you make another person hear.*"

Why does this matter? Sit with the difference between the two tasks.

To *guide* the blind, in a sense, you could imagine taking a hand, pointing the way. *Tahdi* — Form I — is the action of showing.

But *tusmiʿu* — to make the deaf hear — is something else entirely. You cannot *show* hearing. You cannot *point* someone into hearing. To make a deaf man hear, you would have to **reach inside him and create a faculty that isn't there.** Form IV reaches *into* the other and manufactures the very capacity to receive. And **that is precisely the thing no human being can do to another.**

This is the whole drama of da'wah in one verb-form. You can deliver the sound — you can make the air vibrate, you can say every word perfectly — but you cannot install the *hearing.* The receiving organ is not yours to build. The Form IV is Allah quietly drawing the exact line: *the delivery is yours; the reception is not.*

And notice the pairing — this is the second layer. Allah doesn't say "the deaf and the dumb and the blind," which is the famous triad He uses for the willfully closed-off elsewhere (2:18 — *ṣummun bukmun ʿumy*). Here He names only **two:** the **deaf** and the **blind.** [PAUSE] Read the absence. Where is the *tongue*? Where is the *mute*?

It's gone — and it's gone on purpose. The mute is the one who cannot *speak out.* But this verse isn't about their inability to respond or profess. It's about the two **channels by which guidance comes IN:** *hearing* — the channel of the recited word, the Qur'an, which arrives through the ear — and *sight* — the channel of the visible sign, the *āyah* you're meant to see in the world. Deaf to the word, blind to the sign. Allah names exactly the two doors revelation knocks on, and says: *both are bolted from the inside, and you do not hold the key to either lock.*

What these two verbs reveal: **a messenger's reach ends at the threshold of another person's heart. He can carry the message to the door. He cannot reach in and open the one who has locked it.**

### Element Three: *ḍalālin mubīn* — error so clear that no one in it can see it

Now the final phrase, and this one contains a quiet, devastating irony that I don't want you to miss.

> **وَمَن كَانَ فِى ضَلَـٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ** — *wa man kāna fī ḍalālin mubīn* — "and whoever is in clear error."

Three words to open. First, **كَانَ** — *kāna* — a *perfect-tense* verb. Not "whoever errs" in some fleeting moment, but **whoever has come to** *be* **in error** — a settled, established state. Second, the little preposition **فِى** — *fī* — "in." Allah does not say someone who *has* error, or who *does* error. He says someone who is **IN** it — *fī ḍalāl* — as though error were a *place,* a fog you are standing inside, an environment that surrounds you on every side. You don't carry the straying like an object. You *dwell* in it like a country.

And now the word that turns the whole thing over: **مُّبِينٍ** — *mubīn.*

*Mubīn* comes from the root ب-ي-ن (b-y-n), and like *tusmiʿu* it is built on **Form IV** — it is an active participle that means not merely "clear" but **"clarifying," "self-evidencing," that which makes itself plain.** The error is not subtle. It is not a borderline case. It is *mubīn* — it announces itself, it is obvious, it is visible to anyone with eyes.

Now hold the irony in both hands. [PAUSE]

The person is described as **ٱلْعُمْىَ** — *the blind.* And the error they are standing inside is described as **مُّبِينٍ** — *manifestly visible.*

The thing is glaringly clear. And they cannot see it.

This is the cruelest mechanism of *ḍalāl,* and the Qur'an captures it in the collision of two words. It is not that the error is hidden and hard to find. It is that the error is *blazing in plain sight* — and the one standing in the middle of it is the only person who cannot perceive it. Everyone outside can see the fog. The one inside the fog thinks he's standing in clear air.

This is why the verse is a consolation and not a complaint. Allah is telling His Messenger: *the problem was never the clarity of your message.* You delivered something *mubīn* — manifest, plain, self-evidencing. The failure is not in the light. The failure is in an eye that has chosen the dark. And **you cannot give sight to an eye that has refused it.** That repair is on a different desk than yours.

What this phrase reveals: **the deepest errors are not the obscure ones — they are the obvious ones, lit up for the whole world, invisible only to the person living inside them.**

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So look at what the language has shown us, all three pieces pointing the same direction.

The fronted *anta* redrew the border of the Prophet's responsibility. The Form IV *tusmiʿu* showed that the *receiving faculty* is not a messenger's to build. And *ḍalālin mubīn* showed that the message was never the problem — the message was clear; the eye was shut.

Three different grammatical doors, and every one of them opens onto the same room: **the distinction between what you deliver and what they receive.** Between the call and the hearing. Between the sign and the sight.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about the weight you may have been carrying for years.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You cannot do the hearing for them

Let me start where you already live.

Most of us have someone. A parent who is destroying their health and will not hear it. A sibling drifting away from everything you know to be good. A friend in a relationship that is grinding them to dust. A teenager. A spouse who has stopped praying. Someone you *love* — and you have explained, and pleaded, and stayed up at night composing the perfect sentence that will finally make them *get it.*

And it doesn't land. It never lands. And a particular kind of exhaustion sets in — the exhaustion of someone who has decided, somewhere deep down, that another person's transformation is *their job,* and that the failure of that transformation is *their fault.*

I want to name that feeling precisely, because the Qur'an is about to speak directly to it. It is the feeling that **you are the agent of someone else's change.** That if you just find the right words, the right tone, the right moment, the right evidence — they will hear. And therefore, when they don't hear, the missing piece must be *you.* You weren't clear enough. You weren't patient enough. You weren't good enough.

Now bring that feeling to this ayah — because the man being addressed here is the *best* of all callers. The most eloquent tongue. The most beautiful character. The most patient heart. The clearest message ever delivered, *mubīn,* straight from Ar-Rahman. If anyone's words could install hearing in another chest, it would be his.

And Allah says to *him:* **"Can YOU make the deaf hear?"**

[PAUSE]

Feel what that does. If *he* cannot — with perfect words and a perfect heart and a perfect message — then the equation you've been solving was *never solvable by the messenger.* The variable you keep adjusting — your eloquence, your patience, your worthiness — was never the variable that mattered. Because the *best* possible value of that variable still produces "no." The reception was never an output of the delivery.

This is the release. And I want to be very careful here, because there are two ditches on either side of this road and we have to walk between them.

**The first ditch is despair** — "so why bother? If I can't change anyone, why speak at all?" But look again at what Allah removed and what He *left.* He removed the *outcome.* He did not remove the *call.* The very next verses (43:43) command the Prophet ﷺ: *"So hold fast to what has been revealed to you — indeed, you are upon a straight path."* The speaking continues. The delivery is still your job. Al-Muyassar puts it exactly: *"upon you is only the conveying — the *balāgh* — their guidance is not upon you; rather Allah guides whom He wills."* You are a *deliverer of the message,* not a *manufacturer of the result.* The mailman is responsible for the letter reaching the door. He is not responsible for whether it is opened, read, and obeyed. And no honest mailman lies awake at night because someone refused to open their mail.

**The second ditch is arrogance** — "so their failure is entirely on them, and I wash my hands." But the ayah doesn't let you stand over them in judgement either. Because it's a *consolation,* spoken in *love,* to a man who was *grieving himself sick* over these very people. The release from responsibility for their *outcome* does not release you from *care* for their *souls.* You still call. You still hope. You still make du'a. You simply stop **mistaking yourself for God.**

That's what this whole thing comes down to. The weight you've been carrying is the weight of a job that belongs to Allah — the job of opening hearts. *"Indeed, you do not guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills"* (28:56) — revealed, the scholars report, about the Prophet's grief over his own uncle Abu Talib, the man he loved most, who died without taking the word. If *that* hearing was not in the Prophet's hands, then your loved one's hearing is not in yours.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time you speak to the one you've been trying to save — speak the truth, gently, clearly, completely. And then *put it down.* Do the *balāgh* and release the *result.* Make the du'a that only Allah can answer, and stop trying to answer it yourself. You will find you have energy you forgot you had — because you've been spending it on a lever that was never connected to anything.

### Theme Two: The error that everyone can see but you

Now let's turn the mirror, because this ayah has a second edge, and it cuts the other way.

We have spent this whole reflection comfortably on the *outside* of the deaf and the blind — looking at *them,* at *those rejecters,* at the people *we* are trying to reach. That's the safe seat. But I want to ask the uncomfortable question that *mubīn* forces on us.

Remember the irony at the core of the third phrase: the error is *clear* — *mubīn,* blazing, obvious — and the person in it is *blind* to precisely the thing everyone else can see. The defining feature of *ḍalālin mubīn* is not that it's hard to detect. It's that **it is invisible only from the inside.**

So here is the question I cannot dodge, and neither can you: **what is my** *ḍalālin mubīn?*

What is the thing about my life that is *obvious to everyone around me* — my family sees it, my closest friend sees it, maybe even strangers see it — and that I, standing in the middle of it, in the fog of it, *cannot see at all?* The temper I think is "just how I am." The attachment to wealth I've dressed up as "responsibility." The prayer that's slipped that I keep promising to fix "next week." The arrogance I experience, from the inside, as simply "being right."

Because here's the trap the word *mubīn* exposes: **clarity is not the cure for blindness.** We tend to assume that if something is obvious enough, we'll see it. This ayah destroys that assumption. The error here is maximally clear *and* the person is fully blind, *at the same time.* Obviousness does not penetrate a closed eye. You can be standing in floodlit error and experience it as a perfectly reasonable, well-lit life.

And notice — *kāna fī ḍalāl,* the perfect tense, the settled state, the *dwelling* in it. The most dangerous errors are not the dramatic ones. They're the ones we've *lived in* so long that they've stopped feeling like error and started feeling like *home.* The fog has been there so long we've forgotten the air was ever clear.

[PAUSE]

So how does a blind man, who cannot see his own blindness, ever get free? This is the paradox, and the ayah hands us the answer in its own grammar — almost in passing.

The faculty of hearing, *tusmiʿu,* was Form IV: a capacity that has to be *given* from outside. **No one heals their own blindness by looking harder.** If clarity doesn't reach a shut eye, then *more clarity* isn't the rescue. The rescue is to admit you might be the blind one — and to turn, in humility, to the only One who installs sight. The way out of *ḍalālin mubīn* is not better self-examination by a self that can't see itself. It is the du'a we say seventeen times a day and rush past: **اهدنا الصراط المستقيم** — *"guide us to the straight path."* That word, *ihdinā* — "guide us" — is the very root, ه-د-ي, of the verb in our ayah, *tahdi.* The thing the Prophet ﷺ could not do *for* the blind, we ask Allah to do *for us,* every single prayer.

Which means the posture that *saves* us is the exact opposite of the posture of the people in this verse. They are *certain* they see. The believer, by contrast, prays for sight *as if he might be blind* — because he knows that the one thing a person in *ḍalālin mubīn* never does is *suspect* they're in it.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** ask the people who love you the question your own eyes can't answer — *"what do you see in me that I don't see in myself?"* — and brace to actually hear it. And mean *ihdinā ṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* the next time you say it: not "keep me where I am," but "Ya Allah, show me the fog I can't see that I'm standing in." The humble suspicion that you *might* be the blind one is the only thing that has ever opened a blind eye.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of this ayah fold into one.

When we read it from the *outside* — as the caller — it lifts a weight: *you cannot make them hear; that was never your job.* When we read it from the *inside* — as the called — it plants a fear: *I might be the deaf one, the blind one, and not know it.*

And those are not two different lessons. They are the **same truth from two directions.** The truth is this: **the opening of a heart is in Allah's hands alone.** Read it about *them,* and it sets you free from a god-sized burden. Read it about *yourself,* and it drives you to your knees in du'a. One sentence does both — releases you from playing God for others, and reminds you to beg God for yourself.

The grammar carried all of it. The fronted *anta* drew the border of your responsibility. The Form IV *tusmiʿu* showed that the receiving faculty is built by Allah, not by you. And *ḍalālin mubīn* revealed that the clearest light cannot open an eye that's shut — which is at once your *exoneration* as a caller and your *warning* as a soul.

### Questions to carry

- Who is the person whose transformation I have been treating as *my* job — and what would it feel like to do the *balāgh* and finally set the *outcome* down?
- When someone I love refuses the good I offer, do I grieve like the Prophet ﷺ grieved — *out of love* — or do I grieve out of wounded pride that *my* words didn't work?
- If I asked the three people closest to me, "what is the obvious thing about me that I can't see?" — am I even willing to hear the answer?
- When I say *ihdinā ṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm,* am I asking to be *kept* where I am, or asking to be *shown* the fog I'm standing in?
- Do I believe, deep down, that *more clarity* will fix everything — and has this ayah just shown me that a shut eye is immune to light?

### One-sentence distillation

**You were sent to deliver the message, never to manufacture the hearing — the opening of a heart belongs to Allah alone, so lay down the weight of other people's refusal, and beg Him to open the eye in your own chest you cannot see is closed.**

### The closing invitation

There is someone you've been trying to save with your own hands. Tonight, deliver the truth one more time — clearly, gently, completely — and then let it go, the way the Prophet ﷺ was taught to let it go. And before you sleep, do the braver thing: don't pray only for *their* blindness. Pray for yours.

### A brief du'a

*Ya Allah, You alone make the deaf hear and the blind see. Release my heart from the burden of opening what only You can open — let me deliver Your message and trust the harvest to You. And Ya Allah, if I am standing in a clear error I cannot see, do not leave me in my fog — guide me to the straight path, and let me never be so certain of my sight that I forget to ask You for it. Āmīn.*
