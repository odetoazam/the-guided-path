# Footprints in the Sand: When "We Found Our Fathers" Replaces Thought

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to the words first. Let them settle before we do anything with them.

> [ayah:43:22] بَلْ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا وَجَدْنَآ ءَابَآءَنَا عَلَىٰٓ أُمَّةٍ وَإِنَّا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاثَـٰرِهِم مُّهْتَدُونَ
>
> *Bal qālū innā wajadnā ābā'anā ʿalā ummatin wa innā ʿalā āthārihim muhtadūn.*
>
> "Nay, they say: 'Indeed, we found our forefathers upon a way, and we — upon their footsteps — are guided.'"

And then, almost like an echo from a different century:

> [ayah:43:23] وَكَذَٰلِكَ مَآ أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ فِى قَرْيَةٍ مِّن نَّذِيرٍ إِلَّا قَالَ مُتْرَفُوهَآ إِنَّا وَجَدْنَآ ءَابَآءَنَا عَلَىٰٓ أُمَّةٍ وَإِنَّا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاثَـٰرِهِم مُّقْتَدُونَ
>
> "And likewise, We never sent a warner to a town before you except that its affluent ones said: 'Indeed, we found our forefathers upon a way, and we are following in their footsteps.'"

And then the warner turns and asks one question that should have ended the argument forever:

> **قَـٰلَ أَوَلَوْ جِئْتُكُم بِأَهْدَىٰ مِمَّا وَجَدتُّمْ عَلَيْهِ ءَابَآءَكُمْ**
>
> "He said: 'Even if I bring you better guidance than that upon which you found your forefathers?'"

### Rebuild the world

Picture Mecca in the years before the migration. The Prophet ﷺ is calling people to one God. He is not asking them to learn a complicated philosophy. He is asking them a question their own intellects could answer in an afternoon: *Does a stone hear you? Did your idol create the sky?*

And here is the strange thing. They do not answer the question. They cannot answer the question — because there is no answer that survives it. So watch what they do instead. They change the subject. They don't say, "Here is the evidence for our gods." They say something that has nothing to do with truth at all: **"We found our fathers doing this."**

That is the entire argument. Not "this is true," but "this is ours." Not "we examined it," but "we inherited it."

Now Surah Az-Zukhruf is the surah of *zukhruf* — gold, gilding, ornament, the glittering surface that the eye mistakes for substance. The whole surah is about things that look like one thing and are another. And here, in the center of it, Allah exposes the most dangerous gilding of all: a belief that *looks* like guidance because it is old, because it is familiar, because everyone you love already holds it — but underneath the gold leaf there is no thinking at all.

### The Hook

Here is what should keep you sitting with these verses. The people in verse 22 do not call themselves *followers*. They call themselves **muhtadūn** — *the guided*. They are certain they are on the path.

But Allah, in the very next breath, repeats their exact sentence in the mouths of every nation that ever rejected a prophet — and He changes **one consonant**. One sound. And that single sound silently rewrites everything they thought they were.

Stay with me, because that one consonant is the whole sermon.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: **بَلْ** — the sound of a subject being changed

Let's begin with the very first word, because Arabic does something here that English barely notices.

In any language, when someone is losing an argument, there is a particular move they make. You've heard it. You ask a friend, "Did you actually read the report before you signed off on it?" And instead of saying yes or no, they say, "Look, everyone signed off on it." Notice — they didn't answer. They *pivoted*. They swapped the question you asked for a different one they could survive.

Arabic has a word that is built for exactly that pivot. It is **بَلْ — *bal*.** Grammarians call it a particle of *iḍrāb* — a particle of "turning away," of retraction and redirection. It does not continue a thought; it swerves off it. When you hear *bal*, something just got abandoned and something else got put in its place.

So look at where Allah places it. The verses just before this passage have been pressing the idolaters with reason after reason: *Who created the heavens and the earth? Who sends down rain? Whose are the daughters you so casually assign to Allah?* The argument is closing in. The logical thing — if they had an answer — would be to give it.

And instead the verse opens: **بَلْ قَالُوٓا۟** — *"Nay — instead — they said..."*

[PAUSE]

Feel what that *bal* is telling you before you even reach their words. It is telling you **there is no rebuttal coming.** The *bal* is the sound of the subject being changed. Allah has framed their entire speech, grammatically, as an evasion — before they utter a single word of it. They were handed a question about *truth*, and the *bal* signals they are about to answer with something that is not truth at all.

There is a layer beneath the *bal* that the classical commentators surface. In the verses immediately before this one, these same idolaters had already tried a different escape: they had claimed that divine predestination itself sanctioned their worship — "if the Most Merciful had willed, we would not have worshipped these idols." A theological shield, invoked first. Al-Tabari's commentary identifies this *bal* as the hinge marking precisely that moment: when the predestination argument could no longer hold, they pivoted to ancestry. Two shelters, one after the other. The *bal* is not simply a turn away from reason — it is the sound of a community reaching for its second line of defense after the first has been stripped away.

What this single particle reveals: **when people cannot defend a belief on its merits, they don't fall silent — they relocate the conversation.** And the most common place they relocate it to is the past.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: **وَجَدْنَآ** — the verb of finding, not seeking

Now their actual words. And the load-bearing word is the verb: **وَجَدْنَآ — *wajadnā*** — "we found."

Let me teach the verb before we use it. The root *wajada* (و-ج-د) means to find, to come upon, to encounter. But here is the crucial thing about *finding*: **finding is what happens to you. Seeking is what you do.**

Think of the difference in your own life. There is a difference between the book you *searched* for — went to the shop, read the reviews, weighed it against three others, chose it — and the book you simply *found* lying on the kitchen table because someone in your house left it there. You can read both. But only one of them did you *choose*. The other one merely arrived.

Now listen again: *innā **wajadnā** ābā'anā ʿalā ummah* — "we **found** our fathers upon a way."

They did not say *naẓarnā* — "we examined." They did not say *ṭalabnā l-ḥaqq* — "we sought the truth." They did not say *fakkarnā* — "we thought it through." They said **we found.** The belief was simply *there* when they arrived, like furniture in a house they were born into. They came into consciousness and it was already in the room, and they never once asked who put it there or whether it should be.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it is more uncomfortable than it first appears. *Wajadnā* is not the verb of an investigation. It is the verb of an **inheritance.** And the entire structure of their religion, by their own admission, rests on a verb that contains no thinking in it whatsoever. They have confessed — without realizing it is a confession — that they never chose. They were *delivered* their gods the way you are delivered a surname.

But linger one moment more, because there is a word between *wajadnā* and *āthār* that the passage does not explain and we must not skip. What did they find their fathers *upon*? The word is *ummatin* — "upon an *ummah*." Most of us carry *ummah* as "the Muslim community" or "a nation of people." But the classical commentators are unanimous here: in this verse, *ummah* means *millah* — a complete creed, a confessional religion, a full way of worship. Al-Tabari's gloss is direct: they found their fathers "upon a religion and a creed" (*dīn wa millah*). Al-Jalalayn reduces it to one clarifying word: *millah*.

Feel what this adds to their confession. They are not admitting to a casual inherited habit — a way of eating, a style of dress. They are confessing to having found their fathers committed to a complete religious system, one that answers the deepest questions about God, obligation, and the unseen world. And their response to inheriting that entire edifice was the verb *wajadnā* — passive finding, zero examination. The weight of what they received makes the absence of inquiry not ordinary but staggering.

Here is what is remarkable. Allah does not need to refute them. He simply lets them name their own foundation, and the foundation indicts itself. The most honest word in their sentence is the word that destroys it: **we *found*.**

### Element Three: **ءَاثَـٰرِهِم** — walking in footprints

Now the image. This is where the verse stops describing and starts *painting*, and we have to slow all the way down.

They say: *wa innā ʿalā **āthārihim** muhtadūn* — "and we, upon **their āthār**, are guided."

The root here is *a-th-r* (أ-ث-ر). And **athar** means a *trace* — a mark left behind, the residue of something that has passed. An *athar* is a footprint pressed in the dust. It is the track an animal leaves. It is the impression of something that was here and is now *gone*.

Let this image land fully, because Allah chose it with terrible precision. They did not say "we walk *with* our fathers." They did not say "we walk *toward where our fathers were going*." They said: we walk **upon their footprints.**

[PAUSE]

Picture it literally. You are crossing a desert at night. There is a line of footprints in the sand ahead of you, and you decide: I will simply place my feet where these feet were placed. I will not look up. I will not ask where this trail leads. I will not check whether the one who made these prints knew where he was going. I will just follow the dents in the sand.

Now ask the questions the verse is forcing you to ask. **A footprint does not know the destination.** A footprint only tells you that *someone passed here* — not that he arrived anywhere good. You can follow a perfect line of footprints directly off the edge of a cliff. The footprints of a lost man are just as clear in the sand as the footprints of a guided one. The trail tells you nothing about the truth of the journey. It only tells you that you are not the first to walk it.

And there is something even quieter and sadder in the image. To follow footprints, **you must look down.** Your eyes are on the ground, on the prints, on the past. You never lift your head to the horizon. You never look at the *sky* — which, in a surah obsessed with who made the heavens, is exactly the direction they refuse to turn.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing to an unseen reality through a thing you can see. The footprint is the visible image. The invisible reality it points to is **a whole life lived in the dent left by someone else's feet** — borrowed, second-hand, never once your own.

### The phrase that ties it shut: **مُّهْتَدُونَ**

And now the word they end on — the word that should make us flinch.

**مُّهْتَدُونَ — *muhtadūn*.** From the root *h-d-y* (ه-د-ي), the root of *hidāyah*, guidance — the same root in *ihdinā ṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, "guide us to the straight path," which you say in every rakʿah. It is here in Form VIII (the *iftaʿala* pattern), an active participle: *those who are guided, those who hold to guidance, those who have taken the right path for themselves.*

Do you feel the irony now that we've walked through the footprints? They are looking *down* at marks in the dust, refusing to lift their eyes — and the word they use for themselves is **"the guided."** They are, by their own description, following the residue of people who are dead and gone — and they call that *being on the path.*

They have taken the most passive thing a human can do — placing your feet in someone else's prints without a single question — and they have given it the most active, dignified name in the religious vocabulary: **guidance.** That is the *zukhruf*. That is the gold leaf over the hollow thing. A life of pure imitation, gilded with the word *muhtadūn*.

[PAUSE — let this sit]

### Bridge to the architecture

So we have three confessions hiding inside one sentence: the *bal* that admits there is no argument; the *wajadnā* that admits there was no search; the *āthār* that admits they are following marks, not truth. And over all of it, the gilded label: *muhtadūn*, "guided."

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because Allah is about to do something with this sentence that no human editor would dare.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The belief you never chose

Let's start where you actually live, not in seventh-century Mecca.

Psychologists have a name for one of the most powerful forces shaping human behavior. They call it **the default.** Study after study shows that whatever option is presented as the *already-selected* one — the box that comes pre-ticked — is the one the overwhelming majority of people keep. Not because they weighed it. Because changing it requires an act, and *keeping* it requires nothing. We are creatures who drift, by default, into the default.

And layered on top of that is what's called **social proof** — the deep instinct that if many people, especially people we trust, already do a thing, the thing must be correct. Your brain treats consensus as evidence. It feels like thinking. It is not thinking. It is *counting*.

Now here is the uncomfortable bridge. *Wajadnā ābā'anā* — "we found our fathers upon a way" — is the oldest, purest statement of the default in human language. It is the pre-ticked box, sanctified. It is social proof wearing the robe of religion.

And before you place this comfortably onto idolaters far away, turn it on yourself, because that is what tadabbur is for. **How many of your own convictions are things you *examined* — and how many are things you simply *found*?** The opinions you hold about other kinds of people. The way you assume money works, or success, or what a good life looks like. The things you are *certain* about — are you certain because you tested them, or certain because everyone around you was already certain when you arrived?

[PAUSE]

There is one more detail in verse 23 that deserves to be held up before we move on. When Allah universalizes this pattern — announcing that every town before Mecca received a warner and every town produced this same excuse — He specifies *who* in each town was leading the chorus: **mutrafūhā** — "its *mutrafūn*." The translation "affluent ones" captures only part of it. Al-Tabari identifies them as *ruʾasāʾuhum wa kubarāʾuhum* — the chiefs, the senior figures, those carrying institutional weight in the community. Al-Muyassar sharpens it further: *alladhīna abṭarahum al-niʿmah* — "those whom abundance made insolent."

This is not incidental. The pattern across every prophetic mission is structural, not random: the ones most vehemently defending "we found our fathers" are not the confused or the uninformed. They are the people whose *identity, standing, and authority* are most tightly bound to the inherited order. To genuinely examine the tradition is to risk discovering that the foundations of your own privilege are hollow. The *mutrif* cannot afford to look carefully — because for him, the inherited religion is not just a system of worship. It is the source of his standing. This is the deepest layer of *zukhruf*: a belief preserved not because of its truth, but because of what dismantling it would cost those at the top.
