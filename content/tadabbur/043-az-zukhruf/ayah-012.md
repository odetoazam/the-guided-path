# The Pairs, All of Them — and What Carries You

## Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 12

> وَٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلْأَزْوَٰجَ كُلَّهَا وَجَعَلَ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱلْفُلْكِ وَٱلْأَنْعَٰمِ مَا تَرْكَبُونَ

*Wa-lladhī khalaqa l-azwāja kullahā, wa jaʿala lakum mina l-fulki wa-l-anʿāmi mā tarkabūn.*

**"And He who created the pairs, all of them, and made for you, from ships and cattle, that which you ride —"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Surah Named After Gold Leaf

Let me tell you what this surah is called, because the name is the whole diagnosis.

**Az-Zukhruf.** It means gilding. Ornament. The thin layer of gold leaf laid over something to make it gleam. Later in this very surah, the disbelievers will say that if Muhammad ﷺ were truly favored by God, he'd have been given *zukhruf* — gold, fine houses, the trappings of importance. They could not separate the worth of a thing from how much it glittered. They were a people of surface. A people who mistook the gold leaf for the gold.

And here is the strange thing about such people — and you may recognize yourself in this, because we are all, in some season of life, people of surface. **They were not atheists.** Three verses before ours, Allah says: *"And if you asked them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they would surely say, 'The Mighty, the Knowing created them'"* (43:9). They *knew.* Ask them point-blank who made the sky, and the answer came out clean and correct. The information was in them. It simply changed nothing. The mind conceded the Creator while the heart went on bowing to other things.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that, because that is the exact failure this surah is trying to heal — and it may be the most modern failure there is. **Knowing about God while living as though He were not there.** Believing the fact of Him while feeling none of the weight of Him. The intellect signs the contract and the heart never reads it.

So how does the Quran treat a heart that already has the information but feels nothing?

It does not argue. It *points.* It begins walking you through the world you live inside every single day — the ground you sleep on, the roads you travel, the rain, the pairs, the animals, the ships — and with each one it is quietly asking: *you said He made all this. So look. Look at what that means. Look at how much of it bends toward you specifically.*

Our ayah is one frame in that slow procession. Eleven words. On the surface — and remember, surface is this surah's whole trap — it looks like a simple inventory. *He made pairs. He gave you boats and camels to ride.* You could nod and move on. The people of gold leaf would nod and move on.

But underneath those eleven words is a statement about the entire architecture of existence — about why *nothing* in creation can stand alone, and why exactly **One** thing can. And folded inside that cosmic claim is something almost unbearably intimate: a sudden turn, mid-sentence, away from "everything that exists" toward *you.* Toward what carries your body across water and sand.

Here is the question I want us to carry the whole way through. **Why does Allah, in a single breath, place the most universal fact about creation — that all of it comes in pairs — right beside the most personal favor He could name, the thing that carries you home?** What is the pair learning to do for the camel? What is the camel learning to do for your soul?

Let's not answer yet. Let's earn it.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *al-Azwāj* — The World That Comes in Twos

### First, the everyday thing

Before we open the Arabic, let me put something in your hand that you already own.

Think of a key and a lock. A key, by itself, is a strange little piece of cut metal — useless, almost absurd. It only becomes a *key* in relation to a lock. Its every ridge and notch is a statement *about something else.* The key is shaped by an absence it is built to fill. It carries the lock's portrait on its body.

Hold that image. Because the Arabic word at the center of this ayah is built on exactly that idea — that some things exist only as the answer to another thing.

### The word

Allah says He created **ٱلْأَزْوَٰج** — *al-azwāj.* Translators write "the pairs," and that's correct, but it flattens something the Arabic refuses to flatten.

The singular is **زَوْج**, *zawj.* And here is what's beautiful and easy to miss: in Arabic, *zawj* does not mean "the pair." It means **one member of a pair.** The husband is a *zawj.* The wife is a *zawj.* Each one, alone, is named for the fact of needing the other. The very word for "spouse" is, at root, a confession of incompleteness — *I am the one-of-two.*

Ibn Fāris and the classical lexicographers note that *zawj* is used for each of two things joined together — whether joined by likeness or by contrast. The two shoes of a pair. The two oxen under one yoke. The male and the female. Even, the lexicons say, a thing and its opposite, because opposition is its own kind of binding — night is only night against day; you cannot define one without summoning the other.

So when Allah says *khalaqa l-azwāj* — **"He created the pairs"** — the word itself is whispering: *He created all the things-that-cannot-stand-alone. He created the incomplete-on-purpose.*

### Let's sit with this

Now notice what the scholars do with this word, because they do not agree on its scope — and their disagreement is not a problem to solve. It is a series of camera angles on something too large for one lens.

One reading, reported from Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and reflected in al-Baghawī and al-Muyassar: *al-azwāj* means **all the kinds, all the species** — every category of living thing, every variety of plant. The whole sorted catalogue of creation.

Another, carried in al-Qurṭubī's commentary from al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, goes cosmic: the pairs are **winter and summer, night and day, the heavens and the earth, the sun and the moon, the Garden and the Fire.** The great twinned structures the whole universe is hung upon.

A third reading: the **male and the female** — the pairing written into every animal, the engine of all life continuing.

A fourth, also in al-Qurṭubī: the **pairs of human experience** — good and evil, belief and disbelief, benefit and harm. The moral and emotional dualities you spend your whole life moving between.

Stay with me, because here is what happens when you lay all four readings on top of each other instead of picking one. You get a single, staggering claim: **Everything. At every level. Comes in twos.** The biological — male and female. The cosmic — sun and moon, night and day. The categorical — every species against every other. The very *moral* texture of your inner life — good against evil. From the cell to the galaxy to the conscience, creation is built in pairs.

And much later in the Quran, Allah will state the principle bare: *"And of all things We created pairs, that perhaps you may remember"* (51:49). Of *all things* — pairs. The verse even ends with the purpose: *that you may remember.* The pairing is not decoration. **It is a clue, left on purpose, to be read.**

### Here's what's remarkable

So what is the clue *for*? Why fill creation with pairs and then tell us, twice, that the pairing is something to *remember*?

Because a pair is a confession.

Every paired thing announces, just by being paired, *"I am not enough on my own."* The key needs the lock. The night needs the day to be called night. The male needs the female for anything to continue. The moon has no light that is its own. Each member of every pair is a small flag planted in reality that reads: **dependent. incomplete. relational. made.**

And now ask the question the pairing was *designed* to make you ask.

If everything that is created comes in twos — if to be created is to be one-half-of-something, to need, to lean — then **what does it tell you about the One who has no pair?**

[PAUSE]

This is the secret engine of the whole ayah, and it is pure *tawḥīd.* The same Quran that says *"of all things We created pairs"* says of Allah: *"Say: He is Allah, One"* (112:1). *"He neither begets nor is begotten, and there is none comparable to Him"* (112:3–4). **No zawj.** No counterpart. No complement He leans toward. No opposite that defines Him by contrast. He is not the night that needs a day, not the key that needs a lock, not one-half of anything.

To create the pairs *is itself* the proof that the Creator stands outside them. The pairedness of everything is the negative space around the Oneness of the One. You were never meant to just see the pairs. You were meant to see the *single shape they are all drawn around* — the only thing in all of existence that needs no other.

The people of gold leaf in this surah worshipped partners *for* Allah. And here, in His very first favor of the passage, He buries the refutation in the structure of the cosmos itself: **I am the only One who has no partner — and I wrote that truth into every pair you have ever seen.**

That is what this one word reveals.

## Element Two: *Khalaqa* and *Jaʿala* — Two Verbs, Two Distances

### First, the everyday thing

Imagine an architect. She designs a thousand buildings across a great city — bridges, towers, libraries she will never enter. That is one kind of making: *bringing into existence.* Vast, impersonal, magnificent.

Now imagine that same architect takes a single chair from one of those buildings, carries it into your room, and sets it by your window, angled toward the morning light, *for you, to sit in.* That is a different kind of making entirely. The hands are the same. The *direction* is not. The first making faces the world. The second making faces **you.**

Hold both. Because this ayah uses two different verbs, and the entire emotional turn of the sentence lives in the gap between them.

### The two words

First Allah says **خَلَقَ** — *khalaqa.* "He created." This is the great architect's word. *Khalaqa* is bringing-into-being, originating, measuring-out from nothing. It is the word for the heavens, the earth, the human being, the pairs. It is cosmic. It does not, in itself, bend toward anyone.

And notice its object: *al-azwāja kullahā* — "the pairs, *all of them.*" The widest possible reach. Everything that exists. The architect designing the entire city.

Then the verb changes. **وَجَعَلَ** — *wa jaʿala.* "And He made / appointed / rendered."

Now, *jaʿala* is a subtler word than *khalaqa,* and the difference is the whole point. *Khalaqa* is about a thing's *existence* — that it is at all. *Jaʿala* is about a thing's *assignment* — what it has been turned toward, appointed for, rendered into. *Khalaqa* brings the chair into being. *Jaʿala* sets it by your window. You can almost always tell *jaʿala* by what trails behind it.

### Let's sit with this

And look at what trails behind it here. *Wa jaʿala* — **لَكُم** — *lakum.* **"For you."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the camera move. One clause ago we were at the scale of *all the pairs in all of existence* — the entire sorted cosmos. And now, in the space of a single word, the whole infinite frame swings down and around and points at a chest. *Lakum.* For you.

This is one of the Quran's most characteristic and most overlooked gestures: the **collapse of scale.** From *kullahā* — "all of them," the totality — to *lakum* — "for you-specifically." From the architect of the city to the chair set by your window. And the verb obediently changes with the distance: *khalaqa* for the cosmos that merely *is,* *jaʿala* for the favor that is *aimed.*

Read the asymmetry, because absence speaks here. He did not say He *created* (*khalaqa*) the ships and cattle. He said He *made them for you* (*jaʿala lakum*). And that is exact — because the point of the transport is not that it *exists.* The point is what it has been *turned toward.* Their whole meaning, in this sentence, is the *lakum.* A camel that no one rides is just an animal. A camel *jaʿala lakum* — appointed for you — is a favor with your name on it.

### Here's what's remarkable

And now the detail that should stop us cold.

Among the things He says He "made for you" are **al-fulk — the ships.** But ships are *built.* By human hands. From cut wood and stitched sail and human knowledge. A man saws the plank. A man drives the nail.

So why does Allah say *He* made the ships for you?

[PAUSE]

Because He gave the wood its grain. He gave water its strange, generous property of holding weight afloat — a property you did not invent and could not have negotiated. He gave the wind that fills the sail. He gave the human mind the capacity to look at a floating log and conceive of a hull. **Every single thing your "manufacturing" depends on is something you received and did not make.** The carpenter rearranges gifts. He originates nothing.

And watch what Allah does next: He sets the ships (*al-fulk*) and the cattle (*al-anʿām*) side by side under one act of making and one act of riding. The thing you *build* and the thing He *grows* — placed in the same hand, given the same verb, called by the same favor. He is quietly dissolving the line we love to draw between "what God provides" and "what I made myself." In the grammar of this ayah, **there is no such line.** The ship and the camel are equally *jaʿala lakum.* The factory and the field are equally His.

That is what the change of verbs reveals: not two kinds of making, but two *distances* — the One who brought all of existence into being, leaning the whole weight of that power down toward the single favor of carrying *you.*

## Element Three: *Mā Tarkabūn* — The Word That Leaves a Space

### First, the everyday thing

You know the experience of a sentence that trusts you. A friend says: *"Take whatever you need."* They don't say "take whatever you need from the shelf and put it in your bag and use it." They stop at *need* — and the unspoken rest is left open, because they trust you to fill it, and because leaving it open is itself a kind of generosity. The gap is the gift.

Arabic does something like this with a tiny piece of grammar, and it happens right at the end of our ayah.

### The word, and the space inside it

Allah says: *mina l-fulki wa-l-anʿāmi* — "from the ships and the cattle" — **مَا تَرْكَبُونَ** — *mā tarkabūn* — **"that which you ride."**

*Mā* here is a relative pronoun: "that which / what." And classical grammarians, including the *Jalālayn,* point out something precise: the sentence is missing its **returning pronoun** — the *ʿāʾid.* In full, strict Arabic it would be *mā tarkabūna**hu*** — "that which you ride **it.**" The little *-hu,* the "it," that would close the loop and pin the object down — **Allah dropped it.** *Mā tarkabūn.* "What you ride." Open at the end.

Now, grammarians will tell you, correctly, that dropping the *ʿāʾid* here is a permitted economy — Arabic does it for lightness. That is the documented fact. But sit with the *effect* of that economy, because the Quran chooses where to be light.

### Let's sit with this

There is a second, even quieter precision here, and it lives in one small preposition.

He did not say "I made for you the ships and the cattle" — full stop, all of them. He said *min* — "**from** the ships and the cattle" — *mā tarkabūn* — "**that which** you ride." The *min* is partitive. It carves out a portion. Because not all cattle are for riding — some are for milk, some for wool, some for food. So He specifies: *the part of them that you ride.*

And think about how exact that is. He is not over-claiming the favor. He is naming the *precise function* in front of us in this moment — the carrying, the bearing-of-your-weight — because this whole passage is building toward the act of *mounting.* Read the very next verses: *"so that you may settle yourselves upon their backs, and then remember the favor of your Lord when you are settled upon them, and say: Glory to the One who subjected this to us"* (43:13–14). The ayah is zooming, with total intention, onto the single instant when your foot leaves the ground and the animal — or the deck — takes your weight.

### Here's what's remarkable

So why leave the end open? Why *mā tarkabūn* — "what you ride" — instead of nailing it to a specific "it"?

Because the open end is **you.**

The dropped pronoun leaves a space exactly the size of every vehicle that has ever borne a human being. The Companions heard "ships and cattle" and pictured camels and dhows. But *mā tarkabūn* — "what you ride" — does not close. It is a held-open door. The Quran could have said "camels and horses and ships." Instead it left a verb — *you ride* — trailing into an unspecified *what,* and that unspecified *what* quietly reaches across fourteen centuries and includes the train, the car, the airliner at thirty-five thousand feet. **The grammar made room for vehicles that would not exist for a thousand years.**

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing I want you to feel. Every single one of those — the camel and the Boeing alike — runs on the same two gifts named in this ayah: a *material* that bears weight (the buoyant water, the strong-backed animal, the engineered alloy) and a *force* that moves it (the wind, the muscle, the burning fuel). You did not make water hold a hull. You did not make fire push a piston. You rearranged His gifts and called the result *yours.* And the verb is in the present tense — *tarkabūn,* "you **are** riding," ongoing, habitual — because this is not a one-time historical favor. It is happening to you *today,* every time you sit down inside something that moves and forget, completely, Who is holding it up.

The dropped "it" left a space. **The space was always meant for the thing you climbed into this morning.**

[PAUSE]

## Bridge: From the Words to the Weight

So look at what these eleven words have done.

*Al-azwāj* showed us a cosmos built entirely of pairs — of things that cannot stand alone — drawn around the single, unpaired One. *Khalaqa* and *jaʿala* showed us the whole weight of that cosmic power leaning down, mid-sentence, into a favor aimed at *you.* And *mā tarkabūn* left a door propped open so wide that the favor reaches the vehicle you will ride tomorrow.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about how you are actually living inside this world right now.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: You Were Built to Lean — and Only One Was Not

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular ache that follows the modern person everywhere, and it has a strange shape. We are taught, almost from birth now, to prize *independence.* The self-made person. The one who needs no one. We build whole identities around not-needing — financial independence, emotional self-sufficiency, the curated life that requires no asking. And then, in the quiet hours, the same person who worked so hard to need nothing feels the floor drop out — a loneliness that doesn't make sense, an emptiness that all the self-sufficiency was supposed to prevent.

Here is what I think is happening, and the ayah names it.

**You were created as a *zawj.*** Not the Creator. A creature. And every creature, this verse tells us, comes in pairs — built to lean, built incomplete-on-purpose, shaped like a key cut for a lock. To be created *is* to be relational. Your very design is a confession that you are not enough on your own. The loneliness is not a malfunction. **It is the pair-shape of your soul, telling you the truth about what you are.**

The modern project of needing-nothing is, at its root, an attempt to be the only thing in creation that has no pair. And there is only One who has that property. *"He neither begets nor is begotten, and there is none comparable to Him."* When we try to become self-sufficient in the absolute sense — needing no one, leaning on nothing, complete in ourselves — **we are unconsciously reaching for an attribute that belongs to God alone.** And it cannot fit on us. It was never cut to our shape. That is why it aches.

Watch how the ayah resolves it, though, because it does not leave you in the ache. It moves immediately from *al-azwāj* — you are a pair, you lean — to *jaʿala lakum* — and *He made things for you.* The cure for the loneliness of being-a-creature is not to stop being a creature. It is to lean toward the **right** One. The pair-shape in you was never meant to be filled by self-sufficiency. It was meant to be filled by the only One who has none — by leaning *up,* toward the unpaired Creator who made you to need, and then filled your needing with favor after favor.

So here is what changes tomorrow. The next time you feel that particular emptiness — the one that survives all your achievements — don't reach for *more* independence. Recognize the feeling for what it is: your created nature, your *zawj*-ness, pointing home. Turn the leaning upward. *"You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help"* (1:5) — the daily prayer is literally the *zawj* finding its lock. You were built to lean. The whole question of your life is *toward what.*

## Theme Two: The Glory You Forget Every Time You Sit Down

Now let me take you to the most ordinary moment imaginable.

You get into your car. You pull the door shut. Maybe you're already on the phone, already thinking about where you're going, already somewhere else entirely. You turn the key — or push the button — and a controlled series of explosions begins firing dozens of times per second beneath you, and a ton and a half of steel begins to move, carrying your fragile body at speeds that would have been literally unimaginable to every human being who lived before about a hundred and fifty years ago. And you feel **nothing.** No wonder. No fear. No gratitude. You are checking a text.

That numbness — that total absence of astonishment at the astonishing — is precisely the disease of *Az-Zukhruf.* The people of gold leaf could not feel the source through the surface. And we, gliding down a highway in a climate-controlled miracle, feel nothing at all. We have *normalized the impossible.*

Now hear what Allah does with the act of riding. He does not just mention it. He builds, across three verses, a *liturgy* around it. Verse 12: He made for you what you ride. Verse 13: *"so that you may settle yourselves upon their backs, and then remember the favor of your Lord when you are settled upon them."* And then He hands you the exact words: *"and say: **Subḥāna lladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn** — Glory to the One who subjected this to us, for we could never have brought it under control ourselves"* (43:13).

[PAUSE]

Stay with that last phrase, because it is the whole cure for the numbness. *Wa mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn* — "**we could never have subdued it ourselves.**"

The camel is stronger than you. Far stronger. It could crush you, could refuse you, could throw you and walk away. The sea is vaster than you and indifferent to you and has swallowed more men than anyone can count. By what right does the weak thing — *you* — sit on the back of the strong thing and command it? By no right of yours. You did not earn that dominion and you could not enforce it. It was *sakhkhara* — subjected, made-compliant, *for you,* by Someone else. The strong was bent toward the weak. That is not a fact of nature. It is a daily, repeated **gift,** and the gift has been so reliable for so long that you have stopped seeing it as a gift at all.

This is the precise illusion the ayah is dismantling: the illusion of **self-propulsion.** We genuinely believe we move ourselves. *I* drove here. *I* flew to see them. But you did not make the fuel burn, the water hold, the wind blow, the animal obey. You climbed into a system of gifts and pressed a pedal. The motion was always borrowed. *Mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn.*

And notice the *spiritual mechanics* the verse prescribes. The remembrance is to come **"when you are settled upon them"** — *idhā stawaytum.* Not before. Not after. *In* the moment of mounting. Allah ties the act of gratitude to the precise instant of the favor, because He knows that is exactly the instant we forget. The transition from standing to riding — the moment your weight transfers and the strong thing accepts it — *that* is when the heart is supposed to wake up and whisper *Subḥān.* He installed a trigger in the most habitual act of your day.

So here is what changes tomorrow — and this one is concrete and you can do it within the hour. The next time you sit down inside *anything that moves* — car, bus, train, plane, even an elevator that lifts your weight against gravity you could never overcome alone — let the click of the seatbelt be the trigger. In that instant say it: *Subḥāna lladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn.* Glory to the One who bent this to me, for I could never have bent it myself. Do it once with full attention and you will feel the numbness crack. You will feel, for one moment, the surface go thin and the source shine through. **That is the cure for being a person of gold leaf — to feel, even once a day, the gift inside the ordinary.**

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: A Pair Inside the Verse About Pairs

Now that we've done the work, let me show you something hidden in the *shape* of the ayah itself.

The verse is *about* pairs — *al-azwāj.* And then watch what it lists: *al-fulk* — **the ships** — and *al-anʿām* — **the cattle.** Ships travel the **sea.** Cattle travel the **land.** The verse about pairs gives you, as its closing example, **a pair:** the two domains a human being can cross, sea and land, the built thing and the grown thing, set side by side under one verb.

The ayah doesn't just *tell* you creation comes in twos. In its final breath, it *demonstrates* it — handing you a perfect pair as the proof of the principle it opened with. The form is the content. That is not an accident a person of surface would ever notice. It is gold all the way down, not gold leaf.

## Bringing It Together

See how the two halves of this ayah were always one insight approached from opposite ends.

The **pairs** taught us that everything created leans, needs, is incomplete — drawn around the One who needs nothing. The **transport** taught us that even our movement, even what feels most like our own power, is borrowed — bent toward us by the unpaired One. Both are saying the *same thing.* Half from the cosmic scale, half from the seat of your car: **You are the dependent one. He is the One depended upon. And He has filled your dependence, from the structure of the galaxies down to the camel that knelt for you this morning, with favor.** The pairs are the diagnosis. The transport is the bedside care. Same Hand. Same love. Two distances.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life are you trying to be the only thing with no pair — needing no one, leaning on nothing? What is that costing you?
2. When did you last feel *astonished* by something completely ordinary? What would it take to feel it again?
3. You concede that Allah made all of this — like the people in 43:9 conceded it. Does the concession change how you live, or does it just sit in your mind, true and inert?
4. What is the *one* daily, repeated moment of borrowed power — the car, the elevator, the plane — where you could install a trigger of remembrance this week?
5. Your soul is shaped like a *zawj,* a thing cut to fit another. Honestly: what have you been trying to fill that shape with?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Everything that exists comes in pairs that lean and need — a cosmos-wide confession that there is only One who needs nothing — and that same unpaired One has bent even your borrowed motion toward you as a favor, if you would only feel it when you sit down.**

## The Closing Invitation

This surah is for people who *know* and don't *feel* — and that is most of us, most days. The invitation is not to learn something new. You already conceded the Creator long ago. The invitation is to let the concession descend the eighteen inches from your head to your heart — and the ayah even tells you the doorway: the seat of whatever carries you. Let the most ordinary motion of your day become the place where the surface goes thin and you finally see the gold underneath the gold leaf. Not someday. The next time you sit down.

## A Brief Duʿā

O You who created the pairs, all of them, and left no pair for Yourself — I am one of the leaning, needing things You made, and I have spent so long pretending I could stand alone. Bend my heart toward You the way You bent the strong things toward my weakness. And when I sit down tomorrow inside the gift I always forget, let me remember, and say with my whole chest: *Subḥāna lladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn.* Āmīn.
