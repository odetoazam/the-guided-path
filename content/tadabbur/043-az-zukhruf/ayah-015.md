# A Portion of the Whole

## How the Heart Fragments the One It Cannot Bear

---

### The Ayah

> وَجَعَلُوا لَهُۥ مِنْ عِبَادِهِۦ جُزْءًا ۚ إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَكَفُورٌ مُّبِينٌ

*wa-jaʿalū lahu min ʿibādihi juzʾā · inna l-insāna la-kafūrun mubīn*

**"And they have assigned to Him a portion from among His own servants. Indeed, the human being is clearly ungrateful."**

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

I want you to stand for a moment inside the mind of a man in Mecca, fourteen centuries ago.

He is not an atheist. That category would barely make sense to him. He believes in God — in fact, he believes in *Allah* by name. If you walked up to him and asked, "Who made the sky? Who raised these mountains? Who created you?" — he would not hesitate. He would say: *Allah.* The Qur'an tells us this directly, just a few verses earlier in this very surah:

> *"And if you ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they will surely say: The Almighty, the All-Knowing created them."* (43:9)

So here is a man who knows the Creator's name. Who admits the Creator's power. Who looks at the heavens and credits them, correctly, to the One.

And then — in the same breath, with the same tongue — he says something that should be impossible for a man who just said all that.

He says the angels are the daughters of God.

Not servants of God. *Daughters.* Offspring. Children. Beings who came out of Him the way a child comes out of a parent — sharing His substance, carrying a piece of His essence, a fragment broken off the Infinite.

Now — sit with how strange this is. This is not a man who denies God. This is a man who *believes* in God and then proceeds to take God apart. To divide Him. To break off pieces of the Indivisible and scatter them among created things.

And there is a second strangeness, an even sharper one. *Daughters.* In the culture of these very men, a daughter was a source of shame. When a daughter was born to one of them, the Qur'an tells us elsewhere, his face would darken and he would hide from his people (16:58). Some of them buried their daughters alive in the sand. So watch what they have done: the thing they considered *beneath themselves* to receive — they assigned to God. The lowest portion of their own world, they handed up to the Most High.

This is the world verse 15 walks into. A world of people who know God exists, know He is the Creator — and still find a way to fragment Him, still find a way to insult Him with the very gift they refuse for themselves.

**And here is what I need you to feel before we go a single step further:** the Qur'an is about to take one word — one small, ordinary word — and use it to expose the entire architecture of this madness. And then it is going to do something you will not expect. It is going to turn from *them* — those specific men, in that specific city — and point the finger at *you.* At me. At every human being who has ever lived.

The crime belongs to the Meccans.

The diagnosis belongs to all of us.

Let us find out why.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### Element One: The Word *Juzʾ* — "A Portion"

Before we open the Arabic, let me give you a picture from your own life.

Imagine someone hands you a gold coin — a single, solid, perfect coin. And then imagine they take a hammer and a chisel and they chip a small sliver off the edge of it. That sliver is now a *piece* of the coin. It is made of the same gold. It came *out of* the coin. The coin is now, by exactly that much, less whole than it was.

That act — chipping a piece off a whole — is the entire concept hiding inside the word the Qur'an chooses here. The word is **جُزْءًا** — *juzʾan.* It means **a part, a portion, a fragment, a piece broken off a larger whole.**

You already know this word, even if you don't realize it. When the Qur'an itself is divided for recitation into thirty sections, each section is called a *juzʾ.* A *part* of the whole. A *thirtieth.*

Now look at what Allah says they did:

> *wa-jaʿalū lahu min ʿibādihi **juzʾan***
> "And they assigned to Him, from among His servants, **a portion.**"

Let's sit with this. The Meccans' logic — and the classical commentators name it explicitly — was this: *a child is a part of the parent.* Your child carries your substance. Your child is, biologically, a piece of you continued. So when they claimed the angels were God's *daughters*, what they were really claiming — whether they understood it fully or not — was that there exist beings who are **a piece of God.** Fragments chipped off the Divine. *Juzʾ.*

Al-Jalālayn makes this connection razor-sharp: *"they said the angels are the daughters of Allah — because a child is a portion (juzʾ) of the parent."*

Do you see what the single word has done? It has not described their words. It has X-rayed their *logic* and shown you the broken bone underneath. The moment you say God has a child, you have said God has a *part.* And the moment you say God has a part — [PAUSE] — you have said God can be *divided.*

And here is what is remarkable. Notice what the word *juzʾ* quietly does to the entire idea of God.

A whole that can be divided is, by definition, a whole that is *made of pieces.* A thing that has parts is a thing that is *assembled.* And a thing that is assembled is a thing that depends on its parts — take a part away and it is diminished, add a part and it is increased. Anything with parts is **contingent.** It can be more, it can be less. It can be broken.

But Allah is **al-Aḥad** — the One. Not "one" as in *first in a series.* One as in *indivisible. Not made of parts. Cannot be added to, cannot be subtracted from, cannot be broken.* Surah al-Ikhlāṣ, the surah that is said to equal a third of the Qur'an, exists almost entirely to crush this single error: *"He neither begets nor is begotten"* (112:3) — He has no offspring, because offspring would be a *part,* and He has no parts.

So when the Qur'an says they assigned Him *juzʾan* — a portion — it is not merely reporting a false belief. It is showing you that their belief is **self-detonating.** They claimed to honor God by giving Him children. But to give God a part is to deny that God is God. You cannot fragment the Indivisible and still be talking about Him. The honor was an annihilation.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing in this word, easy to miss. Look at its form: *juzʾan* — **indefinite.** Not *the* portion. Just *a* portion. A vague, unspecified piece. The Qur'an does not even dignify their claim by spelling it out in full here. It compresses the whole blasphemy — *"the angels are the daughters of God"* — down to one small, indefinite, almost dismissive word: *a portion.* As if to say: *look at the smallness of what you imagine, set against the One you imagine it about.*

**What this single word reveals:** to assign God a child is to assign God a part — and to assign God a part is to deny that He is One. Their reverence was a hammer to the coin.

### Element Two: The Word *ʿIbād* — "His Servants"

Now I want you to feel the second word, because it sits right beside the first, and the two of them together create something almost unbearable.

The portion they assigned to God — where did they get it from? The verse tells us: **مِنْ عِبَادِهِ** — *min ʿibādihi* — **"from among His own servants."**

The word is from the root **ʿ-b-d** (Ebd). It is one of the heaviest roots in the entire Qur'an. *ʿAbd* means a **servant, a slave, the one who is wholly owned, the one whose entire reason for being is to serve.** It is the root of *ʿibāda* — worship. It is the root in *ʿAbdullāh* — "servant of Allah," the name the Prophet ﷺ said is among the most beloved to God.

To be an *ʿabd* is to stand at the absolute opposite end of existence from the Master. The *ʿabd* is owned; the Master owns. The *ʿabd* is brought into being; the Master brings into being. The *ʿabd* serves; the Master is served. There is no relationship in human language that expresses a wider gap than that between the slave and the One who possesses him utterly.

Now — watch what the Meccans did. Hold the two words side by side:

They took **ʿibād** — *servants* — and turned them into **juzʾ** — *a part of the Master.*

[PAUSE]

These are not just two different words. They are **opposites that cannot coexist.** A *part* of something shares its essence — a sliver of gold *is* gold. A *servant* of someone is utterly *other* than him — the slave is not made of the master's substance; he is a separate created being who belongs to him. To call the same being both a *servant* and a *portion* is to say it is simultaneously *wholly other than God* and *a piece of God.* It is a contradiction at the level of being itself.

And notice the genius of how Allah phrases it. He does not say "they assigned Him a portion from among the angels." He says **"from among His servants."** He insists, in the very sentence that records their crime, on naming those beings by their *true* relationship to Him: *My servants. My owned ones. My worshippers.*

The angels — the very beings the Meccans tried to elevate into daughters — are in reality the most perfect *servants* in all creation. They are described elsewhere as those who *"do not disobey Allah in what He commands them, and do as they are commanded"* (66:6); who *"glorify Him night and day and do not slacken"* (21:20). The angels themselves would be the first to recoil in horror at being called God's children. Their entire mode of existence is *ʿibāda* — pure, ceaseless servitude.

So the Meccans committed a double insult without even realizing it. They thought they were *honoring* the angels by promoting them to daughters of God. But in doing so, they **demoted** them — they tried to drag the perfect servants *out* of the noble station of servitude and into a false, impossible, blasphemous station that the angels would never want. The greatest honor in existence is to be God's *ʿabd.* The Meccans tried to "upgrade" the angels right out of it.

Here is the principle underneath, and it is one of the deepest truths in all of *tawḥīd*: **the distance between the Creator and the creature is infinite and unbridgeable, and that distance is not a tragedy — it is the very thing that makes worship possible.** You cannot worship what is partly you. You can only worship the wholly Other. The Meccans, in trying to bring God *closer* by giving Him family, actually destroyed the only relationship a creature can truly have with Him: the relationship of the *ʿabd* to his *Rabb.*

**What this single word reveals:** they took the highest of His servants and tried to make them part of the Master — and in collapsing the infinite distance between slave and Lord, they did not honor the servants; they unmade the only honor a creature can have.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Verb *Jaʿalū* — "They *Made*" (Or Did They?)

Now I want to slow down on the very first word of the verse, because there is something here that changes how the whole sin should sit in your chest.

The verb is **وَجَعَلُوا** — *wa-jaʿalū* — usually translated "and they made" or "and they assigned."

In Arabic, the verb *jaʿala* has two distinct senses, and you need both to feel this verse.

The **first** sense is *to make, to create, to physically bring about.* "He *made* the table." "Allah *made* the night a covering."

But there is a **second** sense, and the classical grammarians are emphatic that it is *this* one operating here. Al-Baghawī states it plainly: *"the meaning of jaʿl here is the act of ruling something to be so, and of declaring it — as when you say, 'I made Zayd the most excellent of people,' meaning: I described him so, I judged him to be so."*

In other words, *jaʿala* here does not mean *they manufactured.* It means **they declared. They deemed. They asserted by speech.** It is the *jaʿala* of the courtroom and the tongue, not the *jaʿala* of the workshop.

Let me give you the everyday version. If I point at the most ordinary man on the street and announce, "I hereby *make* him king of England" — I have not changed a single atom of reality. There is no crown on his head. He rules nothing. All I have done is *say* a thing. My declaration is **completely impotent against the truth.** The man remains exactly what he was; only my mouth has moved.

Now feel the verse with that understanding.

> *wa-jaʿalū lahu min ʿibādihi juzʾan*
> "And they **declared** for Him, from among His servants, a portion."

[PAUSE]

This is enormous. It means their shirk **never touched God.** It could not. They did not actually break a piece off the Infinite — that is impossible; the Indivisible cannot be divided. They did not actually turn a servant into a part of the Master — that is impossible; being itself does not bend to human opinion. All they did was **move their tongues.** All they did was *say.*

Their entire rebellion against the Lord of the heavens and the earth amounted to a sentence. A declaration as empty as my crowning a stranger king. The reality of God stood, and stands, and will forever stand — *utterly untouched* by what they assigned to Him.

And this is a mercy and a warning at once. A mercy, because it tells you that no human blasphemy diminishes God by an atom's weight — *"Allah is free of all need, and you are the needy"* (47:38). When people speak evil of your Lord, understand: they are crowning strangers kings in their own heads; the throne of reality does not so much as flicker.

But it is also a warning. Because if the act was *only* speech — *only* a declaration with no power to change anything — then why does it matter so much? Why does the Qur'an treat it as among the gravest of all sins?

Because **the sin was never about damaging God. The sin was about what it revealed inside the human being.** The declaration changed nothing in the heavens. But it exposed *everything* in the heart of the one who made it. And that — that exposure — is exactly where the second half of the verse takes us.

**What this verb reveals:** their shirk was a speech-act, powerless to alter the One it slandered. Which means the verse is not finally about *God* being divided. It is about the *human being* who would even try.

---

### The Bridge

So far the Qur'an has shown us three things, all in the first five words.

That they tried to *fragment* the Indivisible — *juzʾ.*

That they tried to turn the lowest *servant* into a part of the Master — *ʿibād.*

That all of it was finally just a *declaration,* a moving of tongues against an unmovable reality — *jaʿalū.*

Three words, and the entire anatomy of their error is laid bare. But notice — everything so far has been about *what they did.* The verse has not yet told us *what kind of being would do such a thing.*

Now let us ask the deeper question. Not "what did they say about God?" but: **"what does it reveal about the human heart, that it would say such a thing at all?"**

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Disease of Fragmenting the One

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a thing the human mind does when it meets something too large to hold. It breaks it into pieces.

You do this every day, and mostly it serves you well. You cannot comprehend "the economy," so you think about your paycheck. You cannot grasp "all of human history," so you remember a few dates. You cannot picture a billion, so you picture a thousand, a thousand times. The mind is a fragmenting instrument. It survives by chopping the infinite into bite-sized portions it can chew.

And here is the trouble. **When the mind turns this same instrument on God, it commits a catastrophe.**

Because God is the one reality that *cannot* be broken into pieces without ceasing to be Himself. Every other thing you fragment, you understand better. The one thing you must *not* fragment — because the moment you do, you are no longer thinking about Him at all — is the One.

This is what the Meccans did. The pure, transcendent, indivisible reality of Allah was *too much.* A God with no children, no relatives, no parts, no committee, no intermediaries — a God you cannot picture, cannot bound, cannot domesticate — that God is *overwhelming.* He demands a kind of surrender the heart finds terrifying. So the heart does what it always does with the overwhelming: it breaks Him down. It gives Him daughters. It gives Him a family, a household, a structure the mind can finally hold. It chips the coin so it can fit a piece in its pocket.

And — here is the part I need you to feel — **we still do this.** Not with literal idols. With something far more subtle.

We fragment God into the parts we like and the parts we ignore.

We take *ar-Raḥmān,* the Merciful, and we keep that piece close, because it comforts us. And we quietly set down *al-ʿAdl,* the Just, and *al-Muntaqim,* the Avenger, because those pieces frighten us. We build a god out of the attributes that soothe and we file away the attributes that demand. We say, "*my* God would never..." — and in that sentence, do you hear it? — we have chipped the coin. We have assigned ourselves a *portion* of God, the bearable portion, and turned away from the whole.

We do it with His commands, too. We take the religion and we keep the *juzʾ* that costs us nothing — the part that flatters our existing life — and we leave the part that would overturn us. *"Do you believe in part of the Scripture and disbelieve in part?"* the Qur'an asks elsewhere (2:85). *Juzʾ* again. The disease of the portion.

[PAUSE]

The Meccans' shirk and our selective faith are the same illness wearing different clothes. Both refuse the *whole.* Both want a manageable fragment instead of an overwhelming totality. Both are the mind, terrified of the Infinite, reaching for the hammer.

And the cure is in the word the Meccans could not bear: **al-Aḥad.** The One. Not a God assembled from the pieces you prefer. The One who comes *entire* or not at all. *Lā ilāha illā Llāh* is not the rejection of other gods only — it is the refusal to fragment *this* One. It is the heart saying: *I will take Him whole. The Merciful and the Just. The comforting command and the costly one. I will not chip the coin.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tonight, ask yourself one honest question — *which portion of my Lord am I keeping, and which portion have I quietly set down because it asks too much of me?* Then pick up the part you set down. Read about the attribute you avoid. Open the command you've been editing out. Worship Him *whole.* That is the meaning of *tawḥīd* — not just "one God," but **one undivided surrender to the One.**

### Theme Two: Ingratitude as the Burial of Blessing

Now we arrive at the turn — the moment the verse stops describing *them* and starts describing *us.*

> *inna l-insāna la-kafūrun mubīn*
> **"Indeed, the human being is clearly ungrateful."**

Notice first what just happened grammatically, because it is breathtaking. The first half of the verse was **plural**: *jaʿalū* — *they* assigned. A specific group. The Meccans. But the second half does not say "indeed *they* are ungrateful." It says **"indeed *the human being*"** — singular, universal, with the definite article: *al-insān.* **Mankind.** The species.

This is *iltifat* — a deliberate turn. The Qur'an zooms out from one tribe in one city to **the entire human race.** It says, in effect: *do not comfort yourself that this is about those pagans long ago. This is about what a human being IS.* The crime was theirs. The diagnosis is yours.

And what is the diagnosis? **كَفُورٌ** — *kafūr.*

Now we have to open this word carefully, because most people only know one half of it.

The root is **k-f-r** (kfr). And its original, physical meaning — the meaning that sits underneath everything — is **to cover, to conceal, to bury.** The Arabs called the farmer *kāfir* — not because he disbelieved, but because he *covers the seed* with soil, burying it in the dark earth. The night is sometimes called *kāfir* because it covers the world in darkness. To do *kufr* is, at its root, **to take something and cover it over so it can no longer be seen.**

So what does the disbeliever cover? He covers the **truth** he knows is there — like soil over a seed, he buries the obvious God under the dark earth of denial.

But *kufr* has a second face, and it is *this* one the verse is pressing on. The opposite of *kufr* is not "belief" alone — it is *shukr,* **gratitude.** And so *kufr* also means **ingratitude:** to take a *blessing* and bury it. To receive a gift and cover it over until you can no longer see that you were given anything at all.

And the word here is not even the plain form. It is **كَفُور** — *kafūr* — the **intensive** form (*ṣīghat al-mubālagha,* the pattern *faʿūl*). Not "ungrateful." **Profoundly, habitually, relentlessly ungrateful.** One who buries blessings *as a way of life.*

Al-Muyassar's commentary draws out the picture with devastating precision. The *kafūr,* it says, is the one who *"counts up the calamities and forgets the blessings."*

[PAUSE]

Stop there. Because that is not a description of an ancient pagan. **That is a description of the human mind on an ordinary Tuesday.**

Modern psychologists gave this a name. They call it **negativity bias.** The well-documented finding that the human brain registers, stores, and replays negative events far more powerfully than positive ones. One criticism outweighs ten compliments. One thing that went wrong today eclipses the thousand things that went right. Your heart beat one hundred thousand times today without your asking — you did not thank God once for a single beat. But let it *skip* once, and you will think of nothing else for a week.

We *count the calamities and forget the blessings.* The Qur'an named the disease fourteen centuries before the psychologists measured it. And it did not call it a "bias." It called it *kufr.*

Do you see now why the two halves of this verse belong together? Why does Allah follow *"they assigned Him a portion"* immediately with *"the human is clearly ungrateful"*? What does shirk have to do with ingratitude?

**Everything. They are the same act.**

To exist at all is the gift. Every breath, every faculty, every angel that serves the cosmos in which you live — all of it is *from* the One, *by* the One, *belonging to* the One. And what did the Meccans do with that total, overwhelming gift? They took the Giver's own servants — beings *He* created, *He* sustains, *He* owns — and they credited them with a share in divinity. They buried the Giver under His own gifts. They looked at the blessing and forgot the Blesser. They took what was *entirely* His and assigned it elsewhere.

**That is ingratitude in its purest and most total form.** Shirk is not a separate sin from ingratitude. Shirk *is* ingratitude, raised to infinity. It is the *kufr* — the *burial* — of the one truth most worth seeing: that *all* of this, *all* of it, came from One Hand.

[PAUSE]

And now the final word locks it shut: **مُبِين** — *mubīn.* "Clear. Manifest. Self-evident."

It comes from the root **b-y-n** (byn), and this root holds a quiet beauty. *Bayn* means *the space between* — separation, distinction. And in Arabic, a thing becomes *clear* precisely when it is *distinct,* when it stands separated out from everything around it, when there is space around it so you cannot miss it. Clarity *is* separation. The verb here is Form IV — *abāna* — to make something stand out so plainly it announces itself.

So the human being's ingratitude is not hidden. It is not subtle. It does not need a detective. It is *mubīn* — it **separates itself out,** it **declares itself,** it stands so distinct against the backdrop of God's endless giving that it cannot be missed. The very obviousness of how much we've been given is what makes the ingratitude so glaring. The brighter the gift, the darker the burial looks against it.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The cure for *kufr* is *shukr,* and *shukr* begins with *counting differently.* The *kafūr* counts calamities; the grateful soul counts blessings. So tonight, before you sleep, do the one thing the *kafūr* never does — **uncover one buried blessing.** Name three gifts you received today that you had already covered over with forgetting. The eyes reading these words. The mind holding them. The breath you did not have to earn. Dig one seed back up out of the dark soil of your forgetting, and let it see the light. Do it tomorrow. Do it the day after. This is how a *kafūr* becomes a *shakūr* — not by feeling more, but by *seeing* what was always there.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

---

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse as a single built thing, because its structure is the proof of its meaning.

It has two halves, and they mirror each other across a hidden hinge:

**First half — the crime:** *They assigned to Him, from among His servants, a portion.*
**Second half — the diagnosis:** *Indeed, the human being is clearly ungrateful.*

The first half is **plural and particular** — *they,* the Meccans.
The second half is **singular and universal** — *the human being,* all of us.

The first half describes an **outward act** — a declaration about God.
The second half names an **inward condition** — a state of the heart.

And the hinge between them — the word *inna,* "indeed" — is not a pause. It is a *therefore.* It says: *this act flows from that condition.* They could fragment God and bury the Giver under His gifts because that is what the ungrateful heart, left to itself, *does.* The crime in the first half is the symptom; the disease in the second half is the cause. Read the structure and you have read the diagnosis: **shirk is what ingratitude looks like when it grows all the way up.**

---

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of our journey — the words and the themes — were the same insight seen from two directions.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a heart that *fragments* — *juzʾ,* the portion chipped off the Whole; that *inverts* — *ʿibād,* the servant dragged toward the Master's throne; that *declares* impotently against reality — *jaʿalū,* the tongue crowning strangers kings.

The **thematic journey** showed us *why* a heart would do this: because it cannot bear the undivided One, so it breaks Him into bearable pieces; and because it is *kafūr* — it buries the Giver beneath the very gifts He gave.

They are one insight. The mind fragments the One it cannot hold, and the fragmenting *is* the ingratitude, because to break God into portions is to forget that every portion was already, wholly, His gift. **Tawḥīd and shukr are the same act of the heart** — to receive the One, whole, with thanks. And shirk and kufr are the same act of the heart — to break Him up, and bury Him under His own blessings.

### Questions to Carry

1. Which *portion* of my Lord am I keeping close, and which portion have I quietly set down because it asks more of me than I want to give?

2. When I look at my own life, do I — like the *kafūr* — *count the calamities and forget the blessings*? If someone read back my complaints and my thanksgivings from the past week, which list would be longer?

3. The Meccans tried to *honor* the angels and ended up degrading them. Where in my own religion am I "improving" on what Allah has said — and calling my edit reverence?

4. Their blasphemy never touched God; it only exposed their hearts. What do *my* words about God, spoken in frustration or doubt, expose about *mine*?

5. If ingratitude is the *burial* of a blessing, how many blessings lie buried in the soil of my forgetting right now — fully grown, fully given, completely unseen?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The heart that cannot bear the undivided God breaks Him into bearable pieces and buries the Giver beneath His own gifts — and that fragmenting, that burial, is the clearest mark of what a human being becomes when left to himself.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

You came to this verse expecting, perhaps, a story about ancient pagans and their strange beliefs. But the verse refused to leave them in the past. It turned, mid-sentence, and named *you.* Not to condemn you — to *show* you. To hold up a mirror and say: *this tendency to fragment, this tendency to bury — it lives in every human chest, including yours. Now that you can see it, you can resist it.*

So let the invitation be simple. Refuse the hammer. Take your Lord *whole* — the comforting attribute and the demanding one, the easy command and the costly one. And refuse the burial. Each day, dig one blessing back out of the dark and let it see the light of your thanks. To live undivided before the Undivided One, counting His gifts instead of your griefs — that is the whole of the religion, hidden inside nine small words.

---

### A Closing Du'a

*O Allah, al-Aḥad, the One who has no portion, no part, and no peer — let my heart receive You whole, never fragmenting You into the pieces I prefer. You are the Giver of every gift I have buried in my forgetting; make me of the* shakūrīn *and not the* kafūrīn. *Open my eyes to count Your blessings before I count my burdens, and let my tongue declare nothing of You but the truth — clear, manifest, and undivided. Āmīn.*
