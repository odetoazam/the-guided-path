# Whether You See It or Not

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the room they were spoken into.

> [ayah:43:41] فَإِمَّا نَذْهَبَنَّ بِكَ فَإِنَّا مِنْهُم مُّنتَقِمُونَ
> [ayah:43:42] أَوْ نُرِيَنَّكَ ٱلَّذِى وَعَدْنَـٰهُمْ فَإِنَّا عَلَيْهِم مُّقْتَدِرُونَ

*Fa-immā nadh-habanna bika fa-innā minhum muntaqimūn. Aw nuriyannaka alladhī waʿadnāhum fa-innā ʿalayhim muqtadirūn.*

"So whether We take you away — We will indeed exact retribution from them. Or We show you what We have promised them — then indeed, We are fully able to overpower them."

Now let me build the room.

These words come down in Mecca, in a stretch of the Prophet's life that we sometimes rush past because we know how the story ends. But *he* did not know how the story ends — not in the human sense, not as a date on a calendar. What he knew was the daily weight of it. He had stood in front of his own people, the people of his own lineage and language and streets, and he had told them the most important news a human being can carry. And they laughed. They called it sorcery. They called him deranged. They mocked the very idea that this man, with no army and no gold, would be the one Allah chose.

The verse just before these two — verse 40 — had landed on him like a hand on a grieving shoulder: *Can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind out of their error?* In other words: their refusal was never yours to fix. You are not failing. You are doing the one thing a messenger does — you deliver. The hearing is not in your hands.

And here is the wound that hides underneath that consolation. It is the wound of every sincere person who pours their life into something true and does not see it bear fruit. The Prophet ﷺ could look at the hardness in front of him and a quiet, unspoken fear could form: *What if I die before any of this is vindicated? What if I am lowered into the ground while they are still standing in the streets, still laughing, still winning?*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that fear for a moment, because it is not only his. It belongs to the parent raising a child whose character they may never live to see formed. It belongs to the one who builds something good knowing the harvest is decades away. It belongs to anyone who has ever whispered: *but will I be here to see it matter?*

These two verses are Allah's answer to exactly that fear. And the answer is built with such precision — two roads, two endings, one absolute certainty — that once you see the architecture, you will not be able to un-see it.

Here is what I want you to hunt for with me. There are **two possible futures** in these verses. Allah names both. And He arranges them so that no matter which one comes true, *the deniers lose and the truth wins.* He closes every exit. He leaves no third door.

The question I want to hold open is this: **why did Allah make the Prophet's own survival irrelevant to the outcome?** What is He teaching about where the weight of a mission actually rests?

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Sentence With No Third Door — *fa-immā ... aw*

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you a structure, plainly, from your own life.

Imagine someone you love is in surgery, and you are in the waiting room with a friend who steadies you. And that friend says: "Listen. Either the doctor comes out and says it went perfectly — and you'll be relieved. Or the doctor comes out and says there were complications — and we will face it together, right here, and you will not be alone." Notice what that sentence does. It names *both* possible futures. It does not pretend the bad one cannot happen. And then it attaches a stable outcome to *each branch* — relief on one side, "you won't be alone" on the other. By the time your friend finishes, there is no version of the next hour in which you are abandoned. Both doors lead to a room where you are held.

That is the exact shape of these two verses. In Arabic it is built on **فَإِمَّا ... أَوْ** — *fa-immā ... aw* — "whether this ... or that." It is a conditional that splits the future into branches and then assigns a guaranteed result to each one.

Let's open the first branch.

> **فَإِمَّا نَذْهَبَنَّ بِكَ**

"Whether We take you away..." — branch one.

> **أَوْ نُرِيَنَّكَ ٱلَّذِى وَعَدْنَٰهُمْ**

"Or We show you what We promised them..." — branch two.

Now notice something. What are the only two possibilities for the Prophet ﷺ regarding the deniers' downfall? Either he is **gone before it happens**, or he is **present to witness it**. There is no third state. You cannot be partly alive. You are either taken before the day arrives, or you are here when it does.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it is the quiet genius of the construction. Allah does not say, "Be patient, it will probably work out." He does not offer a hope. He performs a kind of logical enclosure. He takes the entire space of possible futures — *every* future the Prophet could be afraid of — and He divides it cleanly into two, and into each half He pours the *same* guarantee: **they will not escape.**

Here's what's remarkable. The fear we named in the introduction — *what if I die before vindication?* — that fear lives entirely inside branch one. It is the worst case. And Allah does not avoid it or soften it into vagueness. He walks straight into the worst case and plants the promise *there first*: even if you are taken away, **فَإِنَّا مِنْهُم مُّنتَقِمُونَ** — "We will exact retribution from them." The thing you feared would cancel the outcome — your own death — changes nothing about the outcome.

Do you feel what that does? Most reassurance works by making the bad branch unlikely. This works by making the bad branch *irrelevant.* It doesn't say the worst case won't happen. It says: even in the worst case, you have lost nothing that matters.

That is the difference between human comfort and divine speech. Human comfort manages the odds. Divine speech removes the stakes from the odds altogether.

And before we move on, one small thing that the ear catches and the eye misses. Listen to the verbs: *nadh-haba**nna**, nuriya**nna**ka.* That doubled, hammering ending — *-anna, -annaka* — is the heavy emphatic *nūn* of Arabic, the **nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah**. It is the grammatical equivalent of an oath, a way of saying "We absolutely, certainly, will." Allah does not merely state the two branches. He *swears* into each one. The structure is already certain; the sound makes it unbreakable.

**What this one feature reveals:** Allah did not comfort His Prophet by improving the odds. He comforted him by closing every door but one — and the one He left open was the promise.

### Element Two: The Gentleness of "Taking You Away" — *nadh-habanna bika*

Now I want to slow down on a single phrase, because the *tone* of it is doing something the translation almost entirely hides.

When Allah refers to the Prophet's departure, He does not use a harsh or clinical word. He says **نَذْهَبَنَّ بِكَ** — built on the root *dh-h-b*, the most ordinary verb in Arabic for "to go." *Dhahaba* is what you say when someone simply *goes* — leaves the room, departs on a journey. And here it is in a particular form: *nadh-haba **bika*** — literally, "We go *with* you," "We take you along."

Think about how tender that is. There were harder, heavier words available. The Qur'an elsewhere speaks of death with the language of *seizing* the soul, of the soul being *snatched* or *drawn out*. But here, for *His own Prophet*, in a moment of consoling him, Allah reaches for the gentlest possible image: We will simply *take you along with Us.* Not *strike you down.* Not *end you.* **Take you with Us.** As one takes a beloved companion by the hand and walks him home.

[PAUSE]

Now, the classical scholars actually open a beautiful range of meaning here, and I don't want to flatten it into one. Let me lay the angles side by side, because together they make a fuller picture than any one alone.

Al-Baghawī and the reading reflected in al-Jalālayn understand *nadh-habanna bika* as **death** — "if We cause you to die before We punish them." Al-Muyassar reads it the same way: "if We take you in death before your victory over the deniers." On this reading, the verse is consoling the Prophet about the most final separation there is.

But al-Qurṭubī preserves a different tradition. He reads *nadh-habanna bika* as **removing you from Mecca** — taking you out, away from the harm of Quraysh, into emigration. On this angle, the "taking away" is not death at all, but the journey to safety, the *hijrah*.

Now watch what happens when you hold both at once. Whether the "taking away" is death or departure from the city — in *both* readings, the meaning is the same shape: **the Prophet is removed from the scene before the reckoning arrives.** Either he is taken to his Lord, or he is taken to safety. And in *neither* case does his absence from Mecca delay or weaken what comes for the deniers. The retribution is not waiting on him to be present. It is not his to deliver or to witness. It belongs entirely to the One who promised it.

So the two scholarly readings are not a fork in the road where you must pick one. They are two camera angles on a single truth: **your removal from the scene — by any means — does not move the promise one inch.**

And here is the human ache underneath the gentleness. To be told "We will take you away" is, in a sense, to be told: *you may not be here for the ending.* That is a hard thing to hear. And Allah wraps that hard thing in the softest verb He has — *We will take you with Us* — so that the Prophet hears, in the same breath, both the truth of his finitude and the tenderness of the One holding him through it.

**What this phrase reveals:** Even when Allah tells His servant the thing he is most afraid to hear, He says it with the gentleness of taking a hand. The hardness is in the fact; the softness is in the speaker.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Twin Endings — *muntaqimūn* and *muqtadirūn*

Now look at how the two verses end. Look at them sitting next to each other.

> ...فَإِنَّا مِنْهُم **مُّنتَقِمُونَ**
> ...فَإِنَّا عَلَيْهِم **مُّقْتَدِرُونَ**

*Muntaqimūn.* *Muqtadirūn.* Say them out loud. Hear the rhyme — they fall in the ear like two strikes of the same hammer. This is not accident; this is the Qur'an's design closing both branches with matched cadence so that the two futures *sound* like one verdict.

But the rhyme is the surface. Underneath it is something I want you to see, and to see it I need to teach you one feature of Arabic.

Arabic verbs come in patterns, called *forms*, and each form bends the base meaning in a characteristic direction. There is a particular pattern — **Form VIII**, *iftaʿala* — that often carries a sense of the action being taken *thoroughly*, *deliberately*, drawn into and completed *by the doer himself*. It adds weight and self-involvement to a root.

Now here is the thing. *Both* of these closing words are Form VIII.

**مُنتَقِمُونَ** — *muntaqimūn* — comes from the root *n-q-m*. The base sense is response to wrong. But in Form VIII — *intaqama* — it becomes the deliberate, measured exacting of justice. Not a flash of temper. A reckoning carried out in full. "We are the ones who exact the retribution — completely."

**مُقْتَدِرُونَ** — *muqtadirūn* — comes from the root *q-d-r*, the root of *qudrah*, power and capacity. Allah is *al-Qādir*, the Powerful. But this is not that word. This is the *Form VIII* intensification — *iqtadara* — power that is not merely possessed but *fully marshalled, decisive, total.* Not "We are able" in the abstract. "We are **completely** able over them — there is no gap between Our will and its execution."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done? He could have ended both branches with simple words. Instead He reached, *both times*, for the intensified form. He matched the two endings not only in rhyme but in *grammatical weight.* And He gave the two futures two different faces of the same divine reality:

In branch one — the worst case, the Prophet taken away — He shows the face of **justice**: *muntaqimūn*, the One who settles every account in full.

In branch two — the Prophet present to witness it — He shows the face of **power**: *muqtadirūn*, the One whose ability over them is total and immediate.

And notice the prepositions, because they too are not random. Branch one: *minhum* — "**from** them" We exact retribution, as you draw a debt *from* a debtor. Branch two: *ʿalayhim* — "**over** them" We have complete power, as authority stands *over* the one it commands. *From* them, the account is collected. *Over* them, the power presides. Two relationships, both sealed.

So the architecture is now complete and you can stand back and see it whole: **two branches** (taken away / present to witness), **two divine names in action** (the Just / the All-Able), **two prepositions** (from them / over them), bound by **one rhyme** and **one grammatical intensity.** No matter which future arrives, it arrives carrying the full weight of Allah Himself.

**What these twin words reveal:** The two roads do not merely both lead somewhere good. They lead to the *same* God, showing two faces of one unbreakable certainty — and the grammar itself, intensified on both sides, refuses to let either road be the weaker one.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Step back and look at what the construction has shown us. A sentence with no third door. A departure named with the gentlest verb in the language. Two endings hammered into matching grammatical weight so neither future is softer than the other.

The words have shown us *how* the certainty is built. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about a life, and about the quiet fear that runs underneath every sincere effort we make.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Mission Does Not Need You to Survive

Let me start where you already live.

There is a particular kind of anxiety that visits people who care deeply about something larger than themselves. It is not the fear of failing. It is the fear of *not being there for the success.* The parent who lies awake wondering if they will live to see their child become who they're praying for them to become. The teacher who pours into students and will never know what they grew into. The person who plants something — a cause, a community, a body of work — knowing the shade of the tree they're planting will fall on people they'll never meet.

There is an old line, attributed across many cultures, that captures it: *a society grows great when old people plant trees whose shade they know they shall never sit in.* It is beautiful. But it is also, if we're honest, a little haunting. Because underneath it is a real grief. *I will do the work, and I will not see it matter.*

Now bring that grief to verse 41. Because that is precisely the grief Allah reaches into.

The Prophet ﷺ is, in human terms, the most invested person who has ever lived in an outcome. This is not a hobby or a career. This is the message of the Creator entrusted to one man, against a city that mocks him. If anyone had the right to need to *see it through* — to need to be standing there when the truth was vindicated — it was him.

And Allah says: **فَإِمَّا نَذْهَبَنَّ بِكَ** — even if We take you away first.

[PAUSE]

Let that land. Allah is gently detaching the Prophet ﷺ from the very thing we cling to hardest: *being present for the payoff.* He is saying — your survival to the day of vindication is not a condition of the mission's success. The success was never resting on your shoulders that way.

And here is the movement that transforms it. We assume that if we don't see the fruit, our work was somehow incomplete — that the harvest is the proof the planting mattered. Allah inverts this entirely. He says the harvest is *His.* **مِنْهُم مُّنتَقِمُونَ** — *We* will exact it. The deniers' reckoning was never the Prophet's deliverable. It was always Allah's. So whether the Prophet lives to see it changes the timing of his *witnessing*, not the reality of the *event.* The event was sealed in the heavens before the Prophet was born.

Now turn that on yourself. The good you are trying to do — the child you are raising, the knowledge you are passing on, the justice you are working toward, the words you are writing, the family you are holding together — *the fruit of it is not your deliverable.* Your deliverable is the *planting.* The *blessing* of the fruit, the *timing* of the harvest, whether it ripens in your lifetime or your grandchildren's — that belongs to Allah, exactly as the deniers' reckoning belonged to Allah and not to His Prophet.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most liberating reframes in the entire Qur'an, and it is hidden inside a verse about retribution. The weight you carry — *I must be there, I must see it work, I must finish it myself* — Allah lifts off your shoulders and places on His own. You are responsible for the sincerity of the planting. He is responsible for the harvest. And His responsibility does not expire when yours does.

There is a related teaching the Prophet ﷺ would later give his community: that when a person dies, their deeds end *except* three — ongoing charity, knowledge that benefits, and a righteous child who prays for them. Look at the logic. Each of those is precisely a *tree whose shade falls after you are gone.* Islam does not ask you to make peace with not seeing the fruit. It tells you that *not seeing the fruit is where some of the greatest fruit grows.* The reward for the planting you never harvest keeps arriving in your record long after **نَذْهَبَنَّ بِكَ** — long after He has taken you with Him.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Do the next right thing without auditing it for whether you'll live to see it pay off. Plant. Teach. Mend. Give. Detach the *worth* of the act from the *witnessing* of the result. The result has an Owner, and He outlives you.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Both Roads Arrive — Living With an Outcome You Cannot Control

Now let me name a second place you already live: the exhaustion of trying to control how things turn out.

We spend enormous energy trying to engineer the *specific* ending we want. We don't just want things to be okay — we want them to be okay *in the exact way we pictured*, on *our* timeline, witnessed by *our* eyes. And when reality refuses to take that shape, we read it as failure, even when something good is happening that simply doesn't match our script.

Watch what these verses do to that. Allah offers the Prophet ﷺ *two* completely different endings — one where he dies before the deniers fall, one where he lives to watch it — and He treats them as *equally* good outcomes. He does not say one is the success and the other is the consolation prize. He pours the same intensified certainty into both. *Muntaqimūn.* *Muqtadirūn.* Twin weights. Twin guarantees.

[PAUSE]

This is the death of "it only counts if it happens *my* way."

Think about how radical that is. We would have ranked these. We would have said: obviously the better ending is the one where I'm alive to see vindication — that's the win; dying first is the sad version. Allah refuses the ranking. He says both roads are roads to the same promise, and your peace cannot be allowed to depend on *which* road, because *that* — which road — is not yours to choose. It is *ʿalayhim muqtadirūn* — the power is His, presiding over the whole thing.

And notice the asymmetry the tafsir surfaces, because it deepens this. Al-Muyassar tells us that in branch two — the one where the Prophet lives to see it — the promise is shown in *this* world, "like the Day of Badr," where Allah disgraced the deniers by the Prophet's own hand and the hands of the believers. But in branch one — where the Prophet is taken first — al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar place the retribution in the *Hereafter.* So the two branches don't even land in the same *realm*: one is settled on a battlefield you can see, the other in a court you cannot. And Allah says: **both are certain.** The visible reckoning and the invisible reckoning carry identical weight. The one you'd get to witness is not "more real" than the one hidden in the unseen.

This is the heart of *tawakkul* — reliance on Allah. *Tawakkul* is not believing you'll get the outcome you want. It is releasing the *form* of the outcome to the One who actually controls it, while holding fast to the certainty that *whatever form it takes, it is held by Him.* The Prophet ﷺ is being trained in the highest *tawakkul*: not "I will see them fall," but "they will fall — whether I watch it or not, here or in the Hereafter, by my hand or by His decree alone — because the One who promised it is *muqtadir.*"

[PAUSE]

And there is rest in this you cannot get any other way. As long as your peace is tied to *one specific ending arriving in front of your eyes*, you are at the mercy of a thousand variables you can't govern. The moment you accept that **both roads arrive** — that you are not the author of the ending but a servant walking faithfully down whichever road you're given — the anxiety loses its grip. You stop demanding to be the witness. You start trusting the Owner.

This same consolation, by the way, is one Allah returns to again and again for His Prophet — almost word for word. In Sūrah Yūnus: "Whether We show you some of what We promise them, or take you in death..." In Sūrah al-Raʿd, the same. In Sūrah Ghāfir, the same. The Qur'an comes back to this exact two-branch shape, like a hand returning to rest on the same shoulder, as if to say: *every time the fear of the ending rises in you, here is the answer again. Both roads arrive. Walk.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** When you catch yourself demanding a *specific* outcome, on your timeline, witnessed by you — practice handing Allah the *form* of it. Say, in effect: *I will do my part faithfully; the shape of the ending is Yours, and I trust that whichever shape You give, You are fully able and perfectly just.* Then act, and let the result be His.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we've done the work, stand back and see the whole structure at once, because it is breathtaking in its symmetry.

Two verses. Two branches of the future:

- **Branch One:** *We take you away* (death, or removal from Mecca — either way, you are off the scene) → **مِنْهُم مُّنتَقِمُونَ** — We exact retribution **from** them → the face of **Justice** → settled, on the reading of most, in the **Hereafter**.

- **Branch Two:** *We show you what We promised* (you live to witness it) → **عَلَيْهِم مُّقْتَدِرُونَ** — We have complete power **over** them → the face of **Power** → settled, on al-Muyassar's reading, in **this world**, like Badr.

Two branches. Two prepositions — *from* / *over*. Two divine attributes — the Just / the All-Able. Two realms — the seen / the unseen. Bound together by **one rhyme**, **one intensified grammatical form** (Form VIII on both endings), and **one heavy emphatic nūn** swearing the certainty into each.

The deniers thought the absence of immediate consequence meant there would be no consequence. The Prophet feared his own absence might mean the consequence never came. And Allah, in two verses, dismantles *both* — the deniers' false security and the Prophet's quiet dread — with a single closed structure that leaves no exit and no doubt.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a sentence with **no third door** and **two equally-weighted endings.** The themes showed us a heart being freed from **needing to witness the result** and from **needing the result to take one specific shape.** They are the same truth: *the outcome is real, it is owned by Allah, it is certain on every branch — therefore your peace can rest in His ownership rather than in your witnessing.*

The gentlest verb in the language — *We will take you with Us* — showed us that even when Allah tells His servant the thing he most fears, He says it with tenderness. And that is the emotional ground the whole thing stands on: you can release the outcome *because of who is holding it.* You are not letting go into a void. You are letting go into the hands of the One who is *muntaqim* and *muqtadir* — perfectly just, completely able — and who takes His beloved *by the hand.*

### Questions to Carry

- Where in my life am I quietly insisting that I must *live to see* the fruit of something good — and what would change if I truly believed the fruit has an Owner who outlives me?
- Am I tying my peace to one *specific* ending, arriving on *my* timeline, witnessed by *my* eyes — and is that a form of the future Allah ever promised me?
- When I fear that the absence of immediate results means my effort doesn't matter, am I confusing the *planting* (my deliverable) with the *harvest* (His)?
- Can I name one act of good I could begin *precisely because* its fruit will fall after me?
- Do I trust the *Owner* of the outcome the way I would need to, to actually put something down?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Your striving does not need your survival to succeed and does not need your chosen ending to be vindicated — because the outcome belongs to the One whose justice and power are certain on every road, whether you live to see it or He takes you by the hand before it comes.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what I'd invite to shift. Not a resolution to try harder — these verses are not asking for more effort. They are asking for a *relocation of weight.* Take the weight you've been carrying — *I must see it, I must finish it, it must turn out my way* — and set it down where it was always meant to rest: on the One who said **فَإِنَّا** — *then indeed, We.* Indeed, **We.** Not you. Plant faithfully. Walk whichever road you're given. And let the harvest belong to its Owner.

### A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, You who take Your servants by the hand and never let the promise fail — free our hearts from needing to witness every result, and from demanding the ending take our chosen shape. Let us plant in sincerity what we may never harvest, trusting that You are *muqtadir* over all of it and perfectly just in all of it. And when You take us with You, let it be gently, with the truth we served already secured in Your hands. Āmīn.
