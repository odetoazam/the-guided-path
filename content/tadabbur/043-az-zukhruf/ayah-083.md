# Leave Them to the Shore

> <!-- ayah:43:83 -->     فَذَرْهُمْ يَخُوضُوا۟ وَيَلْعَبُوا۟ حَتَّىٰ يُلَـٰقُوا۟ يَوْمَهُمُ ٱلَّذِى يُوعَدُونَ
>
> *Fa-dharhum yakhūḍū wa yal'abū ḥattā yulāqū yawmahumu-lladhī yū'adūn.*
>
> **"So leave them to plunge and play until they meet their Day which they are promised."**

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this sentence walked into

Before you hear a single word about the grammar, I need you to be standing where the first listeners were standing. Not reading a page. Standing in a room in Mecca, late in the Meccan period, watching a man who has been talking for years.

For more than a decade, the Prophet ﷺ has been doing one thing over and over: offering people the truth. Patiently. In the marketplace, at the Ka'bah, in private homes, at the annual fairs. And by the time we reach this surah, we are watching something wear down. Not his certainty — that never moves. But something in his chest. The Qur'an itself keeps circling back to it: *"Perhaps you will kill yourself with grief over them"* — this is a man carrying the weight of other people's refusal as though it were his own failure.

Now, listen to what the people around him have become. A few pages before our ayah, in this very surah, Allah describes them with a phrase that should stop you cold. When the example of the son of Maryam was raised, He says, *"they only presented it to you for argument. Rather, they are a contentious people"* — **qawmun khaṣimūn** (43:58). A people of contention. A people who have taken the most serious question a human being can ask — *who is my Lord?* — and turned it into a sport. A debate to be won. A ball to be kicked back and forth for the pleasure of the game.

Sit with what that does to a sincere heart. You are trying to save someone's life, and they keep turning your rescue into a chess move. You bring them water and they want to argue about the cup. You have offered them everything you have, again and again, and they have decided — not failed to understand, *decided* — that they would rather play.

[PAUSE]

And then comes the moment of our ayah. Allah has just, in the verses right before this, dismantled their central confusion — the claim that the Most Merciful could have a son — and declared Himself exalted *"above what they describe."* The argument is over. The truth has been laid down completely. There is nothing left to say to them.

So Allah turns. He turns away from *them* — and He turns toward *His Prophet*. And He says one word first. **Fadharhum.**

*Leave them.*

### The hook

Here is what I could not get past when I first sat with this ayah. That command — *leave them* — sounds, at first, like the coldest thing in the Qur'an. A door closing. A giving-up.

But the deeper I went into the actual words Allah chose — the verb He used, the water hidden inside their "plunging," the strange fact that Allah suddenly *stops speaking to them at all*, and the one small possessive letter attached to the word "Day" — the more I became convinced this is not a door closing.

It is a man being lifted off the floor.

Let me show you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *fadhar*: the word for "leave" that was itself left behind

#### First, the tool

In every language, there are verbs that are — for lack of a better word — *broken*. Incomplete. English has a few. We say "I go / I went" — but "went" comes from a completely different word ("wend"); the original past of "go" just… vanished from everyday use. Nobody decided this in a meeting. The language simply wore a groove in one direction and left the other form behind.

Arabic has this too, and the grammarians have a name for it. There is a small family of verbs whose **past tense fell out of use** — the Arabs simply stopped saying it, and substituted a different verb in its place. You learn these in the second year of any serious study of Arabic morphology, and one of them is our word.

Hold that in your mind. We'll need it in a moment.

#### Now, the word

Allah does not say *utruk-hum* here — from *taraka*, the ordinary, complete, everyday verb for "leaving." He says *fadharhum* — from the verb *wadhara / yadharu*, "to leave, to let be, to release."

And here is the documented fact about this particular verb: **its past tense is, in classical usage, abandoned.** You do not say the past-tense form of it. The Arabs used only its command and its present. When they needed the past — "he left" — they reached for a different word entirely, *taraka*.

Notice what that means. Of all the words Allah could have used for "leave them," He reached for **the one verb in the language whose own past has been left behind.** The word for *leaving* has itself been *left*.

[PAUSE]

Now — I want to be honest with you about what is grammar and what is reflection. That the past tense of this verb is disused: that is documented fact, plain morphology. That Allah *intended* a poetic echo by it: that is my heart's reflection, offered to you as one way to sit with the choice, not as a claim about the hidden intent of the Speaker. I don't know why He chose this verb over the other. But I know He chose it. And I know that of the two available words for "leave," He picked the one that carries no past — the one that lives only in the imperative and the now.

Because that is exactly the tense the Prophet ﷺ needed. Not *"you should have left them years ago"* — that would be a rebuke, a past. The command has **no past to accuse him with.** It only has *now*. Leave them — starting now. The grammar refuses to look backward at the years he spent, and simply opens a door in the present.

#### And notice who Allah is talking to

Look again at the whole sentence and ask a simple question: who is being *spoken to*?

The Prophet ﷺ. *"Leave them"* — *you*, singular, direct. Allah turns His face to His beloved and addresses him personally.

And who is being spoken *about*? *Them.* "Leave **them**… so **they** may plunge and play… until **they** meet **their** Day." Third person. Every single time. The deniers are talked *about*. They are never again talked *to*.

Stay with that, because it is easy to read past and it is the whole ayah. There is a device in Arabic rhetoric called *iltifāt* — a deliberate shift in who is addressed, a turning of the face mid-speech. And here the shift is total and it is permanent within the verse: **God stops speaking to the deniers and begins speaking about them, over the shoulder, to His Prophet.**

Think about what it means when that happens between two human beings. When someone has been arguing with you, pleading with you, and then finally goes quiet, turns to the person beside them, and says — *"leave him"* — you have just heard the worst thing you could hear. Not the shouting. The shouting meant they were still reaching for you. **The turning-away is the verdict.**

The command *"leave them"* is not just an instruction. **It is performed by the grammar itself.** At the exact instant Allah says *leave them*, the sentence leaves them — demotes them out of the second person, out of the circle of the conversation, into the distance of "they." The abandonment isn't described. It's *enacted*. They lose the privilege of being spoken to, in the very syllable that lets them go.

What one word — *fadhar* — reveals: that being left by God does not begin with fire. It begins with silence. It begins the moment He stops arguing with you.

### Element Two — *yakhūḍū wa yal'abū*: the water they are drowning in, and the game they are winning

#### First, the tool

I told you earlier that in this book, the physical is almost never the point. It is the *stepping-stone*. Allah speaks in things you can touch — stars, wombs, mountains, water — to point you toward things you cannot: the states of the soul, the unseen shape of a reality. When a physical image appears, the discipline is: **don't stare at the image. Ask what it is pointing at.**

Keep that blade in your hand, because Allah is about to describe two spiritual states — and He describes both of them with things a child could see.

#### The water

*Yakhūḍū.* "They plunge." The root is *khāḍa* — and its first, literal, physical meaning is this: **to wade into water.** To step into a river or the sea and move through it, stirring it as you go. When the Arabs said a camel *khāḍa* the stream, they meant it walked in and churned the water and the silt at the bottom until it clouded.

That is the picture underneath the word. Not an abstraction — a body, waist-deep, wading, and the water going brown around it.

Now bring the blade. What is that pointing at? The Qur'an uses this exact verb, elsewhere, for people who *plunge into false talk* — who wade out into speculation and mockery and endless argument about the sacred. And the physical image tells you everything about the spiritual state, if you let it:

When you wade into water, three things happen. You move **slower** — every step costs more. You can no longer see your own feet, because you've **stirred up the very silt you needed to see through.** And the further out you go, the **harder it is to turn back** to shore. This is precisely what happens to a mind that plunges into vain dispute for its own sake. It slows. It clouds its own clarity — the more you argue, the muddier the water of your own heart gets, until you can no longer see the simple truth you're standing in. And the deeper you go, the more impossible the return.

They are not swimming. Swimming is going somewhere. They are *wading in circles*, stirring the mud, and calling it depth.

#### Listen to the word itself

Say it slowly. *Khawḍ.* It opens with خ — a sound scraped from the back of the throat, rough, thick. And it lands on ض — the **ḍād**, the single heaviest, most weighted consonant in the language, the letter Arabic is so proud of it calls itself *"the tongue of the ḍād."* This is not an accident of the ear: it is a *heavy* word. It sits on the tongue the way waterlogged clothing sits on the body. **The sound of the word is the weight of the wading.** You can hear the mud in it.

Contrast that in a moment with the lightness of what comes next.

#### The game

*Wa yal'abū.* "And they play." *La'ib* — the root is simply *play*: the activity of children, motion with no destination, effort spent purely for the pleasure of the moment, producing nothing, meaning nothing beyond the next move.

And now feel the two words together, because Allah has set them side by side on purpose, and the pairing is the diagnosis:

**They *plunge* into the trivial. And they *play* with the eternal.**

Look at the inversion. *Khawḍ* is intensity — total, heavy, absorbed immersion. *La'ib* is lightness — nothing matters, it's all a game. And the tragedy of the human being Allah is describing is not that he is lazy and not that he is intense. It is that **he has aimed his intensity and his lightness at exactly the wrong targets.** He brings the whole weight of his soul — *khawḍ*, the heaviest word in the language — to bear on things that do not matter. And he treats the one thing that does matter — his Lord, his death, his return — as *la'ib*, a game, something to bat around for sport.

He is deadly serious about the trivial. And playful about the eternal.

One clear statement of what these two words reveal: **the disease is not a lack of seriousness. It is misplaced seriousness.** The deniers of Mecca were not casual people. They were *ferociously* engaged — they plunged into their contention with everything they had. They simply plunged into the wrong water, and played with the wrong fire.

### Element Three — *yawmahumu-lladhī yū'adūn*: the Day with their name on it

#### First, the tool — a small one

In Arabic, as in English, there is a world of difference between *"the day"* and *"their day."* One is a date on a calendar. The other is an appointment with your name written on it. That single attached pronoun — *-hum*, "their" — turns a general event into a personal summons. Watch what it does here.

#### "Their Day"

Allah does not say *ḥattā yulāqū-l-yawma* — "until they meet **the** Day." He says *ḥattā yulāqū **yawmahum**-u-lladhī yū'adūn* — "until they meet **THEIR** Day, the one they are promised."

The Day of Judgment belongs to everyone. It is the Day of *all* creation. And yet here, for these specific people who are wading and playing on the shore, Allah attaches it to them personally. **Their** Day. As though, out of the one universal reckoning, there is a piece that has been carved off and addressed to them by name. Not *a* reckoning somewhere out there. *Yours.* The one that is coming for *you.*

There is a terror in that possessive that a general statement could never carry. It is the difference between hearing "there will be a court date" and receiving an envelope with your own name on it and a date printed inside. The people playing on the shore think the tide is a general fact about the sea. Allah is telling them: no — **that particular wave has your name on it.**

#### And "they are met"

Two last things, quickly, because they land the whole verse.

First, the verb *yulāqū*, "they meet." It comes from a form of the verb — the pattern the grammarians call *mufā'ala* — that carries the sense of **a mutual, face-to-face encounter.** Not merely *reaching* something, the way you reach a milestone. *Meeting* it, the way two people meet — the Day coming toward them as surely as they are moving toward it. One way to sit with this: while they imagine they are merely killing time until *nothing*, the Day is not passive. It is closing the distance from the other side. The appointment keeps its half of the meeting.

And second — the very last word — *yū'adūn*, "they are promised." It is **passive.** Look at the shape of the whole sentence now, from beginning to end: it opens with *their* verbs, active — *they* plunge, *they* play, doing, choosing, splashing, free. And it closes with a verb done *to* them — *they are promised*, they are met. **The active gives way to the passive.** All that furious agency on the shore — the plunging, the playing, the winning of arguments — collapses, at the end of the sentence, into people to whom something is simply *done*. The game was the only place they ever had the ball. And even that, the grammar quietly takes back before the sentence ends.

One more thing about that word. *Wa'd*, "promise," is normally the language of *good* news — you promise someone a gift, a reward. The harsher word, for a threat, is *wa'īd*. Allah chose the word for a *promise*. Because from His side, this Day is not a threat He might carry out. It is **a promise He will keep** — as certain and as faithful as any promise of mercy. The certainty is the same. Only its taste, on the Day, depends on which shore you were standing on.

[PAUSE — major]

### The bridge

So look at what these words, together, have built. A verb for "leaving" that lives only in the now and refuses to accuse the past. A grammar that enacts abandonment by turning its face away. A picture of people wading heavy into muddy water while playing a weightless game — serious about nothing, careless about everything. And a Day, personally addressed, closing in from the other side, that will quietly strip them of the very agency they're so busy exercising.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not about words, but about *your* life. Because you are somewhere in this sentence. Either you are the one being told to *leave*. Or you are the one on the shore.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The weight you were never meant to carry

Let me start with something you have almost certainly felt, and never had a name for.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from trying to change someone who has decided not to change. You know the shape of it. Maybe it's a sibling who keeps making the same ruinous choice, and you've had the same conversation nine times, and you rehearse the tenth one in the shower. Maybe it's a parent who won't see a doctor. Maybe it's a friend sliding somewhere dark, waving off every hand you extend. Maybe — and this one is so modern it aches — it's a comment thread at one in the morning, a person you have never met and never will, and you cannot stop typing, because if you can just find *the right words*, surely they'll see.

And the exhaustion is not really about them. It's about a belief you're carrying, usually without noticing you've picked it up: the belief that **their outcome is your responsibility.** That if they don't change, you failed. That their refusal is a verdict on *you*.

Psychologists have language for this now — over-functioning, over-responsibility, the fusion of your worth with another person's choices. But you don't need the vocabulary to know the feeling. You know it as the tightness in your chest when you think about them. You know it as the way you carry them into rooms they'll never enter.

[PAUSE]

Now — here is the thing about the man this ayah was first spoken to. He was the single most invested human being who has ever lived in the changing of other hearts. This was not a hobby for him; it was the mission that defined his existence. And Allah watched him bend under the weight of their refusal until the Qur'an had to ask him, gently, whether he intended to *destroy himself with grief.*

And to *that* man — not to a lazy man, not to a man who gave up easily, to the most committed heart in creation — Allah said: **Fadharhum. Leave them.**

Hear what that is, and what it is not.

It is not "stop caring." It is not "they don't matter." It is not cruelty. Remember the verb — the one with no past tense to accuse him with. Allah did not say *"you should have stopped long ago."* He opened a door in the present and said: *set it down. Now.* The result was never yours to force. **You were sent to deliver the message, not to manufacture the response.** The response belongs to them — and then it belongs to their Day.

Think back to the water. They are out in the surf, plunging and playing, waving you off. And the Prophet ﷺ — and every sincere heart after him — keeps wading in after them, soaked to the neck, calling, reaching, going deeper and deeper into water that was never his to drown in. *Fadharhum* is Allah's hand on his shoulder at the water's edge: **Come back to the shore. You cannot swim for someone who has chosen the water. If you go in after them one more time, I will lose you both.**

That is not abandonment. That is a boundary. And a boundary, drawn by the Most Merciful, is itself an act of *raḥma* — mercy toward the one being crushed by a weight that was never assigned to him.

[PAUSE]

So here is what changes tomorrow, concretely. There is someone whose refusal you have been carrying as your own failure. This week, you do the two things this ayah separates. You deliver — clearly, kindly, completely, the way the Prophet ﷺ delivered. You say the true thing, once more if you must, well and gently. And then you *dhar* — you set the outcome down. Not out of contempt. Out of obedience. You hand them, in your heart, to their Day — the one Allah has already promised them, the one with their name on it — and you walk back up onto the shore, and you let your chest unclench, and you let Allah be God over their heart, which was always His job and never yours.

The relief in that is not resignation. It's worship.

### Theme Two — Where is your seriousness going?

Picture a beach in the last hour before the tide turns. There is a child down near the water, and he has built a sandcastle, and he is *completely* absorbed in it. Turrets. A moat. A little wall against the sea. And another child says his castle is better, and now there's a real argument — flushed faces, actual tears, genuine stakes. To that child, in that moment, nothing in the universe matters more than whose castle is bigger.

And you, standing up on the dry sand, can see the whole beach. You can see the tide. You can see that the water is coming, that in twenty minutes there will be no castle and no wall and no argument, that every grain of it goes back to the sea. And you feel this ache of tenderness and helplessness, because the child is not *wrong* to build — he's a child, this is what children do — he's just pouring the entire weight of his heart into the one thing on the beach that will not last, while the one fact that will decide everything, the tide, doesn't even register.

[PAUSE]

Now be honest with me, because this is the hard turn. Which one are you?

Because the Qur'an just told you what the deniers were doing — they *plunge* and they *play* — and if you thought that was a description of ancient pagans in Mecca, look at your own last twenty-four hours. Where did the *weight* of your soul actually go? Where did you feel the *khawḍ* — the full-body plunge, the heavy total absorption where an hour vanished and you didn't notice?

For most of us the honest answer is a screen. A feed we scrolled until our thumb ached. An argument with a stranger about politics or sport or some outrage that will not exist next month. A fantasy league, a market ticker, a rabbit hole. We plunge. We wade out into these things waist-deep and stir the mud and lose the afternoon, with a seriousness that is total and real.

And then the *ṣalāh* comes. The one appointment with the Lord of the tide. And how do we treat it? We rush it. We pack it into the cracks. We think about the feed *during* it. We treat the meeting with our Maker as the interruption, and the feed as the life. We *plunge* into the sandcastle and we *play* with the sea.

The ayah's diagnosis is not "you are too serious" and it's not "you are too frivolous." It is the *inversion*. **You have your seriousness and your play backwards.** You are bringing *khawḍ* — the heaviest word in the language — to the things that will dissolve when the water comes, and *la'ib* — a game — to the only thing that will still be standing.

[PAUSE]

And the reason the ayah ends where it ends — *until they meet their Day* — is that the game has a whistle. This is the mercy inside the warning. The child on the beach is not condemned; he is *called*. The tide turning is not a punishment for building castles; it is simply the truth arriving, the moment the play stops and the real proportions of things become visible all at once. Every soul gets that moment. The only question the ayah leaves you is whether you find out the proportions *now*, with time to re-pour your heart — or *then*, when the water is already at your knees and the castle is already gone.

So here is tomorrow, concretely. Do not try to become a person who cares about nothing — that's not the invitation, and it's not human. Do this instead: pick the *one* thing this week you keep plunging into that you know is a sandcastle. Just one. And when you feel the plunge start — the reach for the phone, the pull into the thread — let it be a bell. Let the very heaviness of the pull remind you: *this is khawḍ, and I am aiming it wrong.* And redirect even ten percent of that weight to the meeting that has your name on it. Pray the next prayer like the tide is real. Because it is.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture — what the sentence was doing the whole time

Now that we've done the work, stand back and look at the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with a precision that could not be accidental.

Watch the arc of *agency* across the sentence. It opens with a command — *leave them* — and then hands the deniers a run of **active** verbs that are entirely their own: *they* plunge, *they* play. For one clause, they are the freest people in the sentence — doing, choosing, splashing. And then, at the exact hinge word *ḥattā*, "until," everything turns. The verbs stop being theirs. *They are met.* *They are promised.* The sentence ends in the **passive**, with things being done *to* them. **The whole verse is a slow-motion transfer of the ball** — from their hands to the Day's — and they never feel it leave.

And laid over that arc is a second one, made of *who is speaking to whom.* The verse begins with the warmth of direct address — *you*, My Prophet, singular, close. And in the very same breath, the deniers are pushed out into the third person and *never let back in.* So the sentence has a near shore and a far shore. On the near shore: the beloved, addressed, held, told gently to come in from the water. On the far shore: the deniers, no longer spoken to, plunging and playing until the tide.

That is the architecture. **Two kinds of leaving, mirrored against each other.** There is the leaving the Prophet is *commanded* to do — small, chosen, temporary, an act of mercy toward himself, a stepping back onto dry sand. And there is the leaving the deniers are *heading into* — total, unchosen, permanent, the silence of a God who has stopped arguing. The one who lovingly lets go, and the Day that will not.

### Bringing it together

And now you can see that the linguistic journey and the two themes were never two things. They were one insight, approached from opposite ends.

The words taught us that God's abandonment begins not with fire but with *silence* — with the turning of the face. The themes taught us that there is a weight we carry that was never ours, and a seriousness we spend on things that will not last. And they meet in a single motion: **you are asked to do a small, merciful, deliberate letting-go now — set down the outcome, come in from the water, re-aim your heart — precisely so that you are never on the receiving end of the great, silent, permanent letting-go later.** The small *dhar* now is how you avoid becoming someone God says *dhar* about.

Where we started: a command that sounded like a door closing. Where we've arrived: a man being lifted off the floor, a boundary drawn in mercy, and a tide that has everyone's name on it — including yours.

Take the trade. Set down what was never yours. Pick up what always was. Come in from the water while the tide is still far.
