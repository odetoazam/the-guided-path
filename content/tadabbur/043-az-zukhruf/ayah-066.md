# The Only Thing They Are Waiting For

### Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 66

> [ayah:43:66] هَلْ يَنظُرُونَ إِلَّا ٱلسَّاعَةَ أَن تَأْتِيَهُم بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ
>
> *Hal yanẓurūna illā as-sāʿata an taʾtiyahum baghtatan wa hum lā yashʿurūn*
>
> **"Are they waiting for anything except the Hour — that it should come upon them suddenly, while they do not perceive it?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word, let us walk back into the room where this verse was first heard.

The surah has spent its closing third on one figure: **Jesus, son of Mary** — peace be upon him. Allah has presented him not as a god, not as a son, but as *a servant upon whom We bestowed favor, and We made him an example for the Children of Israel* (43:59). He is a sign. A mercy. A man.

And then, in the verse immediately before ours, something breaks. Allah says:

> *Then the factions differed among themselves...* (43:65)

The Arabic word is **al-aḥzāb** — the parties, the sects, the camps. The people took a clear sign and *splintered* over it. One camp said one thing about Jesus. Another said the opposite. A third carved a position in between. And the verse ends with a warning of woe — *fa-waylun lilladhīna ẓalamū* — woe to the wrongdoers from the punishment of a painful Day.

Now hold that picture. Picture the arguing. The councils. The competing creeds. Centuries of theological war fought over a man who only ever pointed people back to the One who sent him. Imagine the energy of it — the certainty on every side, the pamphlets, the debates, the bloodshed.

And into the middle of all that noise, Allah drops a single, quiet, devastating question.

He does not refute the factions one by one. He does not enter their debate. He steps back, looks at the whole arguing mass of humanity — and asks them something that stops the room cold:

**What, exactly, are you all waiting for?**

[PAUSE]

That is the verse. Ten words in Arabic. And inside those ten words is a mirror so clear that fourteen centuries later it still shows us our own face — not the face of the ancient sects, but ours. The one that says *later*. The one that says *there's time*. The one that scrolls and plans and argues and postpones, certain that the important moment is always somewhere up ahead, never *here*, never *now*.

Here is the hook. Hold it lightly, because we are going to earn it slowly. The verse uses a word for "waiting" that, in Arabic, is the *same word* as "looking" and "watching." And then it ends by telling us that these very people — the ones supposedly *watching* — **do not perceive a thing.**

How can someone be watching and yet perceive nothing?

That contradiction is the whole verse. Let's go find out how Allah resolves it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Is Actually a Verdict — *Hal... illā*

Let me teach you a structure first, before we touch the verse. In ordinary speech, a question is a request for information. *Are you coming?* I genuinely do not know, so I ask.

But there is another kind of question — one you already use every day — where the question is *not* asking anything. It is **delivering a judgment dressed as a question.** Imagine a parent who walks into a wrecked kitchen and says to the teenager, *"Is this what we agreed on?"* That is not a request for information. The parent knows the answer. The question-shape is doing something a flat statement could not: it forces the listener to *answer it themselves*, silently, and the silent answer convicts them.

Arabic has a precise tool for this. It is the structure **hal... illā** — *"Is there... except...?"* — an interrogative particle, **هَلْ**, paired with the exceptive particle, **إِلَّا**. And when these two clasp around a sentence, the grammarians tell us the meaning becomes **negation by restriction**. *"Are they waiting for anything except X?"* lands as: *"They are waiting for nothing but X."*

Notice what just happened. The verse *could* have said, plainly: *"They wait only for the Hour."* A statement. Instead, Allah chooses the question. **هَلْ يَنظُرُونَ إِلَّا ٱلسَّاعَةَ** — *"Are they waiting for anything except the Hour?"*

Why the question and not the statement?

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. A statement informs you. A question *implicates* you. When Allah phrases it as a question, He turns the listener from a spectator into a defendant. The factions cannot simply receive this information and move on. The grammar reaches out, takes them by the collar, and makes them supply the answer with their own mouths: *"...no. There is nothing else we are waiting for."*

And the four classical commentators are united on the force of it. Al-Jalālayn glosses **هَلْ يَنظُرُونَ** as *mā yantaẓirūn* — "what are they awaiting" — reading the question straight into a negation. Al-Qurṭubī is even more blunt: he writes *"lā yantaẓirūn"* — *they are not waiting* — and *"illā as-sāʿata,"* except the Hour, *meaning al-Qiyāmah*. Al-Baghawī adds a turn that we will need later: he says it is as if they are awaiting it — *fa-ka-annahum yantaẓirūnahā* — because *it will come to them inevitably, no escape.* They are not consciously waiting. But the structure of reality has them in a waiting room whether they know it or not.

So here is what this single grammatical clasp reveals: **the verse is not asking a question. It is closing a case.** It gathers up every faction, every camp, every argument from the verse before — and it says: strip all of it away, and underneath the noise, there is only one appointment left on the calendar. You have been pretending the calendar is full. It has one entry.

[PAUSE — let that settle]

### Element Two: The Word for Waiting Is the Word for Watching — *yanẓurūn*

Now to the strangest word in the verse. **يَنظُرُونَ** — *yanẓurūn.* Our translations render it "they wait." But its root is **n-ẓ-r**, and that root, attested across the classical lexicons, does not primarily mean "to wait." It means **to look. To gaze. To direct the eyes.** It is the root of *naẓar* — eyesight, the act of looking. It is the root of *manẓar* — a view, a vista. It is the root we use when we say *unẓur* — *look!*

So how does "look" come to mean "wait"?

Think about it from the inside. What does it feel like to wait for someone you love at an airport? You don't sit with your eyes closed. You *watch* the doors. You crane your neck. You scan every face coming through the gate. **To wait, in the deepest sense, is to keep looking toward the place from which the awaited thing will come.** Waiting is watching with the body leaned forward. The Arabs captured this exactly: the verb for expectation is the verb for the fixed, forward gaze.

Now feel the irony Allah has built.

These factions are *yanẓurūn* — they are, by the very grammar, **the watching ones.** The leaning-forward ones. The ones with their eyes fixed on the horizon, scanning for what comes next. They are not described as asleep. They are described as *looking.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the blade. The verse ends — we will reach it fully in a moment — with **وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ**: *while they do not perceive.* They are *looking* (yanẓurūn) and yet they *perceive nothing* (lā yashʿurūn).

Stay with me, because this is the heart of the whole verse. How can the watching ones be the perceiving-nothing ones? Because — and this is what Allah is exposing — **they are looking in every direction except the one that matters.** Their eyes are busy. Their gaze is leaned forward. But it is leaned toward the wrong horizon. They scan the politics of their factions, the standing of their camp, the next move in the argument about Jesus. Their *naẓar* — their looking — is fully employed. It is just pointed *away* from the only door through which the decisive thing will actually arrive.

This is not blindness. **Blindness would be an excuse.** This is something far more frightening: full sight, fully active, fully *aimed at the wrong thing.* The eyes are open. The neck is craned. They are watching the departures board for a flight that was never going to land there — while behind them, unwatched, the real arrival is already taxiing in.

Al-Qurṭubī, reading this in context, ties it back to verse 58 — *mā ḍarabūhu laka illā jadalā* — *they raised it to you only to dispute.* That is what their *naẓar* was fixed on. Disputation. Argument as a sport. Looking hard — at the wrong thing.

So this one word, *yanẓurūn*, splits the human condition open. There is a way to be intensely, exhaustingly engaged — eyes wide, mind racing, fully *looking* — and to be, at the same moment, perceiving nothing of what is actually approaching.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider is uncomfortable: *busyness of attention is not the same as direction of attention.* You can be the most "switched-on" person in the room — informed, alert, watching everything — and have your entire gaze aimed away from your appointment.

### Element Three: The Sound of Ambush, and the Loss of the Faintest Sense — *baghtatan* and *yashʿurūn*

Two words close the verse, and Allah chose both with terrifying care.

The first is **بَغْتَةً** — *baghtatan.* Root **b-gh-t**. Say it aloud. *Baghtah.* Feel the mouth do it: the plosive *b*, the guttural *gh* scraping up from the throat, the hard *t* snapping it shut. It is not a smooth word. It *lurches.* It ambushes the mouth the way the thing it names ambushes the soul. The lexicons define it as the arrival of something **all at once, with no warning, no preliminaries, no gradual approach** — fajʾah, as all four commentators gloss it. Al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar — every one of them lands on the same single word: *fajʾah.* Suddenly. Without notice.

Notice what *baghtatan* rules out. It rules out the slow dawn. It rules out the gentle warning, the gathering clouds, the polite knock. The Hour does not approach the way old age approaches — visibly, joint by joint, gray hair by gray hair. It arrives the way an ambush arrives: it is *not there,* and then it simply *is.* There is no moment in between for the looking ones to finally turn their heads.

And here the grammar deepens it. *Baghtatan* is in the accusative, functioning as a **circumstantial description** — it describes the *state* in which the Hour comes. The Hour's very *manner of arriving* is suddenness. It is not that the Hour happens to be sudden this once. Suddenness is woven into its nature. It comes *wearing* surprise.

Then, the final clause: **وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ** — *and they do not perceive.* Root **sh-ʿ-r.**

Now, there are several words in Arabic for "to know." There is *ʿilm* — structured, considered knowledge. There is *ʿaql* — reasoning. But *shuʿūr*, from **sh-ʿ-r**, is not the knowledge of the intellect. The lexicographers connect this very root to **shaʿr** — *hair.* Why hair? Because *shuʿūr* is **the finest, most delicate layer of perception** — the awareness as fine as a single hair, the faintest sensing at the very edge of feeling. It is the word for a *hunch,* an *inkling,* the prickle on the skin before you can name what is wrong. *Laysa hādhā min shuʿūrī* — "I had not the slightest sense of it."

So read what Allah actually says about these people. He does not say *they do not know.* He says **لَا يَشْعُرُونَ** — they do not have even the faintest *sense.* Not the hair's-breadth of an inkling. Not the prickle on the back of the neck. The most delicate instrument of perception a human being owns — that animal sixth-sense that something is coming — in them it reads *nothing.*

[PAUSE]

Put the two words together and feel the full machine of the verse. The Hour comes **baghtatan** — ambush-suddenly, by its very nature giving no warning. And they meet it **lā yashʿurūn** — with not even the finest filament of sensing switched on. The catastrophe approaches in perfect silence, and the smoke detector of the soul has no battery.

Al-Jalālayn pinpoints exactly *what* they fail to perceive: *bi-waqti majīʾihā qablahu* — they have no sense *of the time of its coming, before it comes.* It is not that they doubt the Hour exists. Many of them might concede it abstractly. It is that they have lost the *felt* nearness of it. Intellectually filed away; experientially absent. They "know" the way you "know" you will die someday — a fact on a shelf, gathering dust, touching nothing about how you spent this afternoon.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Look at what these three constructions have built, standing together.

A question that is really a verdict — *hal yanẓurūn illā* — gathering all our arguments into one and revealing there is only one appointment left.

A verb of watching — *yanẓurūn* — that exposes how a person can be fully alert and fully aimed at nothing.

And a closing pair — *baghtatan, lā yashʿurūn* — an ambush met by a soul with its finest sense switched off.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about you and me, tonight.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lie We All Live By — *That There Is Time*

Let me start far from the verse, somewhere you already live.

You have a deadline. It is three weeks away. For the first two weeks and six days, what is the deadline's *emotional weight*? Almost nothing. It floats out there in the future like a small cloud. You know it's real. You could tell someone the exact date. But it does not *press* on you. It does not change what you do on a Tuesday afternoon. And then — on the last day — that same deadline, *the identical fact,* suddenly fills the entire sky. Your heart races. Everything else falls away. You can think of nothing else.

Here is the strange thing: **the deadline did not change. Your perception of it did.** It was always that close in a calendar sense. It only became *real* — *felt* — at the end.

Psychologists have a name for the machinery behind this. They call it *temporal discounting* — the mind's built-in tendency to shrink the importance of anything that lies in the future, no matter how certain or how enormous. The further away a thing sits in time, the smaller our emotions render it, even when our intellect knows it is vast. We are not built to *feel* the future. We are built to feel the next ten minutes.

And layered on top of that is the quiet whisper psychologists call *optimism bias* — *it won't happen to me.* Not yet. Not this year. The accident, the diagnosis, the call in the night — those happen to other people, in other verses.

Now bring this machinery to our ayah, and watch what Allah has done.

The factions in verse 65 are arguing about Jesus. Fully engaged. Eyes leaned forward. And Allah says: *hal yanẓurūn illā as-sāʿah* — the only thing actually coming for any of you is the Hour. It is on the calendar. It is certain. And yet — *wa hum lā yashʿurūn* — they feel *nothing* of it. Why? Because it sits in the "future" file. And the human heart, by its own design, does not feel the future. It discounts it down to a small cloud on a distant horizon, while it spends its whole storm of emotion on the argument in front of it.

[PAUSE]

Do you see how precisely the Qur'an named, fourteen centuries ago, the exact cognitive flaw a modern lab would later measure? Allah is not telling these people something they don't *know.* He is exposing the gap between what they know and what they *feel* — the gap between *ʿilm* and *shuʿūr.* They have the data. They lack the sensation. And a truth you have filed but cannot feel will not move a single one of your tomorrows.

This is the lie we all live by — not a spoken lie, but a structural one, baked into how attention works: **the lie that the important moment is always up ahead.** That real life, real repentance, real turning, real presence with Allah — that all of it begins *after* this current season of busyness resolves. After the argument is won. After the degree, after the wedding, after the career stabilizes, after the kids are grown. We are all, in our own way, *yanẓurūn* — leaning forward, watching the wrong horizon, certain the decisive thing is somewhere we are not yet.

And the verse answers with one word: *baghtatan.* It will not wait for your season to resolve. It does not arrive *after* the busyness. It arrives *through* it, suddenly, unannounced, while the argument is still mid-sentence.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** One concrete thing. The Qur'an's prescription for temporal discounting is not to think about death more *abstractly* — that only adds another file to the shelf. It is to *feel* it nearer. The Prophet ﷺ taught the believer to pray every prayer *as if it is your farewell prayer* — *ṣalli ṣalāta muwaddiʿ.* That is not morbidity. That is *recalibration* — deliberately collapsing the distance the mind keeps inserting, dragging the future appointment forward until the heart can finally feel its weight. Tomorrow, in one prayer — just one — stand as though no other will follow it. Watch how differently you stand. That is *shuʿūr* being switched back on.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Awake but Not Aware — The Difference Between Looking and Seeing

Here is a second human door.

Have you ever driven home — a route you know so well — and arrived in your driveway with **no memory of the drive at all?** You stopped at the lights. You changed lanes. You did everything correctly. And yet you were not *there* for any of it. Your eyes were open. Your hands were on the wheel. *Yanẓurūn* — you were looking the entire time. And you perceived almost nothing. *Lā yashʿurūn.*

We have a name for that state now: *autopilot.* The body fully operational, the awareness switched off. And the terrifying gift of the human mind is that we can spend not just a drive but *years* this way — looking, functioning, succeeding, arguing, even worshipping — entirely on autopilot, present for none of it.

This is the second thing the verse exposes, and it cuts deeper than the first. The factions are not condemned for being *idle.* They are intensely active. They are condemned for being *active without awareness.* For looking without seeing. For having the most sophisticated perceptual machinery in creation — the *naẓar* of the eyes, the *shuʿūr* of the finest senses — and running all of it on autopilot, aimed away from the one reality that gives every other reality its meaning.

Let the ayah illuminate something here. Why does Allah pair *naẓar* (the active gaze) at the start with *shuʿūr* (the faintest sense) at the end? Because He is describing a *complete* failure of perception — top to bottom. The grandest faculty, sight, fully deployed; and the subtlest faculty, the hair's-breadth hunch, completely dark. There is no level of perception left at which the truth gets through. They have, in effect, sealed the entire instrument — loud and quiet, gross and fine — and pointed what remains at a wall.

[PAUSE]

And now the most unsettling turn. *We* are not exempt from this verse by virtue of believing in the Hour. A person can affirm the Hour with perfect creed and still live every waking moment *lā yashʿurūn* about it. You can pass the theology exam and fail the *shuʿūr* test. The factions, after all, were *religious* people — they were arguing about *Jesus,* about *scripture,* about *God.* Their autopilot was a *pious* autopilot. And it perceived nothing.

This is why the Qur'an, again and again, does not merely command belief — it commands **dhikr,** remembrance. Because the enemy of the believer is rarely outright denial. The enemy is *ghaflah* — heedlessness — the slow slide of a real, affirmed truth from the front of awareness into the dusty back shelf where it touches nothing. *Dhikr* is the deliberate act of pulling it back to the front. Of switching the *shuʿūr* back on. Of, for a moment, *seeing* the drive instead of just completing it.

Let this paradox sit without rushing to fix it: the people most in danger of this verse are not the obviously heedless — the openly indifferent, who at least *know* they are not paying attention. The people most in danger are the *busy religious,* the engaged arguers, the ones so certain their gaze is fixed on holy things that it never occurs to them their gaze has been on autopilot the whole time. The factions thought they were defending the truth. They were the ones the verse was aimed at.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** A practice, small and exact. Once tomorrow — pick a routine, *automatic* act of worship, the one you do most on autopilot, maybe the wudū, maybe the first rakʿah — and *interrupt the autopilot.* Slow it by ten percent. Feel the water. Hear the words. Let one act of devotion be *perceived* rather than *completed.* That single deliberate moment of *shuʿūr* is worth more than a thousand actions performed by a body whose driver has left the seat.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now that we have done the work, look at the shape of the verse from above — because Allah built it as a perfect trap, and only now can we see the walls.

It opens with **a faculty of perception in full motion**: *yanẓurūn* — they are *looking,* watching, leaning forward.

It closes with **a faculty of perception entirely dark**: *lā yashʿurūn* — they do not sense the faintest thing.

And between those two — between the active eye and the dead nerve — Allah places the one reality the whole apparatus was built to detect: *as-sāʿah,* the Hour, arriving *baghtatan,* suddenly.

The verse is a sentence about perception that *enacts its own subject.* It surrounds the Hour with two perceiving faculties — and shows both of them failing it. The looking does not catch it. The sensing does not feel it. The truth passes clean through the center of a fully-equipped human being and registers on nothing. That is not an accident of word order. That is the architecture *being* the argument.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were never two things. They were one insight approached from two roads.

From the road of *grammar,* we found a question that was a verdict, a verb of watching that exposed misdirected attention, and an ambush-word met by a soul with no sense switched on.

From the road of *the human heart,* we found temporal discounting — the mind that cannot feel the future — and autopilot — the life lived looking but not seeing.

They meet in one place. **The tragedy of the human being is not that the truth is hidden. It is that the truth is in plain sight, approaching certainly, and we have aimed every faculty we own somewhere else.** The factions did not lack information about the Hour. They lacked *shuʿūr* — the felt nearness that turns a known fact into a lived reality. And so do we, most days. That is the gap the entire verse exists to expose: not the gap between truth and falsehood, but the gap between *knowing* and *perceiving.*

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. What am I *yanẓurūn* — what horizon is my attention leaning toward right now — and is it the horizon the decisive thing will actually arrive from?

2. Of the things I genuinely *believe,* which ones have slipped from *shuʿūr* (felt) to mere *ʿilm* (filed) — true on the shelf, but touching nothing I do?

3. Which of my acts of worship am I *completing* on autopilot rather than *perceiving?*

4. If the appointment came *baghtatan* — through the middle of this ordinary week, not after it — what is the one thing I would wish I had not postponed?

5. What argument, what grievance, what "faction" am I so engaged in that it has become the wrong horizon I cannot look away from?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You can be the most watchful person alive and perceive nothing of the one thing that is certainly, silently, coming for you — unless you drag it from the shelf of what you *know* to the nerve of what you *feel.*

### The Closing Invitation

This verse does not ask you to be afraid. Fear is just another thing the mind files away. It invites you to do something smaller and far harder: to *switch one faculty back on.* To take the Hour down off the shelf of abstract belief and let it press, just a little, on this Tuesday — so that one prayer is prayed as a farewell, one act of wudū is actually felt, one horizon is re-aimed. Not everything. Just one thing, perceived. That is the difference between the factions and the awake. Not what they knew. What they *sensed.*

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You who made the Hour arrive suddenly upon the heedless — do not let me be among the looking who perceive nothing. Awaken in me the faintest true sense of what is coming, that I may turn to You before it turns to me. Move what I believe from my shelf to my heart, and let me meet You having felt You near.*

*Āmīn.*
