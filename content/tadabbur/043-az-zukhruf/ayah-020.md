# A Word of Truth Bent Toward Falsehood
### Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 20

> وَقَالُوا۟ لَوْ شَآءَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ مَا عَبَدْنَـٰهُم ۗ مَّا لَهُم بِذَ‌ٰلِكَ مِنْ عِلْمٍ ۖ إِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَخْرُصُونَ

*Wa-qālū law shāʾa al-Raḥmānu mā ʿabadnāhum. Mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm. In hum illā yakhruṣūn.*

**"And they say, 'If the Most Merciful had willed, we would not have worshipped them.' They have no knowledge of that. They do nothing but conjecture."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Most Dangerous Sentence Is the One That Is Almost True

Let me begin not with the ayah, but with a feeling you already know.

There is a particular kind of argument that is hard to answer — not because it is strong, but because it is *partly right*. Someone says something to you, and your gut tells you it is wrong, but when you reach for the refutation, your hand closes on air. Every individual word they said was true. The premise was true. The logic seemed to follow. And yet the conclusion is poison.

This is the hardest kind of falsehood to fight. Not the obvious lie — that one you can swat away. The dangerous one is the lie that has dressed itself in truth, that has borrowed the clothing of something real, and now walks among true things as if it belonged there.

[PAUSE]

Now step back fourteen centuries, to the dusty streets of Makkah.

The Quran is being recited. Its central, relentless message is *tawḥīd* — that there is nothing worthy of worship except Allah, and that the idols, the statues, the intermediaries the Makkans have set up — none of them deserve a fragment of devotion. The pressure of this message is enormous. It is dismantling their entire religious world, the inheritance of their fathers, the economy of their sanctuary, the architecture of their identity.

And the idolaters of Quraysh, cornered, reach for a defense. But notice — they do not reach for a *crude* defense. They do not say "our idols are powerful." They do not say "our gods will protect us." They have heard the Quran. They have absorbed its vocabulary. And so they construct an argument using *the Quran's own theology against it.*

They say: **"If the Most Merciful had willed, we would not have worshipped them."**

Sit with the cleverness of that. The Quran teaches that nothing happens except by Allah's will. *Fine,* they say. *We accept that. And we are worshipping these idols right now. Therefore — by your own logic — Allah must have willed it. And if He willed it, He must be content with it. So leave us alone. Our worship is sanctioned by the very God you preach.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. **Where exactly is the flaw?** Because there is one. The Quran is about to expose it. But it is buried so deep that most people, then and now, walk right past it. Their premise is *true* — everything does happen by Allah's will. So how does a true premise produce a damnable lie?

The answer to that question is one of the most important things a believing heart can ever learn. And it is hiding inside three Arabic words. Let us go and find it.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Little Word *Law* — A Grammar That Confesses

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you something about a single Arabic particle, because the entire argument of the idolaters is balanced on it like a building on a pin.

The word is **لَوْ — *law***. We usually translate it as "if." But it is not the ordinary "if." Arabic has more than one "if," and they are not interchangeable. There is *in* (إِنْ), which opens a real, live possibility: *if it rains, I'll stay home* — maybe it will, maybe it won't. And there is **law**, which the grammarians call **حرف امتناع لامتناع** — *the particle of preclusion-because-of-preclusion.*

Let me unpack that name slowly, because it is the key to everything.

*Law* introduces a condition that **did not happen**, in order to tell you about a result that **therefore also did not happen**. When you say *law*, you are not opening a possibility — you are looking back at something that was foreclosed.

Here is an everyday example. When you say, "*If I had studied, I would have passed*" — what have you just admitted? You have admitted that **you did not study, and you did not pass.** The sentence is shaped like a hypothetical, but it is secretly a confession. The grammar reaches into the past and concedes the failure even as it imagines the alternative.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that, and listen again to what the idolaters say:

> **لَوْ شَآءَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ مَا عَبَدْنَـٰهُم**
> *Law shāʾa al-Raḥmānu mā ʿabadnāhum*
> "**Had** the Most Merciful **willed**, we would not have worshipped them."

Notice what their own grammar has done to them.

The structure of *law* requires an unfulfilled condition producing an unfulfilled result. So the sentence presupposes: the willing-of-prevention *did not happen* — and therefore the not-worshipping *did not happen either.* Strip it down and what is buried at the bottom of their sentence?

**"We worshipped them."**

They built this entire phrase to make their idolatry sound like something that was decided *above* their heads, something poured down onto them from the heavens, something they merely received. And yet the very particle they chose — *law* — carries inside it the admission of the act. *We did it. We worshipped them.* They cannot even construct the excuse without confessing the deed.

Let's sit with this, because it is exquisite. They reach for the language of *fate* — "it was willed" — and the grammar quietly hands back to them the language of *agency*: "we worshipped." The defense and the confession arrive in the same breath, in the same word.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is remarkable about how the Quran responds. When the idolaters say "everything is by Allah's will," the Quran does **not** answer, "No, your worship was not by His will." It does not deny the decree. Watch the next clause carefully — the refutation lands somewhere else entirely. We will get there. But mark this now: **the Quran lets the true part stand.** It does not waste a word denying what is true. It goes hunting for the precise place where the truth was bent.

What this single word *law* reveals: an excuse can be grammatically dishonest. You can build a sentence whose surface says "I had no choice" while its foundation admits "I chose."

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Al-Raḥmān* — They Reached for the Name of Mercy

Now I want you to notice something that is almost never noticed. It is a matter of *which word is present* where a hundred other words could have stood.

The idolaters are constructing a theological argument about Allah's will. They could have named Him by any of His names. They could have said *law shāʾa Allāh* — "if **Allah** had willed." They could have invoked *al-ʿAzīz*, the Mighty; *al-Jabbār*, the Compeller; *al-Qahhār*, the Overpowering — names of dominion that fit naturally with a sentence about overwhelming divine will.

But they did not. Look at what they actually said:

> **لَوْ شَآءَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ**
> "If **the Most Merciful** had willed…"

They reached, of all the names, for **ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ — *al-Raḥmān*** — the name that signals not power but *tenderness*. *Al-Raḥmān*: the One whose mercy is vast, immediate, overflowing, the mercy from which the very *raḥim*, the womb, takes its name — the enveloping, nourishing, sheltering mercy.

Why? Why, in an argument meant to excuse their idolatry, would they specifically invoke *the Merciful*?

Let's stay with this, because their choice is not innocent. Think about the shape of their reasoning. *A merciful God — surely such a One, if He truly objected to our worship, would have stopped us. He is gentle. He is kind. He has let us continue for generations, untroubled, prospering at our sanctuary. Would the Merciful let His beloved creatures persist in something He despised? His very mercy is our permission.*

Do you see the move? **They are weaponizing His mercy.** They are taking the most beautiful thing about Allah and turning it into a license. They read His patience as approval. They read the delay of consequence as a signature of consent. *He is al-Raḥmān — therefore our continuing must mean He is pleased.*

[PAUSE]

And this is a trap that did not die in Makkah. It is, perhaps, the single most common spiritual error alive in the world today. *God is merciful — so surely He doesn't mind. God is love — so surely this is fine. He made me this way; He is kind; He understands; He would not have let me go on if He truly objected.* The reasoning is identical. We invoke the Merciful precisely at the moment we want to be left alone in our sin.

But here is what they — and we — forget. In the Quran's own world, the patience of *al-Raḥmān* with the wrongdoer is not always approval. Sometimes it is *imhāl* — respite. Sometimes the rope is being let out, not because the act is loved, but because the door of return is being held open, or because a deeper decree is unfolding. To read mercy as endorsement is to misunderstand the very name you are invoking.

And there is a further bitterness here, specific to this surah. Surah Az-Zukhruf returns again and again to the name *al-Raḥmān*. It is the name they had elsewhere even *disputed* — recall how, when told to prostrate to *al-Raḥmān*, some said "and what is al-Raḥmān?" (25:60). And now, in their moment of self-justification, this is suddenly the name they want on their lips. The name they questioned becomes the name they hide behind. Mercy, when you will not submit to it, becomes the thing you exploit.

What the presence of this one name reveals: **the closer an attribute of God is to beauty, the more it can be abused by a heart that does not want to change.** Mercy misread becomes the most comfortable pillow in hell.

### Element Three: *Yakhruṣūn* — The Theology of the Eyeball

We come now to the word in which the entire ayah turns its key. After letting the true premise stand, after refusing to deny the decree, the Quran finally names the disease. And it names it twice — once by what it denies them, and once by what it accuses them of.

First the denial:

> **مَّا لَهُم بِذَ‌ٰلِكَ مِنْ عِلْمٍ**
> *Mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm*
> "They have no **knowledge** of that."

And then the accusation:

> **إِنْ هُمْ إِلَّا يَخْرُصُونَ**
> *In hum illā **yakhruṣūn***
> "They are nothing but **conjecturers**."

Two words sit at the two ends of this verdict, facing each other across it like opposing armies: **ʿilm** — knowledge — and **yakhruṣūn**, from the root **خ-ر-ص — kha-ra-ṣa**. And to understand the genius of this ayah, you have to understand what *kharṣ* actually is.

Let me teach you the word from the ground up — literally, from the ground.

*Kharṣ* is a farmer's word. In the date palm groves of Arabia, before the harvest came in, an experienced man would walk among the trees and **estimate** the yield. He would look up at the clusters hanging high on the palm — clusters he could not count, could not weigh, could not hold in his hand — and he would *guess* the number: this tree will give so many measures, that one so many. This estimation was called *kharṣ*. It had a real use; it was even used to calculate what was owed in charity. But everyone understood one thing about it: **it was a guess.** An educated guess, an eyeballing, a squint at something out of reach. It could be close. It could also be wrong. It was, by its very nature, *not knowledge.* It was the opposite of counting; it was estimating from a distance.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what the Quran has just done.

It says: when these people claim that Allah is *pleased* with their worship — when they peer up at the unreachable reality of God's inner will and announce, "He approves" — they are doing nothing more than ***kharṣ***. They are standing under the palm tree of the divine decree, squinting at fruit they cannot reach, and calling out a number. **They are eyeballing the will of God and calling their guess a fact.**

Can you think of a more devastating image? The inner pleasure of the Lord of the worlds — the *riḍā* of *al-Raḥmān* — is not a date cluster on a low branch. It is utterly beyond the reach of human estimation. It cannot be squinted at. It cannot be guessed. It can only be *told to us* — by revelation, by the messengers, by the Book. And the messengers and the Book have told them the exact opposite of what they claim. So their "He is pleased with our worship" is not knowledge counted and verified. It is *kharṣ* — a yield estimated by a man who never climbed the tree.

[PAUSE]

And listen to the **sound** of the word. *Yakhruṣūn.* The harsh *kh* scraping in the throat, the rolled *r*, the heavy emphatic *ṣ* — *khhh-ru-ṣ*. It does not glide. It grinds. It sounds like guesswork sounds: rough, unsteady, scraping at something it cannot grasp. Compare it to the clean, settled, luminous word it is set against — *ʿilm.* Knowledge sits still and certain. *Yakhruṣūn* scrabbles. The Quran has placed a smooth word and a rough word at the two ends of the verdict, and you can hear which one is true.

Now look at the structure the two words make together:

> *Mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm* — **of knowledge they have nothing.**
> *In hum illā yakhruṣūn* — **of conjecture they have nothing else.**

Total absence on one side; total saturation on the other. They are *emptied* of knowledge and *filled* with guesswork. And both clauses use a tool of absolute restriction — *min* in the negation, draining knowledge to zero; *in… illā*, "they are nothing but," walling them inside conjecture with no exit. There is no overlap permitted. Where there should have been knowledge, there is a vacuum. And rushing into that vacuum: pure *kharṣ*.

What this one word reveals: **the most catastrophic claims human beings make are claims about God's inner state — made by people who are only guessing, and who have mistaken their guess for knowledge.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Wound

So we have walked through three words, and look at what they have shown us.

*Law* — the counterfactual that confesses the deed even as it pleads fate.
*Al-Raḥmān* — the name of mercy, reached for precisely in order to be misread as permission.
*Yakhruṣūn* — the farmer's guess at the unreachable fruit, mistaken for certain knowledge of God's pleasure.

Three words, and already the whole machinery of the lie is visible. They concede their agency in their grammar, they exploit His mercy in their argument, and they crown their guesswork with the authority of fact.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart, and a confusion that lives inside almost every one of us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Confusion That Damns — God's Will Is Not God's Pleasure

Let me start where you live, today, far from any idol.

You have heard the phrase. You have probably *said* the phrase. **"Everything happens for a reason."** "It was meant to be." "If God didn't want it, it wouldn't have happened."

Now — there is a way to say these words that is pure faith, that holds a grieving heart together at a graveside, that lets a person breathe after a catastrophe. That is real, and it is beautiful, and I am not taking it from you.

But there is *another* way these same words get used, and you have felt it too. It is the way we use them **after we have already decided to do the thing we wanted to do.** I cut off my brother — well, Allah decreed it; some people just aren't meant to be in your life. I took the money — the opportunity was put in front of me; it was clearly meant to be. I abandoned the prayer, the fast, the boundary — God made me this way; if He minded, He'd have changed me.

Do you hear it? It is *law shāʾa al-Raḥmānu* in modern dress. It is the exact same move. **We take a true thing about Allah — that nothing escapes His will — and we use it as a sedative for the conscience.** We launder our choices through the doctrine of decree.

[PAUSE]

And here is the precise place where the truth gets bent — the distinction that this entire ayah exists to teach. There are two different realities, and the idolaters collapsed them into one, and we collapse them into one a hundred times a year.

There is **مَشِيئَة — *mashīʾa*** — Allah's *will*, His decree: what He *allows to occur* in the unfolding of His creation.

And there is **رِضَا — *riḍā*** — Allah's *pleasure*: what He *loves, approves, and is content with.*

**These are not the same thing.** Allah's universal will permits a great deal that His sacred pleasure does not love. The thief steals *by His will* — He did not approve the theft. The tyrant oppresses *by His will* — He did not love the oppression. That a thing *happens* tells you it fell within His decree. It tells you **nothing** about whether He is pleased with it.

The idolaters built their entire defense on welding these two together. *It happened, therefore He willed it; He willed it, therefore He approves.* The first link is true. **The second link is the lie.** And it is precisely there — at the jump from "He willed it" to "He is pleased with it" — that the Quran inserts its blade: *mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm.* You have **no knowledge** of His pleasure. His pleasure was never something you could deduce from the fact that the thing occurred. His pleasure is not read off events. **His pleasure is told to you — by His Book and His Messenger.** And both told you the opposite.

[PAUSE]

This is why the classical scholars described this ayah with a phrase worth carving into your heart. Al-Qurtubi calls their statement **كلمة حق أريد بها باطل** — *"a word of truth by which falsehood was intended."* Every individual word the idolaters spoke was true. Yes, His will is sovereign. Yes, nothing happens without it. Yes, He is *al-Raḥmān*. All true. And from that bouquet of true things, they assembled a lie: *therefore He is content with our shirk.*

This is the most important thing I can leave you with from this theme. **Be most suspicious of your reasoning about God exactly when every piece of it is true and the conclusion happens to be the one you wanted.** That is the signature of *kalimat ḥaqq urīda bihā bāṭil*. The devil rarely sells you a clean lie; he is a tailor, and he dresses falsehood in truths until you cannot see the body underneath.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the practical edge. The next time you catch yourself explaining a choice you've already made by appeal to "it was meant to be" — stop, and run one test. Ask not *"did this happen by His will?"* (the answer is always trivially yes, and it settles nothing). Ask instead: *"Is this thing among what He has told me He loves?"* Will is known from the event. Pleasure is known from the revelation. Never read the second off the first again.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Audacity of Speaking for God — Knowledge Versus the Guess

Now let me take you to the second wound, and again, let me start in your world.

Think about how confidently human beings speak about the inner states of others. "She did that because she's jealous." "He only said sorry to look good." We pronounce on the hidden contents of other people's hearts — places we have *never been* and *cannot enter* — with the fluency of eyewitnesses. We guess at the unseen, and then we forget that we guessed, and we treat our guess as something we *know*.

That habit — small and human and constant — is the seed of the catastrophe in this ayah. Because the idolaters did the same thing, but they did it about the heart of *God.* They peered into the unseeable interior of the Most Merciful, announced what they found there — "He is pleased with us" — and built a religion on it. And the Quran's response is to strip the word "knowledge" away from them entirely and replace it with the farmer's squint: *in hum illā yakhruṣūn.* You did not *know.* You *estimated.* And about God, the estimate is worth nothing.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel about *kharṣ*, the estimating of the unreachable fruit. Estimation is not, in itself, a sin. The farmer who guesses his yield is doing something perfectly legitimate — he has no other tool, and he never pretends his guess is a count. The catastrophe is not the guessing. **The catastrophe is forgetting that you guessed.** It is the moment the estimate hardens, in your own mind, into knowledge — the moment you stop saying "I think" and start living as though you *know*.

That is the precise spiritual disease the Quran is diagnosing. Not that they had thoughts about God — everyone does. But that they took a *thought about the unseen* and dressed it in the authority of *certainty*, and then leaned the whole weight of their souls upon it.

And this is the verse's quiet, terrifying invitation to *us.* How much of what you believe about God's verdict on you — your standing, your safety, your secret conviction that "I'm basically fine, He's not really going to hold me to that" — how much of it is *ʿilm*, traceable to His clear word? And how much of it is *kharṣ* — a comfortable estimate you made under the palm tree of your own wishes, and have since forgotten was ever a guess?

[PAUSE]

There is a striking restraint in how Allah handles them, and it teaches the believer how to hold knowledge. He does not say "they are liars" as the first charge. He says **they have no knowledge** — and *then* "they only conjecture." The disqualification comes first. Before the verdict of falsehood comes the exposure of the *method*: you spoke about the unseen without a source. Because there are only ever two licensed sources of knowledge about God's pleasure — His revelation, and the word of His messengers — and the idolaters had neither. They had only the eyeball. *Mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm.*

This is, in the end, a verse about *epistemic humility before God* — about knowing the edge of your own knowing. The believer is not the one who has all the answers about the unseen. The believer is the one who knows **where the knowledge comes from**, and refuses to manufacture the rest. When the believer does not know, the believer says the most powerful four words in the vocabulary of faith: *Allāhu aʿlam* — God knows best. That sentence is the exact opposite of *kharṣ.* It is the refusal to guess where you have not been given to know.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** A single discipline. Begin auditing your sentences about God and about the unseen — the ones in your own head most of all. When you catch a confident verdict — *"He won't mind," "this is surely accepted," "that person is surely doomed," "I'm fine"* — ask one question: *what is my source?* If you can trace it to His clear word, hold it firmly. If you cannot — if it is really just a squint at the high branches — then have the courage to call it what it is. A guess. And let it go soft again, and say: *Allāhu aʿlam.* The whole distance between the people of this ayah and the people of faith is the distance between a guess that forgot itself and a guess that stayed honest.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look now at how the two halves of this ayah are really one insight seen from two sides.

The **first half** — *"If the Most Merciful had willed, we would not have worshipped them"* — is the disease of **mistaking His will for His pleasure.** They read the fact of occurrence as a verdict of approval.

The **second half** — *"They have no knowledge of that; they do nothing but conjecture"* — is the diagnosis of **how that disease operates**: by *guessing* at the unseen interior of God and mistaking the guess for knowledge.

They are the same error from two directions. To collapse *mashīʾa* into *riḍā* — to say "He willed it, so He's pleased" — you have to *claim knowledge of His pleasure that you do not have.* The metaphysical mistake (will = pleasure) and the epistemological mistake (guess = knowledge) are not two errors. They are one error wearing two faces. You cannot make the first without committing the second. And the moment you see that, the structure of the verse opens like a lock: the second clause is not a new accusation — it is the *exposure of the machinery* behind the first.

And notice the architecture once more. The Quran lets the *true* part stand untouched — it never denies the decree. It does not waste a syllable fighting what is true. It locates, with surgical precision, the *one* joint where the truth was bent toward falsehood — the leap from "willed" to "pleased" — and it severs the argument exactly there, with the cleanest of cuts: *mā lahum bi-dhālika min ʿilm.* This is how the Quran fights a *kalimat ḥaqq urīda bihā bāṭil.* Not by denying the truth in it. By finding the bend.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I used "it was meant to be" or "God made me this way" to quiet a conscience that was trying to speak?
2. When I tell myself "He surely doesn't mind" — is that *ʿilm* traceable to His word, or is it *kharṣ*, a guess I made under the palm tree of my own wishes?
3. Have I ever read Allah's *patience* with my wrongdoing as His *approval* of it — mistaking the held-open door of mercy for a closed case?
4. How often do I speak about the unseen — about who is saved, who is doomed, what God thinks of this or that — with a confidence my actual knowledge cannot support?
5. Can I still say *Allāhu aʿlam* and mean it — or have my guesses quietly hardened into certainties I no longer question?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**That a thing happens proves it fell within Allah's will — and proves nothing whatsoever about His pleasure; to read approval off the mere occurrence of an act is not knowledge but a guess at unreachable fruit, dressed up as fact.**

### The Closing Invitation

Let something small shift after this. Not a grand resolution — just a new sensitivity, a new flinch. The next time the sentence *"if He didn't want it, it wouldn't have happened"* rises to your lips to excuse something you already chose, let a little alarm sound. Let the word *yakhruṣūn* echo. Ask the honest question — *not "did He will it" but "did He tell me He loves it"* — and let the answer come from His Book, not from your wishes. To stop confusing His decree with His pleasure is one of the great quiet maturities of faith. It is the day you stop guessing at God and start listening to Him.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, al-Raḥmān, whose mercy we have so often misread as permission — do not let us mistake Your patience for Your pleasure, nor Your decree for Your approval. Teach us the difference between what You allow and what You love. And where we do not know, give us the honesty to say "You know best," rather than the audacity to guess at Your heart and call our guess the truth.* Āmīn.
