# The Confession They Cannot Deny — and the Turning They Cannot Explain

## Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 87

> **وَلَئِن سَأَلْتَهُم مَّنْ خَلَقَهُمْ لَيَقُولُنَّ ٱللَّهُ ۖ فَأَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ**

*Wa la'in sa'altahum man khalaqahum la-yaqūlunna Allāh — fa-annā yu'fakūn.*

> **And if you were to ask them who created them, they would certainly say, "Allah." So how are they turned away?**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question That Already Has Its Answer

Let me begin by asking you to imagine a courtroom. Not a dramatic one. A quiet one.

The defendant is on the stand. The prosecutor asks a single question, and the defendant answers it honestly — fully, without hesitation, without a lawyer's evasion. He admits the central fact of the entire case. And then, having admitted it, he stands up and walks out of the courtroom and lives the rest of his life as though he had never said the words at all.

That is the scene this ayah is describing. Except the defendant is not a stranger. The defendant is the human being who knows his Creator and lives as if he does not.

To feel why this verse lands the way it does, you have to step into the world of the people it was first spoken to. The Quraysh of Makkah were not atheists. This is the thing we so often miss. They were not people who said, "There is no God, the universe made itself." They believed in Allah. They believed He was the Creator. They would swear by Him. They named their children His servants — *Abdullah*, the slave of Allah, was a name in use before the Prophet ﷺ was even born; it was the name of his own father.

So what was the argument about?

The argument was never about whether Allah *exists* or whether Allah *created*. The Quran almost never argues that case with the Makkans, because they conceded it. The argument was about what that admission *obligates*. They were willing to say, "Allah made us." They were not willing to say, "Therefore Allah alone deserves our worship, our obedience, our surrender." They wanted to hold the belief without paying its price. They wanted to acknowledge the Creator at the level of the tongue and then route their devotion, their fear, their hope, their festivals, their identity — everywhere else. To idols. To ancestors. To the gold ornament after which this whole surah is named.

And so the Quran does something surgical here. It does not try to *prove* anything to them. It simply holds up their own confession in front of their own faces.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. There is a word at the end of this verse — the very last word — and the entire weight of the ayah rests on it. It is in a grammatical form that, once you see it, you will not be able to un-see. Because that one word quietly accuses them of something they would never have admitted to. It tells us that what looks like a free choice to walk away from God is, underneath, something far more disturbing. They are not simply leaving.

**They are being taken.**

Stay with me, and let us earn that.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Oath Inside the "If" — How Allah Made Their Confession Unbreakable

Let me teach you something about how Arabic — and frankly how human speech everywhere — handles certainty before we open the verse.

In English, when we are absolutely sure of how someone will respond, we don't say "if you ask him, maybe he'll say yes." We say: "I *guarantee* you, if you ask him, he *will* say yes — no question about it." Notice what we did. We wrapped the conditional — the "if" — inside a frame of guarantee. We swore on it before we even let the "if" finish.

Arabic does this with extraordinary precision, and it does it with two specific tools, and **both of them appear in this verse**, stacked on top of each other.

Look at the opening: **وَلَئِن** — *wa-la'in*.

That little **لَ** — that *lām* — fused to the front of the "if" is not decoration. The grammarians call it the **lām of the oath**, *lām al-qasam*. It is the linguistic residue of a sworn statement. When this *lām* attaches to a condition, the sentence is no longer saying "if such-and-such happens." It is saying: *"I swear to you — should you ever ask them..."* The condition is now spoken under oath. This is why the classical commentators, including al-Jalālayn, open their explanation of this verse by identifying that *lām* as **«لام قسم»** — the *lām* of an oath.

So before we even reach the question, Allah has placed His speech under the structure of a vow.

Now look at their answer: **لَيَقُولُنَّ** — *la-yaqūlunna*. "They will most certainly say."

And here is the second tool, and it is breathtaking once you see what it is doing.

That word carries **two** markers of emphasis welded onto a single verb. There is, again, a *lām* at the front — the answering *lām*, the response to the oath that was sworn back in *la'in*. And then, at the very end, that doubled, stressed *-nna* sound: **نّ**. The grammarians call it **nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah** — *the heavy nūn of emphasis*. "Heavy" is the actual technical name. It is the most forceful confirmation the Arabic verb is capable of carrying. It is the difference between "they'll say" and "**they will assuredly, unfailingly, beyond the shadow of any doubt — SAY.**"

Let's sit with what has happened to this little verb.

To force that heavy *nūn* onto the end of the word, the verb had to be reshaped. Al-Jalālayn points out precisely what was sacrificed: **حذف منه نون الرفع وواو الضمير** — the *nūn* of the normal plural ending was deleted, and even the *wāw* — the little vowel that would normally carry the "they" — was swallowed up. The word is, quite literally, *compressed* by the force of the emphasis being driven through it. The grammar is buckling under the certainty.

[PAUSE]

Now step back and see the architecture of what Allah has built in just five words.

An oath at the front. A condition wrapped inside that oath. An answering oath-*lām* on their reply. And the heaviest emphatic *nūn* in the language clamped onto their confession.

Why this much reinforcement for a single word — *Allah*?

Here is what's remarkable. Allah is not emphasizing this for *His* benefit. He is not unsure of the answer. He is building a confession so airtight, so over-determined, so impossible to wriggle out of, that **they can never later claim they didn't know.** Every layer of emphasis is another nail sealing shut the exit of "but I didn't realize." On the Day when a man says *"I never knew, I was never told, the matter was unclear to me"* — this verse stands up and says: *you said it yourself, and you said it with the full weight of certainty. We have it on oath. From your own mouth.*

The emphasis is not for now. **The emphasis is for later.**

What this single construction reveals: the most damning testimony against the human being is not Allah's word about him. It is **his own word about Allah** — spoken freely, spoken certainly, and then betrayed.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Passive That Has No Subject — *Yu'fakūn*

Now we come to the last word. The one I promised you at the beginning. Everything has been building to this.

**فَأَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ** — *fa-annā yu'fakūn.* "So how — from where — are they turned away?"

First, let me teach you the difference between two ways of saying the same event, because the entire meaning lives in this difference.

Consider these two sentences:
- "He walked away from the meeting."
- "He was led away from the meeting."

The first is **active**. He did it. The agent is him. The second is **passive** — and notice what the passive smuggles in. The moment you say *he was led away*, your mind immediately reaches for a question the sentence has refused to answer: *led away by whom?* Someone's hand is on him. The passive voice creates a hole exactly where the cause should be, and that hole pulls at you.

Now look at the verb Allah chose. He did **not** say *fa-annā ya'fikūn* — "so how do they turn away," active, them as the doers. He said **يُؤْفَكُونَ** — *yu'fakūn* — the **passive**: "**so how are they turned** away," "how are they *made* to deviate," "how are they *deluded*."

Let that land. The verse does not accuse them of walking out of the courtroom. It says they are being *carried* out of it.

[PAUSE]

Now go to the root itself, because the root is doing a second layer of work.

The verb comes from the root **أ-ف-ك** — *a-f-k*. Al-Qurtubī gives us the precise lexical meaning: **أفكه يأفكه أفكاً، أي: قلبه وصرفه عن الشيء** — *to overturn a thing, to flip it, to divert it away from its proper direction.* It is the word for taking something that was facing the right way and *turning its face* to the wrong way. And to prove the meaning, al-Qurtubī cites another verse where the disbelievers accuse their prophet of exactly this: **«أَجِئْتَنَا لِتَأْفِكَنَا عَنْ آلِهَتِنَا»** — "Have you come *to turn us away* from our gods?" (46:22). Same root. To be *afaka* is to have your face wrenched off its true bearing.

Al-Muyassar, in its paraphrase of this verse, reaches for two verbs at once — *yanqalibūna* and *yansarifūna* — to capture what this single root conveys: not merely a turning away but an *inversion*, a *flipping*. The word *yanqalibūna* carries the full force of overturning, of something being upended. As though the truth were a compass that someone reached in and spun. You think you are heading north because you feel the motion of travel. But you have been turned, and you no longer face the direction you were meant to face.

And here is the quiet horror buried in this root. The same three letters — *a-f-k* — are the letters of **الإِفْك**, *al-ifk*: **the great lie. The slander. The fabrication.** It is the very word the Quran uses in Surah an-Nūr for the most infamous false accusation in the Prophet's ﷺ household. So the root itself binds two things together: *being turned away from the truth* and *falsehood*. To be *yu'fak* is not merely to drift. It is to be **flipped by a lie** — to have a falsehood get its hands on you and rotate you off your axis.

[PAUSE]

Now look at something al-Tabari notices in his explanation of this verse. He does not only say "they are turned from worshipping the One who created them." He presses further: **ويحرمون إصابة الحقّ في عبادته** — "and they are *deprived* of attaining the truth in His worship." The verb *yuḥramūn* — to be deprived, denied, prevented from reaching what is one's due — adds a dimension that sharpens the passivity into something more precise. To be turned away is to go in the wrong direction. But to be *deprived* of the truth is to be robbed of something that should have been yours. Al-Tabari's reading suggests that the one who is *yu'fak* is not only a person who missed the road; he is a person from whom something owed has been withheld. The truth of God's worship was his native inheritance — the orientation his fitrah was built for — and in the *afk*, that inheritance is being denied to him.

Now assemble the full picture of this one word.

It is **passive** — something is being done *to* them.
Its agent is **deleted** — the sentence pointedly refuses to name *who* is turning them.
Its root means **to be overturned and inverted by a lie**.
And al-Tabari reads it as an act of **deprivation** — a denial of what should have been theirs by right of their own nature.

So put the unavoidable question to the verse: *who is the hidden hand?* Who is the agent the passive voice is hiding?

This is where the absence is the message. Allah leaves the turner unnamed — and in leaving it unnamed, He opens the question to every candidate at once. Who turns the human being away from the God he has just confessed?

The whisperer who said he would *"lie in wait for them on Your straight path"* (7:16) — Shaytān turns them.
The *nafs* that *"commands to evil"* (12:53) — the self turns them.
The fathers and the inherited tradition this very surah keeps naming — *"we found our fathers upon a way"* (43:22) — the culture turns them.
The gold ornament, the *zukhruf* of the dunya itself — the world turns them.

The verse does not pick one, because the truth is that the human being who confesses God and lives otherwise is being turned by *all* of them, and the most frightening part is the passive itself: it suggests he is *not even fully aware it is happening.* You do not feel yourself being turned. That is the nature of *afk*. You think you are walking straight.

What this single word reveals: the distance between knowing God and surrendering to Him is not usually crossed in one defiant stride. **You are turned across it — slowly, by hands you do not look at — while you tell yourself you have not moved.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So look at what the grammar has shown us. The oath and the heavy *nūn* nailed shut their confession: *they know.* Beyond doubt, on oath, in their own words — they know who created them. And then the passive *yu'fakūn* tells us that despite that airtight knowledge, they are being carried off in the opposite direction by an agent the verse refuses to name.

Knowledge that certain. Conduct that contradictory. A turning that no one is consciously choosing.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Most Dangerous Distance in the World

Let me start where you live.

Every single person listening to this knows something true that they are not acting on. I want you to actually locate it, not just nod at the idea. The person who knows, with total certainty, that the relationship is harming them, and stays. The person who knows the scrolling is hollowing them out, and reaches for the phone the instant they wake. The one who knows exactly what the doctor said, and orders the same thing anyway. We do not lack the information. We have the information. We could pass the exam on it.

Psychologists have a clinical name for the discomfort of this gap — *cognitive dissonance* — the friction of holding a belief and a behavior that contradict each other. But here is what the research found, and it is devastating: when belief and behavior collide, **the human mind does not reliably fix the behavior. More often, it quietly edits the belief** — softens it, reframes it, builds a little story so the contradiction stops hurting. We don't rise to our knowledge. We lower our knowledge to meet our conduct.

That is exactly the human being in this ayah.

He says *"Allah"* — with full conviction. And then he goes and lives a life that the confession should have made impossible. And the gap between the *"Allah"* on his tongue and the orientation of his actual life — that is the most dangerous distance in the world.

Not because it is the largest distance. It is perhaps the smallest. The gap between *knowing* and *surrendering* is sometimes not even a step. It is a pivot — a single turning of the face. But the passive voice of *yu'fakūn* tells us that even this tiny pivot is not always made freely. Something else makes it. And because it is not made openly, it can be sustained indefinitely. A man who has walked away from God in defiance at least knows he has left. But the man who has been turned — quietly, gradually, by inheritance and habit and the weight of the world and the gold ornament — he may not know he has moved at all. He still says *"Allah."* He would be insulted if you told him he was distant.

That is what al-Tabari is pointing at when he says these people are *deprived* of attaining the truth in His worship. Not barred from knowing. *Deprived of the fruit of knowing.* The knowledge is there. The confession is genuine. But the worship — the actual turning of the heart, the actual direction of the life — that has been withheld. And the horror of deprivation, unlike the horror of rejection, is that you may not notice it. Deprivation is quiet. The man who has been robbed of something he never quite held in his hands does not always know what is missing.

## Theme Two: The Two Tawhids — And the Precise Location of the Break

Here is the classical framework that makes sense of what this ayah is doing, and it is worth knowing clearly.

The scholars of *tawhid* distinguish between two kinds of monotheism. The first is **tawhid al-rububiyya** — the oneness of lordship. *Allah is the Creator, the Sustainer, the One who holds the universe in existence.* This is the level at which the Quraysh already believed. They would have said it without hesitation. The Quran repeats the pattern throughout this surah — in 43:9, Allah asks: "If you were to ask them who created the heavens and the earth, they would certainly say, 'The Mighty, the All-Knowing created them.'" Ask about creation: *Allah.* Ask about rain and sustenance (29:63): *Allah.* Ask about who you would call on in the darkest hour of the sea (29:65): *Allah.* This level of tawhid the pagans of Makkah had never lost.

The second kind is **tawhid al-uluhiyya** — the oneness of worship. *Allah alone deserves my fear, my hope, my sacrifice, my direction in prayer, my deepest devotion and surrender.* This is exactly what they refused. They believed in the Creator and worshipped others alongside Him.

Now look at al-Jalālayn's precise gloss on *yu'fakūn*: **يُصْرَفُونَ عَنْ عِبادَة اللَّه** — "they are diverted from the **worship** of Allah." Not from *knowing* Allah. Not from *believing in* Allah. From *worshipping* Him. The classical commentator lands on the same word — *ibada*, worship, surrender — that marks the exact break between rububiyya and uluhiyya. The turning is calibrated. It is not a wholesale abandonment of God. It is a surgical deflection of the one thing the confession demands: that the life be surrendered to the One who created it.

This is the architecture of the most sophisticated idolatry, and it is not limited to the 7th-century Quraysh. The person who says, "I believe in God — I just don't think God is that involved in the details," is operating from tawhid al-rububiyya without tawhid al-uluhiyya. The person who says, "I believe in a higher power — I just direct that belief through tradition / community / my own conscience / the universe," is separating the confession from its demand. The Quraysh had their idols between them and Allah. We have our own. And in every generation the idol takes the shape of the thing that generation loves most — its gold ornament, its *zukhruf*.

[PAUSE]

The verse asks: *fa-annā yu'fakūn* — "so *how* are they turned away?" Notice that *annā* is not asking for the agent. It is not "by whom." It is a cry of astonishment: *by what path, by what road, through what contortion of reasoning does a human being arrive at the Creator's doorstep with a full confession on his lips and then walk away without entering?*

The question is not looking for an answer. It is looking for the listener to feel the impossibility. To feel, for one honest moment, that there is no good answer. That the gap makes no sense.

Because it doesn't.

That is the point.

**The gap between confessing God and surrendering to Him is the most intellectually indefensible position in existence — and it is the position of the majority of the human race, in every generation, without exception.** And the verse does not condemn it with fury. It meets it with astonishment. *How? How did you get here? You knew who made you. How, from that, did you end up here?*

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Three: The Confession They Made Before You Were Born

There is one more layer to this verse, and it requires a single step backward in time.

Among the related ayahs of this reflection is 7:172 — the ayah of *al-mīthāq*, the primordial covenant:

> *And when your Lord took from the children of Adam — from their loins — their descendants, and made them testify of themselves, "Am I not your Lord?" They said, "Yes, we have testified."*

The Quran tells us that before this world existed, before this body, before this argument between the Prophet ﷺ and the Quraysh — every human soul was gathered, and every human soul confessed. *"Balā"* — *"Yes."* Yes, You are our Lord.

Now come back to this verse. When the Prophet ﷺ asks the Quraysh *"who created you?"* and they answer *"Allah"* — they are not confessing for the first time. They are echoing a confession that is older than their grandfathers' grandfathers. The *"Allah"* that comes out of their mouth under the heaviest oath and the heaviest emphatic *nūn* — it is the word their soul first spoke in the presence of their Lord, in a world before memory but not before being.

The fitrah — the primordial nature — is precisely why the Quran can be so breathtakingly confident that they will say *"Allah."* The answer does not need to be taught because it is not learned; it is remembered. And the nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah that makes their answer unshakeable — perhaps it is unshakeable precisely because it is rooted not in argument but in something beneath argument: in what the soul already knows it said, long before the *lām* of any earthly oath was required.

[PAUSE]

This is the ache at the center of the verse. The Quraysh are not strangers to the Creator. They are people who confessed Him before they were born and confessed Him again when asked. The *yu'fakūn* — the being-turned, the being-carried-off — is happening to people who know the way home. The tragedy is not ignorance. **The tragedy is that the creature who knows his Lord most intimately is the one being turned most urgently away.**

And this is why the passive voice is not passive at all. It is not indifference. The passive voice in *yu'fakūn* is the Quran noting, with devastating quietness, that something very active is working against the creature's return to what he has always known. The arch-enemy who swore he would block the road (7:16). The self that has grown comfortable with its idols. The world that has convinced him its gold is worth his whole life.

And the Prophet ﷺ, receiving this verse, watching the people he loves give their airtight confession and then walk away — the very next verse of this surah records what he said to his Lord: *"My Lord, indeed these are a people who will not believe."* Not defiance. Grief. The grief of a man who has held up a mirror and watched people not recognize their own face.

# CLOSING: The Question Addressed to You

So come back to the courtroom.

The question — *man khalaqakum*, "who created you?" — was spoken to the Quraysh of Makkah. But every generation that recites this verse becomes the one on the stand. The question presses through every century it passes through and arrives, right now, addressed to you.

You will answer it correctly. You know who created you. That much is settled — settled before your birth, settled in your fitrah, settled by the testimony of everything you have ever seen and felt and wondered at. The layered emphasis already assumes the answer. *La-yaqūlunna Allāh.* Most certainly, they will say: Allah. Most certainly, you will say: Allah.

The question is what happens in the space between the answer and the rest of your life.

*Fa-annā yu'fakūn.*

So *how* are they turned?

The word is passive. Not: *how do they turn themselves?* But: *how are they turned?* Because the verse has the charity to acknowledge that this process is not usually a single conscious act of rejection. It is a long, slow, hands-on process, and you feel it happening the way you feel yourself getting older — which is to say, you mostly don't. The turning is gradual. It happens in small capitulations, in small comfortable distances, in the small routine of reaching for everything else first. The gold ornament does not storm through the front door. It accumulates quietly, one adornment at a time, until the room is full of it and the direction you were once facing is hard to find.

The verse offers no verdict. It offers only the astonishment. *How?* It is a question that every honest person — every person who has ever caught the gap between what they believe and how they live — has to sit inside.

Not to condemn yourself. To *locate* yourself.

Because the first act of a person who has been turned is to stop and feel that they are facing the wrong way.

The second act — the act that no passive force can perform for you, the one that must be yours — is to turn back.

And that deliberate, chosen turning of the face toward the One the confession already named: that is the act this entire surah has been building toward. *Fa-annā yu'fakūn* is not a statement about the irreversibility of the turning. It is an expression of astonishment at the waste of it. The waste that does not have to continue.

[FINAL PAUSE]

The surah is named for the gold ornament. For the glitter that dazzled the Quraysh so thoroughly that they could confess the Creator and still choose the creation. The lesson the surah ends with is not that gold is evil. It is that gold — every version of it, in every generation — is very effective at turning faces. And the face has to be *turned back*, by a deliberate act of the will, toward the One who made both the face and the gold.

*"So how are they turned away?"*

The question is a mirror. Hold it. Look.
