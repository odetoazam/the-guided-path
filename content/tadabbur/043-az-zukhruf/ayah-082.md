# Above What They Describe

> <!-- ayah:43:82 -->     سُبْحَـٰنَ رَبِّ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ
>
> *Subḥāna rabbi s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi rabbi l-ʿarshi ʿammā yaṣifūn.*
>
> "Glory be to the Lord of the heavens and the earth, the Lord of the Throne, above what they describe."

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

The surah is named *az-Zukhruf* — **the Gold Ornament**. The gilding. The decorative layer of gold leaf hammered thin and pressed over something to make it gleam. That word is not chosen by accident to sit over this chapter. This entire surah is a sustained argument about a single human disease: **mistaking the surface for the substance. Judging the real by the ornamental.** The people it addresses looked at wealth and concluded that wealth was a sign of divine favor. They looked at their inherited traditions and assumed that inheritance made them true. They looked at the sky, at the angels they could not see, and — with breathtaking confidence — they *described* what was up there. They said the angels were daughters of God. They said the Most Merciful had a child. They gilded their own imaginations and called it theology.

Sit with the audacity of that for a moment. Here are human beings — creatures who did not choose their own birth, who cannot add a single hour to their lives, who do not know what tomorrow holds — and they are issuing statements about the inner life of the Creator of the heavens. They are drawing a family tree for God. And the terrifying thing is that they were not, in their own minds, being blasphemous. They thought they were being *religious*. They believed they were honoring God by giving Him offspring, by populating the heavens with a divine household. The gilding felt like devotion.

Now — the ayah just before ours, 43:81, does something that stops you. Allah instructs the Prophet ﷺ: *Say — if the Most Merciful had a son, then I would be the very first to worship.* It meets them almost on their own ground. It reasons with them. It says, in effect: *I am not withholding devotion out of stinginess; if the claim were true, no one would be more devoted than I.* It is a door held open. An argument extended.

And then comes our ayah. And notice — **it does not continue the argument.** It does not offer a second proof. Something else happens entirely. The voice rises. The register changes. After reasoning with them, the response to their description of God is no longer a rebuttal.

It is *subḥān*.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want to plant in you, and I do not want to answer it yet. When someone says something false and wounding about the One you love most — when they *describe* Him wrongly — why would the deepest answer not be a better argument, but a single word of glory? What does that word *do* that an argument cannot? And why does this word — *subḥān* — hide inside it, at the root, an image of **swimming away**?

Stay with me. We are going to earn that.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### One: *Subḥān* — the word that means to swim away

Let me teach you something about how Arabic roots work before we go anywhere near this word, because if I don't, the word will slide past you the way it slides past most of us — as a pious noise we make, a sound we've heard ten thousand times and stopped hearing.

In Arabic, most words grow from a three-letter root, and that root carries a *physical, concrete, often bodily* core meaning. The abstract meanings are branches; the root is the trunk, and the trunk is almost always something you could touch or see or do with your hands. So when you meet a lofty spiritual word, the discipline is to dig down past the branches to the trunk and ask: *what physical thing is this?* Because the physical thing is the stepping stone Allah is using to carry you toward something you could never see directly.

So. *Subḥān.* Its root is *sīn–bā–ḥā*. And here is the trunk: this root means **to swim. To move swiftly through water or through air. To traverse a great distance, gliding.**

This is not a stretch. This is documented, ordinary Arabic, and the Qur'an uses it exactly this way elsewhere. When Allah describes the sun and the moon and the heavenly bodies, He says *kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn* — each one *swimming* in its orbit. The same root. The celestial bodies are pictured as swimmers, gliding through the vastness. In another place He swears by *an-nāziʿāt*, and among them *as-sābiḥāt sabḥā* — those that glide, that swim, swiftly. The root is water and motion. It is a body cutting cleanly through a medium, covering distance, moving *away* from where it was.

Now bring that trunk up into our word. *Subḥān Allāh* — the great declaration of the believer — is not vaguely "praise." At its root it is an act of **motion**. It is to *send God far away* — to actively, deliberately move Him at speed to a distance — **away from every flaw, every defect, every limitation, every unworthy thing the human mind could attach to Him.** The classical lexicographers, Ar-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī among them, tie tasbīḥ directly to *tanzīh*: the declaring of Allah free, removed, purified from all imperfection. And the very sound and shape of the word carries that swift, gliding removal inside it.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? The word you say absentmindedly on your prayer beads is not a static compliment. It is a **swimmer's stroke.** Every time you say *subḥān Allāh*, at the root of the word you are performing a motion: you are taking every small, cramped, human-sized idea of God that has crept close — every reduction, every projection, every gilded lie — and you are stroking away from it, moving God swiftly back to the infinite distance where He actually is, where no description can reach Him.

And now look at *why* this word, of all words, arrives here. They gilded the heavens with a false family. They pulled God down close, down into the shape of a human household, a father with daughters. They shrank Him to something their imagination could hold. And the ayah's answer is not to negotiate the size of the box. **The answer is to swim Him out of the box entirely.** *Subḥān* — He is far, He is removed, He is at a distance no false description can swim across. This is what an argument cannot do. An argument stays in the water with the lie, wrestling it. *Subḥān* leaves the water.

That is the first word. That is the first stroke. [PAUSE]

### Two: *ʿAmmā yaṣifūn* — and the door Allah deliberately left open

Now go to the end of the ayah, because the beginning and the end are speaking to each other. The verse closes: *ʿammā yaṣifūn* — "above what they describe."

Two things live here, and I want to take them one at a time.

First, the root of *yaṣifūn*. It is *wāw–ṣād–fā* — *waṣafa*, **to describe.** And this is a familiar root; from it comes *ṣifa*, an "attribute," a "description," the very word Muslims use for the Attributes of Allah. A *waṣf* is a portrait made of words. To *describe* is to draw an outline around something, to say *it is like this, and this, and not that* — to fix its edges. When you describe a thing, you are, in a sense, containing it in language. You are saying: I have taken its measure, and here it is.

So hear the collision the ayah stages. *Subḥān* at the front — the swimming-away into limitlessness. *Yaṣifūn* at the back — the human act of drawing edges, fixing outlines, taking measure. The whole verse is stretched between these two poles: **the God who cannot be contained, and the creatures busy drawing His outline.** And the ayah's verdict on that drawing is delivered by the little word *ʿan*, which here means *above, beyond, free of, removed from*. He is *ʿan* — clear away from, at a distance beyond — what they describe.

Now the second thing, and this is where I need you to slow all the way down, because it is the kind of thing that is easy to read straight past.

Look at *what* He is above. He does not say: "above the son they claimed." He does not say: "above the daughters they invented." He had every reason to name the specific lie — the whole surah has been about that specific lie. Instead He uses the word **مَا, *mā*** — which is the broadest, most open, most unspecified word Arabic has for "what." Not "the false thing they said." Just — *whatever they describe.*

Notice this. This is not vagueness. This is deliberate, and it is mercy, and it is terrifying in its scope. Arabic could have been specific. Arabic *loves* to be specific. But here the object is thrown wide open. Because if the ayah had named only the son, then the moment you and I stopped believing God had a son, we would think the ayah no longer concerned us. We would file it under "answered." *Ancient problem, solved.*

By choosing *mā* — "whatever they describe" — Allah pulls the verse out of the seventh century and drops it directly onto your prayer mat. **He is above the son they invented, and above the harsh tyrant your childhood invented, and above the indifferent watchmaker your professor invented, and above the cosmic vending machine your anxiety invented, and above the flattering yes-man your ego invented.** The blank in the sentence is where every false description that has ever been drawn, or ever will be drawn, quietly goes. He left the space empty so that all of them would fit.

That empty space — the *mā* where a specific noun could have been — is one of the most spacious things in the Qur'an. The absence is the point. [PAUSE]

### Three: The two Lords and the raised Throne

Between the swimming-away at the front and the drawing-of-edges at the back sits the content — and it is built with a strange, deliberate repetition that we should not smooth over.

Listen: *rabbi s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍi* — **Lord of the heavens and the earth** — *rabbi l-ʿarsh* — **Lord of the Throne.** He says *rabb* twice. He could have said it once and listed everything: Lord of the heavens and the earth and the Throne. Instead the word *rabb* is lifted and set down a second time, deliberately, as its own beat.

Let me first give you *rabb* itself, because we translate it "Lord" and lose almost everything. The root *rāʾ–bāʾ–bāʾ* does not primarily mean "master" in the cold sense of ownership. Its core is **to nurture a thing, stage by stage, from its beginning toward its completion.** It is the root behind *tarbiya* — the raising of a child. A *rabb* is one who owns, yes, but owns the way a gardener "owns" a seedling: by tending it, sustaining it, bringing it to what it was meant to be. So *rabb of the heavens and the earth* is not a distant proprietor. It is the One actively raising, sustaining, moment-to-moment holding-in-being everything you can see.

And what can they see? Heavens and earth. The *whole visible cosmos*, floor to ceiling. Now watch the second *rabb*. It lifts our eyes past the visible ceiling entirely, to *al-ʿarsh* — the Throne. And here is a gift hidden in that root, *ʿayn–rāʾ–shīn.* It does not only mean a seat of authority. Its physical trunk is **a raised structure — a canopy, a trellis, something built up high and overarching.** The same root appears when the Qur'an speaks of a ruined town fallen down *ʿalā ʿurūshihā* — collapsed upon its own trellises, its raised roofs caved in. It appears for what the bees and the builders *raise up* as structures. So *al-ʿarsh* carries, at the root, the sense of **the highest raised thing, the overarching canopy above all creation.**

So follow the geometry the two *rabb*s trace. The first *rabb* sweeps across everything you can see and stand on — heavens, earth, the whole observable world. The second *rabb* rises above the visible altogether, to the highest reality there is. And here is the piece that pierces: **the Throne, the unseen heavenly realm — that is exactly the territory they were making things up about.** Their false descriptions were not about the earth. Nobody was inventing lies about the soil. Their gilding was aimed *upward* — at the heavens, at the angels, at the divine household they imagined furnishing the celestial realm.

Do you see what the double *rabb* does? It is not decoration. It plants Allah's Lordship *as an explicit, named claim* over the precise region their imaginations had colonized. They peopled the heavens with a fantasy. The ayah says: *rabb al-ʿarsh.* **The realm you are describing? I am its Lord.** Not a resident of it — its Sustainer. You are drawing a map of a country you have never been to, and the King of that country is telling you He made the land you are sketching. [PAUSE]

Every linguistic claim I've made — the swimming root of *subḥān*, the describing root of *yaṣifūn*, the open *mā*, the nurturing *rabb*, the raised-canopy *ʿarsh* — each is documented in the root itself. What I've built *on top* of them — that the empty *mā* is aimed at your prayer mat, that the second *rabb* is a flag planted on colonized territory — those are ways I'm inviting you to sit with the grammar, not claims the grammar makes on its own. Hold the two apart. The root is bedrock. The reflection is what we do standing on it.

Now let us ask what all this reveals — not about words, but about you.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The God you argue with may be a God you invented

Let me start nowhere near this ayah. Let me start in a therapist's office, because that is where this theme actually lives in your world.

There is a moment that happens over and over in the counseling of people who have walked away from faith. The person describes the God they rejected: a God who is watching for every mistake, tallying, waiting to catch them out. A God whose love is conditional and always about to be withdrawn. A God who is disappointed in them. And a wise counselor sometimes says a devastating sentence back to them: *"The God you're describing — I wouldn't believe in that God either."*

Sit with why that lands so hard. Because very often, when you trace that harsh, tallying, disappointed God back to His origin, you do not find Him in scripture at all. You find Him in a **father who was never satisfied.** You find Him in a **teacher who shamed.** You find Him in a **culture that made love a reward for performance.** The person did not reject God. They rejected a *portrait* — a *waṣf*, a description — drawn in the ink of their own wounds, and then hung on the wall with God's name under it.

This is the most ordinary thing in the world. We cannot help it. The mind builds its picture of the unseen out of the only materials it has: the seen. Our parents. Our fears. Our culture. Our disappointments. We are *describers* by nature — *yaṣifūn* is not a sin unique to seventh-century pagans, it is the default setting of the human being. We take the raw, unbearable, uncontainable reality of God, and because we cannot hold it, we shrink it to something the size of our own experience. A father-sized God. A judge-sized God. A vending-machine-sized God who dispenses good outcomes if you insert enough prayers.

And here is what this ayah says to every single one of those portraits, without exception, because it left the space blank: *subḥān… ʿammā yaṣifūn.* **He swims free of all of it.** He is not the harsh father your body still flinches from. He is not the indifferent watchmaker. He is not the scorekeeper. Every one of those is a description, and He is *ʿan* — above, beyond, clear away from — every description.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the two edges of this, because it cuts both ways and you must let it cut both ways.

The mercy of it: **the God who terrifies you may not exist.** If your faith is heavy because the God you carry is cruel, this ayah is telling you that you may have spent years in fear of a portrait you painted yourself, in the dark, as a child, out of pain. The real One swam free of that portrait before you ever finished it. You are permitted — you are *commanded* — to stop worshipping the projection and to let Him be bigger, and kinder, and more free than your wound allowed.

But do not walk away with only the comfort, because the same word cuts the other way: **the God who flatters you may not exist either.** The tame, agreeable God who only ever affirms your choices, who has no expectations, who exists to underwrite whatever you already wanted to do — *subḥān ʿammā yaṣifūn.* That is a description too. That is gilding too. He swims free of the comfortable God exactly as He swims free of the cruel one. The ayah does not replace your small harsh God with a small soft God. It removes the *smallness*.

So what do you actually *do* with this tomorrow? Here is the concrete practice, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. The next time you notice you are angry at God, or disappointed in God, or bargaining with God — *stop*, and ask one question: **"Who am I actually describing right now? Is this the Lord of the heavens and the earth and the Throne — or is this my father? My fear? My last bad year?"** And when you catch that it's a projection — and it usually is — say the word. *Subḥān Allāh.* And mean the root of it. **Swim Him free of the portrait.** Send Him back to the distance where He actually lives, the one no description can reach. That is not a ritual phrase. That is spiritual hygiene. That is you, several times a day, cleaning the lens.

### Theme Two: When someone lies about the One you love, why glory answers better than argument

Now I want to go back to the strangest thing about this ayah — the thing I planted in you at the very start — and I want to reach it through something you have actually lived.

Think of the last time someone said something false and ugly about a person you love. Not a mild disagreement — a real distortion. Someone characterized your mother as cold when you know she gave everything. Someone painted your closest friend as a fraud when you have seen their integrity cost them dearly. You know that feeling. Your chest tightens. And notice what your *first* instinct is: **you want to argue.** You want to marshal the evidence. *Actually, let me tell you what she did in 2019. Actually, here are three times he chose the hard right thing.* You reach for the rebuttal.

And here is what you have probably also learned, if you have lived long enough: **the rebuttal often makes it worse.** Because the moment you start arguing point-by-point against the slander, you have accepted the slander's *terms*. You are now litigating your mother's warmth on the accuser's turf, at the accuser's altitude, in the accuser's language. You've climbed down into the mud to wrestle, and now you're both in the mud, and the watching crowd sees two people covered in mud. Something in the dignity of the one you love has been lost the instant the defense began.

Now look again at the seam between ayah 81 and ayah 82. Because Allah does *both*, in order, and the order is the whole lesson.

First, 43:81 — the argument. *Say: if the Most Merciful had a son, I would be first to worship.* The door held open. The reasoning offered. There is a time for argument, and revelation does not skip it. Proof is given.

And then — *subḥān.* The argument is made, and then the ayah **rises above the argument entirely.** It does not offer proof number two. It changes altitude. It stops litigating God's nature on the pagans' terms and does the thing you can only do when you love someone rightly: it *glorifies.* It declares the distance. It says, in effect: *I have answered you — and now I am going to lift the One you slandered back up to where your slander cannot reach, because that is where He belongs and that is where I refuse to let this conversation drag Him down from.*

[PAUSE]

This is the move. This is the thing to carry. **Glorification is not the failure to argue. It is what you do after argument, from a height argument cannot reach.** *Subḥān* — the swimming-away — is the soul refusing to keep the Beloved down in the mud of the dispute. It is the believer saying: *your description of Him is not merely wrong, it is beneath Him, and I will not honor it by treating it as a live possibility one second longer than I must. I will lift Him out.*

And notice the emotional intelligence of the sequence, because it guards against two errors at once. The person who *only* argues and never glorifies has made God into a debate proposition — a thesis to be defended, cold, forever on trial. But the person who *only* glorifies and never argues, who waves away every hard question with "*subḥān Allāh*, don't ask" — that person has made faith into a wall, a refusal to think, a hiding place. The ayah does neither. It reasons *fully* — and then it rises. Mind first. Then heart, above the mind. **Tafsīr, and then tadabbur.** The intellect does its honest work, and then the soul takes the intellect somewhere the intellect could not go alone.

So what changes tomorrow? Two things, one outward and one inward.

Outward: the next time your faith, or your Prophet ﷺ, or your God is mocked or distorted — online, at a dinner table, in a lecture hall — remember the sequence. *Answer.* Give the honest, reasoned answer; do not hide behind piety. **And then rise.** Do not let them keep you down at the altitude of the sneer. Say the word, in your heart if not aloud, and *feel* the distance it declares. You are not obligated to spend your whole life in the mud defending the sun against people who insist it is dark. At some point you glorify, and you walk toward the light, and you let the sun be the sun.

Inward — and this is the deeper one. There is a slander that runs in your *own* head. The days the whisper says *He has forgotten you. He is disappointed in you. He is not really there.* That is *yaṣifūn* too — a false description, drawn this time by your own despair. And you can argue with it, and you should, with the evidence of every mercy you've ever been shown. But when the argument runs out, at 3 a.m. when the arguing is exhausted — there is still the word. *Subḥān Rabb al-ʿarsh.* **Glory to the Lord of the Throne — above what my own darkness describes.** And you swim Him free, one more time, of the portrait your worst night painted. ## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — what the verse was doing the whole time

Now stand back and look at the shape of the whole ayah, because it has been building a structure under our feet this entire time, and only now can we see it from above.

Watch the frame. It **opens** with *subḥān* — transcendence, the swimming-away, the declaration of distance. It **closes** with *ʿammā yaṣifūn* — above what they describe — which is *also* a declaration of distance, transcendence stated from the other direction. The verse begins by removing Him from all imperfection and ends by removing Him from all description. **The two ends are the same gesture, mirrored.** Transcendence, and transcendence. That is the outer ring.

And what sits *inside* that ring, cradled and protected by it? The two *rabb*s. *Lord of the heavens and the earth. Lord of the Throne.* The affirmation. The positive content — that He nurtures, sustains, raises, and reigns over the entire cosmos, seen and unseen, floor to highest canopy.

So the design is this: **transcendence — affirmation — affirmation — transcendence.** The declaration that He is *beyond* all things wraps around the declaration that He is *Lord of* all things, like two cupped hands around a flame. And that is not an accident of phrasing; that is *the doctrine itself, built into the sentence's bones.* Because this is the exact balance the whole surah has been fighting for. The pagans failed on *both* sides at once: they denied His transcendence (they gave Him a family, a body, a household — they described Him) *and* in doing so they misunderstood His Lordship (they imagined the heavenly realm as something other than His). The ayah heals both wounds in one breath, and it arranges the words so that transcendence *guards* the affirmation on both flanks. **You cannot get to the truth of what He is Lord *of* except through hands that first declare Him beyond all you could say.** The *subḥān* has to come first, and last, or the *rabb* in the middle gets shrunk back into a portrait.

That is the summit view. That is why the mountain was worth climbing.

### Where the two paths meet

And now see that the linguistic journey and the two themes were never two things — they were one insight approached from opposite shores.

The words told us: *subḥān* is a swimming-away, and *mā* is a deliberately open blank, and He is Lord of the very realm they mythologized. The themes told us: the God you fear may be a portrait you painted, and glory is what you speak from a height argument cannot reach. But these are the same truth. **The disease is that we describe Him — into a father, a judge, a vending machine, a family with daughters. The cure is that we swim Him free.** Every time. The blank *mā* is left open precisely because the describing never stops — so the swimming can never stop either. Tasbīḥ is not a phrase you say. It is the lifelong labor of refusing to let God shrink to the size of your experience.

We began standing among people gilding the heavens, and we end holding a single word that strips the gold leaf away — not to leave us with less, but to leave us with the real thing, which was always infinitely more than the gilding could imagine.

### Questions to carry

- When you feel resistance toward God — fear, disappointment, distance — sit with it honestly: *whose face* is actually behind that feeling? Is it His, or is it someone else's, wearing His name?
- Which is the God you more often reduce Him to — the harsh one who terrifies you, or the tame one who flatters you? What in your life built that particular portrait?
- The next time your faith is attacked, where is your instinct: to stay in the mud and argue endlessly, or to answer once and then rise? What would it look like to do both, in that order?
- You say *subḥān Allāh* how many times a day? What would change if, even once, you felt the swimming in it — the actual motion of sending Him free of everything small?
- What is one description of God you have been carrying that you now suspect He is *above*?

### The one sentence to keep

**He left the blank open — "above whatever they describe" — because the God who could be captured in any description would not be worth worshipping, and the labor of faith is to keep swimming Him free of every portrait we cannot help but paint.**

### The invitation

Do not take from this a heavier religion — one more thing to get right, one more way to fail. Take the opposite. Take the *lightness* of it. You have been permitted, today, to set down whatever cramped and cruel and too-small God you have been dragging around, and to let the real One be as vast and as merciful and as free as He actually is. That is not a demand. It is a release. The next *subḥān Allāh* on your lips can be the first stroke of swimming toward Him — away from the portrait, into the open water where He has been waiting, past all describing, the whole time.

### A closing duʿā'

*O Lord of the heavens and the earth, Lord of the Throne — we confess that we have described You with the ink of our own wounds and our own smallness. Forgive us the portraits. Teach our hearts to swim free of every idea of You that is beneath You, and let the words* subḥān Allāh *carry us, each time, back to the distance where You truly are — glorified, and far above all that we describe. Āmīn.*
