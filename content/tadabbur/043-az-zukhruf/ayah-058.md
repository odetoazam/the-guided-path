# The Question That Was Never a Question

> وَقَالُوٓا۟ أَءَالِهَتُنَا خَيْرٌ أَمْ هُوَ ۚ مَا ضَرَبُوهُ لَكَ إِلَّا جَدَلًۢا ۚ بَلْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ خَصِمُونَ

*Wa qālū a-ālihatunā khayrun am huwa — mā ḍarabūhu laka illā jadalan — bal hum qawmun khaṣimūn.*

**"And they said, 'Are our gods better, or is he?' They did not present it to you except for argument. Nay, they are a contentious people."**

---

## Introduction: The Sound of a Trap Snapping Shut

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

Imagine you are standing in Makkah, and the Prophet ﷺ has been reciting to the people a verse that landed like a stone in still water. The verse — from Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ — said: *"Indeed, you and what you worship besides Allah are the firewood of Hell."* You and your idols. Both. Fuel.

Now, the classical commentators relate that one of the sharp tongues of Quraysh saw an opening in this. He thought he had found a crack. He said, in effect: *"Wait. The Christians worship the son of Maryam. The Jews venerate ʿUzayr. There are people who worship the angels. So by your own logic, Muhammad — are all of these in the Fire too? Is ʿĪsā in the Fire? Are the angels firewood?"*

And the people — picture this — they did not pause to think. They **laughed**. The verse just before ours says it plainly: *"And when the son of Maryam was presented as an example, your people, at that, laughed aloud"* (43:57). They clamored. They slapped their knees. They felt the electric thrill of a crowd that believes one of their own has just **won**.

[PAUSE]

And in that roar of laughter, in that moment of triumph, Allah revealed our ayah. Not to settle the theological technicality — He does that elsewhere. Here, Allah does something far more surgical. He reaches past the words they said and exposes the **machinery of the heart that said them.**

Here is the hook. There is a question buried in this ayah — *"Are our gods better, or is he?"* — and it looks, on its surface, like a real question. It has the grammar of inquiry. It has a questioning particle. It asks for a comparison. By every external marker, it is a question.

And Allah's response is to tell us: **it was never a question at all.**

How does the Quran take something shaped exactly like a sincere question and reveal that, underneath, it was something else entirely? And — this is the part that should make us sit up — what does that revealing have to do with *you and me*, fourteen centuries later, every time we open our mouths to "discuss"?

Let's go slowly. There is more here than we can exhaust.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Mā ḍarabūhu illā jadalan* — "Nothing but the wrestle"

Before we touch the ayah, let me teach you a small structure in Arabic, because once you see it, you will hear the verse differently.

Arabic has a way of building a fence with two pieces of wood. The first piece is the word **مَا** (*mā*) — a flat negation. "Not." It knocks everything down. The second piece is the word **إِلَّا** (*illā*) — "except." It lets exactly one thing back through the gate.

Put them together — *mā… illā* — and you have built what the grammarians call a structure of **restriction** (*qaṣr* or *ḥaṣr*). It does not merely say "they did this for argument." It says: *they did this for argument — **and for nothing else.*** It demolishes every other possible motive, every charitable reading, every "well, maybe they were genuinely confused," and leaves standing one single, naked purpose.

You use this structure yourself. When you say, "I'm not telling you this to hurt you — I'm only telling you because I care," you have built the same fence. *Mā… illā.* You have knocked down every motive but one.

Now look at what Allah builds:

> مَا ضَرَبُوهُ لَكَ إِلَّا جَدَلًۢا
> *Mā ḍarabūhu laka illā jadalan*
> "They did not strike it for you — except as jadal."

Knocked down: every honest motive. Left standing: **jadal**, and only jadal.

[PAUSE]

So everything now hangs on this one word, *jadal*. And here is where we have to slow all the way down, because this word does not mean what the English "argument" makes you think it means.

Notice first the verb: **ḍarabū** — from the root *ḍ-r-b*, "to strike." In Arabic you do not "make" an example or "give" an analogy; idiomatically you ***strike*** it — *ḍaraba mathalan.* As Jalālayn notes, the *hu*, the "it," refers back to the example, the likeness. So even the verb has a faint violence in it. They *struck* this comparison at you, Muhammad. It was thrown.

But *jadal* — *jadal* is the heart of it.

Go back to the root *j-d-l* in its oldest, most physical life, before it ever meant a debate. Al-Rāghib al-Aṣfahānī, in his *Mufradāt*, traces it to two concrete images, and I want you to hold both.

The first: **جَدَلْتُ الحَبْل** — *jadaltu al-ḥabl*, "I twisted the rope." Not loosely braided it. *Tightly* twisted it — *shiddat al-fatl*, twisting until the fibers bite into each other and the rope goes rigid. *Jadal*, at root, is the act of taking strands and **torquing them against each other** until they lock.

The second image is even more vivid. Al-Rāghib says *jadāla* is **the ground, the bare earth** — and *jadaltuhu* means *"I threw him down onto the* jadāla*"* — I wrestled him to the dirt. *Jadal*, from this angle, is a **wrestling match.** Two men, gripping, each straining to throw the other flat onto his back in the dust.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the verse with those roots restored:

*They did not strike this comparison at you, Muhammad, except as a **wrestling-throw.** Except as a **rope twisted tight to bind you.***

Do you feel the difference? The English "argument" is a cool, intellectual word. It suggests two minds examining a proposition. But *jadal* is not a meeting of minds. It is a **grappling of bodies.** The aim of a wrestler is not to understand his opponent. It is to **put him on the ground.** The aim of twisting a rope is not to find truth; it is to make something rigid enough to *tie another person up.*

This is what Allah reveals about their question. It had the *shape* of inquiry — *"Are our gods better, or is he?"* — but its *purpose* was a throw-down. They did not want to *know* whether their gods were better. They wanted to watch the Prophet ﷺ hit the dirt. The question was a hold. The question was a noose.

And notice — the *mā… illā* fence makes this total. Not "partly a wrestle." Not "a question that got a little heated." **Nothing but** the wrestle. There was no sincere strand mixed in to twist. The whole rope was contention from the first fiber.

What this one word reveals: **a question can wear the face of seeking while its entire body is the will to win.**

### Element Two: *Bal hum qawmun khaṣimūn* — From an Act to a Self

We move now to the second half of the verse, and Allah does something with it that I need you to watch carefully, because it is a single small word that swings the whole door.

The word is **بَلْ** (*bal*).

Let me teach it first. *Bal* is what grammarians call a particle of **iḍrāb** — a word of *turning away from* and *rising above.* It says: *set aside what I just told you; here is the truer, larger thing.* It is the conversational equivalent of a man stepping back from a small painting to show you it was only one corner of a mural.

You use it without thinking. "He didn't just forget to call — *no*, he never cared in the first place." That little *"no"* — that pivot from the small fact to the large truth behind it — that is *bal.*

Now watch where Allah's *bal* takes us.

The first half of the verse described an **action**: *mā ḍarabūhu illā jadalan* — "they did not present it except as a wrestle." That is a sentence about a *thing they did.* A verb. A single maneuver in a single moment.

Then comes *bal*, and the camera pulls all the way back:

> بَلْ هُمْ قَوْمٌ خَصِمُونَ
> *Bal hum qawmun khaṣimūn*
> "Nay — they are a **people**, contentious **ones.**"

[PAUSE]

Feel the shift. We moved from **a verb to a noun.** From *something they did* to *something they are.* From an act of contention to a *category of person.* Allah is saying: do not mistake this for a one-time stunt. This question was not an anomaly that escaped from otherwise sincere people. *Rather* — *bal* — contention is what they **are made of.** The argument was not an event in their lives. It was an expression of their nature.

And now look at the word He chooses to name that nature: **خَصِمُونَ** (*khaṣimūn*).

The root is *kh-ṣ-m*, dispute, adversariness. But Allah does not use the word for "someone who disputed" — that would be *khāṣama,* a person who had an argument. He uses the form **خَصِم** (*khaṣim*), and this form matters enormously.

In Arabic, the pattern **فَعِل** (*faʿil*) — like *khaṣim* — is not the form for a passing action. It is the form for an **ingrained, settled, defining trait.** It is the same family of pattern that gives you *ḥadhir* (someone cautious *by nature*), *fariḥ* (someone joyful *as a disposition*). When you put a quality into this mold, you are no longer describing what someone *did.* You are describing **what someone is at the root.** This is a *thubūt* word — a word of permanence.

So *khaṣim* does not mean "a person who argued." It means **a person for whom arguing is the grain of the wood.** Cut them anywhere, and the cut runs through contention. They do not *have* disputes. They *are* disputers — the way a sharp thing is sharp, the way a wet thing is wet.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the architecture of what Allah has just built across these two halves:

**First half:** Here is the act — pure contention, nothing but the wrestle.
**Bal —**
**Second half:** And do not think the act was a stranger to them. The act came up out of the soil of *who they are.* They are a people; contention is their disposition.

This is the anatomy of how a sin becomes a self. A maneuver, repeated, sinks down through the layers until it stops being something you do and becomes something you are. The *bal* is the sound of that descent being named — the moment Allah stops describing their *behavior* and starts describing their *character.*

What these words reveal together: **an argument made often enough stops being a thing you pick up and becomes a thing you are made of.**

### Element Three: The Question With No Answer In It — *Am huwa*

There is a third thing here, quieter than the others, and it lives in an absence. Read as carefully for what is *missing* as for what is *said.*

Look at their question again:

> أَءَالِهَتُنَا خَيْرٌ أَمْ هُوَ
> *A-ālihatunā khayrun am huwa?*
> "Are our gods better — or **he**?"

First, that opening hamza — أَ — the questioning particle. I want you to notice that in Arabic, as in every language, the *shape* of a question can be borrowed for things that are not questions at all. When a parent says, "Is *this* how you speak to me?" — that hamza is not requesting information. It is a rebuke wearing a question's clothes. The grammarians call this the interrogative used for *taqrīr* or for mockery. Their *"Are our gods better?"* is exactly this: not a request to be taught, but a sneer dressed as inquiry.

But here is the absence I want you to feel. Look at the word **هُوَ** — *huwa,* "he." *Or **he**.* Who is *he*?

The verse does not say.

And the commentators, reading carefully, split on it — and the *split itself* is the lesson. Al-Suddī and Ibn Zayd, as al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī report, say *huwa* means **ʿĪsā** — *are our gods better, or is the son of Maryam, whom you've consigned to the Fire?* But Qatāda, also reported by both, says *huwa* means **Muḥammad** ﷺ — *are our gods better, or is this man telling us to abandon them?*

Now, a careless reader treats this as a problem to solve — *which one did they mean?* But stay with me. **Watch what the ambiguity is telling you.**

The target is interchangeable. It can be ʿĪsā. It can be Muḥammad. It barely matters — and *that is the point.* When a question is sincere, the object is precise, because you actually want to know *that specific thing.* But when a question is only a wrestling-hold, **the opponent is generic.** Any body will do for the throw. The *huwa* is left blurry because, to them, *who* they were knocking down was a detail. *That* they were knocking someone down was the whole event.

[PAUSE]

Do you see how the ambiguity confirms the *jadal*? A truth-seeker's question narrows toward its object like a key toward a lock. A contender's question stays wide, because it isn't reaching for an answer — it's reaching for a victim. The unspecified *huwa* is the linguistic fingerprint of a heart that has stopped seeking and started swinging.

What this absence reveals: **when you are arguing to win rather than to know, even the thing you are arguing about goes blurry — because it was never really the point.**

### The Bridge

Step back and see what these three discoveries have shown us, working together.

The *mā… illā* fence told us their purpose was **nothing but** the wrestle. The root of *jadal* showed us that wrestle is a *throw-down,* a rope torqued tight to bind. The *bal* and *khaṣim* showed us this wasn't a stray act but the **grain of their character.** And the blurry *huwa* showed us the unmistakable signature of a question that has no real interest in its own answer.

This is not, in the end, a verse about Quraysh and a clever theological objection in a Makkan street.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about their words — but about *reality itself,* and about the architecture of every human heart, including the one beating in your own chest right now.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Two Kinds of Question

I want to begin not in the seventh century but in your own week.

Think of the last real disagreement you were in. A spouse. A sibling. A coworker. Someone online. Now ask yourself, honestly: in that exchange, were you trying to *find out what was true* — or were you trying to *win*?

Because here is something psychologists have a clean name for, and it maps onto our ayah with almost unsettling precision. They call it **motivated reasoning.** It describes what happens when the mind is not actually searching for the truth but defending a conclusion it has *already* reached, and bending every fact toward that conclusion. The tell of motivated reasoning is this: the person *looks* like they're thinking, but they are not *open.* No evidence could change their mind, because their mind was never the thing in play. Their *standing* was.

And there is a darker, sharper version of it that lives online now, and you have seen it a hundred times. The bad-faith question. *"I'm just asking questions."* The reply that is shaped like curiosity but built like a trap. The person who isn't confused — they're *hunting.* They post the question, and then they wait, not to learn, but to **pounce** on your answer. We have a dozen slang words for it now because it is so common. The Quran had one word for it fourteen hundred years ago.

**Jadal.**

[PAUSE]

The Quran does something remarkable: it does not condemn *all* disagreement. This is crucial, and it keeps us honest. In Sūrat al-ʿAnkabūt, Allah commands the believers to *"argue with the People of the Book"* — same family of meaning — *"in the way that is best"* (29:46). In Sūrat an-Naḥl: *"argue with them in the best manner"* (16:125). So the act of contending, of going back and forth, is not in itself the disease. Allah *commands* a form of it.

So what is the line? What turns a sanctioned, beautiful exchange into the *jadal* that Allah exposes in our ayah?

It is not the *heat* of the words. It is the **direction of the heart.**

A sincere question is a hand held open, asking to *receive.* A *jadal* question is a hand closed into a grip, reaching to *throw.* One says, *show me, I might be wrong.* The other says, *I dare you to make me change.* The first is willing to lose the argument in order to win the truth. The second would rather lose the truth than lose the argument.

And here is the verse that completes the circle. In Sūrat al-Kahf, Allah says of our entire species: **وَكَانَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ أَكْثَرَ شَىْءٍ جَدَلًا** — *"Man is, more than anything else, given to* jadal*"* (18:54). The most contentious thing in creation. Not the most loving, not the most thoughtful — the most *argumentative.* So when we read our ayah about Quraysh, we are not reading about *them.* We are reading the mirror. *Akthara shayʾin jadalā* — the most argumentative of all things — and you are a member of that species.

[PAUSE]

So here is the application, and it is uncomfortable on purpose. The next time you are about to "make a point," stop and run one test on your own heart: **Would I be relieved or disappointed to discover I was wrong?**

If the honest answer is *disappointed* — if some part of you would rather be *right* than *corrected* — then you have just caught yourself in *jadal,* and you have caught it before it left your mouth. That pause, that one question turned inward, is the difference between a tongue that seeks and a tongue that wrestles.

### Theme Two: When the Wrestle Becomes the Man

Now I want to take you to the more frightening half, the *bal,* because this is where the ayah stops describing a behavior and starts describing a destiny.

Let me start somewhere ordinary. Have you ever met someone — really, picture them — who simply *cannot* receive anything? You give them good news; they find the flaw in it. You offer a kindness; they question the motive. You state the sky is blue; they want to relitigate the definition of blue. Every statement, no matter how warm or true, arrives at their door and is immediately processed not as something to *consider* but as something to **counter.**

That person was not born that way. **They built it.** One argument at a time.

This is the terrifying machinery of *bal hum qawmun khaṣimūn.* Remember what we found: *khaṣim* is not the word for someone who *argued.* It is the word for someone in whom contention has become the **grain of the wood.** And the question that should keep us awake is: *how does a person get there?* How does *khāṣama* — a single dispute — harden into *khaṣim,* a contentious *nature*?

The answer is the most ordinary thing in the world. **Repetition.** You take a posture often enough, and the posture stops being something you adopt and becomes the shape your spine has set into. The first time you argue to win instead of to learn, it is a choice. The hundredth time, it is a habit. The thousandth time, it is a *self.* And one day you wake up and you are no longer a person who sometimes argues. You are an *arguer,* the way some people are *cynics* — not because they decided to be this morning, but because every morning for years they practiced being it until practice became identity.

[PAUSE]

And here is the spiritual catastrophe hidden inside this, the thing that makes it not just a personality flaw but a barrier to **guidance itself.**

Think about what guidance *requires.* To be guided, you must be able to *receive.* You must have an open hand. Hidāyah is something you *take in.* But the *khaṣim* — the one who has made contention his nature — has trained his every faculty to do the *opposite* of receiving. He has built, over a lifetime, a reflex that converts everything incoming into something to *fight.* So when the truth finally arrives at his door — even the clearest, most luminous truth, even *"are your gods better, or him?"* answered plainly — it cannot get in. Not because the truth is weak. Because the door only knows how to slam.

This is why Allah seals our verse the way He does. Look at the order one more time. He does *not* say "they are a contentious people, *therefore* they argued." He says: *they argued — bal — they are a contentious people.* The contention came *first* in their nature; the argument was just it surfacing. Their *jadal* in the street was a *symptom.* The disease was who they had become. And a people who have made adversariness their identity cannot be reached by a better argument, because **the problem was never the argument. The problem is that they have made themselves into something that cannot stop arguing.**

[PAUSE]

So what do we *do* with this? Here is the application, and it is gentler than the warning, because Allah never shows us a pit without showing us the path around it.

It is this: **guard the small postures, because they are voting on who you become.** Every time you choose, in a low-stakes moment, to receive rather than to counter — to say "you might be right" and *mean* it, to let someone be correct without needing to add your amendment, to ask a question you actually want answered — you are casting a vote *against* becoming *khaṣim.* You are keeping the hand open. You are keeping the door able to swing inward. None of these moments feels important. *That is exactly why they are.* Character is not built in the big confrontations. It is built in the thousand tiny ones where nobody is watching and you choose, one more time, to seek instead of to wrestle.

The contentious person was made one argument at a time. **So is the humble one.**

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this verse are really one insight seen from two distances.

Up close, the verse shows you an **act**: a question that was nothing but a wrestling-throw — *mā ḍarabūhu illā jadalan.* Pull back, and the verse shows you a **self**: a people whose every grain runs to contention — *bal hum qawmun khaṣimūn.* And the *bal* between them is the hinge — the place where Allah lets you watch a *behavior* become a *being.*

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey turn out to be the same discovery approached from two doors. The *jadal* root — the twisted rope, the wrestle in the dirt — *is* motivated reasoning given a body. The *khaṣim* form — the trait that has set like cooling iron — *is* the lifetime of small postures that hardened into a self. The grammar was never separate from the soul. The grammar *was* the soul, written down.

And the whole thing rests on a single, quiet horror that should make every one of us more careful with our own mouths: **a question can be perfectly shaped and completely empty.** It can have the hamza of inquiry and the structure of comparison and the *sound* of someone seeking — and underneath, be nothing but a hand reaching to throw you down.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them genuinely open.

1. In my last real disagreement — was I trying to *find* the truth, or trying to *win* the exchange? How do I honestly know which?
2. When someone corrects me, what is my *first* reflex — to consider, or to counter? And which reflex have I been *practicing*?
3. Is there a question I keep "asking" that I do not actually want answered — because the asking lets me keep fighting?
4. Am I, in any corner of my life, slowly becoming a *khaṣim* — someone for whom every incoming truth is just material for a rebuttal?
5. What would it cost me, today, to lose one argument on purpose in order to keep my hand open?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**A question asked to win is not a question at all — and when winning becomes who you are, even the clearest truth arrives only as something to fight.**

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not asking you to stop disagreeing. Allah Himself commands the better kind of argument. It is asking you to *watch the direction of your own heart* in the moment before you speak — and to notice that every small choice between *seeking* and *wrestling* is quietly building the person you will be when guidance finally knocks. Let the invitation be simple: keep the hand open. Keep the door able to swing inward. Choose, in the small unwatched moments, to be the kind of person truth can still reach.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, You who are al-Ḥaqq, the Truth itself — keep my questions honest and my heart open. Do not let me become someone who argues to win when You are offering me the chance to be corrected. Make my hand a hand that receives, and let the truth, whenever it knocks, always find a door in me that still swings inward.*

*Āmīn.*
