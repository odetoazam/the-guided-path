# The One Who Owns the Where, Holds the When, and Receives the Return

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Listen to it before we touch a single word of it.

> **وَتَبَارَكَ ٱلَّذِى لَهُۥ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا وَعِندَهُۥ عِلْمُ ٱلسَّاعَةِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ**

*Wa-tabāraka alladhī lahu mulku as-samāwāti wal-arḍi wa mā baynahumā, wa ʿindahu ʿilmu as-sāʿah, wa ilayhi turjaʿūn.*

**"And blessed is the One to Whom belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth and whatever is between them; and with Him is the knowledge of the Hour; and to Him you will be returned."**

Now — I want you to feel where we are when these words land.

For most of Surah Az-Zukhruf, the air has been thick with argument. The people of Makkah have been making claims about God. They've said the angels are His daughters. They've said that if the Most Merciful had willed, they wouldn't have worshipped these intermediaries — turning their idolatry into a kind of theological excuse. They've dragged ʿĪsā, the son of Maryam, into the dispute, using him as a debating point. The whole surah has been the sound of human beings trying to crowd the throne of God with partners, relatives, intermediaries, committee members. Trying to make the Divine smaller, more manageable, more like the tribal world they understood — a chief surrounded by lesser figures who could be lobbied and petitioned.

And then, after all of that noise, after every false claim has been answered and set down — the surah does something. It stops arguing. It simply *declares*.

It's like a courtroom that has been full of shouting, claim and counter-claim, and then a single voice rises and the room falls silent — and that voice doesn't argue anymore. It just names what is true. **Blessed is the One.** Not "blessed are the ones." Not "blessed is the One and His associates." The One. Alone. To Whom belongs everything that exists, Who alone knows the appointed end, and to Whom every single one of you is going back.

Here is what I want you to wonder about as we begin.

This ayah names three things, in a deliberate order: **what God owns, what God knows, and where you are going.** And underneath those three, there is a single word — the first word — that the Arabic reserves almost exclusively for God, a word that does not mean what you probably think it means. We say it constantly. *Tabāraka. Barakah.* We ask for it over food, over money, over marriage, over children. But what if the thing we're actually asking for is not "more"? What if barakah is the precise opposite of the thing our entire age is starving for and chasing in the wrong direction?

Stay with me. Let's open it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tabāraka* — the word built like an overflow

Before we go to the word in the ayah, let me teach you a piece of how Arabic verbs work, because the whole insight hangs on it.

Arabic doesn't just have words. It has *patterns*. A three-letter root gets poured into a mould, and the mould itself adds meaning before the root says anything. One of those moulds is what scholars call **Form VI** — the *tafāʿala* pattern. You hear it in words like *tarāsala* (they corresponded back and forth with each other), *taʿāwana* (they cooperated, mutually), *tabādala* (they exchanged with one another). The signature of Form VI is usually *reciprocity and sustained, mutual, ongoing action* — something that goes back and forth, that keeps happening, that builds on itself.

Now hold that mould in your mind, and let's bring in the root.

The root of our first word is **ب-ر-ك (b-r-k)**. And here is something beautiful that the classical lexicons preserve. The same root gives us the word *birka* — a pool, a reservoir, a body of water that gathers and stays. And it gives us the image of the camel that does *bark* — that kneels down and settles, lowering itself to the ground and *staying put*, becoming firm and immovable. So buried in this root are two pictures at once: **water that gathers and abides**, and **something that settles in and becomes fixed, permanent, unshakeable.**

Barakah, then, in its root-soil, is not "a little extra." It is *abundance that stays.* Goodness that gathers like water in a reservoir and does not drain away. Good that settles in and takes up permanent residence.

Now pour that root — gathering, abiding, settling, fixed — into the Form VI mould, the mould of ongoing, intensifying, self-multiplying action.

**تَبَارَكَ.**

[PAUSE]

Al-Qurtubi tells us plainly: *tabāraka* is *tafāʿul* from *al-barakah* — it is the Form VI of blessing. And what does that pattern do to the meaning? Al-Muyassar unfolds it for us: *wa takātharat barakatu-Llāh* — "the blessedness of Allah has multiplied and multiplied," *wa kathura khayruhu* — "His good has become abundant beyond counting," *wa ʿaẓuma mulkuhu* — "and His dominion is immense." Al-Jalalayn keeps it to one word: *taʿaẓẓama* — "He is exalted beyond measure."

So feel what the form is doing. This isn't "God is blessed" as a flat statement. The very *shape* of the word is an overflow — a goodness that pours and gathers and abides and increases, an abundance that is its own inexhaustible source, water filling a reservoir that has no bottom and no leak.

And here is the detail that should stop you. **This verb, in this form, is used in the Qur'an only of Allah.** You will never find *tabāraka* applied to a human being, an angel, a prophet, a king. Created things can *have* barakah — placed into them, lent to them, flowing through them. The night of Laylat al-Qadr is *mubāraka* — blessed, made-blessed, a passive recipient. But *tabāraka*, the self-originating overflow — that belongs to one Being. **The source of all abundance cannot be a recipient of it.** A reservoir can be filled. The spring that feeds every reservoir is filled by no one.

Notice what this means for where the ayah has placed it. The surah has just spent its length refuting people who wanted to attach partners to God. And the answer opens not with "there is no partner" — it opens with *tabāraka*. As if to say: the very category error you are making is laughable. You are trying to add to the One whose defining attribute is that He is the unaddable source — the One from whom all addition flows. You cannot give water to the sea.

What does this single word reveal? That before God owns anything or knows anything, He simply *overflows*. Existence itself is downstream of Him.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *ʿindahu ʿilmu as-sāʿah* — the one drawer that stays locked

Now move with me to the middle of the ayah, because something shifts here, and the shift is the whole point.

Look at the structure of what He owns: *mulku as-samāwāti wal-arḍi wa mā baynahumā* — "the dominion of the heavens and the earth, and whatever is between them." Notice how *total* that is. Not just the heavens. Not just the earth. **And whatever is between them.** Arabic could have said "the heavens and the earth" and we'd have understood "everything." But it adds *wa mā baynahumā* — and the in-between. The gaps. The space you didn't think to count. There is no crack in creation that is unowned. The inventory is exhaustive. He owns the *where* — completely.

Classical commentators reach for even stronger language when they unpack this totality. Al-Tabari renders *mulk* not merely as "dominion" in the sense of title-holding, but as *sulṭān* — active authority, sovereignty-in-motion. His *ḥukm*, His decree, runs over all of it; His *qaḍāʾ*, His judgment, is already passing through everything He owns right now. Al-Muyassar extends the scope further: the heavens and the earth He possesses are the seven heavens and the seven earths — *as-samāwāt as-sabʿ wal-arāḍīn as-sabʿ* — and every thing between them and within them. No register of creation, visible or hidden, falls outside this compass. And al-Tabari then draws out the anti-shirk logic this ownership generates, in a question that cuts to the bone: *fa-kayfa yakūnu lahu sharīkan man kāna fī sulṭānihi wa ḥukmuhu fīhi nāfidhun* — "How can one who lives *inside* His authority, whose every affair is governed by His running decree, be posited as a partner to the source of that decree?" A being that exists within God's sovereignty cannot coherently be called its co-holder. The dominion claim alone — stated plainly, without argument — is the complete refutation of every association.

And then, immediately, comes a different kind of phrase: **وَعِندَهُۥ عِلْمُ ٱلسَّاعَةِ** — *wa ʿindahu ʿilmu as-sāʿah* — "and with Him is the knowledge of the Hour."

I want you to notice the word *ʿinda*. It's a small word — a locative, meaning "with," "at," "in the presence of." But Arabic word order is doing something deliberate here. It doesn't say *ʿilmu as-sāʿati ʿindahu* — "the knowledge of the Hour is with Him." It front-loads it: *wa ʿindahu* — "**and with Him** is the knowledge of the Hour." When Arabic moves a word forward like that, ahead of where you'd expect it, it is doing the work that in English we'd do with the voice — leaning on it, pressing it. *With Him* — and the unspoken second half rings in the silence: *and with no one else.*

This is the grammar of exclusivity. Al-Jalalayn glosses *ʿilmu as-sāʿah* as *matā taqūm* — "**when** it will come to pass." Not *that* it will come — everyone in this surah is being told *that* it will come. The withheld thing is the *when*.

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you the architecture, because it is staggering once you see it.

He has just declared that He owns *all of space* — every inch, every gap, the seven heavens, the seven earths, the in-between. Total, exhaustive, nothing held back. He shows you the entire map of *where*.

And then He comes to *time* — specifically to the one moment that hinges all of time, the Hour — and He does the exact opposite. He does not hand it to you. He locks it. *ʿindahu* — it stays with Him.

Think of it like this. Imagine being given a tour of an immense estate by its owner. He throws open every door. Every room, every wing, the cellars, the towers, the grounds, the gardens, the space between the buildings — "all of it, look, all of it is mine, and I am showing you all of it." And then you come to one small drawer. And he puts his hand on it and says: *this one stays closed. This one is with me.*

That is the shape of this ayah. **Infinite generosity with space. Absolute reserve with one piece of time.**

And here is what you must read in the *absence* — because the gap is where the meaning lives. He told you He owns everything. He could have, by the same logic, told you He knows the time of everything, including the Hour's arrival. Knowledge of His own dominion would seem to be the smallest of His attributes. But He deliberately marks the Hour as the thing kept *ʿindahu* — with Him. Elsewhere the Qur'an makes this explicit: *inna-Llāha ʿindahu ʿilmu as-sāʿah* (31:34) — the very same construction — placed among the things "no soul knows." The timing of the end is not withheld because it is small. It is withheld because giving it to you would *ruin* you.

Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both spell out what the Hour actually encompasses: it is the moment when creation is gathered *from their graves* — *min qubūrihim* — for the *mawqif al-ḥisāb*, the standing place of the reckoning. What God holds withheld is not merely a date on a cosmic calendar. It is the precise timing of the day when graves open, when every hidden act stands disclosed, when every soul is gathered to account for what it did. No wonder no other soul is given access to this knowledge. The knowledge of *when* everything will be exposed is the last thing any of us could handle in advance.

Sit with that. If you knew the date, you would game it. You would live recklessly until the year before and then perform a frantic, calculated repentance — and it would be worthless, because it would be the repentance of someone cornered, not someone who loves. The hiddenness of the Hour is what makes every single day a real test of who you actually are. **The locked drawer is not a wall against you. It is mercy toward you.** He withholds the *when* so that your *now* can be sincere.

What does *ʿindahu* reveal? That there is exactly one thing in all of existence that the Owner of everything chooses to keep in His own hand and place in no other — and that He keeps it there *for* you, not *from* you.

### Element Three: *turjaʿūn* — the verb that turns and faces you

Now look at the last two words, and watch what happens grammatically, because the ayah does something with *direction of address* that you can feel even before you can name it.

For the whole ayah up to now, Allah has been spoken about in the *third person*. *Alladhī* — "the One Who." *Lahu* — "to Whom." *ʿindahu* — "with Him." We have been listening to a description, a portrait held up before us. God is being talked *about*.

And then the final word: **تُرْجَعُونَ** — *turjaʿūn* — "**you** will be returned."

Did you feel the camera swing? Suddenly it is not "they" and not "Him" — it is *you*. The verb is second person plural. The whole majestic description of the cosmic Owner has been a slow zoom outward — heavens, earth, the in-between, all of time — and then in one word it snaps back and points directly at your chest. *And to Him — you — are going back.* The classical readers preserve both registers, in fact: Ibn Kathīr, Ḥamza, and al-Kisāʾī read it *yarjaʿūn*, "**they** will be returned," keeping the cosmic third person to the end; the others read *turjaʿūn*, "**you**," turning to face the listener. Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both record this difference, and al-Jalalayn simply notes *bi-l-yāʾ wal-tāʾ* — "with yā and with tā" — preserving both readings without resolving between them. The very existence of the two readings tells you what this final word is built to do — to turn the abstract grandeur into something personal. One reading keeps you watching the cosmos; the other makes the cosmos turn and look at you.

But there is a deeper thing in this verb, and it's in the *voice*.

Teach yourself this distinction first, in plain English. There's a difference between "I return" and "I am returned." "I return" — I'm the one doing it, on my own steam, by my own choice, in my own time. "I am returned" — something is bringing me back, whether I planned it or not. The first is active. The second is passive.

**تُرْجَعُونَ is passive.** It is not *tarjiʿūn* — "you will return." It is *turjaʿūn* — "**you will be returned.**" You are not the one making the journey home as a free agent choosing a destination. You are being *brought*.

[PAUSE]

Feel the weight of that. Think of how we imagine death and the afterlife — we picture ourselves "going to God," as if it were a trip we take. But the Arabic strips that illusion. You don't go. *You are returned.* Like the tide that doesn't decide to come back to shore — it is pulled. Like a river that doesn't choose the sea — it bends to it by the shape of the ground itself. The return is not optional and it is not your initiative. It is built into the architecture of existence. Every soul that has ever drawn breath is being returned — *turjaʿūn* applies to all of you, every human being present or future, collectively passive before this single directing force.

And then there is the preposition. Not just *turjaʿūn* — "you will be returned" — but *ilayhi turjaʿūn* — "**to Him** you will be returned." The destination is front-loaded, just as *ʿindahu* was front-loaded. The return point comes before the returning, because the destination is the whole point. And the destination is the same Being described in the first two declarations: the One who overflows with abundance, who owns every inch of creation and whose decree runs actively through all of it, who holds the knowledge of the Hour in His hand alone. You are being returned to *that One*. Not to a vague afterlife, not to dissolution, not to some impersonal accounting mechanism. To *Him*.

Al-Muyassar draws out what the return is *for*: *fa-yujāzī kullan bi-mā yastaḥiqq* — "He will recompense each one according to what they deserve." The return is not homecoming in a diffuse sense. It is return for accounting. You are being brought back to the One who owns everything and knows everything — the only Being in existence who has the complete picture. He missed nothing. He forgets nothing. He withheld knowledge of the Hour from you, but He withheld nothing from Himself. Every moment of your life that you thought no one was watching was inside His world, under His running decree, heading toward His reckoning. The passive voice of *turjaʿūn* now carries a second weight: not only are you not the agent of your return, you are not the judge of it either. That, too, belongs entirely to Him.

What does *turjaʿūn* reveal? That the architecture of existence has a direction. Everything in the heavens and the earth, everything between them, every soul that passes through any of it — all of it is bending, like a river, toward the One it came from.

## PART 2: THE THREE DECLARATIONS

Stand back from the ayah now and feel its shape as a whole.

It opens with *tabāraka* — an overflow so complete it cannot receive, only give. Then it names what that overflowing Being *possesses*: dominion over every inch of the seven heavens and seven earths and the space between — sovereignty whose decree is already in motion through all of it. Then it names the one thing He *withholds*: the knowledge of when it all ends, the timing of the day when graves open and every soul stands to be recompensed. And then it names where you are *going*: back to Him, passively, by force of the same architecture that governs everything else.

Three declarations. Three coordinates. Together they fix your existential position more precisely than anything a philosophical system could manage. You are *inside* His ownership, with no outside to retreat to. You are *without access* to the one piece of knowledge that would let you plan around the end. And you are *aimed at Him* whether you have oriented yourself that way or not.

This is not a list of theological facts arranged for completeness. It is a *map* of your situation, spoken precisely because the people in this surah had lost the map. They had crowded the throne with associates. They had decided they could manage their relationship with the Divine through intermediaries — lesser figures who could be lobbied, petitioned, used as a buffer between themselves and the full weight of God. They had, in effect, tried to make the situation smaller and more manageable: a chief surrounded by a council, not a God whose authority runs through all seven heavens and whose decree governs every moment including the last one.

And the ayah responds not by winning the argument but by simply *restoring the picture*. Here is what is actually true. Here is what has always been true. The picture is this large. The dominion is this total. The knowledge you lack is this decisive. The return is this certain.

Al-Tabari frames the anti-shirk logic that lives inside this picture with a question that, once you hear it, you cannot unhear: how can one who is *inside* God's authority — whose affairs are already governed by His running decree — be posited as a partner to the source of that decree? A being that God owns, that exists within God's dominion, that is itself heading back to God in the passive return — that being cannot be God's partner. It can only be a fellow traveler in the same direction you are going. And you do not petition a fellow traveler for what only the destination can give.

This is why the ayah begins with *tabāraka* before it says anything else. Before the ownership, before the knowledge, before the return — *He overflows*. The note struck first is not authority or power or judgment. It is *abundance*. The One you are inside of, the One who holds what you cannot hold, the One to Whom you are returning — is the Source of all good. Not a hostile sovereign. Not a distant administrator. The overflow-of-all-good, whose dominion is the shape of the world you live in, and whose receiving of you is the shape of the life you are living.

[PAUSE]

Come back, now, to the question I asked you at the very beginning.

We say *tabāraka* and *barakah* constantly. We ask for barakah in our food, our money, our time, our children. And I asked you: what if we're chasing the wrong thing?

Here is what the word tells you when you trace it back to its root-soil — to the reservoir that gathers and stays, to the camel that settles and does not move.

Our age craves *more*. More certainty. More control. More advance knowledge of how things will turn out. More time. More. And when we ask for barakah we often mean the same thing, only dressed in religious language. *Give me more.* But the word *tabāraka*, used only of God, marks the *source* of all addition. It is not the word of something that receives more. It is the word of the One from whom more flows. And what the ayah is doing — by opening with *tabāraka* and then naming what God possesses, withholds, and receives — is pointing you to the actual address of abundance.

It is not in accumulation. It is not in certainty about the future. It is not in having the locked drawer opened for you. It is *at Him*. The barakah you are looking for lives at the same address as the dominion over all space, the withheld knowledge of the Hour, and the destination of the return. Every tributary of good in your life traces back upstream to that source. And you can spend your life collecting water from the tributaries, or you can orient yourself toward the spring.

The ayah ends with *turjaʿūn* — you are being returned. Which means the orientation is, in a certain sense, already set. The question is whether you spend your journey turned *toward* the One you are going back to, or turned away — arriving, as everyone must, but arriving having spent the journey looking the wrong direction.

---

And so the three declarations resolve into a single invitation.

The One who owns the *where* — every heaven, every earth, every gap between them, every inch of the world you live in, His decree already running through all of it: He is not distant from you. You are inside His estate.

The One who holds the *when* — the single piece of knowledge that would let you calculate and strategize and manage the end: He kept it from you not to diminish you but to free you. So that today can be real. So that every day of your life is a genuine question of who you actually are, not a performance timed to a deadline you know in advance.

The One who receives the *return* — to Whom every passive, inevitable, ungameable homecoming leads, Who will recompense each soul exactly according to what it deserves: He is *tabāraka*. He is the overflow. The One you are going back to is not a ledger waiting to tally debts against a frightened soul. He is the spring that filled every good thing you ever tasted.

This is what the doxological summit of Surah Az-Zukhruf is saying, at the top of its voice, to a people who had cluttered the horizon with lesser things.

There is only One.

He overflows.

He owns everything.

He holds the one thing you cannot.

And you are going back to Him.
