# Turn Your Face Gently: The Final Word of the Surah of Gold

> فَٱصْفَحْ عَنْهُمْ وَقُلْ سَلَـٰمٌ‌ۚ فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ
>
> *Faṣfaḥ ʿanhum wa qul salām, fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn*
>
> **"So turn away from them and say, 'Peace.' But soon they will know."**
> — Surah Az-Zukhruf 43:89

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

I want you to stand at the end of a long argument.

Not a shouting match — a *long* argument. The kind that has gone on for years. You have brought every piece of evidence you have. You have been patient. You have been gentle. You have answered the same objection forty times in forty different ways. And the person across from you has not moved an inch. Worse — they have started to *enjoy* not moving. Your sincerity has become their entertainment. They mock the very thing you are trying to save them from.

That is where this surah ends.

Surah Az-Zukhruf is named after **gold** — *zukhruf*, ornament, the glittering surface of the world. From its opening it confronts a people in Makkah who had three diseases of the heart. They worshipped their **wealth** — "Why was this Qur'an not sent down to some great man of the two cities?" they asked, as if revelation should follow money. They worshipped their **ancestry** — "We found our fathers upon a way, and we are following their footsteps." And they worshipped their own **mockery** — when the example of Jesus, son of Mary, was raised, the surah tells us, "your people laughed and turned away."

For eighty-eight verses the surah reasons with them. It shows them the heavens, the cattle, the ships, the rain that revives a dead land. It tells them stories — Ibrahim, Musa, Isa. It warns them of a Day when the screaming regret will come too late, when "friends on that Day will be enemies to one another, except the God-conscious." It does everything that argument can do.

And then, at the very last breath of the surah, Allah turns to His Messenger ﷺ — exhausted, mocked, rejected by his own people — and gives him not a new argument, but an **exit**.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what should stop you.

When a cause is just and the opponent is stubborn, every instinct in us screams for one of two endings: *crush them*, or *convince them*. Win the fight, or win the argument. Get the last word. Make them admit you were right.

Allah gives His Prophet ﷺ neither.

He gives him six words. *Turn away. Say: peace. They will soon know.*

And here is something the classical commentators take care to preserve: these six words did not arrive in the abstract. Al-Tabari frames this entire closing ayah as Allah's *jawāb* — His **answer** — to the Prophet's own words. For the Prophet ﷺ had turned to his Lord, as the surah itself implies, with a cry from the heart: *"O Lord, these are a people who will not believe."* Al-Muyassar describes him as *shākiyan ilā rabbih* — **complaining to his Lord** about those who had rejected him. The six words of the closing ayah are not merely a directive handed from above. They are *a response to grief*. Allah heard His Prophet's exhaustion and answered with a path: *here is how you carry this without being crushed by it.*

This changes everything about how you receive the command. *Iṣfaḥ ʿanhum* is not cold instruction delivered to a soldier. It is a consolation given to a man who has been crying out to his Creator. The six words are simultaneously a command and a mercy — the way out of a room that the Prophet's own love for these people had made it hard for him to leave.

And inside those six words — in a single verb drawn from the human face, in a word of peace with no name attached to it, and in a sentence that ends with a verb whose object Allah deliberately **leaves out** — there is one of the most dignified, most psychologically complete instructions in the entire Qur'an for how a human being walks away from people who will not be reached.

Let's go slowly. There is more here than there appears to be.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Iṣfaḥ* — The Verb Made of a Cheek

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In Arabic, a great many abstract ideas are built out of the body and the physical world. The word for "to decide firmly" comes from "to knot a rope." The word for "to be patient" comes from a plant so bitter you could barely swallow it. The Arabs did not invent words for inner states out of thin air — they reached for something they could *see* and *touch* and let it carry the meaning.

So when Allah wants to tell the Prophet ﷺ how to leave these people, the word He chooses is not a neutral, abstract command. It is a word built out of a part of the body.

The word is **ٱصْفَحْ — *iṣfaḥ*.** "Turn away."

Now stay with me, because here is where it opens.

The root of this word — ص-ف-ح, *ṣād-fā-ḥā* — gives us the Arabic word **ṣafḥa**. And *ṣafḥa* means a flat, broad surface. It is the word for the **side of a thing**. The flat of a sword's blade is its *ṣafḥa*. The broad surface of a page in a book is its *ṣafḥa* — to this day, the word for "page" in Arabic is *ṣafḥa*.

And — here is the heart of it — the *ṣafḥa* of a man's face is his **cheek**. The side of the face.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what the verb is literally asking?

To do *ṣafḥ* to someone is to **turn the side of your face toward them.** To present them with your cheek instead of your full glare. Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt* both trace the word here: *al-ṣafḥ* is to turn away the *ṣafḥa* — the broad side — of the face, to overlook, to turn the page.

Let's sit with how different this is from the other word it could have used.

There is another word for pardon in the Qur'an: **ʿafw** — to wipe away, to erase. *ʿAfw* is internal: you delete the offense from your record. But *ṣafḥ* is something more. *Ṣafḥ* is **bodily**. It is what you do with your face, your posture, your whole orientation. You don't just forgive in your heart — you physically *turn*, you give them your side, you move on past them like turning a page in a book that you are choosing to keep reading rather than tear out.

Scholars of the language said exactly this: *al-ṣafḥ ablaghu min al-ʿafw* — **the turning-away is more complete than the mere pardon.** Because a man may pardon you in his heart while still glaring at you. But *ṣafḥ* is forgiveness that has reached the body and changed the way you stand.

Notice something else. There is no bitterness in turning a page. You don't turn a page in anger. You turn it because you are **done with that page and moving to the next.** The wound is not denied — it is simply no longer where you are living.

[PAUSE]

And now feel the *sound* of the word. *Iṣ-faḥ.* It opens with that whispered *ṣ* and closes on an open, breathed-out *ḥ* — *faaḥ*. Say it slowly: it is the sound of an **exhale**. The word for releasing someone is itself shaped like a breath let go. The Arabic does not just *mean* "release" — it *sounds* like release.

So before we go a single word further, look at what Allah has handed the most rejected man in Makkah. Not "ignore them" — that would leave the heart clenched. Not "defeat them" — that would chain him to them. But *iṣfaḥ*: **turn the side of your face, gently, the way you turn a page, and exhale.**

What this single verb reveals is that the believer's release is not a denial of the wound and not a war over it — it is a *bodily turning* that lets the soul stop living on the page that hurt it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Salām* — A Peace With No Name On It

Now the second word. And this one teaches by what it **leaves out**.

First, the concept. In Arabic, when you greet someone, you normally attach the greeting to *them*. *Salāmun ʿalaykum* — "peace be **upon you**." The peace has an address. It is sent *to* a specific person, like a letter with a name on the envelope. The "upon you" is what makes it warm, relational, a gift handed to that face in front of you.

Now look at exactly what Allah tells the Prophet ﷺ to say.

Not *salāmun ʿalaykum*. Just — **سَلَامٌ. *Salām.* "Peace."**

The word stands completely alone. There is no *ʿalaykum*. No "upon you." No name on the envelope. And grammatically, notice it is *salām-**un*** — **indefinite**, carrying that open *tanwīn* ending, a peace of no specified, narrowed kind. A bare word, set down and left.

Al-Tabari takes care to note the grammar here: *salām* is nominative with an implied *ʿalaykum* or *lakum* — "upon you" or "for you" — hovering behind it, structurally present but spoken away. That deliberate withholding of the address is not an accident of phrasing. It is the point.

Let's ask the question the grammar is forcing on us: **why strip off the "upon you"?**

Because this is not the warm greeting you give a brother. This is something the classical commentators named with great precision. Al-Baghawi says the meaning here is **al-mutāraka** — *the peace of parting.* The peace of disengagement. And he points you to its twin elsewhere in the Qur'an, where the righteous, when the ignorant address them, say: *"Salāmun ʿalaykum, lā nabtaghī al-jāhilīn"* — **"Peace be upon you; we seek not the ignorant"** (28:55). The same in *Sūrat al-Furqān*: the servants of the Most Merciful, when the ignorant address them, "say: peace" — *qālū salāmā* (25:63).

So this *salām* is not "I love you." It is **"I wish you no harm, and I am leaving."** It is the word you lay down on the table as you stand up to go. It draws a clean line: *I will not sink to your level, I will not trade insult for insult, I hold no malice — and this conversation is over.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what is so stunning about pairing this with the verb before it.

*Iṣfaḥ* — turn your face away. *Qul salām* — and say "peace." Notice that the turning-away is **not silent**. Allah does not say "turn away from them and say nothing." He could have. Silence would have been easier. Silence is what wounded pride usually chooses — the cold shoulder, the slammed door.

But *ṣafḥ* in this ayah is not a cold shoulder. As you turn the page, you leave a word on it: **peace.** You disengage, but you disengage *blessing them* rather than cursing them. You give them your side, not your back — and as you go, you give them a clean word, not a parting shot.

Think about how nearly impossible that is. The natural shape of leaving a fight is the last insult — the thing you say so that *you* feel you won the exit. Allah removes the last insult and replaces it with *salām*. The peace is not for them, really. **It is what keeps your own heart clean as you walk out.** A heart that exits with a curse carries the fight home with it. A heart that exits with *salām* leaves the fight behind.

Al-Muyassar names who this *salām* belongs to: it is what *ūlū al-albāb wal-baṣā'ir* — **the people of deep intellect and penetrating sight** — say to the ignorant. They do not sink to revile; they do not mirror back the cruelty aimed at them. The bare word of peace you give on your way out is not just a social form. It is a marker of which register of understanding you inhabit. The one who exits with a curse reveals that the fight still owns him. The one who exits with *salām* has already left — inwardly — long before his feet followed.

And the indefinite, unaddressed bareness of the word — *salām*, no "upon you" — is itself the dignity. It is not warmth he does not feel; it is not coldness either. It is a clean, neutral, untouchable peace. He hands it down and walks on.

What this word reveals — by everything it deliberately leaves off — is that the highest exit from those who will not be reached is neither warmth you cannot honestly give nor a parting wound that poisons you, but a bare, dignified word of peace that keeps your own soul clean.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Fa-sawfa Yaʿlamūn* — The Sentence With Its Object Cut Out

Now the final three words. And this is where I want you to read an **absence** as carefully as you would read any presence.

The concept first. In Arabic, a verb like *yaʿlamūn* — "they will know" — is what's called a *transitive* verb. It *wants* an object. "They will know" — know *what*? When you say "they will know," the listener's mind leans forward, waiting for the thing they will know. *They will know the truth. They will know their mistake. They will know the punishment.* The sentence feels unfinished until the object arrives.

Now look at what Allah does.

**فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ.** *Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.* "But soon — they will know."

And then... **nothing.** The ayah ends. The surah ends. The object never comes.

[PAUSE]

Allah cut it off.

In the language of Arabic rhetoric this is a deliberate device — *ḥadhf al-mafʿūl*, the **omission of the object** — and the scholars were emphatic about why it is done. When you delete the object of "they will know," you do not narrow what they will know. **You make it limitless.** Had Allah said "they will know *the punishment*," the dread would have a ceiling — it would be *just* the punishment. By deleting it, He lets your imagination fill the silence with *everything*: they will know the truth they denied, they will know Who they mocked, they will know what they threw away, they will know the weight of every laugh. The unspecified object becomes **every** object.

Al-Muyassar gives voice to what the classical scholars heard in that open silence: *mā yalqawnahū min al-balā' wal-nakāl* — **what they will meet of trial and severe punishment.** The scholars named the terror that the grammar refuses to name. But notice: the grammar's refusal is itself the point. A named threat is a smaller threat. The unnamed one fills the whole horizon.

And notice the timing-word: **سَوْفَ — *sawfa*.** Arabic has two ways to say "will": the quick prefix *sa-* (soon, near), and the fuller word *sawfa* (the more drawn-out, more emphatic future). Allah chooses *sawfa*. Not "they'll find out in a minute" — but a deep, certain, deliberate "they *will* come to know," stretching out over time. It is the patience of certainty. There is no hurry in it, because there is no doubt in it.

[PAUSE]

One further layer, preserved in the record of readings. Al-Tabari notes that the readers of Madīnah heard this final clause not as *yaʿlamūn* — **"they will know"** (third person: reported to the Prophet *about* them) — but as **taʿlamūn** — **"you will know"** (second person: addressed *directly to them*). Al-Jalalayn acknowledges both readings and describes the whole phrase as a warning and a threat. Together they add a layer of stereo depth. In the third person, the ayah consoles the Prophet in private: *Allah has this in hand; you do not have to be there when the truth lands.* In the second person, it arms him with a final, quiet sentence he is commanded to *say to their faces* as his parting word — not shouted, but placed like a stone set down: *you will know.* Both are sound. Both are real.

Now put the whole structure together and feel the **transfer of the verb of knowing**.

Look back at the surah. These were people who said, in effect, *we know better* — we know our fathers' way, we know who deserves revelation, we know enough to laugh. The surah is full of their false, mocking certainty.

And the surah's final word, *yaʿlamūn*, takes the verb "to know" out of their mouths and places it in their **future** — as something that will be **done to them**, not claimed by them. *You think you know. Soon you will be made to know.* The very faculty they were so arrogant about is the faculty through which the truth will one day overtake them.

So the Prophet ﷺ is told: you do not have to be the one who makes them know. You do not have to win the last word, force the admission, see the vindication land on their faces. *Iṣfaḥ* — turn away. *Qul salām* — leave them peace. And the knowing — the proving, the verdict, the moment the truth lands — *fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn*: **leave that to the One who alone can deliver it.**

[PAUSE]

One small, honest note for those who study these verses closely. The classical scholars differed on the legal status of "turn away" in this ayah. Some — Qatāda, and as narrated from Ibn ʿAbbās, and Muqātil — held that this command of forbearance was later abrogated by the verses of fighting revealed in Madīnah; others, as al-Qurtubi reports, held *"hiya muḥkamatun lam tunsakh"* — **it is firm and was not abrogated.** Al-Jalalayn stands plainly with the first camp, stating of this ayah: *"hādhā qabla an yu'mara biqitālihim"* — **"this was before he was commanded to fight them."** That is a juristic discussion about a specific Makkan-versus-Madinan question of legal scope. What neither camp disputed is the *spiritual* core: the inner act of *ṣafḥ*, the clean exit, the refusal to let their mockery continue living inside you — that posture belongs to every time and circumstance, beyond the reach of any abrogation.

## PART 2: THE CRY BEHIND THE COMMAND

There is a detail about this moment that the classical commentators preserve and that the ayah itself implies, and it matters deeply for how you receive what you just read.

The Prophet ﷺ did not receive this ayah in the abstract. The context, as al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both frame it, is that he had turned to his Lord with a cry: *"O Lord, these are a people who will not believe."* It is a phrase that appears in these late Makkan surahs as a kind of recurring ache — not a prayer for destruction, not a theological argument, just an honest report from the heart of a man who has tried everything: *my Lord, they simply will not believe.*

Think about what it costs to say that. He was not made from granite. He wept. He lost sleep. The Qur'an itself will later tell us that he was *almost destroying himself* over them in his grief (18:6, 26:3). And in this particular moment, at the end of this long surah, his report to Allah is not strategy — it is *confession*: I have done everything I know to do, and it is not working.

And Allah answers: *iṣfaḥ ʿanhum wa qul salām. Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.*

Not: "you failed." Not: "try harder." Not even a new argument to carry back to them. Just — **the way out.** The dignified, bodily, spiritually clean way to walk away from a door that has been shut from the inside.

This is how Allah cared for his Prophet. Not always with victory. Sometimes with an exit. Not always with the answer the man was hoping for, but with the grace he needed to survive the chapter he was in.

[PAUSE]

And here — quietly, for those who have found themselves in the same cry: *my Lord, I have tried everything, and they will not believe* — is the response that was given once to the best of people in the worst of circumstances. It was not a miracle that would change them. It was a posture that would preserve *him*.

Sometimes the mercy is not the breakthrough. Sometimes the mercy is the *ṣafḥ* — the clean page-turn — and the *salām* that keeps your heart whole on the way out.

## PART 3: THREE MOVEMENTS FOR A LIFE

This ayah gives the believer three distinct movements, and each one is worth sitting with on its own.

**The first movement: turn your face.**

*Iṣfaḥ* is not a one-time decision. It is a posture you choose and rechoose. There will be people in your life — perhaps family members, perhaps colleagues, perhaps people you once loved — who have made a fixed choice not to hear. Not to change. Who find your conviction an amusement. Whom you have addressed forty times in forty different ways.

The first movement is this: you are allowed to stop fronting them. To give them your side instead of your full face. Not in contempt — not even in distance — but in the quiet recognition that your continued *fronting* is keeping you trapped in the argument more than it is reaching them. *Iṣfaḥ*: turn the page. The wound is real; you are just choosing not to live there.

[PAUSE]

**The second movement: leave a word of peace.**

And as you go, do not let the exit be a last strike. This is the hardest part. The temptation to say the final cutting thing — the sentence that will follow them as you walk out, the last shot that proves you were right — is nearly overwhelming when you have been genuinely wronged.

*Qul salām.* Say: peace. Not warmth you cannot honestly give; not coldness that will calcify into bitterness. A bare, clean, indefinite peace — a word that holds no malice and costs you nothing but the pride that wanted to wound them on the way out. Leave it on the table and walk. The *salām* is as much for your own soul as for theirs. The people of deep intellect and penetrating sight — al-Muyassar's *ūlū al-albāb wal-baṣā'ir* — do not lower themselves to revile, do not mirror back what was aimed at them. To say *salām* on your way out is to join that company. It is how you arrive at the next chapter with a clean heart.

[PAUSE]

**The third movement: hand the verdict to Allah.**

*Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.* This is the movement that makes the first two possible. The reason you can turn your face, the reason you can say peace and walk — is because you have genuinely transferred the verdict out of your hands and into His.

This is not resignation. It is not the cold shrug of someone who has stopped caring. It is the active, trusting surrender of someone who has recognized the limits of their own authority. You are not the one who was ever meant to make them know. You were meant to bring the word. You were not meant to force the landing. *That* — the making-known, the moment the truth overtakes a soul — belongs to Allah alone, in His timing, by His means, in ways you may never witness.

And so you hand it over. Not the care — you still care. Not the hope — you may still hope. But the *need for it to happen before you leave the room*, the need to see the vindication with your own eyes, the need for them to admit it — *that*, you release. *Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.* They will know. Allah has said it. It is enough.

## CONCLUSION

This is the last ayah of the Surah of Gold.

The surah spent eighty-eight verses arguing against the golden surface of the world — the wealth that made people think they were worth more than a Prophet, the ancestry that made them think they knew more than revelation, the mockery that made them feel superior to the truth.

And at the end, it did not crush them. It did not argue with them one final time. It gave its Prophet — exhausted, mocked, grief-broken enough to cry out to his Lord — six words.

*Turn your face. Say: peace. They will know.*

In six words, Allah gave him his body back. The body that had been straining toward people who kept walking away. The posture that had been locked in confrontation with people who found the whole thing entertaining. *Iṣfaḥ* — you are allowed to turn. You are allowed to be done with this page.

In six words, He gave him his heart back. The heart that wanted to leave them a wound — because that is what hearts do when they have been wounded. *Qul salām* — no. Leave them the word that keeps you clean. Give them what cost you the most to give: your wish that they be well, untouched by malice, setting nothing alight behind you as you go.

And in six words, He gave him the verdict — not for the Prophet to carry, but for Allah to hold. *Fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn.* The object was left blank, because Allah does not need to specify what He already knows in its fullness. They will know. The certainty is the *sawfa* — deep, patient, as certain as dawn. You do not have to be there when it lands.

[PAUSE]

This is not a surah that ends in defeat. It ends in something rarer and harder and more complete: a dignified release. The kind that requires more from you than a fight would. A fight needs only your anger. This needs your trust.

The surah of gold ends with a man who had been offered the whole glittering world's judgment — and turned away from it, said peace, and left the rest to God.

That is the ending the Qur'an chose for this surah. It is an ending worth sitting with for a long time.

فَٱصْفَحْ عَنْهُمْ وَقُلْ سَلَـٰمٌ‌ۚ فَسَوْفَ يَعْلَمُونَ

*So turn away from them and say: peace. But soon they will know.*
