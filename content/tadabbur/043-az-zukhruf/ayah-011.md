# The Measure of Mercy: How Rain Rehearses Your Resurrection

## Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 11

> وَٱلَّذِى نَزَّلَ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءًۢ بِقَدَرٍ فَأَنشَرْنَا بِهِۦ بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ تُخْرَجُونَ

*Wa-lladhī nazzala mina-s-samāʾi māʾan bi-qadarin fa-ansharnā bihī baldatan maytan — kadhālika tukhrajūn.*

**"And He who sends down water from the sky in due measure — then We revive with it a dead land. Thus will you be brought forth."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Argument You've Been Living Inside

Let me ask you to do something before we open a single word.

Picture water.

Not a glass of it. Picture water as a *force*. Picture the water that came in the days of Nuh, peace be upon him — the sky opening, the springs of the earth bursting upward, the world disappearing under a grey rising weight until the mountains were islands and then the mountains were gone. Water that killed everything it touched. Water as the instrument of the end of a world.

Now picture a different water. A grey morning. A field that has been brown and cracked for months, the soil like old skin. And it begins — not a wall of water, but a *drizzle*, then a steadier fall, soft, patient, soaking in. And three weeks later you drive past that same field and it is **green**. Not just alive — *crowded* with life, stems pushing against stems, a whole population that did not exist a month ago.

Same substance. **Water.** One drowned a civilization. The other raised a field from the dead.

[PAUSE]

What is the difference between the two?

Hold that question. Because the entire weight of this ayah is balanced on the answer, and Allah places the answer in a single word — a word so small you could read the verse a hundred times and slide right past it.

Here is where we are standing. The surah, Az-Zukhruf — "The Ornaments of Gold" — is speaking to people who had a strange kind of faith. If you asked them, *"Who created the heavens and the earth?"* they would not hesitate: *"The Mighty, the All-Knowing created them."* They believed in the Creator. They were not atheists. Their problem was different and far more familiar to us than we like to admit: **they believed in God and lived as though He were not the point.** They acknowledged Him with the tongue and crowded Him out with their lives, their idols, their ornaments of gold.

So Allah does not argue with them about whether He exists. He does something more piercing. He starts pointing. *Look. Look at this. Look at that.* He recites the world back to them — the earth made into a cradle beneath them, the roads threaded through the mountains, the rain, the pairs of every created thing, the ships and the animals they ride. A whole catalogue of the ordinary, made strange again.

And in the middle of that catalogue comes our ayah. It begins as a description of *rain* — something you have seen ten thousand times. And before the sentence is over, it has reached forward and seized hold of **the Day you will be pulled out of your grave.**

That is the journey of twelve words. From a drizzle you ignored this morning — to your own resurrection.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Bi-qadar* — The One Word That Separates Mercy from Ruin

Let me teach you something about the Arabic before we touch the ayah, because if I just translate the word you will nod and miss it entirely.

There is a root in Arabic — **q-d-r** (ق د ر). It is one of the great load-bearing roots of the whole language. From it comes *qadar* — destiny, decree, the divine determining of all things, the word you have heard your whole life as "fate." But step back from the theology for a moment, because the root underneath all of that is humbler and more concrete. *Qadara* means **to measure. To proportion. To determine the exact amount of a thing.**

Think of a pharmacist. Not a chef throwing in a handful of salt — a *pharmacist*, weighing a compound on a balance to the milligram. Too little and the medicine does nothing. Too much and the same medicine is poison. The healing and the harm are *the same chemical*. The only difference — the entire difference between cure and corpse — is the **measure**.

Hold that image. Now look at what Allah says about the water:

> نَزَّلَ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءًۢ **بِقَدَرٍ**

*He sends down from the sky water — **bi-qadar.** In a measure. By an exact amount.*

Now. Why is that word there at all? You could send rain without it. *"He sends down water from the sky"* — a complete, beautiful sentence. The information is delivered. So why does Allah hang this extra word — *bi-qadar* — at the very end of the gift, like a seal?

Let's sit with this.

The classical commentators heard the whisper in this word, and they all heard the *same* thing — and where four scholars who often differ all turn to the same memory, you should lean in. Al-Qurtubi reports it from Ibn ʿAbbas, and al-Baghawi, al-Muyassar, and the Jalalayn all echo it: **water *bi-qadar* — meaning, not like the water sent upon the people of Nuh.**

There it is. The flood is *in the room.* The word *bi-qadar* is not just telling you how much rain falls. It is quietly drawing a line between two waters: the water that **measures** to your need, and the water that came **without measure** and drowned a world.

[PAUSE]

Sit in that for a second, because it is staggering.

The same Lord. The same substance falling from the same sky. In one revelation it is the end of everything; in this ayah it is *life from the dead.* And the only variable Allah names — the single hinge on which the entire difference swings — is **the measure.**

So what is mercy?

We tend to think mercy is *more.* More rain, more money, more health, more of the thing we like. We imagine the merciful God as the God who opens the tap all the way. But this ayah is teaching something the nafs finds almost impossible to accept:

**Mercy is not the abundance. Mercy is the measure.**

The rain that loves you is the rain that knows when to *stop.* Ibn ʿAbbas catches both edges of it — al-Qurtubi records his words precisely: the water comes *"neither as a drowning flood, nor falling short of the need"* (*lā ṭūfānun mughriqun wa-lā qāṣirun ʿani-l-ḥājah*). Both failures are named. Too much drowns. Too little starves. And mercy lives in the **narrow, exact, deliberate** space between them — the space a pharmacist works in, the space *bi-qadar* names.

And notice the word is **indefinite** — *bi-qadarin*, "by *a* measure," not "by *the* measure." There is something unbounded in that openness. It is not one fixed ration stamped once for all places. It is a measuring that is alive — different for this valley and that desert, this season and that one, this need and that one. A measuring that is *attentive.* The rain is not poured; it is **portioned**, place by place, to a knowledge that holds every field at once.

Here is what is remarkable. We spend our lives asking Allah for *more* and reading every limit as His withholding. The empty account, the closed door, the relationship that didn't open, the body that won't do what we ask of it. We read the *measure* as the absence of mercy. And this one word turns that inside out and tells us: the limit **is** the mercy. The "no" is the same hand as the "yes" — it is the hand of the One who measures, who knows that the un-measured gift is the flood that drowns you.

Sit with that the next time the rain in your life seems to be falling short. *Bi-qadar.* It is measured. By Someone who has seen what the un-measured does.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Two: *Nazzala* — The Gentleness Hidden in the Grammar

Now I want to show you something that lives one layer deeper, in the *shape* of a single verb — and it confirms everything *bi-qadar* just told us, from a completely different direction.

In Arabic, a verb is not a flat thing. The same three root-letters can be *built* into different forms, and each form bends the meaning. Let me give you the everyday version first.

Take the idea "to send down." In Arabic, the simple form is *anzala* (أنزل) — *to send down.* Clean, single, done. One act. But our ayah does not use *anzala.* It uses a built-up form, the form the grammarians call **Form II**: **nazzala** (نَزَّلَ) — with the middle letter doubled, *thickened.*

What does that doubling do? In Arabic, doubling the middle radical typically signals **intensity, repetition, or gradualness** — the act spread out, done again and again, distributed over time rather than dumped all at once. Think of the difference, in English, between *"he dropped it"* and *"he kept dropping it, bit by bit."* The doubling is the "bit by bit." It is the difference between a bucket emptied in one heave and a watering can tilted slowly along a row.

This is well-established in the language — and the scholars of the Qur'an have long noticed that the Book itself uses this very distinction about *its own* descent: *nazzala* for the gradual, staged sending of the revelation across twenty-three years, *anzala* for the single act.

Now bring that back to the rain.

> **نَزَّلَ** مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءًۢ بِقَدَرٍ

He did not *anzala* the water — fling it down in one act. He **nazzala** it — *sent it down in stages, gradually, portion after portion.*

Do you feel what just happened? The grammar and the word *bi-qadar* are **saying the same thing twice, in two different languages.** *Bi-qadar* tells you the *amount* is measured. *Nazzala* tells you the *delivery* is measured — slow, staged, soaking, a drizzle and not a deluge. The mercy is in the *how* as much as the *how much.*

And this is exactly how rain that *gives life* actually behaves. A flash flood — water arriving all at once, *anzala*-style — strips the topsoil and runs off and drowns the seed. It is water without gentleness. But the soft, staged, *nazzala* rain — that is the rain that *soaks in*, that the ground can drink, that wakes the seed instead of burying it.

[PAUSE]

Notice what the *form of a verb* is teaching you about the nature of your Lord. He does not overwhelm what He intends to revive. He comes to the dead field of the heart the way the good rain comes — **gradually.** A little understanding today. A small softening tomorrow. An ayah that lands this month that would have bounced off you last year. We so often want our transformation *anzala*-style — all at once, flooded, done by Friday. And Allah, in His mercy, deals with us *nazzala*-style: in measure, in stages, in a tempo the soil of the soul can actually absorb without being washed away.

The God who measures the *amount* of the rain also measures its *pace.* That is in the bones of the word.

### Element Three: *Fa-ansharnā* — He Used the Resurrection Word for a Field

We come now to the word where the whole ayah quietly detonates.

Watch the camera move. The verse begins in the third person: *"And **He** who sends down…"* — *al-ladhī*, "the One who," Allah spoken *about*, at a respectful distance. And then, mid-sentence, without warning:

> **فَأَنشَرْنَا** بِهِۦ

*"then **WE** revive with it…"*

Did you catch the shift? *He* sends down — then *We* revive. The grammar leaps from third person to first person, from "He" to the majestic "We." The rhetoricians of Arabic have a name for this deliberate turn of the camera — *iltifāt*, "the turning." And it is never decoration. The third person was the distant, general truth: *this is the kind of God He is.* And then, at the precise instant of *giving life to the dead*, Allah switches to **"We"** — and suddenly it is direct, immediate, present-tense divine action, the Speaker Himself reaching into the dead ground in front of you. The grammar steps *closer* exactly at the moment of resurrection. As if to say: *do not file this under general facts about the universe. This is Me. Watch Me do it.*

Now the verb itself. *Ansharnā* — from the root **n-sh-r** (ن ش ر). And here you must stay very still, because this is the heart of the whole thing.

The root *nashr* means **to spread out, to unfold, to unroll** — and from there, **to spread life through what was lifeless, to revive.** It is the word for opening out something that was folded shut. And from this exact root, the Arabic language — and the Qur'an itself — draws one of its primary names for **the Resurrection**: *al-nushūr.* The Day of being *spread back out*, unfolded again into life, after the folding-up of death.

Do you see what Allah has done?

He is describing a *field.* A patch of dead dirt coming green after rain. And to describe it, He reaches for **the very word He uses for raising the dead on the Last Day.** He calls the greening of a field by the *name* of your resurrection.

[PAUSE — major pause]

The vocabulary itself is the argument.

He doesn't *compare* the field to the resurrection in some loose, poetic way. He uses the **same root** for both, so that every spring, when the dead earth is "spread back out" into life, the very *word* for it is whispering *al-nushūr* — *the unfolding, the resurrection.* The proof is not in a syllogism. It is wired into the language, so that the believer who knows Arabic cannot watch a field turn green without the word for Judgment Day pressing at the edge of his tongue.

And what is being revived? **بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًا** — *baldatan maytan,* "a dead land." Notice He says the land is *dead* — *maytan* — using the very word for the death of a person. Not "barren," not "dry," not "fallow." **Dead.** The same death. He is deliberately collapsing the distance between the dead field and the dead human. (And there is a small, exquisite grammatical touch here that the scholars long noted: *baldah* is feminine, yet He describes it with *maytan*, the masculine form — because *baldah* is understood as *makān*, a *place* — as if the word strains past the field toward something larger, a place, a *site* of death, that the resurrection word will undo.)

So lay the three discoveries side by side, because they are about to become one sentence:

- *Bi-qadar* — the gift is **measured.**
- *Nazzala* — the gift is **gently, gradually given.**
- *Ansharnā* — the gift's true name is **resurrection.**

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Already Believe in Resurrection — You Just Call It "Spring"

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of doubt that almost everyone carries, even the believer, even the one who would never say it out loud. It is not the doubt that says *"there is no God."* It is the quieter, more stubborn doubt that says: *"...but how, really? How does a body that has become dust, that has been scattered, that has been forgotten by the world — how does **that** stand up again? It's too much. It's too far. I believe it because I'm supposed to, but I cannot picture it."*

That doubt is ancient. It is, in fact, the exact doubt the people of this surah threw at the Prophet ﷺ. Elsewhere in the Qur'an they say it plainly: *"Who will revive these bones when they are decayed?"* They held up a crumbling bone and treated it as a checkmate.

And notice how Allah answers them. Not with metaphysics. Not with a lecture on divine power in the abstract. He answers by **pointing out the window.**

Here is the thing we have to confront about ourselves: **we believe in resurrection constantly. We just don't notice we're doing it.**

Every single autumn, you watch a world die. Leaves let go. Green drains out of everything. The fields go brown, the branches go bare, the ground goes hard. A *whole living world* is lowered into the grave of winter, and you don't grieve, because you *know* — with a knowledge so deep it isn't even a belief, it's an assumption — that it is coming back. You have *seen* the dead earth rise. You have watched, with your own eyes, life pour back into ground that had nothing in it. You drive past the resurrection every spring and you don't even slow down.

This is the *istidlāl* — the proof Allah builds. He takes the resurrection you have *already witnessed and already accepted* — the field — and He says, through the very word *ansharnā*: this is not a *metaphor* for the resurrection. **It is a smaller instance of the same act, by the same Hand.**

And then comes the verdict, the four words that close the ayah and turn the whole sky over your head:

> كَذَٰلِكَ تُخْرَجُونَ

*"**Thus** will you be brought forth."*

*Kadhālika* — "thus," "in just this way," "this is the manner of it." He doesn't say *your* resurrection will be *like* this. He says it will be **thus** — by this same process you are watching. The dead land and the dead you are the *same problem*, and He has already solved it, in public, in front of you, every year of your life. The God who can do it to the field is not *probably* able to do it to you. He is doing the *identical thing* — the only variable, again, is the *measure*, the timing, the *bi-qadar* of when your spring comes.

[PAUSE]

And feel the last word: **تُخْرَجُونَ** — *tukhrajūn.* It is **passive.** "You *will be brought* forth." Not "you will come out" — *you will be brought.* The same way the green shoot does not decide to push through the soil — it is *pushed*, it is *brought*, the life is done *to* it by Another. You will not author your own rising any more than the seed authors the spring. You will be *brought out*, lifted by the same Hand that lifts the field — whether you are ready or not, whether you believe it or not. The doubt does not get a vote. *Thus you will be brought forth.*

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you do with a field. With a tree budding. With the absurd, casual, overlooked miracle of green returning to dead ground. Most of us have trained ourselves to see nature as *scenery* — pretty, neutral, mute. This ayah re-consecrates it. The next time you see new growth where there was nothing, let it do its appointed work: let it be a *rehearsal* you are attending. Allah is not asking you to believe in your resurrection on blind faith. He is asking you to *connect two things you already accept* — and to let the spring you trust testify to the Day you fear.

The believer's practical task is small and enormous: **stop letting the daily resurrection be invisible.** Let one greening field a year stop you in your tracks. *Kadhālika tukhrajūn.* Thus. You. Will be brought forth.

### Theme Two: The Mercy You Keep Mistaking for Deprivation

Now let me take you back to the pharmacist's scale, because *bi-qadar* has a second teaching, and this one is not about the next life. It is about the ache you woke up with this morning.

Here is a truth about the human heart that the marketing of the entire modern world is built to hide from you: **we are convinced that more is always better.** More money is better than less. More comfort, more options, more food, more attention, more capacity, more *yes.* We carry, deep in the nafs, an unexamined equation: *abundance equals blessing, limitation equals lack.* And so we read every limit in our lives as a *withholding.* A wound. A place where the mercy *didn't reach.*

And then we open this ayah and find that the water that *gives life* is precisely the water that is **limited.** *Bi-qadar.* Measured. Held back from being too much. And the water that was *unlimited* — the flood that knew no measure — is the water that **killed.**

Stay with that, because it overturns the equation you've been running your whole life.

The flood of Nuh was not a *failure* of generosity. It was generosity *without measure* — water given without the restraining wisdom that knows when to stop. And it drowned the world. Which means: **the un-measured gift is not the highest mercy. It is the catastrophe.** The thing that makes a gift *merciful* is the very thing we resent in it — the *limit*. The "enough." The "this much and no more."

Now turn that on your own life.

The provision that was *capped* — the door that opened only this wide and no wider. The success that came *slowly* instead of in a flood. The relationship you wanted *desperately* that never opened. The capacity you begged for — more energy, more time, more reach — that was given to you in a strict daily ration that always seems to run out before the day does.

We read every one of those as *bi-qadar* in the sense of *stinginess.* And the ayah is telling you to read it as *bi-qadar* in the sense of *the pharmacist.* What if the success that came as a flood would have drowned the part of you that still needed to grow slowly? What if the un-capped wealth was the *ṭūfān mughriq* — the drowning flood — for a heart not yet able to carry it? What if the "no" you are still grieving was not the absence of His mercy but its *most precise expression* — His hand measuring out *exactly* the amount that would give you life and not one drop of the amount that would drown you?

[PAUSE]

This is the hardest reframe in the whole religion, and the ayah hands it to you in one word. *Bi-qadar.* He is not careless with your rain. He is not *out* of rain. He is **measuring** it — *neither a drowning flood nor falling short of the need*, as Ibn ʿAbbas said — calibrating it to a knowledge of you that you do not have of yourself, watering the field of your life at a pace, *nazzala*, gentle enough that you can absorb it without being washed away.

And here is the quiet devastation of it: the *same Lord* sends the flood and the life-giving rain. It is not that a stingy god gives you less while a generous god gives others more. It is **one God, one hand, one mercy** — expressed *as abundance* where abundance gives life, and *as limit* where limit gives life. The measure is not the withholding of His love. The measure **is** His love, doing arithmetic on your behalf.

So what changes tomorrow?

The next time you hit the wall of a limit — the budget that won't stretch, the energy that runs out, the door that stays shut — try, just once, a different sentence. Not *"why is it not more?"* but *"this is bi-qadar. This is measured. By the One who has seen what the un-measured does to a soul like mine."* You do not have to *feel* the truth of it instantly. You only have to *entertain* the possibility that the limit in front of you is not a leak in His mercy but the *shape* of it — the pharmacist's hand, weighing out exactly your life, and refusing to give you your death.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the whole ayah was secretly one thought the entire time.

We found three things in the words:
- **Bi-qadar** — the rain is *measured.*
- **Nazzala** — the rain is *gently, gradually given.*
- **Ansharnā** — the rain's true name is *resurrection.*

And we found two things in the themes:
- The dead field is *rehearsing your own rising.*
- The measure you resent is the *mercy you've been missing.*

Now watch them fuse.

The *same word* — **qadar** — is the bridge between the two halves of your life. *Bi-qadar* governs the rain that gives life *now.* And *qadar* — the decree — governs the timing of the resurrection that gives life *then.* The God who *measures* the rainfall is the God who *measures* the appointed Day. Your spring and your resurrection are the *same act* — *kadhālika*, "thus" — governed by the *same attribute*, His perfect measuring. The mercy that calibrates the drizzle outside your window is the *identical* mercy that has already calibrated the morning you will be brought out of the ground.

The flood drowns. The measured rain revives. The dead field rises. And *thus you will be brought forth* — by the One who has never once, in the whole history of the sky, gotten the measure wrong.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *limit* in your life right now that you have been reading as God's absence — and what would change if you read it as *bi-qadar*, His measure, His mercy doing arithmetic on your behalf?

2. When was the last time the greening of dead ground actually *stopped* you — and what has to change for the daily resurrection to become visible to you again?

3. You want your transformation *anzala*-style — flooded, finished, all at once. He gives it *nazzala*-style — measured, gradual, soaking. Where are you resenting the *pace* of a mercy that is being gentle with you precisely so it doesn't wash you away?

4. The verb is passive — *you will be brought forth.* What would you do differently this week if you truly internalized that your rising is not up to you, that it is *being done to you*, as certainly as spring is done to the field?

5. If the field testifies to your resurrection, what is your own life *currently* testifying to?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The same water that drowned a world gives life to a dead land — because the entire difference between ruin and mercy is *measure* — and the God who measures the rain to raise a dead field is rehearsing, in front of your eyes every spring, the Day He will raise *you*.

### The Closing Invitation

You did not need a new piece of information today. You needed permission to *connect two things you already accept.* You already believe the field comes back. You already feel every limit in your life as a kind of rain. This ayah simply takes your hand and lays it on the seam between them and says: *it is one God, one measure, one mercy — the rain, the limit, the field, and you.*

So let the invitation be small enough to actually keep: this week, find one piece of green growing where there was nothing. Stop. And let it preach to you the sermon Allah wired into the very word — *ansharnā, al-nushūr* — *thus you will be brought forth.*

### A Brief Du'a

*O You who send down the rain in measure and never once mistake the amount — teach our hearts to read the limits of our lives as the calibration of Your mercy, and not the absence of it. You who revive the dead earth and call it by the name of our rising — let us not stand before a greening field unmoved, and let us not stand before You on that Day un-prepared. As You bring the dead land forth, bring us forth into Your pleasure. Āmīn.*
