# The Confession They Cannot Escape

### Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 9

> وَلَئِن سَأَلْتَهُم مَّنْ خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ لَيَقُولُنَّ خَلَقَهُنَّ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ

*wa la-in sa'altahum man khalaqa as-samāwāti wal-arḍa la-yaqūlunna khalaqahunna al-ʿAzīzu al-ʿAlīm*

**"And if you ask them, 'Who created the heavens and the earth?' they will surely say: 'The Almighty, the All-Knowing created them.'"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Verse

Let us begin by placing ourselves in Makkah.

This is a city built around a sanctuary — the Kaʿbah — that the people themselves trace back to Ibrāhīm, peace be upon him. They are not atheists. They are not people who deny that there is a God. Quite the opposite. They are intensely religious. They make pilgrimage. They give in charity. They swear oaths by their Lord. They name their children ʿAbdullāh — *servant of Allah.* The grandfather of the Prophet ﷺ carried the name ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, and his own father was named ʿAbdullāh, *before* Islam came.

So when we imagine the people the Prophet ﷺ is sent to in this surah, let us correct a picture we may have inherited. These are not people who never heard the word "Allah." These are people who say "Allah" constantly. They believe He made the world. They believe He sends the rain. They believe He gives life and takes it.

And yet — they set up idols around His house. They carved gods out of stone and named them, and they bowed to them, and they said: *these bring us closer to Allah.*

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the strangeness of that. A people who genuinely believe that one Being created the entire universe — and who nonetheless kneel to objects they shaped with their own hands. How does a mind hold both of those at once?

That is the question this surah walks straight into. And ayah 9 is the moment Allah turns to His Messenger and says, in effect: *Test them. Ask them the simplest possible question about ultimate reality — and listen to what comes out of their own mouths.*

**The hook is this:** Allah is so certain of how they will answer that He swears an oath about it before they ever speak. He does not say "they might say" or "they could say." He guarantees their reply. And the reply they give is not a small thing — it is, word for word, the foundation of the entire message they are rejecting.

How can someone confess the truth with total certainty and still build their life on its opposite? That is what we are here to understand. Because it turns out this is not a verse about seventh-century idolaters.

It is a verse about us.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Oath Before the Answer — *wa la-in… la-yaqūlunna*

Before we touch the Arabic, let me teach you the device, because once you see it you will never un-see it.

In everyday speech, there is a difference between three sentences:

- "If you ask them, they say it was Allah."
- "If you ask them, they *will* say it was Allah."
- "I swear, if you ask them, they will *definitely, absolutely, without fail* say it was Allah."

The first is a flat report. The second adds futurity and confidence. The third is something else entirely — it is a *guarantee under oath.* The speaker is putting their own credibility on the line about what *someone else* will do. You only speak like that when you know a person better than they know themselves.

Now look at how Allah constructed this sentence.

It opens: **وَلَئِن** — *wa la-in.* That little **لَ** (*la*) at the front is not decoration. The grammarians call it the **lām of the oath** — the *lām muwaṭṭi'a li-l-qasam*, the lām that "prepares the ground" for a sworn statement. It signals: *what follows is being said with the weight of an oath behind it.* The verse is not predicting. It is testifying.

And then, when the answer comes, watch what happens to the verb: **لَيَقُولُنَّ** — *la-yaqūlunna.* There is that **لَ** again — the answering lām of the oath — and at the end, that heavy, doubled, drawn-out **ـنَّ**.

Stay with me, because this is the heart of it.

That **ـنَّ** is what Arabic calls **nūn al-tawkīd al-thaqīlah** — *the heavy nūn of emphasis.* Arabic has a tool that English simply does not possess: you can fuse certainty *into the body of the verb itself.* You don't add an adverb beside it; you weld emphasis into its bones. *Yaqūlūna* means "they say." *La-yaqūlunna* means something we can barely render — "they will most surely, most certainly, unavoidably say."

Notice the sound of it. *La-ya-qū-lun-na.* It thickens. It lands hard, like a door slamming. The phonetics themselves close off escape. There is no soft exit from that word.

[PAUSE]

So here is what the linguistics has put in front of us. Allah is not reporting on the idolaters. **Allah is swearing an oath about what is buried so deep inside them that even they cannot keep it down.**

Think about who is being described. These are the people most invested in *denying* the message. If anyone had a motive to dodge the question — to mumble, to deflect, to credit their idols — it is them. And Allah says: no. Corner them with this one question, and the truth will come out of their mouths *under its own weight,* against their own interests, every single time.

What kind of knowledge is that? Knowledge a person cannot suppress even when suppressing it would serve them — that is not learned knowledge. That is **fiṭrah.** The innate, built-in recognition of the Creator that Allah pressed into every soul. You can bury it under stone idols. You can talk over it for a lifetime. But ask the right question and it surfaces, sworn and certain.

The grammar is telling us: *disbelief in a Creator is not the natural state. It is the effortful one.* The truth is the thing that escapes; the lie is the thing they have to hold down.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Answer Echoes the Question — *man khalaqa… khalaqahunna*

Here is something easy to read past in translation. Let us slow it down.

Allah tells the Prophet ﷺ to ask: **مَّنْ خَلَقَ** — *man khalaqa* — "**Who created**…?" The verb is *khalaqa*, from the root **خ-ل-ق**, to bring into existence, to originate.

And how do they answer? **خَلَقَهُنَّ** — *khalaqahunna* — "**He created them**." Same root. Same verb. They take the exact word of the question and hand it straight back.

Why does that matter?

Because notice what they did *not* do. They did not reinterpret the question. They did not say "well, the gods help," or "the heavens have always been there," or "who can really know?" There is no hedging, no philosophy, no fog. The question used the word *khalaqa* — and their answer uses *khalaqa.* **The confession matches the question seam for seam.**

In a debate, when your opponent simply repeats your own framing back to you and affirms it, the argument is over. There is nothing left to dispute. Allah has engineered a question whose only honest answer is total surrender of the point.

Now look closer at that pronoun: **خَلَقَهُنَّ** — *khalaqa-hunna* — "He created **them**," where *hunna* is the **feminine plural** object pronoun. It reaches back and gathers up "the heavens and the earth" — these vast, immovable, overwhelming realities — and refers to them with a single small pronoun, **hunna.** All of it. Every galaxy, every horizon, the entire ceiling of existence and the ground beneath their feet — collected into one word and laid at the feet of one Maker.

[PAUSE]

And now we arrive at the most important thing in the entire verse, and it is something that is **not there.**

Read their answer again, all the way to its end: *"The Almighty, the All-Knowing created them."*

And then… the sentence stops.

Where is the conclusion? Logic demands a next line. If you genuinely believe that one overwhelming, all-knowing Being created the heavens and the earth — then the only sane response is: *"…therefore He alone deserves my worship."* That is the conclusion their own premise is screaming toward.

But it never comes. They confess the premise with an oath's worth of certainty — and they refuse the conclusion. They will say *who made the universe* without hesitation, and then walk back to their idols.

This is the **asymmetry the verse is built on.** The presence is their flawless confession of the Creator. The absence — the deafening, deliberate silence — is the act of worship that should have followed and didn't. The classical commentators saw this precisely. Al-Qurṭubī writes that they *"acknowledged Him as Creator and Originator, then worshipped others alongside Him out of their ignorance."* Al-Baghawī adds that they *"affirmed that Allah is the Creator, and affirmed His might and His knowledge — then worshipped other than Him and denied His power to resurrect, out of the depth of their ignorance."*

They got the hardest part right. They got the easy conclusion that follows from it catastrophically wrong.

Hold that gap open. We will return to it, because it is not their gap alone.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Two Names They Were Made to Speak — *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm*

When the answer finally names the Creator, watch *which* names rise to the surface. Not "Allah" plainly. Not "the Merciful." The answer is: **ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ** — *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm* — **"the Almighty, the All-Knowing."**

Two names. Let us open each, then ask why *these two,* together, are the right answer to "Who created?"

**al-ʿAzīz** comes from the root **ع-ز-ز**. The Arabs used this root for something with a fascinating triple meaning. *ʿIzzah* is might and overpowering strength — the one who cannot be overcome. But the same root also means *rarity, preciousness* — *ʿazīz* is something so valuable it is hard to come by. And it carries the sense of *impenetrability* — land that is *ʿazāz* is hard, firm ground that resists the plough. Put them together and *al-ʿAzīz* is **the One of utter, unbreakable, irresistible might — strength that nothing can wear down, soften, or get past.**

**al-ʿAlīm** comes from **ع-ل-م**, knowledge — but in this intensive form, *ʿAlīm*, it means knowledge that is total, exhaustive, without gap or edge. Al-Muyassar glosses the verse beautifully: *"the All-Knowing of them and of everything within them — nothing is hidden from Him."*

Now — why these two, paired, as the answer to creation?

Teach yourself with something you already understand. Think of anything ever *made.* To bring something into being and have it actually work, two things are always required: **power** and **knowledge.** You need the *power* to do the thing — and the *knowledge* of how it should be done. A person with all the strength in the world but no understanding builds nothing but rubble. A person with perfect understanding but no power builds nothing at all. Every act of true creation is power and knowledge marrying.

Now lift your eyes to the heavens and the earth — the very things in question. The *scale* of it: governed by **al-ʿAzīz**, a might vast enough to hang galaxies on nothing. The *precision* of it: governed by **al-ʿAlīm**, a knowledge fine enough that not a leaf falls without it. The two names are not random. **They are the exact two attributes the act of creation logically demands.** Their own answer, without their realizing it, contains the complete proof.

[PAUSE]

But now feel the weight of *who* is named here, and circle it back to what they were doing.

They are bowing to idols. What is an idol? A thing with **no power** — it cannot move, cannot defend itself, cannot answer a prayer. And **no knowledge** — it does not know its worshipper, does not hear the whisper, cannot tell the sincere from the faithless. An idol is, precisely, the *absence* of al-ʿAzīz and al-ʿAlīm.

So in a single breath they confess that the universe was made by the One of all power and all knowledge — and then they turn to serve things that have neither. The two names are not just a description of God. **They are the verdict on the idol.** The answer they were *made* to speak indicts the very objects they kneel before.

There is one more thing the commentators noticed, and it is worth your attention. Look at the seam in the verse. Their reported answer is *"khalaqahunna"* — "He created them." And the magnificent naming — *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm* — al-Jalālayn reads as the speech continuing, *Allah Himself completing the description of who that Creator is.* The truth begins in their mouths and is finished in His. They start the confession; He perfects it. As if to say: *even your own answer is not fully yours — the truth borrows your tongue and then transcends it.*

---

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things. **First:** the confession of the Creator is sworn into them so deeply that Allah takes an oath about it — it is fiṭrah, the truth they cannot hold down. **Second:** their answer matches the question seam for seam, and then stops dead exactly where worship should have begun — the loudest silence in the verse. **Third:** the two names they were made to speak, *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm,* contain the full proof of God and the full verdict on the idol in a single breath.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about the human heart. About *your* heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Knowing Is Not the Same as Living

Let me start where you actually live.

Every one of us is carrying a truth right now that we have fully confessed and completely failed to act on.

The person who *knows* — with total certainty, no debate — that the cigarettes are killing them, and reaches for another. The one who *knows* this relationship is corroding them, and stays. The one who *knows* the prayer is the anchor of their day, and lets it slide to the edges of the day, and then off the edge. We are not, most of us, suffering from a *shortage of information.* We have the information. We can recite it. We would win a debate defending it.

And still we do the opposite.

There is a name for this in modern psychology — *cognitive dissonance,* the strange human capacity to hold a belief and a contradicting behavior side by side without the wall between them ever cracking. We are experts at it. We compartmentalize. We keep the thing we know in one room and the way we live in another, and we are careful never to open both doors at once.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what the idolaters of Makkah are doing. It is not a foreign disease. It is *this exact disease,* in its most naked form. They *know* — *la-yaqūlunna,* they will surely say it — that the Almighty, the All-Knowing made the heavens and the earth. The knowledge is not weak. It is sworn, certain, fiṭrah-deep. And it changes *nothing* about who they bow to in the morning.

This is the verse's quiet devastation. We tend to imagine that the disbeliever's problem is a *thinking* problem — that if they only *understood,* they would submit. The Qur'an dismantles that comfort. **Their problem was never that they didn't know. Their problem was that knowing didn't travel the eighteen inches from the head to the heart, and from the heart to the hands.**

And if knowledge of the Creator — the single most certain fact a soul can hold — could sit inertly inside them while they walked to their idols, then ask yourself, with some fear: *what truths are sitting inertly inside me?*

You believe Allah sees you. You *know* it. Do you live like someone seen? You believe death is coming and certain. You *know* it. Did it shape a single decision today? You believe this dunya is a passing shade and the next life is the real one. You would swear to it under oath — *la-yaqūlunna* — and then you spend yourself entirely on the shade.

[PAUSE]

The terror of this verse is that the idolater and the heedless believer are standing closer together than we would like. Both confess. Both fail to conclude. The gap that the grammar left open — *"the Almighty, the All-Knowing created them"… and then silence where worship should be* — that gap runs straight through the middle of our own lives.

So what does this *change* about tomorrow?

It moves the question. The question is no longer *"Do I believe?"* — you do, and so did they. The question becomes: **"Where, specifically, is the truth I have confessed not yet allowed to touch my hands?"** Name one. Just one. The prayer you believe in but rush. The tongue you know you should guard but don't. The wealth you know is a trust but treat as a possession. Take *one* confessed truth and force it the final eighteen inches into a single concrete act this week. That is the entire war. Not getting more knowledge — letting the knowledge you already swore to finally *land.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: He Who Made It All Is the One Who Knows You

Now let us go to the names, because they are not abstract. They are personal in a way that should make the hair stand up.

Begin again in your own experience. There is a particular loneliness peculiar to being human: the sense that no one fully knows you. People know *versions* of you — the work version, the family version, the version you post. But the whole of you — the thought you would never say aloud, the wound you've never named, the intention behind the action no one saw — that, you carry alone. We are, each of us, partly unwitnessed. And it aches.

Now hold that ache against the verse.

The Maker of the heavens and the earth is named here as **al-ʿAlīm** — and al-Muyassar told us what that means: *the One who knows them and everything within them; nothing is hidden from Him.* The same knowledge that designed the orbits of the stars is the knowledge trained on *you.* The intelligence vast enough to engineer the cosmos misses *nothing* of the small interior life you thought was unwitnessed.

For the heedless heart, that is a terror. For the awakened heart, it is the end of the deepest loneliness there is. **You are not unwitnessed. You never were.** The thought you couldn't say, the good you did that no one clapped for, the tear no one saw — al-ʿAlīm registered every one.

[PAUSE]

And He is not only al-ʿAlīm. He is **al-ʿAzīz** — and remember the triple meaning of that root: irresistible might, *and* preciousness, *and* unbreakable firmness. So the One who knows you completely is also the One whose power nothing can overcome — which means His care for you cannot be defeated, and His decree for you cannot be wrestled away by any rival force. There is no idol, no enemy, no circumstance with the strength to override what al-ʿAzīz has willed for the one He knows.

This is why the idolaters' choice is not just *wrong* — it is *tragic.* Step back and see what they did. They had, in their own confessed answer, the One of total knowledge and total power. The One who knew them entirely and could do anything. And they turned from Him — who knows everything and can do everything — toward objects that know *nothing* and can do *nothing.* They traded the All-Knowing for the blind, and the Almighty for the powerless.

Why would anyone do that?

Because — and here is the unbearable honesty of it — a stone idol makes no demands. It does not see your sins. It does not call you to account. It asks for a sacrifice and then leaves you alone. To worship the One who is *al-ʿAlīm* is to be fully known, and being fully known is frightening, because it means being fully responsible. The idol offers the relief of not being seen. People will trade a true God who knows them for a false god who leaves them comfortable.

[PAUSE]

So bring it home. When *you* turn from Allah toward something lesser — the approval of a crowd that does not really know you, the security of wealth that cannot really protect you, the comfort of a habit that cannot really hear you — you are doing, in your own quiet way, the thing the Makkans did out loud. You are leaving al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm for an idol with no power and no knowledge.

And what does this change about tomorrow? It reframes every act of worship. The next time you stand in prayer, stand as someone *fully known* — there is no point in posturing before al-ʿAlīm, so let the prayer be honest, ragged if it must be, but real. And carry one fear and one comfort out of this verse together: the fear that nothing is hidden, and the comfort that nothing is hidden. The same fact. The One who made the heavens and the earth knows the whole of you — **so stop running to what cannot see you, and turn, finally, to the One who already has.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this reflection were the same insight approached from two directions.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a confession sworn so deep it surfaces under oath — and then *stops,* short of the worship it demands. The **thematic depths** showed us that this is the universal human condition: we *know* and we do not *live;* we confess the Creator and run to idols that cannot see us.

The grammar is the diagnosis. The themes are the mirror.

And the architecture of the verse itself is the proof. Watch how it is built: it opens with an oath about *their certainty* — *la-yaqūlunna* — and it closes with the *two attributes their certainty contains* — *al-ʿAzīz al-ʿAlīm.* The verse begins with how sure they are and ends with *what they are sure of,* and the thing they are so sure of is the very thing that should have ended their idolatry. **The verse is a closed circle of self-indictment.** They walk in confessing and walk out condemned by their own confession — and the only step they had to take to be saved was the one step they refused: to let what they *knew* become what they *did.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one truth about Allah that I would swear to under oath — and yet live as though I did not believe?
2. Where, in my life, is the *gap* — the place where my confession ends and my behavior should have begun, but doesn't?
3. If knowing the Creator did not save the Makkans, what is it that actually saves — and do I have *that,* or only the knowing?
4. Have I ever turned from the One who fully knows me toward something that cannot see me, *because* being unseen felt safer than being responsible?
5. The same fact — *He knows everything about me* — is a terror to one heart and a comfort to another. Which is it to mine, today, and why?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The idolaters confessed, under the weight of an oath, that the Almighty and All-Knowing made the heavens and the earth — and then walked back to gods with no might and no knowledge, proving that the soul's crisis was never *not knowing* the truth, but refusing to let the truth it already held reach its hands.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being invited to *learn* something new. You already know more than you live. The invitation is smaller and harder than learning: take one truth you have already confessed — just one — and let it travel the final distance into a single, concrete act before this week is over. Close, even slightly, the gap the Makkans left open. That is what it means to refuse to walk out of this verse condemned by your own confession.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, al-ʿAzīz, al-ʿAlīm — You who created the heavens and the earth, and who know the whole of me that no one else has ever seen — do not let me be among those who confess You with the tongue and abandon You with the life. You know the truths I am holding without living. By Your might, move them the last distance into my hands; and by Your knowledge of me, accept the turning of a servant who has finally stopped running to what cannot see him. Āmīn.*
