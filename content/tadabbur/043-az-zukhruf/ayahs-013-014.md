# The Settled Rider

## How a Journey Becomes Worship and a Vehicle Becomes a Reminder of Home

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you back the world they came from.

> **لِتَسْتَوُۥا۟ عَلَىٰ ظُهُورِهِۦ ثُمَّ تَذْكُرُوا۟ نِعْمَةَ رَبِّكُمْ إِذَا ٱسْتَوَيْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ وَتَقُولُوا۟ سُبْحَـٰنَ ٱلَّذِى سَخَّرَ لَنَا هَـٰذَا وَمَا كُنَّا لَهُۥ مُقْرِنِينَ ۝ وَإِنَّآ إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا لَمُنقَلِبُونَ**

*Litastawū ʿalā ẓuhūrihi thumma tadhkurū niʿmata rabbikum idhā istawaytum ʿalayhi wa-taqūlū subḥāna alladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn. Wa-innā ilā rabbinā la-munqalibūn.*

"That you may settle yourselves firmly upon their backs, and then remember the favor of your Lord when you are settled upon them, and say: *Glory be to the One who subjected this to us, for we could never have brought it under our control. And indeed, to our Lord we are surely returning.*"

Now — the world.

### Rebuild the world

Imagine you are standing in the open country outside Makkah, fourteen hundred years ago. There is no engine anywhere on earth. There is no road in the sense you know roads. If you want to cross the desert — to reach family three weeks away, to carry trade, to flee danger — there is exactly one technology that can do it for you, and it is alive. It breathes. It has a will of its own. It is taller than you, heavier than you, and stronger than you by an order of magnitude you cannot close with your own muscle.

The camel.

Think about what it actually means for a human being to travel in that world. You walk up to an animal that could kill you with a single kick, that could refuse you, that could throw you and walk away — and you climb onto its back. And it *lets* you. It folds its legs. It carries your weight and your water and your children across a furnace of sand that would kill you on foot in a day. You are placing your entire survival on the back of a creature you did not make, cannot manufacture, and could never, by raw force, compel.

And here is the thing the original listener understood in his bones, the thing we have almost entirely lost: **this was never normal.** That the strong should carry the weak. That the wild should kneel for the soft-handed. There is nothing in the laws of force that requires it. A lion does not carry you. The sea does not hold up your ship because you asked nicely.

So when these verses came down, they were not describing a curiosity. They were naming a daily, life-or-death miracle that the people had stopped seeing — precisely *because* it happened every day.

This passage in Surah Az-Zukhruf is built as a closing argument. Allah has just said: ask them who created the heavens and the earth, and they will admit — *the Almighty, the All-Knowing* (43:9). They already concede the Creator. So Allah walks them, step by step, through what that Creator has done for them right now, in this life, with their own hands on the reins: the earth spread as a cradle, paths laid within it, rain sent down, and the pairs of cattle and ships made to carry them.

And then — at the very summit of that list — comes the saddle. The moment of mounting. The instant your body settles onto the back of the power that is not yours.

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us. Allah does not simply say: *and I gave you animals to ride.* He does something far stranger. He climbs into the saddle *with you* — and He puts words in your mouth. He scripts, syllable by syllable, exactly what you are supposed to *say* at the moment you sit down.

Why? Why would the Lord of the heavens and the earth care what comes out of your mouth in the half-second after you settle into a seat?

And there is a second thing, quieter, more unsettling. The verse begins with you mounting a camel for a journey across the desert. It ends — in the very same breath, the same sentence — with you returning to your Lord. Allah takes a sentence that started with a saddle and, without changing the subject, lands it on your death.

Stay with that. He did not change the subject. As far as Allah is concerned, **it is the same subject.**

Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Litastawū* — the verb that means "to finally come to rest"

Before we open the word, let me teach you a shape in Arabic, because once you see the shape you will never unsee it in this verse.

Arabic builds words from skeletons of consonants, and then it bends those skeletons into patterns that *add meaning*. One of those patterns is what grammarians call **Form VIII** — a pattern that often carries the sense of something done *for oneself*, something achieved, something that settles into the doer and becomes his own state. It's the difference, in feel, between "to spread" and "to spread oneself out and settle in."

Now hold that, and look at the first word of our verse:

> **لِتَسْتَوُۥا۟** — *litastawū*

The root is *s-w-y* (س و ي), and at its heart it means **balance, evenness, equilibrium** — for two things to come level, for something to be made even, settled, squared. From this same root comes the word for being *upright*, for things being *equal*, for a structure being *complete and balanced*.

And here it appears in Form VIII: *istawā* — which means **to settle, to be firmly established, to come to a stable, balanced rest upon something.**

Notice what the standard translations reach for. Not "that you may *sit* on their backs" — too weak. They say *settle firmly*, *mount and be established*, *sit securely*. The lexicons and the classical commentators gloss it the same way. Al-Jalālayn says *litastawū* means **li-tastaqirrū** — "that you may come to rest, to settle, to be at peace upon it."

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because it is doing something beautiful that English cannot quite carry.

The verb Allah chose for *getting on a camel* is the verb of **achieving equilibrium.** It is the language of balance restored, of stability attained. And think about what that physically describes. A camel rising to its feet with a rider on its back is one of the most violent, unbalancing motions in the natural world — it lurches backward, then forward, then up, and a first-time rider is certain he is about to be thrown into the sand. And then — it ends. The animal stands. And suddenly you are *up there*, high above the ground, and you are **still.** Balanced. At rest. *Settled.*

That precise moment — the moment the lurching stops and the equilibrium arrives — **that** is the moment Allah names. *Litastawū.* "That you may come to your settled rest upon their backs."

Now notice something, and this is the part I want you to really hold. **He says it twice.**

> **لِتَسْتَوُۥا۟ عَلَىٰ ظُهُورِهِۦ ... إِذَا ٱسْتَوَيْتُمْ عَلَيْهِ**
>
> "*that you may settle upon their backs* ... *when you have settled upon it*"

The same verb, the same root, opens the purpose and then opens the command. First as the *goal* of the gift — *so that* you may settle. Then as the *trigger* of the response — *when* you have settled. Allah brackets the entire instruction between two moments of *istiwā*, two moments of coming to rest.

Why repeat it? Why not say "when you have mounted" the second time, with a different word?

Because the repetition is a clock. It marks an *exact instant*. Not when you decide to travel. Not while you are struggling up. Not later, when you are bored on the long road. **The instant the balance arrives.** The half-second when the lurching stops and your body, for the first time, is held — high, level, secure — by a strength that is not your own.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this single word reveals. There is a precise moment, repeated in your life a hundred times a week, when a power outside you takes your weight and brings you to rest. And Allah is saying: *that exact moment* — the moment of being settled by something stronger than you — **is the moment I designed for you to remember Me.** The stability itself is the summons.

### Element Two: *Sakhkhara* and *Muqrinīn* — the two words that measure the gap

Now I want to put two words next to each other, because the verse puts them next to each other, and the whole theology of the verse lives in the space between them.

First teach the shape again. **Form II** in Arabic — you make it by doubling the middle consonant — very often *intensifies* a verb or makes it *causative*: it takes a simple state and makes one thing *impose* that state on another, often with force, repetition, thoroughness. "To know" becomes "to teach." "To be many" becomes "to multiply." It is the form of *making it so*, deliberately, completely.

Now the first word:

> **سَخَّرَ** — *sakhkhara*

Root *s-kh-r* (س خ ر), Form II, perfect tense. The base meaning is **to subdue, to subject, to compel into service** — to take something and *make* it submit to a use. In Form II it is causative and intensive: not merely "it serves," but *He forced it into service, He made it docile, He broke its resistance and bent it to your need.* Al-Baghawī glosses it with a word that is almost tender in its precision: **dhallala lanā** — "He made it *humble* for us, He gentled it, He made it lowly and yielding to our hand."

And it is in the **perfect tense** — *sakhkhara*, "He has already subjected." Done. Finished. Before you ever walked up to the animal. Before you were born. The submission of that strength to your weakness was completed by Allah while you were nowhere in the picture.

Now hold that word — *He subjected, He gentled, He bent its strength to you* — and read what comes right after:

> **وَمَا كُنَّا لَهُۥ مُقْرِنِينَ** — *wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn*

Root *q-r-n* (ق ر ن). And this root is gorgeous, so let me open it. *Qarana* means **to couple, to pair, to yoke together, to join two things as equals or partners.** From this root comes *qarīn* — your *companion*, the one paired to you. From this root comes *qarn* — a pairing, a coupling. The root is fundamentally about **two things being matched, joined, brought level enough to be bound together.**

*Muqrin* — the active participle here, in **Form IV** — carries the meaning the classical commentators give it: **one who is *capable* of something, one who is *equal to* a task, one who can *bring it under control* and *manage* it.** Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī gloss *muqrinīn* as **muṭīqīn** — "able, having the strength for it" — and al-Baghawī adds another reported gloss: **ḍābiṭīn** — "able to *restrain, control, hold it in check.*"

But feel the root underneath the gloss. *We could not have **yoked** it.* We were not its *qarīn*, its *match*, its *equal partner.* We could never have brought ourselves *level* with that strength so as to bind it to our will.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done? He has placed two words from two roots side by side, and the two roots are *measuring the same gap from opposite ends.*

*Sakhkhara* — from the height of divine power looking down: **I bent this strength to your smallness.**

*Mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn* — from the depth of human weakness looking up: **we could never have reached up and matched this strength to bind it.**

One word descends. One word confesses it could never have ascended. And the truth sits in the canyon between them: *the only reason you are sitting up here, balanced and at rest on a power that dwarfs you, is that Someone reached down and did for you the one thing you could never have done for yourself.*

This is not a verse about transportation. This is a verse about the structure of every gift you have ever received. **You are never the *qarīn* of the power that serves you.** You did not earn it by being its equal. It was bent to you. And the moment you forget that — the moment you start to feel like the saddle is your right and the strength beneath you is your achievement — you have lost the entire meaning of the ride.

### Element Three: *Ẓuhūrihi* — the back, and a grammar that breaks on purpose

There is one more thing, and it is the kind of thing you can read past a hundred times. Look closely:

> **عَلَىٰ ظُهُورِهِۦ** — *ʿalā ẓuhūrihi* — "upon **their backs** ... of **it**"

Here is the strangeness. **Ẓuhūr** — *backs* — is **plural.** But the pronoun attached to it — *-hi*, "its / his" — is **singular.** Word-for-word it reads almost broken: "upon the *backs* of *it*." Backs, plural. It, singular.

This is not an accident, and it is not a flaw, and the classical grammarians caught it immediately and explained exactly why it is there. Al-Qurṭubī reports it from Abū ʿUbayd and al-Farrāʾ; al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn note the same point. Earlier in the passage Allah spoke of *mā tarkabūn* — "**that which** you ride" — using the word *mā*, "that which," which is grammatically **singular in form** but **plural in meaning**, because it refers to an entire *category* — every mount, every vessel, the whole genus of things that carry you.

So Allah does something deliberate. He honors the *meaning* — there are countless backs, a whole species of carriers — and says **ẓuhūr**, backs, plural. And He honors the *form* of the word *mā* — singular — and says **-hi**, *its*. As al-Farrāʾ put it: it is like saying "the army" or "the troop" — one word, but a multitude underneath it.

[PAUSE]

Now why does this matter for *us*, sitting in cars and planes the original audience never imagined?

Because that little grammatical seam — *plural backs, singular it* — is the verse quietly refusing to be limited to camels. The word will not stay singular and it will not stay plural, because what it is really naming is a *category that has no end.* Backs upon backs upon backs — every carrier that has ever borne a human being. The camel's back. The horse's back. The deck of the ship. The seat of the car. The cabin of the airliner. The grammar itself is built to stretch across fourteen centuries and find you, today, settling into a seat the seventh-century listener could not have dreamed of.

And notice one more thing in this root. *Ẓ-h-r* — the same root that gives us *ẓahr*, "back" — also gives us *ẓāhir*, "the manifest, the apparent, the clearly visible," and *ẓahara ʿalā*, "to prevail over, to overcome." The *back* is the place of **carrying**, but it is also, in the root's own field of meaning, the place of **strength and prevailing.** The back is where the load goes precisely because the back is the seat of power. And the verse seats *you* — soft, light, incapable — exactly *there*, on the locus of a strength that, in any honest contest, would prevail over you in an instant.

You are riding on the back of *what should overpower you.* And it carries you instead.

### The bridge

So look at what these three discoveries have shown us, woven together.

A verb of **equilibrium**, *istiwā*, repeated like a clock to mark the exact instant a power outside you brings you to rest. Two words, *sakhkhara* and *muqrinīn*, measuring from both ends the unbridgeable gap between the strength that serves you and your own incapacity to have ever earned it. And a deliberate seam in the grammar, *plural backs, singular it*, stretching the whole image across every carrier in every century — and quietly reminding us that we are seated on the very locus of a strength that should have prevailed over us.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, and not just about camels, but about reality itself, and about the strange shape of a life that is, from beginning to end, a journey on a back that is not our own.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The forgetting that lives inside the everyday

Let me start where you actually live.

This morning, in all likelihood, you got into a car. You put a key in, or pressed a button, and a controlled series of explosions — fire, hundreds of times a second — pushed two tons of steel down a road at a speed no human being can run, while you sat inside it holding a coffee, thinking about something else entirely. You did not build that engine. You could not. You do not understand, in any real depth, the metallurgy that keeps it from melting, the chemistry of the fuel, the physics of combustion, the chain of ten thousand human beings and ten million prior discoveries that put you in that seat. You simply sat down. And it carried you.

And you felt — what? Almost certainly nothing. A faint impatience if it was cold. Annoyance at the traffic. The everyday is the great anesthetic. **The more reliably a gift arrives, the less we see it.** This is a law of the human heart, and it is merciless. The miracle that happens once makes you fall to your knees. The miracle that happens every single day makes you check your phone.

This is the exact disease the verse is treating. Notice the structure of the command:

> *litastawū* — that you may settle ... *thumma tadhkurū* — **then remember** ... *wa-taqūlū* — **and say**

Allah does not assume the remembrance will come on its own. He *commands* it. *Tadhkurū* — from *dh-k-r*, the root of both *remembering* and *mentioning* — because Allah knows the human heart will sit in the saddle of a daily miracle and feel **nothing.** The verb of remembrance is here precisely because forgetting is the default.

[PAUSE]

And here is the genius of *where* He places the remembrance. Not before the journey, when you are anxious and focused. Not at the destination, when you are relieved and grateful. He places it at the moment of *istiwā* — the moment of *settled comfort.* The moment you are most likely to forget, because the moment you are most at ease.

Think about your own life. When do you turn to Allah? Usually in the lurch — the diagnosis, the loss, the fear, the moment the camel is rising and you think you'll be thrown. And when do you forget Him? In the *istiwā.* When the diagnosis came back clear. When the money came through. When the marriage settled. When the lurching stopped and life held you, level and secure, and you exhaled — *and reached for your phone.*

Allah is targeting the blind spot. He is saying: the precise moment your guard drops — the moment of being comfortably carried — **that** is the moment I want your tongue to move. *Subḥāna alladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā.* Glory to the One who bent this to me. Because gratitude that only shows up in the lurch is not gratitude — it is panic wearing gratitude's clothes. The test of a grateful heart is not what it says when it's afraid. It is what it says when it's *comfortable.*

So here is the practical turn, and it is small enough to actually do. There is a transmitted practice here that is not abstract — it is precisely this. ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar reported that when the Prophet ﷺ would mount his camel setting out on a journey, he would say *Allāhu akbar* three times, and then recite these very words: *Subḥāna alladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn, wa-innā ilā rabbinā la-munqalibūn.* The verse became the dhikr of the road.

So tomorrow, when you sit in your car — at the *istiwā*, the moment you settle into the seat before you reach for anything else — say it. *Subḥāna alladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā.* Glory to the One who bent this to me, for I could never have built it or earned it. Just once. Watch what it does. Watch how a two-minute drive you have taken a thousand times, unseeing, suddenly becomes a thing you can *see.* The verse is not asking you to find a new miracle. It is asking you to stop being blind to the one you are sitting inside of.

### Theme Two: Every journey is *the* journey

Now we come to the strangest move in the entire passage, and I want to walk into it slowly.

You are seated. You have been taught what to say about the gift beneath you. And then, in the *very same sentence*, with no break, no new topic, no transition, Allah adds:

> **وَإِنَّآ إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا لَمُنقَلِبُونَ**
>
> "And indeed, to our Lord we are surely returning."

Stop. We were talking about *transportation.* About the back of a camel, the seat of a vehicle. And without changing the grammar, without even taking a breath, the verse turns to face your *death.* "To our Lord we are surely returning."

The word is *munqalibūn*, from the root *q-l-b* (ق ل ب) — the root of the **heart** (*qalb*), and the root of **turning over, overturning, returning, being flipped back to where you came from.** *Munqalib* — "one who is turned back, who returns, who is brought back round." It is the word for a thing completing its arc and coming home.

And it is fixed to *la-* — the *lām* of emphatic certainty — *la-munqalibūn*: "we are **surely, certainly, beyond all doubt** returning." Of everything in this verse — the gift, the comfort, the praise — the one thing stamped with absolute certainty is the *return.*

[PAUSE]

Why here? Why graft the afterlife onto a verse about saddles?

Because — and this is the whole secret of the passage — **Allah is teaching you to read.** He is teaching you that the journey beneath you is a *sign*, and a sign is something that points past itself. Every time you mount a vehicle, you are enacting, in miniature, the shape of your entire existence:

You begin somewhere. You are carried by a power that is not your own. You did not make the road. You did not make the carrier. You are passing *through* a place you do not own toward a destination set before you climbed aboard. And you will, with absolute certainty, *arrive.*

That is a trip across the desert. **And it is also your life.**

Look at what the verse has done architecturally. It opened with *istiwā* — settling onto the back of your ride. It closes with *inqilāb* — the turning-back to your Lord. *Settling out, turning back.* Departure, return. The verse is shaped like a journey because *it is a journey*, and it ends where every journey ends — not at the marketplace you were headed to, but at the Lord you were always, underneath every smaller trip, traveling toward.

[PAUSE]

Now feel why this is mercy and not threat.

The disbelievers in this surah have made the *dunyā* the destination. The whole quarrel of Az-Zukhruf is in its name — *zukhruf*, "gold, ornament, glitter." Later in the surah Allah will say that were it not that mankind would become one disbelieving nation, He would have given the rejecters houses of silver and stairways of gold to climb (43:33-35) — *because none of it is worth anything.* The glitter is the trap of mistaking the vehicle for the destination, the road for the home.

And here, in verses 13 and 14, Allah hands you the antidote *before* the poison is even named. He takes the most ordinary act of the *dunyā* — getting from here to there — and welds onto it the reminder that *here-to-there is not the real journey.* That every comfortable seat you have ever settled into is a borrowed seat on a borrowed back, carrying you through a borrowed place, toward the one destination that is not borrowed and not optional: *ilā rabbinā* — *to our Lord.*

So consider what this does to a single ordinary day. You are carried to work — and you are being carried home, to Allah. You travel to see family you love — and you are traveling, underneath it, to the Family of the Hereafter, to your Lord. Every *istiwā*, every settling-in, contains within it the *inqilāb*, the turning-back. The small journey is a rehearsal for the great one. And the man who says, every time he sits down to travel, *and indeed, to our Lord we are surely returning* — that man cannot, will not, mistake the road for the home. He has trained his tongue, and through it his heart, to see *through* every destination to the only one that lasts.

Here is the practical turn. The traveler's *duʿā* is not two prayers stitched together — *thank You for the ride* and, oh, also, *we're going to die.* It is one seamless vision. Gratitude for the carrier, and clarity about the destination, are the *same awareness.* So when you say it tomorrow — at the *istiwā*, in your car — do not drop the ending. Say the whole thing. *Subḥāna alladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn — wa-innā ilā rabbinā la-munqalibūn.* Let the praise and the return arrive in the same breath, the way Allah sent them. Because the point is not to be grateful for the journey. The point is to never forget, even in the comfort of the journey, **where the journey actually ends.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and look at the whole shape, because the pieces fit together with a precision you can only see once all the work is done.

The verse is built as an arc — a departure and a return:

- It **opens** with *istiwā* — you settling onto the back of a power not your own.
- It **rises** through *sakhkhara* — the confession that this power was *bent* to you from above.
- It **turns** at *muqrinīn* — the confession that you could never have *reached up* and earned it.
- It **lands** at *inqilāb* — the turning-back, the certain return, to the One who did all of it.

*Settle out — be carried — confess your incapacity — turn back home.*

That is the shape of the verse. **And it is the shape of a life.** You were settled into existence by a power not your own. You are carried every day by gifts you could never have earned. The honest heart confesses it was never the *qarīn*, the equal, the deserver, of any of it. And you are — *la-munqalibūn*, with absolute certainty — turning back to the One who began it.

### Bringing it together

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The *grammar* told us: the moment of *settled equilibrium* is the trigger for remembrance — *plural backs, singular it*, a category with no end, finding you in your car today. The *theme* told us: the everyday comfort is exactly where the heart goes blind, and the cure is a moved tongue at the moment of ease.

The *grammar* told us: *sakhkhara* descends and *muqrinīn* confesses it could never ascend — the gift lives in the gap between them. The *theme* told us: you are never the *qarīn* of the power that serves you, and the instant you feel entitled to the saddle, you have lost the ride.

And both arrive, together, at *la-munqalibūn* — the certainty of return that turns every small journey into a sign of the only journey that matters.

One verse. A saddle and a death, in a single breath, because to Allah they were always the same subject.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them genuinely open.

1. What is the *istiwā* in my life right now — the place where the lurching has stopped and I am being held, comfortably, by a strength that is not mine? And have I said a single word of glory from inside that comfort, or only from inside my fear?

2. Where have I begun to feel like the *qarīn* — the equal, the deserver — of a gift that was only ever *bent* to me? My health? My income? My family? My mind?

3. If every journey I take is a rehearsal for the journey to my Lord, what is the quality of my traveling? Am I a passenger who has confused the road with the home?

4. The verse commands remembrance — *tadhkurū* — because forgetting is the default. What is the daily miracle I have gone most completely blind to, precisely because it has never once failed me?

5. When was the last time my tongue moved in praise not because something went wrong, but simply because I had settled, safely, into an ordinary seat?

### One-sentence distillation

Every time you settle atop a power you did not create and could never have earned, you are being handed the words that turn a journey into worship and a vehicle into a reminder that you, too, are being carried home.

### The closing invitation

Tomorrow you will sit down into something that carries you. A car, a bus, a train, a plane, a seat on a road you did not build, behind an engine you could not make. There will be a half-second — the *istiwā* — when the motion settles and you are simply *held.*

In that half-second, before you reach for your phone, let your tongue move. *Subḥāna alladhī sakhkhara lanā hādhā wa-mā kunnā lahu muqrinīn, wa-innā ilā rabbinā la-munqalibūn.*

Not as a ritual to complete. As eyes opening. As a man who, for one moment, saw the back he was riding on, and remembered Who bent it to him, and remembered where the road truly leads.

You don't have to find a new miracle. You only have to stop being blind to the one you are sitting inside of.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You bent to my smallness a strength I could never have earned, and You carried me when I could not carry myself. Move my tongue to glorify You from inside my comfort, not only from inside my fear. And let me never mistake the road for the home — for to You, with certainty, I am returning.

*Āmīn.*
