# The Rivers Beneath His Feet: When Power Has to Ask You to Look

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to the words first. Let them land before we touch them.

> **وَنَادَىٰ فِرْعَوْنُ فِى قَوْمِهِۦ قَالَ يَـٰقَوْمِ أَلَيْسَ لِى مُلْكُ مِصْرَ وَهَـٰذِهِ ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِىٓ ۖ أَفَلَا تُبْصِرُونَ**

*wa nādā firʿawnu fī qawmihi qāla yā qawmi a-laysa lī mulku miṣra wa hādhihi-l-anhāru tajrī min taḥtī, a-fa-lā tubṣirūn*

"And Pharaoh called out among his people; he said: *O my people — is not the kingdom of Egypt mine? And these rivers, flowing beneath me? Do you not then see?*"

And then:

> **أَمْ أَنَا۠ خَيْرٌ مِّنْ هَـٰذَا ٱلَّذِى هُوَ مَهِينٌ وَلَا يَكَادُ يُبِينُ**

"Or am I not better than this one who is lowly — who can hardly even make himself clear?"

> **فَلَوْلَآ أُلْقِىَ عَلَيْهِ أَسْوِرَةٌ مِّن ذَهَبٍ أَوْ جَآءَ مَعَهُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ مُقْتَرِنِينَ**

"Why, then, have bracelets of gold not been thrown upon him — or angels come with him, rank on rank?"

> **فَٱسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُۥ فَأَطَاعُوهُ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ قَوْمًا فَـٰسِقِينَ**

"So he made his people light — and they obeyed him. Indeed, they were a people gone astray."

### Rebuild the world

Step into the throne room. Or rather — step into wherever it was that this happened, because, as we'll see, the place matters less than the *volume*.

This is the most powerful man on earth. Not a metaphor — in the world of that moment, literally so. He governs the wealthiest civilization of the ancient world. The Nile, the great artery that made Egypt the breadbasket of antiquity, runs through his land. Temples carved to last forever. A bureaucracy, an army, a priesthood, a treasury. When he says a thing is so, it becomes law. There is no court above his court, no king beside his throne.

And into this world walks a man. Alone, or nearly so. A man who grew up in this very palace and then fled it as a fugitive, a man who herded sheep in the desert for years, a man who — by his own admission to his Lord — has a knot in his tongue, a difficulty in his speech. He carries no army. He carries a staff and a message.

And something has begun to happen. The signs have come. The court magicians have already fallen into prostration. There is a tremor in the room. Pharaoh can feel his people beginning to *lean* — to lean toward this man and his strange certainty.

So what does the most powerful man on earth do?

He calls a meeting. He raises his voice. And he begins to *list his possessions.*

### The Hook

Here is what should stop us cold before we go a single step further.

The most powerful man on earth — the one who needs nothing, who answers to no one — stands before his people and *asks them a question.* Three questions, actually. *Isn't the kingdom mine? Don't you see? Aren't I better than him?*

[PAUSE]

Why is he asking?

Why does the man who owns everything need you to *agree* that he owns it? Why does the man whose greatness is supposedly self-evident have to stand up and *point at it*?

There is something hidden in this scene — something the Qur'an wants us to catch not by telling us, but by letting Pharaoh hang himself with his own grammar. Every word he chooses betrays him. And the last thing the passage says about his people is a single, devastating verb that explains exactly what a man like this *does* to the souls of those who follow him.

Let's go and find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: نَادَىٰ — The Man Who Had to Shout

Let's begin with a concept before we touch the word.

In any language, there is a difference between *saying* something and *announcing* it. If I lean over to you and quietly tell you a fact, that is one kind of speech. If I climb onto a chair in a crowded room and *proclaim* it at the top of my voice — that is something else entirely. The content might be identical. But the second one tells you something the first one doesn't: it tells you I *needed an audience.* I needed it heard. I needed it *witnessed.*

Hold that difference. Now look at the very first word.

Allah does not say *qāla firʿawn* — "Pharaoh said." He says:

> **وَنَادَىٰ فِرْعَوْنُ** — "And Pharaoh **called out**."

The verb is *nādā*. Its root carries the sense of calling to someone across a distance — the call of the herald, the summons, the cry meant to *carry*. Grammatically it sits in what Arabic calls Form III, the pattern that bends an action *toward* an audience, that aims speech across a gap at someone you are trying to reach. You don't *nādā* to a person standing at your shoulder. You *nādā* across a courtyard. Across a crowd. Across a distance you need your voice to cross.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The classical commentators paused here too, and they didn't fully agree on the mechanics — and their disagreement is itself a window. Al-Qurṭubī records more than one picture. In one, Pharaoh gathered the great men of his court and *raised his voice* among them, so that the proclamation would then ripple outward through all the Copts — as if he had called out among them all. In another, recorded from Ibn Jurayj, he didn't even use his own voice: he *commanded heralds* to go out and announce it through the people.

Don't treat these as rival theories you have to pick between. Lay them on top of each other and look at the single shape they make. Whether he shouted it himself or sent criers to shout it for him, the meaning is the same: **this was not a conversation. This was a broadcast.** A public-address campaign. A man flooding his kingdom with the sound of his own greatness.

And here is the thing to sit with.

The truly secure do not do this.

The sun does not announce that it is bright. The mountain does not call a press conference to declare that it is tall. Reality that is *actually* solid has no need to be proclaimed; it simply *is*, and you bump into it. It is precisely the thing that is *not* solid — the thing that feels the ground shifting beneath it — that has to raise its voice.

Pharaoh *nādā* because Pharaoh was *frightened.* Al-Qurṭubī says it plainly: when he saw the signs, he feared the people's hearts inclining toward Mūsā, so he gathered them. The shout is not the sound of strength. **The shout is the sound of a man who has felt his foundation move.**

The grammar gave him away in the first word. He hadn't even made his argument yet, and the verb had already confessed his fear.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِىٓ — Owning What Flows Away

Now look at what he actually points to. His evidence. The exhibits he presents to prove his greatness.

> **وَهَـٰذِهِ ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِىٓ**
> "And these rivers, flowing beneath me."

First, a small word doing enormous work: **هَـٰذِهِ** — *hādhihi*, "*these*." It's a demonstrative; it points. He is gesturing. *These* rivers — the ones you can see from here, the ones glinting right there beyond the columns. He is not making an abstract claim. He is pointing his finger at water. *Look. Look at the proof. It's right there.*

And what is the proof? **The rivers run beneath me.** *Taḥtī* — beneath me, under me, under my feet.

The commentators unpack the "beneath" with a beautiful range, and again, layer them rather than choose. Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī read it as *beneath my palaces* — the canals of the Nile threading under his elevated halls. Qatāda heard it as *flowing before me, through my gardens and orchards* — the rivers as private landscaping for a god-king. And al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī heard something else entirely: *they flow by my command* — as if the water itself obeyed his will.

Put all three together and you get the full portrait of the claim: *Everything that moves in this land moves under me, around me, for me, by me. Even the river is my subject.*

Now. Stay with me, because this is where the Qur'an does something almost unbearably precise.

He chose the *river* as his proof of ownership. And the verb he uses for it is **تَجْرِى** — *tajrī*, "flowing," "running." It is in the present tense, the tense of continuous, ongoing motion. The river is *always flowing.* That is what a river *is.*

But think about what that means.

[PAUSE]

**A river is the one thing you can never actually own.** The water you are pointing at — *these* rivers, *hādhihi* — by the time you finish the sentence, that water is gone. It has flowed past. It is downstream now, miles away, on its way to the sea. The river that is "beneath you" is, by its very nature, *leaving you* every single second. It does not stay. It cannot be held. Its entire essence is that it *passes.*

Pharaoh chose, as the monument to his permanence, **the most temporary thing in the landscape.** He pointed at flowing water and said: *that is mine, forever.* And the water, even as he pointed, was already flowing away from him.

And then — the irony the original listener could never miss, because they knew the end of the story before Pharaoh did. *These rivers, flowing beneath me.* This same Nile, this same water he claimed flowed *beneath his feet* in submission — these same waters would one day **close over his head.** The thing he stood *above* would become the thing that swallowed him. The proof of his dominion would become the instrument of his drowning.

He said the rivers flow *beneath* me.

They were, in the end, *above* him.

[PAUSE — let it sit]

This is the Qur'an's method everywhere: it points at something physical you can see — flowing water — and uses it to teach you something about a reality you cannot see. And the unseen reality here is this: **everything you point to as proof of your permanence is, in truth, flowing away from you.** Your wealth, your status, your strength, your youth — *these,* you say, *hādhihi,* these are mine. And all of it is a river. All of it is *tajrī.* All of it is, even now, flowing past your feet and out toward the sea.

### Element Three: أَفَلَا تُبْصِرُونَ — and فَٱسْتَخَفَّ — The Two Verbs That Frame Everything

Now I want to put two verbs side by side — one at the start of the passage, one at the end — because together they are the secret architecture of the whole scene.

The first is how Pharaoh ends his opening proclamation:

> **أَفَلَا تُبْصِرُونَ** — "Do you not, then, **see**?"

The verb is *tubṣirūn*, from the root *b-ṣ-r*, the root of sight, of vision, of perception. Notice what his entire case rests on: **the eye.** *Look at my kingdom. Look at these rivers. Can't you SEE?* His whole argument is an appeal to the visible. He is asking them to use their eyes — and to let their eyes do their thinking for them. *The evidence is what you can see. So look, and be convinced.*

Hold that — *tubṣirūn,* "do you not see" — and now travel to the last thing the passage says about how it all turned out:

> **فَٱسْتَخَفَّ قَوْمَهُۥ فَأَطَاعُوهُ** — "So he **made his people light**, and they obeyed him."

The verb is *istakhaffa.* Its root is *kh-f-f* — the root of *lightness,* of weightlessness, of having no heft. From this root come words for the feather-light, the flighty, the things that have no gravity to them. And the form here, the *istaf'ala* pattern, opens onto a lexical range the commentators and lexicographers preserve: *he found them light* — light-witted, lacking the weight of judgment — *and so he made light of them, and stirred them into recklessness, swept them up.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at what just happened across these four verses.

He began by commanding them to *see* — *tubṣirūn.* He ended by making them *weightless* — *istakhaffa.* And those two are not separate events. **The second is the result of the first.**

When you teach a people that reality is only what the eye can see — when you train them to weigh everything by the visible, the glittering, the gold — you do not make them wise. You make them *light.* You hollow them out. Because the things that give a soul *weight* — truth, conscience, the unseen, the bond with the Real — are exactly the things the eye cannot see. Strip a person down to their eyes, and you strip them of their gravity. And a soul with no weight can be *moved* by anything. Swept by any current. Like the river he was so proud of, they now simply *flowed* wherever he pointed.

He asked, *Don't you see?* — and the tragedy is that they looked, and obeyed, and in looking lost the very weight that might have let them stand.

And then the final, surgical diagnosis: **إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ قَوْمًا فَـٰسِقِينَ** — "Indeed, they were a people gone astray." The verb *fasaqa,* at root, is what a date does when it bursts out of its skin — when the kernel breaks free of what was meant to contain it. They had slipped out of their own shells. They had no skin left to hold them in. Weightless, unhoused, they could be carried anywhere.

Notice, too, where the Qur'an lays the responsibility. *He* made them light — *but they obeyed.* *Fa-aṭāʿūhu.* The tyrant pours, but the cup has to be willing to be empty. He could only make light a people who had already let go of their own weight.

### The bridge

So look at what the construction has shown us, word by word, before we've drawn a single "lesson."

The *verb of his speech* — *nādā* — confessed his fear before he argued. The *evidence he pointed to* — *tajrī min taḥtī* — was a thing that, by its nature, flows away and would one day rise to drown him. And the *two framing verbs* — *tubṣirūn* and *istakhaffa* — revealed the whole mechanism: appeal to the eyes, and you hollow out the soul.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About power. And about the scale on which we, every one of us, secretly weigh our own worth.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Theater of Power — and the Fear Underneath It

Let me start where you actually live.

You have seen this. You have scrolled past it a hundred times. The post that is just a little too eager to show you the car, the watch, the view from the seat, the table at the restaurant. The person who can't tell you about their day without telling you who they had lunch with. The colleague who works the word "obviously" into every sentence to remind you they're the smart one in the room.

And here is the quiet thing you already know, somewhere, about all of it: **the display is in inverse proportion to the security.** The people you've met who were genuinely, deeply accomplished were almost never the ones telling you about it. The ones who needed you to *know* — those were the ones who weren't sure themselves.

We have a word for this now. We call it *insecurity.* The need for external validation. The performance of a self you're not certain you possess. We pay therapists to help us see it in ourselves.

And here is the Qur'an, fourteen centuries ago, handing us the purest case study in human history — and letting us *diagnose it ourselves.*

Because look again at the scene with fresh eyes. The most powerful man alive. And what is he doing? He is *flexing.* He is, in the most literal sense, posting his net worth. *Isn't the kingdom mine? Look at the rivers. Aren't I better than him?* This is the ancient throne-room version of every anxious display you have ever scrolled past. And it comes from the man who, of everyone in history, supposedly had the *least* to prove.

[PAUSE]

So let the question land on us properly: **why does power perform?**

Because performed power isn't power. It's *fear wearing power's clothes.* Real authority is silent — it doesn't need your agreement, because your agreement adds nothing to it. The moment a person *needs* you to witness their greatness, they have told you that, without your witnessing, the greatness might not exist. They have outsourced their reality to your eyes. And anything that depends on an audience can be taken away the moment the audience leaves.

That is why Pharaoh, who feared nothing on earth, was in fact the most frightened man in his kingdom. The tremor in the room — the people leaning toward Mūsā — terrified him precisely because his power was *made of* their attention. Mūsā had something Pharaoh could never have: a worth that did not depend on a single pair of eyes in that room. Mūsā could have been ignored by every person present and lost *nothing,* because his standing was with the One who doesn't need to look to see. **And that is the most threatening thing in the world to a man whose entire self is a performance — to stand in front of someone who simply does not need the show.**

Now bring it home, because it would be too easy to leave this with Pharaoh.

The performance instinct is not a tyrant's disease. It's a human one. We perform our piety, sometimes, more than we *live* it. We perform our generosity. We perform our certainty in arguments we secretly aren't sure of. And every time we do, we are running Pharaoh's program on a smaller scale: *needing to be seen* in order to feel real.

So here is the practical turn — the thing to carry into tomorrow. The next time you feel that pull to display — to mention the accomplishment, to make sure they noticed, to post the proof — pause and ask the Pharaoh question: *Am I sharing this, or am I shouting it? And if I'm shouting — what am I afraid isn't true unless they agree?* The answer, when you're honest, is almost always a small fear. And the cure for the fear is not a bigger audience. It is the same cure that was always on offer in that throne room and that Pharaoh refused: to anchor your worth in the One who sees you when no one is watching — so that you no longer need anyone to be.

### Theme Two: The Wrong Scale — Gold Bracelets and the Weight of a Soul

Now the deepest cut. The one this entire surah is built around.

Here is where you live again. We all carry an invisible scale. All day, without noticing, we are weighing people — and weighing ourselves. And the weights we put on the scale are almost always *the ones we can see.* The car in the driveway. The title on the email signature. The school the kids got into. The followers. The fluency, the polish, the way someone *carries* a room. We say we don't judge by these things. Our scale does it anyway, automatically, a thousand times a day.

Now watch Pharaoh load his scale, out loud, in front of us. He looks at Mūsā — a Messenger of God, carrying the clearest truth a human being can carry — and here is his appraisal:

> **هَـٰذَا ٱلَّذِى هُوَ مَهِينٌ وَلَا يَكَادُ يُبِينُ**
> "this one who is *lowly* — and can hardly even make himself *clear*."

*Mahīn* — from the root of lowliness, weakness, of being held in contempt; the word for someone with no standing, no resources, no weight in the world's eyes. And *lā yakādu yubīn* — "he can hardly make himself clear" — a contemptuous jab at the very knot in Mūsā's tongue, the speech difficulty Mūsā had once begged his Lord to ease.

Look at the two failings Pharaoh lists. *He's poor. And he's not eloquent.* That's it. That's the whole indictment. By Pharaoh's scale — wealth and presentation — Mūsā weighs nothing.

And then Pharaoh names exactly what *would* have impressed him:

> **فَلَوْلَآ أُلْقِىَ عَلَيْهِ أَسْوِرَةٌ مِّن ذَهَبٍ أَوْ جَآءَ مَعَهُ ٱلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةُ مُقْتَرِنِينَ**
> "Why have *bracelets of gold* not been thrown on him, or *angels* come with him in ranks?"

There it is. *Gold.* If God really favored this man, says Pharaoh, *where's his bling?* Where's the jewelry, the entourage, the spectacle? Where's the *zukhruf*?

[PAUSE]

And now you need to know the name of the surah you are standing in. It is called **Az-Zukhruf.** *Zukhruf* means *gold ornament* — adornment, embellishment, gilding, the glittering decoration laid over a surface. This entire chapter is an argument about gold. And here, at its heart, Pharaoh stands up and demands gold as proof of God's favor — and in doing so, he speaks the surah's whole error out of his own mouth.

Because just pages earlier, this same surah told us something staggering. It said that if it were not for the risk that all humanity would rush toward disbelief, Allah would have given the rejecters of faith *houses with roofs of silver,* staircases of silver, gold ornaments without end — *and all of that*, the surah says, *is nothing but the fleeting enjoyment of this lower life.* In other words: **gold is so meaningless to God that He nearly hands it out to the people who reject Him.** Wealth is not the prize. It is almost the consolation given to those who lose the real one.

So when Pharaoh demands gold bracelets as the signature of divine favor, the surah has *already* dismantled him. He is using, as his gold standard for worth, the one currency God treats as nearly worthless. He has the scale exactly upside down.

[PAUSE]

And here is the unbearable irony of his appraisal of Mūsā. He called him *mahīn* — weightless, of no account. He mocked him as a man who *couldn't even make himself clear.* But we have already learned, in this very passage, where real weight comes from and where it goes. Pharaoh, with all his gold, was about to *istakhaffa* — to make his entire nation *light,* weightless, swept off like the river. And Mūsā, the man with empty hands and a knot in his tongue, carried a weight that would outlast Egypt itself — a weight that, three thousand years later, you and I are still feeling in this room.

The man drowning in gold was the lightweight. The man with nothing was the one with gravity.

*Lā yakādu yubīn* — "he can hardly make himself clear." And yet whose words still ring? Whose message still *bears down* on human hearts across the centuries? Not the eloquent god-king who could flood a kingdom with proclamations. The one with the knot in his tongue. **Because clarity was never about the smoothness of the speech. It was about the weight of the truth behind it.**

So bring it onto your own scale.

The thing to carry into tomorrow is a single, ruthless habit: *catch your scale in the act.* The next time you feel yourself sizing someone up — or sizing yourself *down* — by what you can see, by the gold, by the polish, by the markers, stop and ask: *Am I weighing this person the way Pharaoh weighed Mūsā?* The poor one, the awkward one, the one who can't quite get the words out, the one with no entourage — by the world's scale they weigh nothing. And the world's scale is the one that drowned Pharaoh.

There is another scale. On the Day the real weighing happens, no one will be asked for their gold bracelets. The things that will give a soul *weight* on that Day are precisely the things the eye could never see — the sincerity, the secret prayer, the kindness no one witnessed, the truth held onto when it cost everything. *Those* have gravity. Everything else is *zukhruf* — gilding over a surface, glittering, weightless, flowing away beneath your feet even as you point to it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see how tightly these four verses are built — because the structure is the proof.

Watch the movement:

- **Verse 51** — Pharaoh points *outward,* at the visible: the kingdom, the rivers. *Don't you SEE?* He builds his case on the **eye.**
- **Verse 52** — He turns the eye on a *person,* and weighs Mūsā on the visible scale: poor, inarticulate. *Mahīn.*
- **Verse 53** — He names the missing visible proof: *gold,* the *zukhruf,* the spectacle of angels. The scale is fully exposed.
- **Verse 54** — The result: he makes his people **light** — *istakhaffa* — weightless, and they flow wherever he points.

It begins with *seeing* and ends with *weightlessness.* And that is the entire spiritual physics of the passage: **train a soul to live by the eye, and you rob it of its weight.** The opening verb and the closing verb are the two ends of a single chain. Pharaoh asked them to look — and looking is exactly what hollowed them out.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight seen from two directions.

The *grammar* showed us a frightened man — *nādā,* the shout of insecurity — pointing at a river that was flowing away from him even as he claimed it, asking a people to *see,* and in the end making them *light.* The *themes* showed us the same thing in the language of the soul: power that performs because it is afraid; a scale that weighs by gold and so weighs everything wrong; and a people hollowed out until they had no gravity left to resist.

Pharaoh stood on the bank of a river he thought he owned, holding a scale he thought measured worth, and he was wrong about both. The river was leaving him. The scale was upside down. And the only man in the story with any real weight was the one he called weightless.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life do I *nādā* — where do I shout, perform, display — and what small fear is underneath the volume?
2. What am I pointing to as proof of my permanence — and is it, honestly, a river *flowing away beneath my feet*?
3. Whose scale am I using when I weigh other people? When I weigh myself? Is it the scale of the eye — or the scale of the unseen?
4. If gold is so light to God that He nearly gives it to those who reject Him — what am I actually chasing, and what does that say about where I've placed my worth?
5. What gives *my* soul weight? Name one thing I do that no eye sees — and ask whether there is enough of it to hold me down when the current comes.

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you are taught to worship what the eye can see, you are being taught to become weightless — and a weightless soul will be swept away by whatever current its master points to.

### The Closing Invitation

This is not, in the end, a passage about a dead king. Pharaoh is just the clearest mirror. The invitation is to walk out of this scene and quietly check two things: the *volume* of your life — how much of it is shout, display, performance, the need to be seen — and the *scale* of your life — what you are using to measure who matters, including you. And then to begin, gently, to transfer your weight off the river and onto the ground that doesn't move. Not because you're commanded to perform less, but because a soul with real gravity is the only thing in this story that didn't drown.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who see without needing to look — free us from the need to be seen. Give our souls the weight of sincerity, so that no current can sweep us off; and let us never weigh ourselves, or anyone, by the gold that flows away beneath our feet. Make us, like Your servant Mūsā, heavy with truth and light of all else. *Āmīn.*
