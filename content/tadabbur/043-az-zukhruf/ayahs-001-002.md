# The Unclear Letters and the Clear Book

### Surah az-Zukhruf, Ayahs 1–2

> **حمٓ**
> *Ḥā Mīm.*
>
> **وَٱلْكِتَٰبِ ٱلْمُبِينِ**
> *Wa'l-kitābi'l-mubīn.*
>
> "Ha Meem. By the clear Book —"

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Surah of Gold

Let me put you somewhere before we open a single word.

You are in Makkah. The city runs on trade, and trade runs on gold. Status here is not a feeling — it is a number. It is the size of your caravan, the weight of your purse, the height of your house, the names you can claim among your ancestors. When the people of this city look at a man, the first thing they calculate is his worth, and by *worth* they mean exactly what you think they mean.

Into this city comes a man with a recitation. And the city does the only arithmetic it knows. They look at him and they say — the Qur'an records their words just a few lines into this very surah — **"Why was this Qur'an not sent down upon a great man from the two cities?"** (43:31). A *great* man. They had a man of overwhelming character standing in front of them, and they could not see him, because he did not arrive plated in gold.

This is the surah of **az-Zukhruf** — and the word *zukhruf* means *gold ornament, gilding, the decorative plating you lay over a thing to make it glitter*. Allah named an entire surah after the thing this people had mistaken for value. He named it after their idol — not a stone idol this time, but the idol of *adornment*, the worship of surface, the conviction that what shines must be what matters.

Classical commentators are precise about what the surah is actually doing with this name: it places the gold of this world squarely in the category of what *perishes*. The surah names *zukhruf* specifically in the context of describing the fleeting pleasures of the here-and-now, then sets them against the permanent pleasures of what comes next. The surah's name is an indictment and an invitation held in the same word: *here is the thing you built your entire civilization around — now watch what happens when eternity opens.* The charge against the Quraysh was not merely that they loved gold. It was that they had staked their permanent worth on the most temporary thing they owned.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that. A people drowning in gold, measuring every soul by its plating.

And how does Allah open His address to them?

He does not open with gold. He opens with two bare letters and an oath. He opens with **ḥā mīm** — and then He swears. He swears not by the Ka'bah, not by the heavens, not by the dawn. He swears **by the Book**. By the Clear Book.

Before this surah says one word against their wealth, it has already moved the treasure. It has taken *value* out of their hands and laid it somewhere else entirely. *You think gold is the precious thing? I swear to you by the Book.* The real treasure of this people was sitting in their language, in their letters, in a recitation they could have produced from the same alphabet they used to write their poetry and tally their debts — and they were too busy weighing gold to notice.

Here is what I want you to feel the strangeness of, and carry it through everything we do today:

The Clear Book — *al-kitāb al-mubīn*, the Book that makes everything plain — opens with two letters whose meaning Allah did not make plain. The clearest thing ever sent down begins with something deliberately withheld.

[PAUSE]

Why would the Book that clarifies everything begin with something it refuses to clarify?

Hold that question. We are going to earn its answer.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: حم — The Letters With the Meaning Withheld

Let me teach you something before we touch these two letters, because if you don't have it, you'll rush past the most honest moment in the opening of this surah.

There are twenty-nine surahs in the Qur'an that begin with detached, single letters — *alif lām mīm*, *kāf hā yā ʿayn ṣād*, *ṭā hā*, *yā sīn*, and our two today, *ḥā mīm*. The scholars call these **al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿāt** — *the disconnected letters*. They are letters recited as letters. Not words. Not abbreviations of words you're meant to fill in. You say *ḥā*, you say *mīm*, the way a child says the alphabet — and then you stop.

Now, here is the thing I need you to sit with. When the early commentators reached these letters, watch what the most careful of them did. They did not invent. They did not perform. Al-Jalālayn, opening this very surah, writes four words and then stops: **«حم» الله أعلم بمراده به** — *"Ḥā Mīm: Allah knows best what is meant by it."* Al-Muyassar simply points you back to where the discussion was already had, at the opening of al-Baqarah, and lets it rest. Al-Ṭabarī, for his part, notes that he has already treated the question of *ḥā mīm* in his commentary on an earlier surah, and that "what was said there suffices, sparing us from repeating it in this place" — a gesture that treats all seven of the Hawāmīm surahs as a single family, the settled answer carried forward without fanfare across all seven openings.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because something is happening here that we are no longer trained to notice.

We live in an age that cannot tolerate *"I don't know."* We have built an entire civilization on the promise that everything can be looked up, decoded, explained, resolved. The unexplained feels to us like a *bug* — like a question someone simply hasn't gotten around to answering yet. And so when a modern reader hits *ḥā mīm*, the reflex is panic or theory: *it must be a code, it must be a secret, surely someone has cracked it.*

But look at what the masters of this language — men who knew Arabic the way you know your own heartbeat — *did* with it. They knew every root, every form, every shade of every letter. And in front of these two, the greatest of them lowered their eyes and said: **Allah knows best.**

That is not a failure of scholarship. That is its summit.

Notice something. The very first thing the Qur'an does to you, at the threshold of this surah, before a single argument, before a single ruling, before a single story — is teach your tongue to say two letters and teach your heart to say *"I do not fully know, and my Lord does."* It humbles the intellect at the door. It makes you take off the shoes of your certainty before you walk in.

[PAUSE]

Now — there is more than one camera angle on these letters, and they don't contradict; they layer.

One reading, which al-Qurṭubī preserves for us, is that **ḥā mīm is itself a qasam — an oath.** *"Wa qīla: ḥā mīm qasam."* On this reading the letters are not silent mystery alone; they are a swearing, a solemn invocation, leaning forward into the explicit oath that follows in the next breath. We'll come back to that, because it matters.

And there is a reflection that many scholars across the centuries have offered — and I'll mark it clearly as a reflection, not a decoded meaning: these are *Arabic letters*. *Ḥā* and *mīm* are not exotic. They are two of the ordinary building blocks the Arabs used for their finest poetry, their proudest boasts, their contracts and their love-songs. And the surah lays them bare — *here they are, the very letters you know* — and then immediately swears by the Book built from them. The unspoken challenge underneath: *the alphabet is in your hands. It was always in your hands. Now produce something like this.* The miracle is not made of letters you've never seen. It is made of exactly the letters you have, arranged by a hand that is not yours.

[PAUSE]

But here is what I most want you to carry from these two letters. Listen to them. *Ḥā* — it comes from the deep of the throat, the breath opening, almost the sound of exhaling your whole chest. *Mīm* — the lips close. The mouth seals shut. The sound begins as far inside you as a letter can begin and ends with your lips pressed together.

I'll flag this as contemplation and not grammar: the two letters travel the entire length of speech — from the deepest opening of the throat to the final closing of the lips. They *begin* and they *end*. And between them — silence. Meaning withheld. As if the Book is saying, with the very mechanics of your mouth: *here is the whole span of what your speech can do, and even at its full extent, there is a meaning here you cannot reach. That part is Mine.*

What these two letters reveal, before they reveal anything else: **the Clear Book opens by teaching you the posture you must hold to receive it — a humility that can say "Allah knows best" and not flinch.**

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Two: وَ — The Oath, and What It Means That Allah Swears

Now the second ayah opens, and it opens with a single letter that we almost step over: **wa** — *and / by*.

Let me teach you what this letter is doing, because in English it nearly disappears.

In Arabic, an oath has a recognizable shape. You begin with a particle of swearing — most often the letter **wāw**, called *wāw al-qasam*, the *wāw of the oath*. After it comes the thing you swear *by* — and Arabic marks it: it goes into the genitive case, the *majrūr*. That's why it is *al-kitāb**i*** with the *kasra* at the end, not *al-kitāb**u***. The grammar itself is bowing. The Book is grammatically *sworn by*. And then, after the thing sworn by, comes the *answer* of the oath — the thing the whole oath was sworn to affirm.

So when you hear *wa'l-kitāb*, an Arab ear immediately leans forward, because it knows: *an oath has just been sworn, and now something weighty is coming that this oath exists to guarantee.*

Now let's think about oaths the way you actually live them.

Why do human beings swear? When do you reach for *"I swear to you"*? You reach for it when your bare word might not be enough. You invoke something *larger than yourself* — *I swear by God, I swear on my mother's life, I swear on everything I hold dear* — because you are borrowing weight. You are saying: *let this thing I love stand as collateral for my truthfulness.* An oath is always a reaching *upward*, toward something greater than the one swearing, to lend his word a gravity it doesn't have on its own.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that ordinary human fact against what is happening here.

**Allah swears.**

The One greater than whom there is nothing — the One every other oath in existence reaches up *toward* — swears. And the question lands with full force: by what could He possibly swear? When you swear, you reach above yourself. There is nothing above Him. When He swears, He cannot be borrowing weight, because all weight is already His. So what is the oath *doing*?

Stay with me, because this is the turn.

When Allah swears by a created thing — by the dawn, by the fig, by the pen, by the Book — He is not lending it His authority. **He is directing your gaze.** An oath from Allah is a finger pointing. It is the Lord of all things saying: *look here. Of everything I could draw your attention to, I am stopping you at this. Do not walk past it.*

And of all the things He could have made you stop at — at the opening of a surah named after gold, addressed to a people who could not see past gold — He stops you at **the Book**.

[PAUSE]

Feel the relocation. The Quraysh swore by their idols, by their ancestors, by the things they treasured. Their oaths revealed their hearts — you swear by what you value most. And here Allah swears by *the Book*, modeling, in the first oath of the surah, where value actually lives. *You want to know what is precious enough to swear by? Not the gold in your vaults. This. The recitation you keep mocking. I swear by it.*

Al-Ṭabarī is careful to name precisely what this oath is swearing to establish: that this Book is a revelation from a Wise, Praiseworthy Lord — *not an invention of Muhammad ﷺ, nor a fabrication by anyone.* This gives the oath its full weight in context. A people who dismissed the Prophet ﷺ because he had not arrived wearing enough gold were preparing, just a few verses ahead, to say the Qur'an should have descended upon someone grander. The oath by the Book preempts all of it. Before the accusation can land, the Lord Himself swears — on the most luminous of all things — that what Muhammad ﷺ carries is not his, and never was. The form of the oath is already a refutation of the charge they had not yet finished forming.

And remember al-Qurṭubī's reading of the letters before it — that *ḥā mīm* may itself be a qasam. If so, then this surah opens with oath upon oath: the mysterious swearing of the letters, then the explicit swearing by the Book. Two solemn invocations stacked at the threshold, both pointing at the same treasure, as if to say: *whatever else you have failed to take seriously, do not fail to take this seriously.*

What this single letter — this *wa* — reveals: **the Creator of value Himself is telling you, by the act of swearing, where to find it; and He points not at the world's gold but at His own speech.**

### Element Three: ٱلْمُبِينِ — The Clarity That Also Clarifies

And now the word the whole oath has been reaching toward: the Book is described. *Al-kitāb* — the Book, from the root *k-t-b*, *to write, to inscribe, to bind together* (a *kitāb* is gathered, ordered writing, things drawn into one). And then its single, towering description: **al-mubīn.**

Let me teach you the shape of this word, because its grammar is its meaning.

*Al-mubīn* comes from the root *b-y-n*, which carries the idea of *distinctness, separation, a thing standing out so clearly from its surroundings that there can be no confusing it with anything else.* From this root, *bayna* means *"between"* — the gap that separates two things and lets you tell them apart. To be *bayyin* is to be unmistakable.

Now, *mubīn* is not the simplest form of this root. It is the active participle of **Form IV** — *afʿala*, *abāna*. And here is the beautiful thing about this particular verb, attested in the classical lexicons: *abāna* carries **two valences at once.** It can be intransitive — *to be clear, to be manifest in oneself*. And it can be transitive — *to make clear, to clarify something else, to render another thing distinct.*

Sit with what that means. *Al-mubīn* does not settle into one meaning. It holds both:

The Book that **is** clear — luminous, unmistakable, manifest in itself.

And the Book that **makes** clear — that takes everything else and renders *it* distinct, that separates truth from falsehood, the lawful from the forbidden, the path from the wandering, so that *you* can finally tell them apart.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means. The Book is not a lamp you merely admire for being bright. It is a lamp by which you *see everything else*. Its clarity does not terminate in itself; it pours outward onto the whole of your life and lights it up. *Al-mubīn* is clarity that clarifies. A brightness that brightens.

But here is what al-Ṭabarī adds to this — and it should stop you.

The Book makes clear *to whoever contemplates it and reflects on its lessons and admonitions*. That phrase is al-Ṭabarī's own, and he places it at the very heart of his explanation of *al-mubīn*. The clarity of the Book, on his reading, is not a property that fires automatically upon contact. It does not flood the room the moment someone cracks the spine. It opens — specifically, precisely — to the one who does *tadabbur*. Who slows down. Who sits with the text and turns it over and asks what it is saying and why.

[PAUSE]

Carry this for a moment, because it reframes everything about the people this surah is addressing.

The Quraysh were not men who lacked access to the Book. They *heard* it. Some of them heard it recited in the streets, in the homes of believers, in the very air of the city they lived in. Their problem was not that the Qur'an was obscure to them in the sense of being unavailable. Their problem was that they never *stopped*. They never turned toward it with the posture of reflection. And a lamp, however bright, cannot give light to a man running in the other direction.

The *mubīn* was always there. The clarity was always available. What was not there — what the surah is, in a sense, inviting into existence — was the *tadabbur* that lets the light through.

[PAUSE]

And now — *now* — return to where we began, because the architecture snaps shut.

The first ayah: **ḥā mīm.** Two letters whose meaning Allah withheld. The most *un*clear thing in the entire Qur'an. The place where the greatest scholars in the history of the Arabic language — men whose whole lives were given over to its mastery — lowered their eyes and said: *"Allah knows best."*

The second ayah: **al-kitāb al-mubīn.** The clearest thing ever sent down. Clarity that clarifies everything it touches. A brightness that brightens — but that opens, specifically and precisely, to the one who contemplates.

And they are placed side by side. In the same breath. In the same pair of ayahs.

[PAUSE]

Why would the Book that clarifies everything begin with something it refuses to clarify?

Here is the answer you have earned.

Because the Qur'an is teaching you, before it teaches you anything else, that **clarity and mystery coexist in the same revelation**. That a heart which can only receive the clear — only the resolved, only the decoded, only the answered — is a heart not yet ready to receive the whole of what this Book has to give. Because the Book contains both. There are verses as clear as noonday light, and there are depths that no civilization has yet reached the bottom of. There are rulings precise enough to build an entire civilization on, and there are openings that the greatest scholars across fourteen centuries have not finished entering.

What the Qur'an needs from you, at the very door, is a heart that can hold **both** without collapse. A heart that can say *"I understand this"* and *"I do not understand that"* in the same sitting, trusting that both are true of the same Book, from the same Lord, and that the mystery is not a defect in the revelation but a different kind of invitation — not to decode, but to *rest in Allah.*

[PAUSE]

*Ḥā mīm: Allah knows best.*
*By the clear Book.*

Two statements. One breath. The first is your posture. The second is your inheritance.

## CLOSING: What You Carry Out

The Quraysh stood in front of something they had the letters to read and the ears to hear, and they could not receive it because their hands were too full of gold. And their eyes — already trained to calculate worth by what glittered — could not recognize the thing in front of them precisely because it did not glitter. It was bare. Two letters. An oath. A description. No genealogy. No accumulated wealth. No social proof from the great men of the two cities.

*That's it?* they might have said. *That's what all of this is built on?*

Yes. Exactly that. Because the one who has learned to say *"Allāhu aʿlam"* at the threshold is precisely the one who will understand what is on the other side of it. The humility is not the obstacle to the clarity. The humility is the door.

You are not them. But the question the surah opens with is still a live question for you, now, today:

**What are your hands too full of to hold this?**

What have you decided is more worth measuring, more worth accumulating, more worth arguing about than the Book that swears it is the measure of all things?

The letters are *ḥā mīm* — they begin somewhere inside you that you cannot name, and they end with your lips sealed shut, and between them is something that belongs to Allah alone. And then, in the very next breath, without apology and without explanation: *the Clear Book.*

The mystery is not the opposite of the clarity.

The mystery is the door.

[PAUSE]

*Allāhu aʿlam.*
