# How Many a Prophet: Mockery, Might, and the Mercy of Precedent

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first, the way they arrive in the surah — abruptly, like a hand placed firmly on a shaking shoulder.

> **وَكَمْ أَرْسَلْنَا مِن نَّبِيٍّ فِى ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ﴿٦﴾ وَمَا يَأْتِيهِم مِّن نَّبِيٍّ إِلَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ ﴿٧﴾ فَأَهْلَكْنَآ أَشَدَّ مِنْهُم بَطْشًۭا وَمَضَىٰ مَثَلُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ﴿٨﴾**

*Wa kam arsalnā min nabiyyin fī l-awwalīn. Wa mā ya'tīhim min nabiyyin illā kānū bihī yastahzi'ūn. Fa-ahlaknā ashadda minhum baṭshan wa maḍā mathalu l-awwalīn.*

"And how many a prophet did We send among the former peoples. And no prophet would come to them except that they used to ridicule him. So We destroyed those who were greater than these in striking power — and the example of the former peoples has already gone before."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel what these verses carry, you have to stand where the Prophet ﷺ was standing when they came down.

This is Makkah. Not the Makkah of victory, not the Makkah of crowds streaming into the faith. This is the long middle of the Makkan period, when the message had been public for years and the response had hardened into something colder than rejection. The Quraysh were not merely refusing. They were *laughing*. They mimicked his prayer. They invented nicknames. They sat in their circles and turned the most serious words a human being had ever spoken into entertainment. When he passed, eyes met and lips curled.

There is a particular kind of pain in being mocked for the thing you hold most sacred. Rejection you can argue with. Mockery refuses the argument. Mockery says: *you are not even worth taking seriously.* It is the cruelty that pretends to be cleverness. And it does something specific to the one who receives it — it makes him doubt not the truth, but himself. *Maybe I am saying it wrong. Maybe if I were a different kind of man, they would listen.*

Now notice where in the surah these verses sit. Az-Zukhruf opens by anchoring the Quran in its source — the *Umm al-Kitāb*, the Mother of the Book, with Allah, exalted and full of wisdom. Then comes verse 5, a question that almost takes your breath: *"Should We then withdraw the Reminder from you altogether, because you are a people who transgress?"* Should We just... stop? Should We give up on them?

And the answer to that question — the reason the Reminder will **not** be withdrawn, the reason the messenger must **not** be broken — is these three verses. [PAUSE]

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hold as we begin.

Allah does not console His Prophet ﷺ here by telling him the mockery will stop. He does not say *be patient, it will get easier.* He does something stranger and far deeper. He reaches back across the entire span of human history — and He uses a single small word, a word about *quantity*, to dissolve the loneliness of this moment.

And then, having dissolved the loneliness, He does something that should make every powerful person on earth go quiet. He talks about the mockers who came before — and He describes them with one specific word. Not *richer*. Not *more numerous*. **Stronger in the grip.**

Why that word? Why, of all the things He could say about vanished civilizations, does He reach for the language of a *fist*?

Stay with me. The answer is in the sound of the word itself.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: كَمْ — the small word that ends loneliness

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse.

There is a little word, *kam* (كَمْ), that you will meet very early when you learn the language, because it is how you ask "how many?" *Kam kitāban ʿindaka?* — How many books do you have? It is a question word. It opens its hands and waits for a number to be placed in them.

But Arabic has a second *kam*, spelled identically, that is not a question at all. The grammarians call it *kam al-khabariyya* — the *kam* of statement, the *kam* of report. It does not ask. It *declares*. And what it declares is **abundance**. When you say *kam* in this mode, you are not waiting for a number — you are overwhelmed by one. The closest English can come is the exclamation: "How many...!" — said with a shake of the head, the way you might say, "How many times have I told you," not asking for a count but drowning in one.

Now look at the very first word of our passage.

**وَكَمْ أَرْسَلْنَا** — *Wa kam arsalnā.*

Al-Qurtubi stops here deliberately. He says plainly: *"kam* here is *khabariyya*, and what is intended by it is *takthīr*" — the making-many. *Mā akthara mā arsalnā min al-anbiyā'* — "How very many are the prophets We sent!" He even cross-references another verse where the same construction appears — *kam tarakū min jannātin wa ʿuyūn*, "how many gardens and springs they left behind" — abundance, again, not inquiry.

So the surah does not open the consolation with "We sent prophets before you." That would be true. It would also be flat. Instead it opens with a word that *cannot be counted to the bottom*. [PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because something is happening underneath the grammar.

The Prophet ﷺ, in this moment, is experiencing his suffering as *singular*. That is what mockery does. It isolates. It makes you feel you are the only one who has ever stood in this exact humiliation, that something about *you* has drawn this contempt. The lie at the center of the pain is the lie of *aloneness*.

And the first word Allah chooses is a word whose entire grammatical function is to refuse a single number. Not "one before you." Not "a few." Not even "many," which you could at least try to picture. *Kam* — so many that counting them is the wrong activity. So many that the right response is not to tally but to *exhale*.

Al-Ṭabarī makes something explicit here that the grammar alone implies: this *kam* is a direct address — *yā Muḥammad*, he notes, "among the generations who passed before your generation in which you were sent, just as We sent you among your people from Quraysh." The consolation is not abstract. It is spoken *to* someone, aimed with precision at the specific ache of a specific man standing in a specific city where specific people are laughing at him right now.

Notice something. He could have said *qad arsalnā rusulan min qablika* — "We did send messengers before you." Clean, factual, finished. Instead He reaches for the one word in the language that opens onto a horizon you cannot see the end of. Every prophet — and there is a sound report numbering them in the many thousands — every single one stood somewhere on a road, and somewhere on that road, someone laughed at him.

Here's what is remarkable. The consolation is not "your pain will end." The consolation is "**your pain is not yours alone**." It places the Prophet ﷺ not at the lonely tip of a spear but in the middle of an immense, unbroken procession of the mocked-but-true. Nuh, who built a ship on dry land while his people walked past pointing. Hud. Salih. Ibrahim, who was thrown into a fire. Musa, called a sorcerer and a madman. Every one of them heard the laughter. *Kam.* How many.

And this is the first mercy of the verse: it tells the suffering soul that the laughter is not a verdict on *you*. It is the oldest sound in the story of guidance. [PAUSE — let this land]

What this single word reveals: **the deepest comfort is not the removal of the trial but the dissolving of its loneliness.** Allah does not promise the mockery away. He folds the Prophet ﷺ into a company so vast it cannot be numbered. And being in true company changes the entire weight of a thing.

### Element Two: مَا ... إِلَّا and the verb that will not stop — يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ

Let me teach you a second structure, and this one is about *certainty*.

In Arabic, one of the most powerful ways to make a statement absolute is to wrap it between a negation and an exception — *mā... illā*. You deny everything, and then you carve out the one exception, and the effect is total. *Mā jā'a illā Zayd* — "No one came except Zayd" — which is a far stronger way of saying "only Zayd came." The mind first sweeps the whole field empty, then plants one fact in it. Nothing survives outside that one fact.

Now hear verse 7:

[ayah:43:7] وَمَا يَأْتِيهِم مِّن نَّبِىٍّ إِلَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ

"And there does not come to them a prophet **except** that they used to ridicule him."

Watch the architecture. *Mā ya'tīhim min nabiyyin* — no prophet comes to them — the field is swept completely empty. And then: *illā kānū bihī yastahzi'ūn* — except they were mocking him. The exception is not an escape from the rule; it *is* the rule, stated with the force of something that admits no counterexample.

And notice that little *min* before *nabiyyin* — *min nabiyyin*. In Arabic this is an "extra" *min*, what the grammarians call *zā'ida*, and its job is to generalize, to leave no gap. Not "no prophet" in a loose sense, but "not so much as a *single* prophet, of any kind, in any age." The net is woven tight. Nothing slips through.

Let's sit with what this is claiming. It is claiming a law of human nature as reliable as a law of physics. Not *most* prophets were mocked. Not *the major* prophets. **Every prophet, without one exception across all of history, met the laugh.** [PAUSE]

Al-Ṭabarī's reading of this verse makes the universality even more vivid: "there did not come to a generation of those generations, nor a nation of those early nations, a prophet of ours calling them to guidance and the path of truth, except that those who received him used to mock him." Every generation. Every nation. Every true caller. The same door, and always the same response at the door.

Now the verb itself. *Yastahzi'ūn.*

The root is *h-z-a*, mockery. But notice the shape it has been poured into — *yas-tah-zi'*. That extra weight at the front, the *ist-*, is a recognizable Arabic pattern (Form X), and one of the things this pattern can carry is a sense of *seeking out*, of *going after* something deliberately. This is not mockery that slips out by accident. This is mockery as a *pursued activity*, something the nafs reaches for and works at.

And then look at the tense. The verse does not say *istahza'ū* — "they mocked," past and done. It says *kānū... yastahzi'ūn* — "they **used to be** mocking." This is the construction of habit, of the continuous, of the thing-they-kept-doing. *Kānū* sets it in the past; *yastahzi'ūn*, in the present-continuous form, keeps it *moving*. The grammar refuses to let the mockery finish. It is permanent activity, frozen mid-motion, like a film of a laughing crowd that loops and never ends. [PAUSE]

And one more thing — the smallest thing, and maybe the sharpest. The word order. It is not *kānū yastahzi'ūna bihī*. It is **كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِءُونَ** — *kānū bihī yastahzi'ūn.* The *bihī* — "at him" — has been pulled *forward*, placed before the verb. In Arabic, moving a word ahead of where it naturally sits puts a spotlight on it. The spotlight here falls on *him* — on the person of the prophet. It was not the message in the abstract they mocked. It was *him*. His face, his manner, his voice. Mockery is always personal. That is what makes it cut.

Here's what is remarkable when you put it together. Verse 6 is in the *past* — *kam arsalnā*, "how many We sent." But verse 7 slides into the *present-continuous* — *ya'tīhim... yastahzi'ūn*, "comes to them... they keep mocking." The grammar itself performs a quiet miracle: it lifts the mockery out of history and drops it into the *present tense*, so that the laughter of every vanished generation and the laughter ringing in the streets of Makkah become **one continuous sound**. The Prophet ﷺ is not hearing a new mockery. He is hearing the *same* mockery, the one that has never stopped, simply arriving at his door.

What this construction reveals: **the rejection the messenger faces is not an event but a pattern, not personal failure but the signature of truth meeting the nafs.** When something true and demanding arrives, the laugh comes. It has always come. Its arrival is not evidence that something has gone wrong. It is evidence that the oldest story is repeating exactly on schedule.

### Element Three: بَطْشًا — the word with a fist inside it

Now we come to the word I promised you, and I want you to listen with your ears before your mind.

Say it slowly: **baṭshan.** *Ba — ṭ — sh.*

Feel the architecture of the sound. It opens with the plosive *b*, then strikes the heavy, emphatic *ṭ* — one of the dark, weighted letters of Arabic, a letter you make by pressing the tongue hard against the roof of the mouth and *releasing* — and then it closes on the *sh*, a sound of friction, of crushing. The word does not flow. It *seizes and grinds*. It is built to be uncomfortable in the mouth. [PAUSE]

This is no accident. The root *b-ṭ-sh* in Arabic means to seize violently, to grip with overwhelming force, to strike with a power that cannot be resisted. It is not the word for "strong" in general. It is the word for the strength of the *fist that closes around you*. When Allah elsewhere describes His own decisive power against tyranny, He uses this same root — *inna baṭsha rabbika la-shadīd*, "indeed the grip of your Lord is severe" (85:12). It is the vocabulary of the hand that takes hold and does not let go.

So now read verse 8 with that fist in your ear:

**فَأَهْلَكْنَآ أَشَدَّ مِنْهُم بَطْشًا**

"So We destroyed those who were **greater than these in the grip** — ashadda minhum baṭshan."

Let's sit with the comparison, because it is doing something precise. Allah does not say the destroyed nations were richer than the Quraysh, though some were. He does not say they were more numerous than the Quraysh, though they were. He does not say they were more storied or more ancient or more feared, though every one of those things was true of the civilizations the Quran calls to mind when it speaks of the *awwalīn*. He reaches for **one specific dimension**: *batshan*. The grip. The power that seizes and does not release.

Why that word? Because that is the one advantage the Quraysh actually believe they possess. Not piety. Not wisdom. Not moral standing. *Power.* The control of the trade routes, the grip of tribal dominance, the fist of the city that makes the desert routes safe or unsafe at its pleasure. They are the people who matter. No one laughs at them and lives to do it twice.

Al-Muyassar names both dimensions of this superiority: the destroyed nations were *ashadda quwwatan wa ba'san* — greater in power *and* might. *Quwwa* is the internal capacity for force; *ba's* is the outward expression of it, the fearsome reputation that makes enemies think twice. Both. And they are gone.

And notice the *fa* — **فَأَهْلَكْنَآ**. That small letter does important work. *Fa* in Arabic often carries consequence: *therefore, and so, as a result.* The logic of the verse is clean and devastating: they came, they mocked, and *therefore* — *fa* — We destroyed. Not: eventually, after warnings, after patience, after long delay. The *fa* is quick. The sequence does not pause to marvel at itself. It simply reports what happened when power put itself in the path of prophecy. [PAUSE]

Classical commentators (al-Muyassar among them) identify the destruction as arising from a confluence of three things: *kufruhum wa ṭughyānuhum wa istihzā'uhum bi-anbiyā'ihim* — their disbelief, their transgression, and their mockery of their prophets. Not mockery alone. But notice that mockery is named *last*, closing the list — perhaps because it is the one the Quraysh are performing in real time as these words descend, the very thing happening in the streets of Makkah at this moment. The other two causes are attitudes. The mockery is an *act*, visible, ongoing, and the act that seals what the attitude already decided.

What this word *baṭshan* reveals: **the Quraysh's most cherished asset — their power, their grip, their sense of being the ones who determine what happens — is not the shield they believe it to be. It is the very measure by which the verdict against their predecessors was calibrated.** The stronger the grip that was destroyed, the more completely does *their* grip stand in the shadow of that precedent.

### Element Four: وَمَضَىٰ مَثَلُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ — the example already concluded

**وَمَضَىٰ مَثَلُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ** — *wa maḍā mathalu l-awwalīn.*

"And the example of the former peoples has already gone before."

*Maḍā* — passed. Gone. The root carries finality, the sense of something that has moved past a point of no return. It is what you say for a journey completed, for time that has elapsed and cannot be recalled. What *maḍā* does not return.

And *mathal* — usually translated "example," but Arabic *mathal* carries more than that word suggests. It is closer to *precedent* — an established pattern, a case that has already been decided and sits in the record. Legal Arabic would understand it as the judgment that was handed down, now on the books. The case of the first peoples went to trial. The verdict was rendered. *Maḍā* — it is over.

So what is the verse saying?

It is saying: the story of the mocked prophet who is ultimately vindicated and the powerful mocker who is ultimately destroyed — this story *is already finished*. Not just for the people of 'Ād, not just for Thamūd, not just for the people of Lūṭ. It is finished as a *type*. As a pattern. As a law of history that can no more be reversed than you can unseal a verdict that has been stamped and filed.

Al-Ṭabarī's framing is quietly devastating: he reads this passage as Allah addressing Muhammad ﷺ directly, saying that these prophets were sent in generations before *your* generation, just as Allah sent *him* among the Quraysh. The word *awwalīn* — the *first ones*, the *former peoples* — places the Quraysh by implication in a position they have not earned and may not escape: the position of the people who come *after* those examples. And people who come after examples have a choice the examples themselves did not — they can see the road that was already walked.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

Now step back and notice what the grammar of these three verses has done.

Verse 6: *arsalnā* — simple past, completed. We sent. It is done.
Verse 7: *ya'tīhim... yastahzi'ūn* — present-continuous, ongoing. They keep mocking. It is happening right now.
Verse 8: *maḍā* — perfect completion. It has already concluded. It is over.

The passage moves us from the uncountable past of the prophets sent, through the unwavering present-tense of mockery-as-response, and lands on the definitively completed past of the reckoning. The grammar has quietly performed a revelation: the mockers, though they feel entirely *in* the present, are already *in* the past. They are living inside a story whose final chapter has already been written in every court of history that has heard this case before.

What this construction reveals: **the present moment of Makkan persecution is not actually the present. It is the beginning of a past that has not yet finished arriving.** The verdict is already in the record. The question is only *when* it reads itself into this particular story.

## Part 2: The Deeper Stream

Now let's go beneath the grammar into what these three verses are doing to a soul — specifically, to the soul that receives them as addressed to it.

Al-Muyassar names the primary function of the passage directly: *wa fī hādhā tasliya li-l-Nabī ﷺ* — "and in this is consolation for the Prophet ﷺ." The classical commentators recognized *talsiya* — consolation, relief from grief, the lightening of an unbearable weight — as the announced *wazīfa* of the passage. This is not incidental to what the verses do. It *is* what they do. And yet — as we have been tracing through the grammar — the consolation is deeply precise. It does not say *be patient*. It does not promise that victory is near. It offers something more structural and more lasting: a **reframing of what is happening**.

Here is the reframe, in three movements:

**Movement one: de-singularization.** *Kam arsalnā.* The word that refuses to let you count to the bottom. The Prophet ﷺ is not the only mocked truth-teller in human history. He is the latest in a procession so vast it has no visible beginning. And this — this *companionship in the pattern* — changes the nature of the pain. You can endure a road when you know others have walked it. You cannot endure it when you believe it is yours alone.

**Movement two: reclassification.** *Mā ya'tīhim min nabiyyin illā kānū bihī yastahzi'ūn.* What appears to be evidence against the message is quietly reread as evidence *for* it. If every true prophet was mocked, then being mocked by these people is not a mark against you. It is, in the most unsettling way, a credential. The laugh that is meant to diminish actually *places* you. The Quraysh think they are disqualifying the Prophet ﷺ with their contempt. They are, without knowing it, enrolling him in a company they cannot see.

**Movement three: the already-decided verdict.** *Maḍā mathalu l-awwalīn.* The case of the mocker-versus-prophet has already been decided in every court of history that has heard it. The current moment — with its confident Quraysh laughter — is not actually an open question. It is the beginning of a past that has not yet finished writing itself. The verdict exists. The question is only its arrival.

### What the mirror shows the Quraysh

There is a second audience for these three verses, and we should not ignore them.

While the Prophet ﷺ receives consolation, the Quraysh receive something else: a mirror. They are laughing. They feel entirely in command. And these verses hand them a clear picture of the last people who felt this way — people who were, note carefully, *stronger in the grip than they are*. The comparison is not flattering. The former peoples were greater in *quwwa* and *ba's*, in force and fearsome reputation, and even that did not save them. What does the Quraysh's comparatively modest grip amount to in that light?

The three causes al-Muyassar names — disbelief, transgression, mockery — are not arbitrary. They form a progression. *Kufr* is the refusal to receive. *Ṭughyān* is what that refusal produces when it goes unchecked — the swelling of the self past every limit. And *istihzā'* is what ṭughyān sounds like when it meets prophecy: it cannot argue, so it laughs. The Quraysh are doing all three in real time. The mirror these verses hold up is exact.

And yet — notice the extraordinary mercy underneath even this warning. *Wa maḍā mathalu l-awwalīn.* The past tense is mercifully aimed. It refers to the fate of the *awwalīn*, the *first* ones — not the Quraysh. The former peoples are the example. The Quraysh are still, in this moment, the audience. Audiences can leave through a different door than the characters in the story used. The precedent is on the books precisely so that those who hear it can choose not to become the precedent themselves. The Quran is not threatening. It is warning. And warning, unlike threat, still contains a door.

[PAUSE]

This is why the verse does not say *maḍā mathalu hā'ulā'i* — "the example of *these*" [the Quraysh]. It says *mathalu l-awwalīn* — "the example of the *first* ones." The first ones are done. The Quraysh are still, at this moment, reading.

## Arriving

Three verses. Three movements of a single act of mercy — because that is what *talsiya* is at its root. Not simply comfort, but the mercy that rearranges your understanding of your position so that the weight becomes bearable and the path becomes visible again.

The Prophet ﷺ is told: you are not alone — the uncountable *kam* dissolves the lie of singularity. What is happening to you is not evidence of your failure but the oldest signature of truth meeting the nafs — the iron *mā... illā* removes the sting of the laugh by reclassifying it. And the ones who are laughing at you have already lost, in every court this story has ever been heard — the decisive *maḍā* closes the case file.

Linguistically, the passage moves in three grammatical registers:
- *Arsalnā* — simple past, completed action: the prophets were sent, it is done
- *Yastahzi'ūn* — present-continuous, the habit-pattern still in motion: mockery is always present-tense
- *Maḍā* — perfect completion, the thing that is already over: the reckoning is already concluded

And the effect of this movement is that the listener is pulled across all three moments simultaneously — backward into the uncountable past of the prophets, into the unchanging present-tense of mockery-as-response, and forward into the already-decided future of the mockers' fate. By the end of verse 8, you are holding all three at once, and the present moment of Makkan persecution has been placed — very precisely — in its correct position in a story far larger than itself.

There is a word for what these three verses do to the soul: *thubāt* — groundedness, the quality of not being moved by what is trying to move you. Not because the pain has gone. But because the pain has been located. And a located pain — a pain whose company you have been given, whose pattern you can name, whose precedent you can hold — is a pain that does not break you.

*Wa maḍā mathalu l-awwalīn.* The example has already gone before. The road is marked. Walk.
