# The Heel That Follows

### Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 25

> فَٱنتَقَمْنَا مِنْهُمْ ۖ فَٱنظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ عَـٰقِبَةُ ٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ
>
> *Fa-ntaqamnā minhum, fa-nẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu-l-mukadhdhibīn.*
>
> **"So We took retribution from them. Then look — how was the end of those who kept crying lies?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Verse

I want you to imagine a conversation that has been happening for thousands of years. The same conversation. In different languages, on different soil, under different skies — but the same words, almost exactly, every single time.

A man arrives in a town. He is not asking for money. He is not raising an army. He brings a single sentence: *there is One God, and you will stand before Him.* And the people of comfort in that town — the Quran has a specific word for them, **al-mutrafūn**, the ones softened by ease — they look at him, and they say something that has been said in every century of human history.

They do not say, "Prove it." They do not say, "Your logic is flawed." Listen to what they actually say, just two verses before ours, in 43:23:

> *"We found our fathers upon a way, and we are guided by their footsteps."*

[PAUSE]

That's it. That's the whole argument. *This is what we inherited. This is what everyone around us does. This is who we are.*

And the warner — every warner, in every town — gives one stunning reply in verse 24: *"Even if I bring you better guidance than what you found your fathers upon?"* Even if what I'm offering is **truer** than your inheritance — would you still refuse?

And they say yes. They say: *we reject what you were sent with.*

That is the world standing behind our ayah. Verse 25 is what comes next. It is the verdict — and then something the verdict has no obligation to do. It turns to **you**. It taps you on the shoulder. And it says: *now look.*

Here is the hook, and I want you to hold it the whole way through: this ayah is seven words long. It contains a punishment, a command, and a question. But the most important word in it is not the word for retribution. It is a word that, in the Arabic, literally means **a heel** — the part of you that follows behind, that you cannot see, that walks every step you walk. Allah took a word for the back of your foot and made it the word for the destiny of nations. By the end of our time together, I want you to understand why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Fa-ntaqamnā* — The Verb That Refuses to Be Rage

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — scholars call them *forms*, numbered I through X. The base meaning sits in the three root letters, and the *form* bends that meaning: it can make it causative, repetitive, mutual, or — and this is the one we need — **reflexive**, meaning the action loops back and involves the doer himself. It's the difference, in English, between *he broke* and *he took it upon himself to break.* The second one tells you something about the agent, not just the act.

Now look at the first word of our ayah:

> فَٱنتَقَمْنَا — *fa-ntaqamnā*

The root is **n-q-m** (نقم). And in the lexicons — in Lisān al-ʿArab, in al-Rāghib's Mufradāt — *naqama* does not simply mean "to punish." It means **to disapprove of something so deeply that you respond to it.** *Naqamtu ʿalā fulān* — I held something against him, I found it intolerable. The root carries *moral objection* before it carries *consequence.*

And here the verb is in **Form VIII** — *iftaʿala* — the reflexive pattern. *Intaqama.* Not *ʿāqaba* (the plain word for "he punished"). Not *ʿadhdhaba* (the word for "he tormented"). Allah chose the form that means **to take retribution upon oneself, to claim the response as one's own act of justice.**

Let's sit with the difference, because it is everything.

A punishment can be cold administration. A bureaucracy punishes. A machine punishes. But *intiqām* — measured retribution — is the response of someone who **saw**, who **weighed**, who found the act intolerable, and who answered it *deliberately and personally.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. We tend to imagine divine punishment as a kind of explosion — wrath, fury, the sky falling in rage. But *intaqama* is the opposite of an explosion. An explosion is undirected. *Intiqām* is **aimed.** It is slow. It is the response of a Judge who has heard the whole case. When al-Muyassar explains this verse, notice he doesn't reach for the language of fury — he lists *methods*: "by sending down the penalty upon them — by swallowing them in the earth, by drowning, and other than that." These are not the actions of someone who lost control. These are sentences, handed down, each fitted to a people.

And then there is the *nūn* — the "We." *Intaqam-**nā**.* The royal plural, the plural of majesty. Not "I was angry." **"We took retribution."** The voice of sovereignty, not the voice of a wounded party. Allah is not hurt by the deniers. He is not diminished by their denial. *Intiqām* here is not Allah recovering His honor — it is Allah upholding the order of reality, the way a true thing must finally assert itself against a lie.

So already, before we leave the first word, the ayah has corrected something in us. We came expecting wrath. Allah gives us **justice that took its time.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Al-Mukadhdhibīn* — The People Who Became a Verb

Now look at the last word. This is where the ayah does its quietest, deepest work.

> ٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ — *al-mukadhdhibīn*

The root is **k-dh-b** (كذب) — to lie, to declare false. But watch the form carefully, because Arabic is about to tell you something English cannot.

There is a word *kādhib* — a liar, someone who says what is untrue. That is **Form I**, the plain form. But that is *not* the word here. The word here is built on **Form II** — *kadhdhaba* — and Form II does something specific: it **intensifies and makes deliberate and repeated.** You can hear it in the doubled middle letter, the *shadda*: *kadh-DHA-ba.* The tongue has to press on it twice.

*Kādhib* is "one who lies." *Mukadhdhib* is **"one who actively, repeatedly, insistently declares the truth to be a lie."** It is not passive disbelief. It is a *labor.* It is someone to whom the truth came — clearly, by the tongue of a messenger — and who set themselves the ongoing task of calling it false. Again. And again. And again.

Let's stay with this, because it reframes the whole verdict.

Allah did not destroy these nations for *not knowing.* The Quran is endlessly merciful toward the one who hasn't heard, the one who's confused, the one still searching. *Mukadhdhib* is none of those. The truth **reached** them — that is built into the word, because you can only call false what has already been presented to you. They heard it. They understood it. And they made a *practice* of denial.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the part I need you to see, and it is an absence — something the ayah *refuses* to give you.

Allah does not say *"how was the end of ʿĀd"* or *"the end of Thamūd"* or *"the end of Pharaoh's people."* He had every option to name them. He names them, instead, by **what they did.** *Al-mukadhdhibīn.* The deniers.

Do you feel what that does? Their nation, their wealth, their architecture, their ancestry — the very thing they were so proud of, "we found our fathers upon a way" — **all of it dissolves.** When the final accounting comes, they have no name left except the name of their action. They wanted to be remembered as the inheritors of a great tradition. They are remembered as *the ones who cried lies.* The identity they built collapses entirely into the verb they kept repeating.

This is one of the most sobering patterns in the Quran: **in the end, you are called by what you did, not by what you belonged to.** The tribe does not survive the reckoning. The action does.

[PAUSE — let this one sit]

### Element Three: *Fa-nẓur Kayfa Kāna* — The Turn Toward You

We've looked at the punishment and the punished. Now look at the strange, beautiful middle of the verse — because something happens here that should stop us.

> فَٱنظُرْ كَيْفَ كَانَ — *fa-nẓur kayfa kāna* — "then look — how was…"

Up to this point the verse has been *narration.* "We took retribution from them." Past tense, third person, a story about other people in another time. And then, without warning, the grammar **pivots and grabs you by the collar.** *Fa-nẓur* — **"look!"** — is an imperative. A command. Second person. *You.*

Arabic rhetoricians have a name for this sudden shift of address: **iltifāt** — literally, *the turning of the face.* The verse was facing the past; it turns its face to you. And the effect is electric. The destroyed nations stop being a history lesson and become an **exhibit.** You are no longer reading about them. You are standing in front of them, and a voice beside you says: *look at this.*

And notice *exactly* what the command asks. It does not say *unẓur **mādhā** kāna* — "look at **what** happened." It says *unẓur **kayfa** kāna* — "look at **how** it was." **Kayfa** — *how.* The interrogative of manner.

Why does that matter? Because "what happened" only wants a fact — *they were destroyed, the end.* But "**how** it was" wants you to *contemplate the shape of it.* The manner. The way the comfortable became the cautionary, the way the proud inheritance led precisely to the grave, the way denial ripened slowly into consequence. *Kayfa* is an invitation to **reflect**, not just to record. Allah is not handing you a verdict to file away. He is handing you a scene to study.

[PAUSE]

And here is the bridge into everything that comes next. The command *fa-nẓur* — "look" — runs like a refrain all through the Quran. *"Travel through the land, then look how was the end…"* (30:42). *"Say: travel through the land, then look…"* (6:11). Over and over, Allah does not ask us to *imagine* the consequences of denial. He asks us to **go and look at them,** because they are written into the actual world — into ruins, into history, into the heel that follows every life.

So we've handled the words. Now let's ask what this verse reveals not just about grammar — but about **reality itself.**

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: "We Found Our Fathers Upon a Way" — The Tyranny of the Inherited Self

Let me start not in the seventh century, but in your own mind, this morning.

You woke up today inside a worldview you did not choose. The language you think in, the politics you assume are obvious, the things you find embarrassing and the things you find admirable, the gut sense of *what people like us do* — almost none of it did you build from scratch. You inherited it. From parents, from a culture, from an algorithm that learned what keeps you scrolling. Psychologists have a hundred names for the machinery: *the default effect, motivated reasoning, identity-protective cognition, the bandwidth of the crowd.* But the Quran said it in five words, fourteen centuries ago, and put it in the mouths of the people who were about to be destroyed:

> *"We found our fathers upon a way."* (43:23)

I want you to notice that **this is not stupidity.** The people saying it are described as the *mutrafūn* — the affluent, the comfortable, the established. These are the *successful* ones. The argument from inheritance is not the argument of fools. It is the argument of people who have *too much to lose* by examining. And that is exactly why comfort is so dangerous to the soul: **the more your identity has paid off, the more it costs you to question it.**

[PAUSE]

Think about what the warner offers them in verse 24. He doesn't insult their fathers. He asks one surgical question: *"Even if I bring you better guidance than what you found your fathers upon?"* He grants that they have *something.* He simply asks: *and if there's something truer — does truer matter to you, or does only familiar matter?*

And their answer — *"we reject what you were sent with"* — reveals the whole disease. They are not weighing truth against falsehood. They have stopped weighing at all. **Familiar has become their definition of true.** The inheritance is no longer a *claim* they hold; it is the *eye* they see with, and you cannot ask an eye to examine itself.

Here is where it lands for us. Every one of us has an inheritance we have mistaken for reality. For one person it's the religion of their parents, held without conviction. But for another — and this is the trap of our moment — it's the *secular* inheritance: the assumption that the modern, the comfortable, the consensus of one's social feed *is* the way things are, and that the messenger's question — *but what if there's something truer?* — is not even worth entertaining. The mutraf of 43:23 has a smartphone now. The sentence is identical. *We found our people upon a way, and we follow their footsteps.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this verse ask of us tomorrow? Not that we discard everything we inherited — that's impossible and foolish. It asks one thing: that we be **willing to answer the warner's question.** When something true and demanding arrives — a command of Allah that cuts against our comfort, a correction that costs our pride — can we say the words the mutrafūn could not? *Even if it's truer than what I found my fathers upon… then yes, the truer thing wins.*

That single willingness is the entire difference between the people in this verse and the people who are saved. **The saved are not the ones with no inheritance. They are the ones who held their inheritance loosely enough to let the truth correct it.**

### Theme Two: *ʿĀqibah* — Learning to Read Life from Its Ending

Now we arrive at the heel.

I told you at the beginning that the central word of this verse — *ʿāqibah*, "the end, the outcome" — comes from a root, **ʿ-q-b** (عقب), whose first and most physical meaning is **the heel.** The *ʿaqib* is the back of your foot. From that same root: *to follow behind, to come after, to succeed something.* The *ʿāqibah* of a thing is **what comes walking up behind it** — its consequence, the part of the story that arrives last.

Sit with the image, because it's doing real work. Your heel is the part of you that **follows every step you take, that you cannot see, and that you cannot outrun.** It is always there, always behind you, always arriving exactly where you've been. The Arabic made *that* the word for destiny. Your *ʿāqibah* is not waiting at some distant finish line. It is following you right now, one step back, taking the shape of every step you take.

[PAUSE]

Here is the human problem, and it is *ancient* and it is *yours.*

**We judge by the beginning and the middle. Allah teaches us to judge by the end.**

When the mutrafūn looked at their lives, they saw the beginning and the middle: the wealth, the lineage, the established way, the messenger looking small and strange before all their success. By every measure available *in the middle of the story*, they were winning and he was losing. And that is precisely why they felt safe to deny. They were reading life forward, from its impressive opening.

And Allah's response — *fa-nẓur kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu-l-mukadhdhibīn* — is to drag the camera all the way to the **last page** and say: *now read it backward. Now look at the heel. How did it actually end?*

[PAUSE]

We do this constantly, this misreading. We look at the one who cut every corner and got rich — and in the *middle* of his story, he looks like he won. We look at the one who lied and was believed, who oppressed and was obeyed — and in the *middle*, the lie looks like strength. The Quran's entire pedagogy of history is built to break this illusion. It says, again and again: *do not appraise a life by its middle. Travel the land. Look at the ruins. Read the ending.* Because the *ʿāqibah* — the heel — tells the truth that the impressive opening concealed.

And this is not only about ancient nations. The most personal application is this: **you are, right now, in the middle of your own story.** You cannot see your *ʿāqibah* — it is behind you, following, taking shape from your steps. The believer's discipline is to live each moment *as the people of the good ending lived it* — to ask not "how does this look now?" but "what heel is this step printing? What is following behind me as I do this?"

[PAUSE]

So here is tomorrow's practice, the most concrete thing I can give you from this verse. Twice in the Quran Allah promises that the good *ʿāqibah* belongs to *al-muttaqīn* — the God-conscious (28:83). The end is not random; it grows from the steps. So tonight, take one decision you are facing — one comfort you're protecting, one truth you've been avoiding because it's inconvenient — and do what verse 25 commands. *Fa-nẓur.* **Look.** Not at how it feels in the middle. Look at the heel. Play it to its end. Ask: *if I keep walking this exact way, what comes up behind me?* And then take the step you'd want following you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back and see how the seven words have become a single, seamless instruction.

The verse opened with **intaqamnā** — not rage, but *measured, deliberate, sovereign justice* that took its time. It named the destroyed not by their proud lineage but by their action: **al-mukadhdhibīn**, the ones who made a labor of denying a truth that had already reached them. And in the middle it *turned its face to you* — **fa-nẓur kayfa kāna** — and commanded you not merely to note *what* happened but to contemplate *how.*

And the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the **same insight from two directions.**

From the side of the *words*: the people who built their identity on inheritance — "we found our fathers upon a way" — lost that identity entirely and were left with only the name of their action.

From the side of the *theme*: that is exactly what *ʿāqibah* means. The heel. The thing that follows behind you, taking the shape of your steps, not the shape of your ancestry. In the end you are not what you inherited. **You are what followed your feet.**

The mutrafūn read their lives forward from an impressive beginning and felt safe. Allah commands us to read backward from the *ʿāqibah* — and live now as the people of the good ending lived.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I let *familiar* quietly become my definition of *true* — so that I no longer even weigh the warner's question?
2. If a truth arrived tomorrow that was genuinely better than what I "found my fathers upon" — would I have the willingness the mutrafūn lacked?
3. When I appraise my own choices, am I reading from the middle (how does this look, how does this feel now?) or from the *ʿāqibah* (what is following up behind me)?
4. What is the one action I am currently *repeating* — the way *mukadhdhib* is built on repetition — that I would not want to be the name I'm finally called by?
5. If my *ʿāqibah* is taking shape from today's steps, what heel did I print today?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When comfort lets us mistake our inheritance for the truth and our denial hardens into a habit, Allah's answer is justice that took its time — and His command to us is to stop reading life from its flattering middle and **look instead at the end, the heel that follows every step we take.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will not leave this verse and overthrow your entire inheritance tomorrow. That is not the invitation. The invitation is smaller and far harder: the next time something true and inconvenient arrives — a command that costs you, a correction that bruises your pride, a warner's question you'd rather not answer — *don't answer it the way the mutrafūn did.* Don't reach for "but this is what we've always done." Pause. Look at the heel. And take the step you'd want walking up behind you.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You who took retribution with justice and not with haste — do not let us be among those who call Your truth false after it has reached us. Loosen our grip on every inheritance that is not from You, and give us the courage to choose the truer thing even when it costs us. And when our steps have run their course, let the heel that follows us be the good ending You promised to those who are conscious of You. Āmīn.*
