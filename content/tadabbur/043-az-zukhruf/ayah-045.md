# Ask the Messengers: The Unbroken Testimony Against Ancestral Faith

## Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 45

> وَسْـَٔلْ مَنْ أَرْسَلْنَا مِن قَبْلِكَ مِن رُّسُلِنَآ أَجَعَلْنَا مِن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ءَالِهَةً يُعْبَدُونَ

> *Wa-s'al man arsalnā min qablika min rusulinā — a-jaʿalnā min dūni-r-Raḥmāni ālihatan yuʿbadūn.*

> **"And ask those We sent before you of Our messengers: did We ever appoint, besides the Most Merciful, gods to be worshipped?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

I want you to stand in Mecca for a moment, before this ayah arrives.

There is an argument happening in the streets. It is not really an argument about the existence of God — almost everyone in Mecca believed in *Allah* as a supreme Creator. If you asked them who made the heavens, who sends down the rain, who holds the stars, they would say *Allah*. The Qur'an itself records this: when you ask them who created them, they say *Allah* (43:87).

So the argument is not about *whether* there is a God. The argument is about whether anyone or anything deserves a share of your devotion alongside Him. The Meccans wanted both. They wanted the great Creator far away in the heavens — and they wanted their intermediaries, their idols, their inherited shrines, their ancestral saints, close at hand. Beings to touch. Beings to bargain with. Beings their fathers had honored before them.

And when the Prophet ﷺ called them to worship Allah *alone*, they reached, again and again, for one defense. Listen to it, because the whole surah is built around it:

> *"They said: We found our fathers upon a way (ummah), and we are guided by their footsteps."* (43:22-23)

This is the wall. This is the real obstacle. Not philosophy. Not evidence. **Inheritance.** *Our fathers did this. We are simply walking in their footsteps. Who are you to tell us that everyone who came before us was wrong?*

It is one of the oldest and strongest arguments a human heart ever makes. It feels like loyalty. It feels like humility. *I am not arrogant enough to think I know better than my whole lineage.* And it has stopped more people at the threshold of truth than any clever objection ever has.

So how do you answer it?

[PAUSE]

Here is where the ayah does something I want you to feel before we even open it. The Meccans appealed to an authority: **the fathers.** And Allah does not respond by saying "ignore authority, think for yourself." He responds by raising a *higher* authority — and inviting them to investigate it.

You want to appeal to those who came before you? *Fine. Let me show you who really came before you.* Not your fathers. **The messengers.**

> **"And ask those We sent before you of Our messengers..."**

### The Hook

There is a command buried in this ayah that should stop us in our tracks. Allah tells His Prophet to **ask**. To pose a question. To investigate.

Think about how strange that is. The One who knows all things is staging an inquiry. And the question He frames is one whose answer is so certain, so total, so unbroken across the entire history of revelation, that the *asking itself* becomes the argument.

What is the question? And who, exactly, is being told to ask — and who is really meant to hear the answer?

Stay with me. Because by the end, I think you will see that this single ayah quietly dismantles the most powerful excuse the human heart has ever invented — and it does it not with a rebuke, but with an *invitation.*

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: وَسْـَٔلْ — The Command to Ask

Let me begin with something we do every day without noticing.

When you are completely certain of something, and someone doubts you, you don't usually argue. You say: *"Go ask anyone. Ask my whole family. Ask everyone who was there."* The strength of your position is not in your insistence — it is in your **willingness to be checked.** Only someone telling the truth invites the cross-examination. The liar wants to end the conversation; the truthful one opens it up.

There is a name for this in human speech. When someone says "go ahead, ask around," they are not really seeking information. They already know what the answer will be. The command to ask is itself the proof. The Arabic rhetoricians called this kind of construction **taqrīr** — using a question or a command not to gather knowledge, but to *establish a settled fact* in the mind of the one you are addressing.

Now look at the very first word of our ayah.

> **وَسْـَٔلْ** — *wa-s'al* — "And ask."

It is an imperative. A direct command. The root is **س-أ-ل** (s-'-l), the plain, ordinary verb "to ask, to question, to inquire." Nothing exotic. The verb you use when you ask a neighbor for directions.

And it is given to the Prophet ﷺ himself. *You, Muhammad — ask.*

Let's sit with this. The Prophet ﷺ is being commanded to conduct an inquiry. To go and pose a question to the messengers who came before him.

Now here is the first thing that should make us pause. **Ask them — how?** The messengers before him had died. Musa, ʿIsa, Ibrahim, Nuh — centuries, millennia separated them from this moment in Mecca. How does one "ask" the dead prophets a question?

The classical scholars sat with exactly this difficulty, and the ways they answered it are themselves illuminating. Let me give you the composite picture, because each angle adds a layer.

**One tradition** — narrated as an *athar* attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, reported by al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī — connects this to the night of al-Isrāʾ, the night journey. On that night, the report says, Allah gathered the messengers — Adam and the prophets from among his descendants — and the Prophet ﷺ led them in prayer in Jerusalem. And so, on this reading, the "asking" was literally possible: the messengers were *present*, assembled, and could be questioned directly. (We should be careful and honest here: this is an *athar*, a companion-report, not a saying traced in a continuous chain to the Prophet ﷺ himself. The scholars preserved more than one account.)

**A second reading**, which al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar lean toward, takes "those We sent" to mean the *followers* of the messengers — the sincere people of the earlier scriptures, the carriers of the prophetic message. *Ask the People of the Book who held faithfully to what their prophets brought: did any prophet ever sanction the worship of another beside Allah?*

Notice that al-Jalālayn then says something remarkable: **on either reading, the Prophet ﷺ did not actually need to ask.** Why not? Because — and here is the word — the entire point of the command is **taqrīr**: to establish, before the watching mushrikīn of Quraysh, the settled and undeniable fact that *no messenger and no scripture ever came from Allah commanding the worship of any besides Him.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The command "ask" was never really aimed at gathering an answer. The Prophet ﷺ already knew the answer. Allah already knew the answer. **The command to ask is the argument itself.** It is Allah saying to all of Mecca, and to all of us: *the case for tawḥīd is so total that I am willing to put it to cross-examination. Go. Ask every messenger I ever sent. Check the entire record of revelation from the first prophet to the last. See if you can find a single one who pointed worship anywhere but to Me.*

And the silence that follows that question — the fact that not one messenger in all of history can be produced who said otherwise — *that* silence is the verdict.

Here is what this single word reveals. Truth invites investigation; only falsehood fears it. The Meccans had built their religion on an authority — the fathers — that conveniently *could not be cross-examined*, because the dead fathers cannot be asked whether they were guided or merely guessing. Allah counters with an authority that *welcomes* the inquiry: the messengers, whose unanimous testimony stretches unbroken across all of time.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ — Of All the Names, *This* One

Let me ask you to do something. When you imagine a verse condemning false worship — a verse about people who set up rivals to God — what name of God would you expect it to use?

Most of us, instinctively, would expect a name of *power*. Al-ʿAzīz, the Almighty. Al-Jabbār, the Compeller. Al-Qahhār, the Subduer. A name with thunder in it. Because the offense seems to call for majesty: *how dare you set up rivals to the All-Powerful?*

Now read what Allah actually chose.

> **أَجَعَلْنَا مِن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ءَالِهَةً يُعْبَدُونَ**
> "Did We appoint, besides **Ar-Raḥmān** — the Most Merciful — gods to be worshipped?"

Not al-ʿAzīz. Not al-Jabbār. **Ar-Raḥmān.**

The root is **ر-ح-م** (r-ḥ-m), the root of the womb (*raḥim*), the root of mercy, tenderness, encompassing compassion. And *Ar-Raḥmān* is not merely "the merciful one" — its very structure in Arabic, the form *faʿlān*, conveys a mercy that is full to overflowing, a mercy that is vast, immediate, saturating everything. It is the mercy that gives the sun and the rain and the breath in your lungs to believer and denier alike, without being asked, without being earned.

Stay with the choice. Why *here*? Why name **mercy** precisely at the moment of exposing the crime of false worship?

Let's think about what the word *besides* is doing. **مِن دُونِ** — *min dūni* — "besides, apart from, to the exclusion of." The picture is of turning *away* from one thing toward another. *Did We appoint, in place of the Most Merciful, other gods?*

Now feel the weight of it. The thing they turned away from is not described as raw power demanding submission. It is described as the **overflowing tenderness** that sustains them. The tragedy is not framed as *you defied the Strong One.* It is framed as: **you turned your devotion away from the very source of every mercy you have ever received.**

[PAUSE]

This is the genius of the word choice, and it is doing structural work no other name could do. Consider what the Meccans believed about their idols. They did not worship idols because the idols *fed* them or *sustained* them — even they admitted Allah was the Provider. They worshipped intermediaries to draw *near*, to gain favor, to feel cared for. They were, in a confused and broken way, *reaching for mercy.* Reaching for closeness. Reaching for a tenderness they could touch.

And Ar-Raḥmān is Allah saying: **the very thing you are reaching for in those stones — I AM that thing, in its infinite form, and you have turned away from Me to find a counterfeit of My own mercy.**

It is like a child who has a parent of boundless love, and who runs instead to a cold statue in the yard hoping it might love them back. The horror is not the disobedience. The horror is the *misdirection of longing.* The thirst was real. The well it ran to was dry.

And notice the asymmetry — read the absence here as carefully as the presence. Allah names *His* mercy: Ar-Raḥmān. But the false gods receive no quality at all. They are not "the merciful gods" or "the powerful gods." They are simply *ālihatan* — "gods," indefinite, bare, undescribed. One side of this verse overflows with named mercy. The other side is an empty noun. The contrast is the argument. You traded an ocean of named tenderness for an undefined nothing.

What this single word reveals: the call away from false worship is not, at its root, a threat. It is a wounded invitation from the One whose defining attribute is mercy — *why did you go elsewhere, when mercy itself was here all along?*

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: يُعْبَدُونَ — Gods That Only Receive

There is one more word, and it is the last word of the ayah, and it is easy to read past it. But Arabic has hidden a quiet devastation inside its grammar.

> **ءَالِهَةً يُعْبَدُونَ** — *ālihatan yuʿbadūn* — "gods **to be worshipped.**"

Look at the verb يُعْبَدُونَ. The root is **ع-ب-د** (ʿ-b-d), to worship, to serve, to devote oneself. But notice the *form* of the verb. It is **passive.** Not "gods who *do*" anything. "Gods *to be worshipped*" — gods who are *acted upon.*

Let me teach the device first, because it lands harder once you see it as a general principle. In any language, the difference between active and passive voice is the difference between a doer and a done-to. "The lion hunts" versus "the lion is hunted." The grammar itself tells you who has agency and who is merely on the receiving end of someone else's action.

Now feel what Allah has done. When describing the *true* object of worship, this verse overflows with action: *We sent. We appointed. Ask.* Living verbs. And the divine name itself, Ar-Raḥmān, is pure active giving — mercy that pours out, sustains, creates.

But when the verse arrives at the false gods, the grammar goes **passive.** *Gods to be worshipped.* They do nothing. They send nothing. They give nothing. They create nothing. They are not even described as *receiving* worship by their own merit — they are simply the inert object on the far end of a misdirected verb. All the motion in their existence comes from the worshippers who prop them up. Take away the devotee, and the "god" does not even register as a god.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it reframes the entire nature of idolatry. We tend to imagine the false god as a rival *power* — a competing force pulling worship toward itself. But the grammar says: the false god has no pull at all. It is utterly passive. **The entire energy of idolatry comes from the human being, not from the idol.** The idol does not seduce; the heart projects. The stone does not reach for you; you reach for the stone and pour your own longing into it.

And here is what completes the picture when you set the three words side by side. The verse contains exactly one true active Giver — *Ar-Raḥmān*, mercy in motion. It contains an inquiry that no one can answer against Him — *ask.* And it ends on the false gods frozen in the passive voice, recipients of a devotion they can neither earn, return, nor even acknowledge — *yuʿbadūn.*

What this final word reveals: every false object of worship — then and now — is fundamentally *passive.* It receives your devotion and gives back nothing, because it has nothing to give. The only One in the entire sentence who *acts*, who *sends*, who *shows mercy*, is the One you were made to worship.

[PAUSE — major]

### The Bridge

So look at what the grammar of this ayah has shown us. A command — *ask* — that is itself the proof, because truth alone welcomes cross-examination. A divine name — *Ar-Raḥmān* — chosen with surgical precision to reveal that the crime of false worship is, at heart, a misdirected longing for mercy. And a passive verb — *yuʿbadūn* — that strips every false god of agency and locates all the energy of idolatry in the human heart itself.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way we live, and the things we still, today, set up beside Ar-Raḥmān.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of "We Found Our Fathers"

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, almost certainly, kept doing something — believed something, practiced something, avoided something — for one reason and one reason only: *that's how I was raised. That's what we've always done.* Maybe it was harmless. Maybe it shaped your entire life. But here is the uncomfortable truth: the strongest beliefs most of us hold were not chosen. They were **inherited.** We absorbed them before we were old enough to evaluate them, and by the time we could evaluate them, they no longer felt like beliefs — they felt like *reality itself.*

Psychologists have many names for this. Status quo bias. The appeal to tradition. The deep human tendency to mistake *familiarity* for *truth.* We assume that what has been around the longest, what the most generations have practiced, must be the most validated. The footpath worn deepest must lead somewhere good.

This is exactly the wall the Meccans put up. Remember it:

> *"We found our fathers upon a way, and we are guided by their footsteps."* (43:23)

And notice — this is not stupidity. There is something genuinely *worthy* in it. Loyalty to one's people. Humility before accumulated wisdom. Reluctance to believe that everyone you loved and respected was simply wrong. These are not vices. The heart that says "who am I to overturn my whole lineage?" is, in part, a *good* heart, afraid of arrogance.

So how does Allah answer a good instinct that has been pointed in a fatal direction?

He does not say: *your fathers were fools.* He does not say: *throw off all tradition.* He does something far more surgical. He says — **you appealed to those who came before you? Then let me introduce you to who *really* came before you.**

> **"Ask those We sent before you of Our messengers..."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the move. The Meccans built their authority on a chain of ancestors — but a chain that, conveniently, *terminates in silence.* You cannot ask your great-great-grandfather whether he had any actual evidence, or whether he, too, was just copying *his* father. The chain of "we found our fathers" is a chain of people each of whom was, in turn, simply following the one before. It is following all the way down. **No one in the chain ever checked.**

Allah offers a different chain. Not a chain of *imitators*, but a chain of *messengers* — each one independently sent, each one receiving revelation directly from the source, separated by centuries and oceans and languages, who never met one another, who could not have conspired. Adam. Nuh. Ibrahim. Musa. ʿIsa. Across all the corners of the earth, across all the ages of history.

And the staggering fact — the fact the whole ayah turns on — is that **every single one of them said the same thing.**

> *"And We sent no messenger before you except that We revealed to him: there is no god but Me, so worship Me."* (21:25)

> *"And We certainly sent into every nation a messenger, saying: Worship Allah and avoid false objects of worship."* (16:36)

This is the answer to ancestral conformity. Not "ignore the past" — but *look at the past more honestly.* Your fathers were a chain of people copying each other in one corner of Arabia. The messengers were a chain of independent witnesses, scattered across all of human history, who all testified to the *exact same truth* without ever coordinating. Which chain is the real "tradition"? Which is the inheritance worth keeping?

Here is the movement I want you to take home. **Inheritance is not the enemy. Unexamined inheritance is.** The ayah does not ask you to become rootless, to throw off everything you were given and reinvent yourself from nothing — that is the modern delusion, and it is its own kind of slavery, to whatever is fashionable *now.* The ayah asks something harder and better: *trace your beliefs back to their source, and see whether the source is revelation or merely repetition.*

What does this change about tomorrow? It means taking one belief you hold — about God, about worship, about what you owe and to whom — that you have never actually examined, and asking it the question this ayah asks: *not "did my fathers do this?" but "did the messengers bring this?"* That single question, honestly applied, is the difference between a faith you *inherited* and a faith you *hold.*

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Counterfeits of Mercy

Now let me bring this all the way into your own chest, because it would be too easy to leave this ayah in seventh-century Mecca with people bowing to stone idols. None of us bow to stone idols. So we close the verse and feel safe.

Don't close it yet.

Remember what we discovered about the divine name. The Meccans were not, in their own minds, rejecting mercy. They were *reaching* for it — reaching, through their intermediaries, for closeness, for favor, for a tenderness they could feel and touch and bargain with. The idol was a *counterfeit of mercy.* It promised the thing only Ar-Raḥmān truly gives, and delivered nothing — *yuʿbadūn*, passive, inert, unable to return a single ounce of the devotion poured into it.

Now ask yourself, honestly: **what do *you* reach for, when what you are really thirsty for is mercy?**

Because the mechanism has not changed at all. It has only changed costume. We do not carve idols anymore. We build them out of other things.

Think of the person who pours their entire sense of worth into their career — who needs the promotion, the title, the recognition, not for the money but because they are thirsty to feel *valued*, to feel they *matter.* That is a thirst for mercy. And the career is *yuʿbadūn* — passive. It takes the years, the health, the evenings, the relationships, and it gives back nothing that lasts; it cannot love you, it cannot forgive you, and the day you cannot perform, it discards you.

Think of the person who needs the relationship to complete them — who has made another human being the source of all their security, the answer to every ache. That is a thirst for mercy, aimed at a finite creature who was never built to carry the weight of a god. And when that creature inevitably fails them — as every creature does — the collapse feels like the end of the world, because they had made it their world.

Think of the endless scroll, the next purchase, the chasing of status, the need for the approval of the crowd. Every one of these is a heart reaching for the thing only *Ar-Raḥmān* truly holds — to be seen, to be held, to be enough, to be loved without condition — and pouring that infinite longing into a finite, passive thing that can only receive and never give.

[PAUSE]

This is what makes the divine name in this ayah so merciful even in its rebuke. Allah does not say to the heart: *stop wanting.* The wanting is not the problem. The thirst for mercy, for closeness, for unconditional love — **that thirst is sacred. He put it there.** The problem is the *address.* You are mailing the deepest letter of your soul to a recipient who cannot read it.

> *"Did We appoint, besides the Most Merciful, gods to be worshipped?"*

Read it now not as an accusation but as the wounded question of the One who is *Ar-Raḥmān*: *Why are you giving the devotion that was meant for infinite mercy to things that can give you nothing back? I am the One you are actually looking for. I always was.*

And here is the paradox we should let sit without rushing to resolve it. The very things we make into false gods are often, in themselves, *gifts from Ar-Raḥmān.* The work, the spouse, the beauty, the comfort — these are His mercies. There is nothing wrong with the gift. The disorder is only this: **we love the gift in the place of the Giver. We worship the river and ignore the spring.** The cure is not to throw the gifts away. The cure is to follow every gift back upstream to its source, until the love of the gift becomes a *doorway* to the love of the One who gave it — not a wall that hides Him.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is a single question you can ask of any attachment that has begun to rule you: *am I reaching, through this thing, for a mercy that only Allah can actually give?* And if the honest answer is yes — then you have not found a thing to be ashamed of. You have found a prayer that was addressed to the wrong house. Re-address it. Take the longing, and turn it, finally, toward Ar-Raḥmān.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this ayah turn out to be one truth seen from two directions.

The **first half** — *ask the messengers* — is the answer to the *outer* authority that enslaves us: the tyranny of inheritance, of "we found our fathers upon a way." It says: do not let an unexamined chain of imitation decide your eternity. There is a truer chain, a chain of independent witnesses across all of history, and it speaks with one voice.

The **second half** — *besides Ar-Raḥmān* — is the answer to the *inner* disorder that enslaves us: the misdirection of our deepest longing onto passive, counterfeit gods. It says: the thing you are truly reaching for is mercy, and mercy itself is the One you have been turning away from.

And the two are the same insight. The unbroken testimony of every messenger Allah ever sent — the thing you would discover if you actually *asked* — is precisely this: *there is nothing besides Ar-Raḥmān worth the weight of your worship.* The outer journey of examining your inheritance and the inner journey of redirecting your longing arrive at the identical destination: **Him, alone.**

### The Architecture

Notice now, having done the work, how the verse is built. It opens with an *act* — *ask.* It moves through a chain of *living* divine verbs — *We sent, We appointed.* It names the one true Giver in motion — *Ar-Raḥmān.* And it lands, finally, on the false gods frozen in the passive voice — *yuʿbadūn*, done-to, inert, silent. The grammar itself is a descent from all the action of the divine to the total stillness of the idol. The sentence *performs* its own argument: everything that acts, gives, sends, and shows mercy is on one side — and on the other side is a noun that can only be acted upon. The structure is the proof.

### Questions to Carry

- Which of my beliefs about God and worship have I actually *examined* — and which have I simply *inherited* and never traced back to their source?
- When I feel my deepest thirst — to be seen, held, valued, loved without condition — where do I instinctively send that longing first?
- What in my life have I made *yuʿbadūn* — passive, taking everything from me and unable to give anything back — and yet I keep returning to it?
- If I could "ask the messengers" about the thing I am most attached to, would their unbroken testimony reassure me, or unsettle me?
- The gifts I love most — am I letting them become doorways back to the Giver, or walls that hide Him?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Across all of history not one messenger Allah ever sent was given leave to point human worship at anything besides the Most Merciful — so the deepest longing of your heart was always addressed to Him, and to Him alone.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not need to dismantle your life. You need only to follow your longing upstream. Take the one attachment that has quietly begun to rule you — and ask it the ayah's question. Was it the messengers who taught you to give it your heart, or only the habit of your own thirst? And then take that thirst, gently, and turn it toward the One whose name is mercy itself. He is not a stranger demanding submission. He is the One you were looking for the whole time.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Ar-Raḥmān — the longing in us is Yours; You placed it there. Forgive us for the times we mailed it to addresses that could never answer. Untangle our inheritance until only what the messengers brought remains, and gather every scattered devotion of our hearts back to You — for there was never, in all of creation, any god besides You worth the weight of a single prostration.* Āmīn.
