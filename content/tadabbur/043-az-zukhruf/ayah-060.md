# The World That Never Was

### Surah Az-Zukhruf, Ayah 60

> وَلَوْ نَشَآءُ لَجَعَلْنَا مِنكُم مَّلَـٰٓئِكَةً فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَخْلُفُونَ
>
> *Wa law nashāʾu la-jaʿalnā minkum malāʾikatan fī al-arḍi yakhlufūn*
>
> **"And if We had willed, We could have made among you — in your very place — angels, succeeding one another in the earth."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let's place ourselves where this verse falls.

The whole surah has been circling a single nerve. The people of Makkah, and behind them a longer argument with the People of the Book, have been disputing about ʿĪsā — Jesus, son of Maryam, peace be upon him. A man born without a father. A sign that broke the ordinary chain of cause and effect. And human beings, when they encounter something that breaks the chain, do one of two things: they worship it, or they argue endlessly about it. Both were happening.

So Allah, just before our verse, says something disarmingly simple about ʿĪsā: *"He was nothing but a servant whom We favored, and We made him an example for the Children of Israel"* (43:59). Not a god. Not a partner. A servant — upon whom a gift was placed.

And then comes our ayah. And here is where you have to feel the room.

Imagine you are standing in a crowd that is utterly amazed at one extraordinary man. Amazed that he came into being in a way no one else did. And the Owner of all being leans into that amazement and says, almost quietly:

**You think *that* is remarkable? If I had simply wished it, I would not have made one man unusual — I would have wiped the slate of the entire human race and filled this earth with a completely different kind of creature, living where you live, doing what you do, generation after generation, as if you had never been here at all.**

[PAUSE]

That is the scale of what is being said. And notice — it is said as a hypothetical. *"If We had willed."* Which means He did not will it. Which means you are here. You are reading this. You exist.

And that little word — *if* — is hiding something that, once you see it, you may never be able to un-see.

**The Hook:** This is one of the shortest verses in its passage — eight words. And in those eight words is a door to a world that never came into being: a parallel earth, populated, busy, inherited across generations — *without you in it.* The question this verse forces is not "Could Allah have done that?" Of course He could. The question is far more intimate. It is: **Why are you here at all? And what does it do to a human heart to discover that its own existence was never necessary — only willed?**

Let's go find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: لَوْ — The Grammar of the World That Never Was

Before we touch the verse, let's learn one small thing about how Arabic — and really, how every language — handles the *unreal*.

In English, you can feel the difference between two sentences:

- "*If* I leave now, I'll catch the train." (Maybe I will, maybe I won't — it's open.)
- "*If* I had left earlier, I would have caught the train." (But I didn't. It's closed. It's over.)

The second one isn't really about the train. It's about a road not taken. It describes a world that *did not happen.* Grammarians call this the **counterfactual** — the "if" of things that never came to pass.

Arabic has a dedicated tool for exactly this, and it is the word our verse opens with: **لَوْ** (*law*).

Classical Arabic grammarians described **لَوْ** with a famous phrase: it is *ḥarfu imtināʿin li-mtināʿ* — **"a particle of prevention because of prevention."** That is dense, so let's unpack it slowly. **لَوْ** tells you that the *result* did not happen — *because* the *condition* did not happen. It is the grammar of a sealed door. It opens onto a possibility precisely in order to tell you that possibility was never realized.

Compare it to its cousin **إِنْ** (*in*), the open "if": *"if you study, you will pass"* — the door is still open, the outcome still hanging in the air. **لَوْ** is different. **لَوْ** is the closed door. It shows you the room behind it and then tells you: *you were never in this room.*

Now look at how the verse begins.

> **وَلَوْ نَشَآءُ** — *"And if We had willed..."*

Allah did not begin with **إِنْ**. He began with **لَوْ**.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The very first grammatical choice of the verse is a choice to describe **a world that does not exist.** Before we are told anything about angels or earth or succession, we are told the entire scenario is *counterfactual* — sealed off, prevented, never-was.

Why does that matter so much?

Because of what it does to *you*, the listener. The moment Allah says **لَوْ نَشَآءُ** — "had We willed" — He has placed your entire existence on one side of a divine choice. There was a possibility: an earth without human beings. And there is the reality: an earth *with* you. And the only thing standing between those two worlds is **نَشَآء** — *"We will."*

Notice the second linguistic detail here, and it is exquisite. The willing — *nashāʾu* — is in the **imperfect tense** (the corpus confirms it: present/ongoing form). Not "if We *had once* willed" frozen in the past, but a living, present, ongoing *mashīʾah* — divine will, eternally active. The verb's own tense whispers that this is not a decision made and filed away long ago. It is a will that holds your world in place *right now*, this second, as you breathe.

Here is what this single word **لَوْ** reveals, and then we will let it sit:

**You are not standing on solid ground. You are standing on a divine "if." Your existence is not a fact that simply *is* — it is a possibility that was *chosen*, and is being chosen, against an equally real possibility in which you never appear at all.**

### Element Two: مِنكُم — The Smallest Word with the Largest Threat

Now we come to a word so small you would walk straight past it. Two letters fused to a pronoun: **مِنكُم** (*minkum*).

On the surface it just means "from you" or "among you." But Arabic has a quiet feature here that the classical commentators caught, and once you see it, the whole verse tilts.

Let's first teach the concept in plain life.

The little preposition **مِن** (*min*) in Arabic is a workhorse. It usually means "from" or "some of." *"Some of the people"* — *minan-nās.* That is its everyday job — the **partitive** *min*, the *min* of "a portion drawn from a whole." If we read the verse that way, it would mean: *"We could have made, from among you, some of you into angels."* A strange thought, but grammatically possible.

But the classical scholars noticed something. Al-Qurṭubī records that al-Azharī, the great lexicographer, said **مِن here carries the meaning of *badal* — "in place of," "instead of."** He even used *this very verse* as his proof that **مِن** can mean substitution. And the same reading comes through al-Suddī and Mujāhid: *"angels in your place,"* *"angels who would cultivate the earth instead of you."*

Do you feel the difference between the two readings?

- **Partitive:** "some *of* you would become angels" — you are still in the picture, transformed.
- **Substitutive (badal):** "angels *instead of* you" — **you are gone. Erased. Replaced.**

[PAUSE]

The dominant weight of the classical tradition lands on the second. Al-Baghawī is blunt about it: *"if We willed, We would have destroyed you, and made — in your place — angels who would inherit the earth, cultivate it, worship Me and obey Me."* Al-Jalālayn says the same in a single chilling phrase: the angels would replace you *bi-an nuhlikakum* — **"by destroying you."** Al-Muyassar: *"angels succeeding one another, in place of the children of Adam."*

Now read the verse again with that weight restored:

> **لَجَعَلْنَا مِنكُم مَّلَـٰٓئِكَةً** — *"We would have placed — instead of you — angels."*

This is the load-bearing center of the verse. And here is the analogy that makes it real.

Think of what it means to be *replaceable* at a job. Not fired in anger — simply *replaced*, smoothly, by someone who does what you did, sits where you sat, and within a month no one remembers the chair was ever yours. There is a particular cold feeling in that. It is not pain exactly. It is the discovery that the world's machinery did not need *you* specifically — it needed *the function*, and you happened to be filling it.

Now scale that feeling up to the size of the entire human race, across all of history, in the eyes of the One who made it. That is **مِنكُم** in this verse. Allah is saying: the role of "earth's inhabitant," the role of "the one who cultivates and dwells and inherits" — that role does not belong to *you* by right. It was *assigned* to you. And it could, with a single act of will, have been assigned to a completely different creature who would have done it perfectly — perhaps better, *"worshipping Me and obeying Me,"* as al-Baghawī adds, never sinning, never disputing about ʿĪsā or anyone else.

And now read the **absence** in the verse — because this is where it truly opens.

The verse does *not* say: *"We could have made angels somewhere else, in another realm, alongside you."* It says **فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** — **"in the earth."** *This* earth. *Your* earth. The same soil. The verse deliberately places the replacement *in your exact location.* It does not give the angels a separate stage. It gives them *your* stage and removes you from it.

What this single word reveals:

**Your place in creation is not yours by entitlement. It is yours by appointment. And what is appointed can be re-appointed.**

### Element Three: يَخْلُفُونَ — The Word That Remembers Who You Were Supposed to Be

The verse ends on a single verb, and it is the most theologically loaded word of the eight: **يَخْلُفُونَ** (*yakhlufūn*).

Its root is **خ-ل-ف** (*kh-l-f*) — and you already know this root, even if you don't know that you know it. It is the root of **خَلِيفَة** (*khalīfah*) — the word Allah used when He first announced the creation of the human being:

> *"And when your Lord said to the angels, 'Indeed I am placing upon the earth a **khalīfah**...'"* (2:30)

Let's teach the root before we feel its weight here.

**خ-ل-ف** in the Arabic lexicon clusters around one core idea: **to come after, to succeed, to take the place of one who has gone.** From it comes *khalaf* (a successor), *khilāfah* (succession, vicegerency), *ikhtilāf* (to differ — because to come "after and behind" is to diverge), and *khalīfah* — the one who succeeds, who stands in for another, who inherits a responsibility and carries it forward.

Now here is the breathtaking move. In Sūrat al-Baqarah, *you* — humanity — are the **khalīfah.** You are the successor placed on earth, the one given the trust, the inheritor. The angels, in that scene, *questioned* the appointment: *"Will You place upon it one who will cause corruption...?"* (2:30). And Allah's answer was, in effect: *I know what you do not know.* The role went to the human being. The angels were *not* chosen for it.

And now, in our verse, watch what happens to that very arrangement:

> **مَّلَـٰٓئِكَةً فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَخْلُفُونَ** — *"angels, in the earth, succeeding one another."*

The verb is **يَخْلُفُونَ** — from the *same root* as **khalīfah**. The classical scholars give it two complementary senses, and we take them as one composite picture, the way you'd circle a sculpture to see it whole:

- Al-Baghawī and al-Muyassar: the angels would **succeed one another**, generation upon generation — *yakhluf baʿḍuhum baʿḍā* — the way human generations replace each other, dying and being born, handing the earth forward.
- And al-Baghawī adds the deeper sense: they would be **khalaf minkum** — *successors in place of you.* They would inherit the trust that was yours.

Do you see what the verse has done?

[PAUSE]

It has taken the exact dignity Allah gave the human being in 2:30 — *khilāfah*, vicegerency, the honor the angels themselves did not receive — and it has shown that this very dignity could have been handed to those same angels with a single word. The role you were *honored* with in Sūrat al-Baqarah is the role that is *quietly threatened* in Sūrat al-Zukhruf.

This is not a different teaching from 2:30. It is its shadow. In 2:30 you learn *what you were made.* Here you learn *that it was never owed to you.* The same word — *khalīfah / yakhlufūn* — carries the gift in one surah and the contingency of the gift in the other.

What this final verb reveals:

**The highest honor ever given to humanity — to be Allah's appointed successor on the earth — was, from the very first moment, a loan. The angels who once questioned your appointment could have received it instead. You hold the trust not because you earned the title, but because He withheld it from everyone else and gave it to you.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these eight words have built, working together:

- **لَوْ** told us this is a world that never was — your existence rests on a divine "if."
- **مِنكُم** told us the alternative world was *your* world, with you erased and replaced in your exact place.
- **يَخْلُفُونَ** told us that even your highest honor — *khilāfah* — was a gift that could have gone to another.

Three words, one message, descending like three steps into the same depth: **you are contingent.** Not necessary. Not owed your place. Held in existence by an active, ongoing, present-tense will.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about how to *live* inside a body and a life that you did not have to be given.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Illusion of Indispensability

Let's start where you actually live.

There is a quiet belief most of us carry, so deep we never say it aloud. It is the belief that the world, in some sense, *needs us.* That our being here matters to the structure of things. That if we were removed, there would be a hole shaped exactly like us, and the world would feel the draft.

Psychologists sometimes call the everyday version of this *"main character" thinking* — the unexamined sense that you are the center around which events arrange themselves, that you are the protagonist and everyone else is supporting cast. It is not arrogance, exactly. It is just the default setting of a self trapped inside its own viewpoint. You have never experienced a single moment of reality from outside your own eyes. Of *course* you feel central. You're the only camera you've ever looked through.

And then comes a verse like this one, and it does something very gentle and very total.

[PAUSE]

It says: *I could have run the entire earth without you. Not "without humans" — without you, and without your whole species, replaced by a creature that would have done the job and never missed you.*

Now — be careful here, because the *nafs* will try to turn this into despair. "So I don't matter. So I'm nothing." That is the wrong door, and it is the door the ego runs to *because despair is just wounded pride.* The ego would rather be tragically worthless than honestly contingent, because worthlessness is still *about me.*

The verse is not offering you despair. Watch the actual logic.

If Allah *could* have filled the earth with angels — sinless, obedient, perfect worshippers, as al-Baghawī described — and **He did not** — then your presence here is not an accident and it is not a necessity. **It is a choice.** *His* choice. You were *willed.* Out of all the creatures that could have inherited this earth, He looked at the possibility of *you* — flawed, forgetful, disputing, sinning *you* — and He said **نَشَآء**, "We will it," and waved away the perfect alternative.

Do you feel the inversion?

The same verse that demolishes your sense of *being necessary* hands you, in the same breath, the staggering fact of *being wanted.* You are not load-bearing in the structure of the cosmos. The cosmos does not lean on you. And yet here you are — not because the universe required you, but because your Lord *preferred* a world with you in it over a flawless world without you.

That is a completely different foundation to stand on. Indispensability is a brittle foundation — the moment you're shown to be replaceable, it shatters, and most modern despair is the sound of that shattering. But *being chosen* is unbreakable, because it never depended on your being necessary in the first place.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tonight, before you sleep, try one sentence: *"I did not have to exist. An earth full of angels was on the table, and He chose me instead."* Sit in it until the two feelings arrive in order — first the smallness, then, underneath it, the astonishment. That order matters. The smallness is the floor. The astonishment is what you build on it. A heart that lives in that order stops demanding that the world treat it as central, and starts living in quiet gratitude that it was let in at all.

### Theme Two: The Trust You Did Not Earn

Now let's go to the second depth, and it grows directly out of **يَخْلُفُونَ.**

Here is a human experience almost everyone has had. You inherit something — a position, a responsibility, a role — and at first you hold it tightly, as if it were yours by right. And then, somewhere along the way, you realize it was *handed* to you. Someone retired and you took their desk. A parent passed and you became the one the family leans on. You did not create the role. You *succeeded into* it. You are, in the precise sense of the word, a *khalīfah* — one who came after, and took the place.

The Quran's vision of the human being on earth is *exactly this.* We did not author our position. We *succeeded* into it — and one day others will succeed into ours. The earth is described again and again as something we are made to *inherit* and then *hand on*:

> *"It is He who has made you successors (khalāʾif) upon the earth..."* (35:39, 6:165)

We are not owners. We are *tenants in a relay.* Each generation takes the baton, runs its leg, and passes it. *Yakhlufūn* — succeeding one another.

And our verse adds the detail that breaks the heart open: **this relay was almost run by someone else entirely.** The angels could have been the runners. The trust — *khilāfah* — was offered, in the architecture of 2:30, against the angels' own objection, and given to us. And here in 43:60, Allah reminds us how thin that margin was: *one act of will and it would have been theirs.*

So what kind of person holds a trust they know they did not earn and could lose?

[PAUSE]

Consider two ways of holding a borrowed thing.

The first is the way of the person who *forgets it's borrowed.* He starts to think the house is his, the body is his, the years are his, the breath is his. And because he thinks they are his by right, he is *careless* with them, and he is *ungrateful*, and when one of them is taken back — health, wealth, a person he loved — he is *outraged*, as though he'd been robbed. He was never robbed. The loan was simply recalled.

The second is the way of the person who *never forgets it's borrowed.* She holds the same house, the same body, the same years — but lightly, the way you hold something precious that belongs to someone you love and who lent it to you in trust. She is careful with it because it isn't hers. She is grateful for it because she knows it could have gone to another. And when part of it is recalled, she grieves — but she is not *outraged*, because you cannot be robbed of what you always knew was a loan.

The entire spiritual difference between those two lives is contained in one realization: **the trust was not earned, and the angels could have had it instead.**

This is why the verse sits exactly where it does — right after Allah calls ʿĪsā *"a servant whom We favored"* (43:59). The whole passage is teaching a single posture: *everything good about your existence is a favor placed upon you, not a wage you were owed.* ʿĪsā's miraculous birth — a favor. Your ordinary birth — equally a favor. Your role on this earth — a favor that was *withheld from the angels and granted to you.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Pick one thing in your life you have been treating as *yours by right* — your job, your health, a relationship, your standing in someone's eyes. Tomorrow, for one day, hold it as a *loan from the One who could have given it to someone else.* Notice how differently you treat what you handle as borrowed. Notice that gratitude and carefulness arrive together, automatically, the moment ownership leaves. That shift — from owner to trustee — is the entire practical content of *khilāfah.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two journeys — the words and the themes — turn out to be the same insight approached from two sides.

From the **linguistic** side, three words descended into one truth: **لَوْ** (you are a willed possibility, not a necessity), **مِنكُم** (you could have been replaced in your exact place), and **يَخْلُفُونَ** (even your highest honor was a transferable gift).

From the **thematic** side, two human realities rose to meet them: you are *not indispensable but you are chosen,* and you *hold a trust you did not earn and could lose.*

They are one truth: **Your existence and your honor are both gifts of pure divine will — and a gift, by its nature, is something that could have gone elsewhere, which is exactly what makes it a gift and not a debt.**

The verse takes the thing the *nafs* most wants to believe — *I matter because I am necessary* — and replaces it with something far better and far harder: *I matter because I was chosen, out of a world of perfect alternatives, by the One whose choosing is the only reason anything exists at all.*

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I holding something as *mine by right* that is actually *mine by appointment*?
2. When I imagine being replaced — at work, in a family, in someone's affection — does my despair come from love, or from wounded pride that I was not *necessary*?
3. If Allah chose a world *with me* over a flawless world of angels without me, what does He want from the creature He preferred?
4. The angels questioned whether humanity deserved this trust (2:30). Living as I live — would I be the evidence for their objection, or against it?
5. What would I do differently today if I truly held my place on this earth as a loan that could be recalled at any moment?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**You were never necessary — you were chosen; and the honor you carry on this earth is a trust that could have been given to angels, withheld from them, and placed on you, so that gratitude, not entitlement, is the only honest way to hold your own life.**

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this verse asks you to feel worthless. It asks you to feel *contingent* — and then to feel the warmth hidden inside contingency. The whole earth could have run without you. It does not. You are here. That is not a fact to take for granted; it is a decision to be grateful for. Let the smallness come first. Then let the astonishment come after it and stay. Walk out of this reflection not crushed by your replaceability, but quietly undone by the fact that the One who could have replaced everyone, kept you.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You could have filled this earth with creatures more worthy than me, and You chose me instead — let me never mistake Your favor for my entitlement. Make me a trustee who never forgets the trust is borrowed, grateful for the place You gave me and gentle with all that You have only lent. And when You recall what is Yours, find me thankful that You let me carry it at all.*

*Āmīn.*
