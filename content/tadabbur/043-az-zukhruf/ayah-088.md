# "O My Lord, These Are a People Who Do Not Believe"
## The Prophet's Whispered Grief — Az-Zukhruf 43:88

---

> *wa qīlihi yā Rabbi inna hāʾulāʾi qawmun lā yuʾminūn*
>
> "And [Allah holds] his saying, 'O my Lord, indeed these are a people who do not believe.'"

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

I want you to stand for a moment inside the surah this verse ends.

It is called *Az-Zukhruf*. The word means gold — specifically, gold as **ornament**. Decoration. The gilding on a thing. It is the surah of adornment, and from its opening it is preoccupied with people who cannot separate the **value** of something from the **glitter** of it.

Listen to the argument these people made, earlier in the very same surah. When the Quran came to Muhammad ﷺ — an orphan, a man of modest means, not the wealthiest of Mecca, not the chief of Ta'if — the leadership of Quraysh looked at him and said, in effect: *if this revelation were really from God, why wasn't it sent down to a great man from one of the two cities?* A man of standing. A man of gold. They had a mental scale, and on that scale, **truth weighed the same as wealth.** A message must be as important as its messenger looks. And their messenger, to their eyes, did not look like much.

So the surah takes this scale apart, piece by piece. It says: We distribute even the *livelihood* of this world by measure — so how could We possibly hand out **prophethood** by the standard of your bank accounts? It goes further, and it says something almost frightening: if it weren't that all of humanity would then rush to disbelief, chasing the glitter — *We would have given the deniers of the Most Merciful houses with roofs of silver, and staircases of silver, and doors of silver, and couches of silver, and gold — zukhruf.* In other words: **the gold you worship is so worthless to Me that I would pave the homes of My enemies with it, the way you might pave a gutter.**

This is the world of the surah. A people dazzled by surfaces. A people who inherited their religion the way you inherit furniture — *"we found our fathers upon a way, and we are following in their footsteps"* — and who could not tell the difference between a truth and a tradition, between gold and God.

And into this world, for years, walks one man. He argues. He pleads. He recites the story of Ibrahim smashing the idols. He recites Musa before Pharaoh. He recites Isa and the mockery. He gives them everything. Every angle. Every mercy. Every warning.

And the surah is now nearly over. You can feel it winding toward its close. Verse 87 lands one final blow to their inconsistency: *"And if you asked them who created them, they would surely say: Allah."* They **admit** the Creator with their tongues. The confession is right there in their mouths. *"So how, then, are they turned away?"*

And then — [PAUSE] — something happens that happens almost nowhere else in the Quran.

The camera turns.

For eighty-seven verses, the voice has been the voice of God, speaking about them, to us, at them. And now, for a single verse, we are allowed to overhear a **different** voice. A quiet one. Not addressed to us. Not an argument for anyone. A man, at the end of himself, turning his face away from the crowd and up toward his Lord, and saying six words that every exhausted human heart already knows how to say.

*"O my Lord — these are a people who do not believe."*

Here is what I want you to feel before we touch a single word of it. This is not a verse of victory. The surah does not end with them believing. It ends with **a sigh.** And the astonishing thing — the thing we are going to spend the next hour on — is what Allah *does* with that sigh. Because your whole life may depend on understanding it.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### First: the word for what he said — *qīl*, not *qāl*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we go near the verse, because if I don't, the first word will slip right past you the way it slips past almost everyone.

In Arabic there is a world of difference between a **verb** and a **verbal noun**. If I say "he ran," that's a verb — an action, happening, tied to a doer and a moment. But if I say "the running," I've turned the action into a **thing.** A noun. Something that can sit on a shelf. Something you can hold, name, point to, keep.

Now — the most ordinary way in the world for Allah to report what the Prophet ﷺ said would be the verb: *wa qāla* — "and he said." That's the natural phrasing. It's how the Quran reports speech hundreds of times. *Qāla Mūsā. Qāla Rabbukum.* He said.

But that is **not** what Allah chose here.

The verse opens: *wa qīlihi.* And that word, *qīl*, is not the verb "he said." It is the **noun** — "his saying," "his utterance," "the saying of his." Allah did not report the Prophet's complaint as an *action that passed.* He preserved it as an **object that remains.**

Sit with that difference for a second, because it's doing enormous work. A verb happens and is gone. *He said it* — and the saying dissolves into the past. But a noun is a **kept thing.** By choosing *qīlihi*, Allah is telling us: this whisper was not a passing event. It was received. It was **caught.** It became a thing that Allah now holds. The complaint has a weight and a location, and the location is with Him.

And notice the pronoun clinging to the end of it — that little *hi*. **His** saying. Not "a saying." Not "the words." *His.* It is attributed, owned, personal. Whose? The one the whole surah has been about defending — Muhammad ﷺ. This is his private word to his Lord, and Allah has stitched the man's name into it with a single letter.

Now — the classical scholars looked at how this word connects grammatically to what comes before it, and here is where it gets beautiful, because they didn't fully agree, and their disagreement is a gift. Some read *qīlihi* as linked to an earlier phrase in the surah about what is **with Allah** — *and with Him is the knowledge of the Hour... and his saying.* On that reading: the Prophet's complaint sits in the same treasury as the knowledge of the Day of Judgment — held by God, known by God. Others read it as governed by a hidden verb: *and He knows his saying.* On that reading: nothing of this whisper escaped Allah's hearing. And still others heard in it a kind of solemn weight laid on the utterance itself, the way you'd set down something heavy and precious.

Here is what's remarkable — you don't have to choose. Each reading is a different camera angle on the **same** reality, and when you lay them over one another the picture only gets richer: the Prophet's grief is *known* by Allah, *held* by Allah, and *weighty* to Allah. Heard. Kept. Honored. That is the composite image. However you turn the word, it points to a Lord who did not let the sigh fall to the ground.

And one more thing about this specific word, *qīl.* It is rare. It is not the everyday word for speech. Where else does the Quran use it? In describing Paradise: *they hear no idle talk there, no sin — only a saying: Peace, Peace.* Only a *qīl* — salām, salām. So this uncommon, weighty word for "an utterance" belongs, elsewhere in the Book, to the speech of Paradise. Hold onto that. We are going to need it before the end.

[PAUSE]

So the first thing this verse does, before it says anything about the people at all, is establish **who is listening.** And the answer is: the One who keeps what you say to Him.

### Second: *yā Rabbi* — the name he reached for

Now watch which name he uses. Because in a moment of pain, the name you reach for tells you everything about what you're actually looking for.

He does not say *yā Allah.* He does not invoke the name of majesty, the name of the Judge, the name of overwhelming power. In the depth of his exhaustion he reaches for one word: *Rabb.*

We translate *Rabb* as "Lord," and the translation is thin, because in Arabic *Rabb* is not primarily about a master's authority. Its root carries the meaning of the one who **nurtures a thing gradually from its beginning to its completion.** The *rabb* of a garden is the one who tends it from seed to fruit. The *rabb* of a child is the one who raises it, feeds it, corrects it, brings it stage by stage into its fullness. *Rabb* is the God of tending. The God who is **committed to bringing you through.**

And clinging to it — again, that little suffix — *Rabb-**i**.* **My** Lord. The possessive. Not "the Lord of the worlds," grand and distant, but **my** one. The one who tends *me.* In his most public failure — a nation unmoved — he retreats into the most private word available: *my* nurturer.

Feel the shift that just happened in the verse. This is what the scholars of Arabic call *iltifāt* — a turning. The whole surah has been a courtroom: arguments, evidence, the third person, "they," "these people," charges laid out. And suddenly, mid-flow, without warning, we are not in the courtroom anymore. We are in a **private room.** The voice has dropped. The public advocate has stopped addressing the jury and turned his back on the whole assembly to face a wall and speak to **One.**

That turn is the emotional center of the entire verse. Because notice **what** he turns *toward* in his lowest moment. Not toward revenge. Not toward the crowd. He turns toward the one relationship in the universe that is not failing him. When *everyone* refuses to receive him, he goes to the One who has been receiving him all along. The One who tends.

There is something here that I think anyone who has ever burned out in service of other people will recognize instantly, and we'll come back to it. For now just hear the sound of it. *Yā Rabbi.* It is soft. It is short. After all the argument, all the thunder of the surah, it comes out almost as a breath. This is not the sound of a man building a case. This is the sound of a man **coming home.**

### Third: what he does *not* say — the mercy hidden in the missing word

Now I want to do the hardest kind of listening there is. I want us to listen for what **isn't** there. Because sometimes the loudest thing in a verse of the Quran is the word Allah left out.

Here is the complaint in full: *inna hāʾulāʾi qawmun lā yuʾminūn* — "indeed, these are a people who do not believe."

Read it again and ask yourself: what did he *do* with that sentence? He **described.** That's all. It is a diagnosis, not a demand. He states a condition — *these do not believe* — and then... nothing. The sentence ends. He asks for nothing.

To feel the weight of that silence, you have to set it next to what other prophets said in the same position.

Nuh, after nine hundred and fifty years, said: *"My Lord, do not leave upon the earth a single one of the disbelievers."* A prayer of destruction.

Musa, standing before Pharaoh's cruelty, said: *"Our Lord, obliterate their wealth and harden their hearts so they will not believe until they see the painful punishment."* A prayer against them.

These were not wrong. They were prophets, they knew their peoples, and the moment called for it. But look at what Muhammad ﷺ — the one sent as *a mercy to all the worlds* — does with the same grief. He arrives at the identical breaking point. His people are just as immovable. And his sentence... **stops** at the diagnosis. *These are a people who do not believe.* Full stop.

There is no *"so destroy them."* There is no *"so punish them."* There is no curse at all. And that missing curse — that word Allah did not put in his mouth — **is the mercy.** It is the whole man in a single absence. The complaint of the Prophet of mercy is a complaint with the teeth taken out of it. He brings his God the pain, and he leaves the outcome entirely in his God's hands. He will not even *ask* for their ruin. He states the fact and falls silent, and lets his Rabb decide.

And now — [PAUSE] — watch what Allah does with a man who complained without cursing. The very next verse, verse 89, is the reply. And Allah does not say "yes, I will destroy them." He says: *"So turn away from them, and say: Peace. They are going to know."*

*Say: Peace.* **Salām.**

Do you feel the circle closing? The Prophet withheld the curse — and Allah, in return, does not merely spare him a harsh command; He hands him the highest possible response: *answer them with peace.* Not agreement. Not surrender. **Salām** — the dignified word of release, the word that says *I am letting this go and letting God carry it.* The man who refused to ask for their destruction is given, in exchange, the one word that is spoken in Paradise. *Qīl — salāman salāma.* Remember that rare word for "utterance" from the very first thing we studied? The complaint was a *qīl.* And the answer he is told to give is the *qīl* of the Garden itself. The circle is exact.

### The bridge out of the words

So look at what the language alone has shown us, before we've said a single thing about our own lives.

A man reaches the end of his strength. He turns from a crowd that will not receive him toward the One who **keeps** everything said to Him. He reaches for the name of the God who **tends**, and he makes it personal — *my* Lord. He lays down his grief as a plain fact and, being the mercy to the worlds, he **withholds the curse.** And Heaven answers the withheld curse with the word of Heaven itself: *peace.*

That is the machinery of the verse. Now let us ask what it is really about. Because it is not, in the end, about the disbelievers of Mecca at all.

It is about **you** — and what you are supposed to do with love that is refused.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: What do you do with love that isn't received?

I want to start somewhere that has nothing to do with religion, because I suspect it has everything to do with why you're listening.

Think of the parent of an adult child who is destroying himself. Not a small mistake — a slow ruin. The addiction, or the person who's poisoning her life, or the choice that everyone can see is going to cost him everything except him. And the parent has *tried.* God, has that parent tried. The conversations at midnight. The money. The bargaining. The rules and the softening of the rules. The looking-up of programs. The rehearsed speeches given in the car so they'd come out calm. Years of it. And the child, every single time, turns away.

And there comes a night — you may have lived this night, or you may be living it now — where the parent is sitting on the edge of the bed at three in the morning, and they are not planning the next intervention anymore. They are just... **done.** Not done loving. Done believing they can fix it. And out of that person, in the dark, comes something that is barely even words. Just: *God. He won't listen. I've done everything. He won't listen.*

That sentence. That exact, flattened, exhausted sentence — *these people will not receive what I'm giving them* — [PAUSE] — that is the sentence in our verse.

Because here is what I need you to see, and it is the thing that makes this the most quietly comforting verse in the surah: **the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ arrived at that same three-in-the-morning sentence.** The best of creation. The one whose prayers moved the heavens. He poured himself out for years, and there came a point where the only thing left in him was the diagnosis: *yā Rabbi, these are a people who do not believe.* He hit the wall that you have hit. The Quran does not hide his exhaustion. It **enshrines** it. It made his sigh into a verse that a billion people would recite until the end of time.

And that tells you something staggering about the exhaustion itself: **it is not a sin, and it is not a failure.** The point where you have given everything and it was not received — that point is not evidence that your faith is weak or your love was defective. The Prophet reached it. It is simply the place where a certain kind of effort ends. The question is never *how do I avoid this place?* You won't avoid it. If you love anyone who has free will, you will one day stand in it. The only question is: **what do you do when you get there?**

And the verse gives you the answer in the direction the Prophet turned. He did not turn to the crowd, to argue one more round. He did not turn inward, to rot in the failure. He turned **up.** He took the exact grief that had nowhere to go, and he gave it to the one Being in existence who could actually hold it.

This is the thing about unreceived love that nobody tells you: it doesn't disappear when it's refused. It has to go **somewhere.** The love you poured toward a person who wouldn't take it does not evaporate — it comes back on you, and it turns into that crushing weight on the edge of the bed at 3am. And you have, really, only three places to put it. You can turn it *outward* as resentment and let it become bitterness toward the one who refused you. You can turn it *inward* as guilt and let it become the conviction that you failed. Or you can do what he did — turn it **upward**, and hand it, as a plain and honest fact, to your *Rabb.* The one who **tends.** The one who is committed to bringing you, personally, through.

And remember what we learned about that first word — *qīlihi.* The **kept** thing. When you give your grief to Allah, it is not the same as venting into an empty room. Venting is a verb; it happens and it's gone and you feel just as alone after. But this — this is a *noun.* A thing received and held. Allah did not let the Prophet's sigh dissolve into the Meccan night. He **caught** it, and made it eternal. [PAUSE] Which means the whisper you make in the dark about the person you couldn't save is not lost either. It is not weakness leaking out of you. It is being **received.** By the only One whose receiving actually changes anything.

So what changes tomorrow? Something very concrete. The next time you hit the wall with someone you love — the child, the parent, the spouse, the friend who will not be helped — I want you to notice which direction you turn. Because you *will* turn somewhere; the grief demands it. Catch yourself before you turn it into bitterness at them or a verdict against yourself, and instead do the small, ancient, prophetic thing: turn your face to the wall, and say *yā Rabbi.* Lay it down as a fact. Don't dress it up. *My Lord — I've done what I can, and they will not receive it.* And then — this is the hard part — **leave it there.** It is now a kept thing, in better hands than yours.

### Theme Two: The last word you don't take — *salām* as strength

Let me take you somewhere you live every single day: the moment right after someone is wrong about you, and you are *right,* and you could prove it.

You know the feeling in your chest. Someone has misjudged you, dismissed you, mocked what you believe, twisted what you said. And there is a comment box, or a group chat, or a dinner table, and you have the perfect reply loaded. The one that would end them. The clapback. The receipts. Every fiber of you is straining toward that last word, because the last word feels like **justice,** and letting it go feels like **losing.**

We have built an entire culture on this instinct. The whole architecture of the internet runs on it. "My truth" versus "your truth," delivered with maximum volume. The dunk. The ratio. Being *seen* to be right. And we have quietly come to believe that the person who walks away without the last word is the weak one — the one who couldn't hack it, who had no answer.

Now put the Prophet ﷺ into that frame, and watch it shatter.

Here is a man who was **actually** right — right in the most absolute sense a human being can be right, right with revelation on his side. And here are people who mocked him for being poor, who called him a liar and a madman, who said God would never pick someone like him. If anyone in history ever *earned* the last word, it was him, in this verse.

And what is the reply he is handed by God? *"Turn away from them, and say: Peace."*

**Salām.**

I need you to understand what that word is and what it is not, because we misread it constantly. *Salām* here is **not** agreement. He is not saying "you're right and I'm wrong, let's agree to disagree." He has just, one breath earlier, called them exactly what they are — *a people who do not believe.* The diagnosis stands. *Salām* is not the abandonment of the truth. And it is not weakness, either — it is not the white flag of someone who ran out of arguments. The whole surah was arguments; he had plenty left.

*Salām* is **release.** It is the strong man's word. It is the sentence that says: *I have stated what is true, I have handed the pain to my Lord, and now I refuse to spend one more ounce of my soul trying to force your heart open — because that was never my job, and it was never in my power.* Salām is what it sounds like when a person stops trying to control an outcome that belongs to God.

And look — this is the part that should stop you — look at what Allah puts *right after* the command to say peace: *"...for they are going to know."* **They will know.** The reckoning is real, and it is coming, and it is **His.** Do you see the trade being offered? Allah is essentially saying to His Prophet, and through him to you: *Let go of needing them to know* now, *because I guarantee you they will know* then. You are being asked to release the last word in this life in exchange for the promise that the truest last word — the only one that finally matters — is already secured, and it is not yours to speak. It is His.

The clapback is an attempt to make them know **right now,** by your power. *Salām* is trusting that they will know **for certain,** by His.

So what does this change tomorrow? Here is the concrete practice, and it is brutally simple and brutally hard. The next time you are wronged and the perfect devastating reply is sitting in your fingers — pause. Ask one question: *Am I about to speak this to establish the truth, or to control how they feel about me?* Because you are allowed — even obligated — to state what is true. The Prophet stated it plainly. But the second thing, the *making them bow,* the winning — that is the part you hand to God with a single word. State your truth if it must be stated. And then, instead of the dagger, say the strong thing. Say *salām.* Walk. And let the knowing be His to give, in His time.

I will tell you what I have found, and maybe you've found it too: the peace in *salām* is not first for the person you're releasing. It's for **you.** The resentment you were about to fire off would have lived in *your* chest for a week, not theirs. *Salām* is you setting down a weight you were never strong enough to carry anyway — the weight of other people's hearts. That was always God's to carry. It was never yours.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — from gold to peace

Now let me show you the design, because I don't think you can see it until you've climbed all the way up here, and once you see it you cannot un-see it.

This is the surah of *zukhruf* — **gold.** It opened with people who could not tell value from glitter, who weighed a prophet on a scale of silver and lineage, who inherited their god like a piece of furniture. Their entire lives were about **acquiring** — wealth, status, ancestral pride, the shining surface of things. And the surah's verdict on all of it was withering: God would pave His enemies' rooftops with that gold, it means so little to Him.

And where does the surah **end?** It ends with the one man they mocked for having *none* of it — no palace, no lineage of kings, no roof of silver — and it shows you what he possessed instead. He owned exactly two things by the last verse. A **whisper to his Lord** — *yā Rabbi* — and a word of **peace** — *salām.* And here is the reveal: those two things are the only wealth in the entire surah that Allah **kept.** Remember? *Qīlihi* — his saying was the *kept* thing, caught and held forever. The gold was disposable enough to line a gutter. The whisper was precious enough to become scripture.

The whole surah is one long movement from the **outer** treasure that turns to dust — *zukhruf* — to the **inner** treasure that lasts forever: intimacy with the One who tends you, and a heart at peace. It begins with hands grasping gold and it ends with a face turned to the wall in trust. That is the mountain we climbed. That is why it was worth climbing.

And there is a smaller, sharper piece of the design hiding inside it — one you can hear in a single root. Go back to verse 87, one step before ours: *"if you asked them who created them, they would surely* ***say*** *— Allah."* The word there is from the root of speech, *q-w-l* — they **say.** And our verse, verse 88, is built on the very same root: *wa* ***qīl****ihi* — and **his saying.** Two utterances, side by side, from one root, headed in exactly opposite directions. **Their** saying is a confession that never reaches the heart — the tongue admits "Allah" while the soul stays frozen: they *say* it but *lā yuʾminūn,* they do not believe it. **His** saying is a grief that flies straight to the Throne and is kept there forever. One utterance is empty words that die in the mouth. The other is a broken whisper that becomes eternal.

That is the whole tragedy and the whole hope of being human, packed into one repeated root. **It was never about what your mouth can say. It was always about where your heart is turned.** They *knew* about Allah and stayed lost. He *turned toward* Rabb and was held.

### Where we started, where we've arrived

We started with the machinery of the words — a kept noun instead of a passing verb, the tender name *Rabb* made personal, a curse deliberately left unsaid, and an answer of peace. And we arrived at your own life: at the 3am edge of the bed with the person you couldn't save, and at the comment box with the last word you didn't take. And I hope you see now that these were never two separate things. The linguistic verse and the lived verse are the **same** verse. Both say: *when love is refused and words are exhausted, turn upward, lay it down as a fact, withhold the poison, and release it in peace — because the outcome was always His, and never, ever yours.*

### Questions to carry

- When you last hit the wall with someone you love who would not be helped — which direction did your grief actually turn: outward as resentment, inward as guilt, or upward, to your Rabb?
- Is there a "last word" you are still holding onto right now — a reply you're rehearsing, a person you need to make *know*? What is it costing you to keep carrying it?
- The Prophet stated the hard truth *and* withheld the curse. Where in your life have you collapsed those two things together — either staying silent when truth needed saying, or firing off the curse when only the truth was needed?
- What would actually change in your body, this week, if you truly believed that "they are going to know" — that the final reckoning is real, is certain, and is *not yours to deliver?*
- Whose heart are you still trying to carry that was only ever God's to carry?

### The one sentence to keep

**When your love has been refused and your words are spent, the highest thing faith can do is not to curse the one who turned away, but to whisper your grief to the Lord who keeps it — and to answer their rejection with peace.**

### The closing invitation

So here is what I'd invite you to carry out of this. Not a heavier burden — a lighter one. You have been trying to carry things that were never on your list: other people's belief, other people's change, other people's final verdict. The verse is quietly setting all of it down for you. It's showing you a Prophet who did everything, reached the wall, and then simply **turned** — toward the One who tends, with a word that gets *kept,* letting go of everything he could not control.

You are allowed to reach the wall. You are allowed to be exhausted by love that wasn't received. And you are allowed — invited — to do with it exactly what he did: turn your face upward, say *yā Rabbi,* speak the plain truth of your pain, and then release the rest in peace. That is not giving up. That is the strongest thing a human being ever does.

### A brief du'a

*Yā Rabbi* — the One who tends us from our beginning to our completion — the grief we cannot fix, and the people we could not reach, we lay before You now as a plain fact, the way Your Prophet did, and we leave the outcome entirely in Your hands. Teach our tongues, when we are wronged, to say *salām* and mean it. And catch every whisper we make to You in the dark, as You caught his — and keep it. *Āmīn.*
