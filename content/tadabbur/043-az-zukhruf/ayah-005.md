# The Reminder That Refuses to Leave

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Let them sit in the air before we say anything about them.

> **أَفَنَضْرِبُ عَنكُمُ ٱلذِّكْرَ صَفْحًا أَن كُنتُمْ قَوْمًا مُّسْرِفِينَ**

*Afa-naḍribu ʿankumu-dh-dhikra ṣafḥan an kuntum qawman musrifīn.*

"Then should We turn the Reminder away from you, withholding it, because you are a people who have exceeded all bounds?"

Eight words. A single question. And it is one of the most surprising questions in the entire Quran — not because of what it asks, but because of *who it is asking, and when.*

### Let's rebuild the world this landed in.

Picture Makkah. Not the Makkah of pilgrims and packed prayer lines. The early Makkah — where the message is new, raw, and surrounded by contempt. The Quraysh have heard the Quran now. They have had time to form their verdict. And the verdict is mockery.

They are not neutral. They are not merely undecided. By this point in the Surah's own description, they are a people who turn away, who ridicule, who demand to know why the message wasn't sent to some great man of the two cities instead of this orphan. They are, in the language of this very ayah, *musrifīn* — people who have gone past every limit.

Now — put yourself in the shoes of the one delivering this message. You have been mocked. Your words, which you know to be from the Lord of the worlds, have been laughed at in the marketplace. And a thought begins to form, the kind of thought any human being would have:

*Maybe they don't deserve this anymore.*

*Maybe the door should close.*

*Maybe a people who treat the light this way should simply be left in the dark they chose.*

That is the emotional climate this ayah walks into. And notice what comes just before it. In the four ayahs preceding, Allah swears by the clear Book, He calls it an Arabic Quran made so that you might understand, and then He says something staggering: this Reminder is *in the Mother of the Book, with Us, exalted, full of wisdom* (43:4). The Quran has just been described as something held high in the very source of all decree.

And then — this question.

### The Hook

Here is what should stop you.

When a people reject a gift, the expected response — the *human* response, the response that would feel like justice — is withdrawal. *You didn't want it? Fine. It's gone.*

So when this question arrives — "Should We turn the Reminder away from you because you have exceeded all bounds?" — every instinct in you braces for the answer *yes.* That would be fair. That would be deserved.

But this is not a real question. It is a question that already contains its own answer — and the answer is the exact opposite of what fairness would demand.

Stay with me. Because once you see *why* the answer is no, and once you see the strange logic that connects their transgression to Allah's response, you will never read the phrase "Allah is merciful" the same way again.

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Has Already Decided — *Afa-naḍribu*

Before we touch this word, let me teach you something about how questions work — in Arabic, and in your own life.

Not every question is a request for information. Think about the last time someone you love did something careless and dangerous, and you said: *"Do you think I would ever just give up on you?"* You were not asking. You did not want them to answer. The question itself **was** the answer. The grammar was a question; the meaning was a fierce, total denial of the very thing you named. You raised the possibility *only to crush it.*

Arabic has a precise name for this. It is called **al-istifhām al-inkārī** — the interrogative of denial. You ask a question whose entire purpose is to make the listener feel how *impossible*, how *unthinkable*, the thing being asked actually is.

Now look at the opening: **أَفَنَضْرِبُ** — *Afa-naḍribu.*

That tiny letter at the front, the *hamza* — "a" — is this very particle of denial. It does not open a genuine inquiry. It opens an impossibility. Allah is not wondering aloud whether to withdraw the Quran. He is raising the idea **precisely so that you feel how unthinkable it is.**

Read it again with that in your ear: *"Would We then — really — turn the Reminder away from you?"* The way you'd say to your child: *"Would I then just walk away and leave you?"* The question doesn't seek an answer. **It IS the answer.**

[PAUSE]

Now let's open the verb itself, because it hides something beautiful.

The root is **ضرب** — *Ḍ-R-B.* And your first instinct, if you know any Arabic, is: *to strike, to hit.* That is the everyday meaning. *Ḍaraba* — he struck.

So why would Allah say "should We *strike* the Reminder away from you"? Here is where the lexicons open a door. The Arabs had an idiom: **ḍaraba ʿan** — to strike *away from* something — and it does not mean to hit. Al-Baghawi, in his commentary, spells it out plainly: *yuqālu ḍarabtu ʿanhu wa aḍrabtu ʿanhu idhā taraktahu wa amsakta ʿanhu* — "one says *ḍarabtu ʿanhu* when you **leave** something and **hold yourself back** from it."

Notice something. The image underneath is still forceful. This isn't a gentle setting-aside. It's the decisive gesture of a hand that strikes the matter away — turns from it firmly, the way you'd sharply wave off something you've finished with. The verb carries weight. To "strike the Reminder away from you" is to **forcefully cut it off**, to end the giving.

So the question, fully unfolded, is: *"Shall We then forcefully cut off the Reminder from you — withdraw it, end the sending — because of what you are?"*

And remember the *hamza* of denial wrapping the whole thing. Allah raises the image of a hand striking the Quran away from a people — and the very grammar that raises it **annihilates it.** No. That hand will not fall. The giving will not end.

[PAUSE]

What has this one word shown us? That Allah names the harshest possible response — the total withdrawal of guidance — and names it *only to refuse it.* The door He raises is the door He is bolting shut against ever closing.

### Element Two: The Cheek That Turns Away — *Ṣafḥan*

Now to the word at the heart of this ayah. The word that, once you see what's inside it, reorganizes the whole verse.

**صَفْحًا** — *ṣafḥan.* The translations give you "disregarding you," "turning away," "as a thing neglected." All true. But all of them are downstream of something physical, something you can see with your own eyes. And remember our principle: when the Quran reaches for a physical image, the image is never the destination. It is the stepping stone.

So let's find the physical thing.

The root is **صفح** — *Ṣ-F-Ḥ.* And the core meaning, the thing your hand can touch, is **the side, the flat surface, the cheek of the face.** *Ṣafḥat al-wajh* — the side of your face. *Ṣafḥat al-jabal* — the broad face of the mountain. The *ṣafḥa* of a sword is its flat side. The *ṣafḥa* of a book — a *page* — its flat face.

Now watch what the verb does with that. Al-Baghawi again gives us the picture exactly: *al-ṣafḥ maṣdaru qawlihim ṣafaḥtu ʿanhu idhā aʿraḍta ʿanhu, wa dhālika bi-an tuwalliyahu ṣafḥata wajhika* — "*ṣafḥ* comes from when you turn away from someone, **and that is by turning toward him the side of your face.**"

Feel that. **To turn someone the *side* of your face.**

You know this gesture. You have *done* this gesture. It is the most human act of dismissal there is. Someone speaks to you, and you don't argue, you don't shout — you simply **turn your cheek away.** You give them your profile instead of your eyes. You present the *side* of your face, never the front. It is the body's way of saying: *I am done looking at you. You are no longer worth my gaze.*

[PAUSE]

So now read the ayah as a single, devastating picture:

*"Should We then turn the Reminder away from you — giving you the side of Our face, the way one gives up on a person not worth facing — because you are a people who have exceeded all bounds?"*

That is the image. Allah describes the possibility of His own turning-away in the bodily language of a relationship breaking. The cheek presented instead of the face. The relationship reduced to a profile.

And the *hamza* of denial still rings over all of it. **No.** That face will not turn. You will not get the side. The Reminder keeps coming to you full-on, face-to-face.

[PAUSE — let this one breathe]

But there is one more thing inside *ṣafḥ*, and it is the kind of thing that makes you sit very still.

This exact root — *Ṣ-F-Ḥ* — is the Quran's own word for **forgiveness.** Not a different root. The *same* one. When Allah says elsewhere, *"so pardon and forgive"* — *fa-ʿfū waṣfaḥū* (2:109) — that "forgive," that **iṣfaḥ**, is this root. When He commands the believers to turn away from harm with grace, He uses *ṣafḥ.* The lexicographers explain why: to forgive is to turn the *side* of your face away from someone's wrong — to refuse to keep staring at the offense, to let it pass by your cheek rather than burn in your eyes.

Do you see the staggering thing the single word is doing?

The very same gesture — *turning the side of your face* — is, in one direction, **abandonment.** And in the other direction, **forgiveness.** Turn your cheek to dismiss someone, and it is contempt. Turn your cheek to overlook their wrong, and it is mercy.

And here Allah takes this double-edged word and uses it in the dismissive sense — *"shall We give you the cold side of Our face?"* — only to **deny it.** Which means the only *ṣafḥ* left standing, the only turning-of-the-cheek that survives this ayah, is the *other* one. Not the *ṣafḥ* of giving up. The *ṣafḥ* of forgiveness. He will not turn away from you in contempt — and so the door is left open for Him to turn from your sins in pardon instead.

[PAUSE]

One word. *Ṣafḥan.* It holds a turning cheek that could mean *"I'm done with you"* or *"I forgive you"* — and Allah names the first only to bury it, leaving you standing in the light of the second.

### Element Three: The Logic That Runs Backwards — *An Kuntum Qawman Musrifīn*

Now the last piece, and the strangest. Because once you assemble the sentence, the *logic* of it should stop you cold.

The ending: **أَن كُنتُمْ قَوْمًا مُّسْرِفِينَ** — *an kuntum qawman musrifīn* — "because you are a people who have exceeded all bounds."

Let's open that final word first. **مُّسْرِفِينَ** — *musrifīn,* from the root **سرف** — *S-R-F.* Its meaning is **to exceed the limit, to go past the proper bound, to spill over into excess.** It is the word used for the one who wastes, who squanders, who pours out beyond all measure. A *musrif* is not someone who made a small mistake. A *musrif* is someone who has **blown past every line** — in disbelief, in rejection, in mockery. The form here, *musrifīn*, is the active participle: not "people who once erred," but **people whose very state is excess.** This is who they *are.*

So they are not borderline cases. They are the ones who have gone furthest. The worst-positioned. The least deserving.

Now hold the whole sentence together and feel the logic:

*"Shall We turn the Reminder away from you ... **because** you are a people of utter excess?"*

Listen to what that little word *"because"* is doing. In ordinary human logic, "because you have exceeded all bounds" is a **reason to withdraw.** *You went too far — therefore the gift is revoked.* That is how cause and effect work in every courtroom, every relationship, every disappointed heart. Excess earns withdrawal. Rejection earns abandonment. *Because* you failed, *therefore* you lose access.

But the *hamza* of denial has already detonated under that logic.

Read it as Allah means it: *"Shall We withdraw the Reminder because you've gone too far? — **No.**"* Which inverts everything. The clause "because you are people of excess" stops being a *reason to withdraw* and becomes the very thing Allah's mercy is **responding to** by *not* withdrawing. The greater your excess, the more impossible it becomes that He would leave you without the cure.

[PAUSE]

Let me make sure this lands, because it runs against the deepest grain of how we think.

We assume the equation is: **more sin → less access to God.** Go far enough, and the line goes dead.

This ayah reverses the equation. **More sin → more need → and therefore the Reminder keeps coming.** The transgression is not the reason the light is withdrawn. The transgression is the reason the light *insists.*

Al-Baghawi names exactly this: the question is *istifhām bi-maʿnā al-inkār* — "an interrogative carrying the meaning of denial" — *ay lā nafʿalu dhālik* — "meaning: We will **not** do that." We will not withhold the Reminder because of your excess. Qatāda, he reports, swore an oath on it: *wallāhi* — *by Allah* — *had this Reminder been lifted when the first people rejected it, they would have perished.* The very fact that you are still being reminded is the proof that you have not been written off.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these three pieces have built together.

A verb — *naḍribu* — that raises the image of a hand striking the Reminder away, and a grammar that refuses to let the hand fall.

A word — *ṣafḥan* — that pictures the cheek turning away in contempt, and then quietly holds inside it the *opposite* turning of the cheek: forgiveness.

And a logic — *because you have exceeded all bounds* — that should justify withdrawal, but instead becomes the very reason mercy stays.

Three different doors, and every one of them opens onto the same room.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About God, and about you.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The One Who Keeps Showing Up

Let me start where you already live.

Think about what it feels like to be *given up on.*

Maybe you've felt it. The teacher who, somewhere in the school year, quietly decided you were a lost cause — and you could *feel* the decision, even though she never said it. She kept calling on the other students. Her eyes stopped landing on you. You got the *side* of her face.

Or the parent who, after enough disappointments, stopped fighting with you — and that was worse than the fighting. Because the fighting at least meant they still believed you could change. The silence meant they had closed the file.

Or the friend who didn't have a falling-out with you, exactly. They just... faded. Stopped reaching. And you understood: *I have become not worth the effort.*

Here is what every human being knows in their bones: **the deepest abandonment is not anger. It is being disregarded.** Anger still means you matter. Being turned away from — given the cheek, not the face — means you have dropped below the threshold of being worth someone's attention.

And here is the fear that sits underneath the religious life of almost everyone who has ever sinned and known it: *Have I done that to God? Have I gone far enough, failed often enough, that He has quietly turned the side of His face away from me? Am I still being reached for — or have I become not worth the reminder?*

[PAUSE]

Now hear this ayah speak directly into that fear.

The people being addressed here are not strugglers. They are not the ones trying and slipping. They are *musrifīn* — the ones who went all the way, who mocked the message in the streets, who exceeded every limit. If anyone had earned the cheek instead of the face, it was them.

And to *them*, Allah says: *Should We turn the Reminder away from you because of what you've become? Should you get the side of Our face?*

**No.**

The Quran in your hands — every ayah you have ever stumbled across, every reminder that found you in your worst week, every time a verse landed exactly when you needed it — is the standing proof that the face has not turned. That you are still being reached for. That the file has not been closed.

Think about what revelation actually *is* in light of this. The Quran did not arrive at a community of saints. It descended, ayah by ayah, onto a people in the thick of their rejection. Every verse was an act of *not giving up.* The medicine kept coming **while the patient was still spitting it out.**

[PAUSE]

Let me show you the depth of this with one more turn.

The doctor's clinic. A patient comes in, sick, and the doctor prescribes the cure. The patient ignores it. Comes back sicker. The doctor prescribes again. The patient mocks the doctor, throws out the medicine, comes back sicker still.

At what point does the doctor stop?

A *human* doctor stops. There is a limit to human patience, a point where "I've done all I can" becomes the honest truth and the file is closed.

But what is the logic of the *true* Physician? *The sicker you are, the more the medicine comes.* Your worsening is not a reason to stop the treatment — it is the reason the treatment *intensifies.* The *musrif* — the one furthest gone — is not the one the Reminder abandons. He is the one the Reminder most relentlessly pursues.

This is why the ayah's backwards logic is not a puzzle. It is the very signature of divine mercy. Human mercy is a *quantity* — it runs out. Divine mercy is an *attribute* — it does not. *"My mercy encompasses all things"* (7:156). *"Do not despair of the mercy of Allah"* (39:53). The Reminder that keeps coming to the transgressor is mercy refusing to be exhausted by the very thing that exhausts human patience.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** This: the next time the thought arrives — *I've gone too far, why would He still want me* — recognize it as a lie that the grammar of this ayah has already refuted. The proof that you have not been abandoned is in your hands. You are still reading. You are still being reminded. The face has not turned. So **turn back to the face that never turned from you.** Open the Book — not as someone sneaking back in through a side door, but as someone who was never actually shut out.

### Theme Two: The Two Ways to Turn Your Cheek

Now I want to take you back into that one word — *ṣafḥ* — because it carries a second teaching, and this one is about *you,* not only about God.

We saw it: the same gesture, turning the side of your face, means two opposite things. Turn your cheek in contempt, and it is *abandonment.* Turn your cheek away from someone's wrong, and it is *forgiveness.* Same root. Same motion. Opposite hearts.

Let me bring this to where you live.

Someone wrongs you. A real wrong — a betrayal, an insult, a debt unpaid, a cruelty. And you have, broadly, two ways to *turn away* from them.

The first is the *ṣafḥ* of being done. You turn your face because they are no longer worth your eyes. This is the turning-away of contempt — *you have fallen beneath my notice.* It looks like peace. It is actually a quiet war. You have not forgiven; you have *filed them away.*

The second is the *ṣafḥ* of pardon. You turn your face away from the *offense* — you refuse to keep staring at it, replaying it, feeding it. Not because they don't matter, but because you have chosen to let the wrong pass by your cheek instead of lodging in your heart. This is the *ṣafḥ* the Quran commands: *waṣfaḥ aṣ-ṣafḥa al-jamīl* — "turn away with a beautiful turning" (15:85).

[PAUSE]

Here is what is breathtaking. This ayah shows you Allah Himself standing at exactly this fork — and choosing.

He raises the first *ṣafḥ:* *Should We turn from you in dismissal, give you the cheek because you've gone too far?* And He refuses it. Which leaves only the second *ṣafḥ* on the table — the turning-away that is pardon. He will not turn from *you* in contempt; He will, instead, turn from your *sin* in forgiveness.

Now sit with the demand this places on you.

You worship a Lord who, faced with a people who mocked Him, chose the *ṣafḥ* of forgiveness over the *ṣafḥ* of abandonment. So when *you* stand at that same fork — wronged, justified, fully entitled to write someone off — which *ṣafḥ* will you choose? The Quran's answer is not subtle. Right after commanding pardon in Surah An-Nūr, Allah asks: *"Do you not love that Allah should forgive you?"* (24:22). The turning-of-the-cheek you want *from* Him is the turning-of-the-cheek He asks *of* you.

[PAUSE]

And notice the trap most of us fall into. We think we have forgiven, when actually we have only performed the *first* ṣafḥ. We have stopped arguing. We have gone quiet. We have "moved on." But inside, we have given the person the cold side of our face — they have simply dropped beneath our notice. That is not pardon. That is contempt wearing the mask of peace.

True *ṣafḥ* is harder and rarer. It is to turn your face from the *wrong* while keeping your heart open to the *person.* It is what Allah does with the *musrifīn:* He turns from their excess without turning from them.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** This: examine the people you have "let go of." Be honest about *which* ṣafḥ you performed. If you find you've given someone the cheek of contempt and called it forgiveness, you have a choice in front of you — the same choice Allah modeled in this ayah. Turn from the wrong; turn back toward the person. And know that the measure of cheek you turn toward others in pardon is bound up with the measure of cheek He turns toward you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this reflection are the same insight seen from two sides.

From the **linguistic** side: a hand that could strike the Reminder away but does not; a cheek that could turn in contempt but turns instead toward forgiveness; a "because" that should justify withdrawal but instead becomes the reason for staying.

From the **thematic** side: a God who refuses to give up on the very people who deserve to be given up on — and who, in refusing, teaches *you* how to refuse to give up on those who have wronged you.

The grammar and the lesson are one thing. *Ṣafḥ* — the turning of the cheek — is the hinge of both. Allah names the dismissive turning only to bury it, and what is left standing, for Him and for us, is the turning that forgives.

The physical image was the side of a face turning away. The unseen reality it pointed to was the persistence of a mercy that does not run out — and the kind of forgiveness it asks us to learn.

### Questions to Carry

1. When the thought comes — *I've gone too far for God to still want me* — can I now recognize it as a lie that the very Book in my hands refutes?
2. What in my life *is* the Reminder still coming to me — the verse, the reminder, the pull back — and have I been reading it as proof that I've been abandoned, when it is proof of the opposite?
3. The people I have "let go of" — did I give them the cheek of contempt, or the cheek of pardon? Am I sure?
4. Do I want from Allah a turning-away of forgiveness while I give others a turning-away of dismissal?
5. If my transgression is not a reason for God to withdraw but a reason He keeps reaching — what excuse do I have left for staying away?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah names the possibility of abandoning you — striking the Reminder away, turning the cold side of His face — only to deny it utterly, revealing that your excess is not the reason His light withdraws, but the very reason it keeps coming.

### The Closing Invitation

Something should shift now. Not guilt — the opposite of guilt. The fear that you have been written off was never true; the Book in your hands was the disproof all along. So let the invitation be simple: stop sneaking back to God as though through a side door He left ajar by accident. The door was never shut. The face never turned. Walk back in fully — and as you receive the *ṣafḥ* of His forgiveness, learn to give it.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, You did not strike Your Reminder away from a people who had earned it — so do not let us strike ourselves away from You. You turned the side of Your face only to refuse the turning; turn from our sins with the *ṣafḥ* of Your pardon, and teach our hearts to forgive as You forgive. And let every ayah that still finds us be what it truly is: the proof that You have never once given up on us. Āmīn.
