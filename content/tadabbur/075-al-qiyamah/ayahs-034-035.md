# The Word That Draws Near
### A Tadabbur of Surah al-Qiyamah, 75:34–35

---

## INTRODUCTION

> أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰ ۝ ثُمَّ أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰ

*Awlā laka fa-awlā. Thumma awlā laka fa-awlā.*

**"Woe to you, and woe! Then woe to you, and woe!"**

Six words. That is the entire passage. Three of them are the same word, repeated like a hammer falling on the same nail four times. There is no new information here. No description of Hell. No list of punishments. No argument. Just a phrase, said, and then said again.

And yet when you understand what is happening underneath these six words — what root they come from, who exactly is being spoken to, and the strange thing Arabic is doing with its grammar in this moment — you find that this may be one of the most frightening sentences in the Qur'an. Not because of what it says. Because of what it refuses to say.

### Let us rebuild the world

To feel this, you have to know who is standing there.

The surah has just finished sketching a man. Look at the three verses right before these:

> *"For he neither affirmed the truth, nor did he pray. But he denied, and he turned away. Then he went off to his family, strutting."* (75:31–33)

Picture it. A man hears the truth. He does not believe it. He does not pray. He actively pushes it away. And then — this is the detail that stings — he *walks home*. He goes back to his people. And the word the Qur'an uses for his walk is **yatamaṭṭā**: he swaggered, he strutted, he moved with that slow, self-satisfied roll of the shoulders of a man who is very pleased with himself. He turned his back on God and he felt *good* about it. He felt like he'd won.

He is walking away. His back is to us. He is heading home, comfortable, important, surrounded by the people whose opinion of him is the only opinion he cares about.

And then the voice comes.

Many of the early authorities report that this passage was revealed about, or recited at, one specific man — Abu Jahl, the great enemy of the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah. In one narration the Prophet ﷺ himself seized him and spoke these very words to his face. We should hold that report gently — it is a *narration about the occasion*, one tradition among the reports, not a certainty of creed — but it tells us something true about the *tone*. This is not abstract. This is a finger pointed at a specific, swaggering back.

### The hook

Here is what you need to know before we begin, the thing that should make you lean in:

The word repeated four times here — **أَوْلَىٰ**, *awlā* — comes from a root in Arabic that, everywhere else in the Qur'an, is one of the most *beautiful* words in the language. It is the root of intimacy. The root of protection. The root of the word Allah uses to describe how *close* He is to those who love Him.

And here, that same root has been turned around and aimed at a man like a blade.

How does the language of closeness become the language of doom? [PAUSE]

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The root that means *nearness*

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about how Arabic words are built, because everything here depends on it.

In Arabic, words grow from roots — usually three consonants — like trees from a seed. Every word from the same root carries the *flavor* of that root, no matter how far it branches. Take the three letters **و - ل - ي** (*w–l–y*). This root means **nearness, closeness, to be right beside something with nothing in between.**

From this one seed, the Arabic language grew some of its most tender words:

- **walī** — the one who is *near* to you, your protector, your intimate ally, your closest friend.
- **mawlā** — your master, your patron, the one in whose nearness you are safe.
- **wilāyah** — the bond of loving closeness, of being under someone's care.

And here is where it stops being grammar and becomes something that should make the hair on your arms rise. This is the very root Allah uses for *Himself*:

> *"Allāhu **waliyyu** alladhīna āmanū"* — **"Allah is the close-protector of those who believe."** (2:257)

That word — *walī* — is from *w–l–y*. The nearness of God to the heart that turns to Him. The most secure closeness in all of existence.

Now. **أَوْلَىٰ** — *awlā* — the word repeated four times in our verse — **is from this exact same root.** [PAUSE]

The lexicographers and the early commentators read *awlā* here in a way that is grammatically rooted in that nearness. Al-Jalālayn explains the phrase *awlā laka* as meaning, in essence: *"what you hate has drawn near to you — it is more befitting of you, nearer to you, than to anyone else."* The destruction you ran from is not far off in some distant fire. It is **right beside you.** It is *closer to you than your own people you were strutting home to.*

So sit with what has happened. The man **turned away** from the One whose nearness was protection. And the response is not "something bad will happen to you someday." The response is built from the root of closeness itself: **the wrong thing is now near.** You wanted nearness to your people; nearness has come — but it is the nearness of ruin.

Some commentators read *awlā* instead as connected to **al-wayl** — "woe," the cry of one who is destroyed. And that reading is also true. Al-Muyassar simply renders the whole phrase as *halāk laka fa-halāk* — **"ruin to you, then ruin"** — naming the destruction outright. Think of these not as competing answers but as camera angles on one reality. From one angle: *woe to you.* From another: *ruin to you.* From a third: *it is near to you.* And when you put the lenses together you get the full image — **ruin has drawn near.** The cry of destruction and the closeness of destruction are the same moment. [PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: **the closeness you were built for does not disappear when you refuse God. It simply changes what draws near.**

### Element Two: The voice that suddenly turns to face you

Now I want you to notice something about *who* is being spoken to, because Arabic is doing something here that has a name, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

Let me teach the concept first with an everyday example. Imagine someone telling a story about a coworker: *"He came in late. He ignored everyone. He didn't do his work…"* — and then suddenly, mid-sentence, the speaker spins around, points at the actual person who just walked in, and says: *"…and **YOU** — you've got some nerve."*

That sudden swing — from talking *about* someone to talking *at* them — is jarring. It's a pivot of the whole body. In Arabic rhetoric this device has a name: **iltifāt**, "the turning." It is one of the most powerful tools in the language precisely because it breaks the frame and makes it *personal*.

Now look at what the surah just did. Verses 31–33 spoke about the man in the **third person** — *he* didn't believe, *he* didn't pray, *he* turned away, *he* strutted home. He is a figure being described. His back is to us.

And then — verse 34 — the grammar **spins around to face him.** Al-Jalālayn flags this exact thing: *fīhi iltifāt ʿan al-ghayba* — "in it there is a turning away from the third-person." But he goes further, and the technical detail is worth holding. He notes that *awlā* here is an **ism fiʿl** — a "noun-of-action," a word that is not grammatically a verb yet carries a verb's whole force inside it, like a coiled spring. And he points out that the *lām* in *laka* — the little "to" in "to you" — is *lil-tabyīn*, "for specification." That tiny letter is the grammatical equivalent of a pointing finger: its only job is to pin down *who.* The voice stops describing him and grabs him.

> *He* turned away… *He* strutted home…
> **"You. Woe to YOU."** [PAUSE]

Do you feel the violence of that pivot? The man thought he had walked off-stage. He thought the conversation was *about* him, somewhere behind him, fading as he swaggered home. And the voice of God reaches out, takes him by the collar, spins him around, and makes him face it directly: **laka** — *to YOU.*

There is no escaping into the third person. There is no being "talked about" at a safe distance. The grammar itself is the seizing of the collar.

And here is the deeper thing. Remember the word for what he did — he "turned away," *tawallā* (75:32). **That word is also from the root w–l–y.** Al-Ṭabarī glosses it with brutal plainness: *tawallā fa-adbara ʿan ṭāʿat Allāh* — "he turned away, **putting his back** to the obedience of God." Not drifted, not neglected — *turned his back.* He performed his own *turning* — turning his back, turning away from the nearness of God. And the divine response is a *turning of its own*: the iltifāt, the voice turning to face the one who turned away. **He turned his back; the word turns to face him.** His turning away is answered by a turning toward — but not the turning he would have wanted.

What this construction reveals: **you can turn your back on God in this life and feel like you've walked away clean. But there is a turning that answers your turning. The conversation you thought you left is about to face you directly.**

### Element Three: Why it is said four times

Now the repetition. *Awlā laka fa-awlā. Thumma awlā laka fa-awlā.* Four beats. Why four? Why not say it once with force? Why this drumbeat?

First, feel the *sound* of it. Read it aloud: *aw-lā la-ka fa-aw-lā… thum-ma aw-lā la-ka fa-aw-lā.* It pounds. It is rhythmic and relentless, like blows landing one after another, or like a bell tolling that will not stop. The phonetics are doing the work of the meaning — this is not a single verdict, it is a verdict that keeps coming, that doubles back on itself, *fa-awlā… thumma… awlā.* Even the little word **thumma** — "then," "and after that" — stretches the dread across time. It is not over. There is a *then*. There is more. [PAUSE]

But the early scholars saw something more precise in the number. Al-Qurṭubī, reflecting on this passage, reads it as **a threat for each failure** — *waʿīd arbaʿa li-arbaʿa*, "four warnings answering four faults." Go back and count the man's failures in verses 31–33. There were exactly four:

1. He did **not affirm the truth** (*fa-lā ṣaddaqa*).
2. He did **not pray** (*wa-lā ṣallā*).
3. He **denied** (*kadhdhaba*).
4. He **turned away** (*tawallā*).

Four refusals. And now: **four times** the word falls. *Awlā… fa-awlā… thumma awlā… fa-awlā.* [PAUSE]

Here it is worth being honest that the classical scholars did not all read the repetition the same way. Al-Qurṭubī hears four-for-four, a warning answering each fault. Al-Jalālayn, by contrast, reads the second verse (75:35) simply as *taʾkīd* — emphatic reinforcement, the same verdict said again to drive it past all argument, not a fresh count. The two readings are not enemies. Whether you hear arithmetic (one ruin per refusal) or intensification (the verdict repeated until it cannot be shrugged off), the experience of the man addressed is identical: it will not stop. Take the symmetry, then, as one illumination among the tradition's readings rather than the only key.

Do you see the terrible symmetry? Every single act of turning away is met, one for one, with its echo of ruin drawing near. Nothing he did is left unanswered. The bookkeeping is exact. He gave four "no"s to God, and four times the word comes back to him. There is no act so small that it falls through the cracks. The two negations — *he didn't affirm, he didn't pray* — the things he *failed to do*, the empty spaces in his life where worship should have been — those count too. He is not only answered for what he did. He is answered for what he left undone.

What this structure reveals: **nothing is dropped. The refusals we think disappear because they were only absences — a prayer not prayed, a truth not affirmed — are counted as precisely as the things we actively did.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

So look at what these six words have shown us. A root that means *nearness*, turned into the nearness of doom. A grammar that *spins to face* the man who turned his back. A fourfold drumbeat that answers, one for one, four separate refusals — including the two that were only *absences*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About you, and the nearness you are seeking right now, today, in your own life.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You will be near to something. The only question is what.

Let me start not with the verse but with something you already know in your bones.

You are built for closeness. Every human being is. We are not made to stand alone in the universe; we are made to belong — to a person, a people, a place, a cause. Think about your own life. Think about how much of what you do, you do to be *near* something. To be in the circle. To be accepted by the group whose opinion you can't stop caring about. To not be left out. Belonging is not a luxury of the human heart; it is its hunger.

The man in this surah had that hunger too. Look again at where he was going. He "went off **to his family**, strutting." He turned away from God and he walked *toward his people.* He chose his closeness. He picked his circle. And he was proud of the choice — that's the strut. He was returning to the warmth of belonging, to the ones whose approval made him feel important, and his whole body said: *I have chosen well.*

This is the thing I need you to see. **He was not running toward nothing. He was running toward a different nearness.** He didn't reject closeness — no human can. He just chose the wrong walī. He chose the nearness of the crowd over the nearness of God. *Wilāyah* of his people over *wilāyah* of his Lord.

And here is the verse's answer, built — as we now know — from that very root of nearness: *awlā laka.* **Something has drawn near to you.** You wanted nearness? Nearness has come. But because you turned from the One whose nearness is protection, what arrived in its place is the nearness of ruin. [PAUSE]

Let me make this unbearably present. The crowd he strutted home to — where are they on the Day of Qiyamah? Gone. Scattered. Each soul standing alone. The whole surah has been describing that Day — the day when *"no, there is no refuge! To your Lord that Day is the place of return"* (75:11–12). The people whose nearness he chose cannot stand beside him now. Their *wilāyah* evaporates exactly when he needs a walī most. And the only One who could have been his protector in that moment is the One he turned his back on to go be with them.

This is the quiet horror underneath the strut. He traded the one closeness that would have stood by him on the Day of Resurrection for a closeness that lasts only as long as the walk home.

So here is the question for *us*, because we are all walking home to some family, some circle, some crowd. **Whose approval are you strutting toward?** Whose nearness are you organizing your life to keep? Because you *will* be near to something. You are not capable of belonging to nothing. The only question — and it is the question of your entire life — is *what* you let draw near.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Notice, this week, the moments when you make a choice to stay in the circle's good graces at the cost of God's. The white lie to fit in. The prayer skipped because the group was busy. The truth softened because saying it would cost you belonging. Each of those is a tiny *tawallā*, a small turning-away toward a nearness that cannot save you. Catch one. Just one. And turn the other way.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The dread that is never named

I want to end on the strangest and most frightening feature of these verses — and it is something the words *don't* say.

Read the phrase one more time: *Awlā laka* — "[something] is near to you." And now ask the obvious question that Arabic grammar *forces* you to ask: **near to you — *what*?** What is the thing that has drawn near? What is the subject of this sentence?

Allah does not say. [PAUSE]

And here is the striking thing: even the commentators who supply the missing piece can only reach for a category, not a thing. Al-Jalālayn fills the gap with *waliyyuka mā takrah* — "what is near to you is **whatever you hate.**" Not a named fire. Not a specified torment. A blank labeled *whatever-you-most-dread.* The classical reading does not close the hole; it confirms how wide it is.

The sentence has a verb-force — that *ism fiʿl* we met earlier, the coiled spring of a word that acts like a verb — *something is befalling you, drawing near you* — but the *something* is left completely unnamed. There is a hole in the grammar exactly where the threat should be specified. And that hole is not a flaw. **That hole is the whole point.**

Think about how fear actually works in the human heart. What is more terrifying — a danger you can see and name, or a danger you can only *feel* approaching in the dark? When someone says "the punishment is X," your mind can at least grab the X, measure it, brace against it, begin to bargain with it. But when someone says only *"it's near… it's near you… it's getting nearer"* and refuses to name it — your imagination fills the silence with something worse than any word could hold.

This is what the verse does. It leaves the dread *unnamed* so that it becomes *unbounded.* By refusing to specify the thing that draws near, the verse makes it everything. It could be every horror the surah has been describing — the day the sight is dazed, the moon eclipsed, the sun and moon joined (75:7–9); the day there is no refuge (75:11). All of it, unnamed, simply: **near. Nearer. And after that, nearer still.**

And here the two threads of our whole reflection — the root and the gap — finally braid together. The root *w–l–y* told us this is the language of *nearness*. The grammatical gap tells us *what* is near has no name. Put them together and you have the most precise description of the deniers' dread: **an unnamed something, drawing ever closer, with no distance left to run.** The man who strutted home thought he had created distance between himself and God. The verse tells him the truth: there is no distance. Something is near. It was always near. He just had his back to it. [PAUSE]

And notice the mercy hidden even here — because this surah opened with a strange oath. Allah swore *"by the self-reproaching soul"* (*al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, 75:2) — the conscience that blames you, that pricks you, that whispers *something is wrong, turn back.* That voice in *your* chest right now, the one that made you uncomfortable somewhere in this reflection — that is the *lawwāmah*. It is the early warning. It is the alarm that goes off *while there is still time to turn.* The man in the verse silenced his. He strutted past it. And so the only thing left to draw near to him was the unnamed thing with no name.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The dread of *awlā* is the silenced conscience come home. So do not silence yours. When that voice pricks you — about a prayer, a wrong, a turning-away — do not strut past it. That discomfort is a *mercy*; it is the closeness of God reaching for you *before* the other nearness can arrive. Tonight, find the one thing your *nafs lawwāmah* has been quietly blaming you for. Don't argue with it. Turn toward it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we have done the work, stand back and see the whole structure, because it is built with terrible precision.

Look at how the passage is engineered as a perfect mirror:

> **Four failures** (75:31–33): | **Four answers** (75:34–35):
> 1. did not affirm | *awlā*
> 2. did not pray | *fa-awlā*
> 3. denied | *thumma awlā*
> 4. **turned away** *(tawallā — root w–l–y)* | *fa-awlā* *(awlā — root w–l–y)*

The fourth failure and the answers come from **the same root.** His final act was *tawallā* — he *turned away*, from the root of nearness. And every beat of the response is *awlā* — *nearness* — from that same root. The structure is a closed loop: **he turned away from nearness, so the wrong nearness turned toward him.** The crime and the sentence are written in the same letters.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were never two things. They were one insight approached from two doors.

From the **language**, we learned that the root of closeness was turned into the closeness of doom, that the grammar spins to face the one who turned his back, and that nothing — not even an absence — falls through the cracks.

From the **themes**, we learned that the human heart is built for nearness and *will* draw near to something, and that the deepest dread is the unnamed thing approaching the one who chose the wrong closeness.

Both doors open into the same room: **You are made for nearness to God. When you turn away, the nearness does not vanish — it inverts. And the One you turned from turns to face you.**

### Questions to carry

1. Whose approval am I actually strutting toward — and would that circle stand beside me on the Day there is no refuge?
2. When my conscience — my *nafs lawwāmah* — pricks me, do I turn toward it, or do I strut past it like the man in this surah?
3. What in my life right now is a quiet *tawallā*, a small turning-away I've stopped noticing because it feels so normal?
4. The two failures that were only absences — a prayer not prayed, a truth not affirmed — what are mine? What is the empty space in my life where worship should be?
5. If I am being drawn ever-nearer to *something* — and I am — what am I letting it be?

### One-sentence distillation

**The same nearness you were created for will arrive no matter what you do — and the only thing your turning decides is whether what draws near is your Protector or your ruin.**

### The closing invitation

You will not leave this reflection neutral, because no one in this surah is allowed to stand at a distance. The grammar itself reaches out and makes it *laka* — *to you.* So let the turning that happens be the good one. Not the swagger home to the crowd. Not the silenced conscience. But the small, quiet pivot of the heart back toward the One whose nearness is the only nearness that holds on the Day everything else scatters. You don't have to run far. He is already near. You only have to stop turning away.

### A brief du'ā

*O Allah, You are the Walī of those who believe — let me be among them. Do not let me strut past the warnings You place in my own chest, and do not let the nearness that comes to me be anything but the nearness of Your protection. Turn my heart toward You before anything else can turn toward me. Āmīn.*
