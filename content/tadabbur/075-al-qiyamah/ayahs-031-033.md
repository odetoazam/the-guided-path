# The Man Who Went Home Swaggering

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, in three languages, so they can settle in you before we begin.

> [ayah:75:31] فَلَا صَدَّقَ وَلَا صَلَّىٰ
> [ayah:75:32] وَلَـٰكِن كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ
> [ayah:75:33] ثُمَّ ذَهَبَ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِۦ يَتَمَطَّىٰٓ

*Falā ṣaddaqa wa lā ṣallā.*
*Wa lākin kadhdhaba wa tawallā.*
*Thumma dhahaba ilā ahlihi yatamaṭṭā.*

"For he neither affirmed the truth nor prayed; but rather he denied and turned away; then he went off to his family, swaggering."

Eleven words. Three short verses. And in them, Allah does something He does only rarely with such intimacy: He films a man walking home.

### Rebuild the world

To feel where these verses land, you have to feel what comes right before them.

Surah Al-Qiyamah is a surah about the Day that everyone in Makkah was laughing off. *The Resurrection? You're telling me these scattered bones will be put back together?* The whole surah is an argument with that smirk. And just five verses before our passage, Allah takes us somewhere no human being has ever come back to describe: the deathbed.

*Kallā idhā balaghati t-tarāqiya* — "No! When the soul reaches the collarbones..." (75:26). The breath climbing the chest. The family gathered around asking, *Is there anyone who can heal him?* The shins twisting together in the final agony. *Ilā rabbika yawma'idhin al-masāq* — "To your Lord, that Day, is the driving" (75:30).

That is the last frame of the previous scene. A man being driven — *driven*, like livestock, with no say in the matter — toward his Lord.

[PAUSE]

And then Allah cuts away. From the deathbed, the camera pulls back in time. It rewinds. And it shows us the same man — not dying now, but *living*. Healthy. Comfortable. Walking. We see how he spent the years that led to that collarbone moment.

The whole emotional weight of these three verses depends on you holding both scenes at once. The soul climbing the throat — and the man strolling home. The body that will one day twist in agony — and the body that, today, *struts*.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I'm going to make you wait for it.

Allah could have summarized this man's entire life with a theological verdict. *He disbelieved.* Done. Instead, the last thing Allah chooses to show us — the final image, the one that lingers — is not what the man *believed*. It's **how he walked**.

Why does the portrait of a soul end with the movement of a body?

Stay with that. We're going to earn the answer.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Two Things He Did Not Do — and the Word Left Hanging

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the verse.

In Arabic, as in English, a verb often wants an object. "He affirmed" — affirmed *what*? "He believed" — believed *whom*? The mind reaches for the missing piece. When a speaker leaves that piece out on purpose, the silence becomes part of the meaning. The gap does work.

Now look at the first verse:

> فَلَا صَدَّقَ وَلَا صَلَّىٰ
> "He neither affirmed the truth nor prayed."

The verb is **صَدَّقَ** — *ṣaddaqa*. Its root is *ṣ-d-q*, the root of *ṣidq*, truth, truthfulness. But notice the shape of the word. It's not the simple *ṣadaqa* ("he spoke truly"). It's the doubled, intensified Form II: *ṣaddaqa*. And Form II here carries a specific flavor the grammarians call the *declarative* or *estimative* sense — not "to be true," but **"to hold something to be true, to declare it true, to give it your inner ratification."**

This is the word for *taṣdīq* — the heart's act of saying *yes, that is real.* It is the inner half of faith. Before the tongue confesses and before the limbs obey, something inside a person leans toward a truth and accepts it. That leaning is *taṣdīq*.

And here Allah says: *falā ṣaddaqa.* He did not do that.

Now — affirmed *what*?

Let's sit with this. The verse doesn't say. There is no object. Allah leaves *ṣaddaqa* hanging, open, unspecified.

The classical commentators rush in to fill the silence, and watch how they fill it differently — not as competitors, but as a chorus circling one reality. Al-Baghawi says: he did not affirm **the Qur'an**. Al-Tabari states it plainly — he did not affirm **the Book of Allah** — and reports Qatada to the same effect. Al-Muyassar widens the frame: he believed neither in **the Messenger ﷺ nor in the Qur'an**. Others say it was **the Resurrection** he refused — the very Day this whole surah is about. One reported view even says: he did not give in **charity** what was owed, did not *ṣaddaqa* in the sense of *ṣadaqa*, almsgiving, storing it up with his Lord. And al-Jalalayn does something quietly telling: he declines to specify the object at all, glossing the verse as nothing more than *he did not affirm and he did not pray* — leaving the blank as open as Allah left it.

Here is what's remarkable. Every one of those readings fits in the gap, because **the gap was left open for them.** The man did not affirm — and you can put almost anything true into that blank, and it remains accurate. He was not a man who said *yes* to truth. To any of it. The unspecified object isn't vagueness. It is a verdict on a whole orientation of the soul: *this was a person whose inner posture toward reality was* no.

[PAUSE]

Then the second verb: **وَلَا صَلَّىٰ** — *wa lā ṣallā* — "nor did he pray."

Notice the architecture. *Ṣaddaqa* is invisible. It happens in the chest where no one can see. *Ṣallā* is visible — it is the body bowing, the forehead on the ground, the limbs enacting what the heart claims. Allah pairs them deliberately: **the inner and the outer.** He did not affirm in his heart, *and* he did not pray with his body. Belief and its embodiment, both absent. The denial was total — it went all the way down, and it went all the way out.

Al-Muyassar keeps this concrete: he never *performed for Allah the obligatory prayers* — *farāʾiḍ al-ṣalāt* — the daily appointments a servant owes his Lord. Al-Tabari, just as bare, says he "did not pray to Him a prayer." But some commentators read *ṣallā* even more tenderly: not just the formal prayer, but *to turn toward your Lord at all* — to call on Him, to incline toward Him. Al-Qurtubi preserves the sense of *daʿā li-rabbihi*, that he never so much as turned his face upward in supplication. Not a single moment of *O my Lord.* In a whole life.

So the first verse is a portrait drawn entirely in **negatives** — *lā... wa lā...* — *not, and not.* Two empty rooms. The heart that never said yes. The body that never bowed.

And an empty portrait would be sad enough. But Allah does not let it stay empty.

### Element Two: "But Rather" — When Absence Becomes Action

Here is a small word that turns the whole passage.

> وَلَٰكِن كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ

*Wa lākin* — "but rather."

Let me teach you what *lākin* does. In Arabic, *lākin* is the particle of *istidrāk* — correction, redirection. It exists to stop you from drawing the wrong conclusion. You might hear "he didn't affirm and he didn't pray" and think: *ah, so he was simply absent. Indifferent. Asleep at the wheel. A passive man who just never got around to faith.*

*Lākin* slams that door. **"No — don't picture passivity. It was not absence. It was action."**

And then come two verbs, and notice they are no longer negatives. The empty rooms fill up — with the wrong furniture.

**كَذَّبَ** — *kadhdhaba.* Look at its shape. It is the exact mirror of *ṣaddaqa.* Same doubled Form II, same intensive declarative force — but built on the root *k-dh-b*, the root of *kidhb*, falsehood. If *ṣaddaqa* is "he declared it true," *kadhdhaba* is **"he declared it a lie."** *Taṣdīq* and *takdhīb*. They are twin words, forged on the same anvil, pointing in opposite directions.

Do you feel what Allah has done? He didn't say "he disbelieved" with some neutral term. He chose the precise antonym of the word He just denied him. *He did not ṣaddaqa — but he did kadhdhaba.* The space where the *yes* should have been was not empty after all. It was occupied by an active, deliberate, intensive *no.* He didn't fail to call it true. **He took the trouble to call it false.**

And then: **تَوَلَّىٰ** — *tawallā.* Root *w-l-y*, which has to do with turning, with the back, with facing away. This is Form V, *tafaʿʿala* — the reflexive form, the form where the action curls back onto the doer himself. *Tawallā* is not just "he turned." It is **"he turned himself away"** — a deliberate pivot of the whole self, presenting his back.

And the classical tafsir splits that turning in two, and the split is illuminating. Al-Jalalayn glosses *tawallā* as turning away **from faith** — *ʿan al-īmān* — the inward defection. Al-Tabari renders it *adbara* — he turned his back **from the obedience of Allah**, *ʿan ṭāʿat Allah* — the outward defection, the limbs walking off the path. Belief abandoned *and* obedience abandoned, the very inner-and-outer pairing this passage keeps building, now folded into a single word.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the symmetry, because it is breathtaking.

Verse 31, the negatives: *ṣaddaqa* (inner) — *ṣallā* (outer).
Verse 32, the actions: *kadhdhaba* (inner) — *tawallā* (outer).

It's a perfect chiasm. *Taṣdīq* of the heart is replaced by *takdhīb* of the heart. The prayer of the body is replaced by the *turning-away* of the body. Every "yes" he refused has a matching "no" he embraced. The inner refusal *and* the outer refusal each have a positive counterpart in active rejection.

This is the difference between an unlit lamp and a smashed one. The first verse showed us a lamp that was never lit. *Lākin* tells us: actually, he picked it up and threw it against the wall. **Disbelief, the Qur'an is teaching us here, is rarely a vacancy. It is usually a choice.**

### Element Three: The Camera Slows Down — *Yatamaṭṭā*

Now we come to the word that the whole passage has been walking toward. And I want you to notice something about *time* first.

Every verb so far has been in the past tense — the perfect tense, the tense of completed action. *Ṣaddaqa, ṣallā, kadhdhaba, tawallā.* Done, done, done, done. Reported from a distance, like a closed file.

Then verse 33:

> ثُمَّ ذَهَبَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ يَتَمَطَّىٰ

*Thumma dhahaba* — "then he went." Still past tense. *Ilā ahlihi* — "to his family, his people, his household."

And then the tense **changes**.

**يَتَمَطَّىٰ** — *yatamaṭṭā* — is not past. It is the imperfect, the present-continuous, the tense of action *unfolding before your eyes.* In English we'd catch it with the *-ing*: not "he swaggered" but "**swaggering.**"

Let me tell you why that shift is a piece of cinematography. The four past-tense verbs are summary — they tell you what the man's life *was.* But the moment Allah wants you to actually *see* him, He flips the lens into the present, and suddenly the camera slows, the file opens, and the man is moving *right now,* in front of you, in slow motion. The grammar itself makes him present. You are no longer reading a report. You are watching him walk.

And what is he doing? *Yatamaṭṭā.*

This is Form V again — *tafaʿʿala*, the reflexive, the action turned back upon the self. The lexicographers connect it to *maṭā*, to stretch, to draw out; and many of the classical scholars connect it to *al-maṭā*, **the back, the spine.** To do *tamaṭṭī* is to stretch your back and swing your shoulders as you walk — to roll your gait, to luxuriate in your own movement. It is the walk of a man who is deeply, lazily pleased with himself. The strut. The swagger. The saunter of someone who has not a care in the world.

But the classical readings press a sharper edge into the word than mere ease. Al-Muyassar glosses *yatamaṭṭā* as *yatabakhtar mukhtālan fī mishyatihi* — he **struts, swollen with conceit in his very gait**; Ibn Kathir's portrait has him *walking in conceit, admiring himself.* This is not only laziness. It is *khuyalāʾ* — arrogance worn in the body. And that drops the word into a whole Qur'anic vocabulary of the forbidden walk: the *mukhtāl* of Luqmān's counsel to his son — *and do not walk the earth in insolence* (31:18) — and the warning *do not walk the earth exultantly* (17:37). The very gait Allah forbids the believer is the gait He films this man performing on his way home. His body is breaking a commandment he never troubled himself to hear.

[PAUSE]

Now *hear* the word. *Ya-ta-maṭ-ṭā.* Say it slowly. It is a word that physically stretches in the mouth — the doubled *ṭ* makes you pause on it, and the long final *ā* draws out and trails off, like a stride that won't be hurried. The sound of the word *is the swagger.* The Qur'an makes your tongue strut before it tells you what strutting is.

And notice the rhyme that has been building the whole time: *ṣall**ā**, tawall**ā**, yatamaṭṭ**ā**.* Three verbs, each ending in that long, open, falling *ā* — like three slow steps. The very music of the passage is a man walking away from God in no particular hurry, with all the time in the world.

That is where Allah chose to end the portrait. Not on a doctrine. On a gait.

### The Bridge

So step back and see what the language has shown us.

A heart that refused to say *yes* (*ṣaddaqa*). A body that refused to bow (*ṣallā*). Then — *but rather* — a heart that troubled itself to say *no* (*kadhdhaba*), and a body that turned its back (*tawallā*). And finally the camera slowing, the tense shifting to the living present, to show us the man *strutting home* (*yatamaṭṭā*), perfectly content, off to his family, his dinner, his evening, his life.

Four completed verbs of rejection — and then one continuous verb of self-satisfaction.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words, but about reality itself — about you, and me, and the way a human being walks away from what matters.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Disbelief Is a Posture Before It Is an Argument

Let me start where you already live.

Think about the last time you decided you didn't believe in something — not a religious thing, just anything. A relationship you concluded was over. A piece of advice you rejected. An institution you stopped trusting. Now be honest about *how* you rejected it.

Almost no one rejects something by sitting down, weighing the evidence, and arriving at a reasoned verdict. That's the story we tell ourselves afterward. What actually happens is more like this: something inside you *closes.* You stop returning the calls. You change the subject when it comes up. You walk a little faster past the building. The "decision" was never an argument. **It was a posture your whole self quietly adopted, and the arguments came later to justify the posture.**

Now look again at our man, because the Qur'an refuses to let him be an intellectual. Notice what Allah does *not* say. He does not say *he weighed the proofs and found them wanting.* He does not say *he debated the Prophet ﷺ and won.* The surah is full of arguments — about bones, about creation, about the drop of fluid — but in *this* portrait, the man never argues at all.

What does he do? He doesn't affirm. He doesn't pray. He denies. He turns his back. And he walks home.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most merciful and most terrifying diagnoses in the Qur'an, and here is why. We tend to imagine that the people who turn away from God are stopped by some great intellectual obstacle — a question no one answered, a doubt no one resolved. And sometimes that's real, and it deserves to be taken seriously. But the Qur'an, here, is pointing at something else, something far more common and far closer to home: **most turning-away is not a conclusion. It is a comfort.**

He turned away — *tawallā*, the reflexive verb, the self pivoting on its own heel — and then he went to *ahlihi*, his family, his people, the warm circle of his life. Do you see the gravitational pull in that verse? He didn't turn toward a competing god. He didn't turn toward a rival philosophy. **He turned toward his couch.** Toward dinner. Toward the people who would never ask him a hard question. The denial wasn't drawn out of him by a better idea. It was drawn out of him by *home* — by the soft, undemanding pull of a life that was already comfortable enough.

And this is why the inner-and-outer pairing matters so much for you and me. Because we are very good at keeping score on only one side. We tell ourselves: *my heart is fine. I believe. The taṣdīq is there.* But the Qur'an binds *ṣaddaqa* to *ṣallā* — the affirming to the bowing — and refuses to let them divorce. A *yes* in the heart that never becomes a forehead on the floor is being quietly contradicted by the body. And the body does not lie. The man's real creed was not in his head. It was in his feet, walking home.

So what does this change about tomorrow? Stop asking yourself only *what do I believe?* and start watching *which way do I walk?* When the adhān calls and something in you turns toward the phone instead — that turning is *tawallā* in miniature. When a truth about how you should live presents itself and you change the subject in your own mind — that is the posture forming. **Faith is not finally measured by the arguments you can win. It is measured by the direction your whole self leans when no one is debating you.** Watch the lean. Correct the lean. The lean is the man.

### Theme Two: The Terror of the Ordinary Evening

Here is the second thing, and it is the one I cannot stop thinking about.

Let me ask you to picture the most *un*-dramatic image possible. A man finishes his day. He walks home. Maybe he's whistling. He stretches his back, rolls his shoulders, takes his time. He's looking forward to seeing his family, to a good meal, to putting his feet up. There is nothing sinister in the scene. If you passed him on the street you would think: *there goes a contented man.*

That is the final image of this entire portrait. *Thumma dhahaba ilā ahlihi yatamaṭṭā.* "Then he went off to his family, swaggering."

And it is one of the most frightening images in the Qur'an precisely **because nothing is happening.**

We expect the rejection of God to look like a clenched fist. A defiant speech. Pharaoh on his throne. Something cinematic. Instead, Allah shows us the rejection of God looking like... a relaxed walk home. The swagger of a man at ease. *Yatamaṭṭā* — that luxuriant, unhurried, self-pleased stretch. He is not tormented. He is not haunted. He turned his back on the Resurrection, on the Messenger, on his Lord — and then he went home and *enjoyed his evening.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the whole point. The horror is not the swagger itself. The horror is the *gap* between what he just did and how he feels about it. He has, moments ago in the moral logic of the surah, committed the gravest act a soul can commit — *takdhīb*, calling the truth a lie, and *tawallī*, turning his back. And his body registers *nothing.* No tremor. No second thought. Just a contented stretch and a stroll. **His conscience is so quiet that the most catastrophic decision of his existence produces in him the body language of a man going to brunch.**

That, the Qur'an is warning us, is what spiritual death actually feels like from the inside. **It does not feel like death. It feels like fine.** It feels like a normal Tuesday. It feels like getting home and putting your feet up. The man who is in the gravest danger is not the one weeping over his sins — that one is alive. It is the one who feels *nothing*, who has turned away so smoothly and so often that the turning no longer leaves a mark, who can reject his Lord and then genuinely look forward to dinner.

And now — *now* — feel the weight of where these three verses sit. Because just before them, what were we watching? The same man, on his deathbed, the soul climbing past his collarbones, the shins twisting together, being *driven* to his Lord. Allah has placed the two scenes back to back on purpose. **The swagger and the death-rattle are the same man.** The body that stretched so contentedly on the walk home is the body that will twist in agony as the soul is dragged from it. The evening that felt so safe was a borrowed evening. He thought he was walking home. He was being driven to his Lord the whole time, just slowly enough that he mistook it for a stroll.

[PAUSE]

So let me bring it home, because this verse is not really about *him.* And here the commentators give us a gift. Yes, many of them — Ibn ʿAbbas among them, as Al-Qurtubi reports — name a specific man, Abū Jahl, as the one this was revealed about. But Al-Qurtubi also preserves a second reading: that the subject reaches back to *al-insān*, "the human being," mentioned at the start of the surah — *ism jins,* a noun for the whole species. Al-Jalalayn, glossing the opening verb, quietly assumes exactly this — the one who *did not affirm* is, simply, *al-insān*, the human being. In other words: this is not only a portrait of one Makkan tyrant. **It is a mirror held up to the human being.** To every human being who has ever turned away smoothly and gone home satisfied.

So ask yourself — gently, but honestly. How many of my evenings are *this* evening? How many times have I let the prayer slip, scrolled past the reminder, quieted the small voice, and then gone home and enjoyed my night with a completely clear conscience — not because I was at peace with God, but because I had stopped *noticing*? The danger sign in the spiritual life is not guilt. Guilt means the nerve is still alive. **The danger sign is comfort where there should be concern.** The swagger.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practice: tonight, before you go to "your family" — to your evening, your comfort, your screen — pause at the threshold and ask one question. *Is there anything I turned my back on today that I'm walking away from a little too easily?* That pause, that single moment of friction before the comfortable evening swallows the day — that is the opposite of *yatamaṭṭā.* That is the soul refusing to go numb. Reintroduce the friction. A believer is not someone who never turns away. A believer is someone who **still feels it** when he does.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now let me show you the whole structure at once, because the order is the argument.

Look at the descent Allah built:

1. **He did not affirm** — the heart's silent refusal. (inner)
2. **He did not pray** — the body's silent refusal. (outer)
3. **But rather he denied** — the heart's active rejection. (inner)
4. **And he turned away** — the body's active rejection. (outer)
5. **Then he went off to his family, swaggering** — the rejection becomes a lifestyle, a comfort, a contented walk home. (the whole self, at ease)

It moves from *absence* (didn't) to *action* (did) to *ease* (strutted). From the heart, outward to the body, outward again to the entire life. And the grammar walks down the staircase with the meaning: four closed past-tense verbs of rejection, and then the lens slows into the living present — *yatamaṭṭā* — so that the very last thing you see is not a verdict but a *man, still walking, right now.*

And remember the frame. This whole staircase descends from one scene — the deathbed, the soul at the collarbones — and is answered by the question that comes just after: *Ayaḥsabu l-insānu an yutraka sudā* — "Does man think he will be left aimless, neglected, with no reckoning?" (75:36). The swaggering man's entire posture is an answer of *yes — I think I'll be left alone.* And the surah's reply is the soul climbing his throat.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discovery and the human discovery are the same truth approached from two sides.

The language showed us that disbelief here is not a vacancy but a *mirror-image choice* — every *taṣdīq* refused has a *takdhīb* embraced, every prayer skipped has a turning-away enacted. The theme showed us that this choice rarely announces itself as a choice — it disguises itself as comfort, as the ordinary pull of home, as a man simply enjoying his evening.

Put them together and you have the whole warning: **the gravest turning-away in a human life can feel, from the inside, exactly like contentment.** That is why Allah ended the portrait of a soul with the movement of a body. Because the soul will lie to you about where it stands. The walk will not.

### Questions to Carry

- When I reject something true about how I should live, do I argue with it — or do I simply walk a little faster past it?
- Where in my life is my *ṣaddaqa* and my *ṣallā* divorced — where does my heart claim a *yes* that my body never bows to?
- What is the "home" I turn toward when I turn away from a hard truth? What comfort is doing the pulling?
- When was the last time turning away from God actually *cost* me something inwardly — and if I can't remember, what does that silence mean?
- Is the calm I feel at the end of my days the calm of peace with my Lord, or the calm of a conscience that has stopped reporting?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The man who rejects his Lord does not always do it with a clenched fist and an argument — most often he does it with a turned back and a contented walk home, and the deadliest sign of all is that it feels like nothing.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked, tonight, to never turn away again — no human keeps that promise. You are being invited to do one smaller, harder thing: to **keep the nerve alive.** To refuse the swagger. To let the small turnings-away still register, still sting, still send you back. The believer and the man in this verse may turn away in similar moments. The difference is that one of them *feels it* and comes back, and the other stretches his shoulders and goes home. Choose to be someone who still feels it.

### Du'ā

O Allah, do not let my heart go numb to its own turning-away. When I refuse You quietly, with my feet and my habits and my comfortable evenings, wound me gently enough to bring me back — and never let me walk away from You so smoothly that I no longer feel myself leaving. Make my *ṣaddaqa* reach my forehead, and keep me, always, among those who feel it when they fall.
