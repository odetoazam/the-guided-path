# The Oath That Begins With No

## Surah Al-Qiyamah, Ayah 1

> [ayah:75:1] لَآ أُقْسِمُ بِيَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ
>
> *Lā uqsimu bi-yawmi-l-qiyāmah*
>
> **"Nay! I swear by the Day of Resurrection."**

---

## Introduction: The World Behind the Verse

Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

Picture Mecca. A man bends down in the dust and picks up an old, crumbling bone — the kind you find bleached white in the desert, brittle, weightless, half-turned to powder. He holds it up. He crushes it between his fingers and lets the dust scatter on the wind. And he laughs. Not a nervous laugh — a confident one. He turns to the Prophet ﷺ and says, in effect: *You're telling me that **this** will live again? That God will gather this back together after it's gone to dust?*

This wasn't an abstract theological debate. This was the central scandal of the message. The Meccans could, with difficulty, entertain one God. What they could not swallow — what genuinely struck them as absurd, even funny — was the claim that the dead would be raised. That a body returned to soil, scattered across years and miles, would be reassembled and made to **stand** and answer for itself.

That is the wall this surah is built to break through.

And here is what should stop us before we even begin. When you want to break through someone's certainty, you usually reach for an argument. You build a case. You stack the evidence. You reason them out of their position the same way they reasoned themselves into it.

[PAUSE]

Allah does not open this surah with an argument.

He opens it with an **oath**. And stranger still — He opens that oath with a word of **refusal**. The very first sound out of this surah is a *no*.

Why would the One who needs no witness, no corroboration, no second voice to back Him up — why would *He* swear an oath at all? And why would He preface that oath with a word that means *Nay*? What is being refused, before anything has even been claimed?

Stay with that question. Because the answer is going to tell us something not just about resurrection — but about the strange, stubborn way the human heart protects itself from the truths it least wants to face.

Let's open the words.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The *No* That Opens Everything — لَآ

Let me first give you a small everyday picture, so the grammar lands in something you already know.

Imagine someone is mid-sentence, building toward a wrong conclusion — *"There's no way you could have..."* — and you cut across them, before they finish, with a sharp **"No —"** and then you state the truth. That *no* isn't really aimed at a complete sentence. It's aimed at the **direction** the person was already heading in. You're not negating a claim; you're stopping a momentum. You're clearing the air before you speak.

Now hold that, and look at how this surah begins. The first word is:

> **لَآ** — *Lā*

A particle of negation. *No. Nay. Not.*

And then immediately:

> **أُقْسِمُ** — *uqsimu* — "I swear."

So the literal surface reads almost like a contradiction: *"No — I swear."* A refusal, and then an oath, pressed right up against each other.

This little word لَا at the head of the surah has occupied the commentators deeply, and I want to walk you through what they saw — not as competing options where you pick one, but as several camera angles on a single, rich moment. Each one shows you something the others can't quite capture.

**The first angle.** Al-Jalālayn reads the لَا here as *zāʾida* — grammatically "extra," not carrying its own negative weight, present for the texture and rhythm of the speech rather than to negate a statement. On this reading the meaning settles into: *"I swear by the Day of Resurrection."* The *no* is, as it were, the breath before the oath.

**The second angle.** Al-Qurṭubī records a closely related view: that the لَا is *ṣila* — a connective. And he gives a fascinating reason *why* it can sit at the very start of a surah. He says the Qur'an is one interconnected speech — *muttaṣilun baʿḍuhu bi-baʿḍ* — woven together so tightly that "it is in the ruling of a single utterance." A thing can be raised in one surah and answered in another. So a particle at the opening of a surah is not really standing alone; it's leaning back against everything that came before. The *no* is connective tissue in a single, vast, seamless speech.

**The third angle.** Al-Baghawī preserves something else entirely — a variant recitation. He reports that al-Qawwās, on the authority of Ibn Kathīr, read the opening not as *lā uqsimu* but as **لَأُقْسِمُ** — *la-uqsimu* — without the long *alif* before the *hamza*. And now the very same letters carry a completely different force. That *la* is no longer a *no* at all — it is the **lām of emphasis**, the *lām al-tawkīd*. The line becomes: *"I most certainly do swear."* An intensifier. A doubling-down.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what just happened. The same opening, depending on how the tongue moves across it, is either a refusal — *No* — or an emphasis — *Most certainly*. And classical commentary holds a further, widely-reported angle alongside these: that the *no* is a genuine negation, but aimed not at the oath — aimed at **the deniers**. *No — it is not as you imagine. And I swear...* The *no* sweeps their crushed-bone laughter off the table before the oath is even spoken.

Now — do not rush to resolve these into one. Here is what's remarkable. Whether you hear *no*, or *connective*, or *most certainly*, or *no-to-the-deniers* — every single reading produces the same emotional gesture: a **clearing of the space before a solemn declaration.** A throat-clear before testimony. A *listen — before you say another word, listen.*

The word does the work of a hand raised flat in the air: *Stop. Before you finish that thought. Before you crush that bone and laugh.*

[PAUSE]

And notice **where** it sits. It is the **first sound of the surah.** Allah did not begin with the noun, with the Day, with the claim. He began with a word that turns *toward the listener* — that acknowledges, in its very first breath, that there is a resistance in the room that has to be met before anything can be received.

That is what this لَا reveals: **the surah begins by meeting our denial, not by ignoring it.**

## Element Two: The Verb That Refuses the Past Tense — أُقْسِمُ

Let me teach you something small about Arabic verbs first, because it's about to matter enormously.

Arabic, like English, can place an action in the past or in the present-and-ongoing. *I swore* is finished — sealed, behind us, a closed event. *I swear* is alive — happening now, and by its nature, repeatable. When you say *"I swear,"* in any language, you are doing the thing in the very act of saying it. The words don't *describe* the oath; they *are* the oath.

Now look at what Allah chose:

> **أُقْسِمُ** — *uqsimu* — "I swear."

Not *aqsamtu* — "I swore." The **present-tense, ongoing form.** The oath is not reported to us as a past event we missed. It is **enacted in front of us, in the present, every time the surah is recited.**

Think about what that means. This oath is not a historical artifact — *God once, long ago, swore something.* No. The verb form keeps it **permanently present.** Every time a believer opens this surah, the oath is being sworn again, freshly, in the now. The *I swear* is as live on your tongue today as it was on the first morning it was revealed in Mecca.

[PAUSE]

And now the root itself. The verb **أُقْسِمُ** comes from the root **qāf-sīn-mīm (q-s-m)**. And here is what the lexicons preserve about this root: at its base, *qasama* means **to divide, to apportion, to cut into shares.** From this root come *qisma* — a portion, a lot — and *qism* — a division, a section. The everyday meaning of this root is the act of **drawing a dividing line** and separating one thing decisively from another.

So sit with this. The Arabic word for taking an **oath** is built from the root that means **to divide and separate.**

Why?

Because that is exactly what an oath *does.* An oath is a verbal blade. It comes down between truth and falsehood and **cuts**. Before the oath, the matter is mixed, contested, hovering in doubt — *maybe it's true, maybe it isn't.* The oath comes down like a cleaver and separates the two. It says: *this side is settled; that side is finished.* An oath is a **decisive cut through doubt.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how the two elements are now speaking to each other? The surah opened with a لَا — a clearing of the doubting space. And now the very verb of the oath is built from the act of **cutting cleanly through that doubt.** First the air is cleared. Then the blade comes down.

This is what the verb reveals: the oath is not God reassuring Himself. It is God **drawing a line** — present, ongoing, renewed with every recitation — between the laughing certainty of the man crushing the bone, and the reality that is coming whether he laughs or not.

## Element Three: The Day That Is Named From "Standing" — ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ

We come now to the thing sworn *by*:

> **بِيَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ** — *bi-yawmi-l-qiyāmah* — "by the Day of al-Qiyāmah."

We translate *al-Qiyāmah* as "the Resurrection," and that is right. But the word is doing something the English completely hides, and I want to recover it for you.

*Al-Qiyāmah* comes from the root **qāf-wāw-mīm (q-w-m)** — the root of **standing, rising, getting up.** This is the same root that gives us *qāma* (he stood), *qiyām* (the standing posture), and — here is the one that should make you sit up — *al-qiyām* in the **prayer**: the standing you do, several times a day, when you rise before your Lord in ṣalāh.

So the Day of Resurrection is, at its linguistic root, **the Day of Standing.** *Yawm al-Qiyāmah* — the Day humanity **rises and stands.**

Think about why this image, of all images.

When are you made to **stand**? You stand when you are **summoned.** You stand when your name is called in a courtroom and you rise to answer the charge. You stand to attention before someone whose authority over you is absolute. Standing is the posture of the one who is **being held to account** — and also the posture of the one who has been **raised from lying down.**

A corpse lies flat. The dead are *laid* to rest, horizontal, lowered into the ground. And the Day of Resurrection is named not "the Day of Living-Again" in some vague sense — it is named **the Day of Standing Up.** The exact reversal of the grave. The horizontal made vertical. The lowered made to rise. The one laid down, *summoned to his feet.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the quiet thread I promised you. That same root — *qiyām* — is the standing of your daily prayer. Five times a day, you rehearse the posture of the Last Day. Every time you rise in ṣalāh and stand before your Lord, your body is practicing for the one Standing you cannot pray your way out of. The small standing prepares you for the Great Standing.

The man in Mecca was crushing a bone and asking *how could these scattered pieces ever live?* And Allah does not even, in this opening, answer the *how.* He simply names the Day by what will happen on it: **you will stand.** Not "you will exist again" — *you will rise to your feet and be present and answer.* The whole terror and dignity of accountability is folded into a single word built from "standing up."

That is what *al-Qiyāmah* reveals: resurrection in the Qur'an is never bare biology. It is never merely *the atoms reassemble.* It is **summons.** It is being called to your feet before the One you must answer to.

## Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what these three words, pressed together, have shown us.

A **لَا** that clears the space and meets our denial before it finishes forming. An **أُقْسِمُ** — a present, living oath built from the root of *cutting decisively through doubt.* And a **قِيَامَة** — a Day named not for biology but for **standing up to be answerable.**

Three words. A refusal, a blade, and a summons.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the architecture of a sentence — but about reality itself, and about the strange machinery of the human heart that needed to be addressed this exact way.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Architecture of Denial — Why the *No* Had to Come First

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You have, at some point, been told something true that you did not want to be true. Maybe a diagnosis. Maybe a hard fact about a person you loved. Maybe a number in a bank account, a result you were dreading. And do you remember your **first** response — before reason, before acceptance?

It was almost certainly some form of: **"No. That can't be right."**

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it **denial** — and they understand it not as stupidity but as a **defense mechanism.** It is the mind's first reflex when a truth arrives that threatens the self. The mind doesn't say *"let me evaluate the evidence."* The mind says *"no"* — instantly, protectively — and *then* goes looking for reasons to support the *no* it has already chosen. The conclusion comes first; the arguments are recruited afterward to defend it.

This is the deepest thing modern psychology and the Qur'an agree on about the human being: **we do not arrive at most of our certainties by reasoning. We arrive at them by wanting, and then we reason backward.**

[PAUSE]

Now look again at the man crushing the bone in Mecca.

We are tempted to think his problem was *intellectual* — that he simply lacked the information, that if someone had explained the science of resurrection he'd have come around. But that is not his problem. His problem is that resurrection means **accountability.** Resurrection means the *no one is watching* fantasy is a lie. Resurrection means the pleasures he took without asking permission, the people he stepped on, the orphan's wealth he ate — all of it is *coming back to stand in front of him.* He doesn't reject resurrection because the evidence is weak. He rejects it because the **implications are unbearable.** The *no* comes first — and the crushed bone is the argument he recruited afterward to defend a *no* he had already chosen.

Now feel the genius of how the surah opens.

It does not begin by reasoning with a man who did not reason his way in. It begins with his own first word — **لَا. No.** — and it **takes that word out of his mouth.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The deniers' fundamental posture is *No.* So Allah opens with *No* — and immediately turns it into the opposite of what they meant. Their *no* was a wall against the truth. Allah's *no* is a wall thrown down *in front of* the truth: *No — not as you imagine; and I swear...* He meets them at the exact location of their resistance. He doesn't go around the denial. He doesn't pretend it isn't there. He **walks straight up to it and stands on it.**

This is mercy disguised as confrontation. Because a denial that is ignored only digs in deeper. But a denial that is **named** — spoken aloud, held up, met directly — that denial loses its hiding place. You cannot keep secretly defending a *no* that has just been said out loud back to you.

So here is the first thing this changes about how you and I live tomorrow.

The next time you feel that instant *"no, that can't be right"* rise up in you — about your own mortality, about a flaw someone names in you, about a truth in this very Book that unsettles you — **pause and ask the question the surah is teaching you to ask:** *Am I rejecting this because it is false — or because it is costly?* Is my *no* a conclusion, or is it a defense?

Because the Qur'an knew, fourteen centuries before the psychologists, that the most dangerous lies are not the ones we're told. They are the ones we **reach for first, to protect ourselves** — and then spend a lifetime crushing bones to justify.

## Theme Two: Why Would God Swear? — The Oath as an Act of Mercy

Now to the deepest strangeness in the verse, and I don't want us to walk past it the way we usually do.

**Allah takes an oath.**

Let that be as strange as it actually is. In our world, who swears? The person who is **not automatically believed.** A witness in court raises his hand and swears *because his bare word is not enough* — because there is a gap between what he says and what the listener will accept, and the oath is meant to bridge that gap by invoking something greater than himself.

But Allah invokes nothing greater than Himself, because there *is* nothing greater. His word does not need corroboration. He is the source of all truth; reality bends to His speech — *"Be," and it is.* If anyone in existence never needs to swear, it is Him.

**And yet here He swears.**

[PAUSE]

Sit in the discomfort of that for a moment before we resolve it. Why would the One whose bare word *creates galaxies* stoop to the verbal form used by a man trying to be believed?

Here is what I want to suggest, and it changes everything.

The oath is not for **His** sake. It is for **ours.**

An oath, remember from the root — *qasama*, to **cut decisively** — is the instrument you use when a matter is **contested in the listener's heart.** Allah does not swear because *He* is uncertain. He swears because **we** are. He has stepped, in His speech, into the courtroom of the human heart where resurrection is the contested case — and He has done the very thing a human does to settle a doubting mind. He has **come down to the level of our doubt and met it in its own language.**

Think of what that is. The King does not need to plead with the servant. The King can simply command. But here, the King — knowing the servant's heart is trembling and resistant and afraid — chooses *not* to merely command, but to **swear**, to reassure, to take the weight of certainty onto Himself and press it into the servant's chest. *I swear. By the Day of Standing. I swear.*

[PAUSE]

That is not the speech of a God who is distant from our weakness. That is the speech of a God who **knows exactly how hard it is for a frightened heart to believe what it cannot see** — and who, out of mercy, condescends to the very form of speech that a doubting heart finds reassuring.

And consider *what* He swears by: **the Day of Resurrection itself.** He swears by the very thing they deny. In Arabic rhetoric, the thing you swear *by* is honored, elevated, made weighty by the swearing. So Allah takes the object of their mockery — the Day they crush bones to laugh at — and He lifts it up as something so weighty that He stakes a divine oath upon it. He doesn't just assert the Day is real. He **dignifies** it. He says, in effect: *this thing you find so small that you laugh at it — I swear by it. Reconsider its size.*

[PAUSE]

So here is what this changes about how we live tomorrow.

When you read the Qur'an and you come upon Allah swearing — by the fig and the olive, by the dawn, by the pen, by the soul, by this Day — do not read past it as a literary flourish. **Read it as tenderness.** Read it as the King leaning down to the trembling servant. Every divine oath in this Book is a place where Allah has chosen *not* to overpower your doubt by force, but to **meet** it — to give your weak, resistant, frightened heart something to hold onto.

And then ask yourself the honest question: *If the One who never needs to swear has sworn — what is left in me that still demands more proof? What more, exactly, am I waiting for?*

The oath is the measure of how far He has come toward us. The least we can do is not crush the bone.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found, from two directions, arriving at one place.

From the **words**: a *no* that clears the air and meets our denial; a present-tense oath built from the root of *cutting decisively through doubt*; and a Day named for *standing up to be answerable.*

From the **themes**: a human heart that defends its *no* before it ever examines the evidence — and a God who, knowing this, does not overpower that heart but *condescends in mercy to swear an oath* to it, staking His own word on the very Day we least want to face.

These are the same insight from two directions. The linguistic refusal — لَا — and the psychological reality of denial are one thing: **Allah opens by meeting us exactly where our resistance lives.** And the present, cutting oath — أُقْسِمُ — and the theology of divine mercy are one thing: **Allah does not crush our doubt; He condescends to address it.**

The whole verse, then, is the opening move of a God who could have simply commanded — and chose instead to *reach toward us.* First He clears the space. Then He swears, gently, by the Day we will all rise and stand.

## The Closing Invitation

Something can shift after this — not as an obligation laid on you, but as an invitation held open.

The invitation is this: stop reading the Qur'an's oaths as decoration. The next time Allah swears in this Book, let it land as what it is — the King leaning down toward a trembling servant, choosing mercy over force. And let the *no* that opens this surah become a mirror. When your own *no* rises up — quick, protective, certain — learn to pause and look at it before you defend it. Because the Day of Standing is coming whether the bone is crushed or not. And the One who swore it by His own word did so not to frighten you, but to reach you in time.
