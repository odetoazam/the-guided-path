# The Disease Was Never Doubt — It Was the Love of Now

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the world they fell into.

> **كَلَّا بَلْ تُحِبُّونَ ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ ۝ وَتَذَرُونَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ**
>
> *Kallā bal tuḥibbūna l-ʿājilah · wa-tadharūna l-ākhirah*
>
> "No! But you love the fleeting, immediate world — and you leave the Hereafter behind."

Six words. Two ayahs you could recite in a single breath. And in those six words, Allah does something He had been building toward across this entire surah: He stops arguing the case, and He names the defendant's real motive.

### Rebuild the world

Stand inside Surah Al-Qiyamah from the beginning. It opens with an oath — Allah swears by the Day of Standing, and then He swears by something stranger: *al-nafs al-lawwāmah*, the soul that blames itself, the inner voice that will not let you rest in your own wrongdoing. Then the argument begins, and it is relentless. *Does man think We will not gather his bones?* He will be told, on that Day, of *everything he sent forward and everything he left behind* (75:13). Then comes the terror — the eye dazzled, the moon swallowed in eclipse, the sun and moon gathered together — and the human heart, cornered, cries out: *Where? Where is the place to flee?* And the answer comes back flat and final: *Kallā lā wazar* — No. There is no refuge.

Now picture the listener in Mecca hearing this. He has been pushing back this whole time. *When* is this Day, he keeps sneering — *yas'alu ayyāna yawmu l-qiyāmah* — asking the date the way a man asks for proof he has no intention of accepting. He has dressed his refusal up as a question. He has made it sound like the resurrection fails *his* standard of evidence.

And here — right here at ayah 20 — Allah pulls the mask off.

### The Hook

Because what comes next is not another proof. Allah does not give him one more sign, one more argument about bones and rain and the first creation. Allah turns to the human heart and tells it something it did not want said out loud:

**You were never confused. You were in love.**

And the thing you fell in love with — listen carefully, because the word Allah chooses to name it is the whole secret — the thing you fell in love with, Allah does not even call by the name we expect. Stay with me. The diagnosis is hidden inside a single noun. Let's go and find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: كَلَّا — the word that slams a door

Before we touch the word, let me teach you the kind of word it is.

In English, when someone is mid-sentence building a wrong conclusion, we have soft tools to interrupt them — "well, actually," "hold on," "I'm not sure that's right." We rarely have a single syllable that means: *stop, that entire direction is false, do not take one more step that way.* Arabic has exactly that. It is a particle of **deterrence and rebuke** — a verbal hand thrown up to halt a thought in motion.

That word is **كَلَّا** — *kallā*.

Notice first how it *sounds*. *Kal-lā.* A hard *kāf* striking the front of the mouth, and then a doubled *lām* that you have to lean on, that stops the word dead with weight. It does not drift off. It lands like a palm on a table. The sound is the meaning: an abrupt, weighted halt.

Now here is something worth sitting with. This is not the first *kallā* in the surah. A few ayahs earlier, when the human cried *where is the refuge?*, the answer was **كَلَّا لَا وَزَرَ** — "No! There is no refuge." That *kallā* shut a door on the *outside* — there is nowhere out there to run.

And now this *kallā* shuts a door on the *inside* — there is no honest confusion in here to hide behind.

Let's sit with the placement. The disbeliever has spent the whole surah asking *when, when, when* — performing doubt, wearing skepticism like a costume. And Allah's response is not to argue the *when*. It is to refuse the entire premise. *Kallā.* No. We are not having the conversation you think we're having. Your question was never a question. [PAUSE]

Now — the scholars hand us a beautiful tension here, and I want you to hold both halves of it. Al-Qurtubi reports that this *kallā* is a rebuke aimed squarely at the deniers of Mecca: *no — you will not believe, you do not pray, you do not purify.* It is a slammed door. But al-Jalalayn notes another reading entirely: that here *kallā* functions as *istiftāḥ* — an opening particle, almost like saying *"Verily —"*, a throat-clearing that announces a truth is about to be told.

Don't choose between them. Let them be two camera angles on one moment. From one angle, Allah is *rebuking* a lie. From the other, He is *unveiling* a truth. And isn't that exactly what happens when someone finally tells you the real reason you've been avoiding something? It feels like a rebuke and a revelation at the same instant. The door slams *and* the light comes on. That is *kallā*.

What this single word reveals: **the resurrection was never on trial. Your heart was.** [PAUSE]

And notice the hinge that follows it: **بَلْ** — *bal* — "rather," "nay but." This is not a particle that merely negates; it negates *and replaces.* It sweeps the false claim off the table and sets the true one down in its place. So *kallā bal* together perform one motion: *No — that is not it; here is what it really is.* Al-Tabari catches exactly this when he unfolds the verse: it is not as you say — that you will not be raised after death, that you will not be requited for your deeds — *but rather the thing that drove you to say it was your love of the hastening world, your preferring its appetites over the deferred reward of the Hereafter.* Read him slowly and the architecture of the sin steps into the light: the denial was never the disease. The denial was a *symptom.* Beneath the man's clever question about dates and bones sat an appetite — and the appetite wrote the argument. *Bal* is the word that walks you from the symptom down to the cause.

### Element Two: ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ — He named the disease by its true name

Here is where I need you to slow down with me, because everything turns on one word.

When Allah tells us what the human loves, what word would you expect? You would expect *al-dunyā* — "the world," "the lower life." It is the most natural word. It is the word we ourselves reach for every time we warn each other: *don't love the dunya.*

But Allah does not say *al-dunyā*.

He says **ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ** — *al-ʿājilah*.

Let me teach you this root before we open it. The root is *ʿayn-jīm-lām* (ع-ج-ل), and across the Arabic language it carries one core idea: **haste — wanting a thing before its proper time.** Al-Rāghib, in his lexicon of Quranic words, defines *al-ʿajalah* precisely as *seeking something before its appointed moment.* From this root comes *ʿajila* — to rush; *al-ʿajūl* — the impatient one; and the word the Quran uses elsewhere when it says the human himself *was created from haste* (21:37). The same root names the calf — *al-ʿijl* — the young animal that bolts and will not be still.

So *al-ʿājilah* is, grammatically, a feminine active participle. It does not mean "the world." It means, almost literally, **"the hastening one"** — *the thing that comes quickly, the thing that arrives now.*

And here is something the classical commentators set right beside this verse that we almost never pause on. Only four ayahs earlier, Allah had turned to His own Messenger ﷺ and said: *do not move your tongue with this revelation* **لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِ** — *li-taʿjala bihi* — "to hasten with it" (75:16). The very same root, ع-ج-ل. Ibn Kathir records the scene behind it: the Prophet, out of his eagerness and his love for the words, would race his tongue ahead of Jibrīl, hurrying to seize the revelation before it was finished — and Allah gently restrained him: *It is for Us to gather it and recite it; so when We recite it, follow its recitation.* Now sit with the placement. The surah first corrects *haste* in the holiest setting imaginable — a beloved servant rushing toward the speech of his Lord — and then, in nearly the same breath, names the deniers' ruin as the love of *al-ʿājilah,* the hastening one. Even sacred eagerness is asked to slow down and trust the unfolding; how much more is the appetite that races toward the world and cannot bear to wait for the Hereafter a thing to be feared? The root that named a Prophet's pardonable impatience names, a few lines later, mankind's fatal one.

Notice what Allah has done. [PAUSE]

He could have named the beloved by its *substance* — the world, wealth, the body, pleasure. Instead He named it by its **timing.** He diagnosed the disease not by *what* we love, but by *when* it pays out.

Stay with this, because it is the whole insight: **the human heart does not actually fall in love with the world because the world is good. It falls in love with the world because the world is *fast*.** Allah reaches past the object of our desire and puts His finger on the real hook — *immediacy itself.* The thing's worth was never the point. Its nearness was the point. We are not, at root, lovers of pleasure. We are lovers of *now.*

And here is the proof folded right into the grammar: Allah pairs *al-ʿājilah* — the hastening one — with *al-ākhirah* — "the latter one," "the one that comes after," from the root *ʾ-kh-r* meaning afterness, lateness, lastness. Al-Tabari, glossing the verse, reaches instinctively for the word classical Arabic sets opposite *al-ʿājil* — *al-ājil,* "the deferred." Hear how close the two sit: *ʿājil* and *ājil,* immediate and deferred, the whole of a human life balanced on the difference of a single stroke. And he spells the trade out plainly — what these people did was *prefer the appetites* of the hastening world *over the deferred reward* of the Hereafter. Not weigh two goods and choose the lesser; simply *prefer the sooner.*

Look at the two nouns side by side. **He named *both* of them by time.** Not "the cheap thing" versus "the precious thing." Not "the false thing" versus "the true thing." But *the soon thing* versus *the later thing.* The Arabic forces you to admit something brutal about yourself: when you chose, you did not weigh worth against worth. You weighed *soon* against *later* — and *soon* won. Every single time, *soon* won.

That is not a moral failure of taste. It is a failure of *time-horizon.* And the words knew it before we did. [PAUSE]

There's one more thread here I don't want to leave on the floor. Al-Jalalayn notes the variant readings of the verbs — *tuḥibbūna / tadharūna* with the *tā'* ("**you** love," direct address) and *yuḥibbūna / yadharūna* with the *yā'* ("**they** love," about them). Sit with the *tā'* reading for a moment. Allah turns from talking *about* the deniers and points straight at the listener: *you.* Not "people like this." *You.* The accusation steps out of the third person and walks up to your face. Whoever recites it, recites an indictment with their own name in the blank.

### Element Three: تَذَرُونَ — the verb of forsaking that was itself forsaken

[ayah:75:21] وَتَذَرُونَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ

The verb is from the root *wāw-dhāl-rā'* (و-ذ-ر), *wadhara*, and its meaning is to leave a thing be, to set it down and walk on, to neglect it. Not to attack — to *abandon*. Casually. The way you set down a book you mean to finish "later" and never open again.

And here is a thing the classical grammarians noticed about this very verb — something so fitting it will make your skin prickle.

This verb is *defective.* In classical Arabic usage, *wadhara* abandoned its own past tense. The Arabs did not say *wadhartu* ("I left") in the perfect; that form fell out of use, and they reached for a different verb — *taraka* — to speak of leaving in the past. What survived in common use was the present and the future: *tadharu, yadharu* — *you leave, he leaves, ongoing, right now, still happening.*

You can watch this happen in the commentaries themselves. When al-Muyassar comes to gloss *tadharūna,* he instinctively reaches for the other verb — *tatrukūna,* from *taraka* — to carry the sense. The suppletion is not a dusty grammarian's footnote; it is alive on the page, the mufassir borrowing *taraka* in the very act of explaining the word whose own past had gone missing.

Let that land. [PAUSE]

**The very word that means "to abandon" was itself, in part, abandoned by the language.** The word for forsaking got forsaken. And the form of it that *did* survive is the present-tense form — the form that means *you are doing this right now, and you will keep doing it.*

So when Allah says *wa-tadharūna l-ākhirah*, the grammar is not describing a one-time rejection in the past. It is describing a *continuous, ongoing leaving* — a thing you are doing in this very moment, and the next, and the next. You are not someone who once decided against the Hereafter. You are someone who is, perpetually, *in the act of walking past it.*

Al-Jalalayn gives the leaving its sharpest, simplest gloss: *fa-lā yaʿmalūna lahā* — *they do not work for it.* Not "they argue against it," not "they curse it" — they simply *do no work* for it. The Hereafter does not lose its place in their creed; it loses its place in their *calendar.* A person can affirm it with the tongue and never lift a finger toward it — and that, the grammar and the gloss agree, is precisely what *tadharūna* names.

And now feel the asymmetry between the two verbs, because this is the masterstroke.

For the immediate world, the verb is **تُحِبُّونَ** — *tuḥibbūna* — from the root *Hbb*, the verb of *love.* Active. Warm. Engaged. The whole heart leaning forward.

For the eternal world, the verb is **تَذَرُونَ** — *tadharūna* — you *leave* it. Cold. Passive. Disengaged. The whole heart turning its back and strolling away.

Notice: Allah did *not* make these two verbs symmetrical. He did not say "you love the immediate and *hate* the Hereafter." Hatred would at least be *engagement* — you'd be facing it, fighting it, taking it seriously enough to oppose it. But that's not the human condition He's diagnosing. The tragedy is gentler and far more damning than hatred:

**We don't hate the Hereafter. We just keep leaving it for later.** [PAUSE]

There is one final thread that ties this verb back to the surah's own spine. Seven ayahs earlier (75:13), Allah said the human will be told of what he *sent forward* and what he **أَخَّرَ** — *akhkhara* — "put off, delayed" — from the very same root as *al-ākhirah.* Do you see it? The thing you *delayed* (*akhkhara*) is literally "the later thing" (*al-ākhirah*). Your sin and its object share a root. You took the matter that was *supposed* to be your final destination and you filed it under "later" — and then "later" never came, because *tadharūna* is present tense: the leaving never stops to let later arrive.

### The bridge

So the words have shown us a precise mechanism. *Kallā* — your denial was never honest doubt. *Al-ʿājilah* — what you loved was not the world's worth but its speed, its nearness, its *now-ness.* *Tadharūna* — and the Hereafter you did not reject so much as endlessly postpone, leaving it in an ongoing present tense that never lets "later" become "now."

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The brain that was built to love "now"

Let me start where you already live.

A behavioral economist sits two choices in front of you. *Here is twenty dollars right now,* she says, *or here is twenty-two dollars in a week.* And reliably — across cultures, across incomes, across education levels — a huge share of people grab the twenty. Now. Today. They walk away from free money rather than wait seven days.

They have a name for this. They call it **temporal discounting** — or more vividly, **present bias.** The mind applies a steep, almost violent discount to any reward that lives in the future. A pleasure available *now* glows at full brightness; the identical pleasure available *later* is dimmed, faded, somehow less real to us — even when it is objectively *larger.* The future self who would collect the bigger reward feels to us like a stranger we owe nothing to.

Neuroscientists have watched this happen inside the skull. The circuits that light up for immediate rewards are older, faster, hungrier. The circuitry that can value the distant future is slower, newer, and easily shouted down. Marketers know this. Lenders know this. Every "buy now, pay later" button ever designed is a monument to this single flaw in us.

Now hold that science in one hand, and hold *al-ʿājilah* in the other.

Fourteen centuries before a single one of those experiments was run, the Quran named the disease and named it *by its mechanism.* Not "you love bad things." *You love the hastening thing.* You love the reward that pays out now. Allah did not call it *love of the world.* He called it, in effect, **present bias** — and He called it a matter of the *heart,* not merely a quirk of cognition.

Here is where the ayah goes deeper than the economist can. The economist will tell you present bias makes you lose twenty-two dollars. The Quran tells you the *same exact bug* — the same preference for *al-ʿājilah* over *al-ākhirah* — is what makes a human being lose his soul. It is not two different flaws. It is *one* flaw, operating at two scales. The man who grabs the twenty dollars and the man who grabs the sin are running the identical broken calculation: *soon is worth more than later, no matter how much bigger "later" is.*

And notice the cruel arithmetic Allah is exposing. In the twenty-dollar experiment, the gap is two dollars over a week. In the real trade, the gap is *a fleeting, hastening life* against *an eternity.* The discount we apply is so steep that it can make a person trade the infinite for the instant — and feel, in the moment, like they got a good deal. [PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the war is not, primarily, a war against *pleasure.* It is a war against your own *time-horizon.* The practical work is not to convince yourself the sin is ugly — often it isn't ugly, it's lovely, that's the whole problem. The practical work is to drag the *later* close enough that your hungry, present-biased brain can finally *see* it. This is precisely why the Quran spends so much ink making the Hereafter *vivid, sensory, immediate* — the rivers, the faces, the fire you can hear breathing. It is not decoration. It is *therapy* for present bias. Allah is pulling *al-ākhirah* forward until it is near enough to compete with *al-ʿājilah* for the same heart. Your daily practice — the salah that interrupts your day five times, the dhikr, the reading — every one of these is a tool for the same purpose: shrinking the distance to "later" until it stops feeling like a stranger.

### Theme Two: The sin of "I'll get to it"

Now let me take you somewhere quieter, because the second verb — *tadharūna* — diagnoses a sin most of us would never confess to, precisely because it doesn't feel like a sin at all.

Think of the relationship you most need to repair. The call you owe a parent. The apology you've been meaning to make. You don't *hate* that relationship. You'd be offended if someone said you did. You fully intend to get to it. You just... haven't. Not today. Today was busy. You *left* it — gently, with every intention of returning — and the leaving has quietly stretched into months, into years.

That is *tadharūna.* That is the exact emotional texture of the word. Not the slamming of a door — the *failure to ever walk back through one.*

And here is what should unsettle us: Allah did not accuse the human of *hating* the Hereafter. He accused him of *leaving* it. Because most of us are not standing in defiance with our fists raised against eternity. Far more of us are doing something softer and, in its way, more dangerous: we *believe* in it, we *intend* to prepare for it, we fully plan to become serious about it — *later.* After this phase of life. After the kids are grown. After the career settles. After Ramadan. After we've had our fill of *al-ʿājilah.*

We have not rejected the Hereafter. We have *scheduled* it. We've filed it under "later." [PAUSE]

And remember what the grammar told us: *tadharūna* is present tense. *Ongoing.* The leaving is not a past decision you can point back to and repent of cleanly. It is a thing happening *right now,* in the gap between hearing this ayah and doing anything about it. And remember the root-link to *akhkhara* — "you delayed." The Hereafter is literally named "the later thing," and our characteristic sin against it is to make it *even later* than it already is. We take the one matter that is supposed to be the *destination* and we treat it as the thing we'll get around to once everything *else* is handled. This is why al-Jalalayn's plain gloss stings: *they do not work for it.* Not because they decided against it — but because the work for it is always the thing that can wait until after the work for everything that pays out sooner.

But here is the trap of postponement that the present tense exposes: **"later" is not a real place.** It never arrives. The man who says "I'll be serious after this" is the same man who, after this, will say "I'll be serious after the next thing." Because the engine driving the postponement — the love of *al-ʿājilah* — is *still running.* You cannot postpone your way out of a problem caused by postponement. The "later" you're banking on is built by the very disease you're hoping it will cure.

So what shifts tomorrow?

The antidote to *tadharūna* is brutally simple and almost impossibly hard: **do the one thing now.** Not the whole transformation — the present-biased brain will reject a reward that huge and that far. *One* thing. The one prayer prayed on time today. The one apology made this afternoon. The one ayah understood this morning. Because the only way to break a perpetual present-tense leaving is with a single present-tense *returning.* You don't defeat "I'll get to it" with a better plan for later. You defeat it with a worse-but-real act *now.* You turn *tadharūna* — "you leave it" — into its opposite, one small, immediate, today-shaped step at a time. Steal the brain's own weapon. Make the *good* thing the *immediate* thing.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the shape of what Allah built, because these six words are a mirror, and the mirror is the message.

> **تُحِبُّونَ** ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ — you *love* … the *hastening* one
> **تَذَرُونَ** ٱلْءَاخِرَةَ — you *leave* … the *latter* one

Two lines. Each one a verb of the heart bound to a noun of *time.* And feel how the whole human imbalance is folded into the symmetry:

- The verb of *passion* — *love* — is married to *al-ʿājilah,* the thing that arrives **now.**
- The verb of *neglect* — *leave* — is married to *al-ākhirah,* the thing that arrives **later.**

Allah did not name these two worlds by their *worth.* He named them both by their *position in time.* And in doing so, He made the structure of the verse *enact* the structure of the sin: the heart's whole energy — its *love* — flows toward the near, and only its *neglect* is left over for the far. The architecture is the diagnosis. The grammar is the verdict. You don't have to be told you're imbalanced; you can *see* the imbalance in the seesaw of the two lines.

### Bringing it together

From one direction, the words: *kallā* — this was never honest doubt; *bal* — and here is the real cause beneath it; *al-ʿājilah* — you loved a thing for its *speed,* not its worth; *tadharūna* — you don't fight the eternal, you simply do no work for it, endlessly postponing it.

From the other direction, the themes: present bias — a brain built to over-value *now*; and the sin of "I'll get to it" — a heart that schedules its own salvation for a "later" that never comes.

They are the same insight arriving from two roads. The unbeliever Mecca produced and the believer distracted by his phone are running the *identical* broken equation. *Soon over later. Now over forever.* And Allah's *kallā* is aimed at both of us — not as a condemnation we can do nothing about, but as the hand on the shoulder that turns the head and says: *Look. The thing you keep leaving for later — that was the only thing that was ever going to last.*

### Questions to carry

1. When I trade the eternal for the immediate, am I really choosing the *better* thing — or just the *sooner* thing? Would I still want it if it were delayed a week?
2. What, specifically, have I been "getting around to" with the Hereafter? Name the *later* I've been living inside.
3. If *tadharūna* is present tense — happening *now* — what am I leaving the Hereafter for in *this* hour?
4. The Quran works to make the unseen *vivid.* What am I letting make *al-ʿājilah* vivid to me — and could I starve it?
5. What is the one small, *immediate* good act I could do today that turns "I'll leave it" into "I returned"?

### One-sentence distillation

Your denial of the eternal was never about the evidence — it was about appetite: you loved what arrives *now*, and quietly, endlessly, kept walking past what waits.

### The closing invitation

You are not being asked to stop loving. You are being asked to *redirect* what you already are — a creature wired to lean toward the near. The invitation of these two ayahs is not "love less." It is: *drag the later close enough to love it too.* Pull *al-ākhirah* near. Make it vivid, make it daily, make it *now-shaped* in some small concrete act before this hour ends — and watch the seesaw of your own heart begin, slowly, to find its balance.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You named our disease before we could hide it — that we love what hastens and leave what lasts. Pull the *ākhirah* near to our hearts until it glows as brightly to us as the fleeting things that fool us, and let us not be of those who keep saying *later* until *later* runs out. Make the love of meeting You the most immediate thing we know. *Āmīn.*
