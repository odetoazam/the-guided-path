# The Question That Does Not Want an Answer
## Surah Al-Qiyamah — 75:6

---

## Introduction

**يَسْـَٔلُ أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ**

*Yas'alu ayyāna yawmu l-qiyāmah.*

He asks: "When is the Day of Resurrection?"

---

Four words. One verse. The entire weight of human denial compressed into a question — and what I want to do with you today is look very carefully at what that question is actually made of. Because there is something remarkable happening inside these four words that goes far beyond the deniers of seventh-century Mecca. There is something here that is, I think, one of the most precise diagnoses of a very human spiritual strategy that the Quran ever delivers.

But first — we need to feel the world this ayah is living in.

### Rebuilding the World

We are in Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ has been receiving revelation and the Quraish — the merchants, the poets, the intellectuals of the Arabian Peninsula's most sophisticated city — have developed a very particular posture toward the message of resurrection. Not outright panic. Not silence. Something more effective than both of those: *performance*.

They have learned to ask a question.

Not to get an answer. But to perform the absurdity of expecting one. "When is this Day you keep warning us about?" They ask it at gatherings. They ask it to get a laugh. They ask it to watch the believers go quiet when no one can give them a date on the calendar. Al-Jalalayn reads this verse and identifies it immediately: سؤال استهزاء وتكذيب — *a question of mockery and denial*. It is not curiosity wearing the costume of inquiry. It is denial wearing the costume of curiosity.

And here is what makes this particular surah so structurally disorienting:

The surah opened — just five verses ago — by swearing *by the Day of Resurrection itself*. وَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ — "I swear by the Day of Resurrection." The very Day the denier is now asking about mockingly — that Day is the witness. The oath-object. The thing so certain, so immediately real to the One taking the oath, that words can be anchored to it.

The denier asks: *ayyāna yawmu l-qiyāmah?*

The surah began by swearing: *bi-yawmi l-qiyāmah*.

**The same four letters. The same four words. Two completely different relationships to the same reality.**

One treats the Day as the foundation of all certainty — present enough to swear by. The other treats it as the punchline of doubt — distant enough to mock. That collision is where this entire surah lives. And verse 6 is exactly where the two meet.

Here is the question I want to hold through this entire reflection: What is really happening inside this question? What does the specific word أَيَّانَ — not just "when" but a very particular kind of when — carry that reveals the *architecture* of this denial? And why does Allah describe this asking in the imperfect tense — as something ongoing, habitual, recurring — a question that keeps being asked, never answered, never intended to be?

Because I think when we look carefully at this four-word verse, we will find something that isn't only about seventh-century Meccans. We will find the mechanics of a very human strategy. A strategy most of us have some version of. A question we keep asking — not because we want the answer, but because asking it protects us from having to prepare.

Let's begin.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### The Weight Hidden in a Single Word for "When"

Before we open the ayah, I need to teach you something about Arabic that changes everything in this verse.

Arabic has more than one word for "when."

The most common — the one that appears constantly in everyday speech — is **مَتَى** (*matā*). "When are you arriving?" مَتَى تَصِل. "When does the class start?" مَتَى تَبدأ الدرس. *Matā* asks about something ordinary, something expected, something that belongs to normal time. When's the meeting? When does it open? When are we leaving?

But Arabic has another word: **أَيَّانَ** (*ayyāna*). Classical grammarians describe it as being used for events that feel remote, tremendous, unknowable — events of such scale that the ordinary word for "when" would feel almost casual. أَيَّانَ is *when* asked about something so distant, so beyond the horizon of the felt world, that the question itself carries a kind of incredulity baked into its structure.

Think about the difference in English between "when's the bus?" and "when exactly does the universe end?" The second question has a different emotional color — almost a built-in absurdity — that the first doesn't. You don't ask the second question expecting a bus schedule. You ask it to make the subject feel unanswerable. That is أَيَّانَ territory.

Now notice where this word appears in the Quran. In Surah 79: يَسْأَلُونَكَ عَنِ السَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَاهَا — "They ask you about the Hour: *when* will it anchor?" In Surah 7 with nearly identical wording. And here: أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ — *when* is the Day of Resurrection?

Every time أَيَّانَ appears in the Quran, it is being asked about the Hour, the Day, the Rising. This is not incidental. The questioners are reaching specifically for the word that *encodes* remoteness — the word that makes the subject feel as though it belongs to a category of reality so far from their present world that asking about it is almost a category error.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what the classical scholars add. Al-Jalalayn reads this asking as سؤال استهزاء وتكذيب — *a question of mockery and denial*. Al-Baghawi says it is asked تكذيباً به — *as a denial of it*. Al-Muyassar says the human asks *musta'bidan* — "considering it remote, distant, treating it as impossible." Al-Qurtubi, the most concise: he asks "when is the Day of Resurrection?" — as if to say: I don't believe there is one.

Four scholars. Different centuries. Different methodologies. And they arrive at the same portrait: **this question is not a request for information. It is a refusal of reality dressed as an inquiry.**

What strikes me is that the *word* أَيَّانَ already carries that refusal structurally, inside itself, before any tone or context is applied. The questioner didn't just choose to ask in a mocking voice. They chose the specific word in the Arabic lexicon that communicates: *this thing is so remote from my world that asking about it is almost a performance of its impossibility.*

There is a kind of denial that works not by arguing against something, not by disproving it, but simply by pushing it to the edge of what feels real. By placing it at the far horizon. And أَيَّانَ is exactly how that move sounds in classical Arabic.

### The Verb That Never Stops

Now let's move to the verb: **يَسْأَلُ**.

In Arabic, the verb tense that describes a completed, finished action in the past is the *māḍī* — the perfect tense. سَأَلَ: *he asked*. Past. Done. One moment, now gone. But يَسْأَلُ is the *muḍāriʿ* — the imperfect, the present-continuous. It doesn't tell us he asked. It tells us **he asks**. He is asking. He keeps asking.

To understand why this matters, think about a question you've watched someone ask over and over — not because they want the answer, but because the *asking* itself is serving a function. A child who asks "are we there yet?" every three minutes isn't genuinely requesting navigation data. The asking is doing something else. It's managing the discomfort of arrival. It's keeping the destination at a distance by pretending it hasn't materialized yet. The question is a stalling mechanism.

The imperfect tense here tells us this is not a moment of doubt. It is a **habit**. A posture. A recurring practice. يَسْأَلُ — he asks, and asks, and keeps asking. The question is not one event. It is a lifestyle.

And what is this lifestyle protecting? Verse 5 told us already, just one line earlier: بَلْ يُرِيدُ الْإِنسَانُ لِيَفْجُرَ أَمَامَهُ — "No — the human *wants* to keep sinning ahead of him." Al-Muyassar makes the connection explicit and devastating: يريد أن يبقى على الفجور فيما يستقبل من أيام عمره — *"he wants to remain in his corruption for whatever days of his life remain ahead of him."*

Read verses 5 and 6 together:

*I want to keep sinning ahead of me.*
*So I'll keep asking: when is the Day of Reckoning?*

[PAUSE]

The question is not philosophical. It is strategic. By keeping the Day perpetually in the future — perpetually in the realm of أَيَّانَ, the remote, the unimaginable — the denier buys himself today. And tomorrow. And the day after. He doesn't need to disprove the resurrection. He doesn't need to out-argue the believer. He just needs the Day to *feel* far enough away that he can live as if it isn't arriving.

And the imperfect verb يَسْأَلُ tells us: this has become a practiced art. He has been at it for a long time. The question is a habit, which means the deferral is a habit, which means the continuation in fujūr is the underlying constant — the one thing the question exists to protect.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### What the Day's Own Name Is Saying

Before we move to the thematic depths, let me sit with one more thing — the word قِيَامَة itself.

Its root is ق-و-م — *qāma, yaqūmu* — to stand, to rise, to be established. You know this root from prayer: قيام الليل, the night standing. From the shahada's affirmation of what Islam establishes. From قَامَ الرجل: the man stood up. This root is about verticality, about rising from the horizontal, about establishment.

قِيَامَة, with the tā' marbūṭa completing the form — this is the Great Standing. In Arabic, the tā' marbūṭa on a verbal noun can mark a specific, single, bounded occurrence: not standing in general, but *the* Standing, the one definitive instance of it. The one moment that everything horizontal finally rises. The bones stand. The records stand. The souls stand before the One they stood before in prayer. The truth stands.

Now sit with the irony that this creates: the human is asking *"when is the Standing?"* while doing everything in his power to keep things lying down. To keep the Day in the earth. To keep the reckoning horizontal. To prevent the bones — his own reality — from rising into his felt world.

He asks أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ القيامة? *When is the Day of Rising?*

And the name of the Day whispers back: the Day of Rising is when everything you pressed down finally stands.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### When "Later" Becomes a Way of Life

You know this feeling.

There's something you need to deal with. A conversation you've been putting off. A habit you know is wrong. A direction your life has been drifting that, if you stopped and looked at it honestly, you would have to address. And you've found a way — a sophisticated, effective, often unconscious way — to not stop and look.

You don't deny the problem outright. That would require a confrontation you'd have to defend. Instead, you put it into the future. *I'll deal with that when things settle down. When the kids are older. When I have more energy. When I'm in a better place spiritually. When I understand it more clearly.* The "when" becomes a suspension mechanism. A way of holding reality at arm's length without technically denying it exists.

Behavioral scientists have a name for the underlying cognitive pattern: *temporal discounting* — the further away an event is in time, the less weight it carries in our present decision-making. This isn't a flaw of character. It's a feature of how human cognition is wired. The problem comes when we *weaponize* this feature. When we deliberately push important realities into the future — not because we've genuinely thought them through and decided to address them later — but because we want the present to stay exactly as it is. Because we want, as verse 5 says with surgical precision, *to continue sinning ahead of us*.

Al-Muyassar's reading of this verse is remarkably diagnostic: the human يريد أن يبقى على الفجور — *wants to remain in his corruption*. The question أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ القيامة is not a seeker's question. It is a *fujūr-preservation* mechanism. The asking is in the service of continuing. The question exists so that the answer — *now, today, this is the accounting* — never has to land.

[PAUSE]

Think about how sophisticated this strategy actually is. The person who outright says "I don't believe in judgment" has made a claim that can be examined, challenged, refuted. They've taken a position that puts them in the open. But the person who says *"when exactly is this judgment? Give me a date. Show me the evidence it's imminent."* — who keeps the question alive, keeps the horizon shifting — has found something far more durable. They've turned the *uncertainty about timing* into a permanent deferral of preparation.

*I'll take the akhirah seriously when the evidence is clearer.*
*I'll work on my prayer when I've sorted out the rest of my life.*
*I'll deal with my relationship with Allah when I'm in a better headspace.*

These are our أَيَّانَ moments. Not outright denial — most of us are not denying the resurrection, not in the crude sense. But something functionally similar: a habit of keeping the most important reality perpetually just out of reach. Perpetually remote. Perpetually *ayyāna*.

And what gives the game away — what verse 5 exposes, and what the imperfect tense of يَسْأَلُ confirms — is that this question does not want an answer. If someone gave the denier the exact date, he would not say "thank you, let me prepare." He would find another question. Because **the function of the question was never to know. The function was to not arrive.**

Let me ask this directly, as a genuine question to carry forward:

What is in your أَيَّانَ zone?

What is the thing you've been treating as real in principle but remote in practice — held at the distance of *someday*, *when things are ready*, *when I'm more settled*?

[PAUSE]

Because the denier in this verse is not a cartoon villain. He is a precise portrait of a tendency that lives in every human being. The tendency to use the uncertainty of *when* as a shield against the obligation of *now*. And the Quran, in four words and two grammatical observations, has mapped the entire architecture of that tendency.

### The Oath and the Question

Now let me show you something about this surah's structure that I think is one of the most quietly staggering features of the Quran.

The surah begins with an oath: وَلَا أُقْسِمُ بِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ. "I swear by the Day of Resurrection."

Let's sit with the logic of taking an oath for a moment. When you swear by something — when you say "I swear by X" — you are doing two things at once. You are invoking X as a witness, anchoring your words to a reality external to yourself. And you are declaring, in the same breath: *this X is so real, so present, so certain, that my words can stand on it*. An oath is only as strong as the reality it's sworn by. You don't swear by rumors. You don't swear by maybes. You swear by what cannot be doubted.

Allah swears BY the Day of Resurrection.

Which means: **for the One making this oath, this Day is present. It is immediate. It is so real and certain that it can bear the weight of testimony.**

Five verses later, a human voice enters: أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ الْقِيَامَةِ — *when is the Day of Resurrection?*

The same event. يَوْمُ القيامة. The same letters. The same words.

But one treats it as a foundation — so real it anchors an oath. The other treats it as so remote and doubtful that asking about it is a performance of its impossibility.

**The oath answered the question before the question was asked.**

[PAUSE]

Allah says in verse 1: I swear by this Day — meaning: this Day is certain, present, real. Then the human says in verse 6: *when is this Day?* — meaning: this Day is uncertain, absent, unreal. By the time the denier opens his mouth, the answer has already been given. Not as an argument. Not as a refutation. As a *prior certainty*. The One who knows swore by it before the one who doubts could mock it.

The denier thinks he's raising a challenge. But he has stepped into a surah that already swore by the answer. His question is not met — it is *surrounded*.

[PAUSE]

And then notice what the surah does after verse 6. It doesn't pause to argue. It doesn't stop to say "here is the refutation of your question." It simply proceeds — from verse 7 onward — to describe the Day in physical, visceral, sensory immediacy. فَإِذَا بَرِقَ الْبَصَرُ — when the sight is dazed. وَخَسَفَ الْقَمَرُ — and the moon is eclipsed. وَجُمِعَ الشَّمْسُ وَالْقَمَرُ — and the sun and moon are gathered together. These are not abstractions. These are events with physical texture. Events you can almost feel.

What the denier did with أَيَّانَ was push the Day to the far edge of felt reality. What the surah does in response is pull it to the near edge. The denier said: *make it distant*. The surah replies: *let me describe how close it is*.

He asked أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ القيامة — when is the Day? Implying: it is far.

The surah answers: here is what it looks like when it is here.

The question tried to create distance. The surah refuses to honor that distance for even a verse. The answer is not a date. The answer is a description so immediate that the distance collapses.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Let me show you the shape of the whole.

The surah opens with Allah swearing BY يَوْمُ القيامة — treating it as immediate, certain, present enough to anchor an oath.

It then moves through the challenge: does the human think his bones won't be gathered? — confronting the denial of resurrection in verse 3.

Then the diagnostic in verse 5: no — the human *wants* to continue sinning. *This is the motive behind the denial.*

Then verse 6: the question as the *mechanism* of that motive. **أَيَّانَ** — the choosing of remoteness as a word. **يَسْأَلُ** — the habitual, ongoing, repeated asking as a lifestyle.

Then verses 7 through the end of the surah: the Day described with such physical immediacy that the remoteness the question tried to manufacture cannot survive it.

The oath *precedes* the denial. The description *follows* the question. The Day the denier tried to make remote is enclosed — before and after — by its own reality.

**The architecture of the surah is the architecture of the answer.** The denier's question is not refuted. It is surrounded. And the thing that surrounds it — on both sides — is the certainty of the very Day he asked about.

### Two Directions, One Insight

In the linguistic journey, we found that أَيَّانَ encodes remoteness structurally — it is not an ordinary "when" but the word Arabic reaches for when the subject is meant to feel beyond the horizon of real things. We found that يَسْأَلُ — the imperfect — reveals not a moment of doubt but a practiced habit, a question that has become a lifestyle. We found in قيامة itself — the Great Standing, the moment everything horizontal finally rises — the answer compressed into the Day's own name.

In the thematic depths, we found that this question is in the service of fujūr — of the desire to continue as we are. And we found that the surah's architecture surrounds the question with its answer before and after.

These are not two separate discoveries. They are the same insight from two directions.

**The question أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ القيامة is the sound that sophisticated denial makes when it is clever enough to dress itself as inquiry.** The word carries the manufactured distance. The tense carries the habit. The surrounding surah carries the answer. And the root of the Day's name carries the irony: the very Day the denier is trying to press down is the Day when everything pressed down finally stands.

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The question "when?" — when asked not to know but to stay away — is the oldest architecture of self-serving denial; and the very name of the Day being questioned already contains the answer: the Standing arrives when everything that has been pressed down finally rises.**

### The Closing Invitation

There is a practice that runs through the tradition — carried by companions and the scholars who followed them — of making يَوْمُ القيامة *present*. Not in an anxious, spiraling way. In a sober, grounded way. Of waking in the morning and holding the knowledge that this day is one closer. Of standing in prayer and understanding: the One I am standing before now is the One I will stand before then, and what passes between us in these moments is the record being written.

The denier uses أَيَّانَ to make the Day remote. This practice does the opposite: it draws it near. Not as a threat — as an orientation. A compass. When the Day is near in this sense — present to the heart, real to the will — the question أَيَّانَ loses its function. You don't need to defer what you've already decided to meet.

The surah that opened by swearing by this Day is inviting us into that relationship: not the relationship of the mocker who asks أَيَّانَ, but the relationship of the witness who swears بِيَوْمِ القيامة — for whom the Day is present enough, real enough, certain enough, that words can be anchored to it.

What would it look like — tomorrow, just tomorrow — to live one day in that relationship?
