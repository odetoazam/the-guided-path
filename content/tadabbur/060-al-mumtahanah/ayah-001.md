# My Enemy First: The Love a Heart Cannot Send Two Directions

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by simply placing the words in front of you.

> **يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تَتَّخِذُوا۟ عَدُوِّى وَعَدُوَّكُمْ أَوْلِيَآءَ تُلْقُونَ إِلَيْهِم بِٱلْمَوَدَّةِ**

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū lā tattakhidhū ʿaduwwī wa-ʿaduwwakum awliyāʾ, tulqūna ilayhim bil-mawaddah…*

**"O you who believe — do not take My enemies and your enemies as allies, casting toward them love…"**

Now let me rebuild the world this ayah fell into, because if you don't feel the world, the ayah becomes a rule. And it is not a rule. It is a surgery on the human heart.

It is roughly the year six after the Hijrah. The believers have a city now — Madinah. But almost every one of them left someone behind in Makkah. A mother. A daughter. A grandfather. An old friend who used to share bread with you before any of this began. The line between Makkah and Madinah is not a line between strangers. It runs straight through families. It runs through the dinner table of memory.

And here is the thing we must feel: the people in Makkah were not faceless. The man being warned in this surah did not hate them. That was exactly the problem. He **loved** them. He had children there. He had no clan to protect his family, and the Makkans did. He was afraid for the people he loved.

[PAUSE]

There is a narration preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī — al-Baghawī records its chain back to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib (رضي الله عنه) — and it is one of the most human stories in the whole Qur'an's occasion of revelation. As the Prophet ﷺ quietly prepared to march on Makkah, a sincere believer named Ḥāṭib ibn Abī Baltaʿah wrote a secret letter to the Makkans, warning them. He gave it to a woman to carry. The Prophet ﷺ was informed by revelation, and sent ʿAlī, al-Zubayr, and al-Miqdād racing to a place called Rawḍat Khākh to intercept her. They found the letter hidden in her hair.

When Ḥāṭib was confronted, he did not deny it. He said: *I am not a disbeliever. I have no clan there to protect my family. I wanted a favor I could call in to keep them safe.* And the Prophet ﷺ said, *He has told you the truth.* ʿUmar wanted to strike his neck. The Prophet ﷺ stopped him.

Hold that whole scene. Because this ayah is Allah speaking **into that exact moment** — into the heart of a believer who never stopped believing, but who let his love leak across a line it should not have crossed.

**The hook is this:** read the ayah again and you will notice it forbids something, then describes the people, then — mid-sentence — it simply *stops*. It begins a condition, "If you have gone forth striving in My way…", and never finishes it. The sentence breaks off. And then it says the strangest, most intimate thing: *"You confide love to them in secret — while I already know what you concealed."*

Why does the most generous response to a man's secret betrayal begin with the words **"My enemy"** — and end not with a punishment, but with the quiet fact that **He already knew?**

Stay with me. Everything is in how the words are placed.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: عَدُوِّى وَعَدُوَّكُمْ — "My enemy" before "yours"

Let me teach you something about word order before we touch the ayah.

In any language, when you list two things, the first one is the one you want to land first. "Blood and water." "Country and family." The order is an argument. Put a word first and you have told the listener what the sentence is really about before you've even finished it.

Now here is the phrase:

> **عَدُوِّى وَعَدُوَّكُمْ** — *ʿaduwwī wa-ʿaduwwakum* — "My enemy and your enemy."

Two words from the same root — **ʿadw** (ع-د-و), the root of enmity, of hostility, of crossing over against someone. The first carries the pronoun **ـِى** — *"My."* The second carries **ـكُمْ** — *"your."*

Notice something. Allah could have said *"your enemy"* and stopped. These were, after all, the people who had tortured the believers, boycotted them, expelled them. From the believers' side, the enmity was obvious and earned. But that is not where Allah begins.

He begins with **عَدُوِّى** — *"My* enemy."

Let's sit with what that does.

[PAUSE]

First, it reframes the entire conflict. The believer in Madinah might think of Makkah as *his* problem — his lost home, his personal grievance, the people who wronged *him*. Allah lifts the enmity off the believer's small shoulders and claims it as **His own**. As if to say: this is not your private feud. The line you are tempted to soften is a line *I* have drawn. These are not merely people who hurt you. These are people in active hostility to *Me* — to the truth, to the Messenger, to the worship of the One who made them.

And here is what that reveals, and it is tender beneath its sternness: **Allah is telling you whose battle it really is.** Your enmity is secondary, derivative — *"and* your enemy." It only exists *because* of His. Strip away the cause — strip away their war on the truth — and your personal enmity has no independent root. You were never asked to hate anyone for yourself. The believer is not the source of this hostility; he is downstream of it.

Look closer at the grammar and it gets sharper. The word **عَدُوّ** — *ʿaduww* — in Arabic does not change for singular or plural in this form. One enemy, a thousand enemies, it can hold the same shape. So the phrase is not pointing at a list of specific named individuals. It is naming a **category**, a standing reality: those set in opposition to Allah.

**Here is what is remarkable.** By placing "My" first, Allah makes the prohibition survive the emotions of the moment. Because Ḥāṭib's problem was precisely that, *personally*, he didn't feel enmity. He felt warmth. He had no private grudge to restrain. So if the ayah had only said *"your* enemy," Ḥāṭib could have answered in his heart: *but they're not really my enemy — these are my people.* Allah closes that door before it opens. **"My enemy."** The question was never whether *you* feel hostility toward them. The question is where *they* stand toward your Lord.

One word — one possessive pronoun, **ـِى** — and the whole center of gravity of the verse moves from your wounded heart to His rightful claim.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: تُلْقُونَ … تُسِرُّونَ — the love you throw, and the love you hide

Now watch the verb of love. Because Allah uses two different verbs for the *same* act of affection — once near the beginning, once near the end — and the gap between them is the whole drama of a divided heart.

The first time:

> **تُلْقُونَ إِلَيْهِم بِٱلْمَوَدَّةِ** — *tulqūna ilayhim bil-mawaddah* — "casting toward them love."

The verb **تُلْقُونَ** comes from the root **l-q-y** (ل-ق-ي), and in this Form IV shape — *alqā* — it means literally to **throw, to cast, to fling** something across a distance. You use this verb for throwing a rope, casting a net, hurling a stone. It is a physical word. It assumes a gap — a space between the thrower and the target — and the object travels across it.

Stay on that image, because Allah chose a *thrown* word on purpose. Makkah is far. The people you love are across the desert, across the line. And the believer is **casting** something across that gap — flinging affection over the wall toward people who, the ayah immediately reminds us, *drove him out.* There is a quiet ache in the physics of it: you are throwing love toward those who threw you out.

And what is being thrown? **بِٱلْمَوَدَّةِ** — *al-mawaddah*. The root **w-d-d** (و-د-د). Now, Arabic has many words for love. There is *ḥubb*, the general word. There is *ʿishq*, the consuming kind. But **mawaddah** is something specific: it is *tender, affectionate love that openly seeks closeness* — the warmth that wants to maintain a bond, to stay connected, to be near. It is the very word the Qur'an uses elsewhere for the gentle affection between spouses. It is the warmest, most relational of the loves.

Feel the tragedy in that choice. The thing being misdirected is not some cold calculation. It is the **tenderest** affection a heart produces. This is not a verse against being warm. It is a verse about *where* your warmth is allowed to flow.

[PAUSE]

Now the second time the phrase appears:

> **تُسِرُّونَ إِلَيْهِم بِٱلْمَوَدَّةِ** — *tusirrūna ilayhim bil-mawaddah* — "you confide love to them in secret."

Same ending — *ilayhim bil-mawaddah*, "to them, with love." But the verb has changed. No longer **تُلْقُونَ** (you cast) but **تُسِرُّونَ** — from the root **s-r-r** (س-ر-ر), the root of **sirr**, a *secret*. Form IV here, *asarra*, means to confide something privately, to slip it to someone under the surface, to keep it hidden.

Do you see what just happened across the ayah? The verb of love **moved from open to hidden.** It began as something *thrown* across a distance — visible, in the air — and ended as something *whispered* underground. The first verb is a stone in flight; the second is a note passed beneath the table.

This is the anatomy of how a forbidden loyalty actually behaves in a human being. It does not announce itself. It starts as a warmth you'd defend out loud — *"they're just my family"* — and it migrates downward into something you no longer say aloud, something you tuck into a letter and hide in a woman's hair. **Mawaddah** that has to become **sirr** has already told on itself. If your affection has to go into hiding, some part of you has already passed judgment on it.

And then — the line that breaks the heart open:

> **وَأَنَا۠ أَعْلَمُ بِمَآ أَخْفَيْتُمْ وَمَآ أَعْلَنتُمْ** — *wa-anā aʿlamu bimā akhfaytum wa-mā aʿlantum* — "while I am most knowing of what you have concealed and what you have made public."

**أَخْفَيْتُمْ** — *akhfaytum*, from **kh-f-y**, to conceal. **أَعْلَنتُمْ** — *aʿlantum*, from **ʿ-l-n**, to make public. The two are placed side by side as opposites — the hidden and the declared — and Allah says *both* are equally open to Him.

Look at the devastating gentleness of the construction. You **tusirrūna** — you make it a *secret*. And in the very same breath, Allah says **anā aʿlamu** — *I* know. The word *sirr* and the word *aʿlam* are standing in the same sentence, staring at each other. You built a secret; He was already inside the room. The thing you concealed in the woman's hair, He had read before the ink dried.

[PAUSE]

And here is the mercy hidden in the sternness — and I want you to catch this, because it is easy to miss. He says *"what you concealed **and** what you made public."* He did not only say He knows your secret betrayals. He says He knows your **open** acts too. Ḥāṭib's open life was years of sincere faith, of *hijrah*, of standing in the ranks. The same omniscience that exposed his secret letter also **witnessed his sincere heart.** That is precisely why the Prophet ﷺ could say *"he has told you the truth"* and let him live. The God who sees what you hide also sees what is genuinely good in you — and weighs the whole.

That is what this doubled verb reveals. **A loyalty that has to go into hiding is already broken — and you cannot hide it from the One whose claim it is. But the same eye that sees your secret also sees your sincerity.**

### Element Three: The Sentence That Stops — إِن كُنتُمْ خَرَجْتُمْ

Now I want to read an *absence* with you, because the most powerful thing in this ayah is something Allah deliberately left out.

Look at the middle of the verse:

> **إِن كُنتُمْ خَرَجْتُمْ جِهَـٰدًا فِى سَبِيلِى وَٱبْتِغَآءَ مَرْضَاتِى** — *in kuntum kharajtum jihādan fī sabīlī wabtighāʾa marḍātī* — "If you have gone forth striving in My way and seeking My pleasure…"

In Arabic, *in* (إِن) sets up a condition. "**If** X… **then** Y." The grammar makes you wait for the second half. *If you have gone out striving in My way… then —* then what?

And the *then* never comes.

The sentence breaks off. Grammarians call this **ḥadhf jawāb al-sharṭ** — the deletion of the answer to the condition. The "then" clause is surgically removed and the verse moves on without it. This is not sloppiness. In Arabic rhetoric, dropping the answer to a condition is one of the most charged things you can do — because it forces the *listener* to finish the sentence in their own conscience.

Let's sit with this, because it is extraordinary.

Allah says: *"If you really went out striving in My path, seeking My pleasure…"* — and then leaves a silence where the conclusion should be. **…then how could you do this? …then this betrayal makes no sense. …then do not take them as allies.** Every possible ending crashes the believer into the contradiction of his own life. He left everything *for Allah's pleasure* — **ٱبْتِغَآءَ مَرْضَاتِى**, the active *seeking out* of Allah's contentment (the verb **ibtighāʾ**, Form VIII of **b-gh-y**, carries that sense of striving, pursuing, going after). He **went out** — **خَرَجْتُمْ** — in *jihād*, in exertion, in struggle. And the people he is now whispering love to are the very ones who **يُخْرِجُونَ** — *drove out* — the Messenger and the believers in the first place.

Catch that mirror in the roots. The believers **kharajtum** — went out (by their own will, for Allah). The enemies **yukhrijūna** — *push* out, expel, cause to go out. Same root **kh-r-j** (خ-ر-ج), opposite directions of the will. The Form IV **yukhrijūna** here is genuinely causative — *to make someone leave*. You left *for* Allah; they expelled you *because of* Allah. And now you would send your tenderest love back toward the hands that did the expelling?

The unfinished sentence is the most respectful thing in the verse. Allah does not spell out the conclusion because **He trusts your heart to feel it.** He lays your sacrifice and your slip side by side — *you went out for My pleasure… you whisper to them in secret* — and lets the silence between them do the work no rebuke could.

[PAUSE]

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Heart Cannot Have Two Homes

Let me start where you already live.

You know the experience of a divided loyalty, even if you've never written a secret letter. You have sat in a room where two people you care about are at odds, and you have felt your heart trying to face both directions at once. You have kept a friendship you knew you should let go of, because the *person* was dear even though the bond was wrong for you. You have stayed warm toward something — a habit, a relationship, an old life — that you had supposedly already left, telling yourself, *I left it, but I can still keep a little affection for it on the side.*

That little side-pocket of affection. That is what this ayah is about.

We tend to think loyalty is about actions. Did I help the enemy or not? Did I cross the line or not? But Allah goes underneath the action to the **mawaddah** — to the *affection itself*, the warmth, the inner leaning. He is legislating not just what your hands do but **where your heart faces.** Because He knows something about us that we resist knowing: **the heart leans toward what it loves, and over time, you become what you lean toward.**

Ḥāṭib didn't switch sides. He would have been horrified at the suggestion. He kept his faith *and* kept a warm channel open to the other side, as insurance, as tenderness, as fear for his children. He thought he could hold both. And the entire surah opens by saying, gently but immovably: **you cannot hold both.** Not because Allah is jealous in a petty way, but because a heart is not built to belong, at its root, to two opposed loves. One of them will, in the end, govern you.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to feel. The ayah does not say *"do not feel any human emotion for your relatives."* Later in this very surah (60:8), Allah will make explicit that those who did *not* fight you over religion may be treated with kindness and justice — *birr* and *qisṭ*. So this is not a verse against natural human warmth. The surgery is more precise than that.

The line is **walāyah** — *alliance, deep belonging, taking someone as the one you turn to.* The forbidden thing is **أَوْلِيَآءَ** — *awliyāʾ*, from **w-l-y**, the root of nearness and protective allegiance, the same root from which Allah calls Himself *al-Walī*, your true Protector. To take Allah's active enemies as your *awliyāʾ* is to install, in the place reserved for your ultimate belonging, someone at war with the One you belong to.

And the heart only has *one* such place.

Think of it like a compass. A compass can only point north. You can hold it near a magnet and watch the needle wobble toward the magnet instead — but the moment it does, it has stopped doing the one thing a compass is *for*. A heart with a second, secret allegiance is a wobbling needle. It still looks like a compass. It has simply stopped reliably pointing home.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It asks you to find your own side-pocket. Not the dramatic betrayal — the *small* preserved warmth toward something you've supposedly left behind. The number you haven't deleted. The door you keep cracked open "just in case." The part of your old life you let yourself love quietly while telling yourself you've moved on. This ayah's mercy is that it caught Ḥāṭib *early*, before the side-pocket became the whole coat. **Examine where your tenderness still flows against your stated direction — and ask, honestly, which one is the insurance policy and which one is the home.**

### Theme Two: Already Known — and the Mercy In It

Now begin somewhere else you live: the universal human craving for a hidden self.

Every person carries a backstage. There is the *you* that you make public — **مَا أَعْلَنتُمْ**, what you declare — and the *you* that you keep concealed — **مَا أَخْفَيْتُمْ**, what you hide. The gap between them is where most of our anxiety lives. We spend enormous energy managing it: curating the public self, guarding the private one, terrified that someone might see the seam.

And the modern world has made a god of the *sirr*, the secret — the private browser tab, the locked phone, the account no one knows about, the thought we'd never say aloud. We have come to believe that the hidden self is the *free* self — that what no one sees, no one can judge, and so there, at least, we are finally alone and unaccountable.

Into that craving, Allah drops six words: **وَأَنَا۠ أَعْلَمُ بِمَآ أَخْفَيْتُمْ وَمَآ أَعْلَنتُمْ** — *"and I am most knowing of what you concealed and what you made public."*

The backstage has no walls.

Now, the first reaction to that is fear. *He sees everything. There is nowhere to hide.* And that fear is real, and the verse intends some of it. But stay with me, because there is something underneath the fear that is the opposite of fear.

[PAUSE]

Consider what it actually *means* to be fully known. Every human relationship you have ever had operates on partial knowledge. People love the version of you they can see. And so a quiet loneliness sits inside even your closest bonds: *if they knew the hidden part, would they still…?* You are loved, but you are loved at a distance — loved through the curated layer. No human being has ever loved the *whole* of you, because no human being has ever *seen* the whole of you.

Allah has.

**أَنَا۠ أَعْلَمُ** — *I* know. The fully concealed and the fully declared, the letter in the hair and the years in the ranks, the side-pocket of fear and the sincere faith underneath it. He sees the **entire** human being at once — and notice what He does with that complete vision in this very story. He exposes the secret, *and He accepts the man.* The Prophet ﷺ, acting on Allah's disclosure, does not erase Ḥāṭib. He says *"he told you the truth"* and shields him from ʿUmar's sword.

That is the staggering thing. **The only One who sees all of you is also the only One positioned to forgive all of you.** A judge who sees half your story might condemn the half he sees. The One who sees the whole story — the fear *and* the faith, the slip *and* the sacrifice — is the only One who can weigh it justly and still show mercy. Being completely known is the precondition for being completely forgiven.

[PAUSE]

There is a paradox here I don't want to resolve too quickly. The same sentence that should terrify the hypocrite should *comfort* the sincere. To the one cultivating a secret betrayal, *"I know what you concealed"* is exposure. To the one who is struggling, slipping, getting up again, terrified that their inner mess disqualifies them — *"I know what you concealed"* is relief. He knows the part of you that you cannot even put into words to defend yourself. You do not have to perform for Him. You could not if you tried. The performance is already over. **You are seen — completely — and still invited.**

So what does this change tomorrow?

It offers to end a particular exhaustion: the labor of the gap. The effort of being one person in public and managing another in private. This ayah says that, before the only audience that finally matters, **the gap does not exist.** You might let that collapse the seam a little — let the private self and the public self move toward each other, not because you've been caught, but because there was never anywhere to hide and there was never any need. **Live, in your hidden hours, as though the One who loves you most is already in the room. Because He is. And He stayed anyway.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back and look at the shape of the whole ayah now, because the architecture is the proof.

It opens with **عَدُوِّى** — *"My* enemy" — pulling the conflict up to Allah. It closes with **سَوَاءَ ٱلسَّبِيلِ** — *"the soundness of the way"* — and the verb **ضَلَّ**, *strayed*. From **My enemy** to **the straight path lost.** The verse is bracketed by Allah on one end and the road home on the other, and the betrayal sits in the middle, knocking the traveler off the *sawāʾ* — the *level, even* ground of the path (the root **s-w-y**, evenness, balance). Misplaced love is not described as a crime here so much as a **disorientation.** *Ḍalla sawāʾ al-sabīl* — he wandered off the even road. The man with the side-pocket didn't become evil. He got *lost.*

And see how the two halves of our journey are one insight from two directions.

The **linguistic** discovery: a love that has to migrate from *thrown* (**tulqūna**) to *whispered* (**tusirrūna**) has already convicted itself — and it cannot be hidden from the One whose claim it is.

The **thematic** discovery: the heart cannot hold two homes, and the One who sees its hidden chamber is the only One who can both expose it and forgive it.

They are the same truth. **Where your love secretly leans is where you secretly belong — and that direction is fully visible to the One you'd hide it from.** The grammar and the heart say the identical thing. The unfinished conditional — *"if you went out seeking My pleasure…"* — is Allah handing you the pen to finish the sentence in your own life.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where is my *side-pocket* — the warmth I still send, quietly, toward something I claim to have left?
2. When my affection has to become a *secret*, what has that secrecy already admitted that my words won't?
3. If the gap between my public self and my private self vanished tomorrow — if everyone saw the backstage — what one thing would I most want to change *first*? And why am I waiting for an audience to change it?
4. The believers *went out* for Allah; the enemies *pushed* them out because of Allah. In my own life, what have I left *for* Him — and am I still throwing love back toward what I left?
5. Do I experience *"He knows what I concealed"* mostly as a threat or mostly as a relief? What does my answer tell me about the state of my secret self?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah forbids you to send your heart's tenderest love toward those who war against Him — not because He needs your enmity, but because a heart cannot secretly belong in two directions and stay whole, and the One you would hide that love from already sees it, and loves you still.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah does not ask you to become cold. It asks you to become *undivided.* It found a sincere man with a secret pocket of fear and warmth, and instead of destroying him, it named the pocket, emptied it into the light, and let him keep walking on the *sawāʾ al-sabīl* — the even road home. That invitation is open to you tonight. Not *be perfect.* Just: **stop hiding the leaning of your heart from the One who already sees it — and let Him reorder it.**

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You are *al-ʿAlīm* — You know what I have concealed and what I have made plain, the letter hidden in the hair and the faith hidden in the chest. Gather my divided heart back to one direction. Let no secret affection of mine lean toward what stands against You, and let me find, in being fully known by You, not exposure but rest — and keep my feet on the even soundness of Your way. *Āmīn.*
