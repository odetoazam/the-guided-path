# Do Not Make Us a Trial: The Prayer Not to Become Someone Else's Disbelief

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single word.

> [ayah:60:5] رَبَّنَا لَا تَجْعَلْنَا فِتْنَةً لِّلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَٱغْفِرْ لَنَا رَبَّنَآ ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*Rabbanā lā tajʿalnā fitnatan lilladhīna kafarū, waghfir lanā rabbanā, innaka anta-l-ʿAzīzu-l-Ḥakīm.*

**"Our Lord, do not make us a trial for those who disbelieve, and forgive us, our Lord. Indeed, You — You alone — are the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."**

Now let me rebuild the world this prayer comes from, because if you don't inhabit it, the words will pass over you like wind over a sealed window.

### The world behind the words

Imagine a small community that has just done the hardest thing a human heart can be asked to do. They have broken with their own people. Not strangers — their *people*. The faces they grew up with. The clan that fed them, named them, buried their dead. And they have said to those people, openly: *we are done with what you worship. There has begun, between us and you, an enmity and a hatred — until you believe in Allah alone.*

That is the verse right before this one. Allah says: **"There has already been for you an excellent example in Ibrahim and those with him"** (60:4). Ibrahim — the man who walked away from his father's house, from his city, from the gods his nation had carved with their own hands. The man who was thrown into a fire for it.

And the believers in Makkah, the ones being squeezed and beaten and starved for their faith, the ones whose own relatives had become their persecutors — they are being told: *this is your model. Pray as he prayed.*

So picture them now. They are weak. They are outnumbered. Their tormentors hold every lever of worldly power — wealth, weapons, the marketplace, the law of the tribe. And the believers raise their hands.

Now — what would *you* expect them to ask for?

You would expect: *Lord, save us. Lord, give us victory. Lord, crush them.* That's the natural prayer of the cornered. That's what the body screams.

[PAUSE]

But that is **not** what they ask first.

### The Hook

The first thing out of their mouths is something so strange, so counter-intuitive, that if you read it quickly you will mistake it for an ordinary prayer for protection. It is not.

They do not say *don't let them harm us.* They say something with a completely different center of gravity. Their deepest fear is not their own pain. Their deepest fear is about **what their pain might do to the souls of the very people hurting them.**

Stay with that. The oppressed are praying about the spiritual welfare of the oppressor. And to understand *how* — to see the precise hinge on which that turns — we have to open one word. A word the Qur'an uses for fire, for gold in a furnace, for civil war, for seduction, for the slow corruption of a soul.

The word is *fitnah*. Let's go.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: تَجْعَلْنَا — "Do not *make* us"

Before we even reach *fitnah*, I want you to slow down on the verb that delivers it: **tajʿalnā**, from the root *jaʿala* (ج-ع-ل) — to make, to place, to render, to set something into a state.

Let me teach you the texture of this verb before we use it, because Arabic has more than one way to say "make."

There is *khalaqa* — to create from nothing, to originate. And there is *jaʿala* — which is not origination but **assignment**. *Jaʿala* is to take something that exists and *place it into a role*, to *render* it into a new state or function. When the Qur'an says Allah *jaʿala* the night a covering, the day a livelihood, the sun a lamp — it is not talking about bringing them into existence. It is talking about Him **assigning them a function**, slotting them into a purpose.

So hold that. *Jaʿala* is the verb of **being turned into something. Being made to play a part.**

Now look at what the believers say: **lā tajʿalnā** — "do not *render* us, do not *turn us into*, do not *assign us the role of*..." a *fitnah*.

Notice the absence here — and absence, in the Qur'an, speaks as loudly as presence. They do **not** say *lā taftinnā* — "do not test us." There is a perfectly good verb for that, from the very same root as *fitnah*. They could have said "do not put us to trial." That would have been a prayer about **their own experience**.

That is not the prayer.

They use *jaʿala* — the verb of assignment — and they make **themselves the object that gets assigned to someone else.** "Do not *make us into* a fitnah — *for them.*" The trial is not landing on the believers. The believers are being turned into the trial. They are afraid of becoming **an instrument**. A thing that happens *to* somebody else's faith.

[PAUSE]

Feel the self-forgetfulness in that grammar. In the moment of their own agony, the construction of the sentence has already moved the spotlight off of them. The "us" in *tajʿalnā* is not the one being tested. The "us" is the **material out of which someone else's test gets built.** This is a prayer that has stopped being about its own pain. The grammar itself has turned outward.

That single choice — *jaʿala* over *fatana*, "make us into" over "test us" — is what this entire ayah is built on. Hold it as we open the word it points to.

### Element Two: فِتْنَةً — the word that means fire, gold, and seduction

Now the word itself. **Fitnah.** Root *f-t-n* (ف-ت-ن).

Let me teach you where this word is born, because its birthplace is everything.

The Arabs used the verb *fatana* for what a goldsmith does. You take raw ore — gold mixed with dross, with worthless rock and impurity — and you put it into the **fire**. *Fatantu-dh-dhahab*: I subjected the gold to the furnace. The fire does two things at once. It **proves** the gold — it shows what is truly precious. And it **separates** — it burns the dross away from the pure.

So at its root, *fitnah* is **the fire that tests by burning.** A revealing fire. A fire that exposes what a thing really is.

And from that single furnace, the word fans out in the Qur'an into a whole spectrum of meanings, and I want you to hold all of them in your hand at once, because *this ayah is using several of them simultaneously*:

- *Fitnah* as **trial and affliction** — the fire that burns you.
- *Fitnah* as **seduction and temptation** — the fire that lures you, the glitter that pulls you off the path.
- *Fitnah* as **persecution** — being put to the fire by other people, tortured for your faith.
- *Fitnah* as **delusion, the corruption of the mind** — where a person's very capacity to see truth gets scrambled.

Now — when the believers say *lā tajʿalnā fitnatan lilladhīna kafarū*, "do not make us a fitnah **for those who disbelieve**" — which fire are they afraid of becoming?

Here is where I want to bring you the classical commentators, and I want to bring them not as a menu of competing options but as **four men standing around the same fire, each describing what he sees from his angle.** Because the early scholars did not disagree here so much as they *circled* a single terrifying possibility.

**Al-Zajjāj and the reading carried by al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn** see it this way: *Do not give our enemies victory over us, do not let them prevail and dominate us — because if You do, they will look at their triumph and our defeat and conclude: "We must be the ones on the truth. Look — God is with us, not them." And so our defeat becomes their delusion. They are seduced, by our suffering, deeper into their disbelief.*

Sit with that. The fear is: *my visible defeat will be read as a verdict from heaven — against the truth.*

**Mujāhid**, as al-Baghawī reports, sees a second face of the same fire: *Do not punish us at their hands, nor with a punishment from Yourself — because then they will say, "If these people were really on the truth, this calamity would never have struck them."*

Same furnace, slightly different angle. Here the believer's *affliction itself* — sickness, loss, catastrophe — becomes the proof-text the disbeliever uses to dismiss the entire religion. "Your God let this happen to you? Then your God is nothing."

**Al-Muyassar** gathers a third strand: *do not give the disbelievers power over us such that they torture us and try to turn us back from our religion* — fitnah as persecution, the fire of the rack and the whip, used to break faith.

And **al-Qurṭubī** lays the two main readings side by side openly — *do not let them prevail so they imagine they are upon the truth*, and *do not give them power to persecute us and torment us* — preserving the breadth rather than collapsing it.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what all four are circling? Notice the center they share.

In every reading, the believers' great fear is not the pain. **The pain is assumed.** They are already being persecuted; that is the world of this ayah. What they are afraid of is something the pain might *produce in someone else's soul* — that their broken bodies, their defeat, their suffering, will be **misread as a sign**, and that misreading will harden another human being in his rejection of God forever.

Here is what is remarkable, and I need you to really receive it. **They are afraid of being used as evidence against God.**

Their prayer is: *Lord, whatever You do with us — do not let our story become an argument for disbelief. Do not let our defeat be the verse the deniers recite. Do not let what happens to us push a single soul further from You.*

That is the most selfless fear in the human repertoire. Stay with it. We will come back and live inside it.

### Element Three: الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ — why *these two* names close the prayer

Now look at how the ayah ends. After "and forgive us, our Lord," it seals with two of the names of Allah: **al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm** — the All-Mighty, the All-Wise.

Here is a principle of Qur'anic reading I want to hand you, because it will change how you read every ayah for the rest of your life: **the names of Allah at the end of an ayah are never decoration. They are the answer to the very thing the ayah was afraid of.** The closing names are the divine response folded into the prayer itself.

So ask: what was the fear in this prayer? Let's name it precisely. The fear was twofold.

The first fear: *that the enemy might prevail* — that the believers would be crushed, dominated, defeated, and that this defeat would be read as God's verdict.

What name answers that fear? **al-ʿAzīz.**

Let me open this name. *ʿIzz*, from the root *ʿ-z-z* (ع-ز-ز), is not merely "strength." It is the kind of might that **cannot be overcome, cannot be reached, cannot be encroached upon.** The Arabs used it for land so hard the plough could not break it. *al-ʿAzīz* is the One whom nothing can defeat, the One who is never overpowered. *al-ʿAzīz alladhī lā yughālab* — "the Mighty whom none can overmaster," as al-Muyassar glosses it precisely here.

Do you feel the answer landing? The believers' fear is *we will be overpowered, and that defeat will dishonor the truth.* And the ayah answers: **you are calling on al-ʿAzīz — the One who is never, ever overpowered.** Your enemy's apparent victory is on loan. The final ʿizzah, the final un-overcome-able might, belongs to your Lord and to no one else. *To Allah belongs all ʿizzah, and to His Messenger, and to the believers* — but they don't know it (63:8). You are not on the losing side. You cannot be, because your Lord is *al-ʿAzīz*.

But there is a second fear, subtler and harder. What about the believer who **is** struck down? The one who *is* tortured, who *does* lose everything, who dies in the dungeon? What about Mujāhid's reading — the calamity that actually falls? If al-ʿAzīz means "never overpowered," how do we make sense of the martyr's blood?

That is exactly why the second name is there. **al-Ḥakīm.** The All-Wise. *Ḥakīm in His sayings and His doings*, as al-Muyassar says.

[PAUSE]

Here is the staggering precision of pairing these two. Might alone — *ʿizzah* alone — would say: *the truth always visibly wins.* But that is not how the world is. Believers *do* suffer. Ibrahim *was* thrown into the fire. So might, by itself, would be a promise that breaks against reality.

But might **wedded to wisdom** says something far deeper: *Yes, I am never overpowered — AND when I permit the believer to be struck, that permission is not a failure of My might. It is an act of My wisdom.* The fire of Ibrahim did not burn him — that was *ʿizzah*. But the fact that he was *placed in it at all* — that was *ḥikmah*. There was a wisdom in the trial that the watching world could not see.

So the two names together are a complete answer to a fear that one name could not answer. *al-ʿAzīz* says: **your enemy will not have the final word.** *al-Ḥakīm* says: **and whatever I allow to touch you in the meantime is woven with a wisdom you will one day thank Me for.**

And notice the emphasis the ayah builds before naming them: *innaka **anta** al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* — "Indeed You — **You alone** — are the Mighty, the Wise." That word *anta*, "You," is grammatically unnecessary; the sentence is complete without it. It is there for restriction, for the heart. *It is You, and not them. The might they seem to have is a costume. The real ʿizzah, the real ḥikmah — that is Yours alone.*

### The bridge

So look at what these three constructions have shown us. **Tajʿalnā** — the verb of being turned into someone else's test. **Fitnah** — the revealing fire the believers fear becoming, not for themselves but for the souls of their enemies. And **al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm** — the two names that answer the two fears: *you will not finally be overpowered, and what you suffer in the meantime is wisdom, not abandonment.*

That is the architecture of the words.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about language — but about reality itself. About your life. Because you, right now, in your office and your family and your group chat, are doing the very thing this ayah is about, and you may not have noticed.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Your life is being read as evidence — for or against God

Let me start where you actually live.

You have a coworker, or a cousin, or an old friend who left the faith, who watches you. You are, to them, an experiment. Whether you intend it or not, your life is a **data point** in their private inquiry into whether God is real and whether this religion is true. When you are generous, patient, unshaken — they file it under "maybe there's something to this." When you are bitter, hypocritical, cruel, or when life crushes you and you collapse — they file it under "see, it's nothing."

You are being **read.** You are an exhibit. You did not sign up for it, but you are evidence.

Now — that is the *easy* version of what this ayah is about. The comfortable version, where we tell ourselves: *be a good example, mind your manners, represent well.* And that's true as far as it goes.

But the believers in this ayah are not praying about their manners. Watch how much deeper the fire goes.

They are praying about the things **they cannot control.** They are not saying "help us behave well." They are saying "do not let what *happens* to us — our defeat, our suffering, things entirely outside our hands — become the proof that breaks someone's faith." The fear is not about their *conduct*. It is about their *fate*.

[PAUSE]

And this is where the ayah stops being comfortable and becomes almost unbearable. Because it means: **even your suffering is public.** Even your defeat is being read. The job you lost, the illness that took you, the marriage that fell apart, the prayer that seemed unanswered for twenty years — somewhere there is a person watching, and your calamity is being entered as evidence in the case against your Lord. *"If God loved them, why did this happen?"*

Here is the movement I want you to make with me. The natural response to suffering is *"Lord, why me?"* — a prayer that curls inward, around your own wound. This ayah models a completely different reflex. It takes the very same suffering and turns it **outward**: *"Lord, whatever this is — do not let it become a reason for someone else to abandon You."*

Do you feel the inversion? The believer in the fire is not praying for the fire to stop. He is praying that the fire not be **misread.** That his pain not be weaponized against the truth. That his story, when it is told, not be told as an argument for despair.

There is a hadith that lives in the same atmosphere — the believer is *ʿajab*, a wonder: when good touches him he is grateful and it is good for him, and when harm touches him he is patient and it is good for him. Why is it "good for him" in *both* cases? Partly because in both cases, his response **testifies**. His gratitude testifies that the giver is real; his patience testifies that the relationship survives the loss. Either way, his life is evidence *for*, not *against*.

So here is what changes about tomorrow. When the next hardship comes — and it will — you have two prayers available. The small prayer: *Lord, take this away.* And the large prayer, the prayer of Ibrahim: *Lord, if You do not take it away, then do not let it become a fitnah. Do not let my struggle push anyone further from You. Let even this — especially this — be read as evidence that You are still here.*

That is a prayer that turns your private pain into public daʿwah. It is the most dignified thing a suffering person can do.

### Theme Two: Why "forgive us" belongs in the middle of this prayer

Now I want to take you to something that should stop you cold, and almost nobody notices it.

Look at the structure of the prayer again. *"Do not make us a trial for those who disbelieve"* — and then, immediately, with no transition — *"and forgive us, our Lord."*

**Waghfir lanā.** And forgive us.

Why? Stop and feel how strange this is. These are people standing on the moral high ground. They are the *persecuted*. They are the *righteous*. They have just been named, in the previous ayah, as followers of *the excellent example*, the *uswa hasana* of Ibrahim. They are the victims, not the criminals.

And in the middle of their noble, selfless, God-centered prayer — they suddenly ask for **forgiveness.** For themselves. Why would the oppressed, in the hour of their oppression, interrupt to confess their own sins?

Let me offer you the connection, because I believe the *wāw* — the "and" — joining these two phrases is one of the most instructive conjunctions in the Qur'an.

[PAUSE]

Begin here: **the believers understand that they might *deserve* to become a fitnah.** Think it through. If a believer is defeated, if calamity strikes him and the disbelievers sneer "see, their God abandoned them" — *why* might that calamity have come? In the logic these very believers are working with, suffering is sometimes the consequence of one's own sins. So the prayer *"do not make us a fitnah"* has a silent partner: *and we know that if we are made into a fitnah, it may be because of what our own hands have earned.* Hence — **immediately** — *"and forgive us."*

Do you see the humility of it? They are not saying *"we are pure, so protect us."* They are saying: *"Do not let our defeat dishonor Your truth — and we recognize that the surest way to invite such defeat is our own sin, so cleanse us of it.* **Forgive us, so that there is nothing in us left for the fire to catch.**"

This is the opposite of how the human ego works under pressure. Under pressure, the ego *defends* itself. It says *I am the victim, I am right, they are wrong.* And the believers *are* the victims and *are* right. But their prayer does not stop to plead its own innocence. It reaches instead for forgiveness. It assumes, even in the hour of being wronged, that the first place to look is *inward.*

There is a second layer, and it is gentler. To ask forgiveness is to **align yourself with mercy.** If you fear being made an instrument of someone's deeper disbelief, the surest protection is to be a vessel of mercy rather than a vessel of judgment. The forgiven heart is soft. And a soft, forgiven, humble believer is far less likely to become a stumbling block, far more likely to become a doorway. *Waghfir lanā* is not a detour in this prayer. It is the **mechanism.** Forgive us — and in that forgiveness, make us the kind of people whose lives draw others *toward* You rather than driving them away.

So what does this change about tomorrow? It is this: stop waiting to feel innocent before you ask forgiveness. The most righteous people in this passage — the people held up by Allah Himself as the example — did not separate "we are being wronged" from "forgive us." They held both in the same breath. The next time you are wronged, the next time you stand on solid moral ground and feel the heat of injustice, let your prayer do what theirs did. Defend the truth — *and in the same sentence*, ask to be cleaned. Both. Always both.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing it together

Now stand back and see the whole shape.

The linguistic journey gave us three things. The verb **tajʿalnā** — "do not *turn us into*" — a grammar that has already forgotten itself and is thinking only of someone else. The word **fitnah** — the revealing fire — which the believers fear becoming not *for* themselves but *for the souls of their enemies.* And the two closing names, **al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm** — the answer that says *you will not be finally overpowered, and what touches you in the meantime is wisdom.*

The thematic journey gave us two things. That **your life — including your suffering — is being read as evidence** for or against God, and the noble move is to pray that even your pain testify *for* Him. And that **"forgive us" sits in the middle of this prayer** because the believers know the surest protection from becoming a fitnah is a forgiven, softened, humbled heart.

And now watch them fuse — because they are one insight seen from two directions.

The whole ayah is the prayer of a soul that has **stopped being the center of its own concern.** Grammatically, it forgot itself in *tajʿalnā*. Spiritually, it fears for its enemies more than its own skin. Morally, it reaches for its own forgiveness before pleading its own innocence. And theologically, it rests the entire weight of its fear on two names of God rather than on any strength of its own. From every angle — language, theme, structure — this is the prayer of a person who is no longer protecting *themselves*. They are protecting **the truth**, **the souls of others**, and **the honor of their Lord** — and trusting their own fate entirely to *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm*.

That is what it means to follow the *uswa hasana* of Ibrahim. Not merely to walk into the fire — but to walk in praying *that the fire not become an argument against the God who let me enter it.*

### Questions to carry

- Whose faith is quietly being shaped by watching how you handle your hardest days? Have you ever once prayed *for them* in the middle of your own difficulty?
- When you suffer, is your instinct to ask *"why me?"* — or *"Lord, do not let this become a reason for anyone to doubt You"*? What would it take to make the second prayer your reflex?
- The most righteous people in this passage asked forgiveness in the same breath as defending the truth. When was the last time you asked forgiveness while you were *in the right*?
- Is there a defeat in your life right now that you are reading as God's verdict against you — when it may be His *wisdom* (ḥikmah) that you cannot yet see?
- Do you live as though *ʿizzah* — the might that is never overpowered — belongs to your circumstances, or to your Lord?

### One-sentence distillation

The deepest prayer of the persecuted believer is not "save me from the fire," but "do not let what happens to me become a fire that burns someone else's faith — and forgive me, so that I am fit to be Your evidence, not Your scandal."

### The closing invitation

I am not going to tell you to be stronger. This ayah does not ask for strength. It asks for a turning — a turning of the heart's whole orientation, away from *what will become of me* and toward *what will my life prove about my Lord.* You can begin that turn with a single sentence, the next time something breaks. Let your first reflex not be the inward curl of "why me," but the outward reach of *Rabbanā, lā tajʿalnā fitnatan* — *do not make me a trial for anyone, and forgive me.* Say it once and mean it, and you will have prayed the prayer of Ibrahim.

### A brief du'a

*Rabbanā,* do not turn our lives, our struggles, or our defeats into evidence against You in the eyes of any soul that watches us. Where we are weak, do not let our weakness dishonor Your truth; where we suffer, let even our suffering testify that You are near. And forgive us, our Lord — soften us, cleanse us, make us doorways and not walls — for You, You alone, are *al-ʿAzīz*, whom none can overpower, and *al-Ḥakīm*, whose every decree is wisdom. Āmīn.
