# The Wish Beneath the Smile

## Introduction

Let me give you the ayah first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single meaning.

> **إِن يَثْقَفُوكُمْ يَكُونُوا۟ لَكُمْ أَعْدَآءً وَيَبْسُطُوٓا۟ إِلَيْكُمْ أَيْدِيَهُمْ وَأَلْسِنَتَهُم بِٱلسُّوٓءِ وَوَدُّوا۟ لَوْ تَكْفُرُونَ**

*In yathqafūkum yakūnū lakum aʿdāʾan wa yabsuṭū ilaykum aydiyahum wa alsinatahum bis-sūʾi wa waddū law takfurūn.*

> "If they gain the upper hand over you, they will be enemies to you, and they will stretch out against you their hands and their tongues with evil — and they wish that you would disbelieve."

### Let's rebuild the world this ayah landed in

To feel this verse, you have to feel a very specific kind of ache — the ache of a person who still loves people who are no longer safe to love.

Picture the believers in Madinah. Many of them did not come from Madinah. They were *Muhājirūn* — migrants. They had been driven out of Makkah. Out of the only city they had ever known. Out of the homes their grandfathers built. And they did not leave because of a war over land or money or tribe. The ayah right before this one tells us exactly why they were expelled: *"they drove out the Messenger and yourselves because you believe in Allah, your Lord."* Their only crime was *lā ilāha illā Allāh.*

Now — here is the human part. Here is what makes this ayah necessary in the first place.

Back in Makkah, those believers still had fathers. Uncles. Cousins. Childhood friends. Business partners. Men they had laughed with for thirty years. And the heart does not switch off thirty years of attachment the way you switch off a lamp. So some of the believers — sincere believers, not hypocrites — felt a quiet pull to stay soft toward Makkah. To send a friendly word. To keep the door of affection open. *Surely* these are still my people. *Surely* the old bond counts for something.

The classical commentators connect this passage to the story of a sincere companion who, out of exactly this kind of human softness toward relatives still in Makkah, secretly sent word to Quraysh — not out of disbelief, but out of that lingering attachment. Allah exposed the letter. And then Allah revealed an entire opening to this surah to address not the act, but the *feeling underneath it.*

[PAUSE]

So this ayah is not shouted at enemies. It is whispered to people who are still *in love with the wrong door.*

### The Hook

Here is what should unsettle you before we even begin.

In the ayah just before this one, Allah describes these believers offering the disbelievers something with a very specific name: **mawadda** — tender affection. The believers were *extending* affection.

And in *this* ayah, the disbelievers are also described as *extending* something, and *wishing* something. Allah uses a word for their wish that comes from **the exact same root** as the affection the believers were offering.

The believers reach out with one thing. The enemies reach back with a word built from the same three letters — pointed in the opposite direction.

What is that word? And what does it mean that Allah chose it? Stay with me. Because by the end, this single root is going to reorganize how you think about every one-sided relationship you have ever been in.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Yathqafūkum* — the verb that hides a confession

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we open the word.

In Arabic, a conditional sentence — an "if/then" — does something subtle that we often skip over in English. The "if" does not only describe the future. It quietly *confesses something about the present.* When I say to you, "If I ever get the chance, I'll tell you what I really think," I have just told you that I am *already* holding back what I really think. The condition is about the future. The confession is about *right now.*

Hold onto that. We will need it.

Now — the word.

> **إِن يَثْقَفُوكُمْ** — *in yathqafūkum*

Most translations soften this to "if they meet you" or "if they overcome you." But the root here — **ث-ق-ف** — is far more textured than that.

Al-Qurtubī opens it for us. He says *yathqafūkum* means *"yulqūkum wa yuṣādifūkum"* — to come upon you, to catch you. And then he gives the image that unlocks everything. He says it comes from **al-muthāqafa** — and *muthāqafa* is a word from the world of swordplay. It means *"ṭalab muṣādafat al-ghirra fī al-musāyafa"* — **the seeking of the unguarded moment in a sword fight.**

[PAUSE]

Feel that.

This is not a neutral "if they happen to meet you." This is the language of a duel. It is the moment in combat when your opponent is circling, watching, waiting — not for friendship — but for the half-second your guard drops. *Al-ghirra.* The opening. The lapse.

Al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn add the other layer: *yathqafūkum* means *"yaẓfarū bikum"* — **if they get the upper hand over you, if they gain power over you.**

So put the two lexical senses together, the way the classical scholars lay them side by side, and you get a single, chilling picture. The word describes someone who is *watching for your unguarded moment in order to seize power over you.*

Now return to what we said about conditionals. *"If they gain the upper hand over you..."*

Notice what that "if" silently confesses about the present.

It tells you that **right now, in this moment, the only thing standing between you and their enmity is that they do not yet have the power to act on it.** The hostility is not absent. The hostility is *waiting.* The smile you are receiving from them today is not the absence of the sword. It is the sword, sheathed, because the arena is not yet in their favor.

This is what makes the believers' soft-heartedness so dangerous. They were reading the *current calm* as *current safety.* And Allah, in one verb, says: you are mistaking a paused duel for peace.

What does this one word reveal? **That present friendliness is not proof of a friendly heart — it can simply be proof that the moment has not yet arrived.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Yabsuṭū* — the hand and the tongue under one verb

Let's open the next movement.

> **وَيَبْسُطُوٓا۟ إِلَيْكُمْ أَيْدِيَهُمْ وَأَلْسِنَتَهُم بِٱلسُّوٓءِ**

> "And they will *stretch out* toward you their hands and their tongues with evil."

The verb is **يَبْسُطُوا** — *yabsuṭū*, from the root **ب-س-ط**. And I want you to notice the verb itself before its objects, because the verb is doing quiet work.

*Basaṭa* means to spread open, to stretch out, to extend fully. It is, in its ordinary life, a *generous* word. You *basaṭa* your hand to give a gift. The Quran elsewhere speaks of Allah *yabsuṭ ar-rizq* — He *spreads out* provision. *Bast* is openness, expansion, the opposite of clenching.

So here is the first strange beauty of the word choice. Allah takes a verb whose natural home is *generosity and openness* — and turns it toward harm. They will "open up" toward you — but what they pour out of that openness is *as-sūʾ*, evil. It is the posture of giving, emptied of any good. An open hand that, instead of a gift, carries a blow.

But now look at what this single verb governs. Watch carefully, because Arabic lets you do something here that English fights against.

One verb — *yabsuṭū* — stretches out **two** things at once:

- *aydiyahum* — **their hands**
- *alsinatahum* — **their tongues**

Al-Qurtubī, al-Baghawī, al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar — they are unanimous on the meaning. The hands, they say, extend *"bil-ḍarb wal-qatl"* — with striking and killing. The tongues extend *"bis-sabb wash-shatm"* — with cursing and insult.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what Allah has done here.

He has placed **the tongue and the hand under the exact same verb.** The same *stretching out.* The same *reaching toward you with intent to harm.* Grammatically, in the eyes of this ayah, the insult and the assault are *the same motion.* The curse and the blow come out of the same extended limb.

We do not think this way. We have a whole proverb built to deny it — *"sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me."* We rank the hand as real harm and the tongue as lesser harm. "They were *just* words."

Allah does not grant the distinction. In this ayah, the tongue is not a softer enemy than the hand. It is *the second blade of the same attacker.* The slander campaign and the physical assault are listed in one breath, driven by one verb, aimed by one will.

And notice — *as-sūʾ*, "evil," sits at the end and colors *both.* It is not that the hands do evil and the tongues do something milder. The evil saturates both extensions equally.

What does this reveal? **That the one who would strike you with his hand if he could, and the one who strikes you with his tongue because that is all he can yet reach — these are not two different kinds of people. In the sight of Allah, they are one extended hostility, using whichever limb the moment allows.**

And quietly, this loops back to Element One. *If* they had the upper hand, the hands would come. They do not have it yet — so for now, the tongues come. The duel is paused at the level of the hand. It is already live at the level of the tongue.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Waddū* — the tense that gives the whole game away

Now we reach the word I promised you at the start. And this is where the ayah stops describing behavior and reaches all the way into the *heart.*

> **وَوَدُّوا۟ لَوْ تَكْفُرُونَ**

> "And they *wish* that you would disbelieve."

The word is **وَدُّوا** — *waddū*, from the root **و-د-د.**

I need to teach you one piece of Arabic grammar here, and then everything will detonate.

Look at the verbs in this ayah. Almost all of them are in what Arabic calls the *imperfect* — the open, ongoing, not-yet-completed tense:

- *yathqafū* — "if they **gain** the upper hand" — not yet happened
- *yakūnū* — "they **would be**" — conditional, future
- *yabsuṭū* — "they **would stretch out**" — conditional, future

These are all hanging on the "if." They are *contingent.* They are *waiting for a condition* — the condition of power. *If* the opening comes, *then* these things follow.

But *waddū* — "they wished" — is in the **perfect** tense. The completed tense. The tense of something that has *already happened* and is *settled.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it?

Allah switches the grammar at the exact moment He moves from their *actions* to their *desire.* The actions are conditional and future — *they depend on getting power.* But the wish for your disbelief is stated as an **accomplished, completed fact.** It is not waiting for any opening. It is not contingent on any duel. It is already there, fully formed, in the past tense of the heart.

In other words: their *hands* may never reach you. The condition for the violence might never be met. You might live your whole life and they never once "gain the upper hand." But do not let that absence of action fool you — because **the wish does not wait for the opportunity.** The hostility you can *see* is conditional. The hostility you *cannot* see is already complete.

Al-Muyassar captures it with a haunting phrase. He says they wish for your disbelief *"ʿalā kulli ḥāl"* — **in every case, under every circumstance.** The striking might or might not come. But this — *this* they have wished for already, unconditionally, with no "if" attached.

And now — the word itself. *Waddū.* The root **و-د-د.**

Here is the unveiling I promised you in the introduction.

In the ayah immediately before this one — 60:1 — Allah describes what the believers were doing toward these very people. He says: *tulqūna ilayhim bil-**mawadda*** — "you extend to them **affection**." And *mawadda* — that warm, tender affection — comes from the **exact same root: و-د-د.**

[PAUSE — let this land]

So look at what Allah has constructed across these two ayahs.

The believer reaches toward the enemy with **wadd** — affection. *Mawadda.* Tenderness. An open heart.

And the enemy reaches back with **wadd** — but their *wadd*, their deepest wish, the thing they have *already* completed in their hearts, is *law takfurūn* — **"that you would abandon your faith."**

Same root. Opposite direction.

Your *wudd* toward them is: *may you be well, may the old bond hold, may there be peace between us.*

Their *wudd* toward you is: *may you lose the one thing that makes you who you are. May you fall out of your faith. May you become like us in our disbelief.*

The believer is offering love. And the enemy, using the very same verb of the heart, is "loving" — *wishing for, longing for* — the believer's spiritual destruction.

This is the cruelest mirror in the surah. It is not that the affection is unreturned. It is that the affection is returned *with its meaning inverted.* You wish them life; they wish you death of the soul. And Allah lets you hear it in a single shared root, so that you can never again pretend the two reaches are the same reach.

What does *waddū* reveal? **That the truest measure of where someone stands toward you is not what they do — actions wait for opportunity — but what they wish. And these hearts had already, completely, with no condition attached, wished you out of your faith.**

[PAUSE — major]

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have built, stone by stone.

*Yathqafūkum* told us the hostility is **waiting** — paused only by a lack of power.

*Yabsuṭū* told us the hostility is **already moving** — through the tongue, since the hand cannot yet reach.

*Waddū* told us the hostility is **already complete** — finished, settled, in the heart, regardless of whether the hand or even the tongue ever acts.

The ayah walks you from the *potential* harm, to the *present* harm, to the *innermost* harm — and at the deepest layer, it hands you a word that is the dark twin of your own affection.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself, and about a kind of pain almost every one of us has known.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The one-sided door

Let me start where you already live.

Almost everyone listening to this has, at some point, kept a door open that the other person had already closed.

You know the shape of it. There is a friend who stopped showing up — but you keep texting first. A family member whose every word about you carries a quiet contempt — but you keep explaining yourself, keep softening, keep hoping this will be the visit where it finally turns warm. An ex, a former partner, a person who hurt you — and yet some part of you still curates your life for their approval, still wonders what they would think, still leaves a lamp on in a house they have no intention of returning to.

There is a specific exhaustion to this. It is the exhaustion of being the only one *reaching.* Of *basaṭa* — stretching out your hand — into a room where no hand comes back. And the reason it exhausts us is not the rejection itself. It is the **disbelief.** We refuse to update our picture. We keep reading the past warmth as present reality. We keep treating a paused relationship as a living one.

This is precisely the spiritual condition the believers were in. They were *basaṭa*-ing affection toward Makkah. And Allah, with the tenderness of a father who will not let his child keep walking into the same wall, did not say *"stop loving them, you fool."* He did something gentler and far more surgical. He simply **told them the truth about the other side of the door.**

He did not command the feeling away. He *informed the feeling.* He said: here is what is actually in those hearts. *Waddū law takfurūn.* Now — *with this knowledge* — decide where to place your affection.

[PAUSE]

And here is the movement that should change something in you.

We usually think the problem with one-sided attachment is that it *hurts us.* It does. But Allah names a deeper problem. Look again at what they wish for: *law takfurūn* — that you would **leave your faith.**

The danger of the one-sided door is not only the heartbreak. It is that **prolonged affection toward someone who wants you to be like them slowly bends you toward becoming like them.** You cannot keep your heart soft and open and yearning toward a person whose deepest wish is your spiritual undoing, and expect that yearning to leave you untouched. Affection is not neutral. Affection is *directional.* It pulls you toward the thing you love. And if the thing you love is pulling you toward the cliff, your love is doing the cliff's work for it.

This is why Allah's concern in this surah is never merely social — *don't trust these people.* It is always, underneath, spiritual — *guard the thing they are trying to take from you.* The whole surah is named **al-Mumtaḥana**, "the woman examined" — it is a surah about *testing what loyalties are real.* And the test is not "do you still feel the old warmth?" The test is "when you learn what the other heart truly wishes, where do you let your own heart go?"

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. Take the relationship that drains you — the one where you are always the one reaching. Do not ask the soft question, *"do they still care about me?"* Ask the clear question this ayah teaches you to ask: **"What do they wish for me? Not what do they do — what do they *want*?"** Do they wish for your growth, your faith, your wellbeing, your nearness to Allah? Or does some part of them wish for your smallness, your compromise, your drift? You will usually already know the answer. The ayah is not asking you to become cold. It is asking you to *stop spending your warmth on a wish that is aimed at your ruin* — and to redirect that same warmth toward the people, and the One, who actually wish you well.

### Theme Two: Reading the wish, not the smile

Let me take you into the second depth, and again I'll start where you live.

We are, all of us, far too easily governed by *surfaces.*

Someone is polite to us, so we relax. Someone smiles, so we trust. Someone has not yet harmed us, so we assume they never will. We are pattern-readers, and the pattern we most readily read is *behavior in the present moment.* It feels like solid data. It feels like proof.

But think about how often that data has betrayed you. Think about the person who was perfectly pleasant right up until the moment they weren't. The colleague who smiled in every meeting and undercut you in every one you weren't in. The relationship that felt fine — *fine* — until the day it suddenly, catastrophically, wasn't, and looking back you realized the warmth was never warmth at all. It was just the absence of a trigger. The duel had simply not yet found its *ghirra* — its opening.

This is the human blindness Allah is treating. We mistake *the calm of the unguarded moment* for *peace.* We read *yathqafūkum* — the paused duel — as friendship.

And so Allah does something for us that we struggle to do for ourselves. He teaches us to read past the behavior, down to **the wish.**

[PAUSE]

Now I have to hold a paradox here without rushing to flatten it, because this ayah is dangerous if you take only half of it.

The lesson is *not*: become suspicious of everyone. The lesson is not paranoia. A heart that reads malice into every smile is not a discerning heart — it is a sick one, and it will cut itself off from the very people Allah commands it to love. In fact — and this is crucial — just five ayahs later in this same surah, Allah says: *"Perhaps Allah will place between you and those you have treated as enemies, affection — **mawadda**."* The *same root again.* Allah Himself promises that hostility can turn to warmth. So this is not a doctrine of permanent enemies.

So how do we hold both? How do we read the wish without poisoning the heart?

Here is the resolution, and it is precise. Allah is not telling you to *guess* at people's hidden wishes and condemn them on suspicion. That would be the very *ẓann* — assumption — He forbids elsewhere. He is telling you something narrower and far more reliable: **when a heart's wish has actually been revealed — when someone has shown you, through their consistent posture, that they wish your faith and your good undone — believe them.** Believe the revealed wish over the pleasant surface. The believers in this ayah were not being asked to *invent* Makkah's hostility out of suspicion. Makkah had *expelled them for their faith.* The wish had already been demonstrated in the most concrete way imaginable. The error was not failing to guess it — the error was *continuing to discount it* after it had been proven, because the old affection wanted to keep believing in a Makkah that no longer existed.

[PAUSE]

So the discipline this theme asks of you is a strange balance of *softness and clarity.*

Soft enough that you never decide a heart is your permanent enemy — because the God who wrote *waddū law takfurūn* in ayah 2 wrote *ʿasā Allāhu an yajʿala* — "perhaps Allah will make affection" — in ayah 7. No heart is sealed shut to you forever. Keep that door of hope open.

But clear enough that you stop *overriding revealed reality with nostalgic hope.* When someone has shown you, consistently and unmistakably, what they wish for you — stop arguing with the evidence. Stop spending your trust on a version of them that the facts no longer support.

What does this change about tomorrow? When you are deciding how much of your heart, your time, your vulnerability to give a person, run them through both filters. First: *has their wish toward me actually been revealed, or am I just assuming?* If you are only assuming — stop, soften, give the benefit of the doubt; that is the command. But if it *has* been revealed, clearly and repeatedly — then second: *let your trust follow the revealed wish, not the pleasant surface.* Be the kind of person who is warm enough to hope for anyone's heart to turn, and clear enough never to be the believer who keeps mailing affection to a city that expelled them.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of this reflection turn out to be one insight approached from two sides.

The **linguistic journey** walked us down through three layers of hostility: the *waiting* hostility of *yathqafūkum*, the *moving* hostility of *yabsuṭū*, and the *completed, innermost* hostility of *waddū* — and at the deepest point, it handed us a word that was the dark twin of the believers' own *mawadda.*

The **thematic depths** took that same descent and turned it into a mirror for our own lives: the one-sided door we keep open, and the smile we keep mistaking for safety.

And they meet in a single sentence. The Quran is teaching us to **locate the truth of a relationship not at the surface where the behavior lives, but at the depth where the wish lives** — and to grasp that affection, *wadd*, is the most directional force in the human heart. It either pulls you toward someone's wish for your good, or it pulls you toward someone's wish for your ruin. There is no neutral affection. The only question is *which wadd you are walking toward.*

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and see what Allah built across two ayahs.

Ayah 1: *the believers extend* **mawadda** *(و-د-د) toward the enemy.*
Ayah 2: *the enemy has already completed their* **wadd** *(و-د-د) — that you leave your faith.*

The surah opens by placing the **same root** in both hands — yours, reaching out in affection; theirs, reaching back in a wish for your destruction. The whole opening of al-Mumtaḥana is structured around that one inverted word. And it is no accident that the surah is *named* for examination — because the entire passage is built to make you *examine* the direction of a single root of the heart. That symmetry is the proof. The ayah does not merely *tell* you the affection is misplaced. It *shows* you, by handing the same word to two opposite wishes.

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. Where in my life am I the only one still reaching through a door the other side has quietly closed?
2. Am I reading someone's present calm as *peace* — when it may only be the unguarded pause before the duel?
3. For the people I love most — and for myself — do I actually know what I *wish* for them? Their good and their faith? Or something smaller?
4. Have I confused *being warm* with *being undiscerning* — or confused *being discerning* with *being cold and suspicious*?
5. Is there a heart I have sealed shut as a permanent enemy, when Allah Himself left that door open in ayah 7?

### The one-sentence distillation

**Stop measuring people by what they do in the calm of the moment, and start hearing what they have already wished in the silence of the heart — because affection always walks you toward the wish it loves.**

### The closing invitation

This ayah is not an invitation to become hard. It is an invitation to become *clear* — and to let your softness finally land where it is safe. To stop pouring your *wadd* into wishes aimed at your ruin, and to redirect that same precious warmth toward the One whose wish for you is nothing but guidance, and toward the people who genuinely wish you near to Him. Let your heart stay open. Just let it open *toward the right door.*

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You who know what every heart has already wished — let my affection rest only where it is safe, and turn it away from every wish aimed at my faith. Make me warm enough to hope for any heart to soften, and clear enough never to spend my trust on a reach that wants my ruin. And place between us and those we have known as enemies a true *mawadda*, as only You can. Āmīn.
