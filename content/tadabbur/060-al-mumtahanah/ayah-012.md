# The Trade You Cannot Lose

## The Women's Pledge and the Forgiveness Built Into the Covenant

---

### The Ayah

> [ayah:60:12] يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ إِذَا جَآءَكَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتُ يُبَايِعْنَكَ عَلَىٰٓ أَن لَّا يُشْرِكْنَ بِٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا وَلَا يَسْرِقْنَ وَلَا يَزْنِينَ وَلَا يَقْتُلْنَ أَوْلَـٰدَهُنَّ وَلَا يَأْتِينَ بِبُهْتَـٰنٍ يَفْتَرِينَهُۥ بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِنَّ وَأَرْجُلِهِنَّ وَلَا يَعْصِينَكَ فِى مَعْرُوفٍ ۙ فَبَايِعْهُنَّ وَٱسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُنَّ ٱللَّهَ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuha-n-nabiyyu idhā jā'aka-l-mu'minātu yubāyiʿnaka ʿalā an lā yushrikna bi-llāhi shay'an wa-lā yasriqna wa-lā yaznīna wa-lā yaqtulna awlādahunna wa-lā ya'tīna bi-buhtānin yaftarīnahu bayna aydīhinna wa-arjulihinna wa-lā yaʿṣīnaka fī maʿrūfin fa-bāyiʿhunna wa-staghfir lahunna-llāha inna-llāha ghafūrun raḥīm.*

> "O Prophet! When the believing women come to you, pledging to you that they will not associate anything with Allah, and will not steal, and will not commit unlawful intimacy, and will not kill their children, and will not bring a slander they invent between their hands and their feet, and will not disobey you in what is right — then accept their pledge, and ask Allah to forgive them. Indeed, Allah is Most Forgiving, Most Merciful."

## INTRODUCTION: The Day the City Surrendered and the Souls Were Set Free

Let me take you to a specific morning.

Mecca has fallen. After years of exile, after the boycott in the valley where they ate leaves to survive, after Uhud and the Trench, after a brother turned against a brother and a tribe turned against the most truthful man among them — the city that drove the Prophet ﷺ out has now opened its gates to him. And he enters not as a conqueror with a drawn sword, but with his head bowed so low in humility, the tradition tells us, that it nearly touched the back of his camel.

There is no massacre. There is no revenge. There is the most stunning sentence a victorious man has ever spoken to those who hunted him: *"Go — you are free."*

And then something happens that I want you to picture carefully.

The men come first to pledge. They line up. The Prophet ﷺ sits, and one by one, they place their commitment in his hand. This is the *bayʿa* — the pledge of allegiance.

Then the women come.

Among them — and the classical commentators preserve this scene vividly — is a woman in a veil, hiding her face. Her name is Hind bint ʿUtbah. If that name means nothing to you, let me tell you who she is. At the Battle of Uhud, when the Prophet's beloved uncle Hamza was killed, it was Hind who is reported to have mutilated his body. This woman, who had stood as the very symbol of the enmity against him, is now standing in the line — *veiled, disguised, hoping he will not recognize her* — waiting to enter his covenant.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment. The door of this pledge is so wide that *she* is standing in the line.

That is the world this ayah lives in. It is not an abstract list of rules. It is the moment the gate of faith swings open after a war, and Allah turns to His Prophet and gives him the precise terms on which a human soul — a soul that may have done terrible things — is allowed to walk through it.

Now here is the question I want to plant in you, and I am not going to answer it yet. When you make a deal — a real deal, a contract — there are two parties, and each one *gives* something and each one *gets* something. So when a soul comes to give itself to God, what exactly is the soul giving? And here is the stranger question: **what is God giving back?** Because the word Allah chose for this moment is not the word for "promise." It is not the word for "obedience." It is a word taken straight from the marketplace.

Stay with me. Let's open it.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

---

### Element One: *Yubāyiʿnaka* — They Come to Make a Trade With You

#### First, a concept from your own life

Before we touch the Arabic, let me give you something ordinary.

There is a difference between *begging* and *trading*. When you beg, you stand below the other person. You have nothing to offer. You hope for charity, and whatever you receive is a gift you did not earn — and so it leaves a small wound in your dignity. But when you *trade*, you stand across from the other person as a party. You have something in your hand. You place it on the table, they place something on the table, and you shake hands as two who have each given and each received. Trade dignifies. Trade says: *you are not nothing in this exchange. You bring something real.*

Hold that distinction. It is the entire key to this word.

#### The word itself

> يُبَايِعْنَكَ — *yubāyiʿnaka* — "they pledge to you"

Look at the root. It is **ب-ي-ع** — *bāʿa*, *yabīʿu*. And this root, in its plainest, most everyday meaning, means **to sell. To trade. To do business.** *Bayʿ* is the word the Quran uses for an ordinary commercial sale — "Allah has permitted *bayʿ* and forbidden usury" (2:275). It is the word for what happens in a shop.

The same root that runs the marketplace is the root Allah chose for the moment a believer gives herself to God.

[PAUSE]

#### Notice the shape of the verb

Now, here is where the grammar does work that no translation can carry. The verb is not in its simple form. It is in what Arabic grammarians call **Form III** — the pattern *fāʿala* — *bāyaʿa*. And one of the primary functions of this form in Arabic is to signal **reciprocity, mutuality — an action that goes both directions between two parties.**

You can feel this in other Form III words. *Kātaba* doesn't just mean "he wrote" — it means he *corresponded*, wrote back and forth. *Qātala* doesn't mean he killed — it means he *fought*, traded blows. *Sā'ala* — he questioned mutually. The form itself bends a one-way act into a two-way exchange.

So *bāyaʿa* is not "she sold." It is **mubāyaʿa** — a *mutual* transaction. A trade where both hands are full. Both parties bring something. Both parties take something away.

Let that land. Allah did not describe the believing women as *submitting* a request, or *offering* obedience, or *begging* for entry. He described them as entering a **two-way trade**.

#### And then — the proof, in the next breath

Here is what convinces me this is not my imagination reading commerce into the word. Watch what Allah commands the Prophet to do at the end of the ayah:

> فَبَايِعْهُنَّ — *fa-bāyiʿhunna* — "**then trade back with them**"

Same root. Same Form III. The women come to *bāyaʿa* you — so you, O Prophet, **bāyaʿa them back.** The reciprocity is not just implied in the grammar; it is spelled out as a command. They trade with you; you complete the trade with them. The deal is closed by both hands moving.

[PAUSE]

#### So what is actually being traded?

Now stand back and look at the terms of the contract, because Allah lists them right here. What do the women place on the table?

- *that they will not associate anything with Allah*
- *and will not steal*
- *and will not commit unlawful intimacy*
- *and will not kill their children*
- *and will not bring a slander they invent*
- *and will not disobey in what is right*

Look closely. **Every single term is a thing they will *stop doing*.** It is a list of abstentions. Not "they will pray," not "they will fast," not "they will give." It is: *I will hold my hand back from these.* What the soul brings to this trade is, at its floor, **restraint** — the willingness to no longer harm.

And what does the soul receive in return? Read the closing line: *fa-bāyiʿhunna — wa-staghfir lahunna-llāh — inna-llāha ghafūrun raḥīm.* "Accept their trade, and ask Allah to forgive them. Indeed Allah is Most Forgiving, Most Merciful." What is placed on the table from the other side is **forgiveness. Mercy.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see the terms of this trade now? You bring your *refraining from evil* — and you walk away with the *erasure of your past* and the *mercy of the Lord of the worlds.*

This is the trade where everything you give was destroying you anyway, and everything you receive is beyond price. The Quran says it openly elsewhere with the same commercial root family: *"Indeed Allah has purchased (ishtarā) from the believers their selves and their wealth — that for them is the Garden"* (9:111). It is a trade. And it is the only trade in existence where one party gives away poison and receives paradise.

**What this one word reveals:** turning to God is not a humiliation. It is the most dignified, and the most lopsidedly generous, transaction a human being will ever enter.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

### Element Two: *Buhtān* — The Lie That Strikes You Dumb, and the Crime Named Without Naming It

#### A concept first: there are lies, and then there is *this*

Not all falsehood is equal. There is the small lie that smooths an awkward moment. There is the exaggeration. And then there is the lie so brazen, so detached from reality, that when you hear it you are *physically stopped* — your mouth opens and nothing comes out. You are stunned. That category of lie has its own name in Arabic.

#### The word

> بِبُهْتَـٰنٍ — *bi-buhtān* — "with a slander"

The root is **ب-ه-ت** — *bahata*. And the lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, the *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib) tell us its core meaning: **to be struck dumb, to be stupefied, to be left baffled and speechless.** *Buhita* — he was dumbfounded. A *buhtān*, then, is not just a lie. It is a calumny so audacious that it *stuns the very person it is told about.* You hear what they have accused you of, and you are frozen — because there is no foothold of truth in it anywhere to even begin to respond.

Feel the sound of the word, too. *Buh-tān.* The "b" and the "t" are abrupt, plosive — the consonants slam shut. It even *sounds* like the slap of a shocking accusation. The word strikes the way the thing it names strikes.

#### Now — the strange description Allah attaches to it

Here is where this verse becomes extraordinary, and where we must read what Allah *does not say* as carefully as what He does.

> يَفْتَرِينَهُۥ بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِنَّ وَأَرْجُلِهِنَّ — *which they invent between their hands and their feet*

Stop. What does that mean — a slander invented "**between their hands and their feet**"? It is a deeply odd, deeply specific phrase. Why these body parts? Why this geography?

The classical commentators — al-Jalālayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurṭubī — are united on what this points to. *"Between her hands and her feet"* is the position of a **newborn child** at the moment of birth. When a woman delivers, the child falls *between her hands and her feet.* And so this phrase is understood to mean: **a woman bringing a child that is not her husband's, and falsely attributing it to him** — passing off another man's child as his, or a foundling as her own, to be raised on his name and his inheritance.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what Allah has done with the language here. He is naming one of the gravest betrayals imaginable — a lie about *paternity itself*, a lie that would corrupt a lineage, defraud an inheritance, and bind a man for life to raise a deception. And He names it **without ever using a crude or explicit word.** He does not say "an illegitimate child." He draws, instead, the quiet physical picture of a baby arriving *between a mother's hands and feet* — the most intimate, most vulnerable geography of birth — and lets that image carry the entire weight of the meaning.

This is what we keep finding in the Quran: a *physical* image standing in for an *unseen* reality. The bodily space "between hands and feet" is the stepping stone. The destination is the hidden treachery of a corrupted trust — and the protection of the most fundamental human bond, that between a parent and the child who is truly theirs.

And notice the dignity in the indirection. Allah names the sin precisely enough that no one can mistake what is forbidden — but gently enough that He never strips the woman of her decency in the naming. He convicts the *crime* without coarsening His own speech. This is divine *adab* — even in legislating against the ugliest deception, the speech of God does not become ugly.

**What this reveals:** the most serious matters can be named with the most refined tongue. And the lies that corrupt our deepest bonds — about who belongs to whom, about what is real — are weighed by Allah as among the gravest things a soul brings to this trade and must lay down.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa-staghfir* — The Forgiveness Asked at the Very Moment of Arrival

#### A concept: when do you usually ask forgiveness?

Think about the ordinary logic of an apology. You ask forgiveness *after* you have done wrong. The wrong comes first; the seeking of pardon comes after. That is the normal sequence. Crime, then contrition.

Now watch this verse break that sequence completely.

#### The command, in sequence

The women have just pledged. They have committed to abandon the gravest sins. They are, in this moment, at their *most* sincere, their *most* resolved, their *cleanest.* This is the high point. And what does Allah command the Prophet to do *at this exact peak*?

> فَبَايِعْهُنَّ وَٱسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُنَّ ٱللَّهَ — *then accept their pledge, and ask Allah to forgive them*

> ٱسْتَغْفِرْ — *istaghfir* — "seek forgiveness"

[PAUSE]

Look at the order. They commit — *and in the very same breath*, before they have taken a single step into their new life, before they have had the chance to fail even once, Allah commands that **forgiveness be sought for them.**

#### The shape of *istighfār*

The word *istaghfir* is from the root **غ-ف-ر** (*ghafara*, to cover, to conceal, to shield), and it sits in **Form X** — the pattern *istafʿala*. The defining function of Form X is **to seek, to request, to ask for** the thing in the root. *Istaghfara* literally means: **to seek the covering-over.** To ask that one's faults be wrapped up, concealed, blotted from view.

And the closing of the verse tells us *who* does the covering: *inna-llāha **ghafūrun** raḥīm* — "Indeed Allah is the One who covers, the One who shows mercy." The Prophet is commanded to *seek* (Form X, the asking) — and Allah is named as the One who *grants* (*ghafūr*, the doing). The human side asks; the divine side covers. The grammar itself stations each party where they belong.

#### Why here? Why now?

This is the heart of it. Why would the moment of *commitment* be paired with the seeking of *forgiveness*?

Sit with this. There are at least two reasons, and they layer on top of each other.

The first: their **past.** These are women coming out of *Jāhiliyyah* — a whole prior life. Some of them, like Hind, carry heavy histories. The *istighfār* reaches *backward*, sweeping clean what came before, so that they cross the threshold *unburdened.* The pledge is the new page; the *istighfār* is the erasing of the old one.

But the second reason is the one that should make your heart tremble. The *istighfār* also reaches **forward.** Allah, who knows His creation, knows that no soul — however sincere its pledge — will keep that pledge perfectly. They *will* stumble. And so, at the very gate, before the journey begins, **the mercy for the future failures is already requested.** The covenant you are entering *already contains, built into its terms, the provision for the times you will break it.*

[PAUSE]

Think of what that does to fear. The single greatest thing that stops a person from committing to God is the silent terror: *"What about when I fail? I'll commit, and then I'll fall, and then it'll be worse — a broken promise on top of a sin."* And Allah dismantles that terror at the door. He does not wait for you to fail and then offer repair. He has the repair *placed in your hand as you enter.* You sign the contract, and the first clause is mercy.

**What this reveals:** the covenant of faith is not a trap that springs shut on your imperfection. It is a door that opens with forgiveness already in the room.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

So look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Yubāyiʿnaka* — this is a **trade**, two hands moving, the most generous exchange in existence. *Buhtān* — what you lay on the table is heavy and real; even the gravest deceptions are named and surrendered, named with a tongue that stays pure. And *istighfār* — the moment you complete the trade, before you have walked a single step, the covering of your faults, past and future, is already being sought.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of the relationship between a human being and God.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Only Trade Where the Terms Are Rigged in Your Favor

#### Start where you live

You are, whether you notice it or not, a creature of transactions. You weigh things constantly. Is this job worth the commute? Is this relationship worth what it costs me? You are suspicious of anything labeled "free," because some part of you has learned that *free* usually means *you are the product*, or *there's a catch in the fine print.* You read terms and conditions — or you feel guilty for not reading them — because you have been burned by deals where you gave more than you got.

This is the instinct Allah is speaking to when He frames the covenant as a *trade.* He is not bypassing your transactional mind. He is **meeting it.** He is saying: *fine — you want to understand this as a deal? Let's lay out the terms. Here is exactly what you give, and here is exactly what you get.*

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

So let's be ruthless accountants for a moment. Let's audit the deal.

**The column of what you give up:**
- *Shirk* — worshipping things that cannot hear you, that never helped you, that you yourself carved.
- *Theft* — taking what corrodes your own trustworthiness and poisons your community.
- *Zinā* — a betrayal that fractures families and hollows out the very meaning of trust.
- *Killing your children* — in their world, the burying of infant daughters out of fear of poverty or shame; the killing of your own future out of fear.
- *Buhtān* — the lie that destroys lineages and bonds.

Look honestly at that column. **Every single thing in it is a thing that was already destroying you.** Allah is not asking you to surrender your joys. He is asking you to put down the knives you were holding by the blade. The "cost" of this trade is *the cessation of self-harm.*

**The column of what you receive:**
- *Ghufrān* — your past, covered, erased, gone.
- *Raḥmah* — the active, ongoing mercy of the Lord of the worlds.
- And as the related verse names it plainly: *the Garden* (9:111).

[PAUSE]

What kind of merchant offers you this? You bring poison to the table and walk away with paradise. You hand over the things that were killing you and receive, in exchange, to be *covered and loved.*

#### Sit with the imbalance

There is a discomfort that honest people feel here, and I want to name it rather than rush past it. *It's too good.* It doesn't feel like a fair trade — and it isn't. A fair trade gives equal value for equal value. This is wildly *unequal* — in your favor. And the human heart, trained on fairness, almost can't accept a deal this generous. We keep waiting for the catch.

But here is the thing about *this* merchant: His generosity is not a marketing tactic with a hidden cost. The two closing names tell you why the deal is structured this way. *Ghafūr. Raḥīm.* He covers and He shows mercy — **not because the trade obligates Him to, but because that is simply who He is.** The lopsidedness of the deal is not a trick. It is a *self-portrait.* The terms are generous because the One offering them is generous in His essence.

#### What it changes tomorrow

Here is the practical turn. The next time you approach an act of worship — a prayer, a fast, a charity, a moment of holding your tongue — stop framing it as a *cost* you are reluctantly paying. That framing is a lie about the nature of the deal. Reframe it as **your half of a trade where the other side has already overpaid.** You are not losing the sleep, the money, the pleasure. You are *placing your small thing on a table where God has already set down the Garden.*

Worship, understood through *bayʿa*, stops being a tax and becomes the most intelligent investment a rational being could possibly make.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Entering Through the Door of Your Own Weakness

#### Start where you live

Let me describe a feeling you almost certainly know.

It is the reason the gym is empty by February. It is the reason you've started the same journal four times. It is the quiet, corrosive voice that says: *"Why bother committing? You know how this goes. You'll be on fire for a week, and then you'll slip, and then the failure will feel worse than if you'd never tried. Better not to make the promise at all than to break it."*

This is the **fear of commitment born from the certainty of failure.** And it is, I would argue, the single biggest obstacle between most people and a serious relationship with God. Not disbelief. Not doubt. *Self-knowledge.* You know yourself too well to trust your own pledge. You've broken promises to yourself before. Why would the promise to God be any different — and isn't a broken promise to *God* infinitely more dangerous?

So you stay at the threshold. You admire the room from the doorway. You never walk in, because you're afraid of what your own weakness will do once you're inside.

#### Now let the ayah walk straight into that fear

Watch the sequence one more time, because it was *built* for exactly this fear:

The women pledge. They commit. They are at the peak of resolve. And in the *very same sentence* — *wa-staghfir lahunna-llāh* — "ask Allah to forgive them."

[PAUSE]

Do you understand what Allah is telling the frightened heart at the door?

He is saying: **I know you will stumble. I built that knowledge into the contract. The forgiveness for your failure is not something you'll have to come back and beg for later, hat in hand, ashamed. It is being sought for you *now*, at the entrance, before you've even crossed.**

The covenant does not assume your perfection. It *assumes your weakness* — and provisions for it in advance.

#### Let the movements build

Move one: think about what this means for the *first* failure. Most of us, when we commit to God and then slip, hear a voice — and we mistake it for the voice of conscience — that says: *"See? You're a fraud. You broke it. You might as well give up the whole thing."* That voice tells you the failure *cancels the covenant.* But this ayah says the opposite. The covenant *anticipated* the failure. Your slip did not break a deal that assumed you were perfect. It triggered a clause that was written *because* you are not. The failure is *inside* the contract, not a violation of it.

Move two: notice *who* is doing the asking. The Prophet ﷺ is commanded to *istaghfir* — to seek forgiveness *for them.* These women are not even told, in this moment, to seek it for themselves. The Messenger of God is interceding for them *as they enter.* You are not walking in alone and unvouched-for. You are walking in with prayers already being said over you.

Move three — and this is the paradox I want you to let *sit* without resolving it too quickly. The cleanest, most sincere moment — the moment of the pledge — is the moment Allah pairs with the seeking of forgiveness. Why ask forgiveness at the *cleanest* moment? Because Allah is teaching that even our *best* moments are not free of need. Even our purest devotion is stained enough to require covering. And rather than letting that crush us, He folds it into the welcome. *Yes, even your best needs My mercy — and here it is, freely, at the door.*

[PAUSE]

Move four: feel what this does to the relationship's entire emotional texture. A relationship that demands perfection is a relationship of *fear* — you are always one mistake from exile. A relationship that builds the forgiveness in at the foundation is a relationship of *security.* You can be fully honest. You can return after failing. You do not have to perform a flawlessness you don't have. The home does not throw you out when you track mud through it; the welcome mat already said *come in.*

#### What it changes tomorrow

So here is the invitation, the thing to actually carry. **Stop standing at the threshold.** The reason you've been refusing to fully commit — *"because I'll fail"* — is the very thing Allah already addressed before you raised it. You are protecting yourself against a danger He disarmed at the door.

Make the pledge. Start the prayer, the habit, the return. And when you fail — *when*, not if — do not read the failure as proof you should never have started. Read it as the clause activating exactly as designed. Then come back. The covering was sought for you before you ever fell. *Ghafūr.* He covers. *Raḥīm.* He shows mercy. These are not consolations for the defeated. They are the *opening terms* — the conditions on which you were invited in.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look now at how the two halves of this verse speak to each other — because they are the same insight from two directions.

The verse is built as a **trade**, and the verse **closes with forgiveness**. Those are not two separate facts. They are one structure. Because what makes this trade the most generous in existence is *precisely* the forgiveness clause. Take any ordinary contract — break your end, and the deal is void; you forfeit. But in *this* trade, breaking your end triggers not forfeiture but *mercy.* The thing that voids every human contract — the breach — is the thing this divine contract was *designed around.*

So the linguistic discoveries and the themes converge on a single point:

- *Yubāyiʿnaka* told us: this is a trade, and you are a dignified party in it.
- *Buhtān*, named with such restraint, told us: the things you lay down are real and weighty, and even in convicting them Allah's speech stays pure.
- *Istighfār* at the threshold told us: the breach is pre-forgiven; the door opens with mercy already in the room.

Put them together and you have the whole shape of how a soul comes to God: **You enter a trade where you give what was harming you and receive what you could never earn — and the contract itself contains the mercy for every time you'll fail to keep your end.**

It is the only deal in the universe where the fine print, when you finally read it, turns out to say: *you cannot lose this one.*

### Questions to Carry

1. Where am I still standing at the *threshold* of a commitment to God — keeping it at arm's length — because I am secretly protecting myself against the failure I'm sure will come? And what would change if I truly believed the forgiveness for that failure was *already sought* at the door?

2. When I think about worship, do I feel I am *paying a cost* or *completing a trade where the other side has already overpaid*? Which of these is actually true — and which one have I been living as if it were true?

3. The women laid down specific, named things to enter this covenant. What is the specific thing — the one I'd rather not name — that I am still holding by the blade, calling it a possession when it is really a wound?

4. When I slip after a sincere intention, whose voice do I hear: the one that says *the covenant is broken, give up* — or the one this verse teaches, that says *the clause has activated, come back*?

5. Hind stood veiled in that line, certain her past was too heavy. Whose past — including my own — have I quietly decided is too heavy for this door, when Allah built the door wide enough for *her*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The pledge of faith is a trade in which you hand over the things that were destroying you and receive forgiveness and mercy — and the moment you enter it, the covering for every future failure is already being sought, so that the covenant you sign already contains the grace for the times you'll break it.

---

### The Closing Invitation

Something can shift today, and it is small and it is enormous.

You can stop treating your relationship with God as a test you are failing and start treating it as a *trade you have already won.* You can walk in from the threshold you've been hovering at — not because you have become strong enough or clean enough, but precisely because the verse tells you that *strength and cleanness were never the entry requirement.* The entry requirement was that you *come.* The forgiveness was waiting on the other side of the door before you knocked.

Hind crossed that threshold. She lived the rest of her life on the other side of it. The door has not narrowed since.

This is not an obligation laid on you. It is an invitation extended to you — by the only Merchant who deals at a loss to Himself so that you might profit beyond measure.
