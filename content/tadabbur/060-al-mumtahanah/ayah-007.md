# The Enemy You Have Not Yet Loved

## Introduction: A Word Spoken Into Grief

Let me put the ayah in front of you first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single meaning.

> [ayah:60:7] ۞ عَسَى ٱللَّهُ أَن يَجْعَلَ بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَ ٱلَّذِينَ عَادَيْتُم مِّنْهُم مَّوَدَّةً ۚ وَٱللَّهُ قَدِيرٌ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*ʿAsā Allāhu an yajʿala baynakum wa-bayna alladhīna ʿādaytum minhum mawaddah. Wa-Allāhu Qadīr. Wa-Allāhu Ghafūrun Raḥīm.*

**"Perhaps Allah will place, between you and those of them you have treated as enemies, love. And Allah is All-Powerful, and Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

Now — before we open a single word — I want you to feel the room this verse was spoken into. Because if you don't feel the room, the verse will sound like a pleasant sentiment. It is not a pleasant sentiment. It is a rope thrown to drowning people.

### Rebuild the world

The surah is called **Al-Mumtahanah** — "She Who Is Examined," "The Woman Put to the Test." We are in the years after the migration to Madinah, in the tense, charged span around the treaty of Hudaybiyyah, before Mecca opened. And the surah begins with one of the hardest commands the early believers were ever asked to carry:

*Do not take My enemies and your enemies as intimate allies.*

Read coldly, that sounds like geopolitics. It was not geopolitics. **The enemies in Mecca were their own families.** The man who tortured you in the streets of Mecca was your uncle. The woman who turned the city against you was your aunt. The people who drove you from your homes, confiscated your property, and would have killed you — those were your fathers, your sons, your brothers, the people whose faces you had loved since childhood.

And now the believers are being told: the bond of faith outranks the bond of blood. You cannot give your secret loyalty to the people leading the war against this religion, even if they share your name, even if you nursed at the same breast.

[PAUSE]

Imagine what that did to the heart. Imagine the grief of it. al-Baghawi captures it in a single phrase — he says **"Allah knew the intensity of the believers' anguish (shiddat wajd al-muʾminīn) over this,"** the raw ache of being severed from people they still loved. They obeyed. But obedience is not the same as not bleeding. They cut the cord, and they grieved the cut.

It is *into that grief* — not into a strategy session, into a grief — that this verse descends.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice, and then we will earn it slowly over the next hour.

Allah does not respond to their grief by telling them to be strong. He does not say, "These enemies deserve your hatred." He does not say, "Forget them, you have a better family now."

He says something almost unbearable in its gentleness. He says: *the very people you are treating as enemies right now — I might put love between you and them.*

And the word He uses for "might" — `عَسَىٰ` — is one of the most quietly explosive words in the entire Qur'an. Because when *you and I* say "maybe," we mean we don't know. **When Allah says "maybe," He is not uncertain about anything.** So why does the One who knows every future, the One for whom tomorrow is as visible as today — why does He speak in the language of *maybe*?

That question is our doorway. Let's walk through it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We have three things to open, and we will not rush any of them. First, the strange word of hope, `عَسَىٰ`. Second, the shape of the word "enmity," `عَادَيْتُم`, which hides its whole theology in its grammatical form. And third, the word He chose for love — `مَّوَدَّةً` — and the architecture of *where* He places it.

### Element One: `عَسَىٰ` — When God Says "Perhaps"

Let me teach you the concept before we touch the verse.

In ordinary human speech, the words of hope and expectation — "maybe," "perhaps," "I hope," "it may be that" — all carry a hidden confession. The confession is: *I do not control the outcome.* When you say "maybe it'll rain tomorrow," you are admitting two things at once: that you want or expect something, and that it is **outside your hands.** Hope, for us, always grows in the soil of our own limitation. We hope precisely *because* we cannot guarantee.

Hold onto that. It will matter enormously in a moment.

Now look at the word. The verse opens — not with a command, not with a verdict — but with `عَسَىٰ`. *Perhaps.* "Perhaps Allah will place love between you and your enemies."

[PAUSE]

Here is the problem. A problem the classical scholars of language saw immediately and could not let go of.

`عَسَىٰ` is a word of hope and expectation. But **Allah does not hope.** Hope requires a gap between what you want and what you can secure. There is no gap in Allah. He does not expect outcomes; He *authors* them. He does not wait to see if the rain comes; He is the One who sends it. So when the All-Powerful — and note, He tells us in this very verse, `وَٱللَّهُ قَدِيرٌ`, "Allah is All-Powerful" — when *that* Being says "perhaps," the word cannot possibly carry its ordinary meaning. The ordinary meaning is logically impossible here.

So what is it doing?

The mufassirūn and the scholars of the Arabic language answered with something beautiful. They said: **when `عَسَىٰ` issues from Allah, it does not mean "maybe." It means "it shall be."** A word that signals doubt in the mouth of a human becomes a word that signals *certainty* in the speech of God. al-Jalalayn glides right past the "maybe" and reads it as accomplished fact — he writes that Allah *did* it, `wa-qad faʿalahu baʿda fatḥi Makkah`, "and He indeed did this after the Conquest of Mecca." al-Baghawi says the same: `fa-faʿala Allāhu dhālik` — "so Allah *did* that." The grammarians did not treat `عَسَىٰ` here as a question mark. They treated it as a sealed promise.

Stay with this, because it's not just a grammar curiosity. Ask the real question: *if Allah is certain, why didn't He just say so?* Why dress a certainty in the clothing of a maybe? Why not say "Allah **will** place love between you" — `sa-yajʿalu`, plainly, with no perhaps at all?

[PAUSE]

Here is what I think the `عَسَىٰ` is doing, and it is an act of tenderness so precise it should stop us in our tracks.

**The "perhaps" is not for Allah. The "perhaps" is for them.**

Think about who is receiving this verse. People in fresh grief. People who have just been commanded to sever ties with relatives they still love. If Allah had said, flatly, "I *will* make you love your enemies," what would that have done to a heart that, right now, in this moment, is full of righteous anger at those same enemies — enemies who tortured and expelled them? It would have felt like a violation. It would have felt like being told their present pain was illegitimate, that their loyalty to the truth was somehow temporary or naive.

But `عَسَىٰ` — *perhaps* — does something gentler. It **opens a door without shoving them through it.** It honors their present state — yes, today these are your enemies, today this hostility is real and even appropriate — *while* it quietly refuses to let that present become permanent. It says: hold your grief; you are allowed to. But do not seal the future shut. Leave it cracked open. I might do something here you cannot yet imagine.

A flat promise would have argued with their pain. A "perhaps" sits down beside their pain and whispers that it will not last forever.

[PAUSE — let this land]

So we have a word that is, for Allah, a certainty — and for the grieving believer, a permission to hope. The same three letters carry both at once. That is not sloppiness. That is the Qur'an speaking two truths into a single breath: *the matter is decided in the heavens, and your heart is allowed to take its time on earth.*

One word. And it has already told us how Allah handles a wound: He does not rush it, and He does not abandon it.

### Element Two: `عَادَيْتُم` — The Grammar That Knows Enmity Takes Two

Now to the word for enemies. And here the Arabic does something that English simply cannot reproduce, because the entire insight lives in the *shape* of the verb.

Let me teach the concept first.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns — "forms" — and the form is not decoration. The form carries meaning. There is a particular pattern, what the grammarians call **Form III**, the `mufāʿalah` pattern — you hear it in the long *aa* sound after the first letter: *qātala, jāhada, ʿāmala, kātaba.* And this Form III has a signature function. It almost always signals **a reciprocal, two-sided, mutual action.** Something done *back and forth between two parties.*

Think of it in plain terms. There is a verb `qatala` — "he killed." And there is `qātala`, Form III — which does not mean "he killed" but "he *fought*," he traded blows, he was locked in mutual combat. `kataba` is "he wrote." `kātaba`, Form III, is "he *corresponded* — he and another wrote back and forth to each other." The long *aa* is the sound of *two people facing each other,* the action bouncing between them.

Now look at the verb Allah chose for the enmity:

`عَادَيْتُم` — *ʿādaytum.* From the root `ʿ-d-w`, the root of enmity, `ʿadāwah`. And it is **Form III.** Hear the long *aa*: `ʿā-day-tum`. The corpus confirms it — Form III, the reciprocal pattern.

He did not say `ʿadawtum` — a simple Form I, "you were hostile to them," a one-way act. He said `ʿādaytum` — and that word means something far more specific. It means **"those with whom you were locked in *mutual* enmity."** The hostility went both directions. You hated them; they hated you. You struck; they struck back. It was a relationship — an ugly, reciprocal, two-sided relationship — but a relationship nonetheless.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter so much? Stay with me, because here is where the grammar becomes theology.

Allah is naming the disease with absolute precision. The disease is not your private feeling. The disease is **a bond** — a real connection between two parties, only the connection is made of enmity instead of love. The `mufāʿalah` form tells us these two parties are *engaged with each other.* They are not strangers. Strangers don't have `ʿadāwah`; they have indifference. Enmity, like love, requires that the other person *matter* to you. You cannot be the enemy of someone you have never thought about. **Enmity is a dark form of intimacy.** It binds you to the very person you cannot stand.

And now watch what Allah does with that.

He takes this reciprocal, two-sided bond — `ʿādaytum`, the back-and-forth of mutual hostility — and He proposes to *replace* it. Not to dissolve it into nothing. Not to make these people vanish from your life. To **transform** the bond, while keeping it two-sided. The mutual enmity becomes mutual love. The back-and-forth of blows becomes the back-and-forth of affection.

How do we know He keeps it two-sided? Because of one more detail — and this is the thing I most want you to see.

Look at how He describes *where* the love goes:

`بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَ ٱلَّذِينَ عَادَيْتُم` — *baynakum wa-bayna alladhīna ʿādaytum* — "between you, and between those you fought."

He says `bayna` — "between" — **twice.** *Between you* and *between them.* In Arabic He did not have to repeat it; "between you and those" would have been grammatically complete with a single `bayna`. But He doubled it. *Baynakum wa-bayna.* And that doubling is the linguistic mirror image of the `mufāʿalah` verb. The enmity was two-sided — `ʿādaytum`. So the love is placed two-sided too — `bayna... wa-bayna`, with a "between" planted on each side of the relationship, one anchored in your heart, one anchored in theirs.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the architecture? **The cure is shaped exactly like the disease.** The hostility was reciprocal; the remedy is reciprocal. The verb form that held the enmity (`ʿā-`) and the repeated `bayna` that holds the love are doing the same structural work — insisting that what is broken between two people will be healed *between* two people, on both sides at once. Allah doesn't just soften your heart toward them and leave them still hating you. He reaches into the space *between* and changes what flows through it, in both directions.

One verb form. One repeated preposition. And together they tell you that Allah's reconciliation is never half a reconciliation.

### Element Three: `مَّوَدَّةً` — Not Just Love, But Love That Acts

We come to the word at the center of the verse — the thing being placed between former enemies. He could have used several words for love. The choice He made is deliberate, and it is the most hopeful word He could have reached for.

The concept first.

Arabic has more than one word in the family of love. There is `ḥubb` — the broad, general word for love and liking, the inclination of the heart. And there is `mawaddah`, from the root `w-d-d` — and `mawaddah` is something more specific. The lexicographers describe `wudd` as love that **shows itself.** It is love that has become *visible* — affection that has translated into warmth, tenderness, kindness, the active wishing of good. `Ḥubb` can sit hidden inside you. `Mawaddah` leans outward; it does things. It is love you can *feel coming off another person.*

And the word matters even more when you remember that one of Allah's own names is built from this exact root: **`al-Wadūd`** — The Most Loving, The Source of `wudd`. So when Allah says He will place `mawaddah` between former enemies, He is placing *His own attribute* into the space between them. The love that flows back into that broken relationship is not manufactured by the human beings. It is poured in from a divine source. He is `al-Wadūd`; the `mawaddah` is His to give.

Now hold the full picture. Between people who were `ʿādaytum` — locked in *mutual combat* — Allah will place not mere tolerance, not a cold peace, not "they'll leave you alone now." He will place **`mawaddah`** — active, warm, visible, tender affection. The largest possible swing. From the back-and-forth of blows to the back-and-forth of `wudd`.

[PAUSE]

And notice it is `mawaddatan` — **indefinite.** Grammatically unbounded. He does not say "*the* love" — a specific, measured, rationed amount. He leaves it open, unquantified. A love whose ceiling is not named. As if to say: do not assume the reconciliation will be small and grudging. Do not assume the best you can hope for with a former enemy is an uneasy truce. The love He can place there is of an amount only He defines.

al-Muyassar renders the promise as `maḥabbah baʿda al-baghḍāʾ, wa-ulfah baʿda al-shaḥnāʾ` — "**love after loathing, and harmony after hostility.**" Listen to the rhythm of that: each phrase names the poison first and then the antidote that *occupies the very same place.* Love standing exactly where the loathing stood. Harmony filling the exact space the hostility had filled. Not a different relationship somewhere else — *this* relationship, transformed.

And then the verse closes the linguistic case with three names, and we cannot pass over them, because they are the *mechanism* of the whole thing:

`وَٱللَّهُ قَدِيرٌ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ`

— **"And Allah is All-Powerful. And Allah is Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

Why these three, in this order, sealing this particular verse? Because each one answers an objection your heart is silently raising.

When you hear "Allah might make you love your enemy," your heart protests: *that's impossible — hearts don't turn like that.* And Allah answers: `قَدِيرٌ` — **I am All-Powerful.** Hearts are nothing before My capacity to turn them. The hardness you think is permanent is not permanent to Me.

Then your heart protests again: *but look at what they did — the blood, the cruelty, the years of it. How could that ever be wiped clean enough for love?* And Allah answers: `غَفُورٌ` — **I am Oft-Forgiving.** I can erase the record that you think makes reconciliation impossible. The past is not a locked door to Me.

And your heart protests one last time: *but why would He even bother — why reach down into something this broken?* And Allah answers: `رَّحِيمٌ` — **I am Most Merciful.** This is who I am. Reaching into broken things is My nature.

[PAUSE]

Look at what those three names give you, together. `Qadīr` — the **power** to do it. `Ghafūr` — the **clearing of the past** that would otherwise block it. `Raḥīm` — the **motive**, the *why*. Power, pardon, and mercy: the three ingredients any true reconciliation requires. Allah names them as His own. The verse doesn't just promise the transformation; it lists the exact divine attributes that make the transformation possible, like signing the promise three times over.

### The Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. A word of "perhaps" that is, in the mouth of God, a certainty dressed in mercy. A verb-form that knows enmity is a two-sided bond — and a doubled "between" that insists the healing be two-sided too. A word for love that does not sit hidden but pours outward, drawn from Allah's own name, left grammatically *unbounded* — and then sealed with the three attributes that make it real: power, pardon, mercy.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the people we have written off. And about a future we keep trying to slam shut, that Allah keeps insisting on holding open.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Person You Have Frozen

Let me start where you already live — not in seventh-century Mecca, but in your own chest.

There is a thing the human mind does, and it does it to *protect* you, which is why it feels so natural and so right. When someone hurts you — deeply, repeatedly — your mind does something efficient. It **files them away under a fixed label.** "He's a narcissist." "She's toxic." "That's just who they are." "My enemy." And once the label is applied, something quietly closes. You stop expecting them to change. In fact, you begin to *need* them not to change, because your anger has organized itself around the version of them that hurt you. If they changed, your anger would have nowhere to stand.

Psychologists have a name for the harder edge of this. They call it **dehumanization** — the process by which we stop seeing another person as a full, complex, changing human soul, and start seeing them as a *type*, a category, a fixed thing. And here is the unsettling research finding: dehumanization is not something only cruel people do. It is something *ordinary* people do *all the time*, to anyone they have decided is on the other side. It feels like clarity. It feels like finally seeing them for who they "really are." But what it actually is, is **the freezing of a person in time** — taking one chapter of who they were and declaring it the whole book, forever.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the verse back.

The believers had *every* earthly justification to freeze the Meccans. These weren't people who said something annoying at a dinner party. These were torturers. Murderers. People who had driven the believers from their homes. If anyone ever earned the label "permanent enemy," it was these people. The freezing would have been *fair.*

And into that fair, justified, earned hatred, Allah drops `عَسَىٰ`. *Perhaps.* And the entire force of that word is aimed at **one thing: the lock you have placed on another human soul.**

Because here is the theology hiding underneath, and it is staggering. When you freeze a person as "permanent enemy," you are not just making a statement about them. You are making a statement about **the limits of Allah's power over their heart.** You are saying: *this soul is beyond reach. This one, Allah cannot turn.* And the verse answers that the instant it can: `وَٱللَّهُ قَدِيرٌ` — **Allah is All-Powerful.** Over that heart. The one you sealed shut. The one you filed under "they'll never change." al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both point to the proof in history: Abū Sufyān — the *commander* of the armies against the Muslims, the single most prominent face of the enmity — became a Muslim after Mecca opened. The man at the very top of the believers' "permanent enemy" list. The freeze was real. And it broke.

[PAUSE]

So sit with what this asks of you.

It is not asking you to pretend the harm didn't happen. The verse does not deny that these people were enemies — it says it plainly, `عَادَيْتُm`, "you fought them," and the surah's other verses preserve real, lawful boundaries against those still waging war. Allah is not naïve about evil. **He is just not finished with people the way we are finished with people.**

The hardness in *your* heart you cannot move by willpower. You have tried. You know you cannot simply *decide* to stop resenting someone who wounded you; the resentment has roots you didn't plant and can't pull. But that same hardness — in *their* heart, and in yours — is `هَيِّن` before `al-Qadīr`. Light. Movable. The thing you experience as a permanent feature of reality is, to Allah, a temporary weather pattern.

What does this change about tomorrow? Something very concrete. **There is a person whose name, when you hear it, makes your jaw tighten.** You have a fixed verdict on them. The verse is not asking you to call them, or to trust them, or to pretend the wound isn't there. It is asking for something smaller and harder: **to stop praying, in effect, for them to stay the way they are.** To unclench one finger from the lock. To allow the sentence "perhaps Allah will change this" to be true. You don't have to make it happen — that's `Qadīr`'s job, not yours. You only have to stop insisting it's impossible. Leave the door Allah cracked open... open.

### Theme Two: The Mercy of a "Maybe"

Now I want to take you somewhere more interior. Not the enemy out there — the future in here. Your relationship with the unknown.

Begin with something you already feel. There is a particular kind of suffering that comes not from a bad thing that *has* happened, but from a bad thing you have decided **must** happen. We do this constantly. "This marriage will never heal." "I will always be like this." "That relationship is dead and that's final." "My child will never come back to the faith." And notice: there is a strange, bitter *comfort* in these sentences. Closing the future feels safer than leaving it open. If I have already decided the worst, at least I can't be ambushed by hope and then disappointed. **Despair feels like protection.** It is the heart pre-grieving so that nothing can surprise it.

The Qur'an has a word for the sealing of the future against good. It calls it `qunūṭ` — despair of Allah's mercy — and it names it, elsewhere, as something only the *misguided* fall into: `wa-man yaqnaṭu min raḥmati rabbihi illā al-ḍāllūn` — "and who despairs of his Lord's mercy except the astray." Despair is not humility. Despair is a *theological error.* It is a claim about what Allah will and won't do.

[PAUSE]

Now look again at how Allah chose to speak in our verse — and feel the mercy in the *form* of it.

He could have left the believers with no word at all about their lost families. He could have said only "they are your enemies, move on." Instead, He hands them `عَسَىٰ`. A *maybe.* And I want you to feel what a gift a "maybe" is to a grieving person.

A flat promise — "I will reunite you in love" — would have been *too much* for that moment. The wound was too fresh; the heart, still angry, would have recoiled from being told it would soon love the people who had just expelled it. But total silence — leaving the future sealed shut — would have been *despair*, the heart concluding "I have lost them forever." So Allah gives the exact thing the human heart can actually *hold* in the middle of a fresh wound: **not a certainty that overwhelms, and not a silence that crushes, but an open door.** A "perhaps" you can carry. A future you are *permitted* not to give up on.

This is `al-Wadūd` — the Most Loving — knowing precisely how much hope a broken heart can bear in a single dose. Not so much that it feels like a lie. Not so little that it feels like abandonment. Exactly enough to keep the heart from slamming the door.

[PAUSE]

And here is the deepest turn. Remember what we established in the linguistic journey: for *Allah*, this `عَسَىٰ` is not a maybe at all. It is a *certainty.* He knows Mecca will open. He knows Abū Sufyān will believe. He knows the families will be rejoined in `mawaddah`. The outcome is sealed in the heavens.

So what is actually happening in this verse is almost overwhelming when you see it clearly. **Allah, who knows the certain future, deliberately speaks it to you as a "perhaps" — so that you can live inside it.** He downsizes His own certainty into a human-sized hope, *for your sake.* Because a heart cannot live inside someone else's certainty. But it can live inside a "perhaps." The "perhaps" is the doorway through which His certainty becomes something *you* can walk in.

There is a tenderness here that should reorganize how you face every closed-looking future in your life. The thing you have written off — the relationship, the version of yourself, the prayer you stopped making because you decided it would never be answered — Allah may be holding, on His side, as a *certainty,* while gently offering it to you as a *perhaps,* precisely because a perhaps is all your wounded heart can currently carry.

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes what you are *allowed* to stop deciding. You do not have to manufacture confident optimism — that's not what's asked, and the believers in their grief couldn't have managed it either. You are simply asked to **stop signing the death certificate.** To take the futures you have declared dead — "this will never heal," "I will always be this way" — and downgrade each one from *never* to *perhaps.* Not because you can see the good coming. Because the One who can see it is telling you not to bolt the door. Let your sentences end in `عَسَىٰ`. Leave them open. The God who holds the certainty is asking you only to hold the maybe.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole verse at once, because its design is exact.

It opens with **`عَسَىٰ`** — a hope sized for human hearts. It moves to **`بَيْنَكُمْ وَبَيْنَ`** — a "between" planted on *both* sides of a relationship. It names the disease, **`عَادَيْتُم`** — a *mutual* enmity, in the very verb-form that means "two-sided." It places the cure, **`مَّوَدَّةً`** — active, visible, unbounded love, drawn from Allah's own name `al-Wadūd`, set down in the exact spot the enmity occupied. And it seals all of it with three signatures: **`قَدِيرٌ`** — the power to do it; **`غَفُورٌ`** — the pardon that clears the past blocking it; **`رَّحِيمٌ`** — the mercy that wants it done.

The structure *is* the message: a two-sided wound, healed by a two-sided love, made possible by power, pardon, and mercy — offered to a grieving heart in the one form it can hold.

### Bring It Together

The two halves of this hour are the same truth seen from two directions.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a God who speaks with surgical tenderness — who says "perhaps" to a certainty, who shapes the cure like the disease, who pours His own attribute into the gap between enemies.

The **thematic depths** showed us why we needed exactly that tenderness — because we freeze people into permanent enemies, and we freeze futures into permanent verdicts, and both of those freezings are, underneath, a quiet denial of what `al-Qadīr` can do.

Put them together and you get the single sentence the whole verse is built to deliver: **the heart you have written off — whether it is your enemy's heart, or your own — was never beyond His reach; you only thought it was, because you mistook your limitation for His.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Whose name makes your jaw tighten — and have you, without admitting it, started *needing* them to stay the way they hurt you?
2. Which futures in your life have you signed the death certificate on? What would it cost you to downgrade one of them from *never* to *perhaps*?
3. When you say "they'll never change," whose power are you actually making a claim about — theirs, or Allah's?
4. Is there a despair you've been mistaking for humility — a closed door you've been calling realism?
5. If `al-Wadūd` can place `mawaddah` between torturers and their victims, what is the relationship in your life you've decided is beyond repair — and on what authority did you decide it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The God who calls Himself al-Wadūd holds open — even toward the one you now call enemy, and even over the future you have buried — a possibility you are forbidden to seal shut, because the heart you wrote off was never beyond His power, His pardon, or His mercy.**

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this verse asks you to be naïve. It does not ask you to trust the untrustworthy, to reopen a door that safety requires closed, or to pretend a wound is not a wound. The same surah that gives us this verse also draws hard, lawful lines against those still at war. Allah is not asking you to lower your guard.

He is asking you to lower your *verdict.* To stop being more finished with people than He is. To take the one relationship, or the one future, or the one version of yourself that you have declared permanently dead — and to let the word `عَسَىٰ` fall over it. *Perhaps.* Not as a forced optimism you have to perform, but as a door you simply agree to stop nailing shut. That is the whole invitation. Unclench one finger. Leave it to `al-Qadīr` whether the door ever opens. Your only task is to stop insisting it can't.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, al-Wadūd, You place love where there was only loathing, and You hold open what we have sealed in despair. Soften the hardness in our hearts that we cannot soften ourselves, and the hardness in the hearts we have given up on. Where we have frozen people as permanent enemies, teach us that You are not yet finished with them — and where we have buried our futures, let the word "perhaps" live in us again. You are al-Qadīr over every heart, al-Ghafūr over every past, al-Raḥīm over every broken thing. Amīn.*
