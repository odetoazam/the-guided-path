# The Example Laid Before Everyone, Received Only by the Hopeful

### Surah Al-Mumtahanah, Ayah 6

> لَقَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فِيهِمْ أُسْوَةٌ حَسَنَةٌ لِّمَن كَانَ يَرْجُو ٱللَّهَ وَٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلْأَخِرَ‌ۚ وَمَن يَتَوَلَّ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ

**Laqad kāna lakum fīhim uswatun ḥasanah — li-man kāna yarjū Allāha wal-yawmal-ākhir. Wa-man yatawalla fa-inna Allāha huwal-ghaniyyul-ḥamīd.**

*"Certainly there was for you in them a good example — for anyone who hopes in Allah and the Last Day. And whoever turns away, then indeed Allah — He is the Free of all need, the Praiseworthy."*

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Word Said Twice

Let me take you back into the room where this was first heard.

The young community in Madinah is being pulled apart at the seam — not by swords, but by *blood*. The believers have relatives among those who drove them out of Makkah. There are uncles, cousins, fathers, sisters on the other side of the line. And the most natural thing in the human heart — the pull toward your own people — has become the hardest test of faith. Whom do you stand with when your kin and your Lord pull in opposite directions? That is what *Al-Mumtahanah* means: **she who is tested.** The whole surah is an examination of where loyalty finally lodges.

And into that ache, Allah does not give them a rule first. He gives them a *person*. He says: look at Ibrahim. Look at the man who said to his own father and his own people, *"We are done with you, and with what you worship beside Allah."* There is your model. There is how it's done.

But here is the thing I want you to hold from the very first breath of this reflection.

Allah says it **twice.**

In ayah 4, He says: *"There was for you a good example in Ibrahim and those with him."* And then — two verses later, in the ayah we are sitting with tonight — He says it again: *"Certainly there was for you in them a good example."*

[PAUSE]

The same phrase. *Uswatun ḥasanah.* A good example. Why would the Most Eloquent, the One whose every word is weighed, say the same thing twice in the space of three verses?

The early scholars noticed. Al-Qurtubi records the question openly. Some said: it is repeated *for emphasis* — *kurrira lil-taʾkīd.* Others said: the second was revealed *after a span of time* following the first. But here is what I want you to feel before we even open the words: **when Allah repeats, He is not stuttering. He is turning the diamond.** The first time He shows you the example. The second time — this time — He tells you something He did *not* say the first time. He tells you **who actually gets to keep it.**

That is the hunger I want you to carry into this ayah. The example was given to *everyone*. So why does this verse quietly narrow it down? And what does it mean that the same sentence which offers you a model ends by reminding you that Allah does not need you at all?

Let's go slowly. There is more in these nineteen words than in most books.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Uswah* — The Word for a Life You Pattern Yourself On

Let me first put an ordinary idea in front of you, before we touch the Arabic.

Think about how a child learns to walk. Nobody hands a toddler a manual. The toddler does not learn the *theory* of balance. The toddler watches the giants in the room move across it on two legs — and tries to become like them. Imitation comes *before* instruction. It is older than language. It is the first way a human being learns to be a human being.

Now hold that, and come to the word Allah chose.

He did not say *fīhim ʿibratun* — "in them is a lesson." He did not say *fīhim ḥikmah* — "in them is wisdom." He said:

> **أُسْوَةٌ حَسَنَةٌ** — *uswatun ḥasanah.*

The root is **hamza–sīn–wāw** (أ-س-و). And the lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* — tell us something quietly beautiful about this root. From the very same root comes the verb *āsā* — **to console, to share another's burden, to put yourself in their condition.** *Muwāsāh* — when you ease someone's grief by joining them in it. And it carries the sense of *mending*, of setting a broken thing right.

So *uswah* is not "an example" the way we put an example on a whiteboard. An *uswah* is **a person whose state you enter, whose condition you take onto yourself, until what was theirs becomes yours.** It is not "look at Ibrahim and learn a fact." It is "*stand where Ibrahim stood. Let his posture become your posture.*"

Let's sit with this. A lesson stays outside you. You can know a lesson and remain entirely unchanged. But an *uswah* — by the very meaning of its root — is something you *consoled yourself into*, something you *mended yourself by*. The model is not data to be stored. It is a shape to be taken.

And notice — this is easy to miss — the word is **indefinite.** *Uswatun*, not *al-uswah.* "A good example," not "the example." In Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite like this, in this position, opens it up — it makes it vast, unbounded. It is not saying *one specific instance* of an example. It is saying *a whole expanse of exemplariness* is laid out for you in these people. There is so much in them to become that Allah will not box it into a single definite thing.

[PAUSE]

So before we have even moved past the second word, the verse has told you what Allah is actually offering the tested believer. Not a verdict. Not a fatwa. **A life to be worn.**

---

### Element Two: The Hidden Narrowing — *Lakum*… then *Li-man Kāna Yarjū*

Now I need to teach you one small piece of Arabic grammar, because the entire heart of this ayah turns on it. I promise it will be worth it.

In Arabic there is a construction called **badal** — substitution. It is when you say something general, and then, a few words later, you name it more precisely — and the second naming *replaces and clarifies* the first. In English we do it with a pause: *"Someone broke the window — the boy next door."* "The boy next door" is a *badal* of "someone." It tells you who the vague "someone" really was.

Now watch what Allah does. He says:

> *"There was, **for you** — lakum — a good example…"*

*Lakum.* For **you** — plural, all of you, the whole community being addressed. The example is set down in front of *everyone.* No one is excluded from the offer.

And then, a breath later, He says:

> *"…**li-man kāna yarjū** — for whoever hopes in Allah and the Last Day."*

The classical grammarians — al-Jalālayn states it precisely — call this a **badal ishtimāl**: a substitution that *narrows down and specifies* who the "you" really is. Notice He even repeats the preposition — *lakum* (for you)… *li-man* (for whoever) — the same *li-* twice, so you cannot miss that the second is reaching back to redefine the first.

Do you see what just happened?

[PAUSE]

The example was placed in front of **all of you** — *lakum.* But the ayah then quietly says: it only *becomes* an example **for the one who hopes in Allah and the Last Day** — *li-man kāna yarjū.*

Stay with this, because it is staggering. **The example is universal. The inheritance is not.** Ibrahim's life is laid out in the open, in full view, for every single person in that room — believer and waverer alike. But the verse tells us that the model only *lands*, only *transfers*, only becomes a true *uswah*, in the heart that is **hoping** toward Allah and the Day to come.

This is why I told you at the start that the second mention does something the first did not. Ayah 4 *gave* the example. Ayah 6 tells you **the receiving organ.** The example is the light. *Rajāʾ* — hope — is the eye that can see by it.

And think about who was sitting in that room. There were believers whose hearts were straining toward Allah. And there were others whose hearts were still half-turned toward Makkah, toward kin, toward the comfort of the old loyalties. The *same* example of Ibrahim sat before both. And Allah is telling them: it will only become *yours* — it will only mend you, console you, reshape you — if your heart is actually *leaning forward*, hoping, toward Allah and the Last Day.

The model is not withheld from anyone. But it cannot be forced into a heart that is not reaching for it.

---

### Element Three: *Yatawallā* — and the Root That Means Both Loyalty and Desertion

Now look at the other figure in this verse. Across from the one who *hopes*, Allah places another:

> **وَمَن يَتَوَلَّ** — *wa-man yatawalla* — "and whoever turns away."

Let me give you the grammar, because it is doing real work. *Yatawallā* is **Form V** — *tafaʿʿala* — of the root **wāw–lām–yāʾ** (و-ل-ي). Form V in Arabic is the reflexive, the *middle* voice: the action curls back onto the doer. It is not "to be turned away." It is **to turn *one's own self* away** — a deliberate pivot of the whole person. To present your back.

But here is the thing about this root that I cannot get past — and I want you to feel it.

This very same root, **w-l-y**, is the root of the word **walī.** The friend. The ally. The protector. The one who is *close.* The Qurʾan's great word for the believer's intimacy with Allah — *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū*, "Allah is the *walī* of those who believe" — comes from this exact root.

[PAUSE]

So consider what the Arabic is quietly telling you. **The root of nearness and the root of desertion are the same root.** *Walī* — to draw close, to take as your intimate. *Tawallā* — to turn away. The same three letters. The difference is only the *direction of the turn.*

Because that is what *walāyah* really is — it is about which way your face is pointed. To make Allah your *walī* is to turn your face *toward* Him. To *tawallā* is to take that very same capacity for loyalty — that same turning of the self — and aim it *away.* The faculty does not change. Only its object. The heart was *built* to attach, to ally, to belong. The only question the whole surah is asking is: **which direction will that built-in loyalty face?**

And notice the absence here — read it carefully. *Wa-man yatawallā* — "whoever turns away." Turns away from **what?** Allah does not say. The object is left open. Al-Baghawī fills it in one way — turns away *from faith and allies instead with the disbelievers.* But grammatically, Allah left the blank unfilled. And I think that is mercy and warning at once. Because the one who turns away rarely names what he is turning from. He does not say, "I am abandoning Allah." He says, "I am just keeping the peace with my family. I am just being practical. I am just not making a scene." The turning is real even when it is never named. The verse leaves the object blank because **that is exactly how desertion happens — quietly, without an announcement.**

---

### Bridge: From the Words to the World

So look at what these nineteen words have built before we even step back.

A word — *uswah* — that means not a lesson but a *life you climb into.* A hidden narrowing that gives that life to *everyone* yet says it only takes root in *the one who hopes.* And a single root — *w-l-y* — that holds both the turning-toward and the turning-away in the same closed fist, so you understand that faith and desertion are not two different organs but two directions of the *one* loyal heart you were born with.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about the way a human soul actually works.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Are an Imitating Creature — So *Who* Are You Imitating?

Let me start where you already live.

You have never, in your entire life, formed yourself from scratch. No one has. The way you laugh — you got it from someone. The way you handle anger, the way you carry disappointment, the cadence of your speech, what you find funny, what you find shameful — almost all of it was absorbed, not invented. Neuroscientists talk about mirror neurons: the brain literally rehearses the actions it watches, firing as if *you* were doing what *they* are doing. We are, at the deepest wiring, **imitation machines.** We become like what we behold.

And in our age, this has not weakened. It has gone into overdrive. You scroll, and for hours every day you *behold* people. Their bodies, their tastes, their certainties, their disdain. And the mirror neurons do not ask whether the person is worthy of imitation. They just *copy.* You are being shaped — right now, every day — by whoever you spend your attention on. The only real question is not *whether* you have an *uswah.* You always do. The question is **whose.**

Now bring the ayah back. Allah, who designed this imitating creature, does not fight the design. He does not say, "Stop having role models." He says: *Laqad kāna lakum… uswatun ḥasanah.* "There was for you a **good** example." He works *with* the faculty He built. He says — since you will pattern yourself on *someone*, since you cannot help it, let Me put the right someone in front of you. Let it be Ibrahim. Let it be the man whose loyalty to Me was so clean that he could love his own father and still not follow him into falsehood.

And here is the movement I want you to feel. Remember the root of *uswah* — *to console, to mend.* Why does the tested believer, torn between kin and Lord, *need* an *uswah* and not just a *rule*? Because a rule cannot console you. A rule cannot stand next to you in the loneliness of choosing Allah over your own people. But a *person who already walked it* can. When you are about to make the hard, lonely choice, and you look and see *Ibrahim already there, having already paid it* — something in you mends. You are not the first. You are not alone. **Someone hoped this much before you, and survived it, and Allah named him a friend for it.**

So what does this change about tomorrow? Be honest about your *uswah.* Not the one you'd name out loud — the *real* one. Look at your screen time, your attention, the voices you let narrate the world to you, and ask: *is this the life I want to climb into?* Because you are climbing into it whether you choose it or not. The ayah is not asking you to find a role model. It is asking you to **stop letting one be assigned to you by accident.**

### Theme Two: Hope Is the Hand That Receives — and Turning Away Robs Only You

Now the harder, deeper thing. The thing that, when it lands, rearranges how you understand your whole relationship with Allah.

We saw it in the grammar: the example is *for you all*, but it only becomes real *li-man kāna yarjū* — for the one who **hopes.** Let me dwell on that word. *Rajāʾ* — hope. And notice the tense Allah chose: *kāna yarjū* — not "the one who hoped" once, but the one who **was, and keeps on, hoping.** A *continuous* posture. A face permanently tilted forward toward Allah and the Last Day.

Why should *hope* be the thing that unlocks the example? Think about it through something you know. Imagine two apprentices placed beside the same master craftsman. One of them desperately *wants* to become a craftsman — he watches every movement of the master's hands, hungry, leaning in. The other is only there to collect a paycheck. They watch the *same* master, for the *same* hours. But only one of them is *transformed*, because only one of them is *reaching.* The teaching was identical. The **receiving** was not.

That is *rajāʾ.* Hope is not a feeling. Hope is the heart **leaning forward.** It is the posture of someone who actually *expects* something from Allah, who is reaching toward the meeting with Him. And only that forward-leaning heart can catch an *uswah* and be changed by it. The one who is not hoping can stare at Ibrahim his whole life and absorb nothing — not because the example failed, but because there was no outstretched hand to receive it.

Now feel the contrast the verse sets up. Across from the hopeful one is the one who *yatawallā* — turns away, presents his back. And what does the heart whisper when it considers turning away? It whispers a lie. It whispers: *this will cost Allah something. He needs my worship. My turning matters to Him; maybe I have leverage.*

And Allah closes the door on that lie with the most magnificent two names:

> **فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ** — *fa-inna Allāha huwal-ghaniyyul-ḥamīd.*
> "Then indeed Allah — **He** is the Free of all need, the Praiseworthy."

Look at the pronoun. *Huwa* — "**He**." Allah does not just say "Allah is free of need." He inserts the standalone pronoun: *Allah — He, He alone, He emphatically — is the Free of need.* It slams the door on any notion that your turning away leaves a hole in the divine. **Al-Ghaniyy** — the One so utterly complete that need cannot touch Him. Your worship adds nothing to Him. Your desertion subtracts nothing from Him.

And then — do not rush past this — the *second* name. He does not stop at *al-Ghaniyy.* He could have. "Free of need" alone would have been a complete answer to the deserter. But He adds **al-Ḥamīd** — the Praiseworthy. And the pairing is the knockout blow.

Here is why. *Al-Ghaniyy* tells you: if you turn away, He loses nothing. But a cold, self-sufficient power could still be *diminished in renown* by being abandoned — a king loses something when his subjects leave, even if he keeps his wealth. So Allah adds *al-Ḥamīd*: not only do I lack nothing if you leave — **I remain perfectly, fully praised** whether you praise Me or not. The angels do not stop. Creation does not stop. He praises Himself. Al-Muyassar puts it beautifully: *al-Ḥamīd* — praised *in His essence and His attributes, praised in every state.* Your withholding of praise does not dim Him by one atom.

[PAUSE]

So now stand inside the full weight of it. The example was offered *to your benefit.* The hope that receives it is *for your benefit.* And the turning away — when you finally see it clearly — harms **only you.** *Wa-man yatawallā* — and whoever turns away — he turns from the One who needed nothing from him in the first place. He slams a door, and finds that the room he locked himself out of belonged entirely to him. He did not impoverish Allah. **He impoverished himself, and called it independence.**

This is the mercy hidden inside the severity. Because if Allah needed your worship, then your relationship with Him would be a transaction, a negotiation, and you could never be sure you were loved rather than used. But *al-Ghaniyy* means: He calls you to Himself while needing **nothing** from you. The invitation is pure gift. The example, the hope, the whole turning-toward — it was never for Him. **It was always, entirely, for you.**

So what changes tomorrow? When the thought comes — *does any of this even matter, would it really matter if I just drifted, just turned my face away a little* — answer it with the names. It would not cost Allah a thing. It would cost *you* everything. The leverage you imagined you had was always a fiction. The only one with anything at stake in your turning is **you.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole verse, which we could not see until we had walked through it.

It is built as two postures facing each other, with a hinge between them:

- **The example is set down** — *uswatun ḥasanah* — for **everyone**, *lakum.*
- **It is received** — by the one *leaning forward in hope*, *li-man kāna yarjū.* (The face turned **toward.**)
- **It is refused** — by the one who *turns his back*, *wa-man yatawallā.* (The face turned **away.**)
- **And the verse names what refusal costs** — nothing to Allah, *al-Ghaniyy al-Ḥamīd*; everything to you.

The hinge of the entire ayah is the **direction of a single turn** — and we saw it sitting inside the very root, *w-l-y*, that builds both *walī* (the intimate friend) and *tawallā* (the deserter). One root. One loyal, built-in, turning heart. Two directions. **That is the whole verse, and the whole surah, in three letters.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes were the same insight approached from two sides.

The grammar told us the example is *given to all but received by the hopeful.* The theme told us *we are imitating creatures who become what we behold — but only the reaching heart is truly changed.* The grammar showed us a single root holding both nearness and desertion. The theme showed us a heart built to be loyal, whose only real question is *which way it faces.* And the two divine names at the end revealed the secret that makes the whole thing bearable: this entire summons — example, hope, the turning of the heart — **was never for Allah's sake. He needs none of it. It was, from first word to last, a gift to you.**

### Questions to Carry

1. If someone watched only your attention for one week — not your words, your *attention* — whose life would they conclude is your *uswah*?
2. Is your heart, right now, *leaning forward* in hope toward Allah and the Last Day — or merely standing in the same room as the example, absorbing nothing?
3. Where in your life are you *turning away* without ever naming what you are turning from — calling it "keeping the peace," "being practical," "not making a scene"?
4. Have you secretly believed your devotion does Allah a favor? What changes when you sit with *al-Ghaniyy* — that He needs nothing from you, and calls you anyway?
5. The same heart that can make Allah its *walī* can *tawallā* away from Him. Today, which direction is it actually facing?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah lays the example of a hope-filled life before every single soul, but it only takes root in the heart that is leaning forward toward Him — and the one who turns his back impoverishes no one but himself, for Allah remains, without us, utterly free of need and endlessly praised.

### The Closing Invitation

You were never going to live without an *uswah* — you are made to become what you behold. So tonight is not an invitation to *find* a model. It is an invitation to stop letting one be chosen for you by accident, and to turn your face — deliberately, the way Ibrahim turned his — toward the One who asks everything of you while needing nothing from you. The reaching is the receiving. Lean forward, and the example becomes yours.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You are al-Ghaniyy — needing nothing from me — and still You set Ibrahim before me as a life to climb into. Make my heart one that hopes, leaning always toward You and the Last Day, so that the example does not merely pass before my eyes but takes root in me. And of the two directions of this one loyal heart, let mine never turn its back on You. Āmīn.*
