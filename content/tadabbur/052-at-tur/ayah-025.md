# When the Toil Is Over, the First Gift Is Each Other

### Surah At-Tur, Ayah 25

> [ayah:52:25] وَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ يَتَسَآءَلُونَ
>
> *wa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatasāʾalūn*
>
> **"And they will turn to one another, asking each other."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Room After the Storm

I want you to begin not in Paradise, but in a much smaller place.

Imagine a family that has just come through something terrible together. A long illness that finally broke. A war that finally ended. A night of fear that finally gave way to dawn. And now it is over. Everyone is safe. The danger has passed.

What do people do in that first hour of safety?

They don't usually celebrate loudly. They don't immediately go enjoy themselves. Watch what actually happens. **They turn to each other.** They sit close. And they start to talk — quietly, almost in disbelief. *"Do you remember when..."* *"I thought we wouldn't make it through that night."* *"How are you? How did you get through it?"*

The first instinct of people who have survived something together is not to consume the safety. It is to **face one another and speak.** To gather the survivors into a circle and recount, together, what they came through.

[PAUSE]

Hold onto that image, because that is exactly the scene Allah is painting in this ayah — except the storm that ended was the entire life of this world, and the safety that arrived has no end.

### The world of this surah

Surah At-Tur is a Makkan surah, revealed into a climate of mockery. The people of Makkah had a set of insults they threw at the Prophet ﷺ: a poet, a soothsayer, a madman. They denied that there was any reckoning coming at all. The surah answers that denial not with an argument but with a **vision** — it tears the veil off the two destinations and shows them in detail.

It opens with a storm of oaths: by the Mount, by the Book inscribed, by the frequented House, by the canopy raised high, by the sea set on fire. Then judgment falls — the deniers are driven to a Fire they once called a lie. And then, at verse 17, the surah turns, and the air changes completely.

Now we are among the *muttaqīn* — the God-conscious. We see them reclining on couches arranged in rows. We see fruit brought to them. We see a pure cup passed around that carries no idle talk and no sin. We see their families joined to them, their children brought up to their level. We see youths like guarded pearls moving among them, serving.

Course after course of delight. And then — verse 25.

After all the food, all the drink, all the comfort, Allah does not give us another physical pleasure. He turns the camera onto something quieter. He shows them **turning to one another.** Beginning to talk.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even open the word.

In a place of infinite enjoyment — where every appetite is answered the instant it arises — the very first thing Allah chooses to report *out of their own behavior* is not them eating, not them resting, not them marveling at the architecture.

It is them **facing each other and asking.**

Why? Of all the things you could show about people in Paradise, why is *conversation* the first thing they do with their own agency? And what could they possibly have left to ask, in a place where nothing is unknown and nothing is lacking?

[PAUSE]

Stay with those two questions. The whole ayah lives inside them.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

This is a short ayah — five words. But Allah has chosen each one with a precision that rewards slowing all the way down. We'll open three things: the way they *turn*, the way they *ask*, and the strange, beautiful word that sits between them twice.

---

### Element One: *Aqbala* — The Full Turning of the Face

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

There is a root in Arabic, **ق-ب-ل** (q-b-l), and it is one of those roots whose meaning fans out into an entire world. From it comes *qabila* — to accept. From it comes *qabūl* — acceptance. From it comes *qiblah* — the direction you face in prayer. From it comes *muqbil* and *mustaqbal* — the one coming toward you, and *the future itself*, literally "that which is approaching." And from it comes the word in our ayah.

What ties all of these together is a single physical idea: **the front. The face. To be turned toward.** The *qiblah* is what your face is set toward. The *future* is what is coming toward your face. *Acceptance* is opening your face to receive something.

Now the word here is **أَقْبَلَ** — *aqbala*. In the grammar of Arabic verbs, this is what's called **Form IV**, a pattern that takes the root and bends it toward motion and direction. *Aqbala* means: **to come forward, to advance, to turn toward, to face.** Not to glance. To turn the whole front of yourself in a direction.

Let me give you the everyday picture. You're sitting with someone, and they're half-looking at their phone, half-listening to you. And then something you say lands, and they put the phone down, and they *turn* — their shoulders square to you, their face comes up, their eyes settle on yours. Everything in them now points at you.

That movement — that is *aqbala*.

[PAUSE]

But there is more, and this is where the ayah does something exact. The word is not just *aqbala*. It is *aqbala ʿalā* — **turned toward, with the preposition *ʿalā*.**

In Arabic, **أَقْبَلَ عَلَىٰ** is an idiom, and the lexicons are clear about it. *Aqbala ʿalā fulān* doesn't just mean you physically faced someone. It means you **gave yourself wholly to them.** You devoted your attention to them. You engaged with them completely, with nothing held back and nothing divided. When you *aqbala ʿalā* someone, you are not multitasking. You are *all there.*

So read the ayah again now with this in your hands:

> **وَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ**
>
> *And they turned toward one another — wholly, with their full and undivided presence.*

Let's sit with this.

Think honestly about the last real conversation you had. Not the words. The *quality of attention.* How much of you was actually there? Were you fully turned, or were you — as almost all of us almost always are — partially elsewhere? Half-composing your reply while they spoke. Half-aware of the phone in your pocket. Half-pulled toward the next thing.

Here's what's remarkable. We have come to accept **divided attention as the normal condition of being with another person.** We have nearly forgotten what undivided presence even feels like, because we so rarely give or receive it. To be fully faced by another human being has become almost a foreign experience.

And the very first thing Allah reports about the saved, in the place of perfected joy, is that they have *aqbala ʿalā* one another. The distraction is gone. The half-presence is gone. For perhaps the first time in their entire existence, they are giving each other the whole of their attention.

[PAUSE]

The physical image is people turning their faces toward each other. But the unseen reality it points to is **the restoration of presence** — the healing of the fractured, scattered attention that the world fragmented in us. In Paradise, you are finally, completely *there* for the person in front of you. And they are completely there for you.

That is what *aqbala ʿalā* reveals: not that they faced each other, but that for the first time they were **undivided** while doing it.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: *Yatasāʾalūn* — The Asking That Goes Both Ways

Now to the last word, and it carries the heart of the ayah: **يَتَسَآءَلُونَ** — *yatasāʾalūn*.

The root here is **س-أ-ل** (s-ʾ-l), and you already know it. It's the root of *suʾāl* — a question. *Saʾala* — he asked. Simple, plain asking.

But the word in our ayah is not *yasʾalūn*, "they ask." It carries an extra layer built into it. In Arabic grammar this is **Form VI**, and I need you to understand what Form VI *does*, because the entire meaning turns on it.

Let me teach it cleanly. Form VI in Arabic — the pattern *tafāʿala* — is the form of **mutuality. Reciprocity. Action that flows back and forth between people.**

Take the root for "help," *ʿawn*. Plain, it gives you *aʿāna* — he helped someone. But put it in Form VI — *taʿāwana* — and now it means *they helped one another.* Mutual aid, going both directions.

Take the root for "correspond." Form VI gives you *tarāsala* — they wrote to *each other.*

Take "see." Form VI — *tarāʾā* — they saw *one another.*

So Form VI is the grammar of the circle. It is not one person acting on another. It is everyone, at once, acting on everyone — the action bouncing back and forth, no fixed giver and no fixed receiver.

Now apply it. *Yatasāʾalūn* does not mean "they ask." It means:

> **They ask one another. Each questioning each. The asking moving around the circle, in every direction at once.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel why this matters?

Picture two completely different scenes. In the first, there is a teacher at the front and students in rows, and the students ask the teacher questions. The asking goes one way — up. That would be *yasʾalūn*. Plain Form I.

In the second scene, there is no front and no rows. There is a circle of people, and everyone is leaning in, and the questions are flying in every direction — *"How did you...?"* *"And you, do you remember...?"* *"What about that time when...?"* Nobody is the audience. Everybody is in it. That is *yatasāʾalūn*. Form VI.

Allah chose the second scene.

This is not Paradise as a lecture hall, where the blessed sit and receive. This is Paradise as a **reunion** — a circle of equals turned toward each other, every one of them both a speaker and a listener, the conversation alive and moving among them all.

[PAUSE]

And now listen to the *sound* of the word. *Ya-ta-sā-ʾa-lūn.* Say it slowly. There's a long, drawn-out vowel in the middle — *sāā* — and the soft catch of the hamza, and then it opens out into *lūn*. It is an unhurried word. It stretches. It has no rush in it.

Compare it to the clipped, tight words the surah uses for fear and judgment. This word *lingers.* It has the texture of people who have all the time in the world — because they do. There is no clock in this conversation. No one needs to leave. Nothing is about to end. The very phonetics of *yatasāʾalūn* carry the leisure of eternity.

[PAUSE]

So here is what this single word reveals: the joy Allah is showing us is not the joy of *receiving.* It is the joy of **mutual exchange** — of being in a circle where you are fully a participant, never merely an audience, with all the time in existence to turn the conversation over and over among people you love.

---

### Element Three: *Baʿḍuhum ʿalā Baʿḍin* — "Some of Them, Upon Some"

Between the turning and the asking sits a small phrase that's easy to read past: **بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ** — *baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin.* Literally, "**some of them upon some.**"

The word *baʿḍ* means "a part," "a portion," "some." And here it's doubled — *baʿḍ* facing *baʿḍ*, some toward some. It's the standard Arabic way of building reciprocity at the level of the group: each part of them toward another part.

Now, I want to read this through something the methodology of reflection insists on: **read absence as carefully as presence.** So let me ask — what is *not* specified here?

Notice that the people are not named. They are not numbered. They are not ranked. There is no "the foremost asked the followers," no hierarchy, no seating chart. There is only *baʿḍ ʿalā baʿḍ* — an undifferentiated, distributed *some-to-some.* The phrase deliberately **dissolves every distinction** among them. It refuses to tell you who is greater and who is lesser in that circle.

[PAUSE]

Think about what that erases.

In this world, almost every gathering carries an invisible map of rank. Who speaks first. Who gets deferred to. Who is at the head of the table and who is near the door. We read these maps instantly and constantly, and they shape every word we dare to say. So much of our self-consciousness in company is really the exhausting work of locating ourselves on that map.

*Baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin* takes the map and folds it away. In the language Allah chose, there is no head of the table. There is just *some, turned toward some.* A circle with no front.

And there's one more thing the grammar quietly does. The word *baʿḍ* — "a part" — means that **each person is incomplete on their own.** A *baʿḍ* is a portion of a whole; it only makes sense in relation to the other parts. The phrase doesn't say "individuals were near individuals." It says *parts* turned toward *parts* — as if the saved are not a collection of separate, self-sufficient souls, but **limbs of a single body** that has finally been made whole by being brought together.

[PAUSE]

So this little throwaway phrase is doing real structural work. It tells us that the companionship of Paradise is **without rank, without distance, and without the loneliness of being a complete and separate self.** They are parts, finally joined. Some, turned toward some.

---

### The Bridge

Let's gather what these five words have shown us.

*Aqbala ʿalā* — they turned toward one another with their whole, undivided presence.

*Yatasāʾalūn* — they entered a circle of mutual asking, everyone a speaker and a listener, with all of eternity to talk.

*Baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin* — and in that circle there was no rank, no distance, no separate and lonely self — only parts of a single whole, finally turned toward each other.

The construction has shown us the *shape* of the joy. Now let's ask what it reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because there are two questions still hanging in the air from the very beginning, and they are the deepest part of this ayah.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why Companionship Is the First Thing They Do

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a study that gets quoted often — researchers followed a group of people across their entire adult lives, decades of data, trying to find what actually predicts a good and healthy life. Not wealth. Not fame. Not achievement. The single strongest predictor that came back, again and again, was the **quality of a person's close relationships.** The people who were most satisfied at the end were the people who had been most connected to others.

You don't need the study, really. You know it in your body. Think about the best moments of your life. I'd wager that almost none of them were moments you were *alone.* The best moments are nearly always *shared* — and often the sharing *was* the moment. A meal is not really about the food. A journey is not really about the destination. We are built, at the deepest level, for one another.

And yet — here is the ache — this world is almost designed to **starve** that need while pretending to feed it. We are more "connected" than any humans in history and lonelier than almost any. We have hundreds of contacts and few people who would *aqbala ʿalā* us — turn toward us, whole and undivided. We are surrounded and unseen.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the ayah back.

Allah has just spent several verses describing a Paradise of staggering physical delight. Couches. Fruit. A pure cup. Every pleasure of the body, perfected and unending. And then, at verse 25, when He finally shows us the blessed *acting on their own* — the first thing out of all the things He could show is **them turning to each other to talk.**

Do you see what that ordering is telling you?

It's telling you that in the hierarchy of joy, **companionship sits above consumption.** The food and the drink and the couches are real — but they are the *setting.* The thing that happens *in* the setting, the thing the saved themselves reach for first, is **each other.** Allah is quietly correcting our entire instinct about what Paradise — and therefore what *happiness* — actually is.

We imagine the reward as *things.* Allah shows us the reward is, at its heart, *people, fully present, with all the time in the world.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the movement that should stop you. The need you feel right now — the ache to be truly seen, truly faced, truly known by people who have time for you — **that ache is not a flaw to be managed. It is a signal of where you are from and where you are going.** You feel it because you were made for a reality where it is permanently, perfectly answered. The hunger is shaped exactly like the food that is coming.

This world keeps that hunger sharp and never quite fills it. It gives you crowds and withholds presence. It gives you contacts and withholds *iqbāl.* And the disappointment you feel at that — the loneliness in the middle of connection — is the world being honest with you about what it is, and the ayah being honest with you about what it is not.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means that when you give someone your undivided presence — when you put the phone down and *aqbala ʿalā* the person in front of you, square your shoulders, lift your face, and let them have the whole of your attention — **you are not doing a small kindness. You are doing a Paradise-shaped thing.** You are rehearsing, in this fractured world, the very behavior of the people of the Garden. Every time you truly turn toward another person, you are practicing eternity.

And the specific application is almost embarrassingly concrete: **the next conversation you have, give it your whole face.** No divided attention. No half-presence. Turn toward them the way the people of Paradise turn toward each other. You will be shocked at what one person's undivided presence does to another human being. You will be giving them a taste of the Garden.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: What Could They Possibly Have Left to Ask?

Now to the second question — the one that's been waiting since the introduction.

What do you *ask about* in a place where nothing is unknown and nothing is missing?

In this world, almost every question is born of a lack. We ask because we don't know. We ask because we're worried. We ask because we need something. *"Will we be okay?" "What's going to happen?" "How will we pay for this?" "Is the test result back?"* Strip away the questions that come from fear and need and ignorance, and how many of our questions are left?

So when Allah shows the people of Paradise *asking* — *yatasāʾalūn* — He is showing us a kind of question that has no lack underneath it at all. A question asked not from emptiness, but from *fullness.* What could that even be?

The ayah itself holds back the answer. Look — the verb *yatasāʾalūn* has **no stated object** in verse 25. Allah tells us they are asking, and then leaves the asking hanging, unspecified, for one more verse. We lean in, exactly as you lean in when you walk into a room and catch the middle of an intense conversation. *Asking about what?*

[PAUSE]

The answer comes in the very next breath, in verses 26 and 27. They say:

> *"Indeed, we were before, among our families, fearful. So Allah conferred favor upon us and protected us from the punishment of the scorching fire."*

There it is. **They are asking each other about the past.** About the world they left. About the fear they used to carry.

And now look at what the classical commentators do with this, because they circle the same reality from different angles, and together they paint one complete picture.

Al-Jalālayn says they ask one another about *"what they used to do and what they have now reached"* — and he names the spirit of it precisely: *taladhdhudh wa-ʿtirāf bi-l-niʿma* — **as a savoring, and as an acknowledgment of the blessing.** The asking is a form of pleasure and a form of gratitude at once.

Al-Baghawī, narrating from Ibn ʿAbbās, says they *"recall together what they used to be in — the toil and the fear, in the world."* The Arabic word he uses is *yatadhākarūn* — they reminisce, they bring it back up between them.

Al-Qurtubī gathers the threads: they *"recall the hardship and the fear of the outcome that they lived in the world, and they praise Allah for the lifting of that fear."* And he records one more flavor of the question — *"by what did you reach this lofty station?"* — *"How did you get here? What did you do?"*

And al-Muyassar pulls it all together: they ask one another *"about the magnitude of what they are in, and its cause."*

[PAUSE]

Put the camera angles together and one image stands clear.

**The conversation of Paradise is the remembering of the fear that is now over.**

They sit in perfect safety, turned toward one another, and they say — *do you remember? Do you remember how afraid we were? Do you remember the nights of dread, the fear of the ending, the trembling before our Lord? Do you remember how heavy it all was — and look. Look where we are now.*

Stay with this, because it is one of the most tender things in the entire Quran.

[PAUSE]

Why would they *want* to remember the fear? In a place of perfect joy, why drag up the hardest part of the past?

Because — and you know this from your own life — **a hardship that is truly over becomes the sweetest thing to remember together.** Think again of the family after the storm. Think of soldiers who came home, who for the rest of their lives seek each other out to retell the very worst nights — not because they want the fear back, but because *surviving it together* is the closest bond they will ever know. The remembered struggle is what makes the present safety *taste* of something. Comfort that was never threatened is bland. Comfort that was *rescued from terror* is overwhelming.

This is the genius of the scene. Allah does not let the people of Paradise simply *have* the joy. He lets them **savor** it — and savoring requires contrast, and the contrast is the remembered fear. Their gratitude is not abstract. It has a *texture*, and the texture is *"we were afraid, and now we are not."*

[PAUSE]

And notice the exact word they use for their old fear — in verse 26 it is *mushfiqīn*, from *ishfāq*: a fear mixed with tenderness, the trembling of someone who *cares* about the outcome, not the panic of a coward but the watchfulness of a lover who is afraid of disappointing the Beloved. **That** is the fear they carried in the world. And **that** is the fear they now sit and recall with wonder.

Here is the part that should land on you directly.

The fear you carry *right now* — the watchfulness, the *ishfāq*, the quiet dread of standing before Allah, the weight of taking your soul seriously — **that fear is not your enemy. It is the raw material of your eternal gratitude.** The very thing that feels heavy today is the thing the people of Paradise will reminisce about with joy. Your present *khawf* is their future *dhikr*. The trembling is the seed of the savoring.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you hold your own God-consciousness. Most of us experience the fear of Allah as pure burden — something we'd be lighter without. This ayah reframes it entirely. **You are, right now, living the chapter of the story that the people of Paradise will one day sit in a circle and lovingly retell.** You are *inside* the very night that becomes the cherished memory. The fear is not subtracting from your future joy — it is *building* it.

And the concrete application is this: the next time the weight of taking your faith seriously presses on you — the discomfort of resisting a desire, the dread of falling short, the loneliness of caring when others don't — **whisper to yourself that you are gathering material.** That this exact heaviness is what you will one day, fully safe, turn to your companions and say: *do you remember? Do you remember when we were afraid? And look where He brought us.*

The fear is temporary. The remembering of the fear is forever. And the remembering is *sweet.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Let's see how the two halves of this reflection are really one thing seen from two directions.

The **linguistic journey** showed us the *shape* of the scene: *aqbala ʿalā* — whole, undivided turning-toward; *yatasāʾalūn* — a circle of mutual, unhurried asking; *baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin* — a fellowship without rank or distance, parts of one whole finally joined.

The **thematic depths** showed us the *meaning* inside that shape: that companionship sits at the summit of joy, above every physical delight — and that the content of the joy is *remembering, together, the fear that is now forever over.*

And here is where they become one insight. **Why do they turn toward each other** *(aqbala)* **with their whole presence? Because of what they are about to do — recall the storm they survived together.** And **why is the asking mutual** *(yatasāʾalūn)*, **going in every direction at once? Because the memory belongs to all of them** — every single one of them was afraid, every single one was rescued, so every single one has both a story to tell and a story to hear. The grammar of reciprocity and the theme of shared survival are the same truth. The circle has no head *because the rescue had no favorites.*

The undivided turning and the remembered fear are not two scenes. They are one: **survivors of the same storm, finally safe, turning their whole faces toward each other to say — *do you remember?***

[PAUSE]

### The Architectural Reveal

Look now at where Allah placed this verse, and you'll see the design.

Verses 17-24 are all about what the people of Paradise *receive* — couches, fruit, drink, family, servants. Everything *comes to them.* They are, in those verses, beautifully *passive* — the recipients of an endless generosity.

Then verse 25 is the **hinge.** It is the first verse in the passage where they *act* — *aqbala*, they turn; *yatasāʾalūn*, they ask. And what is their first act? Not to consume more. To **face each other and remember Allah's mercy together.**

And then verses 26-28 give that conversation its words, ending on: *"Indeed, we used to call upon Him before. Indeed, He is the Most Kind, the Most Merciful."*

So the architecture moves: **receive His gifts → turn to one another → and let the turning become, in its very content, gratitude to Him.** The horizontal love between the companions and the vertical gratitude to the Lord are not separate. The companionship *is* the worship. They turn toward each other, and in doing so, they turn — together — toward Him.

That is the proof inside the structure: in Paradise, loving each other and remembering Allah are **the same motion.**

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. When was the last time someone gave you their whole, undivided face — and when was the last time you gave yours? What is the gap between those two answers telling you?

2. If the conversation of Paradise is the *remembering of fear now over* — what fear are you living through right now that is quietly becoming the material of your future gratitude?

3. You treat your God-consciousness, your *ishfāq*, as a weight. What would change if you held it instead as a *seed* — as the very thing you will one day reminisce about with joy?

4. We chase Paradise as a collection of pleasures. But the saved reach first for *each other.* Have you built, in this life, the kind of companionships that are worth keeping for eternity?

5. Who, in your life, survived a storm *with* you — and when did you last turn fully toward them and say, *do you remember?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the long fear of the world is finally over, the first thing the saved reach for is not the reward but each other — turning their whole faces toward one another to remember, together, the very dread that now tastes like the sweetest mercy.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is an invitation to live, now, the way you will live then.

To turn your whole face toward the people in front of you — undivided, unhurried, fully present — as a rehearsal of the Garden. And to stop resenting the fear you carry, and start *recognizing* it: as the night that becomes the cherished memory, the storm that makes the safety sweet, the trembling that is, even now, building the conversation you will one day have in a circle that has no end and no head and no rank — only some of you, turned toward some, asking.

You are already inside the story they will lovingly retell. Live this chapter as someone who knows how it ends.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who turn hearts toward one another, let our present fear of You be the seed of our everlasting gratitude. Make us, in this fractured world, people who give our whole faces to one another for Your sake — and gather us, when the long fear is over, into a circle that has no end, turning toward those we love, remembering Your mercy together. O Most Kind, O Most Merciful — let us be among those who one day say: we were afraid, and look where He brought us.*

*Āmīn.*
