# Leave Them Until the Day They Are Struck Down

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the silence they came out of.

> [ayah:52:45] فَذَرْهُمْ حَتَّىٰ يُلَـٰقُوا۟ يَوْمَهُمُ ٱلَّذِى فِيهِ يُصْعَقُونَ
>
> *Fa-dharhum ḥattā yulāqū yawmahumu-lladhī fīhi yuṣʿaqūn.*
>
> **"So leave them be — until they meet that Day of theirs in which they will be struck down senseless."**

> [ayah:52:46] يَوْمَ لَا يُغْنِى عَنْهُمْ كَيْدُهُمْ شَيْـًٔا وَلَا هُمْ يُنصَرُونَ
>
> *Yawma lā yughnī ʿanhum kayduhum shayʾan wa-lā hum yunṣarūn.*
>
> **"The Day their scheming will avail them nothing, nor will they be helped."**

Now — where do these words sit? Because this is one of those moments where the *position* of an ayah in its surah is half the meaning.

### Rebuild the world

Picture the surah you are standing inside. At-Tur opens like a thunderclap: oaths sworn on the Mount, on a Book inscribed, on the roof raised high, on the ocean set ablaze. And then Allah does something He does almost nowhere else in the Qur'an with this intensity. He begins to *interrogate* the deniers. Question after question after question. **Or were they created out of nothing? Or are they themselves the creators? Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Or do they own the treasuries of your Lord? Or do they have a ladder by which they eavesdrop on the heavens? Or do they have a deity other than Allah?**

It is relentless. It is the sound of every door being closed, one after another, until there is no room left to stand in. By the time you reach verse 44, the Qur'an says: even if they saw a piece of the sky falling down on them, they would call it "a heap of clouds." Meaning — the problem was never the evidence. They have decided. No fact will move them.

And so a person listening to all of this — and the first person listening was the Prophet ﷺ himself, exhausted, grieving, pouring himself out over people who would not budge — that person is left with a burning question:

*Then what do I do with them? If nothing reaches them — then what?*

[PAUSE]

And the answer comes in two words. Not a new argument. Not a stronger proof. Not a louder warning.

**فَذَرْهُمْ.** *Leave them.*

Here is the hook I want you to carry into everything that follows. The most strategically loaded command in this passage is not about *them* at all. It is about *you*. And hidden inside the word "leave" is a mercy you would never expect, and hidden inside the word "until" is a horizon that changes everything. Let's go and find them.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: فَذَرْهُمْ — the verb that has no past

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how commands work in any language.

When you tell someone to *do* something, you are loading them with a task. "Go. Build. Fight. Convince. Fix it." The weight transfers onto their shoulders. But there is a rarer kind of command — the command to *stop* doing. "Let it go. Put it down. Step back." And that command, strangely, can be far heavier than the command to act, because it asks you to surrender the one thing the ego clings to most: the feeling that *as long as I'm still pushing, I'm still in control.*

Now look at the word.

> **فَذَرْهُمْ** — *"so leave them."*

The root here is **و-ذ-ر** (*w-dh-r*), and it carries a beautiful peculiarity in Arabic that the grammarians have noted for centuries. This verb — *dhar*, "leave, let be" — barely uses its past tense at all. The Arabs let its perfect form fall almost entirely out of use, and lean instead on its present and its command: *yadharu*, *dhar*. **It is, in a sense, a verb that lives only forward.** You do not say "I left it" with this root nearly so much as you say "leave it" — pointing ahead.

Sit with that for a moment. The very word Allah chooses for releasing these people is a word that does not look backward. It does not relitigate. It does not say "after all you tried." It simply opens a door in front of the Prophet ﷺ and says: *forward, without them.*

Notice something else. The command is singular — *dhar-hum*, an order spoken to one man, the Messenger ﷺ. But the real subject of the sentence, the people in question, are *hum* — "them," third person, talked *about*, not talked *to*. Feel the shift that has happened across this surah. For verse after verse Allah was speaking almost *at* the deniers, question after question aimed straight at their chest. And now, suddenly, He turns away from them. He no longer addresses them. He speaks over their heads, to His Prophet, *about* them — as you might finally stop arguing with someone and simply turn to a friend and say quietly, "leave them." 

[PAUSE]

That turning-away is itself the verdict. To still be argued with is to still be pursued. To be handed over to "leave them" — *that* is the door closing.

And here is where I have to be honest with you about the scholars, because they noticed something in this word that matters. Al-Qurtubī records that some held *fa-dharhum* — "leave them" — to be **abrogated by the later verses of fighting**; that is, that its *legal force* as a standing instruction was superseded once other commands came. I want to be careful and precise: that is a juristic discussion about rulings, and scholars differed over it. It does not touch the thing we are here to reflect on. As a *spiritual posture* — the command to release what cannot be moved and entrust it to its appointed Day — this word has lost none of its weight. The fiqh of when-to-fight is one conversation; the heart's instruction to stop clawing at a closed heart is another. We are sitting with the second.

What does this single word reveal? **That sometimes the most obedient, most God-conscious thing you can do is to stop.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: حَتَّىٰ — the leash on the silence

Now I want to show you the smallest word in the verse, and I want to argue that it is doing the heaviest lifting.

> فَذَرْهُمْ **حَتَّىٰ** يُلَٰقُوا...
>
> "Leave them **until**..."

Let me teach the concept first. There is a world of difference between "let it go" and "let it go *until*." The first is abandonment — you release the thing into a void and it disappears into nothing. The second is *deferral* — you release the thing toward an appointed terminus. One is despair. The other is trust.

If Allah had simply said *fadharhum* and stopped — "leave them," full stop — the heart could have heard it as: *give up. It's hopeless. Nothing comes of any of this.* That is the reading the exhausted soul reaches for.

But Allah does not stop. He hangs a single word off the end of the command — **ḥattā**, "until" — and that word is a leash. It tells you the silence you are being asked to keep is not infinite. It has an end. **"Leave them" is not the end of the story; it is the pause before a scene that is already scheduled.**

Stay with this. The thing that makes patience unbearable is not the waiting. It is not knowing whether the waiting *leads anywhere*. We can endure almost any delay if we know there is a fixed point at the end of it. What breaks us is suspecting the delay is forever. And so Allah, in His mercy to a grieving Messenger, does not just say "stand down." He says "stand down *until*" — and points to a fixed horizon on which everything will be settled.

The patience He asks for is not the patience of a man shouting into an empty desert. It is the patience of a man who has been *told the appointment is on the calendar.*

[PAUSE]

What does this tiny word reveal? **That divine forbearance is never the same as divine forgetting.** Every "leave them" in the Qur'an comes with an "until." The reprieve has a clock.

### Element Three: يُصْعَقُونَ — the word that sounds like the thing it names

Now we arrive at the word I have been waiting to give you, and I want you to listen to it before you understand it. Say it slowly:

> **يُصْعَقُونَ** — *yuṣʿaqūn.*

Feel what your mouth has to do. The emphatic **ṣād** (ص), heavy and pressed against the roof of the mouth. The guttural **ʿayn** (ع) torn up from the throat. The deep, percussive **qāf** (ق) struck at the very back. Three of the most forceful, ground-shaking sounds in the entire Arabic phonetic inventory, packed into one word. **The word does not whisper. It detonates.** [PAUSE]

This is no accident. The root is **ص-ع-ق** (*ṣ-ʿ-q*), and from it comes *ṣāʿiqah* — the thunderbolt, the lightning-strike that splits the sky and drops a man where he stands. To be *maṣʿūq* is to be felled by that strike: knocked utterly senseless, the consciousness blown clean out of you in an instant.

This is the Qur'an's *istidlāl bil-maḥsūs* — its argument through the senses. Allah does not describe the unseen terror of that Day in abstract theological language. He reaches for the single most violent sensory event a desert dweller knew: the thunderbolt out of a clear sky, the flash, the crash, the body that simply *drops*. The physical image is the thunderstruck collapse. But the thunderbolt is not the destination — it is the stepping stone. What it is pointing to is a reality no word can carry directly: **the moment when every ounce of a person's self-possession, every shred of the composure they wore while explaining away the falling sky — is taken from them in a single instant.** Gone. Struck out of them.

Now here is the layer the reciters preserved for us. Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī both record that there are two established readings of this word. The common reading is *yaṣʿaqūn* — **they swoon, they fall senseless** — active, something *they* do. But Ibn ʿĀmir and ʿĀṣim read *yuṣʿaqūn* — **they are struck down, they are made to faint** — *passive*. And our written text here carries that passive form: *yuṣʿaqūn*.

Do not pass over this. In the passive, the collapse is not something they *do*. It is something *done to them*. There is a striker, and they are the struck. They spent their whole lives as the active ones — the planners, the explainers, the ones in control of the narrative — and on that Day the grammar itself flips. They become the object of a verb whose subject is left unnamed precisely because everyone already knows Who it is.

And what does *ṣaʿiqah* — the collapse — actually *mean* here? The scholars, beautifully, do not agree on a single moment, and al-Qurtubī gathers their voices like camera angles on one reality. Qatādah said: it is **the day they die.** Some said: it is **the Day of Resurrection,** when the terror strips them of their minds. Some said: it is **the first blast of the Trumpet,** at which all creation is felled. Some even read it as the day of a worldly reckoning. [PAUSE]

Do not hear these as competing theories you must choose between. Hear them as one truth seen from four windows. Death is a *ṣaʿiqah*. The Trumpet is a *ṣaʿiqah*. The Standing is a *ṣaʿiqah*. **They are all the same event repeating at different scales — the moment the composed self is struck down and can compose itself no longer.** The word holds all of them at once.

What does this word reveal? **That the very faculty they trusted to manage reality — their conscious, calculating, explaining mind — is exactly the faculty that will be switched off first.**

---

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have built. *Dharhum* — a command to stop, a turning-away that is itself a verdict. *Ḥattā* — a leash on the silence, a reprieve with a clock. *Yuṣʿaqūn* — a collapse that is done *to* them, stripping away the very self that did all the scheming.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the two things every human heart is quietly doing all the time: trying to *win*, and trying to *control*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The argument you were never meant to win

Let me start where you live.

You have had this conversation. Maybe with a sibling who left the deen. Maybe with a parent who won't hear you. Maybe with a stranger online at two in the morning, thumbs moving, certain that *this time* — this paragraph, this verse, this perfectly-worded point — will finally make them see. And it doesn't. It never does. And you go to bed hollowed out, replaying it, drafting the better version you should have said.

There is a specific kind of exhaustion that comes from pouring yourself into a heart that has already decided. It is not the tiredness of hard work. It is the tiredness of *futility* — of pushing against a door that was bolted before you arrived.

Now look again at where this ayah falls. It comes *after* the most overwhelming argument in the Qur'an. After Allah Himself has closed every door — *or were they created from nothing, or are they the creators, or do they own the treasuries* — after the proof has been made airtight by the One whose proof cannot be improved upon. And what comes next? Not a better argument. **فَذَرْهُمْ.** Leave them.

Let that land. If *Allah's* perfect, unanswerable case did not move them — then the failure of *your* words to move someone was never a failure of your words. [PAUSE] You were holding yourself responsible for an outcome that was never assigned to you. The Prophet ﷺ himself, the most eloquent of creation, carrying revelation itself, was told: *your job is the delivery, not the verdict. Deliver, and then — release.*

There is a movement here from the verse before. In 52:44 the deniers see the sky falling and call it clouds. The very next breath is *fadharhum.* The Qur'an is teaching a sequence: when you reach the person who would explain away a falling sky, you have reached the boundary of your task. Past that line, more effort is not more sincerity. **Past that line, more effort is you trying to do God's job for Him.**

And notice the mercy folded inside the command. "Leave them" is not "hate them." It is not "curse them." It is the verb that only looks forward — *dhar* — the one with no past tense to drag behind it. You are not being told to carry a grudge. You are being told to *set the weight down* and walk on, unburdened, toward your own appointment with your Lord.

[PAUSE]

**What changes tomorrow?** The next time you feel that compulsion to send one more message, win one more point, fix one more heart that has shown you it will not be fixed — pause and ask: *Have I delivered? Then is the rest mine to carry, or is it His?* Make the dʿuā, make the delivery beautiful — and then practice the hardest worship there is: *fadharhum.* Hand them, gently, to the One who holds their appointed Day. That is not giving up. With the "until" attached, it is the deepest form of trust.

### Theme Two: The day the contingency plan fails

Here is the second place you live, and you may not even know you live there.

Every one of us is running a quiet, ceaseless operation in the back of the mind. We call it being *prepared*. The backup plan. The exit. The savings cushion, the second option, the thing I'll say if this goes wrong, the lever I can pull if I'm cornered. It is the human addiction to *kayd* — to having a scheme in reserve. We are, all of us, constantly arranging the furniture of our circumstances so that we are never truly without a move.

Look at the word the Qur'an chooses for the deniers:

> يَوْمَ لَا يُغْنِى عَنْهُمْ **كَيْدُهُمْ** شَيْـًٔا
>
> "The Day their **scheming** will avail them nothing..."

**كَيْد** (*kayd*), from the root *k-y-d*, is not random action. It is *cunning*, *stratagem*, the cleverly arranged plan, the move designed to secure an outcome. It is the human ego's masterpiece: *I have engineered my own safety.* And the verse says that on that Day this masterpiece will not *yughnī ʿanhum shayʾan* — will not avail them, will not enrich them, will not ward off from them *a single thing.*

Look at the structure of verse 46, because it is built with terrifying symmetry, and the symmetry is the whole point. Two clauses. The first: **their own scheme** — the thing they *do for themselves* — fails. *Lā yughnī ʿanhum kayduhum.* The second: **nor are they helped** — *wa-lā hum yunṣarūn* — the rescue that *comes from outside* fails too, and notice it too is passive, *yunṣarūn*, "they are helped," by a helper left unnamed because there will be none.

Read the absence here as carefully as the presence. A human being in trouble has exactly two hopes: *I'll figure something out myself,* or *someone will come for me.* The inner resource and the outer rescue. And this verse, in one balanced breath, **closes both doors at once.** The scheme from within: useless. The help from without: absent. [PAUSE] There is no third option. The Qur'an has mapped the entire space of human hope and shown it empty — on that Day, for those people.

Now connect this back to the word we already opened: *yuṣʿaqūn.* Why is the contingency plan useless? Because of *what the Day does to them.* A scheme requires a schemer — an alert, calculating mind to deploy it at the right moment. And the very first thing taken on that Day is the mind itself. *Struck down. Senseless.* You cannot execute your clever exit when the consciousness that holds the plan has been blown out of you like a candle. **The thunderbolt does not just defeat the plan; it removes the planner.**

Here is the paradox, and I want to let it sit without rushing to soften it. The thing we trust most — our capacity to manage, to arrange, to have-a-move-in-reserve — is precisely the thing that will be most thoroughly stripped from us, and at the exact moment we would most want it. The illusion is not that we have *no* control. In this life we have a little, and it is real, and it is a gift. The illusion is that the control is *ultimately ours* — that when it matters most, *kayd* will be there to save us. It won't. *Lā yughnī ʿanhum kayduhum shayʾan.*

[PAUSE]

**What changes tomorrow?** Not that you stop planning — the believer plans, and ties the camel. What changes is *what you ultimately lean on*. Every contingency plan you build, build it with an open hand, knowing it is a tool and not a god. And let the one preparation that actually carries across the *ṣaʿiqah* be the one the deniers neglected: not a cleverer scheme, but a relationship with the One who holds the Day. Because when *kayd* is switched off and no helper comes, the only thing left standing is what you sent ahead to Him.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now step back and see how these two ayahs are built, because the structure is the proof.

Verse 45 is a command and a horizon: *leave them* — **until** — *the Day they are struck senseless.* Verse 46 then takes that Day and zooms all the way in: *the Day their scheming avails nothing, nor are they helped.* The second verse is a microscope held over the single word *yawm* — "Day" — at the end of the first. Allah names the appointment, and then He shows you, in close-up, exactly what is *missing* from the deniers in that moment: their composure (struck senseless), their cunning (availing nothing), their rescue (no helper). Three subtractions. The Day is defined entirely by what is *taken away.*

And here is the symmetry that ties the whole passage to its surah. The deniers spent forty verses being *active* — explaining, dismissing, scheming, calling the falling sky "clouds." Across these two ayahs, every active verb is quietly turned passive. *They are left.* *They are struck.* *They are not helped.* The grammar enacts the theology: **the ones who thought they were the authors of their fate become, in the end, the objects of verbs they do not control.**

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two directions.

The word *dharhum* — the verb with no past — taught you to *release the argument.* The word *kayduhum* — the scheme that avails nothing — taught you to *release the control.* And these are the same act. To stop clawing at a closed heart and to stop clutching your own contingency plan are both the same surrender: *taking your hands off an outcome that was never yours, and placing it on the One whose "until" is already on the calendar.* The deniers in this passage refused that surrender on both counts — they would not yield the argument, and they trusted their own *kayd* to the end. The verse shows you where that road terminates: struck senseless, scheme useless, no helper. The mercy is that you are being shown the road *now*, while you can still choose the other one.

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life am I still pushing on a door that was bolted before I arrived — and calling that "sincerity"?
2. When I say *fadharhum* and walk away from an argument, can I do it the way the verb does — looking forward, with no grudge dragging behind?
3. What is my *kayd* — the contingency plan I secretly lean on more than I lean on Allah?
4. If my conscious, calculating mind were switched off in an instant, what would be left of me to meet my Lord?
5. Do I treat divine forbearance toward the wrongs I see as divine *forgetting* — and has that quietly eroded my trust that the "until" is real?

### One-sentence distillation

When you have delivered the truth and the heart will not move, your worship is to release them to the One who has already scheduled the Day their composure, their cunning, and their rescue will all be taken away — so that you carry neither the grudge nor the illusion that control was ever yours.

### The closing invitation

Tonight, find the one argument you are still secretly fighting — the person, the outcome, the thing you keep replaying — and try saying the word over it. *Fadharhum.* Not in despair. In trust. Hand it, with an open palm, to the One who holds its appointed Day. And then notice how much lighter you walk toward your own.

### Du'ā

Loosen my grip on my own *kayd*, that I may lean on You and not on my schemes; and when the Day comes that strikes every composure senseless, let me be among those who sent something ahead to You, and not among those whose plans availed them nothing.
