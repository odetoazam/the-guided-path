# The Dare That Was Never Answered

> [ayah:52:34] فَلْيَأْتُوا۟ بِحَدِيثٍ مِّثْلِهِۦٓ إِن كَانُوا۟ صَـٰدِقِينَ
>
> *Fa-l-ya'tū bi-ḥadīthin mithlihi in kānū ṣādiqīn*
>
> **"Then let them produce a statement like it — if they are truthful."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sentence Before the Sentence

I want to begin not with this verse, but with the one that comes right before it. Because you cannot feel the weight of this reply until you have heard the insult it answers.

In the verse just before, **52:33**, Allah quotes the deniers of Makkah. He tells us what they were saying about the man standing in front of them reciting:

*"Or do they say: **he has invented it**?"*

Sit inside that accusation for a moment. The word the Quran uses elsewhere for this charge is *iftirāʾ* — fabrication, invention, making something up out of nothing and pinning it on God. It is one of the ugliest things you can accuse a human being of. It says: *you are a liar, and not an ordinary liar — you are a liar in the name of the Most High. You took your own words, your own ambition, your own imagination, and you stamped God's name on them.*

Now — I want you to imagine the emotional climate of that moment. This is Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ is reciting in a city that **lives and breathes language**. These are not a people impressed by poetry the way we are impressed by it. Poetry was their science, their sport, their nobility, their war. They hung their finest poems on the walls of the Kaʿbah. A single line of verse could raise a tribe's honor or destroy a man's reputation forever. They had ears so fine-tuned to Arabic that they could detect a forced syllable the way a master musician detects a note slightly out of tune.

And these connoisseurs of the word — these grandmasters of Arabic — heard the Quran, and they did not say "this is bad." They could not say that; their own ears would have betrayed them. They said something far more revealing. They said: *he made it up.* They reached past the words to attack the man, because the words themselves they could not touch.

[PAUSE]

So here is the situation. A man is accused of forgery. The most serious forgery imaginable. He is surrounded by the greatest forgers of words the Arabic language has ever produced — and they are the jury.

How do you answer that accusation?

You might expect a defense. *No, I did not invent it. Here is my proof. Here is my character. Here are the years you have known me as al-Amīn, the trustworthy one.* All of that would have been reasonable.

But that is not what comes. What comes in **52:34** is one short line, and it does something I want us to spend the next hour understanding. It does not defend. **It dares.**

*"Then let them produce a statement like it — if they are truthful."*

Here is the hook I want to leave hanging over everything we do today. There is a way of answering an accusation that is so confident it actually hands your accuser the weapon and says: *go ahead — prove me wrong. The means are in your hands. The language is your language. The pen is yours.* And there is something about the **silence that follows** such a dare that is louder than ten thousand arguments.

That silence has now lasted fourteen hundred years.

Let's understand how four words built it.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Command That Travels Through a Third Person — *fa-l-ya'tū*

### First, the concept

Let me teach you something small about how commands work in language — in any language — before we touch the Arabic.

There are two ways to tell someone to do something. The first is direct: *"Bring me the book."* I am speaking to you, face to face, second person. The second is indirect, issued through a third party: *"Let him bring the book."* I am not speaking to him at all. I am speaking *about* him, to someone else, and the command reaches him sideways.

Now feel the difference in tone. *"Bring the book"* is a request between equals or near-equals. But *"let him bring it"* — there is a distance built into it. A kind of dismissal, even. Think of a king who does not address a petitioner directly but turns to his court and says, *"Let him state his case."* The third-person command carries authority precisely *because* it does not stoop to direct address. The speaker is so far above the matter that he issues the order over the head of the one who must obey it.

Hold that feeling. We will need it.

### Now, the word

Look at the opening of our verse:

> فَلْيَأْتُوا۟ — *fa-l-ya'tū*

This is built from the root **ʾ-t-y** (Aty), which means *to come*, and with the preposition *bi* that follows it, *to come with* — that is, *to bring, to produce, to bring forth*. The verb itself is in the imperfect form, but it is bent into a command by a small, easily-missed letter at its front: the **lām of command**, *lām al-amr*. That little *li* turns "they bring" into **"let them bring."**

And here is the first thing to notice. **Allah is not addressing the deniers.** He does not say *"bring a statement like it!"* — second person, to their faces. He says *"let them bring"* — third person. He is speaking *about* them, over them, to the listening world.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because it is doing real work.

If Allah had addressed them directly — *"O you who reject this, bring something like it!"* — it would have been a confrontation between two parties. A duel. Me against you. And a duel, however lopsided, still grants the opponent the dignity of being an opponent.

But the third-person command does not grant that. *"Let them bring it."* It is the posture of a sovereign so utterly unthreatened that He does not even turn to face the challenger. He announces the terms to the gallery. The deniers are not interlocutors here; they are a case being adjudicated in front of everyone. The *li* puts them at arm's length and leaves them there.

Notice something else. A command, by its nature, assumes the one commanded is *capable* of obeying. When I say "let him bring the book," I am presupposing the book is bringable, that he has hands, that the thing is possible. So when Allah commands them to produce the like of the Quran, the command itself wears the mask of possibility. *Go ahead. The floor is yours.* It is offered as though it could be done.

And that is the trap of mercy inside it. Because the whole power of the verse depends on what happens when capable people, handed every tool, holding every advantage, **do not move.** The command opens a door and steps back. The silence in the doorway is the proof.

### What this one word reveals

The very grammar of *fa-l-ya'tū* — a command issued in the third person, over their heads, to the world — stages the entire scene. It is not God arguing with men. It is God setting a challenge on the table and turning to the rest of us to watch whether anyone picks it up.

## Element Two: The Smallest Possible Demand — *bi-ḥadīthin*

### First, the concept

Here is a principle about challenges. **The strength of a challenge is inversely related to how much it asks for.**

Imagine two people defending the same claim. The first says: *"No one can paint exactly like me — using my custom paints, my private studio, my exact brushes, on my specific canvas."* The second says: *"Here is a pencil. Here is ordinary paper. Draw anything that matches mine."*

Which one is more confident? Obviously the second. The first hedged the challenge with conditions, narrowing the field until victory was guaranteed by the rules. The second stripped every advantage away from himself and handed it to the rival. *Use anything. Use the most ordinary tools in the world. I still say you cannot.*

The less you demand, the more terrifying your confidence. Keep that in mind.

### Now, the word

What exactly is Allah commanding them to produce?

> بِحَدِيثٍۢ — *bi-ḥadīthin* — "a statement," "a piece of speech," "a discourse."

Now — stop. I want you to notice what this word is *not*. Allah does not say *"bring a Qurʾān like it."* He does not say *"bring a revelation like it"* or *"bring a sacred book like it."* He uses the most ordinary, unglamorous, everyday word available: **ḥadīth.** Speech. Talk. A statement. The kind of thing every human being produces every single day from the moment they wake.

And it is **indefinite** — *ḥadīth*, not *the* ḥadīth. *A* statement. **Any** statement. There is no qualifier narrowing it down, no specification of length, subject, or style. The challenge could not be drawn more broadly or more generously. *Bring any piece of speech of the same caliber. That's all. Just words. You have words, don't you?*

[PAUSE]

Here is the genius of choosing this word, and I want us to go slowly.

The root of *ḥadīth* is **ḥ-d-th** (Hdv), and its core meaning is **newness** — something that came into being, that did not exist before and now does. From this same root comes everything in Arabic that means *new, fresh, recently originated.* A *ḥadīth* is "new speech" — an utterance freshly brought into the world.

Now feel the precision of the placement. What was the accusation in the verse before? *He invented it.* He **originated** it himself. He brought something **new** out of his own mind and called it revelation. They are saying: *this is merely new human speech.*

And Allah takes the exact category of their accusation — new speech, originated talk, *ḥadīth* — and says: **fine. Granted. Treat it as new speech. Now bring new speech like it.** He meets them precisely on the ground they chose. He does not argue the category. He says: *you call it invented human discourse? Then you, the masters of invented human discourse, invent its equal.*

There is no escape route in this framing. They cannot say "but it claims to be from God, that's an unfair advantage" — because Allah has set the bar at the level of *mere speech.* He has removed every claim of sanctity from the demand. He is not asking them to match a miracle. He is asking them to match a **sentence**. Words against words. Arab against Arab. Tongue against tongue.

And that is the most dangerous thing He could have asked, because it removes every excuse. If the challenge had been *"bring a book revealed by God,"* they could shrug — *we cannot manufacture revelation.* But *"bring a statement like this one"*? That is not asking for the supernatural. That is asking them to do their job. Their *one* job. The thing their entire civilization was built to do better than any people on earth.

[PAUSE]

The scholars note that the Quran's challenge appears in more than one form across the revelation — elsewhere the like of the whole Book (17:88), or ten chapters (11:13), or even a single chapter (10:38, 2:23). And here, in At-Tur, it stands in its **broadest, most accommodating** form: not a chapter, not a book — just *a statement like it.* Bring **anything** comparable. The field is wide open.

And it has stayed open. The masters of the language, in their own golden age, with every motive of pride and survival pushing them to silence this man with a single competing surah — produced nothing. Not because they lacked the words. They had all the words. They produced nothing because the words, arranged that way, were not theirs to arrange.

### What this one word reveals

By choosing *ḥadīth* — the smallest, most ordinary, most generous possible demand — Allah strips Himself of every advantage and hands them every one. And a challenge that gives the opponent everything and *still* goes unanswered is not really a challenge anymore. It is a verdict.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Word of Doubt and the Tense of the Past — *in kānū ṣādiqīn*

### First, the concept

In Arabic there are two main words for "if." This is subtle, but the Arabs felt it in their bones, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

One word is **idhā**. *Idhā* is the "if" of expectation — really, the "if" of *when*. *"When the sun rises."* *"If — and it surely will — the rain comes."* It is used for things considered likely, even certain.

The other word is **in**. *In* is the "if" of doubt, of the unlikely, of the hypothetical that the speaker does not really expect to happen. *"If — though I doubt it — you ever climb that mountain."* When a speaker chooses *in* over *idhā*, he is quietly signaling that the condition is improbable in his eyes.

Remember that difference. Now watch which one Allah chooses.

### Now, the words

> إِن كَانُوا۟ صَـٰدِقِينَ — *in kānū ṣādiqīn* — "if they are truthful."

He chooses **in**. The "if" of doubt. Not *idhā kānū* — "when they prove truthful," as though it were expected. But *in kānū* — *if, hypothetically, against all likelihood, they should turn out to be truthful.* The very particle casts a shadow of improbability over their truthfulness before the sentence even finishes. The word "if" is already passing judgment.

[PAUSE]

But there is a second blade here, and it is hidden in the verb.

> كَانُوا۟ — *kānū* — from the root **k-w-n** (kwn), "to be."

This verb is in the **perfect tense** — the past. Literally, *"if they **were** truthful."* Not *"if they are"* in some timeless present, but a form that reaches into the settled, completed past. Their truthfulness is framed as a matter already concluded — a finished question — rather than a live possibility hanging in the air.

And then the final word:

> صَـٰدِقِينَ — *ṣādiqīn* — "truthful ones."

This is an *active participle* from the root **ṣ-d-q** (Sdq) — the great root of *truth, sincerity, the correspondence between what is in the heart and what is on the tongue.* A *ṣādiq* is not merely someone who states a true fact. A *ṣādiq* is someone whose **claim matches reality** — whose words can be cashed out, backed up, made good. *Ṣidq* is truthfulness you can stand on.

Now put the whole phrase together and feel what it does. *In kānū ṣādiqīn* — *"if they were truthful."* Truthful in what? In their accusation. In the claim they made one verse earlier: *he invented it.* That was their statement of fact. They said: *this is human speech, fabricated by a man.*

And Allah's reply attaches a price tag to that claim. *You say it is fabricated human speech? A truthful claim is one you can back up. So back it up. If your accusation is true — if you are people of ṣidq and not just people of noise — then the production of a matching statement should be trivial. It follows directly from your own premise. You said a man made this. Fine: you are men. Make its like.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is the deep mechanism of the verse.

He has bound their **accusation** to a **test**, and made the test the proof of the accusation. Their claim was: *this is producible human speech.* Well — *produce it.* If you are truthful in your claim, the claim itself guarantees you can meet the challenge. And if you cannot meet the challenge... then your own accusation has collapsed in your hands. The failure to produce a *ḥadīth* like it is not a side issue. It is the **refutation of everything you said.**

This is why the *in* — the "if" of doubt — and the past-tense *kānū* are so devastating. The grammar already knows the outcome. Allah is not anxiously waiting to see whether they are truthful. The shadow of improbability over *in*, the settled finality of *kānū* — the sentence is built like a door already closing. He frames their truthfulness as the unlikely, all-but-finished thing it is, and then walks away and lets the empty page they leave behind say the rest.

### What these words reveal

*In kānū ṣādiqīn* turns the accusation into a wager the accusers cannot win. It says: *your charge and your inability to meet this challenge cannot both stand. Choose. And your silence has already chosen for you.* The truthfulness of the deniers is staked on a production that never came.

[PAUSE]

## The Bridge

Look at what these four words have built together.

A command issued in the third person — *fa-l-ya'tū* — that stages the whole scene as a verdict announced to the world, not an argument between rivals. A demand reduced to its smallest, most generous form — *bi-ḥadīthin* — *just speech, any speech,* removing every excuse and every advantage. And a condition — *in kānū ṣādiqīn* — that binds the deniers' own accusation to a test they cannot pass, with a grammar that already knows they will fail.

This is not the language of a man defending himself against forgery. **No forger talks like this.** A forger lowers his head, marshals his alibi, hopes the question goes away. He does not turn to the experts and say: *the floor is yours, the tools are yours, the language is yours — match me.*

So now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the nature of truth, the nature of confidence, and the strange and terrible power of an unanswered dare.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Confidence That Hands You the Weapon

Let me start where you already live.

You have met two kinds of confident people in your life, and you can tell them apart instantly. The first kind is *defensive-confident.* They assert loudly. They control the conversation. They never let you finish a sentence. They protect their claim by never letting it be tested — they change the subject, they attack you, they raise their voice. Deep down, you sense, they are afraid. The fortress walls are high because there is something fragile inside.

The second kind is something else entirely. They make a claim — quietly — and then they do the most unnerving thing. They *invite the test.* They say: *don't take my word for it. Check. Here's all my data. Here's my method. Try to break it.* A scientist who publishes not just his result but every step of how to reproduce it — and then says, to the entire hostile world, *go ahead, try to disprove me.* That is not the confidence of a loud man. That is the confidence of someone who has *seen the truth* and knows it will survive any test you throw at it, because the truth is not his — he is only pointing at it.

[PAUSE]

The most confident thing a person can do is **hand their critics the means of their own destruction** — and watch the critics fail to use it.

Now look again at our verse with that lens.

*"Let them produce a statement like it — if they are truthful."*

This is the second kind of confidence, raised to the absolute. Allah does not raise the fortress walls around the Quran. He does the opposite. He throws every gate open. He says: here is the language — it's Arabic, the language you've mastered for centuries. Here are the tools — words, grammar, eloquence, all of it native to you, none of it native to him. Here is the bar — not a miracle, just *a statement.* Here are the experts — you, the finest poets on earth, the jury and the contestants at once. *Now break it.*

And then — He waits. And the waiting itself is the argument.

Let the paradox sit for a moment, because it is genuinely strange. **The Quran proves itself by inviting attack.** It does not say "do not question this." It says "question it as hard as you can — produce its rival — and notice what happens when you try." Its security is not in being shielded from challenge. Its security is in being *unbreakable under challenge.* Those are opposite postures, and only one of them belongs to the truth.

Now build with me, because there is a deeper movement here.

Why could they not do it? They had every motive. Understand the stakes for the Makkans: this man was dismantling their gods, their economy, their social order, their ancestral pride. A single surah that matched the Quran would have ended him overnight. It would have said, to all of Arabia, *see — any one of us can do this; he is no prophet, just a clever poet among poets.* The reward for meeting the challenge was the survival of their entire world. The motivation could not possibly have been higher.

And the *means*? They were the undisputed champions of the very thing being asked. This is not a case of asking a child to lift a boulder. This is asking the strongest men alive to lift a stone they lift every day — and watching their hands close on empty air.

[PAUSE]

So what does the silence mean?

It means the thing in front of them was not of their kind. You can imitate what is like you. A poet can imitate a poet; a human can match a human; like answers like. But you cannot imitate what comes from a different order of being altogether. The reason the masters of speech could not produce *a statement like it* is the same reason a candle cannot produce a sunrise. Not because the candle is dim — it makes real light — but because the sunrise is not more candlelight. **It is a different thing entirely.** The challenge was never really about skill. It was about *source.* And the silence revealed the source.

### What this changes tomorrow

Here is what I want you to carry out of this. We live anxious about our faith in a world that loves to test it. We feel we must defend, deflect, protect, keep the hard questions away from the fragile thing inside. But this verse models a different posture entirely. **Truth does not need a bodyguard.** The Quran did not survive fourteen centuries by being shielded from scrutiny; it survived by inviting it and outlasting it.

So the next time a hard question comes at your faith — from a skeptic, from the internet, from inside your own restless mind at 2 a.m. — remember the posture of this verse. You do not have to be the loud, defensive, fortress-walls kind of believer. You can be the other kind. The kind who says: *I don't have every answer, but I am not afraid of the question, because I am pointing at something that does not depend on my ability to defend it.* The Quran handed its enemies the weapon. You can afford to let the question be asked.

## Theme Two: The Price Tag on a Claim — *Ṣidq* in an Age of Cheap Words

Begin again where you live.

We are drowning in claims. Open any feed and you will be told a hundred things in five minutes, each stated with total confidence, almost none of them backed by anything. *This causes that. They did this. The truth is hidden. Trust me.* Words have never been cheaper to produce and harder to verify. A sentence costs nothing now. You can accuse anyone of anything, in public, in seconds, and walk away while the accusation does its work — and never once be asked: *can you back that up?*

We have lost something in this flood, and it has an old name. The Arabs called it **ṣidq.**

*Ṣidq* is not just "telling the truth" in the narrow sense of not lying about a fact. *Ṣidq* is the **integrity between your claim and reality** — the quality of a word that can be cashed. A *ṣādiq* is someone whose statement has *substance behind it*, whose tongue writes no check his reality cannot pay. The opposite of *ṣidq* is not only the deliberate lie. It is the **empty claim** — the assertion made loudly and abandoned the moment someone asks you to stand behind it.

[PAUSE]

Now return to the deniers in 52:33. *"He invented it."* Feel that, now, as a *claim.* A statement of fact, hurled in public. *This is fabricated. This man made it up.* It cost them nothing to say. It was the cheap word, the loud accusation, the assertion flung and abandoned.

And the entire force of our verse is this: **Allah picks up their abandoned claim and asks them to pay for it.**

*"...if they are truthful."* If you are people of *ṣidq* — if your accusation is a real claim and not just noise — then there is a bill attached, and here it is. You said this is fabricated human speech. A truthful claim can be tested against reality. *So test it.* Fabricate its like. If you are *ṣādiqīn*, the test is trivial, because your own claim guarantees the result.

Do you see what He has done? He has installed a **price tag on the accusation.** He has refused to let the cheap word stay cheap. He has said: *you do not get to make this claim and walk away. A claim is a debt. Pay it, or admit you never had the funds.*

And they could not pay. The accusation, tested, bounced. *In kānū ṣādiqīn* — and they were not, because *ṣidq* is the word that survives the test, and theirs did not.

[PAUSE]

Now let this turn inward, because it must.

It is very easy to read this verse as being about *them* — the long-dead deniers of Makkah, the people of empty words. But the Quran does not preserve their failure as a history lesson. It preserves it as a **mirror.** Because the disease of the cheap claim is not theirs. It is ours. It is *mine.*

How many things do I assert that I have never tested? How many opinions do I hold loudly that would collapse the moment someone said *back it up*? How much of my own faith — and here is the hard part — how much of my own faith is *claim* without *ṣidq*? I say *I believe.* I say *I trust Allah.* I say *this life is nothing and the next is everything.* These are enormous claims. And the test of a claim is whether reality matches it — whether my fear, my spending, my time, my anxiety, my late nights actually look like the life of someone who believes what he says he believes.

[PAUSE]

This is the most uncomfortable place this verse can take us, so let's not flinch from it. The challenge *"if they are truthful"* is not only aimed at the enemies of the Quran. It is the question the Quran puts to every reader, including the believer. **Are you a ṣādiq?** Not — do you say the words? Everyone says the words. But does your reality back the words? Is there *ṣidq* between your tongue and your life, or have you, too, made a loud claim you have quietly failed to fund?

There is something merciful in being asked this, though. Because the deniers were asked to produce something *impossible* — the like of divine speech — and they could not, and the door closed on them. But the believer is asked to produce something *possible*: a life that matches the claim. Imperfectly, slowly, with stumbling — but really. The challenge to them was a verdict. The same challenge to us is an *invitation.* We are still able to bring our reality closer to our words. The page is not yet finished.

### What this changes tomorrow

Here is the practice I want to leave you with. Take **one** claim you make — to yourself, to God. *"I trust Allah with my provision."* *"The next life matters more to me than this one."* Pick the one you say most and examine least. And then, gently, like Allah testing a claim against reality, ask: *where is the ṣidq?* Find one place tomorrow — one decision, one expense, one hour, one worry surrendered — where you make your reality *match the word by even a single degree.* That is what it means to be a *ṣādiq.* Not to never have a gap between word and life, but to refuse to leave the gap unfunded. To keep paying down the debt of what you have claimed.

Because in the end, faith is the one claim where Allah does not ask us to produce the impossible. He asks us to produce a life. And that, by His mercy, is bringable.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of today meet.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a sentence built like a verdict: a command over their heads, a demand reduced to nothing, a condition that already knew its answer. The whole architecture of the grammar was the architecture of unshakable confidence — the speech of One who is not defending, but *deciding.*

And the **thematic journey** showed us what that confidence *is.* It is the confidence that hands the critic the weapon and outlasts the attack — because the truth does not belong to the one defending it; he is only pointing. And it is the confidence that puts a price tag on every claim, demanding *ṣidq*, the integrity between word and reality — first from the accusers, who could not pay, and then, in the mirror, from us, who still can.

These are the same insight from two directions. **The grammar is the confidence, and the confidence is the truth.** A man defending a forgery shrinks his claims and hides from the test. The Quran broadcasts its claim, shrinks the demand on its rivals to nothing, throws open every gate — and waits. Only one kind of speech can afford to do that. Not the speech that *says* it is from God. The speech that, when the whole world is invited to imitate it and cannot, *shows* that it is.

The accusation was: *a man made this.* The reply was: *then you — the masters of making — make its like.* The silence that answered is still answering.

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. I do not have your answers.

1. When my faith is challenged, am I the believer of high fortress walls — loud, defensive, afraid of the question — or the believer who can let the question be asked because I am pointing at something that does not depend on me?

2. What is one claim I make about Allah, loudly and often, that I have never once tested against the way I actually live?

3. The deniers were asked to produce the impossible and failed. I am asked to produce a *life* that matches my words — which is possible. So where, exactly, is the gap between my tongue and my reality, and what is one degree I could close it tomorrow?

4. Have I ever truly *let* the Quran challenge me — opened it as the thing that tests me — or have I only ever read it as the thing I defend?

5. If my own truthfulness — my *ṣidq* — were put on trial by my calendar and my bank statement and my private worries, what verdict would the evidence return?

## One-Sentence Distillation

When they accused him of inventing it, Allah did not defend the Quran — He handed the masters of language the pen, dared them to invent its equal, and let the silence that followed stand as the proof no argument could equal.

## The Closing Invitation

I am not going to ask you to win an argument today. The argument was won fourteen hundred years ago, in an empty page that no master of Arabic could ever fill.

What I am inviting is something quieter. A shift in posture. Stop standing guard over the Quran as though it were fragile — and start standing *under* it, as the thing that examines you. Let it be the dare again. Not *"can anyone match this?"* — that question is settled. But the harder, more personal one it whispers to every reader who claims to believe: *if you are truthful — show me a life that matches your word.*

That challenge is still open. And unlike the one given to the deniers, this one you are able to meet.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You whose speech no tongue could ever rival — let me not be among those whose claims outrun their lives. Place ṣidq between my words and my reality, so that when I say I believe in You, my hours and my fears and my choices say it too. And make Your Book not the thing I defend, but the light that tests me, until what is on my tongue and what is in my heart are finally one.*

*Āmīn.*
