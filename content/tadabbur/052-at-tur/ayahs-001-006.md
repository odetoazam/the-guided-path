# The Five Witnesses and the Unnamed Hand

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first the way it would have first reached an ear in Makkah — slowly, with the drum of its sound intact.

> [ayah:52:1] وَٱلطُّورِ
> *wa-ṭ-Ṭūr*
> By the Mount,
>
> [ayah:52:2] وَكِتَـٰبٍ مَّسْطُورٍ
> *wa kitābin masṭūr*
> and by a Book inscribed,
>
> [ayah:52:3] فِى رَقٍّ مَّنشُورٍ
> *fī raqqin manshūr*
> in parchment unrolled,
>
> [ayah:52:4] وَٱلْبَيْتِ ٱلْمَعْمُورِ
> *wa-l-Bayti-l-Maʿmūr*
> and by the much-frequented House,
>
> [ayah:52:5] وَٱلسَّقْفِ ٱلْمَرْفُوعِ
> *wa-s-saqfi-l-marfūʿ*
> and by the canopy raised high,
>
> [ayah:52:6] وَٱلْبَحْرِ ٱلْمَسْجُورِ
> *wa-l-baḥri-l-masjūr*
> and by the sea set brimming.

Did you hear it? Read the endings again, just the endings: *Ṭūr… masṭūr… manshūr… Maʿmūr… marfūʿ… masjūr.* Six oaths, and every single one closes on the same heavy, resonant sound — that long *ūr* that lands like a drumbeat, six times, before the surah has told you a single thing. We will come back to that sound, because it is doing real work. Hold it for now.

### Rebuild the world

To feel why these verses exist, you have to stand where the first listeners stood.

Makkah. A small group of people have begun to say something the city finds offensive — not that there is a God; the Makkans believed in Allah as a creator. The offensive claim was narrower and stranger: that you will be raised after you die, gathered, and made to account for every dirham and every word. *That* is what they laughed at. They would pick up a crumbling, decayed bone, crush it to powder between their fingers, let the wind take it, and say: *this — this will live again?* Resurrection was, to them, the punchline. The one absurdity.

And so much of the early Quran is aimed precisely there — at the man holding the crumbled bone.

Now here is a moment I want you to actually picture, because the early biographers preserved it and it tells you what these verses *do* to a heart. **Jubayr ibn Muṭʿim** — at the time still a polytheist — came to Madinah after the Battle of Badr to negotiate the release of prisoners. He arrived while the Prophet ﷺ was leading the Maghrib prayer, and the Prophet was reciting this very surah, Sūrat aṭ-Ṭūr. Jubayr, a hardened man on enemy business, stood and listened. Later he described that moment. When the recitation reached the verses that ask *"Were they created from nothing? Or are they the creators?"* — he said, **"It was as if my heart was about to fly."** (Reported in the Two Sahihs.) That recitation was among the threads that eventually drew him to Islam.

I tell you that not as trivia. I tell you so you understand: this surah was *built* to land on a resistant heart. And it begins not with the argument that cracked Jubayr open — that comes later — it begins with **six oaths**. Allah is winding the spring before He strikes.

### The Hook

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows.

The God who owns all of existence, who needs no witness, who is believed by no one's belief and harmed by no one's denial — **why is He swearing?** Why does the Creator of the Mount take an oath *by* the Mount? You and I swear because we are not trusted and cannot guarantee the future. What does it mean when the One who guarantees everything raises His hand to swear?

And a second thing, quieter, that almost no one notices on a first reading. Of these six things Allah swears by, look at the words: *inscribed… unrolled… frequented… raised… set brimming.* Five of the six are things that have been **done to**. Written by someone. Unrolled by someone. Filled by someone. **By whom?** The verses never say. There is a hand all over these verses, and it is never named.

Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The wāw that is not an "and": the oath of the One who needs no oath

Before we touch the verse, let me put an everyday thing in your hand.

In ordinary speech, when do *you* swear? You swear when your plain word isn't enough. "I'll pay you back." That's a statement. "I swear I'll pay you back" — now something has changed. The oath appears precisely at the point where you sense the other person's doubt. You are reaching for something larger than yourself, something you hold sacred, and you are staking it on your claim. *Let this thing I love be forfeit if I'm lying.* An oath is a transfer of weight. You borrow the gravity of something heavy to press down on a claim someone is tempted to let float away.

Now. In Arabic there is a tiny word that does this. The **wāw al-qasam** — the *wāw* of oath-taking. It looks identical to the ordinary *wāw* that means "and." Same single letter: **وَ**. But here it is not joining; it is *swearing*. When it sits before a noun in this construction, pulling that noun into the genitive case — *wa-ṭ-Ṭūr-**i*** (notice the *-i* ending) — every Arab ear instantly hears: **an oath is being sworn.**

So the surah does not begin "And the Mount…" It begins **"By the Mount! — I swear by the Mount…"**

Let's open it.

> **وَالطُّورِ** — *By the Mount!*

The root is **Ṭ-w-r**, and *aṭ-Ṭūr* simply means "the mountain." But it is *the* mountain, definite, and the four tafsīrs we lean on are unanimous about *which* one. Al-Jalālayn: «الجبل الذي كلم الله عليه موسى» — "the mountain upon which Allah spoke to Mūsā." Al-Muyassar concurs, naming it the mountain upon which Allah addressed Mūsā, peace be upon him. This is **Mount Sinai. The mountain of the conversation.** The single place on this earth where Allah spoke directly to a human being and that human heard the speech of his Lord.

Sit with the choice. Allah could have sworn "by the mountains" — by their mass, their permanence, their thrones of granite. He does that elsewhere. Here He swears by *one* mountain, and He selects it not for its rock but for its *event*. **He swears by the place where the unseen broke through into the seen.** The Mount is not being honored as geology. It is being honored as the site where revelation touched the earth — where a man climbed up an ordinary slope and came back down carrying the words of God.

Do you feel what's already happening? The very first oath is a stepping stone. The physical thing — a mountain — is pointing past itself to an unseen reality: **Allah speaks. Revelation is not a poet's invention. It happened, on a mountain, to a man named Mūsā, and here it is happening again to a man named Muḥammad ﷺ.**

Now the hook returns: *why* swear at all? The classical scholars give an answer worth holding. Allah does not swear because *He* needs the oath to be believed. He swears to **arrest you**. An oath is a hand on the shoulder. It says: *stop — what comes after this is not ordinary speech, brace yourself.* When you hear a sober person suddenly say "I swear to you," your whole posture changes; you lean in. Allah is doing to the denier exactly that. He is reaching for the heaviest objects in creation — a sacred mountain, a divine book, a heavenly house, the sky, the sea — and stacking them up, one on top of another, six high, so that by the time the claim arrives in verse 7, the listener is leaning all the way in.

But the scholars who traced the purposes of this surah — al-Muyassar names it explicitly among the surah's *maqāṣid* — observe that this oath-chain has a second audience besides the denier. The Makkans were not only skeptics of resurrection; they were actively charging the Prophet ﷺ with fabrication. They called his words invented. They said: a poet, a soothsayer, a man who dreams and calls it revelation. And so when Allah swears by Mount Sinai — the mountain where He spoke to Mūsā, where no one disputes that revelation came down — He is also answering that accusation before verse 7 has been reached. *These are My oaths, staked on the greatest things in creation, on behalf of this speech.* A man fabricating words cannot conscript a divine oath to their defense. The oath-chain grips the denier and vindicates the Messenger in the same breath.

And there is a second, gentler layer the scholars note. To swear *by* a thing is to **honor** it. Al-Qurṭubī says of the Mount that Allah swore by it «تشريفًا له وتكريمًا» — "to ennoble and honor it." So the oath does two things at once: it grips the listener, and it crowns the thing sworn by. When Allah swears by the Mount, He is also telling you: *look again at this mountain you walk past. There is more here than rock.*

[PAUSE]

What the first oath reveals: **the One who needs no witness is calling witnesses — not for His sake, but to wake you.**

### Element Two — Five things that were *done to*: the Hand the verses refuse to name

This is the heart of the passage. I have to teach you one grammatical shape first, and then I promise the verses will open like a door.

In Arabic, as in English, you can build a word that means "the one who does" or "the one done to." *Teacher* and *taught*. *Writer* and *written*. The first kind — the doer — Arabic calls the **active participle** (*ism al-fāʿil*). The second — the one acted upon — is the **passive participle** (*ism al-mafʿūl*). And here is the thing about the passive: **it hides the doer.** Say "the written book," and a question opens behind the word that the word itself will not answer — *written by whom?* The passive participle is a finger pointing at an agent it refuses to name.

Hold that. Now walk back through the six oaths with me and *watch the word-shapes*.

- *aṭ-Ṭūr* — the Mount. (Just a noun. It simply **is**.)
- *masṭūr* — **inscribed.** Passive participle, root *S-Ṭ-R*. Written-by-someone.
- *manshūr* — **unrolled.** Passive participle, root *N-Sh-R*. Spread-open-by-someone.
- *al-Maʿmūr* — **made-full, frequented.** Passive participle, root *ʿ-M-R*. Filled-by-someone.
- *al-marfūʿ* — **raised.** Passive participle, root *R-F-ʿ*. Lifted-by-someone.
- *al-masjūr* — **set brimming / set ablaze.** Passive participle, root *S-J-R*. Filled-or-kindled-by-someone.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it now? **Five of the six are passive participles.** Five times Allah names a thing not by what it is but by *what was done to it*. And every single one of those five carries the same suppressed question on its back: *by whom?*

The Book — *masṭūr* — written. By whom?
The parchment — *manshūr* — unrolled. By whom?
The House — *al-Maʿmūr* — thronged, kept full. By whom, with whom?
The sky — *al-marfūʿ* — held up. By whom?
The sea — *al-masjūr* — brimmed to the edge, or set to burn. By whom?

The verses do not answer. Not once. The agent is veiled the whole way through. And that veil is not an accident of grammar — **it is the message.** Because the listener cannot help but supply the missing word. With every passive participle the heart whispers the answer the tongue was not given: *by Allah. By Allah. By Allah. By Allah. By Allah.* The grammar makes you say His name in the silence.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* in its purest form — reasoning from the sensible to the intelligible. You can see the book; you cannot see the writer. You can see the raised sky; you cannot see what holds it. You can see the brimming sea; you cannot see what fills it. Allah lines up five visible *done-to* things, each one a window, and behind every window is the same unseen, unnamed, un-pointed-at **Doer** — and your own grammar drags your heart to Him.

Let's stay with two of these, because each opens onto something.

> **وَكِتَابٍ مَّسْطُورٍ ۝ فِي رَقٍّ مَّنشُورٍ** — *and by a Book inscribed, in parchment unrolled.*

*Masṭūr* comes from *saṭara* — to write in **lines**, ruled, ordered, straight. Not scrawl. Lines. And *raqq* is fine, thin parchment — the most lasting writing-surface the ancient world knew, *manshūr*, **unrolled and open**, not sealed away. Put the picture together: a text written in perfect ordered lines, on permanent skin, and held *open* for reading. Al-Muyassar identifies this Book as the Qurʾān itself, «القرآن في صحف منشورة» — the Qurʾān in unrolled pages; other scholars include the preserved Tablet and the registers of deeds. The composite image is one thing: **a record that is fixed, ordered, lasting, and open.** Not whispered and gone. Written. And what is written does not blow away with the man's crumbled bone in the Makkan wind. Hold that thought; it returns in our second theme.

> **وَالْبَيْتِ الْمَعْمُورِ** — *and by the much-frequented House.*

*Al-Maʿmūr*, from *ʿamara* — to be full of life, inhabited, kept up, never abandoned. A *maʿmūr* place is the opposite of a ruin. Al-Muyassar and the classical commentators identify *al-Bayt al-Maʿmūr* as a House **in the heavens**, directly above the Kaʿba, around which the noble angels make ṭawāf without ceasing. In a narration the Prophet ﷺ gave during the Night Journey (in al-Bukhārī and Muslim), seventy thousand angels enter it each day **never to return** — a fresh seventy thousand the next day, and the next, forever, so vast are the ranks of the worshippers of Allah. *Maʿmūr* — kept full. By whom? By an ocean of devotion you have never seen and never will, churning above your head right now as you read this.

[PAUSE]

That is the gem of this passage. **The whole oath-series is built on grammatical passives whose Agent is deliberately hidden — so that creation testifies to a Creator it will not name, and your own heart is made to name Him.**

### Element Three — The drum: *masjūr*, and the six-fold *ūr* that binds the cosmos into one chord

Come back now to the sound I asked you to hold at the very beginning.

*Ṭūr… masṭūr… manshūr… Maʿmūr… marfūʿ… masjūr.*

Six oaths. Six rhymes on the same deep *ūr*. This is not decoration; in the Arabic, **sound is argument.** Consider what Allah has gathered into this single rhyme: a *mountain* on the earth, a *book* of revelation, a *house* in the heavens, the *roof* of the sky, the *sea* in the depths. Earth and heaven, scripture and ocean, the highest thing and the lowest thing — and the recitation **forces them all to chime on one note.** The sound itself is saying what the meaning is saying: *all of this is one.* One chord. One Maker. The sound of the surah is a cosmology: everything real chimes together because everything real comes from the same Source.

Notice something else. The surah's own name is *aṭ-Ṭūr* — the very first word, the very first oath. The whole surah takes its name from the note it opens on. And the last oath — *al-masjūr* — closes the chain on that identical note. The six oaths do not just rhyme internally; the series is framed, first to last, by the same sound the surah itself is named for. **The beginning and the end ring in unison.** Before the surah has mounted a single argument, it has demonstrated through sound what it is about to argue: that everything, from first to last, belongs to the One who made it.

Then there is that sixth oath. The one that closes the series and that deserves a moment all its own.

> **وَالْبَحْرِ الْمَسْجُورِ** — *and by the sea set brimming.*

*Al-masjūr* carries a weight the translation cannot hold in one word, because the root *S-J-R* carries two meanings that pull in different directions. It means **to fill** — as you fill a vessel to its brim, as a harbor swells with water. And it means **to kindle** — *sajara al-tannūr*, "he stoked the furnace," he lit the oven and fed it until it burned. Both meanings are lexically attested, and the scholars of tafsir disagreed about which the verse intends. Some said: the sea is *masjūr* because it is full, held at its margins by nothing except Allah's permission, a restraint that should itself occasion wonder. Others said: the sea is *masjūr* in the sense of kindled — not as it is now, but as it will be then. And the related ayah leaves little ambiguity about what that "then" means: *"When the seas are set ablaze"* (**وَإِذَا الْبِحَارُ سُجِّرَتْ**, 81:6) — the same root, the same passive form, an event on the Day the world ends.

[PAUSE]

Both readings are at work at once. The sea is already vast, brimming, and held at its edge; that is the present oath, the witness that stands before you now. And the sea will be set alight on the Last Day; that is where the spring of these oaths is aimed. The sixth oath stands with one foot in the world you can see and one foot in the world that is coming. It is the hinge between the six witnesses and the verdict of verse 7. *Allah swears by the sea as it is — and what you see in it is already the sign of what it will become.*

## PART 2: THE CASE THAT CREATION BUILDS

Return now to the man holding the crumbled bone.

He has a question he thinks is unanswerable: *how*? How does a God — any God — take this powder, this dust, this handful of nothing that was once a man, and make it a man again? He is not asking in good faith; he is asking to mock. But the question is real, and the Quran takes it seriously. And the six oaths of Sūrat aṭ-Ṭūr are already answering him — not with syllogism, but with *witnesses*.

The *jawāb al-qasam* — the **answer to the oath**, in Arabic rhetorical convention — does not arrive until verse 7: *"Indeed, the punishment of your Lord will surely come to pass."* But before that verdict lands, Allah has called six things to the stand. And each one, if you listen, is testifying to something specific that the man with the crumbled bone denied.

**He denied that records are kept.** The Book (*masṭūr*) answers him: records are already kept. Written in lines. On parchment that does not decay. Unrolled and open. Not a rumor, not a tradition whispered through generations — *written*, in the most permanent medium the ancient world knew. The accounting has not been completed yet. But the records are already made.

**He denied that the unseen world is real.** The House (*al-Maʿmūr*) answers him: the unseen is more populated, more alive with devotion, than any city he has ever walked through. Seventy thousand angels move through a single heavenly house every single day, an infinite rotation of worship, and not one of them has ever returned — so vast is the queue of those who want to enter and praise. The man who dismisses the unseen because he cannot see it is standing beneath a ceiling that never stops echoing with prayer, and he calls it silence.

**He denied that Allah would track individual lives.** The parchment (*manshūr*) — *open*, not rolled shut — tells him: it is already open. It is being read. Nothing has been sealed away to be forgotten. What is written is *manshūr*, displayed and accessible, like a scroll in a court of law that the judge has already unrolled and set before him.

**He denied that any power could reconstruct what had crumbled.** The raised sky (*al-marfūʿ*) answers: look up. What holds that there? The sky does not sit on columns you can point to; it is *raised*, held aloft by the same Power whose capacity you doubt. The thing keeping the roof above your head has never once let it slip. You question whether Allah can restore a body from dust — while the demonstration of His preserving power is literally above you, at every moment of your life.

**He denied that the Day of Judgment would actually arrive.** The sea (*al-masjūr*) answers with its double testimony. As it stands now, it is a warning — vast, brimming, held at its edge by a decree you did not write and cannot revoke. As it will be then, it is the verdict: what is full will overflow, what is brimming will be set ablaze. The seas of this world will become the fuel of the next. The thing you pass every day in boats and think you know — you do not know it. You do not know what it is being held ready for.

[PAUSE]

This is the architecture of the oath-section. Six witnesses. Five of them built in the passive voice so your own grammar fills in the Agent. And together they are not merely impressive objects Allah selected for their grandeur. They are **calibrated** — chosen so that each one, traced carefully, answers a specific strand of the denial the surah exists to address.

When verse 7 finally arrives — *إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّكَ لَوَاقِعٌ* — "Indeed, the punishment of your Lord will surely come to pass" — it is not the first thing said. It is the last thing said, after six witnesses have already established the ground. The spring has been fully wound. The claim is loaded before it is fired.

### The argument you cannot argue against

There is something particular about the structure of an oath that makes it difficult to dismiss.

If Allah had opened this surah with "The punishment is coming — here is why," you could engage with the argument. You could pick at its premises. You could say: *but you haven't proven that records are permanent,* or *but you haven't established that the sky is truly held up by divine will.* Argument invites counter-argument. That is how argument works.

But an oath is not an argument. An oath is a **staking of something sacred**. When you hear a man swear — seriously, gravely, by the thing he loves most — the question that rises is not *is his reasoning sound?* The question is: *does he believe this enough to stake that on it?* And what Allah stakes His oath on is the mountain of revelation, the book of revelation, the house of angelic worship, the sky, the sea. He is not offering a syllogism. He is offering six of the most undeniable things in creation and saying: *I swear by all of these that the judgment is coming. Dispute my reasoning if you wish. But first, dispute the Mount.*

The man with the crumbled bone cannot dispute the Mount. He cannot argue that the sky is not raised. He cannot deny the sea. And so the oaths have done their work: they have transferred the weight of the undeniable onto the claim. **The judgment has borrowed the certainty of creation.**

Al-Muyassar notes among the surah's purposes that it then challenges the deniers to produce something like this Quran, and refutes one by one the accusations they level against the Prophet ﷺ. But that challenge could only land after the oaths had first established an uncontestable ground. You cannot challenge a man to a contest he can walk away from. The oaths are the walls of the arena.

## CLOSING: THE MAN ON ENEMY BUSINESS

Come back to Jubayr.

He came to Madinah on enemy business. He came to negotiate, to secure terms, to perform a transaction and return. He was not there to be changed. He was not standing in the mosque because he wanted to be in a mosque. He was standing in the vicinity because business required it, and the recitation found him before he could leave.

The surah did not begin with the argument that would eventually crack him — the *"Were they created from nothing?"* verses that made his heart fly. It began with six oaths. Six things he already knew and could not deny: a mountain, a book, a house in the heavens, a sky, a sea. By the time the argument arrived, the oaths had already done something to him that he could not have named in that moment. They had stacked up, six high, in a sound that never changed (*ūr… ūr… ūr… ūr*), and they had pressed him into readiness. The spring was wound. The argument, when it came, had somewhere to land.

That is what an oath does. It does not convince the mind before the heart is ready. It prepares the heart first.

And if you are reading this with any distance from these words — any part of you that has ever held a crumbled thing and wondered whether there is anyone keeping track, whether any of it matters, whether the accounting will come — then these six verses are doing the same thing to you that they did to Jubayr. They are not asking you to believe yet. They are asking you to *notice*. The mountain. The book. The house above you full of those who will not stop. The sky, still up. The sea, still in its place.

[PAUSE]

*By these things — by all of these — the word that follows is true.*

That is what Allah is saying. And the man on enemy business heard it, and something in him began to move.

*What comes next: verse 7 fires the spring — إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّكَ لَوَاقِعٌ — "Indeed, the punishment of your Lord will surely come to pass" — and from there the surah goes to both destinations: the fire and the garden, the criminals driven to Jahannam and the believers settled in bliss. Al-Muyassar names this contrast as the surah's second great purpose — threat answered by promise, warning paired with the most vivid descriptions of what Allah has prepared for those who believed. The six oaths were not the end of the case. They were only the beginning of the wind-up.*
