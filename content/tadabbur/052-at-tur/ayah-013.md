# The Thrust Returns: When the Hand That Shoved Is Shoved

> **يَوْمَ يُدَعُّونَ إِلَىٰ نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ دَعًّا**
>
> *Yawma yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama daʿʿā*
>
> "The Day they will be thrust toward the Fire of Hell with a violent thrust."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Verse

Before we open this ayah, we have to feel the room it was first spoken into.

Picture Makkah. A small community of believers — poor, mostly. Among them, freed slaves. Among them, orphans with no clan to protect them, no father's name to stand behind. And across from them, the chiefs. The men of standing. The men who, when one of these believers approached — when a ragged orphan or a stammering poor man came near their gathering — would do a particular thing with their hand.

They would wave it away. **Shove it off.** A flick of contempt. *Get back. You don't belong near us.*

The Arabic has a word for that gesture. Not "reject." Not "refuse." A more physical, more bodily word — the word for shoving someone in the chest, driving them back by force, pushing them away as you would push away something beneath your notice.

That word is **daʿʿ** — دَعّ.

And in the surahs revealed in that same period, Allah had already used it to draw the portrait of the man who denies the Day of Judgment. In Surah al-Māʿūn He asks: have you seen the one who calls the Reckoning a lie? *"Fa-dhālika alladhī **yaduʿʿu** al-yatīm"* — **that** is the one who **shoves away** the orphan (107:2). Same word. The denier of accountability is identified, fingerprinted, by a single gesture: the way his hand drives the helpless back.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that gesture in your mind. The hand that shoves.

Because here, in Surah At-Tur, on the Day those same men insisted would never come, Allah describes their arrival at the Fire. And He reaches — out of the entire Arabic language — for **the exact same word**.

*Yawma **yudaʿʿūna** ilā nāri jahannama **daʿʿā**.*

**The Day they are shoved — thrust, driven, repelled by force — into the Fire, with a shoving.**

The hand that shoved is now the body being shoved.

That is the verse. Six words. And almost everything in it is already living inside one doubled, heavy, violent root. Let me show you what is buried in here — because once you see it, you will not be able to un-see it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: A Word Built From a Doubled Letter

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verb.

Most Arabic words are built on three different consonants — a root of three distinct letters. *K-T-B* gives you writing. *S-J-D* gives you prostration. Three different sounds, three different doors.

But there is a special category of roots where **the second and third letters are the same** — they double back on themselves. Grammarians call these *al-afʿāl al-muḍāʿafa*, the doubled or "geminate" verbs. The root folds a letter onto itself, and when you pronounce it, the two identical letters merge into a single stressed, drawn-out, weighted sound — marked in writing by the *shadda*, that small symbol that means *press here, hold here, double this.*

Think of the difference in English between "miss-stated" and "misstated" — when you actually lean on that doubled 's', the word slows, thickens, gets heavier in the mouth.

Now look at the root of our verb. It is **د-ع-ع** — *dāl, ʿayn, ʿayn*. The second and third letters are the same: ʿayn folded onto ʿayn.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The central word of this verse — the word for being thrust into the Fire — is built, in its very bones, on a **doubled, repeated, hammering letter.** Before the verse intensifies it grammatically, before any meaning is added, **the root itself is already a doubling.** The violence is in the skeleton of the word.

And the ʿayn is no gentle sound. It is a deep, constricted sound made low in the throat — guttural, pressed, almost a grunt of effort. *Daʿʿ.* Say it. It is the sound of a shove. It is the sound the body makes when it strikes something, or when something strikes it. **The word sounds like what it means.** This is *istidlāl* through the ear — Allah lets you *hear* the thrust before you understand it.

What does the root mean? The lexicographers and the classical commentators are unanimous and concrete. Al-Qurṭubī writes: *"daʿaʿtuhu adaʿʿuhu daʿʿan, ay dafaʿtuhu"* — "I daʿʿa-ed him, meaning **I shoved him**." Al-Baghawī glosses *yudaʿʿūna* with a single word: *yudfaʿūna* — they are pushed, driven, repelled. And both of them, along with the broader tradition, add the texture: *bi-ʿunfin wa-jafwah*, **with violence and harshness.** Al-Muyassar adds one more layer that we must not miss — *bi-ʿunfin wa-mahānah*: with violence **and with humiliation.**

So this is not the neutral physics of falling. This is not "they enter" or "they arrive." This is being **handled with contempt.** The *daʿʿ* is the shove you give to something you consider beneath you — something not worth the dignity of being led, only worth being driven.

Hold that. We will return to it.

---

### Element Two: They Are Shoved — The Stolen Hand

Now notice something the English barely shows you. In Arabic, the verb is *yudaʿʿūna* — and it is in the **passive voice.**

Let me make sure we feel what passive voice does. "He shoved" — active; he is the one with the hand, the agent, the one who acts. "He **was** shoved" — passive; now he is the object, the thing acted upon, the body with no say. Active voice is the voice of the one who *does.* Passive voice is the voice of the one to whom things are *done.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable.

In the world — in Makkah, in that gathering — these men were the **subjects** of the verb *daʿʿ.* They were the ones who shoved. *Yaduʿʿu al-yatīm* — **he** shoves the orphan. Active. The orphan was the object; the chief was the agent. He had the hand. He had the power. He decided who was driven back and who was let near.

And now? *Yudaʿʿūna* — **they are shoved.** Passive. The hand is gone. The agency is gone. The man who spent his life as the one-who-drives-others-back has become the one-who-is-driven. He is now occupying the exact grammatical position he once forced upon the orphan: the **object** of someone else's force.

Notice what Allah does *not* tell us. He does not name who is doing the shoving. The verb is passive; the agent is hidden. The commentators fill it in from the tradition — the *khazanat jahannam*, the stern guardians of the Fire, who (in the imagery al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī relay) bind their hands to their necks and drive them forward on their faces. But the *Qurʾān itself* leaves the shover unnamed. Why?

Because the point is not *who* shoves him. The point is that **he is now the one being shoved.** The identity of the new hand does not matter. What matters — what the silence forces you to feel — is the total reversal of his position in the sentence. He has lost the subject's chair. He has been moved, grammatically and really, to the object's place.

This is what Arabic can do that a flat translation cannot. The reversal is not stated as a moral; it is enacted in the **grammar.** The man who lived in the active voice dies into the passive voice.

---

### Element Three: A Shoving With a Shoving

And then Allah does one more thing — and this is the seal on it.

The verse does not end at *yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama* — "they are shoved into the Fire of Hell." That would already be complete. Instead, one more word follows: **daʿʿā** — دَعًّا.

This is a grammatical structure called the *mafʿūl muṭlaq* — what we might call the **cognate accusative.** Arabic has this device where you take a verb and then immediately follow it with a noun *from the very same root,* purely to intensify and confirm it. We have a faint echo of it in old English: "he dreamed a dream," "she lived a life," "they died a death." You don't add information — you add *weight.* You stamp the action down. You say: *and I mean it.*

So *yudaʿʿūna... daʿʿā* is, woodenly, **"they are shoved... with a shoving."** They are thrust with a thrust. They are driven with a driving.

Hear what the doubling does. The verb already carries the geminate, doubled root. Then the cognate noun repeats that *same* doubled root *again.* The ear receives the harsh ʿayn-on-ʿayn sound **twice in four syllables**: *yuda-ʿʿū-na... da-ʿʿā.* The verse hammers the same blow, then hammers it again. The *form* of the sentence is itself a repeated shove.

[PAUSE]

And here is the quiet devastation in it. The grammarians say the *mafʿūl muṭlaq* most often serves *taʾkīd* — emphasis, certainty, *this absolutely happens, make no mistake.* Remember the room this was spoken into. These were the men who called the Day of Judgment a **lie.** Who treated it, as the previous verse says, as *khawḍ* — idle, playful chatter (52:12). The whole reckoning was, to them, a thing that would *never really happen.*

So Allah does not merely tell them it happens. He **doubles down on it grammatically.** *Daʿʿā.* And I mean it. With a shoving — a real, actual, no-question-about-it shoving. The very mockery they aimed at the Day's reality is answered by a grammatical structure whose entire job is to insist: **this is real.** You said it was empty talk. Here is the word that exists only to say: *it is not.*

---

### The Bridge

Step back and see what these six words have done with one root repeated three times.

The **root** د-ع-ع carries violence in its doubled bones and its guttural sound. The **passive voice** strips these men of the hand they once used to shove the helpless. The **cognate accusative** doubles the blow and stamps it with *certainty* — answering their denial with grammatical insistence.

Three features. One word, *daʿʿ,* turning in the light. And every turn says the same thing: **the gesture you made toward the weak is the gesture the universe will make toward you — and it is certain.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about a word, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Shove Comes Home

Let me start where you live.

There is a particular kind of cruelty that leaves no marks and gets no headlines. It is not the punch. It is the **brush-off.** The wave of the hand. The way a busy, important person can dismiss another human being so completely that the other simply *ceases to register* — moved out of the way like furniture.

You have felt it from both ends.

You have been the one dismissed — spoken over, waved off, made to feel like an interruption in someone else's more important day. You remember exactly how it felt: not the pain of being struck, but the colder thing — the sense of being treated as *not worth the effort of being treated well.* That is the sting of *mahānah,* humiliation. Not "you hurt me," but "you decided I didn't matter."

And — be honest — you have been the one doing the dismissing. The server you didn't look at. The relative whose call you let ring because handling them was beneath the value of your evening. The vulnerable person — the new convert, the awkward one, the one with no status — whom you edged away from because proximity to them bought you nothing.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the Qurʾān understands about that gesture, and it is profound: **we reserve the shove for those who cannot shove back.** No one *daʿʿ*-es the powerful. You do not brush off the man who can ruin you. The shove is, by its nature, **aimed downward** — at the orphan, the poor man, the one with no clan, no advocate, no leverage. *Yaduʿʿu al-yatīm.* He shoves *the orphan* — precisely because the orphan is the one human being in Arabia who had no one to answer on his behalf.

That is what made it feel safe. There was no cost. The orphan could not retaliate. There was no clan to demand blood-price for a wounded dignity. He was, in the cruel arithmetic of that world, **a free target.**

And this is the lie at the heart of every act of contempt toward the powerless: *that there will be no return.* That because *this* person cannot answer me, **no one** will. That the shove disappears into the air and is never weighed.

Now hear the verse again. *Yawma yudaʿʿūna ilā nāri jahannama daʿʿā.*

There **is** an answer. There is One who stands as the *walī,* the advocate, the guardian, of the very orphan you waved away — the One who calls Himself, repeatedly, the patron of those with no patron. And on a Day these men swore would never come, the gesture they thought disappeared into the air comes back **with their own name on it.** The hand they used is the body they become. *Jazāʾ min jins al-ʿamal* — the recompense is **from the same family as the deed.** Not a random punishment assigned to a crime. The crime, **returned to its author**, with the grammar reversed.

You shoved? You are shoved. You decided he wasn't worth gentle handling? You are handled without gentleness. You made another human being feel like furniture in your important life? On that Day you are *moved* like furniture into the Fire. The verb does not change. **Only the voice changes** — from active to passive. From the one who does, to the one to whom it is done.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means there are no free targets. It means the test of your character is not how you treat the people who can affect you — anyone is polite to power. The test is the **orphan in your life.** The person who can do nothing for you and nothing to you. The one you could dismiss with zero earthly cost. *That* gesture — the one you think no one sees, the one that has no consequence — is the one being recorded in its own grammar, ready to be returned to you in the passive voice.

Tomorrow, watch your hand. Watch the small wave of dismissal. And ask: *do I want this exact gesture coming back to me, with my name on it?*

### Theme Two: When the Joke Walks Through the Door

There is a second human truth buried in the surrounding verses, and it is about **how denial protects itself.**

Look at what the verse just before this tells us about these men: *alladhīna hum fī khawḍin yalʿabūn* — "those who are in idle discourse, **playing**" (52:12). The Reckoning, to them, was not a terror they wrestled with. It was a **game.** A topic for clever, dismissive chatter. Something to *play* with.

You know this move. It is one of the most sophisticated defenses the human heart has ever invented: **when something is too threatening to take seriously, make it a joke.**

Notice — we do not mock what we find absurd. We *ignore* the absurd. We mock what we are **afraid might be true.** The laughter is not a verdict that the thing is false; the laughter is a *wall* built quickly, in a panic, to keep the thing from being looked at directly. Mockery is the most respectable form of running away. You get to flee the question while looking, to yourself and everyone else, like the smartest person in the room.

These men "played." They turned the Day into *khawḍ,* into banter, into a thing not-worth-the-dignity-of-seriousness. Do you hear the echo? They treated *the Reckoning* the same way they treated *the orphan* — as something beneath them, to be **waved off.** The contempt was the same contempt. They *daʿʿ*-ed the truth exactly as they *daʿʿ*-ed the helpless.

[PAUSE]

And now read the verse that comes *right after* ours — verse 14 — because verses 13 and 14 are a single, unbroken motion. They are shoved to the Fire, *daʿʿā,* and the instant they arrive, a voice meets them:

*hādhihi an-nāru allatī kuntum bihā **tukadhdhibūn*** — **"This is the Fire that you used to call a lie."**

Feel the staging. The thing they laughed at — *here it is.* Not described. Not argued for. **Present.** The joke has walked through the door and is standing in front of them, and it is made of fire. There is no longer anything to *play* with. The *khawḍ* is over. The wall of mockery they built is gone, because the thing they built it against is now **touching them.**

This is the deepest terror of denial: reality does not require your belief in order to arrive. *Kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn* — you *used to* deny it. Past tense. That whole project — the clever dismissal, the comfortable laughter, the lifetime of treating it as not-serious — is now spoken of as a finished, almost quaint thing. *Used to.* As one looks back on a child's mistaken idea. The denial did not change the Fire by one degree. It only changed where **you** were standing when it arrived.

So where do you live this?

Every one of us has a *khawḍ* — a serious thing we keep at arm's length by refusing to take it seriously. The state of our prayer. A relationship we are slowly killing with neglect. The death we treat as a topic for other people. The summons we feel, late at night, and then bury under noise and irony the next morning because looking at it directly is too much. **Mockery, distraction, "I'll think about it later" — these are all the same wave of the hand.** The same *daʿʿ,* aimed this time not at an orphan but at the truth itself.

The verse asks you, gently and terribly: *what are you currently waving off — and what happens on the day it stops being a topic and starts being a place you are standing?*

What does this change about tomorrow? It means: the thing you are most casual about, the truth you handle most lightly, the summons you keep turning into a joke — **go and look at it directly, now, while it is still only a thought and not yet a Fire.** Take seriously, today, the one thing you are most committed to not taking seriously. That is the whole difference between *kuntum tukadhdhibūn* — "you *used to* deny" — and a heart that turned to face it in time.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two roads we walked are the **same road** approached from opposite ends.

From the **language**, we found a single doubled root, *daʿʿ,* turning three times: a violent geminate root, stripped into the passive voice, then stamped with the certainty of the cognate accusative. *The gesture you made toward the weak returns to you — in the passive voice — and it is certain.*

From the **themes**, we found two contempts that were secretly one contempt: the hand that waves off the orphan, and the laughter that waves off the truth. Both are *daʿʿ.* Both treat something as beneath the dignity of being taken seriously. And both come home — the shove returns as a shove, the denied Fire returns as a present Fire.

It is the same insight from two directions. **What you treat as worthless, worthlessly, returns to you in kind.** The orphan you shoved and the Day you mocked were never powerless and never empty. There was an Advocate behind the one and a Reality behind the other. And the grammar of this verse is the proof — because it does not invent a new punishment for these men. It simply takes **their own verb** and turns it from active to passive. Their crime, conjugated against them.

### Questions to Carry

1. Who is the *orphan* in my life — the person who can do nothing for me and nothing to me — and how does my hand move when they come near?
2. When I dismiss someone with a brush-off, what am I quietly assuming about whether it will ever be weighed?
3. What is the *khawḍ* I am most committed to — the serious thing I keep safe by refusing to be serious about it?
4. If my casual gestures came back to me in the passive voice, with my name on them, which one would I most dread receiving?
5. Is there a truth I am currently calling a lie with my schedule, even while my tongue would never call it a lie with words?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The hand that shoves the helpless, and the laughter that shoves away the truth, are the same gesture — and on a certain Day that gesture comes home, conjugated into the passive voice, with you as its object.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not remember most of this by next week. But you might remember one thing: **the shove comes home.** Let that travel with your hand. The next time you feel that small, costless wave of dismissal rising toward someone who cannot answer you — the tired worker, the awkward seeker, the relative you've written off — let the verb of this verse flash across your mind. *Yudaʿʿūna.* And choose, instead, to extend the hand rather than drive it back. Not out of fear alone — but because you have seen, now, that there are no free targets, and there never were.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, Advocate of the one who has no advocate — guard my hand from ever driving away the weak You sent toward me, and guard my heart from waving off the truth I am too comfortable to face. Let me take seriously today what I do not want to take seriously, so that I am not among those of whom it is said: this is the thing you used to deny. Āmīn.*
