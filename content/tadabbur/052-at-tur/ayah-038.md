# The Ladder They Do Not Have

> أَمْ لَهُمْ سُلَّمٌ يَسْتَمِعُونَ فِيهِ ۖ فَلْيَأْتِ مُسْتَمِعُهُم بِسُلْطَٰنٍ مُّبِينٍ
>
> *Am lahum sullamun yastamiʿūna fīh — fal-ya'ti mustamiʿuhum bi-sulṭānin mubīn.*
>
> **"Or do they have a ladder by which they listen in? Then let their listener bring a clear authority."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Question

I want you to imagine a courtroom — but not a quiet one. Imagine the moment near the end of a long cross-examination, when the questioner has already closed off every exit, and now he is moving fast. One question. Then another. Then another. Each one removes a floorboard the witness was standing on. There is no pause for the witness to recover, because the questions themselves are the verdict. By the time the last one lands, everyone in the room already knows.

That is the sound of where we are in Surah At-Tur.

If you read the verses just before this one, you hear a cascade. *Were they created from nothing — or are they themselves the creators? Did they create the heavens and the earth? Do they hold the treasuries of your Lord?* Question after question, each one opening with that small, relentless Arabic word **أَمْ** — *am* — "or...?" It is the particle of a turning blade. Every time it comes, it turns to a new angle and asks: *is THIS your ground? Then is THIS your ground?*

And the deniers of Makkah were not simple people. They were not saying "we just don't feel like believing." They were making a claim — a quiet, enormous claim hiding underneath their rejection. The claim was this: **we know better. We have our own access to the truth. Muhammad says he receives revelation from above — well, who is to say we haven't received our own? Who is to say the truth he reports is the truth at all?**

[PAUSE]

So Allah turns the blade one more time. And He asks the most devastating question of the entire sequence — devastating because it takes their hidden claim, makes it visible, and dares them to stand behind it in public.

*Or do they have a ladder?*

A ladder. Into the sky. Up which they climb to press their ear against the door of heaven and hear, for themselves, the decree of the universe — so that they can come back down and say, with authority, *we checked, and he is wrong.*

Here is the hook I want you to carry into everything that follows. This ayah is not really about a ladder. **It is about the oldest hunger in the human heart — the hunger to know for ourselves, directly, without anyone in between — and what Allah reveals about that hunger by handing us a challenge we can never, ever meet.** Stay with me, because by the end, the ladder we don't have is going to tell us something tender about the One who sent the knowledge *down* instead.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: سُلَّمٌ — The Ladder, and the Word Left Wide Open

Before we touch the word, let me teach you something small about Arabic that changes how this verse lands.

In Arabic, as in English, a noun can be **definite** ("*the* ladder") or **indefinite** ("*a* ladder"). When you say "*the* ladder," you and I both already know which one we mean — it's the one leaning against the wall over there. But when you say "*a* ladder," you've opened the word up. It could be any ladder. It is unspecified, unfixed, hypothetical. In an argument, the indefinite form has a particular flavor: it says, *suppose, for the sake of argument, there were such a thing at all.*

Now look at the word Allah chooses:

**سُلَّمٌ** — *sullamun.* A ladder. Indefinite.

The four classical commentators are in agreement on its plain meaning. Al-Qurṭubī tells us *al-sullam* is the singular of *al-salālim* — the ladders one climbs. Al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn gloss it as **مِرْقَى وَمَصْعَد** — "an ascent, a place of climbing up." This is a physical object. A literal staircase reaching toward the sky.

And here is the first thing I want us to simply sit with. Allah does not say, *do they have knowledge of the unseen?* He does not ask the abstract question. He gives them a thing you can lean against a wall. A thing with rungs. He makes the abstract claim *physical* — and the moment He does that, He makes it absurd, because the second you picture a wooden ladder propped against the sky, you feel in your body that no such thing exists.

This is something Allah does throughout the Qur'an: He speaks of what you can see and touch in order to point you at what you cannot. The ladder is not the subject. The ladder is the stepping-stone. What it is pointing at is **access** — the question of whether any human being has a private route up to the source of ultimate knowledge.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something easy to miss. The ladder is **indefinite** — *a* ladder, *any* ladder. The verse does not even grant them a specific, named route. It says: *suppose there were, anywhere, by any means, a ladder at all.* It opens the possibility as wide as language can open it — and even with the door thrown completely open, they cannot walk through it. There is not even a hypothetical ladder they can point to.

And there is one more silence here, and silences in the Qur'an are never accidental. Look at what comes next: **يَسْتَمِعُونَ فِيهِ** — "by which they listen *in*." Listen in on *what?* Allah does not say. He leaves the object of their listening completely unstated.

Stay with that absence, because it is doing real work. The unspoken object is the *kalām* of the heavens — the speech of the angels, the decree, the knowledge of the unseen, the very thing al-Muyassar names as **al-waḥy**, the revelation itself. But Allah does not spell it out. Why? Because to name the thing they claim to hear would be to grant that it is hearable by a ladder. By leaving it unspecified, the verse keeps the whole enterprise floating in the air where it belongs — a claim with no object, an ear pressed to a door that the deniers cannot even locate.

**What this single word reveals:** the deniers' confidence rested on an access they could never produce — and Allah exposes it not by argument but by making it concrete enough to picture, and absurd enough to collapse.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: يَسْتَمِعُونَ … مُسْتَمِعُهُم — The Straining Ear, and the Crowd That Shrinks to One

Let me teach you the second tool, because it unlocks the heart of this verse.

Arabic verbs grow from three-letter roots, and the root can be poured into different *forms* — patterns that bend the basic meaning in specific directions. Our root here is **س-م-ع** (*s-m-ʿ*), the root of *hearing.* The simplest form, **سَمِعَ** (*samiʿa*), just means "he heard" — passive, accidental, the sound that reaches your ear whether you wanted it or not.

But the word in our verse is not *samiʿa.* It is **يَسْتَمِعُونَ** (*yastamiʿūna*) — and that *-sta-* in the middle is the signature of **Form VIII**. Form VIII characteristically turns an action *inward and effortful* — the agent does it deliberately, for himself, with strain and intent. So *istamaʿa* is not "to hear." It is **to listen** — to lean in, to strain the ear, to *eavesdrop.* It is the difference between sound arriving and you going out to catch it.

Feel that difference. Allah is not asking, "do they have a ladder by which sound happens to reach them?" He is asking, "do they have a ladder by which they *climb up and deliberately eavesdrop* on heaven?" The picture is active. Someone scaling the rungs, pressing close, ear cupped, trying to *catch* what was never meant for them.

This is the same image the Qur'an draws elsewhere — of those who would *strain to listen* at the assembly on high and are driven off by a piercing flame (37:8-10). The verb itself carries the posture of the thief at the keyhole.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

Now here is the thing I most want you to see in this whole reflection. Watch what happens between the start of the verse and its end.

At the start: **يَسْتَمِعُونَ** — *they* listen. Third person **plural.** A whole crowd of them, climbing, eavesdropping, the entire body of deniers pressed against the door of the sky.

At the end: **مُسْتَمِعُهُم** — "*their* listener." This is the Form VIII active participle — *mustamiʿ*, "the one who listens" — and it is **singular.** *Their* one listener.

Do you see what just happened? The crowd collapsed to a single person.

It begins with all of them listening together — a comfortable, anonymous, collective confidence, the safety of *everybody knows.* And then the challenge lands — **فَلْيَأْتِ مُسْتَمِعُهُم** — "then let *their listener* come forward" — and suddenly the verse demands one named individual to step out of the crowd. Not "let them all assert it." **Let the one who actually heard step out, identify himself, and bring proof.**

This is the move the verse makes, grammatically, with nothing but a shift from plural verb to singular noun. It is the courtroom going quiet. A hundred voices were saying "we know better." The judge says: *fine — bring me the one who was there.* And the hundred voices fall silent, because a crowd can hold a conviction that not a single one of its members can stand behind alone.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, that the root *s-m-ʿ* now appears **twice** — once as the act (*yastamiʿūn*) and once as the title (*mustamiʿ*). Allah lets them have the word. He grants them the title of "listener." He says, in effect: *all right — you have a listener? Wonderful. Produce him.* The repetition is not redundancy; it is the verse handing them the very label they claimed and then asking them to fill it with a real, single, accountable human being.

**What this construction reveals:** the deniers' certainty was a *collective* feeling masquerading as *individual* knowledge — and the instant Allah asks the crowd to produce the one person who actually climbed the ladder and heard, the whole edifice of confidence is exposed as having no one inside it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: بِسُلْطَٰنٍ مُّبِينٍ — Not Just Proof, but Proof That Proves Itself

We come to the demand itself. *Let their listener come* — **بِسُلْطَٰنٍ مُّبِينٍ** — *bi-sulṭānin mubīn.*

Two words, and each repays slow attention.

First, **سُلْطَان** (*sulṭān*), root *s-l-ṭ.* We know this word today mostly as "sultan" — a ruler, authority, power. And that is its core: *sulṭān* is dominating force. But the classical commentators here are unanimous that its meaning in this verse is **حُجَّة بَيِّنَة** — a *clear, decisive proof.* Al-Jalālayn: *bayyina wāḍiḥa*, manifest evidence. Al-Qurṭubī: *ḥujja bayyina*, a binding argument.

Sit with why the Qur'an uses the word for *power* to mean *evidence.* Because a real proof is not a polite suggestion. A genuine demonstration of truth *dominates* the mind — it overpowers your ability to deny it. When you truly see that two plus two is four, you cannot choose to disbelieve it; the proof has authority over you. So Allah does not ask them for an *opinion* or a *feeling* or a *report.* He asks them for *sulṭān* — something with the commanding force to compel assent. He is demanding the highest grade of evidence that exists.

And He knows they have none.

[PAUSE]

Now the second word: **مُّبِينٍ** (*mubīn*), root *b-y-n.* This is a Form IV participle — from the verb **أَبَانَ** (*abāna*). And here I need to be precise, because it matters. Form IV here is not a dry grammatical label; *abāna* means **to make clear, to be manifest, to show itself plainly.** So *mubīn* is not merely "clear" in a passive sense, as if waiting for someone to interpret it. *Mubīn* is **self-clarifying** — that which announces its own meaning, that which makes itself evident, that leaves no room for the listener to spin or doubt.

Put the two together. Allah does not say: bring proof. He says: bring **sulṭān mubīn** — *commanding* proof that is *self-evidencing.* Proof that has the force to compel *and* the clarity to need no defense. Proof that, the moment it is set down on the table, ends the argument by itself.

That is the bar. And it is the bar the verse sets precisely because no human "listener" eavesdropping from a ladder could ever clear it. The demand is calibrated to the impossibility.

[PAUSE]

And now I want to point you at something in the *sound* of this verse — not an etymology, just what the ear hears. Listen to the two words that open and seal the ayah:

**سُلَّم** … *sullam* … the ladder.
**سُلْطَان** … *sulṭān* … the authority.

Different roots. Different meanings. But your ear catches the echo — *sul-… sul-…* They rhyme at the front. The verse begins with the **sullam** they cannot produce and ends with the **sulṭān** they cannot bring. The thing that would grant access, and the thing that access was supposed to yield — and the sounds shadow one another like two empty hands held up side by side. The ladder is missing; therefore the authority is missing. The phonetic echo binds the absence of the means to the absence of the result.

**What these words reveal:** Allah did not lower the standard to expose them — He *raised* it. He demanded the most commanding, most self-evident proof imaginable, knowing the demand could only be met by what *descends* from heaven, never by what we climb to steal from it.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these words, together, have shown us. A ladder no one has — made concrete so we could feel its absurdity. A crowd that listens together but cannot produce one person who heard. A demand for proof so total that only revelation could satisfy it. Three pieces of the same exposure: **human beings have no private staircase to ultimate truth, and the confidence that pretends otherwise dissolves the moment it is asked to step forward and prove itself.**

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about us. About the hunger underneath the denial, and the mercy hidden inside the challenge.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Private Staircase — Our Hunger to Know for Ourselves

Let me start where you actually live, far from any ladder.

You have felt this. Someone tells you something important — a diagnosis, a piece of news, a warning — and a voice rises in you that says: *but how do they know? I want to see it for myself.* That voice is not evil. It is deeply human. We are built to want firsthand knowledge. We trust our own eyes over another's testimony. "I'll believe it when I see it." We would all, if we could, build a ladder up to the truth and check it ourselves rather than take anyone's word.

Now scale that hunger up to its largest object. Not a diagnosis — but the meaning of existence. Why we are here. What happens when we die. Whether there is justice behind the curtain of the world. This is the knowledge the deniers were really after when they dismissed the Messenger ﷺ. Underneath "we don't believe him" was "we would only believe what we'd verified ourselves — directly, from the source, with no prophet standing in between."

And Allah's response, in this verse, is breathtaking in its honesty. He does not say *how dare you want to know.* He says: *all right. Do you have the ladder? Because that is what direct access would require — a way to climb up to the source yourself.* He takes the hunger seriously enough to name exactly what it would cost. **And then He shows us, gently and devastatingly, that we cannot pay it.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want to walk you through. The deniers thought their problem was Muhammad ﷺ — that he was an obstacle between them and the truth. But the verse reveals the opposite. The Messenger was not the barrier to the knowledge of the unseen. **He was the only bridge to it.** Remove him, and you do not get *direct* access — you get *no* access. You get a wall and a ladder you don't own.

Think of how the very same image appears earlier in the Qur'an, in Sūrat al-Anʿām, where Allah addresses the Prophet ﷺ about those who turned away: *"...then if you are able to seek a tunnel into the earth or a **ladder** (*sullam*) into the sky to bring them a sign..."* (6:35). There, the ladder is the impossible lengths the Messenger would go to in order to *give* them a sign. Here, the ladder is the impossible access *they* would need to *grasp* a sign themselves. Either way, the *sullam* marks the same boundary: **the sky does not have a staircase. The unseen is not climbable. It can only be given.**

And this is where the hunger meets its answer. We wanted to *ascend* to the knowledge. Allah's mercy is that He chose, instead, to *send it down.* The word for revelation, *tanzīl*, *nuzūl*, means precisely *coming down.* We have no ladder up — so the Most High did not ask us to climb. He descended His word to us. The knowledge we could never steal by force, He gave as a gift.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes the posture you bring to what you do not know. There is a kind of modern exhaustion that comes from believing you must personally verify everything — that no truth is yours until you have climbed up and checked it at the source. About the things of this world, verify all you can. But about the deepest things — the unseen, the meaning, the after — this verse offers rest. **You were never required to build the ladder.** The humility to receive what was sent down, rather than the pride of demanding to climb up and audit heaven yourself, is not a lesser knowledge. It is the only kind available to a creature without a staircase. And there is peace in finally setting down a ladder you were never going to finish building.

### Theme Two: When the Crowd Goes Quiet — Conviction Is Not Evidence

Let me bring you to something you have seen a hundred times, maybe felt yourself.

There is a particular kind of confidence that lives only in groups. You have watched it. A room full of people all certain of something — "everyone knows that's true," "we all agree," the warm, dense conviction of the crowd. And then someone asks the simple question: *how do you know? Who actually saw it? Bring me the one person who was there.* And the room changes. The collective certainty had a hundred owners and not one author. Each person was leaning on everyone else. No one had checked. The conviction was real as a *feeling* and empty as *knowledge.*

We live in the golden age of this. Information spreads faster than verification. A claim travels around the world, repeated by millions, each repetition adding *confidence* without adding a single *fact.* The crowd grows louder and the evidence stays at zero. "Everyone is saying it" becomes its own proof — until someone asks for the source, and the trail leads back to no one.

That is exactly the human mechanism this verse reaches into and exposes — fourteen centuries before our timelines and feeds.

[PAUSE]

Remember the grammar. It begins with the plural — *yastamiʿūn*, they all listen, the comfortable collective. And the challenge collapses it to the singular — *mustamiʿuhum*, **their** listener, one person, step forward. Allah does not argue with the crowd. He simply asks the crowd to produce *the individual who actually heard, and the proof he carried.* And the silence that follows is the verdict.

Here is the deeper movement. The verse is teaching us the difference between two things that feel identical from the inside but are worlds apart: the *feeling* of certainty and the *fact* of knowledge. The deniers were not lying about their feeling — they genuinely felt sure. But Allah's standard is not *sulṭān* of *feeling.* It is *sulṭān mubīn* — proof that compels and is self-evident. And a feeling, no matter how many people share it, is not a proof. A million convinced hearts do not equal one verified witness with evidence in his hand.

[PAUSE]

Now turn the blade — gently — on yourself, the way the verse turns it on them. Because here is the uncomfortable gift. We are quick to demand *sulṭān mubīn* from others. But how much of what *we* are certain about could survive the same question? How many of our own convictions are us, standing in a crowd, *feeling* the warmth of agreement, having never once climbed the ladder to check? The verse is a mirror. It asks the deniers, *bring your one listener* — and it asks me, *and what about yours? When you reject something, or cling to something — do you have a* sulṭān*, or do you have a crowd?*

What does this change about tomorrow? It plants a single quiet question to carry into every strong opinion you hold and every confident claim you hear: **where is the listener, and what did he bring down?** Not to become a cynic who believes nothing — but to become honest about the difference between *I have verified this,* *I have received this from a source I have weighed and trusted,* and *I am simply standing in a warm crowd.* The believer's certainty is not crowd-certainty. It rests on the one report that *did* come down with authority — and is humble about everything that didn't.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Step back now and see the shape of where this verse sits. From verse 35 to verse 43, Sūrat al-Tūr is a ladder of its own — a rising sequence of **أَمْ** questions, each a rung, each removing one ground the deniers might stand on. *Were you made from nothing? Did you make yourselves? Did you make the heavens? Do you own the treasuries? Do you have a ladder to heaven? Do the angels' daughters belong to Him? Are you asking a wage that bankrupts them? Do they hold the unseen and write it down?*

Every rung asks: *is THIS your authority?* And verse 38 is the rung that names the deepest claim of all — not material, not cosmic, but **epistemic**: the claim of private access to divine knowledge. It is the secret engine under all their denial, and Allah pulls it into the light and asks them to produce it.

And see how the verse seals itself. It opens with **سُلَّم** — the ladder, the means of access — and closes with **سُلْطَان** — the authority, the fruit of access. The means they don't have, echoing in sound against the result they can't bring. The verse is built like the two empty hands of someone who claimed to be holding something, opened to show there was nothing there all along.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are one truth seen from two sides.

The **ladder left indefinite and the listening with no stated object** showed us, in grammar, that *humans have no private route to the unseen* — which is the first theme: our hunger to ascend, and the mercy that descended instead.

The **crowd that collapses to a single listener, and the demand for self-evidencing proof** showed us, in grammar, that *collective conviction is not knowledge* — which is the second theme: the difference between the warmth of agreement and the weight of evidence.

One insight, two directions: **certainty about the deepest things cannot be climbed to, crowd-sourced, or felt into being. It can only be received — sent down — from the One who alone holds the unseen.** The ladder we don't have is the very reason the Qur'an had to come down.

### Questions to Carry

1. What do I claim to *know* about the deepest things — and is it a ladder I climbed, a report I weighed, or a crowd I am standing in?
2. Where in my life has the *feeling* of certainty been quietly impersonating the *fact* of knowledge?
3. When I demand proof from others, would my own convictions survive the same demand — *bring your clear authority?*
4. Have I been exhausting myself trying to build a staircase to truths that were only ever meant to be *received?*
5. The Messenger ﷺ was not the wall between me and the truth, but the bridge to it. Where have I mistaken a bridge for a barrier?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You reject the message as though you owned a private staircase into heaven's secrets — so produce the one who climbed it and the commanding proof he carried down; you cannot, because no such ladder exists, and the knowledge you could never steal by ascent, the Most High chose to send down as a gift.

### The Closing Invitation

Let something shift, not as a duty but as a relief. You can set down the ladder. You were never going to finish it, and you were never asked to. The invitation of this verse is to trade the proud, exhausting demand to audit heaven yourself for the humble, restful posture of one who receives what was sent down — and, alongside it, to carry one honest question into every certainty you hold: *where is the listener, and what did he bring?*

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You who hold the unseen and send down what we could never climb to reach — we have no ladder, and no proof of our own to bring. Save us from the false confidence of the crowd, and the pride that demands to verify what You alone can give. Make us among those who humbly receive Your word as the clear authority it is, and let our hearts find rest in what descended, since we were never able to ascend.* Āmīn.
