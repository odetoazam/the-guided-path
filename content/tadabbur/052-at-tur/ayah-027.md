# But Allah Favored Us
### Surah At-Tur, Ayah 27

> فَمَنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا وَوَقَىٰنَا عَذَابَ ٱلسَّمُومِ
>
> *Fa-manna-llāhu ʿalaynā wa waqānā ʿadhāba-s-samūm*
>
> **"But Allah conferred favor upon us, and protected us from the punishment of the Scorching Fire."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Conversation Overheard in Paradise

I want you to picture a scene. Not imagine it loosely — *inhabit* it.

The accounting is over. The Day that was fifty thousand years long has ended for some, and they have crossed. They are inside now. And the first thing the Quran chooses to show us about them is not the rivers, not the gardens, not the thrones — though those come.

The first thing it shows us is that **they turn to each other and talk.**

> "And they will approach one another, inquiring of each other." (52:25)

They lean in. They ask. There is a warmth here, an intimacy, people who have arrived somewhere together turning to one another the way survivors do. And what do they talk about?

They talk about *who they used to be.*

> "They will say, 'Indeed, we were previously, among our people, fearful.'" (52:26)

Sit with that for a moment. In the place of eternal security, the conversation turns backward — to a time when they were anxious. *Mushfiqīn.* Trembling. Walking through their own homes, among their own families, carrying a quiet fear that most people around them did not share and could not see.

And then comes our ayah. The hinge. The turn.

> **فَمَنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا**
>
> **"But Allah conferred favor upon us..."**

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to notice before we go a single step further. These are people who *worked.* These are people who lay awake at night afraid of their Lord. People who held their tongues, restrained their hands, gave when it hurt to give, stood in prayer when sleep was sweeter. They earned this. By any human accounting, they earned this.

And the very first word out of their mouths about how they got here is not "we." It is not "our struggle," not "our patience," not "our nights."

It is **"Allah favored us."**

Why? After all that effort — why does the language of *earning* vanish completely the moment they speak? And what is this fear they keep returning to, this anxiety they felt in the world — why does it sound, in their mouths, almost like the thing that *saved* them?

Six words. Two verbs. One Name. Let's open them slowly, because there is a whole theology of the human heart folded inside this sentence — and it has everything to do with how you and I are living right now, tonight, among our own people.

---

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — فَمَنَّ: The Word That Is a Vice in You and a Glory in Him

Let me teach you something about a word before we ever look at the ayah.

In Arabic, there is a verb — *manna*, from the root *mīm-nūn-nūn* (م-ن-ن). At its core it means: to bestow a favor, to give a gift, to grant something out of pure generosity.

But this same root has a shadow side. *Al-mann* also means: **to remind someone of the favor you did them.** To give a gift and then hold it over their head. "After all I did for you." "Don't forget who helped you when you had nothing."

You know this feeling. You've been on the receiving end of it. Someone helps you, and then the help becomes a leash. Every conversation afterward carries the faint weight of debt. The gift was real, but it was never really *given* — it was loaned, and now the interest is your dignity.

The Quran itself names this as something that *destroys* charity. In Surah al-Baqarah:

> "O you who believe, do not invalidate your charities with reminders of generosity (*al-mann*) and injury." (2:264)

So here is a word that, when a *human being* does it, is a moral failure. To remind people of your favors is to ruin them. It is listed alongside *injury.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at our ayah.

> **فَمَنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا**

The very same verb. *Manna.* Allah *did the thing that is a vice when we do it.* He bestowed favor — and the entire Quran is, in one sense, Allah reminding us of His favors. Surah ar-Rahman: "Which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?" thirty-one times. He recounts what He has given. He names it. He returns to it.

How can the same act be a flaw in the servant and a glory in the Lord?

Here's the answer, and it's worth everything. When *I* remind you of my favor, I am revealing that **I needed something back.** The reminder exposes that my gift was never free — it was a transaction, and I am now collecting. The *mann* is a vice in me because it unmasks my poverty. I gave to get.

But when Allah recounts His favors, He needs **nothing.** He gains nothing from your gratitude and loses nothing from your denial. So when He says *"I favored you,"* He is not collecting a debt — He is *waking you up.* He is doing it entirely for your benefit, so that the heart that was about to forget its Giver turns back toward Him and lives.

The same word. From a needy creature it is manipulation. From the Self-Sufficient it is **mercy in the form of a reminder.**

And notice *who is speaking it* in our ayah. It is not Allah saying "I favored you." It is **the people of Paradise saying "Allah favored us."** They have taken the divine language onto their own tongues. They are doing the *mann* — they are recounting the favor — but they are recounting it *against themselves and for Him.* They remind, but the one they credit is not their own effort. It is Him.

[PAUSE]

And there's the little letter at the front we shouldn't rush past: the **fa** — فَ — "so," "and then." It is a particle of *immediate consequence.* In 52:26 they were fearful. *Fa-manna* — and so, immediately, as the direct result, Allah favored. The grammar puts almost no distance between their trembling in the world and His favor in the next. The fear and the favor are stitched together with a single letter. Hold onto that. We will come back to it.

**What this one word reveals:** The people of Paradise speak of their salvation using the very verb that, from human lips, would be arrogance — but they aim it entirely at Allah. They have learned the one lesson that took them through the gates: *the favor was never mine to claim.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two — وَوَقَىٰنَا: They Guarded, So He Guarded Them

The second verb. *Wa waqānā* — "and He protected us." Root: *wāw-qāf-yā* (و-ق-ي). To shield. To shelter. To place a barrier between someone and a harm.

Now let me ask you to hold two words in your mind at once, because they grow from the same root.

*Waqā* — to protect.

And **taqwā.**

[PAUSE]

Yes. *Taqwā* — that word we translate, usually clumsily, as "God-consciousness" or "fear of Allah" or "piety" — comes from this exact root, *wqy*. At its heart, *taqwā* means **to guard yourself.** To place a shield between your soul and the things that would destroy it. The *muttaqī*, the person of taqwa, is fundamentally someone who is *protecting* something precious.

Now read the ayah again with this in your hand.

In the world, these people *guarded.* That is what their fear in 52:26 amounted to — a lifetime of placing shields between themselves and ruin. Guarding the tongue. Guarding the gaze. Guarding the wealth from haram. Guarding the heart from arrogance. *Taqwā* — they spent their days as people who shielded themselves for the sake of Allah.

And now, on the other side, what is the reward? The very same root, turned back on them by the hand of Allah:

> **وَوَقَىٰنَا — "and He guarded us."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the symmetry? They guarded; *He guards them.* The shield they built around their souls in dunya becomes the shield He builds around them in akhira. The verb of their effort becomes the verb of their reward. It is the same word — once in their hands, now in His.

This is one of the quiet laws written through the entire Quran: *the way you turn toward Allah is the way He turns toward you.* "Remember Me, I will remember you." "Be grateful to Me." The servant who spends his life as a *guardian* of his own soul finds, on the Day, that the role reverses — and the One who taught him to guard now stands as his Guardian.

There is a tenderness in this that's easy to miss. The person of taqwa in the world often feels *alone* in their guarding. Among their families, the ayah says — among the people closest to them — they were the anxious one, the careful one, the one holding a shield no one else could see and few understood. It can be a lonely posture, this constant self-guarding.

And the ayah's answer to that loneliness is: *you were never guarding alone.* The whole time you thought it was just you and your fragile vigilance, He was teaching you to do it, and on the Day He takes the shield from your tired hands and holds it Himself. **وَوَقَىٰنَا.** He guarded us.

**What this verb reveals:** The effort of taqwa is not repaid with something *unrelated* to it. It is repaid with *itself*, perfected and returned. You guard for His sake now; He guards you forever then.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three — ٱلسَّمُومِ: The Fire That Finds Its Way In

Now the last word, and it is a strange and terrible one. *As-samūm.* Root: *sīn-mīm-mīm* (س-م-م).

The translation says "the Scorching Fire," and that's not wrong. But the choice of *this specific word* — out of all the words the Quran uses for the Fire — is doing something deliberate. Let's open it.

The classical commentators give us a composite picture, and like several camera angles on one object, each adds a layer:

- Al-Hasan al-Baṣrī, as reported by al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī, says *as-samūm* is **a name of the Fire itself** — or a particular layer of Jahannam.
- Al-Kalbī, as al-Baghawī narrates, says simply: **the punishment of the Fire.**
- And al-Qurṭubī preserves the root's original sense in the language: *as-samūm* is **the scorching wind** — the burning desert wind, *samma yawmunā* — "our day became scorching." Abū ʿUbayda notes it is the wind that comes by day.

So far: a name for the Fire, drawn from the most punishing thing an Arab in the desert knew — the wind that scorches.

But now listen to what al-Jalālayn adds, because it unlocks the whole word. Why is it called *as-samūm*? **النار لدخولها في المسام** — "the Fire, because it enters into the *masāmm*."

And what are the *masāmm*?

[PAUSE]

**The pores.** The pores of your skin.

Look at the root again — *s-m-m.* From it comes *samūm*, this fire. From it comes *samm* — **poison.** And from it comes *masāmm* — **the pores of the body.** All three from one root. Poison. Pores. The scorching wind.

Do you see what the word is telling you?

This is not a fire that burns you from the *outside.* This is not flame against skin, terrible as that is. This is a punishment named for its capacity to **get in.** It enters through the pores — the tiniest openings, the places you cannot close, the doors of the body you didn't even know were doors. Like poison, it penetrates. There is no surface it cannot pass, no barrier of skin that keeps it out. It seeps into the very interior of you.

[PAUSE]

Now hold *as-samūm* — the fire that finds every opening and enters — against the word we just studied, **waqānā**, *He shielded us.*

A shield, in the ordinary sense, protects a *surface.* It stops the blow from the outside. But what shield protects you from a thing that enters through your *pores* — through openings you cannot seal, faults you cannot even locate in yourself?

There is no such shield. *Except one.* The protection of Allah is the only barrier that works against a punishment designed to penetrate. Human caution guards the surface. Only *His* wiqāya reaches the interior. The people of Paradise are not saying "we kept the fire off our skin." They are saying: *He sealed us against the one thing that gets into everything.*

And remember the loneliness of their guarding — the *taqwa* that felt so much like just watching their own surface, their own visible deeds. The reward exposes how much more He was protecting them than they ever knew. They guarded what they could see. He guarded them from what *no one could see coming in.*

**What this word reveals:** The fire is named for its power to penetrate, to enter, to poison from within — precisely to show that the only real protection in existence is a protection that reaches deeper than any wall a human being could ever build. And that protection has only ever been His to give.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what these three words have done, standing together.

**Manna** — the favor that, by their own confession, they did not earn.
**Waqānā** — the shield that is the perfected return of their own lifelong guarding.
**As-samūm** — the fire that gets *in*, against which no created shield avails.

The grammar has been pointing at one reality the entire time: *the people of Paradise arrived through effort, and they credit none of it to themselves; they were shielded against a danger that reaches deeper than their effort could ever protect them from.* The words are not describing a transaction. They are describing **a rescue that looked, from the inside, like a struggle.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how you and I are meant to live in the time we have left.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Fear That Saves

Let me start where you live.

We are, right now, living through what people are calling an epidemic of anxiety. More than at any point in recorded history, human beings report waking with a knot in the chest, a low hum of dread, a sense that something is not right and they cannot quite name it. We have built an entire industry to manage it — apps, medications, retreats, breathing techniques. And nearly all of it shares one assumption:

**Fear is the enemy. The goal is to make it stop.**

I want to be careful and honest here, because some fear is genuinely a sickness — a brain misfiring, a body trapped in alarm with no real threat, and that deserves real treatment and real compassion. I am not talking about that. Hold that aside.

I am talking about the deeper thing underneath the modern panic. Because here is what I've come to believe: a great deal of what we call anxiety is actually **fear that has lost its object.** It is dread with no address. The human heart was built to fear *something* — to orient its vigilance toward a real and ultimate reality — and when we strip away that object, the fear doesn't disappear. It detaches. It floats. It attaches itself to everything and nothing: the email, the diagnosis, the future, the scroll. Fear without a destination becomes a fog that fills the whole house.

Now look at the people of Paradise.

> "Indeed, we were previously, among our people, **fearful** (*mushfiqīn*)." (52:26)

They were not fog-people. They were not vaguely anxious. Their fear had an *object*, and the object was the most real thing in existence: their Lord, and the Day of meeting Him. *Ishfāq* — the word in 52:26 — is not raw terror. It is a *tender* fear, a fear mixed with care, the kind of fear a person has over something they love and might lose. They feared Allah the way you fear for the safety of your own child — not running from Him, but *toward* a relationship they were terrified to damage.

[PAUSE]

And here is the stunning thing. That fear — the thing the modern world would diagnose and medicate away — *that fear is what they are pointing to as the road that led them here.* Remember the **fa**, the little letter of immediate consequence. *They were fearful* — **fa-manna** — *and so Allah favored them.* The fear and the favor are stitched together. Their anxiety in the world was not the disease. **It was the symptom of a heart that was still oriented toward what is real.**

Think about what their fear *did* to their lives. It kept them awake the right amount. It made them careful with their tongues. It made them give. It made them check themselves. A person who genuinely fears the Day of meeting Allah cannot oppress, cannot exploit, cannot live a careless and cruel life. The fear was a *guardrail.* It was the felt-form of their taqwa.

Here is the reframe I want you to carry out of this room. The question is not *"How do I get rid of my fear?"* The deeper question is: **"What is my fear pointing at?"**

If your dread is floating — attached to your bank account, your reputation, your future, your phone — it will eat you, because none of those things can hold the weight of your ultimate fear. They are too small. They were never meant to be the object.

But if you can do what these people did — if you can take that same trembling capacity and *aim it* at the one Reality that can hold it, at Allah and the Day you will stand before Him — then something extraordinary happens. The fear *organizes your life* instead of consuming it. It becomes a guardrail instead of a fog. It does the work of taqwa: it guards you.

And then — and only then — comes the turn. Because the people who feared *Him* in the world are the only people who are told, in the end: *now you never have to be afraid again.* The whole arc of the believer is that they front-load their fear into this short life so that the next life is pure security. The people who refused to fear Him here will fear forever there. The people who trembled here:

> **"...will Allah protect them from the evil of that Day and give them radiance and happiness." (76:11)**

The fear that saves is the fear you spend *now,* on the right object, so that you have none left to spend later.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Stop trying to anesthetize the fear. Start *re-aiming* it. The next time the dread rises in your chest with no name, gently give it its true name. Turn it toward Allah. Let it make you pray instead of scroll, give instead of grip, check yourself instead of others. A fear pointed at Him becomes a guardrail. A fear pointed at everything else becomes a prison.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two — The Grace No One Earns

Now to the second thing, and it cuts even deeper.

Let me ask you to imagine someone who has worked harder than anyone you know. A student who studied while others slept. An athlete who trained in the dark and the cold for years. A person who built something from nothing through sheer, grinding effort. And now imagine them at the summit, the moment of arrival, and someone asks: *"How did you get here?"*

What is the most common answer? **"I earned this. I worked for this. Nobody handed it to me."**

And we *admire* that answer. The whole modern world is built to admire it. The self-made man. The grind. The hustle. *I did this myself.*

Now look — really look — at how the people of Paradise answer the exact same question.

These are people who, by any measure, *worked.* They spent their lives in the trembling, careful, self-guarding labor of taqwa. They gave when it hurt. They prayed in the dark. They feared for years. If anyone in all of creation had the right to say *"I earned this,"* it would be them, standing inside the eternal reward.

And what do they say?

> **فَمَنَّ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْنَا**
>
> **"But Allah favored us."**

[PAUSE]

The word "I" disappears. The word "earned" never comes. The very first thing they say — in the place where their effort is finally, eternally validated — credits *Him.* They use *manna*, the verb of unearned gift, the verb of pure grace. They do not say "we attained." They say "He bestowed."

Why? They worked. *Why don't they take the credit?*

Because they have finally seen the whole picture. And here is the picture.

[PAUSE]

Every single thing they used to "earn" their place was *itself a gift.* Who gave them the heart that was capable of fear? Allah. Who gave them the guidance to know what to fear? Allah. Who gave them the life, the breath, the years in which to do the deeds? Allah. Who kept them firm when others fell away? Allah. Who accepted the small, broken, imperfect deeds and treated them as enough? Allah.

Their effort was real. But their effort was *built entirely out of gifts.* It is like a child who saves up their allowance to buy their parent a present — yes, the child saved, the child chose, the effort was real and the parent is genuinely moved. But every coin in that gift came from the parent's own hand to begin with. Whose money bought the gift?

That is the believer's relationship to their own good deeds. *Real* effort, made entirely of *borrowed* materials. And the person who sees this clearly cannot say "I earned it" without lying. They can only say: **"He favored me — even with the strength to obey Him."**

Remember al-Jalālayn told us *as-samūm* enters through the pores — through openings you cannot seal. Here is the deepest reason they cannot claim credit: *they could never have shielded themselves anyway.* Their best guarding only ever covered the surface. The fire that gets in through the pores — through the hidden faults, the secret diseases of the heart, the sins they didn't even know to repent of — *only His wiqāya could stop that.* No amount of effort reaches the interior. So even their salvation from the deepest danger was something they could not, by definition, have earned. It had to be **favor.**

[PAUSE]

And there is a freedom in this that the "self-made" person never tastes. The one who believes they earned everything carries a terrible weight: they must *keep earning it,* forever, and they live in dread of the day they cannot. Their identity is a debt they are always repaying. But the one who knows it was all grace? They can rest. They can work hard *without* the crushing pressure of believing it all hangs on them. They strive with their hands while their heart leans entirely on His favor. They give everything *and* claim nothing.

This is the posture, by the way, the Prophet ﷺ taught directly: that none of you will enter Paradise by his deeds alone — not even him, ﷺ, except that Allah envelops him in mercy. The deeds are necessary. The deeds are *commanded.* But the deeds are not the *price* — they are the *response.* The entry is, was, and always will be **favor.**

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Work as hard as the people of Paradise worked — and refuse to take the credit they refused to take. When something good comes through your effort, train your tongue to the believer's reflex: not "I did this," but *"Allah favored me — even with the ability to do this."* It will do two things at once. It will keep arrogance out of your heart. And it will lift the unbearable weight of self-salvation off your shoulders. You strive; He saves. That is the whole arrangement, and it is mercy from beginning to end.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two roads meet.

From the **linguistic** side, we found three words. *Manna* — the favor that, from human lips, would be a vice, but which they aim entirely at Allah. *Waqānā* — the shield that is the perfected return of their own lifelong guarding, *taqwā* answered by *wiqāya.* And *as-samūm* — the fire that enters through the pores, against which no created shield avails.

From the **thematic** side, we found two truths about the heart. That the fear they felt in the world was not their disease but their *guardrail* — fear aimed at the right object, spent now so there is none left to spend later. And that the effort which got them here was real but made entirely of borrowed materials — so the only honest word for their arrival is *favor.*

And now see that these are **the same insight from two directions.**

The fear that saves (theme one) *is* the taqwa, the self-guarding, hidden inside the root of *waqānā* (element two). And the grace no one earns (theme two) *is* the *manna*, the unearned gift, sitting in the very first word (element one). And the reason it must be grace — the reason even a lifetime of guarding could not be enough — is written into *as-samūm* (element three): because the real danger gets in through the pores, and no human shield reaches that deep.

So the whole ayah is one motion: **they spent their lives guarding what they could, in fear of Him — and He, by pure favor, guarded them from what they never could.** Their part was the trembling. His part was the rescue. And the people of Paradise, with eternity stretched out before them, choose as their *first words* to make this exactly clear.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

1. When I feel dread rising in my chest, what is it actually aimed at? What would it mean to gently turn that same fear toward Allah — and would my life look different tomorrow if I did?

2. Among my own people — my family, the ones closest to me — am I the one quietly guarding something they cannot see? And do I believe I am guarding it *alone*?

3. When something good comes through my effort, what is my reflex — "I earned this," or "He favored me with the ability to do this"? What does my honest reflex tell me about my heart?

4. Is there a part of me being slowly entered, like the *samūm* through the pores — some hidden fault no surface effort is reaching — that I have never actually asked Allah to shield me from?

5. If I knew that the fear I carry now were the very thing being stitched, by a single *fa*, to an eternity of security — would I run from it, or would I let it do its work?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The people who trembled before Allah in this world, guarding what little they could, will stand in eternal safety and say with their first breath that they earned none of it — *He favored us, and He guarded us from the fire that we never could have kept out ourselves.*

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to wait for Paradise to learn its language. The conversation in 52:27 is not only a record of what *they* will say — it is a *rehearsal* offered to you now. Tonight, before sleep, you can practice their two sentences. You can take your fear and aim it at Him until it becomes a guardrail instead of a fog. And you can take your good deeds — your prayers, your patience, your giving — and hand the credit back to the One who lent you the strength for every one of them. That is not a burden. It is the lightest way a human being can possibly live: striving with the hands, resting in the favor.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who favored people with grace they could not earn — favor us with the ability to fear You rightly, and then favor us with security from every fear. Teach our hands to guard our souls for Your sake, and on the Day we cannot guard ourselves, be our Guardian against the fire that reaches what we cannot see. And let our first words, here and there, be the truest ones: *but for You, we would have nothing — You favored us, and You protected us.* Āmīn.
