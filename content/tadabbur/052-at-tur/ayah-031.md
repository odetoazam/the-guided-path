# The Same Word for Both Sides: When Mockery Becomes a Countdown

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first, three ways.

In Arabic:

> **قُلْ تَرَبَّصُوا۟ فَإِنِّى مَعَكُم مِّنَ ٱلْمُتَرَبِّصِينَ**

In transliteration:

> *Qul tarabbasū, fa-innī maʿakum mina-l-mutarabbiṣīn.*

And in plain English:

> **Say: "Wait — for indeed I am, with you, among those who wait."**

That's it. Six words in the Arabic. One imperative, one emphatic clause. You could speak it in a single breath. And on the surface it sounds almost like a shrug — *fine, wait then, I'm waiting too.*

But I want you to feel how strange this reply is before we go anywhere near its depths.

[PAUSE]

### Rebuild the world

Put yourself in Makkah, in the years before the migration. A man stands among his own people — people who watched him grow up, who called him *al-Amīn*, the trustworthy one — and tells them that everything they have built their lives around is about to be answered for. And they cannot dismiss him as a liar, because they know him. So they reach for other weapons.

The verses just before this one are a barrage of accusations, thrown one after another like stones. *Are you a soothsayer? Are you possessed?* And then, in verse thirty, they land on what they think is their cleverest move:

> *"Or do they say, 'A poet — we await for him the calamity of fate'?"* (52:30)

Sit with what they're actually saying. They've decided he's just a poet — a man of pretty words, a passing voice. And they have a strategy for poets: **you wait them out.** *Rayb al-manūn* — the disasters of time, the accidents of fate, and above all, death. Their plan is patience. Their plan is to do nothing. *He'll die. His movement will die with him. Time is on our side. We just have to outlast him.*

This is not the rage of people who feel threatened in the moment. This is the cold confidence of people who think they have already won, and just need the clock to run out.

Now — imagine you are the one being talked about this way. Your enemies are not even bothering to fight you. They are simply *waiting for you to die.* What do you say to that?

### The Hook

Here is what's remarkable. Allah does not tell His Prophet to argue. He does not tell him to defend himself, or to threaten, or to plead. He tells him to take **the exact word they just used** — their word, their whole strategy — and hand it right back.

They said: *we await.* And the reply comes: *Say — wait, then. I'm waiting too.*

The same root. The same verb. Given to both sides.

But one of those two sides knows what's coming. And the other only thinks it does.

Let's find out what's buried in a single shared word.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The shape of the word *tarabbaṣa* — waiting that leans forward

Before we open the verse, I want to teach you something about how Arabic builds its verbs, because this entire ayah turns on it.

In English, a verb is mostly a fixed thing. "Wait" is "wait." If you want to change its intensity, you add other words around it — *wait carefully, lie in wait, wait eagerly.* The verb itself doesn't change shape.

Arabic does something English cannot. It takes a three-letter root — here the root is **ر-ب-ص** (*r-b-ṣ*), which carries the basic sense of watching for something, lying ready for it — and it pours that root into different *molds*. Each mold is a recognizable pattern, and each pattern carries its own flavor of meaning. Scholars number these molds: Form I, Form II, all the way up to Form X.

Think of it like this. Imagine you have one lump of clay — that's the root, the raw meaning. Now imagine ten different molds you could press it into. Same clay, but one mold makes it a simple cup, another makes it a deep bowl, another makes it something with handles for carrying. The clay didn't change. The *shape* changed what it can do.

The simple, plain mold is **Form I**. If this verb were in Form I, it would just mean "to watch for, to expect" — flat, neutral, like setting something down and glancing at it now and then.

But this verb is not in Form I.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the word in the ayah:

> **تَرَبَّصُوا — *tarabbaṣū***

This is **Form V** — the mold the grammarians write as *tafaʿʿala*. And Form V has two fingerprints you can both *see* and *hear*. First, a *ta-* sound is added at the front. Second — and this is the one I want you to notice — **the middle letter is doubled.** You can see it in the Arabic: that little mark, the *shadda*, sitting on the *bāʾ*. The *b* is pressed twice. *Ra-b-b-aṣa.*

Why does that doubling matter? Because across the Arabic verb system, doubling the middle radical is the language's way of saying: *this is not a light, glancing action. This is intensive. This is sustained. This is done with the whole self poured into it.* And the *ta-* on the front of Form V adds something more: a sense of the action turning back on the doer — deliberate, self-involved, something you take *upon yourself.*

So put those two fingerprints together. *Tarabbaṣa* is not "to wait" the way you wait for a bus, half-distracted, scrolling your phone. *Tarabbaṣa* is **to lie in wait.** To station yourself. To fix your eyes on a horizon and not look away, because you are personally, deeply invested in what is going to appear on it. It is the waiting of a hunter in the brush. It is the waiting of someone who has staked everything on a particular outcome and is leaning their whole body toward it.

Now hear it. Say the word slowly: *ta-rab-ba-ṣū.* Notice how that doubled *b* makes you *hold* — your mouth catches and lingers on it before releasing into the long final syllable. The word itself makes you wait inside it. The sound enacts the meaning. This is a word that *leans forward and holds its breath.*

[PAUSE]

So when the Meccans, back in verse thirty, used a related expression — *we await for him the calamity of fate* — they were not saying *we'll see what happens.* They were saying: **we are lying in wait for this man's destruction. We have fixed our eyes on it. We are leaning toward it with everything in us.** It was an active, invested, almost predatory patience.

And what does Allah tell His Prophet to say back? Not a different word. **The same word.** *Tarabbaṣū* — *go on, then. Lie in wait. Lean toward what you're waiting for. Fix your eyes on the horizon.*

Here is the first thing this single word reveals: **the verse does not lower itself to their level by arguing. It rises above them by agreeing.** *You want to play the waiting game — the intense, invested, leaning-forward waiting game? Fine. Let's both play it. Let's both lean toward the horizon.* And the moment you accept that frame, a question detonates underneath it — a question we have not answered yet:

*Leaning toward **what**?*

Hold that. We're coming back to it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The mirror — when both sides carry the same name

Let me show you something now that you can only see in the Arabic, and that I find genuinely breathtaking.

The verse uses the root *r-b-ṣ* **twice.** Once as the command to them, and once as a description of him.

To them: **تَرَبَّصُوا** — *tarabbaṣū* — "you all, lie in wait." A Form V verb.

About him: **ٱلْمُتَرَبِّصِينَ** — *al-mutarabbiṣīn* — "those who lie in wait." This is the *active participle* of the very same Form V verb. A participle, in Arabic, is what you get when you turn a verb into a *kind of person* — not "he waits" but "a waiter, one-who-waits," as a standing description, almost a title.

So follow the architecture of the sentence. Allah does not say: *they wait, but I will act.* He does not say: *they scheme, but I trust.* He places **the identical root, in the identical mold,** on both sides of the line.

> *Lie in wait* (to them) … *I am among the ones-who-lie-in-wait* (about him).

It is a mirror. The Prophet ﷺ is told to describe himself with **the exact same word He just commanded for his enemies.** Both parties are *mutarabbiṣūn.* Both are leaning toward the horizon. Both have fixed their eyes. On the surface — and this is the genius of it — they look like they are doing *precisely the same thing.*

[PAUSE]

Now, why would Allah do that? Why give the believer and the mocker the same label?

Think about what it does to the mockery. The Meccans intended *tarabbuṣ* — lying in wait — as something *they* were doing *to* him. He was the prey; they were the patient hunters. The word, in their mouths, had a victim and a victor built into it.

And the reply quietly dissolves that structure. *No. We are both hunters now. We are both watching the same horizon. We are both waiting for something to appear on it.* The Prophet ﷺ steps fully into their own posture — not as the hunted, but as a fellow watcher. He refuses the role of prey entirely.

And that refusal is devastating, because of what it implies. **You only join someone in waiting if you are unafraid of what the waiting will reveal.** A man who feared the horizon would beg, or run, or fight. He would do anything but calmly say, *yes, let's both keep watching.* The willingness to share their exact posture is itself the loudest possible claim of confidence. It says, without a single word of argument: *I am so certain of what is coming that I am happy to stand right next to you and wait for it together.*

The mirror of the word is a mirror that flatters one face and condemns the other — and from the outside, in that first instant, you cannot tell which is which. That's the trap. That's the irony loaded and waiting.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The missing word — what are they waiting *for*?

Now I want you to notice something that *isn't* there. And I want to teach you, first, why an absence in the Quran is never an accident.

When you read carefully, you learn to listen for the word that the grammar *expects* but the verse *withholds.* Arabic, like any language, has slots. Certain verbs reach out for an object — they feel incomplete without one. And in classical Arabic, this very verb, *tarabbaṣa*, normally takes its object through a little preposition, *bi-*: *tarabbaṣa bihi* — "he lay in wait **for him**," "he watched **for** it." The verb naturally wants you to name *the thing being waited for.*

Look at the ayah again:

> *Say: "Lie in wait — for indeed I am, with you, among those who lie in wait."*

Lie in wait **for what?**

The verse does not say. *Both* uses of the word — the command to them and the description of him — are left hanging, with the object stripped away. Nobody is told what they are watching the horizon *for.* The slot is left deliberately, conspicuously empty.

[PAUSE]

Now — the classical commentators do not leave us guessing about what each side was actually waiting for, because the surrounding verses make it plain. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and the others fill in both blanks the same way. They explain: the Meccans were lying in wait for **his death** — *tarabbaṣū* the Prophet's demise, his hilāk, the day time would carry him off. And he was told to say he was lying in wait, with them, for **their punishment** — the *ʿadhāb* that God's command would bring down upon them. As al-Baghawī puts it, he waits *"until the command of Allah comes upon you."* And several of them note, with a kind of quiet finality, where that horizon actually arrived: *fa-ʿudhdhibū yawma Badr bi-s-sayf* — they were punished on the Day of Badr, by the sword.

So the meaning is known. Why, then, does the *verse itself* leave the object out, when it could so easily have spelled it out?

Here is what the absence accomplishes — and I think it is the deepest thing in the ayah.

By stripping away the object, the verse makes the *two waitings look identical.* On the surface of the Arabic, the disbelievers and the Prophet are saying word-for-word the same thing: *we are watching the horizon.* Same verb, same mold, same empty slot. You cannot tell them apart from the words alone.

**But the object — the thing each side is actually waiting for — is the opposite.** They lean toward his end. He leans toward theirs. The grammar makes them twins; reality makes them mortal enemies. And the verse refuses to resolve that tension on the surface. It hands you two people staring at the same horizon, and lets you sit in the unbearable question: *which one of them is right about what's coming?*

[PAUSE]

This is what storytellers call *dramatic irony* — when the audience, or in this case the Divine Author and His Messenger, know how the scene ends, while one of the characters struts across the stage with no idea. The Meccans think they are the patient ones, the ones with time on their side. They have no idea that they have just been handed a script in which **their own word becomes the instrument of their own undoing.** They said *we wait.* They were told *wait, then.* And the horizon they were so confidently watching turned out to have *their* names on it.

The empty slot is the whole trap. Fill it in too early and the irony collapses. Leave it empty — as the verse does — and every reader, then and now, has to ask themselves: *if I were standing in that crowd, watching that horizon, so sure I knew what was coming — would I have been right?*

### The bridge

Step back and see what these three features have built together.

A word shaped to mean *intense, invested, leaning-forward waiting.* The same word placed on both sides of the conflict, so that mocker and Messenger wear the identical title. And the object of that waiting deliberately withheld, so that two opposite destinies hide behind one shared surface.

The construction has shown us something about words. Now let's ask what it reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange, telling thing that waiting does to the human soul.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Waiting is the lie detector of the heart

Let me start somewhere completely ordinary.

Two people are sitting in a hospital waiting room. Same hard plastic chairs, same hum of the vending machine, same clock on the wall. From across the room, they look identical — both just sitting, both just waiting.

But one of them is waiting for a routine result they've already been told is almost certainly fine. And the other is waiting to find out whether the worst thing they can imagine is true.

Watch them, and within a few minutes you'll know which is which. Not from anything they say. From their *bodies.* One sits still, breathes easy, glances at their phone, maybe even dozes. The other can't stop their leg from bouncing, checks the clock every ninety seconds, startles at every door that opens.

[PAUSE]

Here is the thing about waiting: **it cannot be faked.** Speech can be controlled. You can say confident words with a shaking heart. But waiting is a state of the whole nervous system, and it tells the truth about what you actually believe is coming. The person who is genuinely certain of a good outcome waits *calmly.* The person who fears the outcome cannot sit still in their own skin, no matter what they tell themselves.

This is precisely what makes the command in our ayah so quietly powerful. *Say: lie in wait — I'm waiting too.* The Prophet ﷺ is being asked to take on, in front of his enemies, the one posture that **cannot be performed.** You cannot bluff your way through years of waiting. If he were secretly afraid — if some part of him suspected the Meccans might actually be right, that maybe time really was on their side — the waiting would have eaten him alive. It would have shown.

And it didn't. For year after grinding year in Makkah, through boycott and starvation and the loss of those he loved, he *waited.* Calmly. The way you only wait when you already know how the story ends.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this home, because it is not really about a hospital room or seventh-century Makkah. It is about you.

Your own waiting is a mirror held up to your own faith. The thing you are anxious about right now — the result you keep refreshing, the conversation you're dreading, the future you cannot stop rehearsing in the dark — the *quality* of your waiting is reporting back to you, with total honesty, what you actually believe about who is in control of that horizon.

If you are frantic, it is not a sign that you are a bad person. It is information. It is your soul telling you: *somewhere, I am not yet sure that the One waiting on the other side of this is good, and wise, and on my side.*

The believer is not someone who never has to wait. The believer is someone whose waiting has begun to grow *calm* — not because they know the specific outcome, but because they have come to trust the One who holds it. *Tarabbuṣ* — that leaning-forward, eyes-on-the-horizon waiting — becomes, for the believer, not anxiety but a kind of *anticipation.* The same posture. A completely different heart.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time you catch yourself in that frantic, leg-bouncing, refresh-the-screen waiting, don't just try to distract yourself out of it. Stop, and ask the diagnostic question this ayah hands you: *What do I believe is on the other side of this horizon — and who do I believe is standing there?* Then take the waiting itself to Allah. Not the outcome — the *waiting.* "Ya Allah, make my waiting calm, because I trust You with what I cannot see." That is *tarabbuṣ* converted from fear into worship.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The cruelest answer to mockery is a calm one

Let me take you somewhere most of us know far too well.

Someone belittles you. Maybe to your face, maybe behind your back — but it gets to you. They've decided you're nothing. Not worth fighting. Just someone to be waited out, talked over, dismissed. And here is what that does to the human heart almost automatically: **it makes you want to prove them wrong, loudly, immediately.** Every instinct screams to defend yourself, to match their contempt with your own, to escalate until they take you seriously.

And almost every time we obey that instinct, we lose. Because the moment you scramble to defend yourself, you have confirmed their frame: *you are someone who needs to prove something.* The frantic defense is itself a kind of admission. They wanted to see you rattled, and there you are — rattled.

Now look again at how Allah answers the mockery of the Meccans. They have just called His Messenger a poet they're waiting to bury. And the reply contains:

- No defense.
- No counter-insult.
- No argument that he's *not* a poet.
- No raising of the voice.

Just: *Say — wait, then. I'm waiting too.*

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most dignified replies in all of human speech, and its power comes precisely from its *restraint.* It does not scramble. It does not escalate. It refuses to take the bait of proving anything. It simply — calmly — agrees to let *reality* be the judge, and steps back to watch.

And think about why only one kind of person can answer mockery this way. **You can only afford a calm reply when you are certain of the outcome.** The person who fires back insults, who has to win the argument in this exact moment — that person is, underneath, *unsure.* They need the victory *now* because they're not confident time will hand it to them. But the one who *knows* how it ends can afford to be gentle, even generous, even quiet. They don't need to win the exchange. They've already won the war; they're just waiting for the news to arrive.

The Prophet ﷺ is given the posture of a man who has read the last page of the book. *You think you're waiting me out? Beautiful. Let's wait together. I have nowhere to be.* There is almost a tenderness in it — and underneath the tenderness, an earthquake.

[PAUSE]

Here is where this presses on your life, and mine.

When you are mocked, diminished, written off — the ayah is not telling you to be passive. *Tarabbuṣ* is not passivity; remember, it's the intense, leaning-forward, eyes-fixed waiting. What the ayah is offering you is a different *source* for your steadiness. You don't have to win the moment. You don't have to make them take you back this instant. You can hand the outcome to the One who actually controls horizons, and then carry yourself with the calm of someone who knows the verdict is already written.

There's a sharp warning folded in here too, and we shouldn't skip it. The Meccans were *so sure* they were the patient, clever ones, watching the foolish poet dig his own grave. And they were the ones standing at Badr. **Confidence is not the same as being right.** The feeling of certainty — *I know how this ends, I'm on the winning side, time is mine* — that feeling lived just as strongly in the chests of the people this verse was warning. So the ayah turns and asks each of us, quietly: *and you — the future you're so sure of, the horizon you think has your name on it for good and your rival's name on it for ill — are you certain you've read the object of the verb correctly? Or have you, too, left the slot blank and filled it in with your own wishes?*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time someone makes you feel small and every nerve is begging you to fire back and prove yourself — try, just once, the posture of this ayah. Say less. Defend less. Hand the outcome upward, and let your calm be the argument. Not the calm of someone who's given up — the calm of someone who has read the last page. And in the very same breath, check your own certainty: am I sure of the ending because Allah has promised it, or only because I want it?

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we've done the work, stand back and look at the shape of the whole thing, because it is built with a precision you can only appreciate from here.

The verse is a **mirror folded in the middle.** On one side: *tarabbaṣū* — the command to them. On the other side: *al-mutarabbiṣīn* — the description of him. The same root, the same Form V mold, reflected across the fold. And right at the hinge, the word that joins the two sides: ***maʿakum*** — "**with you.**"

That little word is the pivot of the entire ayah. He is not waiting *against* them from a safe distance. He is told to stand *with* them — *maʿakum* — in the very act of waiting, sharing their exact posture, watching the exact same horizon. The closeness is the confidence. You only stand shoulder-to-shoulder with your accuser, watching the same skyline, when you are completely unafraid of what's going to rise over it.

And the thing both sides are leaning toward — the object of all that intense watching — is left out of the verse entirely, so that the two destinies stay hidden behind one shared surface until reality pulls them apart.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths turn out to be the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **grammar** gave us a single word worn by both sides, with its object stripped away — twins on the surface, opposites underneath.

The **themes** gave us the reason that matters: because *waiting cannot lie.* Two people can use the identical word, wear the identical posture, watch the identical horizon — and the entire difference between them is invisible in the moment and total in the end. It lives in one place only: *what each of them actually believes is coming, and who they believe is bringing it.*

The empty slot in the grammar **is** the test of the heart. Both sides said "we wait." Only one of them had read the horizon correctly.

### Questions to carry

- The thing I am most anxious about right now — when I examine the *quality* of my waiting, what is it honestly telling me about what I believe is coming, and who I believe is on the other side of it?
- Where in my life am I, like the Meccans, so *confident* I've read the future correctly that I've stopped asking whether my certainty comes from Allah's promise or just from my own wishes?
- When I'm mocked or dismissed, do I scramble to prove myself in the moment — and what would change if I genuinely believed I'd already read the last page?
- Is there a *tarabbuṣ* in my life — a long, hard waiting — that I could convert from anxiety into worship, simply by handing the *waiting itself*, not just the outcome, to Allah?

### One-sentence distillation

When you are certain of what Allah holds on the other side of the horizon, you can wear your enemy's own word, stand calmly at his side, and let the waiting itself become your most powerful reply.

### The closing invitation

You are always waiting for something. That part isn't optional. What this ayah invites is not the end of waiting, but the *transformation* of it — from the frantic, leg-bouncing dread of someone unsure who holds the future, into the steady, eyes-on-the-horizon calm of someone who has entrusted that future to the One who has never once broken a promise. Let your next hard wait be the place you practice it.

### Brief duʿā

*Ya Allah, You who hold every horizon I cannot see — when I am made to wait, make my waiting calm, not because I know the outcome, but because I know You. Strip the anxiety from my watching and leave only trust, and let me never mistake my own wishes for Your promise. And when I am made small, let my stillness be my answer, for I am content to wait with You.* Āmīn.
