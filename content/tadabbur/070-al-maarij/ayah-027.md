# The Twilight Heart: When Fear Is Made of Love

> وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم مِّنْ عَذَابِ رَبِّهِم مُّشْفِقُونَ
>
> *wa-lladhīna hum min ʿadhābi rabbihim mushfiqūn*
>
> **"And those who are apprehensive of the punishment of their Lord —"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Before we open this ayah, I want you to feel the room it was spoken into.

Surah Al-Ma'arij does not begin gently. It begins with a man — history remembers him as one of the mockers of Makkah — demanding that the punishment he keeps hearing about just *arrive already*. "Bring it on," he says, in effect. *Let it come.* And the surah answers him not with a threat first, but with a diagnosis. Allah turns the camera away from the punishment and onto the human heart that is sneering at it.

And here is what He says about us. **"Indeed, the human being was created anxious"** — *khuliqa halūʿā*. "When evil touches him, panicking" — *jazūʿā*. "And when good touches him, withholding" — *manūʿā* (70:19–21).

Sit in that for a moment. This is a portrait, and it is unflattering, and it is *you*. The default human being, the one made of clay and haste, lives on a seesaw. Bad news arrives and he comes apart — the racing heart, the sleepless ceiling at 3 a.m., the catastrophizing. Good news arrives and he clamps down — *mine, mine, don't touch it.* Anxiety on one end, hoarding on the other. That is the unrenovated human heart. That is the factory setting.

And then — the surah does something merciful. It says: *except.* **"Except the ones who pray"** — *illā l-muṣallīn* (70:22). And it begins to list them. Not by their slogans. Not by their labels. By the secret architecture of their inner lives. One trait. Then another. Then another. A blueprint of the heart that escaped the seesaw.

Our ayah is one beam in that blueprint.

> **"And those who are apprehensive of the punishment of their Lord."**

Now here is the thing that should stop you. The surah just told us the broken human being is *jazūʿ* — full of fear, full of panic. And now, describing the *saved* human being, it again uses the language of fear. The broken one fears. The saved one fears. **Both of them are afraid.**

So what on earth is the difference?

That question is our doorway. Because the answer is hidden inside a single, astonishing word — a word built from the same root as the colour of the sky at dusk. Stay with me. By the end, I think you will never hear the word "fear" the same way again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The word that means twilight: *mushfiqūn*

Let me teach you something before we touch the ayah.

In English, we have one main word for being afraid: *fear*. We stretch it to cover everything — fear of spiders, fear of God, fear of failure, fear of the dark. One word, doing the work of a dozen feelings. Arabic does not do this. Arabic has a whole vocabulary of fear, and each word is a different *flavour* of it.

There is **khawf** — raw fear, the alarm of a threat. There is **khashyah** — a fear that comes from *knowing* the greatness of what you fear, an awe-fear. There is **rahbah**, dread. There is **wajal**, the trembling of the heart. And there is **jazaʿ** — the very word the surah used for the broken human — which is panic, the fear that *unravels* you, that makes you fall apart.

And then there is the word in our ayah. **مُشْفِقُونَ** — *mushfiqūn*. The active participle of the root *sh-f-q*.

Now, watch where this root lives in the language. Because Arabic roots are like families — every word from the same three letters is a cousin, and they all share a face.

Do you know the Arabic word for the glow on the horizon just after the sun goes down? That soft, red-gold blush that is neither day nor night? That is **الشَّفَق** — *al-shafaq*. The twilight. The lexicographers define it precisely: it is the lingering redness in the sky after sunset. In fact, the prayer of ʿIshāʾ begins when that red *shafaq* finally fades. Twilight has a legal, lived meaning in the life of a Muslim.

And do you know the Arabic word for *tenderness* — the soft, protective, melting affection a mother feels bending over a sleeping child? That is **شَفَقَة** — *shafaqah*. Compassion. Tenderness. Riqqah.

[PAUSE]

Now stop. Look at what we have. From one single root come three things:

- **Shafaq** — twilight, the place where light and dark are *mixed*.
- **Shafaqah** — tenderness, compassion, love.
- **Mushfiq** — the person our ayah is describing.

This is not a coincidence of sound. This is the Arabic language telling you, at the level of the root itself, what kind of fear this is.

Let al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī — the great lexicographer of Qur'anic vocabulary — say it plainly. In his *Mufradāt*, he defines **al-ishfāq** as: *ʿināyatun makhlūṭatun bi-khawf* — **"a care mixed with fear."** And he explains *why* the word carries both at once: *because the one who is* mushfiq *loves the one he is concerned about, and fears for him at the same time.*

Do you feel it? **Ishfāq is fear with love stirred into it.** It is not the cold terror of a slave before a tyrant. It is the warm, trembling, *caring* fear of someone whose heart is bound by love to the very thing it fears. Just like the *shafaq* in the sky is not pure light and not pure darkness — it is the two *blended* into something more beautiful than either alone.

So when the broken human in verse 21 was *jazūʿ* — that was fear with **panic** stirred in. Fear that dissolves you. And when the saved human in verse 27 is *mushfiq* — that is fear with **love** stirred in. Fear that *refines* you.

Same human capacity. Same trembling heart. But one fear is made of dread, and the other is made of twilight.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this single word reveals: **The believer is not someone who has stopped being afraid. The believer is someone whose fear has been transfigured into tenderness.** The heart of the *mushfiq* lives permanently at dusk — never the false brightness of presuming itself safe, never the total darkness of despair. Always that delicate, glowing in-between, where reverence and love are the same colour.

### Element Two — *Rabb*, not the Avenger: who they are afraid *of*

Now look at *what* they fear. The ayah does not say they are apprehensive "of the punishment." It says they are apprehensive of **"the punishment of their Lord"** — *ʿadhābi **rabbi**him*.

Let me teach this name before we lean on it.

Allah has many names, and they are not interchangeable. He is *al-Qahhār*, the Subduer. *al-Muntaqim*, the Avenger. *Shadīd al-ʿiqāb*, severe in punishment. If the ayah wanted to make you simply *scared*, the language was right there. It could have said "the punishment of the Compeller." It could have said "the punishment of the Severe."

It said **Rabb**.

*Rabb* is the most intimate of the names of lordship. The root *r-b-b* means to nurture, to raise, to sustain something gradually from its weakness to its completion. A *rabb* is the one who *brings you up*. It is the word a child's whole world is built on. *Rabb al-bayt* is the master of the household who feeds it. When Allah teaches us to call to Him in our most desperate, naked moments, the Qur'an almost always puts *Rabbanā* — "**Our** Lord" — on our lips. *Rabbanā ātinā. Rabbanā lā tu'ākhidhnā. Rabbi-shraḥ lī ṣadrī.*

So read the ayah again with this in your bones:

> They are apprehensive of the punishment **of the One who raised them. Of the One who sustains them. Of the One who feeds them every morning and forgives them every night. Of the One they love.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see the tension now? It would be *easy* to fear an enemy. There is no spiritual achievement in dreading a tyrant — even a coward can do that. But to hold in your heart a real, trembling apprehension toward the One who is the source of every kindness you have ever received — that is something else entirely. That is not the fear of the prisoner. **That is the fear of the beloved who cannot bear to disappoint.**

This is exactly why the root had to be *sh-f-q*. You cannot feel *ishfāq* — fear-mixed-with-love — toward someone you do not love. The word and the name lock together like two halves of one truth: a fear that is tender, aimed at a Lord who is cherished. Remove the love and the whole thing collapses back into mere terror — back into *jazaʿ*.

What this single word *Rabb* reveals: **The believer's fear is not the distance between an enemy and a victim. It is the closeness between a child and the One who raised it — a closeness so real that the thought of grieving Him becomes the most frightening thing in the world.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — *mushfiqūn*, the participle: a state, not a mood

One more thing in the grammar, and it is quiet but it changes everything.

The ayah does not use a verb. It does not say "those who *become* afraid" or "those who *felt* fear." It uses an **active participle** — *ism al-fāʿil* — **مُشْفِقُونَ**.

Let me show you why that matters. In Arabic, a verb (*yakhāf* — "he fears") describes an *event*. Something that happens, then stops. But a participle (*khā'if*, *mushfiq* — "a fearer, an apprehensive one") describes a *characteristic*. A settled identity. The difference between saying "he is swimming right now" and "he is a swimmer." One is an act. The other is a *who you are*.

So the ayah is not describing a feeling that visits these people in the mosque on a Friday and leaves them by Saturday lunch. It is describing the **standing temperature of their hearts.** *Ishfāq* is not their mood. It is their constitution. They walk through the marketplace as *mushfiqūn*. They close a business deal as *mushfiqūn*. They lie down to sleep as *mushfiqūn*. The twilight never fully leaves their sky.

And notice — this matches the whole list around it. They are *those who are constant in their prayer* (70:23), *those who guard their prayer* (70:34). The surah is not collecting a checklist of religious *acts*. It is sketching a permanent inner *climate*. The participle *mushfiqūn* is woven from the same cloth: a fear that has become a *feature of the self*, not a feeling that comes and goes.

What this reveals: **Reverence, for these people, is not an occasion. It is a residence.** They do not visit the fear of Allah. They live there.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what the construction has shown us. A word built from *twilight* and *tenderness* — telling us this is fear infused with love. A divine name of *nurture* — telling us they are afraid of the One who cherishes them. And a participle of *permanence* — telling us this is not a mood but a home.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself, and about the strange, saving paradox at the centre of every life that ever found its way to Allah.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — Everyone is afraid. The only question is *of what.*

Let me start where you actually live.

You are anxious. I am anxious. It is the water we swim in. We live in the most medicated, most therapized, most *worried* generation in human history. There is a low hum of dread under modern life — about money, about health, about whether we are falling behind, about the message we sent that has not been answered, about the future curling like smoke at the edge of every quiet moment. We pour billions into trying to make the fear *stop*.

And here is the Qur'an, fourteen centuries early, having already told us the truth about ourselves: *khuliqa halūʿā* — the human being **was created anxious.** Not became anxious. *Created* that way. The fear is not a malfunction. It is a feature. You were built with a heart that *will* attach itself to something and tremble for it. That capacity is not the problem.

So the spiritual life is not — listen carefully — the spiritual life is *not* the project of becoming unafraid. That is the lie the world sells you: *cure your fear, eliminate it, achieve serenity.* But the broken human in verse 21 was full of fear, and the saved human in verse 27 is full of fear. **Faith does not delete your capacity for fear. It re-aims it.**

Think of fear as a river. The unrenovated human heart lets that river run wild — flooding into a thousand small terrors. Fear of the diagnosis. Fear of the layoff. Fear of what they think of me. Fear of being forgotten. Each of these is a little god, and your trembling heart bows to all of them in turn, exhausted, never at peace, because there are *infinite* of them and they never stop coming. That is *jazaʿ* — fear scattered, fear shattered, fear that unravels you because it has no single home.

And the *mushfiq*? He has taken that same river and **dug one channel for it.** He fears *one thing* — to grieve his Lord. And here is the miracle: when all your fear flows into the fear of Allah, **every smaller fear loses its teeth.** Because if I have made my peace with the Owner of all outcomes, then what is the diagnosis? What is the layoff? What is their opinion? They were never the real powers. *The Prophet ﷺ taught us in supplication to ask Allah for "khashyatika"* — Your awe — *as the very thing that comes "between us and acts of disobedience."* The great fear swallows the small fears whole.

This is the paradox that should reorganize your life: **The person who fears Allah correctly is the least anxious person alive.** Not because the fear is smaller — because it is *singular*. He sleeps, while the world that "fears nothing" lies awake fearing everything.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. The next time the 3 a.m. dread comes — the racing, scattered, *jazūʿ* fear — do not try to argue it away. Do not try to become unafraid. **Re-channel it.** Take that exact trembling and turn it, deliberately, toward the only One worth trembling before. Say: *of everything I could fear tonight, let me fear only displeasing You — and into Your hands I give all the rest.* You are not killing the river. You are digging it a home.

### Theme Two — The fear that proves the love: never feeling "safe," because you care too much

Now let me take you somewhere tender.

Picture the child of a truly good father. Not a tyrant — a *good* one. Warm, generous, patient, the kind of father who has never once raised his hand. Now ask: does that child fear his father?

The strange answer is *yes* — but it is a very particular kind of fear. The child is not afraid of being *beaten*. He is afraid of the *look*. The disappointment in the eyes of someone whose love he cannot live without. The child of a tyrant fears pain. The child of a good father fears *grieving him*. And — here is the point — **the second fear is deeper than the first**, because it is made of love. You can stop fearing a tyrant by running far enough away. You can never stop fearing to disappoint someone you adore, because the love goes with you wherever you run.

*That* is *ishfāq*. That is the twilight fear. That is why the ayah said *Rabb* — the One who raised you — and not the Avenger.

Now follow this into the most beautiful detail of the whole passage. Read the very next ayah:

> **"Indeed, the punishment of their Lord is not [a thing] to be felt secure from"** — *inna ʿadhāba rabbihim ghayru ma'mūn* (70:28).

Stop and feel the architecture here. Allah has just spent verses listing these people's extraordinary devotion. They pray constantly. They give the known right of their wealth. They believe in the Day of Reckoning. They are *mushfiqūn*. They guard their chastity, keep their trusts, stand by their testimony, protect their prayer. By any human accounting, these are the *safe* ones. The graduates. The ones who have earned the right to relax.

And immediately after praising them, Allah says: **and the punishment of their Lord is never something one can feel secure from.** Even *they* do not get to feel safe.

[PAUSE]

Now — is that cruel? It sounds, at first, almost harsh. They did everything, and still no security?

Look again. Through the lens of *ishfāq*, this is not cruelty. **This is the very proof that their love is real.** Because the one who truly loves never *presumes*. The moment a heart says "I've done enough, I'm safe now, I can coast" — in that exact moment the twilight has been replaced by a false, flat daylight of self-satisfaction. And self-satisfaction is the death of love. The *mushfiq* never reaches it. Not because Allah is stingy with reassurance, but because the *caring itself* keeps him tender. His refusal to feel "safe" is not anxiety — it is **devotion that never goes cold.**

This is the great balance the scholars called *bayna l-khawf wa-l-rajāʾ* — between fear and hope. The believer flies to Allah on two wings. Pure hope without fear becomes presumption, the lazy assumption of the spoiled. Pure fear without hope becomes despair, the paralysis of the hopeless. *Ishfāq* is the two wings beating together. It is *shafaq* — neither the blinding noon of "I'm certainly saved" nor the black midnight of "I'm certainly doomed." It is the trembling, glowing dusk where a heart stays soft, stays striving, stays *in love*, all the way to the grave.

And do you notice — the Qur'an uses this exact word *mushfiqūn* elsewhere for the same souls. *"Those who fear their Lord unseen, and are apprehensive (mushfiqūn) of the Hour"* (21:49). *"Indeed, those who, from awe of their Lord, are apprehensive (mushfiqūn)"* (23:57) — and that passage ends, just a few lines later, with: *"those are the ones who hasten to good deeds."* Did you catch the movement? The *ishfāq* does not freeze them. It does not crush them into despair. **It launches them.** The tender fear becomes the engine of every good thing they rush to do. Fear made of love does not paralyze. It *propels.*

What does this change about tomorrow? It frees you from a quiet, corrosive question that haunts sincere people: *"Am I safe yet? Have I done enough?"* The ayah, with its neighbour, answers gently: *you will never feel "safe," and that is not a sign of weak faith — it is the fragrance of real love.* So stop chasing the feeling of having arrived. Chase, instead, the *staying tender*. Let the next prayer be offered not by someone trying to reach a finish line, but by someone who simply cannot bear the thought of cooling toward the One who raised them. **Trade the question "have I done enough?" for the posture "I never want to stop."**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see what the surah built.

It opened with a man *demanding* the punishment — sneering, secure in his own disbelief, feeling utterly *safe* from any consequence. The whole surah is, in a sense, the dismantling of that false security. And right at the heart of the believers' description, Allah places a people who are the man's exact opposite: not demanding the punishment but *apprehensive* of it; not feeling safe but holding, lovingly, to the truth that *no one is ever safe* (70:28).

The mocker felt secure and was in the gravest danger. The *mushfiqūn* refuse to feel secure and are precisely the ones who will be — as the surah ends — *"in gardens, honoured"* (70:35). The false safety leads to ruin. The holy un-safety leads home.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistics and the themes were the same truth approached from two sides.

The **word** *mushfiqūn* — built from twilight and tenderness — told us this fear is *made of love*. The **theme** told us why that matters: because only a fear made of love can swallow all your smaller fears and still keep your heart soft.

The **name** *Rabb* told us *who* they fear — the One who raised them. The **theme** told us why: because the deepest fear is never of a tyrant's whip but of a beloved's disappointment.

The **participle** told us this is a permanent *residence*, not a passing mood. And the **next ayah** told us why it must be permanent: because the moment you feel "safe," the twilight goes out, and love goes cold.

One insight, from every direction: **The saved heart is not the fearless heart. It is the heart that took its God-given capacity for fear, poured all of it into love of its Lord, and chose to live forever at twilight.**

### Questions to Carry

- The broken human and the saved human are *both* afraid. Which fears actually run my days — the scattered ones, or the single one?
- When I imagine standing before Allah, do I picture a Tyrant I want to escape, or a *Rabb* I cannot bear to disappoint? What does my answer reveal about whether I truly love Him?
- Is there a quiet place in me that feels "safe" — that has decided I've done enough? What has that false daylight cost my tenderness?
- The *mushfiqūn* of Surah 23 *"hasten to good deeds."* Does my fear of Allah propel me forward, or has it curdled into either despair or complacency?
- If *ishfāq* is a residence and not a visit — do I live there, or do I only knock on the door on Fridays?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The believer is not someone who has conquered fear, but someone whose fear has been transfigured into a tender, permanent twilight of love and reverence for the very Lord who raised him.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not leave this reflection and become unafraid. That was never the goal, and the Qur'an never asked it of you. But perhaps — gently — you can leave it carrying your fear *differently*. Perhaps the next time dread rises, you will recognize it as a river looking for a channel, and you will dig it the only home worthy of it. Perhaps you will stop chasing the feeling of being "safe," and start cherishing the feeling of staying soft. Perhaps you will let your fear become the colour of dusk — and in doing so, discover it was love all along.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, our Rabb — the One who raised us from nothing, who feeds us and forgives us without count — make our hearts mushfiqūn before You. Take the scattered fears that exhaust us and gather them into one trembling, tender awe of You alone. Keep our sky at twilight: never the false daylight of feeling safe, never the black night of despair — only the glowing dusk where fear and love are the same colour, all the way to the day we meet You. Āmīn.*
