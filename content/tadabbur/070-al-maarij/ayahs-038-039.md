# The Garden They Covet and the Clay They Forgot

### Surah Al-Ma'arij — Ayahs 38-39

> أَيَطْمَعُ كُلُّ ٱمْرِئٍ مِّنْهُمْ أَن يُدْخَلَ جَنَّةَ نَعِيمٍ ۝ كَلَّآ ۖ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰهُم مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ

**A-yaṭmaʿu kullu-mri'in minhum an yudkhala jannata naʿīm. Kallā — innā khalaqnāhum mimmā yaʿlamūn.**

*Does every one of them then long to be admitted into a Garden of Bliss? No indeed! We created them from that which they know.*

---

## INTRODUCTION — Rebuilding the World

Picture the scene the way the first listeners would have seen it, because these two ayahs are not floating in the air. They are aimed.

A man stands in Makkah, reciting. He is calling people to one God, to accountability, to a Day that is coming. And a crowd gathers — not to listen, but to perform. They press in close. The ayahs just before these describe them in a way that is almost cinematic: *muhṭiʿīn* — racing toward him with their necks craned forward, eyes fixed, **clustering in knots on his right and on his left** (70:36-37). You can see it. The huddles. The whispered side-comments. The eyebrows raised at one another. The laughter that is meant to be overheard.

The classical commentators preserve what that mockery sounded like. Al-Qurtubi reports from the mufassirūn that the mushrikūn would gather around the Prophet ﷺ, listen to him, belie him, and ridicule the believers — saying, *"If these people are going to enter Paradise, then we'll enter it before them. And if they're given anything there, we'll be given more."* And in al-Baghawī's narration, Ibn ʿAbbās captures the absurdity in a single phrase: *does each of them really expect to enter **My** Garden, the way the Muslims enter it, and enjoy its bliss — while he has called My Prophet a liar?*

Hold that picture. A man rejecting the message with his mouth, and assuming the reward with his heart. Mocking the road, and expecting to arrive at the destination.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the hook. We read these ayahs and we think they are about *them* — those mockers, fourteen centuries ago, in that specific street. But Allah does not phrase it as a description of a crowd. He phrases it as a question about **every single individual**. And the question He asks contains one particular verb — a verb for a very specific kind of wanting — and one grammatical move so quiet that you can read past it a hundred times and never notice that it has dismantled the entire assumption.

By the time we finish, I want you to see why the answer to "Does every one of them long for the Garden?" is a single, door-slamming word — *Kallā* — and why the proof Allah offers is not a sermon about their sins, but a reminder of something they already know about where they came from.

Let's begin where Allah begins: with the word for *wanting*.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yaṭmaʿu* — Not Every Longing Is the Same

Let me teach you something about how Arabic handles desire before we touch the ayah, because English flattens this and the flattening costs us everything.

In English we have one main word — *want*. I want water. I want success. I want Paradise. One verb does all the work. But a language built for precision does not let *wanting* be a single undifferentiated lump. It asks: what *kind* of wanting? Wanting grounded in legitimate effort? Wanting that flows from hope in a generous giver? Or wanting that reaches greedily for something you have not worked toward and have no real claim upon?

Arabic has separate words for these. There is *rajā'* — hope, the kind that rests on a promise and walks toward it. There is *raghba* — desire, an inclination toward a thing. And then there is the word Allah chose here:

> **أَيَطْمَعُ** — *a-yaṭmaʿu*

The root is **ṭ-m-ʿ** (ط م ع). And *ṭamaʿ* is not neutral wanting. *Ṭamaʿ* is **coveting** — an eager, grasping longing for something that lies beyond your reach or beyond your right. The lexicographers tie it to greed. It is the wanting of the eye that fixes on what is not yours. When the Quran wants to name a craving that has detached itself from any basis — a hand reaching for fruit on a branch it never planted — this is the word.

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say *a-yarjū* — does each of them *hope* for the Garden? Because hope would have dignified them. Hope is what the believer carries: he works, he falls short, and he hopes in the mercy of his Lord. That hope is beautiful; it is *built on something*. Allah will not give these mockers that word. He gives them *ṭamaʿ* — **the greed of a man who wants the prize while sneering at the One who awards it.**

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing pressing on this verb, sitting right in front of it: the letter *hamza*, the **أ** of *a-yaṭmaʿu*. This is an interrogative — but stay with me, because it is not a real question. Allah is not gathering information. The grammarians call this *istifhām inkārī* — the **denying question**, the question that is actually a rejection wearing the mask of a question.

Think of how we do this in everyday speech. A man insults you all day, and at sunset asks to borrow your car. You say: *"You expect to drive my car?"* You are not inquiring about his expectations. You are telling him *no* — and you are telling him that his very assumption is outrageous. That is the *hamza* here. **"Does every one of them actually covet the Garden of Bliss?"** is not a question awaiting data. It is the rebuke beginning to fall, and the heaviness of it is already in the air before the explicit *Kallā* arrives.

So the very first word has told us two things: their wanting is a *grasping* wanting, and the form of the sentence has already begun to refuse it.

[PAUSE — let that settle]

### Element Two: *Yudkhala* — The Quietest Word in the Verse

Now I want to show you the move I promised — the one you can read past forever.

The verb for entering the Garden is from the root **d-kh-l** (د خ ل), to enter. Simple enough. But look closely at the *form* Allah chose:

> **أَن يُدْخَلَ** — *an yudkhala*

This is not *yadkhula* — "that he **enter**." It is *yudkhala* — **"that he be admitted,"** "that he be *made* to enter." The difference is the difference between active and passive voice, and in Arabic it is also the difference between two verb-patterns: the simple act of entering, versus Form IV, *adkhala* — to *cause* someone to enter, to *admit* them. Here it stands in the passive: the person is the one *admitted*. The action is done *to* him.

Let me teach you why grammar like this is never decoration. When a sentence is passive — *he was admitted* — the doer disappears from view. *Admitted by whom?* The grammar deliberately holds the agent off-stage. And the question every careful reader must ask of a passive construction is: **who is the hidden hand?**

Read it now and feel the floor shift. They do not *enter* the Garden — as though it were a public square anyone could walk into. They can only ever *be admitted* to it. And admission requires an Admitter. There is an Owner at the gate. The door does not open from their side; it opens from His.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what this single grammatical choice has done to their whole posture?

These are people who are, in that very moment, *rejecting* the One who holds the gate. And the verb Allah selects to describe their fantasy quietly encodes the contradiction: **they covet being *admitted by the very Lord they are mocking.*** It is not that they plan to storm Paradise. It is that they expect *Him* — the One whose Prophet they call a liar — to usher them in personally. Ibn ʿAbbās's phrasing in al-Baghawī suddenly snaps into focus: *to enter **My** Garden*. The Garden is owned. The passive voice is the fingerprint of the Owner, pressed silently into the verb.

Had Allah said *yadkhula* — "that he enter" — the absurdity would have been muffled. The active voice might have let us imagine the Garden as a destination one simply reaches. The passive *yudkhala* makes it unmistakable: there is no reaching it. There is only **being let in** — by permission, by grace, by the decision of its Lord.

I'll add one layer the scholars preserve, as a camera angle rather than a contradiction. Al-Qurtubī notes a variant reading — *an yadkhula*, in the active — recited by al-Ḥasan and others. That reading is not a rival; it is a second lens. Read actively, it exposes their *self-confidence* — "that **he** walk right in." Read in the dominant passive of the muṣḥaf, *yudkhala*, it exposes their *dependence* — "that **he be let in**" by the One he denies. Put the two angles together and the full portrait emerges: people brimming with the confidence of those who walk in freely, about a place that can only ever be entered by being admitted. **The arrogance and the dependence, photographed at once.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Kullu-mri'in* — They Are Counted One at a Time

There is a third choice, and it governs the *scale* of the question.

Allah does not say *a-yaṭmaʿūna* — "do **they** (collectively) covet?" — which would have addressed the crowd as a crowd, a single mocking mass. He says:

> **كُلُّ ٱمْرِئٍ مِّنْهُمْ** — *kullu-mri'in minhum*

*Kull* — every, each. And then a singular, indefinite noun: *imri'* — an individual person, **one man, one soul**, standing alone. Not the group. **Each. Single. One.**

Watch what this does. The grammar reaches into the laughing huddle and pulls the people apart from one another. It dissolves the crowd into a row of isolated individuals, and it sets the question in front of each face, one at a time: *And you — do **you** covet the Garden, while you stand here mocking? And you beside him — do **you**?* The mockery was a group activity. Allah refuses to judge it as one. He individualizes it, because **the assumption of reward is a private fantasy each soul nurses alone**, even while it borrows courage from the crowd.

And here the two ayahs reach back and clasp hands with the rest of the surah — this is the architecture. Earlier in this same Surah Al-Maʿārij, Allah described the Day itself: the Day when *no intimate friend will ask about his friend* (70:10), the Day the guilty man *would ransom himself from the punishment with his own children, his wife, his brother* (70:11-13) — the Day the crowd shatters and **every person stands utterly alone**. So when these mockers cluster together in their knots on the right and the left, drawing confidence from one another, the grammar of *kullu-mri'in* is already previewing the verdict: **the crowd that mocks together will be questioned one by one.** The huddle is temporary. The *imri'*, the single soul, is what stands before the gate.

And the Garden itself — *jannata naʿīm*, a Garden of *Bliss*, with *naʿīm* left **indefinite**. In Arabic, leaving it indefinite here lifts it up: not "the" garden you can picture, but *a* Bliss beyond specifying, beyond imagining. (Al-Muyassar fills in what that indefiniteness implies — *jannat al-naʿīm al-dāʾim*, the Garden of *lasting* bliss, a delight that does not run out.) They are coveting the single greatest gift in existence — while refusing its Giver. The grandness of what they want is itself part of the rebuke.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

Step back and see what three quiet choices have built.

*Yaṭmaʿu* told us their wanting is **greed without a claim**. *Yudkhala* told us the Garden has an **Owner at the gate**, and they covet being let in by the One they deny. *Kullu-mri'in* told us each of them will be **stood alone** before that gate, the crowd dissolved.

The sentence has already answered itself before Allah says a word of answer. And then He says the word.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about a verb, or a crowd in Makkah — but about something coiled inside every human chest, including ours.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Entitlement That Wants the Destination and Refuses the Road

Let me start where you already live.

There is a particular kind of expectation we all carry, often without ever inspecting it. Ask almost anyone — including people who pray little and submit less — *"Do you think you'll be okay in the end? Heaven, the good place, whatever you call it?"* And watch how reliably the answer comes back: *"Yeah, I think so. I'm a good person."* It is one of the most universal human assumptions there is. The reward is **assumed**. It is the default setting of the ego: *of course things will go well for me.*

Psychologists have a plain name for the engine underneath this. It is the gap between **how we judge ourselves and how we judge others.** When someone else fails, we say it's their character. When we fall short, we say it's our circumstances — and we quietly grant ourselves the outcome we haven't earned. We want the diploma without the years. The fitness without the discipline. The trust without the honesty. **The destination, while declining the road.**

This is *ṭamaʿ*. This is exactly the word. Not hope built on effort — greed reaching for a prize it has done nothing to deserve. And Allah is showing us, in these two ayahs, that this entitlement does not stay in the world of careers and diplomas. **It climbs all the way up to Paradise.** The ego will covet *Jannah* itself on no other basis than its own good opinion of itself.

[PAUSE]

Now feel why the mockers are the sharpest possible case. They are not merely neutral toward the Prophet ﷺ. They are *actively* belittling the message — and *still* assuming the reward. Al-Qurtubī's report: *"If these enter Paradise, we'll enter before them."* Read that again. They have decided their entry is so secure that they're already arguing about **seating order.** They want front-row places in a House whose Builder they are insulting.

And here is the part that should make each of us go quiet, because it is so easy to point at *them* and miss the mirror. The mockers are an extreme of a thing that lives, in milder doses, in us. The believer too can slide into a soft *ṭamaʿ*: *I pray, more or less. I'm Muslim. I'm basically a good person. It'll be fine.* The reward becomes assumed. The works thin out, but the expectation stays fat. And Allah's word for that posture — whether in a Makkan mocker or a comfortable believer who has stopped striving — is the same: not *rajā'*, hope that walks, but *ṭamaʿ*, greed that lounges.

How does the Quran cure this? Not by telling us to abandon hope — never. It cures it by **reattaching the wanting to the road.** Look at how Allah speaks of the people who will actually be admitted, elsewhere: *"And those who give what they give while their hearts are fearful"* (23:60) — they *give*, they *strive*, and even then their hearts tremble. That is *rajā'*: wanting that works and still fears. The mockers' *ṭamaʿ* is its photographic negative: not working, not fearing, only assuming.

**The practical turn, for tomorrow:** Tonight, before you sleep, ask yourself one honest question — *On what, exactly, am I resting my expectation of mercy?* If the answer is *because I'm a good person*, that is *ṭamaʿ* — the mockers' word. If the answer is *because I am trying, I am turning, I am asking, and He is generous beyond my deserving* — that is *rajā'*, and it is the hope of the people of the Garden. The cure is not to want Paradise less. **It is to stop assuming it, and start walking toward it** — with a heart that hopes *and* trembles. The same wanting, reattached to the road.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: "From That Which They Know" — The Origin You Forgot On Purpose

Now the answer. And notice *how* Allah answers, because it is not what you would expect.

> **كَلَّا** — *Kallā.*

One word. *No. Never. Not a chance.* The grammarians call *kallā* a particle of **deterrence and rebuke** — it doesn't merely negate, it *slams*. It is the door closing. After the whole fantasy of front-row seats, Allah does not argue. He shuts it: *Kallā.*

And then — here is the surprise — He does not follow it with a catalogue of their sins. He does not say "No, because you lied, mocked, oppressed, disbelieved." He says something far stranger and far deeper:

> **إِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰهُم مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ** — *Indeed, We created them from that which they know.*

*From that which they know.* Allah veils the substance — He does not name it — and that veiling is itself the rebuke. *You know what We made you from.* The classical commentators are unanimous on the referent: the *nuṭfah*, the lowly fluid, the same despised drop the Quran names plainly elsewhere — *"Did We not create you from a fluid, deemed contemptible?"* (77:20). He does not even dignify it by naming it here. *Mimmā yaʿlamūn* — *from that thing you already know about.* The euphemism is a held mirror.

Sit with the logic, because it is breathtaking. Their crime, at root, is **arrogance** — the arrogance that mocks a prophet and then expects a throne in Paradise. And Allah does not refute arrogance with a list of offenses. He refutes it with **biology.** *You — who carry yourself as though Paradise owes you a front seat — were a forgotten drop. We made you from it. You **know** this.*

And al-Muyassar sharpens the blade by adding one word the rebuke is carrying silently: *kaghayrihim* — **"like everyone else."** The despised fluid they were formed from is the *same* fluid as every other human being — including the very believers they were busy claiming they would outrank into the Garden. Their raw material is *identical* to the material of the people they sneer at. So whatever could ever distinguish one soul from another at that gate cannot be substance — it can only be faith and the works of faith. And that is precisely what they withheld. *From where, then,* al-Muyassar voices the verse's challenge, *would they claim the honor — yatasharrafūn — of entering the Garden of Bliss?* The drop confers no rank; faith would have, and they refused it. They reach for precedence with empty hands and a borrowed body.

[PAUSE]

Why is *origin* the perfect cure for arrogance? Because arrogance is, at its core, **a story of self-sufficiency** — *I am the source of my own worth; I generated my own standing.* And nothing detonates that story faster than the memory of where you actually began. You did not assemble yourself. You were assembled. From the lowliest of materials. By Another. **The being who cannot account for his own first cell is in no position to feel entitled to the eternal Garden.**

And this is where the architecture of the entire surah completes itself — and I want you to feel the whole arc snap shut.

Go back to the beginning of this same Surah Al-Maʿārij. Early on, Allah diagnosed the human condition: *"Indeed, man was created **anxious / grasping** — halūʿan — when evil touches him, panicking; and when good touches him, withholding"* (70:19-21). *Halūʿ* — that restless, grasping, never-satisfied disposition. **That is the disease named in the opening.**

And here, at the surah's close, we watch that same disease in its most extreme bloom: the grasping (*halūʿ*) nature has reached so far that it now greedily covets (*yaṭmaʿu*) **Paradise itself**, on no basis at all. The opening diagnosed the grasping; the ending shows it grasping at the highest possible object.

And both verses ground their verdict in the *same place* — **how the human was made.** *Khuliqa halūʿan* — "he was created grasping" (70:19). *Innā khalaqnāhum mimmā yaʿlamūn* — "We created them from what they know" (70:39). The disease is in the creation; and the cure — humility — is in *remembering* the creation. **The same fact that explains why you grasp is the fact that should stop you from grasping.** You were made from a drop. Know it, and the entitlement dies in your chest.

[PAUSE]

The modern bridge here is almost too easy, because we are living through an age that has forgotten its origin *on purpose*. We are told, in a hundred quiet ways, that we are self-made. The self-made man. Build your own brand. You are the author of your story. And the more thoroughly a person believes he authored himself, the more naturally he believes he is *owed* — owed success, owed comfort, owed, in the end, a good eternity. The forgetting of origin and the swelling of entitlement are the **same motion.**

Allah's antidote is not complicated. It is *remembering*. The Quran returns again and again to the drop, the clay, the dust — not to shame you, but to **free** you. Because the soul that truly remembers it was a drop cannot stay arrogant, and the soul that cannot stay arrogant becomes a candidate for the Garden — not by *ṭamaʿ*, but by the humility that *yudkhala*, "to be admitted," was quietly waiting for all along. **You cannot demand entry. You can only become the kind of soul the Owner chooses to admit.** And that soul is a humble one.

**The practical turn, for tomorrow:** The next time you feel that flush of *I deserve this* — about a success, a status, a place you assume is yours — pause and run Allah's own argument on yourself: *mimmā yaʿlamū* — *from what I know.* Remember, concretely, what you were made from. Not as self-hatred — as **truth that liberates.** It is one of the reasons we put our forehead — the highest, proudest part of us, the seat of the ego — onto the lowest ground, the dust, several times a day. Sujūd is *mimmā yaʿlamū* made into a posture. **The drop, returning to the dust, choosing to remember.** Do it tomorrow with this verse in your heart, and watch what it does to your sense of entitlement.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now that the work is done, stand back and see the whole shape, because Surah Al-Maʿārij has been building this since its first line.

The surah **opens** with a mocker *demanding* the punishment, sarcastically, as if he were entitled to summon it (70:1). It **diagnoses** the human as *halūʿ* — grasping, restless, never content (70:19). It **describes** the Day each soul stands utterly alone, ready to ransom his own children (70:11-14). And it **closes** with the grasping nature reaching its final, absurd peak: each isolated soul greedily coveting the Garden of Bliss from the very Lord he mocks — answered by *Kallā*, and by a single fact about a single drop. And in the very next breath, as Ibn Kathīr preserves, that Owner swears an oath of His own: that He is *fully able to replace them with others better than them, and none can outrun Him* (70:40-41). The gate was never theirs to claim; the One who admits could empty the line and refill it at will.

Diagnosis and verdict rest on the **same word**: *khuliqa* — *created*. *Created grasping* (70:19). *Created from what you know* (70:39). The disease and its cure are both written in your origin.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two directions.

From the **words**: *yaṭmaʿu* (greed, not hope) + *yudkhala* (admitted, not entering — there is an Owner) + *kullu-mri'in* (each, alone) = a grammar that exposes entitlement and dependence in the same breath.

From the **themes**: the ego covets the destination while refusing the road, and forgets the origin that would have humbled it — until Allah holds up the mirror of the drop.

They meet in one place: **Paradise is not assumed. It is granted — to the humble, by its Owner, to souls who remembered they were made from a drop and walked the road anyway.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When I imagine my own ending, do I *hope* — or do I quietly *assume*? On what, exactly, does that expectation rest?
2. Where in my life am I wanting the destination while declining the road — the reward without the works that earn it?
3. The mockers argued over *seating order* in a House they were insulting. Where do I do a softer version of this — claiming standing I have not built?
4. What would change in me if I genuinely remembered, several times a day, *what I was made from*?
5. Is my forehead's daily journey to the dust changing my heart's sense of what it is owed — or have I let even sujūd go quiet?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**They greedily covet being admitted to the Garden by the very Lord they mock — but the gate has an Owner, the soul stands alone before it, and the drop they were made from gives them no claim; only the humble are let in.**

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing here asks you to want Paradise less. Want it with everything in you. The invitation is only this: **stop assuming it, and start walking toward it** — trading the *ṭamaʿ* that lounges for the *rajā'* that works and trembles. Let the memory of the drop empty the entitlement out of your chest, and let the empty space fill with the only posture the Owner ever admits: humility that hopes.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who made me from a drop I cannot even bear to dwell on — keep me from the greed that covets Your Garden while forgetting You are its Owner. Do not let me assume Your mercy; let me walk toward it, hoping and afraid. And when the crowd dissolves and I stand alone at the gate, admit me — not because I deserved it, but because You are the One who admits.*

*Āmīn.*
