# The Portion That Was Never Yours

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, slowly, before we go anywhere near them.

> **وَٱلَّذِينَ فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ حَقٌّ مَّعْلُومٌ ۝ لِّلسَّآئِلِ وَٱلْمَحْرُومِ**
>
> *wa-lladhīna fī amwālihim ḥaqqun maʿlūm — li-s-sāʾili wa-l-maḥrūm*
>
> "And those in whose wealth there is a known, appointed right — for the one who asks, and the one deprived who does not ask."

Two short verses. Eight words. And it would be easy to read them as a simple instruction: *the good people give some of their money to the poor.* You could nod and move on.

But I want to stop you at the door before you walk through too quickly. Because there is a single word here — **مَّعْلُومٌ**, *maʿlūm*, "known" — that quietly rearranges everything. And there is a second word — **ٱلْمَحْرُومِ**, *al-maḥrūm*, "the deprived one" — that names a person you have almost certainly never gone looking for.

### Rebuild the world

Before we open these words, you have to feel where they land in the surah, because they are not floating in space. They are an answer to something.

A few verses earlier, Allah delivers one of the most unflinching descriptions of the human being in the entire Qur'an:

> **إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا ۝ إِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا ۝ وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا**
>
> "Indeed, the human being was created *halūʿan* — graspingly anxious. When evil touches him, frantic and despairing. And when good touches him, **withholding**." *(70:19–21)*

Sit with that diagnosis for a moment. This is not a description of bad people. It says *al-insān* — the human being, as such. You. Me. Built with a default setting. And the default is a kind of clenching. When life squeezes us, we panic. When life is good to us, we close our hands. *Manūʿ* — withholding. Hoarding. A fist.

And then, immediately, Allah says: **إِلَّا ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ** — *"except the ones who pray."* Except them. There is a cure. There are people who were born with the same clenched fist as everyone else, and something pried it open.

And then He lists what those people are like. And one of the very first things on the list — the very antidote to the *withholding* of verse 21 — is these two ayahs we are sitting with. The disease was a closed hand. The cure, named here, is a hand that has already given away part of what it holds, because part of what it holds was never its to keep.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

So here is what I want you to notice, and then I want you to wait while we earn it.

This verse does not say *they give from their wealth*. It says there is a **right** — a *ḥaqq* — that lives **inside** their wealth. A right that belongs to *someone else*. And it says that right is **known** — *maʿlūm* — measured, fixed, defined before the giving ever happens.

And it does not say this right is for "the poor." It names *two* people. One of them you would notice across a crowded street. The other one Allah had to name **precisely because you would never notice him at all.**

Let's go find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ḥaqq* — a "right," not a gift

Let me teach you something about one Arabic word before we let it touch this verse, because everything turns on it.

In Arabic there is a word for a favor you do — *faḍl*, *iḥsān*, a kindness, a grace you extend from above. And there is a completely different word for something that is *owed*: **ḥaqq**, from the root **Ḥ-Q-Q** (ح-ق-ق). *Ḥaqq* is the word for truth, for what is established, for what is rightfully due. When you say someone has a *ḥaqq* over you, you are not being generous. You are acknowledging a **debt**. A claim. Something that, if you withhold it, you have not merely been stingy — you have been *unjust*.

Think of the difference in your own life. If I *give* you a gift, I am above you in that moment; I am the benefactor, and you owe me gratitude. But if I *owe* you a debt and I pay it, I am not above you at all. I am simply returning what was already yours. I get no halo for it. I was holding your property.

Now look at what Allah chose:

> **فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ حَقٌّ**
>
> "In their wealth is a **ḥaqq** — a *right*."

[PAUSE]

He did not say *they give a kindness*. He said there is a **claim** inside their wealth. The poor person is not a recipient of your charity in this framing. The poor person is a **creditor**.

Let's sit with this, because it is doing something violent to a feeling most of us carry without examining it. Most of us, when we give, feel a small swell of virtue. *Look what I parted with. Look how good I am.* The word *ḥaqq* takes a blade to that swell. You cannot feel noble for paying back money you owed. You can only feel **relief** that you are no longer holding what isn't yours.

And notice something else. Look at *where* the *ḥaqq* is located:

> **فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ** — *fī amwālihim* — "**in** their wealth."

Not "from their wealth." **In** it. The preposition *fī* means *inside, within, embedded.* The right is not something you carve off the outside of your fortune as an afterthought. It is *mixed into* the substance of what you hold. From the moment the wealth came to you, the other person's portion was already in it — the way salt is already in the seawater, not added later.

So when the poor person's share leaves your hand, nothing of *yours* has left. The water simply gave up the salt that was never the water's to begin with.

**What this single word reveals:** the giving commanded here is not generosity flowing *down* from you to someone beneath you. It is the *release* of a claim that was *inside* your wealth from the start. You are not a donor. You are an honest custodian.

[PAUSE — let that settle]

### Element Two: *Maʿlūm* — the right that has a number

Now to the word I stopped you at the door for.

Let me teach the form first. **مَّعْلُومٌ**, *maʿlūm*, comes from the root **ʿ-L-M** (ع-ل-م) — *to know*. But it is not the word "to know." It is a specific shape: the **passive participle**, the *ism mafʿūl*. In English, the difference between *ʿālim* (one who knows) and *maʿlūm* (a thing that **is known**, that has **been made known**). It is the difference between *the knower* and *the known thing*.

So the right inside their wealth is not just present — it is **a known quantity**. Defined. Fixed. Measured out. It has a number on it.

Now, why does that matter so much? Here is where the classical scholars give us something beautiful, and I want to give you their reasoning, not just their conclusion.

Most of the early authorities — al-Jalālayn states it flatly, al-Muyassar spells it out, al-Ṭabarī affirms it, and al-Qurtubī reports it from Qatāda and Ibn Sīrīn — say this *ḥaqq maʿlūm* is the **zakāt**, the obligatory portion that Allah Himself fixed. And al-Ṭabarī reaches for a word worth lingering on: he glosses the right as a *ḥaqq muʾaqqat* — an *appointed* right, one that carries a set **time** as well as a set **measure**. So the fixedness runs in two directions: not only is the amount known, the moment it falls due is known too. The grasping hand cannot argue down the number *or* defer the date. But al-Qurtubī also honestly records the disagreement: Mujāhid held it was something **beyond** zakāt — voluntary giving over and above the obligation. And from Ibn ʿAbbās there is a narration that it includes ties of kinship and helping carry others' burdens.

So the scholars genuinely differed. But watch how al-Qurtubī settles it — and notice that he settles it **on the strength of this one word**. He says: *the first view is the most sound, because Allah described the right as* ***maʿlūm*** *— known, fixed. And anything beyond the zakāt is not fixed; it rises and falls with need. It cannot be called* maʿlūm. *Only the obligation has a known measure.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? The word *maʿlūm* is not decoration. It is **load-bearing**. It is the very evidence the scholars used to identify what kind of right this is. A fixed, knowable number. Not a mood. Not a moved heart on a good day. A *defined* claim.

And there is a reason your soul needs the number to be fixed. Stay with me here, because this is the deep point.

Remember the disease: *manūʿ* — withholding. The grasping self is a master negotiator. If the right owed to the poor were left vague — "give what you feel is right" — the clenching *nafs* would negotiate it down to nothing, every single time. *"Things are tight this month. I'll give more later. They probably have enough."* A vague duty is a duty the grasping hand will always argue away.

So Allah does not leave it to the heart's weather. He fixes it. He makes it *maʿlūm*. He puts a number on the claim so that on your worst, most clenched, most frightened day — the day the *halūʿ* nature is screaming at you to hold on — the portion still leaves your hand, because it was never a question of feeling. It was a *known* debt.

**What this word reveals:** the mercy of a fixed obligation. Allah did not trust the grasping heart to be generous on its own — He *knows* what He created. So He took the most resistant part of us, the white-knuckled fist around money, and He pried open a fixed amount of it by decree, so that even the stingy soul is dragged into giving. The number is not a burden. The number is the rescue.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: *Al-Sāʾil wa-l-Maḥrūm* — the one who asks, and the one who cannot

Now look at *who* receives this right. Allah names two people, and the choice of two — and the *shape* of the two words — is the whole second half of the meaning.

> **لِّلسَّآئِلِ وَٱلْمَحْرُومِ**
>
> "for the **sāʾil** — the one who asks — and the **maḥrūm** — the deprived one."

Let me show you the grammar, because the grammar *is* the message.

**ٱلسَّآئِل**, *al-sāʾil*, from **S-ʾ-L** (س-أ-ل), *to ask*, is an **active participle** — the *ism fāʿil*. The one who *does* the asking. He is the agent. He acts. He comes to you. He puts out his hand and says the words. You can *see* him.

**ٱلْمَحْرُوم**, *al-maḥrūm*, from **Ḥ-R-M** (ح-ر-م), *to be deprived, denied, cut off*, is a **passive participle** — the *ism mafʿūl*, the same grammatical shape as *maʿlūm*. He is not the one acting. He is the one *acted upon*. He has *been deprived*. Something was done *to* him.

Feel the difference. One word is active — a man who reaches out. The other is passive — a man to whom life has happened, who has been pushed to the margin, and who *does not reach out at all.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the absence I need you to read, because it is the heart of these two verses.

The passive participle *maḥrūm* — "deprived" — leaves something unspoken. **Deprived by whom?** Grammatically, the agent is hidden. He was denied — but the verse does not say by what or by whom. Maybe by circumstance. Maybe by shame that seals his mouth. Maybe by a dignity that will not let him beg. Maybe — and the early scholars felt this — by *us*: the one everyone overlooked, the one no one thought to ask after, the one whose need was so quiet that the community simply *deprived* him by never noticing.

This is why Allah had to *name* him separately. Think about it. If He had only said "give to *al-sāʾil*, the one who asks," our giving would be entirely reactive. We would respond to the outstretched hand and consider ourselves done. The whole moral universe would shrink to: *whoever is loud enough to ask, gets.*

But there is a second kind of poor person, and he is invisible precisely because of his virtue. He is too proud to beg. Too dignified to make his need your problem. He goes home hungry with a straight back and a closed mouth, and he will *never* be the outstretched hand you respond to. If the right were only for the asker, he would starve in silence.

This is exactly how the classical mufassirūn read him. Al-Ṭabarī defines the *maḥrūm* in the plainest terms: *the one who has been denied affluence — poor, and yet he does not ask.* And al-Muyassar adds the word that names his silence precisely: the *maḥrūm* is the one who *yataʿaffaf* — who **restrains himself**, out of dignity, from asking at all. Notice that this is not mere passivity. *Taʿaffuf* is an active virtue, the same continence the Qur'an praises elsewhere in those whom "the ignorant suppose rich, because of their restraint" *(2:273)*. So his silence is not weakness; it is a kind of strength — and it is precisely that strength that would have erased him from your accounting, had Allah not written him into the verse by name.

So Allah names him. **وَٱلْمَحْرُومِ.** *And the deprived one.* As if to say: *I know you will give to the one who asks — that is easy. But there is another, and he will never come to you. You must go and find him. The right in your wealth belongs to him too, and he will not collect it himself.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the act of giving. It is no longer enough to be *generous when asked*. You are now required to be *perceptive when not asked*. To look for the need that hides. To go out and seek the *maḥrūm* whose dignity makes him invisible. The verse turns you from a passive responder into an active seeker — the mirror image of how it described the two recipients. *He* is passive; *you* must become active on his behalf.

**What these two words reveal together:** Allah's justice does not run on volume. It does not reward the loudest need and forget the quiet one. The known right belongs equally to the hand that reaches out and the heart that never will — and finding the silent one is *your* burden, not his.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

So look at what these three words have built between them.

*Ḥaqq* told us the giving is not generosity but the **release of a claim** that was never ours. *Maʿlūm* told us that claim is **fixed and known**, because Allah would not trust the grasping heart to set its own number. And *al-sāʾil wa-l-maḥrūm* told us the claim is owed to **two** people — one who asks, and one who never will, whom we are commanded to seek out.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about wealth itself, and about the kind of creature we are.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The illusion that it was ever "mine"

Let me start where you actually live, not in the verse.

There is a feeling almost everyone carries about money, and it is so deep we rarely examine it: the feeling of **mine**. You worked for it. You earned it. The hours were yours, the effort was yours, the sleepless nights were yours, so the result is *yours* — fully, totally, down to the last unit. And from that feeling flows a particular emotion about giving: that to give is to **lose**. To part with money is to watch something *of yours* leave. Even generous people feel the small tug of it — the watching-it-go.

And notice how this feeling shows up most sharply around *obligated* giving. When the giving is voluntary, we feel noble. But when it is *required* — a tax, a fixed due, a percentage we don't get to choose — something in us recoils. It feels like *taking*. Like a hand reaching into our pocket. *That was mine, and now it's gone, and I didn't even get to feel generous about it.*

Hold that feeling up against this verse, because this verse was built to dismantle it.

The verse says the poor person's portion is a *ḥaqq* — a right — that lives *fī amwālihim*, **inside** the wealth, from the beginning. Which means the feeling of "all of this is mine" was never true. There was always a thread running through your fortune that belonged to someone else. The salt was always in the seawater.

[PAUSE]

So here is the reframe, and it is total. When the *maḥrūm*'s portion leaves your hand, you have not *lost* anything. You have been **relieved of holding someone else's property.** The recoil you feel — *that was mine* — is the feeling of a custodian who started to believe the deposit was his.

Think of it this way. Imagine a friend hands you a sealed envelope and says, *"Hold this for me — it's for someone in need; you'll know when."* And months pass, and you carry it everywhere, and slowly — because it's in your pocket, because it's *near* you — you start to feel as if it's yours. And the day someone in need finally appears and you have to hand it over, you feel a strange pang of *loss*. That pang is absurd. It was never your envelope. You were always just the one carrying it.

That is the *ḥaqq maʿlūm*. A defined portion of everything you hold is a sealed envelope addressed to someone else. Allah deposited it *in* your wealth and made you its carrier. The day you hand it over is not the day you *lose* — it is the day you finally do the one job the envelope was given to you for.

And now you understand why this is the antidote to *manūʿ*, the withholding. The withholding self holds the envelope and calls it *mine*. The cured self holds the envelope and remembers it was always a deposit.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** the next time the fixed due comes out of your wealth — the zakāt, the known portion — try to catch the recoil before it speaks, and replace it with a different sentence. Not *"there goes my money,"* but *"there goes the part that was never mine, and now my hands are clean of it."* Give it the way you'd return a deposit: not with the swell of a benefactor, but with the quiet relief of an honest man settling a debt. Watch what that single shift does to your heart over a year.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The poor person you will never see

Now let me take you to the second person in the verse, the one I think most of us have lived our whole lives without ever consciously looking for.

We are wired to respond to *visible* need. It is not a flaw exactly — it is just how attention works. The outstretched hand, the obvious distress, the person who comes and *asks* — these reach us. We feel the pull. We respond. And then we feel that we have done our part. The need we saw, we met.

But run an honest audit of how need actually distributes in the world, and you find something disturbing. The people in the *worst* need are very often the *quietest*. Shame seals mouths. Dignity hides hunger. There is a particular kind of person — and you know them, you have known them — who would rather go without than let you see them go without. The family that stopped coming to gatherings because they couldn't reciprocate. The student who skips the meal and laughs it off. The elderly neighbor who says *"I'm fine, I don't need anything,"* with a fridge that says otherwise. The friend who is drowning and posts as if he's swimming.

These are the *maḥrūm*. The deprived who do not ask. And here is the hard truth the verse forces on us: **our entire instinctive system of giving is blind to exactly the people who need it most.** We are tuned to the *sāʾil*, the asker. The *maḥrūm* slips right through.

[PAUSE]

This is why I find it almost unbearably tender that Allah named him at all. He could have stopped at *al-sāʾil*. The grammar would have been complete. The instruction would have been clear: give to those who ask. But Allah knew that an instruction to give to the asker is an instruction that *misses the maḥrūm entirely* — and He was not willing to let that person fall through. So He added a word. **وَٱلْمَحْرُومِ.** He reached into the verse and named the one no one names.

And in naming him, He changed *your* job description. Because you cannot fulfill a right owed to a man who will never ask by waiting at home for him to ask. The only way to give the *maḥrūm* his portion is to **go looking**. To develop the second sight that notices the quiet ones. To ask after the family that disappeared. To press the help on the friend who says he's fine, in a way that lets his dignity survive. The verse doesn't just command giving — it commands a kind of *attention*. A perceptiveness. A refusal to let dignity become a death sentence.

Let me say something paradoxical and then let it sit. The *maḥrūm*'s silence is, in one sense, a virtue — it is restraint, it is dignity, it is the refusal to make his pain anyone's burden. And yet that very virtue is what threatens to leave him unhelped. So Allah builds the rescue into *the other person's* obligation. The poor man's dignity is protected by the rich man's duty to *notice*. His silence is honored, and his need is still met — but only if the one with the *ḥaqq maʿlūm* in his wealth understands that part of that right has *legs*, and must walk out the door to find its owner.

[PAUSE]

There is something here, too, about how Allah sees. We see the asker because the asker presents himself to our limited attention. Allah sees the *maḥrūm* — the hidden one, the silent one, the one whose need has no voice — perfectly. And by naming him in His Book, He is, in a sense, lending us *His* sight. He is saying: *the ones you overlook, I see. And I have written them into the right that sits in your wealth. Do not imagine that because you did not see a need, the need did not have a claim on you.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** pick one. One *maḥrūm* in your actual life — the disappeared family, the proud friend, the quiet neighbor, the relative who never asks. Someone whose need you have *sensed* but never acted on because they never made it your problem. This week, find a way to get the portion to them that protects their dignity completely — anonymously, indirectly, framed as a gift or a return-of-favor, however it takes. Do it as the discharge of a *right*, not the bestowal of a charity. And tell no one. That is the *maḥrūm*'s share, and it was sitting in your wealth the whole time, waiting for you to go find its owner.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at how these two verses are built, because the structure is the proof.

Verse 24 defines the **right**: it is a *ḥaqq* (a claim, not a gift), and it is *maʿlūm* (fixed, not optional). Verse 25 defines the **recipients**: the *sāʾil* (active, visible) and the *maḥrūm* (passive, hidden). And notice the symmetry that holds the whole thing together — the same grammatical shape, the passive participle, the *ism mafʿūl*, appears in both halves. The right is *maʿlūm* — *made* known. The poor man is *maḥrūm* — *made* deprived. One thing has been fixed by Allah's decree; one person has been deprived by life's circumstance. And the verse sets them side by side: **what Allah made known is the cure for what life made deprived.** The fixed portion exists precisely to reach the deprived person. The two passive participles are two halves of a single act of justice.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

From the side of *words*: *ḥaqq* and *maʿlūm* tell you the giving is the release of a fixed claim that was never yours.

From the side of *life*: the illusion of "mine" dissolves the moment you realize you were always just carrying someone else's envelope.

From the side of *words*: *al-maḥrūm*, the passive participle with the hidden agent, tells you there is a deprived one who will never ask.

From the side of *life*: the quietest need is the deepest, and Allah lent you His sight so the silent ones don't fall through.

And all of it sits inside the surah's great frame: the human being was made *halūʿ* — grasping, clenched, *manūʿ*. These two verses are the pried-open hand. Not pried open by a mood. Pried open by a *known right*, owed to a *named person*, that was *inside the wealth all along*.

### Questions to carry

1. When the fixed portion leaves my wealth, do I feel *loss* — or *relief*? And what does my honest answer tell me about whether I still believe it was mine?

2. Who is the *maḥrūm* in my own life right now — the one whose need I have *sensed* but never acted on, because they were too dignified to make it my problem?

3. Am I generous, or am I merely *responsive*? Do I give to whoever asks, and call that enough — while the silent ones go unseen?

4. If Allah fixed the obligation precisely because He did not trust my heart to give freely — what does that tell me, honestly, about my heart?

5. Whose envelope have I been carrying so long that I have started to call it mine?

### One-sentence distillation

A measured portion of everything you hold was never yours — it is a fixed right owed both to the poor who ask and to the poor who never will, and finding the silent one is your task, not his.

### The closing invitation

This is not a call to feel guilty about your wealth. It is an invitation to feel *lighter*. To stop carrying someone else's envelope as though it were your own and to feel the strange relief of handing it back. And it is an invitation to open your eyes wider than the outstretched hand — to develop the second sight that notices the *maḥrūm*, and to let your wealth go and find its quiet owners. The grasping hand was the default. The open hand is the cure. And the cure is not a feeling you have to manufacture — it is a right you simply have to release.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, You who made known what You made obligatory — teach my heart to feel the portion of others that lives inside what I hold, so that I release it as a debt and not as a favor. And You who see the deprived one I cannot see, lend me Your sight: let me find the silent ones who will never ask, and let their share reach them through hands that take no credit. Unclench what You created clenched. Amīn.*
