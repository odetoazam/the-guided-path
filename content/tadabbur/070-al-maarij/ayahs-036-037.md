# The Craning Neck and the Scattered Heart

### Surah Al-Ma'arij 70:36-37

> فَمَالِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ قِبَلَكَ مُهْطِعِينَ ۝ عَنِ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ ٱلشِّمَالِ عِزِينَ
>
> *Famāli-lladhīna kafarū qibalaka muhṭiʿīn. ʿani-l-yamīni wa ʿani-sh-shimāli ʿizīn.*
>
> **"So what is the matter with those who disbelieve, that they rush toward you with necks outstretched — from the right and from the left, in scattered clusters?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Crowd That Leaned In

Let me ask you to picture something before we touch a single word.

A man stands in an open space in Mecca. He is reciting. His voice is steady, and what comes out of his mouth is unlike anything the language has heard — not poetry, not the rhymed prose of the soothsayers, not the speech of ordinary men. And around him, people gather. They press in. They crane their necks to hear. Their eyes are fixed on his face. From his right side they come, and from his left. They lean forward the way you lean toward a screen when something astonishing is happening on it.

If you walked into that scene as a stranger, what would you conclude?

You would conclude: **these people believe him.** Look at them. Look at the attention on every face. Look at how they hurry to get close, how they strain to catch every syllable. No one rushes toward something they have written off. No one leans in toward a voice they have already dismissed.

And you would be wrong.

Because these are **the very people who disbelieved.** These are the ones who, when he finished, would turn to each other and mock. These are the ones who had already decided, in the only place a decision actually lives — the heart — that none of this was true. Their bodies said one thing. Their hearts had said the opposite long ago.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that picture, because this is the world we have to inhabit to feel what these two ayat are doing. Surah Al-Ma'arij is a Meccan surah, and it opens with a man — one of these very mockers — sneering, *"Bring on the punishment you keep threatening us with."* The whole surah is, in a sense, an answer to that sneer. It walks through the Day whose measure is fifty thousand years. It x-rays the human being and finds him **halūʿ** — frantic and hollow, panicking when evil touches him, clutching tight when good comes his way. It describes the few who are saved — the ones who pray, who guard, who give, who tremble.

And then, having shown us what a real human heart looks like under pressure, it turns the camera around. It points at the crowd. At the people leaning in. And it asks a question that should make every one of us go still:

**What exactly is going on with these people?**

That is the question. Not a rhetorical flourish. A real question, asked by Allah, about a contradiction so sharp it deserves to be named. They are *right here.* They are *rushing toward you.* Their necks are *outstretched.* And not one of them believes.

Here is what we are going to discover together: the distance between a body and a heart can be the widest distance in the universe — and you can be standing inside the very presence of the truth, eyes wide, neck craned, and be further from it than someone who never heard it at all.

Let us open these words slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: فَمَالِ — The Grammar of Astonishment

Before we go to the verse, let me teach you a small piece of Arabic that you already use in your own language without naming it.

In English, when something is genuinely incongruous — when reality does not match what it should be — you don't ask a normal question. You ask a question that is really an expression of bewilderment. You walk into a room and the furniture is on the ceiling and you say: **"What is *going on* here?"** You are not requesting information. You are registering that something is *wrong with the picture.* The very form of the question carries your disbelief.

Arabic has a construction exactly like this. It is built from **mā** (what) plus the preposition **li-** (for / with). *Mā li-fulān* — literally "what is for so-and-so," but functionally: *"What is the matter with him? What's gotten into him?"* It is the grammar of someone confronting a behavior that does not add up.

Now look at how the ayah opens:

> فَمَالِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ — *Famāli-lladhīna kafarū*

**"So what is the matter with those who disbelieved..."**

This is not Allah asking because He does not know. [PAUSE] This is Allah holding up their behavior to the light and saying, in effect: *look at this contradiction. Look how it does not make sense.* Al-Baghawī, in his commentary, links this opening directly to another verse with the identical structure — *"What is the matter with them, that they turn away from the reminder?"* (74:49). It is the divine register of **astonishment at incoherence.**

And here is a detail you can see with your own eyes if you look at the written Arabic. In the Uthmani script, this word is written in a way that has puzzled and delighted scholars for centuries: **فمال** — the *mā* and the *li* are separated, the *lām* hanging there attached to what follows. The reciter pauses in an unusual place. The word itself is, in a sense, *fractured on the page* — pulled apart. Now, the scholars discuss the spelling carefully and we should not over-build on orthography alone. But notice, at minimum, that the very first word sets a tone of something *not sitting right,* something dislocated. The question is broken open before the sentence even arrives at its subject.

Let us sit with what this opening does to us as listeners. Allah could have simply *stated* their crime: "Those who disbelieved rush toward you and scatter." Flat. Reportorial. Instead He frames the entire scene inside a question of wonder. And the effect is that **we are made to feel the contradiction before we are shown its content.** We are invited to stand beside Allah, as it were, and look at these people and feel the same thing: *how strange. How does this even happen? How can a person be this close and this far at once?*

That is the first discovery. The verse does not accuse. It *marvels.* And by marveling, it forces us to marvel too — which means it forces us to *examine* — which means, if we are honest, it forces us to ask the question of ourselves.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: مُهْطِعِين — The Body That Performs What the Heart Denies

Now we come to the word that holds the whole image in its hand.

> قِبَلَكَ مُهْطِعِينَ — *qibalaka muhṭiʿīn*

First, that word **qibalaka** — "toward you," "in your direction," "right in front of you." It is built from the root that gives us *qiblah,* the direction you face. So already there is an irony coiled in the grammar: these people have made the Prophet ﷺ their *qiblah* with their bodies — they are oriented toward him, facing him, drawn to him — while their hearts face the opposite way entirely. The body has a qiblah the soul has rejected.

And then: **muhṭiʿīn.**

Let me teach the form before the meaning. This word is built on what Arabic grammarians call **Form IV** — a verb pattern that takes a root and shapes it into a posture, an entering-into-a-state. We must be careful here; Form IV is not automatically "causative," as is sometimes claimed. Its work here is to describe a *bodily condition the subject has put himself into* — to render a vivid, fixed posture. *Muhṭiʿ* is one who has thrown his whole frame into a single attitude.

And what is that attitude? Here the classical commentators give us not a contradiction but a **composite portrait** — each one a camera angle on the same astonishing posture. Let me hand them to you one at a time, and watch the picture assemble:

- **Al-Akhfash and others:** *musriʿīn* — **hastening, rushing.** They hurry toward him. There is speed in their bodies.
- **Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī:** *mudīmī an-naẓar* — **fixing their gaze without letting it drop,** and *māddī aʿnāqihim* — **stretching out their necks.** The image sharpens: necks craned forward, eyes locked, unblinking.
- **Al-Muyassar** gathers it: they *rush toward you, necks extended, eyes turned full upon you.*
- **Al-Kalbī:** *nāẓirīna taʿajjuban* — **staring in amazement,** as at a spectacle.
- **Qatāda:** *ʿāmidīn* — **deliberately, intentionally heading** for him; this is not accidental drift.
- And then, startlingly, **ʿAṭiyyah:** *muʿriḍīn* — **turning away.**

Now stay with me, because that last one looks like it breaks the picture. How can the same word mean both *craning toward* and *turning away*?

It doesn't break the picture. **It completes it.** [PAUSE]

Picture the person again. Their neck is stretched toward the reciter. Their eyes are wide and fixed. Their body has *rushed* to be near. Every external sign screams engagement. And yet — *muʿriḍīn* — the heart, the actual organ of reception, has turned its back. The aperture through which truth would enter is shut. So the word can hold both because **the human being it describes holds both.** Maximum bodily approach. Total spiritual retreat. The neck leans in while the soul walks out of the room.

This is the genius of *muhṭiʿīn.* It is a single word engineered to capture a *split human being* — the body and the heart pulling in opposite directions, and the body doing all the convincing work while the heart does all the deciding.

Let me make sure we feel how strange this is. We tend to assume the body follows the heart — that if someone *looks* engaged, they *are* engaged. The Qur'an here exposes that assumption as naïve. The body can be a **performance.** It can mimic every external feature of faith — the proximity, the attention, the urgency, the rapt gaze — while serving as a mask over a heart that has already said no. And what makes it so dangerous is that the performance is convincing *first of all to the performer himself.* A man who rushes to the gathering, who cranes his neck, who never misses a word — surely *that* man is seeking. Surely he is close.

He is the furthest of all. Because he has the *form* of seeking and has therefore stopped *actually* seeking. He has mistaken proximity for arrival.

Here is what is remarkable. Al-Baghawī preserves, among the narrations for this passage, the report that it was revealed about a group of disbelievers who *used to gather around the Prophet ﷺ to listen to his words — and to mock him, and to call him a liar.* (We say "among the narrations" deliberately; the classical sources preserve more than one occasion, and we should not flatten them into a single decree.) Sit with that scene. They came to *listen.* They came *close.* They gave him their *attention* — the most precious thing a human being has to give. And they spent all of it on ridicule. The attention was real. The proximity was real. The neck was really craned. And it was all, every bit of it, aimed at staying *exactly where they were.*

What this single word reveals is this: **the most sophisticated form of rejection looks, from the outside, almost identical to acceptance.** The verse hands us a way to tell them apart — not by where the body leans, but by what the heart does with what it hears.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: عِزِين — The Defective Word for the Fractured Crowd

Now we move to the second ayah, and to a word so unusual it appears only here in the entire Qur'an in this form.

> عَنِ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ ٱلشِّمَالِ عِزِينَ — *ʿani-l-yamīni wa ʿani-sh-shimāli ʿizīn*

**"From the right and from the left, in scattered clusters."**

First the spatial frame: *ʿan al-yamīn wa ʿan al-shimāl* — from the right and from the left. They surround him on both flanks. And immediately you notice what is *missing* from this geometry. There is a right. There is a left. There is no **center.** There is no *in front, gathered as one.* The crowd has flanks but no heart. It encircles a man but does not unite around his message. Read the absence as carefully as the presence: the missing word in this picture is *together.*

And then the word that names exactly that: **ʿizīn.**

Let me give you the morphology, because the *shape* of this word is doing theological work. *ʿizīn* is the plural of **ʿizah** — a band, a cluster, a small detached group. And here is the remarkable thing about *ʿizah*: it is what Arabic grammarians call a **defective noun.** Its third root letter — the *wāw* of the root *ʿ-z-w* — has been **dropped, eroded away,** so that the singular *ʿizah* is, quite literally, a *word with a piece missing.* It is in the same family as words like *sanah* (a year, plural *sinīn*) — nouns that lost a letter and then take this particular broken-sounding plural ending.

Do you see what is happening? [PAUSE] Allah describes a **fragmented crowd** — scattered into little disconnected knots, each cluster talking among itself, no unity, no single body — and He names it with a **word that is itself fragmented,** a noun that has lost one of its own limbs. The form of the word enacts the condition of the people. The lexicographers tell us *ʿizah* means a *jamāʿah mutafarriqah* — a group that is *gathered and scattered at the same time.* Gathered into a clump, scattered from the whole. A piece broken off. Exactly like the noun itself: a root broken off from its full form.

This is not decoration. The word is *load-bearing.* If Allah had said *jamāʿāt* (groups) or *aqwām* (peoples), you would picture organized assemblies. *ʿizīn* gives you something else entirely: **debris.** Human debris, drifting into little eddies around a center it refuses to acknowledge. Al-Muyassar paints it for us: they *gather to your right and your left in scattered rings and separate bands, talking among themselves, amazed.* Rings within rings. Cliques. Each little circle confirming its own commentary, its own mockery, its own version. Many small certainties, no single submission.

And now feel the full architecture of the two ayat together. At the center: **one man, one message, one truth.** Around him: a crowd that has every external mark of devotion — rushing, craning, gazing — and that is, at the level of the heart, **shattered into pieces.** *Muhṭiʿīn* showed us the split *inside each person* — body toward, heart away. *ʿizīn* shows us the split *among them all* — bodies clustered, no unity of soul. The fracture runs through the individual and through the crowd. It is the same fracture at two scales.

What this word reveals is the deep law underneath: **truth unifies; the rejection of truth fragments.** A people gathered around a truth they accept become *one.* A people gathered around a truth they reject become *ʿizīn* — present together, belonging to nothing, each broken cluster orbiting a light it will not let in.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these words have built. *Famāli* held up a contradiction and asked us to marvel at it. *Muhṭiʿīn* split the human being down the middle — the leaning body, the retreating heart. *ʿizīn* took that split and scattered it across an entire crowd, naming it with a word that is itself missing a limb.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words on a page — but about us. About the way attention works. About the difference between being *near* a thing and being *changed* by it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Counterfeit of Attention

Let me start where you actually live, far from seventh-century Mecca.

You have, in your pocket, a device that has perfected the art of capturing your neck. You crane toward it. Your eyes fix on it for hours. You rush toward it the moment it lights up. By every external measure, you are *engaged* — more engaged with that glowing rectangle than with almost anything else in your life. And yet you can scroll for an hour and, at the end of it, retain *nothing.* No change. No movement in you. The attention was total. The transformation was zero.

We have built an entire civilization on **muhṭiʿīn** — on the posture of attention emptied of its result. We have learned to *consume* without being *altered.* To watch the lecture and stay the same. To save the article and never become the person who acted on it. To attend the gathering, nod at every word, feel the warmth of the moment — and walk out unchanged into the same life we walked in with.

This is the counterfeit of attention, and the Qur'an named it fourteen centuries before the feed. The disbelievers around the Prophet ﷺ were the original consumers. They came for the *content.* The recitation was, by their own admission, extraordinary — they could not stop listening. So they listened the way you binge a show: rapt, hungry, neck craned, eyes fixed. And it left them exactly where it found them, because they had quietly decided in advance that listening would have no consequences. They had pre-committed to *not being changed.* The attention was real precisely because the danger had been removed from it. It is safe to lean toward a fire you have already decided not to let warm you.

Now let the ayah illuminate your own life. The question *famāli* — "what is the matter with them?" — is meant to rebound onto you. **What is the matter with us,** that we can spend so many hours near sacred words and remain untouched? That we can read a verse that should break us open and feel a brief, pleasant flicker — and turn the page? The verse is diagnosing a spiritual disease that the modern world has industrialized: the ability to *receive without responding.* To make even revelation into content.

And here is the sharp edge of it. The mockers were not *absent.* That is what makes the verse so frightening. Absence would have been a smaller crime. The danger is in being *present* — *qibalaka,* right there in front — and turning that very presence into proof, to yourself, that you are seeking, when in fact your presence has become a substitute for surrender. The man who never opens the Qur'an knows he has not opened it. The man who reads it daily and is never moved by it has built a defense the first man does not have: *look how close I am.*

So what does this change about tomorrow? It plants one question you must ask at the end of any encounter with the words of Allah — not *"did I understand it?"* and not even *"did I enjoy it?"* but: **"what in me is now different?"** If nothing, then you were *muhṭiʿīn* — neck craned, heart unmoved. The measure of true listening is never the intensity of the attention. It is the *consequence* of it. A single ayah that changes one act of your day has been *heard.* A whole night of recitation that changes nothing has only been *consumed.*

### Theme Two: Why Rejection Always Scatters

Here is something you have felt but maybe never named.

Think of the last time a group of people came together *against* something rather than *for* something. A comment section united in outrage. A circle bound by a shared enemy. There is energy in it — heat, even a kind of intimacy. But have you noticed how such groups *cannot hold?* How they splinter the moment the enemy is gone, fracturing into smaller and smaller factions, each more certain and more alone? Rejection can gather a crowd. It can never make a *people.* It produces **ʿizīn** — clusters, eddies, debris that clumps and scatters and clumps again, bound by nothing that lasts.

The Qur'an is showing us a law here, and it runs deeper than politics. **What you orbit determines whether you cohere.** The crowd around the Prophet ﷺ had a center — there was literally a single man at the middle of them. But they refused to *make* him their center. They surrounded a unity they would not enter. And so, though they stood shoulder to shoulder, they remained *ʿizīn* — together in space, shattered in soul. Right and left, but no shared heart. Proximity without communion. The most crowded loneliness in the world.

Now turn it toward yourself, because this is not only about disbelievers in Mecca. **A single human heart can be ʿizīn.** You can be scattered *inside.* You can have a cluster of you that wants God, and a cluster that wants the world, and a cluster that wants the approval of people, and a cluster that wants to be left alone — all of them orbiting, none of them submitting, you yourself fragmented into little factions that never resolve into one direction. This is the inner condition of the *halūʿ* the same surah described — the anxious, hollow self, frantic at evil, miserly at good, pulled in pieces because it has no true center to gather around.

And here the verse opens into mercy, if we let it. Because the *cure* for ʿizīn is implied by the very thing the crowd refused. They were scattered because they would not make the truth their center. **Coherence is the gift of submission.** The moment a heart genuinely takes one thing as its qiblah — really faces it, not just with the neck but with the will — the scattered clusters of the self begin to resolve into a single direction. The *halūʿ* becomes the one who *prays constantly,* who *guards,* who *gives* — all the qualities this same surah lists as the marks of the saved. Those are not separate virtues. They are what a *gathered* heart looks like. They are the opposite of ʿizīn. They are the un-scattering of a human being.

So what does this change tomorrow? It asks you to find your center and *enter* it — not circle it. It is possible to spend a whole life *qibalaka,* facing the truth, near it, oriented toward it, craning at it — and never step into it. The prayer you stand near every day is not the same as the prayer you have entered. The Book on your shelf is not the Book in your heart. The verse warns: do not become a person who *surrounds* the truth from the right and the left without ever letting it become the single thing you orbit. Stop being a crowd of one. Choose a center, and let it gather you.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, look at how these two short ayat are built, and see if you can feel the design.

At the dead center stands **one figure** — *qibalaka,* "you," the Prophet ﷺ, the single point toward which everything is oriented. Around that center, the verse arranges the disbelievers in a perfect, terrible symmetry:

- **Their posture:** *muhṭiʿīn* — rushing, craning, fixed. The body says *yes.*
- **Their position:** *ʿan al-yamīn wa ʿan al-shimāl* — right and left, flanking, surrounding. They are *everywhere around* him.
- **Their reality:** *ʿizīn* — scattered, fractured, a noun missing its own limb. The heart says *no.*

The geometry tells the whole story without a single word of accusation. One whole truth at the center; a fractured crowd at the edges. Bodies converging; souls diverging. The closer they press in space, the more clearly the verse exposes how far they are in spirit. And the whole tableau is held inside that opening cry of astonishment — *famāli* — so that we never read it as a flat report, but always as a *wonder:* how can presence and absence live in the same person at the same moment?

And the classical commentators catch something in this craning that we might walk right past: it carries a *silent claim.* Al-Muyassar reads the rhetorical force of the passage as spilling straight into the verse that follows — *does every one of them imagine that he will be admitted to the Garden of bliss?* (70:38). Look again at the posture in that light. To press close, to flank the Prophet ﷺ on the right and the left as though one belonged to his circle, is to *behave as if proximity were a credential* — as if leaning in were itself a kind of claim on Paradise. And the answer the surah returns is devastatingly humbling. *We created them from that which they know* (70:39) — al-Muyassar spells out what is meant: from the same lowly fluid as every other human being, *kaghayrihim,* "like everyone else." Then they refused to believe — *so on what grounds,* the commentator asks, *would they be ennobled with entry into the Garden?* Nearness to the Messenger confers nothing on a heart that turns away from him. The body's approach buys no admission. Only the heart's surrender does. The craning crowd had quietly mistaken standing near the truth for having a stake in it — and the verses that follow strip that illusion to the bone.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight approached from two roads.

The words showed us a *split:* *muhṭiʿīn* split the body from the heart; *ʿizīn,* a broken word, split the crowd into debris. The themes showed us the *meaning* of that split: attention without transformation, and proximity without unity. They are the same truth. **You can be near a thing with everything except the one faculty that matters — and nearness, in that case, is not a step toward the truth but a hiding place from it.** The neck leans in so the heart can quietly stay out. The crowd gathers so each cluster can keep its own little certainty. The form of seeking becomes the perfect disguise for the refusal to be found.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I sit with the Qur'an, am I measuring my engagement by the *intensity* of my attention — or by *what changes in me afterward?*
2. Where in my life am I *muhṭiʿīn* — leaning in with my body, my eyes, my time, while my heart has quietly decided not to be moved?
3. Is there a truth I have spent years *surrounding* — near it, comfortable beside it — without ever stepping into its center?
4. Am I *ʿizīn* on the inside — scattered into competing clusters, each wanting a different master — and what is the one center that could gather me?
5. What would it cost me to stop being a crowd around the truth and become a person who has entered it?

### One-Sentence Distillation

You can crane your whole body toward the truth and remain the furthest thing from it, because nearness is measured not by where you lean but by what your heart lets in.

### The Closing Invitation

Let this be the shift: the next time you find yourself near sacred words — reciting, listening, reading — do not be satisfied by the leaning. The neck is easy. The eyes are easy. Even the rushing is easy. Ask instead for the harder thing, the thing the crowd in Mecca refused to give: not your attention, but your *surrender.* Let one verse in, all the way in, and let it un-scatter one corner of your fragmented self. That is the difference between standing *qibalaka* and stepping into the center. It is the whole distance between this verse and the Garden the next verse asks about.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, do not let us be among those who crane toward Your words with their necks while their hearts turn away. Gather us from our scattering — make our fragmented selves face one direction, toward You. And when we hear Your speech, do not let us merely listen; let us be *changed.* Ameen.
