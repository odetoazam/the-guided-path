# The Lord of a Thousand Dawns

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, slowly.

> [ayah:70:40] فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ بِرَبِّ ٱلْمَشَـٰرِقِ وَٱلْمَغَـٰرِبِ إِنَّا لَقَـٰدِرُونَ
> [ayah:70:41] عَلَىٰٓ أَن نُّبَدِّلَ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمَسْبُوقِينَ

*Fa-lā uqsimu bi-rabbi-l-mashāriqi wa-l-maghāribi innā la-qādirūn. ʿalā an nubaddila khayran minhum wa-mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn.*

"So I swear by the Lord of all the risings and all the settings, that We are most surely able — to replace them with better than them; and We can never be outrun."

Sit with that for a moment. Thirteen words. An oath, a claim of power, and a quiet, devastating threat.

But before we can feel the weight of these words, we have to stand where the first listeners stood. Because this ayah is not a calm meditation dropped from the sky. It is a *response*. It has an edge to it. Someone has said something, and Allah is answering back.

### Rebuild the world

Go back a few lines in the surah. Al-Ma'arij — "The Ascending Stairways" — opens with a mocker. Someone has demanded the punishment: *let it come, then, this thing you threaten us with.* The surah then paints the human being as Allah made him to be when left to himself — **halūʿan**, frantic, raw-nerved, panicking at the touch of hardship and clutching tight at the touch of good.

And then comes the scene that these two ayahs answer directly. Picture it. The Prophet ﷺ is sitting. And the deniers are coming toward him — not humbly. They are coming **muhṭiʿīn**, craning their necks forward, **ʿani-l-yamīni wa ʿani-sh-shimāli ʿizīn** — on the right and the left, in scattered little clusters, knots of men. And each one of them, Allah says, is greedy — **ayaṭmaʿu kullu-mri'in minhum an yudkhala jannata naʿīm** — does every single one of them actually expect to be ushered into a Garden of bliss?

A Garden they deny. A Day they mock. And yet some entitled corner of the heart still assumes: *surely, if there's anything good coming, it's coming to me.*

[PAUSE]

That is the human posture Allah is confronting. Not just disbelief — disbelief plus *entitlement*. The assumption that you are the center. That the story cannot proceed without you. That you are, in a word, **indispensable**.

And into that posture, Allah does something striking. He swears an oath. He swears by the horizons. And He tells them exactly how replaceable they are.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice, and then we'll spend the next while unpacking it. Allah does not swear by His Throne here. He does not swear by the angels or the Pen. He swears by the **risings** and the **settings** — and both words are *plural*. Not the east and the west. The easts. The wests. Many of them.

Why would the proof of His power to *replace an entire people* be located in the place where the sun comes up?

And why would He begin an oath with the word **"No"**?

Stay with me. These are not decorations. The whole argument is hidden in them.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The "No" before the oath — فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ

Let me teach you something about oaths before we touch this one.

In any language, an oath is what you reach for when your plain word isn't being accepted. You say "I'm telling the truth," and they doubt you, so you escalate: "I swear to you." The oath is a bridge thrown across a gap of trust. You only build that bridge when there's a gap.

Now in Arabic, the oath has a standard shape: a swearing-verb, then the thing sworn by, then the thing being asserted. *I swear — by such-and-such — that such-and-such is true.*

So look at what Allah actually wrote:

> **فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ**

Not *uqsimu* — "I swear." But *fa-lā uqsimu* — literally, "**So — no — I swear.**" There is a negative particle, **لَا**, sitting right before the swearing.

Open your hand to this oddity. A "no" wedged into the middle of an oath. What is it doing there?

The classical scholars looked hard at this little word, and I want to give you their readings not as a menu of competing options, but as several camera angles on one scene.

Al-Jalālayn says it plainly: the **lā** here is **zāʾidah** — "added," not negating anything, present for emphasis and rhythm. Al-Qurṭubī says the same: **"lā" ṣilah** — a connective, a linking particle; the meaning is simply *"I swear."* On this reading, the "no" is not denying the oath. It is *intensifying* it. It is the held breath before the heavy word.

But notice *why* a language would do that. Why insert a negation that doesn't negate? Here is the texture many of the reciters and commentators feel in it: the **lā** reaches backward, not forward. It is brushing something aside before the oath even begins. It is as if Allah says: **"No — it is not as you imagine. *Now* let Me swear."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference. The deniers have just been pictured craning forward, greedy, entitled, certain of their own permanence and their own claim on the Garden. And the very first sound of Allah's response is a flat, quiet **"No."** Not a "no" shouted. A "no" that simply clears the table of their assumption before stating the truth. *No. Set aside everything you were sure of. Now hear this under oath.*

So the word does double work, and both the grammarians and the rhetoricians are right at once: it is **emphasis** that swells the oath, *and* it is a **dismissal** of the false confidence the oath is about to dismantle. The "no" is the sound of an entire worldview being set down before the sentence has even properly started.

One clear statement, then we move on: **before Allah asserts His power, He negates your certainty.** The oath does not begin with "I swear." It begins with "No."

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The plural horizons — ٱلْمَشَٰرِقِ وَٱلْمَغَٰرِبِ

Now to the thing sworn by. And this is where the ayah opens like a sky.

First, the concept. When you swear "by" something, you are pointing to something whose grandeur or sacredness backs your word. The thing you swear by is meant to make the listener *look up*. So when Allah swears by Himself as **rabb** — Lord, Master, Nurturer, Sustainer — of the *mashāriq* and *maghārib*, He is directing the deniers' eyes to a specific place in the world. He is saying: *look there. That is My evidence.*

And where does He point? To the risings and the settings of the sun.

But here is the feature you must not walk past. He does not say **al-mashriq wa-l-maghrib** — the east and the west, singular. He says **al-mashāriq wa-l-maghārib** — the easts and the wests. *Plural.* (And the commentators note the singular reading existed too — al-Qurṭubī records that some recited it singular — but the reading before us, the dominant one, is emphatically plural.)

Let's sit with this. What does it even mean for there to be many easts?

Al-Baghawī gives us the answer, and it is breathtaking in its precision. He says: **mashriqa kulli yawmin min ayyāmi-s-sanati wa maghribahu** — "the rising-point of every single day of the year, and its setting-point."

[PAUSE]

Do you understand what that means? Step outside tomorrow at dawn and mark exactly where on the horizon the sun breaks. Drive a stake into the ground pointing at it. Now come back the next morning. The sun will not rise from behind your stake. It will have moved — a hair's breadth north, or south, depending on the season. Come back every morning for a year, and you will plant **three hundred and sixty-odd stakes** in a slow arc along the horizon, the sun sliding from its furthest southern doorway at one solstice to its furthest northern doorway at the other, and back again. The sun *never rises from the exact same point twice in a year.*

Every dawn is a new doorway. Every dawn is, quite literally, a different *mashriq*.

And it is not only the sun. The classical commentators — al-Muyassar and al-Jalālayn among them — are careful to specify that these risings and settings belong to *the sun, the moon, and all the other celestial bodies*. So the doorways are not merely the three-hundred-odd of one sun's yearly arc. Multiply them by the moon, tracking its own shifting points along the horizon on its own schedule; multiply them again by every star that climbs and sinks on a timetable of its own. The sky is not one door swinging open and shut. It is an uncountable lattice of doorways, and every single one of them is held to its exact appointed place by its Lord. And al-Muyassar tells us *why* Allah swears by precisely this: because in these risings and settings lie **dazzling signs that point to the Resurrection** — the daily return of light, the faultless reappearance of what had vanished into the dark, is itself the rehearsal of the Day He brings the dead back. The proof of replacement and the proof of resurrection turn out to be the same sky.

And Allah is **rabb** of every one of them. Not the Lord of the east in the abstract. The Lord of *each specific dawn*, the Master of the sun's exact address on every individual morning of your entire life.

Notice what this does to the argument. The deniers think the world is static — that things are as they are, that they themselves are fixed features of it, permanent, load-bearing. And Allah points to the one thing they see every single day and says: *look closer. It was never static. The very sunrise you take as the symbol of "same as always" has been quietly relocating itself every morning since you were born. The order you mistake for stillness is in fact ceaseless, governed renewal — and I am its Lord.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The sun moving along the horizon is the most ordinary, least dramatic thing in creation. Nobody marvels at it. And that is precisely why Allah chose it. He is not pointing to an eclipse or an earthquake. He is pointing to the **daily** thing, the thing so reliable you stopped seeing it — and He is showing you that even *there*, in the most boring fact of your life, creation is being *replaced and renewed* every twenty-four hours by a Lord who never once lets it drift.

Hold that word — *replaced* — because it is about to become the hinge of the whole passage.

One clear statement: **the plurality of the horizons is the proof. A God who authors a different, perfectly-placed dawn every morning of every year is showing you, in the gentlest possible evidence, what "replacement" looks like in His hands.**

### Element Three: "Able" as a permanent state — لَقَٰدِرُونَ

Now the assertion the oath was built to carry: **innā la-qādirūn** — "indeed We are surely able."

A small grammatical thing with a large meaning. Allah does not say *innā naqdiru* — "We have power," using a verb. He uses **qādirūn** — an active participle, from the root *q-d-r*, the same root that gives us *qadar*, decree, and *qadīr*, the All-Powerful.

Teach yourself the difference. A verb names an *act* — something done at a time. A participle names a *state* — something that simply *is*, ongoing, characteristic, not switched on for the occasion. If I say "he is writing," that's an act in progress. If I say "he is a writer," that's who he is. The participle describes the standing reality.

So **la-qādirūn** does not say "We will manage to do it" or "We can pull it off." It says: **being-able is simply Our state.** Capacity is not something Allah summons for the hard cases. It is what He *is*, at rest, without strain. The *lām* of emphasis (**la-**qādirūn) presses down on it: most surely, settledly, We are *the Able.*

And feel where this lands after the horizons. He has just shown that He repositions the sun flawlessly every dawn. Now He says: that effortless, daily, unstrained mastery you just watched — **that** is the capacity I am swearing about. Replacing you is not a greater category of difficulty than tomorrow's sunrise. It is the *same* settled ability.

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So watch what the language has handed us, piece by piece.

A **"No"** that sweeps away the deniers' certainty before the oath begins. A **plural sky** — risings and settings beyond counting, a fresh dawn at a fresh doorway every morning of every year, each one held in place by its Lord. And a power named not as an *act* but as a *permanent state of being.*

The construction has shown us something about words. Now let's ask what it reveals not just about grammar — but about us. About reality itself. About what it means to be a human being who thinks the world cannot do without him.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The illusion that you are indispensable

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular fear that runs underneath modern life, and it wears two faces. The first face is anxiety: *what if I'm not needed? What if I were removed from my job, my family, my circle — and the water just closed back over the spot where I'd been, and everything went on?* We are terrified of being replaceable. We build whole identities to prove we are not — to make ourselves the one piece that, if pulled, brings the structure down.

The second face is its mirror: arrogance. The quiet, almost invisible assumption that things hold together *because of you.* That your team would collapse without you. That your absence would be a wound the world couldn't close. We rarely say it out loud. But it governs how we move.

Now bring that to the deniers in this surah. Their whole posture — necks craned forward, scattered in confident little clusters, each man certain the Garden has his name on it — is the posture of people who assume the universe is organized around them. They are, in the deepest sense, suffering from the **indispensability illusion.** They cannot imagine a creation that simply *proceeds without them.*

And Allah's answer is not a rebuke. It is a fact, stated under oath:

> **عَلَىٰٓ أَن نُّبَدِّلَ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ**
> "...able to replace them with better than them."

Look at the verb. **نُبَدِّل** — *nubaddil*, from *b-d-l*, to substitute, to swap one thing out and bring another in its place. And it is the **second form** — *taf'īl*, the form of deliberate, worked, intentional action. This is not a thing that drifts. This is a *substitution carried out on purpose.*

And here is the absence I want you to read as carefully as the presence. Allah does not say "able to *destroy* them." He does not even say "able to *replace* them." He says able to replace them **خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ — with better than them.** The threat is not annihilation. The threat is that the position you occupy — the gift of being alive, of being addressed by revelation, of standing on the earth in His sight — that position will simply be *handed to someone who will do better with it.*

And the classical mufassirūn make that word "better" startlingly concrete. It is not better-looking, or stronger, or more numerous. Al-Ṭabarī glosses *khayran minhum* as a people "who will obey Me and not disobey Me," and al-Muyassar reads it the same way — *aṭwaʿ lillāh*, "more obedient to Allah." So the measure of "better" is a single thing: obedience. The one who is set down in your seat is better than you precisely insofar as he answers when Allah calls. Which means the threat is not really about being outclassed. It is about a vacancy you created by your own turning-away, filled by someone willing to do the one thing you refused — to say *labbayk*.

[PAUSE]

You are not too heavy to lift out. The seat does not break when you leave it. Someone better is set down in your place, and the call to prayer rises the next morning over a community that does not feel your absence.

Now — feel both edges of this, because the Qur'an means both.

For the arrogant, this is terror. The thing you were sure of — that you matter to the structure, that your refusal counts, that your participation is leverage — is gone. Allah elsewhere says it to all of us: *if you turn away, He will replace you with a people who will not be like you* (47:38); *He will bring a people He loves and who love Him* (5:54). The community of faith does not *need* you. It would be honored by you, but it is not *built on* you.

But for the humble, the very same fact is **liberation.** Because if creation does not depend on me, then I can finally set down the exhausting performance of indispensability. I do not have to hold the sky up. I was never holding it up. The One who relocates the sun every dawn was holding it the whole time. My job was never to be necessary. My job was only to be *faithful* in the small window I'm given — and then to hand the seat on, with good grace, to whoever comes next.

[PAUSE]

There's the paradox, and I won't rush to resolve it: **the same sentence that crushes the arrogant frees the humble.** "You are replaceable" is a death sentence to the ego and a release order to the soul. It depends entirely on which one of those you came in as.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the practical edge. Tonight, find the one place where you are quietly trading on your own indispensability — the relationship you control by making yourself "the only one who can," the work-identity you cling to past the point of health, the resentment you nurse that says *they don't appreciate how much they need me.* And lay it down. Say it plainly to Allah: *I am replaceable to all of this. I was never the pillar. You are. Let me serve without needing to be necessary.* Watch how much lighter the day becomes.

### Theme Two: The God who cannot be outrun

Now the last word of the passage, and it changes the temperature of everything.

> **وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمَسْبُوقِينَ**
> "...and We can never be outrun."

The image here is a **race.** *Masbūqīn* comes from *s-b-q* — to outstrip, to get ahead, to beat someone to the finish, to precede. A *sābiq* is the front-runner, the one who crosses the line first. And **masbūq** is the passive: the one who *gets* outrun, the one left behind, the one beaten to the prize. Allah negates it of Himself with total finality: *mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn* — We are not, in any respect, ones who can be outrun.

And the commentators draw a second sense out of the same word, folded inside the first. Al-Jalālayn glosses *masbūqīn* simply as *ʿājizīn* — "ones rendered incapable" — and al-Muyassar spells out the full force: no one can outrun Us, *escape* Us, or *render Us unable* when We intend to bring another people in your place. So the racing image carries its own conclusion inside it. To outrun Allah would mean catching Him, for a single instant, *unable* — reaching a corner of existence faster than His power reaches it. And that instant has never existed and never will. The footrace and the question of capacity are the same question: there is no finish line beyond His reach, because there is no point at which His reach gives out.

Let me show you where you already know this feeling. You have had the dream — almost everyone has — where you are trying to run from something and your legs won't work. Or the waking version: you are racing a deadline, a diagnosis, a decline, and you can *feel* the thing gaining on you no matter how fast you move. To be **masbūq** is the universal human nightmare: to be overtaken by the thing you were fleeing.

Now flip it. The deniers, in this surah, imagine they are the fast ones. They mock; they demand the punishment come; they assume that by denying the Day they have somehow *outpaced* it, slipped the net, gotten ahead of the reckoning. They think death is an exit. They think the grave closes the case. They are, in their own minds, *running and winning.*

And Allah says, with the calm of someone who has never once lost a race: **you cannot outrun Me.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the depth in it. To resurrect you, to replace you, to gather you after your bones have become dust scattered across continents — the only thing that could *stop* Him would be if something could get ahead of Him, slip beyond His reach, beat Him to a corner of existence He doesn't already hold. *Mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn* says: there is no such corner. There is no matter that has run far enough, no decay that has gone deep enough, no death that has lasted long enough, to be out of His grasp. Time does not outrun Him; He is the Lord of every dawn time will ever bring. Matter does not outrun Him; He authored it. *You* do not outrun Him by dying — death is not a finish line you cross ahead of Him; it is a doorway into the very place He is already standing.

Connect the two ayahs now and watch them lock together. He is **able** (qādirūn) — that's the power. And He **cannot be outrun** (mā masbūqīn) — that's the absence of any obstacle to the power. One ayah says *I can.* The next says *and nothing can stop Me, evade Me, or get there first.* Capacity, and then the removal of every conceivable check on that capacity. The argument is sealed.

[PAUSE]

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Two things, depending on where your heart is. If you are running *from* Allah — from a command you're avoiding, a sin you're outrunning, a reckoning you're pretending isn't gaining on you — then hear the gentlest possible mercy inside the threat: *you will not make it. You cannot. So stop running and turn around, while turning around is still the easy thing.* And if you are running *toward* Him — toward repentance, toward a changed life, toward Him in the dark of the night — then know that the One you are racing to *cannot be outrun in His coming to you.* He gets to the repentant heart faster than the heart gets to Him. The same attribute that makes escape impossible makes refuge certain.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now stand back and see how these thirteen words are built, because the structure is the proof.

It opens with a **"No"** — the clearing away of human certainty. It rises into the **plural sky** — the risings and settings, evidence drawn from the most ordinary daily thing, a fresh dawn at a fresh doorway, renewal mistaken for stillness. It states the power as a **permanent state** — *qādirūn*, not an act but a standing reality. It names the power's content — **substitution**, *nubaddil*, and not mere removal but replacement *with better*. And it closes by removing every possible obstacle — **mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn**, no one and nothing can get ahead of Him.

"No" → the sky → "I am, by nature, Able" → "to replace you" → "and nothing can stop Me." The sentence walks you from the demolition of your ego to the totality of His reach, and it never once raises its voice.

### Bringing it together

See now that the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

The **plural horizons** and the **theme of replaceability** are one thing: a God who renews the dawn at a new doorway every single morning is, by that very act, demonstrating that *replacement is the quiet law of His creation* — and you are not exempt from it. Every sunrise of your life has been a small, gorgeous *tabdīl*, a substitution carried out flawlessly, a rehearsal of the truth that He can swap out anything, even an entire stubborn people, and bring better in its place without strain.

And the **participle "able"** and the **race that cannot be lost** are one thing too: a capacity that is a permanent state, against which no escape, no decay, no death can ever get ahead. *He is, by nature, Able. And He cannot, by nature, be outrun.*

The arrogant hear all of this as a threat. The humble hear it as the deepest rest available to a human being: *I don't have to be necessary. I was never holding up the sky. He is the Lord of every dawn, and He has never once been late, never once been beaten, never once needed me to make the morning come.*

### Questions to carry

- Where in my life am I quietly performing indispensability — making myself "the only one who can" — to avoid the truth that I am replaceable?
- If I were lifted out of my community tomorrow and someone *better* set in my place, what would I want to have already become before that day?
- What am I currently running *from* that I secretly know cannot be outrun — and what would it cost me to simply turn around now?
- The sun rose this morning from a doorway it will never use again. When did I last actually *see* the daily renewal Allah keeps placing in front of me — and call it by His name?
- Is "you are replaceable" landing in me as terror or as relief? And what does my answer tell me about whether I came to this ayah as the arrogant or as the humble?

### One-sentence distillation

The One who sets a different, flawless dawn at a different doorway every single morning, and who has never once been outrun by time or matter or death, swears with an oath that needs no oath that you are not indispensable to His creation — a truth that is annihilation for the arrogant and liberation for the humble.

### The closing invitation

So let something shift. Not a resolution to be more useful — that's the old game. Let it be the opposite: a quiet relief that you were never the pillar, and a freeing of all that effort to be poured, instead, into simple faithfulness in your small window. Be the better one He could have replaced you with. And then, with open hands, be willing to hand the seat on.

### A brief du'a

*O Lord of every rising and every setting, of the dawn that has never once repeated itself in all my years — You are Able, and You can never be outrun. Strip from me the exhausting lie that You need me, and let me rest in the truth that You do not. Make me, by Your mercy, the better one — and let me run toward You faster than the night runs toward the morning.* Āmīn.
