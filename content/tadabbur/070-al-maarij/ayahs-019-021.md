# Halū': The Restlessness You Were Made From

> **إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا • إِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا • وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا**
>
> *Inna l-insāna khuliqa halūʿā • idhā massahu sh-sharru jazūʿā • wa-idhā massahu l-khayru manūʿā*
>
> "Indeed, the human being was created restless and anxious — when evil touches him, all panic; and when good touches him, all withholding."

---

## INTRODUCTION — The Diagnosis Before the Cure

Let me ask you to do something before we read a single word of these three ayahs.

Sit, for a moment, with the last time your phone buzzed and your stomach dropped before you even read the screen. Or the last time something good landed in your lap — money, news, an opportunity — and instead of pure joy, you felt a strange new tightness arrive with it: *now I have something to lose.*

Notice that you didn't choose either of those reactions. They arrived on their own. They were already installed.

That is the territory we are entering. Because in these three ayahs, Allah is not describing the believer or the disbeliever, the saint or the criminal. He is describing **you**, before you became any of those things. He is reaching past everything you've built on top of yourself and naming the raw material underneath.

### Rebuild the world

Surah Al-Ma'arij opens with a sneer. A man — tradition names him among the mockers of Makkah — stands up and effectively says: *fine, bring on this punishment you keep threatening, let it come now.* He treats the Hereafter as something far off, something he can dare from a safe distance.

And Allah answers him not first with fire, but with **scale**. He describes a Day "whose measure is fifty thousand years" — *the angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a Day the measure of which is fifty thousand years* (70:4). The mocker thinks in minutes. Allah opens up a timescale that swallows his entire life like a single breath.

Then — and this is the move that should stop us — the surah turns the camera around.

Having shown us the vastness *out there*, it now shows us the smallness *in here*. The man who dares the punishment to hurry up... what is he actually made of? What is the inner constitution of a creature arrogant enough to taunt his own Creator?

And the answer comes: **he is made of halū'.** He is made of a trembling, grasping restlessness that he himself has never even looked at directly.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Here is what should keep us in our seats.

Allah does not say "the human being *becomes* anxious" or "*chooses* to be greedy." He says he was **created** that way. Built that way. From the factory.

And then — this is the part almost no one notices — Allah uses a word, *halū'*, that the Arabs themselves found hard to pin down. And instead of leaving us to guess, He does something He rarely does. **He defines His own word for us, immediately, in the very next breath.** The two ayahs that follow are not a new topic. They are the dictionary entry for the word that came before them.

So the question we're going to live inside for the next hour is this: What is the one disease, named in a single word, that splits into two opposite symptoms — and why would the Most Merciful build His beloved creature *with it already inside*?

Let's go slowly. This one rewards slowness.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Khuliqa* — The Passive That Hides a Face

Before we touch the famous word, look at the small, quiet verb sitting just before it: **خُلِقَ — *khuliqa*.** "Was created."

Let me teach you something about Arabic — and really about every language — before we use it.

There are two ways to say a sentence. *Active*: "The potter shaped the bowl." And *passive*: "The bowl was shaped." In the active, you see the hand. In the passive, the hand vanishes behind the curtain and you are left looking only at the result. English does this too: "Mistakes were made." Notice how the passive can be used to *hide* the one responsible.

But the Quran uses the passive for the opposite reason. It uses it when the actor is so obvious, so singular, so beyond question, that to name Him would almost be to insult the listener's intelligence.

*Khuliqa.* "Was created." Created **by whom?**

There is only ever one answer to that verb in the entire universe. No human creates. No idol creates. When the Quran says *was created*, the hidden hand is never in doubt. **Allah.**

So why hide it, if it's obvious? Here is what the grammar invites us to feel. By using the passive — *was created* — Allah keeps the human being as the grammatical center of the sentence, under the spotlight, while He Himself steps just behind the light. The effect is intimate and surgical. It's as if a physician turns the patient toward the mirror and says, very gently, *this is how you are made* — without making it about the physician.

[PAUSE]

And there is a weight here we must handle with care. *Khuliqa halū'an* — "was created restless." Does this mean Allah created a flaw? That He is responsible for our anxiety and our greed?

The classical scholars were careful here, and so must we be. The word *halū'* names a **raw capacity**, not a verdict. It is the untreated state — the ore before the smelting, the soil before the seed. Al-Qurṭubī even records that some, like al-Ḍaḥḥāk, read this as describing the one who leaves it untreated — the disbeliever — while the broader reading, carried in al-Muyassar and the majority, takes it as the human default that every one of us is born holding. The composite picture is this: **you were not created guilty. You were created with an intensity that will become a vice if you abandon it — and could become your salvation if you don't.**

Hold that thought. The passive verb has already told us: this is not an accident of your biography. It is a feature of your design. Which means it has a purpose. We'll find it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Halū'* — The Word Allah Defines Himself

Now the word everything turns on. **هَلُوعًا — *halū'an*.**

Two things about its shape, and both are doing real work.

**First, its form.** *Halū'* sits on the Arabic pattern **فَعُول — *faʿūl*.** This is not a neutral descriptive form. In Arabic, *faʿūl* is one of the patterns of **intensification** — *ṣīghat al-mubālagha*. It does not say someone *has* a trait. It says the trait has **saturated** them. *Ṣabūr* is not someone who is patient — it is someone made of patience. *Shakūr* is not someone who thanks — it is gratitude walking around in a body.

So *halū'* is not "a person who feels some restlessness." It is a person in whom restlessness has soaked all the way through. The anxiety is not in him. **He is in the anxiety.**

And keep your ear open — because that *faʿūl* shape is about to ring three times. *Halū'... jazū'... manū'.* The disease and both its symptoms are cut from the identical mold. We'll come back to that. It is not a coincidence. It is an argument made in sound.

**Second, its meaning.** What does *halū'* actually mean? And here we arrive at something remarkable.

The Arabs themselves struggled to translate it. Look at the range the classical authorities give us. Al-Baghawī gathers the early voices into a single chorus: Ibn ʿAbbās — *greedy for what is not lawful for him.* Saʿīd ibn Jubayr — *stingy, miserly.* ʿIkrima — *one who frets and complains.* Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Ḥasan — *tightfisted.* Qatāda — *given to panic.* Muqātil — *narrow of heart.* And al-Qurṭubī distills the lexical core: *halū'* is **the most intense form of craving and the worst, ugliest form of impatience** — *ashaddu l-ḥirṣi wa-aswaʾu l-jazaʿ.*

Sit with that for a second. The word means, all at once: greedy *and* panicky, grasping *and* fragile, never satisfied in plenty *and* never steady in loss. How can one word hold two things that seem like opposites — wanting too much, and breaking too easily?

[PAUSE]

Here is the move that I find breathtaking. Watch what Allah does.

He uses a word so dense that even the native speakers reach for six different glosses. And then — instead of leaving it ambiguous — **He defines it Himself**, in the very next two ayahs. The grammarians and the mufassirūn noticed this directly. Al-Baghawī records the position, on the authority of ʿAṭiyya from Ibn ʿAbbās, in plain words: *"its meaning is what comes after it"* — **tafsīruhu mā baʿdahu.** The definition of *halū'* is not in a dictionary. It is in verses 20 and 21.

Think about what that means for how we read. Ayah 19 is a sealed jar with a strange label. Ayahs 20 and 21 are Allah opening the jar so we can see exactly what's inside.

So *halū'* is a **circumstantial state** — the grammarians call *halū'an* a *ḥāl*, and al-Jalālayn specifies it is a *ḥāl muqaddara*, an **anticipated** state: man is created already *carrying* this disposition, the way a seed is created already carrying the tree. He doesn't acquire it later. He shows up with it.

And what is "it"? Allah is about to show us. *It* has exactly two faces.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Massahu* — When a Mere Touch Is Enough to Break You

Before we meet the two faces, look at the verb Allah chose for how trouble and blessing *arrive*. The same verb for both. **مَسَّهُ — *massahu*. "Touches him."**

Teach yourself the word first. *Mass* in Arabic is the lightest possible contact. Not a blow. Not a strike. Not a seizing. A *touch*. A brush. The grazing of one surface against another. It is the word you'd use for a feather against skin, for the faint contact that barely registers.

Now feel what Allah has done by choosing it.

He did **not** say "when disaster *crushes* him" or "when catastrophe *strikes* him down." He said *massahu sh-sharr* — when evil merely **touches** him. The faintest contact. The lightest graze of hardship.

And *that* is enough.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the diagnosis hiding in the verb? The point is not that this human breaks under great weight — anyone might. The point is that he breaks under a **touch**. The intensity of his reaction is wildly out of proportion to the smallness of the cause. A feather lands on him and he comes apart.

This is the physical image — *touch* — pointing at an unseen reality: **a soul with no ballast.** A ship with no weight in its hold tips over in the smallest swell. A soul with nothing anchoring it from within will capsize at the lightest contact with grief, and clutch in terror at the lightest contact with gain.

And notice the perfect symmetry the word creates. The *same* verb, *massahu*, governs both halves. *Massahu sh-sharr* — evil touches him. *Massahu l-khayr* — good touches him. Allah has built a mirror. Whatever opposite thing the world brings to this man — pain or pleasure, loss or gift — his inner fragility produces a structurally **identical** collapse. Just in two different shapes. The mirror tells us: it was never about the *thing* that touched him. It was always about **what he was made of.**

Now let's look at the two shapes.

---

### The Bridge: From Words to the Reality Itself

We've found three things in the language. A hidden Hand behind a passive verb — *you were made this way, on purpose.* A saturating word that Allah refuses to leave undefined — *halū'.* And a feather-light verb — *touch* — that exposes a soul with no inner weight.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals not just about words, but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Faces of One Fear

Begin where you already live.

You know someone — maybe you *are* someone — who, when things go wrong, spirals. The bad news comes and within minutes it isn't just one problem; it's the proof that everything is collapsing, that it will always be like this, that there is no floor. The mind catastrophizes. The body floods. This is what modern language calls anxiety, panic, dysregulation.

And you know someone — maybe you are someone — who, when things go *right*, clenches. The money comes in and instead of opening the hand, the fist tightens. The success arrives and with it a low hum of dread: *protect this, don't lose this, don't share this, who's going to take this from me.* This is what we'd call scarcity, hoarding, the inability to feel that you have enough.

Now here is the thing we almost never see: **we treat these as two different personality types.** The anxious person and the stingy person. The fragile one and the greedy one. We'd never put them in the same room and say *you two have the identical disease.*

But Allah does exactly that. Watch.

> **إِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا — when evil touches him: *jazū'an*, all panic.**
>
> **وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا — and when good touches him: *manū'an*, all withholding.**

*Jazū'* — that same intensive *faʿūl* form — is not "he feels some worry." It is a person *made of* panic, dissolved into it. And *manū'* — same form again — is not "he's a bit tightfisted." It is a person who has *become* withholding, refusal walking in a body.

Two opposite triggers. Loss and gain. And what does *halū'* do? It produces a saturated, total, identity-level collapse **both** times.

[PAUSE]

Here is the insight that should rearrange something inside you.

Panic in loss and greed in gain are **not two diseases. They are one disease facing two directions.** And that one disease has a name underneath both: **fear that there will not be enough.** Fear of scarcity.

When the feared scarcity *arrives* — evil touches him — the fear erupts as panic: *jazū'.*

When the feared scarcity is *temporarily held off* by some good — good touches him — the very same fear contracts the hand into a fist, refusing to let go of the buffer against the scarcity it still believes in: *manū'.*

The panicker and the hoarder are the same frightened person. One is the fear screaming. The other is the fear hoarding sandbags against the flood it's certain is coming.

[PAUSE]

And do you feel the diagnosis turn personal? Because most of us are *both*. We panic when the bad thing touches us, and we clench when the good thing does. We oscillate between the two faces of *halū'* all day long — terrified of the loss, unable to enjoy the gain. Restless in both directions. Never, for one quiet moment, at rest.

That word — *rest* — is what we're circling. Because *halū'* is, at root, **restlessness.** A soul that cannot sit still with what is, because it is always either bracing against what might be taken or guarding what it has. It is exhausting to be this creature. And the most honest thing in these ayahs is that Allah is telling you: *yes, I know. I made you holding this. I see exactly how tiring it is to be you.*

**What changes tomorrow:** The next time you feel the panic of loss *or* the clench of gain, name it the same thing. Both times, say to yourself: *this is the fear that there won't be enough.* And then ask the only question that dissolves it: *enough — according to whom? Who is actually the Provider here?* You will find that both faces of *halū'* melt in the presence of *tawakkul* — real reliance on the One who already wrote your provision before you were born. The disease is scarcity. The cure is certainty about the Razzāq.

### Theme Two: The Restlessness Was Never the Problem — Aimlessness Was

Now the deeper turn. And this one is good news that you have to earn.

We've been calling *halū'* a disease. But step back. What *is* this restlessness, underneath the panic and the greed? It is **intensity.** It is enormous psychic energy. It is a refusal to be satisfied, a perpetual reaching, a craving that nothing seems to fill.

We are so quick to call that bad. But ask yourself honestly: where did every great thing humans have ever done come from? Not from the placid and the satisfied. It came from the restless. From people who could not sit still with how things were. The dissatisfaction that builds, seeks, climbs, refuses to settle — that is *halū'* too.

So here is the question that splits the whole passage open: **What if Allah did not make a mistake by building you restless? What if the restlessness is the fuel — and the only thing that was ever wrong was where it was pointed?**

[PAUSE]

Listen to what comes immediately after our three ayahs. The passage does not stay in the diagnosis. It opens a door:

> **إِلَّا ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ • ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ دَآئِمُونَ**
>
> *"Except those who pray — those who are, upon their prayer, **constant**."* (70:22–23)

I want you to feel the precision of that exception. The cure for the restless, never-satisfied, always-reaching creature is a people described by one word: **دَآئِمُونَ — dā'imūn.** *Constant. Continuous. Never-ceasing.*

Do you see it? **The disease and the cure are made of the identical energy.**

*Halū'* is restless intensity *scattered* — flung in every direction, panicking at every touch of loss, grasping at every touch of gain, never at rest because it has no center to rest around.

The *musallī* who is *dā'im* is that **exact same** restless intensity — *gathered.* Concentrated. Aimed. The man who could not stop wanting now cannot stop turning toward Allah. The constant craver becomes the constant pray-er. The never-satisfied becomes the never-ceasing. **Same engine. New direction.**

[PAUSE]

This is why the passive verb in ayah 19 mattered so much. *Khuliqa* — *you were created* this way. Allah does not create defects to then condemn. He builds you with a furnace inside — *halū'* — and the entire spiritual life is learning to point that furnace at the right thing.

Untreated, the furnace burns the house down: panic and greed. Disciplined by ṣalāh — by a daily, constant, returning turning-toward-Allah — the same furnace becomes the warmth that the world is cold without. The very intensity that makes you fragile is the intensity that, redirected, makes you a worshipper who *cannot be moved.*

And notice *which* cure Allah names. Not "except the calm." Not "except those with naturally easy temperaments." **Except those who pray** — and pray *constantly.* Because *halū'* is not cured by becoming a different kind of person. It is cured by giving the restlessness a home to return to, five times a day, every day, *dā'imūn,* until the reaching that used to scatter you finally has a direction.

[PAUSE]

**What changes tomorrow:** Stop apologizing for your intensity and start aiming it. The part of you that *cannot let go* — give it Allah to hold instead of dunyā. The part of you that *cannot sit still* — let ṣalāh be the still point it keeps returning to. You were not made to be placid. You were made to be on fire. The only question these ayahs ask is *what are you burning toward.* Build the constancy — the *dawām* — one prayer at a time, and watch the same hunger that used to torment you become the thing that carries you home.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now look back and see how tightly these three ayahs are built. It's a structure you couldn't appreciate until we'd done the work.

**Ayah 19** states the word: *halū'.* The diagnosis. A single sealed term.

**Ayahs 20 and 21** are not new information. They are the **definition** of that word, opened like two halves of a hinge:

- *idhā massahu sh-sharru* **jazū'an** — the panic face
- *wa-idhā massahu l-khayru* **manū'an** — the greed face

And feel the sonic proof Allah laid into it. **Halū' — jazū' — manū'.** Three words, one mold — that intensive *faʿūl* shape ringing three times. The disease and both its symptoms *rhyme.* They are audibly the same substance. You don't just understand that panic and greed are one disease — you **hear** it. The form is the argument.

And the perfect mirror in the middle: *massahu... massahu.* The same feather-touch verb on both sides, so that the man's collapse is shown to depend not on what touched him — loss or gift — but only on what he was made of.

That is the architecture: **one word, defined by its two opposite faces, all three cut from the same sound — a diagnosis so complete it doubles as its own dictionary.**

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery from two directions.

The grammar said: *halū'* is one saturating word with two faces (*jazū'/manū'*), arriving from a hidden Hand (*khuliqa*), triggered by a mere touch (*massahu*).

The themes said: panic and greed are one fear — scarcity — pointed two ways; and that fear is just restless intensity that lost its aim. The cure named next door, *dā'imūn*, is that identical intensity, gathered and aimed at Allah.

Both roads end in the same place: **You were not created broken. You were created intense — and intensity without a center tears you apart, while intensity with Allah at the center is the strongest thing a human being can be.**

### Questions to carry

1. When the bad news comes and I spiral, and when the good news comes and I clench — can I learn to call them by one name: *I am afraid there will not be enough*? And do I actually believe that about the One who feeds the bird that cannot farm?
2. Where is my *halū'* — my restlessness, my hunger, my cannot-let-go — currently pointed? At dunyā, where it scatters me? Or at Allah, where it would gather me?
3. The cure was *dā'imūn* — constancy. Where in my life is my worship *constant*, and where is it only a reaction to crisis — *jazū'* dressed up as devotion?
4. If Allah built this intensity into me on purpose, what was He trusting me to *do* with it?

### One-sentence distillation

You were created with a restless, grasping intensity that fractures into panic when loss touches you and into greed when gain touches you — and the same intensity, turned toward Allah in constant prayer, is the very thing that was meant to save you.

### The closing invitation

Don't leave these ayahs resolving to be calmer, or less greedy, or more patient by sheer force. That is fighting the design. Leave instead with a redirection. The furnace stays. The hunger stays. You were made *halū'* and you will be *halū'* until you die. The invitation is not to extinguish the fire — it is to give it an altar. Let your next ṣalāh be the still point you return to, and the one after that, and the one after that, *dā'imūn,* until the reaching that used to exhaust you finally knows where it was always trying to go.

### A brief du'ā

*Yā Khāliq,* You who created me restless and saw every panic and every clenched fist before I was born — gather what You scattered, and aim what You set on fire. Take the part of me that cannot let go, and let it cling to You instead of the world. Make me of *al-muṣallīn alladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalātihim dā'imūn* — the ones whose hunger finally found its home in standing before You. Āmīn.
