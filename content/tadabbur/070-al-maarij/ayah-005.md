# The Patience That Is Beautiful
## Surah Al-Ma'arij, Ayah 5

> فَٱصْبِرْ صَبْرًا جَمِيلًا
>
> *fa-ṣbir ṣabran jamīlā*
>
> **"So be patient with a beautiful patience."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Command

Three words. That is the entire ayah.

In a Book that elsewhere stretches a single verse across half a page — the longest verse in the Quran runs to a paragraph about debt and contracts — Allah here says everything He needs to say in three words. A verb, a noun, an adjective. *Be patient. Patience. Beautiful.*

And yet when you sit with these three words, something strange happens. They do not get smaller the longer you look. They get larger.

Let me rebuild the world you need to be standing in for this command to land the way it landed on the first person who heard it.

The surah does not open with comfort. It opens with a sneer. A man — the early reports name him, a specific mocker among the Quraysh — stands up and effectively says: *fine, bring it on. You keep threatening us with some punishment. Where is it? Bring it down now.* He is not asking a question. He is taunting. He is using the delay of the punishment as proof that there is no punishment. *If it were real, it would already be here.*

And how does Allah answer the taunt?

He does not bring the punishment. Instead He widens the lens — violently, dizzyingly. He describes a Day. A Day He calls, in the very name of this surah, **al-maʿārij** — the ascending stairways. He says the angels and the Spirit ascend to Him on that Day across a measure — *fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu khamsīna alfa sanah* — a Day whose measure is **fifty thousand years.**

Sit in that for a moment. The mocker is impatient on the timescale of his own afternoon. *Bring it now. Today. I dare you.* And the answer he is given is a timescale of fifty thousand years. [PAUSE]

Then comes the verse just before ours: *innahum yarawnahu baʿīdā wa narāhu qarībā* — "They see it as far off, but We see it as near."

There it is. The whole tension of human existence compressed into one contrast. **They see far. He sees near.** The deniers experience the delay as absence. The believer is asked to experience the very same delay as nearness held in the hand of the One for whom fifty thousand years is *qarīb* — close.

And it is *into this exact gap* — between the man who can't wait until sundown and the God who measures in fifty-thousand-year spans, between *they see far* and *We see near* — that the next three words fall:

**فَٱصْبِرْ صَبْرًا جَمِيلًا.**

So — *given all of that* — be patient. With a beautiful patience.

Here is the hook. Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. We all know what patience is. Or we think we do. Patience is gritting your teeth and waiting. Patience is suppression. Patience is the clenched jaw, the swallowed scream, the "I'm fine" that is a lie. That kind of patience we have all performed.

But Allah did not say *be patient.* He said be patient with a **beautiful** patience. He attached a word to it that you would never expect to find next to suffering. He called the holding of pain *jamīl* — beautiful.

What on earth is a beautiful patience? How is any patience beautiful? And — this is the question that will undo a lifetime of getting this wrong — **is the patience you have been practicing your whole life even the kind Allah is asking for here?**

Let's find out. Slowly.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The "So" That Carries the Whole Sky

Before we touch *patience*, we have to deal with the smallest piece of this verse — the single letter at the front. **فَ** — *fa-*. In English we render it "so" or "then" or "therefore."

Let me teach you what this letter does before we use it, because it is easy to read past it as decoration. It is not decoration. It is load-bearing.

In Arabic, this little particle *fa-* is what grammarians call a particle of consequence and sequence. It does not merely add. It **concludes.** It is the hinge word that turns everything said before it into the *reason* for what comes after it. Think of how, in English, the word "so" works at the end of a long explanation. Your friend describes losing the job, the rent coming due, the car breaking down — and then says: *"...so, I'm moving back home."* That little "so" is doing enormous work. It gathers up everything that came before and converts it into the ground for a single decision.

That is exactly what this *fa-* does here. [PAUSE]

Notice what it is gathering. It is not gathering one thing. It is gathering the mocker's taunt, the threatened punishment, the ascending stairways, the angels rising to Allah, the Day of fifty thousand years, and the staggering contrast of *they see it far, We see it near.* All of that cosmic weight — the entire opening of the surah, the whole architecture of deep time — comes barreling down through this one tiny letter and lands on a single human posture:

**So — be patient.**

Here is what is remarkable. Look at the *scale mismatch* the *fa-* deliberately creates. On one side of this conjunction: fifty thousand years, ascending angels, the Day of Judgment, the structure of the cosmos. On the other side: one human being, asked to do one thing. The grammar yokes the infinite to the personal. The *fa-* is the place where the universe narrows to a command you can actually obey.

And this tells you something the whole surah is trying to teach. **Your patience is not unanchored.** Allah did not say "be patient" floating in a vacuum, as a generic virtue, the way a self-help book tells you to "stay positive." He said *be patient* as the **logical conclusion** of a reality. The reason you can hold steady is not that you have strong character. The reason is *they see far, We see near.* The patience is downstream of the timescale.

Stay with this, because it changes what patience even is. Most of our patience is *willpower* — me, gritting, holding, depleting my own limited reserve until it runs out. But the patience the *fa-* introduces is **consequential** patience. It rests on something outside of me. It says: because the Day is real, because the One who measures in fifty thousand years calls it *near*, because what looks like delay is held in His hand — *therefore* I can let go of the clench. My steadiness is borrowed from His timescale, not generated by my own grip.

The mocker's whole error was a timescale error. *Bring it now.* He measured reality by his own impatience. The *fa-* invites the believer to do the opposite — to measure his own impatience by reality.

One letter. And it has already reframed patience from a feat of willpower into a response to the truth.

## Element Two: The Word Said Twice — Why "Be Patient a Patience"?

Now look at the next two words together, because Arabic is doing something here that English physically cannot reproduce without sounding broken:

**فَٱصْبِرْ صَبْرًا** — *fa-ṣbir ṣabran.*

Literally, word for word: "So be-patient **a patience**."

If you wrote that in an English essay, your teacher would circle it in red. "Be patient a patience" — it's redundant. The verb already contains the idea; why repeat it as a noun right after? *Iṣbir* already means "be patient." Why add *ṣabran*, "a patience," immediately behind it?

Let me teach you the structure, because once you see it you will see it all over the Quran. This is what grammarians call the **maf'ūl muṭlaq** — the cognate accusative, or the "absolute object." Arabic deliberately takes a verb and then plants its own noun-form right after it. And it does this for very specific reasons. It is never empty repetition. The doubling does one of two things, sometimes both: it **intensifies** the action — *he struck a striking*, meaning he struck hard, he really struck — or it **prepares a landing pad for a description.** You echo the verb as a noun so that you can then hang an adjective on it and specify *exactly what kind.*

Here is an everyday example so it lives in your world. Imagine I tell my child, *"Clean your room."* That's the verb. Now imagine I say, *"Clean your room — a real cleaning, a thorough one."* I didn't repeat myself by accident. The moment I echoed "clean" as the noun "a cleaning," I created a slot — and into that slot I dropped "thorough." The repetition wasn't redundancy. **It was the setup for the specification.** I doubled the word precisely so I could tell you *what kind of cleaning I mean.*

That is exactly, precisely what Allah does here. [PAUSE]

He does not just say *iṣbir* — "be patient" — and leave it as a bare command. He says *iṣbir ṣabran* — "be patient *a patience*" — and the very structure of the cognate accusative tells your ear: **a description is coming. Wait for it. The kind of patience matters.** The grammar itself leans forward in anticipation.

And notice the indefiniteness. It is *ṣabran* — "a patience," with the *tanwīn*, the indefinite ending — not *al-ṣabr*, "the patience." Why does that matter? Because the indefinite form here opens the category up. It does not point to one familiar, defined thing you already know. It gestures toward *a kind* — an unspecified magnitude, a quality still to be named. The word almost holds its breath. *A patience... of a sort I am about to tell you about.*

So before we even reach the third word, the first two have already done something profound. They have told you: **the patience being asked for is not generic.** It is not the patience you already have a definition for in your head. The doubling is a flag. It says: *do not import your old idea of patience here. The kind matters. Keep listening.*

And now — only now — does the third word arrive to tell us what kind.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: جَمِيلًا — The Word You'd Never Put Next to Pain

**جَمِيلًا** — *jamīlan.* Beautiful.

The root is *j-m-l*, and it is the same family from which Arabic draws *jamāl* — beauty, loveliness, gracefulness. This is the word you would use for a beautiful face, a lovely scene, something pleasing and harmonious to behold. It carries an aesthetic charge. It is not a moral or functional word like "good enough" or "acceptable." It is the word for *beauty.*

And Allah has placed it directly on top of *patience.* On top of the holding of suffering. He has called the endurance of harm — *beautiful.*

Stop and feel how strange that is. We do not associate beauty with endurance. When we picture a person enduring something hard, we picture strain. A grimace. White knuckles. The face that is *trying.* There is nothing aesthetically lovely about most of our patience. Our patience is *survival,* and survival is rarely beautiful to look at.

So what could *beautiful* patience possibly mean? Here is where we let the classical scholars hand us the definition — and notice, they are remarkably unified on this. This is not a place of wide disagreement. The commentators converge.

Al-Jalālayn gives the core: *ṣabran jamīlan* — *lā jazaʿa fīhi* — "a patience **with no *jazaʿ* in it.**"

Al-Muyassar expands it: a patience *lā jazaʿa fīhi wa lā shakwā minhu li-ghayri-llāh* — "a patience with no *jazaʿ* in it, and no complaint of it **to other than Allah.**"

Al-Qurṭubī gives the same two-part definition — no *jazaʿ*, no complaint to other than Allah — and then adds a stunning third image we will come back to.

So everything now turns on a single word the scholars use to define beautiful patience by its *absence*: **jazaʿ.** Beautiful patience is patience with no *jazaʿ* in it. What, then, is *jazaʿ*?

Let me teach you this word, because the entire ayah lives or dies here. *Jazaʿ* is the opposite of *ṣabr.* If *ṣabr* is holding together, *jazaʿ* is **coming apart.** It is the panic, the agitation, the anguished breaking-down, the frantic distress that spills out of a person when calamity hits. It is the wail. It is the *"why me, why this, why now."* It is the loss of composure — not the feeling of pain, but the *collapse under* the pain. It is grief that has become *protest.*

Now watch what this does to our whole understanding. [PAUSE]

A beautiful patience is a patience with no *jazaʿ* in it. Not a patience with no *pain* in it. **The scholars did not say the beautiful patience has no grief, no sorrow, no tears.** They said it has no *jazaʿ* — no collapse, no panic, no anguished protest. The pain can be total. The grief can be an ocean. What makes it *beautiful* is that it does not *shatter you into protest.* It holds its shape.

And the second half is even more precise — and this is where the whole thing finally opens. Al-Muyassar and al-Qurṭubī both say: no complaint **to other than Allah.** *Li-ghayri-llāh.*

Do you hear the gap in that phrasing? Do you hear what it is *not* saying? It does not say "no complaint." It says no complaint **to other than Allah.** [PAUSE]

Read the absence. Read what they deliberately left open. They drew the line not between *complaining* and *not complaining* — they drew the line between **complaining to creation** and **complaining to the Creator.** Beautiful patience is not silence. It is *direction.* You may pour out the whole flood of your grief — but you pour it in one direction only. Upward. To the One. Not sideways, into the ears of people, as performance, as accusation, as the broadcasting of your wound.

So the definition assembles, piece by piece, into something none of us expected:

**Beautiful patience is not the absence of pain. It is the absence of collapse, and the redirection of the complaint.** You may grieve fully. You may weep. You may carry an ocean. But you do not break into panic, and you take your complaint to Allah alone.

That is not suppression. That is the *opposite* of suppression. Suppression says: *feel nothing, show nothing, say nothing.* Beautiful patience says: *feel everything — and bring all of it to the only One who can hold it.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The proof the Quran itself provides: Yaʿqūb

I told you we earn every insight from the text. So let me show you that this is not my construction. The Quran defines *ṣabr jamīl* for us — by giving us a man who lived it. And it is no accident that the only other places this exact phrase *ṣabrun jamīl* appears in the entire Quran are in the story of one prophet: **Yaʿqūb**, the father of Yūsuf.

When they bring Yaʿqūb the bloodied shirt and the lie that a wolf devoured his son, he says: *fa-ṣabrun jamīl* — "so, beautiful patience" (12:18). Years later, when they take the second son, he says it again: *fa-ṣabrun jamīl* (12:83).

Now — was Yaʿqūb a man who felt nothing? Was his beautiful patience a stone-faced emptiness? Listen to what the same surah tells us about the same man. It says he turned away and said *"yā asafā ʿalā Yūsuf"* — "Oh, my grief for Yūsuf!" It says *wabyaḍḍat ʿaynāhu mina-l-ḥuzn* — **"and his eyes turned white from grief."** He wept until he lost his sight. This is not a man who suppressed his sorrow. This is a man whose grief was so vast it physically blinded him.

So how is *that* beautiful patience? How does the man whose eyes whitened from weeping become the Quran's own portrait of *ṣabr jamīl*?

Because of one verse. When his sons finally rebuke him — *"you'll never stop remembering Yūsuf until you waste away"* — he answers them, and his answer is the definition of the whole thing: *innamā ashkū baththī wa ḥuznī ila-llāh* — **"I only complain of my anguish and my grief to Allah"** (12:86). [PAUSE]

There it is. *Li-ghayri-llāh* — to other than Allah — that is the door he kept shut. To Allah — that is the door he kept open, wide open, flooding. He grieved like an ocean. But he carried the ocean *to Allah,* not to people. His sons got no complaint from him. Allah got all of it.

That is beautiful patience. Not the man who feels nothing. **The man who feels everything, and brings it to the right address.**

So when Allah tells His Messenger here in Al-Maʿārij, *fa-ṣbir ṣabran jamīlā,* He is not asking for a robot. He is not asking for the suppression of the human heart. He is invoking the patience of Yaʿqūb — a patience large enough to contain weeping, large enough to contain grief that blinds, and disciplined enough to take all of it upward, and none of it sideways.

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have built, working together. The *fa-* anchored your patience to a reality outside yourself — *they see far, We see near.* The doubling, *iṣbir ṣabran,* warned you that the *kind* of patience matters, that you must not import your old definition. And *jamīlan* gave you the kind: a patience that does not collapse into panic, and does not leak its complaint into the ears of creation, but holds its shape and carries its whole grief to Allah alone.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way a human being is actually built to suffer.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Two Lies We Tell About Pain

Let me start where you already live.

There are two scripts our culture hands us for dealing with suffering, and both of them are lies. You have been handed both. You have probably tried both.

The **first lie** is the lie of *toughness.* It says: feel nothing. Be strong. Don't let them see you cry. Swallow it. "I'm fine." Push it down, lock it away, present a flat surface to the world. This is the stoic mask, the stiff upper lip, the man who has not wept since childhood because somewhere along the way he was told that the tears were weakness. We admire this. We call it strength. And it is *killing* people — in silence, in isolation, in the slow corrosion of a heart that has nowhere to put what it carries.

The **second lie** is the lie of *broadcast.* It is the newer lie, the lie of our connected age. It says: let it all out, to everyone, all the time. Process your pain *in public.* Post it. Tell every person who will listen. Make your suffering a performance, an identity, a feed. This one wears the costume of honesty — *I'm just being authentic* — but underneath it is often something else: the endless seeking of validation from creation, the turning of a private wound into a public bid for attention, the complaint poured into a thousand ears, none of which can heal it.

And here is the thing. **Both lies fail.** The first lie fails because the pain does not actually disappear when you suppress it; it goes underground and rots, and it comes back as bitterness, as rage, as a hardness that poisons everything. The second lie fails because creation cannot hold your grief; people listen for a while and then they tire, and you are left more exposed and more alone than before, having spent your wound as currency and gotten nothing real in return.

Now bring beautiful patience into the room. [PAUSE]

*Ṣabr jamīl* refuses **both** lies at once. And this is its genius. It is not a compromise between them — not "a little suppression and a little sharing." It is a third thing entirely.

Against the lie of toughness, it says: **you do not have to feel nothing.** Yaʿqūb wept until he went blind. Your grief is not a failure. Your tears are not weakness. The Prophet ﷺ himself, holding his dying infant son Ibrāhīm, wept — and when asked about it, he said, *"the eye sheds tears, the heart grieves, and we say only what pleases our Lord."* Feel it. All of it. Beautiful patience has room for the ocean.

Against the lie of broadcast, it says: **you do not pour it sideways.** You do not turn your wound into a performance for creation. You do not make your grief a transaction with people who cannot heal it. The complaint has *one* address, and it is upward.

So beautiful patience is the only honest posture available to a suffering human being. It tells the truth about the pain — *yes, this hurts, this is grief, I will not pretend otherwise* — and it tells the truth about the cure — *and only One can hold this, so it is to Him I will bring it.*

Think about what this means practically. Think about it for tomorrow. When the hard thing comes — and it will come — beautiful patience is not asking you to be a stone. It is asking you to *change the direction of your overflow.* The overflow will happen; you are human; the heart will fill and spill. Beautiful patience simply asks: when it spills, where does it go? Into the group chat, into the resentful retelling to anyone who'll listen, into the public performance of your wound? Or into sujūd, into the long whispered complaint of the night, into *innamā ashkū baththī wa ḥuznī ila-llāh*?

The practical application is almost embarrassingly concrete: **before you tell the people, tell Him.** Before the complaint goes anywhere horizontal, let it go vertical first. Not because telling people is always forbidden — seeking counsel, leaning on family, these have their place — but because the *first* and *fullest* pouring belongs to the One who can actually carry it. Make Allah the first responder to your grief, not the last resort after creation has failed you.

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: The Patience That Asks for No Witness

I promised you al-Qurṭubī's third image, and now we have earned it. Because it takes everything we've built and pushes it somewhere almost frightening in its beauty.

After giving the standard definition — beautiful patience is patience with no *jazaʿ* and no complaint to other than Allah — al-Qurṭubī reports a further description of *ṣabr jamīl.* He says: it is *"that the one struck by the calamity is among the people, and it is not known who he is."* [PAUSE]

Read that again slowly. The person carrying the catastrophe walks among the crowd — and *no one can tell which one he is.* You could look at a hundred faces and not pick him out. The man whose world has just collapsed is standing right there, indistinguishable from the man whose day has gone fine. His grief is total — and it is *invisible to creation,* because it is entirely visible to Allah.

Now, why is this beautiful and not just, again, suppression in disguise? This is the knife-edge, so let's walk it carefully.

The difference is *where the grief is going.* The suppressor hides his grief and sends it *nowhere* — it festers inside him, witnessed by no one, including, in a sense, by himself, because he refuses to even fully face it. He is hiding it *from* everyone, Allah included, performing "I'm fine" even in his own private prayers, never daring to open the wound even before his Lord. That is not beautiful patience. That is a sealed tomb.

But Qurṭubī's man is different. He is invisible to *people* precisely because he is *completely visible to Allah.* He does not need creation to see his wound, because the One who matters already sees it — fully, totally, with perfect knowledge and perfect mercy. His grief is not buried. It is *placed.* It has an address, and the address is so high and so secure that he feels no need to scatter copies of his pain across the eyes of the crowd. [PAUSE]

Do you see the freedom in this? We exhaust ourselves seeking witnesses to our suffering. We want someone to *see* how hard it is, how much we are carrying, how unfair it has been. So much of our broadcasting of pain is really just this — a desperate cry of *please, someone, witness me.* And the tragedy is that human witnesses are never enough; we always need one more, and one more, because no finite gaze can fill the hole.

Beautiful patience cuts the cord. It says: **I have the only Witness I need.** *Allā yaʿlamu wa antum lā taʿlamūn* — He knows, and you do not. He sees what no one sees. He counts the tear before it falls. He hears the complaint whispered in the dark when every other ear is asleep. And because *He* sees, I am released from the exhausting hunger to be seen by everyone else. I can walk among the people, carrying a mountain, and feel no need for a single one of them to know — because the One whose knowing actually heals already knows everything.

This is the dignity the word *jamīl* was pointing at all along. [PAUSE] Beauty, in the deepest Quranic sense, is *harmony* — things in their right place, in right proportion, in right relation. And beautiful patience is grief *in its right place.* Not crushed down where it rots. Not flung outward where it scatters and shames. But lifted up, in perfect proportion, to the One it belongs to. The grief is not smaller in the beautiful patience. It is *better placed.* And a thing in its right place is, by the deepest definition, beautiful.

There is a practical shift here for your own life, and it is subtle but it changes everything. Notice, the next time you are suffering, the impulse to be *witnessed.* Notice the urge to make sure people *know* how hard it is. And then ask the disarming question: *is the One whose knowing actually heals already watching?* He is. He always is. And if He is, then the frantic need for human witnesses can soften. You can let the wound be private with people and total with Allah. You can become, by His grace, the person in the crowd whose calamity no one can see — not because you've buried it, but because you've *given* it to the only One big enough to hold it.

[PAUSE]

### A note of honesty on the scholars

You should know that the classical commentators did record one further difference of opinion about this verse, and honesty requires that I name it. Al-Qurṭubī, after giving us everything above, reports the view of Ibn Zayd that this command to beautiful patience was later **abrogated by the "verse of the sword,"** once the Prophet ﷺ was given permission to fight — and both al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī note that the command came "before he was commanded to fight." So one classical reading treats this as a verse tied to a specific early Makkan moment of forbearance, later superseded in its legal force.

But notice that even on that reading, what is said to be abrogated is the *legal posture toward the persecutors* — the question of fighting back versus enduring. What is **not** abrogated, and what no scholar suggests is abrogated, is the *inner reality of ṣabr jamīl itself* — the patience that grieves without collapse and complains to Allah alone. That posture of the heart is commanded across the whole Quran, to Yaʿqūb, to the Prophet ﷺ, and through them to us. The legal context shifted; the spiritual summons is permanent.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bringing It Together

Stand back now and see how the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

The **fa-** told us patience is *anchored* — it rests on the reality of "they see far, We see near," on a God who measures in fifty thousand years and calls it near. And the **theme** showed us why that matters: because patience built on your own willpower runs out, but patience built on His timescale does not.

The **doubling**, *iṣbir ṣabran*, warned us not to import our old definition of patience — and the **themes** revealed exactly which two old definitions had to be thrown out: the lie of toughness and the lie of broadcast.

And **jamīlan** — beautiful — gave us the new definition, confirmed by Yaʿqūb and unfolded by Qurṭubī: not the absence of grief, but grief in its right place. Felt fully. Witnessed by Allah. Carried upward, not outward. So well-placed that the man who carries a mountain can walk through the crowd and not be known.

Three words. *So be patient, a beautiful patience.* A whole theology of suffering, folded into a verb, a noun, and an adjective.

## The Architecture

And see, finally, where this verse *sits.* Just before it: *they see it far, We see it near.* Just after it, the surah returns to the Day — *the Day the sky will be like molten metal.* Beautiful patience is the **hinge between two visions of time.** Behind it, the human impatience that sees the Day as impossibly far. Ahead of it, the Day itself, arriving. And right in the middle, the one posture that lets a human being live in the gap without breaking: *fa-ṣbir ṣabran jamīlā.* The verse is the bridge the believer walks across, from *they see far* to *We see near.*

## Questions to Carry

1. When the hard thing comes, which lie do you reach for first — the lie of toughness, or the lie of broadcast? Which one is your habit?

2. Yaʿqūb wept until he went blind, and the Quran calls it beautiful patience. What does that tell you about the grief you have been ashamed of?

3. Before you tell the people, do you tell Him? When your heart overflows, where does the first pouring go?

4. Could you be the person in the crowd whose calamity no one can see — not because you've buried it, but because you've placed it with the only One who can hold it?

5. How much of your need to be *seen* in your suffering would dissolve if you truly believed He was already watching?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Beautiful patience is not the silencing of your pain but the redirection of it — you may grieve like an ocean, you may even break, but you break only before Him.**

## The Closing Invitation

This is not an obligation laid on you like a weight. It is a door opened for you like a mercy.

The next time life hands you something you cannot carry — and it will — you do not have to choose between being a stone and being a spectacle. There is a third way, and it is the beautiful way. Feel it fully. Refuse to collapse into panic. And take the whole flood of it, every drop, to the One who already sees, already knows, and is already near — even when it feels impossibly far.

That is *ṣabr jamīl.* That is the patience worthy of the word *beautiful.*

## Du'a

O Allah, You who measure fifty thousand years and call them near — when our pain feels far from any relief, let us see with Your nearness and not our distance. Teach us the patience that is beautiful: a patience that does not collapse, a grief that does not scatter, a complaint that rises only to You. And make us, by Your mercy, among those who carry mountains through the crowd and ask for no witness but You. Āmīn.
