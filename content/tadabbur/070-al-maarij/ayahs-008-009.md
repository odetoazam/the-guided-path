# When the Roof and the Anchors Melt

## The Day Permanence Itself Dissolves

---

> **يَوْمَ تَكُونُ ٱلسَّمَآءُ كَٱلْمُهْلِ ۝ وَتَكُونُ ٱلْجِبَالُ كَٱلْعِهْنِ**

> *Yawma takūnu s-samāʾu kal-muhl. Wa takūnu l-jibālu kal-ʿihn.*

> **The Day the sky will be like molten copper, and the mountains will be like tufts of wool.**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing Under a Sky That Has Never Fallen

I want you to begin not with these words, but with the world that produced them.

Picture a man in Makkah, standing in the open desert at night. Above him is the sky — vast, black, studded with stars that have not moved in any way he can perceive across his entire life, across his father's life, across every life he has ever heard about. Around him, ringing the horizon, are the mountains. The same mountains his grandfather knew. The same mountains the caravans have navigated by for centuries. The mountains are the very definition of *here* — *this* is the valley between *those* peaks.

[PAUSE]

Now understand the emotional texture of that man's world. There are two things he has never once had to question. Two things so reliable that his mind doesn't even file them under "things that exist" — it files them under "the way reality simply *is*." The sky stays up. The mountains stay put. You do not wake in the morning and check whether the sky is still there. You do not worry that a mountain might wander off. These are not objects in his world. These are the **frame** of his world. The roof and the floor of existence.

And in this surah, just before our two ayahs, something tense is happening. A questioner has stepped forward — sneering — demanding that the threatened punishment be brought *now*, today, to prove it's real. And Allah does not argue with the man's timeline. He simply tells His Prophet ﷺ: *be patient, with a beautiful patience.* Then He says something that should make the hair on your arms stand up:

**"Indeed, they see it as far off. But We see it near."** (70:6-7)

They see the Day as distant — *baʿīd*. Allah sees it as near — *qarīb*. And then, the very next breath, comes our passage. As if to say: *You want to know how near "near" is? You want to know what this Day you're mocking actually looks like? Here.*

**The Day the sky becomes like molten copper. The Day the mountains become like fluffed wool.**

Here is the hook, and I want you to feel the weight of it before we open a single word. Allah did not choose, for this portrait, to describe the screaming of the guilty or the heat of the fire. He chose the two things that man had filed under "*never changes*." He reached for the frame of reality itself — the roof and the anchors — and He showed them **melting and scattering**.

Why those two? Why the sky and the mountains, of all things? And what does it do to a human heart to watch the two pillars it never thought to question simply come undone?

Let us go slowly. There is far more here than seven words should be able to hold.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: "On the Day…" — and the Verb Allah Left Unspoken

Let's begin with the very first word: **يَوْمَ** — *yawma* — "On the Day."

Before we touch its meaning, I need to teach you a small feature of how Arabic builds a sentence, because Allah is about to use it to do something profound through *silence*.

In Arabic, a word like *yawma* — "on the day of" — is what grammarians call a *ẓarf*, an adverb of time. And an adverb of time is hungry. It cannot stand alone. If I say to you in English, "On the day that the rain came…" — you are leaning forward, waiting. On the day the rain came, *what happened?* The adverb demands a verb to complete it. "On the day the rain came, **the river flooded.**" Now you can exhale.

So here is the question every Arabic listener's ear asks automatically when they hear *yawma takūnu s-samāʾu kal-muhl* — "On the Day the sky becomes like molten copper…" — on that Day, *what?* What happens on that Day? The sentence is built to make you wait for the main event.

And here is what stops the breath: **Allah does not say it.**

The classical commentators all noticed this. Al-Qurṭubī writes that the word *yawma* is governed by an unstated verb — the operative word, *al-ʿāmil*, is hidden. He offers what the listener is meant to supply: *yaqaʿu bihim al-ʿadhāb* — "the punishment **falls upon them** on the Day…" Al-Jalālayn says the same: *yawma* attaches to an omitted phrase whose meaning is *yaqaʿ* — "it occurs," "it falls."

Stay with this. Allah builds a sentence whose entire grammatical architecture points toward a missing main verb — *the thing that happens on that Day* — and then He **withholds it**, and fills the space instead with the melting of the sky and the scattering of the mountains.

[PAUSE]

What does that absence *do* to you?

Think about how dread actually works in the human heart. The unnamed thing is always more terrible than the named thing. If I tell you "something is going to happen to you on Thursday" and then refuse to say what — your imagination does worse work than any specific threat could. The blank is louder than any word.

By leaving the main verb unspoken and instead showing you the *scenery* of that Day — the liquefied heavens, the unwound mountains — Allah does something a direct statement could never do. He doesn't tell you what falls. He shows you a universe in which **everything you trusted to hold still is coming apart** — and lets the silence where the verb should be fill with your own dawning understanding. The terror is not stated. It is *staged*. And you walk onto the stage yourself.

This is the opposite of how a mocker speaks. The questioner in verse 1 wanted it loud, immediate, named, *now*. Allah answers with a portrait whose most frightening element is the thing He didn't say.

## Element Two: "takūnu" — The Future Spoken in the Present Tense

Now look at the verb that *is* there — the one repeated in both ayahs: **تَكُونُ** — *takūnu* — "becomes," "comes to be."

This is the verb *kāna* — the most basic verb of being in Arabic — here in its imperfect form, the *muḍāriʿ*. And I need you to notice something about its tense, because Allah had a choice and the choice He made is deliberate.

Let me teach the concept plainly first. Arabic, like English, can speak of the future. Allah could have framed this entire Day in a way that holds it at arm's length — "the sky *will have become*," a finished future event, far away on the horizon of time. But the *muḍāriʿ*, the imperfect, is the tense of the **unfolding present**. It is the tense you use for something happening *right now, before your eyes, in process.* *Takūnu* doesn't merely mean "it will be." It carries the live, present-tense sense of "it **is becoming** — watch — it is happening."

Now hold that against what Allah just said two verses earlier. *They* see it as far off — *baʿīd*, distant, future, not-yet, someday. And what tense does Allah reach for to describe that same Day? Not the distant future. The **unfolding present.** *Takūnu.* The sky **is** becoming molten. The mountains **are** turning to wool.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what the grammar just did?

The deniers pushed the Day far away in time — *someday, maybe, prove it.* And Allah's verb pulls it right up against your face and runs it in the present tense, as though the camera is rolling *now*. The tense itself is the rebuttal to their "far off." This is the grammatical enactment of *naḥnu narāhu qarīban* — "We see it near." Near is not a number on a calendar. Near is a *tense*. And Allah filmed the end of the world in the present continuous.

There's something tender hidden in this too, beneath the terror. A thing you can postpone, you can ignore. A thing happening *now* demands a response *now*. By rendering the Day in the present tense, Allah is not just frightening the mocker. He is doing the merciful work of collapsing the distance every one of us uses to avoid preparing. *Someday* lets you sleep. *Is becoming* wakes you up.

---

## Element Three: "kal-muhl" — The Simile, and the Heaviest Thing on Earth Turned to Liquid

Now we arrive at the image itself: **كَٱلْمُهْلِ** — *kal-muhl* — "like *al-muhl*."

Two pieces. First, that small letter at the front — **كَ**, the *kāf*. Then the word *muhl*. Let me take them in order, because each is doing real work.

The *kāf* here is the *kāf of tashbīh* — the *kāf* of simile, of likeness. It is Arabic's word for "like" or "as." And I want you to pause on the mere fact that Allah is using a *simile* at all. A simile is what you reach for when a thing is *beyond ordinary description* — when you cannot hand someone the thing itself, so you hand them the nearest thing they already know and say: *like this.* You only say "her courage was like a lion's" because you cannot put courage itself in someone's hand. The *kāf* is a confession of transcendence. It quietly admits: **what the sky becomes on that Day is not a thing you have a word for. The closest I can bring you is — *like* this.**

So the *kāf* is already telling you: you are about to be shown an approximation of something that exceeds your vocabulary. Now — like *what*?

**Al-muhl.** And here the classical scholars give us not one meaning but several, and I want to show them to you not as a menu to choose from, but as several camera angles circling one unspeakable reality.

- **Ibn ʿAbbās** — as al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both report — said *al-muhl* is *durdiyy az-zayt*, or *ʿakar az-zayt*: **the dregs of oil.** The thick, dark, oily sediment at the very bottom of the press — sluggish, filthy, the heaviest residue.
- **Ibn Masʿūd** said it is *mā udhība min ar-raṣāṣ wa-n-nuḥās wa-l-fiḍḍa* — **molten lead, molten copper, molten silver.** Metal that has been put to the fire until it runs like water.
- **Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī**, as al-Baghawī narrates, said: **like silver when it is melted.** Al-Jalālayn echoes this — *dhāʾib al-fiḍḍa*, molten silver.
- And **Mujāhid** said it is *ka-qayḥ min dam wa-ṣadīd* — **like pus from blood and discharge.** A wound's foulest liquid.

Now stand back and let all four angles resolve into one picture. Oil-dregs, molten metal, melted silver, pus. What do these utterly different images share?

[PAUSE]

Every single one is **something solid or structured that has been reduced to a thick, dark, sluggish liquid.** Every one is a thing that has *lost its form*. Metal is the very emblem of hardness — and here it runs. Silver is precious and bright — and here it is just heavy molten flow. Oil-dregs and pus are what's left at the bottom when everything good has been pressed or rotted away.

And this is what Allah says the **sky** will be like. *The sky.* The thing you have spent your whole life under, fixed, blue, structured, the roof of the world — Allah says on that Day it will be *like molten metal, like the dregs at the bottom of the press, like a running wound.* The firmament — *firm* is in the very word we use for it — turns to thick, formless flow.

There's one more thing the lexicon offers, and it's almost unbearable. *Al-muhl* is precisely the same substance Allah uses elsewhere as a *drink* of the Fire — *kal-muhl yashwī l-wujūh*, "like molten metal, it scalds the faces" (18:29), and *yaghlī fī l-buṭūn*, "boiling in the bellies" (44:45). So when the listener hears *kal-muhl* describing the sky, an echo arrives from the other passages: this is not neutral melting. This is the very texture of the punishment, now painted across the whole of heaven. The roof of the world takes on the consistency of the torment itself.

## A Bridge Before the Fourth Image

So far the grammar has shown us three things working in concert. An **unspoken main verb** that lets dread fill its own blank. A **present-tense verb** that drags the "far-off" Day right up to the present and refuses to let us postpone it. And a **simile** — a humble admission that the sky's transformation exceeds language — resolving into a single picture: *the hardest, most fixed thing above us, running like molten metal.*

Now let's ask what the second image adds — and what it reveals not just about words, but about the structure of reality itself.

---

## Element Four (Briefly): "kal-ʿihn" — The Anchors Turned to Fluff

[ayah:70:9] وَتَكُونُ ٱلْجِبَالُ كَٱلْعِهْنِ

Same verb — *takūnu*, the unfolding present. Same simile — the *kāf* of likeness. And a new word: **ʿihn.**

The lexicons and the tafsīr are clear and they agree. *Al-ʿihn* is **wool** — and specifically, as al-Muyassar renders it, *aṣ-ṣūf al-maṣbūgh al-manfūsh* — **dyed wool, carded and fluffed**, the kind the wind scatters. In its sister verse in Sūrat al-Qāriʿa, Allah completes the picture with one more word: *kal-ʿihni l-manfūsh* (101:5) — "like *carded* wool," wool that has been teased apart, fiber from fiber, until it is the lightest, most weightless, most easily-blown thing imaginable.

Sit with the precision of this. He does not say the mountains become *dust*, or *sand*, or even *plain wool*. He says **carded, fluffed, dyed wool** — wool at its most insubstantial, the state in which a single breath of wind sends it drifting.

The **mountains.** The heaviest, most rooted, most immovable objects in the entire human imagination. In the Qur'an itself, Allah elsewhere calls the mountains *awtād* — "pegs," "tent-stakes" driven into the earth to hold it steady (78:7). They are the anchors. They are the *definition* of "this will never move."

And on that Day they become the one thing that *cannot* hold anything down — fluff on the wind.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Things You Filed Under "Never Changes"

Let me bring this out of the seventh century and into your actual life, because this is not a description of scenery. It is a diagnosis of the human heart.

Every one of us, right now, is leaning our weight on something we have quietly filed under *"this will always be there."* We don't examine these things. That's the whole point of them — they're the things stable enough that we get to *stop checking.* For the man in the desert it was the sky and the mountains. For you it is something else, and I want you to actually name it.

The salary that lands every two weeks. The body that has always more or less done what you asked it to. The parent whose phone number you can still call. The marriage that is simply *there*, like furniture. Your country, your currency, the institutions that feel like the floor of the world. Your own mind — your memory, your competence — the quiet assumption that *you* will still be *you* tomorrow. The reputation you've spent twenty years building. The sense, deep beneath thought, that the world is solid and you are standing on something.

These are your sky and your mountains. They are the things so reliable you have stopped seeing them as *things*. They have become the *frame* — the part of reality you build everything else on top of, precisely because you've decided it doesn't need watching.

[PAUSE]

And here is what Allah does in seven words. He does not reach for the small, obviously-fragile things — the flower that wilts, the candle that gutters. Everybody already knows those are temporary. He reaches for the **two things the human being treats as the literal opposite of fragile** — the most permanent solid below (the mountains) and the most permanent expanse above (the sky) — and He shows them **liquefying and scattering.** Because His argument is not "small things end." His argument is far more total: **the very category of 'permanent' is something you invented.** There is no such category in reality. There is only *Him* — and everything else, every single thing, including the mountains, including the sky, on a spectrum of *not yet melted.*

This is meant to do something specific to you. Not to make you despair — to make you **stop misplacing your weight.** Because every ounce of trust you have set down on something that will one day be molten metal or carded wool is an ounce of your heart resting on a thing that is, even now, in the present tense, *on its way to coming undone.* You felt it as solid ground. Allah is telling you it was always weather.

**What changes tomorrow?** Tomorrow, when you feel that familiar tightening of fear over one of your "mountains" — the job, the health, the standing — try to catch yourself and ask: *Was I treating this as my anchor? Did I file this under "never changes"?* And then deliberately move your weight. Not off of *caring* for it — off of *depending* on it for your stability. The mountain was never the anchor. It was only ever borrowing its solidity from the One who has not, for one instant, needed to borrow His.

## Theme Two: The Whole Spectrum Melts — So Where Will You Stand?

Now let me show you the second thing, and it lives in the *contrast* between the two images, which is so deliberate it amounts to a proof.

Look at what Allah paired. **Al-muhl** — molten metal, the dregs of oil — is among the **heaviest, densest, most downward-pulling** substances a human being knows. It sinks. It is sluggish with weight. And **al-ʿihn** — carded wool — is among the **lightest, most upward-drifting, most weightless** things a human being knows. It floats. A breath sends it away.

Allah took the **two opposite ends of the entire spectrum of matter** — the heaviest possible and the lightest possible — and He placed one over your head and one under your feet, and He said: *takūnu… takūnu.* Both. Becoming. Now.

Why both ends? Because He is closing every exit.

Think about how the human heart bargains in the face of catastrophe. When one kind of refuge fails, we instinctively reach for its opposite. *If the heavy, structured things let me down — my institutions, my wealth, my solid certainties — then I'll trust the light, free things instead — my own spirit, my cleverness, my ability to drift and adapt.* And vice versa. We are forever fleeing from one pole of created things to the other, imagining that *somewhere* on the spectrum is a stable place to stand.

[PAUSE]

And in seven words Allah collapses the entire spectrum. The heaviest thing melts. The lightest thing scatters. There is no point along the whole range of creation — not the most solid, not the most free — that holds its form on that Day. He has not just removed one anchor. **He has removed the entire ocean floor.** Whichever direction your heart runs for stability *within creation*, the ground is gone there too.

And this is not cruelty. It is the most precise mercy. Because the only thing this leaves standing — the only place left to set your weight — is the One the surah named at its very opening: *dhī l-maʿārij*, the Lord of the Ascending Ways, the One the angels and the Spirit ascend toward. When every created refuge is shown to be weather, the heart that was *forced* to look past creation finds the only thing that was ever actually solid: **the Face of Allah.** *Kullu man ʿalayhā fān, wa yabqā wajhu rabbika* — "Everyone upon it will perish, and there remains the Face of your Lord" (55:26-27).

The whole spectrum had to melt so you would finally stop trying to stand on it.

**What changes tomorrow?** This is the surah's own answer, given just before our verses: *faṣbir ṣabran jamīlā* — "so be patient, with a beautiful patience." When you have truly understood that the heavy and the light, the sky and the mountains, are all *takūnu* — all in the present tense of coming undone — then the thing you were panicking to protect or grasp loosens its grip on you. Patience becomes possible. Not the gritted-teeth patience of someone clinging to a melting ledge — but the *beautiful* patience of someone who has quietly moved their weight onto the one Anchor that the Day cannot touch. Tomorrow, when something you trusted shakes, let it be the reminder rather than the catastrophe: *Ah. This too was only ever borrowing. My weight belongs elsewhere.*

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: A Sentence Built Like the Day It Describes

Now that we've done the work, look back and see how perfectly the *form* of these two ayahs enacts their *meaning*.

Allah builds a sentence with a **missing main verb** — its central event left as a blank — and fills the space instead with two images. He sets those two images at the **opposite extremes of all matter**: the heaviest (molten metal) and the lightest (carded wool). He places one **above** (the sky) and one **below** (the mountains). And He runs the whole thing in the **present tense** — *takūnu… takūnu* — the tense of *now*.

Do you see it? The verse is **structurally identical to the Day it describes.** A reality whose central event is too vast to name. The full vertical span of the cosmos — roof and floor, above and below — both coming undone at once. And no comfortable distance of "later" to hide in, because the grammar itself is present-tense. The sentence does not *describe* the collapse of stability. The sentence **performs** it. Allah composed seven words that fall apart in your hands the same way the universe does in the ayah.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are one insight seen from two directions.

The grammar showed us an **unnamed event**, a **present-tense unfolding**, a **simile for the indescribable**, and a **polarity from heaviest to lightest.** The themes showed us **the false category of "permanent"** and **a creation with no stable place left to stand.** They are the same truth. The reason Allah left the main verb unspoken is the same reason He shows the mountains as fluff — because the thing happening on that Day is the *undoing of the very framework* you use to feel safe, and you cannot name from inside a frame the moment the frame dissolves. The reason He used the present tense is the same reason this is mercy and not merely terror: it is *near*, it is *now*, and therefore it is still — for this breath — something you can prepare for.

## Questions to Carry

1. Name it honestly: what is the one thing in your life you have filed under *"this will always be there"* — the sky or mountain you've stopped checking? What would change if you remembered it was weather?
2. When fear grips you, which way does your heart run — toward the *heavy* refuges (control, wealth, certainty) or the *light* ones (escape, cleverness, drifting)? Both melt. Where does that leave the running?
3. The deniers saw the Day as *far*. Allah filmed it in the present tense. In the quietest part of you — which tense do you actually live in?
4. If every created thing you lean on is, right now, in the present tense of coming undone — have you set down any of your weight on the One who is not?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The two things you trusted never to change — the roof above you and the anchors beneath you — will run like molten metal and scatter like fluffed wool, so that your heart, finding no stable place left in all creation, finally rests its weight on the only One who does not melt.

## The Closing Invitation

You don't need to fear these verses. You need to let them *relocate* you. Somewhere in your life, right now, your heart is resting its full weight on something Allah has just told you is, in the present tense, *becoming molten copper.* The invitation is not to love that thing less, or to stop tending it. The invitation is to stop *standing* on it. Move your weight — quietly, today — onto the One the angels ascend toward. And then watch what happens to your fear when the mountain shakes: it becomes a reminder instead of a ruin. That is *ṣabr jamīl*. That is the beautiful patience the surah asked of you, made possible at last.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, Lord of the Ascending Ways — I have been standing on melting ground and calling it solid. You have shown me that the sky itself will run like molten copper and the mountains drift like wool. Loosen my grip on every refuge that is only weather, and let my whole weight come to rest on Your Face, which alone remains. Grant me the beautiful patience of one whose anchor is not in anything that the Day can touch. Āmīn.*
