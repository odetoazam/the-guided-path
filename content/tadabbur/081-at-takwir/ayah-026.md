# So Where Are You Going? — The Question With No Answer

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the verse first, and then I want us to slow almost to a stop, because this is one of those places in the Qur'an where the fewer the words, the longer we should linger.

> **فَأَيْنَ تَذْهَبُونَ**
>
> *Fa-ayna tadhhabūn.*
>
> **"So where are you going?"**

That's it. In Arabic it is barely more than a breath. A connecting particle, a question-word, and a single verb. You could speak it in under two seconds.

And I want to suggest to you that almost nothing in human language is more devastating than this question when it lands at the right moment.

### Rebuild the world

Picture where we are inside this surah. At-Takwir does not open gently. It opens with the universe coming apart at the seams. *When the sun is folded up. When the stars fall, losing their light. When the mountains are set moving. When the pregnant camels — the most precious wealth a man owned — are abandoned, left untended. When the seas are set on fire. When the souls are paired.* Image after image, each one beginning with the same word — *idhā, idhā, idhā* — "when… when… when…" Like a drumbeat. Like footsteps coming closer.

The original listener — a man of Makkah sitting in the heat, who measured his entire worth by his herds, his lineage, his standing in the marketplace — this man is being shown the day when every single thing he built his life around is folded, scattered, abandoned, or set ablaze. The sun itself, the most permanent thing he knew, is rolled up like a scroll.

Then the surah turns. It pivots from the end of the world to the **source of the warning**. It swears by the stars that retreat and sweep, by the night as it departs, by the morning as it breathes — and it says: *this is the word of a noble messenger. Your companion is not mad. He saw it on the clear horizon. This is no whisper of a banished devil.* The surah is now defending the very revelation you are holding in your hands.

So by the time we reach our verse, an entire case has been laid out. The catastrophe has been described. The trustworthiness of the message has been established. The proofs are stacked one on top of another, clear, blinding, undeniable.

And it is **precisely here** — at the peak of the evidence — that the surah stops arguing and asks a question instead.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to feel before we go any further.

The verse asks *where are you going?* — but it never tells you where. No destination is named. No alternative is offered. The question is asked, and then… silence. The road forks, and one path is lit up like the noon sun, and the question simply watches you wander off toward the other — and asks, quietly, **where exactly do you think that leads?**

Why does Allah ask a question He does not answer? Why "where are you *going*" and not "where have you *gone*"? And why, after the most thunderous opening in the Qur'an, does the whole thing narrow down to two soft words?

Let's find out. Because I think by the end you will see that this is not a rebuke at all. It is one of the most tender things ever said to a human being who is walking away.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ayna* — The Question About Direction

Let me teach you something about questions before we touch this one.

In every human language, there is a difference between a question that wants information and a question that wants to *wake you up*. If you are about to put your hand on a hot stove and I shout, "What are you doing?!" — I am not requesting a description of your activity. I can see what you are doing. The question is not seeking data. The question is a hand on your shoulder. It is meant to **stop you**.

Arabic has a precise word for this kind of question. The grammarians call it *istifhām* that has left its literal sense of asking and turned into something else — a rebuke, an appeal, an astonishment. The form is a question; the function is a summons.

Now look at the word Allah chose: **أَيْنَ** — *ayna*. "Where."

Notice He did not ask *mā taf'alūn* — "what are you doing." He did not ask *limā* — "why." He asked **where**. *Ayna* is the question of **direction and destination**. It is the word you use about a traveler. It assumes movement. It assumes a road. It assumes that the person is *going somewhere*.

Let's sit with this.

The classical commentators are united on what is being addressed here, and they say it with one voice. Al-Baghawī: *"Where are you turning away to, from this Qur'an — when in it is healing and clarity?"* Al-Jalālayn: *"By what path are you traveling in your denial of the Qur'an and your turning from it?"* And then the most beautiful image of all, from al-Zajjāj, preserved in both al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī:

> *"Which road are you walking, clearer than this road that has already been made plain to you?"*

Do you feel what just happened? The scholars heard *ayna* and immediately reached for the image of a **road**. Because that is what the word demands. The Qur'an, in their reading, is not a book lying on a shelf. It is a *path cut clean through a wilderness*. The verse is standing at the head of that path, watching people step off it into the trackless sand, and asking: where? Where are you going? There is nothing out there.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. When you ask a child who is storming out of the house, "Where do you even think you're going?" — you already know the answer is *nowhere*. That is the entire point of the question. You are not asking for a location. You are exposing that there *isn't* one. The question is a mirror. It forces the person to look down the road they have chosen and discover, with their own eyes, that it ends in sand.

This is why the answer is withheld. **Read the absence.** Allah asks "where" and supplies no "there." If He had said "you are going to ruin," it would be a statement, and you could argue with a statement. But a question with no answer? You have to answer it yourself. And the moment you try — the moment you actually ask, *well, where am I going if not here?* — you discover there is no second road. There was only ever one path made plain, and everything off it is wilderness.

The grammar of the question *traps you inside it* until you turn back. That is the mercy in it. A condemnation closes the door. A question leaves it open and waits.

So this single word reveals: **you were never offered a destination. You were offered a road, and a turning-away that leads nowhere — and a God who, instead of slamming the door, asks you to look at where your feet are pointed.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Tadhhabūn* — The Verb That Trails Away

Now the second word, and this is where I want you to listen with your ears as much as your mind.

**تَذْهَبُونَ** — *tadhhabūn.* "You are going."

First, the tense. In Arabic this is the *muḍāriʿ*, the imperfect — the tense of the *unfinished*, the *ongoing*, the *happening-right-now-and-still-happening*. It is not *dhahabtum*, "you went," a closed event in the past. It is not a single completed departure. It is **present-continuous motion**. *You are going. You keep going. Right now, as these words are being recited, your feet are still moving away.*

This matters enormously. Allah does not freeze the deniers in a past mistake, as though to say "look what you did." He catches them **mid-stride**. The walking away is still in progress. Which means — and stay with me here — it can still be stopped. You cannot un-walk a road you have already finished walking. But a road you are *still on*? You can turn around on that road at any step. The tense itself is an open hand. It says: *you are in motion, not in your grave. There is still time to turn.*

Now the root. *Dh-h-b* — to go, to depart. But the lexicons preserve a second, heavier shade of this root: **to pass away, to vanish, to be gone.** When something *dhahaba*, sometimes it does not merely relocate — it *ceases*. It disappears. So underneath the surface meaning, "where are you going," there is a darker resonance the Arab ear would have caught: **where are you vanishing to? Into what nothingness are you dissolving?**

And here is where I want you to *hear* the word.

Say it slowly. *Ta… dhha… būūn.* Notice the soft breath in the middle — that *dh* sound, the one made by letting air slip out past the tongue, a sound that escapes rather than strikes. And then notice how the word **ends**: on a long *ūn*, drawn out, fading, receding — like a figure walking off into the distance until you can no longer make out their shape. The word does not stop sharply. It *trails away*. The very sound of it is a departure. The mouth performs the meaning. You open your lips to say "going," and the sound itself goes, and goes, and dwindles.

[PAUSE]

Compare it for one second to the word that came before it: **أَيْنَ** — *ayna.* That word is sharp. It opens hard — *ay* — and clips shut — *na.* It is the sound of a head snapping around. The sound of someone grabbing your wrist. It *stops* you.

So listen to the two words side by side, and hear the little drama in them: **أَيْنَ تَذْهَبُونَ.** A sharp stop — and then a fading away. The hand that grabs you — and the figure already slipping out of reach. The whole human situation is in those two sounds: God reaching out to halt you, and you, still drifting off into the distance, dissolving into a "where" that has no answer.

This is what the verb reveals: **you are not standing still and you are not finished. You are mid-step, fading down a road that ends in vanishing — and the question reaches you while your feet are still your own.**

---

### The Bridge

So look at what these two small words have shown us. *Ayna* — a question about direction with no destination supplied, a road made plain and a wilderness with no path in it. *Tadhhabūn* — an ongoing, unfinished departure, a sound that trails off into vanishing, caught mid-stride while there is still time to turn.

Put them together and you do not get a sentence about grammar. You get an X-ray of a human life.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way we actually live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Aimless Life — Motion Mistaken for Direction

Let me start somewhere you already live.

Have you ever had one of those days — or weeks, or years — that was *completely full* and felt *completely empty*? You were busy from the moment your eyes opened. Emails answered, errands run, miles driven, notifications cleared, tasks completed. You moved the entire day. And then at night you lay down and could not have told anyone where, exactly, all that motion was *taking* you. You were going, all day long. But you were not going *anywhere*.

Hold onto that feeling, because that is the precise condition this verse is naming.

There is a kind of movement that feels like progress but is actually just *drift*. A man on a treadmill is moving his legs as fast as a sprinter, sweating, breathing hard, covering — nothing. No ground. He arrives exactly where he started, only more tired. And the cruelest part is that the *effort* convinces him he is getting somewhere. The exhaustion feels like accomplishment.

Now hear the verse again: *fa-ayna tadhhabūn* — "so where are you going?" The word is *going*, motion, energy, momentum. These are not lazy people being rebuked for standing still. These are *busy* people. Active people. People with plans and ambitions and a full calendar of denials and pursuits. The verse does not deny that they are *moving*. It asks the one question all that movement cannot answer: **toward what?**

Let the verse illuminate the treadmill. The deniers of Makkah were not idle. They were merchants, poets, leaders, builders of a whole civilization. They were *going* somewhere every single day — to the market, to the council, to the raid, to the pilgrimage trade. And in the middle of all that purposeful motion, Allah asks the question that exposes it all: a road was laid clear before you, the one road that actually arrives somewhere, and you are spending all this magnificent energy walking *off* of it. Where are you going?

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox I want you to sit with, and I am not going to rush to resolve it. **You can be the hardest-working lost person in the world.** Direction and speed are two completely different things, and we constantly confuse them. We measure our lives in speed — how much we did, how fast, how many. The verse measures life in *direction* — where it all points. And a fast life pointed at nothing arrives at nothing, only sooner.

This is why, when this verse was revealed, it was not aimed at the obviously wretched. It was aimed at the *successful* deniers — the ones whose lives looked, by every worldly measure, like they were *getting somewhere*. And that should unsettle us, because we tend to assume that if our life is full and moving and productive, the direction must be taking care of itself. The verse says: no. Fullness is not direction. Speed is not direction. *Where are you going?* is a question your busyness cannot answer for you. You have to stop and answer it yourself.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is very concrete. The verse invites a single, terrifying, clarifying habit: at least once, in the middle of your motion, **stop and ask the destination.** Not "what am I doing today" — your calendar answers that. But "where is all of this *going*? If I keep walking exactly the direction I am walking now, for ten more years, *where do I arrive*?" Most of us never ask, because we are afraid of the answer — afraid we will see sand. But the asking is the turning. You cannot adjust a course you refuse to look at.

### Theme Two: The Question That Is Actually an Embrace

Now I want to take you somewhere the first theme cannot reach, because there is something hidden in this verse that is easy to miss, and it is the most beautiful thing in it.

Let me start with a human picture.

Imagine a child who, in a fit of anger, packs a small bag and marches toward the front door, announcing they are leaving forever. Now — a certain kind of parent meets that with force: *"Fine! Go! See what happens to you out there!"* That is a door slamming shut. But there is another kind of parent, the one who actually loves the child, who kneels down to the child's height, looks at the little packed bag, and asks — gently, without anger — *"Where are you going to go, habibi?"*

Feel the difference. The second question is not a threat. It is not even really a question. It is **an arm opening**. It is a parent who knows perfectly well there is nowhere out there for this child, and who phrases that knowledge not as a wall but as an invitation home. The question *exposes the emptiness of the road out* precisely so the child will turn around and walk *back into the house*.

Now go back to our verse, and I want you to read it with that voice.

*Fa-ayna tadhhabūn.* "So where will you go?"

This is not the voice of a judge passing sentence. **A judge makes statements.** "You are guilty. You are condemned. This is your punishment." A statement closes the case. But Allah did not make a statement here. He asked a *question* — and a question, by its very nature, *waits for you*. It leaves a space. It hands the next move back to you. The Most Powerful, who could have ended the surah with a verdict, chose instead to kneel down to our height and ask the one question that turns a person around.

[PAUSE]

And here is the proof — the architecture itself proves it. Look at what comes *immediately after* this verse, in the very next breath of the surah. After "where are you going?" Allah says: *"It is nothing but a reminder to all the worlds — for whoever among you wishes to walk straight"* — *li-man shāʾa minkum an **yastaqīm**.*

Do you see it? Do you see what He does with the very next word?

The verse before asked where you are *going* — *tadhhabūn*, the wandering, the drifting, the stepping off the road. And the verse right after holds out the alternative and names it: **yastaqīm** — to go *straight*, to stand upright, to walk the road that does not bend. The root is the same root as *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm*, the straight path we ask for seventeen times a day in al-Fātiḥah.

So the two verses are a single gesture. First: *where are you wandering off to?* — the question that exposes the trackless sand. And then, before you even have time to despair, before the question can curdle into condemnation: *here. Here is the straight road. It was here the whole time. Whoever wishes — whoever simply turns and wishes it — may walk it.* The rebuke and the rescue are pressed up against each other with no gap between them. He does not let you stand exposed in the wilderness for even one verse longer than it takes to show you the way back.

[PAUSE]

That is who is asking the question. Not a God who wants to catch you wandering so He can punish you for it. A God who points out that you are wandering *because He is about to point you home*. The question "where are you going?" only stings if you forget the next line. Read together, it is not a verse about being lost. It is a verse about being *found* — about a voice that calls out to a wanderer not to shame him, but because it cannot bear to watch him dissolve into the distance when the road home is *right there*.

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes what you do with the voice of conscience when it asks you, in a quiet moment, *where is this going?* Most of us flinch from that voice. We assume it is there to condemn us, so we drown it out — with more motion, more noise, more drift. This verse retrains the ear. That question, when it rises in you, is **not your prosecutor. It is your way home.** It is mercy wearing the clothes of a question. The single most important thing you can do with it is the one thing the deniers would not do: *stop walking, and answer it honestly.* Because the One asking is not waiting to convict you. He is kneeling at your height, with the door open behind Him, waiting for you to turn around.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Let me now show you the whole shape, because the surah built this with exquisite care.

At-Takwir opens with the entire cosmos *going* — the sun rolled up and gone, the stars falling and gone, the mountains moving and gone, the seas transformed, the whole created order folded away and relocated on the Day of Judgment. Everything you ever leaned on is shown *departing*. *Idhā… idhā… idhā* — when, when, when.

And then, in the middle of a universe that is itself going, going, gone — Allah turns to *you*, the small human standing amid the wreckage of everything permanent, and asks: *fa-ayna tadhhabūn.* "So **you** — where are *you* going?"

It is the most piercing possible placement. The sun does not get to choose where it goes. The stars do not choose. The mountains and seas are *moved*; they have no say. But you — you have feet, and a road made plain, and a will. In a cosmos where everything is being carried off without consent, you are the one creature being *asked*. The question is a kind of dignity. It is Allah treating you as the one thing in the whole collapsing scene that gets to *choose its own destination*.

And then, the moment the question is asked, the answer is laid down: *for whoever wishes to walk straight.* The road home, named, in the very next breath.

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the language and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar gave us *ayna* — a "where" with no "there," a question that traps you until you turn around. And the theme gave us the same thing from the human side: the busy, drifting life that cannot answer its own destination until it stops and looks. The grammar gave us *tadhhabūn* — the unfinished, mid-stride, still-fading departure. And the theme gave us the same thing as mercy: you are caught *while still walking*, not after you have arrived in the grave, which means the arm is still open and the door is still behind Him.

Two small words. A question and a verb. And inside them: the entire drama of a human soul in motion, and a God who would rather ask than condemn.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Do not answer them quickly. Let them stay open.

1. If I keep walking the exact direction my life is pointed right now — not what I *intend*, but the actual direction of my days — *where do I arrive in ten years?*
2. Where in my life have I mistaken *speed* for *direction* — confused being busy with going somewhere?
3. When that quiet voice asks me "where is this going?" — do I treat it as a prosecutor to silence, or a way home to follow?
4. The road was "made plain." Do I actually not know the way — or do I know it, and am choosing the wilderness because the road asks something of me?
5. Allah asked instead of condemning. When *I* see someone wandering, do I slam the door, or do I kneel down and ask?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When God lays one clear road and you still drift off into the sand, the question "so where are you going?" needs no answer — because there is nowhere else to go, and the question itself is the open door, asked by a God who would rather call you home than let you vanish into the distance.

### The Closing Invitation

I am not going to tell you to change your life tonight. The verse does not command. It asks. So I will only ask you what it asks.

Sometime in the next day, in the middle of all your motion — and there will be so much motion — let yourself stop, just once, and hear it spoken to you directly. Not to the deniers of Makkah. To you. *Fa-ayna tadhhabūn.* So where are you going? And then do the brave thing the wanderers would not do: do not flinch, do not drown it out, do not keep walking to escape the question. Stand still on the road for one honest minute, look down it, and answer. Because the One asking is already kneeling at your height, with the way home lit up behind Him, waiting — not to catch you, but to turn you around.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who asked rather than condemned — catch us while we are still walking, before the road runs out. We have mistaken our motion for direction and our busyness for a destination. Show us, plainly, the straight road You laid for us, and make us among those who simply *wish* to walk it. And when that quiet voice in us asks where we are going, let us hear in it not our sentence, but Your open door. *Āmīn.*
