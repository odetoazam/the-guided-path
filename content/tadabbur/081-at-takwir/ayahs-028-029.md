# The Door He Left Open, and the Hand That Holds the Hinge

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the two verses first, and then let me give you the world they came down into.

> [ayah:81:28] لِمَن شَآءَ مِنكُمْ أَن يَسْتَقِيمَ
> *Li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm*
> **For whoever among you wills to walk straight.**

> [ayah:81:29] وَمَا تَشَآءُونَ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ رَبُّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ
> *Wa mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa-llāhu rabbu-l-ʿālamīn*
> **And you do not will unless Allah wills — the Lord of all the worlds.**

Now feel where you are standing when you hear these words.

Surah At-Takwir does not open gently. It opens with the end of everything. *When the sun is folded up. When the stars fall, scattering. When the mountains are set moving. When the pregnant camels are abandoned* — the most precious property a desert man owned, left to wander, because nothing matters anymore. *When the seas are set ablaze. When the souls are paired.* The whole surah is an unspooling — the universe you assumed was permanent being rolled up like a scroll, taken apart thread by thread in front of you.

And then, in the second half, the surah pivots. It turns from the cosmos to a single accusation against the people of Makkah: this Qur'an you are dismissing — it is not the rambling of a madman, it is not the murmur of a fortune-teller. It is *qawlu rasūlin karīm* — the word delivered by a noble messenger. The surah is steadily backing the listener into a corner. The sky is going to be torn open. The book is real. The messenger is honest.

And so by the time we reach the second-to-last verse, the listener is braced. He is waiting for the hammer. He is waiting to be told what he *must* do, or what will be done *to* him.

[PAUSE]

And instead — instead — the surah says: *li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm.* **For whoever among you wants to walk straight.**

Do you feel the strangeness of that? After folding up the sun, after scattering the stars, after putting the entire machinery of the cosmos on trial — Allah does not issue a command here. He extends an *invitation*. He says, in effect: the reminder has been given. The door is open. **Whoever wants to** — come.

Here is the question I want us to carry through everything that follows. If the door is thrown open to *whoever wants* — then the whole thing seems to rest on *me*, on my wanting, on my choice. So why, in the very next breath, before I can even take a step, does Allah say: *and you do not even will unless Allah wills?*

He hands you the key. And in the same sentence, He tells you the key was His to give.

What is He doing? Is He giving with one hand and taking with the other? Or is something far more merciful happening here than we first assume?

Stay with me. Because these two verses, sitting side by side, contain one of the deepest tensions a human being ever has to live inside — the tension between *I am responsible* and *I am dependent* — and the Qur'an does not resolve it by picking a side. It holds both. And it holds them in a way that, if we let it, takes the weight off our chest instead of adding to it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The verb that means *to keep standing* — يَسْتَقِيمَ

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the whole verse opens up through it.

In English, a verb is mostly a verb. "To stand." You add helper words around it — "to keep standing," "to make someone stand," "to try to stand." Arabic does something more elegant. It takes a root — a tiny cluster of consonants carrying a core meaning — and it *bends the word itself* into different shapes, and each shape adds a layer of meaning baked right into the structure. Scholars call these the verb forms, Form I through Form X.

Think of it like this. Imagine a single lump of clay — that's the root. Now imagine ten different molds you can press it into. Same clay, but one mold makes a simple cup, another makes a cup with a handle, another makes a cup you have to actively hold open or it collapses. The clay didn't change. The *shape* changed what it does.

The root here is **ق-و-م**, *q-w-m*. Its core meaning is *to stand, to rise, to be upright.* From this one root the Arabic language builds an enormous family of words. *Qāma* — he stood. *Qawm* — a people, a group who "stand together." *Maqām* — a station, a place where you stand. *Qiyāma* — the Standing, the Day of Resurrection, when all of humanity stands. *Muqīm* — one who stays, who is resident, who remains standing in a place. *Qayyūm* — one of the names of Allah, the Self-Subsisting, the One who stands without needing anything to hold Him up, and who holds everything else up.

All of that — from *standing.*

Now. Allah did not say *yaqūm* here — the simple Form I, "he stands." He used the most elaborate mold in the entire system: **Form X**, *istafʿala*. The word is **يَسْتَقِيمَ**, *yastaqīm*.

[PAUSE]

What does Form X do to a root? Two things, classically. It can carry the sense of *seeking* or *asking for* something — *istaghfara*, to seek forgiveness; *istaʿāna*, to seek help. And it carries the sense of *sustained, deliberate, effortful adoption* of a state — taking something on and *holding* yourself in it.

So *yaqūm* would be "to stand." But *yastaqīm* is not a single moment of standing. **It is to keep standing straight. To hold the line. To seek uprightness, actively, and to stay there.**

Notice what just happened. The word for being guided, in this verse, is not a noun you receive once and pocket. It is not "to become straight" as a finished event. It is a Form X verb — a verb of *ongoing, sought-after, maintained* uprightness.

Let's sit with what that means.

We tend to imagine guidance as an arrival. A moment. "I found Islam." "I came back." "I had my awakening." And those moments are real and they are precious. But the Qur'an, in the very word it chooses, tells you that *istiqāma* is not the moment you stood up. **It is the staying up.**

Think of a long, deep prostration in prayer, and then the rising back to standing. The hard part of standing was never the instant your back hit vertical. The hard part is the next hour. The hard part is staying upright when your knees want to buckle, when the wind pushes, when you're tired and the floor looks comfortable. A flagpole doesn't "achieve" being straight once. It *holds* straightness against every gust, every day, or it isn't straight at all.

This is why, in a famous report, when a man asked the Prophet ﷺ to give him a statement about Islam he would never have to ask anyone about again, the Prophet ﷺ told him: *Say "I believe in Allah," then* **istaqim** *— hold straight.* The whole religion, distilled, was not "stand up once." It was "and then keep standing."

And here is the tenderness hiding inside the grammar. If guidance were a one-time arrival, then every time you stumbled you would have lost it — you'd be back to zero, disqualified. But because the word is *yastaqīm*, an ongoing seeking and holding, then a stumble is not the end of istiqāma. **It is just a moment where you re-seek the straightness.** The Form X verb has room in it for the falling and the getting back up. It was never "be perfectly straight forever." It was "keep aiming yourself straight."

So the door in verse 28 is not "for whoever wants to *become* straight" — a single graduation. It is *li-man shāʾa minkum an* **yastaqīm** — for whoever among you wants **to keep walking straight.** It is an invitation to a lifelong direction, not a one-time entry.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The little word that says *not everyone* — مِنكُمْ

Now look at a word so small you could read right past it. *Minkum.* **Among you. From among you.**

*Li-man shāʾa* would already be complete: "for whoever wills." Grammatically, He did not need to add *minkum.* But He did. *Li-man shāʾa* **minkum** *an yastaqīm* — "for whoever **among you** wills to walk straight."

In Arabic, this little *min* here is what grammarians call *min* of partition — *min al-tabʿīḍiyya.* It carves out a *part* from a whole. It quietly tells you: not all of you. **Some of you.**

And the classical grammarians noticed something even more precise when they read this phrase against the verse just before it. Verse 27 had said: *in huwa illā dhikrun lil-ʿālamīn* — "it is nothing but a reminder **to all the worlds**." Then verse 28 opens: *li-man shāʾa minkum* — "for whoever among you wills." Al-Tabarī and the author of al-Jalālayn both note that this phrase is a *badal* — a grammatical substitution standing in for "all the worlds," with the preposition *li-* deliberately repeated to mark the swap. So the movement across the two verses is itself the teaching. The reminder is *poured out* over everyone, all the worlds, without exception — and then, in the same breath, the grammar narrows who actually *receives* it as guidance down to those who will to walk straight. The light falls on all of humanity; it lands as guidance only in the ones who turn toward it. That entire theology of offer-and-response is carried by a single substituted word.

Why would Allah, in an invitation, build in the acknowledgment that not everyone will accept it?

Here is what I think it does, and it is gentle, not harsh. Imagine someone opens a door to a crowded room and says, "This is open to everyone who wants to come in." Technically true. But there is a different honesty in saying, "This is open — for those of you who want it." The second one *sees the room as it really is.* It knows some will turn away. It is not naive about human freedom. It does not pretend the offer will sweep everyone up.

And in not pretending, it does something profound: **it makes the offer real.** A choice that everyone is guaranteed to make isn't a choice. By saying *minkum* — "those of you who" — Allah dignifies the listener with genuine agency. He is saying: I am not going to drag you. I am not going to flatter you that this is easy or universal. The path is here. Some of you will want it. **And wanting it is allowed to be enough to start.**

There is a thread here that runs across the Qur'an. In Surah al-Insān, almost the same construction appears: *inna hādhihi tadhkirah, fa-man shāʾa-ttakhadha ilā rabbihī sabīlā* — "This is a reminder; so whoever wills, let him take a path to his Lord" (76:29). The Qur'an repeatedly presents itself as a *tadhkirah* — a reminder — laid out in the open, and then steps back and lets the human *want.* It does not grab the collar. It reminds, and it waits.

So far, the picture is overwhelmingly one of human freedom. The door is open. The choice is yours. Whoever among you *wants.* If the surah ended at verse 28, a person could walk away believing the entire matter rested in his own hands.

And that is *exactly* what one man did.

### Element Three: The sentence that closes the gap — مَا ... إِلَّا

Here we have to bring in something the classical commentators preserved, and we have to handle it with care.

Al-Qurtubī records a narration — reported through Abū Hurayra and Sulaymān ibn Mūsā — that when the verse *li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm* was revealed, Abū Jahl said: *"Then the matter is up to us. If we want, we'll walk straight; if we want, we won't."* As if to say: so it's entirely in our hands. Our will is the final word. And Qurtubī notes this attitude became, in essence, the root claim of a later theological faction, the Qadariyya, who overweighted human will to the point of cutting Allah out of it.

I want to flag honestly: this is *one narration* among the material the commentators preserved about these verses, an occasion-of-revelation report, not a unanimous transmission. But whether or not those exact words were spoken, the verse that follows answers *precisely* that misunderstanding — and that is what makes it so illuminating to read them together.

Because watch what comes next, immediately, before the breath of verse 28 has even settled:

> وَمَا تَشَآءُونَ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ
> *Wa mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa-llāh*
> **And you do not will unless Allah wills.**

Let me teach you the structure, because the structure *is* the argument.

In Arabic, one of the most powerful ways to make an exclusive, locked-down statement is the construction **mā ... illā** — *negation, then exception.* You first negate completely: *mā* — "not... at all." Then you carve out one single exception: *illā* — "except." The effect is total restriction. Grammarians call this *qasr* or *ḥasr* — confinement. You wipe the whole field clean, then admit exactly one thing back in.

You know this structure already, because it is the structure of the first half of our faith. *Lā ilāha illā-llāh.* "There is no god — *at all* — **except** Allah." First the total sweep of negation: no gods. Then the single exception: except Him. That is *mā... illā* in its most famous form. It is not a soft statement. It is a door slammed shut on everything, with one and only one thing allowed through.

Now apply that here. *Wa mā tashāʾūna* — "And you do not will" — total negation of your willing. Sweep the field. *Illā an yashāʾa-llāh* — "**except** that Allah wills." One exception admitted back in: your willing exists, but *only* inside the frame of His willing.

[PAUSE]

Notice — and this is everything — **He did not negate your will.** Read it again. He did not say "you have no will." That would have erased verse 28; it would have made "whoever among you wills" a cruel joke. The *mā... illā* structure is far more precise than that. It does not delete your willing. **It locates it.** It tells you exactly where your willing lives: *inside* the willing of Allah. Your wanting is real — and your wanting is held.

And the classical commentators are careful to fill in *what* it is you are willing here, because the verb *tashāʾūna* — "you will" — leaves its object unstated. The early mufassirūn supply it from the verse before. The author of al-Jalālayn and the reading preserved in al-Muyassar both read it the same way: *wa mā tashāʾūna* [al-istiqāma] *illā an yashāʾa-llāh* — "and you do not will **[the walking-straight]** unless Allah wills." So verse 29 is not a loose remark about willing in the abstract. It reaches back and takes hold of the one specific thing verse 28 just offered — *yastaqīm,* the staying-straight — and says: even *that* wanting is held inside His.

And al-Muyassar adds a second layer that is easy to read past. It glosses the verse: *wa mā tashāʾūna l-istiqāma, wa lā taqdirūna ʿalā dhālik, illā bi-mashīʾati-llāh* — "you do not will uprightness, **nor are you able to attain it,** except by the will of Allah." It is not only the *wanting* that is given. The very *power* to carry the wanting into action — the *qudra,* the capacity to actually walk the road you've chosen — that too rests inside His willing. The desire and the strength to act on the desire are both held in the same hands. This is mercy, not menace: it means that on the day your legs are weak, the strength was never something you had to find in yourself alone.

Now look at the verb that repeats. The root **ش-ي-ء**, *sh-y-'*, "to will," appears three times across these two verses, and the repetition is the whole drama:

- Verse 28: *li-man* **shāʾa** *minkum* — "for whoever among you **wills**." (Your will.)
- Verse 29: *wa mā* **tashāʾūna** — "and you do not **will**." (Your will, named again.)
- Verse 29: *illā an* **yashāʾa-llāh** — "except that **Allah wills**." (His will.)

Three willings. Two are yours. One is His. And the grammar arranges them so that *yours are nested inside His.* The same root, the same act — willing — predicated first of you, then of you again, then of God, in a sentence whose architecture places His as the container and yours as the contained.

This is not Allah competing with you for control. **A container does not compete with what it holds.** The riverbank does not fight the river; it is the reason the river can go anywhere at all. Take the banks away and you don't get a freer river — you get a swamp that goes nowhere. Your will, the verse says, is real water, really moving, really yours. And it moves *because* there is a bed for it to move in, a bed He laid down: *an yashāʾa-llāh.*

And then — the final phrase, the signature on the whole thing:

> رَبُّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ
> *Rabbu-l-ʿālamīn*
> **The Lord of all the worlds.**

He could have ended on *yashāʾa-llāh*, "unless Allah wills," and the sentence would be grammatically complete. But He adds His title. And not just any title — **Rabb.** From the root *r-b-b*, which means the one who nurtures, sustains, raises something up from one state to a better one, owns it and cares for it all at once. A *Rabb* is not a distant overlord. A *Rabb* is the one who *grows* you.

So when the verse says your will lives inside the will of *Rabbi-l-ʿālamīn* — the Nurturer of all the worlds — it is not naming a tyrant who confines you. It is naming **the One whose entire relationship to you is cultivation.** The will your willing is nested inside belongs to the One who is, this very moment, *raising you.* The hand on the hinge is the hand of the One who is growing you toward Himself.

[PAUSE]

Let me name what these three elements have shown us, and then we'll ask what it means not just for words, but for the way you wake up tomorrow.

The verb *yastaqīm* told us: guidance is a *staying straight*, not a one-time arrival — a road, not a gate. The word *minkum* told us: the offer is real, freely yours, and not everyone will take it. And the structure *mā... illā*, sealed with *Rabbu-l-ʿālamīn*, told us: your real, free wanting lives *inside* the wanting of the One who is growing you.

Now let's ask what reality itself looks like when both of those are true at once.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The unbearable weight you were never meant to carry

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from believing it is *all on you.* You feel it in your faith and you feel it in your life. The voice says: *if you were a better person, you'd be praying with more focus. If you really wanted it, you'd have changed by now. Your iman is your project, your responsibility, your fault when it slips. Stand up straighter. Try harder. Why can't you just want it more?*

It is the spiritual version of the burnout that flattens people in every other area of modern life — the belief that you are the sole author of your outcomes, the lone engine, and if the engine sputters, there is no one to blame but you. We have built an entire culture on it: manifest it, will it into being, hustle, *you are limitless.* And underneath that gospel of pure self-will is a quiet despair, because deep down every honest person knows: **I cannot even reliably control my own wanting.**

Think about it. Have you ever tried to *want* something you didn't want? Have you ever stood on the prayer mat and tried to force your heart to feel what it refused to feel? Have you ever known, completely, that you *should* want to change — and found that the wanting itself would not come?

The will is not as obedient as the self-help books pretend. You cannot simply decide, from nothing, to desire God. The wanting itself is something that has to be *given.*

[PAUSE]

And this is the door verse 29 opens — not a door of confinement, but of *relief.*

*Wa mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa-llāh.* You do not even will, unless Allah wills.

Read through the lens of exhaustion, hear what this is actually saying to the person crushed under the weight of *it's all on me*: **it was never all on you.** The very wanting you've been straining to manufacture by force — that is not a raw material you were supposed to generate alone in the dark. It is something *He gives.* Which means the wanting you *do* have — that flicker of "I wish I were closer to Him," that ache that brought you to even hear these words — **that flicker is already a gift He placed in you.** It is already evidence He is willing good for you.

Do you see the reversal? The verse that looks, at first, like it's *taking* your agency is actually *lifting the impossible part of the burden off it.* You were trying to be the source of your own desire for God. You are not the source. He is. Your job was never to manufacture the wanting from scratch. **Your job is to honor the wanting He gives you by acting on it** — *an yastaqīm,* to keep walking straight in the direction the gift is pointing.

There is a hadith that lives in exactly this space. The Prophet ﷺ would frequently say: *Yā muqallib al-qulūb, thabbit qalbī ʿalā dīnik* — "O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion." Sit with the fact that the Prophet ﷺ — the most upright human being who ever lived — did not say, "I have decided to be firm." He turned to the One who *holds the hearts* and asked Him to do the holding. He understood verse 29 in his bones. The wanting, the firmness, the staying-straight — you ask for it, because the One who wills is the One who can place the will in you.

So here is what changes about tomorrow. When you find that you *don't* want to pray, that the desire isn't there — the old script says "force it, you failure." The Qur'anic script says something else entirely. It says: *the wanting is His to give — so ask Him for it.* "O Allah, I don't even want this right now. Make me want it." That is not weakness. **That is the single most theologically precise prayer you can make**, because it is built on the exact truth of verse 29: you do not will, unless He wills — so go to the One who wills, and ask Him to will it into you.

The weight comes off. Not because you stop being responsible — you still have to *walk* — but because you were never the manufacturer of your own heart. You are the one who asks the Manufacturer.

### Theme Two: Holding two truths your mind wants to tear apart

Now I want to take you into the harder place, because these two verses do something the human mind finds genuinely difficult: they hold two truths together that *feel* like they should cancel.

Truth one: **you are responsible.** "Whoever among you wants to." The choice is real, the door is real, your wanting matters, and you will be held to account for what you do with it.

Truth two: **you are dependent.** "You do not will unless Allah wills." Even your wanting is held inside His.

The mind wants to *pick one.* And history shows us exactly how the picking goes wrong, in both directions.

Abū Jahl, in that narration, picked truth one and dropped truth two: *"The matter is up to us."* Pure self-will. *I* am the author. And the danger of that road is arrogance — the man who credits himself for every good thing in him and feels no need to bow to anyone, because he believes he made himself.

But there is an equal and opposite error, and many sincere believers fall into it: picking truth two and dropping truth one. *"It's all qadar. If Allah wanted me guided, I'd be guided. My sins are written. What can I do?"* Pure fatalism. And the danger of *that* road is paralysis and the death of responsibility — the man who excuses everything because he has erased his own agency.

[PAUSE]

The Qur'an refuses both amputations. It will not let you keep one verse and throw away the other. It places them *side by side,* deliberately, verse 28 and verse 29, freedom and dependence, and it says: **both. Live inside both.**

How? How does a person actually live inside a paradox instead of just admitting defeat in front of it?

Let me give you the way that has helped me, and it comes straight from the order of the two verses.

Notice the *direction* the verses face. Verse 28 — your freedom, your choice, "whoever wants" — faces *forward,* toward your action. Verse 29 — His will holding yours — when do you reach for it? You reach for it when you look *backward,* at what already happened.

Here is the rule that falls out of that, and it is the practical key to the whole paradox:

**When you are looking forward, at what you are about to do — act as if it is entirely on you.** Choose. Strive. Walk straight. Do not lean on qadar to excuse a single act you haven't done yet. Verse 28 is your stance toward the future: *the door is open, and I am responsible for walking through it.*

**When you are looking backward, at what already happened — see it entirely through His will.** The good you managed? *He willed it; thank Him.* The trial that landed on you? *It passed through His will; trust Him.* Even the sin you fell into and have now repented from — it was not outside His knowing, and His door of mercy is open. Verse 29 is your stance toward the past: *whatever has come to pass, His will held it, and He is Rabbu-l-ʿālamīn, the One who grows me even through this.*

Forward: I am responsible. Backward: He was in control. The exhausted fatalist has it backwards — he uses "it's all His will" to excuse the *future* ("why try?") and uses "it's all on me" to torture himself over the *past* ("I ruined everything"). He has the two verses pointed the wrong way. Turn them around. Use your freedom for what's ahead, and His sovereignty for what's behind, and the paradox stops crushing you — **it starts carrying you.**

Because look at what you get when you live this way. You get to strive with the full seriousness of a free agent — and you get to rest in the arms of a God who was never not in control. You get the dignity of responsibility *without* the despair of total self-reliance. You get the peace of dependence *without* the rot of fatalism. The two verses, held together, give you something neither could give alone: **you work like it all depends on you, and you trust like it all depends on Him — because, in the deepest truth, both are so.**

And the seal on it is *Rabbu-l-ʿālamīn.* The will your freedom rests inside is not random and not hostile. It is the will of your *Rabb* — the One nurturing you, the One whose every decree about you is bent toward growing you home. So even the trust is not a leap into the dark. It is leaning back into the arms of the One who has been raising you all along.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: a door and a hinge

Now step back and see how these two verses are built, because the structure *is* the message.

Verse 28 is **a door thrown open.** *Li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm* — for whoever among you wants to keep walking straight. It faces you, the human, and it hands you agency, freedom, an invitation, a road.

Verse 29 is **the hand on the hinge.** *Wa mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa-llāh, rabbu-l-ʿālamīn* — and your very wanting lives inside His willing, the willing of the One who is growing all the worlds.

A door with no hinge is not freedom — it's a slab of wood lying on the ground; it can't open at all. And a hinge with no door is just hardware bolted to a wall; nothing to walk through. **You need both for there to be a way in.** Verse 28 without verse 29 produces Abū Jahl — "the matter is up to us" — a door that thinks it hung itself. Verse 29 without verse 28 produces the fatalist — a hinge with no door, no walking, no point in trying. The surah gives you the whole working doorway: a real opening, on a hinge held by your Lord.

### The two directions, one insight

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey turn out to be the same discovery seen from two sides.

The grammar showed us *yastaqīm* — a Form X verb of *ongoing, sought, maintained* straightness — and the structure *mā... illā* that *locates* your will inside His rather than erasing it. The themes showed us a way to live: strive forward as a free agent, rest backward in His will, and ask Him for the very wanting you cannot manufacture alone. They are the same truth. **Your straightness is something you actively keep seeking — and the seeking itself is something He gives.** You walk the road; He laid it, and He keeps your feet on it when you ask.

### Questions to carry

- When my desire to pray, to change, to draw close simply *isn't there* — do I curse myself for not having it, or do I turn to the One who gives the wanting and *ask Him for it?*
- Am I using these two verses the right way around — my freedom aimed at the future I haven't lived yet, His sovereignty wrapped around the past I cannot change? Or have I got them backwards, excusing what's ahead and torturing myself over what's behind?
- *Yastaqīm* is a verb of staying, not arriving. Where in my life have I been treating guidance as a moment I once had, instead of a straightness I have to keep re-seeking every day?
- That flicker of wanting that brought me to even reflect on these words — can I see it for what it is: a gift He already placed in me, evidence He is already willing good for me?
- When I succeed at something good, which hand do I reach for first to credit — my own, or my Rabb's?

### One-sentence distillation

The door to walking straight is genuinely yours to enter — and the very willingness to enter it is a gift from the Lord who is growing you, so strive like it all depends on you and lean like it all depends on Him, because both are true.

### The closing invitation

You don't have to resolve the paradox. You were never asked to. You are asked to *live inside it* — and living inside it is lighter, not heavier, than the false certainties on either side. So tonight, or tomorrow, when the wanting is thin, try the prayer that these verses make possible: *O Turner of hearts, You said I do not even will unless You will — so will it into me. Make me want to walk straight, and then keep me standing.* That is not surrendering your effort. **That is putting your effort exactly where it belongs** — in the hands that hold the hinge.

### Du'ā

O Allah, Rabb of all the worlds, You opened the door and called whoever wants to walk straight — so place the wanting in us, for we cannot make it on our own. You told us we do not will unless You will; then we ask You to will our hearts toward You, to keep us standing when we are tired, and to lift us back up each time we fall. *Yā muqallib al-qulūb,* keep our hearts firm upon Your straight path until the day we stand before You. Āmīn.
