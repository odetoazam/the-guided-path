# Eat and Enjoy a Little: When Permission Becomes the Punishment

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the words in front of you. Sit with them before we touch them.

> **كُلُوا۟ وَتَمَتَّعُوا۟ قَلِيلًا إِنَّكُم مُّجْرِمُونَ**
>
> *Kulū wa-tamattaʿū qalīlan innakum mujrimūn*
>
> **"Eat and enjoy yourselves a little; indeed, you are criminals."**

Eight words in your translation. Five in the Arabic. And I want to tell you, before we go anywhere, that this is one of the most frightening sentences in the entire Quran — and almost nothing about it *looks* frightening on the surface.

There is no fire in it. No chains. No screaming. No description of punishment at all. On the face of it, it sounds almost generous. *Eat. Enjoy. Go ahead.* If you handed this line to someone who didn't know the Quran and asked them what it was, they might guess it was an invitation. A blessing. A host welcoming a guest to the table.

[PAUSE]

And that is exactly the horror of it. Hold that thought, because we're going to come back to it, and when we do, I want it to land with full weight.

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this ayah, you have to feel where it sits.

Surah Al-Mursalat is built like a drumbeat. Ten times — *ten times* — across these handful of pages, one sentence returns and strikes again:

**وَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ** — *"Woe that Day to the deniers."*

Imagine sitting in Makkah, in the early years, hearing this recited. The surah opens with oaths — by the winds sent forth, by the storms that blow violently, by the scatterers and the separators. The whole opening is *motion*: things being sent, things being unleashed, forces in transit. And then the hammer falls: *what you are promised is going to happen.* The sky will be torn. The mountains will be scattered like dust. The messengers will be gathered. And again and again — *woe that Day to the deniers.*

By the time you arrive at our ayah near the end, you have been struck by that refrain over and over. The denier has been told what is coming. He has been shown the cosmos coming apart. He has been shown, just a few lines earlier, the people of God-consciousness — *al-muttaqīn* — sitting in shade and springs, with fruits of whatever they desire, being told: *"Eat and drink in satisfaction, for what you used to do."*

[PAUSE]

And now, *immediately after that*, the address turns. The same verb — *eat* — is spoken again. But this time it is not spoken to the people in the gardens. It is spoken to the deniers. And it is not the same sentence at all.

To the righteous: *"Eat and drink in satisfaction — for what you used to do."* A reward, tied to a past of striving.

To the deniers: *"Eat and enjoy yourselves a little — indeed, you are criminals."* The same opening word. A completely opposite world.

### The hook

So here is what I want you to wonder about, and carry with you through everything that follows.

Why would Allah — the Most Merciful, the One whose mercy precedes His wrath — *command* the criminal to eat? Why would He *tell* him to enjoy himself?

We are taught that Allah commands what He loves and forbids what He hates. So what does it mean when the command itself — *go ahead, eat, enjoy* — is the most severe thing He could possibly say?

[PAUSE]

There is a kind of "yes" that is more terrifying than any "no." And this ayah is built entirely out of that "yes." Let us learn, slowly, how to hear it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The command that is not a command

Let me teach you something about language first — and not Arabic yet. Just language. Your language.

Imagine a father has warned his son again and again: *don't touch that, don't break that, stop.* And the son keeps doing it, smirking, defiant. And finally the father goes quiet, looks at him, and says, slowly: **"Fine. Go ahead. Keep going."**

Now — is that a command? Grammatically, yes. "Go ahead" is an imperative. It has the exact form of permission. But every single person in that room knows it is not permission. It is the *opposite* of permission. It is a threat wearing the clothing of permission. The father is saying: *I have stopped restraining you. I am withdrawing my objection. And what is coming for you now is worse than anything I would have done.*

[PAUSE]

The form says *yes*. The meaning says *you are finished.*

Arabic has a precise category for this. The classical scholars of rhetoric call it the imperative used for **tahdīd** — for *threat*. The command form (*ṣīghat al-amr*) does not always mean a command. It bends, depending on context, into many shapes: a prayer, a permission, an honoring, a humiliation, a challenge, and — this one — a *threat*. The grammar is the same. The intention is read from the situation.

Now look at our ayah:

> **كُلُوا۟ وَتَمَتَّعُوا۟ قَلِيلًا**
>
> *"Eat and enjoy yourselves a little."*

Both are imperatives. *Kulū* — eat. *Tamattaʿū* — enjoy yourselves. The form is permission. The form is even, on its face, generosity.

And every classical commentator who handles this ayah tells you the same thing about what it actually *is*. Al-Jalālayn states it plainly: this is *"a threat to them"* — *wa-fī hādhā tahdīdun lahum*. Al-Muyassar opens by naming it directly: *"Then Allah threatened the disbelievers, saying..."* Al-Qurṭubī calls it *"a warning and a threat"* — *waʿīdun wa-tahdīd*. Al-Baghawī ties it to its target: *"Then He said to the disbelievers of Makkah..."*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The mercy of a command — *go ahead, enjoy* — has been turned into the sharpest instrument of warning in the surah. Not because the words changed, but because of *who* is being addressed and *what* is true about them.

And here is what makes it so devastating. Allah does not say to them, *"I will punish you."* He does not describe the fire here at all. He simply *removes Himself from the path of their desire.* He says: *eat. Enjoy.* Go and have the thing you have been chasing your whole life.

Because when Allah stops forbidding you — when the One who restrains out of love withdraws the restraint — that withdrawal *is* the verdict. The denier wanted to be left alone with his appetites. The ayah grants the wish. And the granting of that wish is the most frightening word in the passage.

What this single construction reveals: **the worst thing that can happen to a soul is to be handed exactly what it wanted with no one left to say no.**

[PAUSE — let this sit.]

### Element Two: *Tamattaʿū* — the verb of fleeting indulgence

Now let's open the second verb, because Allah did not simply repeat "eat." He chose a second word, and the choice is everything.

> **وَتَمَتَّعُوا۟** — *wa-tamattaʿū* — *"and enjoy yourselves."*

Let me teach you the shape of this word first, because the shape carries meaning.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns — "forms" we number one through ten. A bare root can be intensified, made causative, made mutual, made reflexive, just by reshaping it. *Tamattaʿū* sits on **Form V** — the pattern *tafaʿʿala*. And one of the core functions of Form V is **reflexivity with effort** — *to do something to and for yourself, to take it on yourself, to immerse yourself in it.* It turns the action inward. You are not just receiving enjoyment. You are *making* enjoyment *for yourself*, actively, deliberately, with your whole self bent toward it.

So already the verb is not passive. It is not *"and you will be made comfortable."* It is *"go, throw yourselves into pleasure, indulge yourselves."* It points the finger at the appetite and says: *yes, that — do that.*

But there is something deeper in the root itself. The root is **م-ت-ع**, *m-t-ʿ*, and the noun from it is one you know: **matāʿ**.

What is *matāʿ*? The lexicons are unanimous and the Quran uses the word constantly in one specific way. *Matāʿ* is **provision that is temporary** — goods, supplies, the stuff of a journey, something you use for a while and then leave behind. When you pack for a trip, your luggage is *matāʿ*. It is, by its very nature, *what does not last.* It serves you for the road and then it is gone.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? Allah did not choose a verb of *joy* (there are such verbs). He did not choose a verb of *contentment* (He used exactly that — *hanīʾan* — for the righteous a few lines up). For the deniers He chose the verb whose very root means **the thing that runs out.**

The Quran does this everywhere with this word. *"The enjoyment of this world (matāʿ al-dunyā) is little"* (4:77). *"Say: the enjoyment (matāʿ) of this world is small"* (4:77). *"So enjoy yourselves (tamattaʿū) — for you are going to know"* (16:55). *"We let them enjoy (numattiʿuhum) a little, then We drive them to a harsh punishment"* (31:24).

Every time this verb appears for the heedless, it carries its own expiry date *inside the word.* Allah is not just telling them to enjoy. He is telling them to enjoy **the thing that is defined by ending.** The verb itself is a countdown.

[PAUSE]

So now hear the line again with this loaded: *"Throw yourselves into the pleasure that is, by its nature, already running out."* The grammar (Form V) shows you their effort — they are *working* at this indulgence, pouring themselves into it. The root (*matāʿ*) shows you its emptiness — what they are pouring themselves into is by definition disappearing in their hands.

What this verb reveals: **the deniers are exhausting themselves chasing a thing whose name means "what does not stay."** And Allah, with a single word choice, lets them hear their whole life in it.

### Element Three: *Qalīlan* — the little that is left unmeasured, and the identity that never moves

Now the third element. And here I want you to watch an *absence* — because in this ayah, what Allah does *not* say is as loud as what He does.

> **قَلِيلًا** — *qalīlan* — *"a little."*

A little *what*?

This is the question. In Arabic, *qalīlan* here is adverbial — it tells you the *measure* of the eating and enjoying. But Allah leaves the noun off. He does not say "a little food." He does not say "a little time." He does not say "a little while." He just says *qalīlan* — *little* — and lets the word hang there, unattached, deliberately open.

And the commentators, reading this gap, fill it in two ways — not as a disagreement, but as two beams of light on the same point. Al-Jalālayn says *qalīlan min al-zaman* — *"a little of time, and its end is death."* The little is *temporal*: you have a short span, and the span ends at the grave. Al-Muyassar reads it as *zamanan qalīlan* — *"a little time."* The little is the leash of your whole worldly life.

[PAUSE]

But because Allah left the noun unspoken, the word also catches everything else. A little food. A little pleasure. A little wealth. A little laughter. A little of *all of it* — because the unspecified "little" refuses to be limited to one thing. It quietly indicts the *entire* harvest of a life spent on the dunya. *All* of it — every meal, every triumph, every indulgence you ever chased — *qalīlan.* Little. A handful. Almost nothing.

Here is the cruelty of mercy in this word. To the person living it, the dunya does not feel little. It feels like *everything*. It feels enormous, urgent, all-consuming. The whole drama of a heedless life is built on the conviction that *this matters, this is real, this is what there is.* And Allah reduces the entire edifice to a single adverb thrown in almost casually: *qalīlan.* The thing you built your soul around — from the vantage point of eternity, it is barely worth a noun.

Now watch what comes immediately after.

> **إِنَّكُم مُّجْرِمُونَ** — *innakum mujrimūn* — *"indeed, you are criminals."*

Feel the *grammatical shift*. Everything before this was a **verb** — eat, enjoy. Verbs are events. They happen and pass. They are tied to time. But *innakum mujrimūn* is a **nominal sentence** — there is no verb in it at all. It is pure identity. *You — criminals.* And it is reinforced with **inna**, the particle of emphatic certainty: *indeed, truly, beyond question.*

This is one of the most important contrasts in the whole ayah, and it is built entirely out of grammar. Look:

- The enjoyment is given as a **verb** — fleeting, temporary, an event with an end. *Eat. Enjoy. A little.*
- The verdict is given as a **noun** — fixed, stable, a state of being. *You are criminals.*

[PAUSE]

The pleasure is the thing that passes. The identity is the thing that stays. They have it backwards — they live as though the pleasure is permanent and their guilt is temporary, a phase, something they'll deal with later. The grammar tells the truth: the pleasure is the verb that ends, and the guilt is the noun that *is*.

And the word for the guilt — **mujrimūn**, from the root **ج-ر-م**, *j-r-m**. Its core meaning in the lexicons is *to cut, to sever* — and from there, *to commit a crime*, to *cut yourself off*. A *mujrim* is one who has severed something. The commentators gloss it variously: al-Qurṭubī says *kāfirūn* (deniers) and also *"those who have earned, by their deeds, what harms them in the Hereafter — shirk and sins."* Al-Baghawī: *mushrikūn* — those who associated partners with Allah, *deserving of the punishment.* Al-Muyassar ties it precisely to *"your crime of associating partners with Allah."*

So the final word names what the cutting *was*: they cut themselves off from their Lord. And the form of the word — *mujrimūn*, an active participle — describes not an act they did once but a *standing condition*. Not "you committed a crime" but "you *are* criminals." It is what they have become.

[PAUSE]

Put the three elements together and the architecture appears. *Eat* — the command that is mercy withdrawn. *Enjoy yourselves* — the indulgence whose very name means *what runs out*. *A little* — the entire life shrunk to an adverb. *Indeed, you are criminals* — and here, finally, is the *reason* the whole sentence is a threat and not a gift. The "go ahead" only becomes terrifying when you reach the last two words. Why is the permission a sentence? *Because of who you are.* The enjoyment is not forbidden to them anymore — not because it became permitted, but because they are already lost, and there is nothing left to protect.

### Bridge

So this is what the construction has shown us: a command that is a threat, a pleasure whose name is its own expiry, a lifetime measured as *little*, and a guilt stated as fixed, certain identity. The grammar of the ayah is a complete theology in five words.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About what it actually means to live, to want, to chase. Because this ayah is not really about *those* criminals, far away, long ago. It is a mirror. Let us stand in front of it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When getting what you want is the sentence

Let me start where you live.

There is an experience almost everyone has had, and it is one of the strangest experiences a human being can have. It is the moment you *get the thing you wanted* — and it is empty.

The promotion you chased for three years. The relationship you ached for. The number in the bank account you swore would make you feel secure. The recognition. The house. The thing. You got it. And there was a strange, hollow afternoon, a day or two later, when you sat with it and a quiet voice inside asked: *is that all?* The wanting was so loud, so vivid, so alive — and the having was so quiet.

Psychologists have a name for one part of this. They call it the *hedonic treadmill* — the way our satisfaction resets to baseline no matter what we acquire, so we have to keep running just to feel the same. But there is something underneath even that. There is the deeper terror of *being left alone with your appetite and nothing else.* Of getting everything you wanted and discovering that the wanting was the only thing that ever felt like life, and now even that is gone, and what's left is just... the having. And the having is cold.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the ayah back.

> **كُلُوا۟ وَتَمَتَّعُوا۟ قَلِيلًا**

What is Allah doing here? He is granting the wish. Completely. The denier spent his whole life wanting to be left to his appetites — *don't tell me what to do, don't restrict me, let me live, let me enjoy, this is my life.* And the ayah says: **done.** *Eat. Enjoy. Have it.*

And this is the thing I need you to feel: **the granting is the punishment.**

Because what is a parent's restriction? What is it, really, when someone who loves you says *no, not that, not now, that will hurt you*? It is the most exhausting form of love there is. It is presence. It is attention. It is someone caring enough about your future to absorb your anger in the present. Every *no* from someone who loves you is a *yes* to your wellbeing.

And the most frightening thing such a person can ever do is **stop.** When they go quiet. When they say *fine, do what you want.* When the corrections end. Any child who has pushed a loving parent to that silence knows the cold that follows. It is the cold of *being released.* And release, when you are heading toward a cliff, is not freedom. It is abandonment wearing the mask of freedom.

[PAUSE]

This is the unseen reality the ayah is pointing at. Allah's commands — *don't, stop, not that* — that you may have spent your life resenting, experiencing as a cage, as a list of joyless prohibitions from a God who doesn't want you to have fun. The ayah flips the whole thing inside out. Those restrictions were the *evidence that He had not let go of you.* The forbidding was the holding. And the most terrifying sentence in the Quran is the one where the forbidding stops: *eat, enjoy, a little.* Go on, then. Have it.

Because here is the logic underneath. You only restrain what you are still invested in. You only correct the course of someone whose destination still matters to you. When Allah says to the criminal *"eat and enjoy,"* He is not being generous. He is communicating that the time for course-correction is over. The verdict is set. *Innakum mujrimūn* — *you are criminals* — and now, there being nothing left to save, the restraint is lifted. *Enjoy your little while.*

[PAUSE]

So let me ask you to feel something uncomfortable and turn it into gratitude.

Every time you feel the pull of a desire and something in you holds back — *I shouldn't, this is wrong, Allah sees this* — that friction, that resistance, that voice that ruins the moment... that is not your cage. **That is your rescue.** That is the sound of not having been abandoned yet. The person who can sin freely, easily, with no inner protest at all, is not free. He is the one to whom the ayah's "yes" has quietly begun to be spoken.

There is a saying among the people of knowledge: *fear the sin you commit easily.* Now you know why. The ease is not a gift. The friction is.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time you feel the discomfort of holding back from something you want but know is wrong — the next time obedience feels like deprivation — reframe it in the instant. That tightness in your chest is mercy. It is the *no* of a Lord who has not released you. Thank Him for the *no*. The day to be afraid is the day the *no* goes silent.

[PAUSE — major pause.]

### Theme Two: The arithmetic of "a little"

Now let me take you to the second reality, and it lives in one word: *qalīlan.* A little.

Here is a way to feel it.

Imagine you are handed a contract. It offers you a sum of money — a real sum, a life-changing sum — to be enjoyed for a fixed term. And the term is one week. After the week, the money is gone, and in exchange you have signed away everything you will own forever after. One week of plenty. An eternity of nothing.

You would call that insane. No sane person signs that. The math is grotesque. A finite, tiny term traded against an infinite one. The number *during* the week doesn't matter — it could be a billion a day — because *any* finite number divided against infinity rounds to zero.

[PAUSE]

That is the arithmetic the word *qalīlan* is quietly performing.

The Quran is relentless about this comparison, and it is worth letting it accumulate. *"The enjoyment of this world is little (qalīl), and the Hereafter is better for the one who is mindful"* (4:77). *"You prefer the worldly life, while the Hereafter is better and more lasting"* (87:16-17). *"What is with you runs out, and what is with Allah remains"* (16:96). Over and over, the same equation: *the dunya is the small term; the ākhira is the lasting one.* And the whole human tragedy is that we feel it backwards.

Because here is the problem. *Qalīlan* is true — but it does not *feel* true. From inside a life, the dunya does not feel little. It feels like the whole of reality. The bills are not little. The heartbreak is not little. The ambition, the fear, the appetite, the seventy or eighty years of it — none of it feels *qalīl.* It feels like everything there is.

This is why the ayah does not *argue* the point. It does not give you a lecture on the brevity of life. It just sets the word down — *qalīlan* — like a single stone, and lets it sit next to the eternity it is being weighed against. And it trusts you, if you are willing, to feel the imbalance.

[PAUSE]

Let me make the imbalance physical, because the Quran's whole method is to point at something you can feel.

You have lived, let's say, some years already. Think back to the most intense pleasure you can remember from ten years ago. A genuine peak. The best meal, the best day, a moment of real delight. Now — *where is it?* Not the memory of it. The *thing* itself, the actual sensation, the actual enjoyment. Where is it right now?

It is *gone.* Completely gone. It serves you nothing this instant. You cannot taste a single bite of it. It was real and now it is not, and the only trace it left is a faint outline in your memory. *Matāʿ.* The provision that was for the road and is now behind you.

That is what *all* of it becomes. Every pleasure you are chasing right now — the one that feels so urgent, so worth it, so *necessary* — is on its way to becoming exactly that faded outline. *Qalīlan.* Little. Already, even now, in the act of being enjoyed, it is leaking away.

[PAUSE]

And here is the turn, because this is not meant to make you despise the dunya or stop tasting your food. The righteous, remember, were *also* told to eat — *"eat and drink in satisfaction."* The dunya's enjoyment is not the crime. The crime is *mistaking the little for the everything.* It is building your soul on the verb that ends instead of the truth that lasts. It is signing the insane contract because the week felt so vivid you forgot about the forever.

The believer eats the same food, enjoys the same gifts — but holds them as *matāʿ*, as provision for a journey, with an open hand. He knows it is *qalīl*, so he never asks it to be what it cannot be. He doesn't demand eternity from a thing whose name means *what runs out.* And so, strangely, he enjoys it *more* freely than the one who clings — because he is not terrified of losing what he never thought he owned.

The criminal in the ayah is the one who looked at the *qalīl* and called it *everything*, and spent the only currency he had — his short, irreplaceable while — buying more of the thing that disappears.

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Take one pleasure you reach for habitually — the scroll, the food, the purchase, the distraction — and the next time you reach for it, attach one word to it in your mind before you take it: *qalīl.* Little. Not to poison it. To *right-size* it. To enjoy it as a traveler enjoys a cup of water on the road — gratefully, fully, and without the lie that this cup is the destination. The moment you stop asking the dunya to be eternity, two things happen at once: the dunya becomes lighter to hold, and the ākhira becomes real enough to live for.

[PAUSE — major pause.]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole shape, because the five words are built with terrible precision, and you can only see the design once you've walked through the pieces.

Watch the descent of the sentence:

**كُلُوا۟** — *Eat.* The command that has become a threat: mercy's restraint withdrawn.

**وَتَمَتَّعُوا۟** — *And enjoy yourselves.* The Form V verb of self-immersion, built on the root *matāʿ* — *the thing that runs out.* They pour their whole selves into what is, by definition, leaking away.

**قَلِيلًا** — *A little.* The entire harvest of a heedless life, reduced to one unattached adverb, refusing to name even *what* is little because *all* of it is.

**إِنَّكُم مُّجْرِمُونَ** — *Indeed, you are criminals.* The grammar flips from verb to noun, from event to identity, from passing to fixed — and finally names *why* the whole line was a threat: not because pleasure is evil, but because *they are already severed*, and there is nothing left to hold them back from.

[PAUSE]

And there is the deeper architecture — the one across the surah. A few lines up, the same imperative, *eat*, is spoken to the righteous: *"Eat and drink in satisfaction, for what you used to do."* Same verb. Opposite world. There, the eating is followed by *for what you used to do* — a past of striving that *earned* the present. Here, the eating is followed by *indeed, you are criminals* — a past of severance that *condemns* it. The grammar is a fork in the road. The same word, *eat*, opens onto Paradise or onto the abyss, and the only difference is what stands behind the word: a life that obeyed, or a life that cut itself off.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The *grammar* showed us a command that is a threat — and the *first theme* showed us why: because to be handed your desire with no one left to forbid it is the deepest abandonment there is.

The *grammar* showed us a verb whose root means *what runs out*, measured by an adverb meaning *little* — and the *second theme* showed us why: because the whole human tragedy is feeling the *little* as *everything*, and spending eternity to buy more of what disappears.

One sentence. One theology. *The pleasure is the verb that ends; the identity is the noun that stays. And the soul that has these backwards is handed, as its sentence, exactly what it wanted.*

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. When you feel Allah's restriction as a cage — as a joyless *no* keeping you from what you want — can you learn to hear it instead as the sound of *not having been let go of?*

2. Is there any sin in your life that has become *easy* — that no longer meets any inner resistance? And does that ease frighten you the way it should?

3. If someone wrote down everything you have chased, worked for, worried over this past year, and labeled it all with one honest word — would *qalīlan* be the word? And does that change what you'll chase next year?

4. The righteous and the criminals were *both* told to "eat." The difference was what stood behind the word. What is standing behind *your* enjoyments — *for what you used to do*, or *indeed, you are*?

5. When you next get something you've wanted — really wanted — will you have the courage to notice whether it filled you, or whether the wanting was the only thing that ever felt alive?

### One-sentence distillation

**The most terrifying thing Allah can say to a soul is "go ahead, enjoy" — because His restrictions were never your cage but the proof He had not let go of you, and the fleeting pleasure you chase is itself, by its very name, the evidence that it was never worth your eternity.**

### The closing invitation

So here is what I'd invite to shift in you — not as an obligation, but as a door now standing open.

Stop experiencing the *no* of Allah as deprivation. Start experiencing it as the most intimate thing in your life — the sign of a Lord who is still, actively, every single day, refusing to abandon you to yourself. The friction you feel when you turn away from a sin is not your loss. It is His hand on your shoulder.

And hold the dunya the way you hold water on a journey — gratefully, fully, lightly — knowing it is *matāʿ*, knowing it is *qalīl*, and refusing, finally, to sign the insane contract that trades the forever for the week.

The criminals in this ayah were not handed fire. They were handed *yes.* May we be among those who, in this life, are still being told *no* — and who have learned, at last, to love it.

### Du'a

*O Allah, do not abandon us to our own appetites. Keep restraining us — keep saying no to us — for as long as we live, and let us learn to feel Your every restriction as Your mercy and not Your burden. Teach our hearts that the dunya is qalīl, that its pleasures are matāʿ that runs out in our hands, and let us never trade the eternity with You for the little while without You. Amīn.*
