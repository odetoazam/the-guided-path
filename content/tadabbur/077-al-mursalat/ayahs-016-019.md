# The Procession of the Destroyed
### A reflection on Surah Al-Mursalat, verses 16–19

> أَلَمْ نُهْلِكِ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ ثُمَّ نُتْبِعُهُمُ ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ ۝ كَذَٰلِكَ نَفْعَلُ بِٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ ۝ وَيْلٌۭ يَوْمَئِذٍۢ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ
>
> *Alam nuhlik il-awwalīn. Thumma nutbiʿuhum ul-ākhirīn. Kadhālika nafʿalu bil-mujrimīn. Waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn.*
>
> **"Did We not destroy the former peoples? Then We make the later ones follow them. Thus do We deal with the criminals. Woe, that Day, to the deniers!"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me ask you to do something before we open a single word.

I want you to stand for a moment in Makkah, somewhere around the early years of the revelation. The Quran has begun arriving, and a large part of what it brings is a warning: that a Day is coming when everything will be weighed, when the sky will crack, when the stars will lose their light. And the response of the people around the Prophet ﷺ — many of them — is not anger, not even argument at first. It is something quieter and more devastating.

It is a shrug.

It is the easy, comfortable confidence of a man who has heard a threat before and watched the threat dissolve into nothing. *We've heard this kind of talk. The world keeps turning. The caravans still leave for Syria in summer and Yemen in winter. The harvest comes. Nothing has happened. Nothing will happen.*

This is the world this passage walks into. A world of people who are not so much evil as **untroubled**. People who have built, out of the steady ordinary rhythm of their days, a kind of armor — a conviction that catastrophe is something that happens in stories, to other people, in other times. Never here. Never us. Never now.

[PAUSE]

And so the surah, which has been building like a gathering storm — swearing by the winds sent forth, by the storms that scatter, announcing that the Day of Decision is real — suddenly stops arguing about the future.

And it turns around to look at the past.

It points behind these comfortable, untroubled people, over their shoulders, down the long corridor of history, and it asks them a single question. Not a question that wants information. A question that wants them to *see* something they have spent their whole lives carefully not looking at.

**"Did We not destroy the former peoples?"**

Here is the strand running through this surah you should hold onto: ten times — *ten* — Al-Mursalat returns to one line, like a bell tolling at the end of every movement: *Woe, that Day, to the deniers.* Our passage contains the second toll. And what's remarkable is not the warning itself. It is what these four short verses reveal about *why* the warning has to keep coming, and about the one small, quiet act that sits underneath every single thing that has ever earned that woe.

Stay with me, because the last word of this passage rewrites the first three.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Is Not a Question

Let me teach you something before we touch the verse, because once you see it you will never un-see it, and it is everywhere in the Quran.

In every language, there is a kind of question that is not looking for an answer.

When a mother stands over the broken vase and says to the child, *"Did I not tell you to be careful?"* — she is not gathering information. She knows perfectly well that she told him. The child knows it too. The question is not a request for data. It is a **summons**. It drags a fact the child already knows out of the shadows and forces him to stand in front of it and admit it. *Yes. You told me.* The question form does something a flat statement could never do — it makes the listener supply the answer himself, from his own mouth, so he cannot escape it.

Arabic has a precise name for this. The grammarians call it *istifhām taqrīrī* — the **interrogative of affirmation**. A question whose only purpose is to force you to confirm what you already cannot deny.

Now look at how the verse opens:

> **أَلَمْ نُهْلِكِ** — *Alam nuhlik*

Two pieces fused together. The interrogative *hamza* — the "did?" — bolted onto *lam*, the particle of negation. **"Did We not...?"**

Notice the architecture of it. Allah does not say *"We destroyed the former peoples."* That would be a piece of information, and information can be filed away, doubted, set aside. Instead He builds it as a question with a negation inside it — *did We not?* — and the moment that question lands, something happens inside the listener that he did not consent to.

He answers it.

Even the most stubborn denier in Makkah, hearing this, cannot stop the answer forming in his own chest: *...yes. You did. The ruins are right there.* The blackened settlements of past nations were not legends to the Arabs — they were geography. They passed the remains on their trade routes. The question reaches *into* the listener and pulls out an admission he has been keeping carefully locked away.

[PAUSE]

This is the genius of the rhetorical question over the flat statement. A statement leaves you a spectator. A question makes you a **witness against yourself**. Allah is not informing these people of history. He is making them testify to it.

And here is what I want you to feel: He could have simply told them. He chose instead to ask. Because the goal was never to deposit a fact. The goal was to break the shrug — to make the comfortable man, for one second, *say yes* to the very thing his comfort depended on ignoring.

That is the first thing this passage reveals. **The truth was never far from them. It was behind them the whole time, and the question simply made them turn around.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Past That Will Not Stay in the Past

Now I want to show you something about *time* hidden in these verbs, and it is one of the most quietly terrifying things in the passage.

Let me set it up first.

When you tell a story about something that ended long ago, you use the past tense. *The empire fell. The flood came. They were destroyed.* The past tense does something for our comfort — it seals the event off. It puts a wall around it. *That happened. It is over. It belongs to then, not now.* The past tense is, in a strange way, a place we put things to keep them away from us.

So when Allah wants to describe nations that were wiped out centuries before the Arabs were born — the obvious, expected choice would be the past tense. *We destroyed them. We made others follow them.* Done. Sealed. Behind glass.

But look at what the verse actually does.

> أَلَمْ **نُهْلِكِ** ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ ثُمَّ **نُتْبِعُهُمُ** ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ

*Nuhlik.* *Nutbiʿuhum.* These are not past-tense verbs. They are *muḍāriʿ* — the **present-ongoing** form. The Leeds morphological corpus confirms it: *nuhlik* is imperfect, present in form, not past.

The most natural English rendering reaches for the past — "Did We not destroy" — because that is what English demands. But the Arabic ear hears something English struggles to carry: a destruction described in the living, unfinished present. **"Do We not destroy the former... then make the later ones follow...?"** Not a closed event behind glass. An open, running pattern. Something that *is happening*, that *keeps* happening, that has not stopped.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the wall come down?

The whole comfort of the Makkan shrug depended on one thing: *that was then.* Those ruined nations belong to the sealed-off past; we belong to the safe, ongoing present. And this single grammatical choice — the present-tense verb where the past was expected — reaches in and **smashes the glass**. It takes the destruction out of "then" and drops it into "now." It says: this is not a fact about history. This is a description of how reality *works*, and reality is still working.

And then the third verse makes it unmistakable. Allah moves from the historical to the explicitly timeless:

> [ayah:77:18] كَذَٰلِكَ نَفْعَلُ بِٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ
>
> *Kadhālika nafʿalu bil-mujrimīn* — **"Thus do We deal with the criminals."**

*Nafʿalu.* "We do." Present tense. Not "thus We dealt with them." Not a closed chapter. **This is what We do.** A standing law. A running engine. *Kadhālika* — "like that, in just that way" — points back at the wreckage of the ancients and says: that is not an old story. That is the *mechanism*, and the mechanism is still on.

Here's what's remarkable. The verse forces the listener into a syllogism he constructed himself. *Major premise:* this is what We do to criminals — always, ongoingly. *Minor premise:* you can see from history it has never once failed. *Conclusion...* The verse doesn't even say the conclusion. It doesn't have to. The grammar has already placed the listener inside the pattern. He is no longer reading about *them*. He is standing in the same machine.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — reasoning from the seen to the unseen. The ruins are the seen, the *maḥsūs*. The coming Judgment is the unseen, the *maʿqūl*. And the bridge between them is one present-tense verb that refuses to let the past be past.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Following — and the Word That Rewrites Everything

Two more pieces, and they are the hinge of the whole passage.

First, look at the verb in verse 17 — the one we translate "make follow":

> [ayah:77:17] ثُمَّ نُتْبِعُهُمُ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِينَ
>
> *Thumma nutbiʿuhum ul-ākhirīn* — **"Then We make the later ones follow them."**

The root is *t-b-ʿ*, to follow. But this is not the simple "follow." This is Form IV — *atbaʿa* — the causative: not "the later ones followed," but "We *make* the later ones follow." And the image inside the word is a **procession**. A line. One group walks the road to ruin, and then — *thumma*, "then," a word of sequence and order — the next group is set walking down the very same road, in the very same direction, to the very same end.

Picture it. *Al-awwalīn*, the former ones, and *al-ākhirīn*, the later ones — and notice Allah does not name a single one of them. He could have. The classical commentators fill in the names: al-Muyassar lists the people of Nuh, of ʿĀd, of Thamūd. Al-Qurtubī draws the line wider still — every denying nation from Ādam down to the time of Muhammad ﷺ. But the verse itself leaves the slots **empty**. *The former* and *the later.* Two open categories with no names in them.

And an empty slot is an invitation.

[PAUSE]

Read the absence as carefully as the presence. Why no names? Because the moment a slot is empty, every reader in every century quietly checks whether *he* fits in it. *The former... the later...* Well — relative to the destroyed nations of old, who exactly are *the later ones*? The Arabs hearing this were, themselves, "the later." And relative to them, *we* are the later ones. And relative to us, our children. The unnamed procession has no end written into it — which means the line is still moving, and you are standing somewhere in it.

Now — the final word. After three verses of historical machinery, the passage suddenly pivots, and everything changes.

Verse 18 named the destroyed as *al-mujrimīn* — **the criminals**, from *j-r-m*, those who cut themselves off, who commit the act, who *do the deed*. That is the language of action. Of crime. Of what shows on the surface.

But verse 19 — the refrain, the bell — does not say *woe to the criminals*. It changes the word:

> [ayah:77:19] وَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ
>
> *Waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibīn* — **"Woe, that Day, to the deniers."**

*Al-mukadhdhibīn.* From *k-dh-b*, to lie, to deny, to declare false. And notice — it is Form II, *kadhdhaba*, with the doubled, stressed middle letter. Form II here is **intensive**: not a single slip of doubt but the *repeated, deliberate, settled work* of calling the truth a lie, over and over, until denial becomes a way of life. You can almost hear it in the word's own mouth — that doubled, stammering *dh-dh* — the stutter of a man insisting, again and again, *it isn't true, it isn't true.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with the swap. The passage spent three verses describing the **criminals** — and then, at the climax, when it pronounces the woe, it changes the charge. Not "woe to the criminals." **"Woe to the deniers."**

Why?

Because Allah is reaching past the deed to the **root of the deed**. *Jurm*, the crime, is what you can see. *Takdhīb*, the denial, is what made the crime possible in the first place. And that is the discovery this whole passage was built to deliver. We will spend the rest of our time with it.

---

**So let us name what the words have shown us.** A question that makes you testify against yourself. A past tense that refuses to stay past, dropped instead into a living present that includes you. An unnamed procession with an open slot you may already be standing in. And a final, deliberate swap of words that moves the verdict from the *crime* to the *lie underneath it*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about grammar — but about the human heart, and about reality itself.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: "That Was Them, Not Us" — The Mind's Refusal to See Itself in the Line

Let me start not in Makkah but in something you already know intimately.

There is a thing the human mind does so constantly that we rarely notice it. Psychologists have a clumsy name for one version of it — the *optimism bias* — but you don't need the term. You already live it. You read the statistic: *this percentage of people who do X will suffer Y.* And some deep, automatic part of you, before you can even think, quietly files yourself in the exception. *Those people. Not me.*

You see the smoker who lived to ninety and you do not see the nine who didn't. You drive past the wreck on the highway and for thirty seconds you slow down, and then the road opens up and the feeling evaporates and you are, once again, the one who *won't* crash. You hear of the marriage that collapsed, the business that folded, the friend who got the diagnosis — and underneath your genuine sympathy there is a second, quieter voice that whispers: *that happens to other people.*

[PAUSE]

This is not stupidity. It is **armor**. The mind builds it to make life livable. If you walked around fully feeling, at every moment, that every catastrophe that has ever struck a human being could strike *you* — you would be paralyzed. So the mind invents a magic circle and draws it around the self. *Inside this circle, the rules that govern everyone else are suspended. In here, I am the exception.*

Now — bring that back to our passage, and watch what it is doing.

The people of Makkah had drawn exactly this circle. The destroyed nations were real to them; they passed the ruins. But the ruins lived *outside the circle.* "Those were ancient peoples. Foolish peoples. We are not like them. That was *them*."

And the verse does something surgical. It does not argue with the circle. It does not say *you are wrong to feel safe.* Instead — remember the grammar — it takes the present-tense verb, *nafʿalu*, "this is what We do," and the unnamed, open-ended procession, *the former... then the later...* — and it quietly **erases the line you drew around yourself.** It says: there is no magic circle. There is only the procession, and one road, and you are on it with everyone who ever walked it. *The former* were once sure they were the exception too. So were *the later.* The slot is open. Step into it or don't — but it is open, and it has your size.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to feel, because it transforms what looks like a verse of threat into a verse of mercy.

Why does Allah keep showing us the ruins? Why ten times in one surah? A cruel God would let us walk into the wall in the dark. He shows us the others who hit it precisely **so that we don't have to.** The procession of the destroyed is not displayed to frighten us for sport. It is displayed the way a parent shows a child the scar — *this is what the stove does; I am showing you so you never have to learn it the way I did.* The optimism bias whispers *you are the exception.* And the verse, gently and relentlessly, says: *no, beloved — but you have something the former ones squandered. You have their story. You can see the road before you walk it. They could not.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It is this. The next time you catch yourself reading the warning — any warning, in the Quran, in your own conscience, in the wreckage of someone else's life — and you feel that smooth automatic voice file you in the exception, *that's other people*: **stop, and recognize the voice.** That voice is the exact voice that ruined the former ones. Not their crimes. Their *exemption.* The single most spiritually dangerous sentence a human being can say is "*that doesn't apply to me.*" The whole of this passage exists to dismantle that one sentence. Tomorrow, when you hear it in yourself — and you will — answer it the way the verse does. *No. The slot is open. The line is still moving. I am in it.*

### Theme Two: Underneath Every Crime, a Lie You Told Yourself First

Now to the heart of it. The swap.

Remember: the passage describes the *criminals* — and then pronounces woe upon the *deniers*. The verdict moves from the deed to the denial beneath it. I told you we'd return to this. Here is why it matters more than almost anything else in how you live.

Let me start, again, where you already are.

Think honestly about the last genuinely wrong thing you did. Not a mistake — a *wrong*. Something you knew, somewhere, you should not do. Now reconstruct the moment just *before* you did it. I am willing to wager that in that moment, you did not say to yourself, *"I am now going to do something evil."* Almost no one does. That's not how it works.

What you did instead was tell yourself a small story. *It's not really that bad. Just this once. They deserve it. Everyone does this. No one will know. It doesn't count. I'll make up for it later. This is different.* A little narration. A small adjustment to reality. A **lie** — told not to anyone else, but to yourself — that re-labeled the wrong thing as somehow permissible, just for the length of time it took to do it.

[PAUSE]

That is *takdhīb*. That is the doubled, stressed *dh-dh* of *mukadhdhibīn* — the work of calling the false true and the true false. And here is the staggering thing the verse is telling you: **the crime was never the first event. The denial was.** No human being commits the *jurm* until he has first performed the *takdhīb*. The deed is downstream. The lie to yourself is the source.

This is why Allah, at the climax, refuses to say "woe to the criminals" and says instead "woe to the **deniers**." Because to punish only the crime would be to treat the symptom. The disease is the denial. *Al-mujrim*, the criminal, is what the *mukadhdhib*, the denier, becomes. You do not first do wrong and then deny the truth. **You first deny the truth, and the wrong walks out through the door denial opened.**

[PAUSE]

Let me build this one more layer, because there's a paradox here worth sitting in.

This means that the most dangerous sin is not, in a sense, any of the dramatic ones. It is the quiet, invisible, internal act of *re-describing reality to suit yourself.* No one sees it. It leaves no mark. It feels, while you do it, like simple honest thinking — *being reasonable*, even. And it is the root from which everything else grows. The Makkan denier did not begin as a monster. He began as a man who heard the truth and found it inconvenient, and so he performed one small operation on it: *it isn't true.* And he repeated the operation — Form II, intensive, again and again — until the operation became his character, and the character produced the crimes, and the crimes earned the woe.

And notice the terrible mercy hidden even here. *Takdhīb* — denial — is something you *do*. It is an act, not an accident. Which means it is something you can *stop*. You cannot always control the wrong impulse that rises in you. But you can refuse to do the lying that licenses it. You can catch the small story in your mouth — *it's not that bad, just this once* — and you can decline to tell it. You can say, instead, the hardest and most freeing words a person can say: *No. This is exactly as wrong as I know it is. I will not re-describe it.*

[PAUSE]

That is *taṣdīq* — affirming the truth — and it is the precise opposite of *takdhīb*, and it is the door out of the procession.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Watch your **inner narrator.** Not your deeds — those come later and you'll often lose those battles. Watch the small voice that, in the half-second before a wrong, quietly re-labels it. The voice that says *this is different, this doesn't count, just this once.* That voice is the *mukadhdhib* in you, doing its intensive, repetitive work. The whole spiritual battle, this passage tells you, is fought and won or lost **right there** — at the level of the lie, before the deed exists. Refuse the small lie, and the crime it was clearing the way for never gets built. Win the war at the source.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back now and see the whole shape, because the linguistic discoveries and the two themes turn out to be one single insight seen from different sides.

The **question** that makes you testify — *did We not?* — exists to break the armor of "that was them." The **present tense** that refuses to stay past exists to break the same armor — to drop the destruction out of "then" and into the living "now" that includes you. The **unnamed procession** with its open slot is the armor's final undoing — *the former, the later,* and somewhere in that open-ended line, you.

And all of that — the question, the tense, the procession — is aimed at exposing the one thing the closing word names: the **denial**. Because the armor of self-exemption *is itself a form of takdhīb.* When you file yourself in the exception — *that doesn't apply to me* — you are performing, in miniature, the exact operation that defines the *mukadhdhibīn.* You are re-describing reality to suit yourself. The optimism bias and the inner narrator are the same disease wearing two masks. One says *the rule doesn't apply to me.* The other says *this wrong isn't really wrong.* Both are *takdhīb.* Both are the small lie that grows into the woe.

So the two halves of the passage clasp together. *Don't exempt yourself from the procession* (Theme One) and *don't lie to yourself about your deeds* (Theme Two) are the same command: **stop calling the true false.** Stop performing *takdhīb*, in either direction. And the whole machinery of the verse — the question, the tense, the open slot, the climactic swap of words — is built to walk you to that one door.

### The Architecture

And now see how perfectly it is built. Four verses, and they descend like steps:

- Verse 16 — a **question**: *Did We not destroy the former?* (the past, made undeniable)
- Verse 17 — a **continuation**: *then the later follow.* (the pattern, made ongoing)
- Verse 18 — a **declaration**: *thus We do to the criminals.* (the law, made timeless and named by *deed*)
- Verse 19 — a **verdict**: *woe to the deniers.* (the sentence, and the charge moved from deed to *denial*)

From question, to pattern, to law, to verdict. From *did We not* to *woe.* And the final word, *al-mukadhdhibīn*, reaches all the way back and reinterprets the first: the reason you must answer *yes* to "did We not destroy?" is that the destroyed were, first and last, **deniers** — and denial is a door anyone can walk through, including the one reading these words tonight.

### Questions to Carry

- When you read a warning — in the Quran, in your own conscience, in someone else's collapse — can you feel the voice that quietly files you in the exception? Can you name it the next time it speaks?
- What is the small story you tell yourself in the half-second before you do something you know is wrong? Can you write it down, plainly, so you'll recognize it when it comes?
- If every crime is downstream of a denial — what truth are you currently re-describing, just slightly, to make your life more comfortable?
- The former ones could not see the road before they walked it. You can. What will you do with the sight they didn't have?
- *Takdhīb* is something you *do*, not something that happens to you. Where, specifically, could you replace it with *taṣdīq* — affirming the truth even when it costs you — this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Before any soul ever commits a wrong, it first quietly calls something true a lie — and the long, unnamed procession of the destroyed is Allah's standing question to every generation that imagines itself the exception.**

### The Closing Invitation

This passage is not, in the end, a threat. It is a mirror held up at exactly the angle where you can see the one thing you most carefully avoid looking at: the small lie at the root of everything. It is not asking you to be afraid. It is asking you to be *honest* — to step out of the magic circle, to take your place in the line with full eyes, and above all to refuse the quiet operation of re-describing reality. The woe falls on the deniers. The door out is simply to stop denying. That door is open right now, tonight, and it is exactly your size.

### A Du'a

*O Allah, You who made the ruins of the former ones a question we cannot escape — break in us the armor of "that was them, not us," and pull us out of the line of the deniers before the road runs out. Guard our inner tongue from the small lies that license the great wrongs, and make us of those who affirm the truth even when it costs us everything. Let us be, on that Day, not among the mukadhdhibīn upon whom the woe falls — but among those who said yes to You while saying yes was still a choice. Āmīn.*
