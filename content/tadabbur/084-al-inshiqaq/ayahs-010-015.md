# The Record Behind the Back, and the Return He Swore Would Never Come

## Introduction: Two Hands, Two Lives

Let me give you the words first, slowly.

> وَأَمَّا مَنْ أُوتِيَ كِتَٰبَهُۥ وَرَآءَ ظَهْرِهِۦ
> *Wa-ammā man ūtiya kitābahu warāʾa ẓahrih*
> **But as for the one who is handed his record from behind his back...**

> فَسَوْفَ يَدْعُوا۟ ثُبُورًا ۝ وَيَصْلَىٰ سَعِيرًا
> *Fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūrā, wa-yaṣlā saʿīrā*
> **...he will cry out for his own destruction, and burn in a Blaze.**

> إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ فِىٓ أَهْلِهِۦ مَسْرُورًا
> *Innahu kāna fī ahlihi masrūrā*
> **He had been, among his people, full of joy.**

> إِنَّهُۥ ظَنَّ أَن لَّن يَحُورَ
> *Innahu ẓanna an lan yaḥūr*
> **He was certain he would never return.**

> بَلَىٰٓ إِنَّ رَبَّهُۥ كَانَ بِهِۦ بَصِيرًا
> *Balā, inna rabbahu kāna bihi baṣīrā*
> **But yes — indeed his Lord had always been Seeing of him.**

### Rebuild the world

To feel these verses, you have to stand where they were first heard.

This is Mecca. Early. The Prophet ﷺ is reciting to people who can see, with their own eyes, that the world runs on cycles that always come back. The sun sets and returns. The moon wanes to nothing and returns. The rain falls, the seed dies in the ground, the green returns. Everything in their experience is a circle that closes.

And to people who live inside those returning circles, Allah makes a claim that strikes them as absurd: *you, too, will be brought back*. The body that goes into the ground like a seed — it returns. The account that you closed on yourself — it reopens.

The mockers had a stock response. You can hear it scattered through the Meccan surahs: *Who will bring us back once we're dust and bones?* It wasn't a question. It was a sneer. The whole architecture of their lives rested on one quiet, unexamined assumption — **that there is no coming back.** That the circle of a human life, alone among all the circles in creation, does not close.

This passage is the surah turning to that man and answering him. Not with an argument. With a scene. It shows you the exact moment his assumption is disproven — the moment the record is placed in his hand — and it shows you, in five swift strokes, the entire interior of the life that led him there.

### The hook

Here is what I need you to notice before we even begin, and then sit with for the next hour.

Just three verses earlier, in this same surah, there is another man. He is given his record too. But he gets it **in his right hand**, and he *returns* to his people *joyful* (84:7-9).

Two men. Both handed a book. Both have *people*. Both touched by the word *joy*.

And yet one of them is in Paradise and one of them is in the Fire.

The difference is not the book. The book is the same kind of book. The difference is **which direction the joy was facing, and which return he believed in.** [PAUSE]

Let's go and find it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Warāʾa Ẓahrih* — The Record That Comes From Behind

#### First, the everyday thing

Think about how you receive something you *want*. A gift. Good news. A letter you've been waiting for. Your hands come up. Your body turns toward it. You face it.

Now think about how something arrives when you've spent your whole life refusing to look at it. A bill you kept ignoring. A truth a friend kept trying to tell you and you kept changing the subject. When it finally lands, it doesn't come into your open hands. It comes at you sideways — from a direction you weren't facing. Because you were never facing it.

Hold that. Because Allah is about to use the geometry of a human body to say something the heart already knows.

#### Open the words

> أُوتِيَ كِتَٰبَهُۥ وَرَآءَ ظَهْرِهِۦ
> *He is given his record — behind his back.*

Look first at the verb: **أُوتِيَ** — *ūtiya*. From the root *a-t-y*. It is **passive**. Not *he takes* his record. Not *he reaches for* it. **He is given it.** It is brought to him; it is handed over; he is the one acted upon. The whole verse is built so that this man does nothing. Things are done *to* him now. The man who spent a lifetime taking what he wanted, reaching for what he pleased — at this moment he can only receive.

And then the location: **وَرَآءَ ظَهْرِهِۦ** — *warāʾa ẓahrih*. From two roots: *w-r-y*, behind, and *ẓ-h-r*, the back. Literally, *behind his back*.

Now — Arabic has a perfectly ordinary way to say "with his left hand." The surah could have said it; it almost expects it, because the right hand was named just verses ago. Instead it goes somewhere stranger. Not the left hand. **Behind the back.**

#### Let's sit with this

The classical commentators worked hard on the physical mechanics here, and they did not all agree — and the disagreement itself is instructive. Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī describe the right hand chained to the neck and the left placed behind the back, so the record is taken with the left, from behind. Ibn ʿAbbās, as reported by al-Qurṭubī, describes the right hand reaching out for the book first — by habit, by hope — and an angel wrenching it back, dislocating it, so the man takes the book with his left from behind his spine. Qatāda and Muqātil describe something even more violent: the chest opened, the hand passing through and emerging from the back.

Al-Ṭabarī then does something worth pausing on: he draws this verse together with the *other* place the Qur'an describes this same moment — the record handed *bi-shimālihi*, "in his left hand" (69:25). These are not two different fates, he notes, but one posture seen from two angles. The left hand named there *is* the hand pinned behind the spine here; sometimes the Qur'an describes the hand, sometimes the direction, and both point to a single scene. So this surah is not correcting the "left-hand" verses. It is showing you the bodily mechanics they leave unspoken — precisely *how* a hand comes to receive from behind.

Notice what *every one* of these pictures has in common, underneath their differences. The record does not arrive the natural way. It arrives **the way he lived.** [PAUSE]

Because here is the thing the Qur'an is doing with the back. In Arabic, and across the Qur'an, to put something *warāʾa ẓahrik* — behind your back — is an idiom for **rejection and contempt**. To turn your back on a thing is to be done with it. Allah says of people who were given scripture before: *they cast it behind their backs* (نَبَذُوهُ وَرَآءَ ظُهُورِهِمْ — 3:187). They had the Book in front of them — the guidance, the warning, the invitation — and they threw it behind themselves, into the one place a person never looks.

So watch what is happening on the Day this man is described.

He spent his life with the truth **behind his back** — refusing to turn, refusing to face it, keeping it in the blind spot where he would never have to deal with it. And on that Day, the record of that life is handed to him from **exactly the place he kept putting the truth.** [PAUSE]

He is not being given a punishment that is foreign to him. He is being handed *his own posture toward Allah*, made physical. The geometry of his judgment is the geometry of his life. He receives from behind because he lived turned away.

This is the principle running underneath the whole scene: the unseen reality — a heart that turned away from its Lord — could not be photographed. So Allah gives you a body. A hand that cannot reach the front. A book that comes from the spine. **The posture you cannot see in a soul, you can see in a back.**

What this single phrase reveals: *On that Day, no one will need to be told what they were. The way the record arrives will already say it.* [PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Masrūran* — The Joy That Was Pointed the Wrong Way

#### First, the everyday thing

There are two completely different things we both call "happiness."

There is the happiness of a man at a party who has stopped thinking about the test he has in the morning. And there is the happiness of a man walking out of the exam hall knowing he passed.

They feel similar in the moment. They are opposites in their nature. One is happiness *because* you've stopped looking ahead. The other is happiness *because* of what's ahead. One is bought by forgetting. The other is earned by facing.

Keep both in mind. The Qur'an is about to put them side by side and let one word carry both.

#### Open the word

> إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ فِىٓ أَهْلِهِۦ مَسْرُورًا
> *He had been, among his people, full of joy.*

**مَسْرُورًا** — *masrūr*. From the root *s-r-r*. It is a **passive participle** — not *one who makes joy*, but *one who has been made joyful*, one upon whom *surūr* has settled. And *surūr*, in the lexicon, is joy that fills the chest and shows on the face. It is not a small word. It is real delight.

And it is wrapped in the past tense: **كَانَ** — *kāna* — *he was, he used to be.* This was his ongoing state. Among his *ahl*, his people, his family, his circle — he was, settled and continuous, a happy man.

#### Now stay with me, because here is the architecture

Three verses earlier, the *other* man — the one with the record in his right hand — is described like this:

> وَيَنقَلِبُ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِۦ مَسْرُورًا (84:9)
> *And he returns to his people — joyful.*

**The same word.** *Masrūr.* **The same people** — *ahl*. Both men end the sentence on joy. Both men have a family.

So what on earth separates them? [PAUSE]

Look at the tense. Look at the direction.

The believer's joy is in the **present-future** — *yanqalib*, he *returns*, he is *moving toward* his people, and the joy is out ahead of him. His joy is something he is walking *into*.

The denier's joy is in the **past** — *kāna*, he *had been* joyful, among his people, back there, in the life that is now over. His joy is something he is walking *away from*. It is behind him now. [PAUSE]

And do you feel where it sits in the verse? His joy (84:13) comes *after* the destruction and the Fire (84:11-12). The order is deliberate and merciless. First you are shown him crying out for annihilation in the Blaze — *then* you are told, almost quietly, *he used to be so happy.* The happiness is recalled like an autopsy. *This is the man who had everything. This is what he was, before.*

His was the happiness of the man at the party who stopped thinking about the morning. It was real — and it was real *because* he had turned the truth to his back. It was joy bought by not looking. And the price of not looking comes due all at once.

The believer's was the other kind. The joy of the man walking out of the exam. It came *after* the looking, not instead of it.

So the word *masrūr* is not the verdict. **The direction the joy faces is the verdict.** One man's joy is his destination. The other man's joy was his anesthesia. [PAUSE]

What this reveals: *You cannot judge a life by whether it contained happiness. Both lives did. You judge it by whether the happiness was facing forward — toward the meeting with Allah — or facing backward, away from it.*

### Element Three: *Lan Yaḥūr* — The Return He Was Certain Would Never Happen

#### First, the everyday thing

There is a particular kind of mistake that is not a mistake of *not knowing*. It is a mistake of *being sure*. The man who is *certain* the bridge will hold. The investor who is *certain* the market only goes up. Their problem was never a gap in information. Their problem was a conviction sitting where a question should have been.

The Qur'an is about to locate this man's entire ruin in one such conviction.

#### Open the words

> إِنَّهُۥ ظَنَّ أَن لَّن يَحُورَ
> *Indeed, he was certain he would never return.*

Two words to slow down on.

**ظَنَّ** — *ẓanna*. We often translate it "he thought," but that is too weak here. *Ẓann* in Arabic spans from a passing guess all the way to firm conviction — and the context decides which. Here it is paired with **لَّن** — *lan* — the particle of **emphatic, total negation** of the future. *Lan yaḥūr* — *he will absolutely never, not ever, return.* You do not stake your entire life on a passing guess. This was not a man who *wondered* whether there was a return. This was a man who was **settled, certain, at peace** with there being none. His *ẓann* was his creed.

And then the word he was so sure about: **يَحُورَ** — *yaḥūr*. From the root *ḥ-w-r*. And this is a rare and beautiful choice.

*Ḥāra, yaḥūru* means **to return, to revert, to come back to a starting point** — but with a specific flavor. It is the word for something completing its cycle, turning back to where it began. The lexicons connect this root to the idea of going and *coming back round*. It is not just "return"; it is *the closing of the circle.*

And the mufassirūn are exact about *what* return he was denying. Al-Muyassar unfolds *lan yaḥūr* as *an lan yarjiʿa ilā khāliqihi ḥayyan lil-ḥisāb* — he was certain he would never go back **to his Creator, alive, to be held to account.** Not return in the abstract. Return to the One he came out from — standing, breathing, answerable. That is the exact circle he swore, of all the circles in creation, would never close.

#### Here is what is remarkable

Remember the world we built at the beginning? These people lived inside circles that *always close*. The sun returns. The moon returns. The seasons return.

And this man looked at the one circle that mattered most — *his own life, going out from his Lord and meant to come back to Him* — and declared: **this circle, alone, does not close. I will never come back round.** *Lan yaḥūr.*

He believed in every return in the universe except his own.

And now hold this against Element One. The believer, three verses up, *yanqalib* — he *turns back* (root *q-l-b*) to his people. He **returns**. The denier *ẓanna an lan yaḥūr* — he was certain he would **never return** (root *ḥ-w-r*).

**Two men. Two different verbs for "return."** One returns and is right to expect it. The other denied the return — and the very denial is what's being reported in a verse that *describes him having already returned.* He is being told, in the hereafter, *you were sure you'd never arrive here* — by the One speaking to him *because* he has arrived. The grammar itself refutes him. [PAUSE]

#### And then — the single most important word in the passage

> بَلَىٰٓ
> *Balā.*

One word. **"But yes."**

Arabic has two ways to say "yes." *Naʿam* agrees with whatever was just said. But **balā** — *balā* only exists to **overturn a negative.** It is the word you use when someone says *"it isn't so,"* and you answer *"oh, but it is."* It is contradiction. It is reversal. It is the universe correcting the record.

The man's whole life was one long sentence: *lan yaḥūr* — *I will never return.*

And Allah answers the entire life with one syllable: **balā.** *Oh, but you will.* [PAUSE]

Everything he built — the comfort, the family, the joy with his back to the truth — rested on a single load-bearing "no." And that "no" is removed with a single "yes," and the whole structure comes down.

And al-Muyassar unfolds what that one syllable carries inside it: *balā — sa-yuʿīduhu Allāhu kamā badaʾahu wa-yujāzīhi ʿalā aʿmālih* — **yes: Allah will bring him back exactly as He first originated him, and repay him for what he did.** Feel the argument folded into the reversal. The man's whole objection was *how could I possibly be brought back?* And the answer is: *the same way you were brought here the first time.* The One who opened the circle is the One who closes it. The proof of the second creation was the first one — sitting in plain view every single day he denied it.

What this reveals: *The most dangerous thing a person can carry is not a sin. It is a certainty — a quiet "this will never happen to me" — sitting unexamined where a question belongs. A whole life can be wrong in exactly one place, and that is enough.*

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us — and notice they are not three separate things. They are one thing seen three times.

A man kept the truth **behind his back** (Element One) because he was **certain there was no return** to answer for it (Element Three), and so he was able to be **completely happy** (Element Two) — a happiness that only worked as long as the back stayed turned and the certainty held.

Pull out the certainty, and the joy collapses and the back is forced to turn. *Balā.*

Now let's stop asking what these words reveal about *him*. Let's ask what they reveal about *us*. Because the terrifying thing about this man is not how different he is. It is how recognizable.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Anesthesia of a Comfortable Life

#### Start where you already live

Most of us are not going to deny the resurrection out loud. We say we believe in the Day of Judgment. We'd affirm it on a form.

But there is a way of *living* that says something the mouth never would.

Think about how a comfortable life actually works on the mind. When things are going well — the family is around you, the income is steady, the weekend is coming, the feed is scrolling — there is a kind of soft pressure that builds, almost without your noticing, that says: *this is the real thing. This, right here, is what life is.* The bills, the school run, the dinner plans — these feel *solid*. And the meeting with Allah starts to feel like a doctrine you hold rather than an event you're traveling toward.

That is exactly the state the Qur'an just diagnosed. **إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ فِىٓ أَهْلِهِۦ مَسْرُورًا** — *he was, among his people, full of joy.* Al-Muyassar glosses his state precisely: *masrūran maghrūran* — joyful **and deluded** — *lā yufakkiru fī al-ʿawāqib* — *he gave no thought to the consequences.* The delusion was not a wild belief. It was simply **not thinking about where this is all going.**

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Here is what is so unsettling. The verse does not accuse this man of cruelty. It does not say he was a tyrant or a thief. It says **he was happy with his family.**

The Qur'an is showing you that a life can look, from the inside, completely *fine* — warm, even blessed — and be heading exactly where this man is heading. The happiness is not evidence that the road is right. For this man, the happiness *was the road*. It was the very thing that kept his back turned.

Comfort is an anesthetic. And anesthetic is not evil — it is *blinding*. It lets you not feel the thing you would otherwise have to deal with. This man was given a long, soft anesthesia, and he mistook the absence of pain for the absence of a wound. [PAUSE]

#### Build it further

Now connect this to the believer in 84:9, who also ends up *masrūr*, joyful, with his *ahl*. Allah is not against joy. He is not against family. He is not telling you to be miserable so you'll be saved. **Both** men get joy and family. The Qur'an is doing something far more surgical.

It is asking: *what is your happiness resting on?*

The denier's joy rested on **forgetting**. Take away the forgetting and the joy dies — which is exactly what happens, *fa-sawfa yadʿū thubūrā*, now he cries out for his own annihilation. A joy that cannot survive remembrance of Allah was never joy. It was sedation.

The believer's joy rested on **the meeting itself**. It is joy that *grows* as the Day approaches, because the Day is what he was walking toward. Take away the forgetting and his joy gets *stronger*.

So the test of your happiness is brutally simple. **Does the thought of standing before Allah make your joy collapse — or make it deeper?** The first kind is anesthesia. The second kind is the real thing. [PAUSE]

#### The practical turn

So tomorrow, here is the one thing this theme asks of you. Not to abandon your comfortable life. To **introduce remembrance into it on purpose** — and watch what your joy does.

In the middle of the good evening with your family, the warm meal, the laughter — deliberately bring the meeting with Allah to mind. *I am traveling toward Him. This table is a gift on the road, not the destination.*

If that thought *darkens* the room, you have found your anesthesia, and now you know where the work is. If that thought makes you hold the people at the table a little more tenderly, a little more gratefully, knowing they too are travelers and this moment is a trust — then your joy is the believer's joy. It is facing forward.

The same dinner. The same family. The whole difference is which way the joy is pointed.

### Theme Two: Living as Though Unwatched — and the Eye That Never Closed

#### Start where you already live

There is research on what people do when they believe no one is watching versus when they think they might be. A famous study found that simply putting a poster of a pair of *eyes* above an honesty box made people pay far more for their coffee than a poster of flowers did. Not real eyes. A *picture* of eyes. The mere suggestion of being seen reorganized their behavior.

This tells you something about the human heart that the Qur'an has been telling you all along. **An enormous amount of what we do is downstream of one question we are constantly, silently answering: am I being seen right now?**

We are different people in private than in public. We say things in the group chat we would never say to a face. We let standards slip in the rooms no one walks into. Not because we are uniquely corrupt — because of this deep, wired assumption that *unwatched* means *unaccountable*.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now hear how this passage ends.

> بَلَىٰٓ إِنَّ رَبَّهُۥ كَانَ بِهِۦ بَصِيرًا
> *But yes — indeed his Lord had always been Seeing of him.*

**بَصِيرًا** — *baṣīr*. From the root *b-ṣ-r*, sight. And it is in the intensive form *faʿīl* — not merely *one who sees*, but *the One whose seeing is perfect, total, perpetual*.

And again the *kāna*: **كَانَ بِهِۦ بَصِيرًا** — *He had always been Seeing of him.* Al-Muyassar fills it in: *baṣīran ʿalīman bi-ḥālihi min yawmi khalaqahu* — *Seeing, fully aware of his condition, from the day He created him.*

Do you feel the devastation in that? [PAUSE]

This man's entire life ran on the assumption of the honesty-box room with no eyes. *No one is watching, so none of this is being counted.* That was his *lan yaḥūr* — *there is no return, no reckoning, no audience.*

And the passage closes by telling him: there were eyes the whole time. Not a poster of eyes. Not a suggestion of being seen. *His Lord.* Watching. Continuously. Perfectly. **From the day He made him.**

Every private room. Every turned back. Every joy bought by forgetting. Watched. The one assumption his whole life rested on — *no one sees* — was false from his first breath. [PAUSE]

#### Build it further

But stay with me, because *baṣīr* is not only a threat. Look at *whose* sight it is. **رَبَّهُۥ** — *his Lord.* Not "the Watcher." Not "the Judge." **His Lord** — *Rabb* — the One who *made* him, *raised* him, *sustained* him every second he spent denying Him.

So the same attribute that condemns the denier is the most comforting truth in the world to the one walking toward Allah.

If you are striving in a room no one sees — *He sees it.* The prayer no one knows about. The temptation you walked away from with no witness. The kindness you did that earned you nothing in this world. The tears at 3am that you told no one about. *Kāna bihi baṣīrā.* He has always been Seeing of you. Nothing good you have ever done in secret has gone into a void. It went into the sight of *your Lord.*

The denier feared *balā* — the reversal. But for the believer, that same all-seeing eye means **nothing is lost.** [PAUSE]

This is the mercy hidden inside the warning. The verse that terrifies the heedless is the exact verse that consoles the faithful. The attribute did not change. The relationship to it did.

#### The practical turn

So here is what this theme asks of you tomorrow. One private moment — pick it in advance.

Choose one act that no human being will ever see or reward: a *dhikr* under your breath in the car, a sadaqah you tell no one about, a wrong you quietly refuse in an empty room, an extra two units of prayer in the dark.

And do it *consciously addressing the One who sees it.* Not for anyone's eyes. For the only eyes that were ever really open. *Yā Baṣīr, You see this, and that is enough.*

Do that, and you have flipped the entire engine of this man's ruin. He lived as though unwatched and was destroyed by the eye he forgot. You will live *knowing* you are watched — and be carried by the very same eye.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now stand back and look at what Allah built across these verses, because the structure *is* the argument.

Surah Al-Inshiqaq gives you two men in two mirror-panels, and the mirror is exact:

| The Right Hand (84:7-9) | Behind the Back (84:10-15) |
|---|---|
| Given his record **in his right hand** | Given his record **from behind his back** |
| An **easy** reckoning | Cries out for **destruction**, burns in a Blaze |
| **Returns** (*yanqalib*) to his people | Was **certain he'd never return** (*lan yaḥūr*) |
| **Joyful** (*masrūr*) — facing forward | **Had been joyful** (*masrūr*) — now behind him |

Look at it. **Both** are handed a book. **Both** have *ahl*. **Both** are touched by *masrūr*. **Both** lives revolve around a "return."

And every single shared element is *inverted across time*. The believer's reckoning, return, and joy are all out ahead of him — his best is coming. The denier's joy is all *behind* him, his return is one he *denied*, and his reckoning, when it comes, is annihilation.

The two panels are the same four words arranged into Heaven and into Hell. The difference between the two men was never the *ingredients* of their lives — both had a book, a family, joy, a return. **The difference was which way the whole life was facing.** One faced his Lord. One kept Him behind his back. [PAUSE]

That is the proof, written in the architecture itself: *you and the person beside you may have identical lives — same book, same family, same joys — and be walking in opposite directions.*

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes are one insight from two doors.

The words showed us a man who kept the truth *behind his back*, was *certain there was no return*, and bought a *joy* that depended on both — until *balā* tore the certainty out and the all-*Seeing* Lord he forgot turned out to have watched it all.

The themes showed us that this man is not a stranger. He is what any comfortable, distracted, privately-lived life becomes when its joy is anesthesia and its working assumption is *no one is watching*.

The whole passage is one question pressed on the heart: **which way is your life facing?** Toward the meeting — so that remembrance deepens your joy and the all-Seeing eye is your comfort? Or away from it — so that remembrance darkens the room and the eye is a threat you keep behind your back?

### Questions to carry

1. Is there a truth about my life, my deeds, or my death that I have quietly placed *behind my back* — kept in the blind spot precisely so I never have to face it?
2. When I deliberately bring the meeting with Allah to mind in the middle of my happiest moments — does my joy collapse, or does it deepen? What does the answer tell me about what my joy is resting on?
3. Where is my own *lan yaḥūr* — the unexamined certainty, the quiet "that will never happen to me," sitting where an honest question should be?
4. Who am I in the rooms with no human eyes — and would I want *that* version of me handed back to me on the Day?
5. If the all-Seeing eye is watching either way, am I living as though that is my terror, or my refuge?

### One-sentence distillation

*Two people can hold the same book, the same family, and the same joy — and be bound for opposite ends, because the only thing that finally matters is whether the whole life was facing the return or keeping it behind its back.*

### The closing invitation

You don't have to overhaul your life tonight. You have to *turn it around* — and turning is a small motion. It is the difference between *yanqalib* and *lan yaḥūr*, and it happens in the heart before it happens anywhere else.

So let one thing turn. Take one joy and point it forward, toward Him. Take one private act and offer it to the eye that never closed. That is the whole motion of this surah — the face that was turned away, turning back. *Balā* — yes, you are returning to Him. The only question this passage leaves open is whether you'll spend the rest of the road facing the right way.

### A closing du'a

*O Allah, You who have been Seeing of me since the day You made me — do not let me be one who keeps Your truth behind his back. Turn my face toward You. Make remembrance of meeting You deepen my joy and not darken it, and let the eye that has watched my every private moment be my comfort and not my dread.*

*And when my record is placed in my hand, O Most Merciful — let it come to my right, and let me return to my people rejoicing. Āmīn.*
