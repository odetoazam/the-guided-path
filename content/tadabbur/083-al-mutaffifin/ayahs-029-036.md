# The Laughter That Turned

## Introduction: Standing in the Marketplace

Let me set you down in a place before we open a single word.

You are in Makkah, somewhere around the early years of the message. You are not one of the great names of Quraysh. You are not the son of a chief. Perhaps you are a freed slave. Perhaps you are poor. Perhaps your name is Bilāl, or ʿAmmār, or Khabbāb, or Ṣuhayb — and the classical commentators tell us these were precisely the kinds of people these verses were spoken about. Al-Jalalayn, glossing the very first line, names them without hesitation: the criminals are *the likes of Abū Jahl,* and the ones they mock are *the likes of ʿAmmār and Bilāl* — **the believers of low status, the ones the city looked down on.**

You walk through the marketplace. And as you pass, you hear it.

A small sound. A snort of laughter. The corner of an eye that catches another corner of an eye. A nudge. The men of standing — the Walīd ibn al-Mughīras, the Abū Jahls of the city, men with money and lineage and the ear of the whole town — they see you coming, and something passes between them. A private joke. **You are the joke.**

And then they go home. And at their tables, with their families, the day's entertainment is told and retold: *Did you see them today? The ones who follow Muhammad? Did you see how they pray, how they walk, what they believe?* And the table laughs. It's good material. It's the kind of thing you savor over a meal.

[PAUSE]

Now — I want you to feel something honestly. Being argued with is one thing. Being hated is one thing. But being **laughed at** is a particular kind of wound. It tells you that you are not even worth taking seriously. That your deepest convictions are a punchline. That when you turn your back, you become a story other people tell to feel superior over dinner.

The Quran does not look away from this. It does something almost no scripture does: it walks into the marketplace, watches the mockery happen in slow motion, photographs every micro-gesture of it — and then it tells you exactly what happens to that laughter.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. **Where does laughter go?** When someone laughs at the truth — at a believer, at a sincerity they don't understand — that laughter feels, in the moment, like it won. It feels powerful. So where does it go? Does it just evaporate? Or is it kept somewhere?

Stay with that. Because the answer is the architecture of this entire passage.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: Two Verbs, One Form, Opposite Destinies

Before we touch the ayah, a small lesson in how Arabic verbs are built — because the very first two verbs of this passage are doing something you can't see in translation.

Arabic verbs come in patterns, called *forms*, numbered I through X. Form I is the plain root. The higher forms add layers of meaning — they can intensify, they can make something happen, they can turn an action back on the doer. Think of it like this: in English, "to be safe" and "to make safe" and "to become safe" are different ideas built from the same word *safe*. Arabic builds those shades right into the shape of the verb.

Now look at how this passage opens:

> **إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا۟**
> *Inna alladhīna ajramū* — "Indeed, those who committed crimes..."

The verb **أَجْرَمُوا** (*ajramū*) is built on the root **j-r-m**, and it is in **Form IV**. The root meaning has to do with cutting off, severing — and from it comes *jarīma*, a crime. Form IV here means something like *to commit oneself to crime, to make oneself a criminal.* It's not an accident that happened to them. It's a thing they entered into, took on, became.

Now hold that, and look at who they are laughing at:

> **مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟**
> *mina alladhīna āmanū* — "...at those who believed."

The verb **ءَامَنُوا** (*āmanū*), "they believed," is built on the root **ʾ-m-n** — and it is *also* **Form IV**.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The same grammatical shape. Two verbs, side by side, in the identical form — and they name the two poles of all humanity.

And here is what the root **ʾ-m-n** actually means. It is the root of *amn* — **safety, security, peace.** To believe, in Arabic, is not first about agreeing with a proposition. It is to *enter into security.* The believer is one who has made it inside, who has found the place of safety. And **ʾamāna**, trustworthiness, comes from the same root — the believer is someone you can be safe *around.*

So now read the opening again with new eyes. The ones who **severed** themselves — *ajramū*, the root of cutting off — are laughing at the ones who have **come home to safety** — *āmanū*, the root of peace.

The criminal thinks he is laughing down at someone beneath him. The grammar quietly tells you the opposite is true. **One group cut themselves off from the source of all security. The other group walked into it.** And the one outside in the cold is laughing at the one inside by the fire, certain he has the better position.

Let that sit. The very first line, before any reward or punishment is mentioned, has already drawn the real map.

[PAUSE]

And one more thing, easy to miss. Listen to the tense:

> **كَانُوا۟ ... يَضْحَكُونَ**
> *kānū ... yaḍḥakūn* — literally, "they *were* laughing," "they *used to* laugh."

In Arabic, when you pair *kāna* ("was/used to be") with a verb in the ongoing present tense (*yaḍḥakūn*, "laughing"), you create what grammarians call the habitual past. It does not describe one laugh on one day. It describes **a settled habit. A way of life.** This wasn't a moment of weakness. Mockery was their *practice.* They got up in the morning and this is what they did. It was, you might say, their full-time occupation.

Hold onto that word — *habit* — because the surah is going to answer it with a habit of its own.

## Element Two: The Anatomy of a Sneer

The Quran now does something remarkable. It slows the mockery down and films it, gesture by gesture, in four frames. And the verbs it chooses are surgically precise.

**Frame one — the passing:**

> [ayah:83:30] وَإِذَا مَرُّوا۟ بِهِمْ يَتَغَامَزُونَ
> *wa-idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūn* — "And when they passed by them, they would wink and nudge at one another."

The verb is **يَتَغَامَزُونَ** (*yataghāmazūn*), from the root **gh-m-z** — which means to wink, to signal with the eye or the eyebrow, to nudge. But notice the shape it's in. This is **Form VI**.

Here's the lesson. Form VI in Arabic — the pattern *tafāʿala* — carries the meaning of **mutual, reciprocal action.** When two people *do something to each other,* you reach for Form VI. *Taqātala* — they fought one another. *Tashāwara* — they consulted with one another. So *yataghāmazūn* is not one person winking. It is **a circle of people exchanging signals about you as you walk past.** The wink that lands and is returned. The nudge that travels around the group.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how exact that is? Mockery is almost never solitary. It is a **social performance.** Its entire pleasure depends on being shared. The sneer needs an audience; the nudge needs someone to nudge back. The Quran did not say "they despised them" — a private feeling. It chose the one verb form in the entire language that means *they did this to one another, as a group, about you.* The reciprocal form captures the conspiracy of it. The bonding of it. The way contempt becomes the glue that holds the in-crowd together.

**Frame two — going home:**

> [ayah:83:31] وَإِذَا ٱنقَلَبُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِمُ ٱنقَلَبُوا۟ فَكِهِينَ
> *wa-idhā-nqalabū ilā ahlihimu-nqalabū fakihīn* — "And when they returned to their own people, they would return relishing it."

The word to hold is **فَكِهِينَ** (*fakihīn*). Its root, **f-k-h**, is the same root as **fākiha** — *fruit.* From it the Arabs built the idea of *relishing, savoring, delighting* — the way you savor sweet fruit after a meal, the way good company and good jokes are the dessert of an evening.

[PAUSE]

So look at what the verse is telling you. The mockery of the believers was not just a passing cruelty in the street. It was **brought home as a delicacy.** It was the family entertainment. They returned to their households *fakihīn* — plump with the pleasure of it, savoring the day's ridicule like fruit at the table. The contempt was so enjoyable it became *food.* It nourished their evenings.

There is something deeply unsettling here, and the Quran wants you to feel it. **Cruelty can be delicious.** Looking down on someone can taste sweet. The verse refuses to let the mockers off as merely thoughtless — it shows them *enjoying* it, taking it home, feeding on it. This is contempt as comfort food.

And there is a genuine variation worth knowing here. The early reciters did not all read this word the same way: some read it *fakihīn,* others *fākihīn,* with a long *ā* — and al-Tabari's own text carries the second reading. The two are close cousins. *Fakih* leans toward *relishing, savoring;* *fākih* leans toward *being delighted, pleased with oneself* — and al-Jalalayn glosses this reading precisely as *muʿjabīn,* self-satisfied, tickled by their own talk of the believers. Either way the picture holds and even deepens: they did not merely enjoy the mockery, they came home *pleased with themselves* for it. The ridicule had become a source of self-congratulation, served at the table like the evening's best dish.

**Frame three — the verdict:**

> [ayah:83:32] وَإِذَا رَأَوْهُمْ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ لَضَآلُّونَ
> *wa-idhā raʾawhum qālū inna hāʾulāʾi la-ḍāllūn* — "And when they saw them, they would say, 'These people are truly lost.'"

And here the mockery puts on a respectable face. Notice how the sentence is built: **إِنَّ ... لَ** — *inna* ("indeed") plus the emphatic *lām* on **لَضَآلُّونَ** (*la-ḍāllūn*, "truly astray"). In Arabic, this is the construction of **heavy, confident assertion.** It's the grammar you use when you are not guessing — when you are pronouncing a *verdict.*

So the snickering of the street now dresses itself up as sober judgment. *These people? Lost. Misguided. Off the path.* The same root, **ḍ-l-l**, that the Quran uses for genuine spiritual ruin — they fling it, with full grammatical force, at the very people who had found the way home (*āmanū* — remember the root: safety). **The ones who are severed are issuing diagnoses of lostness against the ones who arrived.**

And the tafsir is exact about the charge. When al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar unpack what the mockers meant by *ḍāllūn,* it is not a vague insult — they meant the believers were lost *precisely because they followed Muhammad ﷺ.* The one thing the believers had gotten right was, in the mouths of the powerful, the very proof that they had gone astray. Following the Messenger *was,* to them, the madness. This is the oldest inversion there is: the single clear-sighted act in a blind city gets rebranded as the blindness itself.

## Element Three: "You Were Never Appointed"

And now comes the line that, for me, is the quiet thunderclap of the whole passage. After three frames of mockery, the voice of the verse shifts — turns aside, almost like an aside in a play — and says:

> [ayah:83:33] وَمَآ أُرْسِلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ حَـٰفِظِينَ
> *wa-mā ursilū ʿalayhim ḥāfiẓīn* — "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them."

Let's open this slowly, because the power is in what's *missing.*

**أُرْسِلُوا** (*ursilū*) is a passive verb — "they were sent." Passive, in Arabic, when the doer is left unnamed, almost always points to **Allah.** *Sent — by whom?* The sentence makes you ask. And the answer hovers: *no one sent them.*

**حَـٰفِظِينَ** (*ḥāfiẓīn*) is from **ḥ-f-ẓ** — to guard, to keep watch over, to be a custodian. A *ḥāfiẓ* is one charged with protecting and overseeing.

So the line reads: *They were never sent as overseers, custodians, judges set in authority over the believers.*

The commentators split, gently, on *what* they were not sent to guard. Al-Jalalayn offers two readings side by side: they were appointed as keepers neither *over the believers themselves* nor *over the believers' deeds* — not made custodians charged with steering them toward their own welfare. Neither the persons nor the record was ever placed in their hands. And that widens the indictment: the self-appointment was total. They claimed authority over people they had no charge to protect *and* over deeds they had no commission to audit.

[PAUSE]

Do you hear the question buried inside the statement? **Who appointed you?**

Where did these men get the authority to issue verdicts on other people's souls? To declare *these are lost*? To rank, to judge, to assign people to misguidance? **No one gave it to them.** They simply *took* it. They appointed themselves the watchmen of everyone else's guidance — a commission no one ever issued, a post they invented to give their contempt the dignity of judgment.

And this exposes something at the root of all mockery — then and now. The mocker is always, secretly, *claiming a throne.* To laugh down at someone is to position yourself above them, as if you were the appointed assessor of their worth. The Quran walks up to that throne and says, in five words: *You were never sent. This seat isn't yours. Who told you that you were the guardian over anyone's heart?*

Read absence carefully. The verse doesn't even bother to refute their verdict that the believers are "lost." It refutes their **standing to make any verdict at all.** It dismisses the judge before examining the judgment.

[PAUSE]

We've watched the grammar draw a map the mockers couldn't see — the severed laughing at the safe. We've watched four frames of contempt filmed in precise verbs: the reciprocal wink, the savored cruelty, the dressed-up verdict, the stolen authority.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Why We Laugh in Groups

Let me start where you actually live.

You have seen this. Maybe you've been on the inside of it, maybe the outside, maybe both. There is a group chat. Someone screenshots a person who was earnest about something — a sincere post, an unfashionable belief, a person trying hard at something — and the screenshot gets dropped into the chat with three laughing emojis. And then everyone piles on. And for a few minutes, the group feels *wonderful.* Bonded. Sharp. Superior. The earnest person, somewhere out there, has no idea they have just been served as the evening's entertainment.

Now notice what the Quran already told you about this, fourteen centuries early. The reciprocal verb — *yataghāmazūn* — "they wink at one another." The mockery wasn't really *about* the believers. It was the social cement *between the mockers.* And then *fakihīn* — they took it home and savored it. **The pile-on is comfort food for the in-group.** It tells the members: *we are the ones with taste, with standing, with the right read on things. And they — they are the punchline.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what's worth sitting with. Why is this such a universal human reflex? Because contempt is the cheapest possible way to feel elevated. You don't have to *become* anything to laugh down at someone. You don't have to build, or sacrifice, or improve. You simply locate someone you can place beneath you, and the act of placing them there does the work. It's a counterfeit of greatness. Real elevation costs everything; mocking elevation is free.

And that's exactly why the Quran's diagnosis is so merciless about *who these men were.* They were the chiefs. The wealthy. The ones with lineage. By every worldly measure they were *already* on top. So why did they need to laugh at freed slaves and the poor? Because worldly height is never enough to settle the heart. The man on top still needs someone beneath him to confirm that the top is real. **The compulsion to look down is itself the confession of an emptiness that being on top never filled.**

Now turn it on yourself — gently, because this is the work. *When do I reach for contempt?* When do I find that little hit of superiority in noticing how someone else is wrong, behind, cringe, lost? The Quran is not only describing Abū Jahl. It is describing a muscle that lives in every one of us — the muscle that wants to be the one nudging, never the one walked past.

**What changes tomorrow:** The next time the group chat lights up around a target — the next time you feel that warm pull of shared contempt — pause and ask one question. *Who appointed me?* That single question, lifted straight out of verse 33, breaks the spell. Because the honest answer is always: *no one.* I was never sent as a guardian over this person's worth. The seat I'm about to sit in isn't mine.

## Theme Two: Where the Laughter Goes

Now we return to the question I asked you at the very beginning, standing in the marketplace. *Where does laughter go?*

Here is the answer the surah has been building toward:

> [ayah:83:34] فَٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلْكُفَّارِ يَضْحَكُونَ
> *fa-l-yawma alladhīna āmanū mina-l-kuffāri yaḍḥakūn* — "So today, those who believed are laughing at the disbelievers."

[PAUSE]

Read verse 29 and verse 34 next to each other. I want you to actually see them touch.

**Verse 29:** *the criminals* — laughing at — *those who believed.* The verb: **يَضْحَكُونَ** (*yaḍḥakūn*).

**Verse 34:** *those who believed* — laughing at — *the disbelievers.* The verb: **يَضْحَكُونَ** (*yaḍḥakūn*).

**The exact same verb. The subjects swapped.** This is not a coincidence of vocabulary. This is divine architecture. The surah takes the precise word the mockers spent their lives performing — *they used to laugh* — and hands it, unchanged, to the very people they laughed at. The laughter did not evaporate. **It was kept.** It was held in trust, and on the Day it is returned to its rightful owners.

This is what the Quran means by justice. Not merely that the wrong is punished — but that the *exact shape* of the wrong is answered. They laughed; so there is laughter on the Day. They appointed themselves watchers who *looked down*; and look what the believers are now doing:

> [ayah:83:35] عَلَى ٱلْأَرَآئِكِ يَنظُرُونَ
> *ʿalā-l-arāʾiki yanẓurūn* — "Upon adorned couches, looking on."

The **أَرَآئِك** (*arāʾik*) are the canopied couches — al-Tabari calls them *the curtained thrones set within the bridal canopies,* the seats of the honored and the elevated. And the verb **يَنظُرُونَ** (*yanẓurūn*) means "they look, they gaze, they observe." Remember who used to do the looking-down in the marketplace — the chiefs, glancing sideways at the poor. **Now the gaze is reversed.** The ones who were looked down upon are *raised up,* reclining on thrones, and it is *they* who now look. The whole geometry of contempt — who is high, who is low, who looks, who is looked at — is turned completely over.

And here the classical commentators open a beautiful disagreement — one worth carrying, because both answers are faithful to the words. *What* are the believers looking at from those thrones? Al-Tabari and al-Jalalayn read it one way: they look *down* — from their dwellings upon the disbelievers being punished in the Fire — and they laugh at them, in al-Jalalayn's own phrasing, *just as the disbelievers laughed at them in the world.* The gaze that once slid sideways in the marketplace now falls from thrones, and the mockery is answered in its exact coin. Al-Muyassar reads it another way: the believers gaze upon *the honor and bliss Allah has given them,* and the greatest object of that gaze, he says, is **the Noble Face of Allah Himself.** Hold both, because together they are the whole reversal. In the first, it is *completed* — the looked-upon now look. In the second, it is *transcended* — eyes that were once pinned to the ground under the weight of contempt are lifted to the only Face worth seeing. The lowest gaze in Makkah becomes the highest gaze in creation.

[PAUSE]

And then the surah ends not with a statement, but with a question — and I want you to hear who is asking it:

> [ayah:83:36] هَلْ ثُوِّبَ ٱلْكُفَّارُ مَا كَانُوا۟ يَفْعَلُونَ
> *hal thuwwiba-l-kuffāru mā kānū yafʿalūn* — "Have the disbelievers been repaid for what they used to do?"

The verb **ثُوِّبَ** (*thuwwiba*) comes from the root **th-w-b**, the root of **thawāb** — *reward.* It is the word you use for *recompense, the wage you've earned, the reward you are paid.* And here is the bitter blade of it: the Quran uses the word for **reward** to describe **punishment.** This is what the rhetoricians call irony of the sharpest kind. *Have they been... rewarded... for what they used to do?* The mockery they savored as fruit (*fakihīn*) is served back to them as their *wage* — and called, with terrible precision, their *reward.*

[PAUSE]

And notice the final clause: **مَا كَانُوا۟ يَفْعَلُونَ** — "what they *used to do.*" There is the habitual past again, just like verse 29's *kānū yaḍḥakūn.* The surah opened by telling you mockery was their *habit, their full-time occupation* — and it closes by telling you the recompense matches the habit exactly. **What you do habitually is what you are repaid for habitually.** Nothing is lost in the accounting. The wink that traveled around the circle, the joke told at the dinner table, the verdict pronounced with false confidence — all of it kept, itemized, and returned.

Now — who asks the closing question? The classical commentators note that this is spoken *to the believers on their thrones.* It is the question you ask when justice has finally, fully arrived: *Well? Have they been repaid?* And here the mufassirūn do something striking: they refuse to leave the question rhetorical. Al-Jalalayn answers it with a single word — *Naʿam,* **Yes.** Al-Muyassar spells it out: yes, they have been recompensed *jazāʾan wifāqan* — a repayment that corresponds *exactly,* measure for measure, to what they used to do. The verse asks; the tradition answers it for you, so the answer hangs in the air, complete. **Yes. They have. Look.**

**What changes tomorrow:** This is the verse for the moment you are the one being laughed at. The moment your sincerity is someone's entertainment, your faith someone's punchline, your effort someone's screenshot. The world tells you that the laughter has *won* — that it's powerful, that it gets the last word. The surah tells you something else: **the laughter is being kept.** It does not evaporate. Not one nudge is lost. You are not required to win the argument in the marketplace, to laugh back, to defend your dignity in the moment. Your dignity is held in trust by the One who was watching the whole time. *He* was the only One ever appointed as the Guardian. Your job is only to remain among *alladhīna āmanū* — the ones who came home to safety.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole shape, because the surah is built like a mirror, and you can only see it now that we've walked through it.

- **Verse 29:** the criminals **laugh** (*yaḍḥakūn*) at the believers.
- **Verses 30–32:** the *mechanics* of their contempt — the reciprocal **wink**, the savored **homecoming**, the false **verdict**.
- **Verse 33:** the pivot — *you were never appointed.* The stolen throne of judgment is named and dismissed.
- **Verse 34:** the believers **laugh** (*yaḍḥakūn* — the same verb) at the disbelievers.
- **Verses 35–36:** the *mechanics* of their vindication — the **thrones**, the reversed **gaze**, the question, *have they been repaid?*

The first half and the second half are the same scene, photographed twice — once on earth, once on the Day — with the roles exchanged and the verb held constant. The pivot in the middle, verse 33, is the hinge the whole mirror turns on: *the seat of judgment was never theirs.* It belonged to Allah. And on the Day, He sits the wronged upon thrones and lets them see the verdict delivered.

## Two Discoveries, One Truth

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same insight approached from two sides.

The **grammar** told us, in the opening line, that the map was always inverted: *ajramū* (the severed) were laughing at *āmanū* (those who came home to safety) — both Form IV, side by side, opposite destinies. The mockers misread reality from the start.

The **themes** told us *why* humans misread it that way — contempt is the cheap counterfeit of greatness, the in-group's comfort food — and what corrects it: the laughter is *kept,* the gaze is *reversed,* the recompense *exactly matches the habit.*

Both arrive at one place: **Reality is not what the laughter in the marketplace says it is. The one who looks down is not above. The one looked down upon is not below. And the only One ever sent as Guardian over the human heart has been keeping the account the entire time.**

## Questions to Carry

1. When I feel that warm pull of shared laughter at someone's expense — what emptiness in me is it filling?
2. Whose worth have I quietly appointed myself the judge of, when no one ever sent me as a guardian?
3. When *I* am the one being laughed at for my faith — do I believe the laughter has won, or do I believe it is being kept?
4. Do I take contempt home and savor it — *fakihīn* — replaying someone's humiliation for my own pleasure?
5. If my habits were itemized and returned to me exactly — *mā kānū yafʿalūn* — what would I be repaid for?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The laughter the powerful spent on the faithful was never lost — it was kept in trust, and on the Day it is returned to its rightful owners, because the only One ever appointed to judge the human heart was watching the whole time.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to win the marketplace. You do not have to laugh back, or prove your dignity to people who have made it their entertainment to deny it. The invitation of these verses is to set the burden down — to stop guarding a reputation that Allah is already guarding, and to stop appointing yourself the watchman over hearts you were never sent to oversee. Let the laughter go where it goes. It is being kept. Your only task is to stay among those who came home to safety.

## A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, the only One ever sent as Guardian over our hearts — keep us among *alladhīna āmanū,* those who came home to the safety of Your nearness. Guard our tongues from the laughter that wounds, and guard our hearts from needing to look down on anyone to feel raised up. And when we are the ones laughed at for loving You, let it be enough for us that You are watching. Āmīn.
