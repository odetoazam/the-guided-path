# The Forgetful One: When Man Doubts His Own Beginning

## Surah Maryam 19:66-67

> وَيَقُولُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ أَءِذَا مَا مِتُّ لَسَوْفَ أُخْرَجُ حَيًّا
>
> أَوَلَا يَذْكُرُ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنُ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَـٰهُ مِن قَبْلُ وَلَمْ يَكُ شَيْـًٔا

*Wa-yaqūlu l-insānu a-idhā mā mittu la-sawfa ukhraju ḥayyā. A-wa-lā yadhkuru l-insānu annā khalaqnāhu min qablu wa-lam yaku shay'ā.*

**"And man says: 'When I am dead, will I really be brought forth alive?' Does man not remember that We created him before, when he was nothing at all?"**

---

## Introduction: Where We Are in the Surah

Stay with me for a moment, because the placement of these two ayahs is not accidental.

Surah Maryam has just walked us through one of the most extraordinary galleries in the entire Quran. We have seen Zakariyyā in his old age, his bones grown weak, his hair turning to flame with white. We have seen Yaḥyā given wisdom as a child. We have seen Maryam under the date palm, and ʿĪsā speaking from the cradle. We have seen Ibrāhīm arguing tenderly with his father. We have seen Mūsā called near. We have seen Ismāʿīl who was true to his promise. We have seen Idrīs raised to a high station.

And then, just before our two ayahs, the surah delivers a warning: that after these prophets came generations who lost the prayer and followed their desires.

[PAUSE]

Now imagine you are listening to this surah for the first time. Your heart is full from the procession of the chosen. You can almost smell the desert air, almost feel the tremble in Zakariyyā's voice as he prays for a son. And then the surah turns the camera, slowly, away from the lines of light — and points it at you.

At humanity itself.

And asks: *what is the thing al-insān keeps saying?*

What is the line — the doubt — that runs underneath every generation, no matter how many prophets are sent?

The answer comes in one short, almost casual sentence. And it is the deepest disease of the human heart.

**The hook:** Notice that Allah does not respond to this doubt with a thunderbolt. He does not respond with cosmic argument. He responds with one word — *remember* — and points to a fact so close, so personal, so already-inside-you, that the doubt evaporates the moment you actually look at it.

What is that fact? And why does Allah call it *remembering* rather than *thinking*?

Let's open these words.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yaqūlu* — The Tense of "Keeps Saying"

Open your hand and let me show you something small.

In English, when we want to say what a person said, we usually use the past tense. *He said. She said.* Past, finished, located in time.

Arabic has past tense too. It would have been the most natural thing in the world for Allah to say *qāla l-insānu* — "Man said." If you have ever taken even a beginner's Arabic class, you know that *qāla* is one of the first verbs you ever learn. It is the verb of every story. The Quran uses it thousands of times.

But Allah does not say *qāla* here.

He says **يَقُولُ** — *yaqūlu*.

This is the *muḍāriʿ* — the present-imperfect tense. It does not mean "he said." It means *"he says"* — and in Arabic, this form carries something English struggles to capture: **the sense of an ongoing, habitual, repeating action**. Something that is happening, and keeps happening, and shows no sign of stopping.

Let me give you an everyday example. If I tell you, "My neighbor *complained* about the noise," you imagine one event. He complained, it ended. But if I tell you, "My neighbor *complains* about the noise" — now you understand something different. He has complained before. He is complaining now. He will complain again. The complaint is not an event. It is a *condition*.

That is what *yaqūlu* does here.

[PAUSE]

Allah is not telling us about one man, in one century, who once expressed doubt about resurrection. Allah is telling us about the **chronic voice** in the human chest. The thing that keeps surfacing. The doubt that, no matter how many times it is answered, finds its way back to the lips again.

Notice something else. The verb's subject is **الْإِنسَانُ** — *al-insān*, with the definite article. Not *kāfir* (disbeliever). Not *mushrik* (associator). Not even *insān* indefinite ("a human"). It is *al-insān* — **the human**, generic, universal.

This is humanity speaking. This is *us*.

And the tense is *yaqūlu* — and we keep saying it.

Here's what's remarkable: this little word, *yaqūlu*, just by being chosen instead of *qāla*, shifts the entire frame of the ayah. We are not reading about an ancient denier. We are reading a diagnosis of ourselves.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *A-idhā mā... la-sawfa* — The Architecture of Incredulity

Now look at how the doubt is constructed. Don't worry about the grammar terms. Just feel the weight.

> **أَءِذَا مَا مِتُّ لَسَوْفَ أُخْرَجُ حَيًّا**
>
> *A-idhā mā mittu la-sawfa ukhraju ḥayyā*

Word by word, the listener encounters a stacking of particles:

- **أَ** (*a*) — the question marker. A raised eyebrow.
- **إِذَا** (*idhā*) — "when." A particle of time.
- **مَا** (*mā*) — here, an emphatic addition. It does not carry meaning by itself. It is poured onto *idhā* like extra weight, the way you might say "*even when*" instead of "when."
- **مِتُّ** (*mittu*) — "I have died." First person, past tense.

So far we have: *"When I have actually died..."*

Then comes the second half of the doubt:

- **لَ** (*la*) — the emphatic *lām*. Used to swear, to insist, to underline.
- **سَوْفَ** (*sawfa*) — the future particle, but the *long* future. Not the immediate *sa-* of "soon," but the further *sawfa* of "later, eventually."
- **أُخْرَجُ** (*ukhraju*) — passive verb, Form IV: "I will be brought out / extracted." Passive, because he is not doing it himself.
- **حَيًّا** (*ḥayyan*) — "alive," in the accusative as a state.

Read it again with the architecture exposed:

*"What — when I have actually died — will I really, certainly, eventually, be dragged back out into the state of being alive?"*

[PAUSE]

Look at how much **verbal armor** this objection wears. Question marker. Emphatic *mā*. Emphatic *lām*. Future particle. Passive form. State accusative. Layer upon layer of grammatical insistence.

The man is not asking a question. He is dressing up his doubt in every garment he can find. He is making the impossibility *sound* impossible by the sheer weight of his syntax.

This is what disbelief actually does. It does not arrive as a clean, neutral question. It arrives clothed — sometimes in cynicism, sometimes in sarcasm, sometimes in scientific posturing, sometimes in the shrug of *come on now, really?* The grammar of the heart's resistance is always a piling-up.

Now stay with me, because what comes next will take your breath away.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: Allah's Response — The Asymmetry of Effort

If a man came to you wearing seven coats of objection — *a-idhā mā... la-sawfa...* — how would you respond?

Most of us would match him. Layer for layer. Argument for argument.

Allah does not.

> **أَوَلَا يَذْكُرُ الْإِنسَانُ أَنَّا خَلَقْنَاهُ مِن قَبْلُ وَلَمْ يَكُ شَيْئًا**

*A-wa-lā yadhkuru l-insānu annā khalaqnāhu min qablu wa-lam yaku shay'ā*

Look at the contrast.

The man's doubt was decorated with emphatic particles. Allah's reply has almost none. The man stacked *a-idhā mā* and *la-sawfa* — interrogatives and emphatic lāms and future particles. Allah simply says: **"We created him before."**

*Khalaqnāhu min qablu.* Three words. Past tense. No emphasis. No flourish. Just — *We made him before.*

[PAUSE]

This asymmetry is itself an argument. The man's certainty about his impossibility had to be propped up by mountains of grammar. Allah's certainty about His ability to do it again needs no propping at all. The lighter the response, the more obvious the truth.

But notice the verb Allah chooses for what *al-insān* is failing to do.

Not *yaʿlamu* (does he not *know*).
Not *yufakkiru* (does he not *think*).
Not *yanẓuru* (does he not *look*).

He says: **يَذْكُرُ** — *yadhkuru*. **Does he not *remember*?**

This is extraordinary. The root *dh-k-r* in Arabic is the root of memory, of mention, of recall. To *remember* something is to bring back to mind something you already had access to. You cannot remember what you never knew.

[PAUSE]

So Allah is not framing this as new information. He is framing it as something the human being **already possesses** — and is failing to access. The fact of his prior creation is sitting inside him like a key he has dropped under his own foot. The work of faith here is not to *learn* something new. The work is to *remember* something he is forgetting.

This is why the response is so light. Allah does not need to argue. He needs only to remind.

And — listen to this — the classical scholars of Arabic noted something deep about the very word **الْإِنسَانُ** (*al-insān*). Many of them, including al-Rāghib al-Isfahānī in his *Mufradāt*, traced *insān* to the root *n-s-y* — meaning *to forget*. (Others traced it to *uns* — companionship — and Allah knows best; both meanings are documented in the classical lexicons, and they layer rather than contradict.)

If we hold the *nisyān* etymology alongside this ayah, watch what happens. The verse becomes:

> **"And the forgetful one says... does the forgetful one not remember?"**

The diagnosis is in the noun itself. The disease and the cure are in the same word. The being who is named *for* his forgetting — that is precisely the being whom Allah calls to remember.

[PAUSE]

### Element Four: *Wa-lam yaku shay'ā* — The Sound of Nothingness

There is one more linguistic miracle here that I cannot let pass.

The end of the ayah is: **وَلَمْ يَكُ شَيْئًا** — *wa-lam yaku shay'ā* — "and he was not [even] a thing."

Now, in standard Arabic, the negated past form of *kāna* (to be) is *lam yakun*. It carries that final *nūn*. *Lam yakun* — "he was not."

But Allah does not say *lam yakun* here.

He says **لَمْ يَكُ** — *lam yaku*.

The final *nūn* is dropped. The verb is shortened. It almost disappears.

This dropping is a permitted morphological feature in Arabic — the apocopation of the *nūn* when *lam* enters into certain contexts — and the classical grammarians document it carefully. But notice what Allah is doing by *choosing* it here.

He is describing the human being's pre-existence — the moment before his creation — when he was **nothing**. And He chooses the form of *kāna* that **itself fades, that loses its final consonant, that is barely there**.

[PAUSE]

The verb enacts the meaning. The grammar performs the truth. To describe nothingness, He uses a form that is almost not there. The shortened *yaku* sounds, in its very mouth-shape, like a being on the edge of existence — a verb so light it might dissolve. *Lam yaku.* Not even a full *kāna*. Not even a complete "was-not." Just the smallest possible breath of negation.

And then — *shay'an* — "a thing."

You were not even a thing. Not a small thing. Not a faint thing. **You were not a thing at all.**

Stay with this. Before you were conceived, you were not in some waiting room. You were not stored on a shelf. You were not a soul looking for a body. The Quran's testimony is more radical: *lam yaku shay'ā*. There was no *you*. The atoms that would one day spell your name were scattered across the earth and the food chain. The genetic recombination that made *you* and not your sibling had not occurred. The neurons that hold your memories had not fired. **There was no "you" anywhere in the universe.**

And then — there was.

And the One who did *that*, the One who turned *no-you* into *you*, is the One you are now doubting can do it again. From bones, from dust, from preserved atoms — easier material than the absolute nothing He started with the first time.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### Linguistic Bridge

Let me show you what these four observations have done together.

The verb *yaqūlu* told us: this doubt is not historical. It is **chronic**.

The pile-up of *a-idhā mā... la-sawfa* told us: this doubt arrives **dressed in armor**, decorated with the syntax of certainty.

Allah's bare, light response — *khalaqnāhu min qablu* — told us: **the truth needs no armor**. The lighter the answer, the heavier the reality.

The verb *yadhkuru* told us: this is not a debate. It is a **forgetting**. The cure is remembrance.

And *lam yaku shay'ā* told us: what you forgot is **the absoluteness of your prior nothingness** — and therefore the absoluteness of His prior act.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Forgetting at the Root of Doubt

Let me ask you something.

When did you first exist?

Take a moment. Don't answer with a date. Try to actually *remember* the beginning of yourself.

You can't.

[PAUSE]

There is no human being walking the earth who can remember the moment of their own coming-into-being. We have no memory of being a single cell. No memory of the eight weeks when our hearts began to beat. No memory of the moment our brains first registered light. We don't even remember our own birth. Most of us cannot remember anything before age three or four.

Which means: **for every human being, the most certain fact of their existence — that they began — is also the most forgotten fact of their existence.**

We feel as if we have always been here. The continuity of our consciousness fools us. We woke up this morning, we will go to sleep tonight, and the *I* that wakes and sleeps feels *eternal in the present moment*. Death feels like an interruption to a permanent state. As if existence is the default and non-existence is the violation.

But the truth is the opposite. **Non-existence was the default. You are the violation.**

This is the forgetting that Allah is calling out.

The doubt of resurrection is not, at root, a logical doubt. If it were logical, the question *who created you the first time?* would settle it instantly. The doubt is *psychological*. It comes from a heart that has lost contact with its own beginning. A heart that experiences itself as having always been here.

Allah's response is not an argument. It is a **mirror**.

He says: *Look. You are forgetting the most personal fact in the universe. The fact of your own start. Once you remember that you began — once you really sit with the absolute nothingness you came from — the question of whether you can be made again will collapse. Because the One who did the harder thing has already shown He can.*

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars used to call this **the argument from al-nashʾah al-ūlā** — the first creation. The Quran returns to it again and again. *"As We began the first creation, so We will repeat it"* (21:104). *"He gives them life Who created them the first time"* (36:79). *"Does man not see that We created him from a sperm-drop?"* (36:77).

Notice the pattern. Allah is not arguing from the future. He is arguing from **the past you cannot remember**. From the moment of your coming-into-being that you have, by the very nature of consciousness, *forgotten*.

This is why the cure is *dhikr* — remembrance. Not new information. Re-acquaintance with what is already true about you.

**Practical application:** Once a day — try once a day — pause and tell yourself the one sentence you cannot logically deny: *I have not always existed. There was a time when I was not a thing at all. Someone made me.* Watch what happens to the heart that holds that sentence for thirty seconds without rushing past it. Watch what doubts begin to soften. Watch how differently you walk into the rest of the day.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Tense of the Heart's Resistance

Come back with me to *yaqūlu*. The continuous, habitual present.

If the doubt of resurrection were a one-time thing — if the *insān* said it once at age twenty-five during a philosophy class and then moved on — Allah would have used the past tense. *Qāla l-insānu*. He said it.

But He uses *yaqūlu*. He **keeps saying** it.

Why?

Because this doubt is not a position. It is a **mood**. And moods come back.

[PAUSE]

You may have settled the question of resurrection at age fifteen. You may have read the proofs at twenty. You may have wept over your own mortality at thirty and found peace. But notice — the doubt comes back. At a funeral. In a hospital corridor. Lying awake at 3 a.m. when the certainty of the daytime has dissolved. *Will I really be brought forth alive? Is this really what's coming?*

The Quran is honest about this. The doubt is not killed by being answered once. The *yaqūlu* is permanent. As long as you are *al-insān* — the forgetful one — the question will keep surfacing.

And here is the mercy: Allah's response is also in the present tense. **أَوَلَا يَذْكُرُ** — *a-wa-lā yadhkuru*. **Does he not remember?** Continuous. Habitual. Available *every time* the doubt returns.

The reminder is not a one-time argument that was made fourteen centuries ago and is now in a book. The reminder is **a present-tense invitation**. Every time the doubt rises in your chest — *yaqūlu* — the reminder is also rising — *yadhkuru*. The cure is exactly as ongoing as the disease.

[PAUSE]

This is a radical reframe of what faith is. Faith is not a fortress you build once and then live inside. Faith is a **practice of remembering** that you do every time the *insān* in you forgets. The Arabic noun for the forgetful one and the Arabic verb for remembrance are sitting right next to each other in this ayah for a reason. They are two halves of the same human condition.

You are *al-insān* — the one who forgets. The way back is *dhikr* — remembering. And the cycle does not end. It just changes its quality. The faithful person is not someone who never forgets. The faithful person is someone who keeps coming back.

[PAUSE]

**Practical application:** Stop being surprised by the return of doubt. Stop treating it as a crisis. The ayah is telling you it will come — *yaqūlu*, ongoing — and the response is also ongoing — *yadhkuru*, ongoing. When the doubt rises, do not panic. Do not argue with yourself. Just do what Allah says. **Remember.** Bring to mind, again, the same simple fact: *there was a time I was not a thing at all*. The doubt is a wave. The remembrance is the shore. Waves keep coming. Shores keep holding. That is the rhythm of a believing life.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

The verb *yaqūlu* told us the doubt is chronic. The verb *yadhkuru* told us the cure is also chronic. They are paired present-tense verbs — disease and remedy held in the same grammatical breath.

The pile-up of emphatic particles in the doubt told us how much *armor* the resistance wears. The bare simplicity of *khalaqnāhu min qablu* told us how little armor the truth needs.

The repeated noun *al-insān* — used twice, when a pronoun would have done — told us this is a *condition*, not a *person*. Allah names the species twice because the diagnosis is of the species.

The vanishing form *lam yaku* — the verb that loses its own final letter — told us that the nothingness from which we came is so absolute that even the grammar describing it has to thin out and almost disappear.

And the etymology of *insān* itself — the forgetful one — told us that the disease is in the very name.

These are not five separate observations. They are **one insight from five angles**: that the entire human resistance to accountability is not a logical problem. It is a **forgetting problem**. And the entire weight of revelation is one steady hand on your shoulder, every time the doubt rises, saying: *remember.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time you actually sat with the fact that you have not always existed? Not as a concept — but as a felt reality?

2. The verb *yaqūlu* says the doubt keeps coming. Are you treating its return as a crisis, or as the normal weather of a forgetful heart?

3. Allah did not respond to the doubt with new information. He responded with *remember*. What in your life are you trying to *learn* — when what you actually need is to *recall*?

4. The shortened *lam yaku* sounds like the nothingness it describes. What other moments in your day pull you toward forgetting your origin? What pulls you back?

5. If the human being is named *for* his forgetting — what would it mean to live as the small minority of *al-insān* who actively, daily, refuses to forget?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The forgetful one keeps doubting that he can be brought back — but the One who made him from absolute nothing the first time is asking only that he remember it.

### The Closing Invitation

Tomorrow, before you leave the bed, before the phone, before the day's noise begins — give yourself thirty seconds. Tell yourself: *I was not a thing. I was nothing at all. And now I am here. The One who did that is listening to me right now.*

That is the entire response Allah is asking for in this ayah. Not a study. Not a debate. Just remembrance. The kind that lasts thirty seconds and then is required again the next day, because *yaqūlu* is still in the present tense, and so is *yadhkuru*, and that is the rhythm of being human.

### Du'a

O Allah — You created me when I was not a thing. You who turned absolute nothingness into the heart that is now reading these words: forgive my forgetting. Pull me back, every time the doubt rises, to the simple memory of my own beginning. Let me die remembering, and let me be brought forth — alive, alive — into the mercy of the One who did not need my consent the first time, and will not need it the next.

*Āmīn.*
