# Every One of You: The Universal Approach and the Moment of Separation

## Introduction — The Climate of This Ayah

Listen carefully. We are about to read one of the most discussed, most contested, most quietly devastating passages in the entire Quran. And before we open it, I want you to feel the climate it descends into.

By the time these two ayahs arrive, Surah Maryam has been listening to humanity. Listening to its claims. The surah has placed before us:

A people who said about Allah: *He has taken a son.*
A people who said about themselves: *We will not be raised.*
A people who said about their wealth and children: *Look — we are favored.*
A people who said about their religion: *We are saved by lineage. We are saved by belonging.*

Every one of these claims has the same structural feature. Each one tries to **opt out** of the universal moment. Each one tries to construct an exception: *those people are accountable; we are not. Those people will face Allah; our position is different.*

[PAUSE]

And then comes ayah 71.

> *وَإِن مِّنكُمْ إِلَّا وَارِدُهَا*
> *"And there is not one of you — except he will come upon it."*

Notice the address. Not *those people*. Not *the deniers*. Not *the disbelievers*. **You.** All of you. The grammatical "you" is plural, and it is direct, and it includes the believer reading this right now.

The Hook: There is a word in this ayah whose root meaning is **water**. Allah uses a water-word to describe the approach to **Fire**. And the second ayah uses two different verbs for what happens to the saved and what happens to the lost — verbs that are not symmetrical, that do not balance, that reveal something about what salvation actually is.

If you have ever quietly assumed you were outside the moment of judgment — that the moment was for someone else — these two ayahs are spoken precisely to that assumption.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: وَارِدُهَا — The Water-Word for the Fire

Before we open the word itself, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds vocabulary.

In Arabic, words live inside families. Every word descends from a three-letter root, and that root carries a primary scene — a concrete, often sensory image — from which all the related meanings extend. When you encounter a word in the Quran, you are not just hearing a definition. You are hearing **all the meanings that root has ever carried**, layered into a single utterance.

Now look at the word:

> **وَارِدُهَا** — *wāriduhā* — "one who comes upon it."

The root is **و-ر-د** (*w-r-d*). And here is the strange thing: in classical Arabic, this root's primary scene is not about fire at all. The primary scene is **water**.

A *wārid* is, in its original sense, a person or animal that approaches a watering place. The *wird* is the watering hole, the source. The verb *warada* describes the approach of the thirsty traveler to the spring. Caravans in pre-Islamic Arabia organized their entire journeys around the *wurūd* — the points where they would arrive at water.

[PAUSE]

Now let that settle.

Allah is describing every human being's approach to **Hell** — and He chooses the verb that, at its root, describes **the approach of the thirsty to water**.

Notice something. This is not an accident of vocabulary. Arabic has many other verbs for *coming, arriving, reaching, entering* — *atā*, *jā'a*, *dakhala*, *balagha*. Allah did not choose any of them. He chose the one whose primary scene is the parched soul reaching for the spring.

What is this doing?

Stay with the image. The traveler in the desert does not approach the watering hole casually. He approaches it because he **must**. Because his life depends on it. Because thirst has driven him there. The *wārid* is not a tourist; he is someone for whom the water is non-negotiable.

And here Allah is saying: every one of you will approach the Fire **with that same inevitability**. Not as visitors. Not as bystanders. As souls drawn to a destination that the structure of existence itself has made unavoidable for you.

The classical scholars discussed at length what this *wurūd* actually is. Some said it means crossing the *Sirat* — the bridge over the Fire — every soul will pass over it. Some said it means the heat of it touching every soul. Some said it means standing on its edge, witnessing it, seeing what could have been. Each opinion is a camera angle on the same scene: **proximity that no one escapes.**

Hold on to all the opinions at once. Don't choose between them. Allah's word is large enough to hold them all.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The very word that should be a word of relief — *wurūd*, the arrival at water — is being used for arrival at Fire. The traveler reaches the spring expecting life. Allah is saying: the soul arrives at this destination expecting one thing and the question is what it actually finds there.

This is what Allah does with language. He takes a word your tongue knows means *life* and places it on a scene that could mean *death* — and forces you to ask which one your soul is actually walking toward.

### Element Two: حَتْمًا مَّقْضِيًّا — The Doubled Decree

Now look at the end of the ayah:

> *كَانَ عَلَىٰ رَبِّكَ حَتْمًا مَّقْضِيًّا*
> *"This is, upon your Lord, an inevitability decreed."*

Two words at the end: **ḥatman** and **maqḍiyyan**. Both, in any English translation, get rendered as something like "decreed" or "inevitable" or "decided." They look like synonyms. They look like they're saying the same thing.

They are not.

Let me teach you the principle first. In classical Arabic rhetoric, when two near-synonyms are placed back-to-back, this is never decoration. The Quran does not use two words where one would suffice. When near-synonyms are doubled, it means the writer wants you to feel the **shape** of the two roots overlapping — to feel the inevitability from two angles at once.

Now the words.

**ḥatm** — root ح-ت-م — carries the meaning of something fixed, something cut off from negotiation, something that has become unavoidable by its very nature. *Ḥatm* is what the structure of reality itself enforces. It's not just that something will happen; it's that the **possibility of it not happening has been removed.**

**maqḍiyy** — root ق-ض-ي — is the passive participle of *qaḍā*, which means *to decree, to decide, to bring to completion*. *Maqḍiyy* is what has been **judicially settled**. A judge issues a *qaḍā'*. A matter is *qaḍā'* when the deliberation is over and the verdict has been pronounced.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference now?

*Ḥatm* is the inevitability of structure — like gravity. The thing must happen because the universe is built such that it must.

*Maqḍiyy* is the inevitability of decree — like a court verdict. The thing must happen because Allah has spoken it into the books of fate.

So Allah is saying: this is inevitable in **both** senses. It is what the universe enforces, **and** it is what your Lord has decreed. There is no fissure between these two angles. The universe was built such that this would happen, and the Lord of the universe has personally pronounced that it will happen.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this is doing to the listener.

Every other claim in this surah has tried to find a fissure. *We are not accountable.* *We are excepted.* *We are different.* The doubling of *ḥatman maqḍiyyan* closes the fissures from both sides at once. You cannot escape through the structure of reality, because the structure has been built to bring you to this point. You cannot escape through divine mercy redefining the rules, because the rules have been judicially decreed by the One whose mercy you are appealing to.

This is upon **your Lord** — *rabbika*. Notice that. Not *Allah* in some abstract sense. Your Lord. The One who has been guiding you, sustaining you, listening to you. He is the one who has placed His own seal on this inevitability.

When the One who loves you most is also the One who has made this inevitable, where do you appeal?

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: نُنَجِّي and نَذَرُ — The Asymmetric Verbs

Now the second ayah. Look at the two verbs Allah uses for what happens after the universal coming:

> *ثُمَّ نُنَجِّي الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوا وَّنَذَرُ الظَّالِمِينَ فِيهَا جِثِيًّا*
> *"Then We save those who were mindful and leave the wrongdoers within it, on their knees."*

Two verbs: **nunajjī** ("We save") and **nadharu** ("We leave"). At first they look like opposites — save vs. leave. They are not.

Let me show you.

**nunajjī** — this is form II of the root **ن-ج-و** (*n-j-w*). The basic verb *najā* means to be saved, to escape, to come out alive. Form II is the *causative* form — it means *to actively save, to pull to safety, to rescue*. When Allah says *nunajjī*, He is not describing a passive event. He is describing an **active rescue operation**. He reaches in. He takes hold. He pulls out.

The active participle from this root, *nājin*, is the one who comes out alive — who has been saved. The Quran uses it for Nuh stepping off the ark. For Musa walking out of the sea.

This is *active divine rescue*.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the other verb.

**nadharu** — root **و-ذ-ر** (*w-dh-r*). And here is the first thing to notice: this verb is grammatically defective. It has **no perfect-tense form** in standard usage. You cannot say *waḏartu* in classical Arabic; you say *taraktu*. *Wadhara* exists almost exclusively in the present and imperative. It is a partial verb — a verb of incompleteness.

And its meaning is not neutral. Arabic has another verb, *taraka*, which means simply *to leave behind*. It is the standard, neutral term. But *wadhara* carries a different shade. The classical lexicographers connect it to *waḏar* — meaning small bits, scraps, things tossed aside. To *yadharu* something is not just to leave it. It is to leave it as one leaves what one no longer cares to retrieve.

It is the verb you use when you walk away and do not look back.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the asymmetry of these two verbs side by side.

For the *muttaqūn*, Allah does not use a passive verb. He does not say *they will escape*. He says **We will rescue them** — actively, intensively, with His own action.

For the *ẓālimīn*, He does not use a violent verb either. He does not say *We will throw them, We will cast them, We will hurl them*. He says **We will leave them**. We will simply walk on. We will not retrieve.

Do you see what this does to your image of Hell?

Hell, in many imaginations, is a place where Allah is actively punishing — pouring wrath, hurling souls. But ayah 72 frames it differently. Hell is the place where the soul has come, the rescue has happened for those who reached for it, and the rest have been **left**. Left where they already are. Left in the position they had already chosen.

[PAUSE]

Notice also: the verb is *nadharu* — we leave **them**, not *we leave **them in it*** as a separate motion. The leaving and the staying are the same action. The wrongdoers were already there; the rescue lifted out the *muttaqūn*; the wrongdoers are what remains.

This is one of the most theologically precise pictures of damnation in the Quran. Allah does not hurl. He **does not retrieve**. The soul stays where the soul had already taken itself.

And the word for their posture — **jiṯiyyan** — fallen on their knees. We will return to that.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge — From Words to Reality

We have looked at three things.

We have seen that the verb for *approaching* Hell is the verb for *approaching water* — that what should be life-giving is the very motion that brings the soul to the moment of testing.

We have seen that this approach is *ḥatman maqḍiyyan* — inevitable in the structure of reality and decreed in the books of judgment, both at once, with no fissure between them.

We have seen that what differentiates the saved from the lost is not the violence done to the lost but the **rescue done to the saved** — that Hell is what remains when Allah does not retrieve.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the structure of your own life.

## PART 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Universality of the Moment — and What It Dismantles

Imagine for a moment a courtroom. Imagine the largest courtroom you can picture — a courtroom large enough to seat every human being who has ever lived. Imagine yourself walking in.

In your daily life, you live with a quiet assumption. You know there is a Day. You know there is accountability. But somewhere, somehow, in a way you have never quite articulated, you have placed yourself **outside** the central drama. The Day is for the deniers. The Day is for the wrongdoers. The Day is for the people whose names appear in the warning verses. Yes, you will be there, but more as an attendee than as a defendant.

This assumption is so quiet you may never have heard yourself making it. But it is there. It is in the way you read warning verses and think of someone else. It is in the way you read mercy verses and think of yourself.

[PAUSE]

Allah is dismantling this in 19:71. In two words.

> *wa-in minkum illā wāriduhā.*

There is not — *one* — of you — except — he comes to it.

The exceptive construction in Arabic, *in...illā*, is among the most exclusive grammatical structures in the language. It means: I am sealing every door of escape. I am closing every category. I am leaving no remainder. The set has been emptied of exceptions.

And the address is *minkum* — *from you all.* This is the second-person plural. Not a distant third-person about *those people*. Allah is speaking to the room. He is speaking to the believer reading the surah. He is speaking to the scholar quoting the surah. He is speaking to every soul that has ever assumed, *not me.*

[PAUSE]

Let me name what this is doing psychologically.

There is a thing the human heart does, very quietly, that the Quran calls *ghurūr* — delusion of safety. It is not arrogance in the loud sense. It is the soft, subtle, almost unconscious assumption that you are not the one being warned. You are reading the warning, not receiving it.

19:71 is a single sentence designed to break that.

Because here is what happens when you actually internalize *wa-in minkum illā wāriduhā*: you stop being able to read the rest of the Quran the way you used to. You stop being able to read about the wrongdoers and think *those people*. Because **you** are also the one approaching the Fire. You are not on the safe side of the page. You are inside the scene the page is describing.

[PAUSE]

But here is the gift, because Allah does not leave us in terror.

Notice the verse does not say "every one of you will burn." It says "every one of you will come to it." The coming is universal. The **outcome** is not.

The verse establishes a structure: **the moment is the same; the differentiation is what happens at the moment.**

And this is, paradoxically, a mercy. Because if the differentiation happened *before* the moment — if some souls were exempted from approaching, while others were dragged toward — then your salvation would depend on being born into the right category. But Allah does not work that way. Every soul approaches. Every soul stands at the same threshold. What happens at the threshold is determined by what you brought to it.

The ground is level at the moment of judgment.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes the **center** of your spiritual life. If you have lived with the quiet assumption that you are outside the warning verses, you have built your spiritual life around comfort and routine. *I am a Muslim. I pray. I'm fine.* But once you understand that *every one of you* comes — including you — your spiritual life reorganizes around a single question:

**What will I have brought to that moment?**

Not what is my category. Not what is my lineage. Not what is my membership card. **What will I, personally, have built that Allah's hand will reach for when He performs the *nunajjī* — the active rescue?**

That question, when you actually carry it, changes everything.

### Theme Two: The Posture of Separation — *Jiṯiyyan*

Now look at the last word of ayah 72:

> *wa-nadharu al-ẓālimīn fīhā jiṯiyyan*
> *"and We leave the wrongdoers within it, fallen on their knees."*

The word **jiṯiyyan** is the plural of *jāthin* — one who is fallen on his knees. The root *j-th-w* describes this specific posture: not standing, not sitting, not lying — **kneeling**, often the knees buckled by exhaustion, by collapse, by the inability to stand any longer.

This word is not casual in Surah Maryam. Three ayahs earlier, in 19:68, Allah had said:

> *thumma la-naḥḍirannahum ḥawla jahannama jiṯiyyā*
> *"Then We will surely bring them around Hell, fallen on their knees."*

The same word. The same posture. Allah established the image earlier: every soul is gathered around Hell *jiṯiyyā* — kneeling. Then in 19:72, the rescue happens. The *muttaqūn* are lifted up. And the *ẓālimūn* are **left in that same position**.

[PAUSE]

Notice something profound. The kneeling is not the punishment. The kneeling is the **default human posture before the divine reality.** Every soul kneels in 19:68. The believer kneels. The wrongdoer kneels. The kneeling is the universal recognition that comes when the veils are lifted and the soul finally sees what it stands before.

The difference is not who kneels. The difference is **who is lifted up from the kneeling, and who is left in it.**

[PAUSE]

Let me give you a modern image to feel this with.

Imagine an airport at the moment of a great evacuation. Every person in the terminal is told: *get down on the floor.* And every person, regardless of who they are, gets down. Some are scared. Some are calm. Some are praying. But every one of them is on the floor.

Then the rescue teams arrive. They move through the terminal. They lift up some people and carry them out. The others remain on the floor.

The lifting is the salvation. The remaining-on-the-floor is what becomes hellish — not because the floor is now different, but because **the rescue passed and you were not lifted.**

This is *jiṯiyyan*. This is what 19:72 is showing you. Hell is the place where the rescue has come and gone, and you are still on your knees because no hand reached for you.

[PAUSE]

Why was no hand reached?

Look at the verb again. *Nunajjī alladhīna ittaqaw* — We rescue **those who feared**. *Ittaqaw* is from the root *waqā* — to **shield, to protect, to guard against**. The *muttaqī* is the one who, in this life, built a shield. The shield was *taqwā*. It was the constant low-level awareness of Allah's presence. The instinct, when temptation came, to step back. The instinct, when wrongdoing was easy, to feel the cold breath of accountability and refuse.

The hand of *nunajjī* reaches for the souls who carry the *taqwā* shield. Why? Because *taqwā* is the rope they had been weaving their whole lives. The hand reaches and finds something to grasp. The soul without *taqwā* — there is nothing for the hand to grasp. Nothing for the rescue to take hold of. So the soul remains.

This is not a metaphor for arbitrary divine selection. This is a precise description of what salvation actually **is**. Salvation is the encounter between Allah's reaching hand and the rope you wove.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It reframes every act of *taqwā* you have ever performed.

Every time you held your tongue when you wanted to speak harshly. Every time you turned your eyes away from what was forbidden. Every time you gave when you wanted to keep. Every time you prayed when you wanted to sleep.

You were not just performing acts of obedience. You were **weaving the rope**. You were giving Allah's hand something to grasp.

And every time you skipped the *taqwā* — every time you gave in, every time you said "just this once" — you were not just sinning. You were **fraying the rope**. You were making it harder for the hand to find purchase.

The moment of *nunajjī* is not arbitrary. It is the harvest of every small act. Every act is rope. The rope must be there when the hand reaches.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now look at the structure of these two ayahs together. Notice what Allah has built.

**Ayah 71** is universal. *Every one of you.* The address is plural. The verb is singular for each soul. Every individual approaches.

**Ayah 72** opens with *thumma* — *then*. This particle in Arabic always carries the implication of **separation in time**. Not "and then" as if the two events flow continuously, but "and then, after a gap, after the moment has been allowed to stand on its own" — *then* the differentiation happens.

And in that differentiation, the grammar itself enacts the separation. The ayah opens with **plural address** — *minkum*, you-all. By the end of the second ayah, the wrongdoers are **third-person**. *Al-ẓālimīn* — "the wrongdoers." They are no longer "you." They have been separated out grammatically, before they are separated out spatially.

The shift in pronoun **is** the moment of judgment.

This is what Allah's words do. The reality being described is enacted in the sentence describing it.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

The three linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight from different doors.

*Wāriduhā* — the water-word for fire-approach — was telling us: every soul moves toward this moment with the inevitability of thirst toward water. The motion is built into being human.

*Ḥatman maqḍiyyan* — the doubled decree — was telling us: the inevitability is closed from both directions. The structure of the universe and the decree of your Lord both seal it.

*Nunajjī* and *nadharu* — active rescue, passive abandonment — were telling us: salvation is something Allah does to you. Damnation is what remains when He does not.

Theme one — universality — was telling us: there is no opt-out. You are not outside the warning. The warning is being read by the one it is about.

Theme two — *jiṯiyyan* — was telling us: every soul kneels at the moment. The kneeling is universal. The lifting is what differentiates.

All five doors open onto the same room. The room contains a single furniture: **the moment when Allah's hand reaches, and your rope is either there to grasp or it is not.**

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life are you living as if the warning verses are about *those people* — and what would change if you read every warning verse as though it were addressed to *minkum*, *from you*?

2. When you imagine the moment of judgment, do you imagine yourself outside the scene watching? Or inside the scene approaching? What would shift if you could not maintain the watcher's distance any longer?

3. *Taqwā* is described in this ayah as the rope Allah's hand reaches for. What is your rope made of? Which strands of it have you been weaving lately, and which strands have been fraying?

4. The wrongdoers are *left* — not hurled, not thrown, not cast down. They are **left where they already were**. What does it mean to take seriously that Hell is where you remain when the rescue does not reach you?

5. *Nunajjī* is in the present tense — Allah does not say *We will save* but in a sense *We rescue*. The rescue is ongoing. In what way is Allah already reaching for you, today, and what are you doing with the hand that is already extended?

[PAUSE]

### One-Sentence Distillation

Every soul approaches the Fire with the inevitability of thirst toward water — and what determines whether the soul is lifted out or left on its knees is not lineage, not category, not membership, but the rope of *taqwā* the soul wove in this life for Allah's hand to grasp.

[PAUSE]

### The Closing Invitation

If you have read these two ayahs and felt the floor drop slightly — that is correct. That is what these words are designed to do. They are designed to remove the comfortable distance between you and the moment they describe. They are designed to put you in the scene.

But the gift is this: you are reading them while you are still weaving. The rope is still in your hands. *Nunajjī* is still in the present tense. The hand of your Lord is still reaching, and every act of *taqwā* you perform between now and the moment is another strand thickening the rope.

Do not read these ayahs and despair. Read them and **weave**.

### A Brief Du'a

Yā Rabb — every one of us is approaching the place You have described. We confess that we have lived too often as though the warning were for someone else, as though we stood outside the scene. Loosen our quiet delusion. Make us *minkum* — *from those You are speaking to* — so that we begin building before the moment arrives.

Yā Najī — O One who rescues — when our knees buckle in the universal kneeling, let Your hand find a rope to grasp in us. Let the *taqwā* we have woven, however thin, be enough for Your reaching hand.

And do not leave us. *Lā tadharnā*. Do not walk past us in the moment we cannot stand.

Āmīn.
