# The Tongue of Truth — What Allah Gives After You Leave

## INTRODUCTION

**فَلَمَّا ٱعْتَزَلَهُمْ وَمَا يَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ وَهَبْنَا لَهُۥٓ إِسْحَـٰقَ وَيَعْقُوبَ ۖ وَكُلًّا جَعَلْنَا نَبِيًّۭا ۝ وَوَهَبْنَا لَهُم مِّن رَّحْمَتِنَا وَجَعَلْنَا لَهُمْ لِسَانَ صِدْقٍ عَلِيًّۭا**

*Fa-lammā iʿtazalahum wa mā yaʿbudūna min dūni-llāhi wahabnā lahu Isḥāqa wa Yaʿqūb, wa kullan jaʿalnā nabiyyā. Wa wahabnā lahum min raḥmatinā wa jaʿalnā lahum lisāna ṣidqin ʿaliyyā.*

*"So when he had separated himself from them and from what they worshipped besides Allah, We bestowed on him Ishaq and Yaʿqub, and each of them We made a prophet. And We bestowed on them of Our mercy, and We made for them a tongue of truth, exalted."*

---

### Rebuilding the World

To stand inside this ayah, you have to stand inside the silence that came just before it.

The previous verses gave us a young man — Ibrahim — speaking to his father. Not arguing, not shouting. Calling him "yā abati" — "O my dear father" — and asking him gently why he worships what cannot hear, cannot see, cannot help. The father's response was not a counter-argument. It was a threat. *"If you do not desist, I will surely stone you. So leave me — for a long time."*

And Ibrahim says: *"Peace be upon you. I will ask my Lord to forgive you. Surely He has been gracious to me."* And he leaves.

Picture what he leaves. His father — old, hostile, idol-carving by trade. His tribe — the only people who know his face. The land — Ur of the Chaldees, with its temples and its certainties. His name — which until now was the name of a son in a household.

He walks out. Alone. Without an heir. Without a tribe. Without a future that the desert mind would recognize as a future.

[PAUSE]

And then — without transition, without delay — Allah says:

*"So when he had separated himself from them..."*

And in the next breath, gives him everything.

---

### The Hook

There is something Allah gives Ibrahim in these two ayahs that is more remarkable than the son, more remarkable than the grandson, more remarkable even than prophethood. It is the very last thing mentioned. It is two words in Arabic. And those two words are the reason every Muslim, Christian, and Jew on earth still speaks Ibrahim's name with reverence — three thousand five hundred years after he walked out of his father's house.

Most translations render those two words as "a high mention" or "a noble reputation." That translation is true, and it is also tragically thin.

Stay with me. Because the gift Allah gives at the end of this ayah is one of the strangest gifts in the Qur'an — and once you see what it actually is, you will understand why it had to come *after* the leaving, and not before.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will follow three threads through these two ayahs. Each thread is small. Each opens onto something vast.

---

### Thread One: ٱعْتَزَلَ — The Verb That Carries Its Own Reason

Before we look at the word, look at how Arabic builds verbs.

In English, "I washed" and "I washed myself" are two different sentences. The reflexive — the doing-it-to-yourself — has to be added on. In Arabic, the verb itself can carry the reflexive inside it. The shape of the word changes, and suddenly the action is bending back toward the doer.

The basic root here is **ع-ز-ل** (ʿ-z-l). In its simple form — *ʿazala* — it means "to set apart, to remove, to isolate something." A judge can be *ʿazala*'d — removed from his post. An item can be *ʿazala*'d — set aside on a shelf.

But that is not the form Allah uses.

Allah uses **ٱعْتَزَلَ** *(iʿtazala)* — Form VIII. And Form VIII does something specific in Arabic: it folds the action back on the doer. *He himself withdrew.* Not "he was removed." Not "they cast him out." **He chose. He acted on himself.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something. We say in English "Ibrahim was kicked out by his father." The father threatened to stone him; the father told him to leave. From a worldly camera, Ibrahim is being expelled. He is the passive party. He is the one who has lost.

But Allah does not narrate it that way. Allah uses Form VIII. *Iʿtazalahum.* **He separated himself from them.**

Allah is rewriting the story of who is the agent. From the outside, Ibrahim is the one losing his home. From inside the verb Allah chose, Ibrahim is the one who is leaving — sovereign, deliberate, deciding. The father did not push him out. Ibrahim walked.

This is no small thing. Because the same outward event — a young man leaving his father's house under threat — can be the story of a victim, or the story of a hero, depending on who narrates it. Allah narrates it as a deliberate withdrawal.

Here is what's remarkable: the next word makes it stranger.

*Iʿtazalahum* — he separated himself from **them**.
*Wa mā yaʿbudūna min dūni-llāh* — and from what they worshipped besides Allah.

He doesn't just leave the people. He leaves what they worship. Notice that Allah uses **مَا** *(mā)* — "what" — not **مَن** *(man)* — "who." In Arabic, *mā* is for the non-rational, for things, for stones. Allah is silently making a comment on the idols even as He mentions them. Their gods are not "whoms." They are "whats."

So Ibrahim's withdrawal is double. He withdraws from the people. He withdraws from the *whats* they bow to. Both at once.

What this single Form VIII verb has shown us: **the act of leaving for Allah's sake is never described in the Qur'an as loss. It is described as agency.** The one who separates himself is the one who is moving. The world he left is what is now standing still.

[PAUSE]

### Thread Two: وَهَبْنَا — The Verb of Pure Gift

Arabic has many verbs for giving. *Aʿṭā* — to give, neutral, like handing someone a cup. *Manaḥa* — to grant, often used for grants of land or position. *Razaqa* — to provide, in the sense of sustenance. *Atā* — to bring, to deliver.

And then there is **وَهَبَ** *(wahaba)*.

*Wahaba* is a different category. The lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī — are unusually agreed: *wahaba* means **to give with no expectation of return, no transaction, no compensation, no entitlement on the part of the receiver**. It is the giving that is purely the giver's overflow. The closest English word is "to bestow" or "to gift" in the old sense — when *gift* meant something the receiver had not earned.

This is the verb we use in Arabic when we name a child. *Wahb.* "Gift." *Wahbi.* "My gift." Because a child is the prototype of what *wahaba* means — you did not earn this person; this person was given.

Now look at our ayah. The verb *wahabnā* appears **twice**. Twice in two ayahs. And the things gifted are:

> **First wahaba:** Isḥāq and Yaʿqūb — a son, and a grandson. Two generations.
> **Second wahaba:** *min raḥmatinā* — of Our mercy.

Stay with the first one. Ibrahim leaves his father, alone in the desert. He has no tribe, no son, no descendant. And Allah gives him not just a son — but a son AND a grandson. **Yaʿqūb is not Ibrahim's son. Yaʿqūb is his grandson, the son of Isḥāq.**

The classical scholars notice this and pause on it. Why mention Yaʿqūb? Why not just say "We gave him Isḥāq"?

Here is what Allah is doing. Ibrahim left his lineage behind. The lineage of his father, of his tribe — that line is now closed to him. So Allah does not just give him a continuation. **Allah lets him see two generations forward.** "Ibrahim, you walked out of one bloodline alone. Look — you are walking into a bloodline. You will hold your son. You will hold your son's son. You will see the line."

This is the mercy of *wahaba*. It is not transactional. Ibrahim did not bargain — "I will leave my father, You give me a son." He left for Allah. Allah gives, from His own overflow, more than was asked.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the **pronoun shift**.

Ayah 49: *wahabnā lahu* — "We gifted to **him**" — singular.
Ayah 50: *wa wahabnā lahum* — "And We gifted to **them**" — plural.

In one ayah, the gift was to one man. In the next ayah, the same verb is used — but now the indirect object has expanded. The gift is no longer to Ibrahim; the gift is to the line. Ibrahim, Isḥāq, Yaʿqūb — all of them — are now the recipients of "of Our mercy."

What just happened? Allah's gift, in the space of two ayahs, **multiplied across generations**. The recipient grew. Ibrahim left as one. Allah received him as a lineage.

Listen to what this verb is teaching. **The economy of God is not arithmetic.** When you give up something for Allah, you do not get back the same kind of thing. You don't get back the same amount. You get a different order of gift, given by a different verb. *Wahaba* — the verb of overflow.

[PAUSE]

### Thread Three: لِسَانَ صِدْقٍ عَلِيًّا — The Strangest Gift

This is the phrase we have been walking toward.

Three words. Untranslated, they say: **"a tongue of truth, exalted."**

Begin with the literal sense, because the literal is the strangeness.

*Lisān* in Arabic is — yes — the physical organ in your mouth. The tongue. But it is also, by the most natural extension, *the speech that the tongue produces*. And by another extension — the most important one for our ayah — *the speech that other people produce about you*. What gets said. What is in mouths.

When Arabs say *lisān ṣidq*, they are not saying "a truthful tongue" as in "Ibrahim's own tongue is truthful." They are saying **the speech that exists about him in the mouths of others is true and good speech**. His name, when it comes off other people's tongues, comes off truthfully and well.

Now add **ṣidq**. *Ṣidq* is the great Arabic word for truth — but specifically for the kind of truth that *corresponds to reality*. Not just absence of lying. Truth in the deepest sense — what is said matches what actually is.

So *lisān ṣidq* means: **a remembrance, in the mouths of generations, that is true** — that does not need to be propaganda, does not need to be inflated, does not need to be defended. The mention itself is honest, and it lasts.

And then comes **ʿaliyyā**. From the root **ع-ل-و** — height, elevation, loftiness. This same root will appear a few ayahs later, in the story of Idris (19:57): *wa rafaʿnāhu makānan ʿaliyyā* — "And We raised him to a high station." The motif is structural. In this surah, exalted height is what Allah gives to those He chooses.

So the full phrase: **a tongue of truth, exalted**. A name spoken truly, on the tongues of generations, lifted high.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

Three thousand five hundred years ago, a young man walked out of his father's tent in Mesopotamia. He left no books. No buildings. No army. No tomb (none that we can find). No portrait. He left, and he died, and the deserts that he wandered in were swallowed by other empires, other religions, other languages.

And right now, in this room, in this hour, you are saying his name.

Tonight, all over the world, two billion Muslims will pray and in their prayer they will say: **اللهم صل على محمد وعلى آل محمد كما صليت على إبراهيم وعلى آل إبراهيم** — *"O Allah, send blessings upon Muhammad and the family of Muhammad as You sent blessings upon Ibrahim and the family of Ibrahim..."*

Two billion people. Five times a day. For 1,400 years and counting. Mentioning his name. Mentioning his family.

And not just Muslims. Christians and Jews trace their faith to him too. Half of the human race calls him father.

[PAUSE]

This is *lisān ṣidq ʿaliyyā*. This is what Allah gave him. Not just a son. Not just a grandson. Not just prophethood for the line. **A name that is still being spoken — truthfully, with reverence, on a height — so long after the world that knew him in the flesh has dissolved into dust.**

Now look at the architecture. Allah gave him this gift as the very last item in a sequence:
1. Ishaq (the son)
2. Yaʿqub (the grandson)
3. Prophethood for each
4. Mercy on the line
5. **A tongue of truth, exalted**

The list ascends. Each item is greater than the last. And the climax — the final and greatest gift — is the one that Ibrahim could not see when it was given, because it is in the mouths of people who would not be born for thousands of years.

The greatest gift Allah can give a human being is one the human being will never witness in this life.

Here is what this phrase has shown us: **the leaving was not the cost. The leaving was the qualification.** Ibrahim could not have a tongue of truth on the lips of every monotheist on earth if he had stayed in his father's house and inherited his father's idol shop. The withdrawal was not the price he paid for what came after. The withdrawal **is what made the gift possible**.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how reality is actually structured.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Mathematics That Aren't Mathematics

Imagine you run a small business and someone asks you to make a sacrifice. Take a pay cut. Lose a client. Walk away from a deal.

Your mind, automatically, does mathematics. *If I give up X, I lose X. If I give up X for the chance of Y, I'm betting X against Y. If Y is uncertain, I'd better get good odds.*

This is how we are wired. Sunk cost. Opportunity cost. Risk-adjusted return. Modern human beings carry these spreadsheets in their heads everywhere — into our friendships, our marriages, our prayers. *What did I get for what I gave?* And we are baffled, sometimes furious, when the math doesn't work out.

Now look at Ibrahim.

The math, on paper, is catastrophic.

What he gave up:
- Father (the only one he had)
- Tribe (the only social network in the desert; without it, you die)
- Home (the only land he knew)
- Inheritance (idol-carving was his father's trade — there would have been a workshop to inherit)
- Identity ("son of Āzar" — gone)

What he got:
- A son and a grandson, born many years later, when he was old
- His name on the lips of half the planet, three millennia after his death

The mathematics don't balance. They are not even in the same currency.

[PAUSE]

This is the first thing the ayah is teaching: **Allah does not pay you back in the currency of what you gave up.** Ibrahim did not give up a tribe to get a tribe. He gave up a tribe to get prophethood for his sons. He did not give up a name to get a name. He gave up "Ibn Āzar" — son of an idol-carver — to be called "Khalīl Allāh" — the friend of Allah.

The exchange is not a trade. It is a transmutation.

Notice that the verb Allah uses for what He gave is *wahaba* — pure gift, no transaction. Allah is signaling, in the verb itself: *Do not look at this as a payment. This is not what you bought with your sacrifice. This is what I, freely, am giving you, after you made yourself available to receive it.*

The sacrifice did not earn the gift. **The sacrifice cleared the space for the gift.**

Stay with the difference. If the sacrifice earned the gift, then sacrifice would be a form of purchase, and Allah would owe you. He would be your debtor. You would be His creditor. The relationship would be commercial.

But *wahaba* is not commercial. *Wahaba* is the verb of a king who gives to his servant — not because the servant earned it, but because the king is generous and the servant has come close enough to receive.

This is one of the most freeing realizations in the Qur'an. **You are not in a commercial relationship with Allah.** When you give something up for Him, you are not buying anything. You are not running a balance sheet that He has to settle. You are entering a space where His generosity can pour into you, on terms entirely of His own choosing — and His choosing is always more, always different, always better.

Now, what does this mean tomorrow morning, when you wake up and have to make a decision that costs you something?

It means: **the cost is not a loss to be recovered.** It is a doorway. What waits on the other side will not look like the price you paid. It will look like Allah, generous, doing what only He can do. And the moment you start measuring whether the math worked out, you have stepped back into the shop of Āzar — the one Ibrahim left.

---

### Theme Two: The Legacy You Cannot Manage

There is a modern obsession with "leaving a legacy."

You can read about it on every entrepreneur's website. You can see it in the architecture of Manhattan — towers with names of dead financiers etched in stone. You can hear it in the celebrity interview: *"How do you want to be remembered?"*

The modern legacy is an act of curation. It is something you build. You manage it. You hire publicists. You write your memoir. You buy your gravestone. You get the wing of the hospital named after you.

The modern legacy is, essentially, a continuation of self — a prosthetic ego, propped up by stone and ink and money, after the body fails.

[PAUSE]

Now look at *lisān ṣidq ʿaliyyā*.

Ibrahim did none of these things. He did not write a book. He did not commission a monument. He did not endow a college. He could not — he was a wandering shepherd in the second millennium BCE.

He simply did not control his memory. He could not. His memory was, from the beginning, in **other people's mouths** — that is what *lisān* means. Speech that is not his own. Tongues he will never meet.

And yet, three and a half thousand years later, he is not just remembered. He is remembered **truthfully** — *ṣidq*. And remembered **on a height** — *ʿaliyyā*.

How? Allah did it.

Stay with this paradox. The man who **let go** of all the levers of legacy is the one whose name is most powerfully alive. The men whose monuments stand tallest in our cities — most of us couldn't tell you their names. The man who simply walked out of his father's tent — every monotheist on earth says his name.

Here is the second teaching: **the kind of legacy you can build is not the kind that lasts. The kind that lasts is the kind only Allah can build.**

Why? Because *ṣidq* — true mention — is impossible to manufacture. You can buy a building. You can pay for a name. But you cannot pay for the truth of how people speak about you in the privacy of their hearts. Only the One who turns hearts can do that.

Allah, in this ayah, is showing us the architecture of real legacy:
- It does not come from controlling your image.
- It does not come from accumulating the world.
- It comes when Allah Himself decides to make your name *ṣidq* on the tongues of those who do not yet exist.

And the qualification — read carefully, because this is the load-bearing word in the entire passage — the qualification is *iʿtazala*. **He withdrew.** He stopped trying to win in his father's economy. He stopped fighting for inheritance. He left, and let Allah handle what would be said of him afterward.

[PAUSE]

So what do you do tomorrow?

You stop managing your legacy.

You stop performing for the camera that you have set up in your own mind. You stop strategizing about what they will say at your funeral. You stop calculating which deeds will photograph well.

You do the deed for Allah, and you let go of what is said about it. You give the charity, and you do not keep the receipt for your descendants. You teach the child, and you do not need the child to credit you. You make the dua in the night, where no one will ever see, and you walk away.

And one of two things happens. Either no one ever knows — and Allah keeps that record in a Book that does not lose a single word. Or Allah, by His own choice, places your mention on the tongue of someone you will never meet, and you become a small, true echo on the lips of the future.

Either way, the management is no longer yours. And — this is the relief that Ibrahim's story is offering us — **it never was**.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture We Have Walked Through

Look at the shape of these two ayahs now that we have walked them:

> **Ayah 49:**
> *Fa-lammā iʿtazalahum* — when he separated himself from them
> *...wahabnā lahu Isḥāqa wa Yaʿqūb* — We gifted him a son and a grandson
> *wa kullan jaʿalnā nabiyyā* — and each of them, We made a prophet
>
> **Ayah 50:**
> *wa wahabnā lahum min raḥmatinā* — and We gifted to them, of Our mercy
> *wa jaʿalnā lahum lisāna ṣidqin ʿaliyyā* — and We made for them a tongue of truth, exalted

The structure is a ladder, ascending.

At the bottom: a man's act of withdrawal.
First rung: a son.
Second rung: a grandson.
Third rung: prophethood for each.
Fourth rung: mercy for the line.
**Top rung: a true name on the tongues of generations.**

The act at the bottom — the withdrawal — is the smallest thing in the list. It is a single, quiet decision by a single, quiet man, in a single afternoon, in a tent no one has ever found.

And from that smallest act, the ladder reaches into the future and does not stop climbing. The top rung is still climbing. Right now, in your mouth as you say his name, the ladder is still going up.

This is what the verb *iʿtazala* — Form VIII, reflexive, *he himself withdrew* — has been carrying the whole time. The agency is at the bottom of the ladder. The exaltation is at the top. The man who was thought to be losing was, in fact, climbing.

---

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic threads and the themes are saying the same thing from different angles.

**The verb *iʿtazala*** told us he was the agent.
**The verb *wahaba*** told us Allah gave by overflow, not by transaction.
**The phrase *lisān ṣidq ʿaliyyā*** told us that the true legacy comes from outside one's own management.

Together, they are one teaching:
> When you step out of the game the world is playing — by your own decision, for Allah's sake — you are not losing. You are stepping onto a different ground entirely, and on that ground, what is given to you cannot be calculated, cannot be earned, cannot be controlled, and cannot fade.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the *ʿiʿtizāl* — the withdrawal — that I am avoiding because the math looks bad?

2. Where am I treating my relationship with Allah as commercial — keeping a balance sheet of what I gave and what I am owed?

3. Which corner of my life am I trying to *manage* into a legacy, when I should be releasing it to Allah's handling?

4. If everything I tried to make my "name" out of disappeared tomorrow, what would be left of me on the lips of those who actually know me — and is it *ṣidq*?

5. Can I do one deed today that no one will ever know I did — and walk away from it cleanly, without the camera in my own mind recording it?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you leave behind what is yours for Allah's sake, He gives you back what is His — and what He gives is named, remembered, and exalted in the mouths of generations who never knew you.

---

### The Closing Invitation

There is a freedom in this ayah that most of us have not yet allowed ourselves to feel.

You do not have to manage how you will be remembered.
You do not have to make the math of your sacrifices balance.
You do not have to win in your father's economy.

You can withdraw, the way Ibrahim withdrew — quietly, with peace on your tongue, leaving without slamming the door — and you can let Allah handle the rest.

He has been handling it for thirty-five hundred years for the man who walked out of Ur. He will handle it for you.

> And as You raised the name of Your friend Ibrahim on the lips of generations who did not know him, place us — even in the smallest, truest way — among those whose mention is *ṣidq* in Your sight, long after we have gone silent.
