# Surah Maryam 12-15 — Take the Book With Strength

## The Verses

> يَـٰيَحْيَىٰ خُذِ ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ بِقُوَّةٍۢ ۖ وَءَاتَيْنَـٰهُ ٱلْحُكْمَ صَبِيًّۭا
> وَحَنَانًۭا مِّن لَّدُنَّا وَزَكَوٰةًۭ ۖ وَكَانَ تَقِيًّۭا
> وَبَرًّۢا بِوَٰلِدَيْهِ وَلَمْ يَكُن جَبَّارًا عَصِيًّۭا
> وَسَلَـٰمٌ عَلَيْهِ يَوْمَ وُلِدَ وَيَوْمَ يَمُوتُ وَيَوْمَ يُبْعَثُ حَيًّۭا

*Yā Yaḥyā khudhi'l-kitāba bi-quwwah. Wa ātaynāhu'l-ḥukma ṣabiyyā. Wa ḥanānan min ladunnā wa zakātan, wa kāna taqiyyā. Wa barran bi-wālidayhi wa lam yakun jabbāran ʿaṣiyyā. Wa salāmun ʿalayhi yawma wulida wa yawma yamūtu wa yawma yubʿathu ḥayyā.*

> O Yahya, take the Book with strength. And We gave him judgment as a child, and tenderness from Our very presence, and purity. He was deeply God-conscious, and dutiful to his parents — and he was not a tyrant, nor disobedient. Peace be upon him the day he was born, the day he dies, and the day he is raised alive.

---

## Where We Are in the Surah

Surah Maryam begins almost in a whisper. Five mystery letters — *Kāf Hā Yā ʿAyn Ṣād* — and then a single curtain lifts on a memory: *dhikru raḥmati rabbika ʿabdahu Zakariyyā*. The mention of your Lord's mercy to His servant Zakariyya.

We watch an old prophet, his bones gone weak, his head white as a flame, hide himself in the chamber of worship. We hear him call upon his Lord *nidāʾan khafiyyā* — with a hidden cry. We are told that Allah answers. We are told that he will be given a son. And we are told the name.

*"Yā Zakariyyā, innā nubashshiruka bi-ghulāmin ismuhu Yaḥyā lam najʿal lahu min qablu samiyyā."*
**"O Zakariyya, We give you good news of a boy whose name is Yahya. We have not made anyone bear that name before."**

A name no one had carried.

Yahya. *He lives.*

Then the surah does something we don't expect.

It doesn't show us the child growing up. It doesn't narrate the years between Zakariyya's prayer and what comes next. It cuts. It cuts directly from the announcement to a moment that arrives later — when the boy is old enough to receive a command. And we are placed inside that moment as if we are eavesdropping on the most intimate transaction in Yahya's life.

Allah Himself, addressing the boy by name.

## The Hook

There is a word in these four ayahs that occurs **nowhere else in the entire Quran**. Allah uses it once. He gives it to this one prophet. And He never uses it again.

He could have chosen a common word for love. He could have used the familiar word for mercy. He chose neither. He chose this rare word — and then He paired it with a phrase that marks the gift as one only Allah can give.

And there is something else strange. After listing the beautiful qualities Allah placed in Yahya — wisdom, this rare tenderness, purity, God-consciousness, devotion to parents — Allah adds two more qualities. But these two are **negative**. Not what Yahya was. What Yahya was *not*.

Why?

When Allah paints the portrait of one of His prophets, why does He need to tell us what he was not?

These ayahs are a painting. But like the great paintings, the meaning is not only in what is painted. It is in what the painter chose to leave unsaid, what he chose to repeat, and what he chose to source from a place we cannot reach.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Anchor One: *Khudhi'l-kitāba bi-quwwah* — Take the Book With Strength

Before we touch this command, let me ask you a small question.

When you give an instruction to a child, what do you usually leave unspecified?

If you tell a six-year-old, *"Hold this glass carefully,"* you don't usually have to explain *what kind* of carefully. The child can fill it in. They know what it means to hold something that breaks.

But if you tell that same child, *"Take this book with strength,"* they will probably look at you and ask: *with strength how?* With my arms? My grip? My mind? My heart? What kind of strength?

Allah opens these ayahs by addressing Yahya directly. Not "We told him." Not "We commanded him." A vocative. **Yā Yahya.** As if the surah pauses, the camera turns, and Allah speaks to the boy by name in the middle of telling us about him.

And the command itself is two words: *khudhi'l-kitāba bi-quwwah.* Take the Book with strength.

Notice what is missing.

He doesn't say *with the strength of your arms.* He doesn't say *with the strength of your mind.* He doesn't say *with the strength of resolve* or *the strength of memory* or *the strength of purpose.* He says *bi-quwwah* — **with strength** — and stops.

Stay with this.

In Arabic, when you want to specify a kind of something, you have many ways. You can use *iḍāfa*, you can add an adjective, you can attach a clarifying clause. Allah uses none of them. He leaves *quwwah* indefinite, unspecified, **open**.

Here's what is remarkable. The same phrase appears twice in the Quran in connection with the Book — once here for Yahya, and once in Surah al-Baqarah for Banī Isrāʾīl: *khudhū mā ātaynākum bi-quwwah.* Take what We have given you with strength. Both times, the strength is undefined.

This is not vagueness. This is **invitation**.

The Book of Allah is not engaged with one kind of strength. It demands the strength of your body when you stand at night. It demands the strength of your mind when you think about it. It demands the strength of your tongue when you recite it. It demands the strength of your heart when you sit with what hurts in it. It demands the strength of your character when you live it.

Allah does not narrow the command because the command should not be narrow.

The classical tradition offers a gloss that illuminates without closing the door. Al-Tabari records that Qatada and Mujahid — two of the great early commentators — both rendered *bi-quwwah* as *bi-jidd*: **with earnestness, with genuine diligence.** And al-Muyassar, reading these verses together, unpacks what that earnestness means in practice: preserving the words, understanding the meanings, and acting upon them. Three interlocking engagements. The strength of the tongue that recites. The strength of the mind that comprehends. The strength of the character that obeys.

The command is open. But the tradition is telling us: *the kind of strength Allah is asking for is the strength of serious, whole-hearted engagement.* Not the strength of force, but the strength that refuses to treat the Book lightly.

[PAUSE]

One more thing before we move on.

The "Book" here is specific. Al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir both note that the scripture Yahya was commanded to take is the Torah — the Book of the Children of Israel, which their Prophets and scholars used to teach and rule by. Yahya did not come into a vacuum. He came as a prophet to a people with a Book, sent to revive in them something that had grown cold. The command to take it *bi-quwwah* is the same command, in different words, that Musa received at Sinai. The Book was old. The earnestness had to be new.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the second half of the ayah.

*Wa ātaynāhu'l-ḥukma ṣabiyyā.*

And We gave him *al-ḥukm* — judgment, wisdom, the capacity to discern — *ṣabiyyā* — **as a child**.

The word *ṣabiyy* in Arabic is not a generic word for young. It is specific. It refers to a child still in the years of childhood. Some lexicographers say up to seven, some say until adolescence — but the meaning is unmistakable: this is not a teenager, not a young man. This is a child.

And what was given to the child? *Al-ḥukm.* The same root as *ḥākim* — judge. The same root as *ḥikmah* — wisdom. The capacity to weigh, to discern, to rule, to know what should be done. Al-Muyassar adds that alongside *al-ḥukm*, Yahya was given *ḥusn al-fahm* — excellent understanding, a quality of mind that perceives rightly — while still a child in years.

Let's sit with this.

In our world, we associate judgment with experience. Judgment is what comes after suffering, after mistakes, after years. We say *he has good judgment* about a man who has lived enough to know.

Allah says: We gave that to him as a child.

This is not a description of a precocious child. This is a description of a particular kind of soul Allah occasionally places in the world — a soul born already discerning. Yahya did not have to live through accumulated regret to know what mattered. He did not have to make the mistakes most of us make in our twenties to learn the lessons we learn in our thirties. The wisdom was there before the years could have given it to him.

Here is what this invites us to consider. Some of what we call *maturity* in our culture is actually just damage. We become careful because we have been hurt. We become wise because we have lost. We become humble because we have failed. There is nothing wrong with that path — it is the path most of us walk. But it is not the *only* path.

There is another kind of soul Allah sometimes gives the world. A soul that arrives discerning. A child who already knows. A young person who somehow understands what we are still trying to learn. We have all met one or two of them in our lives. They are not pretentious. They are not performing wisdom. The wisdom is simply *there*, the way some children are simply tall.

Allah is telling us: that is a gift, and that gift is from Him.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Anchor Two: *Ḥanānan min ladunnā* — Tenderness From Our Very Presence

Now we come to the strange word.

*Ḥanānan.*

This word occurs **once** in the entire Quran. Right here. In this ayah. About Yahya. And then never again.

The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — trace this word to the root *ḥ-n-n*. From this root come words like *ḥanīn* (the deep yearning of one who is far from home), *ḥannāna* (a she-camel who longs for her young), and the verb that describes the sound a mother makes when her child is hurt — that low, almost involuntary sound from the chest.

*Ḥanān* is not affection. It is not love in general. It is something more specific.

It is the kind of tenderness that *bends*. The kind that aches. The kind a mother feels when she hears her child cry from another room and her body responds before her mind has decided anything.

It is the tenderness of being **moved toward** something or someone. Not the tenderness of being kind in general. The tenderness that pulls.

Allah uses this word once, gives it to Yahya, and never uses it again.

Now ask yourself: why?

There are common Arabic words for love. *Maḥabbah*. *Wudd*. *ʿIshq*. There are common words for mercy. *Raḥmah*. *Raʾfah*. Allah uses these throughout the Quran. He could have said *we gave him love.* He could have said *we gave him mercy.* He didn't.

He chose a word so specific that He could only use it once.

[PAUSE]

It is worth pausing here to acknowledge that the classical scholars themselves disagreed about how to render this word in context — and their disagreement is illuminating rather than troubling. Al-Tabari records that Qatada read *ḥanānan* as *raḥmatan* — mercy. Al-Dahhak held the same, adding a gloss that sharpens the point: *raḥmatan min ʿindinā lā yamliku ʿaṭāʾahā ghayrunā* — "mercy from Us that no one else has the power to give." Other scholars retained the root's texture more directly, understanding *ḥanān* as the gentle, bending tenderness the lexical root describes.

These readings do not cancel each other. They illuminate different faces of the same gift. The root points to its *texture* — an aching, maternal, pulling tenderness. The mercy reading points to its *function* — a divine grace that cannot be manufactured by any creature, that descends only when Allah wills it. Both are true at once.

Stay with me — because the next phrase deepens this even further.

*Min ladunnā.*

This is not the ordinary phrase *from Us*. The ordinary Arabic for *from Us* is *min ʿindinā* — from Our presence. *ʿInd* is the standard word for *with* or *at*. It is what you would use to say *at my house* or *with my friend*.

*Ladun* is different from *ʿind*. *Ladun* signals closeness — a nearness that is not merely proximity but something more intimately sourced, a nearness from within the very sphere of divine origination. You find this same word in Surah al-Kahf, where Allah describes what al-Khiḍr was given: *raḥmatan min ʿindinā wa ʿallamnāhu min ladunnā ʿilmā* — mercy *from Us* and knowledge *from Our very presence*. Notice even there: the ordinary mercy is *min ʿindinā*, but the extraordinary knowledge — the kind no school could have transmitted — is *min ladunnā*. The word recurs in the Quran precisely at moments when what is being given cannot be traced to any ordinary channel, when the gift is something only Allah can originate and only Allah can bestow.

And al-Dahhak's gloss captures what *min ladunnā* is doing here with precision: *mercy from Us that no one else has the power to give.* The phrase is not merely marking the source. It is marking the **exclusivity**. This is a gift with no earthly substitute, no human equivalent, no channel through which it could arrive except directly from Him.

So now hold both halves together.

*Ḥanānan min ladunnā.*

A tenderness — aching, pulling, unlike ordinary love or ordinary mercy — **sourced from the very presence of Allah and marked as His alone to give.**

[PAUSE]

Here's what this invites us to consider.

There is a kind of tenderness in the world that is horizontal. Person to person. We feel it for our children. We feel it for our parents. We feel it for the wounded animal we find at the side of the road. This tenderness is real, and it is from Allah, and it is good.

But there is another kind of tenderness that is not horizontal. It is **vertical**. It descends. It is not learned by experience or earned by suffering. It is poured into a heart from a place above all hearts. And the person who has it cannot quite explain why their chest aches at the suffering of strangers, why they cannot pass a hungry person without being shaken, why their eyes fill at the cry of a child who is not theirs.

That kind of tenderness is *ḥanān min ladunnā*.

It is rare because it cannot be manufactured. You cannot decide to have it. You cannot grow into it the way you grow into discipline. It is not the result of anything you did.

And this is why Allah uses the word only once. Because the world only occasionally gets a soul like this. And when it does, the soul is sourced from above.

What this means for us — for those of us who are not Yahya — is that we do not need to despair if we feel the limit of our own tenderness. Most of us have to *work* on tenderness. We have to remind ourselves to be soft. We have to interrupt our irritation with deliberate compassion. That is normal, and it is not a defect. The point of these ayahs is not that we should all be Yahya. The point is that there is a higher form of this quality, and **it has a source**, and that source is Allah, and the way to draw nearer to it is not to manufacture our own tenderness but to ask Him for it.

You cannot squeeze *ḥanān* out of your own heart. But you can stand in front of the One it descends from, and ask.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Anchor Three: *Lam Yakun Jabbāran ʿAṣiyyā* — He Was Not a Tyrant, Nor Disobedient

Now we arrive at the strange addition.

After listing the gifts — *al-ḥukm*, *ḥanān*, *zakāt* (purity), *taqwā* (deep God-consciousness), *birr* (devotion to parents) — Allah adds a final phrase. But this one is different in shape from everything before.

*Wa lam yakun jabbāran ʿaṣiyyā.*

**And he was not a tyrant, nor disobedient.**

Stop.

Allah has been telling us what Yahya *was*. Now He tells us what Yahya *was not*.

In Arabic, when you negate — *lam yakun, lā yakūnu, laysa* — you are not merely removing a quality. You are doing two things at once. You are defining what *is* by naming what *isn't*. And more than that: you are telling us which dangers were real enough to name.

Allah doesn't say Yahya was not a coward. He doesn't say Yahya was not dishonest. He doesn't say Yahya was not ignorant.

He says Yahya was not a *jabbār*. And he was not *ʿaṣī*.

These two choices are not random.

*Jabbār* comes from the root *j-b-r*. This root holds a remarkable tension in Arabic. In the divine name *al-Jabbār*, it refers to the One who sets broken things right, who compels the cosmos into order, who is magnificently overwhelming in power. Applied to a human being, it curdles. The *jabbār* among people is the one who forces his will on others — who uses his position, his authority, his strength to compel, to dominate, to silence. He does not persuade. He does not invite. He crushes.

[PAUSE]

Look back at what Yahya had been given.

He had been given *al-ḥukm* — judgment. Authority. The capacity to rule. He had been given *quwwah* — strength, or rather, he had been commanded to use it. And he had been given a role as a prophet, which in the tradition of the Children of Israel meant he spoke with the weight of divine commission behind him.

Strength. Judgment. Divine authority.

These are precisely the ingredients from which tyrants are made, when they are assembled wrongly.

Allah is not just describing Yahya. He is making a distinction that runs through the entire Quran. Power without *ḥanān* becomes *jabbār*. Authority without *taqwā* becomes oppression. The one who receives gifts from Allah and uses them to dominate rather than to serve has missed the entire point of being given them.

Yahya had the gifts. He was not a *jabbār*.

The strength he used to take the Book — he did not turn that strength on the people. The judgment he was given as a child — he did not use it to lord over others. The wisdom and the tenderness were paired in him, just as they are paired in these ayahs: *ḥukm* and *ḥanān* in the same breath. And that pairing was not accidental. It was the architecture.

[PAUSE]

And the second negation: *ʿaṣī*.

From the root *ʿ-ṣ-y* — disobedience. *ʿAṣī* in Arabic is not describing a single mistake. It is describing a posture, a settled orientation. The *ʿāṣī* is not someone who slipped. The *ʿāṣī* is someone whose default relationship to command is resistance — who hears what is required and something inside him moves away from it rather than toward it.

Yahya was *taqī* — deeply, inwardly God-conscious. And he was not *ʿaṣī* — not defiant, not resistant, not someone who chafed at what Allah required of him.

The contrast is clean. *Taqwā* is not merely following rules out of fear. It is an interior alignment, a heart turned toward Allah. The outward sign of that alignment is: when a command comes, the *taqī* moves toward it. *ʿAṣī* is the opposite movement. The disobedient one hears the command and something inside resists, delays, deflects. Not necessarily in open rebellion. Sometimes just in that subtle, constant renegotiation.

Yahya had *taqwā*. So *ʿaṣī* had no room in him.

What makes both negations worth something beyond description is this: these qualities — *jabbār* and *ʿaṣī* — mark the two directions in which the gifted and the entrusted tend to fail. Toward others: they become tyrants. Toward Allah: they become defiant. Yahya, given everything that could have fed either tendency, was neither. The portrait Allah draws of him is a portrait of gifts that did not corrupt — wisdom that did not become arrogance, authority that did not become domination, closeness to Allah that did not drift into presumption.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Anchor Four: *Salāmun ʿalayhi* — The Threefold Peace

*Wa salāmun ʿalayhi yawma wulida wa yawma yamūtu wa yawma yubʿathu ḥayyā.*

Peace be upon him the day he was born, the day he dies, and the day he is raised alive.

Three moments. One salām over each.

And the three moments are not random. They are the three thresholds at which a soul is most exposed — most vulnerable to the terror that can seize a created being standing before what it has never faced before.

*Yawma wulida.* The day he was born. Birth is the first crossing. The soul is placed, without preparation and without choice, into a strange and overwhelming new world. Cold. Noise. Limitation. The sudden compression of whatever came before into this small, startled body. Peace upon him at that threshold.

*Yawma yamūtu.* The day he dies. This is the second crossing — the one we fear the most, the one that most of us will not see coming until it is already here. For Yahya, we know from Islamic tradition that this was not a gentle ending. He was a prophet who refused to remain silent in the face of injustice, and silence was what the powerful demanded. The man who was not a *jabbār* was killed by one. And yet: peace upon him at that threshold.

*Yawma yubʿathu ḥayyā.* The day he is raised alive.

*Ḥayyā.* Alive.

Stop there.

*Yaḥyā* — the name given before any of this happened — means *he lives*. It was a name no one had carried. It was given before the boy breathed his first breath. Before Zakariyya could hold him. Before the command came, before the gifts descended, before the portrait was painted. The name came first.

And now, at the very end of the portrait, after the birth and the death and everything in between, the surah closes with the same syllable the name contains.

*Yubʿathu ḥayyā.* Raised. **Alive.**

The name was always the destination.

[PAUSE]

This structure — the threefold salām over the three thresholds — appears again in Surah Maryam itself, twenty ayahs later, for ʿĪsā ibn Maryam: *wa salāmun ʿalayhi yawma wulida wa yawma yamūtu wa yawma yubʿathu ḥayyā.* The words are identical. The surah gives Yahya and ʿĪsā the same seal. Two prophets, two miraculous births, two lives marked by the same divine peace at every crossing.

But with Yahya, the resonance runs one layer deeper.

ʿĪsā's name does not mean *he lives*. Yahya's does.

The peace decreed over him on the day of his raising is not just a divine greeting. It is the fulfillment of a name pronounced before the world had any evidence that this boy would ever exist at all. Zakariyya prayed in secret. Allah answered with a name. And the name said: *whatever happens — he lives.*

The portrait began with a command: *take the Book with strength.* It ends with a peace that endures across every threshold a soul will ever face. Between those two poles — the command to engage and the peace that outlasts death — everything Allah placed in Yahya sits: wisdom given before experience could teach it, tenderness that no creature can manufacture, purity, God-consciousness, devotion to parents, and the quiet, unbreakable refusal to become a tyrant with any of it.

## Closing Reflection

These four ayahs move in one direction: from command to gift to portrait to seal.

*Khudhi'l-kitāba bi-quwwah.* You will engage. Earnestly. Fully. With every kind of strength you have.

*Ātaynāhu'l-ḥukma ṣabiyyā.* And the capacity for that engagement was not built by the years. It was given.

*Ḥanānan min ladunnā.* And alongside the clarity of judgment, He placed something that softened it — a tenderness that cannot be manufactured, that descends from the only source from which such things can come, marked as exclusively His to bestow.

*Wa kāna taqiyyā. Wa barran bi-wālidayhi.* He carried it inwardly, and it showed outward — toward Allah, and toward the ones closest to him.

*Wa lam yakun jabbāran ʿaṣiyyā.* And all that power did not corrupt. All that authority did not harden. The gifts stayed gifts, not weapons.

*Wa salāmun ʿalayhi.* And at every crossing: peace.

This is the portrait Allah chose to paint of Yahya ibn Zakariyya. A boy who arrived discerning. A soul in whom tenderness and judgment did not oppose each other but were given together, by the same Hand, in the same breath. A man who had the ingredients of a tyrant and became the opposite. A prophet who died at the hands of the power he refused to appease — and whom Allah sealed, before and after, with a name that means *he lives.*

[PAUSE]

The question these ayahs leave with us is not only about Yahya.

It is about what happens when we receive anything from Allah — a capacity, an authority, a gift we did not earn — and what we do with it. Do we use it to serve or to dominate? Do we carry it with the *ḥanān* that was paired with it at the source? Do we let the *ḥukm* we are given remain in the company of *taqwā*? Or do we discover, in the exercise of whatever we were given, the first faint movement toward becoming a *jabbār*?

Yahya is the answer Allah offers to a question every soul in authority eventually faces.

He was not a tyrant.

And he lives.
