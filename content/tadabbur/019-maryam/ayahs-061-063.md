# The Garden Promised in the Unseen — Where Laghw Goes to Die

## Surah Maryam, Ayahs 61-63

---

### The Ayahs

> **جَنَّـٰتِ عَدْنٍ ٱلَّتِى وَعَدَ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ عِبَادَهُۥ بِٱلْغَيْبِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ وَعْدُهُۥ مَأْتِيًّۭا**
>
> *Jannāti ʿadnin allatī waʿada ar-Raḥmānu ʿibādahu bi-l-ghayb. Innahu kāna waʿduhu maʾtiyyan.*
>
> **لَّا يَسْمَعُونَ فِيهَا لَغْوًا إِلَّا سَلَـٰمًۭا ۖ وَلَهُمْ رِزْقُهُمْ فِيهَا بُكْرَةًۭ وَعَشِيًّۭا**
>
> *Lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan illā salāman. Wa lahum rizquhum fīhā bukratan wa ʿashiyyan.*
>
> **تِلْكَ ٱلْجَنَّةُ ٱلَّتِى نُورِثُ مِنْ عِبَادِنَا مَن كَانَ تَقِيًّۭا**
>
> *Tilka al-jannatu allatī nūrithu min ʿibādinā man kāna taqiyyan.*
>
> "Gardens of perpetual residence which the All-Merciful has promised His servants in the unseen. Indeed, His promise must come.
>
> They will not hear therein any idle talk — only peace. And they will have their provision therein, morning and evening.
>
> That is the Garden which We give as inheritance to those of Our servants who were God-conscious."

## INTRODUCTION

### The World These Words Entered

Picture for a moment what surrounds these three ayahs.

Just before them, in 19:59-60, Allah laments a generation that came after the prophets — *"so there succeeded after them a generation who lost the prayer and followed their lusts."* A generation drowning in distraction, pulled apart by appetite, and one mark of their condition is that they **lost the prayer**. Not abandoned. *Lost.* As if it slipped through their fingers while they were busy with something else.

Just after these ayahs, in 19:64, the angels speak. *"And we do not descend except by the command of your Lord."* The cosmos opens. Heaven becomes audible.

Between these two moments — between a generation that lost the prayer and a heaven that announces itself — Allah places this description of Paradise.

[PAUSE]

And notice what He does *not* describe.

He does not list the foods. He does not name the rivers. He does not catalogue the palaces and the silken robes. The Quran does that elsewhere — beautifully — but not here. Here, in the middle of Surah Maryam, in the middle of a meditation on Ar-Raḥmān and what it means to be His servant, Allah describes Paradise by **what the soul will hear, what the soul will receive, and how the soul arrived there**.

He describes Paradise by what it *does to a tired person*.

### The Hook

There is a word in this passage that, if you sit with it long enough, will reframe what you think Paradise is *for*.

It is not "gardens." It is not "perpetual." It is not even "the All-Merciful."

It is one tiny, harsh-sounding word — three letters Allah negates in order to describe heaven. And the moment you understand what that word names, you will realize that this word is the air you have been breathing for years. It is the soundtrack of your phone. It is the noise inside your own head when you cannot sleep. It is the wound of dunya so constant you stopped feeling it.

We are going to find that word together. And then we are going to discover that the very *first* feature of Paradise — before the rivers, before the company, before the seeing of Allah's face — is that **this word goes silent**.

But before we can hear that silence, we have to walk into the architecture of the promise itself. So let us begin where Allah begins. With a word that contains the entire grammar of being a believer.

The word is **bi-l-ghayb**.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Bi-l-ghayb* — The Architecture of Believing What You Cannot See

Before we touch the ayah, let us understand what kind of word this is.

In Arabic, the preposition **bi-** is a workhorse. It can mean "with," "by means of," "in the state of," "concerning." It is intimate — it attaches to things. And **al-ghayb** is the great Quranic word for the unseen — the part of reality that is hidden from human perception. It comes from the root *gh-y-b*, which is the same root as the word for being absent, for being away, for the sun *setting* below the horizon. *Ghayb* is what has dipped below the line of your sight.

So **bi-l-ghayb** is literally: *in the state of unseen-ness*, or *while it is unseen*, or *by means of the unseen*.

Now here is something to teach yourself before we open the ayah. In any human language, when someone makes a promise to you, two questions live inside that promise. *Who is making it?* And *can the receiver verify it?* If your friend promises to pay you back next week, you can verify the promise next week. The promise lives, briefly, in the unseen — but soon it migrates into the seen.

Allah is now making a promise. And He is going to tell us *the entire structure* of how this promise relates to those who receive it — in two words.

[PAUSE]

Open the ayah:

> *Jannāti ʿadnin allatī waʿada ar-Raḥmānu ʿibādahu **bi-l-ghayb***.
>
> "Gardens of perpetual residence which the All-Merciful has promised His servants — **bi-l-ghayb**."

Where does this *bi-l-ghayb* attach?

This is the question the classical mufassirūn sat with. And here is what is remarkable — they did not split into a debate. They built a composite sketch. The phrase opens in two directions at once, and both directions are theologically true.

**One reading**: Ar-Raḥmān promised them *while the gardens were unseen* — the promise was made in a realm beyond human sight. This is the promise *from* the unseen.

**Another reading**: Ar-Raḥmān promised His servants *who believed in the unseen* — the *bi-l-ghayb* describes the servants, not the promise. These are the people who said, *we believe*, while seeing nothing. This is faith *into* the unseen.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Because this is not "two interpretations, take your pick." This is a single phrase doing two structural jobs at the same time, and **both jobs together describe what it means to be a believer**.

The promise comes *from* a place you cannot see.
The believer reaches *toward* a place he cannot see.
And in the middle, between the unseen Promiser and the unseen-believing servant, lives the relationship called *īmān*.

If the gardens were visible — if you could walk to a hilltop and see Paradise glittering on the horizon — then the people who turn toward it would not be *muʾminūn*. They would be *witnesses*. They would be calculating. The structure of belief itself **requires** the unseen-ness of the destination.

Allah, in two letters and three letters — *bi-l-ghayb* — has just told you that the architecture of your soul as a believer is built on what you do not see.

This is why the Quran begins, in Surah al-Baqarah's second ayah, *hudan li-l-muttaqīn alladhīna yuʾminūna **bi-l-ghayb*** — "guidance for the God-conscious, who believe in the unseen."

Surah al-Baqarah introduces the believers as "those who believe *bi-l-ghayb*."
Surah Maryam now closes the loop and tells you what is *waiting* for those who believed *bi-l-ghayb*: the Garden that was promised *bi-l-ghayb*.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to your relationship with the time you are living in right now.

Right now, in this moment, you cannot see Paradise. That is not a defect. **That is the design.** The unseen-ness of your reward is the *condition* of your reward being meaningful. Were you to see it, you would behave perfectly out of self-interest, and the entire moral universe of īmān would collapse into a transaction.

Allah is telling you: *I built the cosmos so that the people who matter most to Me are the ones who chose Me when nothing was visible.*

That is what *bi-l-ghayb* names.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Maʾtiyyan* — Why the Promise Is "Brought" and Not "Coming"

Now Allah, having made the promise, must address the question that lives in every human heart whenever a promise is made: *will it actually arrive?*

He says:

> *Innahu kāna waʿduhu **maʾtiyyan***.
>
> "Indeed, His promise — was — **maʾtiyyan**."

Translations render this as "must come" or "shall be fulfilled" or "is to be brought about." All three are circling a single grammatical fact.

Let us teach the structure first. In Arabic, from the root *ʾ-t-y* (to come), you can build two participles. The active one is **ātin** — "the one who comes" or "that which is coming." The passive one is **maʾtiyy** — literally, *that which is come-to, that which is brought, that which arrives by being delivered*.

Allah did not say *waʿduhu ātin* — "His promise is coming." He said *waʿduhu maʾtiyyan* — "His promise is *being brought*."

[PAUSE]

Sit with this difference.

A coming thing is something that moves toward you on its own. A storm is *coming*. A train is *coming*. The verb is intransitive — the agent is the thing itself.

A *brought* thing is something that someone is delivering. The grammar of *maʾtiyy* implies an agent behind the arrival. Someone is making sure this gets to its destination.

Allah is not saying, "The promise will somehow come about, like the weather." He is saying, *"This promise is being brought to its receivers — and the One bringing it is Me."*

The grammatical passive does what the entire surah of Maryam is doing — it places the agency of the cosmos squarely in the hands of Ar-Raḥmān. The promise does not *happen*. The promise is *delivered*. By the One who made it.

And there is something else.

The phrase opens with **innahu kāna** — "Indeed He / it was." That little word **kāna** is the verb of timeless being. When the Quran wants to describe an attribute that was, is, and always will be true of Allah, it uses *kāna*. *Kāna ʿalīman ḥakīma* — *He was, is, and remains All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

So the structure is:

> Indeed He / His promise — *was* — *being brought-to*.

The fulfillment of this promise is not a future event. It is a **standing fact about the universe**. From Allah's perspective, the promise has *already* been brought — because there is no time gap between the promise of the Eternal and its arrival. The waiting is on *our* side. From His side, *kāna maʾtiyyan*. It was — already — being delivered.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this invites us to consider.

When you wait for something Allah has promised — relief, mercy, the restoration of what was taken, the gardens themselves — you are not waiting for something that *might* arrive. You are waiting for something that, in the eternal vision of the One who promised, **has already begun being brought to you**.

The waiting is not the question of *whether*. It is only the question of *when, in your time-bound experience, the delivery will become visible*.

The delivery, in Allah's grammar, is already in motion.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan illā salāman* — The Word Allah Negates to Describe Heaven

We arrive now at the word I promised you. The word that, once you see it, you cannot unsee.

> *Lā yasmaʿūna fīhā **laghwan** illā salāman.*
>
> "They will not hear therein any **laghw** — only peace."

Listen, first, to the *sound*.

**Laghw.** *L-a-gh-w*. The letter *ghayn* in Arabic is one of the most physically uncomfortable sounds in the language — it is a guttural scrape, almost a gargle, made deep in the throat. The word *laghw* ends with that scrape. It is an acoustically ugly word.

Now listen to its opposite in the same ayah: **salām.** *S-a-l-ā-m.* The letter *sīn* opens the mouth, the *lām* glides off the tongue, the long *ā* breathes out, the *mīm* closes softly with the lips. *Salām* is the sound of exhale. *Laghw* is the sound of friction.

Allah, in this single ayah, **enacts the contrast in the sound itself.** The word for ugly speech *sounds* ugly. The word for peace *sounds* like peace.

[PAUSE]

So what is *laghw*?

The classical lexicons converge on a meaning that is hard to translate in one English word. *Laghw* is speech that is **vacuous, idle, of no consequence, that does not yield benefit, that does not contain truth, that wastes the listener's faculties**. It is talk that occupies the ear without nourishing the soul. It is the speech-equivalent of empty calories. It is the sound that fills space and leaves nothing behind.

In Surah al-Muʾminūn (23:3), Allah describes the believers in this dunya: *wa-lladhīna hum ʿani-l-laghwi muʿriḍūn* — "and those who turn away from *laghw*."

In Surah al-Furqān (25:72), the servants of Ar-Raḥmān: *wa idhā marrū bi-l-laghwi marrū kirāman* — "when they pass by *laghw*, they pass by with dignity."

In Surah al-Ghāshiyah (88:11) — describing Paradise itself: *lā tasmaʿu fīhā lāghiyatan* — "you will not hear therein any *laghw*-utterance."

The Quran is consistent. The believers turn away from *laghw* in this world. The believers in the next world will not hear it at all.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the structure of our ayah:

> *Lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan illā salāman.*

The Arabic grammar here is what scholars call *istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ* — a *disjunctive* exception. Salām is being "excepted" from *laghw*, but salām is not actually a *kind* of laghw. The exception is not "they hear no idle talk except this one specific kind of idle talk." The exception is **structural** — it is saying: *the only thing they hear, in the place where laghw would normally live, is peace.*

The grammar enacts the reality. *Laghw* has been so completely evicted from this place that even where one might *expect* to hear it, one hears only its opposite.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Because here is what is remarkable.

When Allah is going to describe Paradise — having infinite resources to draw upon, having the vocabulary of every glory — He chooses, as one of His **first** descriptions, the *absence of laghw*.

Not the *presence* of music. The *absence* of noise.

Not the *abundance* of conversation. The *eviction* of empty talk.

This invites us to consider that perhaps one of the deepest wounds of dunya — a wound so chronic you have stopped registering it as a wound — is the constant siege of *laghw* upon your ears, your mind, your nervous system. You are drowning in *laghw*. The notifications. The autoplay. The ambient podcast you barely listen to. The voice memo from someone who could have just sent two sentences. The argument in the comments section. The opinion you scrolled past. The sentence in your own head that has played eleven times today and meant nothing.

Allah is telling you: *one of the great gifts I have prepared for those who believed in the unseen is that this noise will go away. Forever.*

[PAUSE]

What replaces it? *Salām*. Peace. Wholeness. Soundness. The same root as *Islām*.

The first sound of Paradise is the sound of a soul finally being able to *hear itself think — and finding, when it does, that the One it is thinking toward is also the One greeting it.* Salām is what Allah Himself sends them, what the angels say to them, what they say to one another, what they hear from the rustling of the leaves. *Salām qawlan min Rabbin Raḥīm.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So we have walked through three linguistic doors.

*Bi-l-ghayb* — the unseen-ness that is the architecture of believing.

*Maʾtiyyan* — the promise that is not coming, but being *brought*, by the One who made it.

*Laghw* — the word Allah negates first, because the soul's deepest fatigue in dunya is the noise it could not turn off.

Each of these three has been about the *grammar of the relationship* between the believer and what Allah has prepared. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about the words — but about the realities they are pointing toward.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Wound You Stopped Feeling

I want you to do something with me.

Try, right now, to imagine a full hour of your life — any hour — in which you heard nothing other than truth, beauty, or words of consequence. No background music. No podcast. No notification ping. No one's vacuous opinion on the algorithm. No half-thought you were typing into a group chat. No autoplay video. No sentence in your own head running on the loop of someone else's drama.

[PAUSE]

You may have to go back years.

Some of us, if we are honest, have to go back to childhood. To a moment before the phone, before the input feed, before the constant low-grade hum of *something to attend to*. We have to go back to a time when our ears were quiet enough that the *adhān* could pierce us, when we could sit on a chair and just *be*, when our minds were not filled with twenty residual conversations from the day.

This is the wound of *laghw*. And the reason most of us have stopped feeling it is the same reason the man with chronic back pain forgets that pain is not the natural state of having a body. **You adapt to the wound. Your nervous system normalizes it. You begin to call the wound "life."**

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again.

> *Lā yasmaʿūna fīhā laghwan illā salāman.*
> "They will not hear therein any laghw — only peace."

Allah is not describing a luxury. He is describing **a healing**.

The ayah does not say, "We will give them more entertainment." It says, "We will *take away* the wound that has been chafing your soul since the first day you carried a device in your pocket."

The first thing Paradise does to a believer, according to this ayah, is **return them to the silence in which the soul can finally hear itself again**.

This is staggering. Because it means that one of the great gifts Ar-Raḥmān has prepared for you is, structurally, the *opposite* of what you are demanding from dunya. In dunya, you demand more input, more stimulation, more content. You are anxious when the noise stops. You reach for the phone the moment a moment of silence threatens to open.

Paradise is the place where that reaching ends.

[PAUSE]

Here is a way to live with this insight tomorrow:

The Quran says, *wa-lladhīna hum ʿani-l-laghwi muʿriḍūn* — "those who turn away from *laghw*." This is not just a description of Paradise's inhabitants in the next life. It is a description of the **practice** that prepares you for it.

You cannot, in dunya, achieve the *absolute* silence of Paradise. But you can build *enclosures* of silence. You can put down the phone for the half hour before Fajr. You can let a meal happen with no podcast. You can let a walk happen without earbuds. You can refuse the comment thread, scroll past the argument, end the conversation that has gone in circles for the third time.

Each of these refusals is a small act of faith *bi-l-ghayb*. You are choosing the silence of an unseen Paradise over the noise of a present dunya. You are practicing the citizenship of *jannāt ʿadn* before you live there.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing. Notice that this ayah comes *immediately after* Allah's lament in 19:59 about the generation that **lost the prayer**.

The connection is not accidental.

The generation that lost the prayer lost it to *laghw*. They followed their lusts. Their attention was eaten by the noise. They could no longer hear the call.

The generation that *inherits* the gardens is the one whose ears were not entirely consumed by *laghw*. Whose attention, in some small surviving corner, still belonged to Allah.

So the question this ayah asks you tomorrow is not, *"do I want Paradise?"* Of course you do. The question is, *"what is happening to my attention right now? And which inheritance is my listening preparing me for?"*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Inheritance That Goes Backwards

Now we come to the third ayah, which I have not yet opened with you. Look:

> *Tilka al-jannatu allatī **nūrithu** min ʿibādinā man kāna taqiyyan.*
>
> "That is the Garden which **We give as inheritance** to those of Our servants who were God-conscious."

The verb is *nūrithu* — from *w-r-th* — to make someone an inheritor. The same root as *mīrāth* (inheritance), *wārith* (heir).

Now think about how inheritance normally works. Inheritance is what passes from someone who *had* something to someone who *now has it because the first person no longer can*. It implies a transition — a leaving behind, a passing on.

[PAUSE]

But in this ayah, **Allah is the One giving the inheritance**, and Allah is not departing.

So what is this *inheritance*?

The mufassirūn unfolded it on multiple levels, and again, these are not competing readings — they are facets of one diamond.

**One layer**: every soul that enters Paradise was assigned a place in Hell had they chosen disbelief. And every soul that enters Hell forfeited a place in Paradise. So when the believer enters Paradise, they *inherit* the place that the disbelievers left behind. The inheritance is the architectural reallocation of the cosmos at the end of time.

**Another layer**: this dunya itself is built on inheritance. You inherit your parents' faith. You inherit a language, a tradition, a community of remembrance. The believer who held to that inheritance and added to it is the one to whom Allah, on the Last Day, says, *this is the Garden — and now you inherit it*.

**A third layer**, deeper still: connect this ayah to 19:40, just twenty-one ayahs earlier in the same surah. There, Allah said, *innā naḥnu narithu-l-arḍa wa man ʿalayhā* — "Indeed, We will inherit the earth and whoever is upon it." The earth is *inherited by Allah* from the dead. And here, the Garden is *given as inheritance by Allah* to the living.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means.

In dunya, **Allah inherits** — He outlasts everything; everything returns to Him.

In ākhirah, **Allah bequeaths** — He hands forward, to His servants, what He has prepared.

The same root, *w-r-th*, opens in both directions. Allah is the *Ultimate Heir* of all that was, and the *Ultimate Bequeather* of all that will be. The cosmos pivots on these two motions — and you, the believer, stand at the joint between them.

What you give up in dunya for His sake — what you let pass from your hands into His ownership — is what you find waiting for you, magnified beyond measure, when He bequeaths the Garden.

You did not lose the time you spent in prayer. You handed it to the Inheritor, who gave it back as a Garden.
You did not lose the *laghw* you turned away from. You handed it up. He gave back *salām* in its place.
You did not lose the dignity you held when others pursued lusts. You inherited *ʿadn* — *perpetual residence* — the place where you do not need to defend your dignity again.

[PAUSE]

And to whom does this inheritance go?

> *...min ʿibādinā **man kāna taqiyyan***.
> "...to those of Our servants who **were taqiyy**."

*Taqiyy* — from *w-q-y* — to *protect, to guard, to shield*. *Taqwā* is not abstract piety. It is, at root, **shielding yourself** — placing something between you and the thing that would harm your soul. The *taqiyy* is the one who built shields.

Notice the verb again: **kāna taqiyyan**. Was — is — God-conscious. The same *kāna* that we saw with Allah's promise. The promise of Ar-Raḥmān *was* being delivered. The servant *was* shielding himself.

Two timeless states answering each other across the surah. From eternity, Ar-Raḥmān has been delivering. From the duration of the believer's life, the believer has been shielding.

When these two timeless states meet — in the Garden — the inheritance is given.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Step back and look at what these three ayahs have built.

**Ayah 61** — The promise. *Bi-l-ghayb*. Made by Ar-Raḥmān. Delivered as *maʾtiyy*.

**Ayah 62** — The receiving. The eviction of *laghw*. The greeting of *salām*. The provision *bukratan wa ʿashiyyan* — morning and evening — the rhythm of being fed by the One who first promised the feeding.

**Ayah 63** — The revealing. *Tilka al-jannatu* — *that* is the Garden. The demonstrative pronoun points across the page from heaven down to us, and says: *the Garden you have just been hearing about — that one — is the inheritance.*

The three ayahs form a triangle. The promise is the foundation. The hearing of *salām* is one wall. The provision morning and evening is the other wall. And the inheritance to those who shielded themselves is the roof.

What does the triangle enclose?

It encloses **the soul that believed in the unseen, turned away from the noise, and shielded itself from what would harm it** — and is now, finally, home.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

*Bi-l-ghayb* taught you that the architecture of the believer is built on what cannot be seen. The theme of *laghw* taught you that one of the deepest wounds of dunya is what cannot be unheard. Both insights converge on a single truth: **the soul of the believer is shaped by what it refuses — refuses to demand visibility, refuses to consume noise — in trust that what Ar-Raḥmān is bringing is more real than what is visible and louder than what is audible.**

*Maʾtiyy* taught you that the promise is being delivered, not waiting to occur. *Nūrithu* taught you that the inheritance is being prepared, not held in reserve. Both verbs face you with the same fact: **from Allah's side, Paradise is already in motion toward you. The only question is whether you will be in motion toward it.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, in your day, does *laghw* enter your ears uninvited — and where do you invite it in? What would change if you treated each invitation as a small choice between two inheritances?

2. *Bi-l-ghayb* describes both how Allah promised and how the servant believes. In what specific area of your life are you currently being *asked* to trust *bi-l-ghayb* — to take a step whose outcome you cannot see?

3. The grammar of *maʾtiyy* says the promise is *being brought*. What promise of Allah have you been waiting for as if its arrival were uncertain — and how would your week look if you knew, instead, that it is already in motion?

4. *Taqiyy* means *one who shields*. What is one shield — one boundary between yourself and what harms your soul — that you know you should build, and have not yet built?

5. When you imagine Paradise, do you imagine it primarily by what it *adds* to your life, or by what it *removes*? What does the order Allah chose in this ayah — *salām* before food, silence before company — say to you?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Ar-Raḥmān has promised, from beyond your sight, a Garden where the noise that wounded you will go silent and the One who promised it will, by His own hand, deliver it to those who shielded themselves from harm.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to build Paradise. You have to *prepare to receive it*.

The ayah does not ask you to construct gardens of perpetual residence. You could not. It asks you to be the kind of servant who, when the silence finally falls, **recognizes the salām** — because you have been practicing the listening for years.

Build, this week, one enclosure of silence. Refuse one *laghw*. Trust one promise *bi-l-ghayb*. Each one is a small inheritance, claimed in advance.

### Du'ā

*Yā Raḥmān, You have promised what we cannot see, and Your promise was — is — being brought. Make us among Your servants who believed in the unseen, who shielded themselves from what harms the soul, who turned away from laghw, and who, on the day You bequeath the Garden, hear from You — and from Your angels — and from one another — only salām.*

*Allāhumma āmīn.*
