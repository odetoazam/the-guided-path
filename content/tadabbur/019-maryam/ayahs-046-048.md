# The Word "Perhaps": Ibrahim's Farewell to His Father

## INTRODUCTION

The three ayahs in front of us are, on the surface, an exchange between a father and a son. Ibrahim ʿalayhi al-salām has been speaking with his father — gently, persistently, lovingly — trying to draw him from the worship of stone to the worship of the One who made stone. We've already seen four of his entreaties earlier in this surah, each beginning with the word **"yā abati"** — "O my dear father." A diminutive of affection. A softness. The vocative of a small child looking up.

And now the father responds.

> قَالَ أَرَاغِبٌ أَنتَ عَنْ ءَالِهَتِى يَـٰٓإِبْرَٰهِيمُ ۖ لَئِن لَّمْ تَنتَهِ لَأَرْجُمَنَّكَ ۖ وَٱهْجُرْنِى مَلِيًّۭا
>
> *Qāla a-rāghibun anta ʿan ālihatī yā Ibrāhīm? La-in lam tantahi la-arjumannaka. Wa-hjurnī maliyyā.*
>
> "He said: 'Are you renouncing my gods, O Ibrahim? If you do not desist, I will surely stone you. So leave me — for a long time.'"

> قَالَ سَلَـٰمٌ عَلَيْكَ ۖ سَأَسْتَغْفِرُ لَكَ رَبِّىٓ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ بِى حَفِيًّۭا
>
> *Qāla salāmun ʿalayka. Sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī. Innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā.*
>
> "He said: 'Peace be upon you. I will ask forgiveness for you from my Lord. Indeed, He has ever been gracious to me.'"

> وَأَعْتَزِلُكُمْ وَمَا تَدْعُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ وَأَدْعُوا۟ رَبِّى عَسَىٰٓ أَلَّآ أَكُونَ بِدُعَآءِ رَبِّى شَقِيًّۭا
>
> *Wa-aʿtazilukum wa-mā tadʿūna min dūni-llāhi wa-adʿū rabbī. ʿAsā allā akūna bi-duʿāʾi rabbī shaqiyyā.*
>
> "And I will withdraw from you and what you invoke besides Allah, and I will call upon my Lord. Perhaps I will not be, in calling upon my Lord, wretched."

### Rebuilding the world

Before we go anywhere else, let's stand inside this scene.

Ibrahim is, in this moment, probably young. The classical accounts place him as a youth or young adult. He has spent his entire life inside his father's house — a house we now know was not just any house. Ibrahim's father, Āzar, was not merely a worshipper of idols. According to the historical traditions transmitted by the early commentators, he carved them. He made them. He sold them in the marketplace. The idols Ibrahim is being asked to leave behind are, in a literal sense, **his father's livelihood**. Ibrahim's monotheism is not just a theological dispute. It is an attack on the family business. It is a refusal to inherit the trade.

That's the social weight.

But there's a deeper weight underneath that. In the world Ibrahim inhabits, your father is not just your parent — he is your protector, your tribal anchor, your name, your access to land, marriage, water, justice. To break with your father is, in that society, to make yourself nothing. To unname yourself. To walk out of every social structure that holds a person upright.

And Ibrahim does it.

He does it gently, over many ayahs, in many surahs. He keeps trying. He calls his father "my dear father" four times. He says, "I fear that a punishment from the Most Merciful will touch you." He pleads with him as one pleads with someone they cannot bear to lose.

And then comes ayah 46. The father has had enough.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Now I want you to notice something before we go any further. The father, in his rage, uses one of the most violent words a parent can use: *la-arjumannaka* — "I will surely stone you." Not strike, not banish, not disown — *stone*. The death of a heretic. The death of someone outside the tribe.

Ibrahim's response, after this threat, contains a single word that I want you to keep in your ear for the next hour. The word, in Arabic, is **ʿasā**. In English: **"perhaps."** Or **"maybe."**

Ibrahim — *Ibrahim*, the *khalīl* of Allah, the friend of the Most Merciful, the one whose name is in the daily prayers of every Muslim until the end of time — Ibrahim, in the moment of his most heroic obedience, says **maybe**.

Maybe Allah will accept me.
Maybe I will not be wretched.

The man who has just been threatened with stoning, who is leaving everything, who is doing the right thing at maximum cost — he says **maybe**.

What kind of soul says *maybe* in a moment like that?

Stay with that question. We're going to circle it for a long time before we land.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to walk through three linguistic features in this passage. Each one is doing structural work. Each one rewards the time we spend with it.

### Element One: The asymmetry of "stone" and "peace"

Let me start with a question that has nothing to do with Arabic.

When someone screams at you, what is your first instinct?

Not your considered response. Not the response you would give if you'd had three days to pray over it. Your *first* instinct. The reflex.

Most of us — if we're honest — match the heat. If they raise their voice, we raise ours. If they accuse, we defend. If they threaten, we threaten back, or at least we go cold and defensive. There is a physics to human conflict, and the physics says: heat begets heat. Force begets force. Insult begets insult.

To break that physics, you have to be drawing energy from somewhere else. Some other source. Some other room.

Hold that thought.

Now look at what the father says in ayah 46. He uses three escalating words of violence:

**Word one: *rāghibun ʿan*** — "renouncing." The verb here, **r-gh-b**, is a strange one. In Arabic, *raghiba fī* means "to desire," "to want passionately." But *raghiba ʿan* — the same root, with a different preposition — means the *opposite*. To turn away from something with rejection, even disgust. The father picks the harshest framing possible. He doesn't say, "Are you doubting my gods?" He doesn't say, "Are you having questions?" He says: "Are you turning away from them — *with disgust*?"

Notice he says "**my** gods." Not "the gods" or "our gods." *My* gods. He's making this personal. To reject the idols is to reject *him*.

**Word two: *la-arjumannaka*** — "I will surely stone you."

Here's the grammar. In Arabic, when you want to make a future statement absolutely emphatic, you do three things at once: you put a *lām* of emphasis at the front of the verb, and a *nūn* of emphasis at the end of it, and you bring the pronoun in tight. *La-arjumanna-ka.* Three layers of emphasis stacked on one word. The closest English translation isn't "I will stone you." It's something like, "**I swear to God I will stone you. I will absolutely, certainly, without question, stone you.**"

This is not a slap or a strike. The verb **r-j-m** specifically means stoning — to throw stones, often to death. It's the punishment for the heretic, the outsider, the one beyond the tribe. The father is telling his son: *if you keep this up, I will not just disown you — I will treat you as an enemy of the community.*

**Word three: *wa-hjurnī maliyyā*** — "and leave me, for a long time."

The verb **hajara** means to abandon, to leave, to break with. It's the same root as *hijra* — the great migration. And the word *maliyyā* comes from a root meaning a long stretch of time. Some classical commentators read it as "for a long while." Others read it as "forever" — for the rest of your life. Either way, it's a banishment.

Stack those three together and you have:

*"You are turning away from my gods with disgust — if you don't stop, I will execute you — get out of my sight forever."*

That's what's coming at Ibrahim.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what comes back.

**"*Salāmun ʿalayka.*"** Peace be upon you.

I want you to feel the size of that response.

The father has just threatened to kill him. The father has just expelled him from the family. The father has just made the relationship a matter of life and death. And Ibrahim's first word — his very first word — is **peace**.

Notice the grammar. He doesn't say *wa-ʿalayka* — "and upon you also" — which is what you would say if someone *gave* you peace and you were returning it. The father did not give peace. The father gave the opposite of peace. So Ibrahim is not *returning* a greeting. He is *initiating* one. From a place of zero provocation, with zero need to be polite, in the middle of a death threat — he initiates peace.

Why?

Stay with this.

The classical commentators — Ibn ʿAṭiyya, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī — read this *salām* in different ways. Some say it's a *salām al-mufāraqa*, the peace of farewell — the kind of greeting you give when you are leaving someone forever. Others say it's a *salām al-mutāraka*, the peace of disengagement — *I am stepping back from this fight, and I will not match your violence with mine*. Others say it carries the meaning of *amān* — "you are safe from me." *I will not return what you threatened.*

These are not three different opinions. They are three different angles on the same act. Ibrahim is saying, all at once: I am leaving, I will not fight you, you have nothing to fear from me.

But here's what I want you to notice. Here is the thing that makes this *salām* possible.

Ibrahim has somewhere else to go.

If he had no other source of belonging, no other room to draw energy from, he could not have responded this way. To match insult with insult is the response of someone who has nothing else. Someone who *is* their reputation, *is* their tribe, *is* their inherited identity. When you attack what they are, they have to attack you back — because if they don't defend it, they cease to exist.

But Ibrahim is no longer that person. Ibrahim has, at this point, met **al-Raḥmān**. He has had the conversation we read about in Surah al-Anʿām, where he watched the star set, the moon set, the sun set, and concluded: *I do not love things that disappear.* He has, by now, been emptied of the inheritance. So when his father threatens to kill him, the father is not actually threatening anything Ibrahim is still carrying.

The father can take the body. The father can take the name. The father can take the inheritance, the trade, the standing in the tribe. But the father cannot reach the place Ibrahim now lives.

Which is why Ibrahim can say *salām*.

This is the first lesson of these ayahs, and we have to sit with it before we go further. **The peace of Ibrahim is not weakness. It is not appeasement. It is not "being the bigger person." It is the calm of someone whose treasure has been moved out of the burning building.** The father is shouting at an empty house.

[PAUSE]

So that's the asymmetry I want you to feel. Three escalating words of violence on one side: *rāghib*, *arjumannaka*, *uhjurnī maliyyā*. One word on the other side: *salām*.

And the *salām* is bigger.

### Element Two: The word that explains everything — *ḥafiyyā*

Right after the *salām*, Ibrahim says something that almost slips past us in English:

**"*Sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī. Innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā.*"**

"I will ask forgiveness for you from my Lord. Indeed, He has ever been gracious to me."

I want to slow down on the second sentence. Specifically, on one word: **ḥafiyy**.

In English we render it as "gracious," or "kind," or "attentive." All of those are right, but all of them are thin. Let's open the Arabic.

The root is **ḥ-f-w**, sometimes **ḥ-f-y**. The classical lexicographers — the ones who built the Arabic dictionaries from desert speech and pre-Islamic poetry — describe it like this. *Ḥafī* is not just "kind." *Ḥafī* is the kindness of someone who keeps asking. The host who keeps refilling your cup before you've asked. The one who notices you've been quiet at the gathering and comes over to sit with you. The friend who knows you didn't sleep last night without you telling them. The relative who, every time you walk into the room, gets up to make sure you have what you need.

It is the kindness of *attentiveness*. The kindness of being *seen and tracked*.

Al-Rāghib in *Mufradāt al-Qurʾān* glosses it as the one who shows *biʿinaya* — care, concern, attention — to the point of insistence. Lisān al-ʿArab connects it to *ḥafāwa* — the warmth of welcome, the lavishness of hospitality, the going-beyond-what-is-required.

Now hold this in your mind: *ḥafī* is not the kindness of *one act*. It is the kindness of *a thousand small acts that add up*. The kindness of someone who has been showing up, repeatedly, for a long time.

And now look at how the verb is conjugated:

**"*Innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā.*"**

That **kāna**. That past-tense auxiliary. In Arabic, when you say *kāna* with another descriptor, you're not just saying "He is." You're saying "**He has been, continuously, for as long as I can remember.**" *Kāna* gives the description a duration that runs back into the past and continues into the present. It's a way of saying: *this isn't a recent thing. This isn't one event. This is who He has always been with me.*

So when Ibrahim says *innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā*, what he is saying — slowly, in full — is:

*"He has been, for as long as I have existed, the kind of attentive, lavish, repeatedly-showing-up Lord that I have never had to be alone."*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight of that?

Now place it next to where it appears.

He says it *immediately after* his father has threatened to kill him.

He says it *immediately before* he walks out of the house.

What is the *function* of this sentence in the architecture of Ibrahim's response?

Here's what I want us to see: Ibrahim is not just reporting a theological fact about Allah. He is *explaining how he is able to do this.* He is telling his father — and through him, telling us — *how* he is able to walk away from everything.

The answer is: *I am not actually walking away from everything. I am walking toward someone who has been waiting for me my whole life.*

The father offered Ibrahim violence and banishment. Allah has been offering Ibrahim *ḥafāwa* — that lavish, attentive welcome — since before Ibrahim could name what was happening. The exchange is not loss for loss. It is exit from a small, hostile room into a vast, welcoming hall that has, this whole time, had a place set for him.

This is the second piece of architecture you have to feel. **Ibrahim's ability to say** *salām* **and his ability to say** *aʿtazilukum* **— "I am withdrawing from you" — flow directly from his belief that he is not, and has not ever been, alone.** *Innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā.* That sentence is the floor under his feet. Without it, the whole moment collapses.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing I have to say about this word before we move on.

Notice what Ibrahim does *not* say. He does not say *Allāh kāna bī ḥafiyyā.* He says *Rabbī kāna bī ḥafiyyā* — "**my Lord** has been gracious to me."

The word *Rabb* in Arabic does not just mean "Lord" in the sense of master. The root *r-b-b* means *to nurture, to raise, to bring something to its completion*. *Rabb al-bayt* is the head of the household. *Murabbī* is the one who raises a child. *Rabb* is the one who, over time, takes something small and unfinished and tends it into what it is supposed to be.

So when Ibrahim says *rabbī kāna bī ḥafiyyā*, he's saying: *the One who has been raising me, the One who has been bringing me into who I am supposed to be, has been doing it with the kindness of someone who keeps refilling my cup before I have to ask.*

This is the relationship he is leaving his father's house *for*. Not just for Allah-as-Sovereign. For Allah-as-Rabb. For the one who has been *parenting his soul* the entire time the human father was failing him.

And that — to bring it home — is why Ibrahim is not actually losing a father in this scene. He is finally being free to acknowledge the Father who has been his Father all along.

### Element Three: The word "perhaps"

Now we come to the heart of the passage. The closing line of ayah 48. The moment we've been circling since the introduction.

**"*ʿAsā allā akūna bi-duʿāʾi rabbī shaqiyyā.*"**

"Perhaps I will not be, in calling upon my Lord, wretched."

I want to teach you a small piece of Arabic before we open this line, because without it, the line cannot land.

In Arabic, there is a verb-particle called **ʿasā**. It is one of a small family of verbs that grammarians call *afʿāl al-rajāʾ* — verbs of *hope* or *expectation*. *ʿAsā* introduces a clause and gives it the flavor of: *perhaps, maybe, it is hoped that, it may be that.* It does not assert. It does not promise. It opens a door without claiming to walk through it.

Now here is the strange thing about *ʿasā*. It appears in the Quran on the lips of human beings, and it appears on the lips of Allah Himself. And the classical commentators — al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī, Ibn ʿĀshūr — all note something striking. **When *ʿasā* comes from Allah, it carries the force of certainty.** Allah does not actually "hope" — He knows. So when Allah says *ʿasā*, it is, in effect, a promise wrapped in the softness of a *maybe*.

But when *ʿasā* comes from a human being, it is *real* hope. It is the human acknowledgment that *I do not control the outcome*. That *I am asking, but I am not collecting*. That *I am pointing toward something I cannot guarantee*.

Now look at who is using *ʿasā* in this ayah.

Ibrahim.

The man who has just survived his father's threat with grace. The man who is about to walk out into exile. The man who, in the structure of the surah, is being placed on the same architectural shelf as Maryam and Zakariya as one of the great souls of Allah. The man who in a few ayahs from now will be called by Allah Himself *ṣiddīqan nabiyyā* — a man of supreme truth, a prophet.

*That* man uses the word **maybe**.

[PAUSE]

Let's slow down on what comes after *ʿasā*.

**"*Allā akūna*"** — "that I not be." The construction is *an* + *lā* + the negation of the verb *akūn* ("I am"). So Ibrahim is layering negation on top of hope: *perhaps I might not be.* Already, in the grammar, he is hedging. He is leaving doors open. He is not predicting; he is asking.

**"*Bi-duʿāʾi rabbī*"** — "in the *duʿāʾ* of my Lord." The *bā* here is what classical Arabic calls *bā al-mulābasa* — the *bā* of accompaniment, of being clothed in something. It can also be read as *bā al-sababiyya* — the *bā* of cause. So Ibrahim is saying either *in the act of calling upon my Lord* or *because of my calling upon my Lord*. In either reading, *duʿāʾ* — the act of asking, of calling, of turning in supplication — is the medium through which this whole thing happens.

**"*Shaqiyyā*"** — and here we hit the bottom of the ayah.

The root **sh-q-y** is one of the heaviest in the Quran. It is the opposite of **s-ʿ-d** — the root of *saʿīd*, "blessed, happy, fortunate." *Shaqī* means: wretched, unfortunate, the one whose striving has come to ruin, the one whose efforts have ended in failure. Most heavily, in the Quranic vocabulary, it is the word used for the people of the Fire. In Surah Hūd (11:105), Allah divides creation on the Day of Judgment into *shaqiyyun wa-saʿīd* — the wretched and the blessed. *Shaqī* is the destination of the unsaved.

And so here is the full force of what Ibrahim is saying:

*"Perhaps — perhaps — by the act of calling upon my Lord, I will not be among the wretched."*

[PAUSE]

I want you to sit with this. Really sit with it.

This is *Ibrahim*.

This is the man Allah will, by the end of his life, call *khalīl* — "intimate friend." This is the man whose progeny includes Ismaʿīl, Isḥāq, Yaʿqūb, Yūsuf, Mūsā, Dāwūd, Sulaymān, ʿĪsā, Muḥammad ﷺ. This is the man whose name is in every *tashahhud* of every prayer of every Muslim every day until the world ends.

And he is saying — at the high noon of his obedience, in the most heroic moment of his life, having just done the bravest thing a young man can do — *I might still be wretched. Maybe I will not be. Maybe.*

What does this tell us?

Here is the linguistic claim, grounded in the grammar. Ibrahim is not expressing doubt about Allah. He is not expressing doubt about the truth of monotheism. He is not even expressing doubt about whether his cause is right. He is expressing *doubt about himself*. Specifically, he is expressing the refusal to *count his own obedience as a guarantee of acceptance*.

The father is certain. He is sure he is right, sure his gods are real, sure he can stone Ibrahim and be vindicated. The father has no *ʿasā*. The father has only certainty.

Ibrahim, the one who is right, has *ʿasā*.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most counterintuitive truths in the entire Quran, and the linguistic architecture is exactly the proof of it. **The closer a soul gets to Allah, the more it suspects itself. The further a soul gets from Allah, the more it trusts itself.** The disbeliever is sure. The believer is unsure. Not unsure of the truth — unsure of *whether they have lived up to the truth*.

This is why ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, on his deathbed, said *layta ummī lam talidnī* — "I wish my mother had not given birth to me." This is why the Companions, the *Saḥāba*, were so terrified of *nifāq* — hypocrisy — that they would weep about whether the trace of it was in their hearts. This is why every generation of righteous people produces souls who spent their entire lives in *khawf wa-rajāʾ* — fear and hope — never permitting themselves to settle into "I am saved."

And it begins, in our text, with a single word from a young man being threatened by his father.

*ʿAsā.*

Maybe.

[PAUSE]

Let's add one more layer. Notice what *ʿasā* sits next to.

In the same line, Ibrahim says *adʿū rabbī* — "I will call upon my Lord." That's the act. That's what he's committing to. The *duʿāʾ* itself.

Then, immediately, he qualifies it: *bi-duʿāʾi rabbī* — "in the *duʿāʾ* of my Lord." The same word, repeated, this time on his own lips, this time as the *medium* of his hope.

And then: *ʿasā allā akūna shaqiyyā* — "perhaps I will not be wretched."

So the architecture of the line is:

*I will call → in calling → perhaps I will not be wretched.*

Three steps. Each step softer than the last. Each step less assertive. He starts with an action verb — *adʿū*, "I will call" — but as the sentence proceeds, the certainty drains out. By the end, he is barely making a claim. He is *hoping*.

This is the linguistic shape of *humility before Allah*. You start with the will to act. You proceed into the act. You end with *I do not know if I will be received.*

That shape — that descending humility within a single sentence — is a model. It is a way of *being* before Allah. Many of us, when we do something good, ascend the other way. We start with *I tried*, we proceed to *I think I did okay*, and we end at *yes, surely Allah will count this for me.* We climb into certainty.

Ibrahim does the opposite. He climbs *out* of it.

That is what *ʿasā* does in this ayah. It is the linguistic gesture of refusing to claim what is not yours to claim.

### Bridge

So now we have three pieces. We have the asymmetry of *rajm* and *salām*. We have the word *ḥafī* — the lavish, attentive *Rabb* who has been there all along. And we have *ʿasā* — the maybe of the man who is right.

Three discoveries. All of them flowing from the same instant: a son walking out of his father's house.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Leaving as the deepest act of love

I want to start somewhere that has nothing to do with Ibrahim, because I think most of us are carrying some version of this in our own lives.

There is a particular kind of pain that comes from loving someone who cannot meet you where you are.

Maybe it's a parent whose love came with conditions you couldn't fulfill. Maybe it's a sibling who chose a path you could not walk. Maybe it's a community — a religious community, a family system, a friend group — that asked you to be smaller than you were in order to remain inside it. Maybe it's a culture, an ethnicity, a country, that gave you everything except the room to grow.

If you have been there, you know the shape of it. You know the strange impossibility of it. The people who hurt you the most are often the people you love the most. And the choice to leave — *if* you ever make it — is not the absence of love. It is, in some terrible way, the *expression* of it. You are leaving because to stay would corrode you both. You are leaving because the version of you that they could accept is a version of you that should not exist.

This is the moment Ibrahim is in.

[PAUSE]

I want you to notice something subtle in the text. Throughout the surah, in the four entreaties before this scene, Ibrahim addresses his father with the word *yā abati* — "O my dear father." It's the diminutive. It's tender. The *tāʾ* at the end softens the word into something almost childlike. *Father, my dear one. Father, my own.*

His father, in ayah 46, addresses him as *yā Ibrāhīm.* Just his name. Cold. Formal. A summons.

That asymmetry — *yā abati* on one side, *yā Ibrāhīm* on the other — *is the love asymmetry of the entire scene.* Ibrahim is still calling him *my dear father*, even as the father is detaching, even as the father is escalating, even as the father is preparing to throw him out.

And then, after the father threatens to stone him, Ibrahim — and this is a quiet thing, you have to listen for it — Ibrahim *stops* using *yā abati*. He doesn't use it again. The next words out of his mouth are *salāmun ʿalayka.* He has accepted, finally, that he cannot reach this person. He has lowered the appeal. He has stopped trying to soften the word.

The drop from *yā abati* to *salāmun ʿalayka* is the sound of a son giving up on being able to save his father. Not giving up on loving him. Just giving up on being able to save him.

[PAUSE]

This is what the surah wants us to feel. **Love is not always the thing that keeps you in the room. Sometimes love is the thing that takes you out.**

If Ibrahim had stayed — if he had bent, just a little, just to keep peace — the entire history of monotheism would have collapsed. The promise to him, which is the promise to Ismaʿīl, which is the promise to Muḥammad ﷺ, which is the promise to us, sitting here, reading this — *all of that* depended on Ibrahim being willing to walk out.

So when Ibrahim says *sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī* — "I will ask forgiveness for you from my Lord" — that is the form his love takes after the door closes. He cannot stay. He cannot save his father by staying. So he loves his father in the only way that is left: he carries him, in his *duʿāʾ*, into the presence of Allah. *I cannot fix this between us, but I will not stop bringing you before Him.*

That is the love that survives the leaving.

(There is a later moment, in 9:114, where we learn that Ibrahim eventually stopped doing this — *when it became clear to him that his father was an enemy of Allah*, he relinquished the *istighfār*. So the *istighfār* was not unconditional. It had a horizon. There was a point at which Ibrahim accepted that even his prayer could not bridge the gap. But until that point — for years, perhaps decades — he prayed for him. He carried his father to Allah long after his father had thrown him out of the house.)

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back to your own life.

There are people in our lives who cannot meet us where we are. Maybe they cannot meet us in our practice. Maybe they cannot meet us in our growth. Maybe they cannot meet us in the truth of who we are. And we have a choice. We can shrink ourselves to fit their tolerance. Or we can leave the room.

The choice to leave is not a failure of love. **The failure of love is to stay in a room where staying makes both of you smaller.** Ibrahim shows us a third option: leave the room *while still carrying the person in your prayers*. Walk out *with peace on your lips*. Do not match the violence. Do not match the threat. Refuse to descend, but also refuse to harden. Pray for them in the dark, and let Allah be the one who sorts the rest.

The practical application: if there is someone in your life right now who is hurting you — not someone you can dismiss, not someone you can simply cut off, but someone you genuinely love who is asking you to be smaller — sit with this passage. *Salāmun ʿalayka. Sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī. Wa-aʿtazilukum.* That sequence — *peace, prayer, withdrawal* — is a complete spiritual technology for one of the hardest human situations there is. Peace, so you do not become what hurt you. Prayer, so the love is not lost. Withdrawal, so the corrosion stops.

You can love someone deeply and still need to leave. Ibrahim shows us how.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The humility of the saved

Now let's turn to the harder theme. The one underneath the *ʿasā*.

I want to start with a story that is not in the Quran, just to set the scene.

There is a hadith in al-Bukhārī where the Prophet ﷺ enters the mosque after the death of a Companion named ʿUthmān ibn Maẓʿūn. A woman is grieving over him, and she says — out of the love and pain of losing this righteous man — "*shahida ʿalayka rabbuka bi-l-khayri yā ʿUthmān*." She testifies, in essence: *Allah has surely given you good, ʿUthmān. Surely you are with the saved.*

The Prophet ﷺ corrects her gently. He says: *and what makes you certain Allah has done this? By Allah, even I, the Messenger of Allah, do not know what will be done with me.*

[PAUSE]

Stop and feel that for a second.

The Messenger of Allah ﷺ — the man whose every sin, past and future, has been forgiven by explicit revelation — *says he does not know what will be done with him.* Not as theology. As a felt truth. As a way of carrying the soul.

This is the lineage Ibrahim's *ʿasā* belongs to.

The closer a person is to Allah, the more they *cannot* stand being certain about themselves. They know too much. They have seen too much of their own heart. They know about the small acts of vanity that crept into their devotion. They know about the times they thought they were doing it for Allah and were really doing it for the eyes of others. They know how *thin* their best moments are. So when they look at their own deeds, they cannot find a foundation solid enough to plant a flag of certainty on.

They keep their feet on the floor of *maybe*.

This is not pessimism. This is not despair. It is a particular kind of seeing — a sober seeing of one's own work — that the careless soul never develops.

[PAUSE]

Compare this to the modern emotional landscape we live in.

We live in a culture that runs on *certainty*. Certainty about our identities, our feelings, our righteousness. We are taught that healthy people are *confident*. That to doubt yourself is to be weak. That to question whether your good deeds are good enough is to fall into religious neuroticism.

Many sincere believers have absorbed this and run it into their religious life. They do something good — they give a charity, they make a *duʿāʾ*, they finish a *khatma*, they pray *tahajjud* once — and the immediate inner movement is: *Allah will accept this. I have done a good thing. This counts.*

It might be true that Allah accepts it. But notice: *the certainty is ours, not given.* We have appointed ourselves the auditors of our own deeds. We have read our own ledger and approved the entry.

Ibrahim does the opposite. He has just done the most heroic thing of his young life. He has stood up to his father. He has refused to bend. He has been threatened with stoning and responded with peace. He has chosen Allah at maximum cost.

And he says: *maybe.*

*Maybe* I will not be wretched.

He does not approve his own ledger. He hands the ledger back to its rightful Owner.

[PAUSE]

This is a hard thing to absorb, so let me come at it from another angle.

There are two great enemies of the soul in the spiritual tradition. The first is *despair* — the conviction that you are too far gone to be saved. The second is *security* — the conviction that you are already saved, that the matter is settled, that your good standing is beyond question.

Most religious traditions, including ours, see *security* as more dangerous than *despair*. Despair, at least, knows it has a problem. Security has stopped looking. Security has filed away the question. Security treats salvation as something already in the bank account, accumulating interest while the soul drifts.

The Quran calls this *amn min makr-illāh* — feeling safe from the cunning subtlety of Allah's plan. In Surah al-Aʿrāf (7:99), Allah says: *only the people destined for ruin feel secure from Allah's tadbīr*. To assume your acceptance is to misread your own relationship with the One who, until the very last breath, is the only One who can grant or withhold.

Ibrahim's *ʿasā* is the lifelong refusal of *amn*. It is the refusal to settle the question early. It is the choice to carry the question — *am I being received? am I being heard? am I, after all this, going to be among the *saʿīd* or the *shaqī*? — all the way to the meeting with Allah.

[PAUSE]

But here is the part that breaks open. Here is what makes this *not* a doctrine of fear.

Look at the *ʿasā* in context. It does not appear in a vacuum. It appears immediately after the sentence:

**"*Innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā.*"**

He has been gracious to me. Lavishly, attentively, repeatedly gracious to me.

So Ibrahim's hope structure is: *I know He has been ḥafī with me my whole life. So perhaps — perhaps — He will receive my call now too. I do not assume it. But I have history with Him. I have a long ledger of His kindness, even if I cannot guarantee mine.*

That's the architecture.

The hope is *not* in his own deeds. The hope is in *Allah's track record with him.* The *ḥafāwa* of the past becomes the basis for the *rajāʾ* of the present. *He has shown up for me before. I am asking — perhaps — He will show up again.*

This is the deepest movement of Ibrahim's heart in this scene, and we have to see it: **his hope is anchored in Allah, not in himself.**

That is why the *ʿasā* is not crushing. That is why it does not produce despair. He is *uncertain about his own worthiness*, but he is *certain about Allah's pattern*. The two run side by side. He is humble about his deeds but confident about his Lord. He says *maybe* about himself but says *kāna bī ḥafiyyā* about the One who decides.

Read together, the line is: *The One who has been kind to me my whole life — perhaps He will not let my call to Him end in wretchedness.*

[PAUSE]

Now bring it home.

How do we live?

Most of us live the inversion. We are *certain* about ourselves and *uncertain* about Allah. We are sure our deeds count. We are unsure whether He really hears us, really cares, really bothers with someone like us. Our self-confidence is up; our trust in Him is down.

Ibrahim teaches us to flip the polarity. **Be uncertain about yourself. Be certain about Him.** Let your ledger stay closed. Let your assumptions about your own status go quiet. And then *trust His pattern.* Trust that the same Allah who got you out of bed this morning, who put this language in front of you, who taught your tongue how to say His name, who has been *ḥafī* with you for every day you can remember and every day you cannot — trust that *He* will not, suddenly, abandon you when you turn toward Him in your moment of need.

That is the structure of mature *rajāʾ*. That is the hope of the saints. *Maybe* about myself. *Surely* about Him.

The practical application: the next time you do something good — a real act of obedience, a hard *salāh*, a sincere *duʿāʾ*, a difficult forgiveness — try this. Instead of letting your heart settle into "I have done well," try saying, in Arabic or in English, *ʿasā an yataqabbala minnī* — "perhaps He will accept it from me." Hold the matter open. Refuse to seal the ledger. Trust the One who reads it.

And see what this does to your soul over time.

It produces a quieter person. A more humble person. A person who can do great things without needing to feel like a great person. Who can withdraw from a hostile father without feeling proud about it. Who can say *salāmun ʿalayka* to someone who threatened them and not collect a trophy. Who can spend an entire life in the *khidma* of Allah and arrive at the meeting with the words *ʿasā* still on their lips.

[PAUSE]

That is the inheritance Ibrahim leaves us. Not just the breaking with the father — but the way he broke. Not just the leaving — but the spiritual posture inside the leaving.

A man who could have been certain. Who chose, instead, to say *maybe*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Step back now and look at the whole passage.

Three ayahs.

In the first, the father says three escalating things: *rāghibun ʿan*, *la-arjumannaka*, *uhjurnī maliyyā.* Reject. Stone. Banish.

In the second, the son says three descending things: *salāmun ʿalayka*, *sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī*, *innahu kāna bī ḥafiyyā.* Peace. I will pray for you. He has always been kind to me.

In the third, the son commits to three actions: *aʿtazilukum* (I withdraw), *adʿū rabbī* (I call upon my Lord), and the closing — *ʿasā allā akūna … shaqiyyā* (perhaps I will not be wretched).

You see it?

Three threats answered by three expressions of trust answered by three commitments. The father builds *up* the violence; the son builds *down* into humility; and the closing word is **maybe**.

The architecture of the passage is the architecture of *Ibrahim's soul*. It is descending. It refuses to ascend in pride. The man who has every right to be certain — and the father who has no right to be — invert positions. The father stands tall on a mountain of false certainty. The son kneels into *maybe*.

And the surah will go on, three ayahs later, to call this same man *ṣiddīqan nabiyyā* — a man of supreme truth, a prophet. The honor goes to the one who said *maybe*.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic discoveries are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side, we saw three things: the asymmetry of *rajm* and *salām*; the word *ḥafī* with its lavish, attentive intimacy; and the word *ʿasā* with its sober, hopeful uncertainty.

From the thematic side, we saw two things: that leaving can be the deepest act of love when love asks you to be smaller, and that the closer the soul gets to Allah, the more it suspects itself.

Both sides are saying: *The shape of a soul that knows Allah is downward, not upward.* It descends into peace instead of ascending into rage. It descends into hope instead of ascending into certainty. It does the heroic thing — and then refuses to feel heroic about it.

Ibrahim is not the man we imagined. We imagined the *khalīl* of Allah as a giant standing over the rubble of his father's idols. The Quran shows us a young man, walking out of his father's house with peace on his lips and *maybe* on his tongue.

That is the *khalīl*.

That is who Allah loves.

### Questions to carry

I want to leave you with five questions. They are not rhetorical. They are real. Sit with them this week.

1. **Is there a room in your life that you should leave — not in anger, but in peace?** Is there a relationship, a community, a structure that is asking you to shrink in order to remain inside it? And could you imagine walking out the way Ibrahim walked out — *salāmun ʿalayka, sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī, wa-aʿtazilukum*?

2. **What is your *ḥafī* memory?** When you look back over your life, where can you see Allah being lavishly, attentively kind to you in ways you didn't notice at the time? What is the *kāna bī ḥafiyyā* of your own story?

3. **When you do something good, do you close the ledger or leave it open?** Do you let yourself feel "I have done well" — or can you hold the matter open in the *ʿasā* posture, hoping but not assuming?

4. **Whose *istighfār* are you carrying?** Is there someone in your life whom you have stopped speaking to, but whose name you should still be bringing to Allah in private? Ibrahim made *istighfār* for his father long after the house was closed.

5. **Where are you certain about yourself and uncertain about Allah?** And what would it look like to flip it — to be uncertain about yourself, and certain about Him?

### The one-sentence distillation

The man closest to Allah is the man who, after the most heroic act of his life, says *maybe*.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to be Ibrahim to live this. You only have to take one piece.

Maybe this week, the piece is *salāmun ʿalayka* — refusing to match the heat of someone who came at you hard. Maybe it's *sa-astaghfiru laka rabbī* — bringing one estranged person back into your *duʿāʾ*, even if you cannot bring them back into your life. Maybe it's the *ʿasā* — opening up a single moment of pride, where you had silently decided "I have done well," and quietly handing it back to its rightful Owner.

Do not try to do all of it. Take one piece. Let it sit in you. Let it shape one moment of one day.

This is how the soul of a *ḥanīf* is built. Not in giant gestures. In small descents into peace, prayer, and *maybe*.

### Du'ā

*Allāhumma kun lanā ḥafiyyā kamā kunta li-Ibrāhīm.*
O Allah, be lavishly kind to us, the way You were to Ibrahim.

*Allāhumma ʿallimnā an naqūla* salām *ḥaythu nurīdu an narudda l-saham, wa-an naqūla* astaghfiru laka *ḥaythu nurīdu an naqṭaʿa l-ḥabl.*
O Allah, teach us to say *peace* where we want to fire back, and to say *I will pray for you* where we want to cut the cord.

*Allāhumma lā taj‘alnā mimman yajzimu li-nafsihi bi-l-qabūl, wa-j‘alnā mimman yaqūlu* ʿasā *wa-yarjūk.*
O Allah, do not make us among those who are certain of their own acceptance. Make us among those who say *perhaps* — and hope in You.

Āmīn.
