# The Word of Truth and the Milking of Doubt
## *Surah Maryam · Ayahs 34–37*

### Rebuilding the world

Imagine the throne room of the Najashi.

A small group of Muslim refugees has fled across the Red Sea. Quraysh has sent envoys with gifts to extradite them — to drag them back to Mecca for execution. The case being made against them is theological: *they have insulted ʿĪsā, son of Maryam — the very man your Christian kingdom venerates.*

The Najashi, an Abyssinian Christian king, sits with his bishops on either side. He turns to Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib and says: **Recite to me what your Prophet has revealed about Jesus and his mother.**

Jaʿfar opens his mouth, and what comes out is **this surah** — Surah Maryam — a surah named after the Christian world's most beloved woman, a surah that tells her story with unprecedented honor: her labor pains, her solitude, the date palm, the cradle, the infant speaking. And then — **at exactly the moment we have arrived at** — the surah turns from the story to a verdict:

*That is Jesus, son of Mary. The word of truth. About which they keep disputing.*

The bishops shifted. The hall went silent. The Najashi picked up a small piece of stick from the ground, drew a line, and said: *"By God, the difference between what you have said about Jesus and what we say is no greater than this line."* And he wept until his beard was wet. [PAUSE]

That is the *historical* setting of these four verses. But there is a *spiritual* setting too, and it is older. It is the setting of every human heart that has ever encountered a clear truth — and chosen, instead, to keep disputing about it.

### The hook

Allah has just told us a story. The longest, most tender story about ʿĪsā and his mother in the entire Quran. Maryam in the wilderness. The angel. The pain. The palm tree. The infant in the cradle declaring, *"Indeed, I am the servant of Allah."*

And now, the moment the story ends, Allah does something strange. He does not say *"That was Jesus, son of Mary."* He says: ***Dhālika ʿĪsā bnu Maryam.* That — pointing far away — *is* Jesus, son of Mary.**

Why is the demonstrative *distal*? Why "that" and not "this"? Why does Allah point across a gulf at the very person He has just brought near?

And why — having just told us the story — does He immediately call this story *qawla l-ḥaqq*, **the word of truth**? Why label the truth, when the story has already done it?

And why is the response of humanity not "we believed" — but a verb derived from a peculiar root that originally meant **to milk an udder**?

Stay with me. There is something in this passage about how human beings handle clear truth. It will tell you something about the disputes you are having today.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *yamtarūn* — milking the udder of doubt

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, almost every word descends from a three-letter root, and that root usually started life as a very physical, concrete picture — a desert thing, a body thing, a tool thing. As the language matures, the root branches into abstractions, but the abstractions never lose the shape of the original picture. If you want to feel what a word *does* in Arabic, you have to find the picture underneath it.

Some roots come from peace. Some come from cutting. Some come from drowning. Some come from light.

The root we are about to look at comes from a goat-pen.

#### Opening the word

Look at the end of verse 34: *fīhi **yamtarūn***.

Most translations render it: *"about which they dispute"* or *"in which they doubt."* That is correct, but it is only the surface of the word. The verb is **yamtarūn** — they *imtarū* — Form VIII of the root **م-ر-ي**.

Notice something. Allah did not say *yashukkūn* (they doubt) — there is a perfectly normal Arabic word for that. He did not say *yujādilūn* (they argue). He chose this peculiar verb. **Why?**

#### The root underneath

The root *m-r-y* in classical Arabic, as Lisān al-ʿArab and the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib both record, has its origin in **the act of milking an udder**. *Mara n-nāqata* — he milked the she-camel. The picture is your hand on the teat: pull, release, pull, release, pull, release. The milk does not come out in a single gush. It must be **drawn out** by repeated, persistent motion. You go back to the same place, again, and again, and again.

From this concrete picture, the language built two related verbs:

- **Form III** — *māra* — to argue persistently with someone, going back at the same point over and over.
- **Form VIII** — *imtarā fīhi* — *to extract doubt from a thing*; to keep returning to a question and pulling at it for more.

Both forms preserve the root picture. To argue or doubt in this register is **to milk a question** — to keep coming back to it, drawing more out of it, never letting it settle.

#### Sit with the picture

Picture a man sitting beside a goat. The goat has been milked already. He keeps pulling anyway. The udder is empty, but his hands will not stop. He is not thirsty for milk. He is addicted to the pulling.

That is *imtirāʾ*. That is what Allah is naming.

The factions of humanity, He is saying, are not actually *thinking* about Jesus. They are not arriving at a conclusion. They are *milking him*. They keep coming back to the same question — *was he God? was he the son of God? was he two natures? was he one nature with two wills? was he a hypostasis?* — pulling, pulling, pulling, getting nothing, and refusing to stop.

[PAUSE]

And al-Tabari's reading of this verse is precise: the *imtirāʾ* runs in **two directions at once**. On one side stand those who denied ʿĪsā's prophethood entirely — who called him a man of illegitimate birth and a lying magician. On the other stand those who elevated him beyond prophethood into divinity. Both groups, he says, are included in *yamtarūn*. The udder is being pulled from the left by one set of hands and from the right by another. One is milking denial. The other is milking deification. Both are pulling at what has already been emptied by the truth that descended in the cradle. Al-Muyassar names them plainly: both Jews and Christians are among those who doubted — *shaka fīhi l-yahūd wa-l-naṣārā*.

This is part of why the Quran's account of ʿĪsā is so unusual. It does not argue against only the Christian elevation. It also refuses the Jewish dismissal. *Qawla l-ḥaqq* is a third thing — not the elevation, not the dismissal — it is the truth that sits above both errors and will not be pressed into either hand.

#### Why this verb and not another

Here is what makes the choice devastating. The story you have just heard — Maryam, the labor, the cradle, the declaration "I am the servant of Allah" — is **complete**. The truth has come out. There is nothing left to extract.

But the human being, when faced with a settled truth that he does not want, has a particular reflex. He does not reject it cleanly. He does not accept it cleanly. He **keeps pulling**. He returns to the same teat that has gone dry, hoping that one more session will finally produce the answer he wanted in the first place.

This is not honest inquiry. Honest inquiry has a destination. *Imtirāʾ* has no destination. It is a posture, not a search.

And what makes Allah's word-choice surgical is this: a doubting person who pictured himself as a brave, honest skeptic, "questioning everything," now has to look at the Quranic mirror and see what he is actually doing — *sitting beside a dry udder, pulling*.

#### What this single word reveals

Allah is naming a specific human disease. Not unbelief in general. Not even argument in general. He is naming **the compulsion to keep extracting doubt from a question that has already been answered**.

There is a clear word that has been spoken — *qawla l-ḥaqq*. The story has been told. The infant has spoken. The matter is settled. But the factions, He says, *yamtarūn fīh* — they keep milking it.

[PAUSE]

That word is going to follow you out of this reflection. The next time you find yourself returning to a question for the seventh time, stay with this image. Ask: *am I searching, or am I milking?*

### Element Two: *qawla l-ḥaqq* — the verdict in the accusative

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, every noun has a *case* — nominative, accusative, or genitive — marked by the vowel at its end. The case tells you the noun's grammatical role. But Arabic has a subtle device the classical grammarians loved. They call it the *manṣūb mufassir* — an accusative that does not slot into the obvious sentence structure but stands slightly apart, **as if Allah is pulling that one phrase out of the flow and presenting it as a stamped verdict**.

Think of it like a court ruling. The judge has been speaking in normal prose. Then he stands and says, in a different cadence: *"This — to be the verdict of the court."* That standing-up of the phrase is what an accusative-of-specification does in Arabic.

#### Opening the phrase

Look at how verse 34 is built:

*Dhālika ʿĪsā bnu Maryam* — *"That is Jesus, son of Mary."*

That sentence is grammatically complete. Subject. Predicate. Done. It would be perfectly intelligible Arabic to stop right there and move on.

But Allah does not stop. He adds three more words, and the case-ending of the very first one is the whole point:

*qawla l-ḥaqq*.

Notice the *fatḥa* on the end of *qawla* — it is **accusative**, not nominative. If Allah had wanted to say *"That is Jesus, son of Mary, the word of truth"* in a flat appositional way, He would have said *qawlu l-ḥaqq* — nominative. He didn't. He picked the accusative on purpose.

#### A pause for the other reading

Ibn Kathir records something that deepens this further: among the Quran's reciters, the **majority** in fact read this as *Qawlul-ḥaqq* — **nominative**, not accusative. That reading treats the phrase as an appositive to *ʿĪsā ibn Maryam* himself: *"That is Jesus, son of Mary — [he is] the Word of Truth."* In that reading, *qawl al-ḥaqq* is not a stamp placed on the narrative from outside but a title that belongs to ʿĪsā himself — he *is* the word of truth, embodied, walking, speaking from the cradle.

Both readings are authenticated, and they are not in tension — they are stereoscopic. One says: *this account is the word of truth.* The other says: *this person is the word of truth.* Together they say: the truth about the person is inseparable from the person himself. The narrative is not an external commentary on ʿĪsā. It *is* ʿĪsā.

#### What the accusative does

Where the nominative reading makes ʿĪsā the word of truth, the accusative reading — which the syntax here also supports — does something grammatically distinctive. The classical grammarians — Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, Abū Ḥayyān — discussed this and noted several possibilities, all of which converge on the same effect:

- It can be an **accusative of specification** — *"That is Jesus, son of Mary, [I declare it] as a word of truth."*
- It can be an **absolute object of an implied verb** — *"I speak about him a word of truth."*
- It can be a **circumstantial accusative** — *"That is Jesus, son of Mary — [stated] as the truthful word."*

Don't pick between them. They are camera angles on a single act. **Allah has separated His statement about ʿĪsā from the noise about ʿĪsā by stamping His statement with a different grammatical seal.**

Around ʿĪsā there is *qawl* — many sayings, many councils, many creeds, many disputes. *Qawl* upon *qawl* upon *qawl*. And in the middle of all that *qawl*, Allah lifts His own *qawl* out of the flow of the sentence and labels it: ***qawla l-ḥaqq*** — *the* word, the *truthful* one.

#### Stay with this

There has been a lot of speech about Jesus. There still is. Books, councils, films, podcasts, debates. Most of it is *qawl* — saying. Some of it is sincere. Much of it is *imtirāʾ*. All of it is human.

Allah is saying: in the middle of all that human *qawl*, **here is the *qawl al-ḥaqq*. Here is the verdict-from-the-bench. Hold it apart from the noise. Don't let it sink back into the conversation as just one more opinion. It is not.**

#### A second thing the accusative does

There is something else. *Qawla l-ḥaqq* is not a translation of "Jesus." It is a label *applied* to the entire preceding narrative — Maryam, the angel, the pain, the cradle, the declaration. **The whole story of verses 16–33 is being stamped here.** The accusative reaches backward and gathers everything Allah has just told us, ties it in a parcel, and seals it: *qawla l-ḥaqq*.

This is why the demonstrative is *dhālika* (distal — "that") and not *hādhā* (proximal — "this"). The story has receded; it stands behind us. Allah is pointing back at it. *That* — that whole narrative I just told you — *is* Jesus son of Mary, *as a word of truth, about which they keep milking*.

#### What this single feature reveals

Around any clear truth, human speech proliferates. The discourse multiplies until you cannot hear the original utterance anymore. Allah's response is not to enter the discourse and argue inside it. His response is to **lift His own statement out of it grammatically** and place it on a different shelf. The accusative is the shelf.

[PAUSE]

This is something to remember about how Allah teaches. He does not always argue. Sometimes He simply *names*. He pulls the truth out of the marketplace and says, *qawla l-ḥaqq* — and the marketplace is left to keep talking, but a person with ears now knows where the verdict actually sits.

### Element Three: *kun fa-yakūn* — the immediacy of the *fa*

#### Teaching the concept first

There is a particle in Arabic so small that it takes only a single letter to write: **ف** — *fa*. Most of the time it simply means "and then" or "so." But in certain constructions it carries a particular freight: **consecutiveness without gap**. The grammar textbooks call it *fa al-taʿqīb* — the *fa* of immediate consequence. Whatever precedes it causes whatever follows, and causes it *now*, without interval.

This small letter is doing enormous theological work in verse 35.

#### Opening the phrase

*Idhā qaḍā amran fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu **kun** **fa**-**yakūn***.

When He decrees a matter — He only says to it: **Be** — and *immediately* it **is**.

Three things demand attention here.

First, the verb *qaḍā* — from the root *q-ḍ-w*, which carries the sense of deciding, completing, executing absolutely. This is not tentative planning. It is terminal decree. When *qaḍā* appears in the Quran, the decision is over, made with a finality that admits no revision.

Second, the imperative *kun* — from the root *k-w-n*, the very root of being. God addresses nonexistence directly: *"Be."* This is the grammatical form you would use to give a command to a second person — but the second person here is *the thing that does not yet exist*. It is being commanded into existence from the address of existence itself.

Third, the *fa* — the pivot everything rests on. *Kun* **fa**-*yakūn*. Be — and *immediately* it is. Al-Tabari is precise on the scope: when Allah decrees any matter, *small or great* — the words are important — nothing resists Him. The scale is total. And the *fa* says: between the command and its fulfillment there is no interval to fill with process, mediation, or assistance.

#### Why this belongs here

Verse 35 begins with a negation: *mā kāna lillāhi an yattakhidha min walad* — "It is not for Allah to take a son." Al-Tabari notes the grammatical precision of *min walad* — the *min* is partitive, reaching all the way down: not even a child, not even a portion of sonship, not even the shadow of it attaches to Allah's nature. Then comes *subḥānah* — an interjection that classical commentators read not as a pious aside but as a pointed refutation: *Glory be to Him — He is infinitely far above what the disbelievers have attributed to Him.* Al-Muyassar's language here is exact: it does not merely say this is impossible for Allah; it says it does not *befit* Him. Both weights travel together — ontological impossibility *and* transcendent incompatibility.

Then comes *kun fa-yakūn*.

The argument moves like this: **you do not need a son when you have a *fa*.** The Christians who elevated ʿĪsā to divine status were trying to account for something real — a man born without a father, a man who healed the blind and raised the dead. Their theological reasoning was: surely such power requires divine nature. Surely only God's son could do what this man did.

The Quran's answer is not a philosophical treatise. It is a *fa*.

**Allah does not need an intermediary because He does not need a process.** When He decrees, the *fa* happens: command and fulfillment are separated by nothing. The birth of ʿĪsā without a father is not evidence that his father must be God. It is evidence that *kun* reached him the same way *kun* reaches everything — instantly, completely, without mediation. He was not extraordinary because he was divine. He was extraordinary because the divine command that created him was not routed through a human father — just as it was not routed through any parents at all when Adam was formed, and just as it will not be routed through any earthly process when the last things arrive.

[PAUSE]

This is the Quran's quiet devastation of the theological argument. It does not argue *against* ʿĪsā's miraculous nature. It says: of course he was miraculous — *kun fa-yakūn* reached him directly. But that is the evidence of God's sovereignty, not the evidence of God's paternity.

#### The *fa* against the milking

Notice how verse 35 connects back to verse 34. *Yamtarūn* — they keep milking. And what they keep milking is precisely the gap between divine power and human birth — *how could this have happened without a divine father?* The *fa* is Allah's answer to the milking. You have been pulling at that question for two thousand years. Here is what the question contains: **a single letter**. *Kun* — *fa* — *yakūn*. There is nothing more to extract.

### Element Four: *min baynihim* — the fracture from within

#### Teaching the concept first

Arabic prepositions are compressors. A single word — *min* (from), *fī* (in), *bayn* (between) — can hold architectural information about space, causation, origin, and relationship. When you stack prepositions — *min bayn*, literally "from among the between" — you are not being redundant. You are zooming into a specific spatial and relational truth about *where* a fracture originates.

#### Opening the phrase

*Fa-khtalafa l-aḥzābu **min baynihim***.

**The factions split apart — from among themselves.**

Allah did not say *wa-khtalafū* — "and they differed." He began with *fa-*, the consequential *fa* again — the differing is the *result* of what preceded. They received the word of truth. They heard *qawla l-ḥaqq*. And *therefore* — *fa* — the factions split.

The word *aḥzāb* — factions — is the plural of *ḥizb*. Classically, *ḥizb* denotes a group that has formed around a shared position, usually in opposition to something else. The same word appears for the Confederates who besieged Medina. It carries an edge. These are not simply "people who disagree." These are groups that have *organized around their disagreement* — factions that have made their dispute into an identity.

And *min baynihim* — from among themselves. The fracture did not come from outside. No external enemy broke them apart. The split came from within the community that had received the same truth. They all came from the same source. And from within that common ground, *ikhtilāf* — the kind of difference that pulls people apart — grew.

What history confirms this with is visible even now. The early Christian communities fractured precisely here. There were those who said ʿĪsā was fully divine and only appeared human. There were those who said he was fully human and only appeared divine. There were those who said he was two persons in one body, or one person with two natures, or one person with one nature but two wills. The councils convened — Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, Chalcedon — and each council produced new factions, new anathemas, new splits.

All of them, from the Quran's perspective, were *min baynihim* — from among themselves. From the same community, the same scripture, the same event. And yet, *khtalafū*.

#### The *mashhad* they will stand before

Al-Tabari's language at verse 37 carries a final precision: the woe (*waylun*) falls on those who disbelieved, *min mashhadi yawmin ʿaẓīm* — **from the witnessing of a tremendous Day.** The root of *mashhad* is *sh-h-d* — the same root as *shahāda* (testimony, witnessing), *shahīd* (martyr — one who witnesses), and *shāhid* (witness in court). On that Day, every disputed claim, every council decree, every anathema, every ikhtilāf — will be *witnessed*. Not argued. Not reviewed. Simply *seen*. The word of truth spoken in the cradle will stand, and the factions will stand before it.

*Waylun* — woe — is one of the heaviest words in the Quran. It has no singular form. It does not name a specific punishment. Classical lexicographers note it is said *to* the one being destroyed at the moment of destruction. It is the sound a verdict makes when it lands with finality.

## Part 2: The Theological Pivot

### The speaker of verse 36 — an ikhtilāf worth knowing

There is a known disagreement among classical scholars about who speaks verse 36:

*Wa-inna Llāha rabbī wa-rabbukum fa-ʿbudūh — hādhā ṣirāṭun mustaqīm.*

**"And indeed, Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him. This is a straight path."**

The first-person *rabbī* — **my** Lord — requires a speaker. Who says "my Lord" here?

The majority position, supported by Ibn Kathir, al-Tabari's primary narrations, and the preponderance of classical commentators, holds that this is **ʿĪsā speaking** — a continuation and confirmation of his cradle declaration in verse 30, where he said: *"I am the servant of Allah."* The *iltifāt* — the voice-shift — is from the narrator describing ʿĪsā (vv. 34–35) back to ʿĪsā's own first-person declaration. On this reading, ʿĪsā testifies not only to his own servanthood but to a shared one: Allah is *my* Lord — and yours. Come to the same place I stand.

A minority of scholars holds that the verse is the Quranic narrator's voice — summarizing the truth for the audience without attributing the specific phrasing to ʿĪsā himself. On this reading, *rabbī* is the prophetic or Quranic voice modeling the confession that ʿĪsā's whole life embodied.

Both readings are internally coherent. The majority reading has the stronger classical support and produces a more powerful dramatic structure: ʿĪsā speaks, the factions split, the warning falls. His own testimony is the pivot around which the entire four-verse movement turns.

### Why *rabbī* matters

On the majority reading, *rabbī* — **my** Lord — is doing tremendous weight-bearing work. ʿĪsā is not merely correcting a theological error. He is claiming a *relationship*. He does not say: "Allah is God, and I am not." He says: "Allah is *my* Lord" — and therefore, by definition, he is *not* Lord. He is the one who stands *under* Lordship, in worship, in servitude, in dependence. The same relationship he has with Allah is the one being invited for everyone: *wa-rabbukum* — and *your* Lord too. Come and worship the one who is Lord over both of us.

This is the *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm* — the straight path. The path is not primarily a set of rules. It is a *posture*. The posture of standing as ʿĪsā stands: in acknowledged creatureliness before a Lord who is unambiguously Lord. The phrase *hādhā ṣirāṭun mustaqīm* — "this is a straight path" — lands with the same rhythm as *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* of Surah al-Fātiḥa, the path every Muslim asks for seventeen times a day. ʿĪsā is pointing at it. He walked it. And the factions, instead of walking it with him, milked it.

## Part 3: The Meditation

### What you are carrying out of here

There are four things in these four verses. Each one is a different kind of mercy.

The first is *yamtarūn*. The mercy of being shown your own hand on the teat. Every human being has a question they keep returning to — some theological, some relational, some existential — and every time they return to it they tell themselves they are searching. The Quran offers a different description. *Are you searching, or are you milking?* A search has a destination. *Imtirāʾ* has only the motion of return. The mercy here is diagnostic: you cannot stop a habit until you have been shown, precisely, what the habit is.

The second is *qawla l-ḥaqq*. The mercy of a verdict that stands apart from the noise. In every era, in every discourse, the speech about Jesus multiplies. It will not stop. New books arrive. New councils are called. New platforms launch. And in the middle of all of it — Allah has placed a *fatḥa*. A single vowel on the end of a word that says: *here it is, lifted out of the noise, on a different shelf, waiting for you whenever you are ready to stop milking and simply receive.* Whether you read it as a stamp on the narrative or a title belonging to ʿĪsā himself, the effect is the same: there is a word that is the true one, and it does not compete for shelf space. It stands apart by grammatical decree.

The third is *kun fa-yakūn*. The mercy of the *fa*. The one who made ʿĪsā without a father is the same one who will make whatever you need. The creative command is not rationed. It is not reserved for miracles that happen to famous people in distant centuries. Al-Muyassar says: small matters and large matters alike yield to it — there is no category of difficulty that *kun* cannot address. Every time you feel that something is impossible because the normal channels are blocked — remember: the channels are not the point. *Kun fa-yakūn*. The command is direct, and the *fa* says it is immediate.

The fourth is *waylun*. The mercy of the warning. Woe is not cruelty. It is the sound a map makes when it marks the cliff before you have reached it. The factions that split *min baynihim* — from among themselves — did not intend to fall into *waylun*. They were arguing. They were pulling at the teat. They were attending councils. And they ended somewhere they did not mean to go. The Quran marks that place on the map with a word that has no singular form, because once you have arrived there, there is no singular way to describe what has been lost. The warning is a mercy. It tells you while you still have the choice to stop milking and walk.

### The Najashi wept

Return to the throne room.

The Najashi drew his line in the dust. *By God, the difference between what you have said about Jesus and what we say is no greater than this.*

What did he mean?

He did not mean the two faiths are the same. He meant: your Quran says what I already knew in my heart to be true — that this man was extraordinary, that his mother was pure, that something divine moved through his birth. What divides us, he was saying, is *not the wonder of the event*. It is the *interpretation* of the wonder.

And the Quran says: yes. That is exactly the site of *yamtarūn*. Not the wonder. The interpretation. The wonder is agreed upon by everyone who encounters the story honestly. The wonder is not in dispute. What is in dispute — what has been milked for two thousand years — is the question of what the wonder *means*.

The Quran answers it not by diminishing the wonder but by relocating its source. It was not divine paternity. It was *kun fa-yakūn*. The same *fa* that made the first man from clay. The same *fa* that will make the last things on the last day. The *fa* that has no interval and needs no intermediary.

The Najashi wept. He heard the word of truth. He recognized it. And he did not milk it.

There is a *ṣirāṭ mustaqīm*. ʿĪsā walked it. The Najashi, for that moment, stood on it. It is the path of seeing *clearly* — seeing ʿĪsā as what he is, no more, no less; seeing Allah as what He is, no more, no less; and worshipping accordingly.

*Hādhā ṣirāṭun mustaqīm.*

This is a straight path.
