# The Evil of What He Created
### Surah Al-Falaq, Ayah 2 — *Min sharri mā khalaq*

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let us begin by simply hearing it.

> [ayah:113:2] مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ
>
> *Min sharri mā khalaq.*
>
> **"From the evil of what He created."**

Four words. One preposition, one noun, one little relative word, and one verb. You could say it in a single breath. A child memorizes it before they can read. And yet I want to suggest to you that these four words contain one of the most quietly staggering statements in the entire Quran — a statement that touches the oldest wound in the human heart, the question that has broken philosophers and kept frightened people awake for as long as there have been frightened people.

But before we can see it, we have to rebuild the world this verse first landed in.

### Rebuild the world

Picture the night as the people who first received this surah knew the night. No streetlights. No glow of a phone on the nightstand. When the sun went down, the darkness was total, and the darkness was full. Full of things you could hear but not see. A rustle in the brush. A shape at the edge of the firelight. The cold awareness that out there, beyond the small circle of what you could control, the world was vast and largely indifferent to whether you survived the night.

And it was not only the night. It was the snake under the stone. The fever that took the child by morning. The enemy who smiled at you in the marketplace. The poison. The drought. The whisper of a thought in your own chest that you could not silence. The ancient human being lived pressed up against a thousand threats, most of them unseen, most of them un-nameable.

Now — into that world — comes the very last stretch of the Quran. Two short surahs, Al-Falaq and An-Nas, that the Prophet ﷺ would recite over himself every single night, breathing them into his hands and passing them over his body. The scholars call them *al-Muʿawwidhatān* — "the two that grant refuge." They are the Quran's closing gift to a frightened species: **here is where you run.**

And the very first place the running takes us — the first thing we are taught to take shelter from — is not the snake, not the enemy, not the darkness. It is something far bigger than all of those. It is *this.*

### The Hook

Here is the question I want you to carry with you. When you are afraid — really afraid — what exactly are you afraid *of?* Name it. You will find, more often than you'd expect, that you cannot. The fear is bigger than any single object. It is a dread of *the world itself,* of all the things out there that could go wrong.

This verse names that. And in the way it names it — in one missing word, in one tiny preposition, in where it places the blame and where it refuses to — it does something I have never seen any human philosophy manage to do. [PAUSE]

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The preposition that takes only a sliver — *min*

Let me teach you something about a single Arabic letter before we use it.

The verse opens with **مِن** — *min*. We translate it "from." Simple enough. But *min* in Arabic is not a flat, neutral "from." One of its core functions — grammarians call it *ibtidāʾ al-ghāya*, the starting-point — is to mark **a portion taken out of a larger whole.** When you say in Arabic "I ate *min* the food," you did not eat the food. You ate *some* of it. *Min* reaches into a totality and pulls out a part.

Hold that ordinary fact, because the verse is about to use it with surgical care.

Now look at what we are actually told to seek refuge from:

> **مِن شَرِّ مَا خَلَقَ** — "from *the evil of* what He created."

Notice something. We are *not* commanded to seek refuge from "what He created." We are not taught to flee creation. We are taught to flee **a sliver of it** — *the evil of* it. The *min* reaches into the entire created order and pulls out one thin thread: the *sharr,* the harm-potential. Everything else — the warmth of the fire, the nourishment of the food, the loyalty of the friend, the cool of the night — all of that is left untouched, unfled-from, *good.*

Stay with this. The grammar is making a theological claim before the meaning even arrives. **Creation is not the threat. A fraction within creation is.** The believer is not taught to be afraid of the world. The believer is taught to be precise about exactly *which sliver* of the world to take to Allah.

This is the opposite of how fear actually works in us. Fear generalizes. Fear says: the dark is dangerous, so I fear the dark. The dog bit me, so I fear all dogs. The world hurt me, so I brace against the world. *Min* refuses that. *Min* says: not the whole — only the sliver. Take refuge from the *sharr,* and let the rest of creation remain what it is: a gift.

One letter. And it has already begun to heal the way we relate to everything. [PAUSE]

### Element Two: The word that sounds like what it is — *sharr*

Now the word the *min* reaches for: **شَرّ** — *sharr.* Evil. Harm.

Say it out loud. *Sharr.* Feel what your mouth does. That doubled *r* — *sharr* — the tongue catching and grinding against itself, a sound that hisses and then snags. This is not a soft word. Compare it to the gentleness of the word it is fleeing *toward* — *falaq,* the daybreak, the lips opening like dawn. The surah is built on a sonic contrast: the open mouth of *falaq,* the clenched grind of *sharr.* The language is doing in sound what the meaning does in truth.

Now, the root — ش ر ر — is rich, and the lexicons preserve a thread worth pausing on. The same root family carries the sense of **a spark, the scattering of sparks from a fire**, of something *spreading* and *catching.* Evil, in the Arabic imagination, is not a static block of darkness sitting in a corner. It is a *spark* — small at first, then leaping, scattering, catching what's near it and spreading. Think how a single act of malice spreads. A single lie. A single envious glance — which is, in fact, exactly where this surah will end, in ayah 5, with the envier. *Sharr* is combustion. It travels.

And notice — this is important — the word is **indefinite.** Not *ash-sharr,* "the evil," some specific known evil. Just *sharr* — evil-as-such, evil in its raw, unbounded generality. The verse is not pointing at one named danger. It is throwing a net over *all of it.*

Which brings us to the heart of the matter. Because the question every honest soul eventually asks is: *where does this sharr come from?* If everything is from Allah — does that mean evil is from Allah?

Watch the grammar answer.

### Element Three: The missing word — *mā khalaq*, "what He created"

Here is the most important moment in the verse. I am going to teach you the device first, in plain life, before we open the words.

In Arabic there is a little word, **مَا** — *mā* — which, used like this, means "that which" or "what." It is a relative pronoun with a remarkable property: **it is maximally vague on purpose.** It refuses to specify. If I say "I'll give you *what's* in the box," I have told you that you'll receive something — and I have told you *nothing* about what it is. *Mā* is a deliberately open hand. It names a category without naming a single member of it.

The verse could have been specific. It could have said: seek refuge from the evil of *snakes,* of *enemies,* of *Iblīs.* Instead it says:

> **مَا خَلَقَ** — "what He created."

Everything. Anything. The named and the unnamed. The threat you can see and the thousand you cannot. The classical commentators, faced with this vastness, reach for examples rather than limits. Al-Jalālayn says it covers *every* created thing — the morally responsible and the non-responsible, the living and even the inanimate, "like poison and other than that." Al-Muyassar simply says: "from the evil of *all* created things and their harm." And al-Qurṭubī gathers the voices of the early community like a man holding up a single jewel to different lights — *some said* it means Iblīs and his offspring; *some said* it means Hellfire; *and some said* it is general: the evil of every possessor of evil that Allah created.

Don't hear those as three competing answers. Hear them as three camera angles on one reality. The vagueness of *mā* is so total that the great scholars couldn't narrow it — and that is precisely the point. **You are being given a single refuge wide enough to cover every fear you will ever have**, including the ones you cannot name, including the ones that haven't found you yet.

But now — now — look at what the verse does *not* say. This is the absence I promised you, and it is everything.

The verse says *sharri mā khalaq* — "the evil **of** what He created."

It does **not** say *mā khalaqa min sharr* — "the evil that He created."

Feel the difference. Feel it carefully, because the whole theology of evil turns on this hair's-breadth distinction. [PAUSE]

In the construction we have, the verb **خَلَقَ — *khalaqa,* "He created"** — has only one thing attached to it: *mā,* the *things.* Allah created the *things.* That is what is attributed to Him: the act of bringing into being. And the *sharr* — the evil — is grammatically bound not to the verb of creating but to *mā,* to the created things themselves. The evil is something that *arises within* what was made; it is not the thing that was made.

Allah created the fire — and the fire is a mercy, it cooks your food and warms your night. Its capacity to *burn* is a *sharr* that arises in it under certain conditions. Allah created the scorpion — a marvel of design. Its sting is a *sharr* that emerges in relation. Allah created the human heart — and the envy that can curdle in it is a *sharr* that the heart *develops,* not the heart's created essence. Elsewhere the Quran states it openly: *alladhī aḥsana kulla shayʾin khalaqahu* — "the One who made *excellent* everything He created" (32:7). Everything, as created, is good. *Sharr* is what happens when the good is misused, misaligned, turned, or turned loose.

So the grammar performs a kind of perfect justice. **The creating is Allah's. The evil is creation's.** Not because anything escapes His will — nothing does, and the surah will rest the whole refuge on that very fact — but because His *act,* the act of *khalq,* is pure, and *sharr* is a privation, a distortion, a spark struck off the good, never the good itself.

The single word it refused to say — *"the evil He created"* — is the word that would have made Allah the author of evil. And the Quran, in four words, simply *will not say it.* The omission is not an accident of phrasing. **It is the doctrine.** [PAUSE]

### Bridge

So look what these four words have built. *Min* taught us to flee only a sliver, never the whole. *Sharr* gave that sliver a name and a nature — a traveling spark, not a fixed wall. And *mā khalaq,* with its vast vagueness and its precise refusal, threw a net over every evil that exists while quietly clearing the Creator of authoring any of it.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the trembling thing inside us that needs to hear it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The refuge for the fear you cannot name

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of fear that modern people know intimately, and we have a clinical word for its chronic form: *generalized anxiety.* Notice that word — *generalized.* It is the dread that has no single object. If you could point at the thing and say *that, that is what I fear,* you could at least face it, prepare for it, run from it. But generalized anxiety is the fear of *everything and nothing* — a low hum of threat that attaches to no particular danger because it attaches to *all* of them. You lie awake and your mind cycles through the news, the diagnosis you haven't gotten, the email you haven't sent, the world that feels one bad day away from coming apart. You are afraid *of what He created* — of the whole vast field of things that could go wrong.

We even built a machine for it. We call it the feed. You scroll and you absorb, in a single sitting, a war on one continent, a disease on another, a stranger's cruelty, a collapsing market, a wildfire — *the evil of what He created,* served to you in an infinite stream, until your nervous system, which was built to fear *one* tiger at a time, is trying to brace against *all the tigers in the world at once.* No wonder we are exhausted. We have generalized our fear to the size of the planet and given ourselves no refuge the same size.

Now hear the verse again. *Min sharri mā khalaq.* "From the evil of *what He created.*"

Do you see what it has done? It has taken your generalized, objectless, planet-sized dread — and instead of telling you to shrink it, instead of telling you to manage it threat by threat, it has given you **a single refuge as large as the fear itself.** You don't have to name them. You don't have to enumerate the thousand things. The *mā,* remember, is deliberately vast — it already includes every one of them, the named and the unnamed, the present danger and the one still coming. In one breath you hand the entire field over. *I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak from the evil of everything He made.*

This is the gift the *vagueness* gives you. A specific prayer protects against a specific harm. But a prayer this general is an umbrella over the whole of your dread. The anxious mind says, *but what about this, and what about that, and the thing I haven't thought of yet?* And the verse answers, before you can finish the list: *all of it. It's covered. Come inside.* [PAUSE]

And notice *where* you are running. Not into denial — the verse fully admits the *sharr* is real, it does not tell you the world is safe. And not into your own management, your own vigilance, your own exhausting attempt to control every variable. You are running into the shelter of *the Lord of the daybreak* — the One who splits the dark open with light every single morning, whether or not you stayed awake worrying. The God who reliably cracks dawn over the world is the God you are handing your nighttime fears to.

What does this change about tomorrow? This: the next time the dread rises and you cannot name it — when it's just a tightness, a bracing, a sense that *something* out there is coming — you don't have to identify it to be free of it. You have a four-word door. *Min sharri mā khalaq.* You don't have to know the name of the thing to take it to the One who made it.

### Theme Two: Running to the Maker from the made

Now I want to take you to the oldest argument against God, because this verse walks straight into it and walks straight out the other side, and most people never notice it happened.

Here is the argument, in the form a sincere teenager might pose it at the dinner table, or a grieving person might whisper at a funeral: *If God made everything, then God made evil. If God made evil, then either He is not good, or He is not all-powerful. So how can you worship Him?* It is called the problem of evil, and it has been thrown at the heart of faith for thousands of years, and it has knocked many people off their feet.

Sit with how strange the believer's response is, structurally. We do not answer the problem of evil with a *philosophy.* We answer it with a *posture.* Watch what the surah actually instructs you to do with evil: it tells you to take refuge **in the Creator** **from** **His creation.**

Stop and feel how paradoxical that is. The One you are fleeing *to* is the One who made the thing you are fleeing *from.* You are running into the arms of the Author of the very story that frightens you. If a human author wrote a terror into a tale, you would not run to *him* for safety. But here, the trembling soul runs *toward* the Source — not away from Him. [PAUSE]

Why does this make sense — why is it not absurd?

Because of everything the grammar already showed us. Remember: the *sharr* is not what He created; the *sharr* is what *arises in* what He created. The act of *khalq* is pure. So when you flee *to* Him, you are not fleeing to the author of the evil — you are fleeing to the author of the *good* from which the evil was a falling-away. You are running from the distortion back to the Source of the original. The fire's burning frightens you; you run to the One whose making of the fire was wholly excellent, whose will governs even the spark.

And here is the deepest movement, the one that turns the problem of evil from a wall into a door. The reason it is *safe* to run to the Creator from the creation is that **the creation has no independent power to harm you.** The scorpion cannot sting except by His leave. The fire cannot burn except by His leave — ask Ibrāhīm, standing in flames that were commanded *"be coolness and peace"* (21:69). The envier's glance, which this surah will name in its final breath, cannot touch you except by His permission. So the One who made the threat is *also* the only One with the authority to disarm it. **He is not only the Maker of what you fear — He is the only refuge from it, precisely because He is its Maker.** Nothing else in existence has the standing to grant you shelter, because nothing else in existence has authority over *what He created.* Only the Maker can call off the made.

This is why the answer to evil, in the Quran, is not a closed argument but an open door. The philosopher stands outside the problem trying to *solve* it, and the problem defeats him. The believer walks *through* the problem into the arms of the only One who can hold him inside it. The evil stays real. The fear is fully acknowledged. And yet the soul is safe — not because the world stopped being dangerous, but because it ran to the One who holds the world. [PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? When the hard question comes — from your own doubting heart at 3am, or from your child at the table — you no longer have to win a debate before you're allowed to feel safe. The verse models it: you can *hold the reality of evil in one hand and run to Allah with the other,* at the same time, without resolving the entire philosophy first. Faith here is not the conclusion of an argument. It is a direction you run. From the made — to the Maker.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now step back and see what you could not see at the start.

This verse is the **first** refuge of three, and Surah Al-Falaq is built like a funnel. Watch it narrow:

- **Ayah 2:** *from the evil of what He created* — **all** evil, everywhere, the vast general net.
- **Ayah 3:** *from the evil of darkness when it gathers* — narrower: a *kind* of evil, the threat of the night and what comes with it.
- **Ayah 4:** *from the evil of the blowers upon knots* — narrower still: a *specific* malice, the worker of magic.
- **Ayah 5:** *from the evil of an envier when he envies* — the sharpest point: a single curdled human heart.

The surah begins with the whole ocean of *sharr* and funnels you down to one envious glance. And this verse — 113:2 — is **the umbrella under which all the rest sit.** Whatever specific evil ayahs 3, 4, and 5 will name, it has *already* been covered here, because *mā khalaq* — "what He created" — already included it. The general is stated first so that nothing the specifics name can ever fall outside the refuge. You were sheltered from the envier before the envier was even mentioned.

### Bringing it together

See now how the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

The *grammar* refused to say "the evil He created" and said instead "the evil of what He created" — and that single refusal *is* the answer to the problem of evil that the second theme drew out: the Maker is safe to run to because the making was pure. The little preposition *min* that pulled out only a sliver *is* the cure for generalized anxiety that the first theme named: you flee a fraction, not the whole. The vast vagueness of *mā* that the scholars could not narrow *is* the umbrella-refuge as large as your nameless dread. The words and the wounds are the same shape. The verse was healing you grammatically before it ever healed you emotionally.

### Questions to carry

1. When I am afraid, do I actually try to *name* the thing — or do I let my fear stay general and unfaced? And could I instead hand the whole unnamed field to Allah in one breath?
2. How much of my dread is aimed at *creation itself* — at people, at the world, at the future — rather than at the thin *sliver* of *sharr* within it? What would change if I stopped fleeing the whole and started fleeing only the fraction?
3. Do I treat the things that frighten me as if they have power of their own — or do I really believe nothing in *what He created* can harm me except by His leave?
4. When the hard question about evil rises in me, do I freeze outside it trying to solve it — or do I run *through* it to the Maker?

### One-sentence distillation

**Take shelter with the Maker from the thin sliver of evil that arises in the made — for He authored the good that evil only distorts, and nothing He created can harm you except by the leave of the One you are running to.**

### The closing invitation

Tonight, before you sleep — as the Prophet ﷺ did every night — let these four words be the door you walk through. Not as a chore. As a homecoming. You spend your days bracing against *what He created,* against the vast field of things that might go wrong. For the length of one breath, lay that whole field down. *Min sharri mā khalaq.* You don't have to name your fears. You don't have to win the argument. You only have to run in the right direction.

### A brief duʿā

*O Lord of the daybreak, who splits the darkness with light every morning whether or not we slept in peace — we seek refuge in You from the evil of all that You have made, the evil we can name and the evil we cannot. You made the good that evil only distorts; You hold the leave by which alone anything can harm us. So shelter us in Yourself — the Maker — from the made. Āmīn.*
