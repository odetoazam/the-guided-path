# When Allah Rewrites the Past

## A Reflection on Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 70

---

## Introduction

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

> إِلَّا مَن تَابَ وَءَامَنَ وَعَمِلَ عَمَلًا صَـٰلِحًا فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ يُبَدِّلُ ٱللَّهُ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِمْ حَسَنَـٰتٍۢ ۗ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًۭا رَّحِيمًۭا

**Transliteration:**

> *Illā man tāba wa āmana wa ʿamila ʿamalan ṣāliḥan fa-ulā'ika yubaddilu Allāhu sayyi'ātihim ḥasanāt. Wa kāna Allāhu Ghafūran Raḥīmā.*

**Translation:**

> "Except for those who repent, and believe, and do righteous work — for those, Allah will exchange their evil deeds for good deeds. And Allah is ever Forgiving, Merciful."

---

### Rebuilding the World

To feel what this ayah does, you have to know what just happened in the surah.

Since verse 63, the surah has been painting one unbroken portrait: *ʿibād al-Raḥmān* — *the servants of the Most Merciful.* They walk the earth in stillness and humility — *hawnan*, which al-Ṭabarī glosses not as weakness but as forbearance, calm, and gravity (*ḥilm, sakīna, waqār*), the gait of those who are neither arrogant nor tyrannical nor bent on corruption; when the ignorant hurl abuse at them they answer with peace — and al-Muyassar adds the precise reason: they reply *with what is good*, in a way that keeps *them* safe from sin and from meeting ignorance with ignorance. They pass their nights before their Lord *yurāwiḥūna bayna sujūdin wa qiyām* — al-Ṭabarī's phrase — alternating between prostration and standing (and he notes the grammar: *qiyāman* is the plural of *qāʾim*, the one who stands, just as *ṣiyām* is the plural of *ṣāʾim*); they neither overspend nor withhold. The classical commentators read every line of this passage as a single composite face of the believer — and held under one Name: *al-Raḥmān,* the Most Merciful. Hold onto that Name. It will return at the very end of our ayah, and when it does it will change everything.

The previous two ayat — verses 68 and 69 — name three sins. Not minor slips. Not careless mistakes. They name the catastrophic sins of human life:

*Associating partners with Allah.*
*Taking a life that Allah has made sacred — without right.*
*Zina.*

And then ayah 68 adds: *whoever does these will meet a penalty.* Ayah 69: *the punishment will be doubled for him on the Day of Resurrection, and he will dwell in it eternally, humiliated.*

These three were not gathered at random. The Prophet ﷺ was once asked which sin is the greatest, and — as Ibn Kathīr records from Ibn Masʿūd — he named exactly this progression: *that you set up a rival to Allah when it is He who created you,* then *that you kill your child for fear he would eat with you,* then *that you commit adultery with the wife of your neighbor.* The ayat descend the same ladder of betrayal: against God, against life, against the sacred bond between people. This is the worst the human being is capable of.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment.

The Quran has just described — in a few short verses — the kind of person we tend to write off entirely. The kind of person we cross the street to avoid. The kind of person who, in our private moral arithmetic, has placed themselves outside the reach of redemption. Murderers. Those who broke the most sacred bonds. Those who walked away from God entirely.

And the ayah we are about to enter — verse 70 — is Allah's response to that person.

But more than that.

Many of us, hearing those three sins listed, do not think *that's not me.* We hear those three and we think of our own three. The thing we did when no one was watching. The thing we have not told anyone. The thing that wakes us up at 3am and asks us what we are still doing pretending to be religious. **Every soul carries some version of those verses inside it.** That is part of why they are placed where they are placed.

So when the listener — the original Arab listener and you, right now — arrives at verse 70, they arrive with a particular emotional weight. They arrive having just been shown a door slamming shut. They are bracing for it.

And Allah opens a different door entirely.

---

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah — one word — that the early scholars said is one of the most astonishing statements in the entire Quran about what mercy really is. They said: *if this word were not in the Book of Allah, no human being would have dared to claim that Allah does this.*

Not forgive. Not erase. Not overlook.

Something else.

Something so generous that the classical commentators could not even agree on how far its mercy reaches — and we will stand at that very disagreement before this is done.

Let's earn our way to that word.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word *Illā* — The Door That Cannot Be Shut

Before we open this ayah's content, notice how it begins.

The very first word is **إِلَّا** — *illā*. *Except.*

This is what Arabic grammarians call an **exceptive particle**. *Illā* is the word that carves out an exception from a general rule. In English we use it the same way: *all the students failed — except for one.* That *except* doesn't just include one student. It pulls them out of a category that was about to swallow them.

Let's sit with the placement.

The previous ayah — verse 69 — has just said the punishment will be doubled, eternal, humiliating. It is one of the heaviest descriptions of consequence in the Quran. And the very next word that comes out of Allah's mouth is:

***Except.***

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah did not start a new paragraph. He did not say *and now, by the way, here is a separate matter about repentance.* He grammatically attached this exception to the punishment ayah itself. The exception is **inside** the threat. The mercy is **inside** the warning.

Here's what's remarkable about that. In Arabic, when an exception (*istithnā'*) is grammatically connected like this, it means the door of escape is structurally part of the door of judgment. They are one architecture. Allah did not first close the door and then reluctantly crack it open. He built the door already opened. **The threat itself contains its own exit.**

Stay with me.

We tend to imagine divine justice as a wall, and divine mercy as a window someone might cut into the wall later. But the grammar of this passage tells you something different. The window was always there. The wall was built around it.

And the word *illā* is the hinge.

[PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: **No description of consequence in the Quran ever closes without an exception, because in the moral universe Allah has built, no human is ever placed beyond reach. The exception is not an afterthought. It is the structure.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Word *Yubaddilu* — Exchange, Not Erase

Now let's enter the heart of the ayah. The word the early scholars called astonishing.

The Arabic phrase is:

**يُبَدِّلُ ٱللَّهُ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِمْ حَسَنَـٰتٍۢ**

*Yubaddilu Allāhu sayyi'ātihim ḥasanāt.*

Most translations render this as: *Allah will replace their evil deeds with good deeds,* or *Allah will change their bad deeds into good deeds.*

Both translations are pointing at the right thing, but the word *yubaddilu* is doing something specific that English struggles to carry. Let me teach the concept first.

In Arabic, the root **ب-د-ل** (*b-d-l*) gives us a family of words around the idea of *substitution* or *exchange.* The noun *badal* means *a substitute* — the thing that takes the place of something else. *Tabdīl* means *the act of exchanging one thing for another.*

Now — Arabic grammar has different verb forms that intensify or shape a basic meaning. The basic Form I would be *badala* — *to change.* But here Allah uses **Form II** — *baddala* — which adds intensification, deliberate causation, and often transformation.

Think of it like the difference between *to move* and *to deliberately relocate.* Form I says it happened. Form II says it happened intentionally, completely, and by the agent named.

So when Allah says *yubaddilu*, He is not saying *the bad deeds will fade away* or *the bad deeds will be canceled out.* He is saying:

**Allah Himself, deliberately, completely, will exchange them.**

[PAUSE]

But there's more.

Notice what is being exchanged for what. Read it slowly:

*sayyi'ātihim* — *their evil deeds* (the actual things they did)
*ḥasanāt* — *good deeds* (in their place)

This is not erasure. Erasure would be: *and Allah will remove their bad deeds.* The page would be wiped clean. The bad deed would simply be gone.

But Allah did not say He removes them. He said He **exchanges** them. Which means the bad deed does not disappear. It becomes — *something else.*

Let's sit with this carefully. The thing you did — the actual moment, the actual action, the actual page in the record — that page is not torn out. That page is rewritten. The ink that said *sin* now says *good deed.* The very same moment in your life that was a wound in your record becomes, by Allah's deliberate act, a beauty in your record.

[PAUSE]

Here is an analogy that may do the work.

Imagine you are an artist, and over many years you have painted a canvas. Some of what you painted, you are proud of. But there are stains on it. Black smears you put there in moments you cannot bear to remember. You hate looking at the canvas because of those stains. You would do anything to wipe them off.

Now imagine you bring the canvas to a master. The master does not give you a new canvas. That would be erasure. The master takes the same canvas, with the same stains in the same places, and works *the stains themselves* into the painting — until what was a smear becomes a shadow that gives the whole painting its depth, what was a black streak becomes the central line of beauty in the composition.

You look at the canvas and you cannot find the stains anywhere. Not because they were erased. Because they were **transformed**.

That is *yubaddilu*.

[PAUSE]

Now hear what the early scholars pointed to. There is a hadith preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim — the hadith of Abū Dharr — in which the believer, on the Day of Judgment, will be shown his own record. And he will see his sins one by one. And then — at the end — he will be told: *in place of each evil deed you now have a good deed.* And the man will say: *I have sins here that I do not see counted* — because he is looking for the bad deeds, and they are not there. They have become the good deeds he is now reading.

This is why this verse has stopped the breath of everyone who has truly understood it. Because *yubaddilu* does not just save you. It takes the very moments you would erase from your life if you could — the moments that fill you with the deepest shame — and it makes those exact moments the most beautiful entries in your final record.

The thing you hate most about yourself becomes the thing that pleads for you on the Day.

[PAUSE]

But here is something the tradition is careful about, and we should be too. The classical commentators did not read *yubaddilu* in only one way. Al-Ṭabarī, gathering the early authorities, states plainly that *the scholars of interpretation differed* over what this exchange actually means. Some read it as the transformation we have just described — the recorded sins themselves, on the Day of Judgment, converted into good deeds, which is the reading the hadith of Abū Dharr supports. Others read it of *this* life: the one who turns has his whole pattern of conduct exchanged — the *shirk* of his former life replaced by *tawḥīd*, the corruption replaced by chastity, the deeds he once did *in disbelief* swapped for the deeds he now does *in faith*. Al-Ṭabarī himself leans toward this second reading, and at the very next ayah he anchors it in history: this is what Allah did for the idolaters of Makkah who came to believe — and for the Companions who had already repented, believed, and worked righteousness *before this verse was even revealed.* The ayah simply named, out loud, what their God had already been doing for them.

Notice that the two readings are not rivals. They are the same mercy measured at two ends of a single life. The one who today exchanges a sinful pattern for a righteous one is the same one whose record, on that Day, is found rewritten. What begins as a changed life ends as a changed ledger.

[PAUSE]

What this single word reveals: **Allah is not in the business of damage control. He is in the business of transformation. The God of the Quran does not just forgive your past — He rewrites it.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Closing Names — *Ghafūran Raḥīmā*

The ayah closes with two of Allah's names, and the construction of how they appear is worth slowing down for.

**وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًۭا رَّحِيمًۭا**

*Wa kāna Allāhu Ghafūran Raḥīmā.*

The word *kāna* in Arabic is sometimes simply translated as *was.* But when it is used in this construction — *kāna Allāhu* followed by attributes — the classical grammarians read it as expressing **continuous, timeless being.** Not *Allah was forgiving* in some past tense, but *Allah ever is, has been, and remains forgiving.* Some translations capture it with *ever* — *Allah is ever Forgiving, ever Merciful.*

This matters enormously here. Let me explain why.

The ayah has just described an extraordinary act — Allah taking sins and turning them into good deeds. The mind, hearing such a description, immediately wonders: *was that for them? Was that for the Companions? Is that still on offer?*

And the closing — *kāna Allāhu Ghafūran Raḥīmā* — answers before you can ask. It does not say *Allah forgave them.* It says **Allah is — eternally, by His very nature — the Forgiving, the Merciful.**

The transformation in *yubaddilu* is not a one-time grant. It is rooted in **who Allah is.** And who Allah is does not change.

[PAUSE]

But notice something else. Two names, in this order: *Ghafūr* then *Raḥīm.*

*Ghafūr* — from the root **غ-ف-ر** (*gh-f-r*) — carries the idea of *covering, concealing, shielding.* The Arabs used this root for the helmet a soldier wore in battle (*mighfar*) — the thing that covered him so he could not be struck. *Ghafūr* is Allah covering the sin so it cannot strike you.

*Raḥīm* — from the root **ر-ح-م** (*r-ḥ-m*), the same root as *raḥim* (the womb) — carries deep tenderness, a love that nurtures and sustains.

The order is precise. **First the covering. Then the tenderness.** First the protection from what you did. Then the active love that pours into the space the covering created. He does not just hide your sin; once it is hidden, He fills the place it stood with His mercy.

And notice where that tenderness is rooted. This whole passage — every line since verse 63 — has been the portrait of the *ʿibād al-Raḥmān,* the servants of *the Most Merciful.* The section opened under that Name. And now, at the verse that readmits the fallen, Allah seals with *Raḥīm* — the very same root, *r-ḥ-m.* The one who through his three great sins had placed himself outside the company of the Merciful's servants is, by this closing word, folded back under the very Name the entire passage was named for. He does not return to a stranger. He returns to *al-Raḥmān* — and is counted, once more, among His own.

[PAUSE]

What this single word-pair reveals: **The God you are returning to is not a judge who reluctantly waives a sentence. He is, by His very being, the Coverer and the Tender. The names are not what He does for repentant servants. They are what He always is — and tawbah is how you walk into who He already eternally is.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge to the Themes

Three linguistic discoveries:

The word *illā* — that built the exit into the wall.
The word *yubaddilu* — that exchanges sins for good deeds rather than erasing them.
The names *Ghafūran Raḥīmā* — that root all of this in who Allah eternally is.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what tawbah actually does. About why so many of us misunderstand what is being offered.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Past Is Not What You Think It Is

We carry our pasts like sealed envelopes.

Think about the moment in your life you wish most you could undo. You know the one. Maybe you have spent years trying not to think about it. Maybe you have built an entire identity around making sure no one ever finds out. Maybe you have whispered it once to a trusted friend and then regretted whispering it.

Whatever it is — notice how you relate to it. You probably treat it as a fixed object. *That happened. It is done. The most I can hope for is that no one notices it, that God overlooks it, that it gets lost in the larger record of my life.*

You treat your past the way a court treats evidence. Sealed. Stored. Unchangeable.

[PAUSE]

This ayah is asking you to consider something almost impossible to believe.

In modern psychology there is a concept called *narrative identity* — the idea that we are not really the sum of our experiences but the **story we tell about** our experiences. The same event in two people's lives can be a foundation of shame for one and a foundation of strength for the other, depending entirely on how they have come to understand it. Therapists know this. They spend years helping people reframe — not change what happened, but change what it *means.*

Allah, in this ayah, is doing something that goes beyond reframing.

He is not saying *the meaning of your sins will change in your eyes.* He is saying *the sins themselves, in the actual record kept by the One Who keeps records, will become something else.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with what this means.

The cosmic ledger is not a court transcript. It is not even a memoir you can re-edit. It is something stranger and more merciful than either. It is a living document, and the One Who keeps it has the power to take a line that read *transgression* and rewrite it so that it now reads *occasion of mercy.* And in that final accounting, the very moment that you have spent twenty years hating yourself for becomes the moment for which you are most rewarded.

Not because what you did was good. It was not. Allah is not minimizing the seriousness of *shirk* and murder and *zinā*. He just listed them as catastrophic, two ayat ago.

But because what your repentance produced in you was **so transformative** that the previous moment — the moment of failure — is the soil from which the transformation grew. And Allah, in His generosity, credits the soil for what grew in it.

The sin is the seed of the saint, in the lives of those who turn.

[PAUSE]

Think about what this does to your relationship with your worst memory.

Right now, the memory is something you are trying to escape. You spend energy avoiding it. You pray *for* something despite it. You try to build a life that makes up for it.

What if — instead — that memory is a future good deed waiting for the act of *tāba* to convert it? What if every time you remember it with regret, with the heart turning back to Allah, with the resolve never to return — what if that memory is, right now, in this present moment, becoming something else?

This is what the first believers discovered — the men and women who had come to Islam out of a past full of *jāhiliyyah*, whose former years of *shirk* were, by Allah's promise, exchanged for the faith they now lived: **the very years they most wished they could disown were the years Allah was most generous toward.**

[PAUSE]

**The practical movement:**

Stop sealing your past. Open it. Bring it to Him. Specifically. The thing you have not said in du'ā because you are too ashamed to name it — that is exactly what *yubaddilu* is waiting for. Tonight, in *tahajjud* or just before sleep, name it. Not in despair. In the strange new awareness that this very memory is the raw material of an exchange Allah has already designed for you.

Walk into the building He built. The window was always there.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Three-Part Movement of Tawbah

Look again at how the ayah describes the people for whom this exchange happens. It is not just *those who repent.*

It is three things, in a very specific order:

**تَابَ** — *tāba* — *they turned back*
**وَءَامَنَ** — *wa āmana* — *and believed*
**وَعَمِلَ عَمَلًا صَـٰلِحًا** — *wa ʿamila ʿamalan ṣāliḥan* — *and did righteous work*

Three movements. Pay attention to the order — it is teaching you what real repentance is.

[PAUSE]

The English word *repent* is doing a lot of work, and most of us think of it as a single moment. *I repented.* Past tense, single action, done.

But Allah, in this ayah, is mapping out something architectural. Repentance is not one move. It is three.

**First — *tāba*.** The root **ت-و-ب** (*t-w-b*) literally means *to turn back, to return* — and al-Ṭabarī glosses it here precisely as *returning to the obedience of Allah by abandoning the sin and inclining back (ināba) toward what pleases Him.* This is the orientation shift. You were facing one direction; now you face another. It is the realization, the regret, the turning of the heart away from what you were doing toward Allah. *Tāba* is not yet action. It is direction.

**Second — *āmana*.** *Belief.* The classical gloss is *taṣdīq* — affirming the truth of what the Prophet ﷺ brought. But notice: this affirmation comes *after* the turning. Why? Because for many people who have been deep in sin, belief itself has been damaged. The very capacity to believe — to trust that Allah will receive them, to trust that He is who He says He is, to trust that anything good is still possible — has been worn thin. So tawbah requires a renewal of *īmān* itself. Not just *I am sorry,* but *I trust again. I trust that He hears. I trust that He is what He says He is.* The classical *taṣdīq* — affirming the message — and this inner re-trusting of the One behind it are, for such a person, a single act seen from outside and from within. That is the second movement.

**Third — *ʿamila ʿamalan ṣāliḥan*.** *And he worked righteous work.* Notice the construction: the verb *ʿamila* (worked) and then its object *ʿamalan* (work). It is a doubling. Arabic uses this construction to emphasize. *He really, actually, concretely did the work.* And the classical commentators read this work as having two hands: al-Ṭabarī glosses it as *doing what Allah commanded and desisting from what He forbade.* Righteous work is not only the new good you begin; it is the old harm you actually stop. It is not enough to turn. It is not enough to believe. The transformation must arrive in your hands and feet. In what you actually do tomorrow morning.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this teaches.

We tend to think repentance is mostly about feeling. Feeling bad. Feeling sorry. Crying into the prayer rug. And feeling is part of it — *tāba* is real and necessary.

But Allah is showing us that feeling alone does not produce *yubaddilu.* The exchange — the rewriting of the past — requires all three. Direction. Trust. Action.

This is why some of us repent the same sin a hundred times and still feel nothing has changed. We have been doing one of the three. We feel bad after, and then we go back to the same life. There is regret without trust. There is trust without changed behavior. There is changed behavior without an actual return of the heart.

The three together unlock something the three apart cannot. Al-Muyassar captures exactly this when it renders the verse as *sincere repentance (tawbah naṣūḥ)* and *a firm faith joined to righteous work* — *joined,* because the joining is itself the condition.

[PAUSE]

Think about how this maps onto change in any domain.

Anyone who has ever broken a difficult habit knows that wanting to stop is necessary but not sufficient. Believing it is possible to stop is necessary but not sufficient. Doing the small concrete things that make stopping possible is necessary but not sufficient. You need all three. And they have to feed each other — the direction renews the trust, the trust renews the action, the action confirms the direction.

Allah, the One Who designed the human heart, has named exactly the three movements that produce real change.

And He has tied the rewriting of your past to the doing of all three.

[PAUSE]

**The practical movement:**

When you next make tawbah, stop and ask yourself which of the three is missing.

Are you turning, but not believing He will receive you? Then your work is to renew the trust. Read about His names. Remember His mercy. Refuse the despair that says *not me, not this.*

Are you turning and believing, but not changing what you do tomorrow? Then your work is the third movement — one specific, concrete righteous deed that interrupts the pattern. Not a hundred. One. Repeated.

Are you doing righteous deeds but not actually turning the heart away from the thing? Then your work is the first movement — facing what you have been avoiding, naming it, letting yourself feel the regret you have been numbing.

The three together open the door. The door was always open. You walk through it on three legs.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

The grammar of *illā* — the *except* — showed us that the door of escape is structurally built into the wall of judgment. Mercy is not added on later. Mercy is the architecture.

The verb *yubaddilu* — Form II, intensified, deliberate — showed us that Allah does not erase your past. He **exchanges** it. The very moments of failure become, in the final record, the moments of greatest reward.

The names *Ghafūran Raḥīmā*, framed by *kāna* — the eternal *being* of Allah — showed us that all of this is rooted in who Allah always is. Not what He does sometimes. What He timelessly is.

And the three movements — *tāba, āmana, ʿamila* — gave us the architecture of how a human walks into that eternal mercy. Direction. Trust. Action.

These are not separate insights. They are one insight from different angles. The insight is this: **In Allah's universe, no human is structurally beyond reach, no past is structurally fixed, and no failure is the final word — because mercy is not what Allah does. Mercy is what Allah is.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the specific past event in your life that you most wish *yubaddilu* would be applied to? Have you ever named it directly to Allah, in du'ā, by name?

2. Of the three movements — turning, believing, doing — which one do you most need to develop? And what would it look like to take one concrete step in that movement this week?

3. When you imagine your final record being read, do you imagine yourself trying to hide certain pages? What would it change about your worship if you could believe — actually believe — that those pages will read differently than you fear?

4. Is there someone in your life whom you have written off as beyond redemption? What does this ayah say about your judgment of them?

5. The ayah describes mercy as Allah's eternal being. How does that change the urgency of *now* — the urgency of returning today, this hour, before the heart hardens further?

### The Closing Invitation

You have, somewhere in your life, a memory you would do anything to undo. You probably have several.

This ayah is not asking you to forget them. It is not asking you to forgive yourself for them. It is asking you to bring them to the One Who, by His very nature, exchanges sins for good deeds, and to perform the three movements that activate that exchange.

The door was built already opened. The window was always cut into the wall. *Ghafūran Raḥīmā* is not what He becomes when you arrive. It is what He has always been. You are not asking Him to change. You are walking into who He has eternally been waiting to be — for you.

Tonight, before sleep, name one thing. Turn from it. Renew your trust that He receives you. Resolve one small concrete deed that interrupts the pattern.

And then go to sleep knowing that the thing you most hate about your life is, at this moment, by the deliberate act of the One Who exchanges, becoming something else entirely.

### Du'ā

*O Allah, You who are eternally Forgiving and eternally Merciful — we come to You with the pages of our lives we cannot bear to read. Turn our hearts back to You with the turning that is real. Renew our trust in You when our trust has worn thin. Move our hands and feet to the work that proves the turning. And by Your generosity that no human could have dared to claim for You — exchange our worst moments for the moments we will most love to find on the Day we meet You. Āmīn.*
