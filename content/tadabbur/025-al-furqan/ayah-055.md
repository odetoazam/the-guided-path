# The Backer of His Own Lord

## A Tadabbur on Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 55

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to the ayah first. Hear it before you understand it.

> [ayah:25:55] وَيَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ مَا لَا يَنفَعُهُمْ وَلَا يَضُرُّهُمْ ۗ وَكَانَ ٱلْكَافِرُ عَلَىٰ رَبِّهِۦ ظَهِيرًا
>
> *Wa yaʿbudūna min dūni-llāhi mā lā yanfaʿuhum wa lā yaḍurruhum — wa kāna-l-kāfiru ʿalā rabbihi ẓahīrā.*
>
> **"And they worship, instead of Allah, that which neither benefits them nor harms them — and the disbeliever is, against his Lord, a backer."**

### The World This Ayah Walked Into

To feel the weight of these fourteen words, you have to stand for a moment in the middle of Surah Al-Furqan.

The Quraysh have just been firing objections at the Prophet ﷺ for forty-some verses. *Why does this messenger eat food and walk in the markets? Why isn't an angel sent down with him? Why isn't a treasure dropped into his lap? Why doesn't he have a garden he eats from?* The surah opens with their attacks and lets them keep coming until you feel exhausted on the Prophet's behalf.

And then Allah does something extraordinary. He doesn't argue back point by point. He turns the camera away from them and toward the world.

*Look,* He says. *Have you not seen how your Lord stretches the shadow? And had He willed, He would have made it still. Then We made the sun a guide over it.*

*And it is He who made the night for you a covering, and sleep a rest, and the day a resurrection.*

*And it is He who sent the winds as good news before His mercy, and We send down from the sky pure water...*

*And it is He who released the two seas, this one sweet and palatable, and that one salty and bitter, and placed between them a barrier...*

*And it is He who created from water a human being and made him kindred by lineage and marriage...*

Verse after verse. Sign after sign. Allah is touring them through the universe, asking them — without asking — *what kind of Being do you imagine could do this?*

And right in the middle of this tour, just as the listener's heart is opening to the immensity of what is being shown, Allah turns the camera back to them. And what comes out of His mouth is not anger. It is something stranger and more devastating.

### The Hook

*And they worship, instead of Allah, that which neither benefits them nor harms them.*

That alone would be a verdict. But the ayah doesn't end there. It adds something that should stop us in our tracks. It says the disbeliever is, against his own Lord, **a backer**.

Not just a denier. Not just a forgetter. A **backer**. Someone who has chosen a side — and the side he has chosen is *against the One who is sustaining the very breath he uses to deny.*

How does a creature become a backer against the Being who holds his existence in place every second? What does that even look like? And — here is the question we have to sit with — could that creature be us?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to open three windows in this ayah. Each one is a small thing — a phrase, a grammatical choice, a single word — and each one, when we look closely, opens onto something enormous.

---

### Element One: "That Which Neither Benefits Nor Harms"

Before we get to the Arabic, let me ask you a question.

When you think about why people *don't* worship a stone, or a tree, or a piece of wood — why is that obvious to you? What's the mental shortcut?

Most of us, if we're honest, would say: *because it can't do anything.* It just sits there. It can't help me. It can't hurt me. It is, functionally, nothing.

Hold on to that intuition. Because Allah is about to use exactly that intuition — and then turn it on us.

The phrase is:

> **مَا لَا يَنفَعُهُمْ وَلَا يَضُرُّهُمْ**
> *mā lā yanfaʿuhum wa lā yaḍurruhum*
> "that which does not benefit them and does not harm them"

Let's sit with the structure. Notice first what Allah does *not* say. He does not say: *they worship a thing that cannot create.* He does not say: *they worship a thing that cannot resurrect.* He does not say: *they worship a thing that has no knowledge of the unseen.*

Any of those would be a perfectly valid critique of an idol. But Allah goes lower. Much lower. He sets the bar at the floor.

He says: this thing they worship cannot even **help them or hurt them.**

Why is that the floor? Because help and harm are the two reasons human beings reach for anything outside themselves. We seek what helps us. We avoid what harms us. The entire emotional life of a creature can be mapped onto that one axis: pull toward benefit, push away from harm. Every prayer, every plea, every fear, every hope, every offering — they all live inside that axis.

So when Allah says *this thing cannot benefit you and cannot harm you*, He is not just listing two of its limitations. He is saying: **it is functionally absent from the only axis that matters to you.** It does not exist on the spectrum where your needs live.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with me. Notice something else. The word **مَا** (*mā*) — "that which." In Arabic, **مَا** is typically used for non-rational things. For objects. For inanimate forces. Allah did not say *man lā yanfaʿuhum* — *those who do not benefit them.* He said **mā** — *that* which does not benefit them.

The grammar itself demotes the idol. Even before we hear the verdict, the pronoun has already filed it under "thing." Not "being." Not "person." **Thing.**

Here's what's remarkable. The same Quran that uses **مَا** for an idol will use **مَنْ** — the rational pronoun — for an ant, for the Hudhud bird in Surah al-Naml. Allah grammatically dignifies an ant with personhood and grammatically demotes the worshipped idol to objecthood. The hierarchy isn't about what we call alive. It's about what *responds* — what hears, knows, intends, helps, harms.

The idol is grammatically less than an ant.

Now ask yourself the harder question. Most of us are not bowing to stones. So why does this matter to us?

Because the criterion Allah is using is not *does it look like an idol*. The criterion is *can it actually benefit me, can it actually harm me?* And the moment that becomes the test, every modern thing we lean on starts trembling.

The follower count cannot benefit you. Not really. It cannot reach into your grave. It cannot calm your chest at 3 a.m. It can produce a flash of dopamine that simulates benefit, the way an idol's incense produced a smell that simulated presence. But it cannot actually do anything on the axis of help and harm.

The portfolio cannot benefit you in any real sense. It can buy comfort, yes — but it cannot decide whether you live to see tomorrow's market open. It cannot give you children. It cannot return your father from the grave.

The approval of the room cannot harm you, ultimately. The room cannot summon you to stand on the Day. The room cannot account for a single deed.

What this invites us to consider is this: **the test of whether something has secretly become an idol in your life is not whether you call it god. It is whether you have started fearing its harm or chasing its benefit as though it actually controlled the axis.** Allah is showing the Quraysh that their idols failed the help-and-harm test. He is, by extension, showing us that ours will too.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer here, and it is the layer that breaks the heart. Some of the early commentators noted that *neither benefits them nor harms them* is, in a way, a mercy. Because if these idols *could* harm them, the harm would already be done. The Quraysh have insulted, abandoned, betrayed, mocked. If their stone gods had any agency at all, the worshippers would already be punished for the way they treat them on bad days. The fact that idols cannot harm is, paradoxically, the only reason the idolater is still alive to be addressed.

But the One who *can* harm — the One who *does* hold the axis — He has not yet brought down what He could bring down. That patience is exactly what the rest of the surah is going to expose.

---

### Element Two: "Against His Lord" — Read the Preposition

Now we arrive at the strangest part of the ayah. The closing clause:

> **وَكَانَ ٱلْكَافِرُ عَلَىٰ رَبِّهِۦ ظَهِيرًا**
> *wa kāna-l-kāfiru ʿalā rabbihi ẓahīrā*
> "and the disbeliever is, against his Lord, a backer."

Before we touch the word *ẓahīr* — "backer" — let's pause on a tiny preposition that does enormous work: **عَلَىٰ** (*ʿalā*).

In Arabic, *ʿalā* means "on" or "upon" or "against." It is the preposition of opposition. If you say *I am ʿalā so-and-so*, depending on context, you are saying *I am on top of him* or *I am against him.* It is the preposition you use when two parties are positioned on opposite sides of a contest.

Allah could have said the disbeliever is *with* something else (*maʿa*), or *for* something else (*li-*). He chose *ʿalā* — **against**.

And the object of that preposition is not random. It is **رَبِّهِۦ** — *rabbihi* — **his Lord.**

Now teach yourself something before we go further. The word *rabb* in Arabic is not the same as the English word "lord." *Rabb* comes from a root that means to nurture, to raise, to bring up gradually toward completion. A *rabb* is the one who develops you, sustains you, takes a thing from its smallness and walks it toward its fullness. A mother is, in a sense, a *rabb* of her child during those years of nurture. A teacher is a *rabb* of a student in a discipline.

When Allah uses *al-Rabb* of Himself, He is naming the entire arc of your existence: the One who took you from a drop, formed you in a womb, brought you to breath, gave you eyes that opened, gave you a tongue that learned, gives you food today and breath tomorrow and the eyelids that close when you sleep. That is *rabb*.

And the ayah says the disbeliever is *ʿalā rabbihi* — **against the very One who is doing all of that for him.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment. Don't move past it. The word is not *Allah* here, though it could have been. It is *rabb* — and specifically *rabbihi*, "*his* Lord." The possessive pronoun ties him personally to this Sustainer. *His* Sustainer. The One who is, in this very second, sustaining *him*.

It is one thing to oppose a stranger. It is another to oppose your benefactor. It is a third thing entirely to oppose your benefactor *while still feeding from his hand.* That third thing is what *ʿalā rabbihi* describes.

Here's the analogy. Imagine a child in a hospital bed. He has been there since he was born. He never had a chance to take a breath outside of medical care. There is a doctor — let us call him *the doctor*, with all the meaning packed into that role — who comes every hour to check his vitals, to adjust his medications, to keep the machine running that keeps his lungs moving. The doctor's hand is on the dial of the machine right now. If the doctor lifted his hand for thirty seconds, the child would die.

Now imagine that child, with his eyes fixed on the doctor's hand, declaring loudly to the room: *I am against this man. I do not believe in him. I am positioning myself in opposition to him.*

That is *ʿalā rabbihi*. That is the absurdity Allah is describing. The opposition is not just rebellion. It is rebellion *while being sustained by the One you are rebelling against.*

And what makes it grammatically devastating is that the preposition *ʿalā* doesn't just *describe* the opposition. It *requires* it. *ʿAlā* is the preposition you use when you are making war on someone, when you are siding with their enemies, when you are *against* them in the active sense.

The ayah is not saying the disbeliever has merely turned away. It is saying he has actively positioned himself on the opposing side of a contest in which the other party is sustaining his every breath.

---

### Element Three: The Word *Ẓahīr*

Now we reach the word that the entire ayah builds toward. The word that, if you understand it, the whole verse opens.

> **ظَهِيرًا** (*ẓahīrā*)

Most translations render this as "supporter" or "helper" or "assistant" or "backer." All of these are partial. To feel the word, you have to trace its root.

The root is **ظ-ه-ر** (*ẓ-h-r*). Sit with that root. From this root come some of the most loaded words in the Quran:

- *ẓahara* — to appear, to become manifest, to come out into the open.
- *ẓahr* — the back. The literal back of a body.
- *ẓuhr* — noon. The moment when the sun is at its most apparent, when shadows shrink, when nothing is hidden.
- *ẓāhir* — outward, manifest, visible. (One of Allah's names is *al-Ẓāhir* — the Manifest.)
- *ẓahīr* — a backer. Literally, *one who has your back.*

Notice the unity. *Ẓahr* is the back of a body. *Ẓuhr* is the time when the sun is at its peak — at its most "out in the open." *Ẓāhir* is what is manifest, brought out. *Ẓahīr* is the person who stands behind you, at your *back*, supporting you when the contest comes.

In the desert, in tribal Arabia, *ẓahīr* was a heavy word. Your *ẓahīr* was the one who, when blood was spilled, stood with you. When two clans faced off across a wadi, your *ẓahīr* was the man whose presence behind you made you a force the other side had to reckon with. To be someone's *ẓahīr* was to throw your weight, your honor, your sword, your tribe behind their cause.

This is the word Allah uses for the disbeliever. *The disbeliever is, against his Lord, a* ẓahīr.

[PAUSE]

Now ask the question that should be screaming at us by now. **Whose** *ẓahīr*? Whom is he backing?

The ayah does not say. And that absence is everything.

The classical commentators read this in different ways, and rather than pick one, let me show you the composite. They are camera angles on the same reality.

**One angle:** The disbeliever is a *ẓahīr* for **Shayṭān** against his Lord. Every disobedience is a sword swung on Shayṭān's side of the battlefield. Every choice to follow the whisper is a step taken in the rank behind Iblīs.

**Another angle:** The disbeliever is a *ẓahīr* for **the falsehood** itself — for the very lies he repeats. He is the muscle behind the lie. The lie does not have legs of its own; it walks on his.

**Another angle:** The disbeliever is a *ẓahīr* for **his own nafs** — his lower self has declared war on his Lord, and he has chosen to put his weight behind his nafs rather than his Rabb.

**Another angle, and this one is breathtaking:** Since *ẓahīr* implies a side that is **against** Allah, and there is no actual side that exists against Allah — the ayah is mocking. It is saying: *he positions himself as if he were a backer in a contest, but the contest is imaginary.* He is a *ẓahīr* for nothing — flexing in an empty arena. The cosmic theater has only one Power. He is shadowboxing.

Each of these is true. They are the same image from different angles. The disbeliever has thrown his weight, his life, his energy, his choices behind a side that *cannot win*, against a Lord who *holds his every breath*, in a contest that is, in any real sense, **not a contest at all.**

[PAUSE]

And here is where the root becomes a knife. *Ẓahr* is the back. *Ẓahīr* is the one at your back. But who is at *whose* back here?

The ayah is structured so that the disbeliever has placed himself *as if* he were standing behind some force opposing his Rabb. But anatomically, theologically, what is actually behind him?

His Rabb. His Sustainer. The One whose hand is on the back of his existence, holding him up.

So when the disbeliever turns his face away from Allah, he turns his back — *ẓahr* — to the very One whose support is keeping his back upright. He has made his *ẓahr* into a wall against his Rabb, while his Rabb continues to hold up that very back.

The same root that means "to back someone" also means "the back you turn." The word *ẓahīr* in this ayah carries both. He is a backer for falsehood and he is a back-turner from the truth. The one motion is the other motion.

The Arabic does in one word what English needs a paragraph to do.

### Bridge to the Themes

So we have three things now.

First: an idol — a thing — that cannot operate on the only axis that matters to a creature.

Second: a preposition — *ʿalā* — that turns the disbeliever's relationship with his Lord into an opposition, while that Lord is the very One sustaining him.

Third: a word — *ẓahīr* — that paints the disbeliever as a fighter standing in the rank against his own Sustainer, his back turned to the One whose hand is on his back.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Idols That Cannot Help Us — and Why We Worship Them Anyway

Let me ask you a question that is uncomfortable on purpose.

What do you reach for, first thing in the morning, before you reach for Allah?

Don't answer too quickly. Be honest with yourself. Most of us, before our forehead has touched a prayer mat, have already touched a screen. Before we have whispered *Bismillah*, we have whispered to an algorithm. We have asked the algorithm: *what's happening in the world? Am I being talked about? Did anyone like what I said yesterday? Is the market up?*

We have asked something for news, for validation, for orientation, for reassurance.

That is a form of *ʿibādah*. Not the prostrating kind. The smaller, hourly kind. The kind that says: *this is where I go for help.*

Now run that through Allah's test. **Can the algorithm help me? Can it harm me?**

The honest answer is: it can produce sensations that *feel* like help and harm. It can make me feel briefly important. It can make me feel briefly afraid. But it cannot actually intervene in my life. It cannot change my rizq. It cannot heal my mother. It cannot extend my life by an hour. It cannot make Allah pleased with me.

It is *mā lā yanfaʿuhum wa lā yaḍurruhum* — that which does not benefit and does not harm — wearing a costume of helpfulness so convincing that we have organized our days around it.

[PAUSE]

But here is the real question. Why do we do it? If these things cannot actually deliver, why does every human being keep reaching for them?

Because the human heart was made to lean. We are creatures designed to pour our weight onto something. And when we do not pour our weight onto Allah, the weight does not vanish — it has to land somewhere. So it lands on the closest thing.

The idol-worshipper in Mecca was not stupid. He knew the stone could not move. But he had to pour his weight somewhere, and the stone was right there, and the entire culture around him was leaning on the same stone, so leaning on it felt like leaning on something solid because so many shoulders were touching it.

That is exactly how the modern idol works. The follower count is not solid because *it* is solid; it is solid because so many shoulders are leaning on it. The career is not solid because the career is real; it is solid because everyone agrees to act as if it is. We are leaning on each other's leaning. And underneath the whole pile, there is nothing.

This is the genius of *mā lā yanfaʿuhum wa lā yaḍurruhum*. Allah is not just telling us our idols are weak. He is telling us why they *feel* strong: because we have all agreed to lean on them together. Lift the agreement, and the idol falls.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how we live tomorrow?

It is not that you are required to delete the apps. It is not that you are forbidden from caring about your career. It is that you are required to **run the help-and-harm test on every weight you place.** When you feel yourself leaning, ask: *is this thing on the axis of real benefit and real harm? Or is it a costume?*

And when you find a costume — when you catch yourself fearing what someone will say, or chasing what an account will give — bring that fear or that hope back to the One who is actually on the axis. Pour the weight onto the One who can carry it.

The five daily prayers are precisely this exercise. Five times a day, Allah pulls your forehead to the ground and reminds you: *the axis is here. Lean here. The other things are costumes.*

---

### Theme Two: The Strangest Position in the Universe

Now to the harder theme. The one that should rearrange us.

The disbeliever is *ʿalā rabbihi ẓahīrā* — **against his own Lord, a backer.**

Don't let the abstraction of "the disbeliever" save you here. The ayah is describing a position the human heart can occupy. And we have to ask honestly: how often have we occupied it?

You don't have to deny Allah's existence to be *ʿalā rabbihi*. You only have to use the gifts He is giving you in this very second to do the things that displease Him.

The eyes He gave you, used to look at what He forbade — those eyes have been borrowed and turned against the lender.

The tongue He gave you, used to wound a believer or to speak a lie or to mock the truth — that tongue has been weaponized against the One who fashioned it.

The intellect He gave you, used to argue *against* His commands rather than for them — that intellect has been enlisted on the wrong side.

The wealth He gave you, used in causes that take you and others further from Him — that wealth has been redirected toward the opposition.

This is *ʿalā rabbihi* in real time. The body that was meant to be His instrument has, in moments, been pointed at His face.

[PAUSE]

And the word *ẓahīr* is what makes it worse, because it is not passive. *Ẓahīr* is not "someone who is accidentally on the wrong side." *Ẓahīr* is "someone who has thrown his weight." It is the deliberate verb. It implies energy spent, side chosen, support given.

So the ayah is asking us — though it is addressed to the disbeliever, the question lands on every conscience — *whose* ẓahīr *have I been today?*

When I gave my time, where did the time go? Toward His remembrance, or toward the distraction? When I gave my money, where did it go? Toward what He loves, or toward what He forbids? When I gave my voice, what did I amplify? When I gave my attention, who got it?

A *ẓahīr* is identified not by what he says but by where his weight lands. And Allah, in this ayah, is showing us that there is no neutral. Either the weight is behind His remembrance, or it is — by default — adding mass to the opposing rank.

[PAUSE]

But there is a mercy buried inside this terror, and it is the mercy that should send us to our knees.

The ayah says *the disbeliever* — *al-kāfir*. It does not say *the believer who slips*. It does not say *the one who occasionally drifts*. The condition described — *ʿalā rabbihi ẓahīrā* — is the condition of someone who has *settled* into opposition, not someone who has stumbled into a moment of it.

And the very fact that you are listening to this — that you are running this question through your heart — is evidence that you are not *al-kāfir* in the verse's sense. The fact that the description disturbs you means the description does not apply to you in its fullness. *Al-kāfir* in this ayah is the one for whom this position has become his identity, his rest, his stability. The one who never feels the question. The one whose conscience has gone silent.

If your conscience is awake, then you are not standing on the side described. You are standing on the side that is *worried about being on that side.* Those are not the same place.

The mercy of being shown this image is that you can still choose otherwise. The ayah is the ringing of an alarm bell precisely so that you wake up before the description settles on you. Settling is the danger. Hearing the alarm is the rescue.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how we live tomorrow?

It is this: every choice today is a deposit into one rank or the other. The notion that there are "neutral" hours — hours in which your weight is not landing on either side — is a comfortable lie. The hours in which you are not actively remembering Allah are not blank pages. They are pages on which something else is being written.

The remedy is not to multiply rituals. It is to *re-direct the weight*. Sit down at the start of your day and ask: *whose* ẓahīr *do I want to be today?* Then make small, deliberate choices that put your weight behind that answer. A morning *adhkār*. A pause before sending the message that would wound. A glance turned away. A dollar given. A *Lā ilāha illa-llāh* whispered while waiting at the light.

These are not small. These are the muscles of a *ẓahīr*. You are building a soldier — the question is which army.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### How the Two Halves of the Ayah Speak to Each Other

Now look at the architecture of these fourteen words.

The first half of the ayah describes their idols: *that which neither benefits them nor harms them.*

The second half describes them: *and the disbeliever is, against his Lord, a backer.*

Place the two halves next to each other and something startling appears.

Their idols are **powerless on the axis of help and harm.**

But they have made themselves **useful on the axis of opposition.**

In other words: the idols cannot do anything. But the worshipper has volunteered to do something *for* the idol — to give the idol his weight, his time, his honor, his life. The worshipper has become the idol's *ẓahīr*.

The idols cannot help — but the worshipper helps the idols. The idols cannot harm — but the worshipper, in his servitude to them, harms himself.

The relationship is grotesquely inverted. The thing that should be sustained by the worshipper's Lord is instead sustaining the worshipper's idol. The creature has become the *ẓahīr* of his lower object, against the Lord who is *ẓahīr* of him.

The architecture of the ayah is the architecture of a misplaced loyalty. **The strongest creature in the room is propping up the weakest object in the room — against the Lord who props up the strongest creature.**

This is what shirk *is*, structurally. It is the inversion of the support chain. It is making yourself the back of a thing that has no back, while turning your back on the One whose hand is on your back.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

Don't answer these now. Carry them.

1. **What is the first thing you reach for in the morning, before Allah?** What does the choice of that thing reveal about where you actually believe help and harm live?

2. **Run the test on three things in your life right now:** the praise you crave, the criticism you fear, the security you cling to. Are they on the axis of real benefit and real harm — or are they costumes?

3. **Name a moment this week when you used a gift from your Rabb against your Rabb.** Not in shame. In awareness. So that the conscience stays awake.

4. **Whose** *ẓahīr* **were you yesterday?** Where did your weight land? Where did your attention live? Which side did your hours feed?

5. **What would it look like, tomorrow, to deliberately become Allah's** *ẓahīr*? Not in some grand way. In one small, specific, repeatable way.

### The Distillation in One Sentence

> **To worship anything other than Allah is to give your weight to a thing that cannot carry it, while turning your back on the One who is carrying you.**

### The Closing Invitation

You will not finish this reflection and dismantle every misplaced lean. That is not how the heart works. The heart drifts back. The idols put on new costumes. The phone is in your hand again before the salah rug is rolled up.

But what you can do — what this ayah is asking you to do — is **wake the conscience**. Not to be perfect. To be aware. So that when the weight starts landing on a costume, you feel it. So that when your back starts turning, you notice. So that the description *ʿalā rabbihi ẓahīrā* never gets to settle into you the way it settled into them.

The believer is not the one who never drifts. The believer is the one whose alarm bell still rings.

This ayah is the bell.

### A Closing Du'a

> Yā Rabb, You who hold the back of every creature that turns its back on You — do not let me become a backer of anything against You. Catch my weight before it lands on a costume. Make me a *ẓahīr* of Your remembrance, of Your Book, of Your Messenger ﷺ, of every word that is true. And when I drift — and I will drift — let the alarm in my chest still ring loud enough that I find my way back, my forehead to the ground, where the weight belongs.
>
> *Āmīn.*
