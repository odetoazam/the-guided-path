# When the Command to Prostrate Increases the Distance

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمُ ٱسْجُدُوا۟ لِلرَّحْمَـٰنِ قَالُوا۟ وَمَا ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ أَنَسْجُدُ لِمَا تَأْمُرُنَا وَزَادَهُمْ نُفُورًا

**Transliteration:**
> *Wa idhā qīla lahumu-sjudū lir-Raḥmāni qālū wa mā-r-Raḥmān? A-nasjudu li-mā taʾmurunā? Wa zādahum nufūrā.*

**Translation:**
> "And when it is said to them, 'Prostrate to the Most Merciful,' they say, 'And what is the Most Merciful? Should we prostrate to that which you order us?' And it increases them in aversion."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

To feel what's happening in this ayah, you have to step out of a world where the word *Allah* has been familiar to you since childhood — and step into a world where a brand-new Name of God has just begun ringing through the streets of Makkah.

The Quraysh knew the word *Allah*. They invoked Him as the supreme deity above their idols. What they were not used to — what landed in Makkah like a foreign tongue — was *Ar-Raḥmān*. The Most Merciful. The All-Merciful. A Name so saturated with tenderness that just hearing it changes the temperature of a sentence.

The Prophet ﷺ began calling them with this Name. Bilal in chains kept saying *Aḥad, Aḥad* — One, One. The companions began their letters with *Bismillāhi-r-Raḥmān*. And the Quraysh — who had a thousand ways of saying "Lord" and "Master" and "Great One" — bristled at this Name. They knew its weight. They sensed what it implied. A god of crushing power they could negotiate with. A God of *raḥmah* — that meant He had come close. That meant He had claims on them.

So when the Prophet ﷺ stood before them and said, "Prostrate to *Ar-Raḥmān*," he wasn't merely asking them to bow. He was asking them to bow toward **the Name they were most afraid of**.

### The Hook

Notice something strange in this ayah before we even open it.

When the command comes — *prostrate to Ar-Raḥmān* — they do not say, "We refuse." They do not say, "We won't." They say something far more revealing: **"And what is Ar-Raḥmān?"**

They pretend not to know.

[PAUSE]

And then Allah ends the ayah with five words that should stop us cold: **"And it increased them in aversion."**

Read that again. The very command meant to draw them near *increased their distance*. The mercy they were called to *pushed them further away*. Hold that paradox. We're going to spend the next hour finding out why — and why this is the most important ayah in the Quran for anyone who has ever felt their own heart hardening against something they know is true.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Qīla* — The Voice With No Mouth

Before we go to the word, let me ask you to notice something about how language works.

When I say, "*The teacher told them to sit down*," you immediately picture someone — a face, a posture, a tone. Active verbs in any language carry a doer. Your mind reaches for the agent.

But when I say, "*It was said to them to sit down*" — something shifts. The voice loses its body. You can no longer picture who said it. The command is now floating, ambient, coming from everywhere and nowhere at once.

Arabic has a name for this construction: the *fiʿl majhūl* — the verb whose subject is unknown, hidden, or deliberately erased. The active *qāla* (he said) becomes the passive *qīla* (it was said). And every time the Quran chooses *qīla* over *qāla*, you should pause and ask: **why did Allah hide the speaker?**

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah does in our ayah:

> *Wa idhā **qīla** lahum...*
> "And when **it is said** to them..."

He could have said *qāla lahumu-r-rasūl* — "the Messenger said to them." That would have been historically accurate. The Prophet ﷺ was the one calling them. But Allah doesn't say that.

Stay with me. Why?

Because if Allah had named the Prophet ﷺ as the speaker, the ayah would have become an ayah about the Quraysh's relationship with Muhammad ﷺ. They could have said — and they did say in other contexts — "He's a man like us. Why should we listen to him?" The whole encounter would have been horizontal. Two humans. A power struggle.

By saying *qīla* — *it was said* — Allah lifts the command out of any single speaker. The voice now comes from the ayah itself. From revelation. From everywhere truth has ever reached them. It is as if Allah is saying: *forget who delivered the message. The message is the message. The summons came.*

Here's what's remarkable about this. Notice that immediately after, when the Quraysh respond, Allah switches to the active voice: **qālū** — *they said.* They are named. Their voice has a face. Their refusal is owned.

The pattern is exquisite: **the call is universal; the refusal is personal.** When truth comes, it arrives without a face — because truth is bigger than its messenger. When you reject it, your name is on the rejection.

This is the first thing the linguistic surface gives us. The ayah is not about the Quraysh and Muhammad ﷺ. The ayah is about every human being who has ever heard a summons to surrender — and what they did with it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Mā* — The Grammar of Pretending Not to Know

Now we have to look at one of the most precise word-choices in the entire Quran. It's one tiny word. But it carries the whole psychological drama of this ayah.

Arabic, like English, has different question words. To ask about a person, you use *man* — *who?* To ask about a thing or a definition, you use *mā* — *what?*

If a stranger walks into your office and you want to know who he is, you say *man hādhā?* — "Who is this?" But if someone places a strange object on your desk, you say *mā hādhā?* — "What is this?"

The distinction matters. *Man* assumes there is a person there to be known. *Mā* assumes there is a thing — possibly an object, possibly a concept, possibly nothing recognizable at all.

Now look at what the Quraysh say:

> *Qālū wa **mā**-r-Raḥmān?*
> "They said: and **what** is Ar-Raḥmān?"

Read that with Arabic ears. They have just been told to prostrate to *Ar-Raḥmān*. The Name of mercy. A divine Name. And their response is not *man Ar-Raḥmān* — "Who is Ar-Raḥmān?" — which would have been a sincere theological question.

Their response is *mā Ar-Raḥmān* — "**What** is Ar-Raḥmān?"

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. They have demoted the question. They have grammatically pushed *Ar-Raḥmān* down from the category of "who" — beings who can be known and addressed — into the category of "what" — things, concepts, objects, abstractions. They have turned a Name into a noun. They have taken what is alive and personal and made it an *it*.

This is not ignorance. This is a strategy. A particular kind of intellectual maneuver that we still recognize today.

Think about how a cynic discusses something sacred to you. You say, "I love my mother." The cynic says, "What is *love*, really? Isn't it just neurochemistry?" You say, "I find peace in prayer." The cynic says, "What is *prayer*? Isn't it just a coping mechanism?"

Notice the move. They take the living thing and demand you define it as a *thing*. Once they've forced it into the category of *what*, they've already won — because anything reduced to a *what* can be analyzed, dismissed, and put on a shelf.

The Quraysh are doing this with the Most Merciful. They are not asking to be informed. They are pretending the Name carries no meaning so they don't have to feel its meaning.

And here's the linguistic evidence that this is pretending: classical Arabic scholars note that the root *r-ḥ-m* — from which *Ar-Raḥmān* derives — is one of the deepest, oldest roots in the Arabic language itself. *Raḥim* means *womb*. The Quraysh used the word *raḥm* every single day — in poetry, in tribal bonds, in calls to maintain ties of kinship (*ṣilat ar-raḥim*). They knew exactly what *raḥmah* meant. They knew exactly what intensification the form *Raḥmān* indicated.

So when they ask *mā Ar-Raḥmān*, they are not confessing ignorance. They are performing it.

This is what we might call **strategic incomprehension** — the deliberate refusal to recognize what your own language is telling you, because if you recognized it, you would have to act on it.

And here is the spiritual implication that should make every one of us tremble: you can be fluent in the vocabulary of mercy and still ask "what is mercy?" when its claim arrives at your door. You can have read the entire Quran and still flinch at *Ar-Raḥmān* when He calls you to surrender something. The grammar of pretending-not-to-know is a heart-state, not an information state.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Zādahum Nufūrā* — When the Cure Becomes the Wound

We come now to the ending of the ayah. And this is where the ayah becomes terrifying.

> *Wa **zādahum nufūrā**.*
> "And it **increased them** in aversion."

Two words. Let's open them carefully.

First — *zāda*. The verb means *to increase, to add, to make more*. It is the same root we use when we say *Allah-u akbar wa lillāhi-l-ḥamd* and add *zādan kathīrā* — much addition. *Zāda* takes something that already exists and intensifies it.

Second — *nufūr*. From the root *n-f-r*. This word does not mean *refusal* or *rejection*. It is more visceral than that. *Nafara* in classical Arabic describes the violent flinching motion of a wild animal that bolts when startled. It is the deer that smells the hunter and explodes into flight. It is the camel that shies away from water it suspects is poisoned. The whole body recoils. The legs turn. The animal puts distance between itself and the threat as fast as it can.

So *nufūr* is not a position. It is a **flight**.

Now read what Allah is telling us. The Quraysh were called to the Most Merciful. The summons came. And the call to mercy *increased* their flight.

Stop and let that land.

The cure increased the disease. The medicine inflamed the wound. The very thing meant to heal them caused them to recoil harder. **The closer mercy came, the further they ran.**

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most important psychological-spiritual realities in the entire Quran, and Allah has compressed it into two words. Let me try to draw out what's there.

You and I assume that exposure to truth softens hearts. We assume that if someone hears the right verse, sees the right beauty, encounters the right kindness — surely something will shift in them. Surely the call of mercy will draw them toward mercy.

But Allah is telling us: **a heart in a particular state will respond to mercy by hardening, not softening.** A heart in flight does not see the outstretched hand as rescue. It sees the outstretched hand as a hunter. The closer the hand comes, the faster the heart bolts.

Now — notice what Allah did *not* say. He didn't say *fa-nafarū* (so they fled). He said *zādahum nufūrā* — *it increased them in flight*. Which means **they were already fleeing before the call came.** The flight was already happening. The summons just made the existing flight more violent.

This is the diagnostic that should stop every one of us. Allah is teaching us that the response to a summons reveals a pre-existing condition. If your heart is already running from Allah, then the call to prayer will feel like a chain. The Quran will feel like a burden. The reminder of mercy will feel like an accusation. **Not because the summons is harsh — but because you were already in motion away from it.**

And the inverse, which the surrounding ayahs imply: a heart already turned toward Allah responds to the same summons with *zādahum īmānā* — it increases them in faith. Same words. Same Quran. Same Name. Different hearts.

This is the linguistic anchor of the ayah. Allah is not describing the Quraysh's stubbornness. He is naming a law of the human heart: **what you do with mercy depends on which direction you were already running.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So the linguistic surface has shown us three things. The summons comes from everywhere when you were ready to receive it; the same summons becomes a personal accusation when you weren't. The Name of mercy can be demoted to an "it" by a heart that needs to keep its distance. And the call meant to draw you near can drive you further if you were already running.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the structure of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Asymmetric Effect of Truth

Let me start with something every parent knows.

You have a teenage child who has done something they know was wrong. You haven't said a word. They walk into the kitchen, and you smile at them. You say, "Hey, how was your day?" — gently, no edge in your voice, the most ordinary greeting in the world.

And they explode. "What is that supposed to mean? Why are you always on me?"

You said nothing. You smiled. You greeted them. And the kindness landed as an attack.

What happened?

What happened is that **their guilt converted your kindness into a weapon**. They were already running from a truth inside themselves. Your gentle voice was the call to come back. And because they were already in flight — *zādahum nufūrā*. The very softness of your tone increased their distance.

Now scale that up to the cosmic level. Allah has been calling humanity for as long as there have been humans. He has sent revelation after revelation, prophet after prophet, Name after Name. And the soft Name — *Ar-Raḥmān* — the Name that should have been the easiest to receive — became the Name the Quraysh fought hardest against.

Why? Because mercy makes claims that severity does not.

Severity says: *I am powerful. Stay away.* You can keep your distance and call that obedience.

Mercy says: *I am close. Come.* You cannot keep your distance from mercy without naming what you are doing. To refuse severity is reasonable; to refuse mercy is to convict yourself.

So the heart that wants to keep its distance from God secretly prefers a harsh God. A harsh God lets you stay where you are. *Ar-Raḥmān* refuses to let you stay where you are. *Ar-Raḥmān* keeps coming.

And this is why the Quraysh fled hardest from this Name. Not because mercy was unwelcome. Because mercy was undeniable.

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself — and this is the question this ayah was placed in the Quran to make you ask — **what Name of Allah do you find yourself most resistant to?**

For some of us it's *Al-ʿAdl* — The Just — because there is something we know we owe and haven't paid. For some it's *Ar-Razzāq* — The Provider — because we cannot let go of the illusion that we are providing for ourselves. For some it's *At-Tawwāb* — The Acceptor of Repentance — because we have decided we are beyond repair and the offer of return feels like cruelty.

And for many of us, like the Quraysh, it is *Ar-Raḥmān* — because we have built a self that runs on shame, and a Lord whose default is mercy threatens the architecture of that shame.

The diagnostic this ayah gives us is precise: **find the Name of Allah you flinch from, and you have found the place where your heart is already in flight.**

The practical application is this. The next time a verse, a Name, a call to prayer, a moment of beauty, lands on you and your immediate response is irritation, dismissal, or the urge to change the subject — pause. Don't argue with the irritation. Don't try to suppress it. Just notice: **something is calling, and I am running.** And then ask: *which direction was I running before this called me?* That's where the work is.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: When You Reduce a Name to a Thing

Now let's go deeper into the second move the Quraysh made — the *mā Ar-Raḥmān* move. Because this is something we do constantly, in every domain of our lives, and the Quran is naming it as a fundamental sickness of the heart.

Begin here. There is a difference between *meeting a person* and *having a concept of a person*.

If I tell you about my friend — his stories, his struggles, his face when he laughs — you will form a concept of him in your mind. But if you actually meet him, sit with him, eat with him, hear his voice, see his eyes — the concept dissolves into a presence. He is no longer a *what* you think about. He is a *who* you know.

Now imagine someone refused to ever meet him. They had a thousand reasons not to. And every time you brought him up, they said, "*What* is this friend you keep mentioning? Define him for me. What is he, exactly?"

You would understand instantly that they are not asking. They are protecting themselves from having to know him. The demand for a definition is the refusal of an encounter.

[PAUSE]

This is what the Quraysh are doing to *Ar-Raḥmān*. They are demanding definition because definition keeps the Named at arm's length. As long as *Ar-Raḥmān* is a *what* — a concept, an abstraction, a theological proposition — they don't have to *meet* Him. They don't have to feel what mercy feels like when it's directed at *you*.

And here is the modern application that should make us very honest with ourselves.

We do this with Allah constantly. We turn Him into a *what*. We discuss Him. We have positions about Him. We argue about whether He exists. We compare doctrines about Him. We scroll past His Names in the morning adhkār without letting a single one land. **We have made Him a topic.**

And the moment Allah is a topic, He cannot reach you. Topics don't reach. Topics get filed, sorted, indexed, and shelved. A topic is a *mā*. A topic cannot prostrate you.

The ayah is asking us to do the opposite of what the Quraysh did. They took *Ar-Raḥmān* and demoted Him from *who* to *what*. We are being invited to take *Ar-Raḥmān* and let Him rise from *what* back into *Who*. From a Name we recite to a Presence we are addressed by. From a concept we defend to a Lord who is calling us by name in this very moment.

Notice that this is exactly what the next ayahs in the surah do. Right after this ayah, Allah does not give a theological definition of *Ar-Raḥmān*. He doesn't say "*Ar-Raḥmān* is the entity who possesses such-and-such attributes." He instead points outward and says — *tabāraka-lladhī jaʿala fi-s-samāʾi burūjā* — Blessed is the One who placed in the sky great constellations. He answers *mā Ar-Raḥmān* by showing them *raḥmah*. Not a definition. A demonstration. **You asked what He is — look at what He does.**

[PAUSE]

The practical application is severe and beautiful at once. The next time you find yourself in conversation about Allah — defending Him, debating Him, theorizing about Him — pause and ask: *am I treating Him as a who or a what right now?* Am I in the presence of the One I'm speaking about, or have I left Him outside the room while I discuss Him?

And the invitation: when you say *Ar-Raḥmān* in your prayer tomorrow morning, slow down. Do not say it as a word in a sentence. Say it as a Name — directed at someone listening. Let it be the difference between *talking about* and *talking to*. Because everything the Quraysh got wrong in this ayah comes down to this single inversion: they were addressing the air about a topic when the One they were dismissing was standing right there, listening to every word.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now look at how this ayah is built. Allah has placed three psychological moments in sequence, and the order is the whole teaching.

First — *qīla lahum* — the call comes, and it comes faceless, universal, addressed to whoever has ears.

Second — *qālū wa mā-r-Raḥmān* — they respond by demoting the Caller from a Who to a What. They protect themselves with grammar.

Third — *zādahum nufūrā* — and the very call meant to draw them near drives them further away.

Notice: each step is a deeper layer of the same flight. The first is a question they don't want asked. The second is a category they put it in to neutralize it. The third is the bodily recoil that follows. **The structure of the ayah is the structure of a hardening heart in real time.** And Allah has shown it to us at this length not to shame the Quraysh — but to give us a mirror.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The hidden speaker (*qīla*) tells us the summons is bigger than its messenger. The asymmetric absence of *who* and the demotion to *what* (*mā*) tells us how a heart insulates itself against a summons it cannot afford to receive. And *zādahum nufūrā* tells us that the same summons, falling on different hearts, produces opposite effects — because the summons does not create the heart's direction; it *reveals* it.

The ayah is a single x-ray of the moment a soul refuses *Ar-Raḥmān*. And it is left in the Quran so that no one — including us — can claim they didn't know what was happening when their own heart did the same.

### Questions to Carry

1. Which Name of Allah, when you slow down and recite it, do you find yourself most uncomfortable with? What might that discomfort be telling you about which direction your heart is currently running?

2. When was the last time you treated Allah as a *who* in your prayer — addressed Him as Someone present — rather than as a *what* you were performing words toward?

3. Is there a verse, a reminder, a person, a moment of mercy that has been arriving in your life that you keep flinching away from? What would it look like to stop running for one minute and let it reach you?

4. Where in your life have you been demanding definitions instead of allowing encounters? With Allah. With people. With your own heart.

5. If *Ar-Raḥmān* is who Allah says He is — and the call to prostrate to Him is meant to draw you closer — what would it mean to stop interpreting His mercy as a threat and start interpreting it as the rescue it actually is?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When Allah's mercy approaches a heart already in flight, the heart does not slow down — it bolts harder; and the diagnostic of where you stand with *Ar-Raḥmān* is not what you say about Him, but what happens inside you the moment He is named.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to fix the flight today. You only have to notice it. The Quraysh's tragedy was not that they were running — every human heart runs at some point. Their tragedy was that they used grammar to deny they were running. They turned the Most Merciful into a *what* so they wouldn't have to feel themselves recoiling from a *Who*.

The invitation of this ayah is the opposite move. The next time you sense your heart shrinking from something Allah is offering — a verse, a duty, a forgiveness, a Name — do not call it *what*. Stop and let it be *Who*. Stand still long enough to feel the recoil and not act on it. That single act of standing still is the beginning of the journey *Ar-Raḥmān* has been calling you to all along.

### Du'a

*Yā Raḥmān*, do not let us be among those whose hearts flee You when You draw near. When Your Name reaches us, let it land — not as a threat, not as a topic, not as a *what* — but as the call of the One who has been waiting for us to turn around. Make us of those whom mercy *increases* in faith, not in flight.

*Āmīn.*
