# When the Answer Is Not Removal but Sufficiency

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**وَكَذَٰلِكَ جَعَلْنَا لِكُلِّ نَبِيٍّ عَدُوًّا مِّنَ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِرَبِّكَ هَادِيًا وَنَصِيرًا**

*Wa-kadhālika jaʿalnā li-kulli nabiyyin ʿaduwwan mina'l-mujrimīn. Wa-kafā bi-Rabbika hādiyan wa naṣīrā.*

**"And thus We have appointed for every prophet an enemy from among the criminals. But sufficient is your Lord as a Guide and a Helper."**

### The World Before This Ayah

To enter this ayah, you have to enter the moment that produced it.

It is roughly the eleventh year of revelation in Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ has been speaking these words — *this Quran* — to his people for over a decade. He has lost his beloved wife. He has lost his uncle and protector. He has been mocked in the street. Trash has been thrown on his back while he prostrated. His followers have been tortured to death. His family has been starved in a boycott. He has just walked to Ṭāʾif and returned with blood in his sandals.

And now look at the verse just before ours — verse 30. The Messenger ﷺ turns to his Lord and says something that has the texture of a private prayer overheard:

*"Yā Rabbi, inna qawmī ittakhadhū hādhā'l-Qurʾāna mahjūrā."*

**"O my Lord — my people have taken this Quran as something abandoned."**

That word *mahjūrā* is a wound. It comes from *hajara* — to leave, to abandon, to walk away from. He is not saying "they reject me." He is saying "this Quran — this gift from You that I have been carrying to them — they have walked past it like it isn't even there."

This is the cry. It is the prophetic complaint at its rawest. And our ayah, verse 31, is the response.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

You would expect the response to be one of two things. Either: *"Don't worry, I'll deal with them."* Or: *"Be patient, the victory is coming."*

It is neither.

What Allah says to His most beloved servant — in his moment of most exposed pain — is something so different from what we expect that we may have stopped hearing it. He does not promise to remove the enemy. He does not promise to break them. He does something stranger and deeper.

He tells the Prophet ﷺ two things. First, that the wound itself is the proof of his lineage. And second, that what the Prophet really needs is not the absence of pain but the presence of his Lord.

Stay with me. By the end of this reflection, I want you to feel why this is, perhaps, the most precise consolation Allah has ever given a hurting human heart.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Jaʿalnā* — "WE Appointed"

Before we touch the word, let me ask you something.

When you are hurt by someone — when a colleague betrays you, when a family member turns their back, when someone you trusted becomes a source of pain — what do you say in your head about it?

You usually say something like: *"This shouldn't have happened."* Or: *"How did I end up surrounded by these people?"* Or: *"Why is this happening to me?"*

Notice the architecture of those sentences. They all assume the hurt is an accident. A mistake in the system. Something that slipped through.

Now look at how this ayah opens.

**وَكَذَٰلِكَ جَعَلْنَا** — *Wa-kadhālika jaʿalnā* — "And thus **We** have appointed."

The verb is *jaʿalnā* — first person plural perfect, Form I. The royal "We" of divine action. And the verb *jaʿala* in Arabic is a heavy verb. It is not the same as *khalaqa* (to create from nothing). *Jaʿala* carries the meaning of **designating, rendering, assigning, appointing into a role**. When Allah says *jaʿalnā*, He is saying: *We placed this thing here, on purpose, in this configuration, for this function.*

Now read it again, slowly:

**"And thus *We have appointed* for every prophet an enemy from among the criminals."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that does. The Prophet ﷺ has just said, *they have abandoned the Quran*. And Allah's first move is not to comfort the wound by minimizing it. His first move is to claim authorship of the entire situation. *We did this. We placed them there. The opposition you are experiencing is not a glitch. It is part of the architecture.*

This is the opposite of how we usually receive consolation. When you tell a friend you are hurting, they say, *"That shouldn't have happened to you."* Allah says, *"We made it so."*

And here is what is remarkable: that is not crueler. That is gentler.

Because if your suffering is an accident, then the universe is broken and no one is in charge. But if your suffering has been *appointed*, then Someone is in charge — and that Someone has placed you exactly where He needs you to be.

The Form I active verb *jaʿalnā* with the divine "We" is doing something the simple English "made" cannot carry. It is saying: *the hostility you are experiencing is not outside My knowledge. It is not outside My will. It is part of the very design of being a prophet.*

Notice something else. The word **كَذَٰلِكَ** — *kadhālika* — "and thus / and likewise / in this same manner." It is a comparative demonstrative. It points to a pattern. *In this same way* — meaning, the pattern you are inside, O Muhammad, is the pattern every messenger before you was inside.

So before Allah even names the consolation, He has already done two things in three Arabic words: He has placed His own hand on the situation (*jaʿalnā*), and He has placed the Prophet ﷺ inside a lineage (*kadhālika*).

You are not isolated. You are not a mistake. You are inside the pattern of My beloveds.

That is the first medicine.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Min al-Mujrimīn* — Not "From the Disbelievers"

Now look at how Allah describes the enemy.

**عَدُوًّا مِّنَ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ** — *ʿaduwwan mina'l-mujrimīn* — "an enemy from among the criminals."

I want you to notice what He did *not* say. He did not say *"from among the disbelievers"* (*al-kāfirīn*). He did not say *"from among the polytheists"* (*al-mushrikīn*). He did not say *"from among your tribe"* (*qawmika*).

He chose **al-mujrimīn**.

Let's open that word. The root is *j-r-m*. In its earliest Arabic usage, *jarama* meant **to cut, to sever, to crop**. From there it extended to mean *to commit a crime* — because a crime is, in essence, an act that cuts you away from what is right. *Al-Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib documents this: *jurm* is the cut between you and what should bind you to truth and to Allah.

The form here is **mujrim** — the active participle of Form IV (*afʿala*). Form IV is causative and intensive. So a *mujrim* is not someone who happened into wrongdoing. It is **someone who is actively, deliberately engaged in the cutting**. They are *committed* to the severance.

Now stay with this. Why does it matter that Allah used *mujrimīn* and not *kāfirīn*?

Because **kufr is a theological category — it describes someone's relationship to belief.** But **jurm is a moral category — it describes someone's relationship to action.** And what Allah is telling the Prophet ﷺ is something quietly devastating.

He is saying: the people who oppose you are not opposing you because of an honest theological disagreement they happened to land on. They are opposing you because they are committed to a kind of cutting — they are *mujrim* by character. The opposition reveals them. Their hostility to truth is not a bug in their belief system; it is the symptom of their moral state.

And here is the second thing this word does for the Prophet ﷺ. It removes the burden of self-doubt.

When someone hates what you carry, the natural human response is to ask: *"Was it me? Was it the way I said it? Did I fail in some way?"* Allah is closing that door. He is saying: *the rejection is not a verdict on your delivery. It is a verdict on their character.* They are *mujrimūn*. The cutting was already in them. Your message simply gave it something to cut against.

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer here. Look at the indefiniteness: *ʿaduwwan* — **"an enemy"** — singular and indefinite. Not "enemies" (plural). Not "the enemies" (definite). Just *an enemy*.

In Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite often signals **type** or **kind** rather than a specific countable individual. So Allah is saying: *for every prophet there is, of this very kind, an enemy from the mujrimīn.* It is a category. There has never been a prophet without one. Not Nūḥ. Not Ibrāhīm. Not Mūsā. Not ʿĪsā. Not you, Muḥammad ﷺ.

The opposition is the *uniform* of the prophetic office. If you are wearing the uniform, you will face the opposing force that uniform always faces.

What this invites us to consider — and now we are going beyond the strict grammar into application — is that the same may be quietly true for anyone trying to carry a fragment of truth in any setting. The opposition you are meeting may not be a sign you are wrong. It may be a sign you are touching something real.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Hādiyan wa Naṣīrā* — The Asymmetry

Now we come to the most beautiful thing in this ayah, and the easiest to miss.

After saying *We appointed enemies for every prophet*, Allah closes with:

**وَكَفَىٰ بِرَبِّكَ هَادِيًا وَنَصِيرًا** — *Wa-kafā bi-Rabbika hādiyan wa naṣīrā* — "And sufficient is your Lord as a Guide and a Helper."

Three things are happening here at once. Let me teach the structure first.

**The Construction *Kafā Bi-***

In Arabic there is an idiom: *kafā bi-X* — literally "It suffices, by X" — but the *bi-* is what grammarians call *zāʾida*, a particle added not for ordinary meaning but for **emphasis**. The construction means *"X is sufficient, fully and completely, needing no additional support."* It is not a casual claim of adequacy. It is a closing of the case. *X is enough — full stop.*

So when Allah says *kafā bi-Rabbika* — *"sufficient is your Lord"* — He is sealing the matter. There is no incompleteness here. No *"and also you'll need..."* No *"in addition to..."* Just: **He is enough.**

**The Personal Address: *Rabbika***

Notice He does not say "sufficient is *Allah*." He says "sufficient is *your Lord*" — *Rabbika*. Second person. Possessive. Intimate.

*Rabb* is not a generic word for God. *Rabb* means the One who nurtures, owns, sustains, brings something from infancy to completion. It is the word a child might use for the one raising them. And here Allah attaches it to the Prophet ﷺ with the *-ka* — *yours*. **The One who has been raising you, Muhammad — He is enough.**

This is not theology. This is intimacy in the middle of pain.

**Now: The Asymmetry**

Look at the two qualities Allah names:

**هَادِيًا** — *hādiyan* — "as a Guide"
**وَنَصِيرًا** — *wa naṣīran* — "and as a Helper"

Both are accusatives of specification (*tamyīz*) — they are answering the question: *sufficient as what?* But notice their **morphological forms** are different.

*Hādī* (هادٍ → هاديًا) is the simple active participle, on the pattern **فاعل** (*fāʿil*). It is the standard form: *one who guides*.

*Naṣīr* (نصير → نصيرًا) is on the pattern **فعيل** (*faʿīl*) — and *faʿīl* in Arabic morphology is one of the well-known forms of *mubālagha* — **intensification, abundance, repeated and constant action.** A *naṣīr* is not just *one who helps* — it is *one who helps abundantly, repeatedly, mightily*.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question. Allah is the perfect Guide and the perfect Helper. Why pair the simple form with the intensive form? Why is "guide" stated with no intensification while "helper" is stated with the intensive *fa'īl*?

Stay with me, because what this opens is breathtaking.

**Guidance is uncontested.** When Allah guides a heart, no force in creation can stop the light from entering. The Prophet ﷺ is not in a war over the *guidance* itself. The guidance descends, the heart receives — that transaction is sovereign. Allah does not need to *struggle* to be a Guide. He simply *is* the Guide. The simple participle suffices. *Hādī*.

**But help — *nasr* — is contested.** Help in this world means winning against opposition. It means standing the Prophet ﷺ up while entire civilizations push him down. It means defending him at Badr and at the Trench and through every plot. Help is repeated, ongoing, intensive intervention against active resistance. So Allah does not say *nāṣir* (the simple form). He says **naṣīr** — the intensive form. *I am the Helper who helps repeatedly, against forces that will not stop. As often as they push, I will be there.*

The morphology matches the metaphysics. Guidance is given. Help is *waged*.

[PAUSE]

And now look at how this ties back to the first half of the ayah. Allah says: *We appointed enemies from the mujrimīn.* Then He says: *I am sufficient as a Helper* — but in the **intensive** form, because the helping is going to have to match the appointing.

It is as if He is saying: *Yes, I placed those enemies there. And yes, I will keep helping you against them — repeatedly, mightily, never finishing — because I am the kind of Helper whose helping never runs out.*

That is what the *fa'īl* form is doing. It is the divine guarantee that the help will never be exhausted by the opposition.

### Bridging to the Themes

So far we have seen three things:

That Allah claims authorship of the situation (*jaʿalnā*).
That the enemies are revealed by their hostility, not legitimized by it (*al-mujrimīn*).
That Allah declares Himself sufficient — not as removal of the enemy, but as Guide and intensive Helper (*kafā bi-Rabbika hādiyan wa naṣīrā*).

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how Allah actually answers a hurting heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Reframe Before the Rescue

Imagine you walk into your friend's house in tears. Your closest colleague has betrayed you. You sit at their kitchen table and you spill the whole thing. You tell them: *I cannot believe this is happening. I did not see this coming. I feel like an idiot. I feel completely alone.*

Now imagine two responses.

The first friend leans forward and says: *"Don't worry. I'll call them tomorrow and confront them. We'll fix this."*

The second friend pours you a cup of tea, sits across from you, and says: *"Listen. Every person I've ever respected who has done what you do has been through this exact thing. This is what it means to do that work. You are not crazy. You are not alone. You are inside something."*

Which one of those responses, ten years later, will you still remember?

The first friend offers *intervention*. The second friend offers *orientation*. And it is the orientation that actually heals, because the suffering was never primarily about the colleague. The suffering was about the **isolation of the suffering** — the feeling that *this should not be happening to me*.

This is precisely what Allah does in our ayah.

The Prophet ﷺ has just complained. *They have abandoned the Quran.* And Allah does not say, *"I will turn them back to it."* He does not say, *"I will replace them with a better people."* He says: **"And thus We have appointed for every prophet an enemy from among the criminals."**

He reframes before He rescues. He orients before He intervenes.

[PAUSE]

There is something we have to understand about pain that the modern mind has forgotten. **Pain that is meaningless is unbearable. Pain that is meaningful is bearable, even precious.** Viktor Frankl wrote an entire book around this insight after surviving the camps: those who survived were not the strongest or the smartest. They were the ones who could place their suffering inside a story.

Allah, in this ayah, is placing the Prophet's suffering inside the only story large enough to hold it. *This is not happening to you because something has gone wrong. This is happening to you because something is going right. You are wearing the uniform of the prophets, and this is what wearing that uniform looks like.*

And here is where it touches you and me. Because most of us are not prophets. But all of us, at some point, will carry something true into a setting that does not want it. We will hold the line at work when it is uncomfortable. We will say the word in a family that does not want to hear it. We will refuse the easy lie. And when we do, we will meet the *mujrim* of our own context — the one whose hostility is not really about us but about the truth we are carrying.

In that moment, the temptation is to ask, *"Why is this happening to me?"* This ayah is teaching us to ask a different question. Not *why* — but *whose pattern am I inside?*

Because if your suffering looks like the suffering of those Allah loved, you are not lost. You are located.

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is small and immediate. The next time you face opposition for something you are doing for Allah, before you ask Him to remove it, **ask Him to show you the lineage you are inside**. Ask Him to let you feel for one moment that you are walking the road His prophets walked. The pain may not lift. But it will become bearable. Because it will become *meaningful*.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Sufficiency Is Not Removal

Now we come to the deepest part of this ayah, and the place where most of us, if we are honest, struggle the most with our Lord.

When you pray, what do you pray for?

If you are like most of us, you pray for outcomes. *Ya Allah, fix this. Ya Allah, give me this. Ya Allah, take this away from me.* And there is nothing wrong with that — the Prophet ﷺ taught us to ask for everything, even the strap of a sandal. But notice what is **almost never** in our prayer: *Ya Allah, be enough for me in this.*

That is the prayer this ayah is teaching us.

Look at the closing again:

**وَكَفَىٰ بِرَبِّكَ هَادِيًا وَنَصِيرًا**

**"Sufficient is your Lord as a Guide and a Helper."**

The Prophet ﷺ has complained about a problem. Allah's response does not promise to solve the problem. It promises **Himself**. *I am enough. I am your Guide. I am your Helper. The presence of Me is the answer to the absence of them.*

[PAUSE]

This is hard. Let me say why it is hard.

We are wired to think that the answer to a problem is the **absence of the problem**. The headache goes away. The bills get paid. The enemy backs down. We define resolution as removal. So when we pray, we ask for removal.

But Allah, in His mercy, sometimes refuses to give us removal. Not because He cannot. But because if He removes the problem, we will leave. We will go back to forgetting Him until the next problem comes.

So instead of removal, He gives us **Himself**. He keeps the wound open and pours His presence into it. He does not take the *mujrim* away from the Prophet ﷺ. He stays with the Prophet ﷺ through the *mujrim*.

And then He calls Himself, with that same intensive form we saw, **naṣīr** — the One whose help will never run out before the opposition runs out.

[PAUSE]

There is an analogy that might help. Imagine a child afraid of the dark. The child cries to the parent. There are two ways the parent can respond.

The first parent flips on the light. Problem solved — for tonight. Tomorrow night the child will be afraid again. The fear was never about the dark; it was about being alone in it. But the parent has trained the child to need the light to feel safe.

The second parent leaves the dark dark. They sit on the edge of the bed. They take the child's hand. They say nothing. And the child, feeling the parent's hand, falls asleep — *in the dark*.

The second parent has given the child something the first parent cannot: **the discovery that presence is more fundamental than circumstance.** The child has learned that they are not safe because the dark is gone. They are safe because they are not alone in it.

This is what Allah is doing for the Prophet ﷺ. He is not flipping on the light. He is taking his hand. *I am your Guide. I am your Helper. Sleep in the dark — I am here.*

[PAUSE]

And now hear how this connects back to the morphology. Why is *naṣīr* in the intensive form? Because **the help has to last as long as the dark lasts**. Allah is not promising a one-time intervention that ends the trial. He is promising sustained, repeated, never-exhausted accompaniment. *As long as you are in this, I am in this with you. As often as they come, I will be here. The intensive form means My helping does not have an expiration.*

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is the hardest practice in the spiritual life. **Begin learning to pray for sufficiency before you pray for removal.**

The next time you are in a hard situation, before you say *Ya Allah, take this away*, try saying: *Ya Allah, be enough for me in this. Be my Guide in it. Be my Helper through it. Let me find You here, where I am, before I ask You to take me somewhere else.*

That prayer, if it becomes habitual, will reorganize your relationship with Allah. Because you will stop using Him as a service provider for outcomes. And you will start knowing Him as the One whose **mere presence** is the resolution your soul was actually searching for.

The Prophet ﷺ asked for relief from a wound. Allah gave him *Himself*. And in the prophetic life that followed, the wound never fully closed — Mecca did not stop, persecution did not stop, even at Uhud the blood flowed. But the Prophet ﷺ never again, in all the recorded prayers we have of him, asked Allah to remove his enemies. He asked Allah to *be enough*. He had received the answer of this ayah — and he had let it become his orientation.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at the whole ayah at once.

**وَكَذَٰلِكَ جَعَلْنَا لِكُلِّ نَبِيٍّ عَدُوًّا مِّنَ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِرَبِّكَ هَادِيًا وَنَصِيرًا**

There are two halves, divided by the small pause-mark in the middle. The first half is about **the wound**: appointed by Allah, populated by *mujrimūn*, shared with every prophet. The second half is about **the answer**: not removal, but *sufficiency* — Allah Himself, named with intimate possession (*Rabbika*), sealed with the strongest construction of completeness (*kafā bi-*), and described with the morphological mirror of what is needed — simple *hādī* for the unilateral gift of guidance, intensive *naṣīr* for the contested labor of help.

The ayah is built like a question and an answer. *What about my pain?* — *I appointed it.* *Then what do I do?* — *I am enough.*

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The morphological choice of *jaʿalnā* in the divine "We" — that is the linguistic shape of the theme of *the reframe before the rescue*. It is the grammar of Allah claiming the situation.

The morphological choice of *kafā bi-Rabbika* with *naṣīr* in the intensive — that is the linguistic shape of the theme of *sufficiency over removal*. It is the grammar of Allah giving Himself instead of solving the problem.

The whole ayah, at every level, is teaching one thing: **Your Lord's first answer to your suffering is not its end. It is His presence inside it.**

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Do not answer them quickly.

1. When you pray about a hard situation, what percentage of your prayer is asking for the situation to change, and what percentage is asking Allah to be enough for you in it? What would shift if those percentages reversed?

2. Is there a hostility in your life right now — a person, a critic, a difficulty — that you have been treating as evidence that something has gone wrong? What would change if you began to read it as evidence that you might be inside the pattern of those Allah loves?

3. The Prophet ﷺ never again, after this ayah, asked Allah to remove his enemies in the way he had complained in 25:30. What does that tell you about how this ayah landed in his heart? And what would it mean for you to let it land that way in yours?

4. When was the last time you experienced Allah's *sufficiency* — not His solution, but His sufficiency? What did that feel like? Could you recognize it again if it came?

5. The intensive form *naṣīr* tells us Allah's help is repeated and inexhaustible. Is there a place in your life where you have stopped asking for help because you assumed the help had run out? What would it look like to ask one more time?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you cry out about what is hurting you, Allah's first answer may not be the removal of the wound, but the revelation that your suffering is not exile from the pattern of His beloveds — and that He Himself, present and intimate, is what you were really asking for.

### The Closing Invitation

Let one thing shift after this. Not a behavior — a posture.

The next time pain comes — and it will — let the first words that rise to your lips not be *"Why me?"* but *"Whose pattern am I inside?"* And let the second words not be *"Take this away,"* but *"Be enough for me in this."*

That is not resignation. It is not passivity. It is the deepest prayer a heart can pray, and it is the prayer this ayah is teaching us, by showing us Allah teaching it to His most beloved.

### Du'a

Yā Rabb — when we cry to You about the *mujrim* in our lives, let our hearts hear Your real answer. Place us inside the lineage of Your beloveds, so our suffering is not exile but location. And be enough for us — *kafā bika lanā Rabban wa hādiyan wa naṣīrā* — be enough for us as our Lord, our Guide, and our Helper, who never runs out.

Āmīn.
