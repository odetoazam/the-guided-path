# The One Who Made — And the Invitation to Ask

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 59

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
[ayah:25:59] ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ ٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْعَرْشِ ۚ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ فَسْـَٔلْ بِهِۦ خَبِيرًا

**Transliteration:**
*Alladhī khalaqa as-samāwāti wal-arḍa wa mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh — ar-Raḥmān, fas-al bihi khabīrā.*

**Translation:**
"The One who created the heavens and the earth and everything between them in six days, then established Himself over the Throne — the Most Merciful. So ask about Him one who is deeply acquainted."

---

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah the way it was first heard, you have to walk back into Makkah.

The surah is called **Al-Furqan** — the Criterion. The thing that separates. Imagine standing at a crossroads where two paths look identical from a distance, and someone hands you a tool that lets you see — instantly — which one leads home and which one leads off a cliff. That is what *al-furqān* means. It is the divine sorting-tool.

And in Makkah, that tool is desperately needed. Because the city is full of confident voices. The leaders of Quraysh have answers for everything. They speak with the authority of inheritance — *this is what our fathers did, this is what our fathers knew*. And here comes a man, alone, saying: **none of that is true. The real Reality is something else.**

So the question that hangs over the entire surah is: *who do we listen to?* Whose account of reality is correct?

Just before our ayah, Allah commands the Prophet ﷺ to place his trust in **the Living One who does not die**. And then — as if to answer the listener's natural follow-up question, *and who is that, exactly?* — Allah introduces Himself.

[PAUSE]

But notice how He does it. He doesn't begin with a list of attributes. He doesn't begin with theology. He begins with **something the listener has never stopped looking at since the day they were born**.

The sky above their head. The earth beneath their feet. The space between.

---

### The Hook

This single ayah does something almost no other ayah in the Quran does in this exact way.

It tells you what Allah **did**. It tells you **where He is**. It tells you **what He is**. And then — in three Arabic words — it tells you **how to come to know Him**.

The four pieces fit together like a key turning in a lock. And the most stunning part is the last instruction: **"so ask about Him one who is deeply acquainted."**

A command to ask. A command from Allah Himself — to go and ask someone else. Why? Whom does Allah want you to ask? And why does He use a word for "knowing" that no human being can ever fully claim?

Stay with me. Because by the time we open these words, you will see that this ayah is not describing a distant deity. It is teaching you the architecture of how a heart comes to know its Lord.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will walk through three things. The strange word *thumma*. The unexpected appearance of the name *Ar-Raḥmān*. And the final command — *fas'al bihi khabīrā* — which is one of the most generous instructions in the entire Quran.

---

### Element One: The Word *Thumma* — And the Architecture of Time

Before we touch this ayah, let me teach you something about Arabic.

In English, when we want to connect two events, we usually use the word "then." *I went to the store, then I came home.* It's a flat word. It tells you the order of events but nothing else.

Arabic is more precise. Arabic has at least three different words for what English crushes into one "then":

- **Wa** — "and" — joins two things without specifying any order at all.
- **Fa** — "then" — implies immediate succession. *This happened, and right after, that happened.*
- **Thumma** — "then" — implies a gap. A delay. Something significant happens, and *afterward* — with space, with weight — something else.

When you hear *thumma* in Arabic, you should hear a deep breath between two events. Not a comma. A pause. A break that says *what comes next is not just sequential — it is on a different register.*

Now look at the ayah:

> *khalaqa as-samāwāti wal-arḍa wa mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin* — **He created the heavens and the earth and everything between them in six days**
>
> ***thumma*** *istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh* — **then He established Himself over the Throne**

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah does not say *fa-istawā*. He does not say *wa-istawā*. He says ***thumma*** *istawā*.

Let's sit with this. What is the gap *thumma* is creating?

The classical scholars have been careful here for fourteen centuries. They have said, with one voice, that we affirm that Allah established Himself over the Throne in the manner that befits His majesty — *bilā kayf*, without asking how. Imam Mālik's words still stand: **the istiwāʾ is known, the how is unknown, asking about it is innovation, and believing in it is obligatory.**

So we are not going to ask *how*. That door is closed by the scholars who knew the Arabic better than anyone.

But we are allowed — invited, even — to ask: **what is the *thumma* doing?**

Here is what is remarkable. In our human experience, the most natural reading of *thumma* is a gap in time. *He made the universe, then later, He did something else.* And that reading would suggest that Allah was somehow *engaged* in creating, then *finished*, and then *did the next thing*.

But that cannot be the meaning. Because Allah is not bound by time. He created time. He is not a craftsman who clocks in and clocks out.

So what is the *thumma* doing?

The classical commentators offer a sketch — and each opinion is not a competing theory but a different angle on the same reality.

**The first angle:** *Thumma* here is signaling **rank, not time.** Like when we say in English, "He is a great teacher, *and then* on top of that, he is also a great human being." The "and then" isn't temporal — it's hierarchical. *Thumma* can carry that weight in Arabic. The point is: do not think of *istiwāʾ* as one item on a list. It is on a different register entirely.

**The second angle:** *Thumma* is creating a deliberate **gap of awe**. The ayah is asking you to slow down. To take in the heavens, the earth, the space between, the six days — and *then*, with reverence, lift your gaze to something even greater. The *thumma* is the silence the listener is supposed to feel before encountering the *ʿarsh*.

**The third angle:** *Thumma* is marking the difference between Allah's act *toward creation* (the heavens and earth) and Allah's relationship *to His Throne*. The first is His doing in the realm of the seen. The second is His being in the realm of the unseen. The two are not the same kind of statement. *Thumma* is the wall between them.

[PAUSE]

Put those three angles together and you get a single composite picture: **the *thumma* is teaching you that the act of creation — vast as it is — is not the deepest reality.** Above the heavens, above the earth, above the space between, above even the six days, there is something more. The Throne. And the One above it.

The universe is not the headline. The universe is the **footnote**.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Name *Ar-Raḥmān* — Why This Name, In This Place

Let me teach you something else before we touch the ayah again.

In Arabic, names of Allah are not interchangeable. They are not synonyms. Each name reveals a specific facet of the divine reality, and the name Allah chooses in a given verse is *deliberate*. Switch the name, and the meaning shifts.

The name **Ar-Raḥmān** comes from the root *r-ḥ-m* — the same root as *raḥim*, the womb. In Arabic, the womb is the archetype of unconditional, encompassing, life-giving care. Care that is given before the recipient has earned anything. Care that surrounds the one who cannot yet even ask.

There is another name from the same root — **Ar-Raḥīm**. Both translate awkwardly into English as "the Merciful." But they are not the same.

The classical scholars have traced this distinction with great care:

- **Ar-Raḥmān** is the all-encompassing mercy that reaches every single creature — believer and disbeliever, animal and angel, tree and stone. It is the mercy that makes existence possible at all. It is *general*.
- **Ar-Raḥīm** is the specific, intimate mercy reserved for the believers. The mercy of being guided. The mercy of being forgiven. The mercy of paradise.

*Ar-Raḥmān* is the wider sky. *Ar-Raḥīm* is the inner room.

Now look at our ayah again:

> *fī sittati ayyāmin thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh — **ar-Raḥmān** — fas-al bihi khabīrā*

[PAUSE]

Of all the names Allah could have chosen here, why this one?

The ayah has just walked us through immensity. The heavens. The earth. Everything between. Six days. The Throne. The listener's mind is — by design — small. Overwhelmed. The ayah has been zooming out, out, out, until the human is a speck.

And then, just when the listener might feel crushed by the scale — just when the One above the Throne might feel infinitely far — Allah says one word.

***Ar-Raḥmān.***

Notice something. He does not say *Al-Jabbār* — the Compeller. He does not say *Al-Qahhār* — the Subduer. He does not say *Al-ʿAẓīm* — the Magnificent. Any of those names would have *fit* the architecture of the ayah. Any of them would have crowned the immensity.

But Allah chooses the one name that says: **the One above the Throne is the One whose default posture toward you is mercy.**

Stay with this. Because in the surah of *al-furqān* — the surah of the Criterion that separates truth from falsehood — the *most distinguishing* truth about the One above the Throne is not His power. It is His *raḥmah*.

The God of the Quran is not a God who is merciful *despite* being above the Throne. He is a God whose **being above the Throne is itself an act of mercy** — because it means the universe is not random, is not abandoned, is not running on autopilot.

There is more. *Ar-Raḥmān* is also the name the Makkans of that era *struggled with the most*. There is a famous moment when Quraysh refused to write *Bismillāh ar-Raḥmān ar-Raḥīm* in the treaty of Hudaybiyyah because they said *we do not know who Ar-Raḥmān is*. They had drifted so far from the original tradition of Ibrāhīm that this name had become foreign to them.

And here, in their hometown, in a Makkan surah, Allah introduces Himself by exactly that name.

It's as if He is saying: **you have forgotten who I am. Let me tell you. The One above the Throne — yes, that One — is Ar-Raḥmān. The One whose mercy makes your every breath possible. Have you really been mocking Him?**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Fas-al Bihi Khabīrā* — The Most Generous Command

Now we come to the most surprising three words in the ayah.

> ***fas-al bihi khabīrā***
>
> **so ask about Him one who is deeply acquainted.**

Before we open this, let me set something up.

In Arabic, there are two main words for "knowing":

- **ʿAlīm** — from the root *ʿ-l-m* — knowing in general. Knowledge as content. The word a teacher might use.
- **Khabīr** — from the root *kh-b-r* — knowing through *direct experience*. Knowing because you have walked through it. *Khubrah* in modern Arabic still means "experience" — the kind you put on a résumé. The kind you cannot fake.

If you ask an *ʿālim* about a country, he will tell you what the books say. If you ask a *khabīr* about a country, he will tell you what the air smells like. What the people sound like when they laugh. What it feels like to be there at dusk.

*ʿAlīm* knows *about*. *Khabīr* knows *from within*.

Now look at the command:

> *fas-al **bihi** khabīrā*

The word *bihi* literally means "about Him." Some grammarians read it as "ask Him" — but the most precise reading, preserved by the classical commentators, is **"ask, about Him, one who is deeply acquainted."**

Three things are stunning here.

**First:** The verb *is'al* — "ask" — is in the singular imperative. Allah is speaking directly to the Prophet ﷺ, and through him, to every single listener. *You. Ask.* It is not "those who want to know may inquire." It is **a command**. Asking, in this ayah, is not optional. The closing of the ayah is an order to seek.

**Second:** What kind of God commands you to ask someone else about Him? Notice what is *not* happening here. Allah does not say "ask Me." He does not say "I will tell you everything." He commands the listener to seek out **a *khabīr*** — someone deeply acquainted.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because it is teaching us something profound about how Allah designed the path of knowing Him.

The classical scholars debated who exactly the *khabīr* in this ayah is. And — as before — their opinions are not alternatives. They are layers.

**Some said:** the *khabīr* is **Allah Himself** — and the command means *ask, and you will find Him to be the One deeply acquainted.* On this reading, the ayah is saying: the only One truly *khabīr* about Allah is Allah, so direct your asking to Him.

**Some said:** the *khabīr* is the **Prophet Muhammad ﷺ** — because no created being is more deeply acquainted with the reality of Ar-Raḥmān than the one who received the revelation from Him. Ask the Messenger.

**Some said:** the *khabīr* is the **scholar of revelation** — the one who has spent a life inside the text, who knows the Quran not as content but as a country he has walked through. Ask the people of *dhikr*, as another ayah commands.

Put these together and you get the composite: **knowing Allah requires asking — and the asking has tiers. Ask Allah Himself. Ask the Messenger ﷺ through the Sunnah. Ask the inheritors of the prophetic knowledge.** All three are valid. All three are commanded by this single word *khabīr*.

**Third — and this is what I want you to feel:** Allah did not say *ask an ʿālim*. He said *ask a khabīr*.

He could have said *ʿālim*. The ayah would have made grammatical sense. But it would have meant something colder.

By saying *khabīr*, Allah is telling the seeker: **don't just go to someone who has read about Me. Go to someone who has *lived close to Me*. Go to someone whose knowledge has the warmth of experience, not just the precision of information.**

[PAUSE]

This is the most generous command in the ayah. Because it is saying: *I am not hiding from you. I have placed people in your world who have walked toward Me long enough that they know the path. Find them. Ask them.*

The ayah that began with the immensity of the heavens and the earth ends with **a finger pointing the listener toward another human being.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### The Bridge

So we have three pieces.

A *thumma* that creates a deliberate gap, lifting the listener from the seen universe to the unseen Throne above it.

A name — *Ar-Raḥmān* — that softens the immensity into intimacy and reveals that the One above the Throne is fundamentally a God of mercy.

A command — *fas-al bihi khabīrā* — that turns the listener outward, toward asking, toward seekers who have walked further than they have.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Architecture of Awe — Why Mercy Has to Be the Last Word

Let me start somewhere unexpected.

Have you ever stood under a truly dark sky? Not the muted orange-grey of a city night — but the kind of sky you see in the desert, or on a remote island, where the Milky Way is not a metaphor but a visible river of light arching from horizon to horizon.

If you have, you know what happens. There is a moment where the sky becomes *too much*. You feel — physically — how small you are. Some people cry. Some people laugh. Some people just go quiet. The technical word for this in modern psychology is *awe* — and researchers have found that awe shrinks the self. It makes your problems feel smaller. It makes you more generous. It softens you.

But here is what is also true. **Awe, by itself, is dangerous.**

Pure awe — without mercy — is the experience of standing before something so vast that you cease to matter. It is the cold of cosmic indifference. The atheist astronomer Carl Sagan described it: *we are a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam*. There is a kind of beauty in that — but there is also a horror. Because if you matter to nothing, then your suffering matters to nothing, and your love matters to nothing, and your dying matters to nothing.

This is why so many people walk away from contemplating the universe with despair instead of worship. Because they encountered the *thumma istawā ʿalā al-ʿarsh* — the immensity above the immensity — without the next word.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah.

He builds the immensity, layer by layer. Heavens. Earth. Everything between. Six days. The Throne. By the time you reach the *thumma*, your soul is supposed to be small. Quiet. Maybe a little afraid.

And then — at the precise moment when the human heart is most likely to feel crushed — He says the one word that changes everything.

***Ar-Raḥmān.***

Notice the architecture. Mercy does not come *first*. It comes *after* the immensity has done its work. Because mercy that arrives before awe is sentimentality. *"God is love"* without context becomes greeting-card theology — a small God who exists to make you feel okay.

But mercy that arrives **after** awe is something else entirely. It is the recognition that **the One who could crush you with His vastness has chosen not to.** The One above the Throne — the One whose creation took six days and contains every galaxy — *that One* — is mercy.

This is not a soft God. This is a terrifyingly powerful God whose chosen posture toward you is mercy.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment. Because there is a practical implication that matters.

Many of us were taught about Allah backwards. We were taught mercy first — *Allah loves you, Allah forgives you* — without ever being taught the immensity. So when we encountered hardship, when we encountered death, when we encountered the parts of life that felt impossibly large, our small mercy-only God felt insufficient.

And many of us were taught immensity first — *Allah is watching, Allah will judge, Allah punishes* — without ever being taught that the One above the Throne is *Ar-Raḥmān*. So we lived in fear and called it faith.

This ayah corrects both. It builds the immensity *and* names the mercy. **The terror and the tenderness are about the same Being.**

The practical application: when you make duʿāʾ tonight, hold both. Let your mind walk through the heavens and the earth. Let yourself feel small. And then — at the exact moment you are about to despair of mattering — say: *Ar-Raḥmān*. And know that the One who could ignore you has made it His name to not.

### Theme Two: The Permission to Ask — Why Allah Built a Path That Goes Through Other Humans

Now I want to take you somewhere that might be uncomfortable.

There is a story you may know. A young Muslim — maybe 17, maybe 23 — encounters a question about Islam that they cannot answer. Maybe it's a verse that confuses them. Maybe it's a critic online. Maybe it's a quiet doubt that has been growing for months.

What do they do?

In our era, most often, they Google it. They open Reddit. They read a thread. They find a stranger's confident answer. They compare it to another stranger's confident answer. They drift further from the question without ever finding solid ground.

Here is what they almost never do: **find a *khabīr* and ask.**

Why? Because we have inherited — from the broader culture — a deeply individualistic story about knowing. *No one can tell you what to think. Find your own truth. Trust yourself.* It sounds liberating. But it has produced a generation that is drowning in unanswered questions and has lost the muscle of *asking*.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what this ayah does.

The ayah whose subject is **the Lord of the universe** — the highest possible subject — ends with a command to **ask another human being**.

Let that sit.

Allah, who could have said *I will teach you Myself directly through dreams and visions* — chose instead to build a tradition. He sent a Messenger. He raised up scholars. He commanded that knowledge of Him be passed *through people*.

This is not an accident of human history. It is the design of the path.

Look at the verb: *fas-al*. **Ask.** It is active. It requires you to lower yourself, to admit you don't know, to identify someone who knows more, and to *speak the question out loud to another soul*.

This is hard. It is hard because asking exposes you. It says *I need*. It says *you have something I don't*. The ego resists this with everything it has.

[PAUSE]

But here is what Allah is teaching us. **The path to knowing Him is not a private path.** It cannot be walked alone with a phone. The very structure of the command *fas-al bihi khabīrā* tells us that knowledge of *Ar-Raḥmān* is **transmitted, embodied, relational** — not downloaded.

There is a deeper layer. Notice the word *khabīr* again. *One who knows from within. One who has walked.* Allah is telling you: do not just ask anyone. Ask someone whose *life* shows that they have been close to what you are asking about.

This is a filter. It rules out the influencer with the largest audience. It rules out the loudest voice in the comments. It rules in the quiet teacher in the corner of the masjid whose *taqwā* you can feel in the room.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing I want you to feel before we close this theme.

The ayah does not say *ask, and your question will be answered immediately*. It says *ask*. The asking is the obedience. The answer is in Allah's hands.

When you find a *khabīr* and put your question in front of them, you have done the thing the ayah commanded. Whether the answer comes today, in a year, or never — your obedience was in the *seeking*.

This reframes the doubt-anxious life. You do not have to *solve* your questions to be a faithful person. You have to **ask them well, to the right people, with the right sincerity.** That is the command. The rest is mercy.

The practical application: identify *one* question you have been carrying — about Allah, about the Quran, about a verse that troubles you — and find a *khabīr* this month. Not Google. Not Reddit. **A human being whose life you can see.** Make an appointment. Send the email. Speak the question out loud.

The ayah that began with the heavens and the earth ends by sending you to your local masjid. That is not an anticlimax. That is the path.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what this single ayah has done.

It walked us from the largest possible scale — heavens, earth, everything between, six days — to the smallest possible action: *you, ask, a person.*

It moved from immensity to intimacy. From the cosmic to the conversational. From the Throne above the universe to the teacher in the next room.

And the reason this works is the name in the middle. ***Ar-Raḥmān.*** Without that name, the trip from the Throne to the *khabīr* would be a fall. With it, it is a homecoming. The God above the universe and the path to Him through your local scholar are connected by a single thread: **mercy.**

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions:

- The *thumma* lifts you above the universe — and the *khabīr* roots you in your community. **Both are mercy.**
- The name *Ar-Raḥmān* in the middle of the ayah is the hinge that makes the immensity bearable — and it is the same mercy that made Allah build a path through humans rather than leave you alone with a book.

The ayah is teaching you: *I am vast beyond your comprehension, and I have made knowing Me reachable through the person beside you.*

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time I felt awe of Allah's vastness — and did I let mercy have the last word, or did I walk away crushed?

2. Have I been carrying a question about Allah, or about a verse, that I have not yet asked a *khabīr*? What is keeping me from asking?

3. When I picture *Ar-Raḥmān*, what do I actually see? Does the picture include the One above the Throne — or have I made a smaller, softer god who fits in my pocket?

4. Who, in my life, is a *khabīr* — someone whose knowledge of Allah is from *experience*, not just from books? If no one comes to mind, what does that tell me about the path I am on?

5. The ayah commands *asking*. Where in my life have I stopped asking — out of pride, out of laziness, or out of fear of what the answer might cost me?

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah does not ask you to understand the *istiwāʾ*. The classical scholars closed that door for our protection. It asks you to do something far more practical: **let the architecture of awe-then-mercy become the architecture of your prayer.** And let the command to ask become the architecture of your seeking.

Tonight, when you stand for *ṣalāh*, pause before the takbīr. Imagine — for two breaths — the heavens and the earth and everything between. Feel small. And then say *Allāhu akbar* knowing that the One you are facing is *Ar-Raḥmān*.

This week, identify your *khabīr*. Send the message you have been postponing. Ask the question.

The ayah is not literature. It is **a map.**

### Du'a

O *Ar-Raḥmān* — the One above the Throne, the One whose mercy reached us before we knew to ask — let our awe of Your vastness never crush us, because You named Yourself before You named anything else.

Open for us a path to a *khabīr*. Send into our lives someone whose closeness to You we can feel, and soften our pride enough to ask them what we have been afraid to ask.

And let our knowledge of You move from *ʿilm* to *khubrah* — from information about You to a life lived close enough to You that, one day, someone might ask *us*.

Āmīn.
