# The Day Their Deeds Became Scattered Dust

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 23

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:25:23] وَقَدِمْنَآ إِلَىٰ مَا عَمِلُوا۟ مِنْ عَمَلٍ فَجَعَلْنَـٰهُ هَبَآءً مَّنثُورًا

**Transliteration:**
> *Wa qadimnā ilā mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin fa-jaʿalnāhu habāʾan manthūrā*

**Translation:**
> "And We will turn to whatever deeds they did and make them as scattered dust."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before The Verse

Before we open this ayah, I want you to picture something with me.

Picture a man who has lived seventy years. He has worked. He has built. He has been generous to neighbors, kind to strangers. He kept his word in business. He fed people. He visited the sick. He raised children who turned out well. By any measure that the world uses to weigh a life — he lived a *good* life.

[PAUSE]

Now picture his deathbed. Picture the moment after. And picture his arrival at a place where every single thing he did is laid out before him — every act of kindness, every coin he gave, every patient hour he sat by someone's pain.

And then picture all of it — *all of it* — turning, in front of him, into something less than smoke. Less than ash. Into the kind of dust you see floating in a beam of sunlight through a window. Just **floating**. Going nowhere. Settling on nothing. Useless.

That is not my image. That is the image Allah gives us in this ayah.

This sūrah, Al-Furqan — *the Criterion* — was revealed in Makkah, in years when the Prophet ﷺ was being mocked daily for the message he carried. The deniers had a refrain: *bring us angels, show us your Lord, why doesn't a treasure fall from the sky for him?* They wanted to see the unseen on their own terms. They were sure of themselves. They were comfortable. Many of them were, by the standards of their society, **good people** — generous to their tribe, brave in war, scrupulous about their word.

And Allah, in this passage of the surah, takes us forward in time — past their deaths, past the gathering, to the moment they will see what they refused to see. The angels they demanded will come. But the angels will not be carrying credentials. The angels will be carrying *the verdict*.

And right in the middle of that scene — between the demand for angels in verse 21 and the long, slow lament of the wrongdoer in verses 27-29 — Allah inserts this single sentence. Eleven words. One image.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows:

**What makes a deed real?**

A man fed the hungry. The hungry person ate. The food became flesh and blood and another day of life. That happened. It was *real*. So why — by what mechanism, by what justice — does Allah say that on a coming day, He will turn to that very deed and make it **scattered dust**?

What does that mean? And what does it require of me, sitting here, doing the things I do?

[PAUSE]

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Qadimnā* — "We will turn to"

I want to start with the very first word of this ayah because, the moment you understand what it's actually doing, the entire scene changes shape.

The verb Allah uses is **قَدِمْنَا** — *qadimnā*. Translations almost always render this as "We will turn to" or "We will proceed to." Both are reasonable. Both are also too quiet.

Before we look at the word inside this ayah, let me teach the concept it sits inside.

**In Arabic, there are several verbs you can use to describe one party approaching another.** You can say *jāʾa* — they came. You can say *atā* — they came toward. You can say *aqbala* — they advanced, they turned their face toward. Each of these has its own texture. Each of them sits at a different distance from the listener.

*Qadima* — the root ق-د-م — is different. It is the verb you use when someone of significance **arrives**. When a king *qadima*, he didn't just walk into the room. He **made his approach**. The whole space reorganizes around his arrival. *Qadima* is the verb of presence claiming a space. It's the verb you use when the person who has been awaited has finally come, and now everything that was waiting is no longer waiting.

The noun from the same root, *qadam*, means **foot** — specifically the part of you that moves you forward, that announces you, that arrives somewhere before the rest of you does.

Now look at the word in our ayah: not *qadima* — *qadimnā*. **We arrived. We made Our approach.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment. Allah is not saying "We will judge their deeds" — though that is true. He is not saying "We will weigh their deeds" — though that is also true elsewhere. He is saying something far more intimate and far more terrifying. He is saying: *We will come to where their deeds are. We will arrive at them.*

Notice the preposition that follows: **إِلَىٰ** — *ilā*, "toward." So the picture is not "We will examine from above." The picture is: **We will move toward.**

Here is what is remarkable. In every other place in the Qur'an where Allah describes Himself approaching a thing, the language is one of **honor and welcome** — He approaches the believers, He turns toward those who repent. *Qadimnā* is the verb of an arrival that *changes everything around it*.

But here, in this ayah, Allah's "arrival" is to a heap of deeds done by people who lived their whole lives as if He were not watching, as if He did not exist, as if He were not the One they would meet. They acted as though their deeds belonged to *them*. The deeds were a private archive — *my* generosity, *my* good name, *my* legacy.

And then, on a day they cannot postpone, **the Owner arrives**.

Think of what it means for a king to walk into a hall full of things claimed in his name. Things done supposedly for him, but in his absence — by people who were not really thinking of him at all. He walks in. He sees what was done. And he sees that **none of it was actually for him**. It was for the people who did it. It bore his name on the outside, but inside there was no him.

What happens to those things, when the real King arrives?

[PAUSE]

That is what *qadimnā* is doing here. Allah is announcing an **arrival** — and the chilling implication is that the deeds are about to be exposed for what they truly were, in the presence of the One they pretended to be for.

There is one more layer in this verb I do not want you to miss. *Qadimnā* is in the past tense. Grammatically: "We have arrived." Allah is describing a future event — the Day of Judgment — using a *completed* tense. This is a documented Quranic device: future certainties spoken in the perfect tense, because their happening is not in question. From the standpoint of the One who is outside time, **it has already happened**. The arrival is not pending. It is settled. The only thing pending is *our* arrival into the moment when we meet it.

So before we have even gotten to the word *deeds*, the ayah has already done something to us. It has placed us in a future scene where the **King has arrived**, where every action we've ever performed lies before Him, and where we are about to find out what those actions were actually made of.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin* — "Whatever deeds they did, of any deed"

The second movement of this ayah is structured in a way that English completely smooths over. Let me show you what's there.

The Arabic phrase is: **مَا عَمِلُوا۟ مِنْ عَمَلٍۢ** — *mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin*. A literal rendering: "**whatever they did, of any deed.**"

Notice that the word for *deed* (*ʿamal*) appears **twice**. Once as a verb — *ʿamilū*, "they did" — and once as a noun — *ʿamalin*, "of deed." The same root, ع-م-ل, said back to back.

Before we examine why, let me teach the construction.

**In Arabic rhetoric, there is a structure where a verb's action is repeated as a noun derived from the same root, often with the preposition *min* in between.** It does not mean "some of." It means **"of any kind whatsoever."** It is a structure of *exhaustive inclusion*. When Arabic wants to scoop up every conceivable instance of something and leave no exception, it doubles the root and links them with *min*. Translators sometimes render it as "of any deed at all." That is a fair attempt, but the rhetorical force is closer to: *every last thing they ever did, however small, however large, however hidden, however public — every single one of them.*

Sit with that.

The ayah is not saying "We will turn to their bad deeds" — those would be expected. The ayah is not even saying "We will turn to their evil deeds and convert them to ash." The ayah is saying: **whatever they did, of any deed at all** — the full inventory. The seventy-year ledger. Generosity, kindness, the time they let someone merge into traffic, the cup of water they gave, the orphan they fed.

**Every single one.**

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. The verb *ʿamilū* — "they did" — is in the past tense, and so is *qadimnā*. So the timeline of the ayah goes like this: *they did* (in their lifetime) → *We arrived* (on the Day) → *We made it* (the verdict).

The deeds are *finished*. The book is closed. They cannot add. They cannot revise. They cannot say "wait, I meant well." Whatever they did is whatever they did. And now it sits there, complete, awaiting the arrival.

Here is what is remarkable about this construction. The doubling — *ʿamilū min ʿamalin* — does not just mean "everything they did." It also has a tone of **deliberate cataloguing**. It is the language of a register being read, line by line. Every entry. Every transaction. Nothing skipped.

Imagine an auditor, not a casual observer. Imagine someone going through a man's bank statements over fifty years, every single line item — the morning coffees, the major purchases, the charitable transfers, the tips. *That* level of itemization is what *mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin* names.

And then, at the end of all that itemization — every last line item examined — Allah says: *fa-jaʿalnāhu*. "**So We made it.**"

Notice the singular pronoun: *jaʿalnā-**hu**.* Not *jaʿalnāhā* (made them, plural). The whole catalogue, the entire seventy-year inventory of every deed they ever performed, is collapsed into a single grammatical *it*. **All of it together. As one mass. One verdict.**

The doubling lifted everything up. The singular pronoun pressed it all back together into a single clump of debris. And then comes the image.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Habāʾan Manthūrā* — "Scattered Dust"

We are now at the image. And I want you to slow down with me here, because the listener of this ayah in the desert understood this image with a precision that we, sitting in glass-and-concrete cities, have to *work* to recover.

The phrase is **هَبَآءً مَّنثُورًۭا** — *habāʾan manthūrā*.

The first word, *habāʾ*, comes from the root **ه-ب-و / ه-ب-ء**. In classical Arabic lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib), *habāʾ* is the term for the **finest possible airborne particles** — the dust motes you can only see when a single beam of sunlight cuts through the dim interior of a room and suddenly reveals what was always floating there, invisible. *Habāʾ* is dust so fine it is below the threshold of normal sight. It cannot be picked up. It cannot be gathered. It cannot be put to any use. It does not even have the dignity of mud, which can at least be shaped. It is dust that has *nothing left of substance*.

The second word, *manthūr*, comes from the root **ن-ث-ر**. *Nathara* means **to scatter, to strew, to disperse without arrangement**. It is the opposite of *nazama* — to arrange, to thread, to compose into order. (In Arabic literary terms, *nathr* is **prose** — speech without the order of meter — as opposed to *nazm*, **poetry** — speech threaded into form.) When you *nathara* something, you have not just thrown it; you have **let it lose its form**. Whatever pattern it had — gone. Whatever structure held it together — undone.

So *habāʾan manthūrā* — taken together — names not one quality but two:

**First** — it is reduced to the finest, most insubstantial form matter can take. *Habāʾ.*

**Second** — and this is crucial — it is **scattered**. *Manthūr*. There is no pile. There is no heap that could be re-collected. Each particle is going its own direction, drifting, never to meet another.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The ayah did not have to say *manthūr*. *Habāʾ* alone would have been devastating — "We made it dust." But Allah added the second word, the participle of scattering. Why?

Here is what I think the ayah is doing — and notice I am stepping past pure grammar into interpretive application now: it is denying these deeds **even the dignity of remaining together**. They cannot be re-collected, re-weighed, re-counted, re-presented. The catalogue that was so meticulously itemized in the previous phrase — *every deed they ever did* — is, in the same instant of judgment, dispersed beyond recovery. They cannot be argued for. They cannot be appealed. They cannot be re-presented under a new motive. They are gone, in every direction, *as a single act*.

Let me show you why this image, specifically, lands so hard for the original audience.

In the desert, the wind picks up. A dust storm passes. After it has passed — what is left? On the ground, *something*. But in the air, in the sunlight that returns afterward, you see those finest particles still floating, going in directions the wind chose, settling nowhere you can name. **That** is *habāʾ manthūr*. The remains after a storm has stripped a thing of all weight.

Or another image — and this one I think is even closer to what Allah is naming. You walk into a tent. The sun is low. A single shaft of light enters through the door. And in that shaft, suddenly, you see a million tiny particles dancing — particles that were always there, particles that constitute much of what you breathe, particles that you cannot grasp, cannot use, cannot organize. They are the residue of *something* — something that crumbled, something that wore down, something that was once whole. But now they are nothing. Not nothing in the sense of *gone*; nothing in the sense of *useless*. They occupy space without occupying purpose.

[PAUSE]

Now bring back the question we started with. **What makes a deed real?**

This image gives the answer. A deed's reality is not in its outward shape. The hungry person was fed. The widow was helped. The mosque was built. **All of that physically happened.** And yet, when the King arrives at those acts, He turns them into the floating residue you see in a sunbeam.

Why? Because **the substance of a deed is not the deed itself. The substance of a deed is what it was for.**

If a deed was for Allah, it has *weight*. It has the kind of density that survives a storm. The classical scholars said that an atom's worth of sincere good will outweigh mountains of insincere good — and now you see why. The mountains were always *habāʾ*. They only looked like mountains.

If a deed was for the self — for the name, for the reputation, for the comfort of being known as good, for the easing of a guilty conscience, for the love of one's tribe more than the love of the One who made the tribe — then it has the *form* of a deed but not the *substance* of a deed. It is a balloon. It looks full. The instant the right pin touches it, all that is revealed is that it was nothing inside.

This is what Allah's *qadimnā* does. **It is the pin.**

When the King arrives — when the One the deeds were claimed to be for actually shows up — every deed that wasn't really for Him is exposed for what it was: shaped air. And shaped air, once the shape is broken, becomes *habāʾ manthūr*. Dust scattering in directions no one can call back.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Look at what these three pieces have done together.

*Qadimnā* established that **the deeds will be visited by their supposed Owner**.

*Mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin* established that **the inventory will be exhaustive — every single one, no exceptions, no hidden corners.**

*Habāʾan manthūrā* established that **what is found will be reduced to a residue beyond recovery, beyond use, beyond gathering.**

Three movements: arrival, audit, dissolution.

Now we ask the harder question. What does this reveal not just about the words — but about reality itself? About my deeds? About the difference between a life that *looks* substantive and a life that *is* substantive?

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Foundation Underneath the Foundation

I want you to think for a moment about a building.

Picture a beautiful building. Forty stories. Glass and stone. People go in and out of it every day. It serves a real purpose. It houses families, businesses, stories. By every visible measure, it is a real building.

Now picture an inspector arriving — not a casual visitor, the **owner of the land**. And imagine that the owner has a tool that lets him see, in a single glance, what the building was actually founded on. Not the steel beams. Not the concrete. The **footing** beneath the foundation. The thing the foundation was poured into. He looks down through forty stories of stone and steel, through the basement, through the foundation slab — and he sees what's underneath.

What's underneath determines whether the forty stories are a *building* or a *temporary illusion*.

If what's underneath is bedrock, the forty stories are real. They will outlast the inspector's visit. They will outlast everyone.

If what's underneath is sand — finely-packed, beautifully-leveled, carefully-tamped sand — the forty stories are not a building. They are a **delay**. The day the sand shifts, the day the rain comes hard enough, the day a tremor passes — the whole structure goes.

[PAUSE]

This is the way I want you to receive the ayah's first theme. **Every deed has a foundation underneath it. And the foundation is not visible from the outside.** It cannot be seen by neighbors. It often cannot be seen by the doer himself. It is the **why**. It is the answer to the question: *for whose sake did I do this?*

The Quran's deepest claim about deeds is not that good deeds count and bad deeds don't. The Quran's deepest claim is that **a deed is the shape of its intention**. The visible part is just the cap on top of an invisible foundation. And on the Day of Judgment, the visible part is removed, and only the foundation is weighed.

Hear me carefully: the people in this ayah were *not necessarily doing visibly evil things*. The classical mufassirūn — including ʿAlī, Mujāhid, and others quoted by Ibn Kathīr — said this verse is talking about people whose **outward deeds appeared good** but whose foundation was disbelief, or showing off, or seeking the world's praise. People who fed the poor, but whose feeding was for the *being-seen-feeding*. People who kept their word, but whose word-keeping was about *being-known-as-trustworthy*. People who were generous, but whose generosity was a way to feel themselves morally above the people receiving.

These people built forty-story buildings. The buildings were real to look at. People walked through them. People benefited from them. **And then the King arrived, and looked at the foundation, and saw sand.**

And the sand became wind. And the wind became *habāʾ manthūr*.

[PAUSE]

This is the part of the ayah that should make every one of us pause. Because the deniers in the surah are *named* as deniers — they are people who explicitly rejected the Prophet ﷺ and the message. But the warning the ayah carries reaches further. The mechanism — *foundation determines substance* — applies to anyone whose deeds were not founded on *Him*.

So the question the ayah hands you is not "am I a good person?" Most of us could check that box without trying. The question is: **what is my deed founded on?**

When I was generous yesterday — was I generous because the Receiver of the act was the One I love, or because the watcher of the act was the one I wanted to impress?

When I held my tongue this morning — was the silence held for *Him*, or held because I didn't want to look like the kind of person who couldn't hold their tongue?

When I prayed last night — was I in front of Him, or in front of the version of myself I want to keep believing in?

These are the foundation questions. The visible deed is the same in either case. **The substance is not.**

And this is what *qadimnā* means. It means: a day comes when the King shows up at the building, and the building is appraised not on its facade but on its footing.

What can a person *do* with this, sitting in their kitchen on a Wednesday morning?

**Two things.** Right now. Today.

**First** — when you are about to do a deed that you know others will see, **add a private one**. Pair every visible act with a hidden one. The Prophet ﷺ taught us through his life and his prayers a relentless habit of inner action that no one would ever know about. Make it your habit too. Visible giving, paired with a private giving no one will be told about. Visible kindness, paired with a private mercy you'll never narrate. The hidden ones are what re-anchor the foundation.

**Second** — every night, before sleep, ask yourself a single question about *one* deed from your day: *who was that for?* Not all of them. Just one. Be honest. Don't punish yourself for the answer, but don't lie about it either. Over time, this single question rebuilds the floor of your soul. It teaches you to feel the foundation while the deed is still being built — so the King's arrival, when it comes, is not an exposure. It is a recognition.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Mercy Hidden Inside the Warning

I want you to look at this ayah one more time before we leave it. Not at its terror — we have spent enough time there. At what is *underneath* the terror. Because every Quranic warning has a mercy folded inside it, if you are willing to unfold it.

Here is the mercy.

**Allah has told us, in advance, what will happen.**

He did not have to. He could have left this hidden. He could have let the Day arrive and let the deniers discover, all on their own, what their lives had been built on. The dust would have scattered, the verdict would have been just, and they would have had no defense — they were warned, in their own intuition, by their own conscience, by every messenger who had ever come.

But He told us. He gave us the image — the *habāʾ manthūr*, the dust in the sunbeam — *now*, in time, while we still have time. He let us see the verdict before the verdict. He showed us the foundation test before we are tested.

[PAUSE]

Why?

Because **a warning given in advance is mercy made visible**. The deniers in this ayah did not lack information. They lacked *willingness*. They had a Prophet living among them — a man whose entire life was the foundation we're talking about — and they refused to look. The Quran's advance announcement of the Day is not a threat from a tyrant. It is the patience of a Lord who would rather you arrive at the King's appearance with a building made of bedrock than with a tower of beautiful sand.

This is what changes everything about how we *hear* a verse like this.

When you hear a verse about Hell — or about deeds becoming dust — your first reaction might be fear. That is not wrong. The Prophet ﷺ said the believer holds two states in the heart: hope and fear, like the two wings of a bird. Both are needed.

But underneath the fear, hear this: **He told you so you wouldn't.** He warned you so you would have the chance to live differently. He described the dust so you would build with stone. The very existence of this ayah in the Book you are reading means He has already extended you a mercy — the mercy of seeing the test before you have to take it.

And here is something else.

The ayah says *qadimnā* — *We* arrived. Allah does not delegate this. He does not send only an angel. The verb is in the first person. He Himself approaches the deeds.

Think about that for a moment. The same Allah who will *arrive* at the dust-builders' deeds is the same Allah who is, **right now**, reading the foundation of every deed you are doing today. He is **already there**. The arrival on the Day is not a journey from far to near; it is the *unveiling* of a presence that was always present.

[PAUSE]

Which means: you are not actually performing for an absent audience. You never were.

Most of the unease in our lives — the strange exhaustion that comes from living for the gaze of others — is the exhaustion of performing for an audience that, in the long view, isn't real. The real audience is *Him*, and *He has been there the whole time*. The performance was always happening in front of Him.

The mercy of this ayah is that it returns us to that fact. Stop performing for the floating audience. Realign. The One who matters is **here**. Has been. Will be. The day He "arrives" is just the day you get to see what you should have known all along.

And once you live as if He is here — as if every deed is being done, right now, in front of the only One whose verdict is final — something extraordinary happens.

**Your deeds become smaller and heavier at the same time.**

Smaller, because you stop needing them to be impressive. You stop needing them to be photographed, narrated, broadcast, witnessed. You start doing things in private. You start giving without telling. You start praying when no one would think you were praying.

And heavier, because each deed now carries the entire weight of being for *Him*. A glass of water given for His sake outweighs a public dinner given for the camera. A whispered prayer at three a.m. outweighs an hour of well-lit reciting at the front of a hall. **The foundation is bedrock. The building, however small, is real.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It changes one specific thing. **You give yourself permission to do less, but for Him.**

Stop measuring your spiritual life by visibility. Stop measuring it by quantity. Start measuring it by foundation. One deed a day, **really** for Him, builds a tower that the dust-storm of the Last Day cannot scatter. Twenty deeds for the gaze of others builds nothing — just an architecture of air.

The mercy of this ayah is that you have been told. You have been given the test in advance. You can rebuild the floor of your soul. Tonight.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have seen.

Through the linguistic journey: **a King arrives, a complete catalogue is read, and what is found becomes scattered dust.** *Qadimnā. Mā ʿamilū min ʿamalin. Habāʾan manthūrā.* Three movements compressed into eleven words.

Through the thematic depths: **a deed is the shape of its intention, the foundation determines the substance, and the warning is a mercy because it lets you fix the foundation while there is still time.**

These are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic side tells you what the King will do. The thematic side tells you why, and what to do about it now. Both converge on the same place: **the deed itself is not the deed. The deed is what's underneath it. And on the Day of arrival, only what was for Him will weigh anything at all.**

This is also the architecture of the surah. Surah Al-Furqan is named "the Criterion" — and a criterion is *exactly this*: the line between what is real and what only looks real. The criterion is not which deeds were big and which were small. The criterion is which deeds were **for Him**, and which were dressed-up versions of the self.

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

Take these questions with you. They are not rhetorical. They are real:

1. If, tonight, the King arrived at the deeds I've done in the last seven days — which of them would survive the wind, and which would scatter?

2. What is the most "impressive" thing I've done recently — and was it impressive *to whom*?

3. Is there a private deed in my life right now? A thing I do for Him that no one knows about? If not, what is one I could begin today?

4. When I think about my reputation as a "good person" — does that thought give me peace, or does it quietly worry me? What is the worry telling me?

5. What does it mean for me, *practically*, that Allah is already present at the deeds I am performing — that the arrival is not in the future but is the unveiling of a presence that is here now?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**On the Day the King arrives at the deeds we did, only what we did for Him will remain — everything else will be revealed to have been dust all along.**

### The Closing Invitation

You have been warned. That is the mercy.

You do not have to live the rest of your life as a tower of beautiful sand. You can lay one stone, today, on bedrock. And then another tomorrow. The tower built that way may be smaller than the one your old life was building — but it will still be standing on the Day the wind passes.

Let this verse change *one* thing. Not everything. One thing. Pick one deed today and do it only for Him — no one else informed, no one else thanked, no one else witnessing. Place that stone. Then place another tomorrow. **Build something that the dust-test cannot dissolve.**

### A Brief Du'a

> *Yā Allāh,* You are the King who will arrive. You are already here.
>
> Re-found my deeds on You. Take from them what was for the eyes of others, and let what remains — however small — be only for You.
>
> Spare me, on the Day You come to my deeds, the sight of dust scattering in the sunbeam where I had thought a building stood. Let what You find be little, but real. Let what You find be Yours.
>
> *Āmīn.*
