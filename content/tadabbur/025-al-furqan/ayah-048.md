# The Wind That Carries Mercy Before It Arrives

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 48

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَرْسَلَ ٱلرِّيَـٰحَ بُشْرًۢا بَيْنَ يَدَىْ رَحْمَتِهِۦ ۚ وَأَنزَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءً طَهُورًا

**Transliteration:**

*Wa huwa alladhī arsala ar-riyāḥa bushran bayna yaday raḥmatih. Wa anzalnā min as-samāʾi māʾan ṭahūrā.*

**Translation:**

*And He is the One who sent the winds as glad tidings before His mercy. And We sent down from the sky water — pure, purifying.*

---

## Introduction

### The World Before This Ayah

To stand inside this ayah, you have to stand inside a particular kind of climate.

Not weather. Argument.

The Surah is named *Al-Furqan* — "The Criterion." The thing that separates. And from its opening verses, this surah is in the middle of an argument with people who keep asking the wrong question. They want a different kind of proof. They say: *Why didn't an angel come down with him? Why doesn't he have a treasure? Why doesn't a garden grow under his feet?* They are demanding a sign loud enough, dramatic enough, custom-built enough to overwhelm them into belief.

And the Surah, patiently, keeps redirecting them.

It points to the shadow that the sun stretches and shortens. It points to the night that wraps you like a garment. It points to the sleep that mimics death. It points to the two seas that meet but don't mix. And it points — in the ayah we are about to enter — to the wind that arrives before the rain.

This is not Allah running out of arguments. This is Allah saying: *the proof you are demanding is not greater than the proof you are ignoring.*

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Stay with me here. Because in this single ayah — sixteen words — Allah does something extraordinary.

He describes one of the most ordinary moments any human being has ever experienced: the breeze that picks up just before a rainstorm. The shift in the air. The smell of something coming.

And He chooses one specific word — one preposition, really — to describe the relationship between that breeze and that rain. A phrase that, once you see it, you can never unsee. Because what Allah is teaching us in this ayah is not really about wind. And it is not really about water.

It is about how mercy moves toward you.

It is about how you are being prepared, right now, for arrivals you cannot yet see.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to slow down on three things in this ayah. The plural word for **winds**. The phrase **between His hands**. And the word that describes the water — **ṭahūr**.

Each one, on its own, is small. Together, they unlock the ayah.

---

### Element One: Why "Winds" and Not "Wind"

In Arabic, this ayah does not say *al-rīḥ* (the wind, singular). It says **ar-riyāḥ** — the winds, plural.

Before we open what that means here, let's teach the concept first.

In Arabic, singular and plural are not just about counting. They carry mood. The classical scholars — and you can trace this through the lexicons — noticed something across the Quran: when Allah uses the singular *al-rīḥ*, the context is almost always punishment. The destroying wind sent against ʿĀd. The cold wind that withers. The barren wind. The single, focused, devastating gust.

But when Allah uses the plural — *ar-riyāḥ* — the context shifts. The winds that scatter. The winds that bring news. The winds that pollinate. The winds that herald rain.

[PAUSE]

It's as if the singular wind is one fist, and the plural winds are a thousand open hands.

Now hear the ayah again with that ear:

*And He is the One who sent **the winds** as glad tidings...*

Not one wind. Many winds. From many directions. Each carrying a different note of the same announcement.

Stay with this for a moment. Because there is something here about how Allah's mercy reaches you. Notice — Allah did not need plural winds to deliver rain. Meteorologically, one wind would do. The plural is not because Allah lacked efficiency. The plural is because Allah was being **generous**.

When mercy is on its way to you, it does not arrive through a single channel. It arrives from multiple directions at once. A friend's text. A line in a book. A dream. A failed plan that you didn't know you needed to fail. A breeze on your face on a hard day.

These are not separate events. They are *ar-riyāḥ* — a chorus of small announcements, each one whispering: *something is coming. Get ready.*

Here's what's remarkable: most of us are trained to wait for the rain. We are trained to count only the moment of arrival as mercy. But Allah, in this ayah, is renaming the breeze itself as part of the gift.

[PAUSE]

The wind is not what comes before mercy. The wind **is** mercy, dressed in its earlier form.

---

### Element Two: *Bayna Yaday Raḥmatih* — "Between the Hands of His Mercy"

Now here is the phrase that, once you see it, changes the whole ayah.

The translation says: "before His mercy." But the Arabic is more vivid than that. The phrase is **bayna yaday raḥmatih** — literally, *between the two hands of His mercy*.

Let's teach this phrase as a concept first, because it appears in many places in the Quran and it is doing very specific work.

*Bayna yaday* — "between the two hands of" — is an Arabic idiom for "in front of." But it is not a neutral "in front." It is the space that opens up directly ahead of someone. The space their hands can reach into. The space they are about to enter.

When a king walked into a court, the heralds went *bayna yaday* the king. They were not separate from the king's arrival. They were the leading edge of it. They announced him. They prepared the room. By the time the king's body entered, the room had already been adjusted by the heralds' presence.

Now hear the ayah with this lens:

*He sent the winds as glad tidings — between the two hands of His mercy.*

[PAUSE]

The winds are not random air movement that happens to precede rain. The winds are heralds walking in front of the King. They are the leading edge of a mercy that is already on its way. The mercy hasn't arrived yet — but its presence is already being felt, because what is in front of it is part of it.

Sit with this image. Allah is describing His mercy as something with a body. Something that moves. Something that has a *front* — a leading edge — and the air itself is rearranging in anticipation of that arrival.

Notice something. The grammar attaches the mercy to Allah Himself: *raḥmatih* — *His* mercy. With the pronoun. This is not generic mercy. This is a mercy belonging to the One who owns the heavens and the earth — and even *that* mercy travels with announcers in front of it. Even *that* mercy does not just appear. It arrives in stages. It gives you time to receive it.

[PAUSE]

Here's what this phrase invites us to consider. When something good is about to enter your life, the room around you starts changing first. You don't always see it as the changing. You feel restless without knowing why. A thought won't leave you alone. A conversation keeps replaying. A door you assumed was closed begins to creak.

These are not noise.

These are the heralds. They are walking *bayna yaday* a mercy you cannot yet name.

---

### Element Three: *Ṭahūr* — Not Just Pure, but Purifying

Now we come to the third word. And this is where the ayah does something quietly devastating.

Allah describes the water that descends as **ṭahūr**.

In English, both *ṭāhir* and *ṭahūr* tend to get translated the same way: "pure." But in Arabic, these are not the same word. They are not even doing the same job.

Let's teach this concept first.

In Arabic morphology, certain word-shapes carry intensification or extension of meaning. The shape *faʿūl* — the shape *ṭahūr* takes — is one of them. The classical grammarians and lexicographers — Al-Rāghib in *Mufradāt*, the *Lisān* — note that *ṭāhir* describes something that is itself pure, but *ṭahūr* describes something that is pure **and that purifies whatever else it touches**.

The difference is enormous.

A pure thing keeps itself clean. A purifying thing transmits its cleanness to what it meets.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what Allah just said about rain.

The water descending from the sky is not merely clean water. It is water that, by touching, makes other things clean. It washes the leaf. It washes the soil. It washes the body. It is — and this is the linguistic point — water with the capacity to *give* purity, not just *have* it.

This is not a poetic flourish. This is a precise theological claim being made about what Allah sent down. The classical jurists, in fact, built an entire chapter of fiqh on this single word — the rules of *ṭahārah*, of ritual purification, rest on the recognition that rainwater carries an active power, not a passive state. Scholars differed on the details and that ikhtilāf is its own conversation, but the linguistic foundation underneath their debate is this word: *ṭahūr*.

Now zoom out. Allah is describing the rain as the final stage of the mercy.

What does this final stage do?

It doesn't just arrive. It **transforms what it touches.**

[PAUSE]

This is what real mercy is. Real mercy is not just present with you. Real mercy makes you cleaner than you were before it found you.

Stay with this. Because the mercy of Allah, in its fullest form, is not water that puddles next to you. It is water that washes through you. You are not the same after it has passed.

### Bridge

Three words. *Ar-riyāḥ.* *Bayna yaday.* *Ṭahūr.*

Plural winds. The space in front of two hands. Water that purifies.

Each one, on its own, you might pass by. Together, they describe an entire architecture of how Allah's mercy moves.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: You Are Always Living in the Wind

Here is something most of us live without realizing.

Imagine standing on a porch on a hot afternoon. The air is still. The sky is bright. Nothing has happened. And then — almost imperceptibly — the leaves start moving the wrong direction. The temperature drops two degrees. A smell rises from the soil. The light shifts.

If you were not paying attention, you would not even know something had changed. But the storm is already on its way. The change of air is the storm. Or rather — the change of air is the storm announcing itself before it has form.

Most of human suffering, I think, comes from being unable to read the wind.

We sit inside our lives waiting for the rain — by which we mean, we are waiting for the moment of arrival. The job. The healing. The marriage. The child. The clarity. The release. We have decided that *that* is when mercy starts.

And so we walk through the windy stretch of our lives — the restless months, the unexplained delays, the closed doors, the unsettled feeling — as if it is empty time. As if Allah has left the room and we are waiting for Him to come back.

[PAUSE]

But this ayah is doing something to that assumption.

This ayah is saying: **the wind is part of the mercy.** The unsettled feeling, the strange closing of one door, the breeze of restlessness that won't leave you — these are not signs that mercy is far. They are signs that mercy is close enough that the air around you has begun to rearrange.

Think about how a Muslim's life is supposed to work in this picture. You are not someone waiting for arrivals. You are someone reading the wind. You are someone trained — by the Quran, by salah, by suhba — to notice the small announcers and to recognize them as heralds, not as noise.

And here is the practical shift this ayah asks of you.

Tomorrow, when something small in your life feels disturbed — a plan unsettles, a relationship strains, a routine breaks — instead of asking *why is this happening to me*, try asking the question this ayah teaches: *what is being announced?*

Not because every disturbance is a herald. But because some of them are. And the Muslim who walks through life assuming all wind is empty air will miss the very mercies that were trying to reach them.

[PAUSE]

The opposite of despair, in this ayah, is not optimism. The opposite of despair is **attention**.

---

### Theme Two: Mercy That Doesn't Just Arrive — It Cleans

Now the second movement.

When most of us imagine a good thing arriving in our lives, we imagine an addition. Something we did not have, we now have. The job we didn't have. The peace we didn't have. The relationship we didn't have. Mercy as plus-one.

But the rain in this ayah is not described that way.

The rain is described as **ṭahūr** — purifying.

This is not water that gets added to the soil and sits on top. This is water that goes *into* the soil and changes it. This is water that touches the dust on the leaf and lifts the dust off. This is water that meets the body of the believer and prepares them for prayer.

The rain in this ayah is not an addition. It is a **transformation**.

[PAUSE]

Now apply this to mercy.

The mercies Allah is sending toward you are not just things you don't yet have. They are forces that, when they reach you, will *clean parts of you that you did not know were dirty*.

Think about the mercies that have already touched your life. The relationship you didn't get. The opportunity that fell through. The illness that, in retrospect, slowed you down before you walked into something worse. None of these arrived as additions. All of them arrived as washes. They removed something from you that you would not have removed on your own. Pride. Speed. The wrong company. The wrong ambition. A wrong picture of yourself.

This is what Allah's mercy does in its fullest form. It does not leave you with more of who you already were. It leaves you cleaner.

And here is where this ayah quietly reframes a question many of us carry.

[PAUSE]

We sometimes ask: *why doesn't Allah just give me what I want?* And the answer, hidden inside this single word *ṭahūr*, is this: because the mercy you are asking for would arrive as water, and water doesn't just deliver — it **cleans**. And cleaning sometimes hurts. Cleaning sometimes takes longer than delivery.

The delay is not the absence of mercy. The delay is the cleaning.

[PAUSE]

The practical application here is hard but clear. When you next feel that something good has been delayed in your life, do not only ask *when will it come?* Ask *what is being washed off me in the meantime?* Because Allah's mercy, in this ayah, is not merely a destination. It is a process. And the process is not separate from the gift — it is part of the gift.

The wind is part of the mercy. The wash is part of the mercy. The arrival is the last frame. Not the only frame.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what this short ayah has been quietly teaching.

The plural winds — *ar-riyāḥ* — taught us that Allah's mercy reaches us from many directions at once, never one channel.

The phrase *bayna yaday raḥmatih* — between the two hands of His mercy — taught us that mercy has a leading edge, that the room rearranges before the King enters, that the announcers are part of the arrival.

The word *ṭahūr* — purifying water — taught us that when the mercy fully lands, it does not merely add to our lives. It cleans them.

Notice what these three are doing together. They form a single arc:

**Many announcers (the winds) → walking in front of (bayna yaday) → a mercy that cleans (ṭahūr).**

This is not a description of weather. This is a description of how Allah works in a life.

And one more thing. Look at the shift in the ayah's grammar. It begins, *And **He** is the One who sent...* — third person. *He.* But then it shifts: *And **We** sent down from the sky water — pure, purifying.* From *He* to *We*.

This is *iltifat* — the rhetorical shift the Arabic critics noticed all over the Quran. Why here? Watch the placement. The shift to "We" happens precisely at the moment of the rain. The moment of the cleansing. The arrival. As if to say: *the announcers were Mine. The cleansing — that is closer. That is intimate. That is My majestic plural reaching you directly.*

The pronoun itself moves closer when the mercy lands.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Don't rush to answer.

1. In the last six months of your life — what wind have you mistaken for empty air?

2. Is there a delay in your life right now that, if you stopped asking *when*, you might begin to ask *what is being cleaned*?

3. When mercy has arrived in your past, did it arrive as addition or as washing? Which one did you remember more clearly?

4. What in your life right now feels "between the two hands" — restless, anticipatory, unsettled — that you have been treating as a problem instead of an announcement?

5. If Allah's mercy is described as something that purifies what it touches — what part of you are you guarding from being touched?

### One Sentence

**Allah's mercy does not arrive — it travels, with announcers in front of it, and it cleans whatever it lands on.**

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to do anything dramatic with this ayah.

But the next time you feel a breeze on your face on a difficult day — the next time something small in your routine shifts and you don't know why — try, just for that moment, to remember that the wind is not empty.

It is *bayna yaday raḥmatih*.

It is the leading edge of a mercy that has a name written on it. Yours.

And whatever it is bringing, when it arrives, will not just be added to your life.

It will wash through it.

O Allah, when Your mercy comes to us, let us not only receive it as a gift but as a washing. And when the air around our lives shifts and we cannot yet name what is coming, give us the patience to recognize that You are already on Your way.*
