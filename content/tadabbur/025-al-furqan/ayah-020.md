# The Messengers Who Walked the Markets

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 20

> [ayah:25:20] وَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَا قَبْلَكَ مِنَ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ إِلَّآ إِنَّهُمْ لَيَأْكُلُونَ ٱلطَّعَامَ وَيَمْشُونَ فِى ٱلْأَسْوَاقِ ۗ وَجَعَلْنَا بَعْضَكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ فِتْنَةً أَتَصْبِرُونَ ۗ وَكَانَ رَبُّكَ بَصِيرًا

*"And We did not send before you any messengers except that they ate food and walked in the markets. And We have made some of you a trial for others — will you have patience? And ever is your Lord, All-Seeing."*

---

## Introduction: The World Behind the Complaint

To understand what this ayah is doing, we have to back up. We have to walk into the streets of Makkah in the years when this surah came down. We have to listen to what the Quraysh were actually saying about the man who walked among them.

A few ayahs earlier in this same surah, Allah quotes them word for word. They look at this Prophet ﷺ — the man they had known for forty years before revelation, the man whose honesty they had relied on, the man whose lineage they could trace — and they say:

*"What is it with this messenger — he eats food and walks in the markets? Why was an angel not sent down to him to be a warner alongside him? Or why was a treasure not thrown down to him, or why does he not have a garden from which he eats?"*

Stay with that complaint. Don't move past it.

They are not saying he is a bad man. They are saying he is **too ordinary** to be a messenger.

He gets hungry. He goes to the market like everyone else. He bargains for dates. He worries about the price of barley. He sweats in the sun. He has children who die. He buries them with his own hands. He has a wife who passes away and he weeps for her. He has an uncle who refuses to believe and he grieves over it.

And the Quraysh look at this and say: **a real messenger wouldn't be like this.**

A real messenger would be guarded by angels in public. A real messenger would have a treasure chest follow him through the streets. A real messenger would never have to ask "how much for this?" A real messenger would float, not walk.

[PAUSE]

What's stunning is that this complaint is not a Makkan invention. It is an ancient human reflex. Every people that received a messenger said some version of it. Every people. The Quran will tell us that elsewhere — that this exact disbelief, that this exact disappointment, that this exact "you eat what we eat, you sleep when we sleep, why should we follow you" — is a script humanity has been running for as long as Allah has been sending guidance.

So when this ayah opens, Allah is not just defending Muhammad ﷺ. He is making a statement about every prophet who has ever walked the earth.

And He is going to do something more. He is going to take the very thing the Quraysh used as a *complaint* — the ordinariness of the messenger — and reveal it as a *design*.

But the ayah doesn't stop there. It pivots. Mid-ayah, with no warning, Allah turns to a completely different topic. He says: *"And We have made some of you a trial for others — will you have patience?"* And then He closes with two words about Himself: *"And ever is your Lord, All-Seeing."*

Three statements. One ayah. They look like they don't belong together.

By the end, you'll see why they are the same statement said three times.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

There are three pieces of language here that I want to open with you. Each one is small. Each one looks unremarkable on the surface. And each one is doing enormous structural work.

### Element One: *Wa-mā arsalnā qablaka mina-l-mursalīn illā* — The Universal Negation

Before we touch this phrase, I want to teach you something about how Arabic builds emphasis.

In English, when we want to emphasize a universal claim, we tend to *add* words. "Every single one of them, without exception, did this." We pile up qualifiers.

Arabic does it the opposite way. It builds the universal claim through a structure called **negation followed by exception**. *Mā... illā* — "There is not... except." It's the same shape as the shahādah: *lā ilāha illa-llāh* — "There is no god *except* Allah." You start by emptying the room. Then you let one thing back in. What's left is what's true.

When Arabic uses this structure, it's not just saying "X is true." It's saying "the universe has been swept clean of every alternative, and only this remains."

Now look at how this ayah opens.

*Wa-mā arsalnā qablaka mina-l-mursalīn illā innahum la-yaʾkulūna-ṭ-ṭaʿāma wa-yamshūna fī-l-aswāq.*

"And We did not send, before you, any of the messengers, **except** that they were eating food and walking in the markets."

Notice something. Allah didn't have to phrase it this way. He could have said: "All of the messengers ate food and walked in the markets." A simple positive statement. It would have been linguistically true. It would have answered the Quraysh.

But that's not what He did. He swept the room. He took every messenger from Adam onward — every nabi, every rasūl, every prophet sent to every nation in every era — and He held them up against the Quraysh's complaint. And He said: *not a single one of them was exempt.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. Because the Quraysh's complaint was that a *real* messenger should be different. Should be elevated. Should be marked off from ordinary humanity by some visible sign — an angel, a treasure, a garden.

And Allah's answer, with this *mā... illā* construction, is to say: **find me one.** Find me one prophet in all of history that fits your description. Reach back as far as you want. Adam ate. Nūḥ ate. Ibrāhīm walked through markets. Mūsā worked for ten years tending sheep for his father-in-law. Dāwūd made armor with his hands. ʿĪsā walked from village to village.

The room has been swept. Not one is left.

What this construction is teaching us — at the level of grammar, before we even reach the meaning — is that the ordinariness of the messenger is not a flaw in the system. It *is* the system. The Quraysh aren't pointing out a bug. They're describing the design and calling it a defect.

And there's something else. Look at the verb tense. *La-yaʾkulūn... wa-yamshūn.* Both verbs are in the imperfect — the *muḍāriʿ* form. In Arabic, this form carries ongoing, repeated action. Not "they ate, once." Not "they walked, once." But: *they were always eating. They were always walking.* This was the texture of their daily existence. It happened every day. It happened in front of everyone. It was unhideable.

The grammar refuses to let the messengers float above the world. It pins them, in real time, to the same hunger and the same dust as everyone else.

Let's sit with this. The Quraysh wanted a messenger who was unlike them so they wouldn't have to follow him. Because if he's unlike you, you have an excuse. *I can't be like him. He's a different category of being.*

Allah's design strips that excuse away at the root. The messenger eats what you eat. Walks where you walk. Bleeds when he's cut. **He is followable precisely because he is reachable.**

That's what this opening grammatical sweep is doing. It's not just history. It's mercy.

### Element Two: *Wa-yamshūna fī-l-aswāq* — The Marketplace, Not the Sanctuary

Now I want to slow down on a single phrase: *yamshūna fī-l-aswāq.* "They walk in the markets."

Notice what Allah did *not* say.

He did not say "they walked in places of worship." He did not say "they walked in gardens of contemplation." He did not say "they walked in the company of angels." He did not say "they walked in solitude on the mountainsides."

He chose **the marketplace.**

Now here's where we have to teach the concept before we feel its weight. In the ancient world, in the Makkan world, in almost every traditional society — what is the *sūq*, the marketplace?

It is the loudest, dirtiest, most worldly space a city has. It is where prices are haggled, where lies are sometimes told, where tempers flare, where rich and poor crash into each other, where the smell of meat and sweat and dates and animals all mix together. It is the very opposite of the sanctuary. The temple is sacred space; the market is profane space. The temple is silent; the market is loud. The temple is set apart; the market is right in the middle.

If you wanted to describe a saintly figure to ancient ears, you would put them in the temple, on the mountain, in the cave, in the desert. You would mark them off with **distance from the marketplace.**

And Allah, in this ayah, deliberately puts every messenger He has ever sent **right into the marketplace.**

[PAUSE]

Why?

Because Allah is not building a religion of withdrawal. He is building a religion of presence.

He is not sending men who escape the world to teach you to escape the world. He is sending men who walk through the world with their feet to teach you how to walk through the world with your feet. They went to the market because the market is where life happens. The market is where you discover whether you are honest under pressure. The market is where you discover whether you give the right weight when the buyer isn't watching. The market is where greed and need crash into each other, and faith has to make decisions in real time.

The messengers were sent into the market because the market is the testing ground of religion.

Look at how Arabic emphasizes this. *Fī-l-aswāq* — *in* the markets, not "near" the markets, not "past" the markets, not "around" the markets. The preposition *fī* puts you *inside* the space. The messengers weren't passing through. They were *in* it. Embedded. Available. Reachable.

And this teaches us something we badly need today. Many of us, when we get serious about religion, want to leave the world. We want to retreat. We want to find a sanctuary where we don't have to deal with people who haggle, who are dishonest, who tempt us, who irritate us, who are loud. We imagine that holiness lives in withdrawal.

This ayah is saying: **your prophets did not believe that.** Your prophets walked into the noise. They bought and sold. They handled coins. They were jostled. They sweated. And that was not a stain on their prophethood. **That was the shape of it.**

There's one more layer here that I have to name, because the Quraysh themselves felt it. The marketplace was where the *common* people lived. The elite of Quraysh — the leaders, the wealthy — they moved in different circles. When they saw the Prophet ﷺ walking in the *sūq*, mixing with the slaves and the merchants and the foreigners and the poor, part of their offense was a class offense. *A messenger, mingling with* those *people?*

And Allah's response is to confirm it. **Yes. With those people. Because there is no class of people My messengers were too good to walk among.**

The marketplace is where the unseen reality of equality before Allah becomes visible. The messenger walks where everyone walks because everyone is being called.

### Element Three: *Wa-jaʿalnā baʿḍakum li-baʿḍin fitnah* — The Pivot

This is where the ayah turns. And I want you to feel the turn.

The first half of the ayah was about messengers. About a complaint and an answer. About prophets walking through markets.

And then, with no transition, no narrative bridge, Allah says:

*"And We have made some of you, for some others, a trial."*

*Wa-jaʿalnā baʿḍakum li-baʿḍin fitnah.*

The construction here is worth slowing down on. *Jaʿalnā* — "We have made," "We have placed," "We have appointed." This is not description. This is **ordainment.** Allah is not saying "it just happens that some of you trial others." He is saying: *I have placed this. This is a feature of the world I built.*

*Baʿḍakum li-baʿḍin* — "some of you for some [others]." The word *baʿḍ* means "a portion," "a part." Notice what Allah didn't say. He didn't say "the rich for the poor." He didn't say "the strong for the weak." He didn't say "the believer for the disbeliever." He left it unspecified. **Some of you. For some others.**

The vagueness is the point. Because the trial is not running in one direction.

The poor man is a trial for the rich man — *will you give? will you see him?* And the rich man is a trial for the poor man — *will you envy? will you trust your provision?* The healthy person is a trial for the sick — *will you visit? will you remember?* And the sick person is a trial for the healthy — *will you accept? will you stop assuming?* The leader is a trial for the led, and the led are a trial for the leader. The parent for the child, the child for the parent.

**Some of you, for some others, is a fitnah.** Every direction.

Now teach the word. *Fitnah*, in classical Arabic, originally referred to **the burning of gold to separate the pure from the impure.** When goldsmiths wanted to know whether a piece of metal was real gold or alloy, they would put it in the fire. The fire didn't *create* the gold's nature. The fire *revealed* it. What was already pure stayed pure. What was already mixed broke down.

That is what Allah is naming about other people in your life. **They are the fire. You are the metal.** They don't make you who you are. They reveal who you already are.

The neighbor who irritates you is not creating your impatience. He is *revealing* your impatience. The boss who is unjust is not creating your bitterness. He is showing you what was already in your heart. The wealthy person who acts proud is not creating your envy. He is exposing the envy that was lying dormant.

[PAUSE]

And now look at what Allah does next. Right after naming the design, He asks a question.

*A-taṣbirūn?*

"Will you have patience?"

The question is sharp. The hamza of interrogation, *a-*, is doing real work — it's not just rhetorical, it's a direct address. Allah is not informing you about other people's purpose in your life. **He is asking you what you are going to do with them.**

And notice what He didn't say. He didn't say *a-taḥkumūn* — "will you judge them?" He didn't say *a-taʿtazilūn* — "will you withdraw from them?" He didn't say *a-tantaqimūn* — "will you take revenge on them?"

He said *a-taṣbirūn.* **Will you be patient?**

Because the *only* legitimate response to fitnah-by-people is *ṣabr* — patient endurance with restraint, with self-mastery, with continued faith. Sabr is not passivity. Sabr is the active refusal to let the fire of another person's behavior turn you into a different metal than you were.

And here is where the linguistic genius of this ayah comes through, because **the question** *a-taṣbirūn?* **is the bridge between the first half and the second half of the ayah.** The Quraysh's complaint about the Prophet ﷺ — that he eats and walks in markets — is itself a fitnah. They are testing him. They are testing each other. The wealthy of Makkah who refused to follow because he wasn't elevated enough were a fitnah for the slaves and weak who *did* follow. And the slaves and weak who followed were a fitnah for the wealthy of Makkah who couldn't bear the social cost.

The very ordinariness that the Quraysh were rejecting — that's the *fitnah.* It was designed to be a fitnah. It was designed to sift.

And the ayah is asking the Prophet ﷺ — and asking us — *will you have patience under it?*

And then it closes.

*Wa-kāna rabbuka baṣīran.*

"And ever is your Lord, All-Seeing."

Two words doing massive work. *Kāna* — the perfect tense, but used here to indicate continuous, established, eternal reality. *Your Lord has always been, and always will be, baṣīr.* Not a passing attention. A permanent gaze.

And *baṣīr* — from the root *b-ṣ-r*, which is sight, but specifically **insight, perceptive seeing, sight that grasps meaning, not just images.** *Baṣīr* sees through. *Baṣīr* sees what's *behind* the action.

So when you are in the fire of another person — when someone has hurt you, when someone has misjudged you, when someone has demanded you justify your existence — and you exercise sabr, *kāna rabbuka baṣīran.* He sees what they cannot see. He sees what *you* cannot fully see. He sees the metal becoming pure under the heat.

The pivot is complete. The ayah has gone from the messengers walking in markets, to a statement about mutual trial, to a closing reassurance about divine sight. And every piece of it is the same teaching said three times.

---

**Bridge:**

The grammar has shown us that the ordinariness of the prophets is not a defect — it is a design. The marketplace has shown us that this ordinariness is *embedded* in the messy, real, worldly arena where faith is actually tested. And the pivot to "some of you are a trial for others" has shown us why — because *people* are the test, and the messenger walks among the people because *being among the people* is the very texture of the test.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The People Who Disappoint You Were Always Part of the Plan

Let me start somewhere that has nothing to do with the Quran for a moment. Stay with me.

Most of us, when we look back at our suffering, divide it into two categories. There's the suffering that came from the world — illness, loss, disasters, accidents — and there's the suffering that came from people. And we treat them very differently.

When suffering comes from the world, we tend to find a way to absorb it into a story about Allah. *It was a test. It was qadar. It was meant to be.* We reach for divine framing because there's nowhere else to put it.

But when suffering comes from people, we don't do that. We obsess. We replay the conversation. We rehearse what we should have said. We catalogue the injustices. We carry the wound for years. Decades, sometimes.

We treat people as a separate, lesser category of trial — almost as if they were a glitch in the system rather than a feature of it.

This ayah closes that gap.

[PAUSE]

*Wa-jaʿalnā baʿḍakum li-baʿḍin fitnah.*

"And We made some of you, for some others, a trial."

*We made.* Allah is putting His own name on the architecture. He is saying: the difficult father, the betraying friend, the misunderstanding spouse, the cruel boss, the family member who couldn't accept you, the community that judged you — **these are not glitches.** These are not the universe failing you. These are not Allah losing track for a moment. These are the test, designed and ordained and placed in your life with precision.

That's the first move.

But there's a second move that's even harder, and we can't avoid it. Because the ayah doesn't say "We have made *some* people a trial *for you*." It says: *We have made some of you a trial for some others.*

You are also somebody else's trial.

You — the one reading, the one listening — your existence in someone else's life is a fire that reveals what they are made of. The way you irritate your sibling, the way you disappoint your parent, the way you fail to live up to what your spouse expected, the way you stress out your colleague — *you are their fitnah.*

And someone right now is exercising sabr because of you. Or failing to.

This ayah refuses to let us occupy only the position of the tested. It plants us also in the position of the test.

Let me give you a parallel from somewhere we already understand. Imagine a teacher who walks into a classroom on the first day of school. The students don't know each other. By the end of the year, every student in that room will have been deepened or diminished by the others. Not by the curriculum. By **each other.** The kind one will have been made kinder by the bullies' presence. The patient one will have learned patience under the loud one. And — here's the harder truth — the bullies will have been made more or less of bullies by what the others permitted.

The classroom is not just a place where students *encounter* each other. The students *are* the curriculum.

That's what this ayah is saying about life.

The people in your life are not the obstacles preventing you from doing the spiritual work. **They are the spiritual work.** The mother-in-law you can't stand — she's not what's keeping you from being a better Muslim. *She is the chapter on patience that you've been assigned.* The colleague who takes credit for your work — he's not what's stopping you from being honest. He is the test of whether your honesty is actually honesty or just the absence of provocation.

And the second move — that you are their trial — is a mercy in disguise. Because once you accept that you are also a fire in someone else's furnace, you start asking a different question. You stop asking "why is this person so difficult?" and you start asking "**what kind of fire am I?**"

Are you a fire that purifies, or one that just burns? Are you a fire that reveals the gold in others, or one that hardens the alloy? Do people become better Muslims after spending time with you, or worse?

You can't avoid being a fitnah for somebody. The ayah just told you you can't. The only choice you have is what *kind* of fitnah you will be.

[PAUSE]

Practical application: tonight, before you sleep, name three people you are a trial for. Not three people who try you — flip it. Three people whose patience, whose forbearance, whose forgiveness you regularly require. Sit with that list. And then ask Allah to make you the kind of fire that purifies them, not the kind that burns them down.

That's a different prayer than most of us pray. Most of us pray for people to stop trying us. This ayah tells us to pray for the people we are trying.

### Theme Two: The Followable Messenger

The Quraysh said: *"What kind of messenger is this — he eats food and walks in markets?"*

And what they meant, underneath the words, was: *He is too much like us to be above us.*

Now I want to flip that. Because the very thing they treated as a defect is the very thing the Quran will teach us, in dozens of places, is the gift.

Allah says elsewhere: *"Say: I am only a man like you, to whom it is revealed..."* (18:110).

He says: *"And the messenger you send before — they were not bodies that did not eat food, nor were they immortal..."* (21:8).

This is a relentless theme in the Quran. Allah keeps insisting on the *bashariyyah* — the **humanness** — of His messengers. And He insists on it precisely against the human reflex to push prophets into a different category of being.

Why does this matter so much? Why does Allah refuse to let the Prophet ﷺ become a quasi-divine figure? Why does He keep planting his feet in the mud of the marketplace?

Because **a messenger you cannot reach is a messenger you cannot follow.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Think about how this works psychologically. If the Prophet ﷺ had been an angel, or had floated above hunger, or had never had to worry about money, or had never grieved, or had never been tempted, or had never been mocked — and someone said to you, *"be like him,"* what would you say?

You would say: *I can't.* And you'd mean it, and you'd be right. You can't be like a being who lives in a different category from you. The gap is unbridgeable.

But the Quran's prophet ate when he was hungry, sometimes wrapped a stone against his belly when there was no food. Walked when he was tired. Slept when he was exhausted. Bled when cut. Wept when his son Ibrāhīm died — and when his companions asked him about the tears, he said: *"The eyes weep and the heart grieves, but we say only what pleases our Lord."*

He grieved. He just grieved while submitting. **That's followable.**

He got hungry. He just got hungry while remaining grateful. **That's followable.**

He was wronged. He just was wronged while restraining anger. **That's followable.**

A perfect being who never had any of these experiences would be admirable. But not followable. **A man who had every one of them and submitted through them all — that is the only kind of human a human can imitate.**

This is why the Quraysh's complaint is so revealing. They wanted a messenger they couldn't follow. Because if he was unfollowable, his message couldn't be binding on them. *He's a different kind of being. The rules don't apply to me.*

The marketplace strips that excuse away. The eating, the walking, the buying and selling — all of it is saying: *you have no excuse. He went through what you go through. He answered the call from inside the same conditions you face. So can you.*

There's a deeper layer here. When we romanticize the Prophet ﷺ — when we imagine him as a man without struggle, without temptation, without difficulty — we're not honoring him. We're **letting ourselves off the hook.** Because the more inhuman we make him, the less his life can demand of us.

But the Quran refuses. It keeps planting him in the marketplace. It keeps making him eat. It keeps reminding us that he had to bury his children, that he had to flee his hometown, that he had to bleed in battle, that he had to mourn his wife, that he had to feel the fear and the loneliness and the doubt that any of us would feel.

And then — *and then* — it asks: *will you have patience?*

Because if he had it, you can have it.

Practical application: stop reaching for the Prophet ﷺ when you want to admire someone. Reach for him when you want to know what to do. The next time you are angry and the desire to lash out is rising in your chest, don't ask "what would an angel do?" Ask: *what did the man in the marketplace do, when his teeth were broken, when his back was bloodied at Ṭāʾif, when his uncle died on disbelief, when his beloved wife was buried?* He did the next breath. He did sabr. He kept going to the market.

You can do the next breath too.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Three pieces of language. Two themes. They were never separate.

The grammatical sweep — *mā arsalnā... illā* — established that every messenger ate and walked in markets. The marketplace itself revealed *why* — because faith is not a withdrawal from the world but a presence within it. And the pivot — *wa-jaʿalnā baʿḍakum li-baʿḍin fitnah* — revealed the deepest reason of all: because **people are the test**, and the messenger walked among the people because *that is where the testing happens.*

The messenger eating food and walking in markets is not an embarrassment Allah is defending. It is the very instrument of the trial. **The Prophet ﷺ was the Quraysh's fitnah, and they were his.** His ordinariness was the heat that revealed which of them was gold and which was alloy. The slaves and the weak who saw him eating and walking and *recognized* him as the messenger — they had submitted to the test. The elite of Quraysh who saw him eating and walking and used it as their excuse — they had failed it.

And the ayah closes — *kāna rabbuka baṣīran.* The Lord sees. He saw who was truly testing whom. He saw the gold form under the heat. He still does.

### Questions to Carry

1. Who in your life have you been treating as an obstacle to your faith, when in fact they were always *the chapter you were assigned*?

2. If you were honest, **what kind of fire are you** in the lives of the people closest to you — purifying or just burning?

3. The Quraysh wanted a messenger who was *above* them. Are there ways you keep wanting your faith to be *above* your daily life — set apart from the marketplace, separated from the noise — instead of *embedded in it*?

4. When the Prophet ﷺ was asked the impossible question — "why are you not different from us?" — Allah's answer was to lean into the sameness, not away from it. What in your own life are you trying to elevate yourself above, when the path forward is to walk *into* it?

5. *A-taṣbirūn?* Allah's question to the Prophet ﷺ is also His question to you. Right now, in the specific person who is currently testing you — **will you have patience?**

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah sent His messengers to walk in the marketplaces because the marketplace — with all its noise and friction and difficult people — is not what stands between you and faith; **it is where faith happens**, and the people who try you are not glitches in the path but the path itself.

### The Closing Invitation

Don't try to escape the marketplace.

Don't ask Allah to remove the difficult people from your life. Ask Him instead to make you patient under them, useful to them, and — when it is your turn to be the fire — *gentle* in your burning.

Reach for the Prophet ﷺ not as someone who lived above the world, but as someone who walked through it on the same legs you walk on, hungry the way you get hungry, grieving the way you grieve, and — *and* — submitting through every single moment of it.

And when the next person disappoints you, remember: this was always part of the design. The fire is not an interruption of the gold's life. The fire is what reveals it as gold.

### A Closing Du'ā

*O Allah, You have placed us as trials for one another. Make us fires that purify, not flames that destroy. Give us the sabr of Your messenger, who walked the markets and ate the food and bore the wounds, and never once stopped pointing toward You. And You — You who have always been All-Seeing — see in us what we cannot yet see, and bring it forth.*

*Āmīn.*
