# The Town They Kept Passing — and the Diagnosis Beneath Their Eyes

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 40

**Arabic:**
وَلَقَدْ أَتَوْا۟ عَلَى ٱلْقَرْيَةِ ٱلَّتِىٓ أُمْطِرَتْ مَطَرَ ٱلسَّوْءِ ۚ أَفَلَمْ يَكُونُوا۟ يَرَوْنَهَا ۚ بَلْ كَانُوا۟ لَا يَرْجُونَ نُشُورًا

**Transliteration:** *Wa laqad ataw ʿalā-l-qaryati-llatī umṭirat maṭara-s-saw'. A-falam yakūnū yarawnahā. Bal kānū lā yarjūna nushūrā.*

**Translation:** "And they have indeed come upon the town that was rained on with the rain of evil. Have they not been seeing it? No — they have been not hoping for any resurrection."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Behind the Ayah

To feel what this ayah is doing, you have to stand for a moment on a Meccan trade road.

The Quraysh were merchants. Twice a year their caravans moved — one to Yemen in the south, one to Syria in the north. The northern route was the lifeline. Camels, goods, profit, status — all of it flowed up that road. And that road, the road every Meccan merchant knew, passed near the southern shore of the Dead Sea.

Which is to say: it passed the ruins of the people of Lūt.

The town that had been overturned. The town that had been pelted with stones from the sky. A region of strange, ash-grey desolation that travellers commented on for centuries afterward. It was not folklore to them. It was geography. It was on the map. It was what you saw out of the corner of your eye while you watered the camels.

[PAUSE]

The same merchants — the very men of Quraysh who were now mocking the Prophet ﷺ in Mecca, who were laughing at the warnings of resurrection, who were demanding miracles before they would believe — these were the men who had personally passed those ruins. Not once. Year after year. Caravan after caravan. They had *seen* what happens when a people are warned and refuse the warning.

And it had changed nothing.

This is the moment Allah is addressing. This ayah is not a story being told *about* a faraway people. It is a finger pointed at men who had walked past the evidence with their own feet.

### The Hook

Here is what is going to ache, if we let it.

The Quran could have asked, *"Why didn't they believe?"*

It could have asked, *"What more proof did they want?"*

It could have asked, *"Are their hearts made of stone?"*

It does not ask any of these. It asks something stranger. It asks, *"Have they not been seeing it?"* — and then, before we can answer, it answers for us. And the answer it gives is not what we expect. The answer is not *"Their eyes were closed."* The answer is not *"The evidence wasn't strong enough."* The answer goes somewhere else entirely. Somewhere deeper. Somewhere that turns the question back on us.

By the time we reach the last word of this ayah, we will understand why a person can drive past a graveyard every morning of their life and still not be ready to die.

[PAUSE]

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Umṭirat Maṭara-s-Saw'* — "Was Rained on with the Rain of Evil"

#### Teaching the Concept First

Before we open this phrase, hold something in mind.

In any language, there are two kinds of words for the same thing. Think about the difference between *gift* and *bribe*. Think about the difference between *crowd* and *mob*. The objects in the world are similar. The words are not. One word carries warmth, one word carries menace. The choice of word tells you what the speaker thinks of what they are describing.

Arabic does this with extraordinary precision. There are multiple words for rain. The most common in everyday classical Arabic is *ghayth* — the merciful rain that revives the earth, the rain that ends drought, the rain that comes as relief. *Ghayth* shares its root with *istighātha* — to call out for help. Rain as the answered cry.

But there is another word. *Maṭar.*

If you study where *maṭar* shows up in the Quran, you will notice something. Almost every time the verb form *amṭara* — "to cause to rain" — is used in the Quran, it is used for **destruction**. The sky raining stones, sulphur, calamity. The classical commentators noticed this centuries ago. *Maṭar* in the Quran is the rain that does not nourish. It is the rain that buries.

Now look at the ayah.

#### Opening the Word

Allah says: ٱلَّتِىٓ **أُمْطِرَتْ** مَطَرَ ٱلسَّوْءِ — "the town which **was rained on** with the rain of evil."

The verb *umṭirat* is passive. *It was rained on.* No active subject is named. The sky simply opened on it. And the rain it received was not *ghayth*. It was *maṭar*. And not just any *maṭar* — *maṭar al-saw'*, the rain of *al-saw'*, the rain of evil, calamity, ruin.

Notice what is happening to the most ordinary mercy in the desert.

For a desert people, rain is salvation. It is what you pray for. It is what your livestock depend on. It is the meaning of *ghayth*. To be from a parched land is to know that nothing falls from the sky except blessing.

And Allah, in choosing this word, is showing us something terrifying. **The very category that should mean blessing — sky, water from above, the gift descending — has been inverted.** What used to be the answered prayer has become the instrument of execution. The same direction your hands lift in du'a is the direction the stones came from.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that for a moment. The geometry of mercy and the geometry of punishment can be the same geometry. What changes is not where it comes from. What changes is what is meeting it from below.

#### Deepening

There is something else hidden in this construction. *Umṭirat* — *was rained on* — is built so that the town is the receiver, not an actor. In Arabic this passive form often carries the sense of "having something done *to* you that you did not invite and cannot resist." The town is grammatically helpless. The sky acts. The town receives.

And then *maṭara-s-saw'*. The phrase is what Arabic grammarians call an internal object — using the verbal noun of the same verb as its own object. *Was rained the raining of evil.* This construction in Arabic intensifies. It does not just describe an event; it presses on it. It is the difference between saying "they ate" and "they ate an eating that no one had ever eaten before." The phrase wraps the event in its own emphasis.

So Allah is saying: it was not a light shower. It was not a glancing event. It was *the raining* — the kind of raining that defines what raining can become when mercy is withdrawn.

And then — here is the most striking lexical choice — *al-saw'*. From the root *s-w-ʾ*, which carries everything ugly: harm, evil, what one would recoil from. Lexicographers note that *al-saw'* is what makes a person turn their face away. It is not just *bad*; it is *unbearable to look at*.

#### What This Single Phrase Reveals

In four words — *umṭirat maṭara-s-saw'* — Allah has shown us the architecture of how blessing becomes its opposite. The same sky. The same rain. The same downward motion. But what arrives is not what arrives in answered du'a. It is what arrives when du'a was never made.

[PAUSE]

This phrase is not decorative. It is doing real work. It is preparing us for the question that follows. Because if rain itself can be inverted, if the most ordinary mercy can become the most precise punishment, then **the world is not safe in the way the disbelievers thought it was safe.** And the ruins of that town are not a one-time historical event. They are a demonstration of what is always available, latent in the sky, waiting on what is happening on the ground.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *A-falam Yakūnū Yarawnahā* — "Have They Not Been Seeing It?"

#### Teaching the Concept First

In English, we have one past tense for most things. *I saw it. I have seen it. I used to see it.* These are different but they collapse easily in conversation. We don't always feel the difference.

Arabic does not collapse them. Arabic has a structure called *kāna + the imperfect verb* — *kāna yafʿalu* — and it means something specific. It does not mean *"he did it once."* It means *"he was in the habit of doing it,"* or *"he kept doing it,"* or *"he was, at that time, in the continuous state of doing it."* It is the difference between *"I went to the gym"* and *"I used to go to the gym every morning."*

The first is a fact. The second is a pattern.

#### Opening the Phrase

Now look at what Allah says: أَفَلَمْ **يَكُونُوا۟ يَرَوْنَهَا** — "Have they not **been seeing it**?"

He does not say *a-falam yarawhā* — "did they not see it." That would be a single act of seeing. He says *yakūnū yarawnahā* — "were they not in the habit of seeing it." Have they not been continuously, repeatedly, on every caravan, with every journey, **seeing it**?

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that does.

This is not addressing someone who looked once and missed it. This is not addressing someone who was never given the evidence. This is addressing a people for whom the evidence was *part of the landscape*. It was not even something they had to seek out. It was something they could not have avoided seeing if they tried. It was on the route. It was on the way home. It was what their fathers had pointed to and what they would point out to their sons.

And it had become invisible to them precisely because it was so visible.

#### Deepening

Notice something else. The verb *yarawnahā* — "they see it" — is in the imperfect, the form Arabic uses for ongoing or unfolding action. So the structure is *they were continuing to be seeing it.* Continuous on top of continuous. Allah has stacked two layers of "still happening" onto a verb of perception.

Why?

Because the question He is asking is not, *"Did your eyes register the photons?"* The question He is asking is, *"Was there ever a single moment, in all those years of repeated witnessing, where seeing became something more than seeing?"*

There is a kind of looking that does nothing. The eye intakes the image. The image is filed. The brain catalogues it. *Ruined town. Sulphur smell. Strange grey rocks. Move on, the camels need water.*

And there is another kind of looking, where what enters the eye does not stay in the eye but travels somewhere — somewhere it begins to *cost* you something. Somewhere it begins to ask of you.

The first kind is *raʾā* in its most surface sense. The second is what *raʾā* becomes when the heart is open.

The Quran does not say their eyes failed. The Quran says their seeing failed to become anything more than seeing.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most haunting realizations in the entire Book. **The presence of evidence is not the same as the perception of evidence. And the perception of evidence is not the same as being changed by it.** A person can stand inside the proof and still walk past it.

#### The Question Form Itself

There is one more layer. *A-falam yakūnū yarawnahā* — the *a-* at the front is the interrogative particle. Allah is *asking* something. But who is He asking?

Grammatically, He is asking a kind of rhetorical question to the Prophet ﷺ and to us as listeners. *Have they not been seeing it?* The expected answer is: *Yes, of course they have been.* The question is not seeking information. The question is forcing us to confront the gap between what someone saw and what someone did with what they saw.

But the question also turns. Because we, the listeners, are now invited to ask it of ourselves. *And what about us? What have we been seeing — repeatedly, daily, our whole lives — and how much of that seeing has actually moved anything inside?*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Bal Kānū Lā Yarjūna Nushūrā* — "No — They Have Been Not Hoping for Any Resurrection"

This is the heart of the ayah. This is where the whole construction has been building. Take a breath before we open it.

#### Teaching the Concept First

In English, when someone fails to be moved by something they should be moved by, we tend to diagnose it in one of two ways. We say *they didn't believe it* — a *belief* problem. Or we say *they didn't fear the consequences* — a *fear* problem. Belief and fear are our two main vocabularies for this kind of failure.

Arabic and the Quran have a third word, and it is the word that lands here. *Rajā'.*

*Rajā'* is one of the most important words in the spiritual vocabulary of the Quran. It is usually translated as *hope*, but that translation does it a disservice. *Rajā'* is more precisely *active expectation of something that has not yet arrived but that you orient your life toward*. It is the disposition of someone who lives as if a future event is real and is on its way. The merchant who *expects* the caravan to return prepares for it. The farmer who *expects* the harvest plants for it. The traveller who *expects* the destination provisions for it. *Rajā'* is what makes you behave today as if tomorrow is a fact.

The opposite of *rajā'* is not despair, exactly. The opposite of *rajā'* is **functioning as if the future event will not happen.** Not denying it intellectually, necessarily. Just not building any of your life around it.

#### Opening the Phrase

Now look at what Allah says.

After asking why they did not see, He pivots. *Bal* — "No, rather." This is the Arabic word that overrides what came before. It is the word of correction, of redirection. *No — that is not actually the right framing. Here is the real framing.*

And then: كَانُوا۟ لَا يَرْجُونَ نُشُورًا — "they have been not hoping for resurrection."

[PAUSE]

Notice the structure. Allah does not say *they did not believe in resurrection.* He could have said that. He has the word *yu'minūn*. He does not use it.

He does not say *they did not fear resurrection.* He has the word *yakhāfūn*. He does not use it.

He says they did not *hope for* it. They did not *anticipate* it. They were not *oriented toward* it. *Lā yarjūna nushūrā.*

#### Deepening

Why is this the diagnosis?

Because **belief alone does not change behavior.** A person can intellectually believe in the resurrection and still live exactly as they would live if they did not. Belief, as a cognitive content, is cheap. The Quran knows this. It does not say their problem was that they had the wrong content in their heads. It says their problem was that **they had no functional orientation toward what was coming.**

*Rajā'* is the orientation. *Rajā'* is what converts a fact about the future into a force that shapes the present.

And without *rajā'*, evidence cannot land.

This is the connection. This is why the question and the diagnosis are placed back to back. *Have they not been seeing it? — No, they have been not anticipating resurrection.*

[PAUSE]

Read those two clauses together and feel what the ayah is doing.

The ayah is saying: **the reason their seeing did not become perception is that they had no expectation of being held to account.** Without that expectation, the ruins of Lūt's people are not a sermon. They are a tourist site. They are a geological curiosity. They are a story to tell at the campfire and laugh about. They cannot pierce, because there is nothing inside the heart that is reaching forward toward an arrival. Nothing waiting. Nothing oriented.

The ruins do not become a warning to a person who is not expecting anything to be warned about.

#### The Word *Nushūr*

And then the final word. *Nushūrā.*

*Nushūr* comes from the root *n-sh-r*, which has a beautiful and very physical primary meaning. *Nashara* means *to spread out, to unfold, to unroll.* You *spread out* a folded cloth. You *unfurl* a banner. You *open out* something that was bundled up.

So *nushūr*, used here for the resurrection, is not just a synonym for *baʿth* (being raised) or *qiyāma* (standing). *Nushūr* is the resurrection as **the unfolding of what was folded away.** You — your full reality, your full record, your full self — are right now folded up like a scroll. *Nushūr* is the day Allah unfolds you. Lays you out flat. Spreads you open.

[PAUSE]

That image is doing immense work. Because notice — *if you have folded up something inside yourself, hidden things between the folds, sins tucked in the creases, a self you keep collapsed so that even you do not see all of it* — *nushūr* is the day all of that is laid out flat in front of the One who needs no unfolding to know.

The disbelievers were not *hoping for* — not actively oriented toward — *the day they would be unfolded*. And so they walked past the ruins without flinching. Because the ruins are only frightening if you know your folds are about to be opened.

#### What This Final Phrase Reveals

The ayah has now completed its movement. It began with a physical fact (a town was rained upon). It moved to a question of perception (have they not seen). It ended with a diagnosis of orientation (they were not anticipating their unfolding).

And the architecture is doing this: it is telling us that **the failure of perception is downstream of the failure of orientation.** You cannot fix what they saw by giving them more to see. You can only fix what they saw by changing what they were oriented toward.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge to the Themes

Three linguistic facts, side by side now:

— A blessing-shaped sky can deliver a punishment-shaped rain.
— A people can spend a lifetime *seeing* something and never *perceive* it.
— And the missing piece is not their eyes. The missing piece is the absence of *rajā'* — the absence of an active orientation toward the day they will be unfolded.

These are not three separate observations. They are one observation seen from three angles. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we live, right now, with our own eyes open and the same evidence around us.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Distance Between Seeing and Being Changed

#### Where We Already Live

Let me describe a Tuesday morning.

You wake up. You scroll. Within ninety seconds, you have seen: a video of a car accident on a highway you drive. A photo of a friend's parent who has died. A headline about a cancer diagnosis at the age you currently are. A clip of a war you cannot do anything about. A reminder that someone you went to school with is gone.

Then you put the phone down, get up, get dressed, drink coffee, and go to work as if none of that just happened.

[PAUSE]

I want you to feel that gap, because that gap is the gap of this ayah.

The Quraysh did not lack data. We do not lack data. Our problem is not the same as a man living in a cave with no information. Our problem is the inverse. We are submerged in information about mortality, accountability, and the precariousness of every life — submerged to a depth no human society has ever experienced. And we walk through it the way the Quraysh walked through Sodom: noting it, perhaps for a moment registering it, and then continuing as if it has nothing to do with us.

This is what *kānū yarawnahā* names. The continuous, repeated, daily seeing. We have been seeing. And our seeing has not become perception.

#### Letting the Ayah Illuminate

Here is what is so painful in the ayah's framing: it does not blame the evidence. It does not say the ruins were too subtle, or too far, or too ambiguous. The ruins were enormous, geographic, in their face. The evidence was *as undeniable as evidence can be*.

This means we cannot save ourselves by waiting for *more* evidence. There is no amount of evidence that will, on its own, convert into perception. Evidence does not metabolize itself. Something inside the witness has to do the metabolizing.

And the ayah is telling us what that something is.

It is *rajā'*.

It is the active expectation of being held. Without it, every funeral you attend is somebody else's funeral. Every diagnosis you read about happens to other people. Every piece of evidence about how this world treats its inhabitants gets filed under *interesting* and *sad* and never under *this is happening to me*.

#### Deepening

There is a step further here, and we have to take it.

When a person's seeing fails to become perception over a long period of time, something hardens. The ayah, by using the continuous past *kānū yarawnahā*, is showing us a process — not a moment. It is naming the slow petrification of a heart that has been exposed to evidence again and again and has trained itself, over time, to remain unmoved.

This is more dangerous than initial denial. Initial denial can soften. Trained unmovedness becomes a structure.

The Quraysh did not start out with stones for hearts. They acquired them. Every caravan that passed those ruins without being shaken added a layer. The first time, perhaps something stirred. The fifth time, less. The fiftieth time, nothing. By the time the Prophet ﷺ stood among them and recited this ayah, the ruins of Sodom had become invisible to them in the only way that matters — invisible *to the heart*, even as they remained painfully visible to the eyes.

[PAUSE]

This is the warning, then, embedded in the form of the verb. **What you keep seeing without being changed by, you will eventually become incapable of seeing.** Not because the thing has changed. Because you have.

#### The Practical Application

So what does this ayah ask us to do, tomorrow morning, when we pick up the phone?

It asks us to slow the seeing.

When you see news of a death, do not let the eye consume it and move on. Stop. Speak to your soul: *that was a person whose unfolding has now begun. That will be me. What did I do today that I would want unfolded?* Make the seeing land. Make it cost something — even just five seconds of stilled attention.

This is not morbid. This is the only known antidote to the slow petrification the ayah is warning us about. The Quraysh's tragedy was not that they walked past the ruins. The Quraysh's tragedy was that they walked past the ruins *without pausing*. The pause is the entire act. The pause is the seed of *rajā'.*

### Theme Two: *Rajā'* as the Lens That Makes Evidence Mean

#### Where We Already Live

There is a strange feature of human life that almost everyone notices and almost no one explains.

Two people can read the same news, watch the same events, see the same evidence, and walk away with completely different lives. Not different *opinions* — different *lives*. One is shaken. The other is amused. One restructures. The other scrolls on.

We usually explain this in terms of personality, or upbringing, or sensitivity. The Quran explains it differently. The Quran explains it in terms of what each person was *oriented toward* before the evidence arrived.

The orientation determines what evidence is.

[PAUSE]

Think of it this way. To a man waiting for a letter, every footstep on the porch is the postman. To a man not waiting for anything, no footstep means anything. The footsteps are the same. The waiting is different.

*Rajā'* is the waiting. *Nushūr* is the letter that is coming. The world is the porch.

And to a person with no *rajā'*, the porch is silent. Not because nothing is happening on it. Because they are not listening for anything in particular.

#### Letting the Ayah Illuminate

This is why Allah's diagnosis in the ayah is so precise and so devastating.

He could have said the Quraysh's problem was disbelief in His existence. He does not. They believed in Allah's existence — they swore by Him constantly. He could have said their problem was love of dunyā. He does not, here. He could have named pride, or stubbornness, or rejection of the Prophet ﷺ.

He names something more upstream than any of those. He names the missing forward-orientation. The missing waiting. The missing expectation that something is coming for which all of *this* is preparation.

Because if you are not waiting for *nushūr*, then nothing in the visible world is preparation for anything. It is all just what is. The ruins of Sodom are not a preview. They are a memory of something that happened to other people.

But the moment a person begins to *expect* — actively, in their bones, with the kind of expectation that makes them prepare — *the moment the unfolding becomes real to them in the way the next paycheck is real* — every single piece of evidence in the world reorganizes itself. The ruins of Sodom stop being history and start being a sermon. The funeral of a stranger stops being other-people's-news and starts being a rehearsal. The illness of a friend stops being a tragedy-elsewhere and starts being a postcard from your own future.

The world has not changed. Your *rajā'* has changed. And so the world has changed everything it means.

#### Deepening Through the Paradox

There is a paradox here that the ayah lets sit without resolving, and we should let it sit too.

The paradox is this: the disbelievers' problem is that they do not *hope* for resurrection. But resurrection is the day of judgment. Resurrection is the day of accounting. Resurrection is what they should *fear*. Why does Allah call its absence a failure of *hope*?

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators wrestled with this. Some, like Ibn Kathīr drawing on earlier authorities, noted that *rajā'* in Arabic can carry the sense of *expectation* whether what is expected is good or feared. Others noted something more subtle: that *rajā'* is the right word here precisely because for someone who is preparing, the day of resurrection is *also* the day they have been hoping for. It is the day everything they did in secret will finally be seen by the One whose seeing they wanted. It is the day the unfair will be made fair. It is the day the unseen will be unfolded.

A person of faith does not only fear the resurrection. They also, in some real way, *long for it*. They long for the day the truth is told.

A person without faith does neither. They neither fear it nor long for it. They are simply not oriented toward it. It is not part of their inner geography.

And so Allah uses the word that captures the *full orientation* — not just fear, not just hope, but the disposition of a soul that is leaning forward into the future. *Rajā'.*

This is what the disbelievers lacked. Not a belief. A leaning.

#### Practical Application

What does this mean, practically, for us?

It means our work is not primarily evidential. It is orientational.

When we feel ourselves becoming numb to the warnings — and we will, because we are human — the response is not to seek more dramatic evidence. The response is to sit with *rajā'*. To rebuild the leaning. To let our inner posture shift from "this world is what is" to "this world is preparation for what is coming."

Practically, this can be very small. It can be one minute, before bed, where you sit and ask: *what if the unfolding happens tonight? What of today would I want laid out flat?* That minute, repeated, builds *rajā'*. *Rajā'* is not a feeling that descends on you. It is a practice that you build by orienting your attention, again and again, toward what is real and what is coming.

And once you have it — even a little of it — the ruins of Sodom start to speak. The funeral of a stranger starts to teach. The illness, the loss, the fear — all of it starts to do the work it was always trying to do, but could not, because no one inside was leaning forward to receive it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we have walked through it, look at how the ayah is built.

It opens with **a physical fact**: they came upon the town.
It moves to **a sensory fact**: it was rained on with the rain of evil.
It poses **a question of perception**: have they not been seeing it?
It delivers **a diagnosis of orientation**: they have been not hoping for resurrection.

Four steps. Each one moves inward. From the body, to the senses, to the mind, to the core. And the core — the deepest layer the ayah reaches — is not belief, not knowledge, not fear. It is *rajā'*. The forward-leaning of the soul.

Allah has written the ayah so that the diagnosis is the destination. Everything else is the road in.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

Linguistically, we found that *umṭirat maṭara-s-saw'* shows the inversion of mercy into punishment — the same sky doing both. We found that *kānū yarawnahā* shows the long, repeated, normalized seeing that becomes its own kind of blindness. We found that *bal kānū lā yarjūna nushūrā* names the absence of forward-orientation as the deepest cause.

Thematically, we found that the gap between seeing and being changed is not an evidence problem but a meaning-making problem. We found that *rajā'* — the active expectation of being unfolded — is the lens that makes evidence mean.

These two paths arrive at the same place. **The world is full of warnings, but warnings only land in a heart that is leaning forward toward the day it will be opened.**

### Questions to Carry

— What evidence have I been *seeing* my whole life — repeatedly, daily — that I have somehow trained myself not to *perceive*?

— When was the last time a piece of news about another person's mortality made me change a single thing about my own day?

— Am I building *rajā'* — actively orienting my soul toward the day of unfolding — or am I waiting for evidence to do that work for me, evidence that will not, on its own, ever come?

— What inside me is folded — tucked between the creases — that I have not let myself look at, knowing it will be unfolded soon enough whether I look or not?

— If the ruins of Sodom did not move the Quraysh, what would have? And what is the analog in my own life — the warning I keep walking past?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Evidence does not change a soul that is not leaning forward toward the day of being unfolded — and the deepest cause of unmovedness is not blindness, but the absence of *rajā'*.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking us to seek more evidence. We have enough. It is asking us to do something more interior and more demanding. It is asking us to lean forward.

Build the leaning. Build *rajā'*. Build the inner expectation that what is folded will be unfolded, that what is hidden will be laid flat, that what is being seen now will, one day, be all that ever mattered about this short walk through a world full of ruins we keep passing without stopping.

Stop, even once today, at one of those ruins. Let it speak.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who unfold what is folded and lay flat what is hidden — do not let our seeing remain only seeing. Plant in us the *rajā'* that makes the ruins speak, the warnings land, and the days we have left become preparation for the day You spread us open. Soften what has hardened in us from too much watching without being moved, and let our hearts begin again to lean forward toward You.

Āmīn.
