# The Hands That Took, The Friend Unnamed, and the One Who Always Abandons

## Surah Al-Furqan 25:27–29

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Arabic

> وَيَوْمَ يَعَضُّ ٱلظَّالِمُ عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ يَقُولُ يَـٰلَيْتَنِى ٱتَّخَذْتُ مَعَ ٱلرَّسُولِ سَبِيلًۭا
> يَـٰوَيْلَتَىٰ لَيْتَنِى لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ فُلَانًا خَلِيلًۭا
> لَّقَدْ أَضَلَّنِى عَنِ ٱلذِّكْرِ بَعْدَ إِذْ جَآءَنِى ۗ وَكَانَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ لِلْإِنسَـٰنِ خَذُولًۭا

### The Translation

> "And [mention] the Day the wrongdoer will bite on his two hands, saying:
> 'Oh, would that I had taken — with the Messenger — a way.
> Oh, woe to me! Would that I had not taken so-and-so as an intimate friend.
> He led me away from the Reminder after it had come to me.
> And Shaytan is, to the human being, ever a great deserter.'"

### Building the World

We are in *Surah Al-Furqan*. The very name of this surah is **"The Criterion"** — the line that separates truth from falsehood, light from darkness, the path that leads home from the path that leads off the cliff.

The surah opens by establishing the Quran itself as that criterion. And then — as the surah unfolds — it does something remarkable. It does not argue with the disbelievers in abstract terms. It shows you, in scene after scene, what their objections will *look like* on the Day they are answered.

The surah is almost cinematic. It cuts from one tableau to another. The angels descend. The wrongdoers cry out. The pious of the Most Merciful walk gently on the earth. Each scene is a frame from a film whose ending the surah wants you to see *now*, while you still have time to step out of the frame.

[PAUSE]

The original listener in Mecca knew the texture of this drama intimately. The Prophet ﷺ would call individuals to Islam — and immediately, the call would meet a counter-call. Friends, fathers, uncles, peers would pull on the sleeve. *"Don't listen to him. Stay with us. Remember who you are. Don't break with the family."* Each Companion who eventually entered Islam had to make a single, devastating choice: **whose voice will I let into the inner chamber of my heart?**

That is the world this passage is speaking into. A world where two voices were calling — and the listener had to choose which one to follow home.

### The Hook

I want to point at three things — and then we will leave them sitting there until we have earned the right to look at them properly.

**One:** Allah says the wrongdoer will bite his **hands**. Not his lips. Not his tongue. His hands. *Both* of them.

**Two:** Allah names the Messenger. Allah names the Reminder. Allah names Shaytan. But the friend who destroyed this person's life — Allah refuses to name him. He just says **"so-and-so."** A blank slot. As if waiting for something to be filled in.

**Three:** Allah closes with a single word for what Shaytan *is*. Not what he *does*. What he **is**. And that word, in its very grammatical shape, is a warning that should have been visible long before the Day came.

Let's see what is here.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Hands That Took

Let me start with a piece of grammar so small you might walk past it.

In Arabic, when you talk about a body part you have one of, you use the singular. When you talk about a body part you have two of, you can use the dual — a special form of the noun that means *exactly two*. English has nothing like it; we just say "two hands." Arabic has a built-in suffix — **-ayn** — that says: *not one, not many, exactly the pair*.

So when Allah says the wrongdoer will bite **عَلَىٰ يَدَيْهِ** — "on his hands" — He is using the dual. **Both hands.** The pair. Together.

Hold that.

Now look at what verb is used: **يَعَضُّ** — *yaʿaḍḍu* — "he bites." Listen to the sound of that word. Feel the **ḍād** in your mouth — the heavy, pressed-down letter that Arabic grammarians say cannot be replicated cleanly in any other language. It is a sound that *clamps*. The very phonetics of the verb mimic teeth pressing into flesh.

Why bite the hands?

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Look at the next ayah. The wrongdoer says: **اتَّخَذْتُ** — "I took." And then again: **لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ** — "I did not take." His entire confession is a confession about *taking*. About what he reached out and grasped, and what he failed to grasp.

What is the body part that takes?

The hand.

So when the regret comes, when the bill arrives, the wrongdoer turns on the very instrument that performed the choice. He bites the hands that took. The hands that reached out and pulled the wrong companion close. The hands that pushed away the Messenger's path.

This is something the Quran does again and again — and you have to slow down to see it. **The body part that performed the sin becomes the body part that bears the consequence.** The eye that looked, the tongue that spoke, the hand that took — on the Day of Reckoning, each of these will speak. Each will testify. And in this scene, the hands are not even waiting for the courtroom. The wrongdoer turns on them himself.

And notice — *both* hands. Not the right one alone, not the left one alone. The dual form, **يَدَيْهِ**, says: *the entire pair*. Because the choice was made with the entire self. There was no half of him that resisted. There was no innocent hand. He took with both, and so he bites both.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari describes what the biting expresses: the wrongdoer bites **ندما وأسفًا** — out of regret and grief. Two words, close in meaning and yet distinct. *Nadam* is the regret of looking backward at what you did. *Asaf* is a deeper, more piercing grief — a sorrow that comes from realizing the scale of what has been lost. So the bite is not simply anger at oneself. It is something more layered: the biting of regret, and underneath that the biting of grief, and underneath both of them, the biting of a self that finally cannot escape what it chose.

Let's go one layer deeper, because there is something here that should make us uncomfortable.

The verb form. The wrongdoer doesn't just say *"akhadhtu"* — "I took." He uses **اتَّخَذْتُ** — *ittakhadhtu*. This is **Form VIII** of the root أ-خ-ذ. In Arabic morphology, Form VIII typically carries the meaning of doing something *for oneself*, with deliberation and effort, taking it as one's own.

It is the difference, in English, between "I took a pen" and "I adopted a pen as mine."

So when the wrongdoer regrets the friend he took — he is not regretting an accident. He is not regretting that a bad person bumped into him on the street. He is regretting **a choice he made deliberately, with effort, taking the friend as his own**.

And when he wishes he had taken the Messenger's path — same form. **اتَّخَذْتُ مَعَ الرَّسُولِ سَبِيلًا**. He is wishing he had *deliberately, with effort, made the Messenger's path his own*. Al-Tabari glosses that path as **طريقا إلى النجاة** — a road toward salvation, toward rescue from the punishment. Not a road to reward alone, not a road to comfort, but a road to *rescue* — النجاة carries the urgency of escape, of being pulled from something that would have consumed you.

The Day of Judgment is not the day someone surprises you with charges you never saw coming. It is the day the deliberate, considered, freely-chosen nature of every choice you made becomes utterly impossible to deny.

That is what the hands are bitten *for*. They are not bitten because of something that happened to him. They are bitten because of something he **did**.

> **What this single image reveals:** The Day will not bring the discovery of new sins. It will bring the discovery — beyond all evasion — of what your sins always were: deliberate acts performed by a self that knew it was choosing, on a road it could still have exchanged for the road to salvation.

### Element Two: The Blank Slot Called "So-and-So"

Now look at the second ayah. The wrongdoer wails:

> **يَـٰوَيْلَتَىٰ لَيْتَنِى لَمْ أَتَّخِذْ فُلَانًا خَلِيلًۭا**
> *"Oh, woe to me! Would that I had not taken **so-and-so** as an intimate friend!"*

I want to teach you the word **فُلَان** — *fulān* — before we look at what Allah is doing with it.

In Arabic, *fulān* is the placeholder word. It is what Arabs say when they want to refer to a person without naming him. In English we say "so-and-so" or "what's-his-name" or, in legal writing, "John Doe." It is the **blank slot**. It is the word you use when the *name does not matter* — or when the name needs to be *withheld*.

Now think about everything else Allah names in this passage.

He names **the Messenger** — *al-Rasūl*.
He names **the Reminder** — *al-Dhikr*.
He names **Shaytan**.
He names **the human being** — *al-insān*.

But the friend who destroyed this man? **Allah uses** ***fulān***.

Why?

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators were not silent about the history behind this blank. Al-Tabari records a genuine *ikhtilāf* — a disagreement among the early exegetes — about who the wrongdoer and the friend actually are. One prominent view, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, identifies the wrongdoer as ʿUqba ibn Abī Muʿayṭ — a man of Mecca who apostatized after his Islam, seeking the pleasure of his companion Ubayy ibn Khalaf. In this reading, *fulān* is Ubayy: the man whose approval ʿUqba chose over the Messenger's path, the khalīl whose influence led him back from the brink of belief. Al-Tabari, drawing on the same narration, describes the cost of that choice with a devastating phrase: **أوبق نفسه** — he annihilated himself.

There were real people behind these words. A real relationship. A real moment in Mecca when a specific man chose a specific companion over the Reminder that had come to him.

And yet: the Quran's choice to write *fulān* — when it could have fixed the verse to Ubayy's name — is itself a choice that opens the verse beyond that single occasion. Allah named the Messenger, named the Reminder, named Shaytan. And left the friend as a placeholder. Scholars who held the universal reading have argued — across the tradition — that this is precisely what makes the verse cut across centuries: every generation of listeners is invited to supply the name from their own life.

Both readings illuminate something real. The specific reading gives the verse roots in actual soil — a real man, a real friendship, a real defection from the edge of salvation. The universal reading draws those roots forward through time, asking every generation to stand where ʿUqba stood and ask: who is my *fulān*?

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. Allah is the One who, when He wishes, names entire nations across centuries — Pharaoh, Hāmān, Qārūn, Abū Lahab. He has no shortage of names. He could have written Ubayy's name directly. He chose not to — even as many commentators believed it was Ubayy.

And the result of that non-naming is this: **it is asking you to fill it in.**

Whose name belongs in that slot in your life?

Who is the *fulān* in your story — the friend, the influencer, the colleague, the cousin, the group chat, the algorithmic feed — whose pull on you has, slowly, quietly, turned you away from the Reminder you once knew?

Allah did not engrave a name on the mirror, because the moment a name is engraved on the mirror, it stops being a mirror. It becomes a portrait — of someone else.

[PAUSE]

Let's go even deeper, because there is more.

Look at the word the wrongdoer uses for this person: **خَلِيلًا** — *khalīl*. Not *ṣadīq* — friend. Not *rafīq* — companion on the road. **Khalīl.**

The root of *khalīl* is خ-ل-ل, which classical lexicographers connect to the idea of *penetration into the very interior of something*. *Khalāl* refers to the gaps and spaces between things — to that which has worked its way *between*, into the inner crevices. A *khalīl* is not someone who walks beside you. A *khalīl* is someone whose love and influence have penetrated into the inner chambers of your heart — into the gaps between your thoughts, into the spaces where your beliefs are formed.

This is why the title given to Ibrāhīm عليه السلام is *Khalīl Allāh* — the intimate beloved of Allah. The relationship is not surface. It has penetrated to the core.

So the wrongdoer is not saying *"I wish I hadn't been on chatting terms with that guy."* He is saying: **"I wish I had not let him into the deepest, most interior chamber of who I am."**

And notice what the companion actually did once inside that chamber. He did not merely distract. He *led him astray* — **أَضَلَّنِى** — from the Reminder. Al-Muyassar specifies that *al-dhikr* here is the Quran itself. And the devastating phrase that follows deserves to be read slowly: **بَعْدَ إِذْ جَآءَنِى** — *after it had come to me*. The wrongdoer is not lamenting that he never encountered the truth. He is lamenting that the truth *arrived*, that it was present, that it was on the table — and the khalīl talked him out of it.

The *fulān* in your life is not the casual acquaintance. The *fulān* is the one whose worldview has slipped into the gaps of yours. The one whose laughter at certain things has retrained what you find ridiculous. The one whose silence about certain things has retrained what you find acceptable. The one whose heart has, by sheer proximity, started reshaping yours.

> **What this single absence reveals:** The blank slot in this verse is an invitation, made across centuries, to look honestly at whose voice occupies the interior chamber of your own heart. The historical record gives it a face — a real man, a real friendship, a real moment of defection from the Reminder that had come. The placeholder draws that face forward into your present. Both are asking the same question.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Word for What Shaytan Is

We come to the closing line of the passage:

> **وَكَانَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ لِلْإِنسَـٰنِ خَذُولًۭا**
> *"And Shaytan is, to the human being, ever a great deserter."*

I want to teach you one piece of Arabic morphology before we open this up.

In Arabic, you can take a verb's root and pour it into different *molds* — different patterns — and the mold itself carries meaning. There is one mold called **فَعُول** — *faʿūl*. When you take a root and pour it into the *faʿūl* mold, the resulting noun typically means: **one who does this thing constantly, intensely, by their very nature**. Not one who happened to do it once. *One who does it as a defining trait.*

So *ṣabūr* — from the root for patience — means not just "patient" but "habitually, intensely patient." *Shakūr* — from the root for gratitude — means "intensely grateful as a way of being."

Now look at the word Allah uses here: **خَذُولًۭا** — *khadhūl*. *Faʿūl* mold.

The root خ-ذ-ل carries a very specific meaning in classical Arabic. It does not just mean "to abandon." It means specifically **to abandon someone at the moment they needed you most**. The classical lexicographers describe *khadhl* as the betrayal of one who was relied upon — the friend who walks away the instant the trouble starts, the supporter who vanishes the second the support is required.

Pour that meaning into the *faʿūl* mold, and you get **khadhūl**: *the one whose defining, habitual nature is to abandon you at the precise moment you most need him.*

Al-Muyassar confirms this quality in a single word: **دائمًا** — *always*. Not sometimes. Not under certain conditions. Shaytan is, to the human being, a habitual deserter — always. The *faʿūl* mold and the classical commentary converge on the same point: this is not an occasional betrayal. It is the shape of what Shaytan is.

This is not what Shaytan *does sometimes*. This is what Shaytan **IS**.

[PAUSE]

And now — feel the structure of this closing line. **وَكَانَ ٱلشَّيْطَـٰنُ** — *wa kāna al-Shayṭān* — "And Shaytan was/is/ever-has-been..." The verb *kāna* with this construction often signals a permanent, timeless quality. *Shaytan, as a fixed reality of the cosmos*, is *khadhūl* to the human being.

He whispers you toward the choice. He flatters the choice. He helps you justify the choice. He helps you explain it to your family. He helps you laugh at those who warn you against it.

And then — at the precise moment the reckoning comes, at the exact hour when you most need someone to stand with you — he is not there.

He was never going to be there. That was always the design.

[PAUSE]

There is another ayah that makes this plain in a way that reverberates against everything we have just read. On the Day of Judgment, Shaytan himself will speak (14:22) — and what he says is perhaps the most devastating speech in the Quran. He will tell those who followed him: *I had no authority over you. I only invited you, and you responded. Do not blame me — blame yourselves.* He will disavow the very people he spent a lifetime cultivating.

*Khadhūl*. This is not a surprise arrival. This is the *faʿūl* mold doing its work — telling you, right now, in this life, before the Day, what was always going to happen. The word was a warning embedded in the grammar. The desertion was always baked into the nature.

Now feel how the verse's closing line is positioned in the structure of the passage.

The wrongdoer speaks first. He says: *the friend led me astray*. He tries, even in that moment of breaking, to place the weight of his choice onto another.

And then Allah responds — not to the wrongdoer, but to the listener: *And Shaytan is, to the human being, ever a deserter.*

The phrasing is precise. It does not say *Shaytan led him astray*. It does not name Shaytan as the khalīl. It states — as a fixed, cosmic reality — that Shaytan is always *khadhūl* to the human being. The implication hangs in the air: every companion who leads you away from the Reminder carries the character of Shaytan's role. And Shaytan himself — the one behind the whispering — was never on your side. He was always building toward the abandonment.

> **What this closing line reveals:** The word *khadhūl* is structured, grammatically, to be a warning you can hear *before* the Day. Every relationship that pulls you from the Reminder is following a script whose final act is desertion. The grammar itself is telling you not to trust the one who is always whispering you away from your path.

## PART 2: THE ROAD TO SALVATION

There is something easy to miss in this passage, because the weight of attention falls so naturally on the wrongdoer's grief — on the unnamed friend, on the *khadhūl* at the end.

But look at what the wrongdoer actually wishes for first.

He does not wish, first, that he had avoided the wrong friend. His first wish is different: **اتَّخَذْتُ مَعَ الرَّسُولِ سَبِيلًا** — *would that I had taken with the Messenger a way*. A path. With. The Messenger. Al-Tabari glosses this as **طريقا إلى النجاة** — a road toward salvation, toward rescue from the punishment. Not a road to comfort or reward alone, but a road to *rescue* — the word النجاة carries the urgency of escape, of being pulled from something that would have consumed you.

This is what was on the table. Not an abstract creed. Not a distant obligation. **A path. Alongside someone. With the one who was sent.**

*Maʿa al-rasūl*. The preposition *maʿa* carries presence and accompaniment. Not: I wish I had agreed with his message intellectually. Not: I wish I had believed in him from a distance. *I wish I had walked the road alongside him.* The wrongdoer's first regret is not about doctrine. It is about direction, and about company, and about the particular form of deliberate, effortful taking — Form VIII, **ittakhadha** — that he gave to the wrong companion instead of to this road.

[PAUSE]

And this tells us something about the nature of the choice that was always on offer.

The wrongdoer had two roads. Both roads were offered through human companionship. The Messenger's road was a road walked *with* the Messenger — the living embodiment of the criterion. The other road was walked with *fulān* — the unnamed friend whose influence penetrated the interior chambers and led him away from the Reminder that had already arrived. The choice, in the end, was not between a path and no path. It was between **two possible khalīls** — two who each wished to occupy the innermost space — and a self that had to decide which one to take as its own.

The path with the Messenger was available. It was not hidden, not locked behind scholarship or status. It required one thing: the willingness to let the right voice into the chamber. To make that path *your own* — with the same Form VIII deliberateness that the wrongdoer recognizes, too late, he gave to the wrong companion.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar closes its commentary on these three ayahs with a warning that is direct and simple in the way the best classical commentary sometimes is: *these verses are a caution against the companionship of the evil companion — for he may be the means of bringing his companion into the Fire*.

Not a gentle note about being careful with your social circle. **He may be the means of your entering the Fire.** The classical commentary does not soften what these verses are actually saying. The *khalīl* you choose is not a peripheral concern. It is a theological stake — laid down in the structure of the passage, confirmed by the grief of the wrongdoer who regrets nothing more than this single choice, and by the *faʿūl* of a word at the end that was always a warning in plain sight.

[PAUSE]

Now stand for a moment where the wrongdoer stands — not in his grief, but before it. Before the Day. In the life where the choice can still be made.

Two roads. Two companions. Two possible uses of the verb **ittakhadha** — to take deliberately as one's own.

The wrongdoer chose to make *fulān* his khalīl. He let that influence into the inner chamber. He gave both hands to the wrong path. And that choice — made freely, with effort, for no better reason than the pleasure of his companion — destroyed him. Al-Tabari calls it *awbaqa nafsah*: self-annihilation.

But we are not at the Day. We are at the place where the choice is still unmade, or still reversible, or still being made again each morning.

Whose voice is in the inner chamber?

Which *fulān* has worked their way between your thoughts, into the gaps, into the spaces where your relationship with the Reminder is formed — and how long have they been there?

And — more pressing still — have you made the Messenger's path *your own*, with the same Form VIII deliberateness that the wrongdoer recognizes, too late, he gave to the wrong companion?

## CLOSING

We began with a surah named for a *criterion* — the line that separates.

And these three ayahs are, in a sense, the criterion made personal. Not abstract. Not theological in the distant sense. **Personal.** A single human being. A single regret. A single relationship. A single word for what Shaytan always was.

The *furqān* — the criterion — is not only the Quran sitting on a shelf. It is the active question asked across every relationship: is this drawing me toward the Reminder or away from it? Is this khalīl bringing me closer to the road of salvation, or is this the *fulān* whose name I will one day regret?

The criterion operates here, now, in the choosing of companions. In the deciding of whose voice enters the chamber. In the deliberate taking — Form VIII, both hands — of a path that you make your own, or fail to.

The wrongdoer's regret is the testimony of someone who had the criterion and did not use it in time. He had the Reminder. It came to him. He had companions who could have become his khalīls on the road to rescue. And he chose *fulān* instead — the one whose influence annihilated him.

We have been given the same criterion. The same Messenger. The same Reminder that comes to us. The same *khadhūl* who is, right now, whispering reasons not to take it.

The surah's name is its warning. And these three ayahs are the warning made vivid — a face, a wail, two bitten hands, a friend whose name no one will remember, and the only one who was always going to leave.

[PAUSE]

The Day will not surprise us with what we chose.

We already know. We are, right now, in the act of choosing.
