# The Accusation of Forgery and the Patience of the Reply

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayat 4-6

> [ayah:25:4] وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ إِنْ هَـٰذَآ إِلَّآ إِفْكٌ ٱفْتَرَىٰهُ وَأَعَانَهُۥ عَلَيْهِ قَوْمٌ ءَاخَرُونَ ۖ فَقَدْ جَآءُو ظُلْمًا وَزُورًا
>
> [ayah:25:5] وَقَالُوٓا۟ أَسَـٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ٱكْتَتَبَهَا فَهِىَ تُمْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا
>
> [ayah:25:6] قُلْ أَنزَلَهُ ٱلَّذِى يَعْلَمُ ٱلسِّرَّ فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا

*"And those who disbelieve say, 'This is nothing but a fabrication he has invented, and other people have helped him with it.' They have committed injustice and falsehood. And they say, 'Tales of the ancients which he has had written down, and they are dictated to him morning and evening.' Say: 'It has been sent down by the One who knows the secret in the heavens and the earth. Indeed, He has always been Forgiving, Merciful.'"*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

Surah Al-Furqan opens with one of the most majestic declarations in the Quran. *Tabāraka alladhī nazzala al-Furqāna ʿalā ʿabdihi* — *Blessed is the One who sent down the Criterion upon His slave.* The surah announces itself as a book that distinguishes — between truth and falsehood, between what is real and what only seems real, between the Lord who creates and the gods people invent.

And then, before the surah does anything else, it does something almost shocking.

It quotes the people who hate it.

The Quran pauses, mid-introduction, and records — in their own voice — what the disbelievers of Makkah were saying about the Messenger ﷺ as he walked through their streets. Not as a paraphrase. Not as a summary. The accusations are reproduced *as accusations*, with their grammar intact, their sneer intact, their malice preserved on the page.

You have to feel what this means before you can read these ayat.

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine a man you have known your whole life. You watched him as a boy. You saw him become a young man whom everyone in the city called *Al-Amīn* — the trustworthy one. People would entrust him with their valuables before traveling, knowing they would find their property untouched on return. Even his enemies, when they had something precious, brought it to him for safekeeping.

Then, in his fortieth year, he begins to recite something.

He stands in the marketplace and recites words that sound unlike anything you have ever heard. The words have a rhythm that breaks every rhythm you know. They speak of God, of accountability, of mercy. They reduce poets to silence. They make hardened men weep into their cloaks when they think no one is watching.

And the city splits.

Some believe him. They say: *We have known this man for forty years. He never lied. He cannot start lying now — and certainly not about God.*

But the leadership cannot afford to believe him. Because if he is right, their gods are nothing. Their inherited authority is nothing. The whole architecture of Quraysh — the trade routes, the pilgrim economy, the family honor codes — depends on him being wrong.

So they need a story. A story that explains the words without conceding the source.

And the story they construct — the story Allah quotes back to them in these ayat — is not one accusation but **a cascade of escalating accusations**, each one contradicting the last, each one reaching for whatever might stick.

### The Hook

Here is what is strange.

Allah quotes the slander. Allah names it as *injustice and falsehood*. And then Allah replies.

But the reply does not match the heat of the accusation. The reply is not a thunderbolt. It is not a curse. It is not even, primarily, a defense.

The reply ends with two words that should not be there. Two words that, if you read them quickly, you will miss entirely. And those two words are the entire teaching.

Stay with me. Let's build to them slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Vocabulary of Slander — *Ifk*, *Iftirāʾ*, *Zūr*

Before we open these words in the ayah, we need to understand something about how Arabic builds the vocabulary of lying.

In English, we have one main word — *lie* — and we modify it with adjectives. *A small lie. A big lie. A malicious lie.* The shading lives in the modifier.

Arabic does it differently. Arabic has **separate roots** for separate kinds of falsehood, and each root tells you what *kind* of departure from truth is happening. The grammar carries the moral diagnosis built into the word itself.

Let me show you with a simple example. If I say in English, "He lied," you don't yet know what shape the lie has. But in Arabic, the choice of root tells you immediately whether the speaker turned the truth upside down, or invented something out of nothing, or distorted something genuine, or testified falsely. Each root gives a different X-ray.

Now look at what happens in this single ayah.

In ten words of accusation, Allah uses **three different roots for falsehood**, stacked one after another.

> إِنْ هَـٰذَآ إِلَّآ **إِفْكٌ** **ٱفْتَرَىٰهُ** ... فَقَدْ جَآءُو ظُلْمًا وَ**زُورًا**

*Ifk*. *Iftarāhu*. *Zūr*.

This is not a writer reaching for synonyms. This is forensic precision. Watch.

#### *Ifk* — the inversion

The root *ʾ-f-k* in classical Arabic carries the sense of something **turned over, flipped, inverted**. The classical lexicons connect it to the overturning of cities — *al-muʾtafikāt*, the cities of Lūṭ that were turned upside down. The wind that flips its direction is from the same family.

So when they say *in hādhā illā ifk* — *this is nothing but ifk* — they are not saying "this is a small lie." They are saying: **this is reality turned upside down. The truth has been flipped. What is up is presented as down. What is false is dressed as revelation.**

It is the most violent kind of lying — because it doesn't deny truth, it inverts it. It takes the very thing pointing toward Allah and presents it as pointing away.

[PAUSE]

Notice what they have done. They are accusing the Quran — *the Furqān*, the very Criterion that distinguishes truth from falsehood — of being **the inversion of truth itself**. They are saying the instrument that flips falsehood right-side-up is itself a flip of falsehood.

The accusation is the diagnosis of their own condition projected onto the Messenger ﷺ.

#### *Iftarāhu* — the manufacture

Then immediately: *iftarāhu* — *he forged it*.

The root *f-r-y* in its base sense means **to cut, to slit, to fashion something by cutting**. Classical usage describes cutting leather, splitting hide, fabricating something through the work of the hands. From this comes *iftirāʾ* — to fabricate, to manufacture a lie as a craftsman manufactures an object.

So *ifk* told us **what** the lie is — an inversion. *Iftarāhu* tells us **how** it was supposedly produced — manufactured, crafted, pieced together by the work of his own hands.

And notice the verb form. *Iftarā* is form VIII (*iftaʿala*) — the reflexive intensive. The form itself implies effort, deliberation, sustained craft. They are not accusing him of a slip of the tongue. They are accusing him of **a sustained workshop of forgery**, line by line, image by image.

#### *Zūr* — the dressing-up

Then, after the accusation is finished, Allah's verdict: *fa-qad jāʾū ẓulman wa-zūrā* — *they have come with injustice and zūr.*

The root *z-w-r* in classical Arabic carries the sense of **deviation, leaning away from truth, something that visits but does not belong**. From the same root comes *ziyāra* — to visit. The connection is striking: a *zāʾir* is one who comes to a place that is not his own, then leaves. *Zūr* is testimony that *visits* the truth, *poses* as truth, but does not *belong to* truth. It is dressed-up falsehood. False testimony that wears the costume of testimony.

Allah's verdict pairs it with *ẓulm*. The root *ẓ-l-m* carries the sense of placing something where it does not belong — of darkening what should be light, of displacement from the right order. To call the truthful one a forger, to call the Criterion a recycled tale: this is *ẓulm* in the deepest sense. A cosmic misplacement. And Allah names it plainly before the reply even begins.

So watch the layers Allah has just exposed:

- *Ifk* — the moral content of the lie: it is an inversion of reality
- *Iftirāʾ* — the labor of the lie: it is a manufactured product
- *Zūr* — the disguise of the lie: it visits the appearance of truth without owning it
- *Ẓulm* — the verdict on those who brought it: a darkness placed where light belongs

[PAUSE]

The disbelievers are accusing the Messenger ﷺ. The Quran is performing a forensic dissection of *their accusation*. Four different angles on the same act, and the act is found false on every one.

This is what it means to say the Quran is a *Furqān*. It does not just answer accusations. It anatomizes them.

[PAUSE]

What this single word-cluster reveals: when falsehood comes against truth, truth does not need to raise its voice. It only needs to name the falsehood with sufficient precision. The lie cannot survive being seen clearly.

### Element Two: The Strange Verb *Iktataba* and What It Concedes

Now move to the second accusation, in ayah 5.

> وَقَالُوٓا۟ أَسَـٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ٱكْتَتَبَهَا فَهِىَ تُمْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

*And they said: "Tales of the ancients which he has had written down, and they are dictated to him morning and evening."*

Two words here are doing extraordinary work. We need to teach a small grammatical concept first.

#### A short detour: the difference between *kataba* and *iktataba*

In Arabic, the simple form of "to write" is *kataba*. *He wrote.* If I want to say "he had it written," meaning he asked someone else to write it for him while he himself did not write it — Arabic has a specific form for this: *iktataba*. Form VIII of the root *k-t-b*.

This is form VIII serving a **request-causative** sense: *he had it written for him, he procured the writing of it*.

It is the difference between *I cooked dinner* and *I had dinner cooked for me*. Same root, different form, very different meaning. The first claims the action. The second outsources it.

#### What the accusation is actually saying

Now read it again: *asāṭīr al-awwalīn **iktatabahā***. *Tales of the ancients which **he had written down for him**.*

They are not saying he wrote them. They are saying he was illiterate — and so he hired scribes. He went to people who could read the old books, and he had them transcribe the old stories for him.

And then: *fa-hiya **tumlā ʿalayhi** bukratan wa-aṣīlā* — *and they are **dictated to him** morning and evening.*

*Tumlā* is from the root *m-l-y*, in the passive — *to have something dictated, to have it read out to one*. The picture is of a man sitting while someone else reads the texts aloud to him, repeatedly, to memorize them.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable.

**The accusation, read carefully, is admitting things the disbelievers did not realize they were admitting.**

First admission: *The Messenger ﷺ does not write.* The verb *iktataba* — "had it written" — only makes sense if he himself is not the one putting pen to paper. They are confessing his unlettered state into the record.

Second admission: *The recitation arrives morning and evening, regularly, in a sustained flow.* They are confirming the *steady cadence* of revelation. Not a single composition delivered once, but an ongoing stream that comes again and again at regular intervals.

Third admission, and the deepest: *They cannot point to the alleged teachers.* If the accusation were "we know this man — he visits a Jewish scholar named so-and-so every morning, and he visits a Christian monk every evening" — that would be a falsifiable claim. Al-Tabari records that the accusers did in fact suggest it was Jews who were helping him, calling out *qawmun ākharūn* — but when pressed, they could name no one specific. The passive construction *tumlā ʿalayhi* with no named agent is a tell. It is the grammar of slander that cannot find its object.

This is what slander always does when it cannot find evidence. It uses **passive voice and unnamed agents**.

*People are saying...* (which people?)
*It's been reported...* (by whom?)
*Some have observed...* (who? where?)

The grammar of the accusation is itself the proof that the accusation has no foundation. The disbelievers are forced into the passive voice because they cannot produce a single named teacher. They cannot say "his teacher is Jabr the Roman" or "his teacher is the rabbi from Yathrib" — because the moment they name a person, the claim collapses, since the named person could be examined, and the recitation that surpasses every poet of Arabia could not be traced back to him.

[PAUSE]

So the accusation, when you read it grammatically, contains its own refutation.

This is why Allah quoted them. Not to defend the Messenger ﷺ — but to let their own words convict them.

#### *Bukratan wa-aṣīlā* — the unintended testimony

One last detail in this ayah, and it is small, but it is doing real work.

*Bukratan wa-aṣīlā* — *morning and evening*.

This is a Quranic phrase used elsewhere for the times of remembrance, the cadence of devotion, the rhythm of those who turn to Allah at the edges of the day. *Wa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika bukratan wa-aṣīlā* — glorify your Lord at dawn and in the late afternoon. These are the two thresholds of sacred time, the brackets the Quran places around the hours of heedlessness.

The disbelievers chose these specific words — the very words that everywhere else in the Quran mark the times of *tasbīḥ* and turning toward Allah — to describe what they imagined was deception. Every Makkan listener knew that *bukratan wa-aṣīlā* belonged to the vocabulary of devotion. By selecting it, the accusers were painting — without intending to — a picture of a man bowed in sustained attention at the margins of the day: receiving, attending, disciplined and continuous. Not a picture of a literary thief, but of one whose relationship with what came to him was as regular and sacred as prayer.

[PAUSE]

The slander cannot help itself. It keeps slipping into the language of truth.

### Element Three: The Named Man Behind the Accusation

We have been reading these accusations as general Makkan hostility. But classical commentators transmit that the *asātīr al-awwalīn* charge in ayah 5 had a face.

Al-Tabari records through multiple chains — tracing back to Ibn ʿAbbās via ʿIkrima — that this accusation was associated specifically with al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith, described as one of the most aggressive adversaries among the Qurayshi leadership. Al-Naḍr had done something unusual for a man of his time and class: he had traveled to al-Ḥīra, a cultured garrison city in Iraq then saturated with Persian literary culture, and there he had learned the tales and legends of Persian kings — the stories of Rustam and Isfandiyar, the great heroes of ancient Persian epic.

He returned to Makkah armed with entertainment.

His strategy was pointed. After the Messenger ﷺ would finish speaking — after the city had just heard words about the Day of Judgment, about nations destroyed for their arrogance, about the mercy of Allah — al-Naḍr would sit down in the same gathering and begin to tell his Persian tales. His stories about kings and warriors and legendary battles. And he would say: *By Allah, Muhammad's narrative is no better than mine.*

His point was not theological. His point was aesthetic and political. *You think this is special? I have stories too. Ancient stories. Tales from the first civilizations. What is the Quran but more of the same?*

This gives the word *asātīr* its full weight. *Asāṭīr* is the plural of *usṭūra* — a tale, a legend, something written down and passed on from the old times. The word carries a slight sense of the mythological, the ancient-and-famous. Al-Naḍr was not saying "this is fabricated." He was making a genre claim: *This is just the kind of material that gets written down and circulated. Old stories. I know old stories too. I can tell them just as well.*

He was trying to flatten the Quran to the level of his Persian entertainment.

[PAUSE]

And this is precisely why the divine reply in ayah 6 is aimed at the root of the problem, not the surface of it.

If the accusation had been "the Quran is factually wrong," the reply could have been factual. If the accusation had been "you are a bad person," the reply could have defended character. But the accusation — in its deepest form, the form al-Naḍr gave it — was: *This is human. This is old material. This is the kind of thing we have all seen before.*

The reply does not argue on his terrain. The reply goes straight to the only claim that answers it completely.

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE DIVINE REPLY

### The Asymmetry

> قُلْ أَنزَلَهُ ٱلَّذِى يَعْلَمُ ٱلسِّرَّ فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا

*Say: It has been sent down by the One who knows the secret in the heavens and the earth. Indeed, He has always been Forgiving, Merciful.*

Two ayat of accusation. Three different words for lying. A cascade of slander, each layer contradicting the one before it, each one louder than the last.

And the reply is: *one sentence of source attribution, and two Names of Allah*.

This asymmetry is not weakness. In the Quran's rhetoric, brevity after accusation is not a concession. It is a statement about proportionality. The accusation spent words because the accusation had no substance — it needed volume to seem convincing. The reply uses few words because the reply is entirely substance.

Notice what the reply *does not do*.

It does not name al-Naḍr by name and expose him. It does not list the ways the Quran is superior to Persian tales. It does not call the accusers liars in return, even though they are. It does not demand that they produce their teachers.

The reply redirects entirely. It changes the question.

The question they posed was: *Where did this come from?* Their answer: *From human sources. Old books. Ancient tales. Teachers in the night.*

The reply offers a different answer entirely: *It came from the One who knows al-sirr — the hidden, the secret — in every layer of the heavens and the earth.*

### *Al-Sirr* — the Hidden That Only He Knows

The word *sirr* carries extraordinary weight here.

In Arabic, *sirr* is not simply "secret" in the sense of information kept private. Classical usage gives it a deeper range. *Al-sirr* is the innermost thing — the hidden kernel, the concealed truth at the center of things. It is what is buried so deep that no created being can reach it. It is the news of souls, the movement of hearts, the causes behind apparent things, the knowledge that dwells in the unlit interior of the cosmos.

When Allah says *alladhī yaʿlamu al-sirra fī al-samāwāt wa-al-arḍ*, He is not saying "the One who happens to know some secrets." He is saying: **the One whose knowledge reaches every hidden reality in every stratum of existence, from the heights of the heavens to the depths of the earth**.

Now bring this back to the accusation.

Al-Naḍr said: *This is human. This is the kind of thing circulated in old civilizations. I know Persian tales. What is the Quran but more of the same?* The implication: *There is no mystery here. You can trace it. Old stories, copied scribes, unnamed teachers. Nothing that requires a divine source.*

The reply: *It comes from the One who knows what no human mind has ever reached.* The thing you cannot trace — the knowledge it contains that neither Persian epics nor any human gathering could have produced — is the evidence of its source. Al-Muyassar captures this precisely: the Quran is simply not something any human being could fabricate. Not merely because it is eloquent beyond comparison, but because it contains what only the knower of all hidden things could know.

The accusers' very inability to locate the source — despite their best efforts to invent one, their unnamed agents and passive constructions — is itself the testimony.

[PAUSE]

The question was: *Where did this come from?* The answer does not argue. It declares. And the declaration is unanswerable by the terms the accusers were using, because it relocates the claim entirely outside the domain of human sourcing.

### *Ghafūr Raḥīm* — What Should Not Be Here

And then: *innahū kāna Ghafūran Raḥīmā*. *Indeed, He has always been Forgiving, Merciful.*

Read the accusation again. These men called the Messenger ﷺ a forger. They said he hired accomplices. They sat in gatherings after him and tried to drown out the Quran with Persian entertainment. They spent their social capital circulating the lie through the city.

After all of this — what do you expect the response to close with?

Not *Ghafūr Raḥīm*.

You might expect: *And He is swift in punishment*. Or: *And He is the Overwhelmingly Powerful*. Or: *And He is All-Aware of what the wrongdoers plot*. Any of these would follow logically — a display of divine authority closing a statement of divine knowledge, a warning to those who used their tongues against the Messenger ﷺ.

Instead: *Ghafūr. Raḥīm.*

Classical commentators have observed that this ending is not doing one thing simultaneously — it is doing several, and it would be a mistake to reduce it to a single register.

**The first function is an open door.**

The accusers are still alive. Their names are still in the divine ledger. Whatever they said in the marketplace this morning can still be erased if they turn. *Ghafūr* — the One whose forgiveness is vast, whose erasure of sin is a real act and not a polite fiction — is speaking directly toward the people who just called His Book a fabrication. This is not irony. It is mercy standing at the door of the very people who locked themselves inside their slander. The door is open. Come out. The One who knows every secret of the heavens and earth is still, in this moment, choosing Ghafūr Raḥīm over reckoning.

**The second function is divine *ḥilm* — forbearance.**

*Ḥilm* in classical Arabic ethics is the quality of one who has the power to punish but chooses patience — who is not rushed by emotion, who allows time before acting, whose authority is so settled it has no need to prove itself through immediate force. The pairing of *yaʿlamu al-sirr* with *Ghafūr Raḥīm* creates this deliberately: I see everything — including what is hidden inside your accusations, including the envy and the fear and the calculated malice — and I am choosing Ghafūr Raḥīm. That is not weakness. That is the *ḥilm* of one whose knowledge is complete and whose punishment, when it comes, comes from certainty rather than anger.

**The third function is a vindication and an instruction for the Messenger ﷺ.**

To the Messenger ﷺ, walking through a city where his character is being publicly destroyed — this ending is not merely comforting. It is a posture handed to him. The reply does not say: *Match their hostility. Expose them. Call down the punishment they deserve.* It says: *the One who sent this and who sees all of this is Ghafūr Raḥīm.* The reply models, for the one who will carry it through the streets of Makkah, what his own bearing should be.

He is not being told to answer al-Naḍr's Persian tales with literary counterargument. He is not being told to debate the unnamed-agent problem with the Qurayshi leadership. He is being told: *the One who knows the sirr is Ghafūr Raḥīm.* And that means: you can hold this slander with the same ḥilm, because you too know who the final knower is.

[PAUSE]

This is the architecture of a reply that the human intellect could not construct. A man defending himself would choose power words. He would display his authority, marshal his evidence, humiliate his accusers. The reply that comes from the One who knows the secret of the heavens and the earth chooses, instead, two attributes of mercy — and in doing so, is more devastating to the accusation than any display of force could have been.

Because the only reply that truly exposes the smallness of slander is a reply that seems too large to bother with it.

## PART 3: WHAT THIS PASSAGE TEACHES US TO CARRY

There is something here for everyone who has been accused of something they did not do.

The Quran does not pretend the accusation is easy to bear. It records the slander in full, preserving every word of it on the page of revelation — the *ifk*, the *iftirāʾ*, the unnamed accomplices, the image of the man sitting while stories are read to him like a student who cannot think for himself. The hurt is real. The injustice is named — *ẓulm, zūr* — Allah does not call it a misunderstanding or a difference of opinion. He calls it oppression and fabrication. He calls it darkness placed where light belongs.

But the model of response is not the erasure of the wound. It is a different orientation toward it.

What would it mean to respond to slander the way the Quran responds here?

Not by matching volume with volume. Not by racing to expose and retaliate, to find the al-Naḍr in the room and publicly dismantle him. But by returning — always — to the question of *source*: where does my standing come from? Who knows what is actually hidden in this situation? Who sees what the accusers cannot see and the audience cannot verify?

*Anzalahu alladhī yaʿlamu al-sirr.*

It was sent down by the One who knows the secret.

The person who has been unjustly accused has something that the accusers do not: the knowledge that the One who knows all hidden things is aware. Not just aware in some general sense — but *knows the sirr*. Knows the inward reality. Knows who you actually are beneath whatever story is circulating about you, knows the motives of the ones who spread it, knows the full account that will be rendered.

That awareness is not a passive comfort. It is a reorientation. It relocates the tribunal. The world's judgment, however loud, however sustained, however widely shared, is not the final court. The One who knows the secret of the heavens and the earth is the final court.

[PAUSE]

And He is Ghafūr Raḥīm. Even toward those who accuse.

This is what should not be there — and is.

Not thunderbolts. Not a list of the accusers' crimes. Not a curse that would close the door on every al-Naḍr who ever tried to flatten the sacred to the level of his entertainment.

*Ghafūr Raḥīm.*

Forgiveness and mercy, offered in the same breath as the declaration that all secrets are known — which means the forgiveness is offered with full knowledge of what was done. Not ignorance. Not naivety. Seeing everything, and still: *Ghafūr Raḥīm*.

For the believer who actually sits with this, who lets it land rather than just reading past it, this is the entire teaching.

## DUʿĀ

*O Allah, You are the One who knows al-sirr — what is hidden in the heavens and the earth, and what is hidden in us. You know what we carry that others cannot see. You know the truth of every accusation and the truth of every silence that receives it.*

*When we are accused of what we did not do, steady us with the knowledge that You see. When we are tempted to match slander with slander, to find our al-Naḍr and dismantle him in public, remind us of the reply that chose Ghafūr Raḥīm over force. When the world's judgment feels final, return us to the tribunal that is.*

*And O Allah — turn the door of Your forgiveness toward every heart named in this passage, including the ones who called it fabrication. You kept that door open for them. Make us never the ones who close it for others.*

*Āmīn.*
