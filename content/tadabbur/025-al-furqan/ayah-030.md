# The Messenger's Complaint: When the Quran is Abandoned

## Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 30

### Arabic
**وَقَالَ ٱلرَّسُولُ يَـٰرَبِّ إِنَّ قَوْمِى ٱتَّخَذُوا۟ هَـٰذَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ مَهْجُورًۭا**

### Transliteration
*Wa qāla al-rasūlu yā Rabbi inna qawmī ittakhadhū hādhā al-qur'āna mahjūrā*

### Translation
"And the Messenger will say, 'My Lord, indeed my people took this Quran as something abandoned.'"

## Introduction

### Rebuilding the World

To hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, you have to understand the climate of Surah Al-Furqan.

This surah is not a peaceful surah. It is a surah of complaint and counter-complaint. The disbelievers of Makkah have just been quoted in the verses immediately before this one — *"Why is the Quran not revealed to him all at once?"*, *"Why doesn't an angel come down with him?"*, *"Why doesn't a treasure descend on him?"* They are nitpicking. They are not seeking. They are looking for reasons not to believe and dressing them up as reasons.

The Prophet ﷺ has been reciting the Quran to them for years now. He recites and they walk past. He recites and they put their fingers in their ears. He recites and they laugh. He recites and they say *"old fables, written down for him."* He recites and they say *"if we wanted, we could say something like this."*

And then this ayah arrives.

It arrives like a small, unexpected door in the wall of the surah. The surah has been arguing with the rejecters. Suddenly the camera pans away from them entirely. We are no longer in Makkah. We are no longer in any earthly place. We are looking forward — to a moment yet to come — and we are listening to the Messenger himself speaking.

He is not addressing his people. He has stopped trying to speak to them. He is speaking to his Lord. About them.

### The Hook

There is something in this ayah that, once you see it, you will not be able to unsee.

Allah does not name the Messenger. He does not say "Muhammad." He does not say "the Prophet." He uses a title that is grammatically definite — *al-Rasūl*, **the Messenger** — and it is doing something specific.

And there is a word at the end of this ayah, *mahjūrā*, that the Arabs used for things you would not expect. It does not mean what most translations make it mean.

[PAUSE]

What you are about to hear is the recorded grief of the Prophet ﷺ — preserved in the speech of his Lord — about something that you and I are at risk of doing right now.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa qāla al-Rasūl* — "And the Messenger said"

Before we look at the ayah's verb, we need to understand something small about Arabic that English does not handle well.

In Arabic, when you put the definite article *al-* in front of a noun, you are not just saying "the." You are doing one of several things. You might be pointing to a specific known thing — *al-bayt*, "the house we both know about." Or you might be doing something the grammarians call *al-ʿahd al-dhihnī*, "the mental covenant" — pointing to something already alive in the listener's mind. Or you might be making a statement about *the category itself* — *al-insān*, "humanity as such."

Hold that distinction.

Now look at this ayah. Allah does not say *Muḥammadun*. He does not say *nabiyyī*, "My Prophet." He says **al-Rasūl** — *the Messenger*.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. When you read the surahs of the Quran, you will sometimes find Allah addressing the Prophet ﷺ as *yā ayyuhā al-nabī* — "O Prophet" — and sometimes as *yā ayyuhā al-rasūl* — "O Messenger." The classical scholars noticed this and observed a pattern. *Nabī* tends to emphasize the person — the chosen, the close, the beloved. *Rasūl* tends to emphasize the **function** — the one carrying a message from a sender to a recipient.

A *rasūl* is not just a category of human being. A *rasūl* is a relational role. There is a Sender. There is a message. There is a recipient. Take any one of those three away and the word *rasūl* loses its meaning.

So when Allah says **al-Rasūl** — using the *function* word, with the definite article — He is reminding us of the entire architecture of this moment. Someone sent a message. Someone delivered it faithfully. Someone was supposed to receive it.

And here, *the carrier of the message* is reporting back to *the One who sent him* about *what happened to the message in the hands of the recipients*.

This is the grammar of a delivery report. This is the grammar of a courier returning to the sender after the package has been thrown in a corner of the room.

Stay with that. The Prophet ﷺ in this ayah is not speaking as a man who has been personally wounded. He is speaking as a courier whose courier-ship has been refused. The complaint is not "they hurt me." The complaint is **"the message did not arrive."**

And here is the second thing about the definite article. Some classical commentators read *al-Rasūl* as a category statement — every messenger has, in a sense, made this complaint. The pattern is universal. But the foreground reference, the *al-ʿahd al-dhihnī*, the messenger already alive in the listener's mind, is unmistakably the Prophet ﷺ.

So we have two cameras at once. The wide shot: this is what every messenger eventually says. The close-up: this is what *our* Messenger ﷺ says. And the wide shot makes the close-up unbearable, because we realize we are watching a script play out for the last time.

### Element Two: *Ittakhadhū* — "they took as"

Now we come to the verb, and this is where the ayah opens up.

The verb is **ittakhadhū**. Its root is *akh-dh* — to take, to grasp, to seize. But the form here is not the simple "took." It is Form VIII — *iftaʿala* — which in Arabic carries a particular flavor.

Let me teach you the form before we use it.

Form VIII is *deliberate, intentional, often self-implicating action*. The simple form *akhadha* just means "he took" — neutrally, even passively. *Akhadha al-kitāb* — "he took the book." Maybe he picked it up. Maybe someone handed it to him. The simple form does not specify intent.

But *ittakhadha* is different. *Ittakhadha* means **"he took for himself, deliberately, as something."** It is the verb you use when someone *adopts* a status, *appoints* a thing to be a thing, *chooses* to treat one thing as another.

You see this verb across the Quran in moments where intentional adoption is exactly the point. *Ittakhadhū min dūni Allāhi ālihatan* — "they took, in place of Allah, gods" — they didn't accidentally have idols, they *adopted* idols. *Lā tattakhidhū al-yahūda wa al-naṣārā awliyāʾ* — "do not *take* the Jews and Christians as protectors" — not "do not have them around," but "do not *appoint* them in this role."

And there is one more feature. *Ittakhadha* is what grammarians call a *fiʿl mutaʿaddī ilā mafʿūlayn* — a verb that takes two objects. The structure is: *X took Y as Z*. You took the book — that is the first object — *as a hijra-thing* — that is the second object. The verb forces you to specify what you are converting the thing into.

[PAUSE]

Feel what this means.

Allah did not say *tarakū hādhā al-Qur'ān* — "they left this Quran." That would be passive. Drift. They wandered off and forgot.

He did not say *nasū hādhā al-Qur'ān* — "they forgot this Quran." That would be accidental. Memory failure.

He chose **ittakhadhū**. **They took it. Deliberately. As something.** They received the Book and made an active choice about what to do with it. They reached out, picked it up, and *placed it* in a category — and the category they placed it in is the next word.

This is what makes the ayah devastating. The complaint is not that the people of the Quran ignored the Book. The complaint is that they *processed* the Book — they thought about it, classified it, decided what role it would play in their lives — **and the role they assigned to it was abandonment**.

Hajr is something they *did* to it, on purpose, as a category they chose for it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Mahjūrā* — "as something abandoned"

This is the word the whole ayah is built around. And it is not what most translations let you see.

The root is *h-j-r*. From this root come several words you may know: *hijra* — the migration, the leaving of one place for another. *Muhājir* — one who emigrates. *Hajr* — to abandon, to cut off, to leave.

If the meaning stopped there, the translation "abandoned" would be enough. But the classical lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in *al-Mufradāt* — record several distinct senses of *hajr*, and the early commentators applied multiple of them to this ayah at once.

Here are the senses they document:

**One.** *Al-hajr* as in *to abandon* — to walk away from, to no longer engage. The book is on the shelf, dust is gathering on the cover.

**Two.** *Al-hajr* as in *to speak nonsense, ramble, talk in a delirium*. The Arabs used the verb *hajara* for what a sick person says when fever takes hold of him — incoherent speech, raving. From this comes *al-hajr* meaning the speech of a feverish person, dismissed because no one takes it seriously.

**Three.** *Al-hajr* as in *foul, indecent speech* — speech that is morally repulsive, that you would push away from yourself.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the form of the word. *Mahjūr* is a passive participle — *the thing that has been hajr-ed*. The thing that has been abandoned. Or the thing that has been *treated as the speech of a feverish person*. Or the thing that has been *treated as foul, repulsive nonsense*.

The classical commentators did not pick one of these meanings. They held them together as a composite. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said the people *abandoned* it. Mujāhid is reported to have said they *spoke against it as one speaks against the ravings of a sick man*. Some held that they treated it as foul speech they pushed away.

These are not competing theories. They are camera angles on the same human reality. Watch how they layer:

The first stage is *abandonment* — you do not engage. The Quran sits unread.

The second stage is *dismissal* — when someone brings it up, you wave it off the way you would wave off the muttering of a feverish man. *Yes, yes, the words of a man who didn't know what he was saying.* You do not have to argue with a fever-talker. You just nod and move on.

The third stage is *repulsion* — the words actually become offensive to you. They make demands you do not want. They name things you do not want named. So you treat them as foul speech, as something you want away from your ears.

Each stage requires the previous one. You can only dismiss what you have already abandoned. You can only find foul what you have already dismissed. *Hajr* is not one act. It is a graduated journey of disengagement.

[PAUSE]

And there is something the form of the word adds. *Mahjūr* — passive participle — does not only describe a verbal action. In Arabic, the passive participle often functions as a *state*, an enduring quality the object has come to possess. *Maktūb* is not just "written" — it is "a thing in the state of being written." *Maẓlūm* is not just "wronged" — it is "one who exists in the condition of having been wronged."

So *mahjūrā* is not only "they abandoned it" — it is "they made it into *a thing whose nature, in their lives, is to be abandoned*." They didn't just commit the act once. They built a category for the Book, and the category they built was: *this is the thing we keep at a distance.*

This is what they *adopted* it as. This is the second object of *ittakhadhū*. They took the Book — and they assigned it the status of *the abandoned thing in our house*.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

So look what we have. *Al-Rasūl* — the courier reporting to the Sender. *Ittakhadhū* — a deliberate, intentional, two-object verb of categorization. *Mahjūrā* — a stateful description carrying abandonment, dismissal-as-fevered-rambling, and repulsion-as-foul-speech, all at once.

Let's now ask what this reveals not just about words — but about what is actually happening between human beings and the speech of their Lord.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Abandonment is not absence — it is a posture

Think about the people in your life you have actively distanced yourself from.

Not the people you happened to lose touch with. Not the friend who moved cities and the calls slowed. Those are absences. They happen.

I mean the people you *decided* to step back from. Maybe a relative whose phone calls you stopped picking up. Maybe a friend whose texts you read but stopped answering. Maybe a parent whose voice messages you let pile up unplayed.

Notice something. With those people, the abandonment is not nothing. It is not silence. It is a *posture you maintain*, every day, with effort. You see the missed call. You feel something. You decide, again, not to call back. The abandonment is *active*.

Now ask yourself why you do it.

Usually it is because the relationship makes a demand on you that you do not want to meet. The person, if you let them speak, will ask something of you you are not willing to give. Their voice in your ear will require you to look at something you do not want to look at. So you put them in the category of *the one I do not engage with*, and you maintain that category by doing the small work of not picking up the phone.

[PAUSE]

This is *exactly* what *ittakhadhū hādhā al-Qur'āna mahjūrā* describes.

The Quran is not silent in the lives of those who abandon it. It is a maintained absence. The Quran is *right there*, on the shelf or on the phone, and not opening it is *something they are doing*. Every day they walk past, every day they don't open it, is a small reaffirmation of the category they put it in.

And the reason — when you press into it — is the same reason you don't pick up the phone. The voice on the other end will ask something of you. The voice will name what you don't want named. The voice will require what you don't want to give.

So you place the speaker in a category. *This is the voice I don't engage with.* And the category does the work of keeping you safe from the demand.

This is what makes the ayah devastating. The Prophet ﷺ is not complaining that his people never heard the Quran. They heard it. They knew it. *The complaint is that they heard it and put it in the category of "the voice we do not pick up for."*

[PAUSE]

And now ask the harder question.

Where, in your life, has the Quran been placed? Not theoretically. Practically. *In the actual category you maintain it in*, day after day.

Is it in the category of *the speech of your Lord that you orient your life around*? Or is it in the category of *the thing on the shelf, the thing you'll get to, the thing that's there for emergencies, the thing that's beautiful at funerals*?

Because if it is anywhere other than the first category, you have *adopted* it. You have *ittakhadhtahu*. You have made a choice about what role it plays.

The classical scholars — Ibn al-Qayyim is most explicit on this in *al-Fawāʾid* — listed levels of *hajr*: abandonment of *believing* in it, abandonment of *acting* on it even while claiming to believe, abandonment of *judging* by it, abandonment of *seeking healing through* it, abandonment of *contemplating* it. Most of us, he wrote, are guilty of *some* level. The question is not whether you are clean of *hajr*. The question is which form of *hajr* the Messenger ﷺ will have to mention to his Lord on your behalf.

[PAUSE]

The application is not abstract. It is concrete and small.

Open it. Today. Not to finish a juzʾ. Not to fulfill a quota. Open it the way you would pick up the phone of someone you have been avoiding — because you finally understand that the avoidance was never the absence of relationship. *The avoidance was the relationship.* And the relationship was rotten because it was a relationship of avoidance.

Pick up the phone. Listen to what He has to say to you.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Messenger's grief is the curriculum

We need to talk about something unusual that this ayah does.

Across the Quran, when Allah quotes the Prophet ﷺ in direct speech — *"the Messenger said"* — the moments are surprisingly few. Most of the Quran is Allah's own voice. The Prophet ﷺ is the recipient and conveyer, but his own *quoted words* in the Book itself are rare.

So when one appears, you have to ask why.

And here, the quoted words are not a sermon. They are not a teaching. They are a *complaint*. The Prophet ﷺ is grieving. He is bringing his sorrow to his Lord. And Allah preserved this grief in the Quran itself — meaning, this grief is now something Muslims will recite, generation after generation, until the end of time.

Why?

Because the Prophet's grief is part of what we are supposed to learn.

[PAUSE]

There is a way of relating to the Prophet ﷺ that treats him as a perfect, finished, untroubled figure — as if his life were a series of triumphs and his only emotions were serene. Reading him this way is comforting and it is wrong. It is wrong because the Quran itself does not let us off the hook this way. The Quran shows him grieving. It shows him chest-tight. It shows him asking *"how long will this take?"* It shows him here, in this ayah, complaining to his Lord about his own people's treatment of the message he was sent with.

And it does this because his grief is meant to *train* our grief.

Most of us go through life without grieving anything spiritually significant. We grieve money lost, opportunities missed, relationships broken. But when the Quran sits unopened, we feel nothing. When the prayer is rushed, we feel nothing. When a brother or sister drifts from the deen, we feel a vague concern that does not translate into any action.

The Prophet's grief, preserved in this ayah, is a calibration. It says: *this is what it should feel like when people abandon the Book.* The pain in his chest is the correct response. The shock that a Book like this could be left to gather dust is the correct response. If you feel nothing about the state of the Quran in your life and in the lives around you, *your spiritual nervous system is asleep*.

[PAUSE]

And there is something else.

Notice that the Prophet ﷺ does not respond to *hajr* with rage. He does not curse them. He does not call for their destruction. He raises his hands and says *yā Rabbi* — "O my Lord."

He brings the grief to its rightful Owner.

This is a teaching about what to do with religious pain. The pain of seeing the Book ignored, the pain of seeing the deen mocked, the pain of seeing your community drift — this pain has a destination. The destination is not social media. The destination is not bitter complaint to other people. The destination is *yā Rabbi*.

The Prophet ﷺ in this ayah models what to do when you cannot fix the people you were sent to: *take it back to the One who sent you*.

[PAUSE]

The application here is also concrete.

When you grieve over your own *hajr* — when you realize, reading this ayah, that there is a category in your own life called "the Quran I keep at a distance" — do not stay in self-loathing. Self-loathing does not move. *Yā Rabbi* moves.

Take the grief to Him. Tell Him: *Yā Rabbi, I have done this. I do not want to be the one You hear about in the Messenger's complaint. Bring me back to Your Book.*

The grief, brought to Him, becomes the doorway out of *hajr*. The grief, kept inside, becomes another room of *hajr*.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have seen.

The linguistic journey gave us three things: *al-Rasūl* — the courier reporting to the Sender. *Ittakhadhū* — the deliberate, intentional verb of categorization. *Mahjūrā* — abandonment, dismissal as ravings, repulsion as foul speech, all preserved as a state.

The thematic journey gave us two things: abandonment is not absence but a maintained posture, and the Messenger's grief is the curriculum we are meant to learn.

Notice how these are the same insight from two directions.

Linguistically, *ittakhadhū mahjūrā* tells us that the people *did something* to the Book — they actively assigned it a category. Thematically, abandonment-as-posture tells us that ignoring the Quran is *something we are doing*, daily, on purpose, to maintain the safe distance. The grammar and the lived experience are pointing at the same reality: **there is no neutral relationship with the Quran. You either engage with it or you are actively maintaining its abandonment.**

Linguistically, *al-Rasūl* tells us this is a delivery report — a messenger reporting back to the Sender. Thematically, the Messenger's grief is the curriculum tells us that we are not bystanders in this report. *We are the recipients in the report.* What the Messenger ﷺ says to his Lord is the truth about us. And the question of which kind of *hajr* he will have to mention on our behalf — abandoning belief, abandoning practice, abandoning contemplation — is being answered by the way we live this week.

### Questions to Carry

1. If your relationship to the Quran were graphed honestly over the last year — opened/unopened, contemplated/skimmed, applied/ignored — which of Ibn al-Qayyim's levels of *hajr* would the graph reveal?

2. What demand do you suspect the Quran will make on you, if you actually opened it and listened? Is the avoidance protecting you from that demand?

3. When was the last time you grieved — actually grieved — over the state of the Book in your own life or the lives of those you love?

4. Where does your religious pain currently go? Inward into shame? Outward into bitterness? Or upward, *yā Rabbi*, the way the Messenger ﷺ took his?

5. If, on the Day this ayah is uttered, you had to listen to the Messenger ﷺ describe your relationship to the Book to his Lord — what would he say?

### One-Sentence Distillation

To abandon the Quran is not to forget it but to *adopt* a posture toward it — and the Messenger himself will one day name that posture before the One who sent him.

### The Closing Invitation

The invitation is small and it is tonight.

Open it. Not for a quota. Not for a routine. Open it the way you would finally pick up the phone of someone you have been avoiding — because the avoidance was the rot, and the engagement is the cure.

Read one ayah slowly. Sit with it. Ask what it is asking of you. Answer.

That is how you exit *mahjūrā*. You exit it the way you entered it — one small daily act at a time, reversed.

### Du'ā

Yā Rabb, do not let us be the ones whose category-of-the-Quran is *the abandoned thing in our house*. Do not let Your Messenger ﷺ have to mention our names in his complaint. Open this Book to us, and open us to this Book, before the day it is too late to open anything at all.
