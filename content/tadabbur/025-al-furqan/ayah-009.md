# When the Accusers Lose Their Way

## The Geometry of a Lost Path — Surah Al-Furqan, Ayah 9

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> ٱنظُرْ كَيْفَ ضَرَبُوا۟ لَكَ ٱلْأَمْثَـٰلَ فَضَلُّوا۟ فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ سَبِيلًۭا

**Transliteration:**
> *Unẓur kayfa ḍarabū laka-l-amthāla fa-ḍallū fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā.*

**Translation:**
> "Look how they strike comparisons against you — and so they have lost their way, and cannot find any path."

---

### Rebuilding the World

To feel this ayah, you have to stand inside what came just before it.

The man being addressed is the Prophet ﷺ. And in the verses immediately preceding this one, his enemies have done something strange. They have not engaged his message. They have not refuted his arguments. They have, instead, started to *list things about him* — like a prosecutor presenting a case to a jury that has already made up its mind.

*"What kind of messenger is this — he eats food. He walks in the markets. Why isn't an angel sent down with him to be a co-warner? Why isn't a treasure cast to him? Why doesn't he have a garden to eat from?"*

These are not theological objections. Read them carefully. They are *social* objections. They are the kind of accusations you make when you have decided someone is beneath you and you are reaching for reasons to justify a feeling you already have.

He eats food. He walks in markets. As if eating and walking are crimes. As if the dignity of prophethood requires the prophet to never be hungry, never thirsty, never tired, never seen at the bazaar buying bread.

This is the climate. The Prophet ﷺ stands in Makkah. He has spent years calling people to a single, beautiful message — that there is one God, that human beings are accountable, that mercy is real. And the response he is receiving, in this moment, is not a rebuttal. It is *mockery dressed as critique*.

You know this climate. Anyone who has ever tried to say something true in a hostile room knows it. The objections you receive are not about your ideas. They are about your shoes. Your accent. The fact that you eat food.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Now watch what Allah does.

He does not refute the accusations. He does not say *"and tell them that prophets have always eaten food."* He does not produce a list of past prophets who walked in markets.

Instead, He says one word to His Messenger:

*Look.*

Just look. Look at what their accusations have done — not to you, but to *them*. And in the eight short words that follow, the entire architecture of mockery collapses on itself, and we see something stunning: that when you spend your life striking the people of guidance with comparisons, **the geometry of your own world starts to come apart**.

Stay with me. There is a physics here. And by the end of this ayah, you will see it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Unẓur*: The Command That Turns the Camera

Before we even reach the verb, notice what is happening at the level of *who is speaking to whom*.

Allah is addressing the Prophet ﷺ. The accusers have been talking *about* the Prophet ﷺ in the previous verses. Their tongues have been busy with him. And Allah — instead of speaking to them, instead of correcting them, instead of even mentioning them as the addressees — turns to His Messenger and says: *Look.*

This is a small grammatical fact with an enormous consequence. **The accusers are not given the dignity of being addressed.** They are made into a spectacle for the Prophet ﷺ to observe. They have made him their object of mockery; Allah makes them his object of contemplation.

Notice something. In Arabic, when you want to direct someone's attention to something *worth seeing*, you don't say *raʾā* (he saw, passive perception). You say *unẓur* — from the root *n-ẓ-r* — which carries the meaning of *deliberate, considered looking*. The same root gives us *naẓar*, the kind of looking that includes *thinking about what you see*.

So Allah is not telling the Prophet ﷺ: *notice them*. He is telling him: *contemplate them*. Look at them the way a scientist looks at a specimen. The way a doctor looks at a symptom. The way someone calm looks at someone falling apart.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. If you have ever been on the receiving end of mockery, you know that the natural human response is to look *at yourself*. To start checking your shoes, your voice, your reflection in the mirror, asking: *is what they're saying about me true? Is there something wrong with me?*

Mockery's whole power is that it turns its target inward. It makes the mocked person become their own jury.

And Allah — with one word — refuses to let the Prophet ﷺ do this. He does not say *defend yourself*. He does not say *ignore them*. He says: *take your eyes off yourself and look at them.*

This is not avoidance. This is not denial. This is something far more remarkable. **It is the redirection of the prophetic gaze from the wound to the wounder** — not for the sake of revenge, but because Allah is about to show His Messenger something that will reframe the entire experience.

What the Prophet ﷺ is about to see is not their power. It is their lostness.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two — *Ḍarabū laka-l-amthāl*: The Strange Verb of Striking Comparisons

Now we come to one of the most layered phrases in this ayah. *Ḍarabū laka-l-amthāl.* Three Arabic words, and each one is doing something the English translation cannot quite carry.

Let's teach the structure first.

In Arabic, when you want to say *"to give an example"* or *"to make a comparison,"* you do not use a soft, abstract verb. You use *ḍaraba* — from the root *ḍ-r-b*, which in its primary meaning is *to strike*, *to hit*. The same root gives you the word for striking a coin, striking a tent peg into the ground, striking a path with your feet.

So when Arabic says *ḍaraba mathalan* — literally, *he struck a comparison* — there is a kinetic violence in the phrase that English flattens into *"he gave an example."*

A *mathal* (from the root *m-th-l*) is a likeness, a comparison, a parable. It is what you reach for when you cannot describe something directly and you need to say *it is like such-and-such*.

Now read the phrase again, with the literal weight intact:

> *They have struck comparisons at you.*

[PAUSE]

Let this land. Their objections — *he eats food, he walks the markets, why no angel, why no garden* — these are not described by Allah as arguments. They are described as *strikes*. As *comparisons hurled at* the Prophet ﷺ.

Notice the preposition: *laka* — *at you, against you, with respect to you.* The comparisons are not just *about* him in some neutral descriptive sense. They are *aimed*.

And here is what is remarkable. A *mathal* — a comparison — is meant to *clarify*. When you say *life is like a journey*, the comparison is supposed to illuminate something about life. The whole purpose of striking a comparison is to *reveal* a meaning by mapping it onto something familiar.

But what happens when you strike comparisons at someone for whom no honest comparison applies? When you reach for analogies and similes to describe a reality whose dimensions exceed your categories?

You produce nonsense. You produce: *he eats food, like an ordinary man*. *He walks markets, like a merchant.* As if the prophet's humanity disqualifies him from prophecy. The mockery only works if you accept the unstated premise — that a real messenger must be other than human. But this premise is *itself* a *mathal*, a comparison the accusers have manufactured. They have invented an imaginary template of what a prophet *should* look like, and then accused the actual prophet of failing to match the template they themselves fabricated.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Their *amthāl* — their comparisons — are not descriptions of reality. They are *projections*. They are the accusers showing you, in the very structure of their accusations, what they think a divine messenger ought to be: rich, garden-owning, accompanied by spectacle, exempt from human need.

Their *amthāl* reveal them, not him.

And this is the first hint of why the next word — *ḍallū*, *they have lost their way* — is not a separate punishment but the *natural consequence* of what they have just done. Because once you have spent your effort building a fictional template of reality and then attacking what fails to match it, **you have lost the ability to see reality itself**.

You have struck so many false comparisons that the real has slipped from your hands.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three — *Fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā*: The Asymmetry of Path

Now we arrive at what is, linguistically, the most haunting phrase in the ayah.

*Fa-ḍallū* — *and so they have lost their way*. The *fa* here is not a neutral conjunction. It is the *fa* of consequence. *And so* — *as a direct result of striking those comparisons*, they have become lost. The lostness is not separate from the mockery. The mockery *is* the lostness.

Then comes the closing phrase, which is where the geometry becomes precise:

*Fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā* — *and they cannot find any path.*

There is a deliberate asymmetry here that the translation cannot easily carry. Let's open it.

In Arabic, there are two main words for *path* or *way*. *Ṭarīq* — a track, a road, the kind of path many people might walk. And *sabīl* — a path that has direction and destination, a path that *leads somewhere*. *Sabīl* is the word the Quran uses for *sabīl Allāh* — the path of Allah. It is a path of orientation, of purpose, of *going toward*.

[PAUSE]

Notice the word that closes this ayah. Not *ṭarīqan* — *a road*. But *sabīlā* — *a path with direction*.

This matters. Allah is not saying merely that they have lost a road, the way a traveler might lose the trail. He is saying something more structural. **They cannot locate a direction. They cannot find a way that leads.**

And then notice the verb: *yastaṭīʿūna*. From the root *ṭ-w-ʿ*, which carries the sense of *being able to, having the capacity for*. In Form X — *istaṭāʿa* — it intensifies into *to be capable of, to have the power for*. The negation *lā yastaṭīʿūna* therefore means more than *they do not find*. It means *they are not even able to*. The capacity itself is gone.

Stay with this. Allah is not describing a moment of confusion. He is describing the **loss of the faculty by which paths are found**. They have not just missed the road. They have lost the inner compass that locates roads.

[PAUSE]

And now here is the thing that, when you see it, you cannot unsee.

Notice the indefinite ending: *sabīlā* — not *al-sabīl* (the path), but *sabīlā* (any path, a path, any path whatsoever).

In Arabic, the indefinite is generalizing. It opens the category to its widest scope. So Allah is not saying *they cannot find the right path.* He is saying *they cannot find any path at all.*

Let this land.

You might expect the verse to say *"so they have lost the true path"* — meaning, they have failed to find Islam, they have failed to find the right way among many ways. But that is not what is said. What is said is more devastating: **they cannot find any way at all**.

Why?

Because the moment you commit yourself to mocking the messengers, you have not merely chosen the wrong path. You have *destroyed the conditions under which any path is locatable*. A path requires a destination. A destination requires the recognition of something higher, something worth moving toward. Mockery of the prophets is mockery of the very idea that there is something higher worth moving toward. So the mocker, by the structure of his own mockery, has flattened the moral landscape into a desert with no horizon.

There is no path because there is nowhere to go.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

The linguistic construction has shown us something that we now need to follow into the human heart.

We have seen that *unẓur* turns the gaze from the wounded to the wounders.
We have seen that *ḍarabū laka-l-amthāl* describes mockery as *self-projection* — the accusers reveal themselves in the shape of their accusations.
And we have seen that *fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā* describes a loss not of one path but of *path-finding itself*.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Self-Defeat of Mockery

Imagine someone walking through a forest who decides, for reasons of their own, that they hate trees.

Every tree they pass, they kick at. Every branch they reach, they snap. Every root they trip on, they curse. They spend hours of their walk doing nothing but expressing their contempt for the trees.

Now ask yourself: at the end of that walk, **are they still oriented in the forest?**

The answer, of course, is no. Because the trees were not just objects of their contempt. The trees were the *landmarks*. They were how the walker would have known where they were and where they were going. The very thing they spent their energy attacking was the structure that would have located them.

This is what mockery of the truth does to the mocker.

[PAUSE]

The accusers in this ayah are not random. They are the leaders of Quraysh. They are intelligent, articulate, capable men who have decided, as a matter of social and political calculation, to mock the Prophet ﷺ. And what Allah is showing us — through this single eight-word verse — is that **mockery is not a cost-free strategy**. It is not a neutral rhetorical move. It is a structural commitment that, once made, *unmoors the mocker from reality*.

Because here is what mockery requires. Mockery requires you to look at something and decide, *before you have understood it*, that it is beneath your serious engagement. The whole posture of mockery is a refusal to look carefully. It is a defense mechanism that protects you from the discomfort of taking the mocked thing seriously.

But truth is precisely the kind of thing that *only reveals itself to careful looking*. Truth is the kind of thing that gives itself away when you sit with it patiently and asks for nothing more than your honest attention.

So when you commit to mockery as your stance toward the prophetic message, you have committed to a posture that, by its very structure, *cannot receive truth*. You have decided in advance that this will not be serious — and now no amount of seriousness can reach you.

[PAUSE]

This is why the ayah's logic is *fa-ḍallū* — *and so they got lost*. Not as punishment. As consequence. The lostness is built into the act. **You cannot mock the compass and then complain that you have lost direction.**

And here's the part that should make every one of us, sitting with this ayah, feel the weight of it personally.

We do this. Not in some grand civilizational sense — but in the small motions of our own days. Someone says something true that we don't want to hear. And before we engage with what they said, we find ourselves reaching for some feature of *them* to mock. Their accent. Their job. Their history. Their appearance. Anything that lets us not engage with the content.

And in that small moment, the same physics applies. The thing they were trying to tell us — which might have been the very thing we needed to hear — is now beyond our reach. Because we built the wall ourselves. We struck a *mathal* at them, and the *mathal* became our prison.

The danger of this ayah is not just that it describes Quraysh. It is that **it describes a human pattern**. And when we recognize the pattern in ourselves, the question becomes: *what truths am I currently mocking my way out of receiving?*

What would I rather laugh at than listen to?

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is one of the simplest and hardest disciplines in the spiritual life. It is the discipline of *catching yourself in the moment of mockery* and asking: *what am I refusing to take seriously right now? And what am I afraid would happen if I did?*

Because every act of mockery is, at its root, an act of fear. Fear that the mocked thing might actually require something of you.

To set down the mockery is to make oneself available to be changed by what is true. And that is the doorway out of *ḍalāl*. It is the recovery of *sabīl*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two — The Consolation Hidden in *Unẓur*

Now we turn to the other side of this ayah — the side that faces the Prophet ﷺ, and through him, faces every person who has ever tried to do something true in a hostile environment.

Think about what it feels like to be mocked.

Not in the abstract. In the specific. You stand in a room. You say something you believe — something you have thought about carefully, something that costs you to say. And the response you receive is laughter. Or a shrug. Or an accusation that has nothing to do with what you said but everything to do with who you are: *who do you think you are, anyway? Look at you. Look at where you come from.*

[PAUSE]

The natural reaction is two-fold. First, *self-doubt*: maybe they're right. Maybe there is something wrong with me. Maybe I shouldn't have spoken. And second, *resentment*: how dare they. They have no idea who I am. They have no idea what this cost me.

Allah, in this ayah, is not interested in either of those reactions for His Messenger.

He gives him a third response. **He gives him perspective.**

*Unẓur* — *look* — *kayfa ḍarabū laka-l-amthāl* — *at how they have struck comparisons at you*. The Prophet ﷺ is invited to step *outside* the experience of being mocked, and to look at the mockers from a higher vantage point. The vantage point of someone who can see what their mockery is actually doing — not to him, but to them.

This is one of the great gifts of revelation. **Revelation gives the believer altitude.**

When you are inside the experience of being mocked, you are at ground level, surrounded. Every comparison they strike at you feels like a wound. But when revelation reframes the moment, you suddenly see it from above — and from above, you see something the mockers cannot see. You see that *their accusations are scattering them*. That every *mathal* they strike is not landing on you; it is landing on the floor at their feet, and they are losing their footing.

The Prophet ﷺ is given altitude in this ayah. And every believer who reads it inherits a fragment of that altitude.

[PAUSE]

Here is the consolation, made precise. **The verse does not say *they will not harm you*. It says *they have lost their way*.**

Notice the difference. Allah is not promising the Prophet ﷺ that mockery will have no cost. Mockery did have a cost. It hurt. Years of his life were spent under its weight. The Quran is full of verses comforting him in his grief at the rejection he was experiencing.

What Allah is promising is something different. He is promising that *the weight you are carrying is not the final word about reality*. It feels like the mockers are powerful. It feels like they have set the terms. It feels like the world belongs to them.

But *look* — really look, with the eyes of someone who can see what is actually happening — and you will see that they are unraveling. Their comparisons are not building a case against you. Their comparisons are *evidence of their own dissolution*.

The path you are on — narrow, hated, rejected — is still a path. It still leads somewhere. *Sabīl* still applies to you.

The path they are on — broad, celebrated, full of laughter — is no path at all. *Lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā*. They cannot even find a way.

[PAUSE]

The practical application of this theme is for anyone in this room who has ever been mocked for taking faith seriously. Or for taking truth seriously. Or for taking your soul seriously in a culture that finds soul-seriousness embarrassing.

The application is this: **you have permission to look up.**

You do not have to defend yourself against every accusation. You do not have to refute every mockery. You do not have to carry the weight of responding to every shrug and every rolled eye.

Sometimes the prophetic response is simply to take Allah's invitation: *unẓur*. *Look*. Take the altitude. See what they cannot see — that the people striking at you are losing their own way in the act of striking. And the path under your feet, for all its narrowness, still goes somewhere.

That is enough. That is more than enough.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what this ayah has shown us, from two directions.

From the side of the mockers, we have seen that **mockery is not a rhetorical strategy. It is a structural commitment to lostness.** When you spend yourself striking comparisons at the truth, you do not merely fail to find truth. You lose the faculty by which any path is found. Your *amthāl* become your maze.

From the side of the mocked, we have seen that **the Prophet ﷺ is not asked to defend himself. He is given altitude.** He is invited to look — really look — at what his accusers are doing to themselves. And in that gaze, the experience of being mocked is transfigured. Not into triumph. Into perspective.

These are the same insight from two directions. Mockery is its own punishment. Truth is its own consolation. The mocker loses the path. The truth-bearer keeps it. *Fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā* — *they cannot find any path* — is the dark mirror of the verse's hidden affirmation: *but you, O Messenger, you are still on one.*

[PAUSE]

### The Architecture

There is one more thing to notice, now that we have done the work.

Read the ayah again, and watch its shape:

*Unẓur* — *look*
*kayfa ḍarabū laka-l-amthāl* — *at how they struck comparisons at you*
*fa-ḍallū* — *and so they got lost*
*fa-lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā* — *and they cannot find any path*

The verse begins with an *act of looking* — and ends with an *inability to find a way*. Sight at the top, blindness at the bottom. The Prophet ﷺ is given vision; the mockers are stripped of orientation. The verse itself, in its grammatical movement, *enacts the very asymmetry it describes*.

The one who is told *unẓur* sees. The ones who refused to look — who chose mockery over contemplation — *lā yastaṭīʿūna sabīlā*.

The architecture proves the meaning. **Whoever is willing to look, can find a path. Whoever refuses to look, loses the path entirely.**

### Questions to Carry

- *Where in my own life have I struck comparisons at someone — at some teaching, at some truth — to avoid having to take it seriously?*

- *When I am mocked for what I believe, do I reach for self-defense, or am I willing to take the altitude that revelation offers and simply look?*

- *What is the difference between a* ṭarīq *and a* sabīl *in my own life right now? Am I on a road, or am I on a path that leads somewhere?*

- *Whose mockery have I been allowing to set the terms of my own seriousness?*

- *If I imagine the people who hurt me from above — from the altitude of* unẓur *— what do I see that I could not see at ground level?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

> *To mock the messengers of truth is not to defeat them; it is to become the kind of person who cannot find a path anywhere — while the messenger, simply by looking, retains the way.*

---

### The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this ayah?

Perhaps just this: a slight loosening of the grip. The grip we hold on the mockery we receive. The grip we hold on the mockery we offer.

Both grips, this ayah suggests, are paths to lostness. Receiving mockery as if it were the final word about us — that is its own form of *ḍalāl*. Offering mockery as if it were a clever defense against truth — that is the deeper form.

The invitation is to set both down. To let *unẓur* do its work. To let the altitude of revelation lift us out of the small dramas of being seen and unseen, mocked and praised, defended and accused. To return to the path — the one that, however narrow, still goes somewhere — and to keep walking.
