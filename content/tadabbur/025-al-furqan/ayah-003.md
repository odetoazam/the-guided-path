# The Gods Who Cannot Even Save Themselves

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> وَٱتَّخَذُوا۟ مِن دُونِهِۦٓ ءَالِهَةًۭ لَّا يَخْلُقُونَ شَيْـًۭٔا وَهُمْ يُخْلَقُونَ وَلَا يَمْلِكُونَ لِأَنفُسِهِمْ ضَرًّۭا وَلَا نَفْعًۭا وَلَا يَمْلِكُونَ مَوْتًۭا وَلَا حَيَوٰةًۭ وَلَا نُشُورًۭا

**Transliteration:**
*Wattakhadhū min dūnihi ālihatan lā yakhluqūna shay'an wa hum yukhlaqūn, wa lā yamlikūna li-anfusihim ḍarran wa lā nafʿan, wa lā yamlikūna mawtan wa lā ḥayātan wa lā nushūrā.*

**Translation:**
> Yet they have taken besides Him gods who create nothing but are themselves created — who hold no power to harm or benefit even themselves, and who own neither death, nor life, nor resurrection.

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

Surah Al-Furqan opens with a thunderclap. Ayah 1 declares: *Tabāraka — blessed and exalted — is the One who sent down al-Furqan, the Criterion, upon His servant.* Ayah 2 follows immediately: *the One to whom belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth, who has not taken a son, who has no partner in dominion, who created everything and proportioned it with precise measure.*

That second ayah is a portrait. Allah is painted in seven brushstrokes: dominion, no son, no partner, creator of everything, the One who measured and proportioned. It is a face. It is an identity.

And then — ayah 3.

The very next breath. The very next sentence. Allah turns the camera, and we see what humans have built to stand alongside that face.

[PAUSE]

The architecture of these two ayahs is forensic. Whatever was *affirmed* of Allah in ayah 2 will be *denied* of the false gods in ayah 3. The mirror is exact. Allah created everything — the false gods create nothing. Allah owns the dominion — they own not even harm to themselves. Allah is *al-Khāliq* — they are *yukhlaqūn*, the created.

This is not a sermon. It is a courtroom.

### The World Behind the Words

To feel this ayah, you have to stand in Mecca around the Kaʿbah in the late period of the Prophet's ﷺ mission. Three hundred and sixty idols ringed that sanctuary. They had names. They had biographies. They had priests. Hubal stood at the center. Al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā and Manāt had shrines outside the city. Each tribe had a personal deity. Each household had a small statue.

These idols were not foolish to the people who served them. They were ancestral. They were emotionally-loaded. They were the way you asked for rain, for a son, for protection in caravan. People kissed them. People wept before them. People made pilgrimage to them.

To stand in front of the Kaʿbah in 622 CE and say: *the gods you serve cannot even harm or benefit themselves* — was not a theological statement. It was a demolition.

But this ayah is not only about idols of stone. It is about every god the human heart has ever manufactured. Every "alongside Allah." Every "instead of Allah." Every quiet substitute we lean on when we cannot bear to lean only on Him.

### The Hook

Stay with me on this. Allah does not say these false gods are *evil*. He does not say they are *enemies*. He says something stranger and more devastating.

He says they are **helpless**.

He chose, out of all the things He could have said, to attack them at the level of *power* — and specifically at the level of power *over their own selves*.

Why? Why this attack? Why not "they are lies"? Why not "they are demonic"? Why this very particular angle: *they cannot even help themselves*?

There is a reason. And when we see it, the way we look at every false god in our own lives is going to change.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to walk through three features of this ayah. Each one, on its own, would be enough. Together, they reveal the architecture.

---

### Element One: *Ittakhadhū* — The Verb of Manufacture

#### The Concept First

In Arabic, there is a difference between *finding* something and *taking* something. There is a difference between *having* a god and *adopting* one. There is a difference between *encountering* truth and *manufacturing* a substitute.

Arabic has a verb form — Form VIII, called *iftaʿala* — that carries reflexive intensity. It means: *the subject did this for himself, by his own initiative, for his own purposes.* It is not passive. It is not accidental. It is a deliberate act of self-direction.

When you say in everyday English, "I picked up a job" versus "I took on a job" — the second one is closer to Form VIII. You owned it. You chose it. It is now yours because of what you did, not because of what happened to you.

#### Opening the Word

Allah does not say: *they had gods besides Him.*
He does not say: *gods existed besides Him.*
He does not say: *they were given gods besides Him.*

He says: **wattakhadhū** — *they took for themselves.*

The verb is from the root أ-خ-ذ (to take), in Form VIII (*iftaʿala*). The reflexive is built into the morphology. The taking is for-themselves. The choosing is from-themselves.

#### Sit With This

Let's sit with what this verb does. It assigns ownership of the act of shirk to the human being.

These gods did not descend from the sky. They were not imposed. They were not necessary. Allah is telling us: **you took them. You manufactured them. You chose them. You constructed them in your own house, with your own hands, for your own purposes.**

Notice something. The Quran uses this exact verb — *ittakhadha* — over and over again when describing false gods. *Ittakhadhū min dūnihi ālihatan.* *Ittakhadhū al-ʿijl.* *Ittakhadhū aḥbāra-hum arbāban.* It is a deliberate vocabulary choice. Every time false worship enters the picture, Allah selects the verb that emphasizes **human authorship** of the false god.

Here's what's remarkable. This means there has never, in the history of humanity, been an *innocent* false god. There has never been a god that simply *appeared* and demanded worship. Every false deity, in the Quranic worldview, is a constructed object. A project. A taking-for-oneself.

[PAUSE]

This is heavy. Because it means the false god in your life, whatever it is — was not handed to you. You did not stumble onto it. You did not inherit it like a disease.

You took it. *Ittakhadhta-hu*. You manufactured it for yourself, for some purpose your nafs wanted to serve.

#### The Concluding Statement

The first word of this ayah's predicate already convicts the human heart. Before Allah even tells us *what* these gods cannot do, He has already told us *who built them*.

### Element Two: *Min Dūnihi* — Beside, Not Instead

#### The Concept First

Here is something English-speakers miss. There are two completely different ways to commit shirk.

You can replace Allah. You can say, "I do not believe in Allah; I believe in this other thing." That is the rare form. Almost no one in the Quran does this. The Pharaoh comes close. A few others. But it is uncommon.

Or — you can keep Allah, and add things alongside Him. "I believe in Allah. I also venerate this saint. I also fear this evil eye. I also depend on this person. I also need this idol for rain." This is the common form. It is the form the Meccans practiced. They did not deny Allah. They acknowledged Him as *al-Khāliq*. They simply added intermediaries.

Arabic has a precise preposition for this second kind of shirk. *Min dūn.* Literally, *from below*, or *besides*, or *short of*. It means: not *instead of*, but *alongside, in addition to, in the space below*.

#### Opening the Phrase

Allah does not say *badalan minhu* — *instead of Him*. He does not say *ghayrahu* — *other than Him* in the absolute sense.

He says: **min dūnihi** — *besides Him, alongside Him, in the space of dependence below Him.*

#### Stay With This

This is diagnostic. The Meccans of the seventh century, and the human heart of every century, do not typically *replace* Allah. We *add* to Him.

We say: I trust in Allah — *and also* in my bank account.
I rely on Allah — *and also* on this person whose approval I cannot bear to lose.
I fear Allah — *and also* what people will say.
I worship Allah — *and also* my image, my career, my reputation, the version of me that lives in other people's minds.

*Min dūnihi.* In the space below Him. Alongside.

Here's what's remarkable about this construction: it tells us that shirk does not require atheism. Shirk requires only that something else share the throne. The throne does not need to be vacated. It only needs to be *crowded*.

[PAUSE]

And the crowding is the thing. Because tawhid — the doctrine of Allah's oneness — is not just metaphysical. It is psychological. The heart was built to depend on One. When it crowds the throne, it splits itself, and the splitting is the suffering.

#### The Concluding Statement

The phrase *min dūnihi* tells us where to look for our false gods. Not in temples. In the *alongside*. In the small additions we make to Allah's sufficiency. In the silent partners we name in our hearts when we are afraid.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Cascade of Powerlessness

#### The Concept First

In rhetorical Arabic, there is a device called *taʿdād* — enumeration. When a writer wants to crush an opponent's claim, he does not give one counter-argument. He gives a cascade. Each item in the list is itself sufficient. The list as a whole is a verdict.

But there is something more specific happening here. The list in this ayah is not random. It is a *concentric collapse* — each ring tighter than the last.

#### Opening the Cascade

Watch how the ayah's denials are structured. Allah denies the false gods six things, in three pairs:

**Pair One:** *lā yakhluqūna shay'an wa hum yukhlaqūn* — they create nothing, and they themselves are created.

**Pair Two:** *wa lā yamlikūna li-anfusihim ḍarran wa lā nafʿan* — they hold no power, for themselves, of harm or benefit.

**Pair Three:** *wa lā yamlikūna mawtan wa lā ḥayātan wa lā nushūrā* — they own neither death, nor life, nor resurrection.

#### The Architecture of the Collapse

Let me show you what is happening, because this is one of the most precise pieces of rhetoric in the Quran.

**Pair One** addresses the cosmic scale. *Can you create?* The answer: not only do you not create — you yourselves are *being created*. You are on the wrong end of the verb. You are objects, not subjects. *Yukhluqūn* is passive. The grammar itself reduces them.

**Pair Two** zooms in. The cosmos is too large. Forget creation. Let's ask the smallest possible question: *Can you protect or benefit yourselves?* And here is the linguistic dagger — **li-anfusihim**, *for themselves*. Not "do they have power to harm or benefit *others*" — that comparison would be too generous. The ayah lowers the bar to the floor: can they help *their own selves*?

The answer is no. *Lā yamlikūna li-anfusihim ḍarran wa lā nafʿan.*

Notice something. *Yamlik* — to own, to possess, to have authority over. The verb of *malakūt*, of dominion, of kingdom. The same root that, in ayah 2, was used for Allah: *alladhī lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa al-arḍ*. Allah owns the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. These false gods do not own *one wound or one benefit on their own bodies*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. This is the second ring of the collapse, and it is unbearable for the false god. Whatever else can be said about a deity, surely it can at least *take care of itself*. Surely an idol can keep its own statue from being toppled. Surely a saint can keep his own grave from decay. Surely a manufactured god can defend its own image.

*Wa lā yamlikūna li-anfusihim ḍarran wa lā nafʿan.*

No.

**Pair Three** is the final ring, and it is the heart of the verdict. *Wa lā yamlikūna mawtan wa lā ḥayātan wa lā nushūrā.* They own neither death, nor life, nor resurrection.

This is the deepest possible question. This is the question every human being asks when they are honest. *Where does my life come from? Who controls when it ends? What happens after?* These are the only three questions that finally matter. And the order in the ayah — *mawt, ḥayāt, nushūr* — death, life, resurrection — is staggering.

Why does death come first?

#### A Closer Look at *Mawt, Ḥayāt, Nushūr*

Classical commentators noticed this order and gave several readings, each adding a layer.

One reading: death is mentioned first because it is the most immediate threat to the human being. Of all the things you fear, death is the first thing you would want a god to control. Allah is saying: *your manufactured gods cannot even shield you from the very first thing you bought them to shield you from*.

Another reading: death-life-resurrection follows the human journey backward and forward. The false gods own no point on the timeline of existence. Not your beginning. Not your present. Not your end. Not your return.

Another reading: this is the precise sequence of *al-Bāʿith*, the Resurrector — the sequence of how Allah deals with souls. He gives death. He gives life. He raises again. The false gods own none of these moves. The chess board belongs entirely to One.

Each reading is a camera angle on the same reality: **a god that cannot manage the boundary between existence and non-existence is not a god at all.** It is a decoration. A psychological prop. A self-deception.

#### Sit With What the Cascade Has Done

Look back at the three pairs together:

> They create nothing — they are created.
> They cannot help themselves — much less you.
> They do not own death, life, or what comes after.

This is the structure of total dispossession. The false god has been stripped of cosmic power, then personal power, then existential power. By the end of the ayah, there is *nothing left for them to do*. They are dimensionless. They are zero.

And here is what's remarkable. Allah did not say *they are evil*. He did not say *they are demonic*. He did not say *they hate you*. He said something far more humiliating to a false god:

**They are helpless.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

#### Bridge

We have walked through three linguistic features. *Ittakhadhū* — they manufactured. *Min dūnihi* — alongside, not instead. *The cascade of powerlessness* — cosmic, personal, existential.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart, and the false gods we build inside it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Gods That Cannot Save Themselves

Imagine you are afraid. Truly afraid. Something has happened — a diagnosis, a phone call, a layoff, a betrayal. And in the middle of that fear, you reach for the thing that has always made you feel safe.

Maybe it is your savings account. Maybe it is a person — your spouse, your parent, a friend whose voice has always calmed you. Maybe it is your reputation, your degrees, your control over your routine. Maybe it is your image of yourself as someone competent and put-together.

Whatever it is — you reach for it. And in that reaching, you have just told the truth about what you actually trust.

This is what the Quran calls *ittikhādh ālihah*. The taking-of-gods. It does not require kneeling. It does not require incense. It only requires that, in the moment of fear, your heart turns toward something *min dūnihi* — alongside Him, in the space below Him — and asks it to save you.

[PAUSE]

Now ask the question this ayah is asking. *Can the thing you reached for even save itself?*

Your savings account — can it survive a market that decides otherwise tomorrow morning?
The person whose voice calms you — can they protect themselves from a heart attack at 4 a.m.?
Your reputation — can it withstand one rumor, one lie, one bad day on the internet?
Your competence — can it survive the moment your body finally gives out?

*Wa lā yamlikūna li-anfusihim ḍarran wa lā nafʿan.* They do not own — for themselves — even harm or benefit.

This ayah is not a polemic against ancient idols. It is a diagnostic for the modern soul. Because every single thing we lean on, *besides Allah*, has the exact same problem the idols of Mecca had: **it cannot even save itself.**

Look at the people who lean on wealth. The wealth itself has no defenses. One regulation, one war, one technology shift, and the empire is gone. *Mā lā yamliku li-nafsihi ḍarran wa lā nafʿan.*

Look at the people who lean on relationships. The person they lean on is themselves *yukhlaqūn* — *being created*, contingent, fragile, mortal. They will die. They will change. They will fail.

Look at the people who lean on their own bodies, their own minds, their own discipline. The body breaks. The mind fades. The discipline cracks under enough pressure.

Everything in the *min dūn* category — everything in the *alongside* — shares the idol's fundamental defect. *It cannot even own its own ending.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the practical reframe this ayah is offering you. The next time your heart leans on something other than Allah, ask one question:

*Can this thing save itself?*

Because if it cannot save itself — and nothing in the *min dūn* category can — then asking it to save you is a category error. You are asking a created thing to do an uncreated thing's job. You are asking a drowning man to be your lifeguard.

This is not a call to abandon every relationship, every resource, every plan. It is a call to put each one back in its category. Means are means. Causes are causes. None of them are gods. The One who *holds* the means and *commands* the causes is the only One worth leaning on.

#### What This Changes Tomorrow

Tomorrow, when fear comes — and it will — notice the first place your heart runs. That is the address of your false god.

Then say to it, gently, what Allah said in this ayah: *you cannot even save yourself.*

Then turn — not abandon the means, not despise them, not even refuse to use them — but *turn the leaning* — to the One who created them, who holds them, who can sustain them or remove them with a word.

This is *tawhid* lived. Not in argument. In the direction of the lean.

### Theme Two: Why Allah Attacks Them at the Level of *Help*, Not *Truth*

Notice what this ayah did *not* say.

It did not say: *they are false.*
It did not say: *they are lies.*
It did not say: *they do not exist.*

It said: *they cannot help.*

Why?

Because Allah, who knows the human heart better than the human heart knows itself, knows something about us. We do not abandon our false gods because we discover they are *false*. We abandon them when we discover they are *useless*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. This is one of the most psychologically precise moves in the Quran.

A person can be told, all their lives, that an idol is a piece of wood. They can know it intellectually. They can even laugh at it. And then, when their child gets sick, they will go and bow to it anyway. Because the idol is not, for them, primarily a *truth claim*. It is a *coping mechanism*. They did not adopt it because they thought it was true. They adopted it because it was *useful*.

This is why simply telling people "your god is false" rarely works. The argument lands on the wrong layer. The false god is not in their *intellect*. It is in their *fear*.

So Allah, with surgical accuracy, does not attack the false god's *truth*. He attacks the false god's *function*.

You wanted protection? It cannot protect even itself.
You wanted control over death? It cannot control its own.
You wanted creative power? It is itself created.

In other words: *the very reason you took this god is the reason you should let it go.*

[PAUSE]

This is *Furqan* — the Criterion. The Quran is named in this surah's first ayah as *al-Furqan*, the thing that separates. And here, in the third ayah, we see what it separates. It separates the One who *can* from the many who *cannot*. It separates the helper from the helpless. It separates the lean-worthy from the leaners.

#### Apply This to Yourself

What false god have you built because it served a function?

— Maybe you over-control because chaos terrified you, and control became your protector.
— Maybe you over-please because rejection terrified you, and approval became your shield.
— Maybe you over-work because failure terrified you, and achievement became your savior.
— Maybe you over-plan because uncertainty terrified you, and the plan became your god.

Each of these is *min dūnihi*. Each of these is *ittakhadhta-hu*. You took it. For yourself. Because it served a function.

Now ask the ayah's question. *Can the thing you took — save itself?*

Control cracks under enough pressure. Approval evaporates. Achievement is one bad year away from collapse. The plan dies the moment reality enters the room.

You took, as your god, something that is itself drowning.

#### The Way Out

The way out is not to despise the function. The function — the need for protection, the need to be loved, the need to matter, the need for some structure in the chaos — the function is real. It is *human*. Allah designed you to have those needs.

The way out is to redirect the *address* of the need. To take the lean off the helpless thing and put it on the One who is *li-nafsihi qā'im* — self-subsisting. *Al-Qayyūm*. The One who needs nothing to hold Him up because He is the One holding everything else up.

This is what *lā ilāha illā Allāh* really means. Not "there is no god" — that's only half. *Illā Allāh* — *except Allah*. The negation is not the destination. The destination is the affirmation. Empty the heart of every helpless lean — and then fill it with the One who actually holds everything.

#### What This Changes Tomorrow

Tomorrow, ask yourself: *what fear is this false god serving?*

Don't try to kill the false god first. That never works. Find the *fear underneath it*. Take that fear — directly — to Allah. Tell Him what you are afraid of. Tell Him what you have been leaning on instead of Him. Ask Him to be, for that fear, what your false god has been pretending to be.

He will be. *Wa man yatawakkal ʿalā Allāhi fa-huwa ḥasbuhu* — *whoever puts his trust in Allah, He is enough for him* (65:3).

This is the only god who can save Himself. And therefore the only god worth taking.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at ayahs 2 and 3 together. Stand back from them.

**Ayah 2** says of Allah:
- His is the dominion of heavens and earth
- He has not taken a son
- He has no partner in dominion
- He created everything
- He proportioned everything precisely

**Ayah 3** says of the false gods:
- They create nothing — they are themselves created
- They cannot harm or benefit even themselves
- They do not own death, life, or resurrection

Notice what is happening. Every claim made for Allah in ayah 2 is systematically inverted in ayah 3. Allah owns dominion — they do not own even harm to themselves. Allah created everything — they create nothing. Allah has no partner in dominion — and the proof is given in the next ayah: how could any of *these* be His partners?

This is *al-Furqan* in living form. The Criterion is not a doctrine. It is a *contrast image*. Place the One next to the many. Place the Creator next to the created. Place the self-subsisting next to the helpless. The criterion announces itself.

The surah is named for this very thing.

### Bringing It Together

Three linguistic discoveries. Two themes. One insight.

The linguistic work showed us: human beings *manufacture* (ittakhadhū) gods *alongside* (min dūn) Allah, and those gods cannot help even themselves (the cascade of powerlessness).

The thematic work showed us: every false god in our own lives shares the same defect — it cannot save itself — and Allah attacks them at the level of *help*, not *truth*, because that is the level on which we actually adopted them.

These are not two insights. They are the same insight from two directions:

**Anything alongside Allah is helpless. Therefore everything alongside Allah is the wrong place to lean.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What did I run to, the last time I was afraid? Could that thing save itself?
2. What false god have I taken because it serves a function — control, approval, achievement, image? What fear is underneath it?
3. Where in my life is something *min dūn Allāh* — alongside Him — quietly sharing His throne in my heart?
4. Have I confused the *means* (which are real) with the *Source* (who holds the means)?
5. If I knew, with full certainty, that none of my "alongside" gods could save themselves — how would I lean differently tomorrow?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> Every god you take alongside Allah is itself a drowning thing — and the only One worth leaning on is the One who needs no one to hold Him up.

### The Closing Invitation

You will not stop manufacturing false gods all at once. The nafs is industrious. It will build them faster than you can dismantle them.

But you can do one thing tonight. You can name one. Just one. The one your heart ran to today instead of Him. Name it. See it for what it is — a created, contingent, fragile thing that cannot even save itself. And then, gently, return the lean to the One who can.

Tomorrow there will be another. That is not failure. That is the human condition. The work is the *returning*. The work is the slow, patient migration of the heart from the *min dūn* to the *illā Hu* — from "alongside" to "only Him."

This is what the Criterion was sent for. Not to give you information. To give you a *way to tell the difference* — between what holds you and what is itself drowning.

### A Brief Du'a

> O Allah, You who own dominion, who created everything, who proportioned everything precisely — show me the gods I have taken alongside You. Strip them, gently, of the power my heart has assigned them. Move my lean back to You. You alone own death, life, and what comes after. Be enough for me. *Anta ḥasbī wa niʿma al-wakīl.*
