## Before the Text: The World These Words Fell Into

Let's not begin with Lut. Let's begin with the man who was reciting these words.

Picture Mecca. The Prophet ﷺ is somewhere in the middle of the hardest years of his life. The message has been out long enough that the novelty is gone and the hostility has set in like a permanent weather system. People he grew up with cross the street to avoid him. His followers are a small, frightened cluster — some of them being tortured, some already fled to Abyssinia. And the pressure being applied to him is not only mockery. It is a specific, calculated threat that runs all through the Meccan period and gets named explicitly elsewhere in the Quran: *we will drive you out.* Expulsion. Social death. The Quraysh eventually turned that threat into a formal boycott — a whole clan penned into a valley, cut off from food and trade, for three years.

That is the room these ayahs were recited into. And Surah Ash-Shu'ara is built, deliberately, to speak straight into that room.

Because this surah is not a collection of separate stories. It is one long, drumbeat sequence. Seven messengers, one after another — Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb — and almost every episode runs the same circuit: the people deny, the messenger warns, the messenger is threatened, deliverance comes for the few, destruction comes for the many. After most of them, the same two lines toll like a bell: *"Indeed in that is a sign — but most of them were not believers. And indeed your Lord — He is the Mighty, the Merciful."*

Read it out loud in sequence and you feel what it is doing. It is putting an arm around the Prophet ﷺ and saying: *you are not the first, and this is not new, and you already know how it ends.* Every messenger stood where you are standing. Every one of them was small and outnumbered and threatened. And every single time, the ground opened under the ones who were so sure of themselves.

Lut is the sixth station on that road. And his people reach for the exact weapon Mecca is holding over Muhammad ﷺ. Not swords, not yet. A threat of expulsion. *Stop, or you're out.*

So when the Meccan listener hears the crime of Lut's people — the surah has just described it, men approaching men, the first nation in all the worlds to institutionalize it — and then hears them defend that crime by threatening to *expel the one man telling them the truth*, something should turn over in the stomach of anyone in Mecca doing the same math. You are using the same weapon. Watch, very carefully, what happens to the last people who used it.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the thing I want you to carry into these seven short lines. This passage is famous for its ending — the fire, the rain, the wife who didn't make it. But the weight of it is not in the destruction. Anyone can narrate a punishment. The weight is in one word Lut chooses in the heat of being threatened — a word that tells you *exactly* where his heart was aimed — and in one word Allah uses for a woman who lived under a prophet's roof and still did not make the boat.

Two words. Watch these two words. Everything is in them.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### First: He Answered a Threat About His Body With a Truth About His Heart

Before we touch the ayah, I want to show you something about how Arabic builds a threat and how it builds a stance — because Lut is about to do something with grammar that most people miss entirely.

In Arabic, one very common way to place a person is to say: *he is "of" a group.* You take a category — a plural noun — and you slot the person into it with the little word *min*, "of / from among." *He is min al-fā'izīn* — of the winners. *She is min aṣ-ṣābirīn* — of the patient ones. It is a filing move. You are not describing a passing mood; you are assigning someone to a **category of people**, telling us which drawer they belong in.

Hold that. Now listen to the threat.

*La-in lam tantahi yā Lūṭu la-takūnanna mina l-mukhrajīn.*

"If you do not **desist**, O Lut, you will surely become **of the expelled**."

That verb, *tantahi* — from the root meaning to reach an end, to stop — is Form VIII, and it means to make yourself cease, to pull yourself back from something. They are not asking him to change his mind. They are ordering him to *stop reaching them at all.* Shut up. Withdraw. And then the threat: you will become *mina l-mukhrajīn* — filed into the drawer marked "the driven-out." The root here is *kh-r-j*, to exit, to be made to go out. Notice it is passive — *mukhrajīn*, those who are *made* to leave, forced out by someone else's hand. Their whole threat is about **place and body**. Your location. Your belonging. We will physically remove you from among us.

And notice, too, they call him by name. *Yā Lūṭu.* The vocative. This is a raised voice. A finger in the chest. It is intimate and hostile at once — the way you use someone's first name right before you threaten them.

Now hear his answer. And I want you to hear how he *borrows their own sentence.*

*Qāla innī li-'amalikum mina l-qālīn.*

"He said: indeed I am, of your deed, **of the detesters**."

Stay with me, because this is surgical. He takes their exact grammatical frame — *min* plus a plural participle, the filing move, "one of a category" — and he answers it in kind. They said, *you'll be of the expelled.* He says, *I am of the detesters.* Same shape. Same structure. Same music. But watch what he has done inside that identical frame. **They filed him by his fate. He files himself by his heart.** They talk about what will be done to his body. He talks about what is already settled in his soul. Their category was imposed from outside — *mukhrajīn*, made-to-leave. His category is chosen from inside — *qālīn*, the ones who burn against this. They threatened his geography. He answered with his allegiance.

A threat about where he'll sleep, answered by a truth about what he loves and what he cannot stand. That is not evasion. That is a man who cannot be reached by exile, because the thing they want to take from him — his place — is not where he lives. He lives in his stance.

[PAUSE]

And now the word itself. *Al-qālīn.* From the root *q-l-y*.

This is not the ordinary word for dislike. This root, in its literal, physical, kitchen-table meaning, is the word for **frying** — *qalā*, to roast something in a hot, dry pan until it sears. It is the word you would use for meat sizzling on iron. And the Arabs took that searing, that active burning-off, and made it a word for hatred: not a cool distaste, not a raised eyebrow, but a hatred that *cooks*, that has heat in it, that actively burns against the thing. When Lut says *innī... mina l-qālīn*, he is not saying "I mildly disapprove." He is saying **this thing sears me. I burn against it.**

So here is a man being threatened with homelessness, and instead of flinching, instead of negotiating, instead of softening to buy safety, he tells them the temperature of his heart. *You want to know if you can move me? You cannot even touch me. What I feel about this deed is a fire, and your threat is a breeze.*

But do not walk past the fire too fast — because *where he aims it* is the whole point. And that is the next thing.

### Second: He Burned Against the Deed — Not a Single Person

Here is a small experiment you already run every day.

Someone wrongs you — cuts you off, lies to you, betrays you. Listen to the sentence your heart generates in the next half-second. Almost always it is not *"that was a wrong action."* It is *"he is a snake."* *"She's toxic."* *"They're garbage."* Notice the grammar of your own anger. It leaps, instantly, from the deed to the doer. It converts a thing that was *done* into a thing that someone *is*. We are fusion machines. We melt the act and the person into one lump and then we hate the lump.

Now go back and look at exactly what Lut said. Read the object of his hatred.

*Innī **li-'amalikum** mina l-qālīn.*

"I am, **of your deed**, of the detesters."

Not *of you.* Not *innī lakum mina l-qālīn* — "I detest *you.*" He says *li-'amalikum* — for your **deed**, your action, your work. The root is *'-m-l*, plain labor, the thing you do with your hands and your days. He takes that searing hatred we just measured — the frying-pan fire — and he points it with total precision at the **act**. And he leaves the actors standing.

Sit with how strange and disciplined that is. He is being threatened by these very people. This is not a calm seminar. They have his exile in their hands. Any human being would let the hatred blur — *I hate what you do, and I hate you, and get away from me.* And the man does not do it. Under threat, in the heat, he keeps his hatred *aimed.* The fire is real, and the fire is total, and the fire is pointed at the deed and not the man.

Read the absence, because the absence is where the mercy lives. Arabic gave him the option to say *lakum* — "you." He had the perfect grammatical slot to hate the people and everyone would have understood. He left that slot empty and filled it with *'amalikum* instead. **That gap is deliberate.** A prophet of Allah, in the moment of maximum provocation, refusing to hate the human beings, and hating only the thing they do.

Why does that matter so much? Because his *job* is those people. A moment later he prays for himself, yes — but his mission, the reason he is standing in this hostile square at all, is that he still wants them saved. You cannot call people to Allah with a heart that has already written them off as garbage. The instant the deed and the doer fuse in your heart, da'wah is dead, because you are no longer inviting a person, you are managing a pest. Lut keeps them human. He hates the fire they've lit and still sees the hands that could drop the match.

One clean statement of what this single word reveals: **the measure of a heart is not whether it burns, but whether it can burn hot and still aim true.**

### Third: "Save Me From What They Do" — And the Old Woman Who Wasn't

Now the prayer, and then the exception — because they belong together.

*Rabbi najjinī wa-ahlī mimmā ya'malūn.*

"My Lord, save me and my family from what they do."

Look at the verb of rescue: *najjinī.* Root *n-j-w*. And this root carries a picture worth seeing before we go further. In the desert imagination, *najwah* is high ground — a raised piece of land, a plateau or a hillock that stands clear when the flood comes through the valley. To be *saved*, *najāt*, is at root **to be lifted onto the high place while the water takes the low ground.** Salvation is not the flood missing you by luck. It is elevation — being raised above the level where the destruction runs. And notice the form: *najjī* is Form II, the intensive, causative shape. He is not asking merely to survive. He is asking Allah to *actively lift us out,* to do the raising.

Now the object of the rescue — and here is another empty slot doing quiet work. Save me from *what?* You would expect *najjinī minhum* — "save me **from them.**" From these people. That is the natural sentence. Instead: *najjinī mimmā ya'malūn* — "save me **from what they do.**" From the **deed** again. That same root, *'-m-l.* Look at the consistency of this man. When he stated his hatred: the deed. When he prays for rescue: the deed. Twice in two lines he refuses to make it about the persons and keeps it about the act. He does not pray *against them.* He does not name them for destruction. He asks to be raised up out of the reach of the *deed* and everything it drags down with it.

The classical readers held this in two hands at once, and both hands are holding something real. One: save me from the *consequence* of what they do — the punishment their action is about to summon down on this whole place, don't let it take me and mine. Two: save me from the deed *itself* — from ever being contaminated by it, implicated in it, standing anywhere near it when the fire falls. You do not have to choose. He is asking for both: lift us above the act, and lift us above what the act brings.

[PAUSE]

And now Allah answers. And the answer is where the ground moves under your feet.

*Fa-najjaynāhu wa-ahlahu **ajma'īn.***

"So We saved him and his family — **all of them.**"

Feel the completeness of that word. *Ajma'īn.* "All. Every one. The whole of them." It is an emphasis word, a word that closes the door and seals it — *we saved him and his family, entirely, not one left behind.* You exhale. Everyone made it. The prophet's household, complete, lifted onto the high ground while the valley drowns.

And then the very next breath:

*Illā 'ajūzan fi l-ghābirīn.*

"**Except** an old woman among those who lagged behind."

[PAUSE]

*Except.* Right after *all.* The word *illā* is the knife that carves an exception out of the whole — and it lands on the heels of *ajma'īn*, "every one," so that the sentence gives you total safety and then, in the space of one word, takes a piece back out. We saved all of them. Except one.

And look — *look* — at what she is called. Not "his wife." Not "except for one of his family." She was, by any legal and social measure, part of the *ahl* — the household, the family he just prayed to have saved. Marriage put her inside the very word Lut used. And here, at the end, Allah does not call her his wife. He does not call her by any name. He calls her *'ajūzan* — "an **old woman**" — and it is *indefinite*, "**an** old woman," stripped of every tie, filed instead into a category: *fi l-ghābirīn*, "among **those who lagged behind.**"

That root, *gh-b-r*, is worth hearing. *Al-ghābirūn* are those who stay back, who remain when the others move on, who linger. And the same root gives *ghubār* — **dust.** The lingerers, the ones who stay behind with the settling dust when the caravan of salvation moves forward. She did not go up onto the high ground. She stayed down in the low place with the dust.

Now hold the two halves of this against each other, because this is the asymmetry the whole passage was building toward. Lut prayed: save me *and my family.* Allah saved him *and his family — all of them.* The prayer was answered exactly, word for word — *ahl* for *ahl.* And then, precisely, surgically, Allah shows you that being inside the word *ahl* was never the thing that saved anyone. She was *ahl*. She was in the house. She was married to the messenger, ate at his table, heard every warning first, watched every sign up close. And she is not lifted. She is left in the dust with the ones whose deed she shared.

The bond didn't save her. Her heart's allegiance sank her. And so Allah takes away even her title — *wife* — and hands her the only label that was ever actually load-bearing: not *what she was to Lut,* but *whom she stood with.* "An old woman, among the lagging."

[PAUSE — long]

Then the two closing hammer-blows, quick and heavy:

*Thumma dammarna l-ākharīn.* "Then We destroyed the others." The verb *dammarnā*, root *d-m-r*, Form II — the intensive again, but this time the intensity is destruction: not merely to ruin but to ruin *utterly*, to grind down to nothing. And the people are called only *al-ākharīn* — "the others." Not named. Just: the other category. The ones not on the high ground.

*Wa-amṭarnā 'alayhim maṭaran fa-sā'a maṭaru l-mundharīn.* "And We rained upon them a rain — and evil was the rain of those who had been warned." And here the root *m-ṭ-r*, rain, does something bitter. Rain is the Quran's own picture of *mercy* — Allah sends down *water* to revive the dead earth, over and over, as His gift. Here that same word for the sky's gift becomes the vehicle of ruin. The heavens open — and what comes down is not life but stones. The most familiar sign of mercy, inverted into the instrument of judgment. And the final label on the dead is *al-mundharīn* — "those who had been warned." Root *n-dh-r*, to warn. Their whole tragedy in one word: they were not ambushed. They were *told.* The rain that fell on them was the rain of people who had a prophet in their streets and chose the dust.

**What this stretch reveals:** salvation in this passage is elevation onto high ground, and it is granted by *allegiance*, not *proximity* — so precisely that the messenger's own wife can stand inside the word "family" and still be left in the dust with the deed she loved.

---

Before we leave the words for the world they open onto, hold the thread that ran through all three: **the deed.** He burns for the *deed.* He prays to be saved from the *deed.* And the one who is lost is lost because she stood with the *deed* — not because of any name, tie, or address. Let's ask what that does not just to our grammar, but to how we carry contempt, and how we count on the people around us to save us.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### One: The Precision of a Clean Fire

Open your phone. Scroll for ninety seconds. Count how many times you watch a human being get *dissolved into their worst moment.*

Someone said a stupid thing in 2014 and now they *are* the stupid thing — permanently, totally, no person left underneath, just the deed wearing a face. We have built an entire machinery for this. We call it accountability, but watch its actual grammar: it never says "that was a wrong action." It says "this person is cancelled," which means *this person is now identical to the thing they did.* We take an act — a bounded, dated, specific thing someone *did* — and we melt it down and pour it over the whole human being until you cannot see where the deed ends and the person begins. And then we feel *righteous* about it. The contempt feels like virtue. The heat feels like justice.

And here is the quiet cost we don't admit: it feels *good.* Hating a person is metabolically cheaper than hating a deed. A deed is complicated — it has causes, contexts, a story, a way back. A person you can just write off. Fusion is the lazy heart's shortcut. It lets you stop thinking. The man is a snake, so I'm done — I don't have to hold the harder, more exhausting truth that a human being I could still reach did a thing that is genuinely wrong.

Now put Lut in that scroll.

Here is a man with *more* cause than any of us to fuse the deed and the doer. These aren't strangers on the internet. These are people whose deed the Quran has just called the worst innovation in the history of the world, and who are — right now, to his face — threatening to throw him out of his own life for objecting. If anyone ever earned the right to say "you people are garbage," it is him, in this square, in this moment.

And he will not do it.

*Innī li-'amalikum mina l-qālīn.* I burn — at your **deed.** He keeps the fire at full heat. He does not fake calm, he does not "both-sides" the evil, he does not soften the wrongness one degree — *al-qālīn*, remember, is the frying-pan word, the searing hatred. And with that fire burning at full temperature he holds it steady and aims it, with a jeweler's precision, at the *act* and not the *actors.* He achieves the thing we tell ourselves is impossible: total moral clarity about the wrong, and zero contempt for the human. He proves, standing there under threat, that you do not have to choose between being soft on evil and being hard on people. There is a third thing. **Burn hot. Aim clean.**

And I know the objection, because I feel it too: *isn't that just a technicality? Hate the sin, love the sinner — the phrase people hide behind while they hate plenty of sinners?* It becomes a slogan, yes. But look at what makes Lut's version real and not a slogan: it *costs* him. He keeps these people human in his heart precisely when it would be easiest and most satisfying to discard them — and he keeps calling, keeps standing there, because a heart that has written someone off has stopped inviting them and started only enduring them. The proof that his fire was aimed at the deed and not the doer is that he was still *there,* still speaking, still their prophet, on the day they threatened to throw him out.

So the thing to carry is not "be nicer." It is much sharper than that. It is this: **notice, tonight, the exact instant your heart slides from "that was wrong" to "he is worthless" — and refuse the slide.** Keep the wrong fully wrong. And keep the person fully a person. When your kid does something genuinely bad and you feel the sentence forming — *you always,* *you never,* *what is wrong with you* — that is the fusion, live, in your own kitchen. The clean fire says: *this thing you did is not okay,* and it does not say *and therefore you are not okay.* The deed goes in the frying pan. The child does not.

That is not weakness dressed as mercy. That is the hardest discipline the heart can run — and a prophet ran it with a threat of exile hanging over his head.

### Two: You Can Live in the House and Miss the Boat

Let me describe a comfort most of us are quietly running on. See if you recognize it.

You are surrounded by good. Your parents pray. Your spouse is religious. Your group chat sends du'as. You are in the Muslim community, you go to the Friday prayer, your kids are in the Islamic school, your feed is full of reminders. And somewhere underneath all of it there is a soft, unspoken assumption — you would never say it out loud, but it runs the operating system: *I'm in the right room. I'm around the right people. Some of this has to rub off. I'll be carried.* Proximity as insurance. I am close to the guidance, therefore I am covered by the guidance.

It is the assumption of the student who sits in on every class and never opens the book, because being in the room *feels* like learning. It is the assumption of the person who follows twelve scholars online and has not changed one habit, because the feed *feels* like growth. Nearness masquerading as arrival.

Now walk into the one house on earth where that assumption should have been *safest.*

The house of a prophet. Not a scholar, not a good man — a *messenger of Allah.* If proximity to guidance saves, this is the safest address in the world. She woke up next to revelation. She heard the warnings before anyone else in the city. She watched the signs land in real time, from the inside, closer than any follower ever got. By every measure we count — community, family, access, exposure — she was the most covered person in the story.

And when the high ground was handed out, she was not on it.

*Illā 'ajūzan fi l-ghābirīn.* Except an old woman, among those who stayed in the dust. The word *ahl* — family — reached all the way around her and did not save her, because the criterion was never *ahl.* Allah lets you watch the exact machinery of it: Lut prays *save my family,* Allah saves *the family, every one of them,* and then shows you, with the word *illā,* that being inside "family" was never once the thing that lifted a single person onto the high ground. What lifted them was where their heart stood toward the deed. She stood with the deed. So she stayed with the dust — and even her title went with it. Not *wife* anymore. Just *an old woman,* filed with the lingering ones.

Feel how sobering that is, and don't rush past it into reassurance. The most religiously *surrounded* person in the narrative did not make it, and the reason was not a lack of exposure — she had more exposure than anyone alive. It was a lack of *allegiance.* Her feet were in the prophet's house. Her heart was in the city.

[PAUSE]

And here is where I have to be careful not to hand you the neat resolution too fast, because there isn't one, and the discomfort is the point. This is not "so proximity is worthless" — proximity to good is a mercy; being in the right room is how most of us ever hear the truth at all. But proximity is a *hallway,* not a *destination.* It gets you to the door. It does not walk you through it. The old woman had the whole hallway and never walked through the door, and being in the hallway her whole life did not count as having arrived. The nearness was real. It just wasn't the thing that saved.

So the question this drives into you is not comfortable, and it isn't meant to be: *if the guidance you're near were graded by your allegiance instead of your address — if it counted only where your heart actually stands and not whose house you sleep in — would you be on the high ground, or in the dust?*

Make it concrete, because otherwise it floats. Tomorrow you will be near a great deal of good — a prayer you're present for, a reminder that crosses your screen, a righteous person at your table. Catch the moment your heart treats that nearness as *credit.* As something you've banked just by being close to it. And convert it, on the spot, into one act of actual allegiance — one prayer you pray *as if it were yours* and not just the room's, one habit you drop because *you* decided the deed was wrong and not because someone's watching. The wife of Lut is not a story about a bad woman long ago. She is the mirror held up to every one of us who has ever felt safe *because of the company we keep* — and mistaken the hallway for home.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture: A Sorting, Heard Before It's Seen

Read the seven ayahs again and listen only to how the lines *end.*

*Al-mukhraj**īn***. Al-qāl**īn**. (ya'mal**ūn**). Ajma'**īn**. Al-ghābir**īn**. Al-ākhar**īn**. Al-mundhar**īn**.

Almost every line lands on the same sound — that long, closing *-īn.* And here is what that rhyme is actually doing, once you know the passage. *-īn* is the ending of the **plural**, the ending of a **category of people.** Line after line, the passage is filing human beings into groups. **The expelled.** The detesters. The whole-of-them. **Those who lagged behind.** The others. **Those who were warned.** The rhyme is not decoration. The rhyme is a **sorting machine.** You can hear the drawers closing. Every person in this story gets filed, and the sound of the filing is the rhyme of the surah itself.

And now the deepest thing, the thing the whole passage was built to make you see: **watch what everyone is filed *by.***

Not by name — the wife loses hers, the people are only "the others." Not by tie — *ahl,* family, reached around the wife and did not hold. Not by place — the man threatened with exile from his home is precisely the one who is saved, and the woman safe inside the prophet's house is the one who is lost. Every human category we instinctively trust to save us — name, blood, address, belonging — is deliberately stripped away, one by one, until only **one** criterion is left standing to sort the whole of humanity into those drawers.

The **deed.** *Al-'amal.* Look where it sits. Lut says *I burn at your **deed*** (168). Lut prays *save me from what they **do*** (169). And it is the deed, and nothing else, that decides who goes up onto the high ground and who stays in the dust. The word for the deed sits at the exact center of these seven lines, and everything else — every person, every name, every tie — arranges itself around it, sorted by nothing but its nearness to that fire. That is the architecture. Not an A-B-A of scenes. A single sun — *the deed* — with every human being in the passage in orbit around it, and their salvation determined solely by which side of it their heart faced.

### The Two Roads Were One Road

And now you can see that the two things we walked through were never two things.

The linguistic discovery — that Lut aimed his fire at the deed and refused to hate the doers — and the discovery about the wife — that she was lost not for her address but for her allegiance to the deed — are the *same insight* seen from two sides. Lut had it right in his heart: **it's the deed that matters, not the person.** So he hated the deed and spared the people. And Allah confirmed it in His judgment: **it's the deed that matters, not the person** — so a wife inside the family was filed by her deed, not her marriage. The prophet's clean fire and the woman's cold end are the same law, running in two directions. Aim your hatred by the deed. And know that you yourself will be judged by the deed — not by whose house you slept in.

We started with a man being threatened with the loss of his place, who answered that he could not be moved because he did not live in his place — he lived in his stance. We end with a woman who had the safest place in the world and lost everything, because a place was all she had. The one with no safe address and a true heart went up. The one with the perfect address and a divided heart stayed down. Place never saved anyone. Only allegiance did.

### Questions to Carry

- When someone wrongs you this week, watch the sentence your heart writes in the first half-second. Does it say *that was wrong* — or does it say *they are worthless?* Where, exactly, is your fire aimed?
- Is there a person you have quietly fused with their worst deed — someone you've stopped seeing as reachable? What would change if you un-fused them tomorrow?
- Where in your life are you running on *proximity* — feeling safe because of the room you're in, the family you have, the community around you — without the allegiance underneath it?
- If your standing before Allah were graded by where your heart actually stands, and not by your address, your lineage, or your reputation — which drawer would you be filed into?
- What is one act of real allegiance you could do tomorrow that no amount of *nearness to good* could ever do for you?

### One Sentence to Carry

**Aim your fire at the deed and spare the person — because you yourself will be lifted onto the high ground by your allegiance, not your address, and even "all of them" had an exception.**

### The Invitation

Let this passage give you two gifts, one for how you look outward and one for how you look in.

Outward: a clean fire. Permission to hate what is genuinely wrong with your whole heart — and the discipline to never once let that fire slide onto a human being you could still reach. Full clarity, zero contempt. It is the hardest thing a heart can do, and a prophet did it with exile hanging over him.

Inward: an honest look at what you're standing on. Not to frighten you away from the good people around you — stay near them; nearness is a mercy — but to move you from the hallway through the door. To turn your proximity into allegiance before the rain falls. Because it will fall, and on that day the only thing that will lift anyone onto the high ground is where their heart actually stood.

### A Du'a From These Words

O Allah, You who lift the saved onto high ground while the flood takes the low — *Rabbi najjinī.* Make our hearts burn against what You hate and gentle toward the people You still call. Do not let us mistake nearness to Your guidance for standing on it; move us from the hallway through the door before the rain. And on the day the drawers are closed and every soul is filed, let our allegiance — not our names, not our ties, not our places — file us among those You raise. Āmīn.
