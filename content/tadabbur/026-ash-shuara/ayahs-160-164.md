# The Brother They Refused: Lut and the Wage That Cannot Be Bought

## Surah 26: Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 160-164

---

### The Arabic

> كَذَّبَتْ قَوْمُ لُوطٍ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ ❁ إِذْ قَالَ لَهُمْ أَخُوهُمْ لُوطٌ أَلَا تَتَّقُونَ ❁ إِنِّى لَكُمْ رَسُولٌ أَمِينٌ ❁ فَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُونِ ❁ وَمَآ أَسْـَٔلُكُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ أَجْرٍ ۖ إِنْ أَجْرِىَ إِلَّا عَلَىٰ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

### Translation

> The people of Lut denied the messengers. When their brother Lut said to them: "Will you not be mindful of Allah? Indeed, I am to you a trustworthy messenger. So be mindful of Allah and obey me. I ask you for no wage for this. My wage is only with the Lord of all the worlds."

## INTRODUCTION: The Pattern That Refuses to Break

Before we open these five ayat, we have to feel where we are inside this surah.

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is built like a piece of music. By the time we arrive at ayah 160, the listener has already heard the same melody **five times**. Musa. Ibrahim. Nuh. Hud. Salih. Each prophet enters the stage. Each one speaks the same opening words. Each one is denied. Each story closes with the same verdict: *Indeed in that is a sign, but most of them were not believers. And indeed your Lord — He is the Almighty, the Merciful.*

Five times. Then a sixth. Then a seventh.

When you hear something repeated this many times, with the *same wording*, your ear stops listening for the words. It starts listening for the **deviations**. The places where the pattern breaks. The places where Allah inserts something just for this story.

That is the secret of this surah. The repetition is not redundancy. The repetition is a **frame** — and the frame is there so you will see what's been put inside it.

Now imagine the original audience. The Quraysh in Makkah. They are not strangers to story. They know Lut. They know what happened to his city. Classical commentators placed that city — Sodom, *Sadum* in the Arabic rendering — in the Al-Ghur valley of the Jordan basin, near the mountains of Jerusalem, a landscape so completely overturned that what remained was a stinking, brackish lake. Arab caravans pass near that ruin on their trade routes. The Quran says elsewhere: *And indeed, you pass by them in the morning and at night. Will you not then use reason?* (37:137-138).

So they pass the ruins. They know the headline. They know how this ends — fire, stone, a city overturned. They are settled into the story like someone settling into a familiar pew.

And then this passage opens. And the very first word is going to slow them down.

Stay with me. Because this opening — these five short ayat — contain something very strange. Something the prophet Lut says that, on first reading, sounds completely out of place. He says to a people about to be destroyed for a sin that has nothing to do with money:

***I ask you for no wage.***

Why is he talking about wages?

[PAUSE]

That is the question we are going to answer. And to answer it, we have to first hear what the surah is doing with words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Kadhdhabat* — The Verb of the Many Done by the One

Look at the very first word of the passage:

> **كَذَّبَتْ قَوْمُ لُوطٍ ٱلْمُرْسَلِينَ**
> *Kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin al-mursalīn.*
> *The people of Lut denied the messengers.*

I want to teach a small piece of Arabic grammar before we go anywhere near this ayah, because once you see it, the verse will never look the same.

In Arabic, when you have a verb followed by its subject, the verb agrees with the subject. If a man speaks, the verb is masculine singular. If a group of women speak, it's feminine plural. Simple.

But there is one category of noun that breaks the rule.

In Arabic there is a class called *ism al-jamʿ* — collective nouns. *Qawm* (a people), *nās* (humanity), *qabīla* (a tribe). Words that name a group as a single body. With these nouns, Arabic grammar gives the speaker a choice. You can treat the group as **one** thing — and use a singular verb. Or you can treat the group as **many** individuals — and use a plural verb. Or — and this is the move Allah makes here — you can use the **feminine singular**, which treats the group as a single, unified entity, almost a person.

So look at what's happening: *kadhdhabat* — feminine singular verb. Then *qawmu Lūṭ* — the people of Lut — as the subject.

[PAUSE]

Allah did not say *kadhdhaba qawmu Lūṭ* (treating them as a default masculine). He did not say *kadhdhabū* (treating them as a plural of individuals). He chose the form that fuses them into **one body**.

And He used this exact same form at the start of every prophet-story in this surah that opens with the rejection formula. *Kadhdhabat qawmu Nūḥ.* *Kadhdhabat ʿĀd.* *Kadhdhabat Thamūd.* *Kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭ.* *Kadhdhabat aṣḥābu'l-ayka.*

Notice something. Every time Allah names a people who rejected a prophet, He grammatically welds them together. He does not say "individuals among them denied." He does not say "the leaders denied while the masses were unsure." He says: *they denied — as one organism.*

Why?

Here is what is being shown. There is a mode of group-existence where the individual ceases to exist. Where the city, the tribe, the cohort, the in-group becomes a single moral actor. Where to be inside it is to share its denial whether you spoke or not.

This is something the modern world knows under different names. The crowd. The mob. The culture. We have books on it — Le Bon's crowd psychology, Hannah Arendt on the banality of evil, Solomon Asch's conformity experiments. We have learned, painfully, that human beings inside a unified social body do not behave like the same humans alone. They surrender judgment to the body. The body decides; they merely belong.

Allah named this 1,400 years before any of those books. *Kadhdhabat qawm.* The people — singular, fused — denied.

And here's the second thing this verb is doing. Read it again carefully:

> **The people of Lut denied the *messengers*.**

Plural. *Al-mursalīn.* Not "the messenger." Not "Lut."

But Lut was the only messenger sent to them. So why plural?

This is the same move at every station of the surah. *The people of Nuh denied the messengers.* Nuh alone was sent to them. *The people of Hud — denied the messengers.* Just Hud. Every single time, the lone prophet is grammatically multiplied.

Here's what this is teaching: **to deny one messenger is to deny all of them.** Because every messenger came with the same core message — *alā tattaqūn?* Will you not be mindful of Allah? — to reject any one of them is to reject the message they all carried. The chain is one chain. The call is one call. The gospel of every prophet was the same gospel. You don't get to accept Musa and reject Muhammad, accept Ibrahim and reject Lut. The message is unified, so the rejection is unified.

Classical commentators make this explicit: by denying Lut, the people of Sodom became deniers of all messengers of Allah — because what the messengers all brought of tawhid and the foundational principles of divine law is one and the same. The particular messenger changes; the fundamental summons does not. When you slam the door on one, you have slammed it on the call itself.

So in five Arabic words — *kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin al-mursalīn* — Allah has already taught us two things.

First: a people can become a single moral body, and the body bears the verdict.
Second: every single prophet is one prophet, because the call is one call.

[PAUSE]

Now we move to the second word that should slow us down.

### Element Two: *Akhūhum* — Their Brother

The next ayah opens:

> **إِذْ قَالَ لَهُمْ أَخُوهُمْ لُوطٌ**
> *Idh qāla lahum akhūhum Lūṭun*
> *When their brother Lut said to them...*

Their **brother**.

Stop. Sit with this.

Lut was not, in fact, their brother. Not by blood. Classical scholars identify him as Lut bin Haran bin Azar — the nephew of Ibrahim Al-Khalil, a man who had come to Sodom from the world of his uncle, from the Mesopotamian lands of Ibrahim's origin. He was sent to this mighty nation during the very lifetime of Ibrahim — two prophets working in parallel, uncle and nephew, in different cities under the same mission, each turning to face a different community. By every literal standard, Lut was a foreigner to the people of Sodom. He was not their kin. He was not even their countryman by birth.

And yet Allah calls him *akhūhum*. *Their* brother.

He does this for every prophet in this surah who was sent to a specific people. *Akhūhum Hūd.* *Akhūhum Ṣāliḥ.* *Akhūhum Shuʿayb.* And here: *akhūhum Lūṭ.*

What does the word *akh* — brother — actually mean in classical Arabic? The lexicons (and you can check Lisān al-ʿArab on this) give us a layered word. Yes, it means a sibling by birth. But the deeper root sense is **one who shares your fate, one bound to you, one who walks beside you in your circumstance**. The Arabs would say *akhū al-ḥarb* — "the brother of war" — to mean someone seasoned by battle, fused to it. *Akhū al-safar* — the brother of the journey — for a constant traveler.

Brother is not just blood. Brother is **shared situation**. Brother is *we are in this together*.

[PAUSE]

Now let us watch something.

The Quraysh, as they listened to this surah being recited, had a specific complaint about Muhammad (peace be upon him). They said: he is dividing us. He is splitting families. He is making sons abandon fathers, brothers abandon brothers. The word that the Quraysh weaponized against the Prophet was that he was an **outsider** to the unity of the tribe. A *mufarriq* — a divider.

And here Allah is saying about every prophet, including the one to whom this very surah is being revealed: **he is your brother**. He didn't come to break you. He came as one of you. He is bound to your fate. He carries your situation in his own chest.

But here's what's even sharper. Lut, in the case before us, is **not** by blood one of them. He genuinely is a foreigner. So when Allah calls him their brother, He is doing something even more striking — He is saying: the bond between a prophet and his people is not the bond of birth. It is the bond of **concern**. It is the bond of one who weeps for them. Brotherhood, in its highest sense, is the love that risks rejection for the sake of another's soul.

This is what classical commentators, approaching from different angles, all converge upon. Some said *akhūhum* signaled that he was sent only to them and not universally — this one was *for* them, not dispatched to the world at large. Others said it signaled that he came to them in love and not in hostility. Others said it signaled that the prophets are kin to all who would receive them, that the door of brotherhood is opened wider than the door of blood. These are not competing readings. They are the same diamond turned in the light. The prophet stands among his people as **one bound to them by something deeper than blood**: by the fact that he cannot bear for them to perish.

Now feel the asymmetry that's about to come.

The brother says *alā tattaqūn?* — a question of love. Classical tafsir sharpens what this word is doing: *tattaqūn*, from the root w-q-y, carries the image of erecting a barrier between yourself and something that will strike. Al-Muyassar reads this directly as: *will you not fear Allah's punishment?* Not abstract piety. **Urgent self-preservation.** You are walking toward a thing you cannot survive. Will you not stop, will you not build any shield between yourself and what is coming? He says *innī lakum rasūlun amīn* — I am, *for you* — the *lām* of benefit — a trustworthy messenger. He has come *for* them, not *against* them. And they answer him by attempting to expel him from his city. By saying — as the Quran tells us they said — *akhrijū āla Lūṭin min qaryatikum* — "expel the family of Lut from your town" (27:56).

He calls them *brother*. They call him *foreigner*.

This is the asymmetry of every prophetic moment. The one who comes in love is met as a threat. The one who would die for them is treated as one trying to kill them. And Allah preserves Lut's claim of brotherhood in His own speech, eternal, recited until the Day of Judgment, while the people of Lut have only their refusal — preserved too, as a warning.

[PAUSE]

So we have two words now. *Kadhdhabat* showed us that a society can fuse into one body of denial. *Akhūhum* shows us that the messenger sent to that body is bound to it as a brother — a brother by concern, not by blood.

But we still haven't answered our opening question.

Why is Lut, in a story that has nothing to do with money, talking about wages?

### Element Three: *Lā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr* — The Wage I Will Not Take

Here is the ayah:

> **وَمَآ أَسْـَٔلُكُمْ عَلَيْهِ مِنْ أَجْرٍ ۖ إِنْ أَجْرِىَ إِلَّا عَلَىٰ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ**
> *Wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr. In ajriya illā ʿalā Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn.*
> *I ask you for no wage for this. My wage is only with the Lord of all the worlds.*

Look at this. Read it five times.

Now — here is the thing the procession-structure of this surah was setting us up for. Every prophet in this surah says these exact words. **Every single one.**

Nuh says them in ayah 109.
Hud says them in ayah 127.
Salih says them in ayah 145.
Lut says them here in ayah 164.
Shuʿayb says them in ayah 180.

The same words. The same construction. *Wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr. In ajriya illā ʿalā Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn.*

[PAUSE]

When the Quran repeats a sentence five times, identically, in a single surah, you have to ask: **why did this need to be said five times?**

Here is the surface answer. The Quraysh were accusing Muhammad ﷺ of seeking wealth, status, leadership. They said his message was a ladder he was climbing. They tried, repeatedly, to buy him off — *we'll make you king, we'll give you any woman you want, we'll pool our wealth and make you the richest of us, just stop saying these things*. Allah is showing the Prophet ﷺ: every messenger before you was accused of the same thing. Every one of them faced the same dismissal — *you're doing this for something*. Every one of them answered with the same declaration: **I am doing this for nothing you can give me or take from me.**

Now let us enter the specific words of this declaration.

The root of *ajr* in classical Arabic is a-j-r. The lexicons define it as the recompense given for labor rendered — what you receive in exchange for what you have given. It is the counterweight on the scale of transaction. In the *ajr* relationship, two parties exist: the one who gives labor and the one who owes what the labor has earned.

Look at the construction: *mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr*. The *min* here is used for partitive negation — it makes the denial absolute and exhaustive. Not: "I ask you for no large wage." Not: "I ask you for no wage beyond what is fair." *Min* sweeps it clean. **Not even the smallest portion of wage.** There is no fraction of transaction between us. I am not in your debt, and you are not in mine.

And then the exception: *In ajriya illā ʿalā Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn.* The *in* here functions as *mā* al-hijaziyya — the negating particle paired with *illā*: "My wage is nothing — *except* upon the Lord of all the worlds."

Notice what Lut does not say. He does not say: "I ask for no wage — and I expect none." He says: "I ask *you* for no wage — and my wage is with someone else." There IS an ajr. There IS a transaction. It is just not with them. The account is being kept elsewhere, by a party far more reliable than any human audience.

This is the secret of the declaration. The prophet is not giving something for nothing. He is giving something for **everything** — a wage that no created being could honor, deposited with the only One who can.

[PAUSE]

Now let us bring together the two claims these verses have been building side by side.

Lut says two things in sequence. First: *innī lakum rasūlun amīn* — I am a trustworthy messenger. Then: *wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr* — and I ask you for no wage.

These are not two separate claims. They are the **same claim from two angles**.

Al-Tabari, commenting on *amīn* in this context, notes that the trust being invoked is specifically trustworthiness *upon the revelation and upon its conveyance* — that the messenger has not altered what he was given, has not filtered it through personal interest, has not softened what needed to be hard or shaped what needed to be said for any audience. The *amāna* is not about his general moral character. It is about **fidelity in transmission**. Has the prophet delivered Allah's word intact, or has he shaped it to serve himself?

And here is the connection to the wage. A man who takes wages from his audience has a structural incentive to distort. If the people dislike a part of the message, a paid speaker might soften it. If the people want to hear something, a mercenary preacher might provide it. The wage creates a dependency — and dependency creates leverage — and leverage creates the possibility of corruption.

But a man whose wage is with Allah has no such dependency. His audience can accept him or reject him, honor him or expel him, and his account remains unchanged. He has no reason to soften what needs to be sharp. He has no reason to withhold what they need to hear. His *amāna* — his trustworthiness — is structurally guaranteed by the very fact that he refuses the wage.

*Amīn* and *lā ajr* are not two attributes. They are one attribute seen from two sides: the content side (I have not distorted the message) and the economic side (I have no reason to distort the message). Together they form a complete claim of integrity that cannot be bought and therefore cannot be owned.

[PAUSE]

Now the final word of the passage. *Rabb al-ʿālamīn.* The Lord — of **the worlds**.

Not: "my wage is with the Lord of your people." Not: "with the Lord of this land." Not even: "with my Lord." *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* — the Lord of all the worlds. Every created realm, every order of existence, every thing that can be named as a world.

Why this title here?

Think about what Lut's people might say to his declaration. "Fine — you say your wage is with God. But you are one man in one city. What does the Lord of the cosmos owe you?" The title answers them. The one who holds the account is not a regional deity, not a tribal lord, not a power with bounded jurisdiction. He is the *Rabb* — the Sustainer, the Master, the One responsible for every created thing — of the *ʿālamīn* — the totality of all worlds. His resources are not finite. His ability to honor the account is without limit.

There is also something intimate in the choice of *Rabb* specifically. The Quran has multiple divine names. *Allāh* is the name of essence. *Al-Raḥmān* is the name of unbounded mercy. But *Rabb* is the name of **relationship** — the one who raises, tends, provides, governs. When Lut says *Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn*, he is saying: the one who has been responsible for me from the beginning, who has tended my growth and guided my mission — He is the one who holds my account. The intimacy of *Rabb* meets the infinite scope of *ʿālamīn*. The one who knows me personally is also the one who owns everything.

This is Lut's answer to every person who stands between a messenger and his calling and says: what are you getting out of this?

Everything. Just — not from you.

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE FIVE VERSES

Step back now and look at these five ayat as a unit.

They form a compressed argument, a rhetorical shape that has been honed by repetition across five prophet-stories and refined for Lut's specific situation. Let us trace its logic:

*Kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin al-mursalīn.* — Verdict first. Before Lut speaks a single word, the Quran tells you how the story ends. This is not dramatic irony in the usual sense. It is something more radical: the judgment precedes the testimony, because we are not watching a trial whose outcome is uncertain. We are watching a record being entered into eternity.

*Idh qāla lahum akhūhum Lūṭun alā tattaqūn.* — Then the testimony: the brother, calling with urgency. Not "avoid sin" — but *will you not shield yourselves from what is coming?* The word *tattaqūn* carries the image of creating a barrier, a protective layer between yourself and something that will strike. He is not asking them to be pious in a generic sense. He is asking them to wake up before the thing that is coming arrives.

*Innī lakum rasūlun amīn.* — Here Lut establishes his authority — but notice how. Not "I have been sent to judge you." Not "I have the power to destroy you." *I am, for you* — the *lām* of benefit — *a trustworthy messenger.* The credentials are offered in the register of service, not power. I am here as someone you can rely on. The message I carry has not been altered. This is what the truth actually is.

*Fa-ttaqū'llāha wa-aṭīʿūn.* — And therefore: be mindful of Allah and obey me. The *fa* — "therefore, consequently" — is doing important work. It makes the chain explicit. Because I am a trustworthy messenger sent for your benefit, the logical conclusion is: defer to Allah, and in this specific situation, defer to what I am telling you. The obedience asked for is not arbitrary. It follows from the integrity of the source.

*Wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr. In ajriya illā ʿalā Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn.* — And finally, the seal: I ask for nothing from you. I have no stake in your acceptance. I am not here to take anything. My account is kept elsewhere, by the One who owns everything.

Read these five verses as one movement: verdict, then testimony of concern, then authority by service, then summons, then seal of integrity. It is an argument in five steps. And every prophet who appears in this surah makes the same argument — because the argument is not Lut's. It is the argument that every bearer of truth has ever made to a people who suspected his motives.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the surah has built by repeating this pattern. If you heard it only once, from one prophet, you might question the man. Maybe Nuh was unusual. Maybe Hud had a particular kind of integrity. But you cannot question a pattern that cuts across every prophet in five different civilizations, spanning centuries, speaking the same words. At some point the repetition is no longer evidence about the prophets. It is evidence about the nature of prophecy itself. This is what a messenger *is*: one whose wage is elsewhere.

And notice what that does for the message he carries. If the messenger gains nothing from your acceptance and loses nothing from your rejection, then you cannot explain away his testimony by pointing to his interests. The usual tools of dismissal — *he's doing this for money, for power, for status, for survival* — collapse entirely in the face of a man who has placed his account somewhere you cannot reach. All that remains is the question of whether the message is true.

Which is precisely where Allah wants you.

## CLOSING: What This Passage Asks of the Listener

There is something these five verses leave unresolved, and I think the silence is deliberate.

We know how the story ends. *Kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭ* — and then, a few verses later, the stones. The overturned city. The valley made into a stagnant lake. The ruins that caravans still pass.

But the question Lut asks is addressed to *them*, and it travels through the Quran to reach *us*. *Alā tattaqūn?* Will you not be mindful? Will you not build a barrier between yourself and what is coming?

These five opening verses are about something that precedes the specific sin for which Sodom is remembered. Classical scholars discuss the particular transgression at length in the surrounding ayahs and in the fuller accounts of Lut's story across the Quran (7:80-84, 11:77-83, 27:54-58). But here, in these five verses, the focus is earlier and deeper. The focus is on *denial*. On the fused body of a civilization that has become incapable of hearing. *Kadhdhabat* — they denied as one organism. The city did not debate Lut and conclude he was wrong. The city rejected without examining, expelled without answering, closed without asking a single question. What made destruction possible was not only the sin but the prior hardening — the moment a community became so fused in its wrongness that the individual conscience was absorbed and the brother's voice could no longer land.

Lut stood outside that fusion and called into it. *Alā tattaqūn?* And they heard the question. And they said no — not with argument, but with expulsion. Get out.

What remained of Lut's integrity in that moment was not that he persuaded them. He did not. What remained was what we are reading now: the record of a man who came as a brother, who asked for nothing, who delivered the message without alteration, whose wage was elsewhere, and who therefore could not be bought into silence and could not be impoverished into retreat.

A man whose account is kept with the Lord of all the worlds cannot be paid off and cannot be bankrupted. The only thing his audience can do — and eventually they did it — is to reject him. And even that refusal could not touch his wage.

The ruins near the Jordan Valley are still there. What endures alongside them, recited in every generation, in every language, in every part of the world that carries this Book, is the question the brother asked before the end came:

*Alā tattaqūn?*

Will you not shield yourselves?
