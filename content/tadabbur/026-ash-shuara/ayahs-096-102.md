# The Leveling and the Aloneness
### Sūrah Ash-Shuʿarāʾ, verses 96–102

<!-- ayah:26:97 -->     تَٱللَّهِ إِن كُنَّا لَفِى ضَلَٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ
<!-- ayah:26:98 -->     إِذْ نُسَوِّيكُم بِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ
<!-- ayah:26:99 -->     وَمَآ أَضَلَّنَآ إِلَّا ٱلْمُجْرِمُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:100 -->     فَمَا لَنَا مِن شَٰفِعِينَ
<!-- ayah:26:101 -->     وَلَا صَدِيقٍ حَمِيمٍ
<!-- ayah:26:102 -->     فَلَوْ أَنَّ لَنَا كَرَّةً فَنَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

*Qālū wa hum fīhā yakhtaṣimūn. Ta'llāhi in kunnā lafī ḍalālin mubīn. Idh nusawwīkum bi-Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn. Wa mā aḍallanā illā'l-mujrimūn. Fa mā lanā min shāfiʿīn. Wa lā ṣadīqin ḥamīm. Fa law anna lanā karratan fa-nakūna mina'l-muʾminīn.*

> They will say, while disputing therein: *"By Allah, we were surely in clear error when we made you equal with the Lord of the worlds. And none led us astray but the criminals. So now we have no intercessors, and not one devoted friend. If only we had a return, that we might be among the believers."*

---

## Introduction — Standing at the Edge of a Confession

Before we open a single word, I want you to see where we are standing, because this passage is not spoken on earth. It is spoken *after*.

Rewind with me. We are inside the story of Ibrāhīm — peace be upon him — which runs through the heart of this surah. And Ibrāhīm's story here is not the fire, not the stars, not the shattered idols. Allah gives us his *argument*. Ibrāhīm turns to his people and asks them one devastatingly simple question: *"What is it that you worship?"* They answer, proudly, "We worship idols, and we remain devoted to them." And he presses: *Do they hear you when you call? Do they benefit you, or harm you?* And the people give the most honest and most damning answer in the Qur'an: **"No. But we found our fathers doing so."**

Sit with that for a moment. They did not claim the idols could hear. They did not claim the idols made the rain or raised the dead. They knew. They admitted the stones were deaf. And they worshipped them anyway — because everyone around them did, because their fathers did, because it was the water they swam in and they had never once stood on the bank and looked at it.

Ibrāhīm hears this and he does not argue statistics. He turns to Allah and prays — one of the most tender prayers in the entire Qur'an — asking for wisdom, for a truthful tongue among later generations, and then he says: *and do not disgrace me on the Day they are raised, the Day when neither wealth nor sons will be of any use — except the one who comes to Allah with a sound heart.* [PAUSE]

And *that* is the hinge. Ibrāhīm mentions the Day. And the surah swings forward, past his death, past centuries, all the way to that Day itself — and shows us the very people who said *"we found our fathers doing so."* Now the idols are gathered. Now the Fire is set out plain. And a voice asks them the same question Ibrāhīm asked, but now there is nowhere to stand: *"Where is all that you used to worship besides Allah? Can they help you now? Can they even help themselves?"*

And then they are thrown in. Together. The worshippers and the worshipped, the followers and the leaders, the whole comfortable consensus — tipped in together.

**And our passage is the sound they make as they fall.**

This is not a lecture Allah delivers about them. This is a recording He plays *of* them. He lets us hear the argument break out in the Fire — the accusations, the confession, the wish. Everything Ibrāhīm said on earth, calmly, as a warning, they are now screaming at each other as a confession.

Here is the hook, and then we begin. Every one of these people, at some point in their lives, was *sure*. Sure enough to build a life on it, to raise children in it, to die in it. And in seven short verses, Allah shows us the exact moment their certainty turns inside out — and He does it by making them use a word for their sin so precise, so surgical, that once you see it, you will start noticing that sin in places you never thought to look. Including, if we are honest, in the mirror.

Let's go slowly.

## Part One — The Linguistic Journey

### The First Word: *Nusawwīkum* — the sin was a **leveling**

I want to start not with the Qur'an but with a courtroom, because there is a specific kind of confession I need you to feel before we touch the Arabic.

Imagine a man on trial. The evidence is overwhelming. And at the end, he stands, and instead of the vague *"I made some mistakes,"* he says something exact: *"On the night of the fourteenth, at 11:40, I took the money from the second drawer."* Notice what that precision does. A vague confession lets you keep some dignity — *mistakes were made, we're all human.* But a **precise** confession is different. When someone names the exact act, at the exact moment, you know the fog has fully lifted. They are no longer managing their image. They are just *seeing*.

Now hold that, because the people in the Fire do not say *"we were bad"* or *"we sinned."* They reach for a verb so precise it functions like a coordinate on a map. Listen to verse 97 and 98 together:

<!-- ayah:26:97 -->     تَٱللَّهِ إِن كُنَّا لَفِى ضَلَٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ
<!-- ayah:26:98 -->     إِذْ نُسَوِّيكُم بِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*"By Allah, we were in clear error — **when we made you equal** with the Lord of the worlds."*

The word is **نُسَوِّي** — *nusawwī*. And I want to teach you the shape of this word before we let it land, because the shape *is* the meaning.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns, and the patterns are not decoration — they carry force. There is a base pattern, and then there is what the grammarians call **Form II**, where you double the middle letter of the root and it does something specific: it makes the verb *deliberate, intensive, causative*. The base root here is *s-w-y* — the idea of **evenness, equality, being level**. From it you get *sawā'* — "the same." Now push it into Form II — *sawwā* — and it no longer means "to be equal." It means **to actively make something equal. To level. To flatten two things until they sit at the same height.** [PAUSE]

This is not the verb you use when you *believe* two things are equal. It is the verb you use when you *place* them equal. It is a hand doing something. It is an act of leveling.

So when they say *nusawwīkum bi-Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn*, they are not confessing, "we thought the idols were as great as God." Watch — that's not it at all. They are confessing: *"we took you, and we took the Lord of the worlds, and with our own hands we set you at the same height."*

Now here is where you must stay with me, because this is the part that reorganizes everything.

The idolaters of Makkah — and the classical scholars are unanimous on this — did **not** believe the idols created the heavens. Ask them who made the sky, who sends the rain, and they would say: *Allah.* The Qur'an itself confirms this elsewhere. So what, exactly, did they *level*? Not existence. Not creatorship. They knew the stone did not make the world.

What they leveled was **devotion.** Love. Fear. Hope. The turning of the heart. They took the reverence that belongs to the One who sustains all worlds — and they gave an *equal share* of it to a thing that could not hear them. The sin was never a mistake in their theology. It was a **misallocation of the heart.** They let something created receive the same weight, the same pull, the same "vote" in their lives as the Creator.

And now say the object of that leveling out loud, because Allah does not let them off with a small word. He does not have them say *"when we made you equal with Allah."* He has them say **بِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ** — *bi-Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn* — *"with the Lord of the worlds."* The Sustainer. The One holding every galaxy and every heartbeat and every unborn thing in its due measure — *worlds*, plural, beyond counting. **That** is what they set a carved stone beside. That is the height they dragged the stone up to — or rather, that is the height they *imagined* they could drag the Most High down to.

Here is what this single Form-II verb reveals, and then we pause: **shirk is not primarily a belief. It is an act of leveling.** It is the hand of the heart taking two things of infinitely different weight and pretending they belong on the same shelf.

### The Second Word: *Ta'llāhi* — the oath of wonder, sworn too late

Go back to the very first sound out of their mouths in verse 97. Before "we were in error," before anything — one word:

 **تَٱللَّهِ**

*Ta'llāhi.* "By Allah."

I need to teach you something small and beautiful about Arabic oaths, because it turns this word from a throwaway into a knife.

Arabic swears an oath in three ways. You can swear with *wa* — *wallāhi* — the common, everyday oath; you hear it a hundred times a day in the Arab world. You can swear with *bi* — *billāhi*. And then there is a third, rare particle: **تَ** — *ta* — as in *ta'llāhi*. And here is the striking thing the grammarians and rhetoricians note: this *tā*-oath is used sparingly, and it carries a particular flavor — it is the oath of **astonishment**, the oath you swear when something is genuinely *staggering*, almost unbelievable to the one saying it. And it is reserved, overwhelmingly, for the name **Allah** — it does not roam freely onto other words the way *wa* does.

Feel the sound of it, too. *Ta'llāh.* It is soft on the tongue, breathy, almost a gasp — not the hard consonantal thud of a curse but the exhale of someone whose breath has been taken from them.

So picture it now. These are people who, on earth, would *not* swear by Allah with full seriousness — or who swore by their idols, by their ancestors, by anything but Him. And now, in the Fire, the first thing that leaves their mouths is an oath of *wonder* — **"By Allah!"** — sworn on the very Name they spent their lives leveling.

Do you see the tragedy folded into a single word? They finally give Allah an undivided, astonished acknowledgment — *ta'llāhi*, with all the wonder the word carries — at the one moment when acknowledgment buys them nothing. The heart that would not gasp "By Allah" at the sight of a sunrise, a newborn, a mountain, a mercy — that heart finally gasps it here, in the flames, staring at what its leveling produced.

**The wonder was always available. They just spent it too late.** [PAUSE]

And notice — this is a documented feature of the word, not my invention — that the astonishment is *built into* the oath itself. Allah did not have to describe their shock. He put it inside the particle. One letter, *tā*, and you hear a person who has finally, fully woken up — in the last room where waking up cannot help.

One way to sit with this: somewhere in your life there is a "By Allah" you keep postponing. A moment of undivided wonder at Him you keep meaning to have — after the deadline, after the phase, after the thing you're chasing settles down. This verse is Allah showing you the latest possible moment that gasp can arrive. Everything before that is still on time. That is the mercy hidden in the horror.

### The Third Feature: *Min shāfiʿīn... wa lā ṣadīqin ḥamīm* — the aloneness, counted precisely

Now we come to the feature that, the more I looked, the more I could not stop looking at. And it lives in an *asymmetry* — in a difference of number between two words that most translations flatten into nothing.

Listen to verses 100 and 101:

<!-- ayah:26:100 -->     فَمَا لَنَا مِن شَٰفِعِينَ
<!-- ayah:26:101 -->     وَلَا صَدِيقٍ حَمِيمٍ

*"So we have no intercessors — and not one devoted friend."*

Here is what I want you to catch. **شَٰفِعِينَ** — *shāfiʿīn*, "intercessors" — is **plural**. But **صَدِيق** — *ṣadīq*, "friend" — is **singular**.

Why does that matter? Because it is doing precise emotional accounting.

Think about what these people had been banking on. The whole logic of their idolatry was intercession — the idols were "those who would plead for us with Allah," a *committee* of advocates, a whole panel of intermediaries. So when that hope collapses, Allah has them deny it in the plural: *we have no interceders* — not the crowd of pleaders we were counting on, not one of the whole expected assembly. And He sharpens it with a tiny word, **مِن** — *min* — *"min shāfiʿīn."* That little *min* here means *not even the smallest trace of any* — not "we don't have enough intercessors," but "not a single one from the entire category exists for us."

The plural collapses. The whole imagined committee — gone.

And then, having lost the crowd, they reach downward for the *minimum*. Not a panel of advocates now. Just… one person. One friend. And here the word drops to the **singular** — *ṣadīq* — because they are no longer asking for a committee. They are asking for the least a human being can be given: **one.** One face that is on your side. And even that — *wa lā ṣadīqin* — **not one.** [PAUSE]

Feel the shape of the descent. First they lose the many. Then they discover they don't even have the one. It is the loneliness of a person who realizes not only that the important people have abandoned them, but that there is not a single soul left in the entire world who would sit beside them. From a crowd to zero, in the space of two words, tracked by a shift from plural to singular that you would miss if you weren't listening.

And now the last word — **حَمِيم** — *ḥamīm.* Translated "devoted" or "intimate," and it is that. But its root, *ḥ-m-m*, carries the sense of **heat, warmth**. A *ṣadīq ḥamīm* is not just a friend — it is a *warm* friend, the one whose concern for you "runs hot," whose care actually reaches you and warms something in your chest. That is the specific thing being denied. Not acquaintances. Not contacts. **Warmth.** The human heat of someone who cares whether you live or die.

And here is a resonance worth holding gently — not as something the verse states outright, but as something the root invites you to feel. That same root, *ḥ-m-m*, gives another Qur'anic word: **ḥamīm** as the *scalding, boiling water* of the Fire. So one way to feel the weight of verse 101 is this: in the place where they now stand, the *only* ḥamīm on offer is the boiling kind. They will not lack heat. They will lack the *warmth* of a single caring soul, while surrounded by heat that only burns. Every warmth except the one warmth a heart actually needs.

**What this asymmetry reveals is Allah counting their isolation for them — from the many they trusted, down past the one they'd settle for, down to a warmth that has curdled into scald.** [PAUSE — long]

### The Bridge

So look at what these three features have shown us, standing together.

*Nusawwī* — the sin was a **leveling**: taking the Creator and something created and forcing them to the same height in the heart.

*Ta'llāhi* — the awakening was a **wonder** that came fully alive one moment too late.

*Min shāfiʿīn wa lā ṣadīqin ḥamīm* — the wage was an **aloneness** counted down from the many, to the one, to none.

Three words. A leveling, a late wonder, a total aloneness. Now let's ask what this shows us not just about seven verses in the desert — but about the architecture of a human heart, including the one beating in your chest right now.

## Part Two — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: You are leveling something right now

Let me start where you actually live, not where the idolaters lived.

You have never bowed to a statue. You would find it absurd. So it is tempting to read this passage as history — *those poor benighted people and their stones* — and to feel a quiet, comfortable distance from it. I want to close that distance, because I think it is false, and I think the word *nusawwī* is the tool that closes it.

Here is how modern life actually works. You do not consciously *demote* God. Almost no one does. If I asked you, flat out, "Is your career more important than Allah?" — you would say no, instantly, and mean it. **But that is the wrong question, and *nusawwī* is what tells us it's the wrong question.** The verb is not about *demoting* God below something. It is about *leveling* something *up to* Him — giving it an equal seat, an equal vote, an equal pull.

So the real question is not "what have I put above God?" The real question is: **"What have I quietly allowed to sit at the same height as God when I make my decisions?"**

Think about how a hard choice actually feels from the inside. There is a moment — you know this moment — where two things are pulling on you. The clear thing you know Allah asks of you, and the thing you want, or the thing you fear, or the image you're protecting. And in that moment, if you're honest, they don't feel like a King and a servant. They feel like **two candidates**. Two voices arguing at the same volume, at the same table, with the same standing to be heard. You *deliberate* between them. And the very fact that you deliberate — that His command has to *win an argument* against your comfort rather than simply settling it — that is *nusawwī*. That is the leveling. Quiet. Respectable. Invisible from the outside. You have not built an idol. You have seated a rival at the table and given it a vote.

Notice that this is exactly the mechanism of the people in the Fire. They didn't disbelieve in Allah's power. They *seated something beside it.* The stone got a vote. And the horror of verse 98 is that they can now see, with total clarity, what the vote was worth.

Now — before this floats off into the abstract, let me bring it into a room. Picture Thursday night. You've told yourself Friday morning you'll pray Fajr on time, properly, and start the day with Him. Then Thursday night the phone is warm in your hand and the feed is endless, and there is a small, silent negotiation happening — *just ten more minutes* — and Fajr and the feed are sitting at the same table, arguing at the same volume, and one of them wins. Nobody watching would call that idolatry. There's no statue. But there was a leveling. Something with no power to sustain a single one of the worlds got an equal vote against the One who sustains them all. [PAUSE]

I am not saying this to make you feel crushed. I am saying it because *nusawwī* is a gift of precision. Vague guilt — *I should be a better Muslim* — changes nothing, because it names nothing. But *"I keep seating my comfort at the same height as my Lord in the small hours"* — **that** you can actually do something about. The idolaters got the precise diagnosis only after death, when it was a confession. You are getting it now, when it is still a to-do list.

So the practical turn is small and exact: the next time you feel that internal *deliberation* — that sense of two candidates arguing — treat the deliberation *itself* as the alarm. Not the outcome. The alarm is the moment His command has to *compete* rather than *command*. When you feel that, name it: *I am leveling right now.* That naming, in the moment, is the un-leveling. It is you taking your hand off the scale.

### Theme Two: The friend who cannot follow you in

Now I want to talk about something that sits under the skin of every human being, and it is not theological at all to begin with. It is the fear of being finally, completely alone.

We spend enormous energy against this fear, though we rarely name it. We build networks. We keep the group chat alive. We measure our worth partly in who would show up for us. And underneath the healthy parts of that is a raw nerve: the terror that at the moment it matters most, we will look around and there will be *no one.* Anyone who has sat in a hospital corridor at 3 a.m., or gotten the phone call that splits life into before and after, knows that the crowd thins fast when the stakes get real. The people who "would do anything for you" turn out, mostly, to have limits. And the closer you get to the edge, the fewer come with you.

Verse 101 is Allah naming that exact nerve — and telling us the truth about how far our friendships actually reach.

*Wa lā ṣadīqin ḥamīm.* Not one warm friend.

Here is the hard thing, and I don't want to rush past it. It is not that these people had *no* friends on earth. They almost certainly had many. The whole point of the surah is that idolatry was *social* — *"we found our fathers doing so,"* we belong, we're in the crowd, we have each other. Their friendships were *real*. They laughed together, mourned together, defended each other. So what happened to all of it?

It could not follow them in. [PAUSE]

Because most of what we call friendship is *shared direction.* We bond over the thing we are walking toward together — the same ambitions, the same amusements, the same quiet agreement about what matters and what we can safely ignore, *including* our shared agreement to not think too hard about death and God. And a friendship built on a shared direction can only walk with you as far as that direction goes. When death turns everyone to face the one reality they'd all agreed to ignore together — the friendship has nothing left to stand on. Worse: the Qur'an tells us elsewhere that on that Day, the friends who bonded *over* heedlessness turn on each other, become *enemies* — because each one now sees the other as the company that made the drift feel safe. That is verse 99 breaking out: *"none led us astray but the criminals"* — the friends, turning.

The only friendship that survives the crossing is one built on a direction that *doesn't end at the grave.* A bond in Allah, walking *toward* Him — that friendship has somewhere to go when everything else stops. It doesn't hit the wall of death, because it was never pointed at anything death could take.

Let me make this concrete, because it should change something specific about tomorrow. Look honestly at your closest bonds and ask a single question of each: **what are we walking toward together?** If the honest answer is "nothing in particular" or "the next distraction," that friendship is warm — it may be genuinely warm — but it is warm the way a fire is warm: only while the fuel lasts, and you cannot carry it across. If the honest answer includes *Him* — even a little, even a text that says "did you pray," even a friend who gets *better* when they're around you and not worse — you have found a *ḥamīm* that death cannot cancel. That is worth more than a hundred contacts. Go water it. Call that person. Be that person for someone else.

And now the last verse, because it turns the whole passage from a description into a plea aimed straight at you:

<!-- ayah:26:102 -->     فَلَوْ أَنَّ لَنَا كَرَّةً فَنَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

*"If only we had a **karrah** — a return, one more turn — that we might be among the believers."*

*Karratan*, from the root *k-r-r*, is a single going-back, one more circuit, another turn of the wheel. And it is the most human sentence in the passage, because it is the sentence you have already said. Everyone has said it. *If I could just go back. If I could have that year again, that conversation, that fork in the road, knowing what I know now.* It is the universal ache. And Allah records it here in the mouths of people to whom the answer is, forever, **no.**

But here is what I could not stop thinking about. **You are, right now, standing inside the karrah they are begging for.** The thing they would trade the entire Fire to get — one more turn, as a believer — you are *holding it.* This breath is the karrah. This ordinary Thursday is the karrah. The fact that this reflection is reaching your ears and not your record is the karrah. [PAUSE]

They wished backward for the exact position you are sitting in and calling *normal life.* Don't let the ordinariness of it hide the enormity of it. The most desperate wish in the Fire is a Tuesday afternoon you already own.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture: three axes, all severed

Now that we've done the work, let me show you the shape of these seven verses, because I don't think it's an accident, and I think it's the proof that this passage was *designed*.

Watch what gets stripped away, and in what order.

They begin by naming their crime against **the Most High** — verse 98, *nusawwī bi-Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn* — a crime committed *upward*, against the One above all worlds.

Then, verse 100 — *no intercessors.* Intercession is the *vertical* relationship: those who might reach *up* on your behalf, plead your case to Heaven. **Gone.**

Then, verse 101 — *no warm friend.* Friendship is the *horizontal* relationship: those beside you, at your own level, who might simply stand with you. **Gone.**

Then, verse 102 — *if only we had a return.* The *karrah* is the relationship to *time itself*: the axis backward, the wish to reach into the past. **Gone.**

Do you see it? Up. Across. Back. **Every axis of connection a human being has — vertical, horizontal, temporal — is severed, one by one, in sequence.** No one above to plead. No one beside to comfort. No way back to fix it. The person who spent a life *leveling* — dragging things to the same height in the heart — ends up leveled themselves: flattened into a point, alone on every axis at once.

That is the terrible symmetry. The sin was *taswiyah*, a leveling of what should never be level. And the wage is *taswiyah* of a different kind — the self leveled down to utter isolation, stripped of every relation that once held it up. What you do to your Lord's uniqueness, you ultimately suffer in your own aloneness. The one who would not let God stand alone as God finds, in the end, that *he* is the one left standing alone.

### Where we started and where we've arrived

We began with people who were *sure* — sure enough to build their lives on a stone. We heard them gasp *"By Allah"* with a wonder that finally woke up too late. We watched them confess, with surgical precision, that their sin was a *leveling* of devotion. And we watched every rope of connection they had — upward, sideways, backward — cut, until the last thing they could do was wish for the ordinary breath you are breathing as I speak.

And the strand from Ibrāhīm closes here. He asked them, gently, on earth: *do these idols hear you? do they help you?* They shrugged: *we found our fathers doing so.* This passage is simply that shrug, played back on the Day it comes due — the calm question of a prophet becoming the screamed confession of a people. Ibrāhīm gave them the diagnosis for free. They paid for it in full.

### Questions to carry

Not rhetorical. Sit with them, honestly, in the quiet after this ends.

1. In the last hard decision you made — the real one, with two things pulling — was Allah's command a *King* who settled it, or a *candidate* who had to win an argument? What does that tell you about what you've seated beside Him?
2. Of the friendships you would name as closest: which ones are pointed at something death cannot take, and which are warm only while the fuel lasts?
3. If you were granted the *karrah* — a return, knowing everything you know — what is the first thing you would change? And then the harder question: **why are you waiting for a return to do the thing you'd do if you had one?**
4. Where in your life is there a *"By Allah"* — a gasp of undivided wonder at Him — that you keep postponing until the season settles down?

### The one sentence to carry

**Shirk is the act of leveling the created up to the Creator — and its final wage is to be leveled down into an aloneness so complete that not one pleader, not one warm friend, and not one second chance remains; and the second chance they scream for is the ordinary day you are holding right now.**

### The invitation

So here is the invitation — and it is an invitation, not a verdict. You have been shown the end of the road that begins with a quiet leveling. You have been shown a wonder that came too late, and an aloneness counted down to zero, and a wish for a return that will never be granted.

And you have been shown that you are *inside* the wish. The karrah is not behind you, out of reach. It is under your feet. Everything the people in the Fire would give the whole Fire to get — you have, this morning, for free.

Don't spend it leveling. Take your hand off the scale. Let Him be uniquely, undividedly high in the one place it counts — the deliberation in your chest — and go find the one warm friend whose warmth can survive the crossing, and *be* that friend for someone else. That is the whole of it. That is what a saved *karrah* looks like.

### A closing duʿā

*O Allah, Lord of the worlds — the One we so easily level, the One above every world and every axis of our lives — do not let our wonder at You arrive too late. Keep Your command a King in our hearts and never a candidate. Grant us, before we need it, the warm and faithful friend whose road leads to You, and make us that friend for others. And let this breath, this day, this return we are holding — be the karrah we used well, so that we are gathered, by Your mercy alone, among the believers.* Āmīn.
