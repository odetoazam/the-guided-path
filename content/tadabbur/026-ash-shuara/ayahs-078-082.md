# The One Who Created Me — Ibrahim's Map of Total Dependence

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 78–82

> الَّذِي خَلَقَنِي فَهُوَ يَهْدِينِ
> وَالَّذِي هُوَ يُطْعِمُنِي وَيَسْقِينِ
> وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ
> وَالَّذِي يُمِيتُنِي ثُمَّ يُحْيِينِ
> وَالَّذِي أَطْمَعُ أَن يَغْفِرَ لِي خَطِيئَتِي يَوْمَ الدِّينِ

> *"The One who created me — and so He guides me. And the One who feeds me and gives me drink. And when I fall ill, it is He who heals me. And the One who will cause me to die and then bring me back to life. And the One whom I deeply hope will forgive me my mistake on the Day of Recompense."*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

To hear these five ayahs the way they were first heard, you have to picture a young man — barely past adolescence in some traditions, perhaps older in others — standing in the central square of a city whose entire economic, political, and religious life is built on stone gods.

Ibrahim's father carves them. The temple priests sell offerings to them. The king of the land claims a kind of divinity through his association with them. The whole social fabric — who you marry, whom you trade with, what you eat, where you bury your dead — is woven from these idols.

And Ibrahim, alone, has been asking the most uncomfortable question a person in such a society can ask: **what do these things actually do?**

In the verses just before our passage, he asks his people directly. *"Do they hear you when you call? Or do they benefit you, or harm you?"* And his people give the only answer they have left — the answer that exposes the whole arrangement: *"No. But we found our fathers doing this."*

That is the moment our passage begins.

[PAUSE]

Ibrahim could have continued attacking. He could have piled on more humiliating questions. Instead — and this is the move we have to feel — he turns. He turns from arguing about their gods to **describing his**.

He doesn't describe Him with titles. He doesn't lecture about attributes. He maps a human life — *his own life* — onto the One he worships. Birth. Guidance. Hunger. Thirst. Sickness. Healing. Death. Resurrection. Hope for forgiveness.

Five ayahs. The whole arc of a human existence — from the womb to the Day of Judgment — and at every single station, one Subject.

And at each station, the same devastating counter-question hangs in the air unspoken: *can your idols do any of this?* Classical commentators note that the list is itself the indictment — what Ibrahim is about to enumerate is precisely what his people's gods were just found unable to claim. None of them can feed, none can heal, none will be present on the Day of Recompense. The affirmation and the refutation are one move.

### The Hook

Here is what we are going to discover, and I want you to hold the question lightly until we earn it:

In one of these five ayahs — and only one — Ibrahim does something grammatically that he refuses to do anywhere else in the passage. He breaks his own pattern. He pulls a single experience away from Allah and places it on himself.

Why? What is so different about that one moment in human life that even Ibrahim — the friend of Allah, the master of *tawhid* — would not assign it to his Lord?

The answer, when we get to it, will reframe what *adab* with Allah actually means. Not a polite formula. A surgical understanding of who owns what.

[PAUSE]

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Repeated *Alladhī* — A Pattern That Refuses to Stop

Before we touch the Arabic, let me ask you to picture something.

Imagine someone you love deeply has been hurt by gossip. People have said cruel things about who they are. And you, knowing them better than anyone, decide to defend them. You don't shout. You don't argue. You simply start describing them.

You say: *"This is the one who stayed with me when my mother died. This is the one who sent me money when I had nothing. This is the one who picked me up at 3 a.m. when my car broke down. This is the one who never told a soul about my worst mistake."*

Notice what's happening. You are not making one big claim. You are making a *cascade* of small, undeniable claims. Each "this is the one who..." is a stone. And by the time you've laid five of them, you've built a wall.

In Arabic, this construction has a name: a relative clause beginning with **الَّذِي** — *alladhī*, "the one who."

It functions like a definite description. It picks out a specific entity by what that entity *does*. And when you repeat it — *alladhī... wa-alladhī... wa-alladhī...* — you are not just listing actions. You are identifying a single Being through the fingerprint of His acts.

Now look at our passage:

- **الَّذِي خَلَقَنِي** — *alladhī khalaqanī* — "The One who created me..."
- **وَالَّذِي هُوَ يُطْعِمُنِي وَيَسْقِينِ** — *wa-alladhī huwa yuṭʿimunī wa-yasqīn* — "And the One who feeds me and gives me drink..."
- **وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ** — *wa-idhā mariḍtu fa-huwa yashfīn* — "And when I fall ill, it is He who heals me..."
- **وَالَّذِي يُمِيتُنِي ثُمَّ يُحْيِينِ** — *wa-alladhī yumītunī thumma yuḥyīn* — "And the One who will cause me to die and then bring me back to life..."
- **وَالَّذِي أَطْمَعُ أَن يَغْفِرَ لِي خَطِيئَتِي يَوْمَ الدِّينِ** — *wa-alladhī aṭmaʿu an yaghfira lī khaṭīʾatī yawma ad-dīn* — "And the One whom I deeply hope will forgive me my mistake on the Day of Recompense..."

[PAUSE]

Before moving through the rhythm, look closely at the first link. *Alladhī khalaqanī **fa**-huwa yahdīn* — "The One who created me — **and so** He guides me." That *fa* is not decorative. In classical Arabic, *fa* of this kind expresses consequence: *because* He created you, *therefore* He guides you. Creation is the warrant for guidance. The One who designed you is the only One qualified to orient you. Al-Muyassar notes that this creation was *fī aḥsani ṣūra* — in the most excellent form — which means the guidance that follows is not merely practical correction but something that answers to the quality of what was made: a being fashioned well, led well.

What that guidance actually means, however, is where the classical commentators part ways — and the divergence is worth sitting with rather than resolving prematurely. Al-Tabari reads *yahdīni* as guidance toward right speech and right action — *al-ṣawāb min al-qawl wa-al-ʿamal* — the practical orientation of a life aligned with truth. Ibn Kathir reads it through the lens of divine decree: He guides whomsoever He wills, and each person follows the path that has been destined for him. Al-Muyassar draws both together and says the guidance is toward the interests of both this world and the next. These readings do not cancel each other so much as illuminate different surfaces of the same word: the *huda* Ibrahim invokes is total — encompassing practical rectitude, providential destiny, and comprehensive flourishing all at once. Ibrahim does not say which he means, because he means all of it.

Stay with the rhythm of it. *Alladhī... wa-alladhī... fa-huwa... wa-alladhī... wa-alladhī...*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. In the previous ayah — ayah 77 — Ibrahim has just told his people, *"They are an enemy to me, except the Lord of all the worlds."* He has named one Being. **Rabb al-ʿālamīn**. The Lord of all the worlds.

What follows in our passage is **the unpacking of that single name**. Every *alladhī* is a beam of light fanning out from that one Source.

He is not arguing for God's existence. He is showing you what *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* actually means — concretely, biographically, in the texture of one human life.

And here is what the grammar is doing that the English translation cannot quite carry: each *alladhī* tightens the identification. By the third one, you can no longer slot any other being into the description. By the fifth, the field of candidates has shrunk to one.

This is not poetry decorating a theology. **This is theology done in the form of a fingerprint match.**

[PAUSE — sit with this]

What this single grammatical pattern reveals: Ibrahim is teaching us that *tawhid* is not primarily an argument we win in our heads. It is a way of looking at our own life and noticing — at every station — *the same Hand*.

### Element Two: The One Asymmetry — *Wa Idhā Mariḍtu Fa-Huwa Yashfīn*

Now I want to take you to the strangest line in the passage. It is the line that broke me the first time I noticed what it was doing.

Look at the structure of the other four:

- "The One who **created me** — and so He guides me."
- "The One who **feeds me** and gives me drink."
- "The One who **causes me to die** and then brings me back to life."
- "The One whom I hope will **forgive me** my mistake on the Day of Recompense."

In every other ayah, the verb is attributed to Allah directly. *He* creates. *He* feeds. *He* causes death. *He* forgives.

Now read the third one again:

> **وَإِذَا مَرِضْتُ فَهُوَ يَشْفِينِ**
> *"And when I fall ill, it is He who heals me."*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it?

The verb **مَرِضْتُ** — *mariḍtu*, "I fell ill" — is in the **first person**. *I* did this. *I* got sick.

Ibrahim does not say, "And the One who makes me sick and then heals me." He could have. The parallel with *yumītunī thumma yuḥyīn* — *He causes me to die, then brings me back* — was right there. He could have written *wa-alladhī yumriḍunī thumma yashfīn* — *He makes me sick, then heals me*. The rhythm would have been perfect. The grammar would have been clean.

He refused.

Sickness, alone among all these stations of human life, Ibrahim pulls onto himself. Healing, he hands back to Allah.

Why?

[PAUSE]

Let me teach you a concept the classical scholars call **adab maʿa Allāh** — courtesy with Allah. It is the discipline of *how* you describe Allah, not just what you describe Him with.

The principle, noted by classical commentators on this very ayah, is this: when something carries an outward appearance of deficiency or harm — even if its deepest reality is wisdom — a worshipper, in the posture of supplication and praise, attributes it to himself. And what carries the appearance of mercy and beauty, he attributes to Allah.

This is not theology. Allah *is* the one who decreed the illness. Ibrahim knows this — every classical scholar knows this. Sickness, like every event, occurs by Allah's permission. The Quran elsewhere is explicit about this.

But theology and *adab* are not the same skill.

Theology is what you know. *Adab* is how you speak in the presence of the Beloved.

[PAUSE]

Think of it this way. Imagine a child whose mother is, in fact, the one who has imposed a difficult discipline on him — say, a strict bedtime, or homework before play. The mother is the *cause* of the difficulty. That is true. But when the child grows up and writes about his mother, and he wants to describe her with love, he says: *"My mother fed me. My mother taught me to read. My mother stayed up with me when I was sick."*

He doesn't say, *"My mother imposed bedtimes on me and made me cry."*

Both are true. But one is the speech of a son who knows whom he is talking about. The other is the speech of a court reporter.

[PAUSE]

And notice what the healing side carries. Al-Muyassar observes that the restoration Ibrahim names is not partial: *yashfīni wa-yuʿāfīni minhu* — He heals me and grants me full recovery from it. *Shifa* is the cure; *ʿāfiya* is the return to wholeness. Ibrahim assigns the deficiency to himself with one word, *mariḍtu*, and then assigns the completeness of the restoration to Allah. The asymmetry is total: the wound is mine to claim; the healing, down to its last trace, is His to give.

What Ibrahim has just shown us — in a single grammatical decision — is that the language of *tawhid* is not the language of a court reporter. It is the language of someone who has been loved.

This is why the asymmetry is theologically dazzling. Ibrahim is not denying Allah's role in the illness. He is *demonstrating what intimacy with Allah sounds like*. When you love Someone the way Ibrahim loves his Rabb, you do not list His decrees like an inventory. You attribute beauty to Him with both hands, and you carry your own difficulties on your own back.

And the breathtaking implication: the grammatical "ugly" word in this passage — *mariḍtu*, "I fell ill" — is the most beautiful proof of Ibrahim's *tawhid* in the whole sequence. Because only someone who has fully internalized that Allah is the source of every blessing would think to be careful with how he speaks about pain.

### Element Three: *Aṭmaʿu* — The Verb of Greedy Hope

Now I want to spend time with one verb in the final ayah, because it is doing more work than almost any English translation captures.

Look at it:

> **وَالَّذِي أَطْمَعُ أَن يَغْفِرَ لِي خَطِيئَتِي يَوْمَ الدِّينِ**

The verb is **أَطْمَعُ** — *aṭmaʿu*. The English usually renders it "I hope" or "I aspire."

But in classical Arabic, the root **ṭ-m-ʿ** does not mean polite hope. The lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the *Mufradāt* of al-Rāghib — give it a much more intense meaning. *Ṭamaʿ* is the kind of desire that approaches **greed**. It is the strong inclination of the soul toward something it badly wants and believes it has reason to expect.

In ordinary Arabic, *ṭamaʿ* is often a *negative* word. To be *ṭammāʿ* is to be greedy, grasping, covetous. The Quran itself uses the root in contexts of warning — don't be greedy for what people own, don't covet what is not yours.

So why does Ibrahim use this verb — of all verbs — to describe how he hopes for forgiveness?

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. He could have said *arjū* — "I hope." A polite, measured, theologically safe word. He didn't.

He said: *I am greedy for it. I crave it. I lean toward it with the whole appetite of my soul.*

Here is what this teaches: hope for Allah's mercy is not a balanced, polite, modest emotion. **It is meant to be hungry.**

There is a version of religious life that treats hope like a guest you invite in carefully — make sure you have enough fear in the room first, don't let hope get too loud. The Quran has this balance. Fear and hope are both real.

But Ibrahim shows us that when it comes to *Allah's forgiveness*, hope is permitted to *want*. To want hard. To want the way a thirsty man wants water.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the object of his greed. Look at how Ibrahim describes his sin:

**خَطِيئَتِي** — *khaṭīʾatī*, "my mistake."

Singular. Not *khaṭāyāya* — "my mistakes." Not *dhunūbī* — "my sins."

A single noun, in the singular form. *My mistake.*

This is Ibrahim — Khalīl Allāh, the friend of Allah — speaking. A man whose entire prophetic biography we know. And when he comes before Allah on the Day of Judgment, he does not catalogue his deeds. He does not list categories of error. He gathers his entire life of human imperfection into one humble word: *my mistake*.

[PAUSE]

And then notice the asymmetry one more time — because it returns here, and it returns like a closing chord.

He says: *the One whom I deeply hope will forgive me **my mistake**.*

The mistake — he keeps. He owns it. *My mistake.*

The forgiveness — he begs from Allah.

Just like the sickness. Just like the healing. The principle is consistent across the whole passage. **Lack, error, deficiency: I carry. Mercy, healing, forgiveness: He gives.**

This is the architecture of the entire passage in miniature.
