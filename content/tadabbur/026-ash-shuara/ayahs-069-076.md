# What Are You Worshipping?

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 69-76

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Arabic

> وَٱتْلُ عَلَيْهِمْ نَبَأَ إِبْرَٰهِيمَ ﴿٦٩﴾ إِذْ قَالَ لِأَبِيهِ وَقَوْمِهِۦ مَا تَعْبُدُونَ ﴿٧٠﴾ قَالُوا۟ نَعْبُدُ أَصْنَامًۭا فَنَظَلُّ لَهَا عَٰكِفِينَ ﴿٧١﴾ قَالَ هَلْ يَسْمَعُونَكُمْ إِذْ تَدْعُونَ ﴿٧٢﴾ أَوْ يَنفَعُونَكُمْ أَوْ يَضُرُّونَ ﴿٧٣﴾ قَالُوا۟ بَلْ وَجَدْنَآ ءَابَآءَنَا كَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ ﴿٧٤﴾ قَالَ أَفَرَءَيْتُم مَّا كُنتُمْ تَعْبُدُونَ ﴿٧٥﴾ أَنتُمْ وَءَابَآؤُكُمُ ٱلْأَقْدَمُونَ ﴿٧٦﴾

### The Translation

*"And recite to them the news of Ibrahim. When he said to his father and his people: 'What are you worshipping?' They said: 'We worship idols, and we remain devoted to them.' He said: 'Have you then considered what you have been worshipping — you and your most ancient forefathers?'"*

---

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open this passage, you need to understand where it sits — and why Allah is telling this particular story at this particular moment.

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is a Makkan surah. The Prophet ﷺ is being rejected. His own people — the Quraysh — are not just disagreeing with him. They are dismissing him. And the dismissal has a particular shape. It is not "your arguments are weak." It is not "we have considered and found you wrong." The dismissal is older and harder than that.

The dismissal is: **"this is not what our fathers did."**

That sentence — in various forms — is one of the most repeated objections in the entire Quran. Whenever a prophet calls people to truth, this is the wall they hit. Not "you are wrong." But "you are *new*."

So Allah builds Surah Ash-Shu'ara as a kind of courtroom. Seven prophets are called to the stand: Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb. Each one faces the same refusal. Each story ends with the same refrain. The Quraysh are meant to listen to these stories and recognize themselves — not as the prophet, but as the rejecting people.

And in the middle of this gallery, Allah places Ibrahim.

[PAUSE]

Why Ibrahim, specifically, in front of the Quraysh? Because the Quraysh claimed Ibrahim. They said *we are the children of Ibrahim. We are the keepers of his house. The Ka'bah is his legacy.* They invoked his name as the foundation of their authority.

So when Allah says **"recite to them the news of Ibrahim"** — He is not telling them a story they don't know. He is telling them the story they *thought* they knew. He is saying: you have inherited his name. Now hear what he actually said. And ask yourselves whether you are standing where he stood — or whether you are standing where the people he confronted were standing.

Consider who Ibrahim is. Classical commentators are precise on this: he is *khalīl Allāh* — the intimate Friend of Allah, the one whom Allah Himself took as a close companion. He is described as the leader of pure monotheism, the archetype of *tawḥīd*, the man who stood alone against civilizational consensus and declared that what everyone around him was worshipping was not worth worshipping. And Allah commanded the Prophet ﷺ to recite this story to his community for a specific purpose: so that they could follow Ibrahim's example of sincerity toward Allah, of trust in Him alone, of willingness to renounce what is not Him. This is not folklore. It is a template.

That is the climate. A people clinging to their fathers. A prophet being rejected because he is unfamiliar. And Allah pulling out Ibrahim — the very ancestor they invoke — to show them: **the man you claim as your father did not stand with the fathers. He stood against them.**

### The Hook

Notice something that should stop us before we even begin.

Ibrahim, may peace be upon him, does not arrive at his people with an argument. He does not arrive with a sermon. He does not arrive with a verdict.

He arrives with a question.

**"What are you worshipping?"**

[PAUSE]

A question they could have answered in their sleep. A question whose answer was sitting right in front of them, carved in stone. Why does the prophet open with the most obvious question imaginable?

And then notice what happens after they answer. Their reply itself shifts under examination. The conversation moves not where Ibrahim pushes it — but where they reveal it has always actually been. By the end of these eight ayahs, we discover that the real answer to "what are you worshipping?" was never "idols."

The real answer was something else.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Mā Taʿbudūn* — The Question That Refuses to Name

Let's start with Ibrahim's first words.

In Arabic: **مَا تَعْبُدُونَ** — *mā taʿbudūn*. "What are you worshipping?"

Before we open this phrase, let me teach you something general about how Arabic — and language in general — handles questions. When you ask someone a question, you have already done some of the work for them. You have framed the conversation. You have decided what the conversation is *about*.

Imagine a friend says to you: "Why is your phone broken?" That question has already decided that your phone *is* broken. You are now defending or explaining within their frame.

But imagine they ask: "What is that in your hand?"

The second question is doing something different. It is refusing to name. It is refusing to assume. It is making *you* the one who has to put words on the thing.

Now look at Ibrahim.

He is standing in front of his father — the man who made and sold idols, by most classical accounts — and a people who were known for their devotion to those idols. He could have said: *"Why do you worship these stones?"* He could have said: *"Why do you worship what you have made with your own hands?"* He could have led with the verdict.

He doesn't.

He says: **مَا تَعْبُدُونَ** — *what are you worshipping?*

[PAUSE]

The question is rooted in the verb **عَبَدَ** (*ʿabada*) — to worship, to serve, to enslave oneself to. From the same root we get *ʿabd*, the slave or servant. To worship is to make yourself the servant of something — to give it command over you. That is the lexical reach of the word.

So Ibrahim is not asking a sociological question. He is not asking "what religion do you practice?" He is asking the most piercing version possible: **what is it that you have made yourself a servant to?**

And he leaves the object — the *what* — completely undefined.

Stay with this. The grammatical word **مَا** here is what classical grammarians call *mā istifhāmiyyah* — the interrogative *mā*. It asks about something. But it does not tell you what kind of thing. It is open. It does not even pre-commit to whether the answer is a person, a thing, an idea, or a force.

Ibrahim could have said *"man taʿbudūn"* — "*who* are you worshipping?" That would have implied a being — a deity. By using *mā* instead of *man*, he is already, gently, putting pressure on the very category of what they worship. He is saying: I am not even granting that what you worship rises to the level of a "who." Tell me what it is. Classical commentators, when paraphrasing this question, rendered it as *ayyu shayʾin taʿbudūnahu* — "which *thing* do you worship?" The interrogative *mā* was already heard by native ears as a question about a *thing*, not a being.

But here is the pedagogical genius: he does this without accusing them. He simply asks. He gives them the dignity of speaking their position into the room.

Why?

Here is what this invites us to consider. When we want to change someone, our instinct is to tell them. To declare. To correct. We arrive with the verdict already pronounced. And the listener — sensing the verdict — closes.

The Quranic prophet does the opposite. **He makes the person say their own position out loud, in their own words, in front of themselves.**

Because the moment a belief leaves the dim cave of your assumptions and gets pronounced in syllables — in the open air, where you can hear it — something changes. You become a witness over your own creed. You see it from outside.

Ibrahim is not interrogating his people. He is helping them hear themselves.

[PAUSE]

This is the prophetic method. Not "you are wrong." But "say what you believe, slowly, so we can both look at it together."

### Element Two: *Naẓallu Lahā ʿĀkifīn* — The Tense of Permanence and the Posture of Bondage

Now look at how they answer.

In Arabic: **نَعْبُدُ أَصْنَامًۭا فَنَظَلُّ لَهَا عَٰكِفِينَ** — "We worship idols, and we remain devoted to them."

Notice: they don't just answer the question. They answer it *and then keep talking.* They volunteer more than was asked.

Ibrahim asked *what*. They answer *what* — "idols" — and then they add a whole second clause. They tell him about their *devotion*. About their *posture*. About their *constancy*.

This over-answering is itself revealing. It is the kind of thing a person does when they are slightly defensive — when they sense that the bare answer might not be impressive enough — so they bolster it with their own conviction. *We don't just worship them. We are committed. We are devoted. We do not waver.*

Now let's open the words they used, because the word choice is staggering.

The first word is **نَظَلُّ** (*naẓallu*). This comes from the root **ظ-ل-ل**, which carries the meaning of *continuing in a state*, of remaining throughout something, classically associated with the daytime — to remain doing something across the day. The verb in this conjugation says: *we remain, we continue, we stay in this condition.*

Listen to what they have actually said about themselves: **"we keep on, continuously, we do not stop."**

They are bragging about their permanence.

But here is what classical linguists noted about the verb *ẓalla*: in its most common usage, it is paired with daylight. *Ẓalla yafʿalu* — he kept doing such-and-such throughout the day. There is something temporal about it. It does not, on its own, reach into eternity. It reaches across a stretch — a long stretch — but a stretch that ends.

So buried inside their boast is a quiet limit. They say "we remain" — but the verb itself only stretches as far as the daylight stretches. It is the language of duration, not eternity. They think they are claiming forever. The verb only gives them a day.

[PAUSE]

Now the second word: **عَٰكِفِينَ** (*ʿākifīn*).

This is from the root **ع-ك-ف** — *ʿakafa*. And this root is heavy. It means to confine oneself to something, to bind oneself to a place, to remain attached to it without leaving. From this same root comes *iʿtikāf* — the spiritual retreat in the masjid during Ramadan, where you confine yourself to the house of Allah and do not leave it.

So *ʿākifūn* are people who have *bound themselves* to something. Who have *stayed* with it. Who have refused to leave it.

Among the earliest Quranic interpreters, Ibn Abbas read *ʿākifīn* in this verse with striking precision: he understood it as *ṣalāh* — the ritual prayer, performed for the idols. Not devotion in a vague or general sense, but the full architecture of prostration and attendance, the postures of reverence, the kneeling and the standing and the turning toward — all of it directed at stone. Classical commentators elaborated on what this looked like in practice: these people were *khudaman muqīmīn* — stationed servants, settled permanently in the service and worship of these objects, like temple custodians who never go home, never leave their post, permanently resident in front of what they serve.

[PAUSE]

This means the connection to *iʿtikāf* is not a distant metaphor. It is, by the reading of the earliest masters of this language, precise: they were performing something structurally identical to prayer. The same act. The same architecture. The same orientation of body and submission and turning-toward. The same *ṣalāh* — pointed at stone.

Here is what is breathtaking. The exact same root used for the highest form of devotion — confining yourself to Allah's house — is here being used for confining yourself to stones. And the highest human posture, the prayer-posture, is here being given to the lowest object of the environment.

The same posture. Different object.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the great teachings of the Quran. The *capacity* for worship is universal. Every human being has it. Every human being will *ʿakafa* to something — will bind themselves to something, give themselves over to something, refuse to leave something.

The question is never *whether* you are an *ʿākif*. The question is only *what you are an ʿākif to*.

You will bind yourself to something. The architecture of your soul demands it. The only choice you have is the object.

And so when these people say *"we remain devoted to them,"* they think they are giving Ibrahim their resume. They think they are saying *we are the serious ones, we are the constant ones.* What they are actually saying — without realizing — is *we have given the highest faculty of our being to the lowest object of our environment. We have done iʿtikāf to stone.*

Ibrahim hears this. We should too.

### Element Three: *Wajadnā Ābāʾanā* — The Verb of Discovery That Hides a Failure to Look

Ibrahim then asks two questions that should, in any sane conversation, end the discussion.

**"Do they hear you when you call? Or do they benefit you, or harm you?"**

Three tests, in ascending order: hearing, benefit, harm. If your idol can't even *hear* you, the conversation is over. If it can't *benefit* you, why approach? If it can't even *harm* you — even the most basic claim of power, the threat — then what exactly are you afraid of? What exactly are you hoping for?

Notice that Ibrahim does not assert. He asks. Again. *Hal yasmaʿūnakum* — *do they hear you?* He is offering them the chance to answer it themselves.

And here is where the conversation breaks open. Look at their reply. It is one of the most devastating sentences in the Quran.

**بَلْ وَجَدْنَآ ءَابَآءَنَا كَذَٰلِكَ يَفْعَلُونَ** — *"Rather, we found our fathers doing thus."*

Stop here. Sit with this.

[PAUSE]

Ibrahim asked them three concrete, factual, verifiable questions. *Do they hear? Do they benefit? Do they harm?*

They did not answer any of them.

Not one.

They did not say "yes they hear." They did not say "no they don't hear." They did not even engage with the question. They simply *moved the conversation off the question.* They redirected. They took the discussion from *the idols themselves* — which Ibrahim was asking about — to *our fathers* — which is what they actually wanted to defend.

This is one of the most precise psychological observations in scripture. **When a belief cannot survive the question, the person stops defending the belief and begins defending the inheritance.**

They do not say: "these idols are worthy of worship." They say: "our fathers worshipped." These are not the same claim. But in their mind, in the heat of this conversation, they have become indistinguishable. The evidence for the idol is the father. Not the idol's hearing. Not the idol's power. The father.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the specific word they use. **وَجَدْنَا** — *wajadnā*. "We found." From the root **و-ج-د** — *wajada*. To find, to come upon, to encounter something already present.

This word is worth sitting with, because it is precisely the wrong word if you are trying to claim that your religion is true. *Wajada* is not the verb of investigation. It is not the verb of inquiry, testing, or seeking. It is the verb of coming upon something that was already there — encountering it in its place, as it was, before you arrived.

*We found our fathers doing thus.*

They are describing themselves as people who walked into a room where something was already happening. They came upon the practice. They did not examine it. They did not choose it. They found it. And they have kept it.

The word *wajadnā* is doing something devastating here: it is the language of discovery — active, even exciting — but it describes the most passive possible relationship to a belief. **They are using the grammar of exploration to describe the complete absence of exploration.**

And notice: the grammatical particle **بَلْ** that starts their sentence. *Bal* — "rather." In Arabic grammar, *bal* is a particle of correction and transition. When someone uses *bal*, they are signaling: I am replacing the previous frame with a new one. I am not answering in your terms. I am switching the conversation's coordinates.

Ibrahim asked about the idol. *Bal* announces: we are not talking about the idol. We are now talking about our fathers. The redirect is made explicit, even grammatically.

[PAUSE]

Here is what the passage has unveiled. Ibrahim asked *"what are you worshipping?"* and they said "idols." But when the questions got serious — when he asked *do they hear? do they benefit? do they harm?* — the idols disappeared from the answer. They were never really the point. The idols were the *form* of what they worshipped. The *substance* was something else entirely.

The substance was the chain. The fathers. The continuity of ancestral practice. The comfort of being inside a tradition that goes back before memory.

**They were not, in the end, worshipping idols. They were worshipping inheritance itself.**

That is the thing Ibrahim's question was designed to surface. And it did.

### Element Four: *Al-Aqdamūn* — The Chain Extended to Breaking

Ibrahim's response in ayahs 75 and 76 is one of the most elegant rhetorical moves in the Quran.

He could have said: "Your fathers were wrong." He could have said: "Ancestral practice is not evidence." He could have argued the point directly.

He doesn't.

Instead, he accepts their framework — the fathers — and then extends it.

**أَفَرَءَيْتُم مَّا كُنتُمْ تَعْبُدُونَ ﴿٧٥﴾ أَنتُمْ وَءَابَآؤُكُمُ ٱلْأَقْدَمُونَ ﴿٧٦﴾**

*"Have you then considered what you have been worshipping — you and your most ancient forefathers?"*

Two elements here deserve our full attention.

The first is the verb **أَفَرَءَيْتُمْ** — *afaraʾaytum*. It is built from **رَأَى** (*raʾā*) — to see. The root *r-ʾ-y* covers both physical sight and interior consideration. When used in a context of reflection, *raʾā* means to turn something over in the mind's eye — to *consider*, to *examine*, to bring it before your inner gaze and look at it with attention. The *fa* of consequence and the interrogative *a* together make the question feel like a response to what just happened: *given what you have just said — given that you have invoked your fathers — then have you SEEN it? Has any mind in this entire chain actually looked at what it was carrying?*

Ibrahim is not asking if they know what it is called. He is not asking if they can recite its prayers. He is asking if they have *seen* it. Considered it. Turned it before the gaze that *raʾā* requires.

[PAUSE]

The implicit answer is devastating. No. No one in the chain has seen it. They have found it, received it, continued it, defended it. But no mind — theirs or their fathers' — has ever stopped and truly looked.

And then the second element: **ٱلْأَقْدَمُونَ** — *al-aqdamūn*.

This is the plural of *aqdam* — the superlative form of *qadīm* (ancient), from the root **ق-د-م**. *Aqdam* means: the most ancient, the most primordial, the furthest back. *Al-aqdamūn* are not just your fathers. They are the most ancient of your fathers. The ones at the very origin of the chain.

Ibrahim is extending the question all the way back. Not just to your father, and his father — but to the *aqdamūn*, the ones who first started this. The very first link. The beginning of the chain.

And he includes you and your most ancient forefathers in the same single question: *have you considered what you have been worshipping, all of you, from you to the most primordial of them?*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what he has done? He has accepted the logic of ancestral authority and then followed it to its own conclusion. If the chain is the evidence, then let us look at the whole chain. Let us include every link, all the way back. And let us ask of every link: has this link *seen*? Has any mind in this entire lineage ever had a moment of genuine *raʾā* — of true consideration — about what they were carrying?

Because if no link has ever truly seen it, then the chain is not a chain of wisdom transmitted across generations. It is a chain of transmission without examination. Continuity without sight.

The antiquity of a practice is not evidence of its truth. A chain can be ancient and wrong from the very beginning. *Al-aqdamūn* does not make something right. It only makes it old.

This is the blade edge of Ibrahim's logic. He does not dismiss the fathers. He asks whether the fathers *thought*. He does not say "your ancestors are worthless." He says: *even your most ancient ancestors — have they considered this? Have any of them seen?*

The question becomes both an exposure and an invitation.

## PART 2: THE THREE MOVES OF THE PROPHETIC CONVERSATION

Pull back now and look at the whole passage as a structure.

What Ibrahim does in these eight ayahs is not an argument. It is a sequence. Three moves, each one building on the one before.

**The first move: Make them name it.**

*"What are you worshipping?"*

He creates the space in which they must say, in their own words, what they actually believe. They say: idols. And we remain devoted to them. They have articulated a position.

**The second move: Test the object.**

*"Do they hear you? Do they benefit you? Do they harm you?"*

Now that the object has been named, Ibrahim tests it on its own terms. Not "this is false" — but "does it perform?" He applies the simplest imaginable standard: if you give yourself to something, does it do anything? Does it hear your voice? Does it change your circumstances? Does it have the capacity even to harm you if you leave?

This is the moment the conversation should end. Because the object fails every test. And the people know it. Their non-response confirms it.

**The third move: Reveal the real object of worship.**

Their non-answer is Ibrahim's most important discovery. Because now the actual thing has surfaced. They were not defending idols. They were defending *belonging*. The idols are a symbol, a container, a form. The real content was: *we are our fathers' people. We are inside the tradition. We do not step outside.*

And Ibrahim's final question — *have you considered what you have been worshipping, you and your most ancient forefathers?* — is aimed precisely at this real content. Not the idol. The chain.

[PAUSE]

Notice the economy. Ibrahim uses no more words than he needs. He asks questions. He does not repeat himself. He does not escalate. He simply follows the conversation wherever it goes — and where it goes reveals everything.

This is why the Quran calls this a *nabaʾ* — the news of Ibrahim, literally the report, the account. It is not a parable. It is testimony. A record of what the prophetic encounter actually looks like when it is done rightly. Not with force, but with precision. Not with condemnation, but with the kind of questioning that lets the truth surface from inside the questioner rather than being hammered in from outside.

The mirror is held up. The people see themselves.

And the Quraysh — listening to the Prophet ﷺ recite these words — are hearing it twice: once from Ibrahim to his people, and once from Allah to them. They are both the audience of the recitation and the subject of it. The command *ittlu ʿalayhim* — recite to *them* — sets up the trap with perfect precision. They hear a story about their own position, delivered through the voice of the very ancestor whose name they used to justify that position.

That is the architecture of this surah at work. Seven stories, one mirror. And the face looking back from every one of them is theirs.

## PART 3: FOR US

You have been reading this as a story about other people. People from long ago. People who worshipped stone.

Now close that distance.

[PAUSE]

What is *your* answer to Ibrahim's first question?

Not "what religion are you?" Not "what are your beliefs?" But the more piercing version: **what is it that you have made yourself the servant of? What is it that, without thinking, you are *ʿākif* to — bound to, stationed in front of, giving the prayer-posture of your life to?**

Because the Quran's diagnosis is not "people who lived long ago worshipped idols." The Quran's diagnosis is that human beings have a universal capacity for misplaced *ʿibādah* — for giving the highest human faculty of devotion and servitude to something that does not deserve it. And the most common, most durable, most socially protected form of that misplacement is *inheritance*.

Not conscious choice. Not considered decision. But *wajadnā* — we found it. We came upon it already in motion. We received it. We have continued it. We have not, perhaps, looked at it.

[PAUSE]

Now, this is not an argument against tradition. The Quran itself is a tradition — transmitted, preserved, carried across chains of reliable transmission that we are asked to trust. Islam has a rich jurisprudential culture precisely *because* inherited knowledge is real knowledge and transmission across generations is a gift.

The distinction is not *tradition versus independent thinking*. The distinction is *seeing versus not-seeing*.

A person who inherits a practice and has turned it over in their mind's eye — who has asked the questions, who has looked at it with *raʾā*, who has considered why and what and whether — and who then continues it: this is not what Ibrahim was challenging. This is someone who has made the practice their own.

A person who inherits a practice and has *never* looked at it — who could not tell you why, who defends it only because their fathers had it, who would be unable to answer Ibrahim's questions — this is what the passage is examining.

Ibrahim's question is not *"have you inherited?"* It is *"have you seen?"*

[PAUSE]

There is a second layer here that the passage builds toward but does not make explicit. When they say *we found our fathers doing thus* — the subtext is not only laziness. There is fear embedded in it. The fear of stepping outside the inherited frame: *what will it mean for us if we question this? What will happen to our belonging? To our identity? To our place in the community of the fathers?*

Ibrahim knows this fear because he lived it. He did not just question his community. He questioned his father — the very person who shaped him, who presumably loved him, who had expectations of him. He chose the question over the relationship's peace. He chose sight over belonging.

And yet the passage presents him as *khalīl Allāh*. The intimate Friend of God. As though the willingness to look — to truly see — is itself part of what made him who he was. As though Allah's friendship is given to those who will not carry what they have not examined.

[PAUSE]

So Ibrahim's question comes to each of us in our own time, in our own particulars.

Not "are you practicing Islam?" Not "do you perform the rituals?" But: *have you considered? Have you seen? Can you say, with honesty, that you have turned what you carry before your inner gaze and looked at it — not just received it, not just defended it because it was received — but actually looked?*

And if you have looked, and you have seen, and you find it true — then your practice has moved from *wajadnā ābāʾanā* (we found our fathers doing it) to something else entirely. It has become yours. Not theirs passed to you. But yours, chosen, seen, confirmed.

That is the transformation the question is designed to catalyze.

And the Quran presents Ibrahim — the archetype of tawḥīd, the intimate Friend of Allah — as someone who could not rest until he had looked. Who would not hold a belief because it was given. Who could not worship what he had not, in the truest sense, seen.

## CLOSING

This passage ends not with an argument won, but with a question posed.

**أَفَرَءَيْتُم مَّا كُنتُمْ تَعْبُدُونَ**

*Have you then considered what you have been worshipping?*

Ibrahim does not wait for the answer here. The text does not give us the answer. We do not know what his father said in that moment. We do not know what the people replied.

And perhaps this is deliberate. Because the question is not addressed only to his people. It is not addressed only to the Quraysh. It is addressed, through the vehicle of this recitation, to everyone who hears it.

The *nabaʾ* — the report, the news — travels across time. And it lands the same way each time: not with a verdict but with a question. A question that opens into the interior. A question that requires something of the listener.

[PAUSE]

*Have you seen what you worship?*

Not "do you know its name?" Not "can you recite its prayers?" But have you turned it before the gaze that *raʾā* requires — the interior gaze that considers, weighs, examines, looks at a thing until it yields its truth?

If the answer is no — if you find, honestly, that you are carrying something because you found it, because it was there when you arrived, because your fathers had it before you — then Ibrahim's question is not an accusation. It is a doorway. The prophetic method does not condemn. It asks. And the asking is itself the beginning of sight.

[PAUSE]

May Allah make us among those who see what they worship. Who carry their faith not because they found it in the room but because they have looked at it, from every angle, and found it true. Who can stand before the question and answer it with the full weight of a considered heart — not *wajadnā ābāʾanā*, but something harder and more honest: we looked, and we saw, and we chose.

The chain is long. The question is still waiting for each link.
