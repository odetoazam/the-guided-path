# The Exception That Redeems the Word

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayah 227

### إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَذَكَرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًا وَٱنتَصَرُوا۟ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا ظُلِمُوا۟ ۗ وَسَيَعْلَمُ ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوٓا۟ أَىَّ مُنقَلَبٍ يَنقَلِبُونَ

*Illā alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū as-sāliḥāti wa-dhakarū Allāha kathīran wa-ntasarū min baʿdi mā zulimū. Wa-sa-yaʿlamu alladhīna zalamū ayya munqalabin yanqalibūn.*

**"Except those who believed and did righteous deeds and remembered Allah often and defended themselves after they were wronged. And those who have wronged are going to know to what return they will be returned."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Wall and the Door

Imagine you have spent a long evening listening to a verdict.

A surah of nearly 230 ayahs has unfolded around you. Prophet after prophet — Musa standing before Pharaoh, Ibrahim standing before his father, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb — and every single one of them, on every single page, rejected. Mocked. Called a magician. Called a liar.

And against Muhammad ﷺ specifically, the Quraysh added one more: **called a poet.**

That word — *poet* — has hung over the entire surah like an accusation that won't go away. The Quraysh kept saying it. *He's a poet. His words are pretty noises. His verses are crafted, manipulated, intoxicating, untrue.*

So the surah finally turns and addresses the accusation directly. In the four ayahs just before this one, Allah turns the spotlight onto the poets themselves and offers a devastating diagnosis. *The poets — only the deviators follow them. Don't you see them wandering in every valley? And that they say what they do not do?*

[PAUSE]

That is the wall. The surah has built a wall. Language has been put on trial, and the verdict is harsh: words that are not lived, words that wander, words that intoxicate without obligating — these are the poets, and these are dangerous.

You can feel the weight of it. You begin to wonder: *is the Quran condemning all crafted speech? Is beauty itself under suspicion? Am I, who love language, who write, who speak, who try to move others with words — am I being indicted here?*

And then comes this final ayah.

It begins with one syllable. **One particle.** *Illā.* Except.

A door cut into the wall.

Not a renovation. Not a softening. The wall stands. But a door has been carved into it, and through that door, an entire category of human beings walks free — not by abandoning language, but by transforming it.

Tonight we want to ask: **who are these people who walked through the door?** What did they do that the wandering poets did not do? And why did Allah end an entire surah — a surah about prophets and rejection and language and lies — with this *exception* and not with the accusation?

Stay with me. There is something stunning here.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Illā* — The Particle That Builds the Door

Let's start with the first word. *Illā.*

In English we translate it as "except." It looks small. It sounds small. But in Arabic grammar, *illā* belongs to a category of particles called **adawāt al-istithnā** — the tools of *exception*. And *istithnā* is one of the most powerful structures in Arabic, because of what it presupposes.

Here is the concept, before we apply it.

Imagine I say: "Everyone in the room is tired." That's a statement. It applies universally. Now imagine I say: "Everyone in the room is tired — *except for you*." Notice what just happened. The *except* clause didn't just exclude you. It actually **strengthened** the original claim about everyone else. The exception confirms the rule. *You* are now standing in vivid contrast to a fully-asserted general truth.

This is what *istithnā* does in Arabic. The general claim is not weakened. It is *intensified* by the carving out of the exception. The wall is not knocked down. A specific door is cut into a wall that otherwise stands firm.

Now look at where we are.

Ayah 224: *And the poets — the deviators follow them.*
Ayah 225: *Don't you see them wandering in every valley?*
Ayah 226: *And that they say what they do not do?*

Three ayahs of indictment. The accusation is total. **And then:** *illā.*

Let's sit with this. The poet — as a category, as a way of being in language — has been indicted. The diagnosis is: their words wander, their words don't translate into deeds, their words deceive. This is not a small charge. Allah is putting on trial the entire human capacity to use beautiful speech without inhabiting it.

And then *illā* opens a door.

Notice something. Allah did not say *and there are some good poets too.* He did not soften the indictment. He kept the wall standing — the diagnosis of language-without-life is left fully intact — and then He cut a precise, doorway-shaped exception into it.

What this invites us to consider: **the problem was never beauty. The problem was never craft. The problem was never the love of language.** The problem was the gap between word and life. The exception clause tells us exactly what closes that gap. And anyone — poet, speaker, writer, parent, teacher, anyone who lives by their tongue — anyone can walk through this door. But you have to walk through *exactly* this door. Allah is going to tell us its dimensions in the next breath.

Classical commentators record who first walked through it. Al-Tabari identifies the occasion of this exception as the Companion poets who had taken up their pens in the Prophet's ﷺ defense — men like Hassan ibn Thabit and Ka'b ibn Malik — composing counter-verse against the Qurayshi satirists who were attacking him with poetry. These were not men who wandered in every valley; their words had a precise destination and a precise opponent. Al-Tabari adds an equally important principle: the exception extends to all who carry the qualities Allah described. The door was carved at a specific historical moment for specific living people standing in a specific battle of verses — but its dimensions are not historical. They are permanent. Whoever fits the doorframe, in any century, walks through.

## Element Two: *Dhakarū Allāha Kathīran* — The Word that Remembers

The exception lists four things. Believed. Did righteous deeds. Remembered Allah often. And defended themselves after being wronged.

I want to camp on the third one — *wa-dhakarū Allāha kathīran* — because this is where the surgical genius of the ayah lives.

The root *dh-k-r* in Arabic carries two intertwined meanings in classical lexicons: **to remember** and **to mention.** Not two separate meanings — one meaning. In Arabic, to truly remember something is to bring it to the tongue. To say it. To mention it. The mind and the mouth are linked.

Now, the qualifier here is *kathīran.* Often. Much. Abundantly.

Here is what I want you to notice. In an ayah whose entire context is about **what the tongue does**, in a surah that has just indicted **wandering, hollow speech**, Allah does not say: *except those who stopped speaking so much.* He does not say: *except those who became silent.* He does not condemn abundant speech.

He says: *except those who remembered Allah* — **abundantly.** *Kathīran.*

Stay with that. The cure for hollow words is not fewer words. **The cure for hollow words is words that remember.**

The poets in the previous ayahs were diagnosed with two failures: their words *wandered* (no destination), and their words were *not lived* (no follow-through). What is *dhikr*? *Dhikr* is the exact opposite of both. *Dhikr* is speech with a destination — every word points back to Allah. *Dhikr* is speech that re-enters the heart — you cannot mention Allah often without your heart following your tongue.

So Allah is not asking the poet to stop being a poet. He is asking the poet to **change what the poet remembers.** Keep the abundance. Redirect it.

Here's what's remarkable. The very faculty that condemned the wandering poet — the gift of fluent, abundant, beautiful speech — is the very faculty that, when oriented toward Allah, becomes the badge of the saved. Same faculty. Different qibla. The tongue is not the problem. The tongue's *direction* is the problem.

[PAUSE]

And notice the absence here. Allah did not specify *what* they remembered Allah with. He did not say *they recited verses,* or *they sang praises,* or *they performed dhikr in this particular way.* He left the form open. The *kathīran* is specified — the abundance is non-negotiable — but the form is left to the human being. Whatever your tongue can do, do it abundantly *toward Him.*

The wandering poet is not asked to silence his gift. He is asked to **dedicate** it.

What that dedication looked like in the lives of the earliest poets who walked through this door is preserved in the commentarial tradition. Al-Muyassar describes the redeemed poets as those who said poetry in the tawhid of Allah and in His praise, and who spoke wisdom (*hikma*) and admonition (*maw'iza*) through their verse — cultivating beauty in the service of what is true rather than what is merely effective. The tongue stayed eloquent, stayed abundant. It simply found a truer subject. And when the Qurayshi satirists aimed their verse at the Prophet ﷺ, these Companion poets answered with verse of their own — a counter-literary defense that the fourth condition of the exception, *wantasarū* ("they defended themselves"), captures with precision. The gift was never surrendered. It was conscripted in service of the real.

## Element Three: *Munqalab* and *Yanqalibūn* — The Word That Turns

Now look at the very last words of the surah. *Ayya munqalabin yanqalibūn.*

Both words come from the same root: **q-l-b.** And the root *q-l-b* is one of the most loaded roots in the Quranic vocabulary, because it means *to turn over, to flip, to reverse.* From this root comes **qalb** — the heart — because the heart is the thing that constantly turns. From this root comes **inqilāb** — overturning, revolution, reversal.

Now look at the grammatical form. *Munqalab* is an *ism makān* — a noun of place — derived from Form VII (*infaʿala*), which is the **passive-reflexive** form. So *munqalab* is not just "a return." It is *the place to which one is turned over.* The destination of the flip. And *yanqalibūn* is the verb in that same Form VII — *they will be turned over,* *they will be flipped.*

Here is what is extraordinary. Allah uses the verb of *q-l-b* twice — *munqalabin yanqalibūn* — back-to-back. The same root, twice, in different forms. In Arabic, this is called **maṣdar muʾakkid** when it's done with the verbal noun, and the effect is intensification through repetition. *To what flipping they will be flipped.* The sentence itself **flips on itself** sonically. You can hear the turning in the syllables.

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself: why this root? Why end an entire surah — 227 ayahs — on the image of *being turned over?*

Because the entire surah has been about **reversal.** Pharaoh thought he was the highest — he was reversed. Ibrahim's father thought his idols were truth — they were reversed. The wandering poet thinks his words are powerful — they are about to be reversed. **Every wrongdoer in the surah lives inside a reality that is about to flip.**

The wronged believer who is mocked in this life will be flipped to honor.
The mocking wrongdoer who feels secure will be flipped to ruin.
The wandering word that seemed so glittering will be flipped to evidence against the speaker.
The remembered word that seemed so quiet will be flipped to light on the Day of Standing.

Al-Muyassar's reading of *alladhīna zalamū* here is worth pausing on. The wrongdoers the surah closes by warning are not only those who erred theologically — those who wronged themselves through *shirk* and disobedience. They include those who wronged others: denied people their rights, transgressed against them, or leveled false accusations (*al-tuhum al-bāṭila*) at those who had done nothing. The closing warning is broader than it first appears. It encompasses every form of injustice that rides on the back of power, of privilege, or — and here the surah's final echo lands with particular weight — speech used as a weapon against the innocent.

And here is the part that makes me stop. The grammar is *passive-reflexive.* They are *turned over.* They do not turn themselves. They thought they were the agents — they were running the show, they were composing the verses, they were holding the power — and the surah closes by revealing: **you were never the one turning anything. You were always the one being turned.**

The agency you imagined was a story you were telling yourself. The actual flipping was being done by the One whose Name you would not mention.

Notice one more absence. Allah does not specify the destination. *To what return they will be returned.* He says *ayya* — *which.* Which return? *To what kind of place?* He leaves it open — and that openness is more terrifying than any specification, because the imagination fills it with what the soul most fears. The unnamed destination is the destination the wrongdoer cannot bear to name.

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. *Illā* — the particle that cut a precise door into a fully-built wall. *Dhakarū Allāha kathīran* — the speech that doesn't wander because it has a destination. *Munqalabin yanqalibūn* — the reversal at the end of every story that the wrongdoer thought he was authoring.

Together, these three reveal something the wandering poet could never see. **The universe itself has a grammar.** It has rules of exception, rules of remembrance, rules of return. You can speak all you want, but your speech is happening *inside* a sentence Allah is composing — and at the end of that sentence is a turning you do not control.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Word You Live With Will Either Save You or Indict You

Think about the most fluent talker you know.

Maybe a friend who can captivate a room. Maybe a leader whose words moved you to tears. Maybe — and this is harder — *yourself.* Most of us, if we are honest, have a private suspicion about how much of what comes out of our mouth is real. We've delivered the right line at the right meeting. We've said the warm thing we didn't fully feel. We've crafted the message to land, not to be true.

We are all, in some measure, the wandering poet.

The modern world has multiplied this enormously. Captions. Posts. Comments. Threads. We type more sentences in a day than our great-grandparents spoke in a week. And much of it is performance — saying the kind of thing that gets the reaction. *Wandering in every valley.* The Quranic image is uncomfortable because it is so accurate.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what Allah is doing in this ayah. He is not telling you to stop talking. He is telling you that the same tongue that condemns can save — but only if it is *oriented.*

The wandering poet wanders because his tongue serves whatever valley he is in — flattery in this room, attack in that one, lament in this poem, boast in that one. His speech has no qibla. Whatever the moment demands, the poet provides.
