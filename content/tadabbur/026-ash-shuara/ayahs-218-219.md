# The One Who Sees You When You Stand

> [ayah:26:218] ٱلَّذِى يَرَىٰكَ حِينَ تَقُومُ
>
> [ayah:26:219] وَتَقَلُّبَكَ فِى ٱلسَّـٰجِدِينَ
>
> *alladhī yarāka ḥīna taqūm — wa taqallubaka fī s-sājidīn*
>
> **"Who sees you when you stand — and your turning among those who prostrate."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me put you where these words landed.

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is a long surah, and it is a hard one to read straight through if you have a heart. Because it is, almost from beginning to end, a catalogue of rejection. Musa is sent to Fir'awn and is called a magician and a liar. Ibrahim pleads with his father and his people and is answered with the words: *we found our fathers doing this.* Nuh preaches for what the Quran elsewhere tells us is nearly a thousand years, and is answered with mockery, and told that only the lowest of society follow him. Then Hud. Then Salih. Then Lut. Then Shu'ayb. Prophet after prophet after prophet. And each of their stories ends with a refrain that begins to ring in your ears like a bell struck over and over: *inna fī dhālika la-āyah, wa mā kāna aktharuhum mu'minīn.* Surely in that is a sign — but most of them would not believe.

Sit with what that surah *feels* like as a document. It is the loneliest surah. It is a procession of men, each carrying the heaviest possible message, each standing before a crowd, and each — one after another — turned away. Rejected. Left standing there. And the Prophet ﷺ, receiving this in Mecca, in years the biographers call the years of grief, would have felt every one of these stories land on his own chest. Because that was *his* life. He stood before Quraysh and was called a poet, a madman, a magician. His own uncle cursed him. The people he loved most walked away. He is reading the story of his own days written into the lives of every prophet before him.

Now — here is the emotional climate you have to feel before you can hear these two ayahs. Imagine you are that man. You have just been shown, in vision after vision, that the pattern of your life is the pattern of every messenger: you will pour yourself out, and most will not believe. You are, by every human measure, *alone.* And then the surah does something. It stops telling stories about other people. The camera, which has been panning across ancient nations for two hundred verses, suddenly turns — and looks straight at you.

**"And rely upon the Mighty, the Merciful."**

And then — this is where we are going to live tonight — Allah describes Himself. Not with a name this time. With an *action.* He tells the Prophet ﷺ exactly what the Mighty and Merciful One is doing, right now, at this moment, in the dark:

*The One who sees you when you stand — and your turning among those who prostrate.*

Here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows, and I am not going to answer it for a long time. When a man is drowning in the feeling that no one sees him, that he is shouting into a void, that he stands alone — what does Allah offer him? Not an army. Not a crowd of believers. Not proof that the people will come around. He offers him **a Gaze.** He tells him: *you are being watched.* And somehow — this is the thing — that is presented not as a threat, but as the deepest comfort in the entire surah.

How can being watched be the cure for being alone? Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### The first stone: *yarāka* — "He sees *you*"

I want to teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because if I just translate it you will walk right past the miracle.

In Arabic, there are two ways time can live inside a verb. There is the completed action — *he saw* — a thing that happened and is sealed, over, in the past. And there is what grammarians call the *muḍāri'*, the verb of the present-and-ongoing — *he sees, he is seeing, he keeps on seeing* — an action with no wall at the end of it. It is happening and it does not stop happening. English mostly forces you to choose a moment. Arabic can hand you an action that simply never closes.

Now here is a second thing. In Arabic you can build the object of a verb — the "you," the "him," the "them" — as a separate word standing at a polite distance. Or you can fuse it directly onto the back of the verb as a suffix, so that the doer and the one it is done *to* are pressed into a single breath, a single word, with no space between them.

Hold both of those tools in your hand. Now look at what Allah chose.

*yarā* — the verb of seeing, in the present-and-ongoing form. Not *He saw you once.* **He sees you, and is seeing you, and does not stop.** And onto the back of it, fused, no gap, no distance: *-ka.* You. Not "the believers." Not "My servant." *You.* Pressed right up against the verb of sight so that there is not even a syllable of space between the Seeing and the one who is seen.

Let's sit with what that does. The Prophet ﷺ has just been shown two hundred verses of people who *would not look at him.* Fir'awn looked away. Ibrahim's father looked away. Nuh's people covered their ears with their garments so they would not have to see or hear him — the Quran tells us that elsewhere in exactly that image. His whole crisis is a crisis of *not being seen.* Of standing in a room and being treated as though he were not there.

And the answer arrives in a single fused word. *There is One whose eyes never leave you. Present tense. No end wall. No distance. yarā-ka.*

Notice something about *who* is doing the seeing. This is not a stray, general statement that God is all-seeing — that is true, but that is not the texture here. This seeing is the very thing being tied to the command just before it: *rely upon the Mighty, the Merciful — the One who sees you.* The *'Azīz*, the Mighty, the one with the power to change your entire situation — **that** one is the one whose gaze is fixed on you. And the *Raḥīm*, the Merciful, the one whose default toward you is tenderness — **that** one is the one watching. So the eyes on you are not the cold eyes of a supervisor logging your mistakes. They are the eyes of infinite power wedded to infinite tenderness. The most capable being in existence, and the most gentle, and He is *looking at you specifically,* right now, and He will not look away.

Here is what this single word reveals, and then I want you to be quiet with it for a moment. The opposite of loneliness is not company. **The opposite of loneliness is being truly seen by someone who loves you.** You can be alone in a stadium of eighty thousand and be desolate. You can be alone in a dark room at three in the morning with one Gaze upon you — and not be alone at all.

### The second stone: *ḥīna taqūm* — "when you stand," and the door Allah left open

*ḥīna taqūm.* When you stand. When you rise.

I want to walk you very carefully through an absence here, because this is a place where what Allah *did not say* is doing more work than what He said.

The verb is *taqūm,* from the root that means to stand, to rise up, to establish. And in Arabic, a verb like this, an action verb, usually *wants* something after it. Stand — for what? Rise — to do what? The tongue expects a completion. Stand *to pray.* Stand *to lead.* Stand *in the night.* Stand *before the people.* And Allah — He gives you the verb, and then He gives you nothing. He leaves the object open. *When you stand.* Full stop. He does not tell you what for.

Now, we could rush to fill it. And the scholars, in their care, did offer us the fillings — and I will bring you those in a moment, because they are beautiful. But before we fill the gap, feel the gap itself. What does it mean that Allah declined to specify *what* the standing is for?

It means the Gaze is not conditional on the *purpose* of your rising. He did not say "I see you when you stand for the night prayer" — as if the seeing switched on only for the impressive spiritual acts. He said: *whenever you stand.* When you rise in the dark to pray. When you rise to face the crowd that hates you. When you rise from your bed heavy with grief and simply get to your feet for another day. When you stand and there is no one in the room. Every single rising. The word is left open because **the seeing is not.** There is no posture of your standing, no reason for your rising, that falls outside the Gaze.

And now, having felt the openness, let me hand you the scholars' fillings — not as competing answers, but as layers, each a real thing this word contains. Because the classical commentators saw more than one thing in *when you stand,* and here is what is remarkable: they were each seeing something true.

Some of the early scholars read *ḥīna taqūm* as: when you stand for the night prayer. When you rise in the black hours, when the whole city sleeps, when there is no human witness to reward you with admiration, and you stand there alone before your Lord. He sees *that.* The prayer no one knows about.

Others read it more broadly: when you rise, in general, from any state to your feet — that Allah's attention is on the ordinary mechanics of your day, the getting-up, the standing, the moving through the world. Nothing too small.

And others heard in it: when you rise up to deliver the message, when you stand before Quraysh and your voice shakes and they laugh — *He sees you standing there.* The humiliating stand. The one that costs you.

Do you see what happens when you lay them one over the other, instead of choosing? You get a single image of staggering completeness. The private prayer no one witnesses. The ordinary standing no one notices. The public stand for which they mock you. All of it — seen. The word was left open precisely so that all three could live inside it. **The gap was mercy.**

### The third stone: *taqallubaka* — the word that turns on your tongue

Now we come to the word I have been waiting to give you, and I need you to say it out loud with me before we do anything else, because this word does something no translation can carry.

*ta-qal-lu-ba-ka.*

Say it slowly. Feel your mouth. There is a doubled letter in the middle — a *shadda* on the *lām* — and it makes the word *roll.* The tongue has to turn over on itself in the middle of the word. *taqallub.* The word for turning is a word that *turns* in your mouth. This is Arabic doing what only Arabic does: the sound of the word performs the meaning of the word. You cannot say *taqallub* without your own mouth executing a small turn. **The form is the meaning.**

Now the root. *Taqallub* comes from *qalaba* — to turn something over, to flip it, to overturn, to revolve. And I want you to hold your breath for the next sentence, because from this exact root, this same three letters, comes one of the most important words in the entire Quran: *qalb.* The heart. In Arabic the heart is not named for pumping blood. The heart is named *al-qalb* — **the thing that turns.** The organ of constant turning-over, of fluctuation, of one state flipping into another. The Arabs looked at the human heart and did not see a steady thing. They saw the most *unsteady* thing in the human being, forever revolving, and they named it "the turner."

So when Allah says *taqallubaka fī s-sājidīn* — He sees your *turning* among those who prostrate — He has chosen a word whose very root is the word for the restless, revolving human heart.

What is the turning He sees? Here, again, the scholars laid down layers, and again each of them was catching something real.

The most immediate reading: your movements *in the prayer itself.* You stand, and then you *turn over* into the bowing, and you turn over again into the prostration, and you rise, and you turn again. The prayer is a sequence of the body turning through postures. *He sees every turn of you* as you move through the salah. Ibn Abbas is reported to have read it close to this — your movement through the positions of worship.

A second reading, also carried from the early generations: your *turning among the ranks* — you, the Prophet ﷺ, as the one standing in front, and behind you rows of the faithful in their prostration. That there is a Gaze that sees you turning at the head of those bowed rows, moving among them, the shepherd among his flock of the devoted.

And a third, more astonishing reading that some of the salaf transmitted: *your turning through the backs of the prostrators* — meaning your very lineage, your descent, passing down through generation after generation of believers who bowed to Allah, the light of you carried through the loins of the worshippers of God across the centuries until you arrived. *I saw you turning through them before you were even born.*

Lay them over each other. Your body turning through the postures of prayer tonight. Your turning at the head of the believing ranks. Your turning down through the generations of the faithful to reach this moment. Every scale of "your turning" — the turning of a single rak'ah, the turning of a community, the turning of history — **all of it seen by the same unbroken Gaze.**

And underneath all three, the root will not let you forget: *taqallub,* the turning of the body, is built from the same letters as *qalb,* the turning of the heart. Which invites us to sit with something the grammar does not force but surely opens: the One who sees the turning of your body in the dark also sees the turning of the thing inside your chest. Every flip of your state. Every revolution of your mood. The night your faith surged and the night it guttered like a candle. *He sees your taqallub.* Not just how you move. How you *turn* — in your body, and in the one organ named for turning.

This is what this word reveals, and it is the summit of the linguistic climb: **you are seen not in your steadiness but in your turning.** Not in some frozen ideal version of yourself, but in the very fluctuation you are most ashamed of.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Being seen, in the age of being watched

I want to start where you actually live, not where the ayah lives, because you already know this feeling in your body — you just haven't had the words put on it.

You are more watched than any human beings who have ever existed. Cameras track you into buildings. Your phone knows where you are to the meter. And, most of all, you have built for yourself an audience — hundreds, thousands of eyes — and you feed them images of your life, and you check, obsessively, how many of them looked. Here is the strange thing, the thing everyone quietly feels and no one says: all of that watching, and you have never felt more *unseen.* You can post the photograph and get four hundred of those little hearts and lie awake at two in the morning feeling that not one single person on this earth actually *knows* you. You are watched constantly and seen by no one. Surveilled and lonely in the same breath.

Why? Because the eyes on you are not eyes that *love* you. They are eyes that scroll. They are eyes that consume the version of you that you performed for them and then move to the next thing. That is not being seen. That is being *processed.* Being seen means someone perceives the true thing, the unperformed thing, the you at three in the morning — and does not look away, and does not love you less. And that, exactly that, is what almost no human eye can give you, because human eyes get tired, human eyes judge, human eyes leave.

*Now* hear what this ayah says to that ache. *alladhī yarāka ḥīna taqūm.* The One who sees you when you stand. He does not see the performance — He specifically named the moment you are *not* performing: the standing in the night, the turning in prayer, the private rising that has no audience. The one place you were sure no one was watching is the exact place He said He is watching. And the eyes are the eyes of *al-Raḥīm.* This is the seeing you have been starving for while gorging yourself on the wrong kind. You wanted to be *known* and you kept settling for being *viewed.* The ayah says: the knowing you crave already exists. It has always existed. It was fixed on you in the dark the whole time you were begging strangers to notice your daylight.

Let that reorder something. The person who has internalized *yarāka* stops performing for the small eyes. Not out of piety-as-a-slogan — out of *relief.* Because when the Gaze that actually matters is already, permanently, tenderly on you, the hunger to be seen by everyone else quietly loses its teeth. You can put the phone down. Not because it's forbidden — because you're no longer starving.

What does that change tomorrow? Concretely: the next time you do something good and feel the reflex to document it, to show it, to let people know — pause, and let it belong only to the One who was already watching. One act of worship this week that no one but Allah ever finds out about. Feed *that* hunger. See if the other one shrinks.

### He sees you turning — and does not look away

Here is a scene you know from the inside.

You have a friend — the good kind, the rare kind — and you have known them for fifteen years. They have seen you win and they have seen you fall apart. They saw you the year you were unbearable. They saw the version of you that you would not show a new acquaintance for anything. They saw you *change your mind,* contradict yourself, become someone, then become someone else. They watched you *turn* — over and over, through every phase and mood and collapse and recovery. And they are still here. That friend is precious beyond price precisely because they have seen your *turning* and stayed. A new friend loves the polished you. That old friend loves the *turning* you. There is no substitute for it. Most of us get one or two such people in a whole life, if we are lucky, and we spend the rest of our lives afraid — afraid that if people saw the turning, saw the fluctuation, saw how inconsistent we really are, they would leave.

Now bring the ayah to that fear. *wa taqallubaka fī s-sājidīn.* And your *turning* — He sees it. We spent our time in the words discovering that *taqallub,* the turning, is cut from the same root as *qalb,* the heart, the organ named for its restlessness. So let this land: the thing you are most afraid to let anyone see — your inconsistency, the way your faith surges on Friday and drains out by Tuesday, the way you were so sure and then so lost, the turning-over of your heart that makes you feel like a fraud — **that is the exact thing Allah names Himself as seeing.**

Sit with the direction of that. He did not say *I love you when you are steady.* He said *I see you turning.* He named the fluctuation itself as the object of His gaze. And who is the One doing the seeing? *al-Raḥīm.* The Merciful. It is the friend of fifteen years, raised to infinity. The One who has watched every turn your heart has ever made — every flip from faith to doubt to faith, every mood, every collapse — and has not, for one instant, looked away or loved you less.

This is where so many of us are quietly crushed, and I don't want to rush past it. We think we have to *stop turning* before Allah will look at us with love. We think we have to become steady, consistent, the finished version, and *then* present ourselves. So we hide. We wait to pray until we "feel ready." We stay away from the mosque until we've "sorted ourselves out." We treat our turning as the thing that disqualifies us from the Gaze. And the ayah says — gently, and it should undo you a little — *no. The turning is what I see. The turning is where I am already looking. Come as the turning thing you are.*

I won't tie this off too neatly, because the turning doesn't stop just because you understood a verse — you will still surge and drain, still be sure and then lost, probably by this time next week. That is the nature of a thing named for turning. But something *can* change, and it is this: the turning is no longer done in hiding, under the terror of being caught mid-flip. It is done *fī s-sājidīn* — among those who prostrate — under a Gaze that named your fluctuation and stayed.

So here is tomorrow, concretely. The next time you feel unworthy to stand and pray *because* your heart is a mess, *because* you're turning — go stand precisely then. Not when you've fixed it. Then. Bring the turning heart to the prayer as the very thing He said He sees. Let the mess be the offering. That is what it means to turn *among those who prostrate* instead of turning alone in the dark, ashamed.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture: a servant enclosed

Now let me show you something that has been sitting in plain sight this whole time, and that I think proves these were not casual words but a design.

Look at the four verses as a single built structure. Before our two ayahs, the command:

*"And rely upon the **Mighty, the Merciful**"* — *al-'Azīz, al-Raḥīm.*

Then, at the center, the human being:

*"...who sees **you** when you **stand** — and your **turning** among those who prostrate..."*

Then, immediately after, the seal:

*"Indeed **He** is the **Hearing, the Knowing**"* — *al-Samī', al-'Alīm.*

Do you see it? Feel the shape of it. On one side: *the Mighty, the Merciful.* On the other side: *the Hearing, the Knowing.* And *in between them,* held, enclosed, is the servant — standing, and turning, in the dark. Four divine names, two in front and two behind, and pressed in the middle of them is *you,* on your feet at three in the morning, certain you are alone.

That is the whole message rendered as structure. The man who feels most isolated in the entire surah — the prophet who has just watched every other prophet be rejected and left standing — is *grammatically surrounded* by his Lord's names. Might in front of him. Mercy in front of him. Hearing behind him. Knowing behind him. His standing and his turning do not float in an empty void. They are enclosed on every side by the One who is capable, tender, listening, and aware. **The isolation was an illusion the entire time. He was never standing alone. He was standing in the middle of a circle of divine attention, and he simply could not see the walls of it.**

And that is why the answer to loneliness in this surah is a *Gaze* and not a *crowd.* A crowd would have left him, the way every crowd left every prophet. The Gaze does not leave. The Might can change his situation; the Mercy means the change will be gentle; the Hearing catches every word of his night prayer; the Knowing holds the turning of his heart. Enclosed like that, what is there left to fear from the people who walked away?

### The two paths were one path

We climbed the words and we crossed into the themes, and I want you to see now that they were never two journeys. In the words we found *yarāka* — the eyes that never leave. In the themes we found the ache of being watched-but-unseen, and its cure. In the words we found *taqallub,* the turning, cut from the root of the heart. In the themes we found the terror of letting anyone see our turning, and its release. The linguistic discovery and the human wound are the same thing approached from two doors. Allah built a word that means "your turning," seated it inside a circle of His own names, and aimed the whole construction straight at the loneliest heart in Mecca — and at yours.

### Questions to carry

- Where in your life are you exhausting yourself to be *seen* by eyes that will only ever *view* you — while the Gaze that already knows you goes unnoticed?
- What is the turning in you that you are most afraid to let anyone witness — and what would change if you truly believed it was the exact thing Allah named Himself as seeing?
- When you stand in prayer, do you stand as someone performing for a possible audience, or as someone already, permanently, tenderly seen?
- If you knew — not as a slogan, but in your bones — that you are enclosed on every side by the Mighty, the Merciful, the Hearing, the Knowing, what fear would you set down tomorrow morning?

### One sentence to carry

**You who stand alone in the dark are not alone — you are seen in your very turning, and enclosed on every side, by the One who is Mighty and Merciful, Hearing and Knowing.**

### The invitation

So here is what I would invite you to carry out of these two verses. Not a resolution to be more consistent — the heart is named for turning, it will turn. Carry instead a relocation of where you look for eyes. Stop pressing your face against the small glass of everyone else's attention. There is a Gaze already upon you, and it has been on you since before you were born, turning down through the prostrators to reach you. It sees you when you stand. It sees your turning. It is the Gaze of infinite power married to infinite tenderness, and it has never once looked away. Stand in it tonight. Let it be enough.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who see us when we stand and know the turning of our hearts — let us feel Your Gaze in the hours when we are most certain we are alone. You are al-Raḥīm; look upon our turning and do not turn from us. And as we stand tonight among those who prostrate, enclose us, as You enclosed Your Prophet, within Your Might and Your Mercy, Your Hearing and Your Knowing — until being seen by You is the only being-seen we hunger for. Ameen.
