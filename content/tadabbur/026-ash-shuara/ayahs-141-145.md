# The Two Credentials of a True Guide

*A tadabbur on Surah Ash-Shu'ara, 141–145*

---

## The Passage

*Kadhdhabat Thamūdu-l-mursalīn. Idh qāla lahum akhūhum Ṣāliḥun alā tattaqūn. Innī lakum rasūlun amīn. Fattaqu-llāha wa aṭīʿūn. Wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr; in ajriya illā ʿalā rabbi-l-ʿālamīn.*

> Thamud denied the messengers. When their brother Salih said to them: "Will you not be mindful? I am to you a trustworthy messenger. So be mindful of Allah, and obey me. And I ask of you no reward for it — my reward is only upon the Lord of all the worlds."

## Introduction: The Room Before the Words

Before we open a single word, I want you to stand in the place where these words were first carved into the air.

Not the place where Salih spoke them — we'll get there. The place where they were *revealed*. Mecca. A man in his forties, maybe fifties, who has spent years now watching the people he grew up among — the ones who nursed at the same wells, who knew his face before he could walk — call him a liar to his face. A poet, they said. A magician. Possessed. He goes home to a house that gets quieter every year. And into that grief, Allah begins to send him stories.

That is the setting of Surah Ash-Shu'ara. This is not a history lesson. This is medicine.

Because notice what Allah does across this surah. He tells the Prophet ﷺ the story of Nuh — and his people denied him. Then Hud, and his people denied him. Then Salih — the passage we are sitting with — and his people denied him. Then Lut. Then Shu'ayb. Five times. And every single one of them opens and closes with almost the *same words*. Same call. Same rejection. Same ruin. It is as if Allah is holding His grieving Messenger by the shoulders and saying: *this is not new. This is the oldest story on earth. You are standing exactly where every beloved of Mine has stood before you.*

So when we come to Thamud, we are not meeting strangers. We are meeting a link in a chain that runs from the first prophet to the last — and, if we are honest, right down to us in this room.

**Who were Thamud?** Hold this picture. They were not primitives. They were the great builders of the ancient Arabian world. The Qur'an tells us elsewhere they *carved their homes out of the mountains* — took solid rock faces and hollowed out palaces in them. You can still visit what is likely their remnant at Mada'in Salih, in the north of Arabia: enormous facades cut into cliffs, tombs that have outlasted three thousand years of wind. Ibn Kathir places them in the city of Al-Ḥijr, between Wadi Al-Qura and the edge of Greater Syria — and records that the Prophet ﷺ himself would one day ride past these very dwellings on the march to Tabuk. Sit with that for a moment: the grieving man in Mecca would live to walk through the silent, carved graveyard of a people who once said *we are permanent* — the warning made visible in stone. These were people who looked at a mountain and saw real estate. People whose entire civilization was a monument to one feeling: *we are permanent. We are not going anywhere. What we build, stays.*

Now understand the offense of the message that came to them. A man stands up in a city carved into eternal stone and says: *you are going to die, and you will be asked.* That is the collision. Everything they had built said *stay*. Everything Salih said pointed *elsewhere*.

And here is the hook — the thing I need you to want an answer to before we go one step further.

When Salih finally opens his mouth to this proud, prosperous, stone-carving people — people whose denial has already been announced in verse 141 — **he does not begin with the mountain, or the death, or the punishment.** He does not even begin with a statement. Watch what the very first words out of his mouth are.

He asks them a *question.* And then, before he asks them to do anything at all, he spends more breath establishing *who he is* than *what he wants.*

Why? Why would a man with a civilization-ending message spend his opening seconds on his own credentials? [PAUSE]

Because Allah is about to show us — in five short verses — the entire anatomy of a true guide. And the anatomy of the heart that refuses one.

Let's begin.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### One: The lie that was never singular — *kadhdhabat... al-mursalīn*

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the ayah, because this first word is doing something you'll miss in translation.

In Arabic, a simple verb can be *intensified* by doubling its middle letter. It's a whole verb-pattern — grammarians call it Form II. Take the root that means "to know," *ʿalima*, and double the middle: *ʿallama* — now it doesn't mean "he knew," it means "he *taught*," he drove knowing into someone else, forcefully, repeatedly. The doubling is like pressing your thumb down harder. It turns an action into a *campaign*.

Now the root here is *k-dh-b* — the root of *lying*, of *falsehood*. The plain form, *kadhaba*, means "he lied." But Allah does not use the plain form. He uses the doubled, intensified one: *kadhdhaba* — which does not mean merely "to lie" but to *cry lies against someone*, to actively, aggressively, repeatedly brand another person a liar. It is not a quiet private doubt. It is a public campaign of rejection.

And listen to the word itself. Say it. *Kadhdhabat.* Feel where it sits in the mouth — that doubled *dh* forces the tongue to strike twice against the teeth, a hard stop, almost a spit. It is a choppy, striking, aggressive sound. **The word for their denial sounds like an act of violence** — because that is what it was. Hold that sound in your ear, because in a moment we're going to hear its opposite.

So verse 141 does not say "Thamud doubted." It says *kadhdhabat Thamūd* — Thamud waged the campaign of crying-lies.

But here is where I need you to stay with me, because this is the thing that stopped me cold.

*Against whom* did they wage it? The verse says: *al-mursalīn.* **The messengers.** Plural.

Read it again. *Thamud denied the messengers.* [PAUSE]

But Thamud had **one** messenger. His name is in the very next verse. Salih. One man. One prophet was sent to Thamud, and the Qur'an is scrupulously precise everywhere else about this. So why, at the exact moment of naming their crime, does Allah use the *plural*? Why not *kadhdhabat Thamūdu Ṣāliḥan* — "Thamud denied Salih"?

Notice what's happening. There is a gap here between the number Allah *could* have used and the number He *chose*. And in the Qur'an, that gap is never an accident. That gap is the meaning.

Here is what the plural is telling us. **To deny one messenger is to deny all of them** — because they never came with different messages. This is exactly how the classical commentators read the plural: al-Muyassar explains that when Thamud denied their brother Salih, they became deniers of *the entire body of messengers* — because every one of them called to the same single thing: the worship of Allah alone, *tawhid*. The cargo was never merely "a God"; it was *the* One God, worshipped without partner. Every messenger who ever stood before his people carried that identical freight: *there is one God, you will return to Him, worship Him alone and live accordingly.* The names changed. The languages changed. The miracles changed. The message never moved an inch. So when Thamud rejected Salih, they were not rejecting a man's personality or a local preacher's opinion. They were rejecting the single unbroken message that Nuh carried, that Hud carried, that Ibrahim carried, that Muhammad ﷺ would carry. Reject the one, and you have reached back through history and spat on all of them.

And now the medicine for the Prophet ﷺ sharpens into focus. Do you see it? When Quraysh call *him* a liar, Allah's word here whispers: *they are not denying you. They are denying the messengers — all of them. You are in the finest company that has ever walked the earth.*

But don't let it stay in the seventh century. Turn it on yourself. We tell ourselves we accept "religion in general" but reject this specific teacher, this specific command, this specific verse that inconveniences us. *Kadhdhabat al-mursalīn* says there is no such thing as a partial denial of a single message. When you push away the one truth that has actually reached your door, you are not editing a syllabus. **You are denying the messengers.** All of them. The whole chain.

That's one word and one plural. Let it settle. [PAUSE]

### Two: *Their brother* — the word that made refusal a betrayal

Now to the second verse — and to a single word so gentle you could read past it a hundred times.

*Idh qāla lahum akhūhum Ṣāliḥ.* "When their **brother** Salih said to them."

*Akhūhum.* Their brother.

I want to slow this all the way down, because most of us hear "brother" as a throwaway — a nice word, a bit of warmth, decoration on the sentence. It is the opposite of decoration. It is load-bearing. And I can prove it to you from inside this very surah.

Here is the test. Go through the five prophets of Surah Ash-Shu'ara and watch how Allah introduces each one. Nuh to his people: *their brother Nuh.* Hud to 'Aad: *their brother Hud.* Salih to Thamud: *their brother Salih.* Lut: *their brother Lut.* Four times, the identical frame — *akhūhum,* their brother.

And then the fifth. Shu'ayb is sent to the People of the Thicket. And when Allah introduces him, the word "brother" **is gone.** The verse simply says, "Shu'ayb said to them." No *akhūhum.*

Why the sudden silence? Because the People of the Thicket were **not his own people.** He was not from them by blood or by home. And so Allah, with a precision that should take your breath away, *withholds the word "brother"* in the one case where it would not be literally true — and grants it in every case where the messenger was genuinely one of them.

Do you see what that proves? The word *akhūhum* is not filler. It is chosen, weighed, and — when it doesn't apply — *removed.* Which means when it *is* there, it is there to be felt.

So feel it. **Salih was their brother.** Not a foreigner who parachuted in with strange gods. Not a stranger they could dismiss as "not one of us." He was the boy they watched grow up. He shared their lineage, their dialect, their memories of the same droughts and the same feasts. When he spoke, it was kin speaking to kin.

And this transforms the crime of verse 141. Now their denial is not the rejection of an outsider — which is at least understandable. It is the rejection of *their own brother.* It is a family betraying the one member who came back to warn them the house was on fire.

Here is the tenderness underneath, and it's a tenderness that runs straight to the throne of Allah. Reflect on the mercy of it: when Allah decides to send a warning to a people, He does not send it through someone easy to hate. He sends it through *one of their own.* He reaches into their own family so the truth arrives wearing a familiar face. That is *rahma* built into the very delivery system of guidance. The Messenger to us, ﷺ, was famously *one of us* — an Arab, from the noblest of them, whose face and lineage and honesty everyone in Mecca knew for forty years before a word of revelation came.

**Allah does not send strangers to save you. He sends brothers.** [PAUSE]

And notice the ache in it. The people most equipped to recognize the truth of a messenger — his own family, who know he has never lied in his life — are so often the last to accept him. The closeness that should have opened their hearts became the very familiarity that let them dismiss him. *Isn't this just Salih? We know Salih.* We do this to the guidance closest to us all the time.

### Three: *Amīn* — the sound of safety, and the man who wanted nothing

Now the third word, and this is the one where I want you to *listen* before you understand.

*Innī lakum rasūlun amīn.* "I am, to you, a messenger — *amīn.*"

*Amīn.* Trustworthy. Say it out loud. *Ameen.* Feel the difference in your mouth from *kadhdhabat.* Where *kadhdhabat* struck twice against the teeth, hard and choppy, *amīn* opens — the long *ee* pours out, the *n* hums at the end and doesn't want to close. It is a smooth, open, settling sound. **The word for the trustworthy one sounds like exhaling.** It sounds like safety. Sit for a second with the fact that Allah placed a harsh, spat word on the tongue of *denial* in verse 141, and a soft, opening word on the tongue of the *trustworthy* in verse 143. The sounds are arguing the same case the meanings are.

Now the root. *Amīn* comes from *a-m-n* — and I need you to see the family this root belongs to, because it is one of the most beautiful families in the entire language. From this same three-letter root comes *amn* — safety, security, the absence of fear. From it comes *amānah* — a trust, something precious you hand to someone believing they will guard it. And from it comes — *īmān.* **Faith.**

Faith. *Īmān* and *amīn* and *safety* and *trust* are the same root wearing different clothes. Which tells you something staggering about what belief actually is in the Qur'an's imagination: to *believe* is to arrive at *safety*; to have *īmān* is to finally feel *amn.* And the one who calls you to it must himself be *amīn* — a safe pair of hands for the most precious trust there is.

So when Salih says *rasūlun amīn,* he is not merely saying "I'm honest." He is saying: *I am safe to trust with your souls. The trust I'm carrying to you — I will not tamper with it, add to it, sell it, or use it for myself. It will reach you exactly as it left the Sender.* This is precisely how the classical commentators gloss the word: al-Tabari explains *amīn* as trustworthy *over the message* — *amīn ʿalā risālatihi,* faithful to the very revelation entrusted to him — and al-Muyassar renders it "a guardian over this message, exactly as I received it from Allah." The trustworthiness is not a general character reference. It is *custodial.* He is a safe courier who will not alter the parcel, will not skim from it, will not add a single word of his own.

And how do we know he means it? Because of the last verse. Watch how the argument lands.

*Wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr.* "And I ask of you, for this, no *ajr* — no wage, no reward, no fee." *In ajriya illā ʿalā rabbi-l-ʿālamīn.* "My wage is only upon the Lord of all the worlds."

Here I have to point out an absence, because it is the whole point. He says *min ajr* — "any reward." The little word *min* here, with the negation, sweeps the shelf completely clean. Not "I ask a small reward." Not "I ask a fair reward." *No* reward — of any kind, any size, any category. Money, status, a following, your gratitude, your obedience-as-tribute-to-me. He is standing in front of a wealthy, stone-carving people who could make him rich, and he closes every single door through which payment might enter. [PAUSE]

Now hold the two verses together, because this is the architecture and it is exquisite. Salih makes two claims about himself, and *only* two:

*I am trustworthy* — verse 143.
*I want nothing from you* — verse 145.

And between them sits the actual demand — *be mindful of Allah and obey me* — verse 144. Do you see the design? The command is *sandwiched* between the two credentials. First he establishes he is safe to trust; then he issues the call; then he removes the one thing that would make you suspicious of the call — self-interest. **He earns the right to be obeyed before he asks to be obeyed, and he refuses to be paid after he asks.**

Those are the two credentials of every true guide, in every age. *I am trustworthy, and I want nothing from you.* Anyone who has one without the other is selling something. Salih has both.

And that is the linguistic construction complete: a denial that was never singular, a messenger who was always their brother, and a call bracketed by trustworthiness and by the refusal of any fee.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about the shape of your own life.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: You already worship safety — you've just aimed it low

Let me start nowhere near the Qur'an. Let me start in your house.

Look at how much of your life is *already* organized around protecting yourself. You have insurance on your car, your home, your phone, maybe your life. You lock your doors at night — twice. You have a password on a password. You read the ingredients now; you didn't used to. You went to therapy, or you keep meaning to. You set "boundaries." You have an emergency fund, or the guilt of not having one. There's a whole industry — a trillion-dollar industry — built on a single human instinct: *I do not want to be harmed. I want to be safe.*

We are, all of us, expert self-protectors. It is maybe the deepest instinct we have. Watch a toddler flinch from a hot stove — nobody taught that. We are wired to shield ourselves from harm.

Now hold that instinct in your hand and turn to Salih's very first word to his people.

*Alā tattaqūn.* "Will you not — *have taqwa*?"

We translate *taqwa* as "God-consciousness," or "piety," or "fear of Allah," and all of those are fine, but they've gone abstract on us; they've become church-words that don't touch the nervous system. So let me give you the root, because the root is pure instinct.

*Taqwa* comes from *w-q-y* — and *w-q-y* means, at its base, **to shield, to guard, to protect yourself from harm.** The Arabs used it for putting up a barrier against the desert sun, for shielding your body from a blow. A *wiqāyah* is a protective cover — the thing you wrap around something precious so it doesn't get damaged. *Taqwa,* then, in its bones, is not a mystical feeling. It is **self-protection.** It is the exact same instinct that locks your door and buys your insurance — *pointed in a different direction.*

And the classical mufassirūn fill in exactly *what* it protects against, and toward what it points. al-Tabari glosses Salih's question as: *will you not guard yourselves against Allah's punishment* — for your disobedience to Him, your defiance of His command, and specifically *for taking your orders from the corrupters, the mufsidūn, in the land.* Taqwa on this reading is not vague dread; it names a concrete break — *stop letting those who spread ruin set your course.* And al-Muyassar catches the positive side of the same coin: *will you not fear Him, and so single Him out in worship?* So the very first shield Salih hands them turns out to be *tawhid* itself — the safety of belonging wholly to One.

Now hear Salih's opening question again, and hear how gentle it actually is. He does not say "Fear God, you sinners." He says *alā tattaqūn* — "Will you not protect yourselves? Won't you take cover?" He is speaking to the deepest instinct they already have. He is saying: *you shield yourselves from every small harm — the sun, the enemy, the hunger. Will you not shield yourself from the one harm that actually lasts?* [PAUSE]

Here is the thing that should unsettle us. We have not lost the instinct to protect ourselves. We have simply *narrowed its range.* We will do anything to protect the body — which we will lose anyway, guaranteed, one hundred percent, no exceptions — and almost nothing to protect the *self* that outlives the body. We insure the car and leave the soul uninsured. We read every ingredient label and never once ask what we are feeding the heart.

And this is where a modern illness hides. We have become fluent in the language of protecting the *psychological* self — my peace, my boundaries, my energy, my healing, protecting my inner child — and this is not nothing, some of it is real and good. But notice how the language stops exactly at the edge of the grave. It protects the self *for this life.* Taqwa is the same instinct — *guard what is precious* — extended past the point where the therapist's vocabulary runs out. It is self-care for the self that doesn't die.

So the first move of the true guide is not to install a new fear in you. **It is to redirect a protection you already run.** You already know how to be careful with what you love. Salih is just asking: is the thing you're being careful with actually the most valuable thing you own?

What does this change tomorrow? Something small and concrete. The next time you catch yourself in an act of ordinary self-protection — buckling the seatbelt, checking the lock, refreshing the balance in your account — let that instinct trigger a single second of the other kind. *I guard this body that I will lose. Have I guarded, today, the self I will keep?* Let the lower protection become a doorbell for the higher one. That is *taqwa* — not a new burden, but an old instinct finally aimed at the target worth the effort.

### Theme Two: In a world where everyone's selling, the one who wants nothing is the one you can't compute

Picture the last time someone did something genuinely kind for you — a stranger, let's say — and your very first thought was: *okay... what do they want?*

Be honest. We've all trained ourselves into this. The email that opens "I just want to help you" — you scroll straight to the pitch. The influencer who "just loves this product" — you look for the discount code. The relative who's suddenly warm — you brace for the ask. We have marinated so long in a transactional world that we have half-forgotten how to receive a gift. Every kindness is presumed to be a down-payment on a future invoice. *What's the catch* has become the reflex of the age.

There's a reason. We live inside the most sophisticated persuasion machine in human history. Every screen you hold is monetized. Every "free" app is selling *you.* Every piece of advice comes with an affiliate link. We are the product, the content, and the market all at once — and we know it. So we have grown a protective callus over the part of us that used to be able to simply *trust that someone means well.* And that callus, understandable as it is, has a terrible side effect: **when something true and free finally shows up, we can't recognize it, because our whole detection system is calibrated for the catch.**

Now — into a world exactly like that — stand Salih up.

He comes to a wealthy people. He has a message that, if they accepted it, would make him the most powerful man in the nation — the founder, the leader, the one they'd owe everything. And the fifth verse of his introduction, before they've even responded, is: *wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr.* **"I'm not charging you for this."** Not money. Not power. Not a following. Not even your gratitude. *In ajriya illā ʿalā rabbi-l-ʿālamīn* — "the only account I'm interested in being paid from is Allah's." al-Muyassar puts the same line plainly in Salih's mouth: *I ask of you no wage for my sincere counsel and my guidance to you — my wage is upon none but the Lord of all the worlds.*

Sit with why he has to say this at all. He says it *because he knows the human heart.* He knows that the first defense his people will reach for is not "is he wrong?" It is *"what does he get out of it?"* — the oldest way to dismiss an inconvenient truth without ever engaging it. *He's just after power. He wants a following. This is a play.* And Salih, before they can even raise it, takes the weapon out of their hands. **He removes his own self-interest from the equation so that the message has to be judged on its own terms.**

And here is what should move you. The reason we can't compute the person who wants nothing is that we've forgotten such a person is *possible.* The Qur'an is telling you: the truest thing that ever came to you came *free.* The messengers, every one of them, were sent — the word *rasūl* itself means one who is *sent,* dispatched by another, carrying goods that are not his own and that he is forbidden to sell. Guidance is not a product. It has no price tag because **it was never for sale** — because the One who sent it already owns everything, and has no need of your payment.

Let this land somewhere personal. Think of the one human being in your life who ever told you a hard truth and *wanted nothing back* — a parent, a teacher, an old friend who risked the friendship to say the thing you needed to hear. You were probably furious in the moment. And years later, that person is likely the one you trust most in your memory — precisely *because* there was no angle. The absence of self-interest is the loudest possible proof of love. It is the whole reason a mother's warning lands differently than a salesman's.

That is what Allah built into the delivery of His guidance. The Messenger to us ﷺ died with his shield in pawn to a Jew for a debt of barley. He had the treasuries of two empires flowing toward Medina and he distributed them before nightfall and slept with an empty house. **He wanted nothing.** That is not a footnote to his message. It is the *credential* of it — verse 145, in a human life.

So what changes tomorrow? Two things, one facing out and one facing in.

Facing out: learn again to receive a free gift. When guidance reaches you — a reminder, a verse, a brother who corrects you gently — resist the reflex to hunt for the catch. Some things really are free. The most important thing that will ever reach you is.

And facing in — this is the harder one. When *you* carry a piece of truth to someone else, check your invoice. Are you correcting your child, your friend, your spouse *for their protection* — or for the small reward of being right, of being seen as wise, of winning? The true guide's second credential is not just Salih's. It's the standard for every one of us who ever opens our mouth to say a hard, good thing to someone we love. *Wa mā as'alukum ʿalayhi min ajr.* Say the true thing — and don't send a bill. Not even the quiet bill of *now you owe me.* [PAUSE]

*Will you not protect yourselves?* — the question. (142)
*I am a trustworthy messenger.* — the first credential. (143)
*So protect yourselves before Allah, and obey me.* — the call. (144)
*And I ask no reward; my wage is with the Lord of all worlds.* — the second credential. (145)

See the shape. The **call** sits in the center — verse 144. On one side of it, *I am trustworthy* (143). On the other side, *I want nothing from you* (145). The demand is cradled between the two credentials of the true guide, trust and disinterest, like a jewel between two hands. And the whole thing is wrapped, before and after, in *taqwa* — the question *"will you not protect yourselves"* in 142 becomes the command *"protect yourselves before Allah"* in 144. He begins by naming the instinct and ends by aiming it. The passage is *built.* This is not a man rambling. This is a design.

But here is the summit — the architecture above the architecture.

Remember verse 141. *Kadhdhabat Thamūdu-l-mursalīn.* They denied the *messengers* — plural — though they had only one. We asked why. Now I can give you the proof, and the proof is the surah itself.

Because this exact five-verse structure — *their brother said, will you not have taqwa, I am a trustworthy messenger, so have taqwa and obey me, I ask no reward, my reward is with the Lord of the worlds* — **is not unique to Salih.** It is, almost word for word, what Nuh says. And Hud. And Lut. And Shu'ayb. Allah runs the *same speech* through five different mouths across this one surah. The names change. The message does not move a single letter.

*That* is why verse 141 said "the messengers." The plural was not a slip. It was Allah telling you, in advance, what the structure of the whole surah would then *prove* — and it is exactly what al-Muyassar drew out of the word: **to deny one who calls to tawhid is to deny every messenger, because there was only ever one message.** To deny the brother standing in front of you is to deny every brother who ever stood where he stands. The repetition you might have found tedious — "why does the Qur'an keep saying the same thing?" — *is the argument.* The sameness is the point. It is the fingerprint of a single Author sending a single word through a thousand years and five prophets and, finally, to you.

The linguistic discovery and the themes turn out to be one discovery. The plural in verse 141, the shape of verses 142 to 145, the redirected instinct of *taqwa,* the refused fee of *ajr* — they are all saying: *the truth that reaches you is old, it is one, it comes to you wearing a brother's face, it wants you safe, and it wants nothing in return.* And the only question left on the table is the one Salih opened with. Not "is it true." He'd settle that with his life. The question is the one aimed at *you.*

### Questions to carry

- You protect a hundred things a day. Make an honest list of what you actually guard with real effort — and then ask what rank, on that list, you've given the one part of you that won't die.

- When was the last time someone told you a hard truth and wanted nothing for it? What did you do with it — and what does your answer tell you about how you'll receive the next one?

- Salih was *their brother* — and that nearness is exactly what let them dismiss him. Where in your life have you dismissed guidance *because* it came from someone too familiar to take seriously?

- al-Tabari read their failure of taqwa as a failure to break from *the corrupters in the land* — the voices they kept taking their orders from. Whose orders are quietly setting your course? And would taqwa mean breaking from them?

- When you carry a true thing to someone you love, check the invoice you're quietly writing. What reward — being right, being admired, being owed — are you actually asking for?

- If the message never changed across five prophets and a thousand years, then the thing keeping you from it was never the message. What is it?

### The one sentence to keep

**The one sent to save you comes as your own brother, asks only that you shield yourself, and wants nothing in return — so your turning away was never really about him.**

### The invitation

You don't have to build anything new. You already know how to protect what you love; you've done it all your life. Salih's invitation — and it is an invitation, not a summons — is simply to widen the circle of what you're willing to protect until it includes the one thing you're actually going to keep. And to trust, just once, the voice that told you a hard truth and refused to send you a bill. That voice has been the same voice the whole time. It reached Nuh's people, and Hud's, and Thamud's in their carved mountains, and Mecca in its grief. It has reached you, in whatever room you're standing in now. It is still not charging.

### A closing du'a

*O Allah, You who are Rabb of all the worlds and owner of every reward — make us of those who protect themselves before it is too late to take cover. You sent us the truth through a trustworthy brother who asked us for nothing; do not let our familiarity with it become the reason we let it pass. Give us the taqwa that shields the self that outlives the body, and let us carry Your truth to those we love without ever writing them a bill. Ameen.*
