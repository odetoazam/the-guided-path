# And We Brought the Others Near
## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 64-68

---

## Introduction

### The Ayahs

> وَأَزْلَفْنَا ثَمَّ ٱلْءَاخَرِينَ ۝ وَأَنجَيْنَا مُوسَىٰ وَمَن مَّعَهُۥٓ أَجْمَعِينَ ۝ ثُمَّ أَغْرَقْنَا ٱلْءَاخَرِينَ ۝ إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَءَايَةً ۖ وَمَا كَانَ أَكْثَرُهُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ ۝ وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ

*Wa azlafnā thamma'l-ākharīn. Wa anjaynā Mūsā wa man maʿahu ajmaʿīn. Thumma aghraqna'l-ākharīn. Inna fī dhālika la-āyah, wa mā kāna aktharuhum mu'minīn. Wa inna rabbaka la-Huwa'l-ʿAzīzu'r-Raḥīm.*

> "And We brought the others near. And We saved Moses and all those with him. Then We drowned the others. Indeed, in that is a sign, but most of them were not believers. And indeed, your Lord — He is the Mighty, the Merciful."

---

### Rebuilding the World

Before we can hear these ayahs, we have to stand where the listener was standing.

Picture the scene the original audience was being asked to picture. A sea. Two groups of people. One group is in front of the water — Moses and the children of Israel, exhausted, terrified, with nowhere to run. They have been slaves for generations. The army of the most powerful empire on earth is bearing down on them from behind. They look at the water. They look at the dust cloud. They say what slaves have always said: *we are caught*.

And then — this part is essential to feel — the sea opens.

The greatest empire on earth has its army at the back. More than one scholar of tafsir noted, as Ibn Kathir records, that Pharaoh did not come to the shore with soldiers alone: he brought the whole weight of Egyptian civilization — the decision-makers and princes, the ministers and nobles, the influential figures and leaders who had built everything that stood for power in that world. The army that came down to the sea was, in a real sense, Egypt itself.

And the sea, the impassable thing, the boundary that defined the edge of the world for these people, has split into walls of water with a dry road between them.

Now hold that image and add this: the people are not yet across. They are walking through. Pharaoh sees the path. Pharaoh sees the slaves disappearing into the sea. And Pharaoh — and this is one of the most psychologically arresting moments in any scripture — Pharaoh follows.

[PAUSE]

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is structured as a chain of seven prophetic stories. Moses, Abraham, Noah, Hud, Salih, Lot, Shu'ayb. Each story ends with the same refrain: *"Indeed, in that is a sign, but most of them were not believers. And indeed, your Lord — He is the Mighty, the Merciful."* Eight times in all — once in the surah's prologue before the stories begin, and once at the close of each of the seven narratives. Like a heartbeat through the surah.

These five ayahs are the climactic moment of the first and longest of those stories.

---

### The Hook

There is a verb in the first ayah of this passage worth pausing over. The classical commentators — Ibn Abbas among them, whose reading al-Tabari preserves through multiple chains of transmission — were in clear, documented agreement about what it means. Al-Tabari himself records: *"the scholars of interpretation said the same as what we said."* And what they all said was: the word means *qarrabnā* — *we brought near*. That consensus, reached quickly and held unanimously, is itself worth inhabiting for a moment.

Allah does not say *"We brought the others to the sea."*
Allah does not say *"We let the others approach."*
Allah does not say *"We allowed the others to follow."*

Allah says: **"We brought the others *near*."**

Brought them near to *what*?

And the verb itself — the classical sources agree on its meaning, but its texture inside the surah rewards examination — is not one you would naturally reach for when describing an army marching into destruction. It is a verb that, in another part of the Quran, describes the moment Paradise is brought close to the righteous.

[PAUSE]

Why would the Quran reach for this particular word — a word of *drawing-close*, of *proximity brought about* — to describe Pharaoh entering the sea that would drown him?

That question is the door we are walking through.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to look at three things. The verb *azlafnā*, the verb *anjaynā*, and the closing name-pair *Al-ʿAzīz Ar-Raḥīm*. Each one is a thread; together they weave the whole.

---

### Element One: *Azlafnā* — "We Brought Near"

#### Teaching the Concept First

Before we open the word, let us notice something about how language works.

In every language there are verbs that come from *outside* the situation and verbs that come from *inside* the situation. If I say "the police arrested him," that is from outside. If I say "they brought him in," that is from outside. But if I say "they drew him close" — suddenly the angle has shifted. The phrase implies relationship. It implies that the one being drawn close is, in some way, being treated with a kind of intentionality.

Arabic does the same thing. There are verbs of approach that are neutral, like *qaraba* (to draw near), and there are verbs of approach that carry a specific texture of *being-brought-close-with-purpose*.

#### Opening the Word

The word here is **أَزْلَفْنَا** — *azlafnā*.

Its root is **z-l-f** (ز ل ف). The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib, the Tāj al-ʿArūs — all converge on a single core meaning. *Zulfah* is **nearness, proximity, drawing-close**. From the same root comes the word *zulfā* — "nearness to Allah" — which appears elsewhere in the Quran for the rank of those drawn close to their Lord.

Al-Tabari, when he glosses this verse, cites the classical Arabic poet al-ʿAjjāj, who uses the root in the form *zulafan fa-zulafan* — **stage by stage, draw by draw** — to describe the nights pulling the months forward, each night a small narrowing of distance. That texture belongs to the root: not a single leap of proximity but an incremental, gathering *bringing-near*. The approach implied by *z-l-f* is one that accumulates. Distance closes in stages.

The verb *azlafa* is the Form IV — the causative. It does not mean *to be near*. It means **to cause something to be near**. To bring close. To pull toward.

Notice something. Allah is not saying the army came near. He is saying **He** brought them near. The action is His. The army thinks it is hunting. The army thinks it is closing the distance. But the verb is *azlafnā* — *We* brought them near.

[PAUSE]

#### Stay With Me

Now here is the detail the early commentators recognized as linguistically significant.

This same root — *z-l-f* — appears in the Quran in another, very specific context. Surah Qāf, ayah 31: *"And Paradise will be brought near (uzlifati'l-jannah) to the righteous."* Surah At-Takwīr, ayah 13: *"And when Paradise is brought near (uzlifat)."* Surah Ash-Shu'ara itself, ayah 90, in this very surah: *"And Paradise will be brought near (uzlifati'l-jannah) to the righteous."*

The same verb. The same root. The exact same conjugation pattern.

Al-Tabari himself draws this parallel, using the Paradise verse as a lexical illustration of the root's meaning of proximity. In the Quran, *uzlifa* / *azlafa* is a verb that appears in **eschatological contexts** — in the language of the Last Day, in the moment of the great reckoning when each group is finally brought to what it prepared for.

And here, at the sea, that verb is being used for **Pharaoh's army**.

[PAUSE]

#### Here's What's Remarkable

What is the Quran doing with this word?

The classical commentators — and here this reflection parts from them to propose something they did not — were content with the basic sense: *brought near to the sea, close enough to enter it*. That is the correct reading, the documented consensus, and it stands. Ibn Abbas said it, al-Tabari preserved it through multiple chains of transmission, and all the interpreters, in al-Tabari's own words, said the same thing.

But the *pattern* the word makes inside the surah is worth sitting with separately.

In every other place this conjugation appears in the Quran, it marks the moment of the great disclosure — the moment when what was always approaching finally arrives. If you have been reading *uzlifat* as the verb of Paradise drawing close to the faithful, and then you encounter *azlafnā* here, at the sea, the word carries that freight whether or not the classical commentators pause to name it. The Quran has trained your ear to hear *azlafa* as a verb of arrival, of disclosure, of the world being finally set in order.

What that means for how we hear this moment is this reflection's own proposal: Pharaoh is not being *cast away*. Pharaoh is being **brought near** — near to the truth he spent his life refusing, near to the reality his palaces had insulated him against, near, finally, to the One he had claimed to be.

The drowning, in this reading, is not the end of the encounter. The drowning *is* the encounter. That is where the title of this reflection comes from — not from classical tafsir, but from the shape of the verb inside the surah's own lexical world.

#### One More Layer

Notice the word **ثَمَّ** — *thamma* — that follows *azlafnā*. *Thamma* means **"there"** — a pointing word, a "right at that spot." *"And We brought the others near — right there."*

Right there. At the edge of the sea. At the threshold where the path through the water ends and the walls of water are about to close.

The whole geography collapses into a single word. *Thamma*. There. At that exact line.

This is what the verb *azlafa* does that no other verb of approach could do: it tells us Pharaoh was not stopped at the edge. He was not turned back. He was *brought to the line.* Brought — stage by stage, *zulafan fa-zulafan*, as the poet put it — to the precise edge where the divine and the human meet, where denial finally meets the One it had been denying.

#### What This Word Reveals

One word. *Azlafnā.* And it tells us that:

1. The destruction of Pharaoh's army was an act of divine agency, not historical accident. The verb's subject is *We*.
2. The drowning echoes the Quran's eschatological vocabulary — the same root the Book reaches for when Paradise is unveiled to the faithful on the Last Day.
3. Even if one reads no more into it than the classical scholars did — *brought near to the sea, to its edge, to the moment of its closing* — the word says something unmistakable: **Pharaoh was not chasing Moses. Pharaoh was being drawn.**

### Element Two: *Anjaynā* — "We Saved"

#### Teaching the Concept First

In every language there is a difference between *being rescued from* and *being delivered to*. To rescue someone from a fire is to remove them from danger. To deliver them is to bring them through danger to a place of safety. Arabic has different verbs for these subtly different acts.

#### Opening the Word

The word is **أَنجَيْنَا** — *anjaynā*.

Its root is **n-j-w** (ن ج و). The classical lexicons give the core meaning as **to be raised up out of, to escape, to be delivered**. *Najwā* is the high ground that rises out of a flood. The verb in Form IV — *anjā* — means **to cause someone to be lifted out, to deliver them**.

Now notice the structure of this passage. Allah does not arrange the three actions in chronological order, the way a historian would. Watch.

> *Wa azlafnā thamma'l-ākharīn* — "And We brought the others near."
> *Wa anjaynā Mūsā wa man maʿahu ajmaʿīn* — "And We saved Moses and all those with him."
> *Thumma aghraqna'l-ākharīn* — "Then We drowned the others."

The Pharaoh's army is mentioned first (*brought near*), then **Moses' rescue is wedged in between**, then Pharaoh's army is mentioned again (*drowned*).

#### Notice the Architecture

This is not random. This is the architecture of the ayah doing theological work.

The salvation of Moses is **literally surrounded** by the description of Pharaoh's destruction. Read it again with the architecture in view:

```
PHARAOH brought near
    MOSES delivered (everyone with him, all of them)
PHARAOH drowned
```

The believers are protected on both sides. Pharaoh is the outer ring; Moses is the inner sanctuary. The ayah is shaped like the sea itself — walls of water on either side, a dry path in the middle where the believers walk safely.

The grammar performs the geography. The sentence is the sea.

[PAUSE]

#### Stay With Two Details

First detail. *"And all those with him"* — *wa man maʿahu* — is followed by *ajmaʿīn*, which means **"all of them, every single one."** Not one was lost. Not one straggler. Not one who fell behind. The Arabic emphasizes totality. Every person who was with Moses was delivered.

Al-Muyassar's reading deepens this further: *"the sea continued in its splitting until they crossed to land."* The sea did not close once Moses reached the far shore. It held — actively, continuously held — until the last person among the believers had crossed. The *ajmaʿīn* is not merely a number but a condition: the sea would not relent until the count was complete. The parting was not a door that closed behind each person who passed through. It was a door held open by the divine hand until every last one was through.

This is set against the *ākharīn* — "the others" — who, by the third line, are all drowned.

Second detail. Notice that Allah does not name Moses' companions. He says *"those with him."* The believers are not catalogued by name. They are identified by **their alignment** — by the simple fact that they were *with* the messenger.

Whereas the others are also unnamed, but in a different way. They are *al-ākharīn* — "the others." Not "Pharaoh's army." Not "the Egyptians." Just *the others*. The ones who were *not with him*. The whole governing apparatus of the most powerful empire on earth — its princes, its ministers, its nobles, its decision-makers, as Ibn Kathir catalogs them — reduced, in the grammar of these ayahs, to a single word: *the others*.

In Allah's grammar at this moment, there are only two categories. Those *with the messenger*, and *the others*.

[PAUSE]

#### What This Word Reveals

The verb *anjaynā* — "We delivered" — sits in the middle of the passage as a sanctuary between two descriptions of judgment. The salvation of the believers is not a separate event from the destruction of the deniers. They are **the same divine act seen from two sides**.

The same water that became walls for Moses became weight for Pharaoh.
The same hour that was rescue for one was reckoning for the other.
The same sea that held its parting open until the last believer crossed closed over the last of those who followed.

### Element Three: *Al-ʿAzīz Ar-Raḥīm* — The Mighty, The Merciful

#### Teaching the Concept First

In Arabic, divine names that come in pairs are not arbitrary. The classical scholars taught that when Allah pairs two names, the pairing itself is a teaching. The two names *qualify* each other. They prevent us from misunderstanding either one in isolation.

If Allah said only "Mighty," we might fear Him without loving Him.
If Allah said only "Merciful," we might love Him without revering Him.

The pair holds the listener in the right place between awe and intimacy.

#### Opening the Names

**ٱلْعَزِيزُ** — *Al-ʿAzīz*. Root *ʿ-z-z*. The classical lexicons give the meanings: **mighty, unconquerable, overpowering, rare, precious, immune to defeat**. *ʿIzzah* is the quality of being beyond reach — beyond defeat, beyond domination, beyond compromise. An *ʿazīz* is one whom no one can humiliate.

**ٱلرَّحِيمُ** — *Ar-Raḥīm*. Root *r-ḥ-m*. The same root as *raḥim* — the womb. The classical lexicons emphasize that *raḥmah* is not just "kindness." It is the specific kind of mercy a womb has for the life it carries — protective, nourishing, sustained, total.

Now the architectural reveal that runs through the entire surah. Surah Ash-Shu'ara has seven prophetic stories. Each one ends with the *exact same refrain*:

> *Inna fī dhālika la-āyah, wa mā kāna aktharuhum mu'minīn. Wa inna rabbaka la-Huwa'l-ʿAzīzu'r-Raḥīm.*

After Moses (ayah 67-68). After Abraham (103-104). After Noah (121-122). After Hud (139-140). After Salih (158-159). After Lot (174-175). After Shu'ayb (190-191). And once more in the surah's prologue, before any of the seven stories has even begun (8-9). Eight times in total. Like a heartbeat through the surah — a pulse that conditions the listener's ear before the first story arrives, and then returns, and returns, and returns.

This is not a closing formula. This is a lesson learned again and again until it cannot be unlearned.

[PAUSE]

#### Stay With the Pairing

Each prophetic story in Ash-Shu'ara ends the same way. A civilization defies its messenger. The messenger warns. The civilization is destroyed. And then — *the same refrain*. Mighty. Merciful. Mighty. Merciful.

Why these two names?

If we are watching civilizations fall — if we are watching the sea close over an army, watching Sodom be overturned, watching the people of Hud be scattered by a wind — why does the surah insist, eight times, that the One doing this is *Merciful*?

The answer is precisely the tension. The surah will not let you resolve it cheaply.

You could read this history and conclude: God is powerful, God judges, beware. That reading produces a picture of *ʿizzah* without *raḥmah* — a God who is mighty but not tender.

You could read this history and conclude: the people who died had, somewhere in their lives, a chance to turn, and what looks like destruction is the final closing of a door they chose not to walk through. That reading adds *raḥmah* to the picture — a God who is tender, but whose tenderness is not sentimental.

The surah refuses to let you choose. It gives you both names after every single story. *Al-ʿAzīz Ar-Raḥīm.* Mighty and Merciful — both, together, always, in that order.

The *ʿizzah* comes first. The *raḥmah* follows. Power grounds the mercy; mercy colors the power.

#### What This Name Pair Reveals

There is one more thing to notice about where this refrain falls in these five ayahs specifically.

The refrain is: *"Indeed, in that is a sign, but most of them were not believers."*

There is a sign. It is there. It is unmistakable — the sea parted, the army drowned, the slave people walked out free. And most of them were not believers.

The refrain does not say: most of the Egyptians were not believers. It says most of *them* — a wider *them*, the audience of the surah, the listeners hearing this story retold. The sign is present. The āyah is real. And still, most people do not turn.

This is what requires both names at once. To watch that happen — to see the sign and watch the turning not occur — requires being Al-ʿAzīz: one whose *ʿizzah* is not diminished by refusal, one who does not need the response to remain who He is. And it requires being Ar-Raḥīm: one who keeps sending the sign, keeps placing the āyah before the people, keeps — as the surah itself demonstrates by telling seven stories instead of one — offering another chapter to hear.

The mercy is in the telling. The surah itself is a mercy.

## Part 2: What Five Ayahs Do

Before we close, let us step back and see the five ayahs as a unit.

They are twenty-eight words. In twenty-eight words, something is accomplished that most writers would need pages to attempt.

First, the army is described as being *brought near* — divine agency, not human initiative, not historical accident. The army did not come; it was drawn. The subject of every verb in this passage is *We*.

Second, the believers are rescued — completely, every single one, the sea actively holding its parting open until the last person has crossed — and the rescue is framed as the heart of the passage, surrounded on both sides by the judgment.

Third, the judgment falls — one word, *aghraqnā*, "We drowned," carrying the weight of an entire civilization's end.

Fourth, the reader is addressed directly: *there is a sign in this*. The event is not narrated for spectacle. It is narrated because it means something. Because it points to something. Because it is an *āyah*.

Fifth, the refrain returns: Mighty. Merciful. Both at once.

In twenty-eight words, the Quran accomplishes the theological work of a long treatise: the question of divine power, the question of mercy in judgment, the question of why people see signs and do not believe, the question of what kind of God governs history — all of it compressed into a passage whose structure *is* its argument.

---

### A Final Pause at the Verb

We began with *azlafnā*. We should end there.

The classical consensus is clear — Ibn Abbas said it, al-Tabari preserved it through chain after chain of transmission, all the interpreters agreed: *qarrabnā*, *we brought near*. That is what the word means. The army was brought near to the sea. Close enough to enter it. Close enough to be engulfed. That reading holds and should not be abandoned.

But language does not travel alone. Every word in the Quran arrives carrying its other appearances. *Azlafnā* arrives at the sea with the weight of every moment in the scripture where a group is drawn close to its final accounting, where Paradise is unveiled to the faithful, where the distance between creature and consequence finally closes — *zulafan fa-zulafan*, stage by stage, draw by draw, until *thamma*: there, at that exact spot, where distance becomes zero.

The army thought it was hunting. It was being gathered.

Pharaoh thought he was closing the distance. He was being drawn to the precise line where his world would finally meet the One he had spent his life refusing.

The mercy hidden in the drowning is not sentimental. It does not say that drowning is painless, or that judgment is kind in the way we prefer kindness. It says something harder and truer: that even in the final reckoning, the verb is *drawing-near*, not *casting-away*. That the God who closes the sea is the same God who opened it. That the One who is Al-ʿAzīz is the same One who is Ar-Raḥīm.

And the surah will say so again. And again. And again.

Until the listener has heard it eight times, and cannot un-know what the surah is teaching.
