# The Question That Cannot Be Answered

## A Tadabbur on Surah Ash-Shu'ara, ayahs 146-150

---

### The Ayahs

> أَتُتْرَكُونَ فِي مَا هَاهُنَا آمِنِينَ ۝ فِي جَنَّاتٍ وَعُيُونٍ ۝ وَزُرُوعٍ وَنَخْلٍ طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ ۝ وَتَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ ٱلْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا فَارِهِينَ ۝ فَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُونِ

**A-tutrakūna fī mā hāhunā āminīn? Fī jannātin wa-ʿuyūn, wa-zurūʿin wa-nakhlin ṭalʿuhā haḍīm. Wa-tanḥitūna mina-l-jibāli buyūtan fārihīn. Fa-ttaqu-llāha wa-aṭīʿūn.**

> "Will you be left, in what is here, secure?
> In gardens and springs,
> and crops and palm trees — whose spathes are tender?
> And you carve homes out of the mountains — with skill, and in pride.
> So fear Allah and obey me."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are Standing

Before we touch a single word of this passage, let's build the world.

Imagine a civilization that has, by every measurable standard, **made it.**

The land beneath their feet is fertile. Water rises out of the ground on its own — they did not have to dig deep, did not have to fight for it. The springs are simply there. Around the springs, gardens. Around the gardens, fields. And among the fields, palm trees so heavy with fruit that the spathes — the soft sheaths that hold the new dates — are bursting with tenderness.

But that is not even the most impressive thing about them.

[PAUSE]

Look up. The mountains.

Other civilizations build *on top of* mountains. The people of Thamud build *into* them. They take the rock itself — granite, sandstone, whatever the geology gave them — and they carve. They hollow out chambers, columns, doorways, entire dwellings sculpted out of the living stone of the mountain. If you visit Madā'in Ṣāliḥ today, in the north of the Arabian Peninsula, you can still see what they did. The facades are still there. The mountains still hold their shape.

Now imagine you are one of them. You wake up in a home carved into a mountain. Your land waters itself. Your trees feed you. Your civilization has lasted longer than memory. And then a man — one of your own — stands up among you and says something so strange that it cuts through everything else you've ever heard.

He doesn't say *this won't last.*
He doesn't say *you should be grateful.*
He doesn't say *one day you will die.*

He says something stranger.

He asks them a question.

### The Hook

The question Salih asks his people in this passage opens with what appears, at first glance, to be a single word. It is the kind of phrase that, once it lands, you cannot answer it and remain who you were.

For three ayahs after the question, Salih does not actually argue with them. He does not threaten. He does not even mention Allah yet.

He just describes their lives back to them.

Their gardens. Their springs. Their crops. Their palms. Their mountain homes.

And only then — only after he has held up a mirror to their abundance and let them see it as if for the first time — does he say five words that change the entire weight of everything that came before.

Stay with me. Because what's happening in these ayahs is more surgical than it looks.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will not enter this passage by translating it. We will enter it by listening to three pieces of it — three places where the grammar itself is doing something the English cannot show.

### Element One: *A-tutrakūna* — "Will you be *left*?"

Before we open this phrase, let me teach you a small grammatical principle that almost no English speaker grows up with.

In Arabic, you can take a verb and reshape it so that the subject — the doer — disappears. The verb becomes passive. *Someone hit me* becomes *I was hit*, and the someone is no longer named. English does this too. But Arabic does something extra: when the doer is left unnamed in this way, classical Arabic grammarians describe it as **majhūl** — literally, *the unknown one*. The action happened. Someone or something did it. But the grammar refuses to name them.

Sometimes a writer hides the doer because the doer is irrelevant. And sometimes — sometimes — a writer hides the doer because **the doer is so obvious, so overwhelmingly the only possible doer, that to name Him would be almost an insult to the listener's intelligence.**

Hold onto that.

Now let's open the phrase.

> **أَتُتْرَكُونَ — *a-tutrakūna***

What we see here is, more precisely, two elements joined inseparably: the interrogative particle **أَ** — a single letter, the *hamza* of interrogation, a particle in its own right — prefixed to the passive imperfect verb **تُتْرَكُونَ**. Not a single verb reshaped into a question, but a question-particle fused onto a passive verb. The distinction matters: the interrogative is a separate grammatical voice, not a reconfiguration of the verb itself. One letter — **أَ** — carries the question; the verb carries the action and its concealed actor.

The root of the verb is **ت-ر-ك** (t-r-k), which lexicographers consistently gloss as *to leave, to abandon, to let be, to release*. From the same root comes the word **tirka** — an inheritance, literally *what someone leaves behind* when they die.

The shape of the verb here is passive. *You will be left.* The doer is hidden. Stripped out of the sentence entirely.

So the opening, fully unfolded, is:

***Will — you — be left?***

Notice something. Salih does not say *will you stay?* He does not say *will you remain?* He says *will you be **left**?*

To be left is not the same as to stay. To stay is something you do. To be left is something done to you. Staying implies your own agency. Being left implies someone else's decision — someone else's hand that has, until now, been holding the door open.

[PAUSE]

And the doer is hidden. The grammar refuses to name Him.

But of course we know who He is. Al-Ṭabarī, reading this very verse, supplies the missing subject without hesitation: *"Will your Lord leave you, O people, in this world, secure?"* — **أيترككم يا قوم ربكم في هذه الدنيا آمنين**. The classical commentary names what the grammar withholds. Al-Ṭabarī is not interpreting creatively — he is simply reading what any native ear would have heard in the silence of the passive construction. The *majhūl* here is not ambiguity. It is restraint.

Salih does not need to say *will Allah leave you?* — because the moment you ask the question *will I be left?*, the answer to *by whom?* is already standing in the room. There is only one Hand that decides whether you are left in your gardens or pulled from them. There is only one Decision-maker over whether the mountains stay carvable. Salih is so confident his people know this — even in their heedlessness, even in their refusal — that he refuses to insult them by spelling it out.

He simply opens with **a-tutrakūna**, and lets the missing subject roar.

This is what classical rhetoric calls speech that **pulls more by what it withholds than by what it says.**

Here's what's remarkable. By the time the listener finishes processing the verb — *will I be left?* — they have already, internally, supplied the missing subject. They have already, in the silent half-second before the next word, asked themselves: *left by whom?* And the answer their own heart returns is the answer Salih needed them to discover without being told.

So before Salih has even finished his first ayah, he has already done two things to his audience.

He has reframed their entire life as something **held open by another's hand**.
And he has made them, in the silence of the verb, name that Hand for themselves.

[PAUSE]

And then the word **āminīn** — *secure*. Al-Muyassar does something arresting in its gloss: it unpacks **āminīn** into three specific exemptions the people of Thamud believed they possessed. Secure **from punishment** (*al-ʿadhāb*). Secure **from cessation** (*al-zawāl*). Secure **from death** (*al-mawt*). Three things. Three layers of insulation the prosperous believe they have purchased.

They do not merely feel comfortable. They feel *exempt*.

This is the texture of their security: not just *I have enough*, but *I will not be punished, this will not be taken from me, I will not die.* And Salih, with one passive verb followed by that single word *āminīn*, is asking: does that exemption come from you? Or from the Hand you have forgotten to name?

### Element Two: *Haḍīm* — Tenderness That Speaks

Now let's move to a single word in the third ayah. A word so specific, so unusual, that lexicographers have circled it for centuries.

> **طَلْعُهَا هَضِيمٌ — *ṭalʿuhā haḍīm***

***Ṭalʿ*** is the spathe of a date palm — that pale, elongated sheath that splits open in spring to release the cluster of new dates inside. Anyone who has lived around date palms knows this sheath. It is one of the most fragile and most generous objects in the natural world. Inside it is the entire harvest the tree will produce that year, packed into a single soft envelope.

So Salih is talking about the spathe.

And then he describes it with one word: **haḍīm**.

The root **ه-ض-م** (h-ḍ-m) carries a cluster of related meanings in classical lexicons. It means *to be soft, to be tender, to be easily broken, to be digestible, to be compressed inward.* From the same root comes the everyday Arabic word for *digestion*: **haḍm**. Something is *haḍīm* when it yields easily — when it breaks down without resistance.

But the classical commentators did not settle on a single gloss, and al-Ṭabarī preserves the ikhtilāf in full. Ibn ʿAbbās, in one reported chain, glossed **haḍīm** as *يانع نضيج* — **ripe, mature, having reached full ripeness.** Not soft-and-fragile, but *at the peak*. The spathe is full and complete, the dates inside it at their most perfect. Al-Muyassar echoes this, reading *يانع لين نضيج* — ripe, tender, mature — three adjectives that are not about brokenness but about completion. Others in the tradition, however, read the word as *متهشم متفتت* — **crumbled, fragmented, already beginning to break apart** — the spathe so overfull that it has started to split and scatter.

Ripe at the peak. Or already falling apart.

Both readings are attested. And the remarkable thing is that they are not as far apart as they first appear. To be fully ripe is to be *at the moment just before the fall*. The peach on the branch that is perfect today will be falling tomorrow. The date that is *yanīʿ* — fully ripe — is the one that is about to detach. The two readings converge on the same image: *abundance at its limit*. Whether the spathe is tenderly swelling or already beginning to fragment, it is doing the same thing — it is at the edge of what it can hold.

Which means — whichever reading the listener receives — they hear the same message in the word. The most generous thing in their garden is also the most impermanent.

[PAUSE]

Salih is standing before a people whose civilization is the opposite of *haḍīm*. They have carved their homes out of granite. They have built their identity on the unyielding. They are, in their own minds, the *un-breakable*.

And yet — at the very moment he is describing the abundance they are most proud of, the lush palms whose harvest is theirs — he chooses a word that means **ripe and yielding, tender and breakable, at its peak — which is to say, at the edge of its ending.**

The thing they are most proud of, in his description, is at the horizon of its own completion.

The spathe of the date palm: yes, full. Yes, generous. Yes, beautiful. But it is *haḍīm* — and whether that means *tenderly swollen* or *beginning to scatter*, it means the same thing: **this will not hold indefinitely.**

Their gardens, their springs, their crops, their palms — Salih lists them all. And the only one he qualifies, the only one he reaches into and touches with a specific descriptor, is the most *haḍīm*. The most tender. The one at its most perfect — which is also its most precarious.

This is what classical balāghah calls *al-tawriya* and *al-īmā'* — the slant. The hint that does not announce itself. Salih does not say *your civilization is fragile.* He cannot say that — they would refuse to hear it. So instead he describes their abundance using a word whose lexical heart is **ripeness, tenderness, the moment of fullness that precedes the fall.** And the word does the work that direct speech could not do.

The whole edifice of Thamud — the carved mountains, the engineered springs, the inheritance of generations — is, in Salih's chosen vocabulary, a **spathe**. Beautiful. Bursting. Tender. And one fingernail away from spilling.

[PAUSE]

There is something almost gentle about this. Salih is not insulting them. He is not even warning them yet. He is simply reaching into their world, touching the spathe of one of their palms, and naming it with the one word — *haḍīm* — that, if they were listening, would tell them everything.

### Element Three: *Fārihīn* — A Word with Two Faces

Now to the fourth ayah, and a word that classical scholars have dwelt on for centuries because the word itself is double-edged.

> **وَتَنْحِتُونَ مِنَ ٱلْجِبَالِ بُيُوتًا فَارِهِينَ — *wa-tanḥitūna mina-l-jibāli buyūtan fārihīn***

The verb **tanḥitūna** is from **ن-ح-ت** (n-ḥ-t) — *to carve, to chisel, to sculpt.* It is the verb you use for what a sculptor does to stone. Not building. Not laying brick on brick. **Carving.** Subtracting from the mountain until what is left is your home.

Hold the image. They are not making homes by addition. They are making them by subtraction. They are taking *away* from the mountain to make space for themselves. The mountain is the raw material. They are the artists.

And then comes the qualifier: **fārihīn**.

Classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī among them — record two clusters of meaning for this root:

**One**: *skillful, masterful, accomplished* — the carver who knows what they are doing. The artisan whose hands have been trained by generations. The craftsman at the height of their craft.

**Two**: *exultant, arrogant, intoxicated with one's own ability, drunk on one's own competence.*

Notice these are not two contradictory meanings. They are the same condition seen from inside and from outside. From the inside: *I am skilled. I am capable. I have mastered this.* From the outside, when that internal voice is left unchecked: *I am untouchable. I am self-sufficient. I am the maker.*

Al-Muyassar gives both glosses in a single paired phrase, with no comma of preference between them: **ماهرين بنحتها، أَشِرين بَطِرين** — *skilled in the carving; insolent, haughty in their prosperity*. Classical commentators preserve this ikhtilāf and disagree on which meaning is primary. But the deeper insight, in the spirit of the composite reading, is that **the Quran chose a word that holds both meanings at once because both are present in Thamud at once**. They *are* skilled. The mountain proves it. The carved facades, still standing thousands of years later, prove it. They are the carvers. They earned it.

And it is *precisely because* they are skilled that they have become *fārihīn* in the second sense too. Their competence has become their intoxication. The very fact that they could carve mountains became the proof, in their own minds, that they did not need to bow to anything.

This is the trap the word names.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because the trap is not the carving. The trap is not the skill. The trap is what skill does to the soul of someone who forgets where the skill came from.

Their hands learned to carve. Their fathers' hands learned to carve before them. The mountains yielded. And somewhere in the long centuries of mastery, **the question of who gave them hands** quietly fell out of the conversation. The verb stayed. The Verb-Giver was forgotten.

And Salih, in choosing *fārihīn*, names them with a word that is, simultaneously, accurate praise — *yes, you are skilled* — and exact diagnosis — *yes, and the skill has become the sickness.*

He does not have to choose between flattering them and warning them. The word does both.

Al-Muyassar's pairing is worth sitting with for a moment: **أَشِرين بَطِرين**. *Ashir* is the one who exults in blessing to the point of forgetting its source. *Baṭir* is the one made arrogant by prosperity, who grows heedless in abundance. They are not villains in this frame. They are people who were given everything — including hands that could carve mountains — and who, over time, forgot to ask the question that Salih is now asking them.

The word *fārihīn* is Salih's way of naming that forgetting without accusing them of evil intent. He describes their carving. He names their competence. And then, in the descriptor he attaches, he shows them — perhaps for the first time — what their competence looks like from outside the walls of their own certainty.

### Bridge

We have now opened three pieces of this passage.

A verb that turns their entire life into something **being held open by an unnamed Hand** — a Hand that al-Ṭabarī, reading the passage, cannot help but name: *your Lord*.

A descriptor that names their proudest abundance as **ripe, tender, and at its peak — which is to say, at the edge of its ending.**

And a qualifier that names their greatest achievement as **both mastery and the sickness that mastery becomes when it forgets its source** — the *ashir*, the *baṭir*, who carves mountains and forgets whose hands he was given.

None of these are attacks. None are explicit warnings. Each is a mirror. Salih is not arguing with Thamud — he is holding up their own world in a frame they have never looked through, and asking them to *see it*.

And that is what the question was for.

*A-tutrakūna* — will you be left? — is not a theological proposition. It is not a syllogism. It is a frame break. It is the question that, once you take it seriously, reorganizes everything you thought you knew about your own security.

After that question, the gardens mean something different. The springs mean something different. The mountain homes mean something different. Not because Salih said anything new about them — he said nothing new. He just *named* them, while the question was still hanging in the air.

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE MIRROR

There is something Salih does in the structure of this passage that we have not yet named.

He does not list the blessings randomly. He moves through them in a specific order. And the order is doing something.

**Gardens** (*jannāt*) — first. The enclosed, cultivated space. The garden presupposes someone who planted, someone who watered, someone who tended. It is the most *intentional* kind of natural abundance: nature shaped by human will.

**Springs** (*ʿuyūn*) — second. Literally *eyes* — the same word in Arabic. Springs are what the land does on its own, without human labor. They rise. They surface. They give themselves. No planting, no irrigation, no engineering. Just gift.

**Crops** (*zurūʿ*) — third. The large-scale agriculture. Fields. The systematic production of food that sustains a civilization rather than a family.

**Palms** (*nakhl*) — fourth. The date palm is not just a tree in Arabian culture — it is a symbol of civilization itself. The palm that feeds, shades, provides fiber and timber. The palm with the *haḍīm* spathe: at its most abundant, at its most precarious.

And then — **carved mountain homes** (*buyūt*). The crowning achievement. The one that required not the partnership of nature but the overcoming of it. Not garden but granite. Not spring but stone. The thing that says: *we are not just recipients of this land. We are its sculptors.*

[PAUSE]

Read it in that order and notice the trajectory.

It begins with partnership: garden and spring, nature and civilization working together. It ends with assertion: the mountain carved, the stone made subservient. The sequence moves from *receiving* toward *possessing* — from abundance as gift toward abundance as accomplishment.

Salih is not describing the blessings in the order of their economic value. He is describing them in the order of the soul's movement: from gratitude toward ownership. From *this was given* toward *this is mine*.

And by describing them in that order — by walking his people through their own spiritual biography — he is asking them: *at what point in this trajectory did you stop looking for the Giver?*

That is the question underneath the question.

*A-tutrakūna* is the frame. The four blessings are the evidence. And the evidence, laid out in this order, shows not just what they have but **how they came to think about it**.

[PAUSE]

There is one more architectural note. The passage contains no explicit threat. No mention of punishment. No prophecy of destruction. Ibn Kathīr reads Salih's address here as one of *reminder of blessings* — the prophet who warns by making the people remember what they have, not what they will lose. He frames Salih as "preaching to them, warning them that the punishment of Allah could overtake them and *reminding them of the blessings that Allah had bestowed upon them*." The reminder comes first. The blessings are the argument. The punishment waits in the wings, in the next passages, but *here* Salih does not go there.

He trusts the question.

He trusts that if a person truly encounters *a-tutrakūna* — truly asks themselves whether they will be left, by whom, and why — that question will do what no threat can do. Because a threat can be resisted. But a question, if it gets inside, does its work without the listener's permission.

## PART 3: THE FIVE WORDS

After three ayahs of mirror-holding — after the question, the gardens, the springs, the crops, the palms, the mountains — Salih gives the command.

Five words.

> [ayah:26:150] فَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُونِ

**So fear Allah and obey me.**

[PAUSE]

Let's start with the **fa-** — that single letter at the beginning.

In Arabic, **fa-** is the particle of consequence. It means *therefore*. *So*. *In light of everything just said, what follows*. It is the word that locks what comes before to what comes after — the hinge between the evidence and the verdict.

Salih has spent three and a half ayahs holding a mirror. He has asked the question that cannot be answered. He has described the gardens and the springs, the crops and the palms, the carved stone homes. He has, in the space of those ayahs, shown his people that *everything you have is being held open by a Hand you haven't named*. He has walked them through the trajectory of a soul that began in receiving and ended in possessing. He has touched their proudest palm and named its spathe *haḍīm* — fully ripe, fully tender, at the edge of its ending.

And then: **fa-**.

*So.*

*Given all of that.*
*In light of what you just saw in the mirror.*
*Because of what the passive verb revealed about your life.*

**Ittaqū-llāh** — *have taqwa of Allah.*

The root **و-ق-ي** (w-q-y) is the root of protection and shielding. **Taqwā** is not quite *fear* in the English sense — it is something more like *the posture of someone who knows that Allah is real and responds to that knowledge*. It is the inner alignment that comes when you stop treating your life as your own possession and start treating it as something held in trust. The wakefulness that follows the question. The way a person stands after they have truly heard *a-tutrakūna* and felt, in their chest, that the answer is not theirs to give.

And then: **wa-aṭīʿūn**.

*And obey me.*

Notice the sequence. Vertical first — *ittaqū-llāh*, the relationship with Allah. Horizontal second — *wa-aṭīʿūn*, the relationship with the messenger. Salih does not say *obey me* before *fear Allah*. He cannot. Because obedience to the messenger only makes sense once the listener has restored their awareness of the One the messenger is speaking for.

And he says *me* — **aṭīʿūn**, obey *me*, not *obey my message*, not *obey the command I bring*. This is consistent across the prophetic passages in this surah — Nūḥ, Hūd, Shuʿayb, Mūsā: each says *aṭīʿūn*, *obey me*. The messenger is inseparable from the message. The truth that the question posed does not arrive through a scroll. It arrives through a person who loves them enough to ask it.

[PAUSE]

After everything that preceded it, **fa-ttaqū-llāha wa-aṭīʿūn** lands with a peculiar kind of quietness.

No raised voice. No threat. No elaboration.

Just: *so — fear Allah — and obey me.*

It is almost anticlimactic in its brevity. Three and a half ayahs of precision surgery, followed by five words that any child could understand.

And that is the point.

Salih is not asking his people to master a theology. He is not asking them to change their lives immediately or explain themselves. He is asking for two things, in two directions, five words.

**The question has already done the heavy work.** If the listener was truly *inside* the question — if they felt, even for a moment, the weight of the passive verb, the ripe and yielding spathe, the double-edged praise of their carved homes — then the command is not a burden. It is a release.

*Of course.* Of course you fear the One who is holding the door open. Of course you orient toward the one who loved you enough to ask you an unanswerable question.

The *fa-* means: this is the only thing left to do.

## CONCLUSION

### The Question Lives in the Present Tense

What Salih asked the people of Thamud is a question that has never become historical.

It is still being asked.

Not in the Arabian Peninsula. Not in the ruins of Madā'in Ṣāliḥ, where the carved facades look out over a landscape that has been empty for millennia. It is being asked wherever someone wakes up in a life that, by measurable standards, **has worked out**.

Wherever there is a garden — physical or metaphorical: a career that feeds, a family that sustains, a body that functions, a mind that still works, a country that is not at war — the question is live.

**A-tutrakūna fī mā hāhunā āminīn?**

*Will you be left — in what is here — secure?*

[PAUSE]

The question does not demand an answer. It demands a *posture*. It is asking: **when you look at your abundance, whose name is on it?**

Not *do you feel grateful* — feeling grateful is easy and fades by noon. Not *do you say Alhamdulillah* — saying it without living it is a kind of forgetting too. But: **when you encounter the spring that rises without your asking, or the palm that produces without your understanding, or the skill in your hands that you did not fully earn — whose hand do you trace it back to?**

Salih asked his people that question. They could not answer it — not because it was difficult, but because to answer it honestly would have required a reorientation they were not willing to make. Their civilization was *fārihīn* — skilled, masterful, and precisely because of that, *ashir* and *baṭir*, exulting in the blessing without seeing the One from whom it came. The hand that carved the mountain had become, in their minds, self-sufficient. And so the question could not get inside.

[PAUSE]

But you are here. Reading this. Which means the question already got inside you, at least a little. The fact that you are sitting with Salih's words rather than walking away from them is already a kind of answer.

The gardens and springs and crops and palms and mountain homes in your life are real. The **haḍīm** — the ripeness, the tenderness, the *at-its-peak-ness* of what you have — is real. Nothing in this passage asks you to diminish your abundance or treat your blessings as burdens.

The passage asks only one thing: that you stop treating the passive verb as a mistake.

*Will you be left?*

Yes — you are being *left*, held, given, sustained by a Hand that named Itself in the silence of a grammar that simply refused to pretend it was anybody else.

And that is the beginning of **taqwā**.

Not the end. Not the completion. The beginning.

**Fa-ttaqū-llāha wa-aṭīʿūn.**

So — in light of all of that —

fear Allah.

And follow the one who loved you enough to ask.
