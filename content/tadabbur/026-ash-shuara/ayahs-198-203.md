# The Foreign Tongue: When Truth Comes Wearing the Wrong Clothes

## INTRODUCTION

Bismillah ar-Rahman ar-Raheem.

Let us listen first to what Allah says — in Arabic, then in transliteration, then in English. Let the sound itself begin its work before any explanation reaches you.

> وَلَوْ نَزَّلْنَاهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضِ الْأَعْجَمِينَ
> [ayah:26:199] فَقَرَأَهُۥ عَلَيْهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ بِهِۦ مُؤْمِنِينَ
> كَذَٰلِكَ سَلَكْنَاهُ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُجْرِمِينَ
> [ayah:26:201] لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِهِۦ حَتَّىٰ يَرَوُا۟ ٱلْعَذَابَ ٱلْأَلِيمَ
> [ayah:26:202] فَيَأْتِيَهُم بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ
> [ayah:26:203] فَيَقُولُوا۟ هَلْ نَحْنُ مُنظَرُونَ

*Wa law nazzalnāhu ʿalā baʿḍi-l-aʿjamīn,
fa-qara-ahu ʿalayhim mā kānū bihi mu'minīn.
Kadhālika salaknāhu fī qulūbi-l-mujrimīn.
Lā yu'minūna bihi ḥattā yarawu-l-ʿadhāba-l-alīm.
Fa-ya'tiyahum baghtatan wa hum lā yashʿurūn.
Fa-yaqūlū hal naḥnu munẓarūn.*

> "And had We sent it down upon any of the non-Arabs, and he had recited it to them, they would not have believed in it. Thus have We threaded it through the hearts of the criminals. They will not believe in it until they see the painful punishment — so it will come upon them suddenly, while they do not perceive — and they will say: 'Are we to be granted any reprieve?'"

### The World Behind These Words

To feel what these ayahs are doing, you must place yourself inside the room.

The Quraysh of Mecca — proud, eloquent, custodians of the Kaʿbah, masters of poetry — are listening to the recitation of this Quran. And it is dismantling them. They cannot match its eloquence. Their best poets have come to the perimeter of the Prophet's recitation at night and crept away embarrassed. They cannot dismiss it as bad Arabic. They cannot dismiss it as confused Arabic. It is, by their own grudging admission, the highest Arabic they have ever heard.

So they do something the human heart always does when truth presses too closely. They invent a different objection. They say: *"This is poetry. He is a poet. He is mad. He is possessed. The jinn whisper to him. He has memorized it from a foreigner."* They spread the story that Muhammad ﷺ was being taught by a foreign slave — one of those *aʿjami* speakers whose Arabic was unmistakably borrowed, whose speech any Meccan ear could identify as not native. Classical commentators, in their discussion of the related passage at 16:103 — where Allah directly addresses this slander — note how obvious the problem was: a man whose Arabic was limited and halting was supposedly the source of the most refined Arabic speech that Mecca had ever encountered.

[PAUSE]

The surah we are in — Ash-Shuʿarā', "The Poets" — has just walked us through seven prophetic stories in a row. Each prophet spoke the language of his own people. Each was rejected anyway. Now, near the end of the surah, just before these ayahs, Allah has said something stunning: *"And indeed, this [Quran] is the revelation of the Lord of the worlds. The Trustworthy Spirit has brought it down — upon your heart, [O Muhammad] — so that you may be of the warners — in a clear Arabic tongue."* (26:192-195)

So the Quran arrives in *clear Arabic*. The Quraysh are Arabs. The eloquence is in their own language. And still, they refuse.

Allah is about to expose what is actually happening underneath their refusal. He is about to ask: *what if the language itself is not the issue? What if you are?*

### The Hook

There is a hypothetical question buried in these six ayahs that will haunt you if you let it.

If a person says they would believe *if only* — if only the proof were clearer, if only the messenger were different, if only the timing were better, if only the words came in a different form — Allah is going to show us, in one devastating sentence, what is really being said.

And then He is going to use a verb for what He does to the hearts of those who refuse — a verb so physically vivid that once you see it, you will never read this passage the same way again.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will dwell on three things. A hypothetical, a verb of motion through the heart, and a phrase that flips an entire timeline upside down.

### Element One: The Hypothetical That Exposes the Excuse

#### Teaching the concept first

Before we open the ayah, let me show you a feature of Arabic — and of human language generally — that you already use without thinking.

There is a kind of conditional sentence called the *counterfactual*. It begins, in English, with "had I…" or "if only…" or "even if…" — and what makes it counterfactual is that the speaker is describing a world that did *not* happen.

Imagine your friend is upset because you didn't call. You say: "Even if I had called, you would still be upset." You are not claiming you called. You are showing them that the call is not the real issue.

In Arabic, this construction begins with the particle **لَوْ** — *law*. And what *law* tells the listener is: *I am about to describe a hypothetical world, precisely so I can show you what is true about the real one.*

The counterfactual is a scalpel. It cuts away the excuse to expose the bone.

#### Opening the ayah

Now look at the first words:

> وَلَوْ نَزَّلْنَاهُ عَلَىٰ بَعْضِ الْأَعْجَمِينَ ۝ فَقَرَأَهُ عَلَيْهِم مَّا كَانُوا بِهِ مُؤْمِنِينَ
> *And had We sent it down upon any of the non-Arabs, and he had recited it to them, they would not have believed in it.*

That word **الْأَعْجَمِين** — *al-aʿjamīn* — is from the root **ع-ج-م** (*ʿ-j-m*). The classical lexicons preserve a precise meaning here. *ʿAjam* is not merely "non-Arab" in the way modern usage might suggest. The root carries the sense of speech that is unclear, indistinct, hard to follow — speech that the listener cannot fully decode. An *aʿjami* is one whose tongue does not produce clear Arabic, whose speech sounds muffled or foreign to the Arab ear.

Here classical commentators diverge on an important point, and the divergence is worth sitting with rather than hurrying past.

Al-Muyassar understands الأعجمين as human beings who do not speak Arabic — foreign-tongue speakers, precisely the kind the Quraysh's own accusations pointed to. On this reading, the hypothetical is: *suppose We had given this Book to one of those broken-Arabic foreigners you whisper about, and he stood before you and recited it in correct Arabic anyway — would you then have believed?* The answer is no.

Al-Tabari takes the word somewhere more extreme. He reads الأعجمين in this ayah as *bahā'im* — animals, creatures that produce no intelligible speech at all. He grounds this in a grammatical distinction Arabic carefully preserves: *aʿjam* (one who lacks clear speech, applicable even to an Arab who cannot express himself) differs from *aʿjami* (one of non-Arab ethnic origin). An animal is the ultimate *aʿjam* — it produces no articulate speech whatsoever. On this reading, the hypothetical becomes still more radical: *even if We had placed these words in the mouth of a creature that cannot speak, and it stood before you and recited them — you still would not have believed.*

A narration preserved by al-Tabari captures this precisely. A Companion was standing at ʿArafah when someone recited this very ayah beside him. He turned and said: *"If it were revealed upon my camel here, and it spoke with it, they would not have believed."* The image of the animal is not a stretch — it is the outer limit of the hypothetical, demonstrating that the argument holds all the way to absurdity.

The two readings do not dissolve one another. Whether the hypothetical foreignness is a human accent or an animal's mouth, the theological argument is identical — and al-Tabari's reading, if anything, makes it sharper: *every conceivable carrier has been tested in imagination, including the most impossible one, and the result does not change.* The issue was never the vessel. The issue is the heart.

#### The deeper movement

Here is what is remarkable. The Arabic word order in *mā kānū bihi mu'minīn* is doing surgical work. A flat translation reads: "they would not have believed in it." But the Arabic places the prepositional phrase **بِهِ** — *bihi*, "in it" — before the participle **مُؤْمِنِين** — "believers." In Arabic rhetoric, fronting the prepositional phrase like this carries a sense of restriction or emphasis. It is not just "they wouldn't believe" — it is "*in this thing in particular*, they would not be believers."

Read it again with that emphasis: *Had We sent it down on a foreigner, and he recited it to them — in this Book, specifically, they would not have come to faith.*

Why does that matter? Because it tells us the issue is the **content**, not the **carrier**. They are not, in fact, rejecting the messenger. They are rejecting the *message*. The carrier is interchangeable in their excuses; the message is what they cannot accept.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar adds a detail that cuts even deeper. Even in the hypothetical world where the foreign-tongue reciter stands before them, the Quraysh would not simply refuse in silence — they would *fabricate a new excuse for their denial*. The classical phrasing is precise: they would *intaḥalū li-juḥūdihim ʿudhran* — appropriate, invent, manufacture a reason to reject. The excuse is not the cause of the refusal. The refusal comes first, and the excuse follows to clothe it. Change the excuse, and the refusal finds a new garment.

Stay with this. Every excuse a human heart constructs against the truth has the same shape. *I would believe if it were a different person. I would commit if the timing were different. I would change if the circumstances were different.* The counterfactual *law* in this ayah is Allah saying to every such heart, in every age: *We can adjust every variable you name. We can change the messenger. We can change the language. We can change the form. And you would manufacture another reason.* The variable that does not change — is you.

That is what *law* is doing. It runs the simulation, removes all the excuses, and leaves the heart standing alone in the room with nothing left to hide behind.

One clear statement before we move:

**The hypothetical sentence in 26:198-199 is Allah dismantling the excuse before it is fully spoken — by showing that even the world the disbeliever wishes for would not produce his belief, because the refusal precedes and generates its conditions rather than following from them.**

### Element Two: *Salaknāhu* — The Verb of Threading

#### Teaching the concept first

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns called *forms* or *abwāb*. Each form modifies the basic root. Form I is the simple verb. Form II often intensifies or makes causative. Form IV often makes something happen *to* something else.

But before any of that, every verb has a *physical origin*. Almost every Arabic verb began as a description of something a person could see or touch. *Fahima* — to understand — comes from a root suggesting a fluid taking shape inside a vessel. *Qalb* — heart — comes from the root for "to flip, to turn over." Arabic does not start abstract. Arabic starts with hands and earth and water and bone.

Let us hold that principle in our hand as we approach the next word.

#### Opening the ayah

> كَذَٰلِكَ سَلَكْنَاهُ فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُجْرِمِينَ
> *Thus have We threaded it through the hearts of the criminals.*

The verb is **سَلَكْنَا** — *salaknā* — "We threaded it." From the root **س-ل-ك** (*s-l-k*).

Most translations soften this. They render it "We caused it to enter," "We have made it pass through," "We have inserted it." These are not wrong. But they have lost the picture.

The classical lexicons preserve the physical origin. *Salaka* is what you do when you push a thread through the eye of a needle. It is what you do when you push a cord through a tight passage. It is what you do when you walk a narrow path that allows only one foot in front of the other. The noun *salk* is a thread or cord; the noun *maslak* is a narrow way. The whole root cluster is about *one thing being made to move through the tight interior of another*.

Now read the ayah again, with the physical image restored:

*Thus have We threaded it — pulled it like a cord — through the hearts of the criminals.*

[PAUSE]

#### The deeper movement

Pause and let the image settle.

The Quran, the very thing they reject, the very recitation they put their fingers in their ears to escape — Allah is saying *we have already pushed it through their hearts*. Not around them. Not past them. *Through* them.

This is the shocking thing about the verb. Allah is not describing people who have not heard the Quran. He is describing people whose hearts the Quran has *already passed through* — and yet they remain unbelievers. The Quran has gone in and gone out, and they are still standing in the same posture they had before.

That is what salaknāhu describes: a substance threaded through a heart that did not retain it.

Think about a needle and thread. The thread enters the cloth. If the cloth is healthy — if the weave is intact — the thread *catches*, it stays, it begins to bind, it joins one piece to another. But if the cloth is rotten, if there is no integrity in the fabric, the needle goes in, the thread goes through, and nothing holds. The thread comes out the other side and the cloth is exactly as it was.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar's reading of this verb adds a dimension the physical image alone might miss. He reads *salaknāhu* as placing — and making to take hold — not just the recitation of the Quran, but the *juḥūd*, the denial, the grooved refusal. What was threaded in found the heart already carved into channels of rejection, and it ran along those channels and settled there, becoming *mutamakkin* — entrenched, firmly fixed. The two readings are not contradictory: the Quran threads through, and what it leaves behind in the heart that cannot retain it is not emptiness but a deepened groove of refusal. The passage of the thread does not leave the cloth as it was. It leaves a trace. For a cloth whose weave cannot hold, that trace is not a bond but a tear.

There is a second physical sense in *s-l-k* the lexicons preserve: to make someone *travel* a path. *Salaka fulānun ṭarīqan* — "so-and-so traveled a road." When the form is causative — with the Quran as object — it can mean "We caused it to travel through." Hold both senses at once. The Quran is *threaded through* the heart. The Quran is *made to travel* through the heart. It is moving through them. It has not stopped to live there.

#### Reading the absence

Look at what the ayah does *not* say. It does not say *salaknāhu fī qulūbihim* — "We threaded it through their hearts," using a pronoun. It says **فِي قُلُوبِ الْمُجْرِمِينَ** — *fī qulūbi al-mujrimīn* — "through the hearts of the criminals."

Allah names them. He does not pronoun-them. He categorizes them.

The root **ج-ر-م** (*j-r-m*) — from which *mujrim* comes — has a physical origin too. The lexicons trace it to the act of cutting, severing, breaking off. A *jurm* is a thing cut off. Arabic poets used the verb for cutting fruit from a tree. A *mujrim*, then, is one who has cut himself off — from his Lord, from his community of conscience, from his own fitrah. Al-Muyassar is explicit about the cause: this state came upon them "because of their injustice and their criminality" — *bi-sababi ẓulmihim wa-ijrāmihim*. The cutting off was their own act, the fruit of accumulated choices, not an arbitrary imposition.

Now hold the two images together. A heart that has *cut itself off* from its source. A Quran that has been *threaded through* it. A thread cannot bind cloth that has been severed at the seam. The image is internally coherent: the message was sent through, but there was nothing left in the fabric to hold it.

[PAUSE]

This is the doctrine of how Allah relates to a heart that has refused. He does not stop sending the message. The message keeps coming. It keeps passing through. But the heart that has cut itself off is no longer able to retain what passes.

One clear statement before we move:

**The verb *salaknāhu* does not describe a failure of divine communication — it describes a heart whose own severing has left it unable to hold what God has already threaded through it.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Baghtatan wa hum lā yashʿurūn* — The Proof That Arrives Too Late

#### Teaching the concept first

There is a particular tragedy in the structure of these final three ayahs, and to feel it you need to understand something about how human beings make demands.

When we say *I will believe when I see proof* — what we are really saying is: *I want the evidence before the commitment.* I want to know before I give myself. I want to see before I bow. This feels, to the person saying it, like intellectual integrity. Like caution. Like rigor.

But watch what these ayahs do to that demand.

#### Opening the ayah

> لَا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِهِ حَتَّىٰ يَرَوُا الْعَذَابَ الْأَلِيمَ
> *They will not believe in it until they see the painful punishment.*

The word is **يَرَوُا** — *yarawū* — "they see." Not "they are told," not "they are informed," not "they conclude." *They see.* Al-Muyassar is precise: the verb he uses in his gloss is *yuʿāynū* — to witness directly with one's own eyes, the most unmediated form of perception. They demanded evidence. Here is their evidence. It arrives. It arrives exactly when they said it would need to.

But notice when that arrival is.

> فَيَأْتِيَهُم بَغْتَةً وَهُمْ لَا يَشْعُرُونَ
> *So it will come upon them suddenly, while they do not perceive.*

The word **بَغْتَةً** — *baghtatan* — is from the root **ب-غ-ت** (*b-gh-t*). The Arabic lexicons preserve the precise force: to come upon a person in the moment of their heedlessness, before any preparation is possible. *Baghata* is the ambush, the unexpected knock, the flood that arrives while the valley sleeps. It is not merely "sudden" in the sense of fast. It is sudden in the sense of *catching you as you were* — finding you exactly in the posture of your ordinary life, before you have had any chance to compose yourself for what is coming.

And **لَا يَشْعُرُونَ** — *lā yashʿurūn* — "they do not perceive." The root **ش-ع-ر** (*sh-ʿ-r*) is the same root as *shiʿr* — poetry, and also hair. The Arabs used *shuʿūr* for the finest, most delicate mode of perception — the awareness that is as thin and sensitive as a strand of hair, that registers even what the mind has not yet articulated. To say *lā yashʿurūn* is to say: they are not even at the level of their finest perception. The punishment comes while the very lowest threshold of awareness is asleep.

[PAUSE]

#### The deeper movement

Now the final ayah:

> فَيَقُولُوا هَلْ نَحْنُ مُنظَرُونَ
> *And they will say: Are we to be granted any reprieve?*

The word **مُنظَرُونَ** — *munẓarūn* — comes from the root **ن-ظ-ر** (*n-ẓ-r*), meaning to look, to wait, to be given time. To *unẓira* someone is to defer them, to grant them a period of respite before what is coming. *Munẓarūn* is the passive participle: those who are given time.

What are they asking for? Time. Specifically: the time to believe, to respond, to do what they could not bring themselves to do before.

And here is the full architecture of the passage's tragedy, assembled:

They demanded proof before faith. The proof — in the form of the painful punishment they were promised — has now arrived, exactly as they said it would need to. They are witnessing it with their own eyes. The condition of their own stated requirement has been met.

And the first thing they say is: *give us time.*

The time they are asking for is the same time that was given to them their entire lives. The *naẓar* they want now — the reprieve, the deferral — is identical to the years they were already living in. They were always *munẓarūn*. They were always the deferred ones, the ones for whom the punishment had not yet come. And they used that time to refuse.

Now the time is up. The proof has arrived. And they want the time back.

[PAUSE]

There is something else in *munẓarūn* worth noticing. The verb *naẓara* also carries the sense of looking — and *unẓira* can mean to be given a period of looking, of consideration. What they are asking for, on this reading, is the chance to *look again* — to turn their gaze back on the message they refused to see. But the moment of seeing is over. The punishment is what they see now.

This is why the ayah renders their question without an answer. *"Are we to be granted any reprieve?"* — and then silence. The surah does not say yes or no. The silence is the answer.

One clear statement before we move:

**The final ayah of this passage does not describe people who were denied proof. It describes people who received proof on the only terms they would accept — witnessing it directly — and discovered that those terms made belief impossible.**

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF SELF-SEALING

What we have seen across these six ayahs is a complete anatomy of the closed heart — how it forms, how it sustains itself, and where it ultimately arrives.

It begins with the hypothetical. The closed heart does not say "I will never believe." It says "I would believe *if*." This is what makes it so difficult to recognize in oneself. It is not an outright refusal. It is a condition. It says: change the messenger, change the language, change the proof, and I will respond. It presents itself as reasonable.

Allah takes that condition seriously enough to run the experiment. *Fine. Let us change every variable.* And the result — which Allah declares in advance — is that belief would not follow. Because the condition was never the real obstacle. The real obstacle was the will that generated the condition. Al-Muyassar saw this clearly: even in the hypothetical world of the foreign-tongue reciter, the Quraysh would not simply refuse — they would manufacture a new excuse for their denial. The excuse is not the cause of the refusal. The refusal produces the excuse. Change the excuse, and the refusal finds a new one. This is what makes the closed heart self-sealing: every opening you try to close it around, it closes around a different air pocket. It remains intact.

Then comes the verb of threading. *Salaknāhu.* And this is where the theology deepens beyond what the closed heart wants to hear. Because the verb does not describe a message that never arrived. It describes a message that was pushed *through*. The Quran has passed through these hearts. It has been threaded through them, moving through the space where faith would have lived, and came out the other side while they stood in place.

This is the doctrine of divine persistence. Allah does not withdraw from the refusing heart. The message keeps coming. The Book keeps being recited. The hearts of the *mujrimīn* are not hearts that were never touched by the Quran. They are hearts the Quran has moved through — completely, relentlessly — and left behind a deepened groove of refusal, because the weave had been severed from the inside by their own accumulated choices. Al-Muyassar names the cause without softening it: their injustice and their criminality produced this state. It did not arrive from outside.

And then, at the end, the devastating reversal. They demanded proof. The proof comes, in the form of the punishment they were promised. It comes *baghtatan* — as an ambush, while their finest perceptions sleep, while they are living in the assumption that tomorrow will also be a day of ordinary existence. The very form of its arrival testifies against them: if they had been alert, if the *shuʿūr* had been functioning, if the hair-thin sensitivity of a wakeful heart had been intact, the arrival might have been prepared for. But the same quality of inattention that let the Quran thread through without catching has also left them unable to perceive what is approaching.

What they ask for, at the end, is time. What they were already living in.

The six ayahs are, taken together, a mirror. They reflect the full cycle of a particular kind of spiritual closure: the conditional refusal, the manufactured excuse, the heart that is threadable-but-not-retaining, the demand for proof that is simultaneously an evasion of it, the ambush, the request for the time that was already spent. It is a complete picture. And Allah draws it with very few words.

## PART 3: THE QUESTION THIS PASSAGE TURNS ON YOU

Now let the mirror turn.

These ayahs were revealed about the Quraysh of Mecca. But the Quran does not come to us as history. It comes to us as *address*. The *law* at the beginning of this passage is not merely a historical statement about seventh-century Arabs. It is a structural description of how the closed heart works. It is a diagnosis that crosses time.

So the question the passage places in front of you is not about the Quraysh. It is about the conditions you are carrying.

What is your *law*? What is the thing you tell yourself stands between you and the response you have not yet made? *I would pray more consistently if my life were less busy. I would be more present in Quran if I had studied Arabic. I would give more if I had more. I would trust more if I understood more.* These are not lies. The conditions often feel genuinely true. But the passage asks: *if we removed that condition — actually removed it — would the response come? Or would another condition appear in its place?*

[PAUSE]

There is also the question of *salaknāhu* — threading. The Quran has passed through you. You have heard it in prayer, perhaps hundreds of times. You have heard this surah, possibly. You have heard ayahs that hit you and that you nodded at and that then — and this is honest — moved through without catching. Not because they were not profound. Because something in the interior was not configured to hold them.

This is not a verdict. It is an invitation. The passage describes the *mujrimīn* — those who have cut themselves off — as those through whom the Quran threads without holding. But the cutting off was something they did, incrementally, over time, through their own choices. Which means the re-weaving is also something that can happen — incrementally, over time, through different ones.

The heart that is being called here is not the heart that has entirely refused. That heart is in the final three ayahs. The heart being called here is the one that is still reading. Still sitting with these words. Still — in some register — listening.

What are you doing with the thread that is passing through?

[PAUSE]

And finally, *baghtatan*. The suddenness. The arrival in the moment of non-perception.

This is not intended as a threat. It is intended as a waking-up. The ayah about sudden arrival is placed here, in this order, after the description of the closed heart — precisely so that the reader who has not yet closed can feel the urgency of now. The heart that is still open, still reading, still asking — that heart is being given information that the *mujrimūn* did not have: that the window is open, and the window is finite, and the window is *now*.

The *munẓarūn* they beg for at the end — the reprieve, the extension, the little more time — you already have it. You are in it. These words are reaching you during your *naẓar* — your period of deferral, your time of looking. What will you do with it?

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING

We began with six ayahs that seem, on the surface, to be about a historical dispute between a prophet and his people over the form of a revelation. A question about language. A question about accent and origin and who could have possibly taught him.

But what we have found inside them is something much more intimate. A complete anatomy of how the human heart seals itself against what it most needs. A diagnosis, made with extraordinary precision, that runs from the first hypothetical excuse all the way to the final plea for more time — with the verb of threading in the middle, naming exactly what happens when the message arrives and finds nothing left to hold it.

The Quraysh are gone. The Quran is still here.

And somewhere in these six ayahs, Allah is still running the simulation. Still demonstrating that every condition you name for your response — *if only, when I have more, when I understand better, when the circumstances change* — can be adjusted, one by one, all the way to its most impossible extreme, and the refusal will remain, until the decision is made to stop generating conditions and simply respond.

The thread is still moving through. What does your fabric hold?

*Allāhumma-jʿali-l-Qur'āna rabīʿa qulūbinā, wa nūra ṣudūrinā, wa dhahāba aḥzāninā, wa jalā'a humūminā.*

O Allah, make the Quran the spring of our hearts, the light of our chests, the removal of our sorrows, and the departure of our anxieties.
