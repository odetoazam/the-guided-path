# The Refrain That Will Not Stop Repeating

## The Ayat

**Arabic:**
> إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَءَايَةً ۖ وَمَا كَانَ أَكْثَرُهُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ ﴿١٠٣﴾ وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ ﴿١٠٤﴾

**Transliteration:**
> Inna fī dhālika la-āyah, wa mā kāna aktharuhum mu'minīn. Wa inna rabbaka la-huwa al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm.

**Translation:**
> Indeed, in that is a sign — but most of them were not to be believers. And indeed, your Lord — He is the Almighty, the Most Merciful.

---

## Introduction: A Door That Keeps Opening

There is a moment in Surah Ash-Shu'ara that almost no one fully absorbs the first time they read it. The surah opens with a statement about the disbelievers of Mecca and their failure to believe — and before the first prophetic story even begins, two short ayat appear:

> *"Indeed, in that is a sign — but most of them were not to be believers. And indeed, your Lord — He is the Almighty, the Most Merciful."*

You note them. You move into the great story of Musa and Pharaoh — the longest narrative in the surah, spanning thirty-four ayat. It builds to its climax: the parting of the sea, the drowning of Pharaoh's entire host. And then — there they are again. The same two ayat. Word for word.

You move into the story of Ibrahim — his patient arguments with his father and his people, his cross-examination of the idol-worshippers, his dismantling of the case for stone gods through logic and speech alone. It builds. It reaches its conclusion. And **there they are again**.

The same two ayat. Word for word.

[PAUSE]

You think it must be a stylistic device. You move into the story of Nuh. It builds. It climaxes. And **there it is again**. The same two ayat.

By the time you reach the end of the surah, you have heard these same two ayat **eight times.** Eight. Not paraphrased. Not varied. Identical. The disbelievers in the Musa story are not the disbelievers in the Ibrahim story or the Lut story. The geographies are different. The crimes are different. The destructions are different.

But the verdict — letter for letter — is the same.

What is Allah doing?

Classical commentators noticed something here that the English reader almost always misses. Al-Muyassar's gloss on the word *āyah* in this context is *ʿibra li-man yaʿtabir* — "a lesson for those who draw lessons." The Arabic root *ʿ-b-r* means to cross over — the same root as the word for crossing a river, for passing from one bank to the other. An *ʿibra* is not a sign that works automatically. It is an experience that requires something of the one who receives it: the act of *iʿtibār*, of crossing from the event on this bank to the meaning on the other. The sign exists. The crossing is yours to make.

And this is precisely why the refrain is here. This is not a chorus in a song, existing to be enjoyed. This refrain is doing something else. It is teaching the Prophet ﷺ how to read history. And by extension — it is teaching us how to read our own grief over the people in our lives who saw the sign and did not cross over.

Let's sit with this for the next hour. Because what looks like simple repetition is one of the most carefully engineered psychological consolations in the Quran. And to see it, we have to first understand what a refrain *does* to a human mind.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Architecture of `Inna ... la` — The Double Lock

Before we open the Arabic, let me show you something about how all human language works.

When you tell your friend, "I love you," the sentence is complete. Subject, verb, object. Done.

But there are moments when "I love you" is not enough. When the air in the room demands something more. So you say: "I really do love you." Or: "I swear I love you." You are not saying anything new. You are *fastening* what you already said. You are taking a sentence and pinning it down so it cannot drift away in the listener's doubt.

Arabic has formalized this. It is called *taʾkīd* — emphasis. And the most common form of it is a structure called *inna ... la*.

`Inna` at the beginning of a sentence — meaning roughly "indeed" or "verily."
`La` attached to the predicate — a particle of emphasis that has no clean English equivalent. It says: *yes, this. exactly this.*

Together, they form a double lock on a statement. Imagine a vault door with two combinations. `Inna` is the first. `La` is the second. Once both are turned, the statement is sealed against doubt.

Now look at our ayat:

**إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَءَايَةً**
*Inna fī dhālika **la**-āyah*

[ayah:26:104] وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ
*Wa **inna** rabbaka **la**-huwa al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm*

Both ayat are *double-locked*. Both have `inna`. Both have `la`. Allah is not just *saying* there is a sign. He is *swearing* there is a sign. He is not just saying your Lord is the Almighty, the Most Merciful. He is *swearing* He is.

Notice something. **Why would Allah need to swear?**

The vault is locked twice when there is a real possibility the contents will be doubted. You don't double-lock something nobody is questioning.

[PAUSE]

So who is doubting?

Stay with me. This refrain comes immediately after the disbelief of the people. They saw the sign. They did not believe. The Prophet ﷺ is watching the same thing happen in Mecca — people seeing his message, watching the Quran descend, and not moving. The natural human reaction to this is a particular kind of doubt. Not doubt that Allah exists. Something more painful: **doubt that the sign was really a sign.**

If a sign was clear, wouldn't they believe? If your Lord is really the Almighty, why doesn't He compel them? If your Lord is really the Most Merciful, why does He let them perish?

The double lock is for *that* doubt. It is for the Prophet ﷺ at his lowest moment — the doubt that creeps in not against Allah but against one's own perception. *Did I really see what I thought I saw?*

And Allah answers, twice-locked: **Yes. There was a sign. Yes. He is the Almighty. Yes. He is the Most Merciful.** All of it, sealed against the erosion that comes from watching people fail to be moved.

This is the first thing the linguistic structure is doing. It is not decorative emphasis. It is *evidentiary*. It is Allah testifying — under His own oath — to a reality that the visible world is not currently confirming.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Strange Word `Kāna` — A Tense That Is Not a Tense

Now look at the middle phrase.

**وَمَا كَانَ أَكْثَرُهُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ**
*Wa mā **kāna** aktharuhum mu'minīn*
*"And most of them were not believers."*

In any English translation, that little verb `kāna` becomes "were." Past tense. Simple.

But `kāna` in Arabic is one of the strangest verbs in the language, and what it is doing here is one of the most consoling sentences in the surah.

Let me teach you the concept first, the way Arabic grammarians teach it.

`Kāna` is technically a past-tense verb. But classical grammarians describe it as carrying a meaning of *establishment* — *thabāt*. Stability. The way something has settled. When you say in Arabic, *kāna Allāhu ghafūran raḥīmā* — "Allah was Forgiving, Merciful" — you are not saying Allah used to be forgiving and isn't anymore. You are saying: this is **how He is established**. This is the settled, abiding nature of the matter.

In some classical grammatical readings, `kāna` carries an almost timeless weight — a description of how a reality is constituted, not when it occurred.

Now apply that to our phrase.

*Wa mā kāna aktharuhum mu'minīn.*

The translation "most of them were not believers" sounds historical. As if Allah is reporting a past fact about a dead nation.

But the deeper reading the grammar permits is: **"And it was never going to be the case that most of them would believe."**

It is a description not of one historical moment but of *the settled pattern.* The way it always is. The way it always *was going to be.*

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, commenting on this very ayah in the context of the Ibrahim narrative, reaches further still. His gloss closes with the phrase *fī sābiq ʿilmihi* — "in His prior/foregoing knowledge." In al-Tabari's reading, the establishment that `kāna` carries is not merely a pattern drawn from history. It is something present in what Allah knew before the stories began. The refrain is not only looking back at what happened. It is disclosing what was always already known — the shape of the human situation as it existed in divine awareness before the first messenger was sent.

Sit with that.

If `kāna` were just past-tense, the refrain would be a sad reporting of one episode. *They didn't believe. Sad. Next story.*

But because `kāna` carries this weight of *established reality* — reaching back into divine foreknowledge, reaching forward into every age — the refrain becomes something else entirely. It becomes a **law of human history.** A pattern Allah is naming. *In every age, when the sign came, most did not move. This is not the failure of any particular messenger. This is the shape of the human story.*

And this is exactly why the refrain repeats eight times.

Allah is not telling us eight different stories with the same lesson tacked on. He is saying: *Look. Look again. Look again. Eight times look.* Because the human heart resists believing this is the pattern. The human heart wants to think: *if my message had been clearer, if my parents had heard it differently, if the sign had been more dramatic — they would have believed.*

The refrain says: **No. This is the established way of things.** Not because Allah has predetermined certain hearts to fail — but because the moral architecture of belief is such that even an undeniable sign meets a heart that has decided not to be moved, and the heart wins.

Notice now what the refrain is doing for the Prophet ﷺ. He is in Mecca. His own uncle has rejected him. His tribe is mocking him. And he is reading these stories. And after every story, Allah whispers: *most were not going to be believers. Not in your story. Not in any story. This is not your failure. This is the pattern.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Pairing That Should Not Work — al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm

Now the second ayah. The one that should not be possible.

**وَإِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلرَّحِيمُ**
*Wa inna rabbaka la-huwa al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm*

Two of Allah's names. Side by side.

`al-ʿAzīz` — The Almighty. The Overpowering. The One whose strength cannot be opposed. From the root *ʿ-z-z*, which carries the sense of being so firm, so unassailable, that nothing can chip it. Lexicons describe this root as connoting both rare strength and the dignity that comes from it.

`ar-Raḥīm` — The Most Merciful. From the root *r-ḥ-m*, the same root as *raḥim*, the womb. A mercy that is womb-like — enclosing, sustaining, gestating, protecting.

Now teach yourself something about how human language pairs concepts.

When you describe a person, the adjectives you choose tell us how you see them. You can say: *strong and gentle.* That's a coherent person. You can say: *wise and patient.* Coherent. But there are pairs that don't work — pairs the listener has to *resolve* before they can accept them.

If I told you about a friend: *"He is brutally cruel and exquisitely kind"* — you would stop. You would say: *which one is he, really? When does the mask come off?*

`al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm` is, on the human ear, that kind of pairing.

Almighty. Most Merciful.

The almighty *crush*. The merciful *cradle*. They live, in the human imagination, in different rooms.

[PAUSE]

And yet Allah places them in a single breath. With no conjunction between them. Not "the Almighty *and* the Most Merciful." Just: **al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm.** As if they are not two attributes meeting in tension but two faces of one thing.

Why does this pairing close the refrain?

Look at what just happened in the surah. A nation rejected the sign. They were destroyed. The destruction is the work of *al-ʿAzīz* — the Almighty whose power could not be resisted.

But the surah does not close on `al-ʿAzīz` alone. It closes with `ar-Raḥīm` placed beside it. And here is the staggering thing the pairing does:

**The same Lord who could destroy a nation that rejected the sign — is the same Lord who is mercy itself.**

The destruction was not the Almighty *overcoming* the Merciful. It was not Allah's power winning out over Allah's mercy. The destruction *was* an act of His mercy — mercy to the next nation, mercy to the believers within the destroyed nation, mercy to the future, mercy to the truth itself.

And in the other direction: the mercy that He shows is not a mercy *despite* His might. It is a mercy *backed by* His might. A merciful being without power is a being whose mercy can be overruled. A powerful being without mercy is a being whose power is terror. But `al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm` is mercy that *cannot be overruled* — and might that *will not be cruel.*

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators did not leave this as philosophy. Al-Tabari, commenting on these two names in this very ayah, gives each name its own address. Al-ʿAzīz, he explains, is the One who is *shadīd al-intiqām* — severe in retribution — against those who worshipped other than Him and died without ever turning back. And ar-Raḥīm is the One who will not punish those who repented for what came before their repentance: *"an yuʿāqibahu ʿalā mā kāna salafa minhu qabla tawbatihi min ithm wa jurm"* — not holding against the repentant soul the sins and wrongs that preceded the turning.

This makes the pairing do something new.

The names are not addressing one undifferentiated mass of humanity. They are addressing *two groups* standing at two different crossroads. Al-Muyassar makes this explicit: al-ʿAzīz is "capable of retribution against the deniers" (*al-mukadhdhibīn*), and ar-Raḥīm is directed specifically "to His believing servants" (*ʿibādihi al-muʾminīn*). The might faces one direction. The mercy faces another. Together, they constitute a complete map.

To the one who has rejected and wonders if there is still a way back: **ar-Raḥīm** — the mercy is real, and it covers what came before the turn. To the one carrying the message and asking how long the rejection will be allowed to stand: **al-ʿAzīz** — the power that governs all of this is not indifferent, and nothing escapes it.

[PAUSE]

Notice now where this lands for the Prophet ﷺ. He is grieving over his people. His mercy for them is fierce — Allah Himself will say elsewhere, *"Perhaps you would kill yourself with grief over them"* (18:6). And the temptation in that grief is to fear: *if Allah is so powerful, and they are so stubborn, what will become of them?*

The refrain answers: **Your Lord is al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm.** Mighty enough that no rejection escapes Him. Merciful enough that the door of ar-Raḥīm has not closed on the one who has not yet turned. Whatever happens to them, happens within a frame both perfectly powerful and perfectly merciful.

This is not abstract theology. This is the only frame in which a prophet can bear watching his people walk past the sign.

### The Bridge

So look at what the linguistic construction has done.

The double lock of `inna ... la` testified — under Allah's own oath — that the sign was real and the Lord is who He says He is.

The strange weight of `kāna` — carrying, as al-Tabari observed, the force of divine foreknowledge reaching back before the first story began — made the disbelief of one nation into a description of the human pattern across all ages, lifting the failure off the messenger's shoulders.

The impossible pairing of `al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm` placed every act of Allah — whether it looks like destruction or like protection — inside a frame of perfect might married to perfect mercy, each name, as the classical commentators saw, addressed to a specific human position.

These are not three points. They are one consolation, refracted through three grammatical features.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Sign That Doesn't Land — and What That Means About Belief

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You have a friend. Or a sibling, or a parent, or a child. Someone you love. And there is something true about their life — a pattern, a truth about a relationship, a truth about how they're treating themselves — that is utterly visible to you. You have shown it to them. Gently, then directly, then gently again. You have shown it in conversation. You have let it emerge in experience. You have given it time and given it space. You have waited.

And they have not moved.

Not because they didn't understand. Not because the sign was unclear. Somehow — and this is the part that tears at you — they understood, and still did not move. The sign was there. The crossing was there. They chose not to cross.

What do you do with that?

The human answer, in the silence after the third or the seventh or the twentieth attempt, is almost always the same. It turns inward. *Maybe I didn't explain it right. Maybe I'm the problem. Maybe if I had come to them differently — at a different time, with different words, with more patience or more directness — the sign would have landed.*

This is the grief the refrain is for.

Al-Muyassar makes a remarkable observation when commenting on "most of them were not believers." The commentator extends the referent beyond Ibrahim's generation: "most of those who *heard this account* did not become believers" — *alladhīna samiʿū hādhā al-nabā'*. The account being recited in Mecca to the Quraysh. The people in the room with the Prophet ﷺ, hearing these words at that very moment, are themselves inside the pattern being described. The refrain is not merely historical. It is addressed to the living audience — and to every audience that will ever hear it.

[PAUSE]

The sign was not insufficient. Ibrahim's argument against the idol-worshippers in Surah Ash-Shu'ara is not a weak argument. It is an unbreachable argument — entirely conducted through speech. He asks them: what do they worship? They say: we worship what our fathers worshipped. He asks: have you considered whether these things have any power over you at all? Have you considered that they are your enemies? He makes his case methodically, through logic and cross-examination, to its unanswerable conclusion. Al-Tabari opens his commentary on this ayah with the phrase *"in what Ibrahim argued against his people with the proofs and arguments we mentioned"* — *al-ḥujaj* — the proofs. The argument was sound. The evidence was clear. And the majority did not cross.

This is the anatomy of the situation the refrain is naming. It is not that the sign was hidden or the argument was weak. It is that the sign requires the act of *iʿtibār* — the willing crossing from event to meaning, from proof to its implications for your life. And the willingness to cross is something the argument itself cannot supply.

A proof can defeat a position. It cannot create a person who wants to be moved.

[PAUSE]

And so the refrain does something very specific for anyone who has carried a truth and watched someone they love refuse it. It removes the question from the register of your adequacy. It puts it in the register of the *established pattern of the human situation.*

**Most of them were not going to be believers.** Not because the sign failed. Not because the messenger failed. Because this is the shape of the encounter between truth and the human will when the will has decided. The pattern holds across Nuh and Hud and Salih and Lut and Shu'ayb and Ibrahim — and let the Prophet ﷺ hear this — it holds in Mecca too. It will hold wherever you are.

This is not fatalism. It is diagnosis. And the difference between fatalism and diagnosis is everything. A fatalist says: *nothing matters, nothing changes anything.* A diagnosis says: *now you understand what you are dealing with, and you can stop blaming yourself for the part that was never yours to fix.*

### Theme Two: The Eight Refrains — What Exact Repetition Does That Variation Cannot

There is a question you might be sitting with by now. If the lesson is clear after two refrains — or three — why does Allah repeat the same two ayat *eight times*? Why not vary the language, rotate the divine names, introduce something new with each recurrence?

The answer, I think, is the same reason a river cuts a canyon.

Not by force. By return.

One pass of water over rock does almost nothing. But the same water, in the same groove, returning again and again and again — it goes deeper each time. It does not need to be a different river. It needs to be *the same* one.

Al-Tabari, commenting on this ayah, identifies what the word *āyah* points to as *sunnatu Allāh fī khalqihi* — the divine pattern, the way of Allah with His creation. The word *sunna* is important. It is not just a lesson to be memorized. It is a *way* — a consistent, established manner of proceeding. The kind of regularity you can learn to trust. When you know the *sunna* of something, you no longer have to be surprised by it each time it appears. You can anchor yourself to it.

The eight refrains are not repeating a lesson. They are establishing a *sunna* in the reader's mind.

The first time the refrain appears, you receive the lesson intellectually. *Yes — signs don't always produce belief. Understood.*

The second time, the lesson drops slightly deeper. *This happened with Musa too. Not just once. A pattern beginning.*

The third time — at 26:103, where we are now — something begins to shift. The lesson is no longer arriving from outside. It is beginning to be recognized from within. You are starting to anticipate it before it arrives.

[PAUSE]

And this is precisely what the Quran is doing to the Prophet ﷺ. When the Quraysh reject him on Monday, he has received the lesson once. When they mock him again on Thursday, twice. By the time it has happened twenty times, forty times, a hundred times — if he has internalized the Quran, the refrain is not a sentence he reads. It is a groove in him. He *knows* this. Not intellectually. In the bones.

The exact repetition is what does this. Variation would keep the lesson in the intellect. Exact repetition engraves it in the will.

Notice also what the refrain does structurally. Each story in Ash-Shu'ara ends differently. The destructions vary. The crimes vary entirely. The details of each narrative are their own. And yet the refrain does not vary by a single letter. It acts as a frame that is stronger than any picture it surrounds. No matter what happened inside the story — no matter how this particular messenger suffered, how this particular nation sinned, how this particular destruction unfolded — the frame around it is always the same. The verdict is always the same.

*In that is a sign. Most were not going to believe. Your Lord is the Almighty, the Most Merciful.*

This is how you train a heart to remain stable inside catastrophe. Not by removing the catastrophe. By giving it an unchanging frame.

### Theme Three: The Prophet ﷺ Reading This Surah — and What It Does to Us

There is one more thing to sit with, and it is the most personal.

This surah was revealed to the Prophet ﷺ in Mecca. The early Meccan period. Before the hijra, before the battles, before the victories. The period in which he was alone, outnumbered, grieving, and wondering.

And he was told: *here are the stories of every messenger before you. Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb. Read them. And at the end of every one, hear these two ayat.*

What is the experience of that?

Think about it from inside. You have spent years calling your people. You have watched your closest relatives reject you. You are lying awake at night wondering if you have done something wrong, said something wrong, been insufficient in some way that a better messenger would not have been. And Allah gives you a surah that says: *read what happened before you.*

But He does not let you draw your own conclusion from the stories. He tells you explicitly — after each one — what the conclusion is. And the conclusion is the same eight times. Not to overwhelm you. To *anchor* you.

The double lock of `inna ... la` is doing something that goes beyond grammar. It is the way a father speaks to a son who is shaking. *Yes. I know this looks impossible. I swear to you: the sign was real. I swear to you: I am who you think I am.* The extra weight in the language is not for a casual audience. It is for someone standing at the edge of a particular kind of despair.

[PAUSE]

And notice: the refrain is addressed in the second person. *"And indeed, your Lord — rabbaka — He is the Almighty, the Most Merciful."* Not "the Lord." Not "our Lord." *Your* Lord. The Prophet ﷺ's Lord. The pronoun is singular, specific, intimate. It is not a theological statement about the universe. It is a personal address.

*Your* Lord — not the abstract first cause of everything, not the God of the philosophers, but the One who is in relationship with *you*, the one reading these words right now at the end of this particular story — *your* Lord is al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm.

No matter what just happened in the narrative. No matter which nation just perished. The Lord who oversees all of it is yours. He is the Almighty. He is the Most Merciful. Both things fully. Both things at once.

[PAUSE]

This is the consolation the surah was engineered to deliver. Not once. Eight times. Engraved.

And because the Quran addresses every generation, it addresses ours. We are among "those who heard this account" — the extension al-Muyassar observed reaches us. Every person who has carried something true and watched it refused, every person who has poured effort into a message that did not land, every person who has sat in the silence after the sign and wondered whether the problem was them — this refrain is in the room with them.

*In that is a sign. Most were not going to believe. Your Lord is the Almighty, the Most Merciful.*

## A Closing Word

There is a discipline the refrain teaches that has no name in English but that the Quran models in its very structure.

It is the discipline of bearing what you cannot change without being broken by it.

Not resignation — resignation is the death of care. Not denial — denial is the refusal to see what is real. Something in between, and stronger than both: the ability to see the pattern clearly, name it honestly, and remain standing inside it.

**Most were not going to be believers.** This is the honest statement. The refrain does not soften it. It does not say *most will eventually come around* or *if you try harder the numbers will shift.* It says: in the established order of things, as al-Tabari observed in the settled foreknowledge of Allah, across the full sweep of prophetic history, this is the shape of the encounter between truth and the closed will.

And then — in the same breath — **al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm.**

The One who is severe in retribution against the one who chose to die refusing — and the One who will not hold against the repentant soul a single sin that preceded the turning. The One in whose hands the full weight of this sits — not in yours.

Eight times. Word for word.

By the eighth time, the reader who has been paying attention has not just learned the lesson. They have been changed by the repetition. The refrain is no longer a statement they encounter. It is a way of seeing they carry.

That is what the surah was trying to give the Prophet ﷺ. And by extension — through every recitation, in every age — it is what it is trying to give us.
