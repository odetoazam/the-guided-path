# The People Beneath You: When Class Becomes a Veil

## Introduction

Listen to these words. Sit with them before we open them.

> قَالُوٓا۟ أَنُؤْمِنُ لَكَ وَٱتَّبَعَكَ ٱلْأَرْذَلُونَ
>
> *Qālū a-nu'minu laka wa-ttabaʿaka al-arẕalūn*
>
> "They said: 'Should we believe in you when **the lowest of people** follow you?'"
>
> *Qāla wa mā ʿilmī bimā kānū yaʿmalūn. In ḥisābuhum illā ʿalā Rabbī law tashʿurūn. Wa mā ana bi-ṭāridi al-mu'minīn. In ana illā naẕīrun mubīn.*
>
> "He said: 'And what knowledge would I have of what they used to do? Their account is only upon my Lord — **if you could but perceive**. I am not one to drive away the believers. I am only a clear warner.'"

[PAUSE]

### The world we have to enter

To hear what Allah is doing here, we have to step into a particular kind of room.

Imagine a council of the established. Men of standing. Their families have owned land for generations. Their names mean something. They sit in their inherited seats and a man — a prophet — has come to them with a call that demands they bow before something higher than their lineage.

And they look out at the people who have already responded to him.

The people who responded are not from those families. They are the workers, the men whose hands are calloused from labor that the council members have never had to perform. The people who, in this society, are *seen* but not *counted*.

The council looks at this group, and then they look back at Nuh, and they say something that is not a theological objection at all. It is a social one. It is a sneer dressed up as a question.

*"Should we believe in you — when **these** are the people who follow you?"*

Notice what they did not say. They did not say "your message is false." They did not say "we have a better theology." They said: **the wrong people have already accepted you.** The fact that those without standing beat them to belief is, for the council, evidence against the message itself.

This is one of the oldest objections to truth that humanity has ever produced. It is older than philosophy. It will outlive every empire. And in five short ayahs, Allah teaches Nuh — and us — exactly how to refuse it without flinching.

### The strand we are inside

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is built on a procession. Prophet after prophet steps forward — Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb — and each one delivers a near-identical refrain: *fattaqullaha wa aṭīʿūn*, "fear Allah and obey me." Each one is rejected. Each rejection sounds different on the surface. But underneath, the surah is exposing a single anatomy of disbelief, broken into seven cross-sections.

The cross-section we are about to cut into is **class**. Of all the prophets in this surah, Nuh is the one whose rejection is recorded in explicitly *socioeconomic* terms. The people of Pharaoh objected on grounds of power. The people of Hud objected on grounds of pride in their own strength — boasting about what they had built and the force they could wield. Nuh's people objected on grounds of *whom* the message had already touched.

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Watch what Nuh does in his response. He is being asked to perform an act of social surgery — to cut away the people they consider beneath him so that they, the elite, can come in. That is the offer hidden inside their objection.

He refuses. But notice *how* he refuses. He does not defend his followers. He does not list their virtues. He does not say "actually, these are good people." He says something far more devastating.

He says: *I do not even know who they really are. And neither do you.*

Stay with that. The prophet of Allah, looking out at the very people who believed in him, says: **I have no knowledge of what they used to do.**

What is going on here? Why would Nuh distance himself from his own followers? And why does Allah preserve this exchange for us — across thirty centuries and into our own scrolling, judging, ranking lives?

Let us open it slowly.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to anchor ourselves in three places in this passage. A word that paints a picture. A construction that performs a shrug. And a tiny phrase that contains an entire theology of human worth.

### Element One: *al-arẕalūn* — the word that is the whole problem

Let us begin with the word the council uses to dismiss Nuh's followers.

> وَٱتَّبَعَكَ ٱلْأَرْذَلُونَ
>
> *wa-ttabaʿaka al-arẕalūn*

Before we open this word, let us teach ourselves something about how Arabic builds plurals.

In Arabic, when you want to say "the most X people," you can use a structure called *afʿal al-tafḍīl* — the elative pattern. *Akbar* means "biggest." *Aṣghar* means "smallest." Now, when you want to make this into a plural that means "the X-est ones, as a class" — you reach for the form *afāʿil*. So *al-akābir* means "the great ones," "the elites." And here, from the root **ر-ذ-ل** (r-ẕ-l) — meaning the lowest, the meanest, the most worthless of a category — Allah gives us **al-arẕalūn**.

Notice something. This is not a description. This is a *categorical ranking*. The council is not saying "your followers are poor." They are saying "your followers occupy the bottom slot in the hierarchy of human beings." The word performs a sorting. It places people on a scale and points to the bottom.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the *sound* of this word. *Arẕalūn*. The *ẕ* is a thick, heavy consonant — a sound that slides down the back of the throat. It is one of those Arabic letters that has weight, that drags. The word physically descends as you speak it. *Ar-ẕal-ūn.* You can hear them spitting it out.

This is the first thing to notice. **The very phonetics of their objection enact the contempt they feel.** Allah did not just record what they said. He recorded *how* it sounded coming out of their mouths. The sound carries the sneer.

Here is what is remarkable. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — note that the root *r-ẕ-l* is used for things that are rejected because they are considered worn-out, defective, of inferior craft. It is the word you would use for the cracked pot you set aside, for the cloth that did not pass inspection. The council is not just calling these believers low-status. They are calling them **defective humans**. The seconds. The rejects. The *unworthy of being at the table*.

And al-Tabari is precise about who exactly is being contrasted. The council's objection, he notes, is framed against *dhawī al-sharaf wa ahl al-buyūtāt* — "people of nobility and established households." Not merely the wealthy. The *named*. Those whose families had positions across generations. The objection is not simply that the believers were poor; it is that they had no *standing*. No ancestral seat. No genealogical claim to be heard.

Ibn Kathir draws out a further layer: what the council truly feared was not contamination — it was *equalization*. They said they would not follow Nuh and thereby "become equal to the meanest of the people." Belief, to them, was a social act with a social cost. Joining this message would require them to stand alongside those they had spent their lives ranking beneath themselves. To pray to the same God. To be accountable before the same tribunal. **The truth of the message was irrelevant to this calculation.** What mattered was the company it would put them in — and that company was too low a company to enter.

[PAUSE]

What this reveals is that the council has confused **invisibility** with **inferiority**. They could not see the believers — could not locate them in their social taxonomy — so they assumed there was nothing there to see. The word *arẕalūn* is not a fact about the believers. It is a confession about the council's own moral vision. They have eyes that only register status.

This single word, then, is doing two things at once. It is the council's weapon. And it is Allah's diagnosis of them. By preserving the exact word they used, Allah is saying to us: *watch what they reach for when they want to reject the truth. Watch the sorting they do. This is what disbelief looks like when it speaks its real language.*

### Element Two: *wa mā ʿilmī bimā kānū yaʿmalūn* — the prophet's refusal to defend

Now look at how Nuh responds. Most of us, if our community were attacked, would defend it. We would list virtues. We would say *but actually they are good, they are devout, they are deserving.* This is what we are trained to do. Defend your tribe.

Nuh does not do this. Listen to what he says.

> قَالَ وَمَا عِلْمِى بِمَا كَانُوا۟ يَعْمَلُونَ
>
> *Qāla wa mā ʿilmī bimā kānū yaʿmalūn*
>
> "And what knowledge would I have of what they used to do?"

Stay with this. It is not what we expect.

Let us teach a small grammatical point first. The construction *mā ʿilmī* — "what knowledge of mine [is there]" — is a rhetorical disclaimer in Arabic. It is the way you push something away from your own jurisdiction. It is the linguistic equivalent of opening empty palms. *I am not the one who knows this. This is not within my competence to assess.*

And then notice the verb: *kānū yaʿmalūn*. This is a particular tense — the imperfect verb embedded inside *kāna*, which gives it a continuous past habitual meaning. *What they used to be doing.* Not "what they did" as a single act, but **the entire fabric of their previous lives**. Their habits. Their patterns. The lives they lived before they walked into the circle of belief.

Why is this the construction Allah chooses for Nuh's response? Here is what is happening.

The council was implicitly demanding that Nuh **vet** his followers. *Prove to us they are worthy. Show us their lineage, their wealth, their record.* Nuh's response refuses the entire frame. He says: *I have no idea what these people's previous lives looked like. That is not what was asked of me. That is not the basis on which they entered this circle.*

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari preserves the classical understanding with striking precision. Nuh's meaning, he explains, is this: *"I know only their outward condition, not their interior. I was not charged with knowledge of their interior — I was charged with the outward. Whoever showed good, I thought well of him; whoever showed evil, I thought ill of him."* The prophet is not being evasive. He is stating the precise boundary of prophetic function. He was sent to call people to faith, to read what was *manifest*, not to excavate the hidden interior of lives. The *bāṭin* — the inner — belongs elsewhere. It belongs to the Lord.

Al-Muyassar draws out the direct implication with magnificent economy: *"I was not charged with knowing their deeds. I was only charged with calling them to faith. The criterion is faith — not lineage, not descent, not craft, not trade."*

Read that line again slowly.

*Not lineage. Not descent. Not craft. Not trade.*

Four categories that the council's entire social order was built upon. The classical commentator lists them as precisely what **does not matter** at the door of this message. The council had constructed an elaborate architecture of social sorting — and Nuh is saying: I was never given blueprints for that building. The only blueprint I carry has one question on it. *Did they come?*

This is also a dignity-preserving move. Imagine if Nuh had defended his followers by listing their hidden virtues. He would have made them, in that moment, into objects of his patronage. He would have implied: *yes, they are low, but here is why I have lifted them up.* Instead, by saying *I do not know their past*, he refuses to place himself between them and Allah. He refuses the role of certifier.

And here is what is even more remarkable. The verb *yaʿmalūn* — "they do" — is left **without an object**. He does not say "what they used to do *of evil*" or "what they used to do *of good*." It is just *what they used to do*. **Allah leaves the object blank.**

Why does that matter? Because the silence in that sentence is the entire point. Nuh refuses to even hint at what their past might have contained. Good or bad, righteous or sinful, distinguished or shameful — none of it is on the table. **The blank space is the dignity.** It is the prophet refusing to make their past part of the conversation at all.

### Element Three: *law tashʿurūn* — the verb that exposes everything

We come to the smallest phrase. Two words. And it may be the most piercing thing in the entire passage.

After Nuh says *their account is only upon my Lord*, he adds:

> لَوْ تَشْعُرُونَ
>
> *law tashʿurūn*
>
> "if you could but perceive."

Most translations soften this. They render it as "if you only knew." But that translation flattens something Allah has carefully placed.

The verb here is from the root **ش-ع-ر** (sh-ʿ-r). And the lexicons are unanimous about what this root means. It does not mean "to know" in the sense of "to have information." That would be *ʿalima*. *Shaʿara* means **to perceive through fine-grained sensitivity** — the way you feel a single hair brush your skin. The root literally relates to *shaʿr*, hair, and the sense is of perceiving something so subtle that only the most attuned awareness would register it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Nuh has just said and what he attaches *law tashʿurūn* to. He has just said: *their account is only upon my Lord.* Their *ḥisāb* — from ح-س-ب, the root for reckoning, calculation, the meticulous tallying of an account. He has placed his followers' lives in the books of Allah. And then he says: *if you only had the sensitivity to perceive that.*

Here is what this exposes about the council. The council is making confident, declarative claims about other human beings. They have ranked the believers. Sorted them. Filed them under *arẕalūn*. They are operating with **maximum certainty**.

Nuh's response is to point out their **catastrophic lack of sensitivity**. He does not say "you do not know"; he says "you do not *perceive.*" You do not have the receptors. The thing that would tell you what these humans actually are — that thing is in *you*, and it has atrophied. **Your moral sense organ is dead.**

The contrast is devastating. The council's vision of the world is not merely limited — it is inverted. They can see social rank with perfect clarity. They can identify who sits at which table, who carries which family name, who has how many holdings. But the dimension along which Allah measures human beings — this they cannot register at all. Their instruments were never calibrated for it.

And al-Tabari draws out precisely what the *ḥisāb* that belongs to the Lord actually covers. It covers the *bāṭin* — the interior, the hidden — "that which was concealed from me." Everything the council is trying to judge, everything Nuh openly admits he cannot see, belongs in that territory. Not Nuh's domain. Not the council's domain. The Lord's domain alone.

[PAUSE]

This is what *law tashʿurūn* means in full. If you had even a hair's breadth of real perception, you would understand that the ground on which you are making your judgment is not yours to stand on. The very act of ranking these believers as *arẕalūn* is a trespass into divine jurisdiction. You have declared a verdict that only the Lord is qualified to render.

And yet they declared it anyway. With confidence. With the assurance of those who have never had to question whether their sense of hierarchy was real.

This is the anatomy of the veil that class builds over the heart. It does not just distort what you see. It convinces you that what you see is *all there is*.

## Part 2: Three Moves and a Wall

Let us now stand back and look at the structure of Nuh's response across all four of his replies in these ayahs. This is architecture. Each move is precise.

**Move One** (112): *I do not know their past.* — A refusal of the role of judge.

**Move Two** (113): *Their account is with my Lord.* — A transfer of jurisdiction.

**Move Three** (113, coda): *If you could but perceive.* — An indictment of the council's capacity to see anything of what actually matters.

**Move Four** (114): *I will not drive the believers away.* — A refusal that is not merely personal but programmatic. Not "I choose not to." But: *this is not who I am.*

**The Final Statement** (115): *I am only a clear warner.* — A compression of identity down to its essential function.

Watch how the logic moves. It begins in epistemology (*I do not know their past*) and ends in ontology (*this is what I am*). It begins by disclaiming authority and ends by claiming something much more specific than authority — it claims a *function*. And that function has no room in it for social sorting.

### *wa mā ana bi-ṭāridi al-mu'minīn* — the refusal that has a grammar

> وَمَآ أَنَا۠ بِطَارِدِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ
>
> *wa mā ana bi-ṭāridi al-mu'minīn*
>
> "I am not one to drive away the believers."

This sentence is more than a statement of intent. It carries a specific grammatical structure that Arabic deploys for absolute negation.

The *bā'* at the beginning of *bi-ṭāridi* is the *bā' al-nafy* — which in classical Arabic attaches to the predicate of a negated nominal sentence to intensify and confirm the denial. This is not a casual "I don't plan to do that." This is: **the thing you are describing is categorically incompatible with who I am.** The grammatical particle stakes a stronger claim than the words themselves.

[PAUSE]

The word *ṭārid* — from the root **ط-ر-د** (ṭ-r-d) — means one who drives away, who chases out, who expels. It is the word for the act of ejecting something from a space. And what Nuh is refusing to eject are *al-mu'minīn* — the believers. Not "these people." Not "those who follow me." **The believers.** The definite plural, the category as a whole, those defined precisely by the fact of their faith.

This is a categorical refusal. It does not say "I will not drive away *these specific* believers." It says: *I do not drive away believers. That is not a thing I do.*

Sit with the weight of that.

The council's offer, embedded in their objection, was a kind of social negotiation. *If you want us, the people of standing, to join you — then show us you are serious. Curate your following. Run a quality check. Let the respectable in and send the arẕalūn back to wherever they came from.* This is the implicit deal they were proposing.

Nuh's response is not merely "no." It is the declaration that he does not have a version of himself in which that deal would ever be on the table. He does not possess, as a prophet, the category of "believers I can eject." His identity has no slot for "gatekeeper of who gets to come."

This exchange echoes through the Quran with remarkable consistency. In Surah al-Anʿam (6:52), Allah instructs the Prophet Muhammad directly: *"Do not drive away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His face."* In Surah Hud (11:29), Nuh addresses his own people: *"I will not drive away those who have believed."* In 11:31, he adds: *"I do not say to those whom your eyes look down upon that Allah will not grant them any good."*

The Quran returns to this moment across multiple surahs and multiple eras. Allah is not simply recording a historical exchange. He is establishing a *principle of prophetic hospitality*: the door of the message does not have a social filter on it. **Anyone who comes to the truth, having come, belongs to its community.** The prophet does not audit entry. He does not manage the guest list.

And this principle costs the prophet something. In Surah ʿAbasa (80:1-10), we see the Prophet Muhammad navigating this same tension — and the surah's very opening is a gentle rebuke: do not let the presence of those with social power make you turn away from those without it. The same pressure Nuh faced in his generation, the Seal of the Prophets faced in his. The council's logic is so persistent, so apparently reasonable, that even prophets must be reminded.

### What it means to be "a believer"

There is something worth pausing on in the specific word Nuh uses: *al-mu'minīn*, not *al-atbāʿ* (the followers) or *al-qawm* (the people) or any other relational term.

He calls them **the believers**. People defined not by their social position, not by their lineage, not by their occupation, but by the thing that has happened in them — the entry of faith.

*Īmān* — from the root **أ-م-ن** (ā-m-n): security, trust, the state of having placed one's safety in something. The believers are those who have found their *amān* in the truth of Nuh's message. They have made an interior move that no external marker can capture or deny. They cannot be seen by the council's instruments. They *are* seen by Allah's.

And when Nuh refuses to drive them away, he is refusing to treat as irrelevant the only thing that actually matters. He is saying: *these people have done the one thing I was sent to ask of them. I cannot now pretend that did not happen.*

## Part 3: *In Anā Illā Naẕīrun Mubīn* — The Identity That Holds

We come to the final ayah of this passage. And in many ways, it is the most compressed and the most complete.

> إِنْ أَنَا۠ إِلَّا نَذِيرٌ مُّبِينٌ
>
> *In ana illā naẕīrun mubīn*
>
> "I am only a clear warner."

Five Arabic words. And in five words, Nuh has answered every demand the council has ever made or ever will make.

The construction *in anā illā* is a structure of *qasr* — restriction, limitation. It is not simply saying "I am a warner." It is saying: **this is all I am.** The *qasr* empties out everything else the council might want to project onto him — social mediator, status gatekeeper, credential-verifier, curator of acceptable followers. He peels all of that away. What remains is one thing: a warner.

[PAUSE]

Let us look at each of the two words that survive this stripping.

**Naẕīr.** From the root **ن-ذ-ر** (n-ẕ-r), whose core meaning in the classical lexicons is to warn with a kind of urgency that comes from genuine care for the one being warned. This is not the warning of a judge who declares penalty. It is the warning of someone who sees what is coming and loves the person enough to speak. The *naẕīr* in classical Arabic was the scout who ran ahead and alerted the camp to danger. He was not the one who determined what the danger was. He was not the one who sorted which members of the camp were worthy of warning. He ran, he called out, he warned — to everyone within earshot.

**Mubīn.** From the root **ب-ي-ن** (b-y-n): to be clear, manifest, distinguishable. The *mubīn* is not merely clear himself; he *makes things clear*. He is the one who removes ambiguity, who creates the conditions under which people can see what they could not see before. The warning Nuh brings is not obscure. It is not hidden behind cryptic ritual or available only to those with the right credentials. It is *mubīn* — open, evident, accessible to all who would hear.

When Nuh says *in anā illā naẕīrun mubīn*, he is doing something that cuts through all social architecture. A warning is only useful if it reaches. It cannot reach if it requires the listener to first be vetted, first be sorted, first be approved by the council of the established. The very function of a warning is universal — it goes to everyone because the danger is for everyone.

This is why the *qasr* — the "I am *only* a warner" — is so significant here. The council wants Nuh to be many things: a social broker, a status-ascending figure, a credentialer of respectable belief. He refuses all of it. He takes the knife of *illā* and cuts away every role except the one Allah gave him.

And in cutting those roles away, he paradoxically becomes more powerful. A warner who doubles as a social gatekeeper is a compromised warner — his message is now filtered through his social negotiations. A warner who is *only* a warner, who has no interest in who stands where, who does not curate the crowd before he cries out — his warning reaches further and rings truer. There is no interference in the signal.

[PAUSE]

Notice also what this final statement does to the council's objection. Their question was, in effect: *how can you be a serious person if serious people haven't accepted you yet?* The implied logic: a true prophet would attract the right kind of followers first.

*In anā illā naẕīrun mubīn* dissolves this logic entirely. Nuh is saying: you have the wrong model of what I am. You are evaluating me as though I were a social movement requiring the endorsement of the respectable classes. But I am not that. I am a warning. And a warning's validity has nothing to do with the social rank of those who first hear it and heed it.

## Part 4: The Class Mechanism in Us

We have now moved slowly through the passage. Let us slow down even further — and turn it inward.

Because the council of Nuh's people did not vanish with the flood. They did not remain behind in the centuries when the mufassirūn were writing. They are present in every community that has ever organized itself, including ours.

Sit with this question.

**When you imagine the ideal follower of truth — what do they look like?** What education do they have? What family do they come from? How do they speak? What markers do they carry? And when someone walks in who does not match that image — someone whose past is unclear, whose social position is low, whose manner is rough — what moves in you?

There is a council instinct in almost all of us. We have learned to associate certain markers with seriousness. Composed affect. Educated speech. Presentable biography. Clean past. The right kind of credentials. And when someone arrives without those markers, something very subtle whispers: *are they really the kind of person this message is for?*

The Quran is precise about what that whisper is. It is not discernment. It is the atrophying of *shaʿara* — that fine-grained perceptive faculty that would allow you to register a human being in the dimension that actually matters. The council lost it so long ago they did not know it was gone. They thought their social perception *was* their moral perception. They could not tell the difference.

[PAUSE]

The tradition of Nuh is preserved not because we need historical information about a people who lived before writing. It is preserved because **we are these people**. Not the believers — though we aspire to that. The council. We are the ones who look at who has come to the truth ahead of us and make social calculations about whether we can afford to sit with them.

And Allah places Nuh's response in front of us not to shame us into silence, but to give us an alternative language. When the council instinct rises — and it will rise, because we are human and we have been shaped by societies that sort us endlessly — Nuh's words are there as a different grammar.

*Wa mā ʿilmī bimā kānū yaʿmalūn.* I do not know what they were before they came. That is not my jurisdiction.

*In ḥisābuhum illā ʿalā Rabbī.* Their account is with Allah. Not with me.

*Wa mā ana bi-ṭāridi al-mu'minīn.* I do not eject believers from this circle. That is categorically not a thing I do.

*In ana illā naẕīrun mubīn.* My function is to warn. Not to sort.

These are not inspiring sentiments. They are a grammar of how to be in a community that the message touches unevenly — because the message always touches unevenly. It always reaches the edge before it reaches the center. It always moves first among those who have the least to lose by accepting it. If that disturbs you, the question is not about your followers. The question is about your vision.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing.

Ibn Kathir is precise about what truly drove the council's refusal: their fear of *equalization*. They could not bear that following this message would place them on the same level as those they had spent their lives ranking beneath themselves. To stand before the same God. To be tallied in the same *ḥisāb*. To have no special entry because of the family name or the ancestral seat.

That fear is not ancient. It lives in every institution, every mosque, every gathering space that has learned to be welcoming to the right kind of people and quietly impenetrable to the wrong kind. The social cost of truth — the leveling it requires, the company it brings — is the oldest barrier to belief that exists. And Allah, by preserving this exchange, is telling us: *I know this barrier. I have seen it in every generation. And I am telling you its name.*

Nuh paid the cost. He paid it by refusing to sell it. By keeping the door open even when the elite offered him something real in exchange for closing it. He held his ground not through argument but through identity: *I am only a clear warner.* And a warner who is only a warner does not negotiate the terms of the warning.

## Closing: Turning It to Supplication

These five ayahs, taken together, are a kind of mirror.

You can hold them up and ask: *where is the council in me?* Whose acceptance of truth have I quietly questioned based on who they are rather than what they have done? Whose presence in the circle of belief have I found uncomfortable — not theologically, but socially? When have I, even in the silence of my own heart, wished the message had attracted a different crowd?

And then you can hold up a different question: *where am I among the arẕalūn in someone else's eyes?* What parts of my history, my presentation, my position, make someone somewhere look at me and think: *not quite the kind we were hoping for?*

Both questions are Quranic. Both are necessary.

And then — having sat with both — you can turn to the One who owns all the *ḥisāb*.

اللَّهُمَّ يَا مَنْ لَهُ حِسَابُ مَا خَفِيَ وَمَا ظَهَرَ

*O Allah, You to Whom belongs the reckoning of what is hidden and what is manifest —*

You see what Nuh could not see. You see what the council refused to see. You see the interior that is hidden from every human eye, including mine. You know what I was before I came. You know what the people beside me were before they came. And You have not made that knowledge the condition of entry.

Let us not impose a condition You did not impose.

Let us not eject those whose *ḥisāb* You alone hold.

Let us be among those who, when the council whispers its ancient question — *should we really sit with these people?* — answer with the grammar of the prophet:

*What knowledge do I have? Their account is with You.*

وَمَآ أَنَا۠ بِطَارِدِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

*And I am not one to drive away the believers.*
