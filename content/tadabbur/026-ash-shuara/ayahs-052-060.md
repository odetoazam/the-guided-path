# "A Small, Contemptible Band": Whose Naming of You Endures

## Introduction

Listen to how these words arrive:

<!-- ayah:26:52 -->     ۞ وَأَوْحَيْنَآ إِلَىٰ مُوسَىٰٓ أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِىٓ إِنَّكُم مُّتَّبَعُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:53 -->     فَأَرْسَلَ فِرْعَوْنُ فِى ٱلْمَدَآئِنِ حَـٰشِرِينَ
<!-- ayah:26:54 -->     إِنَّ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ لَشِرْذِمَةٌ قَلِيلُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:55 -->     وَإِنَّهُمْ لَنَا لَغَآئِظُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:56 -->     وَإِنَّا لَجَمِيعٌ حَـٰذِرُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:57 -->     فَأَخْرَجْنَـٰهُم مِّن جَنَّـٰتٍ وَعُيُونٍ
<!-- ayah:26:58 -->     وَكُنُوزٍ وَمَقَامٍ كَرِيمٍ
<!-- ayah:26:59 -->     كَذَٰلِكَ وَأَوْرَثْنَـٰهَا بَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ
<!-- ayah:26:60 -->     فَأَتْبَعُوهُم مُّشْرِقِينَ

*Wa awḥaynā ilā Mūsā an asri bi-ʿibādī innakum muttabaʿūn. Fa-arsala Firʿawnu fī l-madāʾini ḥāshirīn. Inna hāʾulāʾi la-shirdhimatun qalīlūn. Wa innahum lanā la-ghāʾiẓūn. Wa innā la-jamīʿun ḥādhirūn. Fa-akhrajnāhum min jannātin wa ʿuyūn. Wa kunūzin wa maqāmin karīm. Kadhālika, wa awrathnāhā banī Isrāʾīl. Fa-atbaʿūhum mushriqīn.*

"And We inspired Moses: 'Set out by night with My servants; indeed, you will be pursued.' Then Pharaoh sent gatherers into the cities: 'These are but a small, contemptible band, and they are enraging us, and we are all wary and well-armed.' So We drove them out of gardens and springs, and treasures and every honorable station. Thus. And We caused the Children of Israel to inherit it. So they pursued them at sunrise."

**Rebuild the world.**

Put yourself in the position of the people who first heard this recited — the small, hunted community around the Prophet ﷺ in the years of Makkah. Because Surah Ash-Shuʿarāʾ is a Makkan surah, and it is one long procession of prophets. Nūḥ. Hūd. Ṣāliḥ. Ibrāhīm. Lūṭ. Shuʿayb. And here, longest of them all, Mūsā. Every single story follows the same shape: a messenger stands before an arrogant majority, is mocked, is called a liar, is threatened — and then the arrogant majority is destroyed and the messenger is delivered. Over and over. The surah is beating one rhythm into the chest of a community that was, at that exact moment, small. Mocked. Called liars. Threatened.

They were being called names in the streets of Makkah. *Majnūn.* Madman. *Sāḥir.* Sorcerer. *Ṣābiʾ.* A small band of the deluded, following a man who claimed the sky spoke to him. The Quraysh had the Kaʿbah, the caravans, the honor, the numbers, the gods. The believers had almost nothing but their conviction and each other.

Now hear where this passage sits. The confrontation with Pharaoh is over. The magicians have already fallen into *sujūd*. The lines have been drawn as sharply as they can be drawn. Ibn Kathīr frames the moment precisely: Mūsā had stayed among them a long time, the proof of Allah had been fully established against Pharaoh and his chiefs, and still they clung to their arrogance — so nothing remained but the reckoning. The argument is finished. What follows is not persuasion; it is deliverance and consequence. And Egypt is not some backwater — Egypt is **the** civilization of the ancient world. Pyramids already ancient. Granaries that fed empires. A god-king on the throne who did not merely *rule* the state, he *was* the state, and claimed to be divine on top of it. Against all of that: a nation of slaves. Brick-makers. People with no army, no land, no standing, whose babies had been slaughtered in living memory.

And into that unbelievable mismatch, Allah drops a single command in the dark.

**The hook.**

Watch two men in this passage name the same group of people. One of them is a god-king with the mightiest army on earth. The other is the Lord of the worlds. They choose their words in the same few verses — and by the end of these nine āyāt, only one of those names is still standing, while the man who spoke the other is at the bottom of the sea. The question this passage forces on you is not *who won*. You know who won. The question is: **whose naming of a person actually holds** — and which one you have been quietly living inside of.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### First: the command that came in the dark — *asri bi-ʿibādī*

Before we touch the āyah, let me put a small, ordinary human thing in front of you.

Think about the difference between being *told* to do something and being *entrusted* with something. If a stranger orders you to carry a box across a room, you carry the box. But if your closest friend says, "take this — it's the only photograph I have of my mother, don't let anything happen to it," something different happens in your body. The instruction is almost the same. The *possession* inside it changes everything. The word "my" did that. One little possessive turned an errand into a trust.

Hold onto that, because Allah is about to use exactly that word.

 أَنْ أَسْرِ بِعِبَادِي

The command is *asri.*

<!-- morphology:26:52:7 root=سري pos=V --> The verb *asri* comes from the root *s-r-y* (س-ر-ي), and its meaning is not simply "travel" — it is specifically **to travel by night.** Arabic has a dedicated verb for journeying under cover of darkness, and this is it. It is the same root, the same act, that opens Sūrat al-Isrāʾ — *subḥāna alladhī asrā bi-ʿabdihi laylan* — the Night Journey. To move when the world cannot see you move. Al-Ṭabarī glosses the whole command in the plainest terms: *travel with the Children of Israel by night out of the land of Egypt.* The darkness is not incidental to the instruction; it is built into the verb Allah chose.

Stay with that choice for a moment. Allah, who controls Pharaoh's eyes and Pharaoh's army and the hour of the sunrise, commands the escape to happen **at night.** Not a blaze of daylight, not a triumphant march out the front gate of Egypt. In the dark. Quietly. The way the weak have always had to move — under cover, holding their breath, hoping not to be seen.

Here is what that quietly tells you. Deliverance does not always look like power. Sometimes, at the beginning, it looks exactly like fear. It looks like a hunted people slipping out in the night, hearts pounding. The physical darkness the Israelites walked into is the same darkness every believer knows — the season where you are doing the right thing and it feels like nothing but risk, where obedience and terror are wearing the same clothes. Allah did not remove the night. He commanded them to walk into it.

And then — *bi-ʿibādī.*

<!-- morphology:26:52:9 root=عبد pos=N --> The word *ʿibād* — servants — carries the root *ʿ-b-d* (ع-ب-د), the root of worship and servitude. <!-- morphology:26:52:9 root=عبد pos=N --> And attached to the end is that single letter *yāʾ*: **"my."** *My servants.*

Notice who Allah is talking about. These are not prophets. This is not Mūsā alone. This is an entire enslaved nation — the whole *shirdhima*, as Pharaoh is about to call them — the ordinary mass of them, the frightened, the grumbling, the ones who will complain within days of this very escape that they were better off before. Allah looks at that entire imperfect, terrified, low-status crowd and calls them, with His own tongue: **ʿibādī.** *My servants.* (Al-Ṭabarī reads the possessive broadly — the Children of Israel as a people; and even where a commentator like al-Muyassar narrows it to *those who believed among them,* the "my" holds either way: believer or grumbler, they are bound to Him.)

That is the *idāfa*, the grammatical possession — the same "my" that turned an errand into a trust. Allah does not say "the Israelites." He does not say "Mūsā's people." He binds them to Himself. In the eyes of the empire they are property — Pharaoh's slaves, a labor supply. In the speech of Allah, in the very same breath, they are *His.* Nearness. Belonging. Not owned by Egypt. **Owned by the Lord of Egypt.**

And that is the word that has to be sitting in your ears when Pharaoh opens his mouth. Because he is about to name them too.

### Second: the word that sounds like a sneer — *shirdhima*

Let me show you something about how sound works before we get to the word itself.

Some words are smooth in the mouth. *Salām.* *Nūr.* *Māʾ.* They pour. You can say them gently. And some words are built out of consonants that pile up and catch and have to be almost spat — the mouth has to work, the sound comes out clenched. Every language has both, and skilled speakers reach for the harsh ones exactly when they want you to *feel* harshness, not just hear a definition. When you call someone "a wretch," the word itself scrapes. That scraping is part of the insult.

Now hear how Pharaoh describes the people Allah just called *ʿibādī:*

<!-- ayah:26:54 -->     إِنَّ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ لَشِرْذِمَةٌ قَلِيلُونَ

*La-shirdhimatun qalīlūn.*

<!-- morphology:26:54:5 root=شرذم pos=N --> The word is *shirdhima.* Feel it in your mouth: *sh — r — dh — m.* Four consonants clustering, the *shīn* and the *dhāl* grinding against each other. It is not a smooth word. It is a clenched, contemptuous word — and this is the **only place in the entire Qur'an** that it appears. Allah reached, once, for this exact rasping word and put it in Pharaoh's mouth. It means a small, scattered, contemptible remnant — a rabble, a ragtag scrap of people. Al-Ṭabarī catches the image exactly: *shirdhima,* he says, is the small leftover *remnant* of a thing — and he reaches for a line of old rajaz verse to pin it down: *winter has come, and my shirt is in* sharādhim — *tattered shreds that the onlooker laughs at.* The word belongs to worn-out rags, to the frayed scraps of a garment coming apart at the seams. That is precisely the image Pharaoh is reaching for: not a people, but the shreds of one. The sound *is* the sneer. You do not need the translation to know Pharaoh is curling his lip; the phonetics do it for you.

And then, as if *shirdhima* — which already carries "few and contemptible" inside it — were not enough, he adds:

<!-- morphology:26:54:6 root=قلل pos=N --> *qalīlūn* — "few." He piles a second word for smallness on top of a word that already means a small, worthless scrap. Belittlement doubled. Al-Ṭabarī even pauses over the odd plural — "few," *qalīlūn,* made plural rather than singular — and explains it as fewness clinging to each separate cluster of them, so that when you gather all their little groups together, the smallness only multiplies. Even the grammar of the insult is engineered to shrink them.

There is the second naming. Same crowd. Allah said *ʿibādī* — mine, near, entrusted, worthy of a night rescue. Pharaoh says *shirdhima qalīlūn* — a contemptible little scrap, barely worth the word.

**Two sovereigns. One people. Opposite names.** And every one of us lives, right now, somewhere in the gap between how Allah names us and how the world names us. Hold that. We are coming back to it.

### Third: the speech of a man arguing with his own fear

But do not leave Pharaoh's speech yet, because he keeps talking — and if you listen closely, he *refutes himself in three sentences* without noticing.

Watch the whole statement land:

<!-- ayah:26:54 -->     إِنَّ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ لَشِرْذِمَةٌ قَلِيلُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:55 -->     وَإِنَّهُمْ لَنَا لَغَآئِظُونَ
<!-- ayah:26:56 -->     وَإِنَّا لَجَمِيعٌ حَـٰذِرُونَ

First: *they are a contemptible little band.* (v54)
Then: *and they are enraging us.* (v55)
Then: *and we are all wary and armed to the teeth.* (v56)

Read those three lines the way you would read a person's actual words, not a script. Something does not add up, and the not-adding-up is the entire point.

<!-- morphology:26:55:7 root=غيظ pos=N --> Look at *ghāʾiẓūn* in verse 55 — from the root *gh-y-ẓ* (غ-ي-ظ). *Ghayẓ* is not mild annoyance. It is rage that boils in the chest, fury that fills you up. So Pharaoh, god-king of the mightiest empire on earth, has just admitted — out loud, to his own recruiters — that a scrap of runaway slaves fills him with *boiling rage.*

Now put the sentences side by side and ask the obvious question. If they are a "small, contemptible band," **why are they enraging you?** Why does the god-king feel anything at all toward a rabble? You do not seethe at what you truly despise. You do not fill with rage at an insect. And then verse 56 — *we are all, together, fully on guard.*

<!-- morphology:26:56:6 root=حذر pos=N --> The word *ḥādhirūn*, from *ḥ-dh-r* (ح-ذ-ر), means wary, on-guard, taking full precaution — some of the early reciters read it *ḥadhirūn*, cautious as a settled trait, and others *ḥādhirūn*, fully armed and mobilized for this moment. And here the classical readings do not compete — they stack. One reading tells you Pharaoh's people were *chronically* anxious about this "contemptible" band; the other tells you they were *mobilizing the entire war-machine* against them right now. Put them together and you get the fuller picture: a regime both permanently uneasy about these slaves *and* scrambling every soldier it has to chase them. Either way — **why?** Why does an empire mobilize its full might, in a state of high alert, against a scrap it just called worthless?

Here is what the grammar is doing, and it is devastating. **Pharaoh's own sentence structure betrays him.** He is drowning in emphasis: *inna* here, *inna* there, the emphatic *lām* stuck onto word after word — *la-shirdhima, la-ghāʾiẓūn, la-jamīʿun.* This is a man saying *indeed, indeed, indeed, truly, truly.* And you know who talks like that? Not someone who is calm. Someone who is trying to convince a room — and himself — of something the facts in his own chest are contradicting. The rage in verse 55 and the full mobilization in verse 56 are the truth. "Contemptible little band" in verse 54 is the lie he needs to tell so that the rage and the army feel justified instead of humiliating.

He is not describing the Israelites. **He is managing his own terror in front of his men.** And Allah preserved the whole self-contradicting speech, word for word, emphasis for emphasis, so that across every century a reader could catch a tyrant in the act of being afraid of the people he calls beneath him.

### The bridge

So the words have shown us three things. A rescue commanded into the *dark*, of people Allah calls *His own.* A single spat, one-of-a-kind word — *shirdhima* — in which we can literally hear a tyrant's contempt. And a three-line speech in which that same tyrant, without meaning to, confesses that he is enraged and mobilized and afraid of the very people he is belittling.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how naming, and fear, and worth actually work in a human life. Including yours.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: the ones who diminish you are telling you they are afraid

Think about the last time someone made you small.

Not with a fist — with a word. The colleague who says, in the meeting, "well, *some people* think..." and manages to make your entire contribution sound like a child's drawing. The family member who introduces your work with a little laugh, a little "he's still doing that whole *thing*." The ex who, on the way out, told you exactly what was wrong with you, item by item, as though reading a receipt. Or the crowd online that finds one person and decides, collectively, that this person is a joke — a *shirdhima*, a contemptible little nothing — and says so, loudly, in numbers, with total confidence.

There is a particular kind of ache in being named down. And the ache has a lie folded inside it: *they must be right, because look how sure they are, and look how many of them there are.* Contempt performs certainty. That is its whole trick. It walks in wearing the costume of someone who has already weighed you and found you worthless, and the costume is so convincing that you start weighing yourself the same way.

And here is what this passage does to that lie. It takes you *inside the mouth of the most powerful contemptuous voice in human history* — an actual god-king, an actual empire — and it lets you hear what is really happening behind the contempt. *They are enraging us. We are all on high alert.* Pharaoh's sneer and Pharaoh's fear come out of the same face in the same breath. The Qur'an does not tell you "tyrants are secretly afraid" as a slogan. It hands you the tyrant's own words and lets you catch it yourself.

Sit with what that means for you. **The energy someone spends diminishing you is a measurement.** Nobody mobilizes against a nothing. Nobody rages at what they truly find beneath them. The scale of the contempt is a reading of the threat. When a person has to keep telling the room how little you matter, they are not describing you — they are managing something in themselves that your existence disturbs. The louder the "you're nothing," the more something in them is screaming that you are not nothing.

This is not permission to be arrogant. It is permission to stop *believing the receipt.* The next time contempt comes at you with all its performed certainty, you are allowed to hear underneath it what Pharaoh could not hide: the *ghayẓ.* The boil. The alarm. Someone reaching for the harshest word in the language — *shirdhima* — precisely because the ordinary words were not managing to make you small enough to feel safe about.

What changes tomorrow is quiet but real: when you are belittled, instead of collapsing inward and asking "what's wrong with me that they see," you ask the truer question — *"what in them is this loud reaction protecting?"* You stop auditing yourself through the eyes of the frightened, and you start hearing their fear for what it is.

### Theme Two: two beings are naming you, and only one of the names gets the last word

Now go one layer under the fear, because there is a deeper thing here than "haters are secretly scared."

Everybody is being named all the time. You have a name from your parents' expectations. A name from the number on your paycheck. A name from the algorithm that has decided what you are worth showing to people. A name from that one thing you did years ago that a certain group of people will never let you outlive. A name from the mirror on a bad morning. We walk around wearing a stack of names other people and other systems have stapled to us, and most of our exhaustion is the effort of either living up to them or living them down.

Into that whole exhausting economy of names, this passage sets one confrontation with brutal clarity. Two beings name the same people in the same few verses. Pharaoh — with the throne, the army, the treasuries, the divine claim, the *numbers* — says: *shirdhima qalīlūn.* A contemptible little scrap. And Allah says: *ʿibādī.* Mine.

And then Allah does something with the rest of the passage that is the whole answer to which name holds. Watch:

<!-- ayah:26:57 -->     فَأَخْرَجْنَـٰهُم مِّن جَنَّـٰتٍ وَعُيُونٍ
<!-- ayah:26:58 -->     وَكُنُوزٍ وَمَقَامٍ كَرِيمٍ

<!-- morphology:26:57:2 root=خرج pos=V --> *Fa-akhrajnāhum* — *so We drove* **them** *out.* Notice the flip: for the whole passage the Israelites were the ones being driven, hunted, gathered against. Now the pronoun turns. It is *Pharaoh's people* who get driven out — out of *jannāt wa ʿuyūn,* gardens and springs; <!-- morphology:26:58:2 root=كنز pos=N --> *kunūz,* treasures; <!-- morphology:26:58:4 root=قوم pos=N --> and *maqāmin* <!-- morphology:26:58:5 root=كرم pos=N --> *karīm* — an "honorable station," a place of dignity and rank. Four things, piled up the way Pharaoh piled up his emphatic words — gardens, springs, treasures, honor.

And look at that last word: *karīm.* Honor. Nobility. The very quality Pharaoh's entire regime was built on projecting — *we* are the honored ones, *they* are the contemptible scrap. Allah lets him keep the word "honorable" attached to his station for exactly one verse — and then strips the station out from under him. The one who named others contemptible loses the thing he called honorable. His naming does not just fail. **It reverses onto him.**

<!-- ayah:26:59 -->     كَذَٰلِكَ وَأَوْرَثْنَـٰهَا بَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ

And then one compressed word:  *kadhālika.* "Thus." "Just like that." A whole civilization's wealth and rank changes hands in a two-syllable pivot. <!-- morphology:26:59:6 root=ورث pos=V --> *Wa awrathnāhā* — *and We made them inherit it* — *banī Isrāʾīl.* The gardens, the springs, the treasures, the honorable station: **inherited by the shirdhima.** The scrap gets the palace. The "contemptible little band" becomes the heir.

Do you see what has happened to the two names? Pharaoh's name for them — *contemptible, few, worthless* — is now at the bottom of the sea with the man who spoke it, buried under the very water that let his "servants" pass. Allah's name for them — *ʿibādī,* mine — is the one that carried them across, out of slavery, into inheritance. **The naming that endures is the naming of the One who has the last word over reality itself.**

Here is why this matters for you, tonight, in a life with no Pharaoh and no sea in it. Every day you are choosing, without noticing, which naming to *believe.* When the world's name for you and Allah's name for you disagree — and they will, constantly — you are quietly deciding whose verdict is real. The world can call you a failure, a nobody, a disappointment, a scrap. It can do it with numbers and confidence and the full weight of a culture behind it, exactly like Pharaoh. And Allah calls you *ʿabd* — His servant, bound to Him, near to Him, worth a rescue commanded in the dark. Both names are being spoken over your life. Only one of them gets the last word. Only one of them is still standing after the sea closes.

So the practical change is not "have more self-esteem." It is a discipline of *whose court you appeal to.* When the contempt lands — and it will land — you take it, and instead of filing it as a verdict, you carry it to the only Judge whose naming survives, and you ask: *what does the One who called me His say I am?* You let *His* naming be the one you organize your worth around, because you have now watched, in nine verses, exactly what happens to every other name in the end.

That is not a feeling you manufacture. It is a fact you lean on. The One who named you His has never once lost the last word.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture

Now that we have walked through it, look at how these nine verses are *built*, because the design is going to prove everything we just felt.

Go back to the very first verse. Allah tells Mūsā:  إِنَّكُم مُّتَّبَعُونَ — *innakum muttabaʿūn* — <!-- morphology:26:52:13 root=تبع pos=N --> "indeed, you will be **pursued.**" That word, *muttabaʿūn*, comes from the root *t-b-ʿ* (ت-ب-ع), to follow, to pursue. The commentators fill in what is behind it: al-Ṭabarī reads the pursuit's purpose plainly — Pharaoh and his troops would give chase to *come between them and their escape from his land.* A tyrant cannot bear to lose the hands that built his empire; the labor force walking out the door is, to him, theft. And al-Muyassar draws out the mercy folded into the very same warning: the promise "you will be pursued" carried its own quiet guarantee — they would not be overtaken before they reached the sea. The word that sounds like a threat is also a schedule.

Now go to the very last verse. <!-- ayah:26:60 -->     فَأَتْبَعُوهُم مُّشْرِقِينَ
— *fa-atbaʿūhum* — <!-- morphology:26:60:2 root=تبع pos=V --> "so they pursued them." Same root. *T-b-ʿ.* Again.

The passage is a sealed frame. It opens with Allah *promising* the pursuit and closes with the pursuit *happening* — the same root bracketing everything in between. And that changes how you read the whole thing. The pursuit was never a crack in the plan. Allah announced it, in advance, in the first breath, as part of the rescue: *you will be pursued.* He told them the terrifying thing *before it happened*, so that when Pharaoh's chariots appeared on the horizon, it would not shatter their faith — it would **confirm** it. "He said this would happen. We are exactly where He said we would be." The scariest moment becomes the proof that the plan is on schedule.

And look at the last word inside that frame. <!-- morphology:26:60:5 root=شرق pos=N --> *Mushriqīn* — from *sh-r-q* (ش-ر-ق), the root of sunrise, of the east, of light breaking. They pursued them *at sunrise.* The tyrant's army rides out in the full confidence of the dawn — into the sea. And the *ʿibād*, who were commanded to leave in the *dark* — *asri,* by night — walk out into deliverance. Feel the inversion the sounds are making: the ones in darkness are being saved; the ones riding into the light are riding to their end. The dawn that looked like the tyrant's advantage was the dawn of his drowning.

That is the summit. The frame of pursuit, the reversal of pronouns, the night that saves and the sunrise that destroys — none of it is accidental. It is a designed thing, built to show you that from the first verse Allah already held both ends: the pursued and the pursuer, the named and the namer, the dark and the dawn.

### Bringing it together

And now the two halves of what we found turn out to be one thing. The linguistics showed us a tyrant whose own words betrayed his fear, and a single "my" that bound a nation of slaves to their Lord. The themes showed us that contempt is a measurement of fear, and that only one naming of you survives. They are the same insight from two directions: **the loud, confident, contemptuous voice is never the one that gets the last word — because the last word belongs to the One who was holding both ends of the story before it began.**

We started with a small, hunted community in Makkah being called names in the street. We arrived at the bottom of the sea, where the name-caller lies, and at the honorable station — the *maqām karīm* — now inherited by the ones he called a scrap. The arc runs from contempt to inheritance, from the dark to the dawn, from *shirdhima* to *ʿibādī* — and the whole distance is crossed by trusting whose naming is real.

### Questions to carry

- Whose naming of you have you been quietly organizing your life around — and is it the one that gets the last word?
- When someone diminishes you, what would change if you heard the *ghayẓ* underneath it instead of the verdict on top of it?
- Where in your life are you being asked, right now, to *asri* — to walk into the dark and do the right thing when it feels like nothing but risk?
- Is there a name you have called *someone else* — beneath you, worthless, a scrap — that you would not want said back to you on the day the sea closes?
- What is Allah calling you that you have not yet dared to believe?

### One sentence to carry

**The world may name you a contemptible little scrap with all the confidence of an empire — but Allah names you His, and His is the only naming still standing after the sea closes.**

### The invitation

You do not have to win the argument with the voices that make you small. You do not have to out-shout Pharaoh. You are invited, instead, to simply stop letting the frightened be your judge — to take every contemptuous naming and carry it to the One who called an entire nation of slaves *ʿibādī,* and to let *His* word be the one you live inside. Walk into your night when He tells you to. The dawn that looks like it belongs to the ones chasing you belongs, in the end, to Him.

### Duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who named the hunted and the enslaved *Your servants* and carried them through the sea while the mighty drowned — let us live inside Your naming and not the world's. When we are made small, remind us whose we are; when we are afraid in the dark, remind us that You already promised the pursuit and already prepared the crossing. And do not let us die still believing any name for ourselves but the one You gave us. *Āmīn.*
