# And Do Not Disgrace Me on the Day They Are Resurrected

## The Ayah

[ayah:26:87] وَلَا تُخْزِنِى يَوْمَ يُبْعَثُونَ

**Transliteration:** Wa lā tukhzinī yawma yubʿathūn

**Translation:** "And do not disgrace me on the Day they are resurrected."

---

## INTRODUCTION

Before we open this ayah, I want you to picture something.

Imagine the man who is making this du'a.

This is not a sinner pleading from the depths of his failures. This is not someone who fell and is begging to be lifted. This is **Ibrahim** — peace be upon him. The one Allah Himself called *Khalīl* — His intimate friend. The one who shattered his people's idols with his own hands. The one who walked into a fire that had been built so large the birds could not fly over it. The one who laid his son down on the ground prepared to slaughter him because his Lord had asked.

This man.

This man, who passed every test Allah ever gave him.

This man, who built the Ka'bah with his bare hands and Ismail's.

This man — at the end of a long life of monotheism, sacrifice, exile, and trial — turns to his Lord and makes a du'a. And the climax of that du'a, the highest note he sounds, the deepest cry of his heart, is this:

*"And do not disgrace me on the Day they are resurrected."*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment.

If Ibrahim is afraid of being disgraced on that Day — what should you and I be feeling?

This is the strand running through the entire passage. From ayah 83, Ibrahim has been climbing a ladder of requests: *"My Lord, give me ḥukm and join me with the righteous. Make for me a tongue of truth among later generations. Make me an inheritor of the Garden of Bliss. Forgive my father..."* And then, at the top of the ladder, he doesn't ask for elevation. He doesn't ask for stations. He asks for the absence of one specific thing.

Not punishment. Not Hellfire. Not torment.

**Disgrace.**

In a single word, Ibrahim shows us something about that Day that most of us have never understood. Something the linguistic precision of his choice — and the strange grammatical absence within it — will reveal if we are patient enough to look.

Let's begin.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Tukhzinī* — Disgrace, Not Punishment

The verb Ibrahim uses is **تُخْزِنِي** — *tukhzinī*. From the root **خ-ز-ي** (kh-z-y).

Before we open this word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning.

In English, we have one word — "shame." It tries to do many jobs. It can mean private guilt. It can mean public humiliation. It can mean a sense of unworthiness. It can mean being exposed. We use one word and let context do the work.

Arabic does not do this. Arabic has different roots for different shades of this experience. There is **ḥayāʾ** — the inward modesty that makes you lower your gaze. There is **ʿayb** — the defect or fault that brings reproach. There is **faḍīḥah** — the scandal, the blowing open of what was hidden. And then there is **khizy** — the root Ibrahim chose.

*Khizy*, in the classical Arabic lexicons, carries a specific texture. It is not the inward sting of guilt. It is not the simple feeling of having done wrong. *Khizy* is **public humiliation in front of those whose opinion mattered to you**. It is being exposed before an audience. It is the loss of standing — the collapse of your face — in a crowd that is watching.

Notice something.

Ibrahim does not say, *"Do not punish me."* He does not say, *"Do not throw me into the Fire."* He does not even say, *"Do not be angry with me."*

He says: **do not disgrace me**.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. Why this word?

Because Ibrahim — and this is the breathtaking thing — Ibrahim has understood something about the Day of Judgment that we have to work very hard to feel. He has understood that on that Day, **everyone will be watching**. The first and the last. The prophets and the kings. His father, whom he just prayed for. His sons. His enemies. The people he debated. The king who threw him into the fire. Every soul who ever lived will be standing in one plain.

And in that crowd, every secret will become visible. Every hidden deed will become public. Every motive will be turned inside out.

The Quran says elsewhere: *yawma tublā as-sarāʾir* — *"the Day when secrets will be tested"* (86:9). Tested means brought out. Made visible. The hidden made known.

Now ask yourself — what does Ibrahim fear?

He does not fear that Allah will know his sins. **Allah already knows them.** He fears that *they* will know. The watching crowd. The audience of all creation. He fears the **face-collapse** in front of every soul that ever existed.

This is what *khizy* means. And this is why Ibrahim chose it.

Here's what's remarkable: in Arabic morphology, the verb *tukhzī* is in **Form IV** (*afʿala*) — the causative form. It means "to *cause* khizy in someone." Ibrahim is asking Allah not to be the *cause* of his disgrace. Not to be the One who exposes him. He understands that on that Day, only Allah controls who is exposed and who is concealed. Only Allah controls whose record is opened in the right hand and whose is opened in the left, behind the back.

So when Ibrahim says *lā tukhzinī*, he is saying: *I know You, and only You, decide whose face survives that Day. And I am asking — please — let my face survive.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

Let this land. The intimate friend of Allah is afraid of his face on that Day.

What should we be doing right now?

### Element Two: The Strange Construction *Yawma Yubʿathūn* — They, Not Us

Now look at the second half of the ayah.

**يَوْمَ يُبْعَثُونَ** — *yawma yubʿathūn* — "the Day they are resurrected."

Pause. Read it again.

*The Day **they** are resurrected.*

Did you catch what is missing?

In Arabic, when a speaker refers to a future event involving themselves and others, the natural construction would be the first-person plural — *yawma nubʿath* — "the Day **we** are resurrected." Or the passive that includes everyone — *yawma yubʿath an-nās* — "the Day people are resurrected." Or the explicit collective — *yawma yubʿathu al-khalāʾiq* — "the Day all creatures are resurrected."

Ibrahim chose none of these.

He chose **yubʿathūn** — third-person masculine plural. *They are resurrected.*

But who is "they"?

Let me teach you something about this kind of grammatical absence. In Arabic rhetoric, when a pronoun appears without an explicit antecedent in the immediate sentence, it does one of two things. Either it refers backward to someone already named — or it deliberately leaves the referent open, inviting the listener to supply meaning from context.

Here, the immediate context — ayahs 83-86 — has been Ibrahim addressing Allah in a deeply private du'a. There is no third-person plural noun to point back to. Which means the "they" is doing something else.

Stay with me.

Some classical scholars explained the "they" as referring back to the people Ibrahim mentioned earlier in the surah — those who worship idols, those who reject. *The Day THEY are resurrected* — the deniers, the opposers, the misguided.

Others said the "they" is universal — every soul, all of humanity.

But notice — and this is what's so haunting — both readings converge on the same emotional reality. Whether "they" means the misguided or "they" means everyone, Ibrahim has constructed his sentence such that **he is not in the pronoun**.

He has put a small, almost invisible distance between himself and the verb of resurrection. He does not say "the Day we are resurrected." He says "the Day **they** are resurrected" — and asks not to be disgraced before them.

What is he doing linguistically?

He is **separating his fate from the fate of the watching crowd**. He is implicitly saying: *they will be raised. And I will be there too. But please, my Lord — let me not be among them in their condition. Let my standing be preserved when their standing collapses.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what this construction reveals. Ibrahim does not, in this du'a, presume his own salvation. He does not say, *"The Day I am raised in honor."* He could have. The man who built the Ka'bah, the man who passed every test — he had every reason to speak with confidence.

But he speaks with the language of someone who is **standing in the crowd, not above it**. He uses "they" because, on that Day, he will be a face in a sea of faces. A heart in a sea of hearts. A standing among ten thousand standings.

And from inside that crowd, his prayer is simply: **please, let mine not be the face that collapses**.

This is the prophetic posture. Not "I am safe." Not "I have arranged things." But: *"I am one of them, my Lord. Have mercy on this one of them."*

[PAUSE — major pause]

Now let's bring these two pieces together.

The word *tukhzinī* points to public exposure. The construction *yubʿathūn* puts Ibrahim inside the public. **The fear of disgrace and the standing among the watched are doing the same work.** The vocabulary names the wound; the grammar locates the wound in a crowd.

Both pieces are saying the same thing from two directions: *I will be standing among them, my Lord, and what I cannot bear is to be the one whose face is exposed in front of them.*

This is one ayah. Five words in Arabic. And inside those five words, an entire psychology of accountability has been built.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Anatomy of Public Shame — Why Ibrahim Feared It Most

I want you to think about something in your own life.

Not your worst sin. Not the moment you were most ashamed of yourself privately. Something different.

I want you to think about a moment when **someone walked in on you**. A moment when you were doing something — maybe not even bad, maybe just embarrassing, maybe just a moment you would have preferred to keep private — and someone you respected saw it.

Maybe a parent. Maybe a teacher you admired. Maybe a friend whose opinion of you mattered.

Remember the heat that rose in your face. Remember how time seemed to bend. Remember how, in that single instant, the entire room felt different — because **the watching had begun**, and you had nowhere to hide.

This is a scratch. A tiny scratch. On the surface of what *khizy* names.

Now multiply that experience by an order you cannot calculate. Stretch the audience from one person to **every person who has ever lived**. Stretch the duration from a passing moment to a Day whose length is fifty thousand years. Remove the option to bury the memory later in distraction. Remove the option to never see those people again. Remove the option to laugh it off.

That is what Ibrahim is asking not to face.

[PAUSE]

Here is what modern psychology has begun to understand — and what the Quran has named for fourteen hundred years.

Researchers studying shame distinguish it sharply from guilt. Guilt says: *"I did a bad thing."* Shame says: *"I am a bad thing — and now you can see it."* Guilt focuses on the action. Shame focuses on the self being witnessed in its inadequacy. Guilt can be repaired through apology and amends. Shame cannot be repaired through action, because the wound is not in what you did — the wound is in **being seen**.

This is why people who experience deep shame often respond not by trying harder, but by **hiding**. By withdrawing. By going silent. Because the only relief they can imagine is to no longer be in the field of vision.

But on the Day of Judgment, there will be no field outside the field of vision.

There is no withdrawing. There is no going silent. There is no hiding behind a wall, behind a closed door, behind the night. The Quran describes that Day with the phrase *bārizīn* — *standing exposed* (14:21). Out in the open. Nothing between you and every other soul that ever was.

Ibrahim, peace be upon him, with all the spiritual perception Allah had given him, **felt the weight of this** during his earthly life. He could already imagine the standing. He could already feel what it would be to have his every motive, every private thought, every small betrayal of the truth — laid open before his sons, before his father, before every prophet, before every soul who ever doubted him, before every soul who ever followed him.

And he knew that against that exposure, no human strength is enough. No amount of righteousness can guarantee the preservation of the face. Because the only One who can preserve a face on that Day is the One who created it.

So he doesn't try to earn it. He **asks for it**.

*Wa lā tukhzinī yawma yubʿathūn.*

Notice — Ibrahim does not say, *"My Lord, my deeds will preserve me."* He does not list his sacrifices. He does not invoke his lineage. He does not point to the fire he walked into or the son he was prepared to slaughter. **He doesn't bring receipts.** He brings empty hands and a single petition: *do not disgrace me*.

What does this teach you and me, who have done so much less?

It teaches us that the only posture that survives the standing is the posture of **the asker**. Not the achiever. Not the deserver. The asker.

If Ibrahim arrives at the gate of that Day with empty hands and a request, what business do we have arriving with anything else?

[PAUSE]

The practical application is this. The next time you make du'a — tonight, tomorrow, the next prayer — try borrowing Ibrahim's words. *Wa lā tukhzinī yawma yubʿathūn.* Say it slowly. Mean it. Let it become not an abstract phrase but a **real petition** about a **real Day** when **real eyes** will be watching you.

Most of us pray to be admitted to the Garden. Pray, also, to be **preserved at the standing**. Because before the Garden, there is the standing. And before the standing, there is one face that you carry into it. And only Allah decides whether that face survives.

---

### Theme Two: The Crowd, the One, and the Mercy of Being Among Them

Now I want to take you somewhere unexpected.

We have been sitting with the fear in this ayah. The fear of disgrace. The fear of the watching crowd. But there is another layer here — and it is **mercy**, not fear. It is hope, not dread.

Let me explain.

Look again at what Ibrahim does grammatically. *Yawma yubʿathūn* — *the Day **they** are resurrected.* He places himself inside the crowd. He does not stand apart. He does not say, *"the Day You raise me alone, my Lord."* He says, *"the Day they are raised."*

There is something profound in this.

In our spiritual lives, we often experience our relationship with Allah as a private affair. Just me and Him. My prayer. My du'a. My struggle. And this is true — every soul will be questioned alone. The Quran says clearly: *wa kulluhum ātīhi yawma al-qiyāmati fardā* — *"and every one of them will come to Him on the Day of Resurrection alone"* (19:95).

But Ibrahim's grammar holds something else in tension with this. **He frames his hope inside the collective.** He does not pretend he will be a special exception. He does not isolate himself from the human condition. He says: *I will be one of them, my Lord. I will be in the crowd. I will be raised when they are raised. And in that crowd, my single request is — please, do not let my face be the one that breaks.*

This is humility. But it is also something else. It is **belonging**.

Here is the modern analogy.

Imagine a graduation ceremony. A massive auditorium. Thousands of students standing in rows. They are all going to be called, one by one, to walk across the stage. Some will walk with confidence — degrees earned, futures prepared. Some will walk with relief — they barely made it. And some will walk with their heads down — knowing the announcement of their name will be followed by a silence rather than applause, because their family is not there, or because they know they cheated to get there, or because they know the future they walk into is empty.

Now imagine you are a student in that auditorium. You have prepared. You have worked. But you also know — you are not the most prepared. There are valedictorians here. There are geniuses. There are people who will be celebrated. You will not be.

What is your prayer?

Your prayer is not, *"Let me be the valedictorian."* That ship has sailed. Your prayer is not even, *"Let me be celebrated."* You're past that. **Your prayer is: please, let me not be the one whose name is called and the silence follows. Let me walk across the stage with dignity. Let me not be the disgraced one in this crowd.**

This is Ibrahim's prayer.

He is not asking for the highest station. He is not asking to be the valedictorian of the resurrection. He has already, earlier in the du'a, asked to be *"made an inheritor of the Garden of Bliss"* — and now, having asked that, what remains? What is left to fear?

This. **The walk across the stage.** The standing. The being-watched. The procession of all souls past one another.

And in that standing, even prophets ask for mercy.

[PAUSE]

Here is the unexpected hope inside this ayah.

If Ibrahim — Khalīlullāh — placed himself in the crowd, then **you and I have a place in that crowd too**. The grammar of his du'a does not exclude us. It includes us. He prays as one of them, and we are among them.

Which means — and stay with me — **his du'a, in some sense, covers us**. We can borrow it. We can pray it. The same words that fell from Ibrahim's lips can fall from ours, because the same Day awaits us both. The same standing. The same exposure. The same hope for mercy.

Allah taught us this du'a by preserving it in the Quran. Why? So that we would read it as a museum piece? No. So that we would *take it up*. So that when our hearts feel the weight of that Day, we have prophetic words to lean on.

The application here is **belonging through humility**. When you stand in your prayer tonight, do not stand as if you are alone. Stand as one of them. As one of the millions of people praying *Maghrib* at sunset across the world. As one of the billions of souls who will rise on that Day. As **one of them**.

And from inside that belonging, ask what Ibrahim asked.

Because here is the secret. The one who stands in the crowd with humility — *"I am one of them, my Lord, please cover my face"* — is the one whose face is most likely to be covered. The one who stands apart with arrogance — *"I am not like them, my Lord, look how special I am"* — is the one whose face is most likely to be exposed.

The Day of Resurrection inverts the logic of every social hierarchy we know. The high will be made low and the low high. The first will be last and the last first. **The one who feared disgrace will be honored. The one who claimed honor will be disgraced.**

So Ibrahim's strategy is the strategy of the wise. *Place yourself low. Stand among them. And ask.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

What changes about how you live tomorrow?

You stop trying to be exceptional in front of Allah. You stop performing. You stop curating your spiritual identity. You join the crowd — the crowd of believers, the crowd of strugglers, the crowd of askers — and you pray, *as one of them*, for mercy.

That is the highest spiritual posture human beings can adopt. And it is the posture Ibrahim modeled in five words.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found.

The word **tukhzinī** named a fear. Not the fear of punishment, but the fear of **public exposure** — the collapse of the face in front of every watching soul.

The construction **yubʿathūn** placed Ibrahim **inside the crowd**, not above it. He did not pray as a prophet apart. He prayed as a human being among human beings.

These are not two insights. They are one insight from two angles. The vocabulary names what Ibrahim feared: disgrace before an audience. The grammar names where he placed himself: among the audience, as one of them, asking for mercy from inside the throng.

The whole ayah is a single cry from one face in a sea of faces: *let mine not be the one that breaks*.

And here is the architecture of the larger du'a — because now we can see it. Ibrahim climbs from request to request. *Give me wisdom. Join me with the righteous. Make me remembered well by later generations. Make me an inheritor of the Garden. Forgive my father.* Each rung higher. Each rung more transcendent. And then, at the top — the climax — what is the highest thing he asks?

Not a station. Not a reward. Not even forgiveness for himself.

**The preservation of his face on the Day of standing.**

This is the height of prophetic du'a. Everything he had asked for is meaningless if, on that Day, his face is the one that collapses. So the entire du'a is bent toward this single petition. The wisdom, the righteous companions, the truthful tongue, the inheritance of the Garden, the forgiveness for his father — all of it serves one final hope: *that on the Day they are raised, his face will be among the preserved*.

This is the architecture. The whole prayer points to this last line.

### Questions to Carry

- When you imagine the Day of Resurrection, what do you fear more — the punishment, or the standing?
- Whose eyes, on that Day, would you most fear meeting? What does that tell you about the relationships and choices you are making now?
- Have you ever, in your du'a, asked Allah specifically to **preserve your face on that Day** — not just to admit you to the Garden, but to cover you at the standing?
- What in your private life right now — what hidden thing — would most disgrace you if it were brought into the open before every soul who ever lived?
- Is there a way you stand apart from the crowd of believers — secretly thinking yourself better, more knowledgeable, more pious — that Ibrahim's grammar of *"them"* is calling you to abandon?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Ibrahim — peace be upon him — places himself inside the resurrected throng and asks not for elevation but for the simple mercy of a face that does not collapse, modeling for us that the highest spiritual posture is to stand among them and ask.

### The Closing Invitation

Take this du'a tonight. Make it yours. Five words in Arabic — *wa lā tukhzinī yawma yubʿathūn*.

When you say them, picture the crowd. Picture yourself in it. Picture the watching. And ask, as Ibrahim asked, for the preservation of your face.

This is not an invitation to fear. It is an invitation to **honesty** — the honesty of admitting that you, like Ibrahim, will be in that crowd, and that your face is not in your own hands. It is in Allah's. So go to Him with empty hands. Like Ibrahim did. And ask.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, yā Rabba Ibrāhīm, the One who heard his prayer and answered it — we ask You with the same words, from much smaller hearts: do not disgrace us on the Day they are resurrected. Place us among Your servants whose faces You preserve, whose secrets You cover, whose standing You make easy. We come with no receipts, no list of deeds, no claim to special standing. We come as one of them. Have mercy on this one of them.*

*Āmīn.*
