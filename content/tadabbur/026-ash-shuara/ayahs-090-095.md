# When the Garden Steps Forward and the Fire Comes Into View

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 90-95

---

### The Ayahs

> وَبُرِّزَتِ ٱلْجَحِيمُ لِلْغَاوِينَ
> وَقِيلَ لَهُمْ أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُمْ تَعْبُدُونَ

Then they will be hurled headlong into it — them, and those who lost their way, and the legions of Iblis, all together."

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

Step into the scene before we open a single word.

Ibrahim, peace be upon him, is praying. Not for himself, not for what people usually pray for. He has just asked his Lord for *ḥukm* — sound judgment. He has asked to be joined with the righteous. He has asked for a *lisān ṣidq* — a tongue of truth that will speak well of him in generations he will never meet. He has asked for forgiveness for his lost father.

And then — having asked all of that — Ibrahim does something striking. He starts describing **the Day**.

Not as someone reciting a creed. As someone who can already see it. The man who broke idols with his own hands now stands in front of his people and says: *let me show you what wealth and children come to. Let me show you what a single sound heart is worth on that Day.*

These ayahs are inside that vision. They are not Allah narrating Judgment Day to us in the third person. They are what Ibrahim — *Ibrahim* — asks us to picture, while he is still on his knees praying.

That changes how we read every word. This is a man pleading with his people through imagery so vivid that the unseen becomes visible. The whole Surah is named after the poets — *Ash-Shuʿarāʾ* — and here is Ibrahim showing us what speech can do when it is true.

### The Hook

There is something strange happening in these six ayahs that almost no English translation can carry.

When Allah talks about the Garden, He uses one verb. When He talks about the Fire, He uses a completely different verb. They are not parallel. They are not symmetric. The Garden does one thing. The Fire does something else entirely — and the difference between those two verbs is the difference between *being ushered toward something* and *having something pulled out of its concealment and placed in plain view before you*.

[PAUSE]

And then there is the question. The question Allah will ask the lost ones. It is not the question you might expect.

You will think you know what the question is. You will be wrong.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to open three things in this passage. A word for the Garden. A word for the Fire. And a single, devastating verb in ayah 94.

Three words. That is enough.

---

### Element One: *Uzlifat* — The Garden That Steps Toward You

#### The Concept Before the Word

Picture two ways of meeting someone you love after a long absence.

In the first, they are standing far away in a crowd. You see them. You start walking toward them. You push through people. You close the distance yourself, breath shortening, eyes never leaving them.

In the second, you are standing still — and **they** are the one walking toward you. You see them coming closer. Closer. They are the ones doing the moving. You haven't taken a single step. They are *being brought to you*.

Both are reunions. They feel completely different.

Arabic has a verb for the second kind. The kind where the thing you longed for **comes to you**. Where the distance is closed not by your effort but by something else — someone else — bringing it near.

#### The Word

The verb here is *uzlifat* (أُزْلِفَتِ). The root is *z-l-f* (ز ل ف).

Lisān al-ʿArab and the classical lexicons preserve a striking range for this root. *Zulfah* means *nearness* — but a particular kind of nearness. Not the static nearness of two things sitting beside each other. The nearness of something that has been **drawn close**, **made to approach**, **moved into proximity**.

The form here — *uzlifat* — is Form IV (*afʿala*), and it is in the passive. Two things matter about that.

First: **Form IV is causative**. It does not mean "the Garden is near." It means "the Garden has been *made to* be near." Someone — and we know who — caused it to come close.

Second: **the passive removes the question of effort from the muttaqīn entirely**. The muttaqīn are not running toward the Garden. They are not climbing. They are not earning their entry by some final exertion at the threshold. The Garden is brought. To them. By Allah.

Notice something. The English "brought near" almost gets it. But *uzlifat* carries something English cannot: the suggestion of an honored guest being *ushered in*. In old Arabic usage, *zalafa* could describe one who has been advanced in rank, drawn closer to the king's seat. To be made *zulfah* to a king was to be honored with proximity to him.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah says *wa uzlifati'l-jannatu lil-muttaqīn* — He is not just saying "the Garden will be near them." He is saying:

**The Garden — that vast reality — is escorted toward those who were mindful of Allah, the way an honored guest is escorted into the throne room.**

And what does it come as? The classical commentators note that the Garden arrives adorned and decorated for them to behold it — not merely near, but dressed for the meeting. The proximity and the beauty come together. The Garden is not just brought close; it is brought close *in its full splendor*, presented, the way a gift is placed before someone whose eyes are now ready to receive it.

Al-Tabari, commenting on this passage, defines the muttaqīn as those who feared Allah's punishment in the afterlife through their obedience to Him in this world. The Garden's movement toward them is not arbitrary. It comes to people who, in this life, moved their hearts toward it — who preferred it over whatever the world was offering and arranged their lives accordingly. They moved toward Allah. The Garden moves toward them.

The muttaqīn do not chase Paradise. Paradise is brought to them. But the verb is not erasing the effort they made in this world — it is crowning it. The ushering is the completion of a movement that began in them, here, now.

Stay with this for a moment. Because every spiritual instinct in us assumes that on the Day, the saved will be desperately running toward the Garden. That image is wrong. The Garden — the very Garden — moves first.

That is what the verb does.

---

### Element Two: *Burrizat* — The Fire Made Plain

#### The Concept Before the Word

Now the contrast.

Imagine a thing that has long been covered. A ruin behind a sealed gate. A well filled in and overgrown. A truth that has always been there, kept out of sight, requiring no confrontation so long as no one removes the cover. People know it is there, somewhere. They simply do not turn toward it. Life continues around its concealment.

And then the cover is removed. The gate is opened. The ruin steps out of shadow into the open clearing. **It is now in full view — visible, manifest, no longer tucked away.** Not because you walked up to it. Because it has been brought out.

Arabic has a verb for that kind of uncovering.

#### The Word

*Burrizat* (بُرِّزَتْ). The root is *b-r-z* (ب ر ز).

In Lisān al-ʿArab and Mufradāt al-Rāghib, *baraza* means **to come out into the open** — out of concealment, out of hiding, into a *barāz*: an open clearing where nothing can be hidden.

The form here, though, is not Form I. It is Form II — *burrizat*, the *faʿʿala* pattern, in the passive. **Form II intensifies and causes**. So the verb does not mean "the Fire emerged" — as if the Fire walked itself out of the shadows. It means **the Fire was brought out into the open. Made visible. Exposed in plain view.**

Al-Tabari renders it simply: *uẓhirat al-nār lil-ladhīna ghawaw* — "the Fire was made manifest for those who went astray." Ibn Kathir says it "will be shown to them." The classical exegetes are consistent: this is the language of exposure, of visibility, of something no longer concealed.

And the passive — again — matters. The Fire is not the agent. *Someone exposes it*. The same Allah who escorts the Garden toward the muttaqīn brings the Fire out into the open before the ghāwīn.

[PAUSE]

Notice what is happening. The Garden is *uzlifat* — brought close, ushered in. The Fire is *burrizat* — pulled out into open view.

These are **not the same action**. They are not parallel.

The muttaqīn are met by the Garden coming toward them. The ghāwīn see the Fire emerge from concealment.

The Garden comes as a meeting. The Fire appears as an uncovering.

Why is this so important? Not because the verb *b-r-z* itself carries any particular claim about the inner state of the ghāwīn — its lexical content is simply exposure, emergence into the open. But the theological weight of that exposure is precisely calibrated to the kind of people these are. The ghāwūn, as we will see, are those whose lives were structured around disordered desire — who chose the wrong attachments and built a world around them. The confidence of that world included a particular assumption: what is unseen will remain unseen. *Burrizat* ends that assumption in a single passive construction. The Fire is not reaching toward them the way the Garden reaches toward the muttaqīn. It is simply *uncovered*. Brought out of the concealment that distance, inattention, and this-worldliness had placed over it. And that is enough.

Two verbs. Two destinies. And the difference between them is the entire architecture of the Day.

---

### Element Three: *Ghāwūn* — The Ones Who Lost the Way

We need a word before we move on. *Ghāwīn* (الْغَاوِينَ) and *ghāwūn* (الْغَاوُونَ) are the same thing, in different grammatical cases. The active participle of *gh-w-y* (غ و ي).

The classical lexicons preserve something specific about *ghawāyah*. It is **not the same as *ḍalāl***. *Ḍalāl* is generic straying — the camel that wandered off the path. *Ghawāyah* is more precise. It is the deviation that comes from **disordered desire** — wanting the wrong thing, attaching the heart to what does not deserve it.

Al-Muyassar specifies who the ghāwīn are in this context: those who strayed from guidance, who dared to cross Allah's prohibitions, who denied His messengers. This is not the straying of someone who got lost by accident. It is the straying of someone who was confronted with the path and chose against it — who built desires, loyalties, and certainties in the wrong direction, and maintained them over a lifetime.

This is the same root, by the way, that Iblis used about himself in *aghwaytanī* — *because You let me go astray, I will sit in ambush for them on Your straight path*. *Ghawāyah* is Iblis's own technique. It is not knocking someone off a path by force. It is whispering to them until they *want* to leave.

So *al-ghāwūn* are not just the lost. They are **the ones who lost themselves by following misplaced longing**. People who fell in love with the wrong things and walked toward them. The mushrik did not just intellectually mistake a stone for a god — he *desired* it as a god. He *wanted* it to be true.

Hold that. We will need it.

---

### Element Four: *Kubkibū* — The Most Violent Verb in the Quran

#### The Concept Before the Word

Some Arabic verbs are constructed to imitate the action they describe. The sound of the word does what the word names. We call this *iconic* phonology, and the Quran uses it with surgical precision.

Think about how English does this. The word "tumble" has two unstable syllables that almost roll. "Crash" ends in a sharp percussive consonant. The mouth is doing what the word says.

Arabic has a special form for this. When you take a triliteral root and **double it** — repeat the consonants — you create a verb that **enacts the action through repetition**. Falling, then falling again. Rolling, then rolling again. The repeated consonants carry the sense of *re-doing*.

#### The Word

*Kubkibū* (كُبْكِبُوا). The root is *k-b-b* (ك ب ب). To upend something, to overturn it face-down, to throw it on its face.

But that is *kabba* — Form I. What Allah uses here is the **reduplicated quadriliteral**: *kab-ka-ba*. The *k-b* is doubled. The verb has been built so that **the action repeats inside the word itself**.

Lisān al-ʿArab is explicit about what *kubkiba* means: not just to throw down — but to throw down, **and then have them roll**, **and then fall on their faces again**, **and again**, **tumbling**. The doubled consonants force you to feel the falling-and-falling.

Listen to it. *Kub-ki-bū*. *Kub.* *Ki.* *Bū.* It is not a clean verb. It is a stumbling sound. It trips on itself.

And the verb is in the passive. They are not falling. **They are being thrown** — and the throwing is so violent that they tumble *over and over*.

[PAUSE]

Now connect what we have built.

The Garden is *uzlifat* — escorted forward.
The Fire is *burrizat* — exposed.
The ghāwūn are *kubkibū* — hurled headlong, again and again, into what they spent a lifetime not facing.

The verbs themselves enact the destinies. Garden: ushered. Fire: uncovered. The lost ones: thrown into what they refused to reckon with.

The sonic texture of *kubkibū* is so percussive, so unstable, that even hearing it without translation produces unease. That is not accidental. The mouth is doing what the verse describes.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### The Bridge

Three verbs. One for the saved, one for the Fire's appearing, one for the fall.

Each verb is doing what the rest of the language cannot. *Uzlifat* refuses to make the muttaqīn climb. *Burrizat* refuses to leave the Fire concealed. *Kubkibū* refuses to let the fall be dignified.

The grammar of the passage is itself the theology of the Day. A grammar of asymmetry. A grammar that says: *what people do not realize is that the Garden moves toward you, and the Fire is uncovered, and the fall — when it comes — is not a step. It is a tumbling*.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Question That Is Not About the Idols

You have already lived, in some form, with this question.

*Where is what you used to worship?*

We tend to hear it as a question about idols. About people who bowed before stone statues in seventh-century Arabia. And because we do not bow before stone statues, we imagine the question is not really directed at us — that it belongs to a particular historical moment, a particular kind of ignorance.

That reading makes the question safe. It should not be safe.

The classical commentators, without exception, read this as *tawbīkh* — a word that means reproach, rebuke, the kind of question that is not asked to receive information but to make a person confront what they already know. Al-Muyassar is explicit: the question is addressed to them reproachfully. Allah is not searching for the idols. He knows where they are. The question is put to the ghāwīn so that *they* must answer it — so that on this day, in front of the Fire that is now uncovered before them, they must account for what they gave their lives to.

And notice the specific shape of what they claimed. Al-Muyassar fills in what the question assumes: they used to worship these things claiming that they would intercede. That is the operative word. *Intercede*. They were not merely attached to the idols as objects of admiration. They were *counting on them*. The whole architecture of their spiritual life rested on an assumption: when the time comes, these are the ones who will speak for me. These are the ones I have built relationship with. These are my intercessors.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the question again. *Ayna mā kuntum taʿbudūn min dūni'llāh?* — Where is what you used to worship besides Allah?

And then the follow-up — sharper than the first: *Hal yanṣurūnakum aw yantaṣirūn?* — Can they help you? Or even: can they help *themselves*?

That second option is devastating. The question does not stop at "can they rescue you?" It descends to the minimum possible standard: can they rescue even *themselves*? Can they do for themselves the thing you were counting on them to do for you?

And the answer — implicit, not stated, because it does not need to be stated — is no. They cannot. They could not then. They cannot now. What the ghāwīn placed their ultimate trust in could not, in the end, do anything at all.

This is not primarily a polemic against stone statues. It is a question about the structure of trust. It is asking: what did you build your confidence on? What did you assume would be there when the moment of exposure came? And where is it now?

The question is not asked of people in 7th-century Mecca alone. It is asked of everyone who has ever cultivated the wrong loyalty as their ultimate hedge. Everyone who said, consciously or not: *this — this wealth, this family, this position, this religious routine performed without a living heart — this will carry me*. And who will discover, in the moment when the Fire is *burrizat* and can no longer be avoided, that what they built their confidence on was never in a position to help them.

---

### Theme Two: What the Asymmetry Is Teaching

The passage is built on asymmetry. The muttaqīn and the ghāwīn receive different verbs, different experiences, different final realities. But the asymmetry is not arbitrary. It is the disclosure of something that was always true, even in this world, even when it was invisible.

The muttaqīn were people who feared Allah's punishment in the afterlife through obedience in this world — al-Tabari's definition is precise. They were not simply people who *believed* the right things. They were people who let that belief shape the direction of their lives. They moved toward what mattered. The Garden moves toward them: it is the completion of a vector that began in them here.

The ghāwīn were people shaped by *ghawāyah* — by disordered desire, by the pull of what does not deserve ultimate loyalty. They moved away from what mattered. The Fire is uncovered before them: not brought toward them the way the Garden moves toward the muttaqīn, but simply *exposed* — brought out of the concealment that had, for decades, allowed them to not quite face it.

The asymmetry of the verbs mirrors the asymmetry of how each group lived. This is what makes Ibrahim's vision so devastating as a prayer. He is not telling his people that on the Day, things will be different from how they are now. He is telling them that on the Day, **things will be exactly what they already are** — only visible. The orientation you live with in this world is the orientation that meets you in the next. The Garden comes toward those who were coming toward Allah. The Fire is uncovered for those who spent their lives not uncovering it on their own terms.

The Day does not rewrite people. It reveals them.

[PAUSE]

And this is why Ibrahim's prayer carries such urgency. He is not describing a distant event. He is showing his people the logic that is already operating. The question is not what will happen on the Day of Judgment. The question is what you are becoming *now*, in the choices you make about what your heart moves toward.

---

### Theme Three: *Ajmaʿūn* — Not One Spared

The passage closes with a line that could have ended at ayah 94.

*Fa-kubkibū fīhā hum wal-ghāwūn* — they are hurled into it, along with those who went astray. That would have been complete.

But then ayah 95 comes: *wa junūdu Iblīsa ajmaʿūn* — and the legions of Iblis, all together.

*Ajmaʿūn*. All. Without exception. Not some. Not most. *All*.

This is a word the Quran uses deliberately when it wants to leave no room for escape, no category of exception, no one who slips through. Every soldier of Iblis. Every one of the forces that whispered, that decorated the wrong paths, that made the idols seem worth trusting, that made ghawāyah feel like wisdom — every one of them goes in.

The word recalls Iblis's own declaration earlier in the Quran — *la-ughriwannahum ajmaʿīn* — "I will lead them astray, all of them." He made a comprehensive claim. Ayah 95 answers it with a comprehensive end. His junūd — the very army he recruited — are hurled in *ajmaʿūn*. All together.

And there is something else worth holding. The ghāwīn and the junūd Iblīs are thrown in together — *hum wal-ghāwūn wa junūdu Iblīsa*. The three groups share a single verb. The people who chose the wrong attachments, the forces that cultivated those attachments in them, and the army that executed the long campaign of misdirection — they all meet in the same place. The one who was deceived and the one who deceived: *kubkibū fīhā*, all of them, together.

Iblis told Adam's children he was their friend. He is with them at the end. But not as a friend. As a companion in the fall.

[PAUSE — long pause]

## CLOSING

Ibrahim, peace be upon him, is still praying.

These six ayahs are not a theological treatise. They are a father's voice — the voice of someone who loved his people enough to show them the thing most humans spend their lives looking away from. He stood in front of them and said: *let me show you where this goes. Let me show you what the verbs are. The Garden comes toward those who move toward Allah. The Fire is uncovered for those who lived as if it would stay hidden. The fall, when it comes, is not a fall — it is a tumbling, again and again, with those who led you away.*

And between those two destinies — between the Garden escorted forward in its adornment and the Fire brought out of concealment — is this life. This prayer. This moment.

The muttaqīn are defined not by their freedom from failure but by the direction of their movement. They feared Allah through obedience in this world. They preferred what is real over what the world was offering. They built their trust in the only place that could hold it.

The question is not primarily about idols. It is about what you are counting on.

*Where is what you used to worship besides Allah — can they help you, or help themselves?*

There is still time for the answer to that question to be everything.
