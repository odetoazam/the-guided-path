# The One Who Hears, The One Who Knows
## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayah 220

> [ayah:26:220] إِنَّهُۥ هُوَ ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ
>
> *Innahu huwa as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm*
>
> **"Indeed, He — He alone — is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

To understand why these four Arabic words land the way they do, you have to feel what came right before them.

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is long. It is a procession. Messenger after messenger walks across its pages — Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb. Each one stands before his people. Each one says, in some variation of the same words: *I am for you a trustworthy messenger, so fear Allah and obey me*. And each one is rejected. The pattern repeats so many times the surah feels like a hammer striking the same nail.

Then the surah turns. Toward its end, it makes a bold claim about where this Quran came from. The shayāṭīn, the surah says (26:210–212), could not have brought it down — they are *maʿzūlūn*, barred from even hearing the heavenly transmission. The Quran descended through the trustworthy spirit upon the heart of the Messenger ﷺ. Not a whisper from below. A revelation from above.

This is the surah's own defense of itself. And it matters, because people were saying otherwise. The Prophet ﷺ was being told he was a poet, a madman, that his words came from a jinn. The surah does not look away from this accusation. It names it and seals the door against it. The chain of transmission is clean.

And then comes something still more intimate. Not an argument — a command that changes the texture of the whole surah:

> *"And rely upon the Mighty, the Merciful — the One who sees you when you stand [in prayer], and your movement among those who prostrate."* (26:217–219)

**Stand with that for a moment.**

The Prophet ﷺ is being told — at the end of a surah cataloguing the loneliness of every messenger, the rejection by every nation — *you are seen*. When you rise in the dark to pray. When you move through the rows of those bowing. When the night convinces you no one notices and no one listens — Allah sees you. Allah hears you.

And then comes our ayah:

> *Innahu huwa as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm.*
>
> *Indeed, He — He alone — is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.*

The surah will go on after this — in the verses that follow (26:221–226), it will describe the shayāṭīn who do descend on some: the sinful liars who lend their ears, who trade in the counterfeit. But our ayah is the pivot. It stands between the pure descent of revelation and that world of whispered self-deception, anchoring both by naming the One who hears everything and knows everything — and is not confused by either.

### The hook

Four words. Two names. A pronoun in the middle that, in Arabic, does something the English translation cannot quite show.

Why these two names — *as-Samīʿ*, *al-ʿAlīm* — to close a surah about whispers and rejection and night prayer? Why not *al-Qādir* (the Powerful)? Why not *an-Naṣīr* (the Helper)? Why not *al-ʿAzīz al-Raḥīm*, which Allah just used three ayahs ago?

And what is that little word *huwa* — "He" — doing sitting between *innahu* and *as-Samīʿu*? In Arabic, you do not need it. The sentence is grammatically complete without it. So why is it there?

These four words are not a closing formula. They are a precision instrument. Allah, at the end of a long surah, has chosen exactly these names — and exactly this construction — for a reason.

Let's earn the right to see what they are doing.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The pronoun that does not need to be there

Before we open the Arabic, let's notice something about how language works in any tongue.

Imagine you are sitting in a room and someone walks in and asks, "Who left the door open?" And someone in the room answers — but instead of saying *"He left it open,"* they say, *"It was him. He's the one."* You feel the difference. The second answer is not delivering information. It is **excluding everyone else**. It is saying: *not anyone else. Him.*

Arabic has a precise grammatical tool for this. It is called the **pronoun of separation** — *ḍamīr al-faṣl* — and you are looking at one right now.

Look at the structure of the ayah:

> إِنَّهُۥ — *innahu* — *Indeed, He...*
>
> هُوَ — *huwa* — *He...*
>
> ٱلسَّمِيعُ ٱلْعَلِيمُ — *as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm* — *the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing*

The *hu* attached to *inna* already means "He." The sentence could have ended *"innahu as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm"* — *"Indeed, He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing"* — and it would have been complete, beautiful, grammatical Arabic.

But Allah inserted *huwa* — *He* — a second time. A free-standing pronoun where one was not required.

In Arabic grammar, this is not a stylistic flourish. It is a documented construction whose function is **restriction** — limiting the predicate to the subject. *He, and no one else.* Classical grammarians call it *al-qaṣr* — confinement, restriction.

So a more honest English rendering would be:

> *Indeed, it is He — He alone — who is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.*

[PAUSE]

Now sit with what this does in the context of the surah.

The whole passage has been about the source of revelation. About shayāṭīn being barred from the heavenly transmission. About a Prophet ﷺ being mocked, accused, told the Quran came from somewhere else. The whispered claim is always: *someone else hears you. Someone else knows. Someone else has access to the unseen.*

And Allah's answer is not *"I also hear and know."*

His answer is: **He alone. Him, and no one else.**

The *huwa* is a door slamming shut on every alternative. Every false god. Every fortune-teller. Every whispered conspiracy that says *the unseen is being trafficked through other channels*. The pronoun that did not need to be there is there to say: *the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing — that title belongs to one Being. Stop looking elsewhere.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

Notice what this means for you tonight. When you whisper a du'a so quietly your own lips barely move — there is not a panel of cosmic listeners deciding which whispers to forward upward. There is one Hearer. When a thought passes through your mind that no one will ever know about — there is not a committee of knowers debating whether to file it. There is one Knower.

The *huwa* removes every middleman. Every intermediary. Every "maybe someone is listening, maybe not." It restricts the entire economy of unseen attention to a single Being.

This is what the *pronoun of separation* does. It is a grammatical guillotine for partners.

### Element Two: *as-Samīʿ* — and why this name, here

The first of the two names: **as-Samīʿ** — the All-Hearing.

The root س-م-ع (*s-m-ʿ*) is documented in the classical lexicons as carrying meanings that fan out from the basic act of perceiving sound. It can mean to hear. To listen attentively. To respond to what is heard. The form *Samīʿ* is on the morphological pattern *Faʿīl* — a pattern that, in Arabic, signals **intensity, permanence, completeness**. Not "one who heard" or "one who hears occasionally" — but **the one whose hearing is essential, total, uninterrupted**.

Now teach yourself to ask: *why this name, here?*

Al-Tabari, in his commentary on this specific ayah, is precise: *as-Samīʿ* here refers to Allah hearing the Prophet's ﷺ recitation and dhikr in his prayer — *"السميع تلاوتك يا محمد... في صلاتك ما تتلو وتذكر"*. And al-Muyassar maps it with the same specificity: *"السميع لتلاوتك وذكرك"* — the All-Hearing of your recitation and your remembrance. The primary frame the classical commentators give us is the prayer. Allah is naming Himself with the name that corresponds precisely to what the Prophet ﷺ is doing in those night hours: **reciting, remembering, calling out**.

[PAUSE]

This is not incidental. Look at what immediately precedes our ayah. Verse 218:

> *"The One who sees you when you stand [in prayer]."*

Allah described Himself just two ayahs ago as *the One who sees* — *yarāka*, from the root ر-أ-ي (*r-ʾ-y*), seeing. So we already have **sight** named.

Why, then, when He gives Himself a name in this final ayah, does He not say *al-Baṣīr* — the All-Seeing?

Because sight covers what is visible — the posture, the bowing, the raised hands. The name missing from verse 218 is the one that covers the sound of prayer: the recited words, the du'a breathed quietly in the dark, the dhikr said between postures. *As-Samīʿ* completes what *yarāka* began. Together they encompass the entire act of prayer: seen in its movements, heard in its words. The two verses form a chiasm — sight named in 218, hearing named in 220 — with the reliance command of 219 as the hinge between them.

And because the surah's opening argument has been that the Quran did not descend through shayāṭīn who are barred from the heavenly transmission — the closing name *as-Samīʿ* is the surah's seal. The Prophet's ﷺ recitation does not need an intermediary channel. It rises directly to *as-Samīʿ*, the One whose hearing was never disrupted.

Al-Tabari closes his commentary on this passage with a direct exhortation: *"فرتل فيها القرآن، وأقم حدودها، فإنك بمرأى من ربك ومسمع"* — recite the Quran in it, establish its prescribed elements, for you are within sight and hearing of your Lord. The commentator is saying: knowing what these two names mean, the only rational response is to pray with everything you have.

[PAUSE]

Every du'a whispered so quietly no one in the room could catch the words. Every slander spoken about you when you were not present. Every private prayer breathed into darkness without a human witness. *As-Samīʿ* hears all of it — and hears each differently. The prayer is received. The slander is recorded. The *Samīʿ* who hears recitation in prayer is the same *Samīʿ* who hears the sinful liar who lends his ear. The attribute is not partial. It is total.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *al-ʿAlīm* — and the architecture of the pair

The second name: **al-ʿAlīm** — the All-Knowing.

The root ع-ل-م (*ʿ-l-m*) is documented across the classical lexicons as encompassing knowledge of a thing as it truly is — knowledge that is certain, comprehensive, not dependent on inference. *ʿAlīm*, again on the *Faʿīl* pattern, signals knowledge that is essential and total.

Now here is where you have to slow down. Because Allah did not say *as-Samīʿ* alone. He paired it with *al-ʿAlīm*. And Quranic name-pairings are never decorative.

Al-Muyassar maps this pair with exceptional precision: *"السميع لتلاوتك وذكرك، العليم بنيتك وعملك"* — the All-Hearing of your **recitation and remembrance**; the All-Knowing of your **intention and your action**. The two names divide the prayer's total reality between them. *Samīʿ* takes the audible surface — the words of recitation, the dhikr that leaves the lips. *ʿAlīm* takes the interior — the niyyah that no one else can see, and the ʿamal in its full depth. Together they constitute complete divine witnessing of everything a worshipper does and means.

Ask: *what does hearing alone fail to do, that knowing completes?*

Hearing catches sound. Words. The audible surface of a conversation. But hearing alone cannot judge a heart. Two people can say identical words and mean entirely opposite things. A man can say *"I love you"* as a manipulation. A man can say *"I love you"* as a covenant. The sound waves are the same. The reality behind them is not.

*As-Samīʿ* hears the words.
*Al-ʿAlīm* knows what was meant.

[PAUSE]

This is why the names are paired. Hearing without knowing would catch the slander but miss the malice. Knowing without hearing would grasp the heart but miss the act. Allah names Himself with both — because to fully witness a creature, you need both **the surface and the depth**.

And see what this means for the Prophet ﷺ's situation. The slanderers say words. The words sound, in some ears, plausible. *Maybe he really is a poet. Maybe the Quran really is from a jinn.* The audible surface of their speech can pass for sincere. *As-Samīʿ* hears the words. But *al-ʿAlīm* knows that the speaker himself does not believe what he is saying. The words are insincere, and Allah knows the insincerity better than the speaker does.

The pair is not redundancy. It is **forensic completeness**. Every act has an audible dimension and a knowable dimension. Allah witnesses both. There is no escape into the gap between what was said and what was meant — because there is no gap, in the witnessing.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari extends this further still. *Al-ʿAlīm* here, he notes, encompasses not only what the Prophet ﷺ himself does in prayer but *"ما يعمل فيها من يتقلب فيها معك مؤتما بك"* — what is done by those who move through prayer following him, praying behind him. The knowledge is not narrow. It reaches every shoulder in every row. Every person who stands behind the imam, whose heart is present or wandering, whose reverence is genuine or performed — *al-ʿAlīm* knows them all, in this prayer, behind this Prophet, in this night. The divine attention is not concentrated on one worshipper and diffuse for the rest. It is complete for each.

[PAUSE — major pause]

And now read the pair back into the night-prayer image of verse 218.

The Prophet ﷺ, standing alone in the night.

What does he do in prayer? He **says** things — Quran, du'a, dhikr — and he **means** things. The act has a hearable layer and a knowable layer. The whisperers who accuse him speak words that have a hearable layer and a knowable layer. Both layers are recorded. The night prayer is fully credited — every word of recitation heard, every intention known. The slander is fully indicted — every word heard, every insincerity known.

The pair *as-Samīʿ al-ʿAlīm* is doing simultaneous work in two directions. **Reassuring the worshipper that nothing of his hidden devotion is lost. Warning the slanderer that nothing of his hidden malice is hidden.**

That is why these two names, and not others, close this passage.

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. The pronoun *huwa* that did not need to be there — and what its presence excludes. The name *as-Samīʿ* — and why hearing, not sight, is the name that covers the Prophet's ﷺ recitation in the night and seals the surah's argument about revelation's uncorrupted descent. The pairing of *as-Samīʿ* with *al-ʿAlīm* — and how the two together, as the classical commentators specify, cover recitation and intention, surface and depth, the imam and every person praying behind him.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Witnessed Worshipper in a Whispered World

Begin with where you already live.

You have probably had this experience: you do something good. Quietly. Without telling anyone. You wake up before fajr and pray longer than usual. You give some money you cannot afford to give. You forgive someone who never apologized. You bite back a sharp response that would have been satisfying to deliver.

And no one notices.

In fact — sometimes the opposite. The person you forgave keeps treating you the way they did before. The cause you donated to never sends a thank-you. The early prayer leaves you tired and a little less productive than your colleagues. The world does not register your invisible act. And then a small voice — let's name it for what it is — whispers: *what was the point?*

That voice is older than you. It was whispering at the foot of the Prophet's ﷺ bed every night he stood in prayer while Quraysh mocked him in the morning.

[PAUSE]

The surah's continuation, just after our ayah (26:221–226), names this dynamic: shayāṭīn descend on those who have already chosen to deal in the counterfeit — sinful liars who lend their ears to what they know is false. The mechanism is not brute force. It is a gradual misallocation of audience. The whisper convinces you the audience that matters is the human one. The visible one. The one that can be impressed, that can betray, that can fail to notice.

And then comes our ayah — this pivot between the clean descent of revelation and the world of whispered self-deception — naming the one Audience whose attention has never wavered.

*Innahu huwa as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm.*

Everything you recite in the dark, He hears. Everything you mean in the dark, He knows. The ayah does not say "He will know," as a future promise. The *Samīʿ* and *ʿAlīm* are not future tense. They name His essential, permanent nature. He has always been hearing. He has always been knowing. Your prayer tonight did not newly attract His attention. His attention is His attribute. It cannot be withdrawn.

[PAUSE]

This reframes everything about the economy of hidden worship.

We sometimes speak about invisible deeds as if they are investments — we do the good act now, and eventually the hidden investment will be noticed and rewarded. That framing, while not wrong, subtly keeps the human audience as the measure. *Eventually someone will see. Eventually it will count.* And it creates a dangerous psychological dependence: if the reward never comes, was the act real?

*As-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm* breaks this frame. The act was witnessed the moment it happened. The recitation was heard the moment it left your lips. The intention was known before it reached your lips. There is no gap between the act and its witnessing, no lag between the deed and its being received. The invisible act is the most directly witnessed act — because it bypasses every human intermediary and lands immediately before *al-ʿAlīm*.

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is what made the Prophet ﷺ able to return to the rows of those prostrating after a night of solitary prayer. Not a theory about future reward. A lived certainty about present witnessing. *Fīnaka bimurāy rabbika wa masmūʿ* — within sight and hearing of your Lord right now, in this prayer, with this recitation.

### Theme Two: What the Pairing Protects Against

There is a second dimension to this name-pair, which operates across the whole Quran.

The Quran uses *as-Samīʿ al-ʿAlīm* together in a specific cluster of contexts. It appears when Ibrahim ﷺ finishes raising the foundations of the Kaaba and makes du'a — *"Rabbanā taqabbal minnā, innaka anta as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm"* (2:127). It appears when Maryam's mother dedicates what is in her womb — *"Rabbī innī nadharta laka... wa innaka anta as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm"* (3:35). It appears in moments of deep personal petition, private consecration, sincere vow.

What do these moments have in common? In every one of them, a human being is saying something to Allah that no one else in the room necessarily heard or understood. A du'a. A nadhr. A private offering. And in every one of them, the closing names are *as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm*.

[PAUSE]

The pairing seems to answer a specific anxiety: *did my private act reach anyone?* Did my whispered du'a arrive? Did my silent vow count? The answer, recurring across surah after surah, is: the One who hears everything heard it, and the One who knows everything knows exactly what you meant by it.

In our passage, this same anxiety has been stirred — not by the Prophet's ﷺ private petition alone, but by the public accusation against his private faithfulness. The night prayer that no one witnessed except Allah. The commitment to the mission that no one else fully understood. And the names that close this passage are precisely the names that appear whenever a believer's private act needs its private audience confirmed.

He heard the recitation. He knows the intention. The private is never private from Him.

[PAUSE — major pause]

---

### Theme Three: The Asymmetry That Disappears

Now go back to the image of the slanderer and the Prophet ﷺ.

The slanderer has an advantage that feels very real in the world: **audience**. When he speaks, people hear. When he spreads a lie, it travels through social networks, through market conversations, through passed messages. The Prophet ﷺ, praying alone in the night, is speaking to an audience of — apparently — one. The slanderer's voice is loud and horizontal. The Prophet's ﷺ prayer is quiet and vertical.

The social logic says the slanderer wins this particular battle. He has reach; the Prophet has silence.

[PAUSE]

*As-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm* collapses this asymmetry entirely.

The slanderer's words are heard — by *as-Samīʿ*. His intentions are known — by *al-ʿAlīm*. The horizontal reach of his lies does not add coverage; it adds evidence. Every person he tells, every exaggeration he weaves in, every malicious addition — heard and known. What felt like social capital is being recorded in full.

And the Prophet's ﷺ quiet prayer is heard by the same *as-Samīʿ*. His sincere intention is known by the same *al-ʿAlīm*. The vertical reach of that prayer goes to the one Audience whose verdict operates in reality. It lands, complete and credited, before the One who cannot be deceived by surface.

The asymmetry has not just been reduced. It has been inverted. The prayer with no human audience is the act most precisely witnessed. The slander with the widest human audience is the act most comprehensively indicted.

[PAUSE]

This is not a comfortable thought for those of us who have ever calculated the effect of our good deeds by how many people saw them. Or measured the danger of a wrong done to us by how few people knew about it. The ayah does not leave room for that calculation. The only Audience that processes both sides fully — hears every word, knows every intention, weighs the surface and the depth without error — is *as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm*.

And He misses nothing.

## CLOSING

### What these four words ask of you

We have been inside this ayah for a long time. Let's surface.

You started with a surah that is a procession of rejected messengers. You found at its end a command to rely — on the Mighty, the Merciful — because He sees you in prayer, your standing and your movement among those who bow. And then the surah closes the passage with a four-word sentence that functions as a seal.

The *huwa* that did not need to be there tells you: there is only one Hearer and Knower. The *as-Samīʿ* tells you: the words of your prayer — every recitation, every dhikr — land before the One whose hearing encompasses them. The *al-ʿAlīm* tells you: the intention behind those words — the sincere niyyah, the genuine ʿamal — is known by the One who cannot be fooled by performance. And this is true not only of you, but of every person praying behind any imam in any row in any night on earth.

[PAUSE]

What does this ask of you?

It asks, first, a kind of **relief**. You do not need to ensure that your invisible worship eventually becomes visible. It is already witnessed. The recitation you did in the night is already heard. The du'a you breathed into your pillow is already received. The anxiety of the unseen act can dissolve — not because it will eventually be recognized by the right people, but because it has already been heard by *as-Samīʿ* and known by *al-ʿAlīm*.

[PAUSE]

It asks, second, a kind of **honesty**. Because the same complete witnessing that credits the prayer indicts the performance. The pairing does not only comfort the sincere worshipper; it strips the hypocrite of every refuge. What you meant is known. What you performed without meaning is also known. The *ʿAlīm* knows the difference between the person whose heart is present in prayer and the person who is physically in the row but inwardly elsewhere. There is no benefit of the doubt from a Being whose knowledge has no gaps.

[PAUSE]

And it asks, third — perhaps most surprisingly — a kind of **courage**. Because if *as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm* is the true Audience, and His verdict is the one that operates in reality, then the social verdict is derivative. The crowd's assessment of your prayer, your devotion, your character — accurate or wildly wrong — is not the final accounting. The slander that runs through a hundred mouths without your defense does not define you before the One who hears every word and knows every intention. You can face the whisper with the calm of someone who knows which Audience matters.

[PAUSE — major pause]

The surah closes, just beyond our ayah, with a reminder about poets — those who wander in every valley and say what they do not do (26:225–226). Except those who believe and do righteous deeds and remember Allah often, and are helped when wronged (26:227). The contrast is between speech uncoupled from reality and speech rooted in it. The Prophet ﷺ is not a poet in the surah's sense. He says what he does. He prays in the night what he preaches in the day. He recites the Quran with the same lips that make du'a in the dark.

And every word — heard by *as-Samīʿ*.

Every intention — known by *al-ʿAlīm*.

*Innahu huwa as-Samīʿu al-ʿAlīm.*

Indeed, He — He alone.
