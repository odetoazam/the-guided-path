# The Losing Word of the Winners: When the Staff Drops

---

## Introduction — The Day of Adornment

Picture the largest gathering Egypt could assemble. This is not a private audience in a palace hall. Pharaoh has declared a public festival — the sources call it a *day of adornment*, a day when the whole population is summoned out into the open, in the full light of forenoon, so that everyone can watch. That detail matters. Pharaoh is not trying to defeat Musa quietly. He is staging a spectacle. He wants a crowd, because a crowd is where reputations are made and unmade, and Pharaoh's entire authority rests on reputation — on being *seen* as unopposable. The early narrations even try to fix the place; al-Tabari records a report locating the appointed gathering at Alexandria — a chosen ground, a summoned nation, nothing left to chance.

So feel the composition of that crowd. There are the ordinary Egyptians, and the ayah just before ours has already told us what they are hoping for: *la'allanā nattabi'u s-saḥarata in kānū humu l-ghālibīn* — "so that we might follow the magicians, **if it is they who prevail**." Read that line carefully, because it is easy to hear it as neutral curiosity — a crowd waiting to see who wins before picking a side. But the classical commentators heard something sharper. Al-Tabari notes that the *la'alla* here carries the force of *kay* — *in order that* — and that these were Pharaoh's own people, already standing on Pharaoh's religion. They did not arrive undecided. They arrived *hoping their own side would win*, so that — as al-Muyassar puts it — they could "remain firm on the religion they already held." Their wish was never *who is telling the truth?* It was *let the magicians prevail, so we never have to change.* That is the honesty this ayah exposes: not open-minded spectators, but a crowd quietly praying that the truth loses, so their lives can stay exactly as they are.

And into that arena come the magicians. These are not street performers. In Egypt, the magician-priests were the intellectual elite, the technicians of the sacred, the men whose illusions propped up the whole theology of the god-king. They arrive as the home team, carrying the confidence of professionals who have never lost — walking into a stadium where the crowd is already leaning their way and the king himself has summoned them as his champions.

And opposite all of that stands one man with his brother, and a stick.

Now, here is why this scene sits exactly here in the Qur'an. Surah Ash-Shu'ara is a Meccan surah, revealed to console a Prophet ﷺ who was standing almost precisely where Musa stands — outnumbered, mocked, facing an establishment that held all the visible power and had already decided he would lose. The surah moves through prophet after prophet, and it keeps striking the same two notes at the end of each story, like a bell: *and most of them were not believers* — and then — *and indeed your Lord, He is the Mighty, the Merciful.* Hold onto that second phrase. **The Mighty.** *Al-'Azīz.* The surah keeps telling you who actually owns might, story after story. Keep that word in your pocket. We are going to need it.

So the strand this passage lives inside is a strand about a lonely truth surrounded by a confident falsehood — and what happens when they finally meet in the open.

[PAUSE]

And here is the thing I cannot stop noticing, the thing I want us to chase for the next hour. In these six verses, one particular word gets shouted three separate times — the word for *winner*. The crowd says it. The magicians say it. And at the climax, the magicians swear it as an oath. Three times, the word for victory rings out across that stadium.

Not once does it come from the mouth of Musa.

The man who is about to win never says the word *win*. Sit with that. Because by the time we understand why, we will understand something about truth, and about our own hearts, that this scene was built to teach.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *al-ghālibīn* — the word the winners keep shouting

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah, because it will change what you hear.

In Arabic there is a grammatical shape called the *active participle* — the *ism al-fā'il*. English has a pale version of it: from the verb "to win," we get "winner." But the Arabic participle carries something English "winner" does not. It tends to name someone by a **settled, standing quality** — not "the person who happened to win once," but "the one who is, by description, a victor." When you call someone *al-ghālib*, you are pinning a label to their chest. You are describing what they *are*.

Now here is the everyday version. Think about the difference between a person who says "I won that game last night" and a person who introduces himself at a party as "a winner." The first is reporting an event. The second is making a *claim about his identity* — and the moment he says it, some part of you quietly doubts him. Real winners rarely need the noun. The noun is what you reach for when you need the arena to believe something about you.

Now watch the ayah.

The root here is *gh-l-b* — *ghalaba*, to overcome, to overpower, to prevail. And the Qur'an lets us hear its participle three times in six verses.

First, the crowd: *in kānū humu **l-ghālibīn*** — "if they turn out to be **the victors**" (v. 40). The people frame the entire event as a victory-contest before it has even begun.

Then the magicians, walking in, before they have lifted a finger: *a-inna lanā la-ajran in kunnā naḥnu **l-ghālibīn*** — "is there a reward for us, if we are **the victors**?" (v. 41). They have not competed yet. They are already wearing the label.

And then the third time — and this is the one that should make the hair on your arms stand up. At the peak of the scene, ropes and staffs flying from their hands, they *swear it*: *innā la-naḥnu **l-ghālibūn*** — "it is **we**, surely **we**, who are **the victors**!" (v. 44). Look at how much reinforcement is stacked into that one line. *Innā* — "indeed we." Then *la* — the particle of emphasis, "surely." Then *naḥnu* — "we," the pronoun repeated when the verb already carried it, which in Arabic is like slamming your fist on the table. Then the label itself. Four layers of insistence pressed onto one claim.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel. That is not the grammar of certainty. **That is the grammar of a man convincing himself.** You do not need four braces to hold up a wall that is actually standing. The sheer volume of the claim is the tell. The louder the *innā la-naḥnu*, the emptier the space behind it.

And now — read the absence. This is where the ayah does its deepest work, in what is *not* said.

Go looking for that word in the mouth of Musa. Search verse 43. He is given the floor. He is the one who will, within seconds, actually prevail. And what does he say?

*Alqū mā antum mulqūn.* "Throw down whatever you are going to throw."

That is all. He does not answer their boast with a boast. He does not say "we will be the victors." He does not claim the word *ghālib* for himself even once — not before, not during, not after. The one person in this entire scene for whom the label would actually be *true* is the one person who never reaches for it.

Notice what that silence is doing. Everyone else is fighting over a title — *who gets to be called the winner.* Musa is not in that fight, because Musa is not trying to win a contest. He is delivering a truth. And truth does not need to announce that it will prevail, any more than the sunrise needs to announce that it will beat the darkness. It simply arrives, and the darkness is already over.

So the first thing this passage teaches us, purely from one repeated word and one deafening silence: **the people who most need to say "I am winning" are usually the people who already sense, somewhere underneath, that they are not.** The word for victory becomes, in this scene, the surest marker of who is about to lose.

### Element Two: *alqū… fa-alqaw… fa-alqā… talqaf* — the throwing that gets swallowed

Now I want to slow all the way down and listen to the *sound* of these verses, because the Qur'an is doing something here with the physical noise of the words that a translation cannot carry across.

There is one root that runs through the climax like a heartbeat: *l-q-y* — to cast, to throw down, to fling. Listen to how many times it beats:

Musa says *alqū* — "throw!" (v. 43). He calls them *mulqūn* — "you who are throwing" (v. 43). Then *fa-alqaw* — "so they threw" (v. 44). Then *fa-alqā Mūsā* — "so Musa threw" (v. 45). Throw, throwers, they-threw, he-threw. The whole climax is a rain of casting. Everyone is hurling everything they have onto the ground.

And then — one word later — the Qur'an shifts a single consonant, and the entire scene turns.

*Fa-idhā hiya **talqaf**u mā ya'fikūn.*

*L-q-y* becomes *l-q-f.* Throwing becomes **swallowing.** *Talqaf* — from the root *l-q-f* — is not a gentle word. Say it out loud. Feel where it sits in your mouth: that hard *q* struck deep in the throat and then bitten off by the *f*. It is a word with teeth in it. It means to snatch something up greedily and gulp it down whole, the way a wave takes a footprint, the way a flame takes paper. It is the sound of something being *consumed*.

Do you hear the wordplay? Everything in this arena was *alqā* — cast down. And the one thing Musa cast down did not merely *land* among the others. It *talqaf* — it ate them. The near-identical sound of the two roots is the point: what they threw and what he threw looked, for one instant, like the same kind of act — two casts onto the same floor. And then one of them opened its mouth.

[PAUSE]

Now hold onto that and look at the very last two words, because there is a stunning absence buried in them.

*Talqafu mā **ya'fikūn*** — "it was swallowing up **what they were faking**." The verb *ya'fikūn* is from the root *a-f-k*. This is a heavy word. *Ifk* is the Qur'an's word for a manufactured lie, a fabrication, a slander deliberately assembled and dressed up as truth — the same root used elsewhere for the gravest false accusations. It does not mean an honest mistake. It means *counterfeit produced on purpose.*

Here is the absence — read it carefully. In our passage, the Qur'an never once tells us that the magicians' trick *worked.* In other places we are told their ropes seemed to writhe like snakes; here, in Ash-Shu'ara, that whole moment of illusion is simply… skipped. We go straight from "they threw their ropes and their staffs" to Musa's staff *already swallowing it.* The Qur'an does not give their magic a single frame of glory. It does not let us stand there and be impressed. The fabrication is devoured before we are even allowed to admire it.

Feel what that omission is telling you. **Falsehood, in the sight of Allah, has no standing worth narrating.** The scene refuses to dignify the illusion with description. From the vantage point the Qur'an is writing from, the ropes were never really snakes at all — they were *mā ya'fikūn*, "what they were faking," and a fake has no substance to lose. It just gets swallowed.

And here is where the physical image becomes the unseen reality it was always pointing at. This is not, at its heart, a story about a stick beating some rope. The staff swallowing the fabrication is a visible enactment of an invisible law: **truth does not argue with falsehood. It absorbs it.** Ibn Kathir frames this very encounter as the standing pattern between faith and disbelief — that the two never truly meet as equal contestants, because faith always prevails. He reaches for the Qur'an's own image for it: *bal naqdhifu bi-l-ḥaqqi 'ala l-bāṭili fa-yadmaghuhu fa-idhā huwa zāhiq* — "Rather, We hurl the truth against falsehood, and it crushes it, and at once it vanishes" (21:18). The staff is that verse made visible. Falsehood is not debated down; it is struck, and it is gone. When light enters a dark room, there is no struggle, no scoreboard — the darkness is simply *gone*, because darkness was never a thing in itself, only the absence of the light that has now arrived. That is *talqaf.* That is what it looks like when reality walks in.

The classical commentators sat with this word and each of them saw something real in it. Some emphasized the sheer *speed* and *totality* — *talqaf* means it left nothing, snatched it all, cleared the ground. Others emphasized that what was devoured was specifically *mā ya'fikūn*, the fraud as fraud, so that the destruction was not just of ropes but of the entire enterprise of deception. Layer those together and you get the full picture: a swallowing that was instant, complete, and aimed precisely at the lie. Not one snake left over. Not one illusion survived to be re-examined. The floor was clean.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: *bi-'izzati fir'awn* — the oath sworn on borrowed might

There is one more phrase I cannot leave, and it may be the sharpest blade in the whole passage. When the magicians make their final boast, listen to *what they swear by.*

*Wa-qālū **bi-'izzati fir'awn*** — "and they said: **by the might of Pharaoh!**" (v. 44).

The root is *'-z-z*. *'Izzah* is one of the great words of the Arabic language and of the Qur'an. It means might that is *unbreakable*, honor that cannot be diminished, a strength so absolute that it can never be overcome. From this exact root comes one of the most magnificent Names of Allah: *al-'Azīz* — The Utterly Invincible, The One whose might nothing can touch.

Now here is a general thing about oaths, so we feel the weight of the moment. When a human being swears *by* something, he is reaching for the most solid, most unshakeable thing he can name, and staking his words on it. You swear by what you believe cannot fail you. The object of your oath is a confession of what you think ultimate reality is made of.

And the magicians, in the highest and most decisive moment of their lives, reach for their bedrock — and their bedrock is *a man.* They swear *by the might of Pharaoh.* They anchor the entire weight of their certainty to the *'izzah* of a mortal who eats and sleeps and will one day drown in a sea.

[PAUSE]

Now bring back the word I told you to keep in your pocket. Remember the bell that tolls at the end of every story in this surah: *wa inna rabbaka la-huwa **l-'Azīz** ar-Raḥīm* — "and indeed your Lord, He is **the Mighty**, the Merciful." The surah keeps telling us, again and again, where real *'izzah* lives. It lives with the Lord of the worlds. It is His Name.

So catch what just happened. The magicians took a divine attribute — *'izzah*, invincible might, which belongs by right to *al-'Azīz* alone — peeled it off, and stuck it onto Pharaoh. They swore their most solemn oath on a *counterfeit* of the very thing this surah is built to point back to God. They are like a man betting his life savings on a currency he has not noticed is forged.

And what does the real *'izzah* do to the borrowed one? It does not debate it. *Fa-idhā hiya talqaf.* It swallows it. The oath "by the might of Pharaoh" and the fabrication it was defending go down the same throat, in the same instant. That is the answer of *al-'Azīz* to anyone who swears by a rival: not an argument, a swallowing.

So from three anchors — one repeated word, one shifted consonant, one misplaced oath — the same shape keeps emerging. Now let us stop asking what this reveals about *words*, and start asking what it reveals about *us.*

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The heart that asks for the fee before it will believe

Let me start nowhere near this ayah. Let me start with a feeling you already know.

You have signed a contract before. Maybe a job offer. And before you said yes, you did the reasonable thing: you turned to the page with the numbers. What's the salary. What's the bonus. What's the equity, the title, the upside. That is not a sin. That is how contracts work. Nobody puts their name on a dotted line without first asking, *what do I get?*

The trouble begins when that becomes the only voice we have — when the reflex we learned for contracts creeps into places where it quietly poisons everything. Watch it happen. "I'll be patient with my spouse *if* they change first." "I'll give to charity *once* my own situation is fully secure." "I'll pray properly *when* I start feeling something in it." "I'll commit to God *if* He first shows me the payoff." We turn the deepest relationships of our lives into pending transactions, and we stand at the counter with our arms crossed, waiting to see the reward before we will hand over the commitment.

Now walk back into the arena, because the magicians do this on a stage, in front of all Egypt, in the plainest words imaginable.

They walk in to face a Prophet of God. And their very first sentence — before the contest, before anything — is not "what is the truth here?" It is: *a-inna lanā la-ajran* — "is there really going to be **a fee** for us?" (v. 41). The word is *ajr*, root *a-j-r* — a wage, a worker's payment. Before they will throw a single rope, they want the price confirmed. Their belief is for hire, and they are checking the day-rate — and, as al-Muyassar notes, the *ajr* they were angling for was not only money but *jāh*: rank, standing, prestige. Hold onto that, because it tells us Pharaoh's next move is not a random sweetener — he is answering the deeper hunger they just revealed.

And Pharaoh, who understands transactional hearts because he owns so many of them, does not just say yes. He raises the offer beyond money: *na'am, wa-innakum idhan la-mina l-**muqarrabīn*** — "yes, and you will then be among **those brought near**" (v. 42). The word is *muqarrabīn*, root *q-r-b*, nearness — the inner circle, proximity to the throne. He dangles not just a wage but *closeness to power.* And you can feel the magicians' hearts leap, because that is the prize the transactional soul actually craves underneath the money: to be *near* the one who seems to hold all the strength.

[PAUSE]

Here is the reversal, and it is one of the most beautiful in the entire Qur'an — and it happens just after our passage closes, but it is the destination these very verses are driving toward, so let it land.

These same men — who walked in haggling over a fee, who lit up at the promise of nearness to Pharaoh — the instant the staff swallows the fabrication, they fall down in prostration and say: *āmannā bi-rabbi l-'ālamīn* — "we believe in the Lord of the worlds." The men who bargained for a wage suddenly give up everything, and Pharaoh threatens to cut off their hands and feet and crucify them, and they do not flinch. Where did the negotiation go? Where is the "what do I get" now?

It evaporated — because they finally saw something that cannot be negotiated over. You do not haggle with a sunrise. You do not ask the dawn for a fee before you admit it is light. The moment the truth became *undeniable*, the entire transactional apparatus of their hearts fell silent, and what was left was worship.

And look at the exquisite mercy in the wordplay of it. Pharaoh had offered them *nearness* — *muqarrabīn* — to *himself.* And they traded up. They walked away from being brought near to a drowning king, and toward being brought near to the Lord of the worlds — the true *qurb*, the nearness that the Qur'an elsewhere promises to *al-muqarrabūn*, the ones drawn close to God forever. They came for a wage. They left with their souls.

So here is the question this theme lays in front of you, and I want it to be uncomfortable. Tomorrow morning, when you stand for prayer — are you standing there as a worshipper, or as a negotiator waiting to see the return on your investment? When you are kind to someone who has wronged you, are you *giving*, or are you *lending at interest*, keeping a ledger of what you're owed back? The magicians teach us that a heart can spend its whole life at the bargaining counter — right up until the moment it sees the truth clearly enough that bargaining becomes absurd. **The tragedy is not that we bargain. The tragedy is if we wait for the staff to drop before we stop.**

The concrete change is small and brutal: pick the one relationship — with God, with a spouse, with a parent — where you are currently withholding commitment until you see your reward. And this week, give the commitment first. Throw your rope down without asking the fee. See what it does to your heart to move like Musa instead of like the magicians.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: What you've thrown your whole life behind

Look again at *what* the magicians actually threw.

*Fa-alqaw **ḥibālahum wa-'iṣiyyahum*** — "so they threw down **their ropes and their staffs**" (v. 44). Two words, easy to skim past. *Ḥibāl*, ropes; *'iṣiyy*, staffs. But stop and ask: whose ropes and staffs? *Theirs.* These are the tools of their trade. This is a magician's kit — the instruments they had trained on for years, the equipment that made them who they were, the whole material basis of their careers and their status and their livelihood. When they *alqaw ḥibālahum*, they are not throwing borrowed props. **They are throwing their entire professional lives onto the floor and betting all of it on Pharaoh's side.**

I want to sit in the modern version of this, because we all have a kit.

There is a thing we do where we quietly stake our whole identity on something and then can no longer see that we've done it. A man builds his entire sense of self on his job title, and when the company restructures, it is not his income that collapses, it is *him* — because he had thrown his ropes and staffs, his whole self, onto that one floor. A person pours everything into being admired online, and a bad month of numbers doesn't just disappoint them, it *unmakes* them, because that was the might they swore by. We all walk into the arena of our lives carrying a kit, and we fling it down behind some source of strength, and then we shout — with all the *innā la-naḥnu* we can muster — that this, *this*, is what will make us prevail.

And the loudness is the tell, exactly like it was in the arena. The magicians didn't just throw their tools. They threw them *and swore an oath at the top of their lungs.* "By the might of Pharaoh, it is we who will prevail!" That is a lot of noise for men who, minutes later, would be face-down on the ground. And I think we recognize that noise, because we make it too. The louder we insist that our chosen source of strength is unshakeable — the job, the reputation, the wealth, the person we've made into our whole world — often the more quietly terrified we are that it might not hold.

Now put Musa beside them one more time, and feel the contrast in your body.

They threw *everything* — ropes, staffs, careers, oaths, four layers of emphasis, the whole arena's expectation. Musa threw *one staff*, in silence. *Fa-alqā Mūsā 'aṣāhu.* And it was the single quiet thing on that floor that had anything real behind it.

Why? Not because Musa's stick was better wood. Because of *what stood behind the throw.* The magicians' ropes were backed by *bi-'izzati fir'awn* — the might of a man. Musa's staff was backed by the might of *al-'Azīz*, the One whose strength cannot be overcome. Same floor, same gesture of throwing — utterly different foundation. And when the two foundations met, one *talqaf*'d the other. The borrowed might was swallowed by the real one, instantly, completely, without a contest.

This is the question the theme presses into your chest. **What have you thrown your ropes and staffs behind?** When something shakes your life — the diagnosis, the layoff, the loss, the betrayal — what is the thing you discover you had been quietly leaning your whole weight on? Because whatever it is, if it is not *al-'Azīz*, then it is some version of *'izzati fir'awn*: a might that looked unbreakable right up until the floor opened and swallowed it. And the terrifying gift of a crisis is that it *shows* you what you'd sworn by, the way the dropping of the staff showed the magicians in an instant.

The magicians got the best possible ending, though, and don't miss why. When their false foundation was swallowed, they did not die with it. They *let go of it* and grabbed the real one. They stopped swearing by Pharaoh and prostrated to the Lord of the worlds — and became, in the space of a single breath, the strongest people in that entire stadium, the only ones willing to lose their hands and feet for the truth. **The swallowing was not their destruction. It was their liberation.** It pried their fingers off a rope that was going to fail them anyway, and left their hands free to hold onto something that never would.

So the concrete practice is a kind of controlled demolition you do on purpose, before life does it for you. Sit down this week and ask, honestly: *if this one thing were taken from me tomorrow, would I still know who I am?* Whatever answers that question with a "no" — that is a rope you've thrown behind Pharaoh. Name it. And begin, deliberately, to move your weight off of it and onto the One whose might does not drown.

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — the shape that proves the design

Now that we have climbed through it, stand back and look at the whole scene at once, because it was built with a symmetry that no accident could produce.

Watch how the passage is arranged around a center. It **opens** with a crowd talking about *the victors* — *al-ghālibīn* (v. 40). It **closes** with a staff *swallowing the fabrication* (v. 45). And notice the movement between those two poles: on the outer edges, everyone is *talking* — the crowd talks, the magicians talk and bargain, Pharaoh talks and promises, the magicians talk and swear. Words, words, words, victory-claims stacked on oaths stacked on emphasis. And then, at the very end, there are no more words. There is one action. Musa throws, and the thing *acts.*

The scene is shaped as a long crescendo of *speech about winning* that collapses, in its final verse, into a single silent *deed that wins.* Three times the word *ghālib* is shouted on the way up — and then the actual victory, when it comes, arrives without the word attached to it at all. The architecture itself is the argument: **the more a thing is proclaimed, the less it turns out to be real; and the realest thing in the story needed no proclamation.** The design is the proof. You could not build that by chance.

### Where the two paths meet

And now see that our two journeys — the linguistic and the thematic — were never two. They were one insight approached from two doors.

The word *ghālibīn* shouted by the losers, and the transactional heart asking for its fee — these are the same disease. Both are the soul that stands *outside* the truth, demanding terms: *guarantee me the win, confirm me the wage.* And the oath *bi-'izzati fir'awn*, and the ropes-and-staffs of a whole life thrown behind a mortal — these are the same mistake: anchoring your weight to a might that will be swallowed. Everything the losers do is *loud, transactional, and borrowed.* Everything Musa does is *quiet, free, and grounded in the Real.* The linguistics and the psychology were describing one man's posture the whole time.

We began in a stadium at the height of a crowd's excitement, everyone leaning toward whoever would win. We end on a clean floor with one staff and a silence, and a truth that never had to raise its voice. That is the arc: from the noise of the winners to the stillness of the One who actually prevails.

### Questions to carry

- When you look honestly at your own life — where is the word "winning" loudest in your mouth, and could that loudness be covering something you're afraid isn't true?
- In your relationship with God, name the fee you are quietly waiting to see confirmed before you will fully commit. What is it?
- If the staff dropped in your life tomorrow — if the thing you've secretly built yourself upon were swallowed — what would be left standing?
- The magicians needed the fabrication to be *devoured* before they would let go of it. What would it take for you to let go one hour *sooner* than that?
- Whose *'izzah* are you actually swearing by — with your time, your worry, your weight — when no one is listening?

### The one sentence to carry

**The people who kept shouting that they would win lost everything to the man who never once said the word — because truth does not compete with falsehood, it swallows it.**

### The invitation

You do not have to wait for the staff to drop. That is the whole mercy of being told this story before your own crisis arrives. You are being handed, in advance, the sight that the magicians only bought at the price of a public collapse: that borrowed might gets swallowed, that the transactional heart is bargaining over a sunrise, and that the quietest thing on the floor — the truth, backed by *al-'Azīz* — is the only thing that will still be standing when the noise ends. You are invited to move your weight, gently, today, off of every Pharaoh you've been leaning on, and onto the One whose might no sea can drown. Not because you were caught. Because you were shown.

### A closing du'ā

O Allah, *al-'Azīz* — the Truly Invincible — cure our hearts of the need to be called the winners, and free us from swearing by any might but Yours. Let us not wait until our false foundations are swallowed before we fall down before You; draw us near, and make our nearness to You, not to any throne of this world. And when the staff of truth drops in our lives, let us be among those whose hands were already open to catch it. *Āmīn.*
