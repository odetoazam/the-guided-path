# The Wasteful Ones Who Do Not Reform

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 151–154

---

### The Arabic

> ٱلَّذِينَ يُفْسِدُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَا يُصْلِحُونَ ﴿١٥٢﴾
> قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مِنَ ٱلْمُسَحَّرِينَ ﴿١٥٣﴾
> مَآ أَنتَ إِلَّا بَشَرٌ مِّثْلُنَا فَأْتِ بِـَٔايَةٍ إِن كُنتَ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ ﴿١٥٤﴾

### Translation

> They said: 'You are only one of the bewitched. You are nothing but a human being like us — so bring a sign, if you are truthful.'"

## INTRODUCTION

### The World You Need to Inhabit

Imagine a city carved into red rock. Not built on top of the earth — carved *into* it. Mountains hollowed out by hand, room by room, palace by palace. The people of Thamud were master engineers. They could split a cliff face the way you and I might slice bread. They lived inside the bones of the planet.

This was not a tribe of wanderers. This was a civilization at its peak. Wealth. Architecture. Confidence. The kind of confidence that comes when your grandfather built something that will outlast empires, and you live inside it, and you assume — naturally, without ever putting it into words — that the rules that bind ordinary people don't quite bind you.

And then a man stands up among them. One of their own. His name is Salih. And he says: *Stop. Listen. There is a Lord above us. There is a reckoning coming. And the people you have been listening to — the leaders of this city, the elders whose names are on every wall — they are not what you think they are.*

[PAUSE]

That is the moment we are stepping into. The exact moment. Salih has been speaking, ayah after ayah, building the case. He has reminded them of the orchards and the springs (26:147-148). He has reminded them that what they have was given. And now, at the end of his counsel, he names — for the first time — the actual *enemy* his people must reject.

This naming is what we are going to sit with.

### The Hook

Salih could have called them anything. Tyrants. Disbelievers. Idol-worshippers. Liars. Allah, putting words in His prophet's mouth, chose one specific name for the leadership of Thamud. And then, in case we missed it, He defined the name with a phrase so strange that classical commentators paused over it for centuries.

He called them *al-musrifīn* — the wasteful ones.

And He defined them not by what they do, but by what they fail to do.

[PAUSE]

By the time we are done, you will see why the people of Thamud's response — *"You are nothing but a bewitched human"* — was not insult. It was confession. They told us, in their own words, exactly what was wrong with them. And we still miss it, because we keep thinking the story is about a camel.

It isn't.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Al-Musrifīn*: The Ones Who Spend Past the Edge

Before we open the word, let me ask you something.

When you hear the word *waste*, what do you picture?

Most people picture food in a trash can. A faucet running. Money spent on something useless. *Waste* in modern English is small and domestic. It's a virtue thing — your grandmother taught you not to waste.

[PAUSE]

The Arabic root س-ر-ف (*s-r-f*) is doing something much bigger than that.

The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib — describe *isrāf* not as waste in the petty sense, but as **going past the limit set by Allah**. Crossing a line. Exceeding the proper measure of a thing. The root carries within it a sense of *not knowing where to stop*. A person who cannot find the edge. A person whose appetites have escaped their boundaries.

This is why the Quran uses *isrāf* in places where you would not expect a "wastefulness" word at all. In Surah Al-A'raf (7:81), the people of Lut are called *qawmun musrifūn* — "a wasteful people" — and the context is not food or money. It is sexual transgression. They were *musrifūn* because they had crossed a line that should not be crossed.

So when Salih warns his people about *al-musrifīn*, he is not warning them about leaders who throw away leftovers.

He is warning them about leaders who **do not know where to stop**.

[PAUSE]

And here the classical commentators add a dimension the translation alone cannot carry. Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both phrase this as the leaders being wasteful *upon themselves* — *al-musrifīna ʿalā anfusihim*. The isrāf is first a wound to their own souls. They cross the limits set by Allah, and the first one harmed is not the land, not the people below them, but the one doing the crossing. The corruption that eventually spreads outward across the earth is the overflow of an interior that has first been destroyed. This is not a minor qualifier. It means the *musrif* does not begin in the public square. He begins in the private chamber.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the form of the word. *Al-musrifīn* is the active participle, plural, definite. It is not a description of a single act. *Musrif* — one who does *isrāf* — is the person whose *being* has become the act. The action has folded into the identity. He is no longer a person who occasionally crosses lines. He is, in his very nature, the one-who-crosses.

And then — *al-* in front. The definite article. *The* wasteful ones. Not just any. **The known ones. The recognizable ones. The ones in your city you can name.**

The grammatical force here is significant. Salih is saying: you know exactly who I'm talking about. Don't pretend you don't.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators confirm this precision. Al-Tabari identifies the *musrifīn* of this verse as specifically the nine men of the clan — the same faction the Quran names in 27:48 as those who were corrupting in the earth and not reforming. These were not anonymous elites. They were a known, identifiable group within Thamud. They had faces and houses and reputations. Salih's people could have pointed to them by name. The definite article is not rhetorical — it is forensic.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this for a moment.

Notice that he does not call them *kāfirūn* (deniers). He does not call them *mushrikūn* (associators). He does not call them *ẓālimūn* (oppressors), though he could have used any of these and been correct. He calls them *musrifīn*.

Why?

Here is what I want you to consider. Denial is invisible. Association can be hidden. Oppression can be dressed up as governance. But *isrāf* — the crossing of limits — leaves a mark on the world. You can see *isrāf*. The over-built palace. The over-extracted resource. The over-spoken word in the council chamber. The over-stretched authority of a ruler who has forgotten that he, too, has limits.

Salih names the leaders of Thamud by the most *visible* of their sins. Not the one inside their hearts. The one *engraved into their architecture*.

Now the rocks they were so proud of carving are testifying against them.

This is what one word — *al-musrifīn* — is doing.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two — The Asymmetric Absence: *Yufsidūna wa Lā Yuṣliḥūn*

Before we open the next phrase, I need to teach you something about how Arabic — and especially the Quran — handles paired opposites.

In Arabic rhetoric, there is a device where you describe someone by what they do *and* by the matching thing they do not do. The presence-and-absence pair. *He gives but does not take.* *He sees but does not look away.* The two halves seem to say the same thing twice, and so the careless reader skims over the second half. But the second half is almost always doing something the first half cannot.

Watch what Allah does in 26:152.

He says: *Alladhīna yufsidūna fī-l-arḍ* — those who corrupt the earth.

That alone would be a complete description. *Mufsidūn* (corrupters) is a category in the Quran. We meet them in Al-Baqarah (2:11): *"And when it is said to them, 'Do not corrupt in the earth,' they say, 'We are only reformers.'"* The corrupter and the reformer are already a known pair.

So Allah could have stopped. *Those who corrupt the earth.* Done. We would understand.

But He doesn't stop.

He adds: *wa lā yuṣliḥūn* — **and they do not reform**.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here. This is the most important moment in the linguistic journey.

The root ص-ل-ح (*ṣ-l-ḥ*) means to make right, to repair, to restore something to its proper functioning state. *Iṣlāḥ* is the work of putting things back together. A *ṣāliḥ* person is, at the most basic linguistic level, a *functional* person — someone who works the way a human being is supposed to work.

So Allah is saying about the leaders of Thamud: they corrupt the earth, **and they do not repair**.

Why is this not redundant?

Here is what the absence is doing. There is a kind of person — and we know this kind because we live among them — who breaks something and then *also* fixes it. Or breaks ten things and fixes one. Or pollutes a river and then funds a charity for the river. The world is full of people whose corruption is balanced by partial repair. They sleep at night because the ledger feels even.

The leaders of Thamud were not those people.

They corrupted **and did not repair at all**.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. Allah left the object of *yuṣliḥūn* completely unspecified.

This is the absence within the absence.

In Arabic, when a verb that normally takes an object is left without one, the meaning generalizes to *anything*. *They do not reform — anything. At all. Ever.* Al-Tabari, reading the most natural implicit referent, supplies "themselves" — they do not reform themselves by working in obedience to Allah. And this is the ground floor of the problem: a soul that has never turned toward its own repair cannot repair what surrounds it. But the Quranic ellipsis leaves the meaning open beyond even that. Their hearts? No. Their relationships? No. What they have broken in the earth? No. The next generation? No. The scope is total.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar adds another dimension. These leaders have not merely committed acts of corruption — they have *made corruption their habit*. He uses the Arabic word *dāb*: a settled practice, a routine, a rhythm so ingrained it no longer requires a decision. They wake up and corrupt. They go to sleep having corrupted. The *fasād* is not their departure from normalcy. It is their normalcy.

There is no domain in which these people are *muṣliḥūn*. They are pure corruption. The ledger is one-sided.

[PAUSE]

This is what Salih is warning his people against. Not leaders who are flawed. Not leaders who are sinful but well-intentioned. Not leaders who do harm and good in mixed measure. Those are not *musrifīn* in the sense Allah intends here.

The *musrifīn* of Thamud are the ones whose entire civilizational output is *fasād* — corruption — with **no countervailing reform anywhere**.

You can imagine the kind of leadership this describes. Leadership that uses up. Leadership that extracts. Leadership that builds for itself and for its own monuments. Leadership that, when the cost of its building falls on the poor, the land, or the next generation, simply *does not see that there is anything to fix*.

The chilling thing is not that they corrupt. It is that they have lost the **category** of repair. *Reform* is no longer a thing they fail at. It is a thing they no longer recognize exists.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three — *Al-Musaḥḥarīn*: The Sound of a Dismissal

Now we cross from Salih's voice to theirs. From the warning to the response.

The people of Thamud answer him: *innamā anta mina-l-musaḥḥarīn* — "you are only one of the *musaḥḥarīn*."

Almost every translation renders this as "the bewitched." Salih, they are saying, you are under a spell. Magic has been worked on you.

That translation is correct. But it is not complete. And the gap between what is correct and what is complete is where this ayah lives.

[PAUSE]

The root س-ح-ر (*s-ḥ-r*) means magic, enchantment. The active form *sāḥir* is "magician" — the one who casts the spell. The passive form *masḥūr* is "the one bewitched" — the one upon whom magic has been cast.

But Allah does not use *masḥūr* here. He uses *musaḥḥar*.

Notice the doubled letter. The shadda. *Musaḥḥar* — with the intensified middle radical — is from Form II of the verb. In Arabic morphology, Form II carries the meaning of *intensification, repetition, or thoroughness*. To *saḥḥara* something is not to enchant it once. It is to enchant it repeatedly, deeply, until the enchantment has saturated it.

Listen to the sound of the word. *Musaḥḥarīn.* The doubled *ḥ* — that thick, breathy, almost choking consonant. Try to say it. The throat closes. The word itself sounds like something heavy being dragged through resistance. The people of Thamud are saying — in the very texture of the sound — that Salih is *saturated*, worked through and through, his mind no longer his own.

[PAUSE]

But here is where the classical commentators did not settle on a single reading — and al-Tabari documents the disagreement explicitly: *scholars differed in its interpretation*.

The first position — attributed to Mujahid and cited by Ibn Kathir — connects the word straightforwardly to *siḥr* (magic): *musaḥḥar* as the one who has been thoroughly, repeatedly enchanted. Not just bewitched once but worked over until the spell has saturated him. His prophecy is not revelation. His words are not from Allah. Something got to him first.

The second position connects the word not to *siḥr* but to *saḥar* — in classical Arabic, the lungs and windpipe, the organs of mortal appetite, of breath and hunger and thirst. A *musaḥḥar* on this reading is a creature that *possesses a saḥar*: a being of flesh, dependent on food and drink and air, mortal through and through, like any other body.

On this second reading, the people of Thamud are not primarily calling Salih a madman. They are calling him *merely human*.

[PAUSE]

And notice — this second reading connects directly to the very next line. The accusation of *musaḥḥarīn* and the accusation of *basharun mithlunā* ("nothing but a human being like us") may be a single thought stated twice, or may be two charges stacked on each other. On either reading, the dismissal is the same: Salih is being placed *inside* the ordinary world in order to disqualify him from speaking from outside it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at whose mouth this word is coming out of.

It is coming out of *al-musrifīn*. The wasteful ones. The ones who corrupt and do not reform. The ones who have crossed every limit given to them.

And what did we say *isrāf* was, two elements ago?

**It was crossing the limit. Spending past the proper edge. Going beyond the measure set by Allah.**

The leaders of Thamud are looking at a man who is *not* spent — who is grounded, who is sober, who is calling them back to measure — and they are saying: *"You are the irrational one. You are the one who has been captured. Not us."*

The accusation is a mirror. The *musrifūn* are projecting onto Salih what they themselves are: captive to a force that has consumed their capacity to see clearly. Their isrāf has run so deep that they can no longer recognize sobriety when they face it. The prophetic voice sounds, to an ear shaped by excess, like madness.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Four — *Basharun Mithlunā*: The Argument That Defeats Itself

*Mā anta illā basharun mithlunā* — "you are nothing but a human being like us."

The structure: *mā...illā*. This is the Arabic restriction formula. *Nothing except*. *Only and only*. The most emphatic way to confine someone to a category. You are *entirely* and *only* human. There is nothing else to you. No appointment from above. No voice that is not your own.

[PAUSE]

This objection is not original to Thamud. It echoes through the Quran as the common protest of every rejecting people against every prophet. The people of Nuh say it (23:24). The Meccans say it of Muhammad ﷺ (17:93). It is the oldest charge in the book of rejection. And Surah Ash-Shu'ara rehearses it in story after story — seven prophets, seven nations, and in nearly every exchange the same words come back: *you are just a human like us.*

When a refusal is that consistent, it is worth asking: what is the refusal actually protecting?

[PAUSE]

The argument, when you hold it up to the light, defeats itself.

They are saying: *you are a human being like us*. But if that is true — if Salih is genuinely a human being like them — then what he is saying could, in principle, be correct. A human being can be right. A human being can receive guidance. The fact that he is human does not *disqualify* him from prophethood. It only disqualifies him if you assume, in advance, that no human being could ever be a messenger from Allah. Which is not an argument. It is an assumption dressed as a conclusion.

[PAUSE]

There is something else in this line that the commentators found significant. They say *mithlunā* — "like *us*." Not "like ordinary people." Like *us specifically*. The people of Thamud are placing themselves as the standard. If Salih is not distinguishable from *them*, he cannot be a messenger. They have made themselves the measure of what is possible. They are the ceiling. They are the limit.

And what did Salih call them?

*Al-musrifīn* — the ones who recognize no limit at all.

[PAUSE]

The irony is almost unbearable. A people who set no boundary on themselves have imposed an absolute boundary on Allah. *Allah cannot speak through a mere human.* This is the one line they will not cross. The people who over-spend, over-build, over-take, over-claim in every direction — these are the people who suddenly discover a hard and immovable boundary when it comes to the sovereignty of their Lord.

Then comes the demand: *fa'ti bi-āyatin in kunta mina-ṣ-ṣādiqīn* — "so bring a sign, if you are truthful."

[PAUSE]

They are asking for a sign. But they are asking for it from a position of prior rejection. The sign, when it comes — the she-camel, a living ayah walking among them, drinking from their water, a miracle they demanded and received — will be refused. They already know, in some deep chamber of the self, that they will refuse it. The demand is not sincere inquiry. It is the performance of openness from someone who has already closed.

How do we know?

Because Salih is *already* an ayah. A man of their own people, raised among them, known to them, standing before them with coherent speech and moral clarity — and they cannot see it. The one who cannot see the sign already present in front of him will not receive the sign brought forth from the rock. The problem is not a shortage of evidence. The problem is a different organ altogether.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## PART 2: WHAT THE EXCHANGE TELLS US

Ayahs 151 and 152 are Salih speaking. Ayahs 153 and 154 are Thamud answering.

And if you read the exchange carefully — if you slow it all the way down — you realize that their answer is not a refutation of anything he said.

He said: do not obey the *musrifīn*, the ones who corrupt and do not reform.

They said: you are bewitched. You are just a human. Bring a sign.

[PAUSE]

They did not dispute the charge. They did not say: *"Our leaders are not musrifīn. Here is evidence of their reform. Here are the wells they have dug, the weak they have protected, the earth they have tended."*

They could not say that. Because it was not true.

Instead, they attacked the messenger. *You are crazy. You are just a man. Prove it.*

This is not an argument. It is a deflection. And the form of the deflection — *attacking the one who names the problem* rather than addressing the problem — is itself a variety of *fasād*. It is corrupting the conditions under which truth can be spoken. It is making the air around the prophet too hostile for anyone nearby to breathe it clearly.

[PAUSE]

There is a pattern in Surah Ash-Shu'ara that we should name. The surah tells the story of seven prophets in sequence. And in each story, the people who reject the prophet say almost the exact same things. *You are bewitched.* *You are just a human like us.* *Your followers are only the low and the weak.* *You want power over us.* The surah repeats these lines like a refrain, deliberately, so that we feel the weight of the repetition. These are not seven independent events. This is one event that keeps happening. A single architecture of rejection, assembled again and again, across civilizations that have never met.

[PAUSE]

And who benefits most from that architecture?

*Al-musrifīn*. The nine who corrupt and do not reform.

This is why Salih began where he did. Before he asked for anything from his people. Before he even commanded them to worship Allah more fully. He named their leadership and told them not to follow it. Because without that naming — without that break — nothing else was possible. A people who cannot identify the *musrifīn* in their midst, who cannot say "that is the one who spends past the limit, who corrupts without repairing" — those are a people whose attention has been captured. And captured attention cannot hear a prophet. Every word that reaches the ear must first pass through a filter built by the very leaders Salih is warning them about.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## WHAT THIS ASKS OF US

Salih's words were not addressed to the leaders of Thamud.

Read the ayah again. He says *lā tuṭīʿū* — "do not *you* obey." The plural second person. He is speaking to his people. To the ordinary members of Thamud who had, until this moment, been following their leaders because that is what people do. They follow the path that has been worn smooth by those who went before. They obey the *amr* — the command structure — because no one has ever stood up and named it for what it is.

He is asking them to do something that feels unnatural. To look at the men who have always given the orders — the men whose names are carved into the cliff faces, whose households are powerful, whose commands have shaped the whole world you grew up in — and to say: *No. Not these ones. These are the musrifīn.*

[PAUSE]

The question this ayah puts to us is not: are there *musrifīn* in the world?

There are always *musrifīn*. There has never been a human community without people who spend past the limit, who consume without repair, who cross every boundary set for them and call it progress or power or simply the way things are.

The question is: can we name them?

And naming them requires first understanding what *isrāf* actually is. Not small domestic wastefulness. The crossing of limits set by Allah. The appetite that has escaped its proper measure. The authority that has forgotten it, too, is bounded — that it too, at its very foundation, is first being wasteful toward its own soul.

[PAUSE]

And then — harder still — the question asks: when the one who names the *musrifīn* is dismissed as bewitched, as merely human, as someone asking too much of us, as someone who does not understand how the world works —

Can we still hear him?

The people of Thamud could not. Their ears had been shaped by the *musrifīn* for too long. By the time Salih stood up, the architecture of their hearing had been renovated from the inside out. They heard him and heard madness.

We are not immune to this renovation. Every generation is renovated by its most influential voices. The question is only: which voices have shaped the chambers of our listening? And when a call to reform comes — to *iṣlāḥ*, to putting something broken back together — does our interior architecture amplify it or absorb it into silence?

[PAUSE — long pause]

Salih asked his people to disobey. Not to rebel. Not to overthrow. Not to become *musrifīn* themselves in the name of opposing *musrifīn*.

He asked them to disobey *the command* — *amr*. The directive. The narrative that says: *this is how things are, this is who matters, this is what power looks like, and you cannot imagine it otherwise.*

That *amr* — that command structure — is what he was asking them to step out of.

They could not do it.

May Allah give us the sight to see the *musrifīn*, the clarity to name them, and the firmness — wherever our moment calls for it — to say: *this is not the command we will follow.*

And may He make us among the *muṣliḥūn* — the repairers — in whatever small way He has placed before us to repair.
