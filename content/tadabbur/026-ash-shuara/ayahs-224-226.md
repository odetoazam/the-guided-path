# The Poets and the Valleys: When Words Wander Without a Compass

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> وَٱلشُّعَرَآءُ يَتَّبِعُهُمُ ٱلْغَاوُۥنَ
> أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّهُمْ فِى كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ
> وَأَنَّهُمْ يَقُولُونَ مَا لَا يَفْعَلُونَ

**Transliteration:**
> Wa-sh-shuʿarāʾu yattabiʿuhumu-l-ghāwūn
> A-lam tara annahum fī kulli wādin yahīmūn
> Wa annahum yaqūlūna mā lā yafʿalūn

**Translation:**
> And the poets — the deviators follow them.
> Have you not seen that in every valley they wander,
> and that they say what they do not do?

---

### Rebuilding the World

To feel the weight of these three ayahs, you have to step into a society where poetry was not entertainment. It was the **central nervous system** of public life.

In pre-Islamic Arabia, a tribe without a poet was a tribe without a voice. The poet was the war drum, the diplomat, the historian, the legal advocate, and the propagandist all at once. When two tribes clashed, the casualties were counted twice: once in bodies, once in verses. A famous poet's couplet could redirect alliances. A satire could ruin a reputation across the peninsula faster than any rumor.

The poet was, in many ways, what the influencer is today — except with the moral authority of a journalist, the cultural reach of a celebrity, and the perceived spiritual access of a mystic. Poets were thought to be **shuʿarāʾ** — those who *perceive*, who *know* — because they were assumed to be in contact with unseen forces (jinn-companions, *shayāṭīn al-shuʿarāʾ*) who whispered to them what others could not hear.

Now drop the Quran into this world.

The Quraysh have a problem. The Prophet ﷺ is reciting something that moves people — that breaks family loyalties, that pulls slaves and chieftains alike. They need a category to file it under. So they reach for the most prestigious available label and weaponize it: *He's a poet. He's just a poet. Wait until time runs out on him.* (cf. 21:5, 52:30)

The accusation is not casual. It is calculated. To call him a poet was to say: *his speech belongs in the same room as everyone else's.* It was to flatten revelation into rhetoric. It was to deny that anything new had entered the world.

[PAUSE]

So Surah Ash-Shu'ara — *The Poets* — is named after this lie. And for one hundred and ninety-three ayahs, Allah does not address the lie head-on. He simply tells stories. Mūsā. Ibrāhīm. Nūḥ. Hūd. Ṣāliḥ. Lūṭ. Shuʿayb. Seven prophets. And in every single story, what the prophet *says* is what the prophet *does*. He warns, and the warning lands in his own life. He believes in the unseen and walks toward the unseen. His speech and his footsteps go in the same direction.

Then, after all that narration, Allah turns to the accusation. And He doesn't deny that there are poets. He concedes the category exists. He just opens its inner anatomy.

### The Hook

Here is what should already be strange to you.

The surah is called *The Poets*. But the poets do not appear until ayah 224 — out of 227. Just over one percent (~1.3%) of the surah is actually about poets. And then, in the very next ayah after these three, Allah makes an exception so massive it almost cancels the indictment.

Why name a surah after its smallest section? Why diagnose the poets in a sentence so short — fifteen words — when seven prophets get hundreds of ayahs?

And the diagnosis itself: *they wander in every valley, and they say what they do not do.* That is not a complaint about poetry. That is a complaint about something far more general. About a particular **shape of soul** — a soul whose words have come loose from its life.

Stay with me. There is something here Allah wants you to see about your own speech.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to open three doors in this passage. Each door is a single linguistic feature. Each one will widen what we thought the ayah was saying.

---

### Element One: *Yattabiʿuhumu-l-ghāwūn* — The Direction of Following

Before we look at the Arabic, let me ask you something.

If I told you, "X follows Y," what does that mean? In English, "follow" is almost neutral. You can follow someone on Instagram and feel nothing. You can follow a recipe. You can follow a rule.

But there is a deeper sense of "follow" — the one we mean when we say a person *followed their teacher* for twenty years. There, "follow" means: *I have placed my direction of life in their hands. Where they go, I go. What they say, I weigh.*

That deeper kind of following has a specific Arabic word. It is **ittibāʿ**, from the verb form **يَتَّبِعُ** (*yattabiʿu*) — Form VIII of the root ت-ب-ع. Form VIII in Arabic typically intensifies the verb and makes it *reflexive in commitment* — the subject takes the action *upon themselves.* It is not casual following. It is **discipleship-following**. The same word the Quran uses for following the Prophet ﷺ, for following revelation, for following one's father's religion.

This is the word Allah uses here.

> **يَتَّبِعُهُمُ ٱلْغَاوُۥنَ** — *yattabiʿuhumu-l-ghāwūn*
> *The ghāwūn discipleship-follow them.*

Now look at the structure of the sentence. In Arabic, word order carries weight. The default order in a verbal sentence is *verb-subject-object*. But here, Allah front-loads the object: *And the poets — they are followed by the ghāwūn.*

This **fronting** (taqdīm) does work. It makes the poets the topic — *as for the poets, here is who circles them* — and it makes the *ghāwūn* the end-stress, the emphasized element. Not just "the poets have followers." But: *the followers of the poets are, specifically, the ghāwūn.*

Who are the *ghāwūn*?

The root **غ-و-ي** (gh-w-y) means, in classical Arabic, **to lose the way** — but with a specific texture. It is not merely being lost. **Ghawāyah** is the state of *desiring* something so strongly that the desire itself blinds you to the path. Lisān al-ʿArab gives us the meaning: *deviation born from passion.* The *ghāwī* is not someone who took a wrong turn on a map. He is someone whose **desire has become his compass**, and so wherever desire pulls, he goes.

The opposite of *ghawāyah* is *rushd* — being mature, being oriented, being able to see your destination through your wanting. Iblīs himself uses this word in Surah al-Aʿrāf when he says, *"Because You have caused me to deviate (aghwaytanī), I will lie in wait for them on Your straight path"* (7:16). It is the word of corruption-by-desire.

[PAUSE]

So now hear the ayah again with both words breathing:

> *And the poets — those who have lost the path through their own desire discipleship-follow them.*

Notice what this verse is NOT saying. It is not saying poets are evil. It is not even saying their followers are evil. It is making a precise sociological claim:

**The kind of person whose inner compass has been replaced by their wanting will find, in poetry-without-anchoring, exactly what they came for.**

Why? Because poetry-without-anchoring will tell them whatever the moment requires. It will praise what they want praised. It will mock what they want mocked. It will give their feelings the dignity of language without ever asking those feelings to bow to anything higher.

Here is what this single grammatical choice — *yattabiʿu* in Form VIII, plus the fronting, plus *al-ghāwūn* — is opening for us:

There is a **resonance principle** in human moral life. People do not follow randomly. The follower and the followed are tuned to the same frequency. Shallow speech does not attract everyone. It attracts those who needed exactly that shallowness to keep their desires comfortable.

Which means: when we ask *what kind of speakers are circling us right now*, we are also asking *what kind of listeners we have become*.

[PAUSE — major]

Before we move on, there is a genuine disagreement among the early commentators worth holding. Al-Tabari records it directly: *"wa-khtalafa ahlu al-taʾwīl fī alladhīna wuṣifū bi-l-ghayy fī hādhā al-mawḍiʿ"* — the scholars of tafsir disagreed about who, exactly, the *ghāwūn* are in this ayah. Ibn Abbas — reported through multiple chains including the transmission of Ikrimah — identified them as **رواة الشعر**, the *reciters and transmitters of poetry*: the human enthusiasts who spread the poems, the hangers-on who gathered to memorize and repeat. On this reading, the *ghāwūn* are not abstractions. They are the crowd at the ʿUkāẓ market giving the poet the applause that feeds his wandering.

A second body of interpreters, however, identified the *ghāwūn* as **the shayāṭīn** — the jinn-companions themselves. The same demonic presences the Arabs believed whispered to the poets would, on this reading, be the ones *discipleship-following* the poets: not admirers circling from outside, but the spiritual forces driving the wandering from within. The image darkens considerably. The poet is not merely served by a corrupt audience; he is *followed* by the very forces that animated his gift toward corruption in the first place.

Both readings stand in the classical corpus, and neither cancels the other. Together they bracket something important: the *ghāwūn* might be those *outside* the poet — the captivated human audience — or those *inside* his creative process. In either case, the word *ghawāyah* — deviation born of desire — names the spiritual substance of the relationship. Desire is the gravity that holds this whole system together, from the jinn-companion to the market crowd.

### Element Two: *Fī kulli wādin yahīmūn* — Wandering in Every Valley

The second door opens onto an image. Watch what Allah does.

> **أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّهُمْ فِى كُلِّ وَادٍ يَهِيمُونَ**
> *Have you not seen that in every valley they wander?*

Stop. Read that again. *Have you not seen.* Allah is asking us to *look* at something. But what is there to *see*? Poets are not literally in valleys. So why this image?

Let's take the picture seriously, because in the Quran, physical images are stepping stones to unseen realities.

A **wādī** in Arabia is not a quaint English "valley" of green hills. It is a **dry riverbed cut through stone** — a channel carved by floodwater that has long since drained away. Wādīs are how you move through the desert. They are routes. But they are also places of disorientation: you descend into them, the horizon drops away, and one wādī looks much like another.

Now the verb. **يَهِيمُونَ** (*yahīmūn*) — from the root هـ-ي-م. This is the most beautiful and the most terrible word in the verse.

*Hayamān* is wandering. But not just any wandering. Lisān al-ʿArab and the classical lexicons give us its core meaning: **the wandering of a camel that has lost its mind from thirst.** The camel that has gone *hāʾim* will walk in any direction, into any wādī, drawn by any glimpse of water — sometimes real, sometimes mirage. It cannot be led. It cannot be reasoned with. Its thirst has eaten its judgment.

The same word is used for **a lover deranged by his love**. The poet who composes about a beloved he cannot have, who walks the desert speaking her name — he is *hāʾim*. The word is, ironically, *itself* a poet's word. Allah is using a piece of Arab poetic vocabulary to describe Arab poetry.

Now hold the two together: **valleys** + **camel-thirst-wandering**. What is the picture?

*A creature with no direction, drawn by every shadow of water, descending into one channel after another, lost in each one, searching for what it cannot find, going wherever its thirst pulls.*

That is the diagnosis.

But here is where the ayah opens.

Notice: **fī kulli wādin** — *in every valley.* Not "in some valleys." Not "in their valley." In **every** valley.

What does it mean to wander in *every* valley? Think about what valleys are. A valley is a *direction*. A valley is *committed*. Water in a wādī is going somewhere — it is descending toward the sea. The wādī itself has a slope, an orientation, a destination.

A person who wanders in *every* valley is a person who **commits to every direction and arrives at none**. Today this valley pulls him; he descends into it, sings its praises, gives himself to it. Tomorrow another valley calls; he descends into that one, with the same passion, the same words, the same inner conviction.

He has the *posture* of direction without the *fact* of direction.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators peeled this image into even sharper light, and their readings are worth sitting with. Ibn Abbas — reported in al-Tabari's chain — glossed the valley-image with a single, unadorned phrase: *"fī kulli laghwin yakhūḍūn"* — **in every idle, vain thing they wade**. The word *laghw* is precise: it is not merely falsehood. *Laghw* is speech or action that is *hollow at its center*, that makes noise but carries no weight, no substance, no harvest. It is the very thing the believers in Surah al-Muʾminūn are praised for *turning away from* (23:3). Ibn Abbas is saying: each valley is not a new topic. Each valley is a new *instance of laghw* — a new hollow channel into which the poet pours his gift, wades through to the end, and emerges unchanged, already scanning for the next one.

Mujahid, the great Tabiʿī exegete who learned at the feet of Ibn Abbas himself, read the image from a different angle: *"fī kulli fannin yaftannūn"* — **in every genre and style they are captivated and tried**. The word *fann* means a variety, a mode of expression, a type. And *yaftannūn* carries a double edge: it means both *to be captivated by* and *to be tested and put on trial by*. The poet is not simply drawn to every style — he is *consumed* by each one. His talent is simultaneously his trial.

Taken together, Ibn Abbas and Mujahid are saying: the valleys are simultaneously the *genres* of pre-Islamic poetry — satire, elegy, boasting, love-verse, war-incitement — and the *hollow things* each genre serves. The wandering is through every medium and every emptiness at once.

Al-Muyassar makes the catalog still more concrete: what this wandering looked like on the ground was *"funūn al-kadhib wa-l-zūr wa-tamzīq al-aʿrāḍ wa-l-ṭaʿn fī al-ansāb wa-tajrīḥ al-nisāʾ al-ʿafāʾif"* — the genres of lying, bearing false witness, tearing apart reputations, impugning lineages, and attacking chaste women. And from the other direction: they exaggerated in praising the people of falsehood, and belittled the people of truth.

This is not abstract wandering. In the pre-Islamic context, the poet-as-valley-wanderer was a very specific social function: he was the one who could make a liar look honorable and make a just man look small, all for the right price or the right tribal alliance. Every valley he entered, he bent his gift toward whatever that valley required. The *hayamān* — the thirst-madness — was not random. It was perfectly responsive to whoever was applauding at the mouth of the wādī.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because it is uncomfortably modern.

What does it mean today to wander in every valley?

It means scrolling, where every post requires you to feel something, and the feelings have no through-line. It means being morally outraged on Monday about something you cannot remember by Friday. It means saying with full sincerity in one conversation what you would deny with full sincerity in the next. It means an inner life that has the *texture* of conviction — strong feelings, strong words — without the **continuity** that conviction requires.

A camel deranged by thirst still feels, urgently, that it knows what it wants. *Water. Now. There.* That conviction is what makes it lose its mind. It is not lukewarm. It is *fevered.* But because it is fevered without a **fixed direction**, it ends in nothing.

This is the precise critique.

Allah is not saying the poets do not feel deeply. He is saying: **their depth has no spine.** Their feelings are sincere in each valley and contradicted in the next. Their words rise to fever pitch about whatever the moment demands and then collapse when the moment passes.

And remember — the prophet, by contrast, is a creature of **one valley**. From the day Mūsā stands at the bush, every word he speaks afterward descends in the *same direction* — toward the deliverance of a people. Every word Ibrāhīm speaks descends in the same direction — toward *tawḥīd*. The seven prophets we have just heard about in this surah are the inverse of the wandering valley-walker. They are people of **one slope, one descent, one sea.**

That contrast is the architecture of the surah.

### Element Three: *Yaqūlūna mā lā yafʿalūn* — The Two-Verb Diagnosis

The third door is the smallest and the most piercing.

> **وَأَنَّهُمْ يَقُولُونَ مَا لَا يَفْعَلُونَ**
> *And that they say what they do not do.*

Read it again. Slowly. Because Arabic teaches us something here that English flattens.

The verse contains two verbs: **يَقُولُونَ** (*yaqūlūna* — they say) and **يَفْعَلُونَ** (*yafʿalūna* — they do). Both are in the same tense — the *muḍāriʿ*, the present-continuous-imperfect tense. The Arabic muḍāriʿ describes ongoing, habitual, characterizing action. *They are saying* and *they are doing* — both presented as **habitual inner conditions, not single events.**

This matters enormously.

Allah does not say *qālū* — they said. He does not say *they once said something they did not once do.* He says: **the saying and the not-doing are both ongoing.** This is a **standing condition** of the soul. It is not a stumble. It is the **shape**.

And there is something even more remarkable. Look at what the Arabic does not say.

Allah does not say: *they say, and then they fail to do.* That would imply a sequence — first the word, then the collapse, perhaps with a moment of regret bridging them. Instead, both verbs run in parallel, in the same tense, with no temporal gap between them. The saying and the not-doing are *simultaneous* and *habitual*. They are **two faces of the same ongoing condition**.

This means the problem is not that the poet occasionally promises what he cannot deliver. The problem is that **his speech has been structurally severed from his life**. The word and the deed do not even live in the same house. When he speaks, he is not making a commitment. He is staging a *performance*. The gap between *qawl* and *fiʿl* is not a failure he regrets. It is the baseline he has normalized.

Now hold this against al-Muyassar's concrete description. The wandering poet praised the people of falsehood (*ahl al-bāṭil*) and diminished the people of truth (*ahl al-ḥaqq*). What he said was not merely disconnected from what he did — it was the precise *inversion* of what reality warranted. He did not simply fail to live up to his words. His words were oriented *against* the truth. This is the deepest form of the *qawl-fiʿl* severance: not passivity (I said it but didn't do it), but active inversion (I said the opposite of what both my life and reality required of me).

[PAUSE]

There is a Quranic echo worth naming here. Surah al-Ṣaff opens: *"O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do? It is deeply hateful to Allah that you say what you do not do"* (61:2–3). The word used there — *kabura maqtan* — is profound. Not merely disliked. Profoundly, greatly detestable. And notice who that address is aimed at: **believers**. Not poets. Not pagans. The sickness of *qawl* without *fiʿl* is not only the wandering poet's disease. It is something any soul can contract when it stops watching the distance between its mouth and its feet.

The poet's condition in these ayahs is the acute, professional version — the soul that has made the gap its vocation. The believer's warning in al-Ṣaff is the preventive medicine. Both ayahs are staring at the same interior anatomy.

[PAUSE — major]

What does the three-ayah passage amount to, then?

Three diagnostic notes on a particular shape of soul:

1. **It attracts followers whose own desires are their compass** — the resonance principle. Like calls to like.
2. **It has no sustained direction** — every valley is equally compelling, equally abandoned, equally hollow.
3. **Its speech and its life run on separate tracks** — not occasionally, but constitutionally, as an ongoing condition of both verb-streams.

These three notes, together, describe a person who is *fully articulate and fully unmoored*.

That is the person the Quraysh were comparing the Prophet ﷺ to.

## PART 2: THE COUNTER-MODEL

### What the Seven Prophets Were

This is why the architecture of the surah matters so much. We read the seven prophets first — not as separate stories, but as the sustained counter-evidence for what follows.

Think about Mūsā. He does not merely *speak* to Pharaoh. He has already *crossed* the burning bush. He has already put his hand into his cloak and pulled it out transformed. His words to Pharaoh arrive pre-laden with a life that has already turned in one direction. When he says *"I am a messenger from the Lord of the Worlds"* (26:16), those words are not hollow — they are backed by a body that shook at a burning bush and did not run.

Think about Ibrāhīm. His speech in this surah is a sustained interrogation of idols — *"Do you hear when you call upon them? Do they benefit you or harm you?"* (26:72–73). But Ibrāhīm does not ask these questions from comfort. He has already *lived* his way to monotheism, at cost, from inside a family and a people who built their identity around those idols. His words are not a rhetorical exercise. They are the compressed articulation of a journey his body has already made.

This is what Allah means when He implicitly contrasts the prophets with the wandering poets. The prophet's speech is a **window onto his direction**. It is not separate from his life — it is *produced by* his life. You can hear, underneath every word, the weight of the choices that produced it.

The poet's speech, by contrast, is a **performance over a void**. However beautiful, however emotionally precise, however thunderingly applauded at the market — it is not produced by a life going anywhere. It is produced by a *gift*, unanchored from a *direction*.

[PAUSE]

Here is a question worth sitting with.

Is there speech in your own life — speech you produce fluently, confidently, perhaps even movingly — that is running ahead of a life that hasn't caught up with it yet?

Not lying. Not hypocrisy in the legal sense. Just... the gap. The gap between the beauty you can articulate and the reality you are actually inhabiting. Between what you encourage in others and what you practice in the dark. Between the parent you sound like and the parent you are at 11pm when everyone is tired.

That gap is exactly what this ayah is pressing on. Gently but precisely.

The question is not whether you have the gap. Everyone speaks ahead of themselves sometimes. The question is: **is the gap closing?** Is your life moving in the direction your words point? Or are the words pointing one way and the life drifting somewhere else, both tracks running in parallel, indefinitely, in the present-continuous muḍāriʿ of habit?

The prophet has the gap too, sometimes. He is human. But his life is oriented *toward* the gap — he is walking toward the place his words are pointing. The wandering poet's life is oriented *away* from the gap — his words point in a direction his life never intends to travel.

That distinction — orientation toward versus orientation away — is everything.

## PART 3: THE EXCEPTION THAT CHANGES EVERYTHING

### Ayah 227

> **إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ وَذَكَرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًا وَٱنتَصَرُوا۟ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا ظُلِمُوا۟**
> *Except those who believed and did righteous deeds and remembered Allah much and defended themselves after they were wronged.*

Three ayahs of diagnosis. And then: *except.*

That *except* is one of the most structurally significant words in the final section of this surah. It does not retract the diagnosis — the wandering, the *ghāwūn*-resonance, the *qawl-fiʿl* gap remain true of the category. But it *cleaves the category*. There is a kind of poet who escapes the diagnosis entirely. And the description of that poet is not about poetic craft. It is about the same things the surah has been about all along.

*They believed.* — Their speech has an anchor above language itself.
*They did righteous deeds.* — Their words have footsteps under them.
*They remembered Allah much.* — The direction of their interior life is fixed and does not drift from valley to valley.
*They defended themselves after being wronged.* — Their speech, when it becomes a weapon, is wielded in the service of truth, not in service of whoever is applauding.

Notice the structure of the exception. It is not: *except those who wrote beautiful poetry.* It is not: *except those who were technically skilled.* The exception has nothing to do with poetic ability. It has everything to do with the **relationship between the poet's interior life and his speech**.

A poet who believes, does good, remembers Allah, and uses his gift to defend truth — that poet is not in a valley at all. He is on a road. His gift has been oriented. His camel knows where the water is and is walking toward it, one step at a time, without the fever.

The Companions understood this immediately. Hassan ibn Thabit, the Prophet's own poet, composed verses that were inseparable from his *fiʿl*. The satires he launched against those who mocked the Prophet ﷺ were not in a different moral category from his prayers — they descended in the same direction, toward the same destination. The words and the life moved together.

[PAUSE]

So the surah does not end with a condemnation of poetry. It ends with a **reorientation of it**. The gift is neutral. The direction is everything.

And that is, perhaps, the final response to the Quraysh accusation:

*You said he was a poet. You used that word to file him alongside the wanderers, the valley-dwellers, the ones whose words go nowhere and serve whoever needs serving this season. But look at the one thing that separates the poet of truth from the poet of the market: the relationship between what he says and where he is going. Check the direction of his life. Check what his speech is for. And then tell me: is this man wandering in every valley? Or has he descended, once, all the way to one sea, and stopped?*

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING

### Speech Is a Direction

This passage contains one of the most compressed anthropological insights in the Quran. It was never only about poets.

It is about the **relationship between language and life**.

We are creatures who speak before we act, who conceptualize before we move, who articulate before we commit. Language is ahead of life. That is not a bug. It is what makes human moral development possible — we can speak ourselves into better versions of ourselves, and the speaking can pull the living forward.

But that capacity requires **maintenance**. The gap between word and deed must be watched. It can widen so gradually that you don't notice — until one day you realize that your words are a costume you wear, a performance you give, fully disconnected from anything happening in your chest.

The wandering poet is the person who let the gap become the whole. Who found that the words themselves were satisfying enough, that the applause of the *ghāwūn* was reinforcing enough, that he never actually needed to close the distance. And — as al-Muyassar observed — eventually his words stopped merely failing to reflect his life. They began actively inverting it: praising what he knew to be hollow, diminishing what he knew to be true, because that is what the valley required.

The prophet is the person who kept walking toward what he said.

And the believing poet — the *exception* of 26:227 — is the person who took a gift that could have wandered anywhere, anchored it to something that never moves, and let the anchor pull the gift into direction. Every *wādī* still calls. The thirst is still there. But the camel knows its water. It does not run.

One last question to sit with.

The Quran asks: *Have you not seen that in every valley they wander?*

*Alam tara.* Have you not seen.

The question is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ, yes. But you are reading it. And the question reaches you.

*Have you not seen?*

Have you seen it in your feed, where every crisis gets the language of moral emergency and the attention span of a Tuesday? Have you seen it in your own conversations, where you can hold forth with conviction on things you will forget you said? Have you seen it in your commitments, where the words were real when you made them, and then the valley changed, and the next valley's words were equally real?

Have you seen it in yourself?

The Quran does not ask this to shame you. It asks it because **seeing** is the beginning of correction. You cannot close a gap you have not looked at. You cannot stop wandering until you admit you are in a valley.

Look.
