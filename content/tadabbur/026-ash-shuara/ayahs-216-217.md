# When They Disobey You: The Permission to Stop Carrying What Was Never Yours

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 216-217

**Arabic:**
> فَإِنْ عَصَوْكَ فَقُلْ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِّمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ ۝ وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى الْعَزِيزِ الرَّحِيمِ

**Transliteration:**
> Fa-in ʿaṣawka fa-qul innī barīʾun mimmā taʿmalūn. Wa-tawakkal ʿalā-l-ʿAzīzi-r-Raḥīm.

**Translation:**
> "And if they disobey you, then say: 'Indeed, I am free of what you do.' And rely upon the Mighty, the Merciful."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind These Words

Before we open these two ayahs, we have to understand where in the surah we are standing — and, more precisely, who "they" are.

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is one of the most emotionally heavy surahs in the entire Quran. It is over two hundred verses long, and most of it is taken up by a single, repeating pattern. A prophet is sent. He calls his people. He pleads with them. He warns them. He pours himself out for them. And they refuse.

Nuh — for almost a thousand years.
Hud — to the people of 'Ad.
Salih — to Thamud.
Lut — to a city that mocked him.
Shu'ayb — to people who treated him like a weakling.
Musa — to a king who claimed to be God.

The surah opens with Allah's words to the Prophet ﷺ that almost stop the heart:

> *"Perhaps you will kill yourself with grief that they are not believers."* (26:3)

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. Allah is telling His Messenger ﷺ that he is so consumed with sadness over his people's rejection that he is, almost literally, destroying himself from the inside. And then Allah spends two hundred verses showing him: *every prophet before you carried this exact weight. Every single one. And every single one was rejected — at first, by most, sometimes by all.*

Then, just a few verses before 216, the register shifts. Allah does not address the Prophet ﷺ about all of humanity, or all of Arabia, or all of Quraysh. He narrows it: *"And warn your nearest kindred."* (26:214)

This is the direct antecedent of our two ayahs. The classical commentators are explicit and unanimous on this point. Al-Tabari states it precisely: *"If they disobey you, O Muhammad — your nearest kindred whom you were commanded to warn, and who refused and insisted on remaining in the worship of idols and in associating partners with ar-Rahman."* Ibn Kathir's section on these verses carries the heading: *The Command to warn His Tribe of near Kindred.* The "they" of 26:216 is not primarily a general audience of skeptics. It is the Prophet's own family. His own blood.

This changes everything about the emotional temperature of the verse.

Because all those stories of Nuh and Musa and Lut — they prepared the Prophet ﷺ for rejection *in principle*. But now the instruction is about his own tribe. His relatives. The people he grew up with, shared meals with, whose faces he knew. The grief that was already described as nearly killing him — imagine how that grief intensifies when the specific person who might refuse is your uncle. Your cousin. The people from whose lineage you came.

There is a kind of grief that comes specifically from that. It is different from the grief of strangers refusing you. When strangers refuse, you can tell yourself: *they don't know me.* But when the people who know you most refuse — the people who watched you grow up, who saw who you were before the revelation, who had called you *al-Amīn* their whole lives — the refusal carries a different weight. It touches the question: *is what I am offering real?*

This is the climate. This is the soil. This is what the Prophet ﷺ is feeling when, near the end of the surah, Allah turns directly to him and gives him these two ayahs.

These are not ayahs about strategy. They are not ayahs about how to win an argument. They are ayahs about **what to do with your heart** when you have done everything you could and the people closest to you in the world still walked the other way.

### The Hook

Notice something strange before we even begin.

Allah does not say: *"If they disobey you, try harder. Speak more clearly. Find a better approach."*

He says something else entirely. Something that, if we read it carelessly, sounds almost like Allah is telling the Prophet ﷺ to give up.

But it is not that. It is something far more profound. It is Allah teaching His beloved — and through him, every person who has ever carried someone they could not save — **a kind of release that does not require the other person to change.**

Stay with me. Because the architecture of these twelve words is going to show us how Allah unmakes the weight that crushes the carer.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The *Fa* That Changes Everything

Before we look at the first word of the ayah, we have to understand a tiny grammatical particle that English does not really have.

In Arabic, there is a letter called **fa** (ف). It is a single sound. It can be translated as "then," or "so," or "and so." But that translation flattens it. Because the **fa** in Arabic does something specific. It signals **immediate consequence**. It says: *what came before caused what comes after, and there is no gap between them.*

Compare it with another particle, **wa** (و), which just means "and." If I say "I went to the store *wa* I bought bread" — that's a list. Two things happened.

But if I say "I went to the store *fa* I bought bread" — now there is a logic. The going caused the buying. The second thing flows from the first.

Hold that in mind. Because the ayah opens with a *fa*:

> فَإِنْ عَصَوْكَ
> *Fa-in ʿaṣawka*
> "**And if** they disobey you..."

Technically, this *fa* is the particle of the conditional response — the *fa* of *jawāb al-sharṭ*. Its grammatical job is to introduce the consequence clause that follows *in*. That is its formal, local function. But in a surah that has spent two hundred verses building toward this precise moment — every prophet's story, every pattern of rejection and grief — even the conditional response particle arrives weighted with that accumulated history. The structure does its local grammatical work; the context gives it its emotional force. As a reader, you feel the *fa* as if it said: *as a consequence of everything you have just witnessed, if they disobey you...*

[PAUSE]

Now look at the next word: **in** (إِنْ). This is the conditional particle in Arabic — "if." But Arabic has *two* main words for "if." One is **idha** (إذا), which is used for things that are expected to happen. "When the sun rises" — that's *idha*, because the sun will rise.

The other is **in** (إِنْ), which is used for things that are *uncertain*, things that *might or might not happen*.

Look what Allah does. He does not say *idhā ʿaṣawka* — "when they disobey you," as if it is guaranteed. He says **in ʿaṣawka** — "**if** they disobey you."

Here is what is remarkable about this. The Prophet ﷺ has just been shown two hundred verses of prophets being rejected. The historical pattern is overwhelming. If anyone could justify saying "*when* they disobey you," it would be Allah at this moment, after all that evidence.

But Allah uses **in**. The uncertain "if."

Why? Because Allah is not letting the historical pattern foreclose the present. He is not letting the Prophet ﷺ walk into the conversation with his nearest kindred already convinced they will refuse him. The door is still open. Their disobedience is *not yet a fact*. It is only a possibility.

Even after Nuh's thousand years. Even after Pharaoh. Even after every story in this surah. Allah still holds the future of the Prophet's ﷺ own family as **uncertain**.

That is mercy hiding inside grammar.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Barī'un Mimmā Ta'malūn* — The Word for Release

Now we come to the heart of the linguistic mystery. The Prophet ﷺ is told what to say *if* they disobey:

> فَقُلْ إِنِّي بَرِيءٌ مِّمَّا تَعْمَلُونَ
> *Fa-qul innī barīʾun mimmā taʿmalūn*
> "Then say: 'Indeed, I am **free** of what you do.'"

The word that gets translated as "free" is **barīʾ** (بَرِيء). And this word is one of the most precise and most misunderstood words in the Quranic vocabulary.

The root is **b-r-ʾ** (ب-ر-ء). And from this root come three words you may already know:

- **Barīʾ** — innocent, free, disassociated
- **Bāriʾ** — the Maker, the One who brings into being from nothing (one of Allah's names, *al-Bāriʾ*)
- **Buriʾa** — to be cured, healed, to recover from a sickness

Stop and feel that.

The same root that means *to be cured of an illness* is the root that means *to be free of someone's wrongdoing*. The Arabic linguistic mind heard these as the same kind of event. Recovery from disease, and disassociation from another person's bad action — both are described by the same verb.

Why?

Because in classical Arabic, **barī'a** describes a separation in which **two things that were once joined are now cleanly apart, and what remains is whole**. The fever leaves the body. The body is whole. The contamination is gone. The body is *barī'*.

When the Prophet ﷺ is told to say *innī barīʾun mimmā taʿmalūn* — "I am *barī'* of what you do" — Allah is not telling him to be angry at them. Allah is not telling him to curse them or hate them. Allah is telling him: **their actions are not your sickness. You are not infected by their refusal. What they do does not stick to you.**

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, in his commentary on this phrase, uses a formulation worth pausing over. He says the Prophet ﷺ is to declare himself free *min ʿibādat al-aṣnām wa-maʿṣiyat bāriʾ al-anām* — from the worship of idols and from disobedience toward the Creator of all creation. Notice what al-Tabari has done. The word he uses for God is **al-Bāriʾ** — the Creator — the very same root as **barīʾ**. The disassociation (*barā'ah*) is specifically from the disobedience of the Creator (*al-Bāriʾ*). The root echoes across the clause like a tuning fork struck twice. To declare yourself *barīʾ* is to align yourself with the *Bāriʾ* — to stand on the side of the One who brought all things into being, rather than on the side of those who worship what cannot create anything at all.

Al-Muyassar similarly grounds the disassociation in *al-shirk wa-l-ḍalāl* — from their associating partners with Allah and from their straying. The barā'ah is not a general disassociation from "whatever they might happen to do." It is anchored in a specific, grave refusal: the refusal of the Prophet's own kin to worship the One who made them, after being warned by their closest relative.

Now notice what Allah does *not* say. He does not say *innī barīʾun minkum* — "I am free of *you*." He says *innī barīʾun **mimmā** taʿmalūn* — "I am free of **what you do**."

The disassociation is not from the people. It is from their actions.

This is staggering. Because the Prophet ﷺ — who, just a few verses earlier in this surah, was almost killing himself with grief over these very people — is being told: *You can love them and not carry their actions. You can want their good and not be responsible for their refusal. The two were never the same thing.*

[PAUSE]

And then there is the word **innī** (إِنِّي) — "*indeed* I am." That **inna** is what Arabic grammarians call a particle of *tawkīd* — emphasis, affirmation. It underlines the truth of what follows. On one reading — and this is perhaps the most searching way to receive it — the emphasis is not only directed outward, toward the kin, as a statement of position. It is also directed inward, toward the speaker himself. The Prophet ﷺ does not feel free of it. His heart is still with them. His instinct is to keep carrying. The **inna** gives him a sentence to say with his tongue that his heart is being trained to learn. *Indeed. Indeed. Even though you feel responsible. Indeed you are not.*

This is how Allah teaches the Prophet's ﷺ heart through speech. Not by arguing him out of the feeling. By giving him a true sentence to say until the truth lands.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Al-'Azīz Ar-Rahīm* — The Pairing That Holds Everything

Now we move to ayah 217. After telling the Prophet ﷺ what to say with his tongue, Allah tells him what to do with his heart:

> [ayah:26:217] وَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱلْعَزِيزِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ
> *Wa-tawakkal ʿalā-l-ʿAzīzi-r-Raḥīm*
> "And rely upon **the Mighty, the Merciful**."

Tawakkul (تَوَكُّل) is a word we will return to. But first, look at the names. **Al-ʿAzīz, ar-Raḥīm.** The Mighty, the Merciful.

Allah has ninety-nine beautiful names. He could have picked any of them to attach to this command. Why these two? And why in this order?

To answer this, we have to understand what each name means in its lexical depth.

**Al-ʿAzīz** comes from the root **ʿ-z-z** (ع-ز-ز). The base meaning of this root is *to be unreachable, to be unattainable, to be such that nothing can overcome you*. Classical lexicographers describe **'izzah** as the quality of being so elevated that no harm can reach you. A piece of land is called **'azīz** when it is so hard and rocky that no plow can break it. A person is **'azīz** when they cannot be coerced, cannot be defeated, cannot be diminished.

So **al-ʿAzīz** means: *the One whom nothing can overcome. The One whom nothing can stop. The One whose plan no rejection, no opposition, no enemy can frustrate.*

**Ar-Raḥīm** comes from the root **r-ḥ-m** (ر-ح-م). And here is something most listeners do not know. This root is the same root as **raḥim** — the womb. The Arabs heard mercy and the womb as the same word because of what happens in the womb: a small, vulnerable thing is held inside a larger thing that protects it, feeds it, warms it, and asks nothing in return. **Raḥmah** is that quality. Mercy that is *enveloping, protective, gratuitous, life-giving*.

Now look at what Allah has done. He has paired these two specific names at this specific moment.

Why?

Because the Prophet ﷺ — at this moment in the surah, after two hundred verses of rejection, now facing the prospect of his own family refusing him — needs **both** simultaneously, and **neither** alone is enough.

If Allah had said only *tawakkal ʿalā-l-ʿAzīz* — "rely on the Mighty" — the message would be: *Do not worry. I will crush them.* And that is true. But that is not what the Prophet's ﷺ heart needed. He did not want them crushed. He was killing himself with grief because he wanted them *saved*.

If Allah had said only *tawakkal ʿalā-r-Raḥīm* — "rely on the Merciful" — the message would be: *Do not worry. I am gentle.* And that is true. But that alone could not hold the weight. Because mercy without might can be overcome. A mother's love cannot stop a tank. The Prophet ﷺ needed to know that the mercy was *unstoppable*.

So Allah pairs them. **Al-ʿAzīz, ar-Raḥīm.** *The mercy that nothing can overcome.* The kindness that has the universe behind it. The compassion that no rejection, no Pharaoh, no millennium of disbelief can frustrate.

Al-Tabari offers a precise unpacking of this pairing that sharpens what we have just said. He reads *al-ʿAzīz* here as specifically pointing toward God's might *fī naqmatihi min aʿdāʾihi* — in His retribution against His enemies — and *ar-Raḥīm* as His mercy *bi-man anāba ilayhi wa-tāba min maʿāṣīhi* — toward those who turn back to Him and repent of their sins. This is a remarkable rendering. The names are not abstract divine attributes floating independently of situation. They are active orientations: the *ʿizzah* faces outward toward opposition; the *raḥmah* opens inward toward those who return.

For the Prophet ﷺ — whose heart wants his family saved, not destroyed — this is consoling at precisely the deepest level. He is told to rely on One whose might will not be overcome by their stubbornness, but whose mercy is turned toward *anyone who turns back*. The door, again, is not closed. The *ʿAzīz* cannot be frustrated by their resistance. The *Raḥīm* will receive them if they come.

Notice the order. **ʿAzīz** comes first, then **Raḥīm**. The might is the container; the mercy is what is held inside it. Allah's strength is not threatening to His mercy — it is what *protects* His mercy from being broken by the world. A mercy that could be thwarted by human stubbornness would be a small mercy. But a mercy carried by the Unconquerable — that is a mercy worth resting your heart upon.

[PAUSE]

This is what the Prophet ﷺ is being told to lean on. Not just power. Not just kindness. The two together, in that order. Power *in service of* kindness. The unstoppable in service of the gentle.

Because this is exactly what the situation requires. The Prophet ﷺ has been pouring himself out *in mercy*. And he has been crushed *by his lack of power* to make people believe. Allah is showing him: *the mercy you want for them is not yours to deliver. It is Mine. And My mercy cannot be stopped — not by their refusal, not by your weakness, not by the long pattern of rejection you have just been shown.*

The pairing is the cure for the grief.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

We have looked at three things. The *fa* and the *in* that frame the conditional — structurally local in function, emotionally weighted by everything preceding them. The *barīʾ* that disassociates the carer from the action without disassociating from the person — a healing rooted in the same triliteral as the Creator's own name. And the name-pair *al-ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm* — might turned against opposition, mercy turned toward those who return, the two together forming a shelter that human resistance cannot collapse.

These are not three separate observations. They are three aspects of one single movement: Allah teaching His Prophet ﷺ **how to love people without being destroyed by them**.

The *in* says: the door is not yet closed. Love them as if they might still come.

The *barīʾ* says: their refusal is not your disease. Love them without carrying what is not yours to carry.

The *ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm* says: the mercy you cannot deliver — Someone else can. Rest it there.

[PAUSE]

Now watch how these twelve words accomplish something that centuries of human philosophy have struggled to formulate: a way to remain in loving relationship with someone you cannot change, without being destroyed by the gap between who they are and who you need them to be. The Quran calls it *barā'ah mimmā yaʿmalūn* and *tawakkul ʿalā-l-ʿAzīzi-r-Raḥīm*. The clinical tradition might call it boundaries and surrender. But these twelve words do it in a grammar that ties release to mercy and trust to an actual Person — One who has been watching the whole time.

## PART 2: WHAT THESE AYAHS ARE DOING

Before we go to the application, pause and ask: *what kind of instruction is this?*

Because it is unusual. Most Quranic instructions to the Prophet ﷺ about his people are about what to *do* — preach, warn, remind, be patient. This ayah is about what to *say* and *feel*. *Fa-qul*: then say. *Wa-tawakkal*: and rely. The instruction is verbal and then dispositional. Say these words. Orient your heart here.

This tells us something. There are situations in human experience where the problem is not that you have not tried hard enough or found the right words. The problem is that you have tried everything, and the weight of not succeeding is still on you. For that situation — a situation the Prophet ﷺ was in, and that every sincere carer eventually reaches — the Quran does not give a new tactic. It gives a posture. It gives a sentence to say and a Being to lean against.

### The Specific Weight of the Nearest Kindred

We noted at the outset that "they" in 26:216 refers to the *ʿashīra al-aqrabūn* — the nearest kindred, warned in 26:214. This specificity is not incidental. It is load-bearing.

It was the Prophet's own family who, in the early years, were the hardest case. His uncle Abū Lahab is the subject of an entire surah of the Quran. The gathering at Jabal al-Safa — where, according to the reports, the Prophet ﷺ called his relatives one by one, clan by clan — ended with his closest kin turning away. The people who knew him most intimately, who had every reason to trust the one they had called *al-Amīn* for his entire life, many of them would not follow.

When the Quran says *"warn your nearest kindred"* and then immediately prepares him for the possibility of their disobedience, it is not being pessimistic. It is being pastoral. It is acknowledging, in advance, the particular texture of that grief — and then giving specific provision for it.

The broadened application — every person who has ever carried someone they could not save — is a real and valid extension of these ayahs. Human beings recognize this structure across generations. But that extension stands on a foundation that is more intimate and more painful than a general pastoral message. It stands on a specific historical moment: a man calling his own relatives by name, watching them turn away, and being given by God two lines to say and one place to rest his heart.

### On the Architecture of Release

Look at the structure of the two ayahs again.

Ayah 216: **What to say.** *Fa-qul innī barīʾun mimmā taʿmalūn.*
Ayah 217: **Where to look.** *Wa-tawakkal ʿalā-l-ʿAzīzi-r-Raḥīm.*

Speech, then orientation. External declaration, then internal disposition. The two always come together in the Quran's practical guidance. Because speech without conviction does not hold, and conviction without expression has nowhere to land. Allah gives the Prophet ﷺ both the words and the direction his soul needs to turn.

And this is the order of the healing. First the release — the words that name what is actually true, even when the heart has not caught up to them. *I am not responsible for your choices.* Then the turn — the direction of the heart toward the One whose might and mercy cover everything the human carer cannot cover.

You cannot do the second without first doing the first. The *tawakkul* has nowhere to go if the carer is still holding what was never theirs to hold. The *barā'ah* is not an abandonment — it is a prerequisite. You empty your hands, and then you place what you emptied them of into hands that can actually hold it.

[PAUSE]

### He Sees You

There is one more insight from the classical tradition that belongs here, and it reframes the command of *tawakkul* in a way that removes any abstraction from it.

Al-Muyassar, commenting on this passage, connects the command of reliance in 26:217 to what follows in the very next verses — the description of Allah seeing the Prophet ﷺ when he stands alone in the night for prayer, seeing his movements among those who prostrate alongside him, hearing his recitation, knowing his intention before it is formed into words.

The connection al-Muyassar is drawing is this: the One you are commanded to rely upon — *tawakkal ʿalayhi* — is the same One who has been watching you through everything. The *ʿAzīz ar-Raḥīm* is the *Samīʿ* — the Hearing — of your recitation in darkness, and the *ʿAlīm* — the Knowing — of your intention and your effort.

This reframes *tawakkul* entirely. We sometimes understand reliance upon God as a psychological exercise — *tell yourself it will be okay, let go of what you can't control.* But the Quran's tawakkul is grounded in something far more concrete: **Allah has been watching**. The prayers at night. The careful words with your family. The years of trying before the disobedience even arrived. None of it has been lost into an indifferent universe. The One you are commanded to lean on is not a stranger to your situation. He is the witness of every moment of what you have poured out in trying — and the command to rely on Him arrives from the position of someone who has seen all of it.

That is not generic reassurance. That is a specific, witnessed relationship. The *tawakkul* is not a step into the dark. It is a step toward someone who has been standing there the whole time.

## PART 3: LIVING INSIDE THESE AYAHS

### The Permission

These two ayahs contain something rare in all of human literature: **explicit divine permission to stop carrying what was never yours to carry.**

Not permission to stop caring. Not permission to give up on people. But permission to locate the responsibility correctly — to return it to the One who actually holds it — and then to rest.

The Prophet ﷺ was told this at a moment when he was being consumed by grief over people he could not save, people from his own household and lineage. Every person who has ever loved someone into an illness, or a faith, or a better life — and watched them refuse — knows something of that consumption. The weight does not feel like a weight you chose to pick up. It feels like it grew on you. Like it is part of you. To put it down feels like betrayal.

But the Quran says: it is not betrayal. It is fidelity to the truth of what is actually yours and what is not.

Their *ʿamal* — their action, their choice — is not your ʿamal. You are *barīʾ* of it. Not because you don't love them. Because love and responsibility are not the same thing, and confusing them is not nobility. It is a form of grief that the Quran wants to heal.

[PAUSE]

### The Posture

Notice what the second ayah does not say. It does not say: *tawakkal* — and then list what you can expect to happen. It does not say: *rely on Allah, and your family will believe.* It does not say: *rely on Allah, and the pain will go away.*

It says: *rely on the ʿAzīz, ar-Raḥīm.*

The comfort in this verse is not a promised outcome. It is a direction to face. A character to trust. **This is one of the Quran's most honest consolations** — it does not give a guarantee about how the story ends. It gives an orientation toward the One who holds the ending, and whose might and mercy together make that One worthy of being trusted with it.

For the Prophet ﷺ, many of his nearest kindred did not believe in his lifetime. Some of the most painful refusals were permanent in the earthly sense. The *tawakkul* of 26:217 was not rewarded with their conversion. It was rewarded with something the Quran values more deeply: a heart that did not break under a weight it was never equipped to carry alone. And it was rewarded with the knowledge that al-Tabari captures in his reading of these names — that the *ʿAzīz* would not be outmaneuvered by their stubbornness, and the *Raḥīm* remained open to any one of them who turned back, even at the last.

[PAUSE]

### A Sentence to Return To

We close with the same place we began: the instruction itself. *Fa-qul innī barīʾun mimmā taʿmalūn.*

It is a sentence. A real sentence, with breath and sound. The Prophet ﷺ is told to say it — not just think it, not just feel it, but put it in the air between himself and the situation. There is something in the speaking of truth that the Quran consistently returns to. Not because saying it makes it immediately felt, but because truth said aloud begins its work in a way that truth held silently does not.

If you are carrying someone else's choices — if you are bent under the weight of what another person is doing with their life, a person you love, a person whose face you know — there is a sentence Allah gave His Prophet ﷺ for exactly this moment. It is worth saying out loud. Not as abandonment. Not as anger. As a statement of what is actually, ontologically true: *their action is not mine. I am not its cause. I am not its cure. I am barīʾ of it — as the body is barīʾ of a fever once the fever has left.*

And then: *Wa-tawakkal ʿalā-l-ʿAzīzi-r-Raḥīm.*

Turn. Face the direction that can hold what you cannot. Not into the void. Not into uncertainty. Into the Mighty — whose might is turned against every force that would frustrate mercy — and the Merciful — whose mercy is turned toward anyone who returns, even now, even still, even after everything.

These are twelve words. They have been holding the weight of human grief for fourteen hundred years.

They will hold yours.
