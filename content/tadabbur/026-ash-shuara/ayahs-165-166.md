# What You Were Made For: Lut and the Geometry of the Self

## Introduction

Let's place these two ayat in front of us before we say anything about them.

> **أَتَأْتُونَ ٱلذُّكْرَانَ مِنَ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ ۝ وَتَذَرُونَ مَا خَلَقَ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُم مِّنْ أَزْوَٰجِكُم ۚ بَلْ أَنتُمْ قَوْمٌ عَادُونَ**

> *A-ta'tūna 'dh-dhukrāna mina 'l-ʿālamīn ۝ wa tadharūna mā khalaqa lakum rabbukum min azwājikum ۚ bal antum qawmun ʿādūn.*

> **"Do you approach the males among the worlds — and leave what your Lord has created for you of your mates? Rather, you are a people who have crossed the line."**

[PAUSE]

Before we touch a single word, we need to step inside the world these words first entered.

Imagine a small cluster of cities on a high plain east of the Dead Sea. Trade routes pass through. Caravans rest. The land is fertile — Allah will later describe it as having been "turned upside down," which means it was once the kind of place worth turning. These are not destitute people. They are prosperous, comfortable, and — by their own reckoning — sophisticated. They have developed a culture so confident in itself that when a messenger arrives and names what they are doing, their answer is not argument but threat. The Quran records it in their own words: *If you do not cease, O Lut, you will be one of those driven out.*

And the messenger — Lut, peace be upon him — is not a stranger to them. He is the nephew of Ibrahim. He has lived among them long enough that they know his face, his family, his honesty. He is one of those rare prophets sent not to a people who never knew of God, but to a people who had calcified into a particular distortion of the human form.

Now place yourself inside Surah Ash-Shu'ara, where these ayat live. This surah is built like a hall of mirrors. Seven prophets walk through it — Musa, Ibrahim, Nuh, Hud, Salih, Lut, Shu'ayb — and each of their stories ends with the same closing cadence. Allah is showing us that prophethood is not a series of unrelated events. It is one long argument the heavens have been having with the earth. And in that argument, each prophet is given one *specific distortion* to confront. Hud confronts pride in power. Salih confronts arrogance toward the sign. Shu'ayb confronts dishonesty in trade.

Lut is given the body.

[PAUSE]

Here's what I want you to feel before we begin. The classical commentators do not soften what Lut is naming. Al-Tabari's gloss on *a-ta'tūna* is forensically specific — there is no euphemism in the classical reading. Al-Muyassar grounds the prohibition in two concrete purposes — enjoyment and procreation — that the wives were created for. Ibn Kathir describes Lut as forbidding a particular act and directing his people back to their wives. That concreteness belongs in our reading; we should not abstract away from it.

But these two ayat extend the confrontation beyond the act alone. Alongside what they are doing, Lut names what they are *leaving* — and to name the thing being left, the ayah must identify what it is: *mā khalaqa lakum rabbukum*, what your Lord created *for you*. It is that phrase — the design-bearing gift — that extends the confrontation into deeper territory. What does it mean that your Lord crafted something for you and you abandoned it as if worthless? The question opens onto the architecture of every human desire: *what is a human being for?* About whether the form Allah gave you is information or whether it is raw material you may reshape against itself. About whether the word "leaving" can describe something that was never yours to leave in the first place.

And the entire weight of this question — the entire force of what makes these two ayat shake — rests on three words Allah will choose with terrifying precision. One verb of approach. One verb of abandonment. One word for what was being abandoned.

If we can hear those three words the way the original listener heard them, we will understand something not just about Lut's people, but about the architecture of every human desire we have ever felt pull us in a direction we suspect is not quite us.

Let's begin.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Verb of Approach — *A-ta'tūna*

Before we look at this verb, I want to teach you something about Arabic that English does not preserve.

In English, when we say "you do something," we usually mean: you perform the action. The verb is the action itself. But Arabic often distinguishes between the action and the *approach toward* the action. There are different verbs for "to do" and "to come at" and "to enter into" and "to descend upon." Each one carries a different posture of the self.

Now hear the everyday version. If I tell you, "He hit his brother," that is one report. If I tell you, "He went looking for his brother to hit him," that is a different report — even if the result is identical. The second report says something about *intention*, about *seeking out*, about a deliberate movement toward.

Hold that distinction.

Allah opens these ayat with the verb **أَتَأْتُونَ** — *a-ta'tūna*. From the root **ʾ-t-y**, which means *to come, to come to, to approach.* It does not primarily mean "to do." It means *to come at*. To go toward. To seek something out and arrive at it.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

Allah is not asking, "Do you commit this?" He is asking, "Do you *approach* this? Do you *come at* this?" The verb pictures movement. It pictures someone leaving where they were, crossing a distance, and arriving at a destination they chose.

Notice something. This phrasing requires that the listener admit two things at once. First: that there was a place they were before. Second: that they had to *go somewhere* to get to where they now are. The verb itself testifies that this was not where they started. It was a destination they walked toward.

Here is the rhetorical force. If Allah had said *afʿaltum* — "did you do?" — the listener could have shrugged and said, "Yes, we did. So what?" But the verb *a-ta'tūna* will not let them shrug. It pictures them in motion. It says: *you came from somewhere else to get here.* It implies a previous state — an origin — that this present state is a departure from.

And this is the *interrogative* form, *a-ta'tūna*. Allah is not informing them of what they do. He is asking them, in front of themselves, to confirm what they do. Classical scholars note that an interrogative in Arabic, when posed by one who already knows the answer, is rarely a request for information. It is a mirror held up. It is a way of saying: *say it out loud. Hear yourself say it.*

The classical mufassirun held the mirror exact. Al-Tabari glosses *ta'tūna* in this verse without reaching for euphemism — the verb points at a specific act, and the interrogative forces it into the open. There is a reason Lut did not choose a softer word. The precision of the verb is itself an act of witness: it names where they have arrived, and the question compels them to confirm it in front of themselves.

[PAUSE]

So before we have moved past the first word, the architecture of the question has already done two things to the listener. It has placed them in motion — they came from somewhere. And it has put them in front of a mirror — say it yourself.

This is what tadabbur means. The grammar is already *doing* the work the meaning will later reveal.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Verb of Leaving — *Tadharūna*

Now the second verb. And here Arabic is going to give us something that we, in English, will struggle to feel without help.

Teach yourself this concept first. In Arabic, there are at least three different verbs for what English flattens into "to leave."

There is **taraka** — to leave, to abandon, in the general sense. You leave a city. You leave a meeting. The verb is neutral and capacious. It just means: you are no longer there.

There is **faraqa** — to part from, to separate from. This carries the sense of a relationship that was once connected and is now divided.

And there is **wadhara** — the root of *tadharūna* — which is the most specific of the three. Lexicographers note that **wadhara carries the meaning of *leaving something behind out of disregard*, *leaving it as if it were of no value*, or *leaving it neglected.*** It is not the leaving of someone who must go. It is the leaving of someone who *judges what is left as not worth keeping*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this.

Imagine you walk past a hundred-dollar bill on the sidewalk and keep walking. That is *wadhara*. You did not fail to see it. You saw it, and you walked past, because — by some calculation you made — it did not earn your bending down. *Wadhara* is leaving with a verdict embedded inside it. The verdict is: *this is beneath me; this is not the thing I want.*

Now hear what Allah does next.

> **وَتَذَرُونَ مَا خَلَقَ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُم مِّنْ أَزْوَٰجِكُم**
> *wa tadharūna mā khalaqa lakum rabbukum min azwājikum*
> *And you leave-as-if-worthless what your Lord created for you of your mates.*

Look at what is being left.

It is not a thing.

It is a *creation* of *your Lord* that was *for you*.

The verb *wadhara* — leaving as if worthless — is being applied to something the ayah explicitly identifies as a divine gift, made by *Rabb*, made *lakum*, made *for you*. The grammar is forcing a collision. You cannot use this verb against this object without indicting yourself. The verb says "you treated it as worthless." The object says "this was given to you by your Lord, fashioned for you, intended for you." The collision is the message.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah did not say "you turned away from your wives." He chose the verb that meant: *you treated as worthless what your Lord crafted for you.* The verb is the indictment. The object is the proof of the verb's severity.

Al-Tabari is precise about what specifically is being left. His gloss on *min azwājikum* identifies it as the lawful intimate relationship Allah had made permitted for them — something sanctioned, rendered wholesome, placed within the boundary of what He allowed. The *wadhara* is therefore not the disregarding of a vague category. It is the disregarding of what your Lord specifically made lawful for you. That changes the weight of the verb. When something is left as worthless, it matters whether it was an unclaimed stone or a deliberate gift placed in your hands. The classical reading says: it was the latter.

[PAUSE]

And notice the asymmetry. We have two verbs side by side. One — *a-ta'tūna* — describes movement *toward* something. The other — *tadharūna* — describes movement *away from* something. Together they map the geometry of the human heart: every "yes" to one thing is a "no" to another. To approach the manufactured destination is, by the same motion, to leave the original gift behind.

This is a principle the Quran teaches everywhere, and it is teaching it here through a verbal pair. *Every desire is dual.* What you go toward, you go away from something. There is no neutral motion. The body that walks east is also walking away from west. The heart that fills with one love is, by that same filling, emptier of another.

Lut's people did not just *do* something. They *abandoned*, with disregard, what their Lord had made for them. The act and the abandonment are one motion seen from two sides.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Phrase That Anchors Everything — *Khalaqa lakum rabbukum*

Now we come to the phrase the entire two ayat hinge on. Five words in Arabic.

> **مَا خَلَقَ لَكُمْ رَبُّكُم**
> *mā khalaqa lakum rabbukum*
> *what your Lord created for you*

Teach the concept first. In Arabic, the preposition *li-* (here as *lakum*) carries the sense of *for the benefit of*, *for the sake of*, *intended for*. It is not the preposition of mere location. It is the preposition of *purpose*. When Allah says he created something *lakum* — "for you" — he is saying not merely that the thing exists in your vicinity. He is saying: *this thing's existence is oriented toward your good.*

Now feel the construction.

*Khalaqa* — He created.
*Lakum* — for you.
*Rabbukum* — your Lord.

Three pieces of information packed inside five words. The act: creation. The orientation: for you. The actor: your Lord — the one who nurtures, sustains, raises you toward your maturity. Not "Allah" abstractly. Not "the Creator" technically. *Rabbukum.* The one who has been bringing you up since the moment of your existence.

Here is what this phrase does. It establishes that what Lut's people are abandoning is not just a thing in the world — it is part of the *order of creation itself*, designed by the one who is bringing them up, intended *for them* as an act of nurture.

[PAUSE]

Stay with the implication.

If I make something *for you* — say, I bake bread for my child — and the child throws it on the floor, the child has not just rejected bread. The child has rejected an act of love that took the form of bread. The bread was a vehicle. The thing being rejected is the *for-ness* of the bread. The intentionality. The orientation of one heart toward another.

When Allah says *khalaqa lakum rabbukum* — what your Lord *created for you* — He is naming the gift not in its substance but in its *orientation*. The wives, the mates, the spouses — these were not random by-products of evolution that humans then organized into pairs. They were *crafted*, *for you*, by the one *bringing you up*. The pairing is a message. The geometry is the gift.

Al-Muyassar is precise about what that gift encompasses. The wives were created for two purposes together: *enjoyment* and *procreation* — the pleasurable and the generative, bound into a single design. What is being abandoned through *wadhara* is not one function but the whole architecture of human flourishing. Both dimensions — the intimate and the generative — are named as the content of *lakum*, the "for you." To leave this is to leave both.

And to disregard the gift is, in a real sense, to disregard *the one who gave it*, because the gift was nothing but the form His care took.

[PAUSE]

Now read the absence.

Notice what Allah does *not* say. He does not say "you have left your wives." He does not say "you have left women." He says *mā khalaqa lakum rabbukum min azwājikum* — *what your Lord created for you of your mates.*

The thing being left is named not as a person but as a *creation-act* — *mā khalaqa*. Literally: *that-which-He-created.* Allah is making a structural point. What is being abandoned is not just a category of human beings. It is a layer of the created order. A piece of the design.

There is a textual detail from al-Tabari worth pausing on here. He records that in the reading of Ibn Masud, the verb in this phrase was not *khalaqa* — "created" — but *aslaḥa* — "made wholesome and right for you." *Mā aslaḥa lakum rabbukum min azwājikum.* Both readings are attested in the classical tradition, and they circle the same truth from slightly different angles. *Khalaqa* speaks to the act of creation — this design came from your Lord's hands. *Aslaḥa* speaks to the *fitness* of the design — it was not merely made, it was made *right*, calibrated, rendered wholesome for you specifically. If anything, the variant deepens the indictment: what is being abandoned through *wadhara* is not only a creation-act but a *rectification* — something shaped and fitted for their flourishing, then left in the dust as if it were nothing.

This is why the verse cannot be reduced to a sociological complaint or a cultural preference. The grammar refuses that reading. The thing being left is identified, in Allah's own words, as a designed, oriented, purpose-bearing piece of His creation made for the benefit of the people doing the leaving.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Three words. *A-ta'tūna* — you came at it. *Tadharūna* — you treated as worthless. *Khalaqa lakum rabbukum* — what your Lord made for you.

Together they map a single geometry. There was a state you started in. There was a state your Lord intended for you. You walked away from that intended state — treating it as worthless — toward a destination you sought out. The grammar is not describing a behavior. It is describing a *betrayal of a design*. And the betrayal is not described in moral language; it is described in *creational* language. The wrong is named not as "bad" but as "against what your Lord made."

Then the ayah delivers its verdict, and the word Allah chooses for it carries a specific weight the classical commentators do not miss. *ʿĀdūn* — from the root *ʿ-d-w*, which means to pass beyond, to overstep, to go further than a limit allows. Al-Muyassar reads it precisely: these are people who have crossed from what Allah has made lawful into what He has forbidden — who took the permitted, left it as worthless, and stepped over the line He drew. The word is a boundary word. Not "people who did wrong" in some general sense, but *people who transgressed a specific boundary* — the line between what the Lord made wholesome for them and what He did not.

The creational language gives way to a boundary language. First Allah named what you were made for. Then He named what you have become by leaving it.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.
