# The Word They Couldn't Help Using

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayat 36–39

---

## Introduction

### The Arabic

> قَالُوا أَرْجِهْ وَأَخَاهُ وَابْعَثْ فِي الْمَدَائِنِ حَاشِرِينَ
> يَأْتُوكَ بِكُلِّ سَحَّارٍ عَلِيمٍ
> فَجُمِعَ السَّحَرَةُ لِمِيقَاتِ يَوْمٍ مَّعْلُومٍ
> وَقِيلَ لِلنَّاسِ هَلْ أَنتُمْ مُّجْتَمِعُونَ

*Qālu arjih wa-akhāhu wab'ath fil-madā'ini hāshirīn. Ya'tūka bi-kulli sahhārin 'alīm. Fajumi'a as-saharatu li-mīqāti yawmin ma'lūm. Wa-qīla lin-nāsi hal antum mujtami'ūn.*

**They said, "Give him and his brother some time, and dispatch gatherers throughout the cities to bring you every master sorcerer, every consummate expert." So the sorcerers were assembled for the appointed time of a known day. And it was announced to the people, "Will you not come together?"**

---

### Rebuilding the World

The last thing Pharaoh said was a question.

Not an order. Not a command. A question. He had just watched a staff become a serpent before his eyes. He had watched a hand pulled from a robe emerge luminous white. And then — in a move every historian of power recognizes — he did what every ruler does when the ground shifts beneath them: he turned it outward. *This is a learned sorcerer,* he told his chiefs. *He wants to expel you from your own land. What do you advise?*

That question — *what do you advise?* — is not a casual consultation. It is the sound of a man who has just been disturbed at a depth he cannot afford to name. The chiefs around him have watched his face. They know what they saw there. And so they answer.

These four ayahs are their answer.

We are now in the engineering room of a crisis response. Pharaoh's advisors are not planning a debate. They are not preparing a rebuttal. They are building a spectacle — and these four verses are the blueprint. In precisely forty-four words, Allah shows us the full architecture of how earthly power, when genuinely threatened, manufactures its response.

What's remarkable is not what the advisors say. It is *how* they say it. Because the verb forms they reach for, the vocabulary they choose, the grammatical structures they fall into — all of it speaks a truth that the advisors themselves would never consciously admit.

The advisors are confessing. They just don't know it.

---

### The Hook

There is a single word in these four ayahs that Pharaoh's advisors did not have to use.

They could have used a different form. A simpler one. The same root, but a lighter construction. They used the heavy form — the professional form, the intensified form, the form you reach for when the thing you are dealing with is not ordinary.

Pharaoh had used the lighter form just one ayah earlier, when he labeled Musa.

His advisors needed the heavier form when they described what it would take to fight him.

[PAUSE]

That gap between the two words — a single morphological step — is one of the Quran's most precise psychological portraits. And once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — Two Words, One Root, and Everything in Between

Let us start where Pharaoh's advisors started: with the plan.

*"Bring me,"* they say to Pharaoh, *"every سَحَّارٍ عَلِيمٍ."*

That is the recommendation. Every *sahhār 'alīm*. We'll translate it in a moment. First, we need to understand what Arabic morphology is doing here — because without it, we'll read these words and keep walking, and we'll miss the whole thing.

Stay with me.

---

Arabic is a root-and-pattern language. Every word is built from a core of three consonants — a root — combined with a vowel-and-consonant pattern that determines function, intensity, and meaning. The same root can produce dozens of different words. And crucially, different patterns on the same root produce words that are *related* — but not equal. One can be much heavier than the other. Much more intense. Much more professional.

Think of it this way. If I say someone is a "cook," that means they cook food. But if I say they are a *chef* — with all the professional resonance that word carries — I am not just saying they cook food. I am saying that cooking is who they are. It is their vocation, their identity, their defining characteristic. You can be a cook by accident. You cannot be a chef by accident.

Arabic has this distinction built into its morphology. The basic pattern — *fā'il* (فَاعِل) — means "one who does the action." A sorcerer. Someone who practices sorcery. But the intensified pattern — *fa''āl* (فَعَّال) — means something much heavier: *one for whom this action is professional, habitual, identity-defining.* Not just a sorcerer — a **master sorcerer**. Not someone who performs magic — someone whose entire existence is organized around the mastery of illusion. The doubling of that middle consonant in the pattern is not cosmetic. It carries weight.

A *najjār* (نَجَّار) is not just someone who occasionally works with wood. He is a carpenter by profession. A *qattāl* (قَتَّال) is not someone who once harmed another person. He is a professional killer. The *fa''āl* pattern marks the person for whom the action is their defining vocation.

Now. Open the text.

One ayah earlier — in 26:34 — Pharaoh looked at Musa and said: **"إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَسَـٰحِرٌ عَلِيمٌ"** — *"Indeed this is a sāhir 'alīm."* A sorcerer. The basic form. *Fā'il.* Someone who works with magic.

Now look at what his advisors say. **"بِكُلِّ سَحَّارٍ عَلِيمٍ."** Every *sahhār 'alīm.* The *fa''āl* form. Not a sorcerer. A **master sorcerer**. The professional. The one for whom illusion is identity, not activity.

The root is identical: *س-ح-ر.* The pattern has been upgraded: from *sāhir* to *sahhār.*

[PAUSE]

Let that gap land.

Pharaoh labeled Musa with the basic form. He called him a sorcerer — *sāhir* — and in doing so, tried to domesticate what he had just witnessed. *This is a trick. A sleight of hand. A sorcerer's work.* He was managing the experience by naming it.

But when his advisors plan the response? They don't recommend bringing sorcerers. They recommend bringing *sahhārūn* — masters. Professionals. The extreme form.

Why the upgrade?

**Because the extremity of the response always reveals the depth of the threat.**

When you reach for maximum force in response to something you claim is minor, you have already told the truth about what you really think. Pharaoh's mouth said *sāhir.* His advisors' response said *sahhār.* And those two words, sitting in consecutive ayahs, tell us everything that Pharaoh's pride would never let him say aloud: *what we just witnessed is not ordinary. We don't fully understand it. We are afraid.*

Notice something else. The second word in both phrases is the same: *عَلِيمٌ / عَلِيمٍ.* Deeply knowing. The *fa'īl* pattern on the root *'ayn-lām-mīm* — the same root as Allah's name *Al-'Alīm* — indicates not surface familiarity but settled, penetrating, profound knowing. When used of a human being, it marks expertise at the deepest level.

Pharaoh labeled Musa as **deeply-knowing** in sorcery. His advisors say they need a **master-who-is-also-deeply-knowing** in sorcery.

Both adjectives are identical. The noun escalated. The knowing stayed the same — because they already concede Musa's depth of knowing. What they need is someone whose entire *being* is organized around sorcery at that same level of depth.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment.

What does this tell us? That the people in Pharaoh's circle — his advisors, his chiefs, the architects of the response — already know, somewhere beneath their loyalties, that they are not dealing with a trick. The *sahhār* response is too calibrated, too intentional, to have come from a place of genuine dismissal.

You do not mobilize your most extreme professionals to fight something you genuinely believe is simple.

### Element Two — The Mīqāt: When Sacred Vocabulary Is Conscripted for Spectacle

Now look at the third ayah.

**فَجُمِعَ السَّحَرَةُ لِمِيقَاتِ يَوْمٍ مَّعْلُومٍ**

*"So the sorcerers were assembled for the mīqāt of a known day."*

Most translations render *mīqāt* as "appointed time" — which is accurate, but undersells it. We need to understand what this word carries.

Every Arabic noun pattern carries a structural meaning beyond just its root. The pattern *mif'āl* (مِفعَال) typically denotes the place or fixed occasion of an action. It is the pattern for structured, official, dedicated coordination: the *mihrāb* (مِحرَاب) is the dedicated niche for prayer. The *mīzān* (مِيزَان) is the dedicated instrument for weighing.

And *mīqāt* (مِيقَات) — from the root *wāw-qāf-tā'* (و-ق-ت), meaning "time" — is the **fixed, appointed, dedicated meeting-time** between parties who have agreed to gather. Not just "a time," but *the time* — the scheduled, publicized, sacred coordinate in time for a specific encounter.

This word appears elsewhere in the Quran. And where it appears matters enormously.

In Surah Al-A'raf (7:143): **"وَلَمَّا جَاءَ مُوسَىٰ لِمِيقَاتِنَا"** — *"And when Musa came to Our mīqāt."* Allah speaking about Musa's own appointment at the mountain. The moment where Musa stood at the threshold of divine encounter, where he asked to see Allah, and where he received the Torah. That is a *mīqāt.*

In Surah Al-Wāqi'a (56:50): the mīqāt of the Day of Resurrection, when all of creation will be gathered before Allah. That is a *mīqāt.*

The word carries the weight of divinely-fixed encounter. It belongs to the vocabulary of the sacred and the ultimate.

And Pharaoh's advisors are using it for a magic show.

[PAUSE]

This is not accidental. This is the grammar of co-optation. When human power wants to give its theater the feeling of cosmic inevitability — when it wants its manufactured event to feel like destiny — it reaches for the vocabulary that belongs to genuine ultimacy. *Circle the date. This is not just an event. This is the moment.* The mīqāt.

Now look at the next word: *يَوْمٍ مَّعْلُومٍ* — "a known day." *Ma'lūm* is the passive participle from the same root as *'alīm* in the previous ayah: *'ayn-lām-mīm.* The day has been "made known" — announced, publicized, broadcast throughout Egypt. Pharaoh's apparatus has declared the date.

The same root echoes in both phrases — and it does so deliberately:
- The sorcerers must be *sahhār 'alīm* — masters of deep knowing.
- The day is *ma'lūm* — made known, published, announced.

**Knowing is Pharaoh's instrument.** The expertise of his sorcerers, the publicization of his event — both are wrapped in the vocabulary of *'ayn-lām-mīm.* Knowledge deployed for the purpose of performance. Knowing in service of spectacle.

But there is an irony gathering beneath the surface, one that will only fully emerge in the next passage: the sorcerers, who are the most *'alīm* people in Egypt regarding illusion, are about to have their knowledge shattered by something they cannot explain. The *ma'lūm* day will become famous not for Pharaoh's triumph, but for his exposure.

Allah uses the language of Pharaoh's confidence to underline the precision of his undoing.

### Element Three — The Grammar of Who Is Moved and Who Is Invited

There is a final linguistic feature in these four ayahs, and it may be the most quietly devastating of all.

Look at the verbs:

**Ayah 38:** *Fajumi'a as-saharatu* — "So the sorcerers **were assembled**." The passive voice. In Arabic, the passive (*al-mabnī lil-majhūl*, "built for the unknown") removes the grammatical subject — the one who performed the action — from the sentence. The agent disappears. We know *that* the sorcerers were gathered. We do not know *who* gathered them. The machinery is in motion, and no individual stands accountable for setting it in motion.

**Ayah 39:** *Wa-qīla lin-nāsi* — "And **it was said** to the people." Again the passive. Who said it? The state apparatus. The announcement system. The herald network reaching every village in Egypt. But no one person speaks — "it was said" is the grammar of a system too large and too faceless to attribute to any single actor.

The sorcerers were gathered. The people were told. The agent is absent in both cases. Pharaoh's machine operates without a face.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the contrast.

The sorcerers were *jumi'ū* — gathered, passive, moved. They did not choose to come. They were assembled by a force they could not refuse.

The people, however, are addressed differently: **هَلْ أَنتُمْ مُّجْتَمِعُونَ** — *"Will you not come together?"*

Look at this word: *mujtami'ūn.* This is not the passive form. This is the active participle of Form VIII: *ijtama'a* (اجتمع) — to gather oneself, to assemble together, to come together of one's own accord. Form VIII in Arabic typically carries a reflexive or self-directed quality. *Ijtama'a* is not "being gathered" — it is "gathering oneself," the voluntary, self-directed act of assembly.

And the question itself — *hal antum mujtami'ūn?* — is not a command. It is an interrogative of incitement. In Arabic grammar, *hal* in this construction serves as a rhetorical invitation: *"Won't you? Wouldn't it be right for you? Aren't you going to?"* It is not Pharaoh commanding the people. It is the propaganda machine *inviting* the people — making them feel that their attendance is their own free choice, their own expression of solidarity, their own participation in something momentous.

**The sorcerers are managed. The crowd is invited.**

Two different registers for two different groups. The specialists are handled — driven by imperative machinery, passive in the grammar, objects being moved. The public is made to feel like agents — addressed with voluntary vocabulary, questioned rather than commanded, given the feeling of choice.

This is the complete anatomy of a state spectacle.

### Bridge

Three linguistic observations, and they converge on a single reality.

The morphology of *sahhār* — heavier than the word Pharaoh himself used — tells us that the advisors' confidence is performance. The word *mīqāt* — borrowed from the vocabulary of sacred encounter — tells us that Pharaoh's theater wants to feel like destiny. And the grammar of passives-and-invitation tells us that the spectacle is engineered to make the crowd feel like participants while hiding the machine that produced it.

Allah is showing us not what Pharaoh says. He is showing us what Pharaoh's grammar says.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way power works, and why it has always worked this way.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — Escalation as Confession: The Psychology of Threatened Power

Think about the last time you dramatically overreacted.

Not a little. *Dramatically.* The situation called for a moderate response and you brought maximum force. You called in every resource. You made the problem much bigger in your response than it seemed to be in the facts. You can probably feel a version of this in your memory — some argument where you became far angrier than the words deserved, some situation where you mobilized more anxiety than the stakes required.

What was really happening in those moments?

The size of the response was confessing something the words weren't saying. The escalation was the honesty. The surface said "I'm fine, I just think you're wrong." The escalation said "I am shaken at a depth I cannot afford to acknowledge."

This is what Pharaoh's advisors do in these four ayahs. And it is what they cannot help doing.

Pharaoh stood in a throne room and watched things he could not explain. A staff became something alive. A hand came out glowing. His response was immediate and practiced: *This is sorcery. This is a trick. This man is a sorcerer.* He reached for the most available category — the one that domesticated the experience, made it manageable, kept it within a frame he controlled. *Sāhir.* Sorcerer.

If he genuinely believed it was a trick, the response would have been proportionate. Send one sorcerer. Send five. Demonstrate the trick and be done with it.

But his advisors don't say: "Send a sorcerer."

They say: *"Bring us every **sahhār 'alīm** you can find. Every one. Dispatch gatherers to every city in the land. Bring the most extreme professionals, the most deeply-knowing masters."*

**The extremity of the response is the confession.**

You cannot spend this much energy, mobilize this much apparatus, reach for this level of expertise — and simultaneously maintain that you are dealing with something ordinary. The *sahhār* response is a slip. The advisors are not lying exactly. They genuinely believe that maximum force is required. But the reason they believe that — the reason the *fa''āl* form emerges rather than the lighter *fā'il* — is that something in that throne room landed on all of them at a depth they cannot articulate.

There is a deep mercy in this observation: **truth always leaves a mark, even on those who deny it.**

Even Pharaoh's system cannot look at what Musa just did and remain entirely unaffected. The mark shows in the morphology. The disturbance shows in the escalation. The truth has penetrated even the most fortified minds — just not far enough, yet, to produce surrender.

[PAUSE]

This matters for how we understand our own resistances.

When we find ourselves escalating dramatically against an idea, a person, a truth — when we bring more force than the situation seems to require — it is worth asking: what has actually landed, beneath the defense? What has gotten through the walls that the walls don't want to admit got through?

The advisors' escalation is not unique to ancient Egypt. It is a description of every human encounter with a truth too large for our current frameworks. The first response is always to label it. *Sāhir.* The second response — when the label proves insufficient — is to escalate. *Bring the sahhārūn.* And somewhere in that escalation is the gap between what we say and what we know, at the depth we cannot quite name.

### Theme Two — The Mīqāt of Power: How Spectacle Manufactures Consent

There is a phenomenon you have experienced many times without necessarily having a name for it.

A major event is announced. The date is circled. Coverage begins weeks in advance — the stakes are laid out, the participants introduced, the historical significance established. By the time the day arrives, you feel a certain inevitability about it. The event feels like it was always going to happen. Like it is not manufactured but *destined.* You find yourself watching — even if you weren't initially interested — because something in the atmosphere has convinced you that this is a moment that matters.

This is how power stages reality.

Pharaoh's advisors have built exactly this in four ayahs. Not just a competition between sorcerers and a prophet. A *mīqāt* — a fixed, sacred-feeling, publicized appointment with a *known day.* The announcement goes out: this is the day. The sorcerers are assembled. The crowd is invited. By the time the morning arrives, all of Egypt is gathered — not because they were ordered there, but because the atmosphere created a sense that anyone who wasn't there would be missing something decisive.

[PAUSE]

Notice the two-track system within these ayahs.

**Track one: the specialists.** *Fajumi'a as-saharatu* — the sorcerers were assembled. Passive. Managed. They didn't choose to come; they were moved. Pharaoh's reach into the cities is not a request — it is a requisition. The *hāshirīn* (the gatherers dispatched to every city) are not sending invitations. They are collecting professionals for a state deployment. The sorcerers are objects in the machine, not agents within it.

**Track two: the crowd.** *Hal antum mujtami'ūn?* — "Will you not come together?" The interrogative of incitement. The *Form VIII* voluntary vocabulary. The people are not commanded — they are *invited.* They are addressed as agents who might *choose* to gather, as participants who might *want* to be there. The grammar makes them feel free.

**The sorcerers have no choice. The people are made to feel they do.**

This is not a coincidence. This is design. Every successful propaganda event in history has understood this distinction intuitively, even if it could not articulate it linguistically. The specialists — the experts brought in to *perform* authority — are managed, moved, positioned. But the legitimacy of the event depends on the crowd *choosing* to witness it. A spectacle with only managed participants is a performance. A spectacle where the crowd *came freely* is a verdict.

Pharaoh needs the people to come of their own will. Because if they come of their own will, their presence becomes endorsement. Their witness becomes legitimacy. Their gathering — *ijtimā'* — becomes a referendum he didn't have to ask for.

This is why the interrogative is so precise: *hal antum mujtami'ūn?* Not "gather!" but "won't you gather?" Not a command but an invitation that arrives in an atmosphere so charged with inevitability that declining feels impossible.

[PAUSE]

The language of voluntary gathering (*ijtimā'*, Form VIII) used for the crowd, against the passive language of managed assembly (*jumi'a*, passive Form I) used for the sorcerers — this is one of the most compressed and complete descriptions of manufactured consent in any text in any language.

And it was written down fourteen centuries before the concept had a name.

What does this mean for us?

The Quran is not recounting Pharaoh's playbook as history. It is showing it to us so we can recognize the playbook when it runs in our own time. The form changes — the mīqāt is no longer a public square in Egypt, it is an algorithm-curated timeline, a prime-time broadcast slot, a trending topic — but the architecture is identical:

Announce the date. Frame the stakes in the vocabulary of cosmic significance. Manage the specialists. Invite the crowd. Make the crowd feel like participants rather than audience. Make their presence feel like a choice when it is, in a deeper sense, a consequence of everything that was engineered before they arrived.

The interrogative — *hal antum mujtami'ūn?* — is still being asked. It arrives in different forms. *Won't you watch? Won't you weigh in? Won't you be part of this moment?*

And the question these four ayahs are asking us is not whether we will attend. It is whether we will notice, when we arrive at the *mīqāt*, that the sorcerers were already assembled — managed, passive, positioned — before the invitation ever reached us.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Stand back and look at all four ayahs together.

The passage moves in a descending sequence: from **personal command** (*the advisors speak to Pharaoh — direct address*) to **operational mobilization** (*gatherers sent to every city*) to **passive assembly** (*the sorcerers are assembled, agent unnamed*) to **public invitation** (*the crowd is asked, in the language of voluntary choice, to come together*).

Power in verse 36 still has a face: the advisors. By verse 38 the face has disappeared into the passive voice. By verse 39 the crowd is being made to feel that they are the authors of their own gathering.

This is the descending anatomy of manufactured consent: the human order disappears into an institutional passive, and the crowd — who had no part in any of the commands — is invited to feel like the climax of the story.

### What the Linguistic and Thematic Are Saying Together

The morphological escalation from *sāhir* to *sahhār* — that single step from the basic form to the professional form — is not just a linguistic observation. It is the confessional leak within Pharaoh's confidence. The *mīqāt* vocabulary is not just a word choice — it is the co-optation of sacred temporal language to give theatrical legitimacy to a state event. The passives of verses 38 and 39 are not just grammatical facts — they are the grammar of accountability's disappearance.

The linguistic and thematic are the same insight, coming from two directions.

**The morphology tells us Pharaoh is afraid.** The vocabulary tells us he is reaching for sacred weight to make his fear productive. And the grammar tells us that the machinery of that fear runs best when no one has to stand responsible for running it.

### The Closing Invitation

The Quran is not showing us Pharaoh so we can condemn him from a safe distance.

It is showing us the playbook so that when it runs — in the institutions, the atmospheres, the invitations that surround us, and perhaps in our own internal management of truths we find threatening — we can recognize it for what it is.

The advisors used the word *sahhār* because they had to. Truth had gotten through far enough to make the light form insufficient. It just hadn't gotten through far enough to make them stop.

The question these four ayahs leave us with is not: *will you recognize Pharaoh's system?*

It is: *when truth has gotten through far enough to disturb you — will you reach for the sahhārūn, or will you be honest about what disturbed you?*

---

### Du'ā

يا رَبَّ العَالَمِينَ — Lord of all the worlds — the Lord of the throne that surpasses Pharaoh's throne and every throne that has followed it:

Open our eyes to the *mīqāt* moments we enter as if they were destiny, when they were engineered.

Protect us from reaching for the *sahhārūn* — the maximum professional defense — when what we need is the honesty to name what has actually reached us.

And when the truth disturbs us at a depth the light form can no longer contain: give us not the advisors' escalation, but Musa's stillness — the stillness of one whose identity does not depend on the outcome of the spectacle.

آمين.
