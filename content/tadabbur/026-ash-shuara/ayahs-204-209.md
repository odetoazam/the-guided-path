# The Years Are Not Your Acquittal

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 204–209

> أَفَبِعَذَابِنَا يَسْتَعْجِلُونَ
> أَفَرَأَيْتَ إِن مَّتَّعْنَاهُمْ سِنِينَ
> ثُمَّ جَاءَهُم مَّا كَانُوا يُوعَدُونَ
> مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا يُمَتَّعُونَ
> وَمَا أَهْلَكْنَا مِن قَرْيَةٍ إِلَّا لَهَا مُنذِرُونَ
> ذِكْرَىٰ وَمَا كُنَّا ظَٰلِمِينَ

*A-fa-bi-ʿadhābinā yastaʿjilūn. A-fa-ra'ayta in mattaʿnāhum sinīn. Thumma jā'ahum mā kānū yūʿadūn. Mā aghnā ʿanhum mā kānū yumattaʿūn. Wa mā ahlaknā min qaryatin illā lahā mundhirūn. Dhikrā — wa mā kunnā ẓālimīn.*

**"So is it Our punishment they are trying to rush? Have you considered — if We let them enjoy themselves for years, and then there came to them what they had been promised — what use to them was all they had been given to enjoy? And We never destroyed a town without it having warners — as a reminder. And We were never unjust."**

---

## Introduction: The Long Walk Before This Question

To stand inside these six ayahs, we have to remember what we just walked through.

This is the *end* of Surah Ash-Shu'ara. And the surah opens with one of the most heartbreaking sentences in the Quran. Allah looks at His Prophet ﷺ and says: *Perhaps you are going to kill yourself with grief because they will not believe.* That is the opening register. A man so consumed by the refusal of his people that he is grieving himself toward death.

Then — and this is important — Allah does not respond with consolation. He responds with a parade.

He walks the Prophet ﷺ through seven previous prophets. Mūsā, who stood in front of Pharaoh's court and was called a magician. Ibrāhīm, who looked at his father and the idols of his community and asked, *what is it you worship?* Nūḥ, who stayed among his people for nine hundred and fifty years of calling and being called a liar — until finally he could only cry out: *I am overcome, so give me victory.* Hūd, sent to ʿĀd. Ṣāliḥ, sent to Thamūd. Lūṭ, sent to a city that did what no one had done before them. Shuʿayb, sent to Madyan.

Seven warners. Seven peoples. And a refrain that beats through the whole surah like a drum: *And indeed, your Lord — He is the Mighty, the Merciful.*

Now here is what you have to feel. The Prophet ﷺ is hearing this in Makkah, and the people of Makkah are mocking him. They are daring him. They are saying — *if what you say is true, bring on the punishment. Where is it? When? Hurry up. Tell your Lord we are waiting.*

That is the air these final ayahs land in.

After seven cautionary tales of nations who heard their warners and were destroyed, Allah turns His face to the people *currently* mocking. And He doesn't argue with them. He doesn't threaten them. He asks them a question. Then another question. Then He shows them the architecture of His own justice and walks away.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is going to undo us in this passage. The Quraysh thought their *time* was their proof. They were saying: *if your God were going to do something, He would have done it by now. Look how long it has been. Look how comfortable we still are. Your delay is our acquittal.*

And Allah is going to take that exact argument — the years, the comfort, the delay — and reveal that every single piece of it was something else than they thought it was.

Stay with me. Because by the time these six ayahs are finished, you are going to discover that the most dangerous thing in your life might be the very thing you've been counting as evidence that you are safe.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yastaʿjilūn* — The Verb of Wanting Things to Happen Faster

Let's start with the very first word that has weight.

Allah says: **أَفَبِعَذَابِنَا يَسْتَعْجِلُونَ** — *Is it then Our punishment they are trying to rush?*

The verb is *yastaʿjilūn*. Before we open this word, let me teach you something about Arabic verbs in general, because if you don't know this, you will miss what is happening.

In Arabic, verbs come in patterns called *forms*. Form I is the simple, basic action. *Kataba* — he wrote. *Naẓara* — he looked. But Arabic has nine other forms, each one bending the meaning in a specific way. Form II makes the action intense or causative. Form IV makes it transitive — making someone else do it. Form VII makes it passive. And Form X — the one we are dealing with right now — has a beautiful function. **Form X is the form of *seeking* the action.** Of *trying* to bring it about. Of *asking for* it.

Take the word *ʿafw* — pardon. From it, Form I is *ʿafā* — he pardoned. But Form X gives you *istaghfara* — to *seek* pardon. To request it. To pursue it.

So when you see Form X in Arabic, your ear should perk up. Because the speaker is no longer just doing the action. They are *chasing* it.

Now look at this word again: *yastaʿjilūn*.

The root is ع-ج-ل (*ʿ-j-l*), which means haste, hurry, rushing. In Form I you get *ʿajila* — he hurried. But here, in this ayah, Allah does not say *yaʿjalūn* — *they hurry.* He uses Form X. *Yastaʿjilūn.*

[PAUSE]

This means they are not just hurrying. **They are *seeking the hastening.* They are *demanding* it. They are *asking it to come.*** It is an active, deliberate posture of pursuit.

Notice something: punishment is not a thing a person should ever pursue. To run *toward* punishment is the most disordered relationship to reality you can have. And yet that is exactly the verb form Allah uses to describe these people. Not *they hurry* — but *they actively seek the hastening of Our punishment.*

There is something almost surreal about the picture. Like a man standing in a field shouting at the sky to throw lightning at him. And Allah opens this passage by simply asking: **really? *That* is what you are trying to rush?**

The question is not rhetorical. It is the kind of question a parent asks a child who has just done something so misguided that the parent doesn't even reach for the discipline yet — they just stop and let the child hear their own action played back to them. *Is this what you wanted? Is* this *what you've been demanding?*

[PAUSE]

Classical commentators preserve what this istiʿjāl actually sounded like in practice. The Quraysh were telling the Prophet ﷺ: *We will never believe you until you drop the sky on us in pieces, as you claim.* That was the specific shape of their demand. Not *show us a general sign* — but *make the sky fall, on our schedule, in the form we specify.* They had written their own terms and conditions for belief, and the terms were spectacular and self-designed. Form X captures this precisely: they were not merely restless. They were *dictating the terms of the punishment they insisted they did not fear.*

And then there is a layer underneath that demand which is even more revealing. Al-Muyassar's commentary asks the question implicit in Allah's response: *Has My forbearance deceived them?* The word for forbearance is *imhāl* — the deliberate granting of respite, the withholding of immediate consequence. And the word at stake is *ghurūr* — to be deluded, to be given false confidence, to mistake the open door for permission to stay forever. The Quraysh had looked at years of uninterrupted comfort and heard a verdict: *You are right. Allah has decided not to act.* Every year the sky remained intact above them, they read it as confirmation.

This is the specific disease of the comfortable. Not active opposition to truth — but the quiet translation of delay into acquittal.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

What this single word reveals is that the people Allah is addressing are not simply unbelievers. They are something stranger. They are *eager* unbelievers. They are running toward exactly the thing they should be fleeing.

And that single Form X verb tells you that the disease is not ignorance. The disease is inversion. They have inverted the very direction of human appetite. They are sprinting toward what they should be running from.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Mattaʿnāhum* and *Yumattaʿūn* — The Same Gift From Two Sides

Now Allah pivots. He has named their disordered hunger. Now He is going to show them what He has actually been giving them.

He says: **أَفَرَأَيْتَ إِن مَّتَّعْنَاهُمْ سِنِينَ** — *Have you considered — if We gave them enjoyment for years?*

And then a few words later: **مَا أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُم مَّا كَانُوا يُمَتَّعُونَ** — *What use to them was that which they had been given to enjoy?*

Look at those two verbs. They come from the *same root.* م-ت-ع. The root meaning is enjoyment, provision, the things one is given to use and benefit from.

But the *forms* are different. And this difference is the whole sermon.

The first one — *mattaʿnāhum* — is Form II, active voice, with Allah as the subject. **"*We* caused them to enjoy."** The Doer is named. The hand is visible. Allah is saying — when you trace the comfort back to its source, you arrive at *Me.*

The second one — *yumattaʿūn* — is the *same* Form II, but flipped to *passive voice.* **"They were *being made to enjoy.*"** The doer is removed. The hand is hidden. The grammar itself enacts what happens in the heart of someone who lives long enough in comfort: the Giver disappears.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a second. Because Arabic does something here that English cannot easily translate.

In ayah 205, Allah describes the gift from *His* side: *We gave them.* He is the subject. He is named. The verb is active.

In ayah 207, Allah describes the *same gift* from *their* side: *what they were being given to enjoy.* The subject vanishes. There is comfort, but no Giver. There is enjoyment, but no Source.

This is exactly what happens to the human heart when comfort runs long enough. The food appears on the table. The salary deposits. The body wakes up healthy again. The children come home safe. And after enough years of this, the mind quietly drops the *Who.* The gift is no longer *from Someone.* It is just there. It just happens. It just *is.*

The Quraysh did not look at their wealth and say *Allah is giving us this and we are choosing not to thank Him.* That would have been spiritual rebellion. What they did was subtler and more dangerous: **they stopped seeing it as a gift at all.** They saw it as their environment. As their condition. As neutral fact.

And Allah, in the very grammar of the ayah, *shows* this to the listener. He uses the active voice when describing what He did. He uses the passive voice when describing how they experienced it. The grammar is not neutral. The grammar is the diagnosis.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The word "years" — *sinīn* — sits between these two verbs. *We gave them enjoyment for **years**, and then there came to them what they had been promised — and what use to them was what they had been given to enjoy?*

The *sinīn* is the bridge. The *sinīn* is the long stretch of time during which the active *mattaʿnāhum* slowly collapsed in their hearts into the passive *yumattaʿūn.* The Giver did not change. *They* changed. The hand did not withdraw. They simply stopped seeing it.

What this construction reveals is one of the most precise descriptions in the Quran of how a soul drifts. Not in a single dramatic moment of denial. But in the long, soft erosion of *years* — in which the same gift that started out as a clear arrow from a Giver becomes, slowly, just the air you breathe.

[PAUSE]

And classical commentary gives the *sinīn* a weight even more precise than "passing time." Those years, in the classical reading, are *ta'khīr ājālihim* — the postponement of their appointed times. Every year that passed without the promised consequence was not neutral duration. It was a specifically granted delay: the moment of reckoning held back, the clock of accountability suspended. Which means the terrifying thing about those years is something that could only be seen from the other side of them: every year of comfort was a year in which the appointment had been postponed. And postponement, by definition, cannot be identified as such until the thing finally arrives.

The Quraysh were living inside a postponement. They thought they were living in a permanent condition.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Mundhirūn* — The Plural Allah Refused to Make Singular

Now we come to the second-to-last ayah. And this one rewards careful listening.

Allah says: **وَمَا أَهْلَكْنَا مِن قَرْيَةٍ إِلَّا لَهَا مُنذِرُونَ** — *And We never destroyed a town except that it had warners.*

I want you to focus on one word: *mundhirūn.*

The root is ن-ذ-ر (*n-dh-r*), which means warning. A *mundhir* is one who warns — singular. *Mundhirūn* is the plural.

Notice — Allah does not say *mundhir.* He does not say *a warner.* He says *warners. Plural.*

[PAUSE]

This sounds small. It is not small.

Think about what Allah has just walked us through across the entire surah. Seven prophets. Seven nations. And in the case of most of those nations, the prophet sent to them was the *culmination* of a long line of warnings before him. Even within a single prophet's mission, he warned them again and again, year after year. Nūḥ for nine hundred and fifty years. Repeating. Returning. Trying every angle.

When Allah says *lahā mundhirūn* — *to it, warners* — He is not just saying every town got *a* prophet. He is saying every town got *warners,* plural — meaning the warning came not as a single announcement but as a *sustained chorus,* a multiplicity, an unrelenting reminder across time.

And there is something even sharper in the word order. In Arabic, Allah did not say *kāna lahā mundhirūn* in some neutral arrangement. He fronted *lahā* before *mundhirūn.* *Illā **lahā** mundhirūn.* **Except that *to it* — warners.** The fronting puts the spotlight on the *town.* It is not just that warners existed somewhere. The warners were *for that specific town.* Sent to *them.* Aimed at *them.* Speaking *their* dialect, walking *their* streets, named in *their* genealogy.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this does to the case for the destroyed nation.

A nation cannot stand on the Day of Judgment and say *we did not know.* Because there was not just a warner. There were warners. Multiple. And they were not generic. They were sent *for that town,* in a way that made the warning unmissable.

But there is a deeper layer here, and this is the one I want you to sit with.

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say *every town that was destroyed had warners come **before** their destruction.* That would have been a simple statement of historical fact.

He said: *We never destroyed a town except that **it had** warners.*

The relationship between the destruction and the warners is the relationship of a *condition.* The destruction is *contingent* on the warning. **Allah is, in this single ayah, declaring something about the architecture of His own justice: there is no destruction without prior warning. The warning is not optional. It is structurally required.**

And then He closes with the line that drives the dagger home: **ذِكْرَىٰ وَمَا كُنَّا ظَٰلِمِينَ** — *as a reminder; and We were never unjust.*

The word *dhikrā* means a reminder, a recollection. It is from the same root as *dhikr.* Allah is saying — the warners were not punishment. The warners were not pre-condemnation. They were a *reminder.* A mercy-move. A reaching back toward people who had forgotten.

Think about what the word *dhikrā* does to the whole structure of the passage. Allah has just told us that no town was ever destroyed without warners. And then He names *why* the warners were sent: *dhikrā* — as a reminder. Not as a formality before destruction. Not as a legal requirement that needed to be filed. As a *reminder.*

Which means the warners were sent because the truth was already there, somewhere in the people, buried under the years of comfortable forgetting. The reminder does not create the knowledge. It recovers it. It returns something that was already theirs.

[PAUSE]

And then the final clause. **وَمَا كُنَّا ظَٰلِمِينَ** — *and We were never unjust.*

The word *ẓālimīn* comes from the root ظ-ل-م — *ẓ-l-m* — whose primary meaning is *darkness,* the covering-over of light. From it comes the word for injustice because injustice is, at its root, the covering of what should be clear. To do injustice is to darken what should be visible. To deny someone their right is to throw darkness over it.

Allah closes with this: *We were never unjust.* Not *We were fair.* Not *We tried to be balanced.* The statement is absolute. *We were never — not once — unjust.*

Why does this close the passage? Because the whole architecture of these ayahs has been about establishing, brick by brick, that what looks like injustice from the inside is not injustice at all. The delay looks like acquittal. The comfort looks like approval. The absence of immediate punishment looks like divine indifference. And then — when the punishment finally comes — it looks sudden and arbitrary.

But Allah is saying: at every step, the record was clean. The warning was sent — not once, but many times, by multiple voices, aimed specifically at *you.* The time was granted — not as approval, but as postponement, as respite, as one more window. The reminder was offered — not as threat, but as an act of recovery, a mercy wrapped in the form of a returning voice.

Classical commentary notes that when the consequence finally arrived for previous nations, it came on account of two distinct things. The people had disbelieved in Allah's *signs* — the āyāt of the world, the evidences laid before them — and they had rejected His *messenger.* These are not the same offense, though they are bound together. The first is a refusal of evidence; the second is a refusal of the person sent with that evidence, the relational rupture underneath the intellectual one. Both together complete the case. And against that complete case — the signed, sealed record of warning received and refused — the sentence is not injustice. It is precision.

*We were never unjust.*

## PART 2: SITTING INSIDE THE TEXT

Now let us put down the dictionary and pick up the mirror.

These ayahs were sent to the Quraysh. But they are in the Quran — which means they are addressed to every generation that reads them. Including ours. Including you, reading this right now.

And so the question you have to let land is this:

**What in your life have you been reading as acquittal?**

Not punishment you are *seeking* — that specific pathology of the Quraysh. But the quieter version: the thing you have been doing, or not doing, for which there has been no visible consequence. The habit you carry, the relationship you have left uncorrected, the worship you keep postponing, the wrong you have not set right — and the years of comfortable continuation that you have been, very quietly, reading as *evidence that it is fine.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the logic the Quraysh used: *if it were not okay, something would have happened by now.* And here is the terrifying thing: that logic is almost never spoken out loud. It lives below the level of language. It is a felt sense. A background assumption. *I am still here. Things are still working. Nothing has broken. Therefore, I am safe.*

But listen to what the ayah is saying. The *sinīn* — the years — are *ta'khīr.* Postponement. They are not erasure of what was promised. They are the space before it. And the sinīn are not proof that the *mā kānū yūʿadūn* — what they had been promised — will not come. They are proof that it has not come *yet.*

[PAUSE]

And here is what makes this passage so uncomfortable in a different way than most passages about consequence.

Usually, the passages about punishment describe people who are clearly doing something obviously terrible. Pharaoh's cruelty. The nations' open worship of idols. The immediate violence of rejection.

But these ayahs describe people who are doing something *much more ordinary.* They are comfortable. They are being given enjoyment. They are living in the *sinīn* — the years — that Allah Himself describes as His gift. The only thing they are doing wrong is the reading. They are reading comfort as a verdict instead of reading it as a postponement.

And that quiet misreading is the thing that can happen in *any* life. In a life that looks entirely normal from the outside. In a life where things are going well.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

The other half of the passage does something different. It does not frighten — it reassures. But it reassures in a way that carries weight.

**وَمَا أَهْلَكْنَا مِن قَرْيَةٍ إِلَّا لَهَا مُنذِرُونَ.**

Every town that was ever destroyed had warners. Multiple. Specifically aimed. Speaking the local language of that specific people.

And then *dhikrā.* As a reminder.

Here is what this means for you. If you are reading this right now — if these words are reaching you — then the reminder is reaching you. The voice is in your ears. The *mundhirūn*, the plural chorus, includes everything from the Quran itself to the scholarship that explained it to the person who shared the link that led you here. The warning has arrived. Multiple channels. Aimed at *you.*

And that is actually good news. Not frightening news. Because the warning being sent is *mercy.* It is the recovery of something that was always yours. It is Allah reaching back toward you before the appointed time arrives.

[PAUSE]

The question is what you do with the reaching.

## Closing: The Two Things This Passage Asks of You

Before we leave these six ayahs, I want to name what they are asking. Because they are not just warning. They are also — quietly — offering.

The first thing they ask is the honest audit. Look at where you are comfortable. Look at what has been going on for years with no consequence you can see. And instead of reading that as verdict, ask: is this postponement? Is this the *sinīn* of my own life? And if it is — what does that mean I should do with the time I am still in?

The second thing they ask is trust. *Wa mā kunnā ẓālimīn.* And We were never unjust. Every warning sent was mercy. Every reminder was a returning. Every year of postponement was not indifference — it was an open door, still open, still available to walk through.

The architecture of divine justice, as described in these six ayahs, is not punitive at its foundation. It is *fair.* Scrupulously, precisely, completely fair. The warning comes before the consequence. The reminder reaches before the reckoning. The time is granted before the appointment arrives.

And the one thing that can make all of that — all of that care, all of that mercy-in-structure — useless is the one thing the Quraysh did: **read the delay as dismissal.**

[PAUSE]

These ayahs end a surah that began with a prophet who was grieving himself to death because people would not listen. And they end with Allah saying: *We sent the reminders. We gave the time. We were never unjust.*

The surah does not close with the destruction. It closes with the declaration of the architecture. And that closing — *dhikrā wa mā kunnā ẓālimīn* — is itself addressed to you. The one still living inside the *sinīn.* The one to whom the reminder has just arrived.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

---

*O Allah — You are Al-Ḥalīm, the Forbearing. You are the One who grants respite and does not rush. Make us of those who read Your forbearance as mercy and not as acquittal. Make us of those who hear the reminder while there is still time to act on it. Make us of those who look at the years You have given us and see, behind every one of them, Your hand — active, named, present. And make us of those who turn toward You before what was promised arrives — not in fear, but in recognition: that every voice that called us back was You, reaching toward us. Āmīn.*
