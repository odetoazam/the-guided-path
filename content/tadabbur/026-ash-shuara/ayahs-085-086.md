# The Inheritance Ibrahim Asked For — and the Father He Wouldn't Leave Behind

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayat

> وَٱجْعَلْنِى مِن وَرَثَةِ جَنَّةِ ٱلنَّعِيمِ ۝ وَٱغْفِرْ لِأَبِىٓ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ مِنَ ٱلضَّآلِّينَ

*Waj'alnī min warathati jannati n-na'īm. Waghfir li-abī innahu kāna mina ḍ-ḍāllīn.*

> **"And make me among the inheritors of the Garden of Bliss. And forgive my father — indeed, he was among those astray."**

---

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open these two ayat, we need to remember where Ibrahim — peace be upon him — is standing when he says them.

Surah Ash-Shu'ara is a gallery. One after another, Allah parades before us prophets whose own people rejected them. Musa with Pharaoh. Nuh with his community. Hud. Salih. Lut. Shu'ayb. Each story ends with the same refrain — *إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَآيَةً وَمَا كَانَ أَكْثَرُهُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ* — "Indeed in this is a sign, but most of them did not believe."

The surah is consoling the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah, where his own people — his own clan — had turned on him. *You are not the first*, the surah is saying. *This is what prophethood looks like.*

And right in the middle of this gallery, Allah places Ibrahim. But Ibrahim's case is different from all the others. The other prophets faced rejection from communities. Ibrahim faced rejection from **his father**.

Picture the scene the surah just gave us. A young man in a workshop full of carved idols. His father, Azar, has spent a lifetime making them, selling them, bowing to them. Ibrahim looks at his father — the man who fed him, named him, raised him — and asks the question that will shatter everything: *"What is it that you worship?"*

The father's response, recorded in another surah, is chilling: *"If you do not desist, I will surely stone you. Get away from me for a long time."* (19:46)

Now hold that scene. The father has rejected the son. Threatened him. Banished him.

[PAUSE]

And then, decades later — after Ibrahim has been thrown into fire, after he has migrated, after he has built the Ka'bah, after he has been tested with his own son — Ibrahim raises his hands to Allah in this long, intimate du'a. It does not open with the Garden. It opens quietly, with something interior: a plea for *ḥukm* — wisdom, judgment, the capacity to see rightly — and to be joined with the righteous (26:83). Then comes the request for a truthful tongue: to be remembered honorably among the generations that will follow (26:84).

It is only then that Ibrahim arrives at these two ayat. He asks for the Garden. And in the same breath, he turns and asks for his father.

And the du'a does not end here. It presses forward — Ibrahim will ask not to be disgraced on the Day of Resurrection (26:87), and close on the theological apex that classical commentators have long regarded as the heart of the entire supplication: *"The Day when neither wealth nor children will avail — except one who comes to Allah with a sound heart"* (26:88–89).

The Garden and the father sit in the middle of this vast arc. They are not the climax. They are something more like a hinge.

---

### The Hook

Two short ayat. Fourteen words. They sit side by side without explanation, and the Quran moves on as if nothing extraordinary just happened.

But something extraordinary did happen.

Right here — in the middle of this du'a, between asking for the Garden and before asking not to be disgraced on the Day of Standing — Ibrahim cannot bring himself to ask for his own good without bringing his father along. Even though that father had rejected him. Even though that father had threatened violence. Even though every law of the heart says: *let him go.*

What does it take for a heart to be like this? And what is hidden inside the word **inheritors** that makes the request for Paradise — and the request for the father — pour out in a single breath?

Stay with me. There is something here.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Warathah* — The Word "Inheritors"

Let's begin with a single word. *Warathah* — وَرَثَة — "inheritors."

Ibrahim does not say, *"make me one who enters the Garden."* He does not say, *"give me the Garden."* He says: *"Make me among the inheritors of the Garden of Bliss."*

#### Teach the concept first

In every language, the word "inherit" has a peculiar grammar. You do not inherit something you built. You inherit something that was already there — something prepared, accumulated, held in trust — and at the right moment, it transfers to you.

When a grandmother dies and leaves her house to her grandchild, the grandchild did not build that house. She didn't lay its foundation. She didn't pay its mortgage. She walks into a home already standing, already furnished, already warm. The work was done. She receives.

Inheritance is the language of receiving what you did not earn.

#### Now open the word

The Arabic root و-ر-ث (*w-r-th*) carries exactly this. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib — root it in the transfer of property, status, or station from one party to another, often after the first party has departed. *Warathah* is the plural noun: those who receive an inheritance.

So Ibrahim is not asking, *"let me earn the Garden."*

He is asking, *"let me be among those who receive what was already prepared."*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. This is the language the Quran uses again and again for the believers and Paradise. *أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْوَٰرِثُونَ ۝ ٱلَّذِينَ يَرِثُونَ ٱلْفِرْدَوْسَ* — *"Those — they are the inheritors. Those who will inherit Firdaws."* (23:10-11)

Why this word? Why not "owners," "earners," "achievers"?

Because of what it does to the human heart.

#### Deepen

If Paradise were something you *earned*, then on the Day you walked in, you'd walk in with the posture of a contractor inspecting his finished work. *I built this. I did this. I deserve this.* The nafs would inflate. The ego would have a final, eternal victory.

But if Paradise is something you *inherit*, then on the Day you walk in, you walk in like a child walking into a house her father built before she was born. The posture is different. The heart is different. You did not make this. You were brought to this.

Ibrahim — the man who built the Ka'bah with his own hands, the man who survived the fire, the man whose name is etched into prophetic history — when he asks for the Garden, asks for it in the language of receiving, not earning.

This is who he is. The deeds were enormous. The posture is small.

Here's what's remarkable. The word *warathah* also implies something else: that there is a *previous owner*. You inherit from someone. Al-Tabari, commenting on this very phrase, reads it with striking concreteness: Ibrahim is asking his Lord to grant him the dwelling-places of the perished polytheists — those who rejected Allah have forfeited their portion, and Ibrahim asks to be placed among those who receive what they left behind. Every soul has a position prepared. Those who turn away vacate it. Those who turn toward Allah are brought into the inheritance they left open.

So when Ibrahim says *make me among the inheritors*, he is also saying: *let me be among those who did not forfeit. Let me be among those who showed up to receive what was prepared.*

#### Conclude this element

One word. *Warathah*. And it has already done two things to us:

It has stripped the posture of "I earned this" from the entry into Paradise.

And it has reframed the human task — not as construction, but as *not forfeiting*. The Garden is built. The question is whether you'll be among those still standing when the inheritance is read.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Jannat an-Na'īm* — The Garden of Bliss

Now look at what kind of Garden Ibrahim asks for.

He does not say *jannah* — just "garden." He specifies. *Jannat an-Na'īm* — the Garden **of Bliss**.

#### Teach the concept first

In Arabic, when you attach a quality to a noun in the *iḍāfah* construction — "garden of bliss," "house of peace," "abode of certainty" — you are not just describing the noun. You are saying: *this thing exists for the sake of that quality. This is what defines it. This is its essence.*

A "garden of roses" is a garden whose entire purpose is roses. A "house of mourning" is a house whose entire identity, in this moment, is grief.

So *jannat an-na'īm* is not a garden that happens to contain bliss. It is a garden whose entire reason for existing **is** bliss.

#### Open the word

The root ن-ع-م (*n-ʿ-m*) is one of the most loaded roots in the Quran. It is the root of *ni'mah* — favor, blessing. It is the root of *na'īm* — soft, delicate, ease, pleasure that touches every fiber. It is the root of the word Allah uses in Surah Ar-Rahman over and over: *fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — though that uses *ālā'*, the same semantic field is everywhere — *which of the favors of your Lord do you deny?*

*Na'īm* is not bliss in the modern flat sense. It is the kind of pleasantness that softens, that envelops, that comes through the senses and settles into the soul.

#### Deepen

Here is what is striking. This world has many kinds of joy. There is the sharp joy of victory. The hot joy of revenge. The complicated joy of getting what you wanted but losing something else. The brittle joy that comes with a price tag. The exhausted joy after a fight.

Ibrahim does not ask for any of those.

He asks for *na'īm*. The kind of joy that does not exhaust you. The kind that has no aftertaste of regret. The kind that does not require you to lose something to gain it.

And notice — he asks for it after a life of *enormous* difficulty. This is the man who left his homeland. Who survived the fire. Who buried his wife in a foreign land. Who left his infant son in a barren valley. Who raised the knife over his other son's throat.

After all that, what is the inner ache he names when he asks Allah for the highest thing? Not glory. Not vindication. Not victory.

**Bliss.** Soft, total, untroubled, enveloping.

Stay with this. It tells us something about what a tested soul actually wants. The soul that has been through the fire does not ask for triumph. It asks for rest. For the cessation of struggle. For a place where everything that is good is good without complication.

#### Conclude this element

*Jannat an-Na'īm.* The Garden whose entire reason for existing is the soft, total ease of the soul.

And Ibrahim — having asked to *inherit* it, not to earn it — has just told us two things about Paradise: that it is received, and that what is received there is the ending of the human ache.

### Element Three: The Word *Abī* — "My Father"

Now we move to the second ayah. And here, we must read very, very carefully — because the most important thing in this ayah is also the most easily missed.

*Waghfir li-abī.* "And forgive my father."

#### Teach the concept first

In Arabic, possessive pronouns are not optional decoration. When you attach the *ī* — the "my" — to a noun, you are claiming that thing as part of you. *Bayt-ī* (my house) is different from *al-bayt* (the house). *Abī* (my father) is different from *abī Ibrahim* (the father of Ibrahim) or even *al-ab* (the father).

The *ī* possessive carries the weight of relationship — *this one is connected to me. This one is mine.*

#### Open the word

Now look at the choice Ibrahim makes here. After everything Azar did to him — the threat of stoning, the banishment, the years of disbelief — Ibrahim, in the most sacred moment of his du'a, addresses Allah and says: *li-abī*.

**My father.**

Not "this man." Not "Azar." Not "the one who rejected me." Not even, with distance, "the father I had."

*My father.* Still claimed. Still mine.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepen

There is a kind of human wisdom that says: when someone has hurt you that deeply, you must release the relationship. You must stop calling them yours. You must put them in a box marked "former" — former friend, former family — and close the lid.

That is the wisdom of self-protection. And it has its place.

But Ibrahim does something else. He keeps the claim. *My father.* Even in his du'a to Allah — the most private, honest moment a soul can have — he does not strip the possessive. He does not edit Azar out of his identity.

Why?

Because a heart that has been healed by Allah does not require the elimination of the wounder. A heart still in pain has to push the painful one away to survive. A heart that has been gathered up by Allah Himself can hold the painful one in mercy.

Ibrahim is not naive. He knows what his father did. The very next phrase tells us: *innahu kāna mina ḍ-ḍāllīn* — "indeed, he was among those astray." Al-Tabari's gloss is plain: Azar strayed from the path of guidance and disbelieved. Ibrahim is not pretending. He is naming the truth: *my father was lost.*

But he is naming the truth while still holding the relationship.

This is the prophetic posture. **See clearly. Love anyway.**

And there is something beneath this posture that only becomes visible when we know what came before. This prayer was not spontaneous. It was a fulfillment. Long before this du'a in Ash-Shu'ara, when Azar threatened to stone Ibrahim and banish him, Ibrahim responded not with anger but with a vow: *"Peace be upon you. I will ask forgiveness for you from my Lord. Indeed, He is ever gracious to me"* (19:47). Classical commentators note that this du'a is Ibrahim honoring that pledge — praying in hope that Allah would rescue his father from misguidance into guidance, and forgive him. He made the promise in pain. He is keeping it here.

[PAUSE]

But we must also hold what came after. This prayer — sincere, promised, kept — was eventually withdrawn. When it became undeniably clear that Azar had died entrenched in his rejection of Allah, Ibrahim released the intercession. Al-Muyassar states it directly: when it became clear that his father had persisted in disbelief and polytheism until his death, Ibrahim disowned him. The Quran records this in Surah At-Tawbah: *"And Ibrahim's asking forgiveness for his father was only because of a promise he had made to him. But when it became clear to Ibrahim that his father was an enemy of Allah, he disowned him"* (9:114). And Surah Al-Mumtahanah offers Ibrahim as the model to follow — with this single exception: what he said to his father (60:4).

This is not a story of a prayer answered with yes. It is the story of a prayer offered in hope, kept in loyalty, and released with integrity when the door Allah had not granted him to open was finally, clearly, closed.

What Ibrahim shows us is not that praying for those who have rejected Allah will save them. What he shows us is the posture of the soul while it is still permitted to ask: name the harm clearly, hold the relationship anyway, place the outcome entirely with Allah — and when the answer is no, accept that too, without the mercy having been wrong.

#### Now read the absence

Here is something worth pausing on. Notice what is *not* in this ayah.

Ibrahim does not justify his request. He does not say, "forgive him because he did this good thing once." He does not list his father's redeeming qualities.

He simply asks: *forgive my father.* And then states, plainly: *he was astray.*

There is no defense. No making the case. Ibrahim is not negotiating with Allah.

He is doing something more honest. He is saying: *Ya Allah, You see exactly what he was. I am not asking You to overlook it. I am asking You — knowing it — to forgive.*

That is the only kind of forgiveness request that means anything. The request that does not minimize the wound. The request that names the harm and asks for mercy anyway.

#### Conclude this element

One word — *abī* — has done what an entire essay on forgiveness could not do.

It has shown us that the prophetic heart, when it asks Allah to forgive someone who hurt it, does not first un-claim them. It does not first put distance between itself and the harm. It holds the relationship. It tells the truth about the harm. It keeps the promise it made in the worst moment. It asks for mercy. And when the intercession reaches the limit of what it was given to carry — when Allah's knowledge of a heart replaces the believer's hope for it — it releases, without bitterness, without the prayer having been a mistake.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. *Warathah* — the language of receiving what was prepared, including what the perished left behind. *Jannat an-Na'īm* — the Garden defined entirely by ease. *Abī* — the possessive that survived the wound, the promise kept across the years.

Three pieces of grammar. But each was a window.

Now let's ask the question that has been forming under all of this: what does it mean that these two requests — for the Garden and for the father — arrive together? Why doesn't the du'a separate them? Why, in the same breath that Ibrahim reaches for the highest thing a human can ask for, does he turn and reach for the man who tried to destroy him?

## PART 2: ONE BREATH, TWO REQUESTS

### The Grammar of the *Wāw*

The conjunction linking the two ayat is *wāw* — the Arabic "and." It is the simplest word in the language. It makes no argument. It draws no contrast. It does not say "but also" or "despite this" or "even though." It simply joins.

*Waj'alnī min warathati jannati n-na'īm. **Wa**-ghfir li-abī.*

Make me among the inheritors of the Garden of Bliss. **And** forgive my father.

The grammar does not distinguish which of these costs more to ask. The *wāw* holds them equally. Side by side. As if they belong together.

This is deliberate. The Quran does not join things accidentally.

#### What the "And" is saying

The heart that has learned to receive — the heart that asks to *inherit* rather than to *earn* — is a heart that does not hoard. What it asks for itself, it finds itself immediately wanting for others. Not later. In the same sentence.

This is the logic of the *wāw*. The Garden and the father are not competing requests. They are one continuous movement of a soul that has been opened.

Think of it this way. A person who has never known real generosity, when they receive something precious, clutches it. *This is mine. I found it. I deserve it.* The gift activates the hoarding instinct.

But a person who has been formed by mercy — who has learned that everything they have was given, not earned — when they receive something precious, their first thought is: *who else needs this?*

Ibrahim asks for the Garden in the language of inheritance: I didn't build this, I am receiving it. And that very posture frees him, in the same moment, to ask for the man who hurt him.

The receiving heart and the forgiving heart are not two different hearts. They are the same heart in two consecutive breaths.

### The Promise Honored

There is something else inside the *wāw* that only becomes visible when we know the backstory.

Go back to Surah Maryam. Ibrahim is standing in his father's workshop. He has tried, with remarkable gentleness, to reason with Azar. And Azar has answered with a threat: stones and exile (19:46).

How does Ibrahim respond?

He says: *"Peace be upon you. I will ask forgiveness for you from my Lord. Indeed, He is ever gracious to me"* (19:47).

In the moment of rejection, Ibrahim makes a promise. Not a threat in return. Not a wounded silence. A promise: *I will pray for you.*

Classical commentators understand the du'a here in Ash-Shu'ara as Ibrahim fulfilling that pledge — praying in hope that Allah would guide his father and forgive him. He committed to intercession when the threat was fresh. He is honoring it here, decades later, in the sacred context of a du'a addressed to Allah Himself.

Now the *wāw* carries a new weight. When Ibrahim says, in a single breath, *make me among the inheritors of the Garden — and forgive my father* — part of what he is doing is **keeping his word**. Not because Azar deserved it. Not because the situation warranted it. But because a prophet's promise does not expire with the discomfort of the moment it was made.

He made the promise under threat. He is honoring it in the intimacy of du'a.

This is what it means for a tongue to be *ṣādiq* — true, faithful — not only in testimony, but in commitment.

[PAUSE]

### The Limits of What We Can Give

Now we have to speak about something harder.

This prayer — sincere, promised, honored — was not answered with yes.

The classical scholars recognized an ikhtilāf here: why did Ibrahim pray for Azar at all, given that Azar was visibly a polytheist? Al-Tabari notes that the scholars differed on this question and had addressed it at length in discussion of earlier passages. One position holds that Ibrahim prayed out of genuine hope for his father's conversion — he did not know Azar would die in disbelief, and the prayer expressed that hope while the door was still open. Another holds that the prayer was primarily the fulfillment of the promise made in 19:47, and that Ibrahim understood even then that the outcome rested entirely with Allah. What all the commentators agree on is this: the prayer was real, the mercy was real, and the withdrawal was also real.

The Quran tells us what happened: *"Ibrahim's asking forgiveness for his father was only because of a promise he had made to him. But when it became clear to him that his father was an enemy of Allah, he disowned him"* (9:114). Azar died as he lived. When the finality of that became clear, Ibrahim released the intercession. Not from bitterness. From submission.

[PAUSE]

This is what 26:86 cannot give us without 9:114. These two ayat are a single lesson split across surahs: **You may pray for those who are astray. You are permitted — even honored — to pray for them. And there is a moment when Allah's knowledge of a heart replaces your hope for it.** That moment is not yours to determine. It is His.

What Ibrahim models is not that this prayer will always be answered with what you asked for. What he models is the posture of the soul while it is still permitted to ask: name the harm clearly (*innahu kāna mina ḍ-ḍāllīn*), hold the relationship anyway (*abī*), place the outcome entirely with Allah (*waghfir*). Ask. Keep the promise. And when the answer is no — receive that answer too, in the same posture of inheritance, without the mercy having been wrong.

The mercy was an act of faith. The release was an act of faith. Both belong to the same sound heart.

## PART 3: THE DU'A THAT DOESN'T END WHERE WE THINK

### What Comes After

We have spent this time with ayat 85 and 86. But the du'a doesn't end here.

Ibrahim raises his hands and keeps going. *"And do not disgrace me on the Day they are raised"* (26:87). *"The Day when neither wealth nor children will avail — except one who comes to Allah with a sound heart"* (26:88–89).

Classical commentators have long regarded the *qalb salīm* as the theological apex of this entire supplication. Not the Garden. Not the father. The sound heart.

Why?

Because everything that came before in this du'a flows from a heart that is sound, and everything Ibrahim asked for is only possible for a heart that is sound.

The *warathah* posture — receiving rather than claiming, accepting rather than conquering — is the posture of a sound heart. A heart that knows it did not build what it walks into.

The desire for *na'īm* rather than victory — rest rather than triumph — is the ache of a sound heart. A heart that has been through enough to know what it actually needs.

The claiming of *abī* after the wound. The promise kept across decades. The mercy that names harm and asks for forgiveness anyway. The acceptance of the du'a's limits when the door closes. All of this is what a sound heart does.

[PAUSE]

Ibrahim does not ask for the *qalb salīm* as an afterthought. He asks for it because it is the source. All the other requests in this du'a — ḥukm (26:83), the truthful tongue (26:84), the Garden (26:85), the father (26:86), not to be disgraced (26:87) — are fruit. The *qalb salīm* is the root.

And this gives us a way to read the entire du'a in reverse: what kind of heart has to have formed in order to ask for all of these things, in this order, in this way?

A heart that wants wisdom before it wants reward. A heart that wants to be remembered truthfully before it wants to be remembered fondly. A heart that asks to receive rather than to conquer. A heart that holds the father who tried to stone it, honors the promise made in exile, and can release an intercession when its limits are reached — without anger, without the mercy having been a mistake.

This is the heart that can pray: *"Do not disgrace me on the Day they are raised."*

Because a heart like that has already decided what disgrace means. It is not failing in the eyes of people. It is arriving before Allah with something other than *salīm*.

---

### What He Left Us

Ibrahim — peace be upon him — is called the Khalīlullāh. The intimate friend of Allah. The Quran uses this word precisely once for him. It is not a title given for deeds, though the deeds were immense. It is a title given for a quality of heart.

The heart that can pray this du'a — from ḥukm to *qalb salīm*, holding the Garden and the father in the same hands — is the heart of a *khalīl*.

And here is what the Quran is doing by placing this du'a in Ash-Shu'ara, in the middle of the gallery of rejected prophets, in the ear of the Prophet ﷺ as he faces his own Makkah, his own kinsmen, his own people who have turned away:

*Look at what Ibrahim asked for. Look at how he asked. This is the template.*

Not: ask for victory over those who rejected you. Not: ask for the world to be rearranged so you don't have to hurt. Not: sever the ones who threatened you from your identity and pray for yourself alone.

Ask for the Garden in the posture of a receiver. Ask for the father who hurt you, in the same breath. Keep the promises you made in your worst moments. Name the harm. Name the *ḍalāl*. Place it all before Allah and let His response be what it is — even when His response is: this intercession was not yours to carry to the end.

This is *Millat Ibrāhīm* — the way of Ibrahim. Not primarily a set of rituals, though the rituals are there. A posture. A grammar of the heart.

### A Final Pause on the Garden

We are almost at the end. But before we leave these ayat, let us sit for one more moment.

Ibrahim asked for *jannat an-na'īm*. The Garden of Bliss. The Garden whose entire identity is ease.

He asked for it in the middle of decades of difficulty. He asked for it in a du'a that ends with a request for a sound heart. He asked for it right before turning to ask for the man who threatened his life.

The Garden, in this du'a, is not a reward. It is a destination. The place where what the sound heart has been moving toward finally arrives. The place where the soul that learned to receive, that named harm and loved anyway, that kept promises in exile, that prayed for what it could not control and released what was not given to it to decide — finally rests.

*Na'īm.* Soft. Total. Without the complication that this world puts in every joy.

Ibrahim spent his life in a world where every joy had a price. The son he asked for was tested. The house he built was left behind. The father he prayed for did not return.

He asked for a place where that is no longer the structure of things.

And the Quran records the asking. Not the answer yet — but the asking.

Because the asking itself is a form of the sound heart. The heart that knows what it is moving toward. The heart that is not confused about what it wants, even when everything it wanted here cost more than it expected.
