# The Reminder That Tries to Bury You

## Surah Ash-Shu'ara, Ayahs 18-22

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Imagine, for a moment, returning to the house of someone who hurt your family for generations. Not just hurt — *enslaved.* Killed children. Used the bodies of your people as bricks for their monuments. And when you walk in to deliver a message, the man on the throne — the man whose entire dynasty was built on the suffering of yours — looks at you and says:

*"Didn't I raise you?"*

[PAUSE]

This is the moment we are entering.

Moses has just stood before Pharaoh and said: "I am a messenger from the Lord of the worlds. Send the Children of Israel with me." The whole court is watching. This is not a private conversation. This is theater. This is the most powerful man on Earth being addressed by a man he last saw fleeing for his life as a fugitive.

And Pharaoh, who is no fool, does not engage with the message. He cannot. To engage with it would be to acknowledge that there exists a Lord above him. So instead, he does something every manipulator in history has done. He **changes the subject from the message to the messenger.** And he chooses his weapon carefully.

He chooses **memory**.

The strand running through this surah is the strand of *prophets being rejected by their own people* — Nuh by his community, Hud by 'Aad, Salih by Thamud, Lut by his city, Shu'ayb by Madyan. Moses is the first in this gallery. And the way he is rejected sets the template: not with arguments, but with social leverage. Not with logic, but with the past.

Here is the hook. Pharaoh tries to use kindness as a weapon. Moses does not deny the kindness. He does not get defensive. He does not even argue about it.

He does something far more devastating.

He **renames it.**

And the way he renames it — in a single grammatical move that even a child could miss — collapses the entire moral foundation of Pharaoh's empire in front of Pharaoh's own court.

Let's see how.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Nurabbika* — The Word That Meant Two Things

Before we open Pharaoh's first sentence, we have to understand a feature of Arabic that English does not handle well: **roots that point in two directions at once.**

In English, "to raise" means "to bring up a child." It is gentle. It connotes nurture. But in Arabic, the root **ر-ب-ي** (*r-b-y*) is one of those magnificent and terrifying roots that means *to make something grow, to nurture, to bring to maturity, to be lord over, to dominate the development of.*

It is, by the way, the same root family as **رَبّ** (*Rabb*) — "Lord."

So when Pharaoh says **أَلَمْ نُرَبِّكَ فِينَا وَلِيدًا** — "Did we not *rabba* you among us as a child?" — he is using a word that contains, inside its very letters, the claim he wants to make:

*I was your lord. I made you. I am your Rabb.*

Notice something. Pharaoh has already, earlier and later in the Quran, declared **أَنَا رَبُّكُمُ الْأَعْلَىٰ** — "I am your highest lord." It is not an accident that the verb he reaches for here shares a root with that claim. He is, with one verb, smuggling his entire theology into a sentence that *sounds* like it's just about parenting.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Moses has just said in the previous ayahs. He has just declared that he is a messenger from **رَبِّ الْعَالَمِينَ** — the *Rabb* of all the worlds. The word "Rabb" is hanging in the air of the throne room.

And Pharaoh's response — **أَلَمْ نُرَبِّكَ** — is not a casual choice. It is a counter-claim built on the same root.

*You speak of a Rabb? I was your rabb. I rabba'd you.*

This is not gentle reminiscing. This is theological combat disguised as nostalgia.

Stay with me, because there's more in this single word.

The verb is in the **first person plural** — *nurabbika*, "we raised you." Pharaoh does not say "I raised you." He says "*we.*" Who is the "we"? The court. The system. The empire. He is reminding Moses that this is not a personal favor — it is institutional. The whole regime fed you. The whole regime clothed you. And the whole regime, by implication, is what you are now betraying.

This is what abusers and tyrants do, and what they have always done. They make the victim feel that to speak against the system is to be ungrateful to everyone who was ever kind inside the system. The cousin who was nice. The neighbor who fed you. The teacher who taught you. *They were all part of "we." How dare you turn against "we"?*

Notice also the word **فِينَا** — "*among us,*" or more literally, "*inside us.*" Twice he uses it. *Didn't we raise you* inside us*? And you stayed* inside us *for years of your life.* The word paints a picture of being absorbed into the body of the empire. Not a guest. Not adjacent. *Inside.* You belonged to us.

And note something precise that al-Tabari preserves about the phrase "years of your life": those years refer to Moses's time in the palace specifically *before* the killing — the full accumulation of meals, protection, education, and status that Pharaoh is now presenting as a ledger. The implication is deliberate: *all of that, and* then *you did this.* Pharaoh is not invoking a childhood memory alone. He is invoking a lifetime of accumulated debt and placing the killing at its far end as the ultimate betrayal of everything that preceded it.

And then the chilling final word of the indictment: **وَأَنتَ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ** — "and you were of the ungrateful."

The word *kafir* in Arabic, before it ever meant "disbeliever" in the theological sense, meant *one who covers over.* A farmer who buries a seed in the soil is, in classical Arabic usage, performing an act of *kufr* — he is *covering* something. By extension, **a kafir is one who covers over a kindness shown to them.** One who buries the favor. One who hides what was given to them under the soil of forgetfulness.

So Pharaoh is not just calling Moses a disbeliever. He is constructing a precise accusation: *I planted kindness in you, and you have buried it. You have committed* kufr *against me.*

But the classical commentators preserve something that the English translation cannot hold in a single phrase. Al-Muyassar reads Pharaoh's **كَافِرِينَ** as carrying **two charges simultaneously**: denial of his favor *and* rejection of his claim to divine lordship itself — those who deny his bounty and those who reject his *rububiyyah*, his sovereign authority over them. Pharaoh is not simply accusing Moses of being an ungrateful guest. He is accusing him of **theological defection** — of repudiating the claim to divine sovereignty that Pharaoh has already staked for himself.

This is not incidental. The same Pharaoh who declares **أَنَا رَبُّكُمُ الْأَعْلَىٰ** is using *kafirin* here in precisely that double register: *you have committed kufr against me, your rabb.* What sounds like a complaint about ingratitude turns out to be something darker — a charge of apostasy against Pharaoh's own divinity. The ingratitude frame and the divine-authority frame are not two separate things. They are the same accusation, running in two channels at once.

This deepens everything we have already noted about **نُرَبِّكَ** smuggling a lordship-claim into the parenting language. *Kafirin* makes that same claim explicit from the other direction: *to be ungrateful to me is to deny me as lord.* The cage Pharaoh is building has theological walls, not just moral ones.

And look at what Moses will do with this. When he responds with **ضَالِّينَ** instead of **كَافِرِينَ**, he is not just distinguishing error from ingratitude. He is refusing to acknowledge the entire theological scaffold on which the word *kafir* depends here. You cannot commit apostasy against a false god. By substituting *dall* for *kafir*, Moses rejects not just the label but the lordship-claim embedded in it — without ever arguing against it directly. The cage does not get refuted. It gets walked through.

What does this single ayah reveal? It reveals that **Pharaoh has weaponized the language of lordship, of belonging, and of gratitude all at once** — and the word *kafirin* is the sharpest instrument in the arsenal, because it encodes his claim to divine authority inside what sounds like a simple complaint about ingratitude. He is not arguing — he is constructing a moral cage in front of the entire court, and he is daring Moses to break out of it.

### Element Two: *Fa'altu-ha Idhan Wa Ana Min ad-Dallin* — The Confession That Isn't One

Now watch what Moses does. Pharaoh has just laid out a three-part attack: *I raised you. You killed someone. You are ungrateful.*

Most people, faced with this kind of attack, do one of two things. They either deny ("I didn't really do that, it wasn't my fault"), or they over-apologize ("Yes, I was terrible, please forgive me"). Both responses keep you trapped in the frame your accuser has built.

Moses does something almost no human being instinctively does. He **accepts the fact, refuses the framing, and moves the conversation forward.**

Look at his first words: **قَالَ فَعَلْتُهَا إِذًا** — "I did it *then.*"

The word **إِذًا** — *idhan* — is small but doing immense work. It means "at that time," "back then," "in that moment." It is a temporal pin. Moses is saying: *yes, that act happened, and it happened* in that period of my life. *That was then. We are not* there *anymore.*

He is not denying the deed. He is locating it. Putting it in a box of time so that it cannot define his present.

[PAUSE]

Then comes the line that is one of the most carefully chosen sentences in the entire Quran: **وَأَنَا مِنَ الضَّالِّينَ** — "and I was of those who were *dallin*."

Now you have to understand something. Pharaoh just used the word **al-kafirin** — the ungrateful, the coverers. Moses had every option to push back. He could have said, "I was not ungrateful." He could have said, "You are the disbeliever." He could have argued the label.

Instead, he **swaps the word.**

Pharaoh said *kafir.* Moses says *dall.*

These are different words from different roots, and the difference is the difference between two universes.

The root **ك-ف-ر** (*k-f-r*) — to cover over kindness — is a word about **the will.** It implies someone *chose* to deny what they knew. There is intent. There is rebellion. To be a *kafir* is to have looked at the light and pulled the blanket over your own eyes.

The root **ض-ل-ل** (*d-l-l*) — to be lost, to wander off the path — is a word about **knowledge.** It implies someone *did not yet know.* It is the word for a traveler who took a wrong turn because no one gave them a map. There is no malice in *dalal.* There is only the absence of guidance.

Look at how surgical this is.

Moses is saying: *I did the deed. But I did it from a place of not-yet-knowing. I had no prophetic guidance then. I was a young man in your court, formed by your court, who acted on impulse and took a life he should not have taken. That is not* kufr*. That is* dalal*.*

And here is the deeper move. By calling himself **مِنَ الضَّالِّينَ** — "*of* those who were astray" — Moses is doing something subtle. He is saying: *I was a member of a group of people who didn't know better. And who do you think raised that group of people? Who built the world they wandered in?*

[PAUSE]

He is, without ever pointing the finger, **implicating Pharaoh's entire civilization** as the source of his own *dalal*. Pharaoh said, "*nurabbika fina*" — we raised you among us. Moses is responding: *yes, you did. And the man you raised, in your system, in your court, with your values — that man* was lost*. What does that tell you about the system that raised him?*

This is not aggressive. It is not even argumentative. It is simply *true.* And it is one of the most beautiful examples in the Quran of how to take responsibility for a wrong without surrendering to a narrative that wants to crush you.

What does this single linguistic move reveal? It reveals that **owning what you did is not the same as accepting how someone else describes it.** Moses agrees with the fact and disagrees with the frame. He confesses the deed and rejects the label. And he does it in two words.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa Tilka Ni'matun Tamunnuha 'Alayya* — The Counter-Strike

Now we come to the line that, when you understand what it does, makes you put your hand over your mouth.

After Moses recounts his flight, his fear, and Allah's gift of *hukm* (wisdom, judgment) and prophethood, he turns and addresses Pharaoh's original claim directly. And he says:

**وَتِلْكَ نِعْمَةٌ تَمُنُّهَا عَلَيَّ أَنْ عَبَّدتَّ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ**

"*And is this* the *favor you remind me of — that you have enslaved the Children of Israel?*"

There are three words here we have to open: **ni'mah**, **tamunnuha**, and **'abbadta**.

**Ni'mah** is the standard word for a blessing, a favor, a kindness given. Moses uses it because Pharaoh has been treating his upbringing as exactly that — a *ni'mah* he is owed thanks for.

**Tamunnuha** comes from the root **م-ن-ن** (*m-n-n*). Now here is something fascinating. The same root produces **mann** — the bread Allah sent down to the Children of Israel in the wilderness, "*mann wa salwa*." It also produces **al-Mannan**, one of Allah's beautiful names — the One who bestows freely. *Mann* in its noble form means: *to give generously without expecting return.*

But there is a verbal form — **manna 'ala** — that turns this beautiful root upside down. *Manna 'ala* means: *to remind someone of a favor you did them, in order to make them feel indebted, in order to control them with the memory of the gift.*

It is the form of giving that poisons the gift.

Every Muslim recognizes this from the Quran's repeated warning: **لَا تُبْطِلُوا صَدَقَاتِكُم بِالْمَنِّ وَالْأَذَىٰ** — "Do not nullify your charities by *mann* and harm" (2:264). To remind someone of what you gave them is, in the Quranic ethic, to *destroy* the gift you gave them.

Moses uses the verb **تَمُنُّهَا** — "you do *mann* with it on me." He has already named, with one word, what Pharaoh just did. He has identified the rhetorical move. He has said: *I see what you are doing. I see the form of this attempt. You are not actually asking me to be grateful. You are using the memory of a kindness as a leash.*

[PAUSE]

But here is where it goes nuclear.

The verb at the end of the line — **عَبَّدتَّ** (*'abbadta*) — is from the root **ع-ب-د** (*'-b-d*). And every dimension of this root carries weight that transforms what the sentence means.

The root **ع-ب-د** in its basic form gives us **عَبْد** — servant, slave, worshipper. It gives us **عِبَادَة** — worship, the complete giving of oneself to the divine. These are among the most honored words in the Islamic lexicon: to be *an 'abd of Allah* is the highest human station. The same root that names what a human being is most made to do — submit entirely to the One who made them — becomes here a verb of devastation.

Because **عَبَّدَ** in Form II does not mean "to worship." It means **to enslave** — to *make* someone an *'abd* by force, to extract from human beings the posture of total submission that belongs to Allah alone and redirect it toward yourself.

This is Moses's final move, and it is a precise one. Pharaoh opened by claiming to be Moses's *rabb* — through the root **نُرَبِّكَ**, and through the accusation of *kufr* against his lordship. He said, in effect: *you owe me what an 'abd owes a rabb.*

And Moses ends with the one word that shows what kind of "lord" Pharaoh actually is. **عَبَّدتَّ** — *you enslaved.* Not "you ruled." Not "you governed." You took people — my people, the Children of Israel — and you extracted from them the submission that belongs to God alone. You built your claimed lordship on forced servitude.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the structure of the sentence as a whole. Moses says: **وَتِلْكَ نِعْمَةٌ** — *and that is a favor.* He uses **تِلْكَ** — the demonstrative that points to something known, present, specific. He is not disputing the favor. He is *placing* it. *Yes, that thing — the known, particular thing you have been holding up — that is a favor.* Then: **تَمُنُّهَا عَلَيَّ** — you are doing *mann* with it against me. And then the pivot — **أَنْ عَبَّدتَّ بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ** — given that you enslaved the Children of Israel.

The grammar makes the argument: *here is your favor (تِلْكَ), you are weaponizing it (تَمُنُّ), and the context underlying your 'favor' is your crime against my people (أَنْ عَبَّدتَّ)*. The favor rests on top of the crime. The kindness is downstream of the atrocity.

Think about what Moses is actually saying. Without the enslavement of the Children of Israel, there would have been no decree to kill Hebrew infants. Without that decree, his mother would not have placed him in a basket on the Nile. Without the basket on the Nile, there would have been no child to discover, no child to raise in the palace. The "favor" Pharaoh is invoking — *I raised you* — exists *only because* Pharaoh had already committed the crime against Moses's people that made such a rescue necessary. The favor is a consequence of the oppression. To cite the favor while ignoring the crime that preceded it is not generosity remembered — it is the hand that pushed the basket into the river taking credit for pulling it out.

Moses does not explain this causal chain with elaborate argument. He simply names the two things in the same breath — *your favor, your enslavement* — and trusts the court to hear what they hear.

What does this single linguistic move reveal? It reveals that **naming the system is the most devastating response to a system that depends on you not naming it.** Pharaoh's power in this exchange rests entirely on the court accepting that his *ni'mah* to Moses can be evaluated in isolation, free of the context of what he did to Moses's people. Moses refuses to grant that isolation. He reaches behind the favor and shows what is holding it up.

## Part 2: What Pharaoh Could Not Do

There is something worth noticing about the shape of the whole exchange.

Pharaoh came into this conversation with three weapons: the gift of upbringing (*nurabbika*), the crime of the killing (*fa'alta fa'latak*), and the accusation of ingratitude and theological defection (*kafirin*). He deployed all three in a single speech. It was, by any measure, a well-armed attack.

And Moses answered every point — not in kind, but in register. He did not counter-attack. He did not call Pharaoh a murderer or an oppressor or a false god, even though all three are true. He did something more disciplined: he *clarified*, *located*, and *named*.

He clarified: *I was not kafir, I was dall — not willfully defiant, but without guidance.*

He located: *idhan — that was then. Here is where I am now. My Lord gave me hukm and made me among the messengers.*

He named: *your favor rests on your crime.*

And then — notice this — he stops. The ayahs that follow this exchange shift to Pharaoh's demand for signs and Moses's response with miracles. The rhetorical battle is over. Moses does not continue pressing. He has said what needed to be said, planted what needed to be planted in the ears of everyone present, and moved to the substance of the mission.

This is itself a form of *hukm* — the word Allah used for what He gave Moses after the flight. **حُكْمًا** — translated "wisdom," but in Arabic *hukm* carries the sense of *judgment that is sound and weighty*, the capacity to discern what to say, when to say it, and when to stop. Moses was given prophethood, yes. But he was also given the judgment to know that against a man who has enslaved a people, the most powerful argument is not a long one.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing the exchange reveals, and it is perhaps the strangest thing of all.

Pharaoh's entire opening speech was about Moses. His upbringing, his debt, his crime, his ingratitude. Every sentence pointed at Moses. And Moses, in his response, does something Pharaoh never expected: he **stops talking about himself.** He acknowledges the past in three words (*fa'altuha idhan*), accounts for the flight in one phrase (*khi'ftukum*), and then his language opens outward — to his Lord, to the prophethood, to the Children of Israel. The moment Moses walks out of the "I" and into the "they" — **بَنِي إِسْرَائِيلَ** — the entire moral gravity of the exchange shifts.

Pharaoh had tried to make the conversation about Moses's debt to Pharaoh. Moses made it about Israel's debt to freedom.

And in doing so, he showed that the very thing Pharaoh tried to weaponize — the personal past, the childhood bond, the language of gratitude and lordship — had no hold on a man whose identity was no longer located in the palace where he was raised.

*My Lord gave me hukm. My Lord made me a messenger.* The phrase **رَبِّي** — *my Lord* — is the turning point. Moses uses the same root Pharaoh weaponized at the opening. But now it is not a question (*didn't we nurabbika you?*), and it is not a false claim. It is an acknowledgment — *rabb-i*, my Lord — the one Moses found outside the palace, in the wilderness, at the burning bush. The real one.

[PAUSE]

And here is what Pharaoh could not do, for all his power. He could not answer Moses's last sentence. He does not answer it. In the ayahs that follow, he pivots — demands signs, questions who the Lord of the worlds is, brings in Haman and the tower of bricks. He never returns to the ni'mah. The word *'abbadta* sits in the throne room, unrebutted. No counter-argument. No reframing. Because there is none.

This, too, is a kind of answer. When you name a thing truly enough, the person who built their power on its not being named cannot respond. They can only change the subject.

## For the One Holding a Pharaoh's Memory

We started with an image. Returning to the house of someone who enslaved your people. Being told, by the one who enslaved them, that you owe him something.

Most of us will never face Pharaoh. But most of us know the shape of this exchange.

Someone who hurt you — or hurt the people you love — who holds their one kindness over the full weight of their harm and says: *but I did this for you.* The parent who reminds you what they sacrificed, without mentioning what they took. The institution that credits you with opportunities, without acknowledging what it demanded. The system that fed one of yours while breaking the rest.

The Quran does not give Moses a speech here. It gives him a method.

He does not collapse under the weight of the memory — *fa'altuha idhan*, I did it then, it was then, not now.

He does not deny the kindness — *wa tilka ni'matun*, yes, that is a ni'mah, I will call it what you call it.

He names what the kindness rests on — *an 'abbadta bani isra'eel.*

And then he points to the real Rabb — *wahaba li rabbi hukman*, my Lord gave me judgment.

This is the movement. Not performance. Not explosion. Not retreat. A clear-eyed, four-step walk through a conversation designed to trap you, emerging with the truth on the table and your identity intact.

## Du'a

*O Allah — You are the true Rabb, the One who raised us, the One who formed us, before any palace or power claimed that role.*

*Give us hukm — the judgment to know what to confess and what to refuse, what to carry from the past and what to locate in the past and leave there.*

*When someone holds a kindness over us to control us, give us Moses's clarity: to receive the kindness as kindness and still name the crime as crime — without flinching, without cruelty, and without losing ourselves in the exchange.*

*When we have done wrong, let us say fa'altuha idhan — I did it then, I was without guidance then — without hiding behind it and without being buried under it.*

*And when the real Rabb calls us, as He called Moses from the right side of the mountain — let us know His voice. Let us know the difference between the one who raised us in their house and the One who made us before any house existed.*

*آمين*
