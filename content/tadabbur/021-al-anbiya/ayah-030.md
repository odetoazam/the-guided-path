# Ratq and Fatq — The Tearing That Birthed Everything

## The Ayah

> أَوَلَمْ يَرَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًۭا فَفَتَقْنَٰهُمَا ۖ وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلْمَآءِ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ حَىٍّ ۚ أَفَلَا يُؤْمِنُونَ

*Awa-lam yara-lladhīna kafarū anna-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍa kānatā ratqan fa-fataqnāhumā, wa-jaʿalnā mina-l-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy. Afa-lā yuʾminūn.*

> Have those who disbelieved not considered that the heavens and the earth were a single sealed mass, and We tore them apart? And We made from water every living thing. Will they then not believe?

---

## Introduction — Rebuilding the World

Eight ayahs ago in this surah, Allah delivered the great logical proof of tawhid: *law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illa-llāhu la-fasadatā* — "if there had been within them gods besides Allah, both would have collapsed." That argument is for the philosopher. For the man who reasons.

But what about the man who doesn't reason? What about the man who walks past the sky every morning and the sea every summer and feels nothing? What about the heart that has gone numb to wonder?

Allah does not abandon him. He turns the argument from logic to evidence. From abstract premise to walking-around proof. He says: *aw lam yara* — "have they not **seen**?"

[PAUSE]

You have to understand who is being addressed here. The Meccan disbeliever is not a stranger to the cosmos. He sleeps under the stars every night. He has watched rain crack open the desert. He has held his newborn child and felt the warm wet of water that came from inside another body. **He has lived inside the proof his whole life.** And he does not see.

This is the ayah's accusation. Not that he lacks evidence. That he is drowning in evidence, and he has gone blind in it.

So Allah names a fact about the universe that he could not have known except through this revelation, and a fact about the universe that he sees every single day. He places them side by side. And He asks: *afa lā yuʾminūn* — "will they not, *then*, believe?"

The "then" is doing the work. **Then** — after this. **Then** — given this. There is a "then" in this ayah that the listener is meant to fall through. We're going to spend the next hour falling through that "then."

The Hook: there is a word in this ayah that differs from another word by exactly one letter. The shift of that single consonant — a single parting of the lips — is, in Allah's framing, the entire history of creation. We're going to look at that letter. We're going to look at what stayed sealed when the cosmos was torn open. And we're going to ask why, for the human soul, the seal has migrated from the heavens to the heart.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: Ratq and Fatq — The Two Words That Differ by One Breath

Before we touch the Arabic, sit with this image.

You have a piece of fabric. It is sewn shut along a seam. The seam is so tight, so seamless, that you cannot find where one piece ends and the other begins. From the outside it looks like a single cloth. There is no opening. There is no way in. There is no way out.

That is *ratq.*

Now imagine the same fabric. Same threads. Same material. But the seam has been pulled apart. There is a space now where there was no space before. Through the opening, light comes in. Air moves. Things pass through. The fabric has not been destroyed — it has been *opened.*

That is *fatq.*

Hold those two images. Now let's open the Arabic.

The root **ر-ت-ق** (r-t-q) in classical Arabic dictionaries — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib — carries the meanings: *al-iltiʾām* (the closing-up of a wound), *al-iltiḥām* (the fusing-together), *sadd al-fatq* (the stopping of a tear). Its noun form is used for the closing of a fissure, the sealing of a crack, a thing that has been mended so completely it shows no seam.

The root **ف-ت-ق** (f-t-q) is its exact opposite. *Al-shaqq* (splitting), *al-faṣl* (separating), *naqḍ al-khiyāṭa* (undoing the stitching). When a tailor undoes a seam, that is *fatq.* When a midwife speaks of birth, the membrane *yanfatiq*. When a wound that had closed reopens — that is *fatq*.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the words on the page:

**رَتْقًا** — *ratqan*
**فَتَقْنَا** — *fataqnā*

They are the same word. Three letters. R-T-Q for the seal. F-T-Q for the tear. The difference between cosmos-as-singularity and cosmos-as-multiplicity is the difference between **ر** and **ف**. One letter.

Notice something about how those letters are made in your mouth. To pronounce **ر** (r), the tongue rolls — it is a sound made by *contact*, by the tip of the tongue striking the ridge behind the teeth. The tongue and the roof of the mouth come together. The articulation itself is *meeting*. *Joining*.

To pronounce **ف** (f), the upper teeth touch the lower lip and **air escapes between them**. It is a sound made by *parting*. Breath escaping through a small opening. A tiny *fatq* — a tearing — happens in your mouth every time you say the letter **fā**.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah moves from *ratqan* to *fatqnā*, the listener's mouth has just performed, in miniature, the very act being described. The tongue stops touching. The lips part. Breath escapes. **A small opening is made.** And the cosmos, on a scale you cannot fathom, has just done the same.

Stay with me, because this is not decorative. The Quran is *recited.* It enters the body through the mouth. The reciter who says these words has, in his own throat, enacted the cosmology. The seal has been broken and breath has come through. The reciter himself becomes, for an instant, a tiny image of the universe Allah is describing.

**This is the ayah's first teaching: the universe began with a tearing that let breath through. And every time you recite this ayah, your own mouth re-performs that tearing.**

[PAUSE — long]

What was the seal? The classical mufassirūn offer a composite picture, not contradictory views.

Some understood it as the cosmological seal: the heavens and the earth as a single dense undifferentiated mass, before there was distance, before there was space, before there was *between*. Allah tore the *between* into being. He made *space.* Before fatq, there was no "here" and "there." There was only "is."

Others understood it as the climatological seal: the sky was sealed shut, no rain came down. The earth was sealed shut, no plant came up. Allah tore both open. The sky began to weep, the earth began to push, and life began.

Others understood it as the biographical seal: every womb is sealed shut until Allah's command tears it open at the moment of birth. Every seed is a sealed thing until Allah's command tears it open at the moment of germination. Every egg is *ratq* until Allah's command makes it *fatq*.

These are not three competing readings. **They are three frames around one truth.** Allah is the One who takes what is sealed and tears it open so that life can come through. Cosmos. Sky. Womb. Seed. Egg. Heart. Each is *ratq* until His *fatq* releases it.

The disbeliever has been told the cosmic version. He needs only to look down at his own birth, his own breath, his own children, his own crops, to see the same act happening in front of him every day.

And he does not see.

### Element Two: *Kānatā Ratqan* — When Two-as-One Becomes One Sealed Thing

Now we have to look at something that English cannot show you. The grammar of this phrase is doing something extraordinary, and it is invisible in translation.

Let me teach the concept first, and then we'll open the ayah.

In Arabic, every noun has number: singular, dual, or plural. Every verb agrees with its subject. If the subject is two things — a dual — the verb takes a dual ending. If the subject is one thing, singular. If many, plural.

Here is the strangeness: when you have two subjects joined by *and* — *the man and the woman* — Arabic treats them as a *dual.* Two things. *They two.* The verb takes the dual ending.

Now look:

**كَانَتَا رَتْقًا**
*kānatā ratqan*

The verb **kānatā** is **3rd person feminine dual.** *They two (feminine) were.* The dual is marked by the final long *ā*. So the subject — *the heavens and the earth* — is being treated as **two feminine things.** *They-two-feminine were…*

Now look at what they were: **ratqan**. *Ratqan* is **masculine singular indefinite.** *A sealed mass.* **One** thing.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? The grammar does not match. We have a **dual subject** with a **singular predicate.** The Arabic listener's ear catches this immediately. *Kānatā* prepares you to expect "they were *two things*" — and instead Allah delivers "they were *one thing*."

This is not a grammatical error. This is **the grammar performing the meaning.** The sentence itself enacts what it describes.

Listen to it again: *kānatā* (they-two-were) *ratqan* (one sealed mass). The dual reaches into the singular. Two reach into one. The sentence is a verbal singularity — it begins as two and resolves as one. Right there in the language, Allah has shown you the cosmos as it was: **two-things-that-were-not-yet-two.**

[PAUSE]

And then watch the next word: **فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا** — *fa-fataqnāhumā* — "so We tore *them two* apart." The pronoun ending **humā** is dual. **Now** the dual returns to the grammar. *Now* the heavens and the earth are *two.* Before the tearing, the grammar held them as one. After the tearing, the grammar releases them as two.

The sentence does, in three words, what the cosmos did in unimaginable time:

- *kānatā* — promise of duality
- *ratqan* — collapse into singularity
- *fataqnāhumā* — restoration of duality through the divine act of tearing

[PAUSE — long]

This is what we mean when we say the Quran is not just *about* what Allah did. The Quran *is* what Allah did, in language. The recitation re-performs the creation. **You cannot read this ayah aloud without your mouth becoming a model of the cosmos.**

Now, here is what this teaches that the disbeliever could not have figured out by walking around in Mecca: the heavens and the earth were not always two. There was a state in which they were one. The duality you live in — sky above, ground below, space between — is not original. It was *made.* And it was made by an act of *tearing.*

The modern listener now finds himself, often unexpectedly, agreeing with the seventh-century desert. He has been taught a story in which everything that exists was once compacted into something so dense it had no separation, and from that something all space, all matter, all light came forth. He calls it by his own name. The Quran called it *ratq* fourteen hundred years ago.

But notice what the Quran does that no scientific account does. **It names the actor.** *Fataqnā.* "*We* tore them apart." The cosmos did not split itself. The seal did not undo itself. There was a *Tearer.* The *fa* before *fataqnāhumā* — that little particle that means "so" or "then" — places the divine action immediately, instantly, after the sealed state. There is no gap. *They were sealed; so We tore.* No process. No mechanism. No long causal chain. Just: **the seal, and then the Hand that opened it.**

The disbeliever has, in our age, accepted the *what* of *ratq* and *fatq*. He has not yet accepted the *Who* of *fataqnā*. The ayah's question echoes louder now than when it was first sent down: *afa lā yuʾminūn* — "**will they not, then, believe?**"

### Element Three: *Min al-Māʾ* — From Water, Not In Water

The ayah has one more linguistic gift before it closes. After the cosmos has been torn open, the next thing said is:

**وَجَعَلْنَا مِنَ ٱلْمَآءِ كُلَّ شَىْءٍ حَىٍّ**
*wa-jaʿalnā mina-l-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy*
"And We made from water every living thing."

Look very carefully at the preposition. **مِنَ** — *min*. *From.*

In Arabic, *min* is the preposition of *origin.* Of *source.* Of the thing-from-which-something-comes. It is not the preposition of *location.* That preposition is *fī* — *in.*

If Allah had said *fī al-māʾi* — "in the water" — the meaning would be: living things live *in* water, surrounded by it, dependent on its environment. That would be a true and useful observation. Fish live in water. Plants need water. We are 70% water by mass.

But that is **not** what Allah said. He said *min al-māʾi* — *from* water. As in: water is the *origin* from which living things were *made.* Not the medium they live in. Not the substance they require. **The very stuff they were brought forth from.**

[PAUSE]

The disbeliever in Mecca cannot test this. He has no microscope. He cannot look inside a cell and see that it is, by mass, mostly water. He cannot trace the embryology of his own son and discover that life begins in fluid, develops in fluid, is delivered through a tearing of fluid membranes, is suckled on fluid, weeps fluid, sweats fluid, bleeds fluid, dies and returns its fluid to the earth.

He knows none of this in detail.

But he has **lived it.** He has been born of water. He drinks water. He waters his crops. He weeps when his child dies and washes his child's body with water before burial. The whole arc of his life is bracketed by water on both sides. Allah is asking him to **see what he has been swimming in.**

And then there is one more word: **كُلَّ شَيْءٍ حَيٍّ** — *kulla shayʾin ḥayy* — *every living thing.*

*Kull* means *every.* Not *most.* Not *many.* Not *the higher animals.* **Every living thing.** Without exception. Every plant, every animal, every human, every microbe — anything that is *ḥayy*, that has *ḥayāt*, that is alive. Every single one was made *from water.*

The classical lexicons place the root **ح-ي-ي** at the heart of meaning itself. *Ḥayy* is not just "biologically functioning." It is "able to perceive, to respond, to grow, to want, to feel." When Allah names Himself *al-Ḥayy* — the Living — He is using the same word He uses for the dragonfly and the date palm and the kidney cell. **There is one word for life across the whole of being**, and it is *ḥayy.* And every instance of that word in created things — every dragonfly, every date palm, every kidney cell, every you — was made *min al-māʾ.*

[PAUSE — long]

So: a sealed cosmos that Allah tore open. From the tearing came space. From space came water. From water came every living thing.

That is the four-word summary of the entire history of the universe.

And the disbeliever, who lives in this universe, drinks this water, and is himself a piece of life made from water — does not see.

The bridge has now been built. We have looked at *ratq* and *fatq.* We have looked at *kānatā ratqan* and the grammar that performs cosmology. We have looked at *min al-māʾi* and the universal scope of life-from-water.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart.

## PART 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Migration of the Seal

Sit with this question. Where does the *seal* live now?

Not in the heavens. Allah tore those open. Not in the earth. He tore that open too. Not in the sky — the rain comes down. Not in the seed — the shoot comes up. Not in the womb — the child comes through. The whole of creation is, by Allah's act, *unsealed*. The cosmos He made is a cosmos of openings. Every wall has a door. Every membrane has a moment of tearing. Every closed thing is, on His command, going to open.

So where is the seal?

Read the ayah again. *Aw lam yara-lladhīna kafarū* — "have those who covered up not seen?" The verb at the start of the verse — *yara*, "see" — and the people of whom the question is asked — *alladhīna kafarū*, "those who have covered" — are the same root-image as the seal. **The seal has migrated from the cosmos to the eye.**

[PAUSE]

Think about what the word *kufr* actually means. Before it became a theological term, it was a farmer's word. The Arab lexicons attest its primary meaning: *to cover.* A farmer who buries seeds in the soil is, in old Arabic, a *kāfir* — a *coverer.* Night is called *kāfir* because it covers the world with darkness. A cloud is *kāfir* because it covers the sun.

To be a *kāfir* is to *cover up.* And what every *kāfir* covers, fundamentally, is something that was already there to be seen. **You cannot cover what does not exist.** Kufr is not the failure to find truth. Kufr is the act of pulling something over truth that *was already in the room.*

[PAUSE]

So now look at the architecture Allah has set up in this ayah. He has spent His half of the relationship *tearing open*. He tore the cosmos open. He tore the sky open. He tore the seed open. He tore the womb open. He has been, for as long as there has been time, the *Fātiq* — the One who opens what was closed.

And the human being, in his rebellion, spends his half of the relationship doing the *opposite.* He covers up what Allah uncovered. He pulls a sheet over the open universe and calls the resulting darkness "the way things are." He looks at water and sees H₂O. He looks at his children and sees biology. He looks at the sky and sees weather. **He has reversed Allah's direction.** Allah unsealed; he re-seals.

And this is why the ayah's question is so devastating. *Afa lā yuʾminūn?* — "will they not then believe?" The grammar here, *yuʾminūn*, is from the root **ء-م-ن**, which carries *amn* — *security, trust, peace.* To *believe* in the Quranic sense is not first to assent to a proposition. **It is to enter into a state of trust.** To put down the cover. To stop sealing. To let what is open *be open.*

[PAUSE — long]

Here is the migration of the seal stated plainly: **The cosmos is no longer ratq. The heart of the kāfir is.**

Allah did His work. Now there is no closed door in creation. The whole universe is one extended fatq — a tearing-open through which life has poured for billions of years. The only thing left to be torn open is the human chest.

And this is why the prophets keep coming back to one image — the *heart that has been sealed.* "Their hearts are sealed and they do not understand" (47:16). "Allah has set a seal upon their hearts" (2:7). "They have hearts but do not perceive" (7:179). The vocabulary is consistent. The seal that was once over the cosmos is now, in the rebellious human, over the inner ear.

Notice what this means for our own examination. **You and I are not asked to look out at the cosmos and find a missing seam.** Allah has already torn the seam. The cosmos is screaming with openings. We are asked to look *in*, at the seal that has formed *over the place that should have heard.*

The migration of the seal is the migration we need to reverse.

How do we do that?

The ayah tells us. *Aw lam yara* — "have they not *seen*?" The cure for kufr is not first an argument. The cure for kufr is *to look.* Slowly. Without coverage. At what is in front of us.

The crop coming up. The water in the cup. The child in the bed. The breath in the chest. The moon outside the window. **The places where Allah is currently performing fatq, right now, in front of us.** The unsealing has not stopped. It is happening at this moment, in a thousand places in your body, in your kitchen, in your street. The kāfir has covered his eyes. He could uncover them if he wanted.

Here is the practical application. Tomorrow, choose one ordinary thing — a sip of water, a glance at the sky, a moment with someone you love — and **ask yourself: where is the fatq here?** Where is the unsealing? Where is the act of Allah I have been walking past?

The point is not to manufacture awe. The point is to *stop manufacturing the seal.* You don't need to put wonder there. **It's already there.** Your job is to pull the cover off.

[PAUSE — long]

### Theme Two: The Self as Ratq — Why Every Real Life Requires a Tearing

We have been talking about cosmology. Now turn the lens around.

The most resistant *ratq* in the universe is not the pre-cosmic singularity. It is the human ego.

[PAUSE]

What is the ego, in the way modern life shapes it? It is a self that has built walls. It has decided who it is, what it believes, who it loves, what it deserves, what it will accept and what it will refuse. It has sewn itself shut along its seams. From the outside it looks complete. There is no opening. Nothing gets in that wasn't already approved. Nothing gets out that wasn't already rehearsed.

That is *ratq.*

Every human soul, left to itself, drifts toward *ratq.* It is the path of least resistance. The seal feels safe. The seal feels final. The seal feels like *me.*

Now read the ayah one more time and notice something extraordinary. **Allah did not let the cosmos stay sealed.** The cosmos, if asked, might have preferred to stay one. It was, after all, undivided. Whole. Singular. And Allah, in His mercy, *would not allow it.* He tore it open. Because **a sealed thing cannot give life.**

Listen to what follows in the ayah. *Wa-jaʿalnā mina-l-māʾi kulla shayʾin ḥayy.* The life came **after** the tearing. There is no life without the fatq. The water that birthed every living thing could not have flowed inside the seal. Life requires an opening. Life requires the seam to give.

Apply this to yourself.

[PAUSE]

Every real growth in your life — every one — has been a fatq. A breaking open of something you had sealed. The first time you loved someone enough to be hurt by them, that was a fatq. The first time you admitted you were wrong, that was a fatq. The first time you stood at a janāzah and felt the smallness of your own arrangements, that was a fatq. The first time you prayed and meant it, *really* meant it, when you didn't think it would help — that was a fatq.

Every encounter with truth, with beauty, with suffering, with another human soul, with Allah — has been a small tearing of a seam you thought was final.

And every time, what came through the tearing was *life.*

The ego, like the cosmos, prefers *ratq.* But the ego, like the cosmos, was made for *fatq.* You were made to be opened. You were made to let things in and let things out. You were made for a relationship of breath, of trust, of weeping, of laughter, of prayer — and **none of those work through a seal.**

[PAUSE — long]

This is why Allah keeps sending you small fatqs throughout your life. The illness that interrupts the plan. The grief that won't let you sleep. The child who asks the question you can't answer. The relationship that ends. The verse that lands and won't leave. The prayer that cracks something open in your chest.

These are not punishments. **They are mercies of unsealing.** They are Allah preventing your soul from becoming a sealed cosmos that cannot give life.

The ayah's logic is the logic of every life lived under His hand: **what has been sealed must be opened, so that what is alive can come through.**

Now — here is the paradox we must let sit. The fatq is *painful.* When a seam tears, it hurts. When a wall breaks, the structure shudders. When the ego is opened, it grieves. **Allah does not hide this.** The very word He chose — *fataqnā* — is the same word the midwife uses for birth. Birth is fatq. And every mother who has ever given birth knows: *life comes through tearing.*

This is the secret the ayah is whispering to anyone with ears to hear. **You are being torn for the same reason the cosmos was torn — so that something living can come through.** The pain of the opening is not a sign that something is wrong. It is a sign that something is *coming.*

[PAUSE]

The kāfir in this ayah is the one who looks at the great cosmic fatq, *and at his own life full of small fatqs*, and refuses to draw the connection. He sees the openings as accidents. As damage. As things to be fixed and re-sealed as quickly as possible. He spends his life trying to put the seam back. He is exhausted. The seam keeps coming undone — because **Allah keeps coming, again and again, with His tearing mercy, to keep the soul alive.**

The believer is the one who, instead, names the tearing for what it is. *This is fatq. Allah is opening me. Something living is about to come through.* And he stops fighting the opening. He lets the seam go. He says the only thing a creature can say to a Creator who has named Himself *al-Fātiḥ* (the Opener): *here is my chest. Open me how You see fit. I trust the One who tore the heavens.*

[PAUSE — long]

Practical application: identify one place in your life where you have been working very hard to keep the seam shut. A relationship you haven't forgiven. A weakness you haven't admitted. A prayer you haven't dared. A truth you have been covering. Ask yourself: *what if Allah is trying to fatq this? What if the ratq is mine, not His?* And then, even before you know what comes through the opening — let the seam go.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Look now at how the ayah is built, with all our work done.

It opens with **a question** — *aw lam yara*. Have they not *seen.*
It closes with **a question** — *afa lā yuʾminūn*. Will they not *believe.*

Between the two questions: the cosmos.

The first question is about *vision.* The last question is about *trust.* Between them, Allah places His act — the unsealing of creation, the making of life from water. The ayah's structure is itself a fatq: it opens with sight and closes with trust, and **what runs between them is the divine act that should have moved the heart from one to the other.**

The disbeliever has broken the bridge. He has the sight but not the trust. He has seen and not believed. The ayah is asking: *what is wrong with the bridge between seeing and believing in a human heart?* And the answer it has been pointing at the whole time is — **a seal.** A *ratq* in the place where there should be a *fatq.*

### One Sentence

Allah tore open the cosmos so that life could come through; the only seal left in His creation is the one the human soul places over its own perception, and the question of this ayah is whether we will let Him tear that one too.

### Bringing the Threads Together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic discoveries are one teaching seen from two sides.

**Ratq and fatq** are not just cosmology. They are the inner geography of every human life. Every soul is sealed; every soul is being torn open by the mercy of the One who unsealed the heavens.

**The dual subject with the singular predicate** is not just grammatical curiosity. It is the architecture of every relationship — including yours with Allah. Two-as-one. Distinct, but joined. The grammar of love.

**Min al-māʾ** — from water — is not just biology. It is the universal solvent of mercy. Every living thing came *from* something — an origin that was outside itself, given by Another. There are no self-made living things. **You are not your own source.**

The themes resolve into the same point. Allah is *al-Fātiḥ.* He opens. Reality opens. The cosmos opens. Life opens. The one who walks with Him learns to open. The one who refuses Him spends his life sealing.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life is Allah currently performing *fatq*, and have I been calling it damage?
2. What seam in my soul am I working hardest to keep stitched shut — and what living thing might come through if I let it tear?
3. The ayah asks "*aw lam yara* — have they not seen?" When was the last time I actually *saw* water? Not consumed it. Not ignored it. Truly *looked* at it as a thing Allah named in His Book?
4. If kufr is *covering*, what cover have I become accustomed to — what truth have I dimmed by familiarity until it stopped feeling like truth?
5. *Afa lā yuʾminūn* — will I not then *trust*? What would it look like, this week, to put down one seal and trust the Opener with what comes through?

### The Closing Invitation

You are not asked, by this ayah, to do something you have never done before. You are asked to *uncover what you have been covering.* The fatq has already happened. The cosmos is already torn open. Water is already in your cup. Life is already in your chest. **The reality is already ready. You are the only thing in the way.**

Choose one place this week — one ordinary, walked-past, sealed-over piece of your life — and uncover it. Look at it as if for the first time. Recognize it as a place where Allah is currently tearing the seam so that something living can come through.

That is what the ayah is asking. Not to learn. Not to be impressed. Not to agree. **To let the seal of *kāfir* loosen, in the only place it still lives — your own chest.**

### Du'ā

*O Allah — You who tore the heavens open so that water could fall and life could rise — tear open the seal that I have placed over my own heart. Make me one who sees what You have already shown, who trusts what You have already done, and who lets the seam give when You come, in mercy, to open me. Do not let me die a sealed thing. Bring living water through every wall I have built. Yā Fātiḥ, yā Ḥayy — open me toward You, and keep me open until I meet You.*

*Āmīn.*
