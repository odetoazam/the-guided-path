# The Breach From Every Height

## Introduction

[Arabic text]
**حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا فُتِحَتْ يَأْجُوجُ وَمَأْجُوجُ وَهُم مِّن كُلِّ حَدَبٍ يَنسِلُونَ**

[Transliteration]
*Ḥattā idhā futiḥat Ya'jūju wa-Ma'jūju wa-hum min kulli ḥadabin yansilūn.*

[Translation]
"Until when Gog and Magog are let loose and they descend from every elevation."

---

This ayah appears not in Surah Al-Kahf but in Surah Al-Anbiya — the surah of the prophets — in the midst of a passage about the approach of the Day of Judgment. It is the eschatological counterpart to Dhul-Qarnayn's barrier. In Al-Kahf, we saw the barrier being built. Here, we see what happens when it fails. The connection between the two passages transforms Dhul-Qarnayn's story from a historical narrative into a prophecy about the end of human time.

The placement inside Al-Anbiya matters as much as the content. The ayah just before this one says that no town Allah destroyed will ever come back — *wa-ḥarāmun ʿalā qaryatin ahlaknāhā annahum lā yarjiʿūn* (21:95). Ibn Kathir, quoting Ibn ʿAbbas, reads that as a decree already enforced: what has been ended does not return to this world before the Resurrection. So the ayah before ours seals a door shut. And then, immediately, ours opens one. Sealed, then opened — that is the rhythm of the passage, and it is worth carrying through everything that follows.

---

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. فُتِحَتْ (*Futiḥat*) — The Passive That Hides the Actor

The first critical word is *futiḥat* — "it was opened" or "they were let loose." This is the **passive voice**. Ya'juj and Ma'juj are not described as breaking through. They are described as being *opened* — as if a gate or seal was removed.

The passive voice in Arabic often conceals the actor deliberately. Who opens the barrier? The Quran does not say. But Dhul-Qarnayn already told us: *fa-idhā jā'a waʿdu Rabbī* — "when the promise of my Lord comes." The actor is Allah. The passive conceals not out of uncertainty but out of awe. You don't name the divine actor in the same grammatical frame as destruction — you let the absence speak.

There is a second reason this word choice lands so hard, and it comes from Al-Kahf. When Dhul-Qarnayn finished the barrier, the Quran said of Ya'juj and Ma'juj: *fa-mā-sṭāʿū an yaẓharūhu wa-mā-staṭāʿū lahu naqbā* — "they were not able to scale it, nor were they able to bore through it" (18:97). Ibn Kathir notices that the Quran uses two different shapes of the same verb there — the lighter, shortened form for climbing over, and the fuller, heavier form for digging through — and he reads the change as tracking how hard each task was. The heavier word for the heavier labour. Two ways in, and both of them shut.

Now read our ayah again. The two things they could never do are exactly the two things that do not happen. They do not scale it. They do not bore through it. It is *opened*. The way in was never going to be their strength. It was always going to be permission.

The root ف-ت-ح means to open, to unlock, to inaugurate. It is the same root as *al-Fātiḥa* — the Opener. It is the same root as *fatḥ* — victory, opening, conquest. Al-Raghib al-Isfahani defines *fatḥ* at its base as the removal of a lock and the clearing of an obstruction — the undoing of something that was deliberately closed. That is precisely the picture here. When the Quran says the barrier is *futiḥat*, it uses a word that implies a deliberate unlocking. This is not collapse from age. This is opening by design. The barrier was always a gate with a timer.

And it is sobering to notice what this same verb usually opens elsewhere in the Quran. *Law anna ahla-l-qurā āmanū wa-ttaqaw la-fataḥnā ʿalayhim barakātin mina-s-samā'i wa-l-arḍ* — "had the people of the towns believed and been mindful, We would surely have opened upon them blessings from the sky and the earth" (7:96). Same verb, same divine hand on the lock. What is opened onto a people is not always the same thing. Blessing is an opening. So is this.

There is one more detail worth slowing down for. The verb *futiḥat* is **feminine**, while the two names that follow it stand in the nominative, as though they were the thing opened. Grammatically it is an odd fit, and the commentators fill the gap. Al-Tabari's very first move is to supply what is missing: *ḥattā idhā futiḥa ʿan Ya'jūja wa-Ma'jūj* — "until it was opened *away from* Ya'juj and Ma'juj" — and he names what "it" is: *radmuhumā*, their barrier. So the sentence, unfolded, means the barrier over them was unlocked and pulled back. And al-Tabari immediately identifies them as *ummatān mina-l-umam* — two nations among the nations — which sits comfortably with the feminine verb, since *umma* is a feminine word in Arabic.

That compression is not sloppiness. By naming them where the barrier should be, the ayah fuses the two into one event. The wall coming off and the nations coming out are not two sentences. They are one word.

### 2. حَدَبٍ (*Ḥadab*) — From Every Height

*Min kulli ḥadabin yansilūn* — "from every elevation they descend."

The word *ḥadab* means a height, a hill, a raised place. The image is of Ya'juj and Ma'juj pouring over every ridge, every hilltop, every elevated point — not from one direction but from all directions simultaneously. There is no single front. There is no line to hold. They come from everywhere.

This word appears **once in the entire Quran** — here, and nowhere else. It is not part of the Quran's ordinary vocabulary for land or terrain. It was reserved. When a word is used a single time in the whole Book, the scene it describes has no second instance either; the language is as unrepeated as the event.

This is what makes the release of Ya'juj and Ma'juj different from an ordinary invasion. An army comes from one direction. A flood comes from one low point. This force descends from every height. The spatial language — *min kulli ḥadab* — communicates total, overwhelming, omnidirectional threat. No barrier can contain what comes from every direction at once.

And notice how the heights answer the barrier. Al-Muyassar glosses Dhul-Qarnayn's promise in Al-Kahf this way: when the promise of the Lord comes — the promise of their coming out — He will make the barrier *dakkā'*, torn down and **level with the ground**. The one thing that stood up is flattened. And from every raised place, they pour down. The vertical world inverts in a single stroke: the wall goes flat, and the hills fill with what the wall was holding.

### 3. يَنسِلُونَ (*Yansilūn*) — The Speed of Descent

The verb *yansilūn* — from root ن-س-ل — means to move swiftly, to slip out, to descend rapidly. The word carries a sense of things sliding or pouring out with momentum. It is not a march. It is a cascade.

Al-Raghib traces the root back to something even more physical: *al-nasl* is the coming-away of one thing from another. He gives the plainest possible examples — hair coming off an animal, feathers moulting, a garment slipping off a body. From that base sense of detaching and issuing out, he says, comes *nasala*: to run, to hasten. And he cites this very ayah as his example of it.

Which means the picture is not primarily "fast." It is *coming loose*. Something held is no longer held, and what comes away comes away quickly because it was under pressure. That is what you see when a seal breaks.

The same root gives the Quran its word for offspring — *al-nasl*, "the child, because he issues from his father," as al-Raghib puts it. *Wa-yuhlika-l-ḥartha wa-n-nasl* — "and he destroys the crops and the offspring" (2:205). *Thumma jaʿala naslahu min sulālatin min mā'in mahīn* — "then He made his offspring from an extract of a humble fluid" (32:8). Generations, too, are a thing that pours out of a container it cannot stay in. The root knows both meanings, and the ayah has chosen the one that runs.

There is one more thing about this verb that is hard to unhear once you have heard it. In the whole Quran, this verb form occurs **exactly twice**. Here — and in Ya-Sin: *wa-nufikha fī-ṣ-ṣūri fa-idhā hum mina-l-ajdāthi ilā Rabbihim yansilūn* — "and the Trumpet is blown, and at once they are pouring out from the graves toward their Lord" (36:51). The same verb. The same motion. Out of a place that was sealed, at speed, all at once.

So the Quran uses this word twice, and both times something contained is released as the Hour arrives: once from behind a wall, once from inside the earth. The breach and the resurrection are described with the same word. Whatever is holding you now is holding you on the same terms the barrier was held — until.

Combined with *min kulli ḥadab*, the image is of water breaking over every ridge — swift, unstoppable, covering everything. The Quran has chosen a word that makes you feel the speed and inevitability of the descent. They don't walk down. They *pour* down. From everywhere. All at once.

### 4. حَتَّىٰٓ إِذَا (*Ḥattā idhā*) — The Sentence That Does Not Finish

The ayah opens with *ḥattā idhā* — "until, when..." It is a phrase that promises a second half. "Until, when X happens..." — and then you wait for the *then*. Here, the *then* never arrives inside the ayah. The verse ends on *yansilūn* and stops. Grammatically, we are left standing mid-sentence.

Al-Muyassar does not leave us there. His paraphrase runs the clause straight on into the next ayah: when the barrier of Ya'juj and Ma'juj is opened, and they set out from the high places of the earth and spread swiftly through its every side — *then* the Day of Resurrection has drawn near and its terrors have appeared. Which is exactly what 21:97 says: *wa-qtaraba-l-waʿdu-l-ḥaqq* — "and the true promise has drawn near." Not all commentators join the two clauses in the same way; some hold the answer is left unspoken altogether. But every reading leans the same direction. This ayah is not a stopping place. It is a lean forward.

And then look where this phrase came from. In Al-Kahf, *ḥattā idhā* is the hinge of the entire Dhul-Qarnayn account. It appears five times: "until, when he reached the setting of the sun" (18:86), "until, when he reached the rising of the sun" (18:90), "until, when he reached between the two mountain barriers" (18:93), and then twice inside the building of the wall itself — "until, when he had levelled it between the two cliff faces," "until, when he had made it fire" (18:96). Every stage of the journey and every stage of the construction is a *ḥattā idhā*.

The barrier was built on "until, when." It is taken down on "until, when." The phrase that carried the work carries the ending of the work. Nothing about Dhul-Qarnayn's project was ever framed as permanent — the grammar told us so at every step.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: What Happens When the Barrier Falls

Dhul-Qarnayn built the barrier knowing it would not last. This ayah is the fulfilment of his prophecy. The barrier was *raḥma* — mercy — and its falling is part of the promise he acknowledged as *ḥaqq* — true.

Al-Tabari draws that mercy out carefully. Dhul-Qarnayn, he explains, saw that Ya'juj and Ma'juj could neither top what he had built nor pierce it, and so he said: this thing I have built and levelled as a wall between this nation and those on the other side is a mercy from my Lord — a mercy He extended to the people on the near side. And then al-Tabari adds a line that is easy to miss: Allah *aided him with that mercy* until he built it and levelled it. The wall was not Dhul-Qarnayn's mercy toward the people. It was Allah's mercy, and Dhul-Qarnayn was the hands.

Which is why the ending is not a tragedy. If the wall was a mercy loaned for a season, the ending of the season is not a failure of the loan. Al-Muyassar renders the promise plainly: when the promise of my Lord comes — that is, the coming out of Ya'juj and Ma'juj — He will make it *dakkā'*, torn down and level with the ground.

The destruction of the barrier is not a defeat. It is a transition. The barrier existed in historical time to serve its purpose. Its dissolution belongs to the time of the Hour — a different kind of time altogether. Dhul-Qarnayn operated in the first. This ayah belongs to the second.

What this means practically: the protections we build in this world are real and valid in their time. But they are not designed to survive the transition to the next world. Every wall, every system, every institution will face its *futiḥat* — the moment when it is opened by divine decree and what it held back pours through. The question is not whether this will happen. The question is whether, like Dhul-Qarnayn, you built knowing it would.

### Theme 2: The Scale Beyond Human Containment

The image of Ya'juj and Ma'juj descending from every height is an image of something beyond human capacity to contain. The barrier was the greatest human engineering achievement in the Quran. And it was temporary. What it held back cannot ultimately be stopped by human construction.

But be precise about what "temporary" means here, because the Quran is precise. The barrier did not underperform. For its whole term it did exactly what it was built to do: they could not go over it and they could not go through it (18:97). It held. It held for as long as it was meant to hold. The wall did not lose. The wall was opened.

That distinction is the whole difference between despair and proportion. Build what you can. Protect who you can. Do it with excellence and mercy, as Dhul-Qarnayn did. But know that there are forces in creation that exceed human containment. The barrier buys time. It does not change the final outcome. Only divine promise — *waʿdu Rabbī* — determines the final outcome.

The practical wisdom: do your work knowing that some problems are beyond your capacity to permanently solve. Not every struggle ends in victory. Some struggles are about faithfulness in the interim — building the barrier that serves its purpose until the promise comes. That is enough. Dhul-Qarnayn thought so.

### Theme 3: The Door That Closed and the Gate That Opened

Go back to the ayah just before ours. *Wa-ḥarāmun ʿalā qaryatin ahlaknāhā annahum lā yarjiʿūn* — no destroyed town returns (21:95). Ibn Kathir reports Ibn ʿAbbas glossing that as something already fixed and enforced, and he notes that Abu Jaʿfar al-Baqir, Qatadah and others transmit the same sense: a people that has been ended does not come back to this world before the Resurrection.

Set the two verses side by side and the shape is stark. One door is permanently shut — you cannot go back. One gate is unlocked at its appointed hour — this is what comes forward. Between them there is no third option. The past does not reopen; the future does. That is the direction the whole passage is pointed, and it is why the paragraph ends where it does, with eyes fixed open in terror and the words *yā waylanā qad kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā* — "woe to us, we were heedless of this" (21:97).

Al-Muyassar's paraphrase makes the emotional line explicit: the terrors appear, and the eyes of those who disbelieved are wide open, barely blinking, and they call down ruin on themselves in regret — we were distracted, we were unprepared for this day, and by that we were wrongdoers. The confession names the sin exactly. Not disbelief in the abstract. *Ghaflah* — inattention. They were not paying attention while the thing they were told about was on its way.

Which means the ayah is not really information about a future event. It is a question about present attention.

### Theme 4: What the Commentators Do Not Say

It is worth noticing how restrained the classical commentary is here, because this is exactly the kind of ayah that invites people to say far more than they know.

Al-Tabari, given the chance to describe Ya'juj and Ma'juj, gives them four words: *ummatān mina-l-umam* — two nations among the nations. That is the whole identification. He does not map them, does not date them, does not assign them to a people you could point at. He then does what he characteristically does with matters of the unseen: he stops explaining and starts transmitting. He brings a chain reaching back to Hudhayfah ibn al-Yaman, who reports the Prophet ﷺ listing the signs — the Dajjal, the descent of ʿIsa, a fire that comes out and drives people to the gathering place, the smoke, the Beast, and then Ya'juj and Ma'juj. Ya'juj and Ma'juj are placed in a sequence, not on a map.

That restraint is itself the teaching. What we are given is that they exist, that they are held, that they will be released, and that their release stands near the end of the list. What we are not given is who they are in any way that would let us look around and point. The Quran's own silence on that question is not an invitation to fill it in. Every generation has been tempted to identify Ya'juj and Ma'juj with whatever nation frightened it most, and every generation that did so was reading its own fear back into revelation rather than reading revelation.

The safest posture is the one al-Tabari models: hold what is transmitted, decline what is not, and let the ayah do its actual work — which is not to tell you who is coming, but to tell you that a seal you did not set will be lifted at an hour you were not given.

## Closing Synthesis

This ayah completes the circle that began in Al-Kahf 18:98. Dhul-Qarnayn said: "When the promise of my Lord comes, He will level it." Here, in Al-Anbiya, we see the promise arriving. The barrier is opened. Ya'juj and Ma'juj descend from every height. The passive voice (*futiḥat*) confirms that this is divine action, not structural failure. And the cascade imagery (*yansilūn min kulli ḥadab*) confirms that what pours through is beyond all human containment.

Every word in the ayah is pulling the same direction. *Ḥattā idhā* leaves the sentence unfinished, so you have to lean forward into the next verse to land it. *Futiḥat* is passive, so the hand that unlocks stays out of frame. *Ḥadab* is a word the Quran uses nowhere else, for a scene that has no second instance. And *yansilūn* is the same verb the Quran uses for the dead pouring out of their graves — one release rhyming with the other. A short verse, and not one loose piece in it.

Together, the two passages teach a single lesson: **build with everything you have, for as long as you can, knowing that the final chapter belongs to Allah alone.**

And there is a mercy hidden in the ordering. We are told this in advance. The people in 21:97 confess *kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā* — "we were heedless of this." *This* — the very thing they had been told. The whole reason the ayah exists in the Book is so that no one has to say that sentence.

### Questions to Carry

- What barriers in your life are you maintaining — and what would it mean to acknowledge that they are temporary?
- Where do you mistake the effectiveness of your protections for permanence?
- How does knowing that some outcomes are beyond your control change the way you do the work that is within your control?
- The ayah does not finish its own sentence. Where in your life are you living inside a "until, when..." that you have started treating as permanent?
- The regret in the next verse is *ghaflah* — not rejection, just inattention. What have you been told clearly and stopped paying attention to?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The barrier's falling is not its failure — it is the arrival of the promise that Dhul-Qarnayn called true before it was even built.**

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, give us the faithfulness to build what we can and the humility to know that the final opening belongs to You. When our barriers are breached and what we contained pours through, let us be found standing on the promise we always knew was true.*
