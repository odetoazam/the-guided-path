# Al-Mustaʿān — The Closing Verse of Surat al-Anbiya

> [ayah:21:112] قَـٰلَ رَبِّ ٱحْكُم بِٱلْحَقِّ ۗ وَرَبُّنَا ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنُ ٱلْمُسْتَعَانُ عَلَىٰ مَا تَصِفُونَ
>
> *Qāla rabbi-ḥkum bi-l-ḥaqq, wa rabbunā r-Raḥmānu l-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūn.*
>
> "He said: My Lord — judge in truth. And our Lord is the Most Merciful — the One whose help is sought — against what you describe."

---

## Introduction — Where We Are Standing

Before we open this ayah, we have to know where in the surah we are. Because this is not just any verse. This is the **closing line.** The last sentence the surah utters before it falls silent.

And the surah we have just walked through is called **Al-Anbiya** — *The Prophets*.

Take a moment to feel what that means. We have just been led, scene after scene, through a museum of mocked men. Nūḥ, mocked by his people for centuries until the water came. Ibrāhīm, called crazy by his father's tribe and thrown into a fire. Lūṭ, surrounded by a city that wanted to humiliate his guests. Mūsā, sent to the most arrogant tyrant on earth. Yūnus, who departed in anger without permission and was swallowed. Zakariyyā, an old man begging for a child. Maryam, slandered for the most miraculous birth in history. And finally — Muḥammad ﷺ, whose own people are at this very moment calling him a poet, a magician, a madman.

[PAUSE]

The whole surah is this single, repeated picture: **the messenger arrives carrying truth — the people respond with mockery — Allah vindicates.** Over and over. The pattern is so insistent that by the end of the surah you start to feel it as a law of the universe.

And then, at the very end, the surah turns to the listener. It says, essentially: *now you have seen the pattern. You have seen what was done to every prophet before. You have seen how it ended. Here is what the Prophet ﷺ is told to say when the same script is being run on him.*

That sentence is our ayah. It is the closing line. It is the door the surah leaves open as it walks out.

Now consider: when a man has been mocked and lied about and threatened, and he has tried argument, and he has tried patience, and he has run out of human options — what does he do? What words does he reach for?

That is what this ayah answers. **It is the verbal posture of someone who has nothing left but the Judge.**

Listen to it again, slowly:

*"My Lord — judge in truth. And our Lord is the Most Merciful — the One whose help is sought — against what you describe."*

Three movements in one sentence. A request for judgment. A pivot of pronoun. And a divine name we will spend most of our time inside today — **al-Mustaʿān** — *the One whose help is sought.*

There is a name in the Quran that Allah uses for the moment when human strength has given out. It does not appear in the famous lists you may have memorized. It appears here, in this closing verse, after a whole surah of mocked prophets — and once more on the lips of an old, weeping father in Surat Yūsuf, at the moment his sons lay a bloodied shirt before him and tell him his beloved son is gone.

Today we want to ask: **why this name, here? And what does it teach us about what to do when we have done everything we can do?**

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will work with three things in the text. The first is a small word — the very first word, in fact. The second is a shift between two pronouns that almost no one notices. The third is the name that closes the surah: **al-Mustaʿān.**

---

### Element One — *Qāla*: The Verb That Holds a Whole Posture

The ayah opens with one word: **qāla** — "he said."

In English, this is the most boring possible verb. *He said.* We use it a thousand times a day; we don't notice it. So before we look at why Allah chose this verb here, we have to slow down and look at what verbs *do* in Arabic.

In Arabic, every verb encodes time, person, and action — but it also encodes *posture*. The form of the verb tells you whether the action is being done, was done, will be done, asked of someone, or being repeated. *Qāla* is a perfect tense — past, completed. *He said.* Not "he is saying." Not "he says." Not "he will say."

[PAUSE]

Now look around. The whole surah has been narrating prophets. *He said* this; *they said* that. The verb *qāla* echoes throughout the chapter. Nūḥ said. Ibrāhīm said. Lūṭ said. Sulaymān said. Yūnus said. The surah is a long chain of prophetic *qāla*s.

And now, at the end, the surah closes with one more *qāla.* But the speaker is left **unspecified**. Look at the Arabic carefully. It does not say *qāla Muḥammad*. It does not say *qāla rasūlu-Llāh*. It just says *qāla* — *he said.*

This is what classical scholars notice. There are in fact two famous readings of this opening word in the qirāʾāt tradition:

- One reading is *qāla* — "**he** said" (past tense, narration).
- Another reading is *qul* — "**say**" (imperative, command).

Both are well-attested in the canonical readings; both are part of how the Muslim community has received this verse. Al-Ṭabarī, in his commentary, renders the passage in the imperative: "Say, O Muḥammad — My Lord, separate between me and those who denied me." That he reaches for *qul* in his own explanatory prose, even while the transmitted text carries *qāla*, is telling. It suggests the two readings are almost synonymous in meaning — and yet not quite.

Notice: when Allah says *qul* — *say* — He is commanding the Prophet ﷺ to put these specific words in his mouth right now. When Allah says *qāla* — *he said* — He is reporting that the Prophet ﷺ already does say this. He has internalized it. It is no longer a command being issued; it is a posture already inhabited.

In other words, the two readings together give you the full arc:

> Allah commands him to say it (*qul*) → he says it (*qāla*) → it becomes how he stands.

This is one of those moments where the variant readings are not in conflict. They are layered. They tell you a story together that neither tells alone. Classical scholars treated this as a feature, not a problem — multiple readings as multiple camera angles on the same event.

But notice something else. The speaker of *qāla* is unspecified. The text leaves the subject open. Yes, primarily it refers to the Prophet ﷺ — that is the closest grammatical antecedent and the consensus understanding. But by leaving the subject ambient, the verse opens its mouth wide enough for **every person who has ever found themselves where the Prophet ﷺ stood** to step into it.

Stay with me on this. Every reader of this ayah, in every century, who has been mocked for trying to do right, who has been lied about, who has run out of human options — every such reader can step into the unspecified *qāla*. He said. She said. *I said.*

This is what classical balāghah calls the use of the unspecified subject as an invitation to participate. The verse hands you the script. It does not lock the script to one mouth.

So the ayah opens with this small, ordinary verb — *qāla* — and that single word is already doing three things at once:

1. It connects the Prophet ﷺ to every prophet whose *qāla* has filled the surah.
2. It tells you that he has internalized this — it is not a fresh command, it is a settled posture.
3. It leaves the door open for you to step into the saying.

And we have not even passed the first word yet.

### Element Two — *Rabbi* → *Rabbunā*: The Pronoun That Pivots Mid-Sentence

Now look at the structure of the sentence. There are two clauses joined by *wa* — "and."

> Clause 1: **Rabbi**-ḥkum bi-l-ḥaqq — "**My** Lord, judge in truth."
>
> Clause 2: Wa **Rabbunā** r-Raḥmān al-Mustaʿān — "And **our** Lord is the Most Merciful, the One whose help is sought."

Do you see what happened? The pronoun changed.

In the first half of the sentence: *rabbī* — **my** Lord.

In the second half of the sentence: *rabbunā* — **our** Lord.

This is something Arabic does that English barely registers. In English, "my Lord" and "our Lord" feel almost interchangeable. In Arabic, the shift from singular possessive to plural possessive in the same breath is a deliberate rhetorical move. Classical rhetoricians had a name for it — **iltifāt** — "turning the face."

It is the deliberate, mid-sentence shift in pronoun, person, or direction of address that the Quran uses constantly to do work that no other linguistic tool can do.

Now, why does the iltifāt happen here? Why does the verse start with "**my** Lord, judge" and pivot to "**our** Lord is the Helper"?

Let's sit with this.

In the first clause — *rabbī-ḥkum* — there is a request. *My Lord, judge.* This is a private prayer. It is the man on his face, alone. The grievance is personal. The mockery has been aimed at him, individually. The lies have been about him. The threats have targeted his body. So the request comes from the most intimate, singular place: **my** Lord.

But notice what is being asked for. He does not ask: *my Lord, take revenge for me.* He does not ask: *my Lord, destroy them.* He asks for **judgment in truth** — *uḥkum bi-l-ḥaqq*. He asks for the case to be opened, the evidence weighed, the truth known. Notice how restrained that is. Notice how the personal injury is immediately handed up to a higher court. He does not ask Allah to be on his side; he asks Allah to be on truth's side.

[PAUSE]

And then the sentence pivots. *And* — *wa* — *our Lord is...*

Why does it widen here? Because the second clause is no longer a request. It is a **statement of belonging**. It is no longer "fix my problem." It is "this is who we are. This is the camp we stand in."

When you state belonging, you do not state it alone. *My Lord* is the language of personal need; *our Lord* is the language of communal identity. The Prophet ﷺ pivots from "deal with my situation" to "and by the way — we belong to the Most Merciful, the One whose help is sought." It is no longer about him. It is about the camp the believers belong to.

Listen to the shape of it: *the personal grievance is held alone, the communal identity is held together.* You take your own pain to Allah privately — *my* Lord. You stand inside the believing community publicly — *our* Lord.

That is a posture for a lifetime, not just a verse.

Notice how the iltifāt does this in a single breath. Without the pronoun shift, the sentence would either be all "my" — too narrow — or all "our" — too distant from the immediate hurt. With the shift, both registers are honored at once. *My pain* is taken seriously by being taken to the Judge alone. *Our identity* is taken seriously by being affirmed in the plural.

This is what we mean when we say the Quran does work in three syllables that English would need a paragraph to do. *Rabbi* → *rabbunā* — and a whole theology of how a believer holds personal grief inside communal belonging passes through that small change.

### Element Three — *Al-Mustaʿān*: The Name of Allah for When Strength Has Given Out

Now we come to the heart of this ayah. The name.

> *al-Raḥmānu **l-Mustaʿān** ʿalā mā taṣifūn*
>
> "the Most Merciful — **the One whose help is sought** — against what you describe."

**Al-Mustaʿān**. Let us open this name carefully, because it is doing something that none of the more famous divine names quite do.

The root is **ʿ-w-n** — to help, to aid, to assist. From this root come several words you may know: *muʿīn* (helper), *iʿānah* (assistance), *taʿāwun* (cooperation). Plain helping.

But *al-Mustaʿān* is not from the simple form of this verb. It is from **Form X** — *istafʿala* — which in Arabic adds the meaning of *seeking* or *requesting* the action of the base verb.

Compare:
- *ʿawn* = help
- *istaʿāna* = to **seek** help
- *al-Mustaʿān* = the One **from whom help is sought**

This is a **passive participle** of Form X. The form itself encodes that someone is doing the seeking. Help is not just being given — it is being **asked for** — and the One from whom it is asked is *al-Mustaʿān*.

Now here is what is striking. *Al-Mustaʿān* is not, on its surface, a name about what Allah *does*. It is a name about what *we* do toward Him. It defines Him by the relation we take up — by the asking. It is as if Allah is saying: this is who I am for the one who has nowhere else to turn.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. This matters.

Many of Allah's names describe an attribute He possesses regardless of us. *Al-Quddūs* — the Holy. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty. *Al-Khāliq* — the Creator. These are who He is whether or not anyone is praying.

But *al-Mustaʿān* is different. It is who He is **in relation to the one who is seeking help.** It is the name that requires a hand reaching up. The name that activates the moment a slave has nothing left.

You do not call upon *al-Mustaʿān* when things are going well. You do not whisper this name when the bills are paid and the family is healthy and the work is praised. *Al-Mustaʿān* is the name you reach for when:

- you have argued and the argument is over
- you have planned and the plans have collapsed
- you have run and there is nowhere left to run
- you have spoken and no one is listening

This is the name for the cliff edge.

And notice the **definite article** — *al-*. Not "a one whose help is sought." THE one whose help is sought. As if Allah is saying: when you have run out of human help, do not look around for which door to knock on. There is one door. *Al-*. The One. Exclusively.

[PAUSE]

Now there is one more thing to notice, and it is the most beautiful detail of the whole ayah.

Look at what name *al-Mustaʿān* is paired with.

It is not paired with *al-Jabbār* — the Compeller. It is not paired with *al-Muntaqim* — the Avenger. It is not paired with *al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty.

It is paired with **al-Raḥmān** — *the Most Merciful.*

> *Wa rabbunā **r-Raḥmānu l-Mustaʿān** ʿalā mā taṣifūn.*
>
> "And our Lord is the Most Merciful — the One whose help is sought — against what you describe."

And this pairing is not incidental. It is the theology of the verse in miniature.

When we are mocked and cornered and exhausted, the instinct of our hearts is to want a warrior-God. We want *al-Jabbār* to arrive with force. We want *al-Muntaqim* to avenge every false word spoken about us. There is nothing dishonest about that instinct; the Quran acknowledges it.

But the Prophet ﷺ is taught something different. He is taught to name the God who helps him as **the Most Merciful.** The help that comes when human strength runs out — that help wears the face of mercy. *Al-Raḥmān* is not simply another divine name standing beside *al-Mustaʿān* the way entries stand beside each other in a list. *Al-Raḥmān* names the *mode* of the help. The One who answers when you have nothing left answers as the Most Merciful.

This means something for us. It means that when the breakthrough finally comes — after the long wait, after the exhaustion, after all the argument has failed — you will recognize it not only by its power but by its mercy. It will arrive with a quality of tenderness. It will feel, somehow, like being known.

### *Mā Taṣifūn* — Against What You Describe

We should not pass over the closing phrase without pausing.

*ʿAlā mā taṣifūn* — "against what you describe."

The verb *waṣafa* (root: **w-ṣ-f**) means to describe, to characterize, to paint with words. It is the root for *waṣf* — verbal portraiture. And the "you" here is plural — *taṣifūn* — addressed to the disbelievers who have been the antagonists of the whole surah.

Classical commentators understood "what you describe" (*mā taṣifūn*) as sweeping up the full weight of what the opponents were saying and doing. It covered their theological attacks — ascribing partners to Allah, denying the Prophet's message, slandering the divine — and alongside all of that, their worldly threats: their confident predictions that the believers would be overpowered and defeated, that the mockers would prevail. The phrase bundles all of it together. Both the lies about God and the intimidation directed at His servants are what the believers bring to al-Mustaʿān.

Notice how precise this is. The prayer does not say "help us generally." It says "help us *against what they are describing.*" The help is targeted. It is asked for in direct proportion to the specific shape of the attack. The believer does not pretend the threat is not happening. He does not spiritualize the danger away. He names it — *mā taṣifūn*, all of it, exactly as it is — and hands it to the One whose help is sought.

[PAUSE]

And here is a detail that requires a reader to have Surat Yūsuf close at hand. This closing phrase — *al-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūn* — appears one other time in the Quran. It is in **12:18**, on the lips of Yaʿqūb.

His sons return to him with a bloodied shirt. They say: a wolf ate Yūsuf. And Yaʿqūb, old and weeping, looks at the shirt and says:

> *Faṣ-ṣabru jamīl wa-Llāhu l-mustaʿānu ʿalā mā taṣifūn.*
>
> "Beautiful patience. And Allah is the One whose help is sought against what you describe."

Same name. Same phrase. A different *mā taṣifūn* — here it is the false description his sons have offered him, the invented story, the lie about the wolf.

Yaʿqūb at this moment is not yet blind — his blindness from prolonged grief comes later, at 12:84, after he has lost Yūsuf and then Binyāmīn both. He can still see. But he cannot see the truth of what happened to his son, because his sons have hidden it behind a fabricated account. And so he turns to the One whose sight has no such limitation. The One who already knows *mā taṣifūn* — what they are describing, and what they are concealing.

Two prophets. Two entirely different kinds of betrayal — one from enemies outside the faith, one from inside the family. And in both cases, the person who cannot prove what he knows, who has no earthly recourse, who has only his certainty and his grief, reaches for the same name. This is not coincidence. It is instruction. This is the name for every human being who stands before a false description with no way to refute it on their own.

## PART 2: THE PRAYER AND WHAT IT ASKS FOR

Now we need to sit with *iḥkum bil-ḥaqq* — "judge in truth" — and understand what is actually being requested.

The Arabic word *ḥakama* (root: **ḥ-k-m**) carries a range that English "judge" does not fully cover. It means to rule, to decide, to arbitrate between parties, to pronounce a verdict. And it is related to *ḥikmah* — wisdom — because the act of true judgment is the act of wisdom at its sharpest.

But classical commentators hear something specific in *iḥkum bil-ḥaqq* that "judge in truth" alone flattens. Al-Ṭabarī renders the request as: *ifṣil baynanā wa bayna man kadhdhabana* — "**separate between us** and those who denied us." The word he uses — *faṣl* — is the word for a decisive cutting, a clean verdict that places two parties definitively on different sides of a line. He draws the parallel to the prayer of Shuʿayb in Surat al-Aʿrāf (7:89):

> *Rabbunā ftaḥ baynanā wa bayna qawminā bil-ḥaqq, wa anta khayru l-fātiḥīn.*
>
> "Our Lord, open/decide between us and our people in truth — for You are the Best of those who decide."

This is not simply "be fair, Allah." It is: *I am bringing You a case. There are two parties. One is carrying truth; the other is carrying falsehood. I am asking You — You, the only One who can do this — to issue the verdict that separates them.* Shuʿayb says it. The Prophet ﷺ says it. The same structure, the same submission, centuries apart. It is the posture of every prophet who has made his case, been refused, and is now laying the file before the Supreme Court.

Ibn ʿAbbās, commenting on this verse as transmitted by al-Ṭabarī, offers a sentence that sounds simple and lands like a stone: *Lā yaḥkumu bil-ḥaqqi illā Allāh* — "**None judges in truth but Allah.**"

Read that again. Every mob that gathered against a prophet was convinced it was on the side of truth. Every crowd that called a messenger a madman did so sincerely. Every tyrant who dismissed a divine warning did it with the confidence of someone who felt entirely justified. And behind all of them — behind every human verdict issued against every mocked prophet in this surah — is a judgment that was partial, distorted, or corrupt.

"None judges in truth but Allah."

When the Prophet ﷺ says *rabbi-ḥkum bil-ḥaqq*, he is not asking Allah to validate his feelings. He is submitting the entire case — his credibility, his message, his reputation, the entire argument — to the One Arbiter who is incapable of being wrong.

### The Restraint Inside the Request

There is one more thing to observe about what this closing prayer *does not* contain — and why that matters.

The surah has recorded the fates of those who mocked the prophets before. The water came for Nūḥ's people. The fire rained on Lūṭ's. Pharaoh drowned. And the surah has used some of its most vivid language to describe these ends.

But the Prophet ﷺ does not close with any of that. He does not say: *bring on them what you brought on those before.* He does not name the punishment. He does not describe the suffering he wants for his mockers.

He asks for **judgment in truth** and names Allah as **the Most Merciful One whose help is sought**.

This is the restraint of someone who trusts the Judge so completely that he does not need to write the sentence himself. You do not micromanage the verdict when you trust the court absolutely. You state the injury. You name the Judge. You ask for *ḥaqq* — truth — and you leave it there.

And that restraint is itself a form of *tawakkul* — reliance. It says: I know You will do what is right. I do not need to tell You how.

The Prophet ﷺ is, at the end of a surah about prophets who were all eventually vindicated, making the same choice every one of them made: he is handing the file over. He is not closing the case himself. He is doing the only thing left to do — submitting it to the One whose judgment cannot be appealed, corrupted, or delayed forever.

*Rabbi-ḥkum bil-ḥaqq.*

My Lord, judge in truth.

[PAUSE]

## Closing — The Name You Reach For Last

We have been inside this ayah for a long time. Let us hold the whole thing in one hand before we leave.

The surah of the prophets has just shown you prophet after prophet — mocked, denied, threatened, swallowed, isolated, and vindicated. The whole surah is an argument made in narrative: *this has happened before. It always ends the same way.*

And at the very end, the surah does not summarize. It does not say: "and so you see, the truth always wins." It gives the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every believer in every century — a single sentence to say when they find themselves in the museum.

The sentence has three movements.

*My Lord* — the approach, singular and intimate, the private grief of a man who has been lied about.

*Judge in truth* — the submission of the case to the only court that matters; the restraint of someone who does not need to write the verdict himself. A request not for revenge but for *faṣl* — that decisive separation of truth from falsehood that only Allah can perform.

*And our Lord is the Most Merciful — the One whose help is sought — against what you describe* — the pivot from personal to communal, the naming of the God who helps as the God whose help is mercy, and the precise identification of what we are bringing Him: *mā taṣifūn*, everything you are saying, everything you are threatening, all of it, held up and handed over.

Al-Mustaʿān. The name for the cliff edge.

It appears here, at the end of the surah of the prophets, because this is where every prophet eventually stood — at the limit of what a human being can do. And it appears in Surat Yūsuf, on the lips of a father who cannot see what became of his son and cannot prove his sons lied, because that is also a cliff edge.

The name is for anyone who has run out of road.

**And our Lord is the Most Merciful — the One whose help is sought — against what you describe.**
