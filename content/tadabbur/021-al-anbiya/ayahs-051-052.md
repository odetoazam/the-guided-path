# Rushdahu Min Qablu — The Knowing That Precedes the Giving

## The Two Ayahs

> وَلَقَدْ آتَيْنَا إِبْرَاهِيمَ رُشْدَهُ مِن قَبْلُ وَكُنَّا بِهِ عَالِمِينَ
> إِذْ قَالَ لِأَبِيهِ وَقَوْمِهِ مَا هَٰذِهِ التَّمَاثِيلُ الَّتِي أَنتُمْ لَهَا عَاكِفُونَ

*Wa laqad ātaynā Ibrāhīma rushdahu min qablu wa kunnā bihi ʿālimīn — idh qāla li-abīhi wa qawmihi mā hādhihi t-tamāthīlu llatī antum lahā ʿākifūn.*

**"And We had certainly given Abraham his sound judgment before, and We were of him well-Knowing — when he said to his father and his people, 'What are these statues to which you are devoted?'"**

---

## Building the World

Before we open these words, sit with where we are.

We are at the heart of Surah Al-Anbiya. The surah opened with humanity — *heedless, turning away* — and with the heavens declared as not-play, not-amusement. Then came a parade of certainties: Allah is One, the prophets are one community, the resurrection is real, the cosmos itself is signing every moment.

And now — almost without warning — the camera moves. From the cosmic to a single household. From the heavens turning above us to a young man standing in front of carved stones.

The household is in Babylon. The young man's father, Āzar, makes idols for a living. The community surrounding him has been clinging to these stones for generations — *we found our fathers doing this*. Every adult he has ever known has bowed to these likenesses. Every uncle. Every neighbor. Every elder he has been taught to respect.

The young man is Ibrahim.

[PAUSE]

What you are about to hear is, in the Quranic narrative, **his first recorded sentence to his people**. Not a sermon. Not a treatise. A question.

But before we hear his question, Allah does something extraordinary. He introduces Ibrahim. And the introduction contains a sentence whose grammar is more astonishing than its plain meaning. Allah says: *"and We were of him well-Knowing."*

Past tense. *Were.*

Allah's knowledge, of course, has no beginning and no end — He is *al-ʿAlīm*, the Eternally-Knowing. So why would the grammar here reach for a past-tense construction, as if reporting a completed state? What is this phrasing trying to show us about the *shape* of that knowing, if not its starting point?

[PAUSE]

There is a hidden door in this verse. Let's find it together.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Rushdahu* — Not "Guidance," But *His* Guidance

The first word that asks us to slow down is *rushdahu*. Translated as "his sound judgment," "his right way," "his maturity."

Before we go to the verse, let's understand the word itself.

In Arabic, the root *r-sh-d* doesn't simply mean "guidance" the way we usually think of guidance. The Arabs used a different word for the act of being shown the way — *hidāyah*. *Hidāyah* is the showing. *Rushd* is something deeper. *Rushd* is the **internal capacity** to recognize the right path when shown. Many commentators extend this further — to the maturity that not only recognizes the truth but is drawn toward it — though that volitional shade is an interpretive extension rather than something the lexical root strictly states on its own. At its core, it is the maturity of the inner faculty itself.

A child who is shown the door but does not yet know to walk through it has *hidāyah* without *rushd*. *Rushd* is the developed faculty. The seeing eye behind the eye.

Now — Allah does not say *"We gave Ibrahim rushd."* He says: *"We gave Ibrahim **his** rushd."*

*Rushdahu.* His rushd. The possessive pronoun is doing real work.

Stay with that for a moment.

The Arabic could easily have said *ātaynā ibrāhīma rushdan* — "We gave Ibrahim a guidance." Indefinite. A portion. A measure. Instead, the verse says: *rushdahu* — definite, possessive, **the rushd that was specifically his**.

What does it mean for a rushd to belong to someone before they receive it?

It means: there was a maturity, a capacity, a particular shape of seeing that was *fitted* to Ibrahim. It existed in Allah's knowledge with his name on it. When Allah gave it, He was not handing out a generic gift from a shelf. He was returning to Ibrahim something already inscribed for him.

Think of it this way. There is a difference between a school giving every student a uniform — same size, same cut — and a tailor handing a suit to one specific person who walks in. The first is *rushd*. The second is *rushdahu*.

[PAUSE]

This single suffix — that small *-hu* — is teaching us something theologically immense. **Allah's gifts to His servants are not generic. They are personal. They were yours before you received them.**

### Element Two: *Min Qablu* — Before What?

Now the next phrase: *min qablu*. "From before."

We gave Ibrahim his rushd — *from before*.

Before what?

In Arabic, when you say *qabla* and then specify the thing — *qabla l-yawm*, "before the day"; *qabla an yaʾtiya*, "before he came" — the *qabla* is anchored. You know what it is before.

But here Allah says simply *min qablu* — and stops. No object. No specification. The *qabla* is left **floating**.

The classical grammarians noticed this. When Arabic leaves *qablu* without an object, it often means *before everything that could be said next*. Before the events being narrated. Before the moment of confrontation. Before his prophethood. Before the request to his Lord. Before the breaking of the idols.

But notice — the more you try to fix the "before" to one specific moment, the more it slips. Because every candidate you supply, the verse seems to leap past. Was it before he confronted his people? Yes. Before he was a prophet? Yes. Before he asked? Yes. Before he could speak?

[PAUSE]

The unspecified *qabla* is not a gap in the language. It is a **deliberate openness**. Allah is letting us walk backward through Ibrahim's life, and the rushd is always already there. There is no point at which you arrive and find it absent. It precedes every scene we will be shown.

Classical commentators do offer candidates for what fills that silence. Al-Jalalayn glosses it as *hadāhu qabla bulūghihi* — "He guided him before he reached maturity" — reading the "before" as before adulthood itself, a rushd given while Ibrahim was still young. Al-Muyassar reads it differently again: the guidance Ibrahim was given, and called others to, *before Mūsā and Hārūn* — placing him first in a line of callers to truth. Two different anchors, offered by two different commentators, for the same floating word. That, if anything, confirms rather than resolves the openness: the "before" is spacious enough to hold both readings at once.

This is the linguistic shape of *taufīq* — divinely given enabling. By the time you see the prophet acting rightly, the rightness was already given. The action you are about to witness is not the cause of the gift. It is the **gift becoming visible**.

### Element Three: *Wa Kunnā Bihi ʿĀlimīn* — And We Were of Him Knowing

Now the most astonishing phrase in this short verse.

*Wa kunnā bihi ʿālimīn.* "And We were of him knowing."

In Arabic, *kāna* + an active participle is one of the most theologically loaded constructions in the Quran. *Kāna* is past tense — *was, were*. *ʿĀlimīn* is the active participle — *knowing*. The combination means: *had been knowing, was already in the state of knowing*.

Now think about this carefully.

Allah is *al-ʿAlīm* — the Knower. His knowledge is not something He acquires. It does not begin and does not end. He is the Eternally-Knowing. So why would the Quran here say *"We **were** of him knowing"* — as if knowing is something Allah did at a particular time and is reporting back to us?

The grammarians have a beautiful answer. In Arabic, this construction — *kunnā* + a participle — is sometimes used to express not the *time* of an action but its **anteriority to whatever comes next**. *We were already in this state when the next thing began.*

[PAUSE]

Read the verse with that lens:

> *We gave Ibrahim his rushd from before — and We were already, of him, knowing.*

The structure is: gift, then knowledge. But the *kāna* construction reverses the apparent order. The knowing **predates the giving**. By the time we see Ibrahim receive his rushd, Allah's knowledge of who Ibrahim was — what he would do, what he could carry, what gift would fit him — was already complete.

There is something almost unbearable in this if you sit with it.

**Allah did not learn Ibrahim through giving. Allah gave because He already knew.**

Most of us think of being known as a slow process. Someone observes us. Watches our choices. Builds up a picture. The longer they know us, the better they know us. Our intimacy with another human being is constructed through time.

This phrase tells us: with Allah, the order is reversed. He does not come to know you through your choices. **Your choices unfold what He already knows.** The giving is downstream of the knowing.

Classical exegesis gives this abstract "knowing" a concrete cash-value. Both al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar gloss *kunnā bihi ʿālimīn* as "We knew that he was *ahlun li-dhālika*" — worthy, fit, deserving of it. The knowing was not a bare fact registered somewhere in the divine ledger; it was a knowing *of fitness*. Allah did not simply know Ibrahim's future — He knew that Ibrahim was the kind of vessel that could carry what was about to be given.

So when Ibrahim opens his mouth in the next verse and asks his question — that question was never a surprise to Allah. Not because Allah was watching closely. Because Allah was already, of him, knowing. From before what? From before there was a "before."

[PAUSE]

This is what it means to be known by your Creator. Not surveilled. **Already known.** Before any of it.

### Element Four: *Mā Hādhihi t-Tamāthīl* — The Word That Already Wins

Now we cross into verse 52. Ibrahim opens his mouth.

He could have said many things. He could have asked, *"Why do you worship these gods?"* He could have asked, *"What are these idols?"*

What he says is: *"What are these **tamāthīl** to which you are devoted?"*

*Tamāthīl.* This is a chosen word. Listen to what it does.

In Arabic, there are at least three words for the objects of pagan worship: *ṣanam* (a carved idol), *wathan* (an object of worship more generally), and *timthāl* (plural *tamāthīl*) — a *likeness, a representation, an image of something*.

The root of *timthāl* is *m-th-l* — to resemble, to be like. A *timthāl* is a thing made to **look like** something else. The word is morally neutral on its surface. A statue. A sculpture. A representation.

This is the word Ibrahim uses.

Why does this matter?

Because his people would call them *āliha* — gods. *Aṣnām* — idols, certainly, but holy ones. The whole religious world around Ibrahim has language to dignify these stones. They have liturgy for them. They have lineage of devotion to them.

And Ibrahim — with one word — refuses their entire vocabulary.

He does not say, *"What are these gods?"* He does not honor them with a sacred name. He calls them what they actually are: **likenesses**. Resemblances. Things made to look like something. He has stripped them of their pretensions before the argument has even begun.

[PAUSE]

There is a principle hiding in this word choice that runs through all of prophetic speech: **the prophet does not adopt the language his society uses to honor its idols.** He calls things by their true names. He refuses the spell of the inherited vocabulary.

Notice — Ibrahim does not insult them. He does not call them *junk* or *trash*. He does not editorialize. He simply describes. *Tamāthīl* is a precise, almost clinical word. He says: *these are likenesses. That is what they are.*

And in saying so, he has already won the question before they have answered it. Because if they admit the word *tamāthīl*, they have admitted the stones are not gods. And if they reject the word, they have to explain why a likeness should be called a god.

This is what classical balāghah scholars call *iltifāt al-tasmiyah* — the redirection through naming. **Ibrahim's first weapon is not argument. It is vocabulary.**

### Element Five: *Antum Lahā ʿĀkifūn* — The Verb of Real Worship

The last phrase in verse 52 is the one that should make us shiver.

*Antum lahā ʿākifūn.* "You are, to them, devoted."

The word is *ʿākifūn*, from the root *ʿ-k-f*.

This root will be familiar to anyone who knows Ramadan. It is the root of *iʿtikāf* — the spiritual retreat in the masjid in the last ten nights of Ramadan, when a person *clings* to the place of prayer and refuses to leave.

*ʿAkafa* means to **stay clinging to something, to seclude oneself with it, to refuse to be separated from it**. It is not the verb of casual worship. It is the verb of *attachment that becomes inseparability*.

Stay with this.

Ibrahim is not accusing his people of *worshipping* the idols. The word for worship is *ʿibādah*, and there are other roots for it. He uses a more precise, more wounding word. He says: *you are doing iʿtikāf with these likenesses*. **You are clinging to them in the way one is supposed to cling only to Allah.**

This is devastating, because the form of religious devotion they think is righteous — the *staying near*, the *not leaving*, the *making a home of devotion* — is precisely what is being described, but with the wrong object.

[PAUSE]

The word *ʿākifūn* tells us that Ibrahim's critique is not "you are doing too little." It is: **you are doing the right thing toward the wrong reality.** The shape of your religious life is correct. The depth of your devotion is real. But the object you have placed at its center is a likeness.

There is a sociological truth here. People rarely worship idols out of laziness. Idol-worshippers are often deeply devoted, deeply ritualistic, deeply *ʿākifūn*. The problem is never the absence of devotion. It is the misdirection of it.

Now look at the word order. *Antum lahā ʿākifūn.* "**You** — to **them** — devoted."

In Arabic, the natural order would be the verb first: *taʿkufūna lahā*. But here the pronoun *antum* (you) is fronted, and the prepositional phrase *lahā* (to them) is fronted before the participle. Both subject and object are placed in the spotlight before the verb of devotion is named.

The effect: Ibrahim is forcing his people to look at themselves in the mirror. *YOU. To THESE. Are devoted.* He is making them confront the picture before he describes the picture.

[PAUSE]

A bridge before we move on:

We have seen, through the language, that Allah's gift to Ibrahim was personal — *his* rushd. That the gift preceded the action. That the knowing preceded the gift. That when Ibrahim spoke, his first move was to refuse the inherited vocabulary, to name the stones what they actually were, and to expose his people not as religiously deficient but as religiously misdirected.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Knowing That Precedes the Giving

Sit for a moment with this question.

What does it feel like to think of yourself as someone Allah is *coming to know* through your choices?

Most of us, when we examine our spiritual life, secretly imagine that. We feel like Allah is watching us — sometimes pleased, sometimes disappointed — and that His estimation of us is being built, brick by brick, through our prayers, our sins, our struggles, our recoveries. We feel like we are constructing our identity in His sight, and He is keeping the running tally.

This is, of course, a deep error. But it is an error that lives at the level of feeling, not belief.

Now hear the verse again:

*"And We had given Ibrahim his rushd from before — and We were already, of him, knowing."*

[PAUSE]

There was no point at which Allah did not already know who Ibrahim was. Not the Ibrahim of the moment. **The whole Ibrahim.** Every word he would say. Every test he would pass. The flames he would walk into. The son he would lay down. The house he would build with his own hands in a valley of no plants.

All of it. Already known. From before.

When the gift of rushd came, it did not come because Allah had assessed the data of Ibrahim's potential and decided to invest. It came because Allah had always known what Ibrahim was, and the gift was the unfolding of that knowledge into time.

Now — here is the move that makes this verse pastoral, not just theological.

**The same is true of you.**

You are not a project Allah is evaluating. You are not an ongoing assessment. The shape of your soul, the precise weight of your tests, the gifts that fit *you* and no one else — they are already, in His knowledge, complete. *Wa kāna bika ʿālimā* — and He is, of you, knowing. Not learning. Knowing.

This changes how you stand before Him.

A man who thinks he is being assessed lives in performance. He is constantly trying to demonstrate, to display, to produce the data points that will earn the gift. He is exhausted, because every moment is an audition.

A man who knows he is *already known* lives in a different posture. He is not auditioning. He is **unfolding**. The gifts that come to him are not the results of his performance — they are the unfolding of what was always there in Allah's knowledge of him.

This does not mean effort is irrelevant. Ibrahim's life of effort was world-historical. But the effort was not the *cause* of the gift. The effort was the *shape* of the gift becoming visible.

[PAUSE]

The practical change: when you pray tonight, do not pray as if you are *informing* Allah about yourself. Do not pray as if you are *constructing* your image before Him. Pray as one who is already, fully, completely known. *And We were, of him, knowing.* You are Ibrahim's spiritual descendant. The same Allah who knew Ibrahim from before knows you from before. The gift fitted to you exists in His knowledge with your name on it. Your job is not to earn it. Your job is to be the kind of person through whom it can unfold.

Stop auditioning for an Audience that has already cast you.

### Theme Two: The Tamāthīl of Our Age

Now the second theme. The one that points its finger at us.

Ibrahim's people did not think they were doing anything strange. They were not out hunting for things to worship. They were doing what their fathers had done. They were keeping the religious life of their community. They were devoted in a way they had been taught was righteous.

The problem was not that they were irreligious. The problem was that the object of their devotion was a *timthāl*. A likeness. A representation of something that was not what it claimed to be.

Now ask yourself: what are the *tamāthīl* of our time?

I do not mean stones. We have moved past the era of carved idols. But the heart's mechanism for *iʿtikāf* — the mechanism for clinging to something so totally that we cannot bear to be parted from it — has not gone anywhere. It is still operating in every human chest. The question is not *whether* you are *ʿākif* with something. The question is *what*.

Sit with this.

What is the thing you cannot stop looking at? What is the thing whose absence makes your day feel hollow? What is the thing whose loss would feel like the loss of yourself? What is the thing your hand reaches for first in the morning and last at night?

[PAUSE]

For some, it is a screen. The phone. The endless feed of *tamāthīl* — likenesses of other people's lives, likenesses of the self we wish we had, likenesses of moments we did not actually live but consumed. We are *ʿākifūn* with these likenesses in a way Ibrahim's people would have recognized immediately.

For some, it is an image of the self. A career identity. A reputation. A version of who we are in others' eyes that we cannot bear to see disturbed. That image is also a *timthāl* — a likeness made to resemble a self. We bow to it. We protect it. We make decisions that betray our actual life in order to preserve the *image* of our life.

For some, it is a person we have made into more than a person — a child, a spouse, a parent — to whom we have given the kind of total, anchoring devotion that should have been reserved for the Real.

For some, it is wealth. For some, it is approval. For some, it is being right. For some, it is not being seen as wrong.

Each of these is its own *timthāl*. A likeness of something that promises to anchor us, to be the thing worth clinging to, to be the center of *iʿtikāf*.

And here is what Ibrahim's word teaches us. **He does not say his people had no devotion.** They had immense devotion. They had inherited it, deepened it, ritualized it. The problem was that the object at the center was a likeness.

[PAUSE]

So the diagnostic question is not: *am I devoted enough?* Most of us are devoted to something. The diagnostic question is the one Ibrahim asked:

> *Mā hādhihi t-tamāthīlu llatī antum lahā ʿākifūn?*
> *What are these likenesses to which you are devoted?*

Notice he forces them to **name** the object of devotion. Most of our idolatries survive only because we never name them. The *timthāl* we cling to has no name in our internal vocabulary. We call it *my work, my life, my family, my reputation, my well-being*. Honorable names. Coverings.

Ibrahim's question forces a renaming. *What is this you are clinging to?*

Try this tonight. Pick one thing in your life that has the shape of *iʿtikāf* — something you cling to, something whose loss would feel like the loss of yourself — and ask Ibrahim's question of it. *What is this likeness to which I am devoted?* Do not answer with the noble name. Answer with the truthful name.

Often, the *timthāl*, once named, loses half its power.

[PAUSE]

The practical change: This week, name one *timthāl* in your life. Do not necessarily destroy it — Ibrahim destroyed his only after years of confronting the language and the logic. The first work is the naming. The first work is refusing to call your *timthāl* a god.

The second work — and this is the harder one — is to ask: what is the *real* thing this likeness is pretending to be? Behind every *timthāl* is a longing that was meant for something Real. The longing is not wrong. The object is wrong. **Find the longing. Let it travel home.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two ayahs are now talking to each other.

Verse 51 tells us: *Allah gave Ibrahim a rushd that fit him alone. He was, of Ibrahim, knowing — from before.*

Verse 52 tells us: *Ibrahim used that rushd to name the likenesses for what they were and to expose his people's devotion as misdirected.*

The architectural connection: **the first thing that a personally-fitted divine rushd produces is the courage to name the inherited likenesses.** Not the courage to break them — that comes later in the surah. The first courage is the courage of vocabulary. The first courage is to refuse the language by which a society dignifies its idols.

This is what *rushd* looks like in its earliest form. Not action. **Truthful seeing**. Not destruction. **Accurate naming**.

And the verse asks us to notice: this rushd was *his*. It fit Ibrahim. It does not mean every person of rushd does what Ibrahim did. Your rushd has your name on it, too. But the principle holds: the rushd that fits you will, when it arrives, give you the courage to call your *tamāthīl* by their true names.

### Questions to Carry

1. If Allah's knowing of you precedes His giving, what does it change about how you ask Him for what you need?
2. What is the *timthāl* in your life right now — the likeness to which you are *ʿākif*? Can you name it without using the noble word that disguises it?
3. Ibrahim's first weapon was vocabulary. Where in your life have you adopted the language by which your culture dignifies things that should not be dignified?
4. The verse says *rushdahu* — *his* rushd. What might Allah have inscribed for you that you have been waiting to perform into existence, when in fact it has already been given?
5. The form of Ibrahim's people's devotion was correct — the *iʿtikāf* was real. Where in your life is the form of your devotion sound but the object of it false?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah knew Ibrahim before He gave to him, and the first thing the gift produced was the courage to name the likenesses for what they were.**

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being assessed. You are being known. From before.

Live, then, not as one auditioning for the gift, but as one through whom an already-given gift is unfolding into time. And let the first work of that gift be the work Ibrahim did with his very first sentence — the work of refusing to call a likeness by the name of God.

Find the *tamāthīl* you have been clinging to. Name them. Let the longing they have been carrying travel home to the Real.

### Du'a

*O Allah — You who were knowing of Ibrahim before You gave to him, You who know us from before — give us the rushd that has our names on it. Show us the likenesses we have been calling by Your names, and grant us the courage of Ibrahim's first sentence: to call them what they are. Return our iʿtikāf to its true place — to You, and to You alone.*

*Āmīn.*
