# What They Were Waiting For

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 34

> [ayah:21:34] وَمَا جَعَلْنَا لِبَشَرٍ مِّن قَبْلِكَ ٱلْخُلْدَ ۖ أَفَإِي۟ن مِّتَّ فَهُمُ ٱلْخَـٰلِدُونَ
>
> *Wa mā ja'alnā li-basharin min qablika-l-khuld. A-fa-in mitta fa-humu-l-khālidūn.*
>
> **"And We have not granted to any human being before you everlasting life; so if you die, would they be the everlasting ones?"**

---

## Introduction: The Strategy of Outwaiting

[PAUSE]

Imagine a strategy so cynical, so quietly confident, that it doesn't even need to argue.

You don't have to refute the message. You don't have to debate the messenger. You don't have to win on the merits. You just have to **outlast him**.

He's a man. Men die. His voice will go quiet. His followers will scatter. His words will fade into the noise of history. You don't have to do anything except wait.

This was, in fact, one of the open strategies of the Quraysh against the Prophet ﷺ. Not the only strategy — there was open mockery, there was economic boycott, there was assassination plotted in Dār al-Nadwa. Al-Jalālayn even preserves the occasion directly: this ayah descended, the commentary notes, after the disbelievers said outright that Muhammad ﷺ would die — not a private hope whispered among themselves, but a claim spoken aloud, as though his mortality were already an argument won. But underneath all of those, there was something quieter: a confidence that **time was on their side**. Eventually he would die. And when he died, this strange new thing he was saying about One God and accountability and resurrection — all of it would die with him. They could go back to the way things were.

[PAUSE]

The Quran picks up this strategy and holds it up to the light. And in a single sentence — **eleven words in Arabic** — it doesn't just refute it. It exposes the absurdity at the heart of it. It shows the mockers something about themselves they hadn't noticed.

Here's the hook I want you to carry with me: this ayah uses **the same Arabic root twice**. Once to describe what Allah did NOT give to any prophet. Once to describe what the mockers were implicitly claiming for themselves. And the gap between those two uses is where the entire psychology of the waiting strategy collapses.

Let me show you.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *li-basharin min qablika* — "for any human-being, from before you"

Before we go to the ayah, let me teach you something about Arabic that English flattens.

In English, "human" is a single word. In Arabic, there are at least three different words that all translate as "human" or "person" — and each one **highlights a different dimension** of what it means to be human.

There's *insān* (إنسان) — and here the classical lexicographers didn't all agree on its root. Some trace it to *nasiya* (forgetfulness); others, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī among them, favor *uns* (sociality, intimacy). Either derivation points the same direction: the human as a relational, forgetful, meaning-making creature.

There's *adam* (آدم) — connected to *adīm al-arḍ*, the surface or skin of the earth, recalling the dust we were made from. The human as creature-of-clay.

And there's a third word: *bashar* (بشر) — connected to *basharah*, the outer skin, the visible flesh. **The human as a body. The human as biological. The human as something that ages, bleeds, hungers, and dies.**

[PAUSE]

When the Quran wants to highlight the human as a meaning-making being, it tends to use *insān*. When it wants to highlight the human as a biological organism — when it wants to remind you that prophets are made of the same flesh as everyone else — it tends to use *bashar*. This is a tendency in the usage, not an exceptionless law — both words shift with context — but it's a real enough pattern that the choice here rewards a pause.

Now look at the word Allah chose here.

**لِبَشَرٍ** — *li-basharin*. "For any human-as-flesh."

Not *li-insānin*. Not *li-adamiyyin*. **Li-basharin.** The very word that emphasizes biological mortality. The word that says: this is a body. This is something that decays.

And notice — it's **indefinite**. There's no *al-* on it. The grammar isn't "we did not grant *the* human" — it's "we did not grant *any* human at all." Not even one.

Some classical commentators press this word further than you'd expect. Al-Muyassar notes that this very ayah is cited as evidence that even al-Khiḍr — the mysterious righteous servant of Surah al-Kahf, around whom popular legend has grown claiming he still walks the earth — has died, because he too is *bashar*. If the word closes around a figure people are tempted to exempt from it, then the sweep of *li-basharin* really does mean no one. Not the greatest prophets. Not even the most legendary of the righteous.

Then comes **مِّن قَبْلِكَ** — "from before you."

Stay with me. This phrase is doing something specific. It's not saying "we didn't grant immortality to ordinary people; you, of course, are special." It's saying the opposite. Allah is taking the entire span of sacred history — from Adam, through Nuh, through Ibrahim, through Musa, through Isa — every prophet, every messenger, every righteous servant — **and including them all in this denial**.

Nuh lived nine hundred and fifty years preaching. He died.
Ibrahim was thrown into fire and emerged alive. He died.
Musa parted the sea and spoke to Allah on the mountain. He died.
Every one of them. Every single one.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The mockers might have imagined that there was something special about their own moment, something unprecedented. *This* time, the messenger will fade and the truth will fade with him. But the construction *li-basharin min qablika* says: **this story has been told before.**

There has never been a prophet whose physical body was kept alive forever to guarantee his message. Not one. The messenger's mortality is not a flaw in the system. It is part of the system. Every messenger Allah ever sent walked toward his own grave, and the message did not depend on the breath in his lungs.

So when you hear *li-basharin min qablika*, hear it as Allah saying: **"This pattern is not new. The waiting strategy has been tried before. It has never worked once."**

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Element Two: *al-khuld* and *al-khālidūn* — The Same Root Used Twice

Now we come to what I promised at the beginning. The same Arabic root used twice in this ayah. Once for what Allah denied to prophets. Once for what the mockers were implicitly claiming.

The root is **خ ل د** — *kh-l-d*. It carries the meaning of permanence, abiding, remaining without end. It's the root used for Paradise and Hellfire as eternal abodes — *khālidīna fīhā*. It is the lexicon of **what does not pass away**.

In this ayah, this root appears twice in two different forms.

**First form: ٱلْخُلْدَ — *al-khuld*.**

This is the verbal noun (*maṣdar*). The abstract noun. Not "an eternal one" but **eternity itself, the very quality of permanence**. And it has *al-* on it — definite. Not "some kind of eternity" but *the* eternity, the absolute thing. This is the cosmic-grade, fully-loaded form of the word.

That's what Allah says He did not grant. Not just "long life." Not "extra years." **The absolute quality of permanence itself.** None of the prophets got it. Not even close.

[PAUSE]

**Second form: ٱلْخَـٰلِدُونَ — *al-khālidūn*.**

This is the active participle, plural, masculine. "The ones-who-abide-eternally." Also definite — **the** eternal ones. Not "some who happen to live a bit longer" but the very category of beings whose nature is permanence.

Notice what Allah has done here. He has used the *same root* — the root for absolute permanence — to describe the very thing He withheld from every prophet who ever walked, **and** the very title the mockers were implicitly claiming for themselves.

Listen to it again with this in mind:

*"We did not grant to any human-as-flesh, from before you, **the eternity** (al-khuld). So if you die, would they be **the eternal-ones** (al-khālidūn)?"*

[PAUSE]

The argument is devastating. It says: **you are demanding from a single mortal what was withheld from every mortal who ever lived. And in the very moment you watch his mortality with satisfaction, you are quietly assuming yours is different.**

This is the architecture of the line. Allah doesn't say: "Even if he dies, the message is preserved." That would be one valid argument. He doesn't say: "Even if he dies, others will carry it." That would be another. He says something stranger and sharper: **"Even if he dies — what makes you think YOU won't?"**

The ayah doesn't defend the messenger from death. **It exposes the mockers' assumption that they themselves have escaped it.**

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Element Three: *a-fa-in mitta fa-humu-l-khālidūn* — The Architecture of the Question

Let me teach you something about Arabic interrogative structures.

In Arabic, there's a particular kind of rhetorical question built with the prefix **أَ** (the *hamzat al-istifhām*) — and when this prefix attaches to a conditional, what you get is one of the most pointed rhetorical structures in the language.

Look at it carefully:

**أَ** — the interrogative hamza. "Is it the case that..."
**فَ** — "so / then" — drawing a logical consequence from what was just said.
**إِن** — "if" — opening a hypothetical.
**مِّتَّ** — "you died" — the verb addressed in the second person, masculine singular, perfect tense.

Then the answer-clause:
**فَهُمُ** — "then they..."
**ٱلْخَـٰلِدُونَ** — "the eternal ones."

Read this slowly. The grammar is doing surgery. **"So... if you died... THEY would be the eternal ones?"**

Notice the pronoun shift. *Mitta* — "you (singular) died" — is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ. But *humu* — "they" — points at the mockers. In a single line, the camera pivots: it starts on the messenger's hypothetical mortality, then turns to face the mockers and ask, **"And what about yours?"**

This kind of pivot has a name in classical Arabic rhetoric: ***iltifāt***. The "turning of the face." It's when the grammatical address shifts mid-line in order to deliver a kind of psychological surgery the listener wasn't expecting.

[PAUSE]

Here is the move the ayah makes. The mockers are looking at the Prophet ﷺ. They are watching him. They are studying his face for signs of age. They are running a calculation: *how long until he's gone?*

And the ayah, mid-sentence, **turns the question around and asks them**: while you've been so busy calculating his lifespan, when did you last calculate your own?

[PAUSE]

There's something else worth noticing in the structure. The conditional *in mitta* — "if you died" — uses *in* (إِن), not *idhā* (إِذَا). In Arabic, *idhā* tends to introduce something expected, even certain — "when X happens." *In* tends to introduce something hypothetical, sometimes even unlikely. Allah does not say *idhā mitta* — "**when** you die." He says *in mitta* — "**if** you die." Almost as if to put the messenger's mortality at one degree of distance from the question, while leaving the mockers' implied claim — *we will be the eternal ones* — even more naked and absurd.

The death of the Prophet ﷺ is held in the realm of the hypothetical. The mockers' immortality is held nowhere — because no one would ever say it out loud. But the structure of their waiting strategy assumes it.

This is what the ayah does: **it forces the unspoken assumption to the surface and lets the listener hear how ridiculous it sounds when you finally say it.**

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. The word *bashar* with its emphasis on the human-as-flesh, indefinite and universal, taking in every prophet who ever walked. The double use of the root *kh-l-d* — once for what Allah denied even the most beloved of His servants, once for the impossible title the mockers were implicitly claiming. And the rhetorical structure *a-fa-in mitta fa-humu-l-khālidūn* — the iltifāt that turns the camera from the Prophet's hypothetical death to the mockers' impossible immortality.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about a particular shape the human heart takes when it doesn't want to engage with truth.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Psychology of Outwaiting

I want you to think about something almost everyone has done at some point in their life.

There's a difficult conversation you don't want to have. There's a person whose presence in your life you find threatening — maybe to your worldview, maybe to your habits, maybe to your standing. There's a movement you don't believe in but don't want to engage with. There's a truth-teller in your family or community whose questions make you uncomfortable.

And so you adopt a strategy. Not consciously. Not as a stated plan. But as a posture, a default. **You decide to outlast them.**

You don't refute them. You don't engage their arguments. You don't change. You just wait. Wait for them to lose interest. Wait for them to move away. Wait for them to grow old. Wait for them to die. Wait for the issue to fade. Wait for someone else to deal with it.

[PAUSE]

This is a particular psychology. And the Quran sees it. Not as one isolated sin among many — but as a **shape** that the resistant heart takes when it cannot win on the merits.

The mockers of Mecca had this shape. They weren't saying "we have a better theology than tawhid." They weren't saying "we have a better account of resurrection." They were saying — quietly, mostly to themselves — *"his time is limited; ours is open."*

And the Quran's response is not to defend the messenger. It's to **disturb the comfort of the wait**.

Because here is what the waiting strategy depends on. It depends on the assumption that **your time is structurally different from theirs**. That the question of mortality is, for the moment, only on their side of the table. That you have leisure to outlast.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. When you spend years waiting for someone else to fade, **you are spending your own life on the wait**. Every day you spend hoping someone else will run out of time, you are running out of time. Every breath you spend monitoring their mortality is a breath off your own ledger.

The waiting strategy is not just morally cheap. It is **mathematically incoherent**. You cannot outlast another mortal. You can only die later — or earlier — than they do. And neither outcome has any bearing on whether what they were saying was true.

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah does to the mockers. It doesn't argue with their position. It looks at the *structure* of their position and asks: **on what basis are you the one with time?** And in the asking, the entire scaffolding falls.

I want you to take this honestly into your own life. Where are you outwaiting someone right now? A parent whose questions make you uncomfortable? A spouse whose concern about your spiritual state you've decided to ride out? A community member whose challenges you're hoping will fade? An interior voice — your own conscience — that you're waiting to grow quiet?

Apply the ayah's question to yourself: **a-fa-in māta fa-anta-l-khālid?** "If they fade — will *you* be the one who lasts forever?"

The honest answer is no. Which means the wait is not a strategy. **The wait is a delay. And the delay is itself part of your finite life.**

The practical application: stop outwaiting. Engage. Whatever the truth on the table is — whether you accept it or reject it — engage it on the merits, while you still have the breath to engage anything.

[MAJOR PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Independence of the Message from the Messenger

There's a second thing this ayah is doing, and it's tender, and it's for the heart of the believer.

The Prophet ﷺ heard this ayah. And one of the things it was doing for him — quietly, beneath the rebuke of the mockers — was preparing him for his own mortality. **Reminding him that the truth he carried did not depend on the breath in his chest.**

This is a hard thing to hear if you are a messenger. Every prophet who ever lived had to come to terms with the fact that the message — which felt like it was sustained only by their voice, their presence, their daily struggle — was not, in fact, sustained by them. It was sustained by Allah. They were carriers. They were not pillars.

[PAUSE]

There's a hadith — when the Prophet ﷺ died, Abu Bakr رضي الله عنه stood before the trembling community and recited the ayah from Surah Aal-Imran: ***Wa mā Muḥammadun illā rasūl, qad khalat min qablihi-r-rusul* — "Muhammad is no more than a messenger; messengers have passed away before him."** He continued, in his own words: *"Whoever worshipped Muhammad — Muhammad has died. Whoever worshipped Allah — Allah is alive and does not die."*

That moment in Abu Bakr's voice was the *fruit* of ayahs like this one in Surah Al-Anbiya. The community had been prepared. The messenger had been prepared. Mortality, when it came, did not destroy the message. **Because Allah had been saying for years: the message is not stored in a body. The body is a vehicle. The message is held by Me.**

[PAUSE]

There is a paradox here that I want you to sit with.

The Prophet ﷺ was the most beloved human to Allah. The seal of all prophets. The one whose name is paired with Allah's in every adhān. The one whose intercession on the Day of Judgment is uniquely promised.

And **even he** was not granted *al-khuld*. Even he walked toward his own grave. Even he had to die, like every prophet before him, like every human after him.

What does this tell us? It tells us that **physical permanence is not how Allah preserves what is precious to Him**. Allah does not preserve truth by preserving bodies. He preserves truth by preserving *meaning*. He preserves the Quran by preserving its hearts and its memorizers and its meanings across centuries — not by keeping any single body alive forever.

[PAUSE]

This has a personal application that goes far beyond Mecca.

You will lose people. Teachers. Parents. Scholars whose words shaped your soul. Friends who carried part of your faith for you. Children who came into your life and changed it and then, sometimes, were taken before you. And in the rawness of those losses, a small voice will whisper: *"Without them, the meaning will not survive."*

This ayah is the answer to that voice. **The meaning does not depend on the body that delivered it.** Allah did not preserve even His most beloved Prophet's body forever. He preserved what mattered: the Quran, the Sunnah, the umma, the memory, the trajectory of guidance through history.

The same Allah who preserved meaning across the Prophet's ﷺ death will preserve meaning across yours — across your teacher's, your parent's, your child's. **You can trust the meaning to outlive its vessels, because the One who holds the meaning never dies.**

The practical application: do not tie your faith to a single human. Love them, learn from them, sit at their feet — but know that the One who taught them is the One who will continue to teach you when they are gone. The Quran does not run out when its reciter dies. Allah does not run out when His servant dies. **The well is not the bucket.**

[MAJOR PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### Architecture

Look at what the eleven words have done.

They have used the word *bashar* to insist on the universal flesh-bound mortality of every messenger.
They have used *min qablika* to fold the entire history of prophecy into the denial.
They have used *al-khuld* to name what Allah withheld from even His most beloved servants.
They have used *al-khālidūn* to ironically apply that same root to the impossible self-image of the mockers.
They have used the conditional *in mitta* to keep the messenger's death at one degree of distance.
They have used the iltifāt to pivot the question from his mortality to theirs.

And in doing all of this in a single line, the ayah has accomplished two things at once: **it has dismantled the mockers' waiting strategy, and it has prepared the believing heart for the mortality of even the most beloved.**

The same eleven words that told the mockers "you are not different from him" told the believers "your faith does not depend on him." One ayah. Two surgeries. Two hearts left transformed.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: the word for the universal flesh (*bashar*), the word for absolute permanence (*al-khuld*), and the word for the mockers' implicit claim (*al-khālidūn*) sit in a single line so that the contrast can do its work without commentary.

From the thematic side: the psychology of outwaiting collapses when you realize you cannot outlast mortality, and the grief of losing a beloved teacher collapses when you realize the truth was never stored in his body in the first place.

Both arrive at the same place: **only Allah is *al-Ḥayy*, the Living. Only He is *al-Bāqī*, the Remaining. Everything else is a vehicle.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I outwaiting someone — a parent, a teacher, a spouse, a community member — instead of engaging what they are saying on the merits?

2. What truth am I treating as if it depends on a single human voice — and how would my engagement with it change if I understood that the One who sent it does not die?

3. When I lose someone whose presence carried part of my faith, what would it look like to grieve them fully while still trusting that the meaning they delivered is held by Allah, not by them?

4. Am I quietly assuming I have more time than the people whose mortality I'm watching? On what basis?

5. What would I do today if I took seriously that *al-khuld* was withheld from every prophet — including the one I love most — and that my own breath is borrowed from the same well?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Mortality is the universal human condition — and waiting for someone to die in the hope that truth dies with them is a self-deception about your own time, because the One who preserves truth does not need a body to do it.**

### The Closing Invitation

Stop outwaiting. Engage what's in front of you while there's breath to engage it.
Stop tying truth to bodies. The well is not the bucket.
Stop calculating someone else's lifespan as if yours is structurally different. It isn't.

And when you next read of the Prophet ﷺ — peace be upon him — let your love for him include this: **he, too, was *bashar*. He, too, walked toward a grave. And the One who sent him is still here, still teaching, still preserving every meaning that ever mattered.**

That is the ground beneath your feet. That is *al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm*.

### A Brief Du'a

*Yā Ḥayy, yā Qayyūm — by Your Life that does not end, anchor my heart in You and not in any vessel that will pass.*

*Allāhumma, free me from the strategy of outwaiting, and teach me to engage truth while I still have breath. Let me love Your Messenger ﷺ enough to grieve his absence — and trust You enough to know that what he carried, You still hold.*

*And when my own time comes, let me not be among those who imagined themselves al-khālidūn — but among those who knew, every day, that only You remain.*

**Āmīn.**
