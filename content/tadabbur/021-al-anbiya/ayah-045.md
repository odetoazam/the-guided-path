# The Limits of the Caller — Warning the Deaf

> [ayah:21:45] قُلْ إِنَّمَآ أُنذِرُكُم بِٱلْوَحْىِ ۚ وَلَا يَسْمَعُ ٱلصُّمُّ ٱلدُّعَآءَ إِذَا مَا يُنذَرُونَ

> *Qul innamā undhirukum bi'l-waḥy. Wa lā yasmaʿu aṣ-ṣummu ad-duʿāʾa idhā mā yundharūn.*

> **Say: "I only warn you by the revelation." But the deaf do not hear the call when they are warned.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah is born into

Imagine you are watching a man stand on a hill and call out something urgent. He has come a long way. He is shouting because he loves the people in the valley below — there is a flood coming, or a fire, or something worse. You watch his face. You watch the way his voice strains. And then you watch the people in the valley.

They are not running.

They are not even looking up.

Some of them are mocking him. Some are throwing stones. Most are simply going about their day, as if no voice exists at all.

[PAUSE]

This is what the Prophet ﷺ has been doing in Mecca for years by the time this ayah is revealed. The surah we are in — Al-Anbiya, "The Prophets" — has just walked us through prophet after prophet who lived this exact experience. Nuh. Ibrahim. Musa. Each one carried the same warning. Each one was met with the same resistance. The ayahs just before this one have been listing the excuses: *"This is just confused dreams" — "He has forged it" — "He is a poet."* The disbelievers are not running out of objections; they are inventing new ones the way a mind invents reasons to keep doing what it already wants to do.

And the Prophet ﷺ — peace and blessings be upon him — is feeling something. Not despair. He is too anchored for despair. But there is a weight. A weight any sincere caller knows. *Why won't they hear me? Have I failed them? Am I not speaking clearly enough? Should I try harder, louder, differently?*

Into that weight, Allah sends this ayah. Thirteen words. A command — *Say.* And then a sentence that, if you stay with it long enough, lifts a burden the Prophet ﷺ was never meant to carry. And lifts the same burden from every person, in every century, who has ever tried to call another human being toward the truth and been met with stone.

### The hook

There is something stunning happening in this ayah, and it lives in a single contradiction. Allah tells the Prophet ﷺ to address the disbelievers directly — *"I only warn YOU"* — and then, in the very same breath, refers to them in the third person as *"the deaf."* He is told to speak to people who, the next clause confirms, will not hear him.

[PAUSE]

Why would Allah command a speech to be delivered to people whose inability to receive it is announced inside the speech itself?

Stay with that question. The answer rearranges what the Prophet ﷺ — and what we — think calling to the truth actually *is*.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word *innamā* — and what it does to the job description

Before we open the Arabic, let me show you what this small word is doing in any language.

Imagine a doctor saying: *"I only diagnose. I do not heal."* Notice what that sentence does. It is not a confession of weakness. It is a clarification of role. The doctor is not failing because patients sometimes don't follow the prescription. The doctor's job is the diagnosis. The healing requires something else — the patient's compliance, the body's response, a hundred factors outside the doctor's hands. The word *only* is a fence. It marks where the doctor's responsibility ends and where someone else's begins.

In Arabic, that fence has a name. It is called a **particle of restriction** — *adāt al-ḥaṣr* — and the most common one is *innamā* (إِنَّمَا). It limits a verb to its object and excludes everything else. *"I only X"* — meaning, *not anything else.*

Now look at the ayah. Allah does not say *undhirukum bi'l-waḥy* — "I warn you by the revelation." That would be a true sentence. He says *innamā undhirukum bi'l-waḥy* — "I **only** warn you by the revelation." The fence has been placed.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The fence is placed around the **Prophet's** role — not around the disbelievers' fate. The restriction is on the warner, not on the warned. Allah is not telling the Prophet ﷺ, *"All you can do is warn — sorry."* He is telling him: *"This is the entire job. Warning by revelation. That is what you were sent for. Nothing more is being asked of you. Nothing more is even possible from you."*

Stay with this. This is a relief disguised as a sentence. Every caller to truth — every parent trying to guide a child, every teacher facing a closed student, every friend telling a hard truth to someone who will not receive it — every one of them is being told here: *your job is delivery. The receiving is not in your hands. It was never in your hands. Stop trying to carry what was never given to you.*

And here is what is remarkable: the *innamā* is followed by *bi'l-waḥy* — "by the revelation." This is the **instrumental** *bā'* in Arabic — the *bā'* that names the tool. *I warn you with this one tool, and only this tool.* Not with rhetoric. Not with force. Not with cleverness. Not with crowd-pleasing or threat-making. The Prophet ﷺ has been given exactly one instrument for this work, and it is the revelation itself.

Think about what that excludes. He is not allowed to invent persuasive arguments outside the wahy. He is not allowed to bribe, to pressure, to manipulate. He is not allowed to soften the message to make it easier to swallow, or to harden it to make it more dramatic. The instrument is fixed. *Bi'l-waḥy.* Whatever the wahy says — that, and nothing else, is what he carries.

Classical exegesis sharpens this fence on both ends. Al-Jalalayn glosses the phrase as *bi'l-waḥy min Allāh, lā min qibali nafsī* — "by revelation from Allah, not of my own accord" — making explicit that the restriction excludes not only force and rhetoric but the Prophet's ﷺ own initiative: even his own reasoning is not the tool he was given. And Qatada, as transmitted by al-Tabari, glossed "the revelation" here as *hādhā al-Qurʾān* — "this Qur'an," specifically — the wahy in this ayah is not some diffuse spiritual pressure or private inspiration; it is this recited, memorized, transmitted text, and nothing beyond it.

[PAUSE]

So *innamā* fences the role. *Bi'l-waḥy* fences the instrument. Together, in three words, the Prophet ﷺ is told: *Your responsibility ends here. Anything beyond this point is between Allah and them.*

### Element Two: *Aṣ-ṣumm* — the deafness that has nothing to do with ears

Now Allah turns to the listeners. *Wa lā yasmaʿu aṣ-ṣummu ad-duʿāʾa idhā mā yundharūn.* "But the deaf do not hear the call when they are warned."

Before we open this clause, let's teach a small grammatical point that will matter.

In Arabic — as in English — putting *the* in front of a noun does something. It particularizes. *"A man came to the door"* tells you nothing about him. *"The man came to the door"* tells you that you should know which man. The *al-* in Arabic is the same. It does the work of pointing.

Now look at the word for "the deaf" — **aṣ-ṣummu** (الصُّمُّ). Allah did not say *ṣummun* — "deaf people, indefinite, in general." He said *aṣ-ṣumm* — *the* deaf. With the article. With the pointing finger.

Who is being pointed at?

Not deaf people in the ear-and-canal sense. The disbelievers in Mecca were not physically deaf — they could hear perfectly well. They had heard the Quran. They were quoting it back, mocking it, repeating it. Their ears worked. So whose deafness is this?

[PAUSE]

The Quran has already taught us, elsewhere, what kind of deafness this is. *"They have hearts with which they do not understand, eyes with which they do not see, and ears with which they do not hear"* (7:179). The ears function. The hearing does not. There is a layer beneath the eardrum that the eardrum cannot reach. That is the layer Allah is naming.

And notice — *aṣ-ṣumm*, with the article, particularizes a known group. The disbelievers in front of the Prophet ﷺ. These specific people. Not abstract deaf-people-everywhere — these ones. Allah is identifying them. Naming the condition. The condition is not that they didn't get the message. The condition is that the receiver inside them is closed.

Now look at the verb. **Lā yasmaʿu** — "do not hear." Present tense, indicative — the tense of habitual, ongoing reality. Not *lam yasmaʿū* — "they did not hear, past tense, one event." The verb is in the form that names a continuing state. This is not a one-time failure. This is what they *do*. This is the shape of their listening, every time the call comes.

And then — the object: **ad-duʿāʾa** (الدُّعَاء), "the call." Again the article, again the pointing. Not "a call." *The* call. The specific one — the one the Prophet ﷺ has been making.

So now stack what we have. *The deaf do not hear the call.* Three definite articles in a row, in Arabic — *aṣ-ṣumm, ad-duʿāʾ.* Each one a finger pointing. *These specific people, in this specific state, are not hearing this specific call.* It is almost surgical.

[PAUSE]

And here is what should arrest us. Allah did not say *cannot* hear. He said *do not* hear. *Lā yasmaʿu*, not *lā yastaṭīʿu an yasmaʿu*. The Quranic Arabic does not deprive them of capacity. It describes their condition. The deafness is real, but it is not a metal lock; it is a turning-away. They have positioned themselves so that the call cannot reach the place inside them where calls land. The ears work. The road from the ear inward is blocked.

This matters because if Allah had said *cannot*, the Prophet ﷺ might have wondered: *then why was I sent at all?* But by saying *do not*, the responsibility is located precisely where it belongs — in their turning-away — and the door, in principle, remains open. Some of these very people, history tells us, will later become Companions. The deafness was not metaphysical. It was chosen. And what is chosen can, by Allah's mercy, be unchosen.

### Element Three: The shift in voice — *I warn you* / *they are warned*

Now I want to show you something subtle. So subtle that on a first read, in any language, you would miss it. But once you see it, you cannot unsee it, and it changes everything.

Look at the two verbs from the same root in this ayah:

- **Undhiru-kum** (أُنذِرُكُم) — "I warn **you**." Active. First person. Direct address. *You* are right here, and *I* am speaking to you.

- **Yundharūn** (يُنذَرُونَ) — "**they are warned.**" Passive. Third person. The same root — n-dh-r — but now they are no longer being addressed; they are being described.

This shift — from talking *to* the audience to talking *about* the audience, mid-ayah, mid-breath — has a name in Arabic rhetoric. It is called **iltifāt**: the *turn*. The Quran does it deliberately, and where it does it, it is doing work.

[PAUSE]

What is the work here?

The first half of the ayah grammatically *includes* the disbelievers. *Qul innamā undhirukum.* "Say: I only warn **you.**" They are the *kum*. They are present, addressed, looked at. The Prophet ﷺ is being instructed to face them and speak.

Then comes the *wāw* — *and* — and the camera turns. *Wa lā yasmaʿu aṣ-ṣummu ad-duʿāʾa idhā mā yundharūn.* Suddenly they are no longer *you.* They are *they.* The deaf. The third person. Discussed, not addressed.

Why?

Because grammatically, you cannot address someone who is not there. And although their bodies are in front of the Prophet ﷺ, the part of them that needs to hear is **not in the room**. The first half of the ayah faces them as if they are present. The second half tells the truth about them — that they are not. They are physically there and spiritually absent. So the grammar honors both realities at once. The mouth speaks to them; the description acknowledges their absence.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more layer. The shift to passive voice — *yundharūn*, "they are warned" — strips them of agency. Active grammar would have been *idhā mā samiʿū l-indhāra* — "when they hear the warning." But Allah does not give them that verb. He gives them only the passive. They are not the doers of any verb in their own deafness clause. They are warned, but they don't do the warning. They are spoken to, but they don't do the listening. The verbs of relationship with the message all happen *to* them, around them, above them — never *through* them.

This is what spiritual absence looks like in grammar. They are objects of revelation, not subjects of it.

### Bridge

So here is what these three linguistic moves have done together.

*Innamā* fenced the Prophet's role. *Bi'l-waḥy* fenced his tool. *Aṣ-ṣumm* named the listeners' condition without depriving them of capacity. And the shift from *you* to *they* mapped, in grammar, the gap between physical presence and spiritual presence.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what it means to call to the truth. About what it means to hear it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The relief of knowing where your job ends

I want to start somewhere modern. Anyone who has loved another person has stood in this place at least once.

You see something. You see clearly. You see that the path the person you love is on is going to hurt them. Maybe it is your child making a decision, your sibling in a relationship, your friend in a habit, your parent in a denial. You speak. You speak carefully. You speak again. You try a different angle. You write a letter. You sit in long silences hoping the silence itself will say what your words couldn't. You wait. You watch. You speak again.

And nothing.

[PAUSE]

What does that do to a person? It does something specific. It produces a particular kind of exhaustion. The exhaustion of feeling like *you* are failing because *they* are not changing. As if their continued ruin is a verdict on the quality of your love. The mind starts running an awful loop: *if I had said it better — if I had said it sooner — if I had been more patient — if I had been less patient — if I had —*

Now hold that experience in one hand. And in the other hand, hold this: the Prophet ﷺ — the most articulate, the most patient, the most loved, the most divinely supported caller in human history — was standing in front of his own people, and *they were not changing.* And he was carrying the same loop. And Allah sent down a sentence to lift it off him.

*Innamā undhirukum bi'l-waḥy.* **You only warn them by revelation.** That is the whole assignment.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most quietly merciful sentences in the Quran. Because what Allah is doing in it is drawing a line — *your responsibility ends here. The hearing is not your job. The receiving is not your job. The transformation is not your job. Your job is the carrying and the offering. After that, the soul on the other side is between Me and Me.*

And notice what this is *not* saying. It is not saying *give up*. It is not saying *they are hopeless, walk away*. The Prophet ﷺ kept calling. The next ayah and the one after continue the call. The point is not to stop. The point is to stop carrying their refusal as if it were yours.

There is a phrase elsewhere in the Quran that matches this exactly: *"You cannot guide whom you love"* (28:56) — said directly to the Prophet ﷺ about his uncle Abu Talib. And another: *"It is not upon you their guidance, but Allah guides whom He wills"* (2:272). Across the Quran, the same architecture is being built and rebuilt: **bayān is yours, hidāyah is Mine.** *Clarity is your work. Guidance is My gift.*

What does this change about how we live?

It means that when you call your child, your friend, your sibling toward something true, and they do not move — you are not, by that fact alone, having failed. The non-movement is not your verdict. It is data about the soil, not about the seed you planted. Your job was the planting. The soil is between Allah and the soul.

It means you can keep calling without despair. Because despair comes from confusing your role with Allah's role. You only despair when you think the outcome was supposed to be in your hands. Once you know it was never in your hands, you can speak the truth, leave it on the air, and walk on without becoming bitter. *Innamā* fences the bitterness out.

And it means — this is the practical part, the thing that should change tomorrow — that when you next find yourself replaying a conversation in which you tried to reach someone and could not, you can stop the replay. You said it. The wahy was offered. The hearing is not yours to manufacture. *Allāhumma ballaght. Allāhumma fashhad.* O Allah, I have delivered. O Allah, witness it. Then — sleep.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Spiritual deafness — and the strange gift of being able to recognize it

Now to the harder half. Because this ayah does not only console the caller. It also describes the listener. And the description is uncomfortable, because we are sometimes the listener.

What is spiritual deafness?

It is not the absence of access to the truth. The disbelievers in this ayah had *full* access. The Prophet ﷺ was right in front of them. They could hear the words. They could repeat the words. They could analyze the words. They could mock the words. And still — *lā yasmaʿu aṣ-ṣummu ad-duʿāʾa.* They did not hear.

Hearing, in the Quran, is not what the ear does. It is what the heart does *after* the ear. It is the moment in which the meaning lands somewhere inside you and rearranges something. Without that moment, no matter how clean the audio, no matter how loud the speaker, no matter how brilliant the rhetoric — the call has not been heard. It has only been processed.

Classical tafsir names this precisely. Al-Muyassar glosses the clause as *lā yasmaʿūna samāʿa tadabbur* — "they do not hear with the hearing of tadabbur" — a hearing that turns the words over, that lets them settle, that reflects. Not sama' in the sense of sound striking an eardrum, but sama' in the very sense this whole practice of tadabbur is named for. And al-Jalalayn ties the deafness to something even more concrete: they are "like the deaf" precisely *li-tarkihim al-ʿamal bi-mā samiʿūhu* — "because of their abandoning action upon what they heard." In this reading, the deafness is not a failure of comprehension at all. It is what happens when hearing does not travel all the way to the hands and the feet. You can understand every word and still be counted among the deaf, if nothing in your life moves because of it.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that distinction. Processed but not heard. This is the modern condition. We process more religious content in a day than our great-grandparents heard in a year. Sermons stream. Reminders scroll. Ayahs sit on our screens, waiting to be liked. And the ear is overflowing. But how often does any of it land?

Notice something the disbelievers in Surah Al-Anbiya were doing in the verses just before this one. Look at their excuses again: *"This is just confused dreams." "He has forged it." "He is a poet."* What were these? They were **strategies for not hearing**. Not refutations. Strategies. Each one was a way to put a label on the message so that the soul no longer had to deal with it. *Once it is "just poetry," I am safe from it. Once it is "just dreams," I do not have to change.*

This is what spiritual deafness looks like operationally. It is not silence. It is **labeling.** It is the inner reflex that meets every challenging truth with an immediate category — *that's just X, that's just Y* — so that the truth never gets close enough to do anything to you.

We do this constantly. *That preacher is just being dramatic. That ayah is just talking about people back then. That reminder is just for the very pious. That fear of accountability is just my anxiety.* Each "just" is a little hand pushing the call back out. By the end of the day, the heart is ringed in *justs* and nothing has gotten through.

[PAUSE]

So here is the strange gift hidden inside this ayah.

If you can read this, and you can feel uncomfortable, and you can suspect that you might sometimes be among the *aṣ-ṣumm* — that itself is the beginning of hearing. Because the truly deaf, in the Quranic sense, do not even register the question. They are too deep in the labeling to notice they are labeling. To suspect yourself of deafness is to be partway out of it. The disbelievers in this ayah had no such suspicion. They were certain it was the Prophet's problem.

So how does deafness lift?

Look back at the grammar. Allah said *lā yasmaʿu* — *do not* hear, not *cannot* hear. The capacity is not destroyed. The road from the ear to the heart is blocked, not removed. And the way it unblocks is the opposite of the labeling reflex. It is the **stopping**. It is hearing an ayah and refusing to immediately know what to do with it. It is sitting with it. Letting it accuse you. Letting it confuse you. Not reaching for the label that turns it into something already-handled.

And — this is important — it is asking. The same Surah Al-Anbiya is full of prophets who *asked*. Ibrahim asked, *"Show me how You give life to the dead"* (2:260, but the Anbiya audience would know this Ibrahim well). Zakariyyā asked. Ayyub asked. The deaf do not ask. The hearing do.

What does this change about how we live?

It means that the discipline of being someone who *hears* — really hears — is the discipline of resisting the inner reflex to package every truth into a category that lets you keep moving. It is the willingness to be momentarily unsettled by an ayah before you decide what to do with it. It is the willingness to not know yet. The deaf always know already. The hearing are the ones who can be surprised.

And practically: the next time an ayah passes through your day — in a recitation, in a reminder, in a friend's text — try, for thirty seconds, to not label it. Not "encouraging," not "challenging," not "for someone else," not "I'll come back to it." Just let it be in the air, and let it ask you something. That thirty seconds is what *yasmaʿu* — *to hear* — actually means.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Look at this ayah now from above. It is built in two halves around a central *wāw*:

- **Half one:** *Qul innamā undhirukum bi'l-waḥy* — about the **caller**. Active voice. Second-person address. The fence is placed. The role is defined. The instrument is named.

- **Half two:** *Wa lā yasmaʿu aṣ-ṣummu ad-duʿāʾa idhā mā yundharūn* — about the **called**. Passive voice. Third-person description. The condition is named. The agency is removed.

The ayah has a wall through its middle, and the wall is exactly where the Prophet's responsibility ends and the listener's condition begins. The grammar itself draws the line. *Bayān is yours; hidāyah is Mine.* The structure is not decoration. The structure *is* the teaching.

### Bringing it together

What did the linguistic journey show? That *innamā* fences the role, that *bi'l-waḥy* fences the tool, and that the shift from active to passive — from *I warn you* to *they are warned* — maps the gap between physical presence and spiritual presence.

What did the themes show? That the caller's burden lifts when the line between bayān and hidāyah is drawn. And that the listener's deafness is not a sealed fate but a habit of labeling that the very recognition of it begins to undo.

These are the same insight from two directions. From the caller's side: *your job is delivery, not transformation.* From the listener's side: *the receiving is your work, not the messenger's.* The ayah hands each side back its own piece of the responsibility, and refuses to let either side carry the other's.

### Questions to carry

- When you have called someone toward truth and they did not hear, what part of their non-hearing have you been carrying as if it were your failure?
- What are the *justs* that you reach for — the labels that keep the call from landing? *That's just for the scholars. That's just my anxiety. That's just for someone more devout.* Which is yours?
- If the difference between hearing and processing is the willingness to be unsettled for thirty seconds — when did you last let an ayah unsettle you?
- Whose deafness, in your life right now, are you tempted to take personally — and how would you walk differently if you knew the door from your side closes after the warning?
- If today an ayah came to you and you suspected you were not hearing it — would you be willing to stop, or would you label it and keep walking?

### One-sentence distillation

**You were sent to deliver the call, not to manufacture the hearing — and the only deafness that cannot lift is the one that does not yet know it is deaf.**

### The closing invitation

There is a kind of peace that comes with this ayah, and it is not the peace of giving up. It is the peace of knowing exactly what is yours and exactly what is not. The Prophet ﷺ did not stop calling after this ayah was revealed. He kept calling, every day, until the end of his life. But he kept calling without the weight of believing every refusal was his to carry.

You can do the same. Speak the truth where it is needed. Place it carefully. Place it kindly. And then — leave it where you placed it. The hearing was never in your hands. The seed was. You planted it. *Allāhumma ballaght.*

And if you are on the other side — if today the ayah is asking *you* to hear, and the inner reflex is already reaching for the label — pause. For thirty seconds, let it not yet be categorized. Let it accuse you a little. That pause is the road from the ear to the heart re-opening.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who placed the warning in the wahy and the hearing in the heart — let us be carriers who do not despair when the call is refused, and let us be listeners who do not flee into labels when the call lands close. Lift from us the deafness we have not yet noticed. And let the words we deliver, and the words we receive, both find their way to where they were sent. *Āmīn.*
