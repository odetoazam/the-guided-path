# The Companionship That Burns
## When What You Worship Comes With You — Surah Al-Anbiya 98–100

---

## Introduction

Listen first to the Arabic.

> [ayah:21:98] إِنَّكُمْ وَمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ حَصَبُ جَهَنَّمَ أَنتُمْ لَهَا وَٰرِدُونَ
> *Innakum wa mā taʿbudūna min dūni-llāhi ḥaṣabu jahannama, antum lahā wāridūn.*

> [ayah:21:99] لَوْ كَانَ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ ءَالِهَةً مَّا وَرَدُوهَا ۖ وَكُلٌّ فِيهَا خَـٰلِدُونَ
> *Law kāna hāʾulāʾi ālihatan mā waradūhā, wa kullun fīhā khālidūn.*

> [ayah:21:100] لَهُمْ فِيهَا زَفِيرٌ وَهُمْ فِيهَا لَا يَسْمَعُونَ
> *Lahum fīhā zafīrun wa hum fīhā lā yasmaʿūn.*

In an English that opens the door:

> *Indeed, you and what you worship besides Allah are the fuel of Hell — to it you will be brought.*
> *Had these been gods, they would not have come to it. But all of them will abide therein eternally.*
> *For them therein is groaning, and they will not hear.*

[PAUSE]

### Where we are standing

Before we touch a single word, let's stand inside the moment.

Surah Al-Anbiya is a procession. Twenty-five prophets are walked across its stage one after another — Ibrahim arguing with his father, Nuh saved from the great drowning, Ayyub crying *anni massaniya-ḍ-ḍurr*, Yunus calling from the belly of the fish, Zakariyya pleading not to be left alone. Each story repeats one note, struck again and again until you cannot mistake it: there is **One Lord**, and every prophet who ever lived knew it, and every people who refused it lost.

By the time we reach our three ayahs, the procession has ended. The eschatological signs have been named — the wall of Yajuj and Majuj broken, the promise drawing near, the sky rolled up like a scroll. The surah is closing its argument. And it closes the argument by doing something terrifying.

It puts the worshipper and the worshipped on the same stage.

For the entire surah, idols and false gods have been argued *about* — challenged, ridiculed, exposed. Now they are dragged forward and addressed *with* their worshippers, in the same sentence, in the same fate. The argument has moved from courtroom to verdict.

### The Hook

Here is what we will discover together. Allah does not say in these ayahs: *"your gods will be punished, and you will be punished."* He says something far more devastating. He fuses them. He places the worshipper and the worshipped into a single grammatical unit and a single physical material.

And then, at the very end, He severs one sense.

Not the sense you would expect. Not sight. Not touch. **Hearing.**

Why? What is it about hearing that, when removed, makes Hell into Hell?

Stay with me. We have to build slowly to see it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We will follow three threads. A noun that kindles. A verb that is thirsty. And an absence that closes the door.

---

### Thread One: The word *ḥaṣab* — and what it means to be kindling

#### Teaching the concept first

You can describe fire differently depending on what feeds it.

There is the fire that is tended. The cedar laid carefully in the hearth, the slow-burning hardwood gathered before winter, the cook's fire arranged piece by piece. The fuel has a kind of dignity. It was chosen. It was placed. Something about the arrangement says: *this material was considered.*

And there is the fire that consumes. The scrubwood thrown in to keep an inferno going — dead branches, cast-off husks, dried refuse. No arrangement. No ceremony. No consideration for where it lands. The material is flung in because it will burn, and burning is now its only remaining use.

The word Allah uses for what the worshippers and their false gods become is a word that every classical scholar of the Quran was unanimous in understanding. It is the second kind of fire. And the word's choice tells you everything.

#### Opening the word

The word is **حَصَبُ** — *ḥaṣab*.

Listen to the consonants: *ḥā*, *ṣād*, *bā*. Three percussive sounds. Even before you know the meaning, the word feels like something striking a surface.

When Ibn ʿAbbas — the Companion known as *tarjumān al-Qurʾān*, the Quran's own interpreter — was asked about this word, he said: *"kindling."* Ibn Kathir records this plainly as the first and clearest gloss. Not fuel in some abstract sense, but the material that catches first, the dry scrub that starts a fire and feeds it.

The transmitters in al-Ṭabarī's chain go further — and this is worth sitting with. Some specify *ḥaṣab* as *shajar jahannam*: **the trees of Hell itself**. Not kindling imported from the outside world, but the very vegetation Hell grows from within, the fuel the fire feeds on from its own interior. Al-Muyassar confirms the same register: *waqūd wa ḥaṭab* — fuel and firewood, the material a fire requires to keep burning.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the contrast the Quran is quietly drawing.

In Surah al-Baqarah (2:24), the word used for Hell's fuel is *waqūd*: *wa-ttaqū-n-nāra-llatī waqūduhā-n-nāsu wa-l-ḥijārah* — "guard yourselves against the fire whose *waqūd* is people and stones." *Waqūd* is broad. It means simply "that which the fire feeds upon." It does not specify the character of the material or how it arrived. It is a general word for fuel.

*Ḥaṣab* is not general.

The root **ح-ص-ب** carries in its classical derivational field the action of casting or flinging — *ḥaṣaba* means to hurl small stones, gravel, loose debris at something. *Ḥaṣbāʾ* is pebbles and scatter-material. When the Arabs said *ḥaṣaba al-rīḥ* — "the wind *ḥaṣaba*'d" — they meant the wind was hurling grit and sand in a pelting assault. The lexical history of the root does not disappear when the noun is formed.

So here is the precision of the Quran's word choice. The classical scholars preserve the primary meaning — kindling, firewood, the trees of Hell — while the root's derivational field adds a texture that *waqūd* simply does not carry: this kindling does not arrive with care. It is cast-off material, flung in. Not the cedar laid with intention. The scraps thrown in because throwing is the only thing left to do with them.

#### Notice something

Now read the syntax slowly: *innakum wa mā taʿbudūna min dūni-llāhi ḥaṣabu jahannam* — "Indeed you, **and** what you worship besides Allah, **are** [together] the *ḥaṣab* of Hell."

There is one predicate. One material. One verb of being. The worshipper and the idol are not on different shelves of the warehouse — they are the same pile of kindling, cast into the same furnace.

But pause before accepting the narrow scope of *mā taʿbudūna*. In Arabic, *mā* is the particle for non-rational objects — and it is easy to read the verse as addressed only to stone idols. Al-Muyassar, however, notes that the address encompasses something broader: *"من رضي بعبادتكم إياه من الجن والإنس"* — those from among jinn and mankind who *consented* to being worshipped alongside Allah. The verse's reach is wider than carved wood and polished stone. It extends to any being — however real, however conscious — that was placed on the altar in place of the One. Classical scholars differed on the precise scope of this, but the possibility that *mā* here swallows categories beyond the inanimate is not a modern invention; it lives in the classical corpus.

[PAUSE]

Imagine the man who carved the idol from olive wood. He spent days on it. He chose the grain. He smoothed the surface. He oiled it. He polished it. He hung garlands on its neck. He stood before it in the dark and whispered his fears. He prostrated to it.

In this ayah, that wooden idol and that bowing man **become the same handful of kindling**, cast into the same furnace. Whatever rank he held in his tribe, whatever wealth he commanded, whatever temple he built — gone. Whatever the idol was — however large, however ancient, however adorned — gone. Both reduced to *ḥaṣab*. Scrubwood. Thrown.

The relationship that the worshipper thought was upward — *I worship, it receives, it elevates me* — is revealed to have been horizontal all along. You and it. Together. *Ḥaṣab*.

### Thread Two: The verb *warada* — and the cruelty of arriving thirsty

#### Teaching the concept first

In English, when we say *"to come"* or *"to arrive,"* we do not specify *what we are arriving at*. The verb is neutral. You can come to a wedding. You can come to a funeral. The word does not tell you which.

Arabic is different. Arabic has many words for *coming*, and each one carries the silhouette of *what you are coming to*.

The verb we are about to look at is **وَرَدَ** — *warada*. And in classical Arabic — in the Bedouin Arabic that the Quran's first listeners spoke and lived — *warada* did not mean "to arrive" in general. It meant something very specific.

It meant **to come to water**.

A *wārid* — *wāridūn* in the plural — was a thirsty traveler approaching a well. A *mawrid* was a watering place. *Wirdun* was a flock or caravan that had reached water. The whole semantic field of this root drips. It is the language of the desert. It is the language of survival.

When a Bedouin said *"the caravan waradat,"* every listener saw the same image: animals lowering their heads to drink, men cupping their hands, the relief of arrival after days of dust.

#### Opening the word

Now look at what Allah says.

*antum lahā wāridūn* — **"you, to it, are arriving."**

To what?

To Hell.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this for a moment, because you have to feel the shock of it the way an Arab listener felt it.

The word *wāridūn* has just told the listener: *thirsty travelers arriving at water*. The mind is already filling in the image of the well, the relief, the cool drink. And then the sentence reveals what the *water* actually is.

It is fire.

The relief is a trap. The arrival is into the wrong element entirely. The *coming* that you have been trained your whole life to associate with *survival* now lands you in the thing that was hunting you.

This is one of the cruelest images in the Quran, and it is built entirely out of the right verb. If Allah had said *antum lahā ātūn* — "you are coming to it" — using the neutral verb *ata*, the sentence would still be terrifying, but it would be flat. The horror would be only in the destination.

By choosing *warada*, the horror enters the *verb itself*. Even before you reach Hell, the journey to it is described in the language of thirsty hope. You are not stumbling into Hell. You are *seeking* it the way the dying caravan seeks the spring. You are *arriving* with the gait of someone who thinks salvation is just ahead.

#### Notice something

Now look at the next ayah. The same root, again.

*Law kāna hāʾulāʾi ālihatan mā waradūhā* — *"Had these been gods, they would not have* ***come to it***."

The same verb. *Waradū*. Used a second time, only one ayah later. This is not coincidence. This is architecture.

Here is what the doubling does.

In ayah 98, the worshippers are described as *wāridūn* — coming to Hell as one comes to water. In ayah 99, the test is set: *if* these objects of worship were really gods, they would not be *waradū-hā* — they would not have come to it at all.

The same coming. The same verb. **The condition that proves godhood is whether the so-called god comes** *with the worshipper to that place*.

A real god — by the very definition of godhood — would not arrive at the watering hole of fire. A real god would be elsewhere. A real god would not show up *as fuel*.

Al-Ṭabarī draws out the full logical force of this. A genuine deity, he argues, would have *protected* its worshippers from entering the fire — because those worshippers had spent their lives in service to it. That is the implied contract of worship: I give you my devotion, you give me what you are uniquely able to give. But, as al-Ṭabarī states plainly, an object that has no capacity to benefit itself and no capacity to ward off harm from itself is even further removed from being able to do either of those things for anyone else. The incapacity on its own behalf *is* the argument. Divinity is precisely that which can protect. Whatever cannot protect was never divine.

[PAUSE]

And the verdict?

*Wa kullun fīhā khālidūn* — **"and all of them, in it, are eternal."**

*All*. Worshipper. Worshipped. Both. Forever.

The very fact that the worshipped object can be brought to Hell at all — that it can be a *ḥaṣab*, that it can be a *wārid* — proves it was never a god. Because gods, by definition, are not material. Are not subject. Are not transportable. The moment you can carry it into the fire, you have proven it was never above the fire to begin with.

[PAUSE]

This is what Allah's argument is doing. It is not pleading. It is not warning anymore. It is **demonstrating**. The proof of false godhood is the verb. The verb is *warada*. Whoever can be made to *warada* the fire was never a god.

### Thread Three: The word *zafīr* — and the breath that has nowhere to go

#### Teaching the concept first

You know the difference between a sigh and a gasp. Both are breath. Both are involuntary. But they live in different rooms of the body.

A sigh is the release of held tension. The breath leaves you because you finally relaxed.

A gasp is the opposite. It is the breath that *cannot* leave. Or the breath that comes in too fast. The breath of someone who has been struck. The breath of someone who is drowning.

Arabic has many words for breath. The Quran uses two of them for Hell — *zafīr* and *shahīq* — and they are not interchangeable. They are the exhale and inhale of suffering, named separately because suffering has a rhythm.

Tonight we are looking at *zafīr*.

#### Opening the word

The word is **زَفِيرٌ** — *zafīr*. From the root **ز-ف-ر**.

Listen to it again. *Zafīr*. The first sound, the *zāy*, is a long buzz — almost a moan in itself. The middle, the *fāʾ*, is a forced exhalation through the lips. The word itself is a sound the body makes.

In classical Arabic lexicography, *zafīr* has two meanings that converge. The first is **the labored, repeated breathing of one carrying a heavy load** — *zafara* in Form I means *to carry*. So *zafīr* is the breath of someone who has been *given a burden their ribs cannot hold*. The lexicographers describe it as *taraddud al-nafas ḥattā tantafikha minhu al-aḍlāʿ* — "the going back and forth of breath until the ribs swell from it."

The second meaning is **the first sound the donkey makes when it brays** — that long, ugly, pulled-in groan that precedes the bray itself. The word, in Bedouin ears, was the sound of an animal in distress before it has even formed a full noise.

Both meanings collapse into one image: a body under a weight it cannot lift and cannot set down. The laboring breath. The groan before the scream. The ribs swelling with the effort of being alive inside a situation that should not be survivable.

#### Notice something

Elsewhere in the Quran, when Hell's inhabitants are described, they have *both* zafīr *and* shahīq — "For them therein is *zafīr* and *shahīq*" (Hud 11:106). The two sounds together complete the cycle: the heaving-out and the pulling-in, the full rhythm of a body in agony.

Here, in 21:100, only *zafīr* is mentioned. The exhaling groan. The labored breath going outward.

Why?

Because the next phrase answers: *wa hum fīhā lā yasmaʿūn* — **and they, in it, will not hear.**

The *shahīq* — the desperate inward breath — is the breath of someone listening for a response. You call out, you groan, and then you pull in the breath while you wait. You are listening for the answer. The inward breath is hope still alive in the body.

In Hell, there is no answer. There is nothing to pull breath in for. There is only the *zafīr* — the groan going out, and out, and out, unmet. The circuit of sound is broken at the receiving end.

## Part 2: The Argument That Cannot Be Refuted

By the time we reach the end of these three ayahs, three different kinds of proof have been laid before the listener.

The first proof is **material**. They became *ḥaṣab*. The worshipper and the worshipped became the same kindling — cast-off scraps consumed together. If the worshipped had been real, the ontological distance between them would have been infinite. God and creation do not become the same pile of fuel. But they did. Therefore it was not God.

The second proof is **logical**. Al-Ṭabarī articulates this with precision: a genuine deity would have protected its worshippers from this destination, because the worshippers had given their lives in service to it. The social and theological contract of worship implies intercession: I devoted myself to you, now you must stand between me and destruction. But an object with no capacity to benefit itself, no capacity to ward off harm from itself, is even further removed from the possibility of doing either of those things for anyone else. The incapacity on its own behalf is itself a definition. Divinity is precisely that which can protect. Whatever cannot protect was never divine.

The third proof is **verbal**. The verb *warada* — applied equally to the worshippers and to the things they worshipped — collapses the distinction. In the architecture of that verb, whoever arrives as a *wārid* to Hell was always thirsty, always in need, always a creature of this world. Gods do not travel. Gods do not thirst. Gods do not *warada*. The verb places all of them — worshipper and worshipped — on the same side of the ontological line.

These three proofs are not laid out as three chapters in a theology textbook. They are compressed into eleven Arabic words in a single ayah. This is the Quran's method: the argument is inside the language, not beside it.

## Part 3: Why Hearing — and What the Surah Has Been Doing All Along

We saved the strangest part for last.

*Wa hum fīhā lā yasmaʿūn* — *"and they, in it, will not hear."*

Not: they will not see. Not: they will not speak. Not: they will not move. **They will not hear.**

Out of all the senses, why this one?

Al-Ṭabarī, in his commentary on these ayahs, pauses to identify precisely *which* people this address is aimed at. He says the verse is directed at those mushrikīn *whose description was already given in this very surah — those to whom no reminder came from their Lord but they listened to it while playing.*

He is pointing us back to the beginning.

Surah Al-Anbiya opens, in its second ayah, with a devastating image: *mā yaʾtīhim min dhikrin min rabbihim muḥdathin illā istamaʿūhu wa hum yalʿabūn* — **"No reminder comes to them from their Lord, newly sent, except that they listen to it while playing."**

*Istamaʿūhu*. They heard it. They used the faculty of hearing. The ears worked. The sound reached them. But they were playing. The hearing was technical — the vibration reached the eardrum — but the receiving never happened. The door was open but nothing came through it.

The surah opens with a broken hearing. It closes — in these three ayahs — with hearing removed entirely.

[PAUSE]

Stay with the shape of that.

At the beginning of Al-Anbiya, they *could* hear, but they chose to hear while playing. The faculty was intact. The reminder was reaching them — *muḥdath*, freshly sent, renewed with each prophet — and it was doing everything it could. They were simply not *there* for it. Their ears were present. They were not.

At the end, in Hell: *lā yasmaʿūn*. Not *"they choose not to hear."* Not *"they hear but ignore."* The capacity is gone. What was a choice in the dunya becomes a condition in the ākhirah.

There is a pattern here that the Quran returns to often: the faculty you refused to deploy in the service of truth becomes the faculty you lose. The surah itself gestures toward it — *wa lā yaʾtīhim min āyatin min āyāti rabbihim illā kānū ʿanhā muʿriḍīn* (21:32) — sign after sign came, and they turned away. Turning away repeated enough times is not a posture. It becomes a shape.

But why is hearing *the* sense whose loss defines their state in Hell?

Because hearing, in the Arabic moral imagination, is not merely receiving sound. Hearing is *receiving*. When you truly hear someone, you are open to being changed. You are allowing something outside yourself to enter. *Samʿ* is the first word in the great testimony of servitude: *samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā* — "we heard and we obeyed." The Quran pairs hearing with understanding and with obedience throughout: *la-ʿallahum yasmaʿūn*, "perhaps they will hear." The verb carries both the physical act and the ontological openness it requires.

In Hell, *lā yasmaʿūn*. They are sealed.

They cannot receive. No outside voice can enter them. No intercession reaches them. No mercy can be heard. The *zafīr* — the groan — goes out. Nothing comes back. The circuit of relationship, which requires someone to send and someone to receive, is permanently open on one end and permanently closed on the other.

[PAUSE]

Think of what this means in light of the surah's entire argument.

The surah spent all of its length asking: *will you hear? Will you hear from Nuh? From Ibrahim? From Musa? From all twenty-five of them, the same note, the same Lord, the same invitation?* Will you hear?

They heard while playing. They heard while turning away. The *dhikr* came and came — *muḥdath*, newly, repeatedly renewed — and they met it with amusement.

And now: *lā yasmaʿūn*. The hearing is gone.

This is not arbitrary punishment. It is the outcome made visible. The relationship they refused — between themselves and the divine voice, between themselves and the *dhikr* — is now simply the condition they inhabit. They were always, in some deep sense, not hearing. Now it is all the way true.

## Closing: The Companionship That Could Not Save

Return to the opening of these three ayahs. Allah begins with *innakum* — "indeed, you." A direct address. You are being spoken to, not about. The surah has narrated twenty-five prophets, has reported their arguments and their victories, has described cosmic eschatological signs. And now, at the close, Allah addresses the listener directly.

*You, and what you worship.*

Not "those people." *You.*

The *wāw* in *innakum wa mā taʿbudūn* — "you **and** what you worship" — is a conjunctive particle. In grammar, it joins. In this sentence, it joins the worshipper to the worshipped so completely that they share one predicate, one material, one fate. The whole relationship you had — the temple you built, the prayers you offered, the fear you invested, the devotion that organized your life — is compressed by a single *wāw* into a single pile of *ḥaṣab*.

The companion you chose could not save you.

Not because it failed on the day. Not because it was weak in that moment. But because it was never above the fire to begin with. Because it *warada* just as you *warada*. Because whatever can be brought to Hell was always already in need of what Hell represents — a place that receives what cannot save itself.

The divine argument in these three ayahs is ultimately not about fire. It is about the ontological status of what you chose to rely on. Anything that can be made a *ḥaṣab* was always just material. Anything that can *warada* was always just a creature. Anything that emits *zafīr* without hearing any reply back was always limited to its own interior.

There is One who does not *warada* into fire. One who is not *ḥaṣab*. One before whom even Hell is a created thing, subject and bounded.

That is the One who has been speaking, through twenty-five mouths, across the entire length of this surah.

*And these three ayahs are what happens when the last mouth finally falls silent.*
