# The Fortress Before the Breath

## Maryam, Her Son, and the Singular Sign

> [ayah:21:91] وَٱلَّتِىٓ أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا فَنَفَخْنَا فِيهَا مِن رُّوحِنَا وَجَعَلْنَـٰهَا وَٱبْنَهَآ ءَايَةً لِّلْعَـٰلَمِينَ
>
> *Wa-llatī aḥṣanat farjahā fa-nafakhnā fīhā min rūḥinā wa-jaʿalnāhā wa-bnahā āyatan lil-ʿālamīn.*
>
> "And [mention] she who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her of Our spirit, and We made her and her son a sign for the worlds."

---

## Introduction: A Catalogue of Names — and One Without a Name

For four ayahs before this one, Allah has been calling the roll.

Yunus — the man in the belly of the fish, called from inside the dark.
Zakariyya — the old man who refused to die without an heir.
Yahya — the boy granted when his mother was thought barren.

Each of them named. Each of them a prophet. Each of them announced.

Classical exegesis notes why Maryam's story follows immediately after Zakariyya and Yahya's — Ibn Kathir observes that the one is connected to the other. Zakariyya's story is a child granted to a father of advanced years and a mother regarded as barren — already startling. Maryam's is the far edge of that same scale: a child granted to a mother with no father in the account at all.

Then — without warning — the names stop.

The verse opens with **wa-llatī** — *"and the one who..."* — the feminine relative pronoun, hanging in the air with no antecedent. No name. No title. No lineage.

Just a deed.

**She who guarded her chastity.**

The original listener — sitting in Mecca, hearing this catalogue of prophets being read by the Messenger ﷺ — would have noticed it immediately. Every other figure was introduced by name. This one is introduced by *what she did*. Allah refuses to identify her until after He has named her action.

That choice is not stylistic. It is theological. In a world where her honor was disputed, where her son's birth was used as evidence against her — Allah introduces her not by who slandered her, not even by who she was, but by **what she did before all of it.**

She fortified.

Then He gave her a name through her son: *her son*. And then He told us what she became: **a sign for the worlds.**

[PAUSE]

There is something strange in this verse that we will arrive at slowly. Allah is talking about *two people* — a mother and her son. But when He calls them a sign, He uses the singular. Not "two signs." One. **Āyatan** — one sign.

Why?

That is the question we are going to live with for the next hour.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

There are three features of this ayah that, taken together, reframe how we understand Maryam — and how Allah Himself wants us to understand her.

### Element One: *Allatī* — Identified by Deed, Not by Name

Let me start with something general about how language identifies people.

When we introduce someone, we usually offer a name. *"This is Sarah."* If the name doesn't carry meaning to the listener, we add a relationship: *"This is Sarah, my sister."* If the relationship doesn't anchor it, we add a role: *"This is Sarah, the doctor."*

Names. Relationships. Roles. These are how human introductions work.

But there is another mode — rarer, more deliberate. We use it when the *deed* is more important than the name. *"This is the woman who pulled the child from the river."* Now the listener doesn't even need her name yet. The deed has already told us everything that matters.

Notice what this kind of introduction does. It says: **what she did is who she is, before any other identifier reaches her.**

Now look at how Allah opens this verse.

**وَالَّتِي أَحْصَنَتْ فَرْجَهَا**

*Wa-llatī* — "and the one who" — feminine, definite, but with no preceding noun. Classical Arabic grammar calls this construction a *ṣila* without an explicit *mawṣūl* — a relative clause whose subject is left implicit because it is so well known, or so significant, that it would diminish the verse to fix her in any other category first.

She is, before anything else, *she who fortified*.

Stay with this for a moment. Allah could have said *"and Maryam, who..."* He could have said *"and the daughter of ʿImrān, who..."* He could have said *"and the mother of ʿĪsā, who..."* The Quran uses all of these designations elsewhere. Here, He chooses none of them.

Here is what that choice does. It places her **action** before her **identity**. Before she is anyone's daughter, before she is anyone's mother, before she carries any social location at all — she is a woman who fortified herself.

Notice something. In a culture that knew her by the scandal — *"O sister of Hārūn, your father was not a man of evil"* (Maryam 19:28) — Allah refuses to introduce her through that lens. He doesn't say "the one who was accused" and then defend her. He skips the accusation entirely. He goes straight to her interior act.

**The deed is the introduction.**

Here's what's remarkable. By the time we reach the end of the verse and learn she has a son, our minds are already settled on what she did. The accusation that her culture launched against her cannot stick to a verse that opens with *aḥṣanat*. Allah has pre-empted the slander with His grammar.

[PAUSE]

So when we ask — *what does the verse reveal about her in its very first word?* — the answer is: it reveals that her name is, in a sense, secondary. Allah honors her by letting her action speak first.

### Element Two: *Aḥṣanat* — The Fortress, Not the Closed Door

Now we come to the verb itself. And this is where the entire theology of the verse opens.

Most translations render *aḥṣanat farjahā* as "she guarded her chastity" or "she preserved her chastity." These are correct. But they don't carry the architecture of what the Arabic is doing.

The root is **ḥ-ṣ-n** — *ḥaṣana*. Let me teach you the root before we touch the verb.

*Ḥiṣn* — from this same root — means *fortress*. A fortified, walled stronghold. Not a closed door. Not a locked gate. A *fortress* — a structure built deliberately to be inaccessible, designed from the inside out to resist entry.

*Muḥṣana* — also from this root — is the term Allah uses elsewhere for a chaste, protected woman. Not a passive condition. An *active state of fortification.*

*Ḥaṣīn* — also from this root — means *secure, impregnable*.

So the root is not about absence. It is about **architecture**. A fortress is something *built*. Walls don't appear by accident. Battlements don't grow from the ground. To call something *ḥaṣīn* is to say someone *constructed* it that way.

Now look at the verb form Allah uses. *Aḥṣanat*. This is **Form IV** — the *afʿala* pattern. Form IV in Arabic almost always signals *causation* or *active making*. *He made [something] X.*

So *aḥṣanat farjahā* does not mean "her chastity happened" or "she remained chaste passively." It means **she actively made it a fortress.** She caused her own fortification. She was the architect of her own protection.

Classical commentators record an ikhtilaf over exactly what that fortification reached. Al-Ṭabarī preserves both readings: some understood *farjahā* narrowly, as her own chastity guarded against any illicit act; others traced the fortification more literally, to the very neckline of her garment (*jayb dirʿihā*), which she held closed against Jibrīl himself in the moment before she recognized him as a messenger of her Lord rather than a stranger. Either reading serves the same architecture — a woman who had built walls at every level, from interior conviction down to the very seam of her clothing. And it is at that guarded opening, according to al-Muyassar and al-Jalālayn, that the breath is said to have entered — into the collar she had fortified, reaching down until it formed new life in her womb.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

There is a quiet but devastating implication here. Allah did not say *fa-nafakhnā fīhā* — *"so We breathed into her"* — because she was a passive vessel waiting to be used. He said it because **she had built the fortress first.**

The breath came after the building.

This is one of the deepest patterns in the Quran's spiritual teaching, and most listeners miss it because the translation flattens it. Divine inspiration — divine breath — does not pour into the open. It pours into the **fortified**. Allah is not looking for empty vessels. He is looking for *deliberate ones*.

Notice the order of the verbs in this verse:

1. **aḥṣanat** — she fortified (her act)
2. **nafakhnā** — We breathed (Our act, in response)
3. **jaʿalnā** — We made (Our declaration)

Three movements. Each enabled by the previous.

She built. Then He breathed. Then He declared.

If we are honest about what Allah is showing us here, we have to ask the harder question. Why does He place her act first? Why doesn't He say *"We chose her"* and then describe her response? He could have. The Quran does that elsewhere — *"Allah chose you and purified you"* (3:42). But *here*, at the climactic moment, Allah arranges the verbs to begin with *her* action.

It is as if Allah is telling us: **the breath is real, but the breath enters where the fortress has been built.**

This is not works-righteousness. It is not "she earned the breath." Allah's breath is pure gift. But the gift falls on the *fortified*. Allah's grammar is showing us a spiritual law: **inspiration follows interiority.**

Here's what's remarkable. The Quran is doing something subtle to a misunderstanding that runs through both ancient and modern religious imagination. The misunderstanding is that the holy person is *empty* — that sanctity is achieved by self-emptying, by becoming a passive conduit. Allah's grammar pushes back. Maryam was not empty. **She was fortified.** Her interior was not a void. It was a structure. The breath did not enter a vacuum. It entered a stronghold.

[PAUSE]

Now consider one more layer. The grammar of the Form IV verb implies that fortification is not a single moment. It is sustained. *She fortified* — she kept fortifying, until the moment Allah breathed. The breath did not come at the end of a single decision. It came at the end of a *life of decisions.*

We don't know how many years Maryam spent in the chamber, alone with Allah, before Jibrīl arrived. The Quran tells us elsewhere that Zakariyya found provision with her every time he entered her sanctuary (3:37). What we are told here is that the work of *making the fortress* preceded the breath. She had been building for a long time before she was breathed into.

So when we ask — *what does this single verb reveal?* — the answer is staggering. It reveals that Maryam's chastity was not a *condition*. It was a *construction*. And that construction is what made her capable of receiving what no other woman has ever received.

### Element Three: *Āyatan* — One Sign for Two People

Now we come to the feature that, in some ways, is the entire ayah's secret.

Allah says: *wa-jaʿalnāhā wa-bnahā āyatan lil-ʿālamīn.*

*"And We made her and her son a sign for the worlds."*

Let me show you what most listeners pass over. The grammar is doing something the translation cannot fully convey.

The verse names **two people**: *hā* (her) and *ibnahā* (her son). That is dual reference. Standard Arabic, when referring to two beings, uses the dual form for any predicate that follows. We would expect *āyatayn* — "two signs" — or at minimum a plural construction.

But Allah does not say *āyatayn*.

He says **āyatan** — *a sign*. Singular. Indefinite. One.

Two people. One sign.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. This is not a grammatical error and it is not a stylistic flourish. Classical commentators have long paused on this word, noting how unusual the construction is.

Why does Allah bundle two people into one sign?

Here is what the singular *āyatan* is telling us. **Maryam and her son cannot be understood separately as evidence.** The miracle is not the son alone. The miracle is not the mother alone. The miracle is the *together-ness* — a son born without a father, *and* a mother who fortified herself before he was breathed into her. Take away either pole, and the sign collapses.

Think about what this does to our understanding.

If you tried to make ʿĪsā the sign by himself, you would have to explain his birth without the mother who fortified herself. You would have a miracle floating in air, with no human ground for it. The son needs the mother — not biologically only, but **theologically**. The fortress is the proof of the breath.

If you tried to make Maryam the sign by herself, you would have her chastity but no fruit. You would have the fortress without the breath. Her aḥṣanat would be a private virtue, but not a sign for the worlds.

It is the *combination* that signifies. The fortified mother **and** the breathed-into son. The two of them, inseparable, are one piece of evidence presented to all of creation.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. Allah says *ibnahā* — *her son*. He does not say *al-ibn* (the son), and He certainly does not say *ibnu-llāh* (the son of God). The grammatical possession of the child belongs to **her**. Her son. The verse refuses, through one possessive suffix, the entire theological architecture that would later make this child into something he was not.

The child is **hers**. Made by Allah's breath, but possessed in the verse by his mother.

This is one of the gentlest, most surgical pieces of theological correction in the Quran. Allah does not argue. He does not refute. He simply attaches the genitive pronoun to the right person.

Here's what's remarkable. Some Christian traditions have spent two thousand years trying to find the right relationship between Mary and Jesus — *Theotokos*, *Mother of God*, *Co-Redemptrix*, and on the other side, the Protestant traditions that have at times reduced her to almost nothing, embarrassed by the Catholic veneration. The Quran threads the needle in *one verse*.

Mary is not a goddess. She is a woman who fortified herself.
Jesus is not the son of God. He is **her son**.
And neither of them is a sign by themselves. **Together they are one sign for the worlds.**

[PAUSE]

Now look one more layer. The phrase *lil-ʿālamīn* — *"for the worlds."* Plural. Not for her contemporaries. Not for Bani Israʾīl. For the worlds — every era, every community, every system of meaning that humanity has built. Al-Jalālayn glosses the phrase even more concretely: mankind, jinn, and angels — every order of creation capable of witnessing a sign, not human history alone. And al-Muyassar frames its reach across time as well as space — a lesson for creation, it says, lasting until the establishment of the Hour. This sign is portable. It travels.

And it travels as one piece. You cannot extract Maryam from the sign and leave only Jesus. You cannot extract Jesus from the sign and leave only Maryam. Allah has soldered them grammatically with a singular noun. **Āyatan**.

So when we ask — *what does the singular reveal?* — the answer is: it reveals that to honor Christ without honoring his mother, or to honor his mother without honoring her chastity that preceded him, is to break the sign Allah Himself put in the world.

---

### The Bridge

Look at what the linguistic construction has shown us:

**Allatī** told us she is identified by her deed before any name reaches her.
**Aḥṣanat** told us her chastity was an active fortification, an architecture she built.
**Āyatan** told us she and her son are inseparable — one sign, not two.

These are not three separate observations. They are three angles on a single reality.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About how the divine touches the human, about how the holy is built, about how Allah makes a woman into a sign that travels for two thousand years and counting.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Fortified Interior Receives the Divine Breath

I want to begin somewhere very far from Maryam. Bear with me.

We are living in the most "open" generation in human history. Open inboxes. Open notifications. Open feeds. Always-on availability. Streaming presence. We have built an architecture of *constant accessibility* and called it connection. The contemporary human interior is, by design, **unfortified**.

Now ask yourself something honest. What do you notice happens to your inner life under these conditions?

If you are like most people, you notice it becomes **shallow**. Reactive. Pulled in fragments. The thoughts that arise feel borrowed. The emotions feel inherited from the last thing you scrolled past. You may feel *connected* in some technical sense, but the part of you that is supposed to be deep — the chamber where conviction is formed, where prayer rises from, where you encounter Allah in the silence — that chamber is empty in a strange new way. Not the emptiness of openness to God. The emptiness of having been *occupied* by everyone else.

This is the modern condition. And it is, in the Quran's vocabulary, the *opposite* of *aḥṣanat*.

Maryam's chastity — *aḥṣanat farjahā* — is most narrowly about sexual conduct. But the root is about *fortification*, and the principle extends. There is a fortification of the body. There is also a fortification of the eye, the ear, the tongue, the imagination, the time. The Quran teaches a graduated architecture of *iḥṣān* — building walls so the interior is not a thoroughfare.

Why does this matter? Because the verse tells us — through its sequence — that *the breath of Allah does not enter the unfortified*.

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful here. I am not saying the unfortified are worthless. I am not saying Allah ignores those whose interior is in disrepair. The whole Quran is a call to those whose interiors are in disrepair. But there is a *spiritual law* at work in this verse, and it is this:

**The capacity to receive divine inspiration is correlated with the deliberateness of one's interior architecture.**

This is not abstract. Watch the people in your life who carry a quality of presence that you can feel when you walk into their home. Watch what they do with their time. Watch what they refuse access to. Watch what they have *fortified*. They are not anxious. They are not closed. They are *built*. And something flows out of them that did not originate with them.

That something is the trace of the breath that enters fortified places.

[PAUSE]

The first practical movement of this theme: **inventory your fortifications**. Not from a place of shame, but from a place of construction. What in your life is a fortress? What is a thoroughfare? Where is the chamber that is yours alone, where Allah can speak to you without competition?

Maryam had the *miḥrāb*. The chamber where Zakariyya found her, where the provision came that he could not explain. Her physical chamber was the outward sign of her interior chamber. The two were the same architecture.

What is your *miḥrāb*?

If the honest answer is "I don't have one," then that is the work of this verse. The breath doesn't come because you become passive. The breath comes because you become *built*.

Let me push this one more layer deeper.

There is something the modern self badly wants to hear, which is: *be open, be available, be receptive, be soft, and the divine will pour into you.* The Quran's grammar in this verse contradicts that intuition surgically. Maryam is not described as *open*. She is not described as *receptive*. She is described as **fortified**. And it is into the *fortified* that Allah breathed.

Why might this be? Here is the deepest layer.

A vessel that is open to everything cannot tell the difference between divine inspiration and any other voice. If everything has access, nothing carries weight. The interior becomes a public square — and a public square is no place for revelation. Revelation requires distinction. Distinction requires walls.

Maryam's fortification was not her rejection of the world. It was her ability to recognize the angel when he came. She was startled. She was honest about her startlement — *"How can I have a child?"* (19:20). But she could *recognize* the encounter precisely because she had built an interior that knew the difference between signal and noise.

[PAUSE]

The second practical movement: **build before you ask to be inspired.** Don't pray for divine wisdom and then leave the gates of your attention open to ten thousand voices. The breath comes to the fortress. Build the fortress.

This is not asceticism. Maryam was not a hermit. She was visited, she had a guardian, she lived inside her community's framework. Fortification is not isolation. It is *architecture*. You decide what reaches the inner chamber. You decide what the foyer can hold. You decide what stays outside the gate altogether.

The promise of this verse is that the breath of Allah is real, and it falls. It falls upon the built.

### Theme Two: The Mother and the Son — One Sign That Cannot Be Split

Now let me bring you to the second theme, and this one will hit close to home for many listeners — especially those of you who have wrestled with Christianity, or who came to Islam from a Christian background, or who have Christian family members and friends, or who simply live in a civilization that was shaped for two thousand years by what people made of Mary and Jesus.

There is, in the verse, a precision that is doing something extraordinary. Allah is correcting the entire architecture of how this mother and son have been understood — without polemic, without argument, without naming any opponent. He simply *places the words in the right relationship.*

Let me show you the architecture again.

**She** fortified.
**We** breathed.
We made **her and her son** **one sign.**

Notice what is *not* in the verse. Allah does not say *"and We made her son God."* He does not say *"and Our spirit became flesh."* He does not say *"and her son was Our partner."* He does not even use the language of *kalimatullāh* (the Word of Allah) which He uses elsewhere. He uses the simplest possible language: *her son.*

And He gives them, together, the simplest possible category: *a sign*.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter for your life?

Because most of us have *some* relationship to this story, whether we know it or not. If you grew up in the West, you have seen a thousand depictions of Mary and Jesus. You have a mental image. That image is doing theological work in your head whether you have examined it or not. And the Quran, in this single ayah, is offering you a different image — and the offer is precise.

Here is the offer.

**Mary is not a goddess.** She did not produce a god. She is a woman who fortified herself, and that fortification was honored.

**Jesus is not the son of God.** He is *her* son. The grammatical possession is hers. His relationship to Allah is not paternal but creative — Allah breathed into her of His spirit, the same way Allah breathed into Adam of His spirit (32:9, 15:29, 38:72). The "spirit from Allah" is not unique to Jesus. It is the *signature of every human being.* What is unique about Jesus is the *pathway* of his creation — through breath alone, without a father.

**Together — and only together — they are a sign.**

Now here is the move that the verse makes that most people miss. The sign is not against Christianity. The sign is *for the worlds*. And the sign is not "Mary alone" — which would be a kind of Marian devotion. And the sign is not "Jesus alone" — which would be a kind of Christological abstraction. The sign is **the relationship between them**.

What is the sign saying? It is saying: a fortified woman, breathed into by Allah, produces a son who comes into the world without the ordinary human pathway, and this entire arrangement is *evidence* — for all the worlds — that Allah is not bound by the patterns He has established. He created Adam from dust without a mother or a father. He created Jesus from a mother without a father. He creates each of you from a mother *and* a father. **Four kinds of creation, one Creator, one architecture of power.**

(Classical commentators enumerate this point: each of the four pathways of human creation exists so that no one can claim Allah is bound to a single mode.)

[PAUSE]

Now connect this to your life.

If you have Christian family, this verse is not a weapon to swing at them. It is a way of seeing. Maryam is **revered** in this verse — more reverenced, in some ways, than in Protestant traditions that diminished her. Allah honors her by giving her half the sign. He honors her by introducing her by her deed before her name. He honors her by attaching her son to her possessively. **Islam does not erase Mary. It positions her precisely.**

If you have wrestled with how to honor Jesus without overstating him, this verse is the vocabulary. He is *her son* and a *sign* — extraordinary, breathed into, sent. Not God. Not a partner. Not a third in three. *Her son*. And the sign-hood that Allah grants him is shared with the woman who fortified herself before he existed.

If you have wrestled with women's place in the prophetic catalogue — and many young Muslims have — this verse closes a sequence of male prophets with a woman who is given a *singular* honor: she is **half of one of the great signs** Allah placed in human history. The chamber-builder. The fortifier. The mother of the breath.

[PAUSE]

The practical movement of this theme. When you encounter contested ground around Jesus and Mary — at a holiday table, in a conversation with a Christian friend, in your own private wrestling — return to the *grammar*. **Aḥṣanat** comes before **nafakhnā**. *Her* fortification came before His breath. She is *not* a passive vessel. She is *not* a goddess. *She fortified, We breathed, and We made them one sign.*

You don't need a polemic. You need the verse.

The second practical movement: **honor the women in your tradition the way Allah honors them.** Allah introduced Maryam by her deed before her name. He gave her half a sign that travels through every world. He attached her son to her with a possessive pronoun. The pattern is not subtle. The faithful echo is to identify the women in our communities by what they *built*, not only by whose daughter or wife or mother they are. The fortifications women build — the prayer rugs that get worn down, the children raised against the cultural current, the chastity of attention in a distracted age, the rooms where Allah has been remembered for years — these are sign-territory. Allah's grammar in this verse trains us to see them.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at what Allah has built.

Three verbs, in order:

1. **Aḥṣanat** — *she fortified.* The human act. The architecture.
2. **Nafakhnā** — *We breathed.* The divine act. The descent.
3. **Jaʿalnā** — *We made [them a sign].* The divine declaration. The meaning.

The first verb is hers. The second and third are His. And the second flows from the first with a *fa-* — the fa of immediate consequence. Because she fortified, He breathed. Because He breathed, He made.

The architecture is staircase, not parallel. Each step rests on the one below.

And the two figures of the verse — mother and son — are bound by a singular noun: **āyatan**. Not two signs. One. Inseparable. Soldered into a single piece of evidence by Allah's grammar.

The whole verse is a single sentence. Nine words. One staircase. One sign. One mother and one son, who cannot be honored apart, addressed to all the worlds.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two angles.

The grammar told us: **she is identified by her deed, her chastity is a fortification she built, her son cannot be split from her in the divine accounting.**

The themes told us: **the fortified interior receives the divine breath, and the mother-son pair is a sign that any honest person — Muslim, Christian, or other — must hold together as Allah holds it together.**

These are one teaching. **The fortress comes first. The breath comes to the fortified. And the result is a sign that cannot be cleaved.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my interior life is a fortress, and what is a thoroughfare? If Allah were to look for a fortified chamber in my day, where would He find it?

2. Have I been waiting for divine inspiration to arrive in an unbuilt place? What would it look like to *build* before I ask to be breathed into?

3. When I think of Mary and Jesus — whatever my background — do I hold them as Allah holds them? As a fortified woman and her son, one sign together for all the worlds?

4. What deeds of the women in my own life have I overlooked because I was busy identifying them by name and role rather than by *what they built*?

5. Where in my conversations about contested faith does the *grammar* of this verse — *she fortified, We breathed, We made them one sign* — give me language I did not have before?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Maryam's chastity was not a passive condition but an active fortification, and that fortification is precisely what made her capable of receiving the divine breath that became, with her son, **one sign** for all the worlds.

### The Closing Invitation

Begin to think of your interior as architecture, not as weather.

Weather happens *to* you. Architecture is what you *build*. And the breath of Allah — the inspiration, the conviction, the recognition of Him in the moments that matter — does not arrive in weather. It arrives in built places.

You are not waiting for Allah to fill an empty room. You are waiting for the room you have not yet built.

Build it.

Wall by wall. Decision by decision. Fortification by fortification. Of the eye, of the ear, of the tongue, of the time, of the body, of the imagination. Make a *miḥrāb* in your life — a chamber where the only voice that has ungated access is His.

Then watch what enters.

### Du'a

O Allah, You are the One who honored Maryam by introducing her through her deed before her name — teach us to identify ourselves first by what we have built for Your sake.

O Allah, You who breathed into the fortified — make us fortifiers of our own interiors, not waiters in unbuilt rooms, until Your breath finds in each of us a chamber prepared for it.

O Allah, who made a mother and a son one sign for the worlds — let us honor those You have honored as You have honored them, and let our lives become small signs that point, like theirs, only and ever back to You.

Āmīn.
