# The Two Returns: Coming to Yourself, Then Being Turned on Your Head

## Surah Al-Anbiya 21:65-67

> ثُمَّ نُكِسُوا۟ عَلَىٰ رُءُوسِهِمْ لَقَدْ عَلِمْتَ مَا هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ يَنطِقُونَ
> قَالَ أَفَتَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ مَا لَا يَنفَعُكُمْ شَيْـًٔا وَلَا يَضُرُّكُمْ
> أُفٍّ لَّكُمْ وَلِمَا تَعْبُدُونَ مِن دُونِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ

*Thumma nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim, laqad ʿalimta mā hā'ulā'i yanṭiqūn. Qāla afataʿbudūna min dūnillāhi mā lā yanfaʿukum shay'an wa lā yaḍurrukum. Uffin lakum wa li-mā taʿbudūna min dūnillāh — afalā taʿqilūn.*

> "Then they were turned upside down upon their heads, [saying], 'You already know that these do not speak.' He said, 'Do you then worship — instead of Allah — what does not benefit you in anything, nor harm you? Uff to you, and to what you worship besides Allah. Will you not then use reason?'"

## INTRODUCTION

### The world before the words

Picture a town. A small town built around a temple. Generations have prayed in this temple. Their fathers carved these statues. Their grandfathers commissioned them. The marketplace runs because of the festival days. The priesthood is paid because of the offerings. The political class is legitimized by the religious class. The whole social fabric — the calendar, the economy, the family rituals, the funeral rites, the pilgrimage routes — is woven through these statues at the center.

Now picture a young man inside their temple, with an axe.

By the time we arrive at ayah 65, he has already done what he came to do. The smaller idols lie in pieces on the floor. The largest one stands intact, with the axe slung over its shoulder, like an accusation. The townspeople are summoned. They look at the wreckage. They turn to him. *Did you do this?* He answers — not with a confession, not with a denial, but with a question that detonates underneath the foundations of their entire worldview: *Ask the biggest one. If they speak.*

[PAUSE]

And then something extraordinary happens. The Quran says — in the ayah just before ours, ayah 64 — *fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim*. **"They returned to themselves."** And from inside that returning, they spoke a sentence they had not planned to say: *innakum antum aẓ-ẓālimūn* — "Indeed, you yourselves are the wrongdoers."

For one moment, something cracked. Classical commentators read the *content* of that crack differently. Ibn Kathir understands *innakum antum al-ẓālimūn* as the people blaming each other for having failed to *guard* the idols — still within the idolatrous frame, a quarrel among believers about negligence, not a crisis of faith. Al-Muyassar and others read it as a genuine moment of silencing — *ikhāmuhum* — the argument breaking through, the mouth saying what the intellect had just been forced to confront. The disagreement among classical scholars here is real, and honest engagement with this passage requires holding it. But what remains true across both readings is this: the sentence they spoke was objectively correct. *They were the wrongdoers.* And that truth — even if they did not fully mean what they said, even if the self-blame was aimed at each other rather than at the idolatry itself — was enough to stop them, briefly.

And then — and this is where our ayahs begin — they did the most human thing that has ever been done.

They went back.

### The hook

There are two words in this passage that you would walk past without noticing if you read it in English. One is a position word — *nukisū*, "they were inverted." The other is a single syllable — *uff* — a sound of disgust, of contempt distilled to its barest form, the exhalation that comes when words have become too dignified for what you are witnessing.

These two words hold the entire psychology of why human beings, having once seen the truth, keep walking back into the lie.

What we are about to look at is not a story about ancient idolaters. It is a precise diagnosis of a movement that happens in your chest several times a week. You see something clearly. And then you stop seeing it. Allah names the mechanism.

[PAUSE]

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The architecture of two returns — *rajaʿū* and *nukisū*

Before we open our ayah, we need to read the ayah just before it, because the design of these two ayahs is one design. Allah has placed two movements right next to each other, and the only way to feel what He has done is to hold both at once.

In ayah 64, the people *rajaʿū ilā anfusihim*. **They returned to themselves.**

In ayah 65 — our ayah — *nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim*. **They were turned upside down on their heads.**

Two returnings. Two reversals. Right next to each other. And they are not the same kind of motion at all.

Let me teach the concept first, because Arabic does this with directional verbs in a way English does not. When a verb of movement is paired with a preposition (*ilā*, "to" — or *ʿalā*, "upon"), the preposition is doing real semantic work. *Ilā* names a destination — you are moving *toward* something, *into* it, *into the heart of it.* *ʿalā* names a surface upon which something falls or rests, often with the connotation of being acted upon.

So when Allah says *rajaʿū ilā anfusihim*, the motion is **inward and homeward**. They returned *into* themselves. They came home to themselves. They arrived at the place inside that knows.

But when Allah says *nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim*, the motion is **inverted and external**. The verb *nukisū* — let's slow down here — is the passive form of *nakasa*, root ن ك س, which carries the basic image of **flipping something upside down**. Lisān al-ʿArab attests it for reversing a flag, inverting a body, turning something over so that what was up is now down.

Notice the passive voice. They did not turn themselves over. *They were turned over.* Something happened *to* them.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the architectural insight that the surrounding ayahs are built on.

The first return — *rajaʿū ilā anfusihim* — is something they did. *They* returned. Active. From inside, walking inward, toward their own clarity. The fitra speaking. The intellect, when given air, finding its way home.

Though we should hold this carefully. Classical scholars disagree about the *quality* of what was found in that return. Ibn Kathir reads the self-blame in ayah 64 as the people indicting each other for negligence — for failing to protect the idols — so the *rajaʿū* is a return to accountability *within* the idolatrous system, not a recognition that idolatry itself is false. Al-Tabari records the view that they "returned from what they had recognized of Ibrahim's argument" — meaning the recognition was real and the *nukisū* that follows is its specific reversal. Al-Muyassar describes them as being genuinely silenced and refuted, then immediately repurposing Ibrahim's own words as cover. Three different textures of the same moment. What all three agree on: whatever was recognized, it did not hold.

The second return — *nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim* — is something done *to* them. They did not actively decide to relapse into idolatry. They were *flipped*. Inverted. Turned upside down. The very heads that had just glimpsed the truth — the heads themselves were turned over, so that everything they had just seen now appeared the other way around.

And where were they inverted? *ʿalā ru'ūsihim* — **upon their heads.** The seat of the intellect. The place of reason.

Allah did not say "they were turned upside down on their feet." He did not say "in their hearts." He said: **on their heads.** The very organ that just performed the *rujūʿ ilā anfus*, the return to themselves — the head — is now the site of the inversion.

The reason that just worked is now the reason that fails. The same instrument. Different posture.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, surveying the early generations, records three distinct readings of *nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim* among the scholars, and they are worth sitting with together. Qatada said: *adrakata al-nāsa ḥayrata saw'* — "a bad bewilderment overtook the people." The flip is not a decision but a state; they fell into confusion, and in the confusion, the clarity evaporated. Al-Suddi read it as: *nukisū fī al-fitna ʿalā ru'ūsihim* — "they were inverted *in* fitnah upon their heads" — the inversion naming not just a physical gesture but a resubmersion into the trial and corruption of idolatry itself. And Arabic linguists in al-Tabari's circle read it plainly: they "returned from what they had recognized of Ibrahim's argument" — the reversal targeting the recognition specifically. Three descriptions of the same catastrophe, and they are not mutually exclusive: bewilderment, resubmersion, and the reversal of recognition can all be happening simultaneously in a single courtyard.

[PAUSE]

Here is what's remarkable. Classical scholars have also read *nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim* in two ways at the more literal and idiomatic level, and rather than choosing between them, sit with both at once.

The first reading is **literal**. They lowered their heads. They could not look him in the eye. The shame of the moment of clarity in 64 — *we are the wrongdoers* — they could not stay with. So they bent their heads down. Eyes to the ground. Postural shutdown.

The second reading is **idiomatic**. *Nakasa* is used in Arabic for "relapse," for "regression," for "reversion." Like in Surah Yaseen, where Allah says of old age: *wa man nuʿammirhu nunakkis-hu fil-khalq* — "and whomever We grant long life, We reverse him in creation" (36:68). The body that was strong becomes weak again. The mind that was sharp becomes childlike again. *Nakasa* — reversion to the prior state.

So here, in Ibrahim's courtyard, *nukisū* means: **they reverted to their old idolatry.** They went back. The cognitive flag that had risen for one second — *we are the wrongdoers* — was lowered back down.

Both readings are true. And notice how they reinforce each other. The literal posture (heads bent down) and the spiritual posture (intellect inverted) are the **same gesture**, just at two different scales. The body bends because the mind has bent. The flag of consciousness, briefly raised, has been folded back up.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what they said from their inverted state. *Laqad ʿalimta mā hā'ulā'i yanṭiqūn* — "You already know that these do not speak." Al-Muyassar's observation here is devastating: they did not return with new evidence. They did not produce a counter-argument. They picked up Ibrahim's own argument — the precise argument that had just silenced them — and threw it back at him as a deflection. *You're the one who said ask the biggest one. You said that. And you already know they can't speak.* The weapon Ibrahim had aimed at their idolatry, they repurposed as a legal defense. The very sentence that should have produced repentance became the raw material for rationalization.

This is what inversion looks like from the inside. The same cognitive material, turned over, produces the opposite conclusion. The proof of idolatry's emptiness — that these things do not speak — becomes, in the inverted mind, evidence that Ibrahim's argument was somehow unfair. He set a trap. He knew the answer. The oracle he cited was never going to speak. They had been tricked.

The mind that is upside down can take anything and make it support the lie.

[PAUSE]

So look at the architecture Allah has built. Two adjacent ayahs:

> *fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim* — "they returned to themselves"
>
> *thumma nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim* — "then they were turned upside down on their heads"

The link word between them is *thumma*. **"Then."** Not *fa* (immediately after, as a consequence). *Thumma* implies a small interval — a pause, a moment, a gap. There is a beat between the seeing and the unseeing. There is a moment in which it could have gone the other way.

[PAUSE]

This is the most precisely engineered moment in the entire confrontation. Allah has placed before us, side by side, the two returns that every human being knows.

The return to yourself — when you finally see clearly. The thing you've been doing, the thing you've been telling yourself, the way you've been treating someone, the relationship you've been clinging to, the habit you've been justifying — it suddenly stands exposed. You walk *into* yourself, and from inside, you say the true sentence.

And then — *thumma* — comes the inversion. The flip. You wake up the next morning and you are doing it again. You scroll the same feed. You text the person you said you wouldn't. You consume the thing you said was killing you. You are not actively choosing this. You have been *turned over* on your head. The reasoning that was so clear last night is now upside down — and from this new angle, the old behavior looks reasonable again. And like Ibrahim's people, you may even find a way to use last night's clarity as cover for today's return.

This is the ayah's diagnosis. It is not telling us about Babylonian polytheists. It is telling us about the structure of the human will, on a Tuesday afternoon, after a Monday night of clarity.

What the ayah forces us to ask: **how often, in the time between two prayers, am I turned over on my head?**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Mā lā yanfaʿukum shay'an wa lā yaḍurrukum* — the precise futility

Now Ibrahim speaks. And he describes the idols with a phrase that is doing more work than any English translation can convey.

*Mā lā yanfaʿukum shay'an wa lā yaḍurrukum.*

"What does not benefit you in anything, and does not harm you."

Let me first teach the concept this verse hangs on, because it is one of the most important categories in the Quranic theology of worship: the **benefit-harm** axis (*nafʿ* and *ḍarr*).

Why do human beings worship anything at all? Strip the question down. The answer the Quran gives, again and again, is that we approach beings — visible or invisible — that we believe can do one of two things to us: **help us** (bring benefit, *nafʿ*) or **hurt us** (bring harm, *ḍarr*). The deity, in the religious imagination, is the one who can shift the conditions of your life — make rain fall, make the harvest grow, make the enemy fall, make the womb open, make the disease pass. The idol's power is precisely its imagined leverage on the *nafʿ-ḍarr* axis.

Ibrahim's argument is going for the throat of this entire mental structure. Watch what he does.

He says: *mā lā yanfaʿukum shay'an wa lā yaḍurrukum.*

Notice four things, very precisely.

**First**, the use of *mā* rather than *man*. In Arabic, *man* is "who" — used for sentient, rational beings. *Mā* is "what" — used for non-sentient things. By choosing *mā*, Ibrahim has already, grammatically, demoted the idols out of the category of beings worthy of address. They are not "those whom" you worship. They are "*what*" you worship. *Things*.

**Second**, the verb forms. *Yanfaʿu* (to benefit) and *yaḍurru* (to harm) — both in the imperfect tense, which in Arabic conveys ongoing, present-time, habitual action. He is not saying *cannot* benefit you. He is saying — *do not* benefit you. **In the actual present, in your real lived experience, these things have produced no benefit and no harm.** The argument is empirical. Look at your life. Has any of this object's promised power ever materialized?

**Third** — and this is the surgical word — *shay'an*. "Anything." A single grain of benefit. The smallest possible unit. He is not saying "they don't bring you the great benefits you want." He is saying: *not even one thing.* Not even the tiniest particle of benefit. The accusative *shay'an* attaches itself to *yanfaʿukum* and intensifies the negation: zero. Not a sliver.

**Fourth**, the pairing of benefit and harm. Why does Ibrahim mention both? Because in idolatry, you don't only worship what you hope will help you — you also worship what you fear might destroy you. The two motivations are sister-engines of the same misdirected reverence. So Ibrahim closes both doors at once. They cannot help you. **And they cannot hurt you either.** There is no reason to court them, *and* no reason to fear them. Worship is fully evacuated of any rational basis.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari extends this point further, and what he adds is important: the argument Ibrahim makes is not merely theological — it is *evidential*, and the evidence is lying on the floor around them. These idols were unable to prevent the harm that came to them. Ibrahim smashed them, and they could not stop him. And now, standing broken and silent, they cannot even speak to identify who harmed them. Both capacities that a genuine power would possess — the ability to protect and the ability to communicate — collapsed in the same afternoon. This is why Ibrahim can say *mā lā yanfaʿukum shay'an wa lā yaḍurrukum* with such force: he is not making a theological argument in the abstract. He is pointing at the ruins. The proof of their powerlessness is not in scripture or syllogism alone. It is lying at their feet.

[PAUSE]

Now, here is the question this argument forces upon us, and it is the question that should land in our chest.

If the test of whether something deserves your devotion is *can it actually benefit me, can it actually harm me* — then how do we audit our own quiet idolatries?

Think about the things that quietly occupy the place of god in modern hearts. The number in the bank account. The opinion of a particular person. The algorithm's verdict on the post you just made. The number on the scale. The job title.

Now apply Ibrahim's audit. *Mā lā yanfaʿukum shay'an wa lā yaḍurrukum.*

The number in the bank account — does it actually, by itself, benefit you? Or does Allah, through it or without it, benefit you? Without His sustaining, every digit is meaningless paper.

The opinion of that person — does their approval actually do anything to you? Or has Allah already decreed your worth, your standing, your provision, independently of what any created being thinks of you?

The algorithm — does it actually determine your reach, your impact, the hearts that are moved? Or is that *tawfiq*, and the algorithm is merely the surface through which it sometimes passes?

Ibrahim's audit is not asking you to be naive about the world. Money is real. Relationships are real. Work matters. The question is about the *weight* you assign to these things in the architecture of your heart. Do they live in you as means — instruments through which Allah's decree moves — or as ends, as the actual source of your benefit and harm?

When they live as ends, they become the thing you approach with reverence. The thing you would compromise your values to protect. The thing whose absence terrifies you. The thing you sacrifice for.

That is worship. And it is precisely what Ibrahim is naming.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Uffin lakum* — the language of contempt

And then Ibrahim says just one word. One syllable. *Uff.*

*Uffin lakum wa li-mā taʿbudūna min dūnillāh.*

"Uff to you, and to what you worship besides Allah."

Let us be precise about what this word is, because it is easy to misread — and the misreading matters.

*Uff* (أُفٍّ) is one of the most ancient interjections in the Arabic language. Lisān al-ʿArab records it as an expression of *taḍajjur wa-takrah* — **vexation and revulsion.** Not sadness. Not weariness. Not lament. *Revulsion.* The word that comes before thought, when something is so objectionable, so beneath dignity, so morally repugnant, that the body produces a sound before the mind has composed a sentence.

Al-Muyassar paraphrases *uffin lakum* as *qubḥan lakum* — **"contempt/ugliness upon you."** The word *qubḥ* in Arabic names moral ugliness, the quality of a thing that violates what is beautiful and right. Ibrahim is not saying "I pity you." He is not exhausted. He is not too depleted for full sentences. He is *disgusted* — with the pristine clarity of someone who has seen something so transparently false that the entire edifice produces nothing but revulsion.

[PAUSE]

Now notice the construction of the sentence. *Uffin lakum wa li-mā taʿbudūna min dūnillāh* — a single *uff* sweeping across two objects at once: *you*, and *what you worship*. Not two separate expressions of disgust, but one. You and your idols are a single object of contempt. You cannot be separated from what you worship. What you give your reverence to *is* what you are. The idol-worshipper and the idol, in Ibrahim's grammar, are one thing.

This is an extraordinary claim about the relationship between worship and identity. What you serve shapes what you become. The worshipper takes the form of the worshipped. These people who have spent their lives approaching things that cannot speak, cannot protect, cannot harm, cannot feel — they have organized their minds around objects that are, in Ibrahim's word, repugnant. And that organizing — the habits of attention, the rituals of approach, the postures of petition — has made them repugnant by association.

[PAUSE]

And yet. Notice where Ibrahim places *lakum* — "to you." First. Before the idols. *Uffin lakum* — contempt *to you* — and *then* to what you worship. He does not begin with the objects. He begins with the people.

Because the problem is not primarily the statues. The statues are wood. The problem is the *choice*, repeated, generation upon generation, to organize a human life around something that cannot respond. The statues are symptoms. The people are the diagnosis. The *uff* begins with the one who chooses, then extends to the thing chosen.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Four: *Afalā taʿqilūn* — the question at the end of the rope

Ibrahim ends not with a statement but with a question. *Afalā taʿqilūn.* "Will you not then use reason?"

Let me teach the root, because the root carries everything.

*ʿAql* — ع ق ل — comes from the action of *ʿaqala*, to bind, to tether, to hobble. The original image is of tying the leg of a camel so it does not wander off. To have *ʿaql* is to have the inner capacity that *ties down* the lower impulses — that keeps the self from drifting away from its own clarity. The *ʿaql* is not merely cognition. It is not the ability to process information. It is the capacity to *hold* what has been recognized — to stay with it, to let it govern the next moment and the one after.

The *ʿaql* is the rope.

[PAUSE]

Now see what Ibrahim's question is really asking.

You have just returned to yourselves — *rajaʿū ilā anfusihim*. You have just said the true sentence. And then you were *turned over on your heads* — *nukisū ʿalā ru'ūsihim*. The recognition was reversed. You went back.

*Afalā taʿqilūn* — will you not *bind*? Will you not *tether*? Will you not use the faculty that was *supposed to hold you* in the place of recognition, and prevent the inversion from happening?

The *ʿaql* is precisely the thing that, had it been employed, would have prevented the *nukisū*. The rope keeps you from wandering. But the *nukisū* is the sign that the rope was not in use — or that something cut it. A mind that has been inverted on its head cannot reach up to grab its own tether. The rope only works when you are right-side up.

[PAUSE]

This is the tragic architecture of the closing question. Ibrahim is asking them to use the very faculty that has been disabled by the condition he is diagnosing. The question is not naive. It is offered in full knowledge that the people who most need to hear *afalā taʿqilūn* are precisely the people whose *ʿaql* is not, in this moment, operational. And yet the question is still asked. Because even to a mind that is upside down, a question can be a hook — something to grab, the beginning of a second *rajaʿū*, if they choose to reach for it.

The question does not give up on them. That is worth sitting with.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## PART 2: THE PRACTICE OF SELF-AUDIT

What Ibrahim gives us in these three ayahs is not merely a historical narrative. It is a diagnostic map — precise, clinical, three-layered — of how the human self moves away from clarity and back into what it has already seen through.

Sit with what the map shows.

The *rajaʿū* — the return to yourself — is real. It happens. Something opens, and you see. Maybe it is in prayer, in a moment of solitude, in a conversation, in grief. You arrive at the place inside that knows. The fitra speaks. And what it says is true. Classical scholars may disagree about how deep Ibrahim's people actually went in their moment of return — whether it was genuine recognition or only tactical self-recrimination — but the structure of *rajaʿū* itself, the grammar of going inward, is real and available to you.

The *nukisū* — the inversion — is also real. It is not always a choice. It is often a falling. The flag of clarity, briefly raised, folds back down. The reasoning that illuminated last night darkens by morning. And when the inversion comes, you may do what Ibrahim's people did: take the very insight that opened you up and use it as material for staying closed. *I already knew that. That's not news. That doesn't change anything.* The seeing, repurposed as a reason not to change.

And then: *uffin lakum*. Not Ibrahim's word toward you, but the word that names the structures — the systems of attachment, the habits of approach, the things you have organized your reverence around — that have no power to help or harm, and yet you keep returning to them as if they do. The contempt Ibrahim expresses is not cruelty. It is clarity. He has seen what these things are, and he cannot pretend they are otherwise.

And then: *afalā taʿqilūn.* The invitation — the challenge — to use the rope. To bind the recognition to the next moment. To not let the *thumma* become a *nukisū*.

[PAUSE]

The practice this passage suggests is simple, and it is hard.

**First**: Know when you are in *rajaʿū*. Learn to recognize the moments when you return to yourself — when clarity surfaces, when you see what you have been doing. These moments are gifts. They are the fitra breathing. Do not rush past them. When you say the true sentence — even the one that costs you — notice that you said it.

**Second**: Expect the *nukisū*. Do not be surprised when the inversion comes. It is not proof that the earlier clarity was false. The fact that you relapsed does not mean you were wrong last night. It means you are human, and something acts upon you. The Quran names this not as simple sin but as mechanism — *thumma nukisū* — it happened *to* them. Understanding this does not excuse the return, but it dismantles the shame that would prevent you from attempting another *rajaʿū*. The people in Ibrahim's courtyard relapsed. The question is still being asked of them.

**Third**: Apply Ibrahim's audit. Ask, of whatever quietly holds the seat of deity in your daily life: does this thing actually benefit me? Does it actually harm me? Be honest. Not "could it theoretically benefit me" but: *in the actual lived texture of my experience, has this thing delivered what I organized my life around it to deliver?* Very often the answer is: it has not. And the reorganization of your reverence is not then a crisis. It is a homecoming. The *rajaʿū* — the return to yourself — is the movement this audit, done honestly, produces.

**Fourth**: Use the rope. *Afalā taʿqilūn* — the *ʿaql* is the tether. This is why the practices of daily remembrance — the five prayers, the dhikr, the Quran — are not spiritual decoration. They are the act of re-tying the rope while you are still right-side up. Every *salah* is an attempt to hold the recognition across another interval. The *nukisū* is always nearby. The rope is the answer to it. And the rope has to be tied *before* the inversion comes — because after it, you cannot reach it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING

There is something worth sitting with about Ibrahim's posture in these ayahs.

He has just watched a group of people do something that should be impossible: return to clarity, then immediately abandon it. He has watched the most complete exposure of idolatry's emptiness — the idols broken, the silence deafening, the people themselves saying "we are the wrongdoers" — dissolve back into the same idolatry within moments. He has watched his own argument weaponized against him. He has watched the *rajaʿū* become the *nukisū* before his eyes.

And he does not give up.

He asks *afalā taʿqilūn* as a genuine question, not as a closing argument for an audience he has already written off. The question is still open. The invitation is still live. Al-Tabari's scholars identified bewilderment, relapse, and the reversal of recognition all coexisting in that courtyard — and Ibrahim's response to all three is not silence but a question. A question that implies: the *ʿaql* is still there. The rope is still available. The *rajaʿū* can happen again.

There is something here about the character of the prophet — and about the character of the One who sent him. The message does not end because the first *rajaʿū* collapsed into *nukisū*. The door is not shut because the first attempt did not hold. There is another *afalā taʿqilūn* available. And another after that.

[PAUSE]

You have returned to yourself before. And you have been turned over on your head before. The *thumma* — the gap between clarity and inversion — is the space in which this question is always being asked.

*Afalā taʿqilūn.*

Will you not use the rope?
