# The Two "I Don't Knows" That Close the Surah of the Prophets

## Introduction: The Surah Built of Certainties

Take a step back and look at the surah we have been walking through.

Surah Al-Anbiya is, in some sense, a surah of certainty. Prophet after prophet appears. Ibrahim, who is given the knowledge to argue down idols. Lut, given *ḥukm* and *ʿilm* — judgment and knowledge. Dawud and Sulayman, who are *taught* to judge. Yunus, who is rescued from the dark. Zakariyya, given a son when biology said no. Each prophet is a portrait of a human being granted some certainty by Allah — and then vindicated.

The Meccans, meanwhile, had been demanding certainty of their own. "When?" they kept asking. "When is this Hour you keep talking about? When is the punishment? Give us a date. Give us a sign. Give us something we can plan around."

[PAUSE]

And so we arrive at the closing of the surah. The gallery of prophets has been displayed. The patterns of history have been shown. The mockery of the disbelievers has been answered. Now Allah turns to His Final Messenger ﷺ — the inheritor of this entire gallery — and tells him exactly what to say when, after all of this, the people still refuse.

Here is what is breathtaking. After a surah saturated with prophetic knowledge, after gallery upon gallery of *ʿilm* and *ḥukm* given to chosen men, the closing instruction is for the Prophet to say — twice — **"I do not know."**

> فَإِن تَوَلَّوْا فَقُلْ آذَنتُكُمْ عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ
> *But if they turn away, then say: I have announced it to you on an equal footing.*
>
> وَإِنْ أَدْرِي أَقَرِيبٌ أَم بَعِيدٌ مَّا تُوعَدُونَ
> *And I do not know whether what you are promised is near or far.*
>
> [ayah:21:110] إِنَّهُۥ يَعْلَمُ ٱلْجَهْرَ مِنَ ٱلْقَوْلِ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تَكْتُمُونَ
> *Indeed, He knows what is spoken openly, and He knows what you conceal.*
>
> [ayah:21:111] وَإِنْ أَدْرِى لَعَلَّهُۥ فِتْنَةٌ لَّكُمْ وَمَتَـٰعٌ إِلَىٰ حِينٍ
> *And I do not know — perhaps it is a trial for you and an enjoyment until a time.*

The Prophet of Allah, the recipient of revelation, the one whose knowledge of the unseen has been the very subject of the surah — is told to say, **"I do not know."** And not just once. *Twice.*

[PAUSE]

That is the hook. That is what we are going to sit with. **Why does the surah of the prophets end with the Prophet's confession of ignorance?**

## PART 1: The Linguistic Journey

We have three short ayahs and three places where the language is doing something specific. We will move through them slowly.

### Element One: آذَنتُكُمْ — "I have made you hear"

Before we open this word, let us teach the concept underneath it.

In Arabic — as in many languages — verbs are built from roots, and the same root can be reshaped into different patterns to mean different things. Take the English verb *to hear*. From it we can make *to listen*, *to overhear*, *to make someone hear*. Each form does something slightly different to the basic act.

Arabic does this with extraordinary precision. There is a basic form, and then there are derived forms that intensify, redirect, or causate the meaning.

Now look at this word: **آذَنتُكُمْ** — *ādhantukum*.

The root is **ʾ-dh-n**. And what is the root noun from this same family? **أُذُن** — *udhun*. **The ear.**

The word for *ear*.

Stay with that. Allah does not tell the Prophet to say "I have warned you," or "I have told you," or "I have explained to you." He chooses a verb whose root is **the ear itself**. The form here is causative — to *make* something happen with the ear. The classical lexicographers gloss it as *to make hear* or *to put on notice through hearing*.

The Prophet is being told: *Tell them — I have placed this in your ears. I have made you hear it.*

Notice what this implies. There is a difference between *being told* and *having heard*. Information can be transmitted and yet not register. **آذَنتُكُمْ** is the verb that closes that gap. It says: *the message has reached your ear. Whatever happens after this, the channel is no longer in question.* The signal arrived.

This is not a defensive verb. It is a verb of completion. The Prophet has done his work. The ear has received.

[PAUSE]

And then comes the phrase that finishes the sentence: **عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ** — *ʿalā sawāʾ*.

The root **s-w-y** is the root of equality, level-ness, evenness. Something that is *sawāʾ* is something whose two sides are matched. A horizon line. A balance scale at rest. A level field.

What does it mean to be told something **ʿalā sawāʾ** — on an equal footing?

It means: **none of you knows more than another.** None of you has the inside track. None of you was given private information that the others were denied. *I have announced to you all equally.*

Let's sit with this. In the world of seventh-century Arabia, knowledge was a currency of power. Tribal elders held genealogies. Poets held memories. Priests of idols held ritual secrets. Information was hoarded and traded and used to elevate one over another. The very structure of authority was built on *unequal access*.

And here, at the close of a surah about prophets — about people who, by definition, were given access *others didn't have* — Allah commands the Final Prophet to begin his closing address by **leveling the floor**. *I have told you all the same thing. Equally.*

Here is what is remarkable. The Prophet ﷺ *did* have something they didn't have — revelation. But what he did not have, and what he is being commanded to confess, is **the timing**. *That* is the piece of information he is being told to admit he does not possess. And by saying *ʿalā sawāʾ*, he is flattening the question. *On the matter of when, I am exactly where you are.*

That single phrase reveals what this short ayah is doing. **The Prophet is not negotiating from a position of hidden information. He is putting his ignorance on the table beside theirs.**

This is one word — **آذَنتُكُمْ** — and one phrase — **عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ** — and they are already telling us something extraordinary about how Allah wants His Messenger to stand before mockers. Not above them in possession of secrets. Beside them, on level ground, with the message between them.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: وَإِنْ أَدْرِي — Twice. Spoken. By the Prophet.

Now we come to the structural heart of this passage. There is a phrase that appears twice — **وَإِنْ أَدْرِي** — *and I do not know*.

Before we open it, teach yourself the grammar.

The word **إِنْ** in Arabic has two main uses. One is conditional: *if*. The other — the one operative here — is a **negative particle**, equivalent to *mā*. It simply means *not*. So **إِنْ أَدْرِي** is *I do not know*. Same construction appears in 6:50 — *qul lā aqūlu lakum ʿindī khazāʾinu llāh, wa-lā aʿlamu l-ghayb…* — but here Allah uses the more compressed, almost stark phrasing: *in adrī*. **Not. I. Know.**

The verb **أَدْرِي** is from **d-r-y** — to know with the kind of perception that comes from observation and inference. The lexicographers often distinguish *dirāyah* from *ʿilm*: *ʿilm* is knowledge by any source, including direct disclosure; *dirāyah* tends toward knowledge derived through faculties — through looking, weighing, considering. So **lā adrī** is closer to *I have no way to discern*. *I cannot perceive this from where I stand.*

Now notice something. This phrase appears **twice** in the span of three ayahs.

> **وَإِنْ أَدْرِي** أَقَرِيبٌ أَم بَعِيدٌ مَّا تُوعَدُونَ
> ...
> **وَإِنْ أَدْرِي** لَعَلَّهُ فِتْنَةٌ لَّكُمْ وَمَتَاعٌ إِلَىٰ حِينٍ

Two confessions of ignorance. Bracketing the central ayah.

And what is in the central ayah, between these two "I don't knows"?

> **إِنَّهُ يَعْلَمُ** الْجَهْرَ مِنَ الْقَوْلِ **وَيَعْلَمُ** مَا تَكْتُمُونَ
> *Indeed, He knows what is spoken openly, and He knows what you conceal.*

Two **يَعْلَمُ**. *He knows... and He knows.*

[PAUSE]

Look at this architecture. Look at what Allah has done.

The two ayahs of the Prophet's *I do not know* are wrapped around a center that twice declares *He knows*. The pattern is:

- I do not know [the timing]
- **He knows [the open speech] / He knows [the concealed speech]**
- I do not know [the meaning of the delay]

The center of the passage is not the Prophet's knowledge. The center is **Allah's knowledge.** And the Prophet's two ignorances are the frame around it.

This is structural. This is not accident. The shape itself is the argument: *human knowledge — even prophetic human knowledge — is the frame; divine knowledge is the picture.*

Stay with what this tells us about prophethood itself. We tend to think of a prophet as a person who *knows*. The very word *anbiya* — the title of this surah — comes from a root touching *naba'*, news, information from on high. Prophets are people who know what others don't. *That is their job.*

And yet the closing instruction to the Final Prophet is to say, twice, *I do not know.* The role of prophet, the surah is teaching us, is not exhaustive knowing. **The role of a prophet is faithful transmission of what was given — and absolute honesty about what was not.**

What he was given: the message. *I have announced it.*
What he was not given: the timing, the meaning of the delay. *I do not know.*

A true messenger never inflates his portfolio. He carries exactly what he was handed and nothing more.

[PAUSE]

There is another thing to notice. **The two questions the Prophet says he does not know the answer to are precisely the two questions the Meccans kept asking.**

*When is the punishment? Is it close or far?* — He says: *I do not know whether near or far.*
*Why is Allah waiting? Why hasn't this happened yet?* — He says: *I do not know — perhaps it is a trial for you, perhaps an enjoyment for a while.*

Their two demands. His two confessions. **He does not give them the answer they wanted. He gives them the honesty they did not.**

And here is the remarkable thing: that honesty does more for them than any answer could have. Because the moment the Prophet says *I do not know*, the question is no longer "what does Muhammad know?" The question becomes *what does the One who does know want from me right now?*

The frame collapses. The mockery has nowhere to land. You cannot mock a man for not having information he never claimed to have.

### Element Three: فِتْنَةٌ ... وَمَتَاعٌ — "Trial AND enjoyment"

Now to the third linguistic point — and this one carries enormous weight.

The Prophet is told to say: *Perhaps it* — meaning the delay, the long stretch of time before the promised reckoning — *is a* **fitnah** *for you* **and** *a* **mataʿ** *until a time.*

Before we open the phrase, teach yourself the grammar of the connector. The Arabic word **و** here is — in this context — a *waw of conjunction*. It joins two things that are simultaneously true. Not *or*. Not *one of these two*. **Both.**

This matters. Because in modern English we like to choose. We want to know: *was this delay punishment or mercy? Was this hard period a test or a gift?* We want a verdict.

Allah does not give a verdict. He gives a **conjunction**. *Fitnah* **and** *mataʿ*. **Both at once.**

Open the words.

**فِتْنَةٌ** — *fitnah* — comes from a root that originally referred to placing gold in fire to test its purity. To melt the metal so that what is real and what is dross separate. From there it expanded to mean any kind of test, trial, severe pressure, seduction, or upheaval.

**مَتَاعٌ** — *mataʿ* — comes from a root meaning enjoyment, provision, the goods of this life. The *mataʿ* of the dunya is what makes life pleasurable — food, family, comfort, breath. *Mataʿ* is the **good** of the days.

Now hold these together. *Perhaps the delay is — at the same time — the fire that purifies you, and the enjoyment Allah is giving you.*

Let's sit with this.

A delay you experience as a gift can also be the very mechanism of your test. The years you have on this earth, the breath you draw right now, the relationships you enjoy, the food you eat — *mataʿ ilā ḥīn*, enjoyment until a time — *is the same span of time inside which your soul is being weighed.*

There is no clean separation between *the good of life* and *the test of life*. The test does not arrive on Tuesday and depart on Friday. The test is woven into the fabric of the enjoyment. *Mataʿ* is the medium of *fitnah*.

This is one of the most psychologically liberating ideas in the Qur'an. Why?

Because we tend to ask, when something pleasant happens: *is this a blessing or a deception?* And when something hard happens: *is this a punishment or a purification?* We torture ourselves trying to assign one label.

Allah's answer through the mouth of His Prophet is: **stop trying to assign one label. The delay is both. The enjoyment is the trial. The trial wears the clothes of enjoyment.** Both, until a time.

Notice the final phrase: **إِلَىٰ حِينٍ** — *until a time*. The word *ḥīn* is indefinite. It is not *al-ajal al-musammā* — the appointed term. It is not specified. It is just... *a while*. A span of unspecified length.

And this is the second confession of ignorance. *I do not know* the timing in ayah 109; *I do not know* the meaning of the delay in ayah 111; and at the end of 111, even the duration of the *mataʿ* itself is unspecified — *until a while*. Layer upon layer of indeterminacy.

The Prophet is being commanded to live, and to teach his community to live, **inside an indeterminate span, on equal informational footing with the disbelievers, with full confidence only in the One who knows what is spoken openly and what is concealed.**

That is the linguistic architecture. **A frame of confessed ignorance around a center of declared divine knowledge — the whole resting on the unspecified duration of a life that is simultaneously trial and gift.**

## Bridge

The linguistic work has shown us something. The Prophet of Allah does not exit this surah on a note of triumph. He exits on a note of *honesty about what he does not know.* And the architecture of the closing — *I don't know / He knows / He knows / I don't know* — is the architecture of every believer's life.

Now let us ask: **what does this reveal not just about words — but about how a person of faith is actually meant to walk through the world?**

## PART 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Dignity of "I Don't Know"

Begin where you live.

You have, almost certainly, sat across from someone who would not say *I don't know*. A coworker who confidently declared a project timeline they had no way to keep. A relative who pronounced on a medical condition they had not researched. A pundit on television speaking with absolute certainty about events they had no access to.

You felt something when you heard them. Not respect. Something closer to its opposite.

Now consider the inverse. You have sat across from someone — a teacher, a doctor, a friend — who *did* say *I don't know.* And you remember how that felt. **Trust deepened.** Not despite the admission. **Because of it.**

Why?

Because *I don't know* is the signature of a mind in honest contact with reality. The world is full of things any given person does not know. A speaker who never says *I don't know* is either dishonest or has stopped looking. A speaker who says *I don't know* is telling you *the rest of what I say can be trusted, because I am willing to mark the boundary.*

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah is doing at the close of Surah Al-Anbiya. The most authoritative speaker in human history — the one whose words carry revelation — is being commanded to mark the boundary in the most public way possible. Not whispered. Not in private. In the closing ayahs of an entire surah. **وَإِنْ أَدْرِي.** *I do not know.*

And the surah is teaching us something the dunya teaches us in reverse. The dunya rewards confident-sounding people with airtime, followers, status. **The Qur'an rewards those who are precise about the edges of their knowledge.**

Notice how this dovetails with the surah we have been reading. Surah Al-Anbiya is a parade of prophets — and not one of them, in the surah's telling, claimed to know more than they were given. Ibrahim argued from what he could see. Yunus *cried out* from the dark, he did not declare from it. Sulayman, given dominion over wind and jinn, asked Allah for forgiveness for asking too much. *Every prophet in this surah operated within their portfolio.*

And now the Final Prophet is told: do the same. *Tell them I do not know what they are not entitled to know. The message I do know — and I have made you hear it.*

Here is the spiritual move. **Faith does not require you to know everything.** It requires you to *know what you know* and *not pretend about what you don't*. To say *I have heard this from my Lord* with confidence, and *I do not know the timing* without shame.

Most people are paralyzed by the inverse: they pretend confidence about things they do not know (the will of Allah for their specific life, the meaning of someone else's hardship, the verdict on a stranger's heart) — and they hide in uncertainty about things they *do* know (that prayer matters, that this life is short, that they will be asked).

The closing of Al-Anbiya inverts this. **Be certain about the message. Be honest about the timing. The first is your stewardship. The second is His domain.**

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It means the next time you do not know what God is doing in your life — *whether the delay is mercy or pressure, whether the closed door is protection or test* — you have permission, modeled by the Prophet himself, to say *lā adrī*. *I do not know.* Not as a failure of faith. As an act of it.

And then to do what the architecture of the passage commands you to do: **let your unknowing be the frame around your knowing of Him.** *I do not know what is happening in my life. He knows what I say openly and what I conceal. I do not know whether this period is a trial or a kindness — perhaps both. He knows.*

That is not weakness. That is the posture of every prophet in this surah, distilled.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Living Inside Both/And — When the Trial Wears the Clothes of the Gift

Here is the second movement, and it touches the deepest part of the human predicament.

Most of us, when we look at our lives, want a verdict. *Was this year good or bad? Was that relationship a blessing or a mistake? Was this stretch of comfort a gift from Allah or a slow drift into heedlessness?*

We want one label. We want to file the experience.

And the closing of Al-Anbiya is telling us to stop. *Perhaps it is a fitnah for you AND a mataʿ until a time.*

[PAUSE]

There is a moment that almost every reflective person eventually meets. You have been given some *good* — health, a partner, a job, peace at home, a stretch of relative ease. And you find yourself, late at night, wondering: *is this the good Allah promised, or is this the very thing pulling me away from Him?* You do not know whether to thank Him fully or to be wary fully.

The closing of Al-Anbiya answers: **both.** Thank Him fully *and* be wary fully. The two are not in competition. They are simultaneous postures toward the same gift.

The good of your life right now — the breath in your chest, the people who love you, the food on your table — *is* the *mataʿ ilā ḥīn*. The enjoyment until a time. And it *is* the *fitnah*. The fire in which what is real in you is being separated from what is dross.

You do not have to decide which. Allah does not ask you to decide. He asks you to *live awake* inside the both/and.

Here is what this looks like practically.

When something good arrives in your life, the dunya teaches you to ask, *did I deserve this, and how long can I keep it?* The Qur'an teaches you to ask, *what part of me is being tested through this, and Who gave it?*

When the delay stretches long and the promised reckoning seems far, the dunya teaches you to ask, *was anything ever really promised?* The Qur'an teaches you to say what the Prophet was told to say: *I do not know whether what is promised is near or far. He knows. And perhaps this entire indeterminate stretch is — at once — the trial and the gift.*

This reframes everything.

It means you do not need to know the verdict on this stretch of your life to walk through it well. You walk through every stretch the same way, because every stretch is the same thing: **mataʿ ilā ḥīn** — provision until a while — which is itself the *fitnah* in which your soul is weighed.

The single posture that fits both: **gratitude with vigilance. Enjoyment with a soft hand. Comfort with one eye on the horizon.** The believer does not refuse the *mataʿ*. The believer does not forget the *fitnah*. They sit, in the same hand, like two coins of the same weight.

[PAUSE]

And now look back at what closes the passage. **إِلَىٰ حِينٍ** — *until a time*. Indefinite. Unannounced. This is not a defect of the passage. *This is the passage.*

If you knew exactly how long the *mataʿ* would last, you could relax the *fitnah* until the last week. If you knew exactly when the *fitnah* would intensify, you could squeeze the *mataʿ* until the last day. **The indeterminacy is the test.** *Ḥīn* — a while — is what keeps the soul awake.

This is why the surah ends here. Not with a triumphal punctuation. Not with a date. With *a while.*

And the Prophet, the one most equipped to demand specificity from Allah, is the one commanded to say: *I do not know. Until a while.*

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It means the next time you find yourself trying to *file* a stretch of your life — to label it good or bad, gift or test, blessing or warning — you stop. You let it be both. You let the good in your life remain good while you also remember it is the medium of the test. You let the test in your life remain real while you also remember it is laced with provision and breath and small mercies.

You walk through *a while* — *ḥīn* — exactly as the Prophet was told to walk through it. **With the message clear. The timing unknown. The duration unspecified. And every gift held with two hands: one open in gratitude, one steady on the rope of vigilance.**

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

Look now at the architecture of these three ayahs.

```
وَإِنْ أَدْرِي     —  I do not know (timing)
  إِنَّهُ يَعْلَمُ  —  He knows (open speech)
  وَيَعْلَمُ      —  He knows (concealed)
وَإِنْ أَدْرِي     —  I do not know (meaning of delay)
```

This is a frame. The *human ignorance* surrounds, on either side, the *divine knowledge*. Two confessions of *I don't know* form the outer wall; two declarations of *He knows* form the inner courtyard.

This is the shape of faith itself. **You stand at the perimeter of your own ignorance. He sits in the center of His knowledge. Your job is to be honest about the wall. His glory is what fills the room.**

And notice — the Prophet's two *I don't knows* are about precisely the two things humans most want to know: **when, and why-the-delay.** Allah's two *He knows*, in between, are about the two registers of human speech: **what you say openly, and what you conceal.** Even the symmetry maps perfectly. The Prophet does not know the structure of *time*. Allah knows the structure of *every word*. The Prophet's domain ends at the edge of timing. Allah's domain reaches into the silent corner of every chest.

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two angles.

The linguistic angle: a passage architected as *human ignorance — divine knowledge — human ignorance*, with the keyword **آذَنتُكُمْ** front-loading the Prophet's responsibility (I have made you hear) and **عَلَىٰ سَوَاءٍ** flattening any informational hierarchy he might have claimed.

The thematic angle: a believer is not someone who knows everything. A believer is someone who is precise about what they have been given, honest about what they have not, and able to hold the gift and the trial in the same hand for an unspecified while.

It is the same teaching. **Walk faithfully through *ḥīn* — through *a while* — without demanding to know its end.**

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Do not answer them quickly.

1. *What in my life am I currently trying to assign one label to — gift or trial — when the closing of Al-Anbiya is telling me it is both?*

2. *Where am I pretending confidence about something I do not actually know? And where am I hiding in uncertainty about something I do?*

3. *If the Prophet of Allah was commanded to say "I do not know" twice in three ayahs, what am I doing pretending to know the divine reasoning behind every event in my life?*

4. *What would change if I lived this week as "ḥīn" — an unspecified while — rather than as a stretch of time I am owed an explanation about?*

5. *Right now, in this hour, what is the message I have been "made to hear" (آذَنتُكُمْ) that I have not yet acted on, while I wait for further information I was never promised?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The surah of the prophets ends not with prophetic certainty but with prophetic honesty: I have made you hear what I was given, I do not know what I was not given, and the delay you are inside is — at once — the trial and the gift, until a while only He knows.**

### The Closing Invitation

Something should shift after sitting with these three ayahs.

Not a new theology. Something quieter. **A relaxing of the grip on the verdict.** A willingness to let *this* season of your life — whatever it is — be a season of *ḥīn*. *A while.* Allah's while. In which you carry the message you have been made to hear, you say *lā adrī* about everything else, and you hold the *mataʿ* with one hand open in gratitude and one eye soft on the horizon of *fitnah*.

The Prophet ﷺ stood before mockers who demanded a date. He was told to give them, instead, the most powerful thing a human being can offer: **truthful speech about the limits of his own knowing.** And it was enough. It is still enough. It will always be enough.

### Du'a

*Yā Allah, You who know what is spoken openly and what is concealed — make us honest about what we do not know, and faithful about what You have made us hear. Let us walk through the unspecified while of our lives holding Your gifts with gratitude and Your tests with vigilance, neither pretending to know what You have kept, nor neglecting what You have given. And when the while ends, gather us with those who said* **lā adrī** *with humility and* **āmantu** *with everything else they had.* **Āmīn.**
