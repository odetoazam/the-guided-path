# Each in Its Own Orbit, Swimming: The Cosmos at Worship

## The Ayah

> [ayah:21:33] وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلَّيْلَ وَٱلنَّهَارَ وَٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ ۖ كُلٌّ فِى فَلَكٍ يَسْبَحُونَ
>
> *Wa huwa lladhī khalaqa-l-layla wa-n-nahāra wa-sh-shamsa wa-l-qamara, kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.*
>
> **And it is He who created the night and the day, and the sun and the moon — each in its own orbit, swimming.**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sky That Was Always There

Stop for a moment.

Step outside on a clear night — or just close your eyes and remember what that's like. The dark dome above you. The moon hanging there as though pinned. The stars scattered like spilled salt. The strange certainty that the sun will return in the morning — that it has never failed, not once, in all the days you've been alive, not once in all the days of every human who has ever lived.

You have grown up under this sky. So has every human in history. The sky is the **oldest companion of the human race.** Before there were nations, before there was language as we know it, before there was agriculture or fire or shelter — there was a person looking up.

And the question every human has ever asked, looking up, is some version of: *what is holding this in place?*

[PAUSE]

The Arabs of the Prophet's ﷺ time were among the most accomplished sky-readers in human history. Their poetry is full of stars. Their navigation depended on them. Their calendar was the moon. They could tell you the names of constellations the way you can tell the names of streets in your neighborhood. The sky was not a backdrop for them. It was a **document they had been reading for generations.**

And yet — they were missing what was on the page.

This is the surah of the prophets. Of every messenger Allah sent, every warning He gave, every reminder He delivered to a humanity that, the surah's opening declares, has drawn near its reckoning while still turning away in distraction. Right in the middle of arguments about idols and resurrection and accountability, Allah pauses the human conversation and says: **look up.** Look at the night. Look at the day. Look at the sun. Look at the moon.

And then He says one strange, astonishing thing about all four of them.

The strange thing is hidden in a single grammatical ending — an ending that, in proper Arabic, **shouldn't be there.** It's the kind of thing only someone fluent in Arabic would notice, and once they noticed, they'd never stop seeing it.

What if the sky — the sky you've taken for granted your whole life — is not silent?

What if it has been **swimming, and singing, and praying** above your head for as long as you've been alive, and you've been the only one in the room who couldn't hear it?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yasbaḥūn* — The Verb That Treats the Sky Like a Soul

Before we open the word, learn the rule.

Arabic grammar makes a distinction English does not. When you say "they" in English, it doesn't matter what "they" are — people, books, chairs, planets, all use the same word. But Arabic has a rule that classifies plurals into two categories:

**Rational plurals** — humans, angels, jinn. Beings with intellect, will, moral choice.

**Non-rational plurals** — animals, objects, abstractions, celestial bodies. Things that, in Arabic grammar, do not "decide" anything.

The grammar treats them differently. When a verb refers to a non-rational plural — like a group of stones, or trees, or stars — Arabic uses what's called the **feminine singular treatment**: the verb takes a singular feminine ending, as though the whole group is one collective "it."

Say the sun and moon are doing something. Strict Arabic grammar expects: *taṣbaḥu* — singular feminine. "It (the collective sky-thing) does."

This is not optional poetic flourish. This is **how Arabic works**, the way "is" and "are" work in English. A grammatically literate seventh-century Arab would expect non-rational verb agreement here as automatically as you expect "the books are on the table," not "the books is on the table."

Now open the word.

The verb Allah uses for what the night, the day, the sun, and the moon are doing is **يَسْبَحُونَ** — *yasbaḥūn.*

The ending **-ūn** is the masculine plural ending **reserved for rational beings.**

It is the ending you use for **men praying, scholars studying, prophets speaking, angels descending.** It is the ending of *minds at work, hearts in motion, wills choosing.*

Allah has placed that ending on the night, the day, the sun, and the moon.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this.

The grammatical move is not subtle. To the original Arab listener, it would have struck the ear immediately. It is as if you read in English: "The sun and the moon — *they who are swimming.*" Not "which" — *who.* The relative pronoun for persons. The grammar has just **promoted celestial bodies into the category of rational beings.**

Why?

Notice something: the surah is about prophets. About the long chain of messengers. About human beings whom Allah granted intellect and will and the capacity to either submit or refuse. The whole question of the surah is: **will the human use his rational soul to submit to his Lord, the way every prophet did, the way every prophet's people were called to?**

And in the middle of that question, Allah turns to the sky and uses a verb form that says: *the sun is already doing what I'm asking you to do.*

The cosmos is not a backdrop. It is **the senior worshipper in the room.** Older than you. Steadier than you. Already in posture. Already moving in its prayer. And Arabic grammar — by Allah's choice — treats it as a being-with-a-soul, because functionally, in its submission, it is.

Here's what's remarkable. There is a hadith that says: *the heavens groaned, and they had a right to groan — there is not a hand-span of space in them except that an angel is standing or bowing or prostrating.* And one classical reading of this ayah's grammar is that *yasbaḥūn* doesn't only refer to the celestial bodies themselves, but to **everything riding upon them** — the angels, the dwellers, the entire structure of orbit-and-traveler. All of it, swimming.

Either way, the move stands. Allah has used the **conscious-being verb form** for the sky.

What this invites us to consider: the rational/non-rational binary that we live by — *we* are aware, *that* is just matter — is, from Allah's perspective, **inverted by the actual data.** The sun has not missed its prayer in five billion years. You have not made it five days.

Who, exactly, is the rational one?

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Falak* — The Word That Means Both Orbit and Ship

Now look at the word for what each of them is swimming *in.*

**فَلَكٍ** — *falak.*

Most translations render this "orbit." That is correct, but it is half the word.

The root **ف-ل-ك** in classical Arabic carries one of those wonderful Quranic root-doublings. The same three letters give us two meanings the dictionary preserves side by side:

**Falak** — a sphere, a celestial track, the curved path along which a heavenly body moves.

**Fulk** — a ship. A vessel. The thing that carries you across the water.

These are not two unrelated homophones. The classical lexicographers note them as **the same root** carrying the same underlying image: *something that moves in a curved or rounded medium, gliding rather than walking.* A ship through water. A planet through space. Both are **bodies floating through a medium that yields to them.**

Now read the ayah again with this in your ear.

"Each in its own *falak* — swimming."

The image is not a planet drily tracing a geometric line. The image is **a ship — a celestial ship — sailing through a sea of space.** The verb is *swimming* (sabaḥa, literally what a swimmer does in water). The vehicle is, by root, a *vessel*. The medium is, by implication, an **ocean above your head.**

Stay with the picture for a moment.

Every night, when you look up, you are looking at the **harbor of an unimaginably vast ocean**, and the moon and the sun and the planets are not pinned objects. They are **boats**. Each one launched on its assigned voyage. Each one sailing — slowly, silently, never colliding, never veering, never tiring — through waters that the human eye cannot see.

[PAUSE]

And there's more. Notice that *falak* is **indefinite** — *fī falakin*, not *fī al-falaki*.

Not "in *the* orbit." In **an** orbit. **Each thing has its own.**

The sun is not in the moon's path. The moon is not in the sun's path. The night does not collide with the day. Every body has been given a *falak* of its own — its own private sea-lane, its own unique vessel, its own assigned voyage.

This is what Allah is showing you about the cosmos. It is not chaos. It is not even merely order. It is **personalised order.** Every traveler in the heavens has been given a path that belongs to no one else.

Now ask the question this is preparing you to ask: **what is your *falak*?**

What if every soul, too, has been given an orbit that belongs to no one else? What if your life is not the failed version of someone else's life — but a vessel launched on a voyage assigned only to you, in a sea-lane no one else was made for?

We will return to this. But notice already that the **physical image** — celestial ships in private orbits — is pointing at an **unseen reality** about how Allah deals with souls. He does not assign one orbit to all. He assigns *an* orbit to each.

### Element Three: The Hidden Bridge — *Sabaḥa* and *Tasbīḥ* Are the Same Word

Here is the seam where the whole ayah opens.

The verb *yasbaḥūn* — they swim — comes from the root **س-ب-ح**, *sabaḥa.*

There is another word in the Quran built from exactly the same three letters: **tasbīḥ.** *Subḥān Allāh.* The word every Muslim says dozens of times a day. The word that means **declaring Allah free from every imperfection, glorifying Him, exalting Him above all unworthy thought.**

Same root. Same letters. Same family.

In Arabic morphology, this is not a coincidence. It is a **revealed equivalence.** The lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in *al-Mufradāt* — both note the connection: *sabaḥa* literally means *to move freely through a medium without obstruction.* From this physical image, the same root extends to the spiritual act of **the heart moving freely through Allah's attributes**, finding Him utterly unobstructed, utterly free of every limitation, every imperfection, every fault we might project onto Him.

To swim and to glorify, in Arabic, are **the same motion.** One performed in water, the other in the heart.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what just happened in the ayah.

Allah says the sun, the moon, the night, the day are *yasbaḥūn* — and at the surface, that means *they are swimming through their orbits.* Physical motion. Astronomy.

But the same word, at a deeper register, is the word for **glorification.**

Which means the ayah is doing two things at once. The grammar says: *celestial bodies, swimming.* The root says: *celestial bodies, glorifying.*

And the rational-being verb ending says: **and they are doing it as conscious worshippers, not as inert matter.**

The sun's orbit *is* its tasbīḥ. Its motion *is* its prayer. There is no second thing the sun is doing besides its orbit, nor a second thing besides its glorification — because **those are the same thing.** To move in your assigned path is to glorify the One who assigned it.

This is what Allah elsewhere tells us plainly: *there is not a thing except that it glorifies Him with His praise, but you do not understand their glorification* (17:44). Here, in 21:33, He is letting you peek behind the curtain. The sun's tasbīḥ that you cannot hear — it sounds like **the silent crossing of an orbit.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

So here is what the language has shown you.

The verb form turns the sky into a chamber of conscious worshippers. The root turns the orbit into an act of glorification. The indefinite *falak* turns the cosmos into a multitude of personally-assigned vessels, each on its own voyage. And the word *sabaḥa* binds physical motion and spiritual praise into a single act.

The ayah is not describing astronomy. The ayah is showing you that **the sky has been at prayer your entire life**, and the prayer takes the form of the orbit, and every body has been given its own.

Now let's ask what this means not just for words — but for the soul that has been told to look up.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Loneliness of Being Off-Orbit

There is a particular ache that almost every modern person knows. It does not have a clean name. The closest we come is words like *anxiety, restlessness, comparison, FOMO, imposter syndrome, midlife crisis, quarter-life crisis.* Different labels for what is, underneath, **the same wound:**

*I am not where I am supposed to be. Other people are further along. Other people are doing better. I should be doing what they are doing. I am behind.*

You scroll the feed and watch other lives. You measure your career against people you went to school with. You measure your marriage, your kids, your faith, your body, your bank account against an invisible composite of everyone else's highlights. And the verdict is always, somehow, that you are short.

It is exhausting. It is **the most exhausting thing in the human soul.** Not the work itself. The constant comparison. The constant suspicion that you are running the wrong race.

[PAUSE]

Now look up.

The moon is not running the sun's race. It would not survive the attempt. Step the moon into the sun's orbit and the moon vaporises in seconds.

The earth is not running the moon's race. The night is not running the day's race. Every body in the heavens has a *falak* — and the *falak* is **indefinite, individual, assigned.**

If the sun left its orbit to chase the moon's, the cosmos would collapse.

What Allah is showing you in this ayah is not just astronomical fact. It is **the architecture of reality itself.** Reality is built on **assigned voyages.** Every traveler — celestial body, prophet, soul, atom, angel — has been given a path that belongs to no one else, and the entire harmony of creation depends on each one staying on the path it was given.

The wound of comparison is the wound of **trying to swim in someone else's orbit.**

It is impossible. Not difficult — *impossible.* The orbit was not made for you. The vessel was not launched for you. The voyage was not assigned to you. You can exhaust yourself trying. You can fake it. You can mimic the gestures. But you will never be **on it**, because *it is not yours.*

[PAUSE]

What the ayah is offering — quietly, behind the cosmology — is permission. Permission to stop. Permission to ask the harder question: *what is the falak Allah assigned to me?*

Notice: it does not promise the answer is glamorous. The moon's orbit is not the sun's orbit. The moon is small. The moon borrows light. The moon waxes and wanes and disappears for nights at a time. The moon's voyage is not the sun's voyage, and yet — *kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn.* **Each. In its own.** The moon's orbit is no less an act of glorification than the sun's. The night is no less a worshipper than the day.

In Allah's economy, **the small orbit and the great orbit are equal in dignity** because each is doing exactly what it was made for.

The question is not *how do I get a bigger orbit.* The question is: **am I on mine?**

And here is the practical edge. Tomorrow morning, when you wake up, the test is not whether your life looks like someone else's. The test is whether **you are doing the next thing Allah has put in front of you to do.** The prayer that is in front of you. The parent you are responsible for. The work you have been given. The dhikr you have been assigned. The self you have been told to refine.

The sun does not check what the moon is up to. The sun does its orbit.

Do yours.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Cosmos Is Already Praying — Will You Join?

There is a second wound the ayah addresses, and it is older and quieter than the first.

It is the suspicion of **cosmic loneliness.**

It is the modern person's haunting feeling that the universe is impersonal. That the stars are dead matter. That the sky is a ceiling, not a witness. That space is empty rather than full. That when you pray at night, you are speaking into a vacuum, while above you spin objects that have no opinion of you, no awareness, no participation in your life.

This is the picture modernity has handed you. It is **a closed sky.** The sky as backdrop, not chamber. The sky as machine, not assembly.

Now read the ayah one more time.

*Each in its own orbit — they who swim.*

The verb form is rational. The root is glorification. The indefinite *falak* is personalised assignment. The grammatical move is not poetic decoration — it is Allah, in a single ending, **kicking down the wall of the closed sky.**

The sky is not a ceiling. The sky is **a congregation.** It has been at prayer your whole life. The sun's orbit is its sajdah. The moon's wax and wane is its dhikr. The night and the day are alternating hands on the prayer beads of creation. And every one of them has been assigned its place by the same Lord who is asking you, today, to take your place.

[PAUSE]

This changes the texture of your prayer.

When you stand for ṣalāh tonight, you are not breaking the silence of an indifferent universe. You are **joining a service already in progress.** The sun has been in posture for billions of years. The moon has not skipped a single prayer. The stars are full of angels. The mountains, says another ayah, *fall down in prostration* (16:48). The trees and the wild beasts, says another, *prostrate to Him* (22:18).

You are not initiating worship when you pray. You are **catching up.**

And when you fail to pray, you are not creating a small absence in an empty room. You are **the only chair empty in a hall of worshippers** that stretches from horizon to horizon, from atom to galaxy, from the cell in your fingertip to the farthest star. The cosmos is in posture. You alone are not.

This is meant to dignify your prayer, not to shame you. The dignity of joining. The relief of belonging. The astonishing privilege that the same Allah who set the sun on its orbit has invited *you* — a creature so small you can be killed by a microbe — to **stand in line with the sun.** Same Lord. Same direction. Same act of submission.

When you say *Subḥān Allāh* in rukūʿ, you are saying — in the same root — what the sun is *doing* in its orbit. You and the sun, in that moment, are **doing the same word.**

[PAUSE]

The practical edge here is not complicated, but it is heavy.

Take the prayer seriously. Not because you owe Allah a tax, but because **you have been given a seat at a service the cosmos has been holding for you.** The athān is not a bell. It is the **call to fall in line with the orbits.** When you delay, you are not delaying a personal devotion — you are **declining an invitation to the only assembly that matters.**

And one more thing. The next time you feel utterly alone — as everyone does, sometimes — remember the ayah's grammar. The ending *-ūn* on *yasbaḥūn* says: *they are conscious. They are with you. They are praying with you.* The night that holds you is awake. The stars above your head are not strangers. The sun that will rise in the morning has not stopped its tasbīḥ all night long, even while you slept.

You have never been alone. You have only been **looking down.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Watch how the ayah's pieces converge into one insight from two directions.

The **grammar** says: the celestial bodies are rational worshippers.
The **vocabulary** says: their orbits are their glorification.
The **structure** says: each has its own assigned path.

Linguistically, you have learned that motion-in-its-orbit *is* praise. Thematically, you have learned that the cosmos is already at prayer, and that your own anxious comparison-running is the symptom of trying to swim outside the path Allah assigned you.

These are the same insight, approached from two sides:

**Reality is a single act of submission, and every creature has been given a unique way to participate in it.**

The sun submits by burning in its orbit. The moon by reflecting in its orbit. The night by descending in its orbit. The day by rising in its orbit. The angels by standing in their orbit. The prophets by delivering their message in their orbit. And **you** — you, by doing the small, specific, unglamorous next thing on the path that has been assigned to your soul, and to no one else's.

The ayah is not really about astronomy. It is about **finding your *falak* and swimming in it.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the orbit I have been assigned — the work, the relationship, the responsibility — that no one else can do for me?
2. Where in my life am I exhausting myself trying to swim in someone else's *falak*?
3. If the sun's orbit is its tasbīḥ, what would it mean for me to treat my daily duties as my tasbīḥ — not interruptions to worship, but acts of it?
4. When I look at the night sky, what would change if I genuinely believed the heavens above me were a congregation, not a backdrop?
5. What is the first prayer I have been delaying — and what would it cost the harmony of my own small orbit to delay it one more day?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**The cosmos is a chamber of conscious worshippers, each on a path assigned to no one else, and your only task is to find yours and swim it.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, step outside or to a window and look up.

Don't try to feel anything. Don't perform reverence. Just **let the sky be what Allah said it is** — not a ceiling, but a congregation. The night holding you. The moon, if it is out, swimming. The stars, by His word, glorifying.

Then ask yourself the question the ayah has been asking the whole time: **am I on my orbit, or am I trying to live someone else's?**

If the answer is the second, the ayah is not scolding. It is **calling you home.** Your *falak* is not lost. It has been there since the day you were born. Allah assigned it to you and to no one else. You can step back into it tonight.

The sun did not need a thousand-year plan to begin its orbit. It only needed to begin.

So do you.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who created the night and the day and the sun and the moon, each on its assigned orbit — show me my orbit. Free me from the exhaustion of trying to swim in waters that were never made for me. Let my motion become my tasbīḥ, my next breath become my praise, and let me, at the end of my voyage, be found among those You called to swim.*

*Āmīn.*
