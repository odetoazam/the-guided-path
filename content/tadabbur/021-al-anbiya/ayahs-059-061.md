# The Three Voices and the Public Trial

## When Idols Need Defending

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to this passage with your eyes closed for a moment.

> قَالُوا۟ مَن فَعَلَ هَـٰذَا بِـَٔالِهَتِنَآ إِنَّهُۥ لَمِنَ ٱلظَّـٰلِمِينَ
>
> *Qālū man faʿala hādhā bi-ālihatinā innahu la-min aẓ-ẓālimīn.*
>
> "They said: Who has done this to our gods? Indeed, he is of the wrongdoers."

> قَالُوا۟ سَمِعْنَا فَتًۭى يَذْكُرُهُمْ يُقَالُ لَهُۥٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمُ
>
> *Qālū samiʿnā fatan yadhkuruhum yuqālu lahu Ibrāhīm.*
>
> "They said: We heard a youth mention them — he is called Ibrahim."

> قَالُوا۟ فَأْتُوا۟ بِهِۦ عَلَىٰٓ أَعْيُنِ ٱلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَشْهَدُونَ
>
> *Qālū fa-ʾtū bihi ʿalā aʿyuni n-nāsi laʿallahum yashhadūn.*
>
> "They said: Then bring him before the eyes of the people, that they may witness."

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

Picture the city. It is the morning after a festival. Yesterday the streets were full — drums, processions, the smell of roasted meat, the noise of celebration leaving the city behind as everyone walked out together to the festival ground. The temple was empty. The idols sat in their alcoves with no one to tend them.

Classical narrations record that as the procession departed, they passed by Ibrahim and called to him to join the celebration. He declined and stayed behind. They continued without him. Then, once their backs were turned — once the city was emptied of its witnesses — he entered the temple.

Now the procession returns. Tired. Dusty. Carrying the residue of joy. They climb the temple steps to thank their gods for the harvest, the rain, the year — and they freeze.

Stone faces lie shattered on the floor. Limbs separated from torsos. Heads cracked open. The smaller idols are pulverized into chunks. Only the largest god — the one in the center, the one they named most often — stands untouched. And on its shoulder rests an axe.

[PAUSE]

What follows in these three ayahs is not a courtroom transcript. It is something more. It is the **complete diagnostic of a society's relationship with what it worships**, captured in three sentences and three repetitions of the word *qālū* — *they said*.

Three voices. Three sentences. Three failures.

And nestled inside them, hidden in plain sight: a verb whose double meaning is so quietly devastating that I want you to be ready for it before you hear it. A pronoun whose smallness contains the entire grammar of false worship. A request for witnesses that will boomerang back and convict the very people making it.

Stay with me. The text is doing something extraordinary, and we will only see it if we slow down enough to let it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will look at three things — one from each ayah. Each will deepen what came before. By the end you will see why these three sentences had to be ordered exactly this way.

---

### Element One: ٱلِهَتِنَا — *Our Gods*

Let's begin with two letters at the end of a word.

In Arabic, when you want to say "my book," you don't use a separate word for "my." You attach a tiny suffix — a *yāʾ* — to the end of the word: *kitāb-ī*. "Our book"? Attach *-nā*: *kitāb-unā*. "Their book"? *Kitāb-uhum*. The possessor doesn't get its own word. It gets *attached*, fused, glued onto the thing possessed.

This is grammar. Now let's notice what it does.

When you say *kitāb-ī*, you are saying: this book belongs to me. I picked it up. I own it. It exists within my reach. I am the larger thing; the book is the smaller thing.

Hold that.

Now look at how the people of Ibrahim describe what was attacked:

> بِـَٔالِهَتِنَآ — *bi-ālihati-nā*
>
> "to our gods"

The word *ālihah* is the plural of *ilāh* — the very word at the heart of the *kalimah*: *lā ilāha illā Allāh* — there is no *ilāh* except Allah. *Ilāh* is the most charged word in the Arabic religious vocabulary. It means "the one who is worshipped." It means "the one whose nature commands devotion."

And these people have stuck a *-nā* on the end of it.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that.

*Our* gods. Gods that belong to us.

Notice something: in genuine worship, the relationship runs the other way. *I belong to my Lord*. The slave belongs to the Master. The created belongs to the Creator. The smaller thing is the worshipper; the larger thing is the worshipped.

But these people have inverted it at the level of grammar itself. They are the larger thing. The gods are the smaller thing — small enough to be possessed, small enough to fit inside a possessive suffix.

Here's what's remarkable: the rest of the sentence proves they meant exactly what the grammar says. *Who did this to OUR gods?* Their first instinct is not "the gods will defend themselves." Their first instinct is not "the gods are angry now and we should fear them." Their first instinct is **outrage on behalf of the gods**, as if the gods were *their dependents*. As if the gods were children who could not speak for themselves and the parents had to take up the cause.

Al-Muyassar catches the invisible premise running beneath this outrage: these are gods that "deserve veneration and reverence." The word the commentator uses — from the root meaning *to be entitled to* — reveals that what is dressed as shock is actually a theological claim dressed as common sense. They are not arguing that these objects should be worshipped. They are *assuming* it. The assumption is so total that the word "deserve" does not need to be examined; it is simply the air in the room.

And that presumed entitlement is precisely what Ibrahim is about to place on trial.

This is the diagnostic that will run through the entire passage and that Ibrahim will exploit in two ayahs' time. **A god you have to defend is a god you have already failed**. Because the moment you have to defend it, you are confessing — in the smallest possible suffix at the end of one word — that you are larger than it.

The *-nā* is one syllable long. It contains the entire collapse of false worship.

[PAUSE — long pause]

### Element Two: فَتًۭى يَذْكُرُهُمْ — *A Youth Who Mentions Them*

The second voice speaks. Someone in the crowd has a tip.

> سَمِعْنَا فَتًۭى يَذْكُرُهُمْ يُقَالُ لَهُۥٓ إِبْرَٰهِيمُ
>
> "We heard a youth who *mentions* them — he is called Ibrahim."

Two words deserve our attention. Let's take them in order.

**فَتًى — *fatan* — youth.**

In Arabic, *fatā* means a young man — usually somewhere between adolescence and full maturity, somewhere in the range we might call "twenties." But it carries more than chronology. The word also carries an undertone of *unestablished*, *not yet of consequence*, *junior*. When the Quran calls Yusuf *al-fatā* in Egypt, part of what is being communicated is his social precarity — he is the young one, the underling, the one without standing.

Notice what they do *not* say. They do not say *rajul* — "a man." They do not say *shaykh* — "an elder." They say *fatan* — "a youth." Indefinite. Anonymous. Lowered in status.

This is the language of dismissal. It is the sociological filter their culture has placed over Ibrahim. *He is not someone whose words have weight. He is a young man. The kind of young man who says provocative things at gatherings.*

But here is where Allah's irony begins. The Quran will use *fatā* for Yusuf in the prison, for the companions of the cave, for Yushaʿ ibn Nūn walking with Mūsā. **In the Quran's vocabulary, the *fatā* is the one in whom God places weight that the surrounding society has refused to acknowledge.** The label that society uses to dismiss a person becomes, in the Quran's hand, the label that marks them out for revelation.

There is also something important to notice about *when* these people had heard him. Al-Tabari's account clarifies that the informants who said *samiʿnā* — "we heard" — were not bystanders who happened to know Ibrahim's general reputation. They were specifically those who had heard him swear his declaration before the festival departed: *"By Allah, I will surely plot against your idols after you have turned your backs."* They had been present. They had heard the oath. When they say *we heard a youth*, they mean a very precise prior hearing — not community gossip, but testimony to a specific sworn intention. The identification of Ibrahim is not a discovery made in the morning light. It is the deployment of evidence that has been waiting.

Which means the three-voice sequence is more choreographed than it first appears. The first voice asks who did it, though they already suspect. The second produces the witnesses who confirm it with prior testimony. The third arranges the spectacle. This is not an investigation. It is a performance with a verdict already written.

[PAUSE]

Now the second word. And this one — this one is doing extraordinary work.

**يَذْكُرُهُمْ — *yadhkuruhum* — "he mentions them."**

The root is *dh-k-r*. *Dhikr*. Remembrance. Mention.

This is the same root, the same verb, used hundreds of times in the Quran for what believers do toward Allah: *idhkurū-llāha dhikran kathīrā* — "remember Allah with much remembrance." *Alladhīna yadhkurūna-llāha qiyāman wa quʿūdan* — "those who remember Allah standing and sitting." The verb of devotion. The verb of the worshipper holding the name of his Lord on his tongue.

And here, in the mouth of these people, the same verb is being used to describe what Ibrahim does to *the idols*.

> *Yadhkuruhum* — "he mentions them."

[PAUSE]

Notice the trap their language has fallen into. They are reporting Ibrahim as a threat. They want to say: *he speaks ill of them, he insults them, he mocks them*. But the verb they reach for is *yadhkuru* — the verb of *worshipful mention*.

The classical commentators were not blind to this doubling, and they resolved it with precision. Al-Tabari records the explicit gloss of Ibn Jurayj: *yadhkuruhum* here means *yaʿībuhum* — "he criticizes them, he finds fault with them." Ibn Isḥāq's account in the same source is still more vivid: he reports that what the witnesses actually said was that they had heard Ibrahim *curse them, criticize them, and mock them* — three escalating registers of attack. Al-Muyassar reduces it to a clean summary: Ibrahim "mentions the idols with ill." So the disparagement reading is not a modern inference from context; it is the reading the classical tradition settled on, with multiple lines of transmission converging on the same conclusion.

But notice what that conclusion still leaves intact. Even in the disparagement register — even as *yaʿīb*, even as curse-and-mock — the verb remains *yadhkuru*. It is still the verb of *keeping something present in speech*. Both registers are operative simultaneously, and together they form a composite portrait: Ibrahim is a young man who refuses to let these objects fade into the silent background of habit. He keeps naming them. He keeps making them an issue. He will not let the city pretend they are normal. Whether he is speaking about them as objects of criticism or objects of theological seriousness matters less than this: **he is speaking about them at all**.

What this invites us to consider: there are two ways false gods continue to rule. One is to be openly worshipped. The other — and this is the more common one in our age — is to be *quietly assumed*. Never named. Never examined. Just there, shaping the space. The *fatā* who *yadhkuruhum* is the figure who refuses the second mode. He insists on naming what others have agreed not to name.

That is also why their society found him so disturbing. Not that he attacked the idols — many people have probably attacked them privately. But that he *kept mentioning them*. He would not let them retreat into the comfortable invisibility of the assumed.

[PAUSE — long pause]

### Element Three: لَعَلَّهُمْ يَشْهَدُونَ — *That They May Witness*

The third voice issues the verdict before the trial.

> فَأْتُوا۟ بِهِۦ عَلَىٰٓ أَعْيُنِ ٱلنَّاسِ لَعَلَّهُمْ يَشْهَدُونَ
>
> "Then bring him before the eyes of the people, that they may witness."

Two phrases. Both doing more than they appear to.

**عَلَىٰٓ أَعْيُنِ ٱلنَّاسِ — "*upon the eyes of the people*."**

Arabic has more economical ways to say "in public." *Amām an-nās* would do it. *Bayna an-nās* would do it. The Quran reaches instead for an image: *ʿalā aʿyun an-nās* — "*upon the eyes* of the people." Idiomatic, yes. But the idiom carries a texture: the people's eyes will be *the platform*, the surface on which Ibrahim is placed. He will be set *up*. Elevated *into visibility*. Put where every gaze in the city converges.

This is the language of spectacle. They are not arranging a trial; they are arranging a show. They want a public ritual of condemnation. The eyes of the people are the stage; Ibrahim is the exhibit; the verdict is already written.

[PAUSE]

**لَعَلَّهُمْ يَشْهَدُونَ — "*that they may witness.*"**

Now we reach the heart of the irony, and we need to be careful here, because the verb is doing something the people who said it did not understand.

The root is *sh-h-d*. From this root we get:

- *Shahida* — to witness, to see with one's own eyes
- *Shahāda* — testimony, the formal declaration of what one has seen
- *Shahīd* — the one who bears witness, including the one who bears witness with their life
- And — the *shahāda* itself: *ash-hadu an lā ilāha illā-llāh* — "I bear witness that there is no god but Allah."

[PAUSE]

The same verb. The verb of *the testimony of monotheism*.

These people are saying: bring him out, *that they may witness* — witness *against him*. They want the people to be the legal witnesses to a conviction. *Shahāda* in its courtroom sense.

But Allah has placed this verb here on purpose. Because what is about to happen on that public stage is exactly the opposite of what they planned.

When Ibrahim is brought out, he will ask the gods to speak. They will not speak. He will ask them to defend themselves. They will not defend themselves. He will say — *as the very next ayahs record* — *"ask them, if they can speak."* And in front of the eyes of the people, in the silence of the gods, the verb *shahida* will activate in its other, deeper meaning. **The people will witness. But what they will witness is not Ibrahim's guilt. They will witness the muteness of their own gods.**

This is one of the most devastating uses of dramatic irony in the Quran. The third voice in this passage thinks it is summoning a court. It is actually summoning a moment of *shahāda* in the most theological sense of the word — a public, eye-witnessed, undeniable testimony that the worshipped objects of this city *cannot speak, cannot defend themselves, and cannot be God*.

They wanted witnesses. Allah will give them witnesses. But the testimony will not be the one they wrote.

What this invites us to consider: when falsehood organizes a public stage to convict truth, **the stage itself becomes the instrument of falsehood's exposure**. Because truth does not need to argue. It only needs to be *witnessed in proximity to silence*. And the larger the audience, the more decisive the silence becomes.

[PAUSE — long pause]

---

### Bridge

Three voices have spoken. We have looked at three things:

A possessive suffix that revealed the inverted relationship — *gods* that have been reduced to *ours*.

A verb of remembrance trapped in the wrong mouth — *yadhkuruhum* — exposing that even the attacker treats their gods more seriously than they do.

A request for public witness that will become the public unraveling — *yashhadūn* — the same root as the testimony of monotheism, and Allah is going to use it to make them testify against themselves.

Each one was a small linguistic detail. Together they show us something larger.

Now let's ask what it is.

---

## PART 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF SELF-INDICTMENT

The thing these three voices are building — without knowing it — is not a case against Ibrahim. It is a case against themselves.

Look at what the first voice does. They call Ibrahim *min aẓ-ẓālimīn* — "one of the wrongdoers." The word *ẓulm* in the Quran is not mild. It describes the gravest forms of transgression — placing something in a position it does not belong, denying what is owed to what is owed it. And its deepest application in the Quran is precise: *inna-sh-shirka la-ẓulmun ʿaẓīm* — "indeed, associating partners with Allah is a tremendous wrongdoing." That word. That very word. The one they have aimed at Ibrahim.

[PAUSE]

They have used the Quran's own word for idolatry to condemn the man who is trying to end it.

This is not a coincidence the Quran is unaware of. The text has placed *ẓālimīn* here because the reader is meant to hear both registers simultaneously: the speakers' meaning (*he has wronged our gods*) and the Quran's meaning (*he is doing nothing wrong, and you are the ones doing what this word actually describes*). The charge they level at Ibrahim is the name of their own condition.

The second voice compounds this. We have already seen how the witnesses in *samiʿnā* were those who had heard Ibrahim's prior oath. What that means is that the identification of Ibrahim is not a discovery made in the morning light — it is the deployment of evidence that has been waiting. They knew. They heard the oath before the festival. They left anyway. Perhaps they thought he was speaking wildly. Perhaps they thought he would not dare.

He dared.

And now the *fatā* they dismissed as "not yet of consequence" has forced the entire city to stop, turn around, and account for what they believe. The person they had assigned the lowest social weight has produced the highest possible disruption. Every step of the dismissal — *he's just a youth, he's just talking, it doesn't mean anything* — was wrong. The *fatā* was taken seriously by the only judge who matters.

The third voice has its own architecture. *Bring him before the eyes of the people, that they may witness.* This is the sentence of a system that has never had to defend itself before. These idols have never been questioned publicly. No one has stood before them in a city square and asked, *can you speak? can you show us you are what we say you are?* The very fact that they have to ask this question — that the stage has to be constructed, the crowd assembled, the trial arranged — is already an admission of something they are too invested to see.

A god that does not need defending does not need a trial.

The God of Ibrahim — the One who kept him safe through the oath and through the temple and through the arrest and through the fire that comes two ayahs later — did not arrange a court date. He was not placed *ʿalā aʿyun an-nās* by people who wanted him convicted. He was not in need of a spectacle. The God who *is* needs no defense. The gods that *are not* need all the defense they can get, and still come up short.

[PAUSE — long pause]

There is something else in this architecture worth sitting with.

The three voices — *qālū*, *qālū*, *qālū* — are notably disembodied. We do not know who says what. Are these three different groups of people? Three moments in the same conversation? Three stages in the same crowd's movement through shock to identification to verdict? The Quran does not say.

What this anonymity does is generalize the voices. These are not three named individuals making three named mistakes. These are *patterns*. The first voice is the pattern of the society that reaches first for outrage. The second is the pattern of the informant who supplies the name. The third is the pattern of the institution that converts private outrage into public spectacle. All three patterns are still alive. All three will reassemble anywhere that a *fatā* refuses to let the assumed remain unexamined.

You will recognize them not just in history but in rooms you have sat in.

[PAUSE]

## PART 3: WHAT THIS ASKS OF US

This passage is not primarily about Ibrahim. It is about what it feels like to live in a world where the *ālihah* have been given the *-nā* suffix — made *ours*, made domestic, made possessable.

We do not worship stone idols. But the grammar of *ālihatiinā* is everywhere.

*Our* certainty. *Our* tribe. *Our* way of reading the world. *Our* comfort. *Our* reputation. The moment we describe something with that suffix — when we feel that same outrage the first voice felt, when we rush to defend it, when we find ourselves asking *who did this to what is ours* — we should pause. We should ask: has the relationship inverted? Am I the larger thing? Is what I am protecting *mine*, or am I *its*?

The diagnostic is simple and devastating: if it needs you to defend it, ask yourself whether it was ever actually larger than you.

[PAUSE]

Then there is the question of *yadhkuruhum* — of naming.

Ibrahim was not a political activist. He was not primarily a social reformer. He was a man who refused to participate in the communal agreement not to name certain things. The idols were there in everyone's sight. Everyone who passed through the temple knew them. Everyone who made offerings knew exactly what they were doing, and knew somewhere that what they were doing was strange. But there was a social contract: we do not mention this. We do not *yadhkuru* this. We let it fade into the assumed.

Ibrahim refused.

And the thing about refusing to participate in the silence is that it is almost never comfortable, almost never socially rewarded, and almost always — in the Quran's account — eventually vindicated. Not quickly. Not without cost. But vindicated.

The prophetic pattern is not primarily dramatic action. Before the night in the temple, there were years of *dhikr* — years of Ibrahim keeping the question in the air, mentioning what others had agreed not to mention, treating as theologically serious what others had made socially invisible. The oath the witnesses heard before the festival was the verbal culmination of a long practice of naming. What appeared to them as sudden radicalism was the surface of something much older.

What has your community agreed not to name? What has your own heart agreed not to examine? What lives quietly in the *ālihat-nā* of your daily assumptions, never looked at directly, defended fiercely if anyone approaches it?

*Yadhkuruhum.* Mention them. Even if only to yourself. Even only in prayer. The first act of tawhid is not building an argument; it is being honest about what you are actually worshipping.

[PAUSE — long pause]

And then — *yashhadūn*.

They wanted witnesses. They wanted the crowd assembled so that the verdict could be ratified publicly, so that the social weight of the many would crush the theological seriousness of the one. They wanted performative testimony — *ash-hadu* on command, directed at a conclusion already written.

The divine irony is that genuine testimony cannot be commanded. The *shahāda* is valid only when it comes from *shahida* — from having actually witnessed. When you bring truth into a public space to condemn it, you cannot control what the public sees. The crowd they assembled was meant to see Ibrahim's guilt. What they saw — what they could not unsee, once they had seen it — was the silence of the god in the center, the one that could not speak.

There is a question in this for us about our own *shahāda*. When we say *ash-hadu an lā ilāha illā-llāh*, what have we actually witnessed? Is our testimony the result of having seen something — of having looked at the world and found it unable to be its own source — or is it the result of having been assembled in a crowd where that declaration was expected?

The people in the temple that day were assembled to testify against Ibrahim. But some of them walked away having witnessed something else entirely. That is the risk — and the grace — of arranging a public confrontation between truth and silence: you cannot guarantee which one the witnesses will remember.

[PAUSE]

---

These three ayahs are twenty-six words. They carry three repetitions of a single verb — *qālū* — three brief speeches, and three failures that mirror each other in perfect symmetry. A civilization has reduced its gods to possessions. It has dismissed the one who named this. And it has arranged a public spectacle to confirm what it already believed, not understanding that the spectacle will produce the opposite of its intended testimony.

The Quran records it in twenty-six words because twenty-six words are enough. Everything else — the morning light, the broken stone, the crowd assembling — is context. The theology is in the suffix, the verb, and the irony. The *-nā*. The *yadhkuruhum*. The *yashhadūn*.

Three small things, in different forms, that contain the entire grammar of false worship, the entire prophetic posture of naming it, and the entire reversal that awaits when truth is placed in the center of its own supposed condemnation.

---

*O Allah — show us the possessive suffixes we have placed on what is not ours to possess. Return us to the grammar of servitude: that we belong to You, not You to us.*

*Give us the courage of the fatā who keeps naming what others have agreed not to name — not from arrogance, but from the refusal to treat the false as the obvious.*

*And when we say ash-hadu — let it be real. Let it come from having witnessed. Let it be the testimony of one who has looked at everything that is not You and found it unable to speak.*

*Āmīn.*
