# When? — And the Sentence Allah Refuses to Finish

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to the three ayahs once more, slowly:

> **"And they say, 'When will this promise come — if you are truthful?'**
>
> **If only those who disbelieved knew the moment when they will not be able to ward off the Fire from their faces, nor from their backs — and they will not be helped...**
>
> **Rather, it will come upon them suddenly and leave them stunned silent — they will not be able to repel it, and they will not be granted any reprieve."**

— *Surah Al-Anbiya, ayahs 38-40*

### Where We Are in the Surah

Al-Anbiya has been a long, patient courtroom. The opening ayahs declared that humanity's reckoning has drawn near — *iqtaraba li-n-nāsi ḥisābuhum* — while they remain heedless and turning away. Then came the mockery of the Prophet ﷺ as just a man. Then came the dismissal of resurrection: *"You will be raised? Our fathers turned to dust — and we?"*

By ayah 36, the surah has answered every one of these objections. But there is one more thing the mockers do. They ask a question that *sounds* like a question. It isn't.

They say: **"When? When is this promise? Tell us. If you're truthful, give us a date."**

The classical commentators are unsparing about what this act actually is. Ibn Kathir calls it *istiʿjāl* — a demanding that the punishment be hastened — arising not from genuine inquiry but from "denial, rejection, disbelief, stubbornness, and a conviction that it will never happen." Note that last phrase. They were not strategically calling a bluff they expected to be called. They genuinely believed nothing was coming. Al-Tabari names them *mustaʿjilūn* — those who clamor for the hastening of Allah's judgment upon themselves — and observes that the phrase *in kuntum ṣādiqīn*, "if you are truthful," was directed at both the Prophet ﷺ and the community of believers together: a collective taunt, not a private inquiry.

What the classical analysis establishes, then, is something darker than cunning. It is sincere, settled unbelief wearing the costume of a question. And within that settled unbelief, something else is also at work. The question doubles as a delay.

There is a familiar shape to this move. Demand a specificity you know is beyond the reach of any single human to provide. When the specificity is withheld, treat the withholding as confirmation that the warning was empty all along. The question sounds like rigor. What it is, is armor.

### The World of the Original Listener

Imagine yourself in Makkah, the seventh century. You are a young man, perhaps twenty-five. You have heard your uncle, your father, the elders of Quraysh — men whose authority structures the whole world you know — laughing at the Prophet ﷺ in the marketplaces. *"He says we'll be raised. He says fire is coming. Where? When? Show us."* The crowd laughs.

And you have also heard, alone at night, this man recite. And something in his recitation has wedged itself under your ribs and will not leave.

The next morning he recites these three ayahs. And suddenly the mockery you have been surrounded by is dragged into the light and X-rayed. Allah does not raise His voice. He does not threaten. He does something more devastating.

**He starts a sentence — and refuses to finish it.**

### The Hook

What you are about to see is a grammatically and rhetorically remarkable response. Allah is given the chance to answer "when?" — and instead of answering it, He uses the grammar itself to expose what the question reveals about the questioner.

Stay with this. Because the move Allah makes here is not just about the deniers of Makkah. It is about every part of every soul that uses the word *when* to keep the answer at arm's length.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will sit with two features of these ayahs. The first is a sentence that has no ending. The second is a single word — *baghtatan* — that may be one of the most psychologically precise words in the Arabic language.

### Element One: The Sentence Allah Refuses to Finish

Before we touch the ayah, let me show you how this works in your own daily speech.

#### Teaching the Concept First

Imagine a parent who walks into a room and finds their child doing something the child knows is forbidden. The child looks up, terrified.

The parent says: *"If you only knew what your father is going to do when he gets home..."*

— and walks out.

Notice what just happened. The sentence had a structure: *"If you only knew X..."* — and the *therefore* never came. The "you would do this," "you would feel that" — gone. Suppressed.

And the child sits there imagining. The child's imagination is now doing all the work the words would have done. And the imagination is more terrifying than any words the parent could have said. **Because anything specific is bounded. Anything unsaid is unbounded.**

In Arabic rhetoric this device has a name. The grammarians call it *ḥadhf jawāb law* — the suppression of the apodosis of "if." When you say *law* in Arabic, you are setting up an *if-then*. The "then" is required by grammar. Listeners *expect* it. When it is removed, every Arabic ear feels its absence.

That absence is not a gap. It is a gravitational well. It pulls the listener's mind in.

#### Now Open the Ayah

Look at ayah 39:

> **لَوْ يَعْلَمُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ حِينَ لَا يَكُفُّونَ عَن وُجُوهِهِمُ ٱلنَّارَ وَلَا عَن ظُهُورِهِمْ وَلَا هُمْ يُنصَرُونَ**
>
> *"If those who disbelieved only knew the moment when they will not be able to ward off the Fire from their faces, nor from their backs — and they will not be helped..."*

Let's sit with this.

The grammar is *law yaʿlamu* — "if they knew." The Arabic ear is now braced for a *la-* — for the "then." *Law yaʿlamu... la-* — *they would do this, they would not have done that, they would have feared, they would have wept, they would have run.*

**Allah gives no "then."**

The sentence runs out into silence.

The mockers asked, "When?" Allah begins to describe the *when*. He shows them the moment: faces caught in fire, backs caught in fire, and no helper. And then... nothing. No therefore. No consequence. No closing.

Al-Tabari, pressing toward what the implied apodosis might contain, offers the closest classical completion: *they would not have persisted in what they persist in of disbelief in Allah; they would have hastened to repentance and to faith in Allah; and they would not have demanded the hastening of affliction upon themselves.* It is an honest attempt. It is the best human language can do. And yet — even this feels smaller than the silence. *They would have believed. They would have repented.* True. But neither phrase carries the total weight of what Allah is letting hang in the air.

**The *therefore* is left out because no human language can carry it.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. The mockers came demanding a specific *when*. And Allah's answer is structured around *exactly* the device of withheld specificity. They wanted a date. Allah gives them an unfinished sentence. They wanted Him to fill in a blank. Allah leaves a blank.

The form of the answer is the answer. *You demanded I tell you when. Watch what happens to language itself when it tries to describe the when. It breaks.*

#### One More Layer — The Asymmetry of Faces and Backs

Stay inside ayah 39 a moment longer. Allah does not just say "fire surrounds them." He specifies two directions: ***wujūhihim*** — their faces — and ***ẓuhūrihim*** — their backs.

Why these two?

The face is the direction of forward motion. The face is what you turn *toward* — toward what you want, what you choose, what you pursue. The back is what you turn *away from* — what you reject, what you refuse to look at, what you've left behind.

The mockery in this surah took both forms. Their faces turned toward laughter, idolatry, what they thought was glory. Their backs turned away from the Prophet ﷺ, away from the recitation, away from the warning.

Al-Tabari, in his tafsir of this ayah, reaches for a specific verb: *talfahu* — "the fire *scorches* their faces." The root *l-f-ḥ* in classical Arabic belongs to the vocabulary of scorching wind, the kind that burns exposed skin on contact. And he adds a word for what the people will look like in that moment: *kāliḥūn* — grimacing, faces contorted, lips drawn back from the teeth. It is a word the Quran uses elsewhere for the extreme physical distortion of agony. Al-Tabari is not embellishing. He is being precise about what *lā yakuffūna* — "they cannot ward it off" — actually looks like on a human face.

**Now both directions catch fire at once.** Everything they faced toward and everything they turned away from — both, simultaneously. There is no posture left that is not burning. There is no orientation that is safe.

This is what Allah is letting the unfinished sentence point toward — and then refusing to put into words. Because the mind, having pictured the scorching of faces already distorted with agony, fire from before and behind with no escape direction, has already done the work no sentence could do.

### Element Two: *Baghtatan* — The Word That Sounds Like Cognitive Ambush

Now ayah 40. Allah, having left ayah 39 hanging, says one word — *bal* — "rather" — and pivots. He had been describing what they would understand if they knew. He now describes what they actually will experience.

> **بَلْ تَأْتِيهِم بَغْتَةً فَتَبْهَتُهُمْ**
>
> *"Rather — it will come upon them **suddenly** and **leave them stunned silent**..."*

Two words are doing extraordinary work here. Both come from roots beginning with the harsh, plosive "b" sound. Listen to them spoken aloud:

*ba-gh-ta-tan.*
*fa-tab-ha-tu-hum.*

There is something almost violent in the phonetics. *Bagh* — *baht*. The sounds themselves are sudden. They strike the ear the way the thing they describe strikes the soul.

#### The Word *Baghtatan*

The root *b-gh-t* in classical Arabic carries a very specific meaning. The lexicons define it as something that comes upon you with no prior warning, no signal, no preparation — the kind of arrival that catches you with the wrong foot forward. It is not just "fast." Fast can still be expected. *Baghtatan* is *unexpected*.

Contemporary cognitive science has a name for what *baghtatan* describes — *cognitive ambush*, as some researchers call the moment when an event arrives in a category the mind had not allocated for it — though the Quran names this phenomenon with far greater precision, and centuries earlier. Surprise is one of the most disorienting emotional states the human nervous system can experience. Not pain. Not even fear. *Surprise.* Because pain and fear can be braced against. Surprise cannot.

Stay with that. The Quran's choice of *baghtatan* is not poetic embellishment. It is psychologically exact. **The mockers asked "when?" — and the answer is: it will come at the precise time you cannot have prepared for, because the entire architecture of your denial has been the assumption that you have time to prepare.**

There is a bitter irony here that al-Tabari gestures at. The mockers were *mustaʿjilūn* — those who clamored for hastening. Give it to us now, they said. Bring it sooner, they taunted. And the response is not that it will come slowly. The response is that it will come *baghtatan* — with no scheduled arrival, no announced date, no interval for bracing. They wanted to set the terms of the *when*. They are given a *when* that erases all terms.

#### The Word *Tabhatuhum*

The next word — *fa-tabhatuhum* — is from the root *b-h-t*. The classical lexicons explain *bahata* as: to be struck dumb, to be stunned into silence, to have your mouth open with no words coming out, to be cognitively overwhelmed to the point of muteness.

You know this word from somewhere else in the Quran. In Surah Al-Baqarah 2:258, Ibrahim debates Nimrod about who gives life and death. After Ibrahim's final argument — *"Allah brings the sun from the east; bring it from the west" — fa-buhita alladhī kafara* — *"so the disbeliever was struck dumb."*

Notice what just happened. **The Hour does to the deniers what Ibrahim's argument did to Nimrod.** It silences them. It empties them of speech.

[PAUSE]

This is more than a vocabulary echo. The mockers' whole identity, their whole social standing, was built on speaking — on laughing, on questioning, on demanding *"when, when, when?"* Their power was in their voice.

The Hour does not just punish them. It takes their voice. It puts them in the same posture Nimrod was put in by a single sentence from a young man with the truth.

The questioners are silenced. The mockery dies in the throat.

#### The Closing Pair — *Raddahā* and *Yunẓarūn*

The ayah closes with two final negations:

*"They will not be able to **repel** it, and they will not be granted **respite**."*

The word *raddahā* — to push it back, to send it away, to repel — is the language of someone trying to hold back a flood with their hands. Some commentators note: they had pushed away the warnings; now they cannot push away the reality.

And the last word — *yunẓarūna* — comes from the root *n-ẓ-r*, which gives Arabic its word for sight, but here means: *to be granted a waiting period, to be given delay.* They asked: "when?" The answer arrives in the form of a denied delay. **They wanted a *when*. They are given a *no longer when.* No more interval. No more time.**

The very word they used in their mockery — the question that lived inside the assumption of time — is answered with a word that cancels time itself.

### Bridge from Linguistic to Thematic

Look at what these three ayahs have done.

The mockers stood in the marketplace and asked, *"When?"* — assuming the question itself was safe, that asking it bought them time.

Allah responded with a sentence He refused to finish — letting silence do the work. With a word — *baghtatan* — that named the precise psychological collapse their question presumed could not happen. With another word — *tabhatuhum* — that took from them the very voice they had been using to mock. And He closed by denying them the one thing their entire denial-system had assumed: *delay.*

The linguistic surgery is finished. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Question That Betrays the Questioner

Begin here, in your own life.

Every parent of a teenager knows a particular tone of voice. The teenager is told something they don't want to hear — *clean your room, do your homework, come home by ten.* And the teenager looks up and asks a question.

But the question is not really a question. The question is a tactic.

*"Why do I have to?"* — is rarely a request for reasoning. It is a delay. The teenager is hoping that the act of demanding justification will exhaust the parent enough that the demand quietly disappears.

You and I do this too — about things much more serious than rooms.

The doctor tells the patient: *"You need to stop smoking."* The patient says: *"How long do I have if I don't?"* It sounds like a question. It is a tactic. The patient is hoping the answer will be vague enough — *"five to ten years"* — that the urgency dissolves into a comfortable horizon.

The wealthy man hears the verse about charity. He asks when, exactly, is the niṣāb threshold reached? What counts toward the calculation? How does one handle mixed assets? He sounds diligent. He is buying time. The act of demanding precision holds the obligation at a scholarly distance, where it cannot touch him yet. The question is held open — never answered, never closed — and so the giving is perpetually deferred.

This is not hypocrisy, exactly. It is something subtler. It is the human capacity to sincerely believe the question is genuine while the question is simultaneously functioning as armor. The mockers of Makkah, Ibn Kathir tells us, genuinely believed nothing was coming. The *istiʿjāl* — the demand to hasten — arose from actual conviction that the promise was empty. They were not pretending to be skeptical. They *were* skeptical, all the way down.

And yet the question still served to seal them further in. Every time they asked "when?" and received no date, the unbelief calcified. The question confirmed what the question assumed.

**The word *when* is always the word of someone who has already answered the question — and is asking only to maintain the comfort of its remaining open.**

Now bring this inside. What is your *when*?

When I have more time. When the children are grown. When I understand Islam better. When I feel more ready. When things calm down. When — and the sentence needs no object, because the when is not really about a condition. The when is a structure. A way of keeping a door that has never been opened looking like it might be.

### Theme Two: The Impatient Made Unprepared

There is a second irony buried in these three ayahs, and it cuts even deeper.

The mockers' signature trait — the thing al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both mark — is *istiʿjāl*: impatience. Hurry. Give it to us now. Come on. If you're truthful, why the delay? They were not passive doubters. They were active demanders. They wanted the punishment brought *forward*.

And what does Allah say?

*It will come upon them baghtatan.* Suddenly. With no announcement. With no interval.

They wanted to hasten it. It will come at a moment that makes all their hastening irrelevant.

The word *istiʿjāl* — demanding that things move faster — carries within it the assumption that you are in a position to set the pace. That you have the authority to summon and dismiss. That the timeline is negotiable. It is perhaps the most complete expression of what the Quran calls *kibr* at the cosmic scale: the assumption that creation moves on your schedule.

*Baghtatan* is the answer to *istiʿjāl*. Not "it will be slow." Not "you must wait longer." But: *the whole category of your waiting is canceled.* You wanted to hasten it on your terms? There are no terms. There is no negotiation. There is only the moment, arriving without announcement, that makes every question about when permanently moot.

Al-Muyassar adds the human toll with a simple economy: had they known what they would face — fire on their faces, fire on their backs, no helper — they would not have persisted in their kufr, and would not have demanded the hastening of their own punishment. The tragedy the tafsir is pointing at is not just that they were wrong. It is that they were wrong about something they had been told repeatedly, from a source that was trying to save them.

### Theme Three: The Door That Is Still Open

Here is what these three ayahs do not say.

They do not say: *the deniers have no chance.* They do not say: *the time for returning has passed.* They describe a *future* moment — the moment of the fire, the moment of the sudden arrival — as something that has not yet come.

The grammar of *law yaʿlamu* — "if only they knew" — is the grammar of possibility. It is the grammar of what could still change. Al-Tabari's implied completion of the suppressed apodosis makes this explicit: *had they known, they would have hastened to repentance and to faith.* The *law* is pointing toward a door that, in the lifetime of the original listeners, was still open.

This is what Islamic scholars call the rahma — the mercy — inside a warning. A warning is only given to someone who can still act on it. You do not warn a building before you demolish it. You warn a person. And a person can turn.

The ayahs are addressed, in their original context, to people who were still in Makkah, still breathing, still capable of the *tawbah* that al-Tabari says they would have rushed toward had they truly known. The warning is graphic. The silence in the grammar is terrifying. And both of these are acts of care.

**The sentence Allah refuses to finish is not a door slamming. It is a door held open, with a terrifying view of what lies on the other side of not walking through.**

## PART 3: THE PERSONAL TURN

We have traveled through grammar and through history. Now the question becomes intimate.

### What Is the "When" You Are Holding?

The three ayahs move from a *when* asked in mockery to a *when* that arrives in fire. The arc between these two *whens* is the entire human story of delay.

But there is a *when* that is not mockery. There is a *when* that is honest confusion, honest fear, honest feeling of being not yet ready. That *when* is not the same as the Makkans' *istiʿjāl*. It is different in quality: it knows what it is, and it aches.

The Quran does not address that *when* with threat. It addresses it with the same grammar: *law yaʿlamu* — "if only they knew." The sentence hangs open. The therefore is suppressed. Because the therefore is: *they would have turned.*

The turning is still available.

There is a form of engaging with these ayahs that is purely intellectual — tracing the grammar, admiring the rhetoric, appreciating the psychological precision of *baghtatan* and *tabhatuhum*, and leaving unchanged. That engagement honors the text with the mind. But the text was not sent for the mind. It was sent for the moment when the mind, having understood, passes the understanding to the chest.

The mockers asked *"when?"* and used the open question to stay still.

What would it mean to ask *"when?"* — and answer: *now. The when is now.*

Not because you are ready. Not because all the questions are resolved. But because the sentence Allah left unfinished is not meant to be completed by words. It is meant to be completed by a life turning back.

[PAUSE]

### The Echo in Surah Al-Anbiya Itself

One final note. This surah does not end in punishment. It ends — much later, in ayah 97 — with a moment that rhymes with these three ayahs. *"And the true promise draws near, and suddenly the eyes of those who disbelieved will stare fixed — O woe to us! We were heedless of this."*

*Woe to us.* They found the words, finally. But only after it arrived *baghtatan*. Only after the *bahata* settled in and the mouth found speech again — too late.

The *bahata* of Nimrod: struck dumb by truth, in a debate he had chosen, with time still remaining.

The *bahata* of the Last Day: struck dumb by reality, at a moment no one chose, with no time left.

The gap between those two *bahatās* is a lifetime. It is, in fact, exactly this lifetime. The one you are reading this in.

## CLOSING

The three ayahs we have sat with are a complete act of divine speech. They take a mocking question — "when?" — and in thirty-three Arabic words they:

- Name what the question really is (*istiʿjāl*, a demand rooted in stubborn settled unbelief that it will never arrive)
- Begin a sentence and refuse to finish it, so that language itself demonstrates what words cannot hold
- Describe the faces, already scorched and contorted (*kāliḥūn*), and the backs, with fire on both sides and no direction of safety
- Name the moment of arrival with a word — *baghtatan* — that makes all planning retroactively irrelevant
- Silence the voice that did the mocking with a word — *tabhatuhum* — from the same root used to describe Nimrod's silence before Ibrahim
- Close by denying the one thing the question assumed: more time

Al-Tabari, in completing what Allah left open, said the people *would have hastened to repentance.* He meant it as a description of what they would have done. It reads also as a description of what we can still do.

The *when* that matters is not the one the mockers asked. It is the one you ask yourself, alone, in the middle of a life that is not finished yet.

*When?*

The Quran — gently, and terrifyingly — answers: *the when is not yours to set. But the turning is.*
