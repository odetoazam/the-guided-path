# If There Were Two

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 22

> لَوْ كَانَ فِيهِمَآ ءَالِهَةٌ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ لَفَسَدَتَاۚ فَسُبْحَـٰنَ ٱللَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَرْشِ عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

*Law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illa Allāhu lafasadatā. Fa-subḥāna Allāhi Rabbi al-ʿarshi ʿammā yaṣifūn.*

**"Had there been within them [the heavens and the earth] gods besides Allah, they both would have fallen into ruin. So exalted is Allah, Lord of the Throne, above what they describe."**

---

## Introduction — The Argument the Universe Is Already Making

Before we open this ayah, we have to feel where it sits.

Surah Al-Anbiya begins with a sentence that should disturb anyone who reads it slowly. **"Their reckoning has drawn near for the people, and they — in heedlessness — are turning away."** Then a few verses later: *lāhiyatan qulūbuhum* — "their hearts are entertained, distracted, busied with anything but this."

The surah is named **The Prophets** — plural — because what comes next is a parade of messengers, each one a witness saying the same single thing in different languages, in different centuries, to different stubborn communities. Nūḥ. Ibrāhīm. Lūṭ. Dāwūd. Sulaymān. Ayyūb. Yūnus. Zakariyyā. Maryam. All of them. One message.

But before the parade begins — before we meet the prophets one by one — Allah pauses and offers something different. He doesn't quote a prophet. He doesn't tell a story. He gives us **a proof**. A single, compressed, devastating proof that the universe itself has been making since the moment it began existing.

This proof has a name in the classical tradition. The scholars called it ***burhān al-tamānuʿ*** — the proof of mutual interference, or the proof of cosmic coherence. It is one of the shortest and most powerful arguments for tawḥīd in the Quran.

And here is the hook I want you to carry through this whole reflection:

**The ayah is not really arguing about gods in the heavens.** That argument was settled the moment the cosmos held together this morning. The ayah is making a quieter, more uncomfortable claim — about something happening *inside* you. Right now. About a kind of ruin you have probably already tasted.

Stay with me. Let's open it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will look at three things — three pieces of grammar that, once seen, change how the whole ayah lands.

A single particle. A single verb. A single divine title.

---

### Element One: *Law* — The Word for Things That Could Never Be

Arabic has more than one way to say "if."

In English we have one word. "If it rains, I will stay home." "If I had wings, I would fly." Same word. Same conditional structure. The English speaker has to figure out from context whether the speaker means *probably going to happen* or *impossible.*

Arabic refuses that ambiguity.

Arabic has at least two main conditional particles: **إن (in)** and **لو (law)**.

**ʾIn** is for things that are possible. Open-ended. Could go either way. *In tadrus tanjaḥ* — "if you study, you will succeed." The studying might happen. The success might happen. Real conditional, real future.

**Law** is something else entirely. *Law* is the word Arabic reserves for **counterfactuals** — for hypotheticals that are presented as not the case. *Law* doesn't say "this might happen." *Law* says: **"Imagine, for the sake of argument, a world in which this were true — even though it isn't."**

In English we do this with the subjunctive: "If I *were* a king…" — that "were" instead of "was" is a small linguistic flag that says: I am not a king. *Law* is that flag, made into a particle.

Now look at the ayah.

> **لَوْ** كَانَ فِيهِمَآ ءَالِهَةٌ إِلَّا ٱللَّهُ لَفَسَدَتَا

It does not begin with *in*. It begins with ***law***.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has done before He has said anything else. The very first word in the verse — the first sound off the tongue — is already a signal. The hypothesis He is about to entertain is **not a real possibility**. It is a logical impossibility being given a hearing only so we can watch it collapse.

This is what *law* does. It says: let's pretend, just for a moment, the very thing you cannot actually have. Then watch what unfolds.

The whole ayah, before it has done any work, has already telegraphed its conclusion. *Law*. As if. Imagine, hypothetically. Step into a fiction with me, briefly. Then we'll come out.

Here is what's remarkable: Allah does not refuse to engage the polytheist's premise. He doesn't say "your idea is too absurd to discuss." He goes further — He says, **let me grant you your premise. Let me run your scenario. And then let me show you what your own scenario produces.**

Sit with that for a moment. The Quran is so confident in its case that it will let the opposing position into the room — not as a serious contender, but as a witness against itself.

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: *Lafasadatā* — Not "Destroyed" — *Corrupted*

Now look at the verb at the end of the conditional clause:

> لَ**فَسَدَتَا**

Let's pull this verb apart slowly. There is more in it than first appears.

**The lām of emphasis (لَ).** That little prefix is not silent grammar. It is what scholars call *lām al-jawāb* — the lām that introduces the answer of a *law* clause. And it carries a flavor of certainty, of "absolutely, no question." If *law* sets up the hypothetical, this lām says: *and this is what would absolutely follow.* Not maybe. Not possibly. **Inevitably.**

**The dual feminine ending (تَا).** *Fasadatā* — "they both, feminine, were corrupted." The verb is dual, agreeing with the heavens (samāwāt) and the earth (arḍ) — both grammatically feminine in Arabic. So in a single word the verse holds the entire vertical and horizontal of creation: up and down, sky and ground, the two halves of every cosmos a human being knows.

**Now — the root itself.** *Fasada.* ف-س-د.

This is the word I most want you to feel.

Arabic has a word for "destroyed" — *dammara*. It has a word for "annihilated" — *fanā*. It has a word for "shattered" — *kasara*. Allah did not choose any of those. He chose ***fasad***.

*Fasad* is not destruction. *Fasad* is what happens to fruit you forgot in a drawer. To meat that wasn't refrigerated. To a relationship that wasn't tended. To a city whose laws stopped being enforced. **It is the word for things that have not been annihilated — they are still here — but they have come unglued from their function. They are still present, but they are no longer working as they were meant to work.**

The classical lexicographers — al-Rāghib, ibn Manẓūr — gloss *fasad* as *khurūj al-shayʾ ʿan al-iʿtidāl* — "a thing departing from its proper balance, its proper order." It is corruption-as-disorder. It is the slow loss of coherence.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this changes about the argument.

If Allah had said *ladummirata* — "they would have been destroyed" — the ayah would be saying: *two gods would blow up the cosmos.* Big bang in reverse. The universe vanishes.

But that is not what He said. He said *lafasadatā*. **The cosmos would still be here. It would not vanish. It would unravel. It would lose its laws. It would become a place where galaxies move in two contradictory directions, where gravity holds in one room and not the next, where causes produce different effects on Tuesday than they produce on Wednesday — a universe still existing, but no longer making sense.**

This is a more sophisticated argument than people usually give it credit for. The verse is not saying the cosmos would explode. It is saying the cosmos would become **incoherent**. Two wills cannot produce one consistent set of laws. Two ultimates cannot agree on physics. And what we observe — the staggering, almost monotonous reliability of the universe, the sun rising on schedule, water boiling at the same temperature in Mecca and in Tokyo, electrons behaving the same way in 2026 as they did in the year 622 — *that* is the standing evidence.

The ayah is essentially saying: **look around you. The fact that things still hang together is the proof.**

This is why the classical scholars saw this verse as offering not just a theological claim but an *ongoing* one. Every moment that the cosmos remains coherent is another instant of *lafasadatā* failing to occur — another silent tick of the proof.

[PAUSE]

Now let's hold this idea in our hands for a moment longer, because we are going to need it for the second half of the reflection. *Fasad* is what happens when two ultimate authorities try to share a domain. They don't blow each other up. They produce **disorder.** Slow, low-grade, persistent disorder.

Hold that. We will return to it.

---

### Element Three: *Rabbi al-ʿArsh* — Why This Title, Here

Now look at the second half of the ayah:

> فَسُبْحَـٰنَ ٱللَّهِ **رَبِّ ٱلْعَرْشِ** عَمَّا يَصِفُونَ

After the proof has been delivered, Allah does not refer to Himself with just one name. He could have said *subḥāna Allāh* and stopped. He could have said *Rabbi al-samāwāt wal-arḍ* — "Lord of the heavens and the earth." He could have said *al-Khāliq* — the Creator. He could have said *al-Wāḥid* — the One.

He chose: ***Rabbi al-ʿArsh*** — Lord of the Throne.

Why this name, exactly here?

In the language of the Quran, *al-ʿArsh* — the Throne — is the symbol of **dominion**. *ʿAlā al-ʿarshi-stawā* — "established Himself over the Throne" — appears across the Quran as the image of cosmic sovereignty. The Throne is not just a piece of celestial furniture. It is the metaphor Allah chose for the seat of unified authority over all that exists.

Now think about why that matters in *this* verse.

The ayah has just argued that two gods would fall into incoherence — *fasad*. The argument is about **authority that cannot be split**. The proof is about **dominion that cannot be shared**.

So when Allah turns from the proof to the conclusion, He picks the name that says exactly that. Not "the Creator" — though He is. Not "the Loving" — though He is. He picks the name that says: **the One whose authority is the unified seat from which everything is governed.**

[PAUSE]

There is a quiet symmetry here that the classical commentators noticed. The ayah's logical move is: *if authority were split, the cosmos would unravel*. The ayah's naming move is: *and so, glory be to the One whose name itself is the name of unified authority.* The argument and the doxology rhyme. They make the same point in two languages.

Let's also notice the verb that closes the ayah: ***yaṣifūn*** — "they describe."

It is in the imperfect tense. In Arabic, imperfect signals ongoing, repeated, habitual action. Allah does not say "above what they once said" or "above what they will say." He says, in effect, **above what they keep on describing.** The describing is happening right now. The misnaming of God is a constant human industry. And His exaltation is a constant divine reality, perfectly above it.

[PAUSE]

---

### Bridge — From the Cosmos to the Heart

Three small grammatical features. *Law* — the word for impossibilities. *Lafasadatā* — corrupted, not destroyed. *Rabbi al-ʿArsh* — Lord of unified dominion.

Together they tell us this:

> Two ultimates produce not destruction but disorder. The universe's coherence is the standing proof of its single Lord. And the right response, when you see this clearly, is exaltation.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the heavens — but about something much closer.

---

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Hidden Idols and the Daily *Fasad*

Begin with something you already know.

You have lived through periods in your life when nothing was working. Not catastrophically — you weren't bankrupt, you weren't ill, nothing burned down. But somehow, the days had stopped *cohering*. You were tired but couldn't sleep. You were busy but nothing was finishing. You were in relationships but felt alone in them. Decisions felt weighty in the morning and meaningless by night. You'd say yes to one thing, then resent the thing you said yes to, then say yes again the next week.

If someone had asked you, *"what is wrong?"* — you couldn't have pointed to anything. There was no enemy, no disaster. Just a low, persistent, **incoherence**.

That feeling has a name in the language of this surah. **It is *fasad*.** Not destruction. Not annihilation. Disorder.

Now here is the question I want to put to you. Where does *fasad* come from?

The Quran has just told us, in the previous ayah's argument: *fasad* comes from a domain that has more than one ultimate authority.

Apply that to your own heart.

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars of the heart — Ibn al-Qayyim, al-Ghazālī — said something striking. They said that idolatry — *shirk* — does not require a stone statue. It requires only that you treat something other than Allah as **ultimate**. As a thing for which you would sacrifice everything else. As the source from which your peace, your worth, or your safety actually flows.

By that definition, almost every modern person walks around with a small pantheon in their chest.

There is the god of **career**. The god of **how you appear to others**. The god of **your child's success**. The god of **comfort**. The god of **being right**. The god of **never disappointing your parents**. The god of **the relationship that finally fixes you**.

You don't pray to these. You don't bow to them. But your decisions track them. Your fears orbit them. When they are threatened, you are threatened. When they ask for sacrifice, you give it.

And here is the thing that matches our ayah so exactly that it stops being metaphor and starts being mechanics:

**These gods cannot agree.**

The god of career wants your evening. The god of family wants your evening too. The god of how-you-appear wants you at the event. The god of comfort wants you in bed. The god of being-right wants the argument to continue. The god of being-loved wants the argument to end.

You cannot serve all of them. They have contradictory commands.

What does life feel like when you try? **It feels like *fasad*.** Things still working — but not cohering. Days still happening — but not making sense. You haven't been destroyed. You've been disordered.

The argument of the verse is not somewhere in deep space. **It is in your chest.** *Law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illa Allāhu lafasadatā.* Had there been within them — within the cosmos, yes, but also within *you* — multiple ultimates besides Allah, the whole thing would fall into disorder. It is doing exactly that, in exactly the lives of exactly the people who have not yet sorted out which Lord rules their throne.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The verse is not just a proof of monotheism for some abstract debate. It is a diagnostic instrument for your own life.

If you are experiencing *fasad* — that low-grade, persistent incoherence in your days — the verse is suggesting where to look. Not at your schedule. Not at your circumstances. **At your pantheon.**

Ask yourself, gently:

— Whose approval, if I lost it, would make my life feel like it had collapsed?
— What outcome, if it didn't happen, would I treat as catastrophic?
— What thing am I actually willing to lie for? Who am I willing to neglect for it?

The answers to those questions are the names of your other gods. And the disorder you are feeling is the universe reporting, in miniature, exactly what verse 22 said it would: *lafasadatā.* It would all fall into ruin.

The way out is not to add a stronger god to the pantheon. It is to dismantle the pantheon.

There is **one** Throne. *Rabbi al-ʿArsh*. The whole point of tawḥīd, lived inwardly, is to let one authority sit on one throne, and let everything else become what it actually is — a created, conditional, secondary thing that cannot save you and was never supposed to try.

**The practical application for tomorrow:** Take one of those questions above and answer it honestly, on paper, before you sleep tonight. Just name one of the gods. Don't try to dismantle it yet. Naming is enough work for one night. Most of us go decades without naming them.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Why the Response Is *Subḥān* and Not *Al-Ḥamd*

Now look at how the verse turns at the comma.

> لَفَسَدَتَا ۚ **فَسُبْحَـٰنَ ٱللَّهِ**

The proof ends. And then — *fa-subḥāna Allāh*. **So, glorified is Allah.**

This is worth stopping at. Because Allah has just demonstrated something logical. The natural continuation, in our minds, would be a **conclusion** — *therefore He is One*, or *therefore there is no god but Him*, or *therefore the polytheists are wrong*. And the verse does carry that meaning. But the actual word Allah chose for the response is not a conclusion. **It is a doxology.** *Subḥān.*

What is *subḥān*?

The root *s-b-ḥ* means to swim, to glide, to move through a medium freely. In its theological use, *subḥān* is the verbal noun expressing **transcendence** — the act of declaring God to be far above, far beyond, untouched by, every imperfection ascribed to Him.

When you say *subḥān Allāh*, you are not just saying "praise be to God." Praise is *ḥamd*, and that is a different word. *Subḥān* says: **"You are above this. You are clear of this. You are infinitely beyond what they are saying about You."**

Now ask: why is *this* the right response to *this* proof?

[PAUSE]

Because the proof's content is exactly about transcendence. The argument has just shown that any conception of God that includes partners cannot be true — the universe would unravel. So the right response is not gratitude (*ḥamd*), and it is not even praise. It is **a clearing of the field**. *Subḥān* sweeps away the false descriptions before any true description can land.

Notice the order: first *subḥān*, then the divine name. **First we strip away what He is not, then we name what He is.** This is the deep structure of how the Quran teaches God to the human heart. The first kalimah of Islam — *lā ilāha illa Allāh* — has the same shape. **Negation first. Affirmation second.** "There is no god — *except* Allah." First clear the room, then place Him in it.

Why this order? Because the human heart does not arrive empty. It arrives already crowded. With idols of approval, of anxiety, of inherited belief, of self-flattery. You cannot put tawḥīd into a heart that has not first been cleared. *Subḥān* is the clearing.

And then comes the verb at the very end: *ʿammā yaṣifūn* — "above what they describe."

Here is something worth noticing. The ayah does not say "above what they worship" or "above what they invent." It says above what they ***describe***. The transgression named is description itself — the human hubris of thinking we can speak about Him in terms borrowed from creation. *Yaṣifūn* — they keep describing Him with attributes that belong to created things. And He, *subḥānahu*, is forever above their descriptions.

The implication is humbling. **Even our language about Him is not adequate to Him.** Even our truest statements about God are gestures toward Him, not portraits of Him. Tasbīḥ is the ongoing acknowledgment that He is more than what any tongue, including a sincere believing tongue, can hold.

[PAUSE]

So what is *subḥān Allāh*, lived?

It is the practice of **regularly clearing the field of your inherited and invented ideas about God** — including the small ones. The God who is mostly angry. The God who is mostly distant. The God who serves your tribe. The God who sounds, suspiciously, like your father. The God shaped by the last preacher you liked. All of these are descriptions you have placed on the Throne. *Subḥān Allāh* — He is above what we describe.

This does not mean we cannot speak of Him. The Quran speaks of Him constantly, in His own chosen language. But it means we hold even our truest statements with humility. We say them as gestures, not as containers.

**The practical application for tomorrow:** In your day, when you say *subḥān Allāh* — and most of us say it many times, often without thinking — pause for half a second. Let it do what it was made to do. Let it clear something. Let it sweep one false description, one inherited image, one limiting picture off the throne of your own imagining of Him. Let it return Him to His own infinity.

That half-second, repeated, is one of the great quiet disciplines of the believing heart.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Verse

Now look at what we have. Step back and see the shape.

> *Law kāna fīhimā ālihatun illa Allāhu lafasadatā.*
> *Fa-subḥāna Allāhi Rabbi al-ʿarshi ʿammā yaṣifūn.*

The verse has two halves. The first half is **a proof**. The second half is **a praise**.

The proof argues from cosmic coherence: if there were two ultimates, things would unravel.
The praise responds with cosmic transcendence: glorified is the One whose Throne is unified, above all our descriptions.

And the architecture is exact. The first half names what would happen *if* dominion were split — *lafasadatā*, ruin. The second half names the One whose dominion *is* unified — *Rabbi al-ʿarsh*, Lord of the Throne. The ruin is averted by the very Throne that names Him.

The verse is not just making an argument. **It is performing the response that the argument calls for.** It shows us logically that there is one Lord, and then it does what a heart should do when it sees that — it praises Him with the name that captures it.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar told us: a counterfactual conditional, a verb of corruption-not-destruction, a divine name of unified authority. The themes told us: our hearts host pantheons, and the disorder we feel is the small-scale version of the cosmic disorder the verse argues against, and the way out begins with *subḥān*, the clearing of the field.

It is the same teaching. **Multiple ultimates produce disorder. There is only one Throne. The right response is to clear the field of every other claimant.** The cosmos has done this perfectly since the moment it began. The believing heart spends a lifetime doing it imperfectly, one *subḥān Allāh* at a time.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one specific god in my pantheon that I have not yet named honestly to myself?
2. When I feel that low-grade disorder in my days — what would it look like to read it as *fasad* and ask which authority I have allowed onto the Throne that does not belong there?
3. When I say *subḥān Allāh*, do I let it clear anything — or has it become a sound my mouth makes without my heart present?
4. Am I willing to let the verse's argument apply to me, not just to the polytheist of pre-Islamic Arabia?
5. What inherited image of Allah am I carrying that I have never asked, *is this Him, or is this what they describe?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

> The same incoherence that two gods would produce in the cosmos, multiple idols produce in the human heart — and the only Throne that holds either together is His.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, do not try to fix anything. Just sit, briefly, and ask: **whose throne is the throne of my chest?** Not the answer you would give in public. The honest one. Whatever you find there, you do not have to dismantle it tonight. Naming it is enough. *Subḥān Allāh* — clear the field by even one inch. That inch matters.

Tomorrow you can begin returning the Throne to its rightful Lord.

### Du'a

*Allāhumma, anta Rabbu al-ʿArsh, wa lā Rabba siwāk.* O Allah, You are the Lord of the Throne, and there is no Lord besides You. Show us the small idols we have raised on the seat that belongs only to You. Clear our hearts of every description of You that is not worthy of You. And let the disorder of our days return to coherence under the rule of Your one and only authority. *Subḥānaka Rabbanā ʿammā naṣif.* Āmīn.
