# Do Not Leave Me Alone

> [ayah:21:89] وَزَكَرِيَّآ إِذْ نَادَىٰ رَبَّهُۥ رَبِّ لَا تَذَرْنِى فَرْدًا وَأَنتَ خَيْرُ ٱلْوَٰرِثِينَ
>
> *Wa Zakariyyā idh nādā rabbahu rabbi lā tadharnī fardan wa anta khayru-l-wārithīn*
>
> "And remember Zakariyya, when he called out to his Lord: *My Lord, do not leave me alone — and You are the best of inheritors.*"

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The room we have to walk into first

Before a single word of this verse can land, you have to stand where Zakariyya was standing. And where he was standing was at the far end of a life.

He was old. Not middle-aged, not tired — *old* in the way that the Qur'an elsewhere lets him describe himself: bones grown feeble, hair set ablaze with white (19:4). He was a prophet. He had served in the temple his entire life. He had watched over Maryam and seen provision arrive for her out of season, food in winter that had no earthly supply chain. He knew, better than almost anyone alive, that Allah gives.

And yet here he is, at the end, carrying a very particular ache that his knowledge could not soothe.

He had no child.

Now — you have to understand what that meant for a man like him, and this is where our modern instinct fails us, so slow down here. We hear "he wanted a child" and we file it under *he wanted the ordinary human joy of a family.* That was surely part of it. But Zakariyya was a prophet of a prophetic line. His anxiety was not only the private grief of a childless man. It was the grief of a *custodian.* Somewhere in him was a question that had nothing to do with sentiment and everything to do with responsibility: **when I am gone, who carries this? Who inherits the trust?** In another surah he says it out loud — *I fear for what comes after me* (19:5). The lamp he had tended his whole life was about to be handed to no one.

So sit inside that for a moment. An old man. A lifetime of service. And a fear that when he closes his eyes for the last time, a thread that had been passed hand to hand for generations will simply... end. With him.

### The strand this verse is part of

And now notice *where* Allah places this cry.

Surah al-Anbiya, from verse 76 onward, is doing something almost cinematic. It moves through prophet after prophet, and it does not tell you their full stories. It freezes each one at a single frame — and it is always the *same kind of frame.* Nuh **calls out** and is answered. Ayyub **calls out** from inside his affliction and is answered. Yunus **calls out** from inside the belly of the whale and the darkness, and is answered. It is a gallery of desperate voices, one after another, each one shown at the exact instant they turned to Allah — and each vignette resolves the same way: *We responded.*

You are watching a pattern being built, deliberately, cry by cry. And then, into that rhythm, arrives an old man who has run out of time.

The listener who had been following this surah already knew the shape. Someone calls. Allah answers. So the question hanging in the air as verse 89 begins is not *will Allah answer* — the surah has been drilling that answer into you. The question is subtler and more piercing: **what does a person say, when they've spent their whole life knowing Allah answers, and they've reached the very end, and the one thing they carried is still unresolved?**

What does a master of prayer pray, at the end of everything?

[PAUSE]

That is what this verse is. It is eleven words of Arabic. And it is one of the most extraordinary things a human being has ever said to God. Because in it, Zakariyya does something almost nobody does when they are afraid. Watch what he does with his fear. He is going to tell Allah his terror — and in the very same breath, without a pause, he is going to hand Allah the cure. And the astonishing thing is that the terror and the cure turn out to be made of the *same word.*

Let me show you.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *fardan* — the word that names a state, not a solution

I want to start with something we do every single time we pray, so naturally that we never notice we're doing it.

We arrive at prayer with the solution already written.

Think about how you actually make du'a. You don't come to Allah and say "I am struggling." You come and say "*give me this specific job.*" You don't say "my heart is unsettled." You say "*make this specific person say yes.*" We hand Allah not our problem but our prescription. We've already diagnosed ourselves, already decided what the cure is, and we come to the Physician of all things essentially asking Him to fill an order we wrote. It's the prayer equivalent of walking into a doctor's office and saying "I need exactly these pills," without letting Him look at you.

Hold that habit in your mind. Now look at what Zakariyya says.

He does not say *"give me a son."* He does not name the child. He does not name the cure at all.

He says: *lā tadharnī **fardan.*** "Do not leave me *fardan.*"

*Fardan* — from the root *fā–rā–dāl* — means single, solitary, alone, one-left-over. It is a *state* word, not an *object* word. He is not describing a thing he lacks. He is describing a *condition he is in.* He does not say "the gap in my life is shaped exactly like a son, please fill it with a son." He says: "I am alone. Do not leave me in this aloneness."

Do you feel the difference? **He brings Allah the wound, and trusts Him with the medicine.**

Now — let me be careful and honest with you, because tadabbur that inflates the text is worthless. We know from the other passages (Surah Maryam, Surah Aal-Imran) that Zakariyya absolutely *did* want a child, specifically, and elsewhere he asks for exactly that. So this is not a man who didn't know what he wanted. This is a man who, *in this particular framing that Allah chose to preserve here*, distilled his entire request down past the specific object to the raw human condition underneath it. And that is arguably the deeper thing. Because underneath *"I want a son"* is *"I do not want to be alone in this world and alone in what I leave behind."* And the son is only one of the ways Allah could answer that. Zakariyya names the ache and leaves the *how* to the One who actually knows the how.

Now listen to the *word itself.* Say it: *fardan.* It's blunt. It's closed. *Far–dan.* It doesn't flow — it stops. It ends on that hard *-dan,* clipped, final. There is no music in it. And that is exactly right, because loneliness has no music in it either. **The word sounds like the thing it names.** Allah did not put a soft, flowing word in the mouth of a lonely old man. He put a word that lands like a door closing. *Fardan.*

And notice one more absence — the deepest one. The word is *indefinite. Fardan,* not *al-fard.* It is raw, un-narrowed, un-decorated. He does not dress the loneliness up. He hands it over in its plainest form: I am one. Left over. Alone.

[PAUSE]

That is the first thing this verse teaches before it teaches anything else: **the most mature prayer you will ever make is not the one where you tell Allah your solution. It is the one where you finally trust Him enough to just show Him the wound.**

### Element Two: *lā tadharnī* — the courage of asking in the negative

Now look at the *shape* of the request, because Zakariyya frames it in a way that is easy to skate past.

He does not say *"give me."* He says *lā tadharnī* — "do **not** leave me."

Let me teach you what's happening grammatically, because it matters. In Arabic, you can ask for something in two directions. You can ask *positively* — "give me, grant me, bring me" — where you are the one reaching out with an open hand. Or you can ask *negatively* — "do not leave me, do not abandon me, do not let me" — where you cast yourself not as someone demanding a gift, but as someone in Allah's hands, asking not to be released from them.

The verb here, *tadhar,* comes from a root (*wāw–dhāl–rā*) that means to leave, to forsake, to let be. And there's something quietly beautiful about this particular verb in Arabic: it is what grammarians call defective — it barely exists in the past tense. You almost never hear "he left" from this root; it lives, overwhelmingly, in the present and the command. It is a verb that is always *now.* Always *do not leave me* — in this moment, in the moment I'm in. Not a report about the past. A live cry in the present tense.

And then the *-nī* on the end — "me." Attached directly to the verb, no space, no distance. *Tadhar–nī.* The "me" is fused right onto the action. There is nothing between Zakariyya and the leaving he fears except that one clinging suffix.

Now — why the negative? Why "do not leave me" instead of "give me a son"?

Think about the posture of each. "Give me" puts the initiative in *my* hands: here is my request, please fulfill it. But "do not leave me" — that puts everything in *Allah's* hands. It says: *I am already in Your care. I am not asking You to start something. I am asking You not to stop. Do not set me down. Do not let go.* It is the language of a child who is already being carried, and whose only fear is being put down.

Do you see the humility in it? He does not approach as a creditor come to collect on a lifetime of service — and he *could* have; he had every worldly reason to feel owed. He approaches as someone who knows he was never holding on to Allah in the first place. **Allah was holding him.** And all he asks is: keep holding.

This is what it looks like to ask from inside dependence rather than from inside entitlement. And it is the opposite of how fear usually makes us pray. Fear makes us grab. Fear makes us list demands. Zakariyya's fear made him open his hands and say: *just don't let go of me.*

### Element Three: *wa anta khayru-l-wārithīn* — and the word turns

And now we arrive at the turn. The hinge of the entire verse. And I need you to hold your breath a little here, because this is where the thing that looks like a simple old man's prayer reveals itself as something engineered with staggering precision.

After naming his aloneness, Zakariyya adds — *and there is no pause, no new sentence, it comes in the same breath* — *wa anta khayru-l-wārithīn.* "And You are the best of inheritors."

Let me first tell you what that *wa* is doing. It is not just "and." Grammatically this is a circumstantial *wa* — it means something closer to "*while*" or "*given that.*" So he is not making two separate points. He is saying: *do not leave me alone... **while**, all the while, You are the best of inheritors.* The second clause bends back and reframes the first. It is a single thought folding in on itself.

Now — *al-wārithīn.* The inheritors. From the root *wāw–rā–thā:* to inherit, to receive what remains after someone is gone.

And here is where you have to remember what Zakariyya was actually afraid of. Go back to the very beginning of this reflection. His deepest fear was not just loneliness in the abstract. It was that **he would die with no one to inherit** — no one to inherit his family line, and above all no one to inherit the prophetic trust he had carried his whole life. In Surah Maryam he says it in exactly these terms: he wants a son who will *inherit* from him (*yarithunī* — same root, *wāw–rā–thā*). His terror is an *inheritance* terror. Who will be my *wārith,* my inheritor?

Now read the verse again with that in your ears.

He says: do not leave me alone *(with no inheritor)* — *and You are the best of the inheritors.*

**His fear and his consolation are the same word.**

Sit with this. Stay with me here, because it is almost too precise to be believed. The thing he is terrified of losing is an *inheritor.* And the thing he reaches for to steady himself is that Allah is *the best inheritor.* The wound and the balm are cut from the identical root — *w-r-th.* He voices the exact shape of his fear, and then, in the same breath, he presses on that exact spot and finds Allah already there.

What does *khayru-l-wārithīn* actually mean? It means: everything returns to Allah. Every legacy, every trust, every line, every name — in the end, it all comes back to Him; He is the One who remains after all the "leavers" have left. So Zakariyya is essentially saying: *I am afraid of dying with no one to inherit from me. But even if no human ever inherits from me — **You** inherit everything. And nothing that returns to You is ever lost.* The very thing he feared — that his trust would fall into no hands — he answers by remembering that it will fall into the best of all hands.

He does not wait for Allah to comfort him. He reaches into his own fear and pulls out the comfort that was hidden inside it the whole time.

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, before we go a single step further.

*Fardan* — he brings the raw wound, not a prescription. *Lā tadharnī* — he asks from inside Allah's grip, not from entitlement. *Khayru-l-wārithīn* — he presses on the exact nerve of his fear and finds Allah already occupying it.

This is not the grammar of a man falling apart. This is the grammar of a man who has been *held* his whole life and knows it. But grammar is only the surface. Now let's ask what this reveals — not about words, but about the two fears that run underneath almost every human life.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: bringing the ache, not the order form

Let me tell you about the way most of us actually walk through our wanting.

You want something. And within about four seconds of wanting it, your mind has already converted the want into a *plan.* You don't just feel lonely — you decide the solution is *that specific person.* You don't just feel unmoored at work — you decide the fix is *that specific title, that specific salary.* You don't just ache for meaning — you decide it lives in *that specific achievement.* We are extraordinary at this. We convert open aches into closed orders almost instantly, and then we spend years pounding on doors that may not even be the right doors, because we mistook our prescription for our diagnosis.

And here is the quiet tragedy of it: sometimes Allah is *trying to give us the thing we actually need,* and we can't receive it, because it doesn't match the order form we wrote. We wanted the son; He was offering something the shape of which we never imagined. We wanted *that* door; He had built a hallway we refused to look down, because we were so sure the answer was a door.

Now watch Zakariyya, and let this land as a discovery about something you already half-knew.

He is a *prophet.* If anyone had earned the right to make specific demands, it was him. And yet, in the framing Allah chose to enshrine forever in this surah, he strips his request all the way down. Past the son. Past the plan. Down to the bare human state: *I am alone. Do not leave me alone.* He hands Allah the *ache* and lets Allah write the prescription.

And think about what that actually requires. It requires believing that Allah understands your need *better than you do.* That is not a small belief. When you bring Allah the wound instead of the solution, you are confessing something enormous: *I do not fully know what I need. But You do. So here is the whole of it, undressed — and I trust You to answer it in the way that is actually good, not the way I demanded.*

There's a strange freedom in it, too. When you pray for the *specific* thing and don't get it, it feels like a rejection — like the door was slammed. But when you pray the *ache* — "do not leave me alone" — you have opened yourself to being answered in a hundred ways you never scripted. The lonely person who prays "send me *this* spouse" can be denied. The lonely person who prays "do not leave me alone" has asked for something Allah can pour in through a dozen unexpected windows — a friendship, a community, a nearness to Him in the night that they never knew was possible.

So here is what this actually changes about tomorrow, concretely. The next time you raise your hands, before you list your solution, try — just once — to say the layer underneath it. Not "give me the promotion" but "do not leave me feeling worthless." Not "make them come back" but "do not leave me unloved." Name the ache, and then physically leave a silence there, and let Allah be the one to fill in the *how.* You may find you were never actually praying for the promotion. You were praying not to feel small. And that — He can answer in a thousand ways.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: "You are the best of inheritors" — the cure for the fear of being forgotten

Now I want to name the second fear, and it is bigger than the first, because it sits under almost everything human beings build.

We are terrified of being forgotten.

Look at the machinery of the modern world and you will see this terror everywhere, wearing nice clothes. We call it "building a legacy." We call it "leaving a mark." We call it "making an impact." We build the personal brand, we chase the thing with our name on it, we want the work to *outlive* us, we want to be remembered. And under all of it — under the LinkedIn and the monuments and the naming of buildings and the desperate documenting of our lives — is one primal dread: *when I am gone, will anything of me remain? Or will I simply be erased, as if I was never here?*

This is *legacy anxiety,* and it is possibly the deepest fear there is, because it is the fear of death wearing a mask. We can almost make peace with dying. What we cannot bear is dying *without a trace.*

Now — Zakariyya was staring directly into the purest, most concentrated form of this fear that a human being can face. It was not abstract for him. He had *no heir.* Not "my legacy might fade" but "there is literally no one to receive anything I carried." And it was not even his personal name he feared for — it was the *sacred trust,* the prophetic line, the thing he had guarded his whole life. His was legacy anxiety at its absolute maximum: the trust ends, with me, into nothing.

So what does he do with the biggest version of the fear we all carry?

He does not grasp. He *releases.* He says: *wa anta khayru-l-wārithīn* — **You are the best of inheritors.**

Let this reframe everything. What he is saying is: even in the worst case — even if no human being ever inherits a single thing from me — I have forgotten something. *You* inherit everything. Everything I carried, every act of service, every year in the temple, the whole trust — none of it falls into nothingness, because it all returns to *You,* and You are the One who *remains.* Nothing entrusted to the Best of Inheritors is ever lost. It is not erased. It is *received* — by the only receiver who never dies, never forgets, never lets a single grain of it fall.

Do you see what this does to the fear of being forgotten? It dissolves it at the root. Because the fear of being forgotten is really the fear that *my life will fall into hands that let it drop* — into a forgetful world, into descendants who won't remember, into a future that erases. But if the true inheritor of everything I ever was is Allah Himself — *khayru-l-wārithīn* — then nothing I offered to Him is at the mercy of a forgetful world at all. He has it. He keeps it. Forever.

And here is the piece that should reach all the way down. You do not need a monument. You do not need your name on anything. You do not even need an heir. You need to have handed your life to the Best of Inheritors while you still had breath. The billionaire terrified of being forgotten and the childless old woman with nothing but her prayers stand on completely level ground before this verse — because the only inheritor that matters keeps the same perfect books for both of them. The tears you cried at 3 a.m. that no human being will ever know about — He inherited every one. The kindness no one saw — received, in full. Nothing you gave to Him is lost in the crowd, because to Him there is no crowd.

So what changes tomorrow? This: the next time you feel that clawing need to be *seen,* to be recognized, to make sure someone notices what you did — pause and ask where you're trying to deposit your life. Into people, who forget? Into a world, that erases? Or into the Best of Inheritors, who keeps everything? Do one thing — one small act of good — and tell no one. Let it go entirely unseen by every human eye. And feel, in that hiddenness, not loss but *safety* — because you just handed it to the one Inheritor who will never misplace it. That is the practice: to build your real legacy in the ledger that never burns.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture — what was really built here

Now let me show you the design, and let it be the proof that this was no accident.

Look at the verse as a shape. At its heart is a fear — *fardan,* alone, with no inheritor. And wrapped around that fear, on the far side, is its answer — *khayru-l-wārithīn,* the best of inheritors. Watch the movement: the verse opens with the narrator speaking *about* Zakariyya — "when he called out to *his* Lord" — and then, without warning, the camera turns and we are *inside his mouth:* "*My* Lord, do not leave *me.*" We move from being told about a man to standing inside his prayer. And within that prayer, he travels from the barest statement of his terror — *fardan* — to the fullest statement of his trust — *khayru-l-wārithīn* — in a single, unbroken breath.

And the two ends of that arc are bound together by one root: *w-r-th.* The absence of an inheritor, and the presence of the Best Inheritor. **His fear names the wound; his faith names the same wound as the place where Allah is standing.** The verse is built so that the very word he dreads becomes the word that saves him. That is not the structure of a panicked cry. That is the structure of a man who has understood something so deeply that even his terror comes out pre-reconciled with his Lord.

And do you remember the surah's whole rhythm? *Someone calls, Allah answers.* Read the very next verse. *Fastajabnā lahu wa wahabnā lahu Yaḥyā* — "So We answered him, and We granted him Yahya." Allah did not just give him an heir. He gave him a *prophet* for a son. The man who prayed the ache and released the legacy received back more than the specific thing he never even named.

### Where the two roads meet

And now see that the linguistic journey and the two themes were never two things. They were one insight approached from two sides.

The word *fardan* taught us to bring the ache and not the order form. The word *khayru-l-wārithīn* taught us to release the legacy into the only hands that keep everything. And these are the same act. Both are surrender. Both are a man saying: *I do not have to control the how, and I do not have to secure the after — because the One I am speaking to knows the how better than I do, and keeps the after more safely than I ever could.* Bringing the wound instead of the prescription, and handing the legacy to the Best of Inheritors — these are the front and the back of a single trust.

That trust is the emotional arc of the whole verse. We started with an old man at the end of his life, carrying a fear that his knowledge could not soothe. We arrive at an old man who has taken that fear apart with his bare hands and found Allah inside it. He walked in alone. He walked out held.

### Questions to carry

- When you last made du'a, did you bring Allah your *ache* — or did you bring Him your *order form,* already filled out?
- What is the "son" you keep insisting on — the one specific solution you've decided your ache must be shaped like? What might Allah be trying to give you down a hallway you refuse to look at because you're staring at a door?
- Where in your life are you frantically trying to be *remembered* — and what would change if you truly believed the only Inheritor that matters has already recorded every hidden thing you ever did?
- What is one good deed you could do this week that no human being will ever know about — and can you feel that hiddenness as *safety* rather than loss?
- If you had to compress your deepest fear into a single word the way Zakariyya compressed his into *fardan* — what would the word be? And whose hands are you afraid it will fall into?

### The one sentence to carry

**Bring Allah the wound and not the prescription, and hand Him the legacy no human hand can keep — because the One who knows your ache better than you do is also the One who never loses a single thing entrusted to Him.**

### The invitation

You do not have to walk into your prayer tonight with your solution already written. That was never the price of admission. Let this be the shift: come with less. Come with the bare ache, undressed, the way Zakariyya came with *fardan* — one word, no decoration. And then, before you get up, remember where everything you are afraid to lose is really going. Not into a forgetful world. Into the hands of the Best of Inheritors. Let that be the thing you carry out of here — not a heavier load, but a lighter one, because you finally set it down in the only place it was ever safe.

### A du'a from these words

*O Allah, best of inheritors — we so often bring You our solutions when we should bring You our wounds. Teach us to pray like Your servant Zakariyya: to name the ache and trust You with the cure. Do not leave us alone. And whatever we cannot secure, whatever legacy no human hand will keep for us — receive it into Your hands, for You are the One who remains when all the leavers have left, and nothing entrusted to You is ever lost.* Ameen.
