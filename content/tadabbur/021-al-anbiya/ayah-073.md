# The Imams Made — Guidance That Is Commissioned, Not Claimed

> وَجَعَلْنَاهُمْ أَئِمَّةً يَهْدُونَ بِأَمْرِنَا وَأَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْهِمْ فِعْلَ الْخَيْرَاتِ وَإِقَامَ الصَّلَاةِ وَإِيتَاءَ الزَّكَاةِ ۖ وَكَانُوا لَنَا عَابِدِينَ
>
> *Wa-jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan yahdūna bi-amrinā wa-awḥaynā ilayhim fiʿla l-khayrāti wa-iqāma ṣ-ṣalāti wa-ītāʾa z-zakāti wa-kānū lanā ʿābidīn.*
>
> **"And We made them leaders guiding by Our command, and We revealed to them the doing of good, and the establishing of prayer, and the giving of zakāh; and to Us alone they were worshippers."**

---

## Introduction — The World Before the Word

Stay with me for a moment, before we open this ayah.

Imagine you are standing in a room full of people. Someone walks in. They have done nothing yet — said nothing, taught nothing, written nothing. But the moment they enter, the room reorganizes around them. Voices soften. Bodies turn. There is a center of gravity now where there wasn't one before.

What happened?

Something we don't have a clean word for in English. We say "presence." We say "authority." We say "they're a leader." But these words don't really name the thing. They name the effect of the thing.

The Arabic word is *imām*.

And the Quran, in this ayah, makes a claim about this kind of person that runs against almost every assumption we have inherited from the modern world.

The world we live in says: a leader is what you become through effort. You build a platform. You earn followers. You speak until people listen. You "make a name" for yourself. Leadership is a verb you do *to* yourself.

This ayah says something else. Something almost shocking when you really sit with it.

*Wa-jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan* — "And **We** made them imams."

[PAUSE]

The verb is in the first person plural of divine majesty. The active subject is Allah. The prophets and their descendants — Ibrāhīm, Isḥāq, Yaʿqūb, after a life of trials we have just read about across the previous ayahs — were not *self-made*. They were *made*. By Someone Else.

And the surah we are sitting inside — *Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ*, the surah of the Prophets — has spent thirty ayahs preparing us for this moment. We have watched Ibrāhīm take an axe to idols (21:58). We have watched him thrown into a fire (21:68). We have watched the fire be commanded to be cool (21:69). We have watched him migrate to "the land We have blessed for the worlds" (21:71). We have watched him be given Isḥāq, and Yaʿqūb as a *bonus on top* of the gift (21:72).

And then this ayah arrives — *and the whole journey is named*. All of that was Allah making them imams.

Here is the hook. Here is what we are going to follow.

**The ayah moves in a descent.** It begins on the outside — *imams, guides, public figures the world can see* — and it travels inward, layer by layer, until it reaches a single phrase at the very end: *wa-kānū lanā ʿābidīn* — "and to Us alone they were worshippers." The visible role on the outside. The invisible posture on the inside. And what we are going to discover is that the outside is what we see, but the inside is what made the outside possible.

[PAUSE]

If you have ever wondered why Allah accepts some people and not others doing the same actions — this ayah is going to show you. If you have ever wondered why some teachers transform you and some leave you cold — this ayah is going to show you. If you have ever wondered whether *you* could ever be the kind of person whose presence reorganizes a room toward Allah — this ayah is going to show you what would have to be true for that to happen.

Let's begin.

---

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

We are going to slow down on three things in this ayah. Three pieces of grammar, each one small enough to miss, each one carrying the weight of the entire verse.

### First Element — *Jaʿalnāhum* and the Verb of Constitution

Let me start with a concept that exists in English but is hard to see clearly until you name it.

There is a difference between *making* and *becoming*.

When water turns to ice, the water *becomes* ice. The change is internal, gradual, the result of conditions the water didn't choose. When a craftsman takes wood and carves a chair, the craftsman *makes* the chair. The chair did not become a chair on its own. It was *constituted* into a chair by an act of will from outside it.

In Arabic there is a verb that specifically carries this second meaning — the verb of *constitution from outside*. Not *becoming*. Not even *creating from nothing*. **Constituting** — taking something and *appointing it into* a status, a role, a function it did not have before.

That verb is *jaʿala*.

It is one of the great verbs of the Quran. *Jaʿala lakum mina l-ʾarḍi firāshan* — "He **made** for you the earth a bed" (2:22). The earth existed. But its *function* — being a bed for you — that was a divine appointment. *Jaʿala l-laylawa n-nahāra khilfatan* — "He **made** the night and day in succession." The night and day exist. But their *role* in your life — that was constituted.

Now hear the opening of our ayah again.

*Wa-jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan*.

"And We **constituted** them imams."

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means. Ibrāhīm did not *become* an imam. The world did not *recognize* him into an imam. His followers did not *vote* him into an imam. He was *constituted* into one. By Allah. From outside.

There is even a more famous moment of this same verb in this same role. Surah al-Baqarah, ayah 124. Allah says to Ibrāhīm: *innī jāʿiluka li-n-nāsi imāman* — "Indeed I am going to **constitute** you, for the people, an imam." And Ibrāhīm — and this is the part that should make us tremble — Ibrāhīm asks: *wa-min dhurriyyatī?* — "and from my descendants?" And Allah's answer comes: *lā yanālu ʿahdī ẓ-ẓālimīn* — "My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers."

Stay with that for a moment.

Imamate, in the Quranic vocabulary, is a *covenant*. It is something *Allah grants*. Even Ibrāhīm, the friend of Allah, the one whose hand was already on the hammer of the idols — even he asks for it as a request, not a right. And even his request can only reach those who are not wrongdoers. *He cannot transmit it down his bloodline by sheer paternity.* It must be re-bestowed in every generation.

This is a vision of leadership absolutely opposite to the one we have been trained on.

Modernity says: build it. Earn it. Claim it. Promote it. The Quran says: it is *constituted* upon you, or it is *not yours at all*.

Here is what this changes — for you, sitting where you are, listening.

The pressure modern people feel to *make ourselves into something* — to become important, recognized, influential — that pressure is, in Quranic vocabulary, a category error. It is treating a *jaʿala*-verb as if it were a *ṣāra*-verb (the verb of becoming). It is acting as if you can climb your way into a status that, according to the deepest sentence in this ayah, is not climbable. It can only be granted.

And what *can* be done is the opposite — the inward work, the *ʿibādah* at the end of the ayah, that makes you *receivable*. You don't make yourself an imam. You make yourself someone *upon whom* imamate could be bestowed, if Allah wills.

That is a completely different vocation. It looks nothing like a career. It looks like a life of quiet obedience that the world may or may not ever notice — until the day Allah decides to let the room reorganize around you. And maybe never in this life. Maybe only on the Day when *aʾimmah* will be called by their *imām*, and your name is read.

[PAUSE]

Hold this insight with me. *Jaʿalnāhum*. They were made. Constituted. From outside.

This is the first wall of the building. Now let us turn to the second.

### Second Element — *Bi-amrinā* and the Direction of the Source

Look at the next clause: *yahdūna bi-amrinā* — "guiding by Our command."

It would be easy to skim past this little preposition *bi-* — "by." But Arabic grammar does not skim. Every preposition is doing specific work.

Here is the concept first. In English we say things like "she did it *with* her own hands" or "he opened the door *with* a key." That little word *with* is functioning as an *instrument*. The hands and the key are not the *source* of the action — they are the *means through which* the source acts.

Arabic has a very precise version of this. It is called *bāʾ al-istiʿānah* — "the *bāʾ* of seeking-aid," or the *bāʾ* of instrumentality. When this *bi-* shows up before a noun, it means: the actor is acting *through* this thing as their tool, not *because* this thing is the source.

Now let us read the clause again.

*Yahdūna bi-amrinā* — "they guide by means of **Our** command."

Notice what this is saying about the prophets — and notice what it is *not* saying.

It is saying: they guide. They are real guides. The verb of guidance is given to them. *Yahdūna* — they actively, presently, ongoingly guide.

But the *bi-* immediately follows and says: *not by their command*. The instrument of their guidance is not their own intellect, their own intuition, their own program, their own personality. The instrument is *amrunā* — Our command. The prophets are conduits. They guide *by means of* something that did not originate in them.

This may sound like a small linguistic distinction. But place it next to its mirror image, twenty-six surahs away, and the entire sky lights up.

Surah al-Qaṣaṣ, ayah 41. Allah is talking about Pharaoh and his elite. And He says: *wa-jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan yadʿūna ilā n-nār* — "and We made them leaders calling **toward the fire**."

[PAUSE]

Look at how exact the parallel is. Same verb of constitution — *jaʿalnāhum*. Same word — *aʾimmatan*. The grammar is identical. **The Quran is putting two kinds of leaders on the same shelf.** Both are imams. Both were *constituted* by Allah's decree (in the sense that nothing escapes His decree). Both *lead*.

But look at what changes. In our ayah: *yahdūna bi-amrinā* — guiding **by Our command**. In the Pharaoh ayah: *yadʿūna ilā n-nār* — calling **toward the fire**.

Two pieces have changed. The verb has changed — from *yahdūna* (guide) to *yadʿūna* (call out, summon). And the prepositional phrase has changed — from *bi-amrinā* (by Our command) to *ilā n-nār* (toward the fire).

Here is what the contrast is teaching us. **Every imam is sourced somewhere and pointed somewhere.** What makes one imam an imam of light and another an imam of fire is not whether they have followers, not whether they have charisma, not whether the world celebrates them. *Pharaoh had all of those.* What makes one an imam of light is the *bāʾ* — the source they draw from — and the *ilā* — the direction they point toward.

The prophet's *bi-amrinā* says: my source is not me. I draw from above. The fire-leader's *ilā n-nār* says: my destination is the fire I am leading my followers into. Both are real leaders. Only one is real guidance.

*Tafsīr al-Muyassar* glosses our ayah in almost these same terms: Ibrāhīm, Isḥāq, and Yaʿqūb were made *qudwatan lil-nās* — a model for people — calling them to the worship of Allah and obedience to Him, *bi-idhnihī*, "by His permission." Two different tafsīr traditions, two different words — *amr* here, *idhn* there — landing on the identical claim: the calling was licensed from above. It was never self-authorized.

[PAUSE]

And this is where the ayah pierces our modern condition.

Almost every "leader" we encounter on a screen is a *yadʿūna*-leader — someone who has built an audience, who has captured attention, who is calling that attention *toward something*. Toward a product. Toward a worldview. Toward a self-image. Toward themselves. The Quranic question this ayah asks us to bring to every leader, every teacher, every shaykh, every influencer, every politician, every author, *every voice that asks for our attention* — is just two questions.

By what *bāʾ*? Toward what *ilā*?

By what source do they speak — their own command, or a command from above? And toward what destination are they calling — light, or the fire?

If we were trained to ask only these two questions of every voice that asks to be followed, our spiritual lives would be transformed.

Stay with this. Two prepositions can carry the difference between paradise and hellfire when applied to leadership. *Bi-amrinā*. The first wall of the prophet's life is that he draws his guidance from above. The world looks at him and sees a man speaking. Allah looks at him and sees a man through whom *amrunā* is moving.

This is the second wall. Now the third.

### Third Element — *Awḥaynā Ilayhim Fiʿla l-Khayrāt* and the Revelation of Doing

Now the most surprising linguistic move in the entire ayah. And one that classical commentators paused over because it is genuinely unusual.

*Wa-awḥaynā ilayhim fiʿla l-khayrāti wa-iqāma ṣ-ṣalāti wa-ītāʾa z-zakāt.*

"And We revealed to them **the doing of** good deeds, and the **establishing** of prayer, and the **giving** of zakāh."

Let me ask you to feel the weight of three Arabic words you might normally pass right over. *Fiʿl. Iqām. Ītāʾ.*

In Arabic, when you want to refer to an *action*, you have a choice. You can name the *thing* the action is about — *al-khayr*, "the good." Or you can name *the doing of the thing* — *fiʿl al-khayr*, "the doing of the good." Grammatically, *fiʿl* is the *maṣdar* — the verbal noun, the noun of *the act itself*. Same with *iqām* — it's not just *prayer* (*ṣalāh*) but *the establishing of prayer*. And *ītāʾ* — it's not just *zakāh* but *the giving of zakāh*.

Why does this matter?

Because Allah did not have to phrase it this way. He could have said *awḥaynā ilayhim al-khayrāt wa-ṣ-ṣalāta wa-z-zakāh* — "We revealed to them good deeds and prayer and zakāh." That would be a perfectly Arabic sentence. And it would mean: We taught them about these things.

But Allah did not say that. He inserted the *maṣdar* of action — *fiʿl, iqām, ītāʾ*. And the difference, when you sit with it, is staggering.

[PAUSE]

What was revealed to them, the ayah is saying, was not *information about* good deeds. It was **the doing of them**. *Fiʿl al-khayrāt.* The verb itself was the revelation.

We have to slow down here because this is alien to almost everything we assume about how learning works.

We assume: revelation is a download of *content*. Allah sends words. The words contain *information*. The recipient *learns* the information. Then the recipient, on his own, has to *figure out how to act on* the information. The information is the gift. The action is the human contribution.

This ayah subverts that.

It says: *the doing was revealed*. Allah did not just give them the *what*; He gave them the *how-to-do*. The act itself was inhabited by revelation. The standing in prayer was inspired. The bending. The portioning out of wealth — *that very act, the gesture of releasing it from the hand* — was revelation moving through the body of the prophet.

This is why prophets are sent and not just books are sent. A book can deliver *al-khayrāt*. Only a living prophet can deliver *fiʿl al-khayrāt*. You needed to *see* Ibrāhīm break the idols. You needed to *see* the Prophet ﷺ pray, so that ʿĀʾishah could later say *kāna khuluquhū l-Qurʾān* — "his character was the Quran." The Quran *as lived* was the revelation. Not the Quran *as known*.

Now sit with what this means for us.

[PAUSE]

If revelation is the *fiʿl* — the doing — then *we cannot become inheritors of revelation by accumulating knowledge*. We become inheritors by inheriting the doing. By inheriting *how* a prophet stood in prayer. *How* a prophet gave from his wealth. *How* a prophet kept his tongue. *How* a prophet wept in the night. The information about all of this is in books. The *fiʿl* of all of this can only be transmitted, body to body, generation to generation, through a living chain of human beings *doing it*.

This is one of the deepest reasons Islamic civilization is built on *isnād* — chains of transmission, *living teachers*, the disciple who sits at the feet of the master not to *learn information from him* but to *watch him do it*. The *fiʿl* travels by witness. The information travels by page.

There is a small phrase in the tradition: *al-shaykh la yaqūlu lak iqraʾ — al-shaykh yaqūlu lak unẓur* — "the shaykh does not say 'read'; the shaykh says 'look.'" Not because reading is bad — but because *fiʿl* needs an eye, not a page.

And here is the part that comes back to us. If you are sitting somewhere right now, listening, and you are wondering why all the books you have read have not transformed you — this ayah is one of the answers. The transformation is not in the books. *Allah revealed the doing.* The doing is what changes you. And the doing is learned by doing alongside someone whom Allah has already changed.

Notice the specific actions Allah names:

*Iqām al-ṣalāh* — "**establishing** prayer." The verb here is *aqāma* (form IV), built on the root *qāf-wāw-mīm*, which means *to stand upright*. *Iqām* is not just *praying*. It is *making prayer stand*. It is *uprighting* the prayer. There is a prayer that lies down — performed, technically valid, but not *standing*. There is a prayer that *qāma* — that holds itself upright. The revealed *fiʿl* is the second.

There is even a small grammatical fingerprint of this in the word's shape. The full noun-form of the verbal-noun would be *iqāmah*, with a closing *tāʾ marbūṭah*. Here it appears clipped — *iqām*, the *tāʾ* dropped. Al-Jalālayn's gloss notes this is *takhfīf*, a lightening, because the word sits in construct (*iḍāfah*) with what follows it, *al-ṣalāh*. A small mechanical fact of Arabic grammar — and yet fitting: the word for "establishing" is itself pared down to what is load-bearing, nothing decorative left hanging off it, exactly as the *fiʿl* it names is meant to be — plain, upright, without ornament.

*Ītāʾ al-zakāh* — "**giving** zakāh." The verb here is from *atā*, *to come*. *Ītāʾ* is *making zakāh come* — bringing it to where it belongs. There is a giving that throws. There is a giving that brings. The revealed *fiʿl* is the second — the zakāh that *comes* to the recipient as if delivered, not flung.

Even the verbs Allah chose for the actions are *the verbs of doing-it-rightly*, not the verbs of *technical compliance*. *Make prayer stand. Make zakāh come.* These are the verbs of inhabited action.

[PAUSE]

So now look at the architecture of what we have built so far. Three linguistic walls.

**One.** *Jaʿalnāhum.* They were *made* imams. Constituted. From outside. Not self-built.

**Two.** *Bi-amrinā.* They guide *by means of* a source not their own. They are conduits, not origins.

**Three.** *Awḥaynā ilayhim fiʿla l-khayrāt.* What was revealed to them was the *doing*, not the *information*. They are inhabited actors, not informed teachers.

Three walls of a single house. The house, taken as a whole, is showing us what a real imam *is* — and how absolutely different that is from what the modern world calls a leader.

Now let us ask what this is showing us not just about words, but about reality itself.

## Part 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The Ache to Matter, and the Wrong Door In

Before we open the theme, sit for a moment in something every modern person has felt.

There is an ache in us — almost unspeakable in polite society — to *matter*. To be someone whose absence would be noticed. To have lived in a way that left a print. We don't always admit it. But it is there, under the small talk, under the scrolling. Why do we curate our lives for an audience we don't even like? Why does the silence of being unfollowed sting so disproportionately? Why does the small piece of recognition — a name pronounced, a hand shaken, a comment from someone we admire — feel so out of proportion to its size?

It is the ache of *imamate* misdirected.

Allah created in the human soul a longing to be *constituted* — to be granted significance by something larger than oneself. That longing is not wrong. It is the hardware of being made in such a way that we cannot live without meaning beyond our own four walls. The problem is not the ache. The problem is the door we walk through to satisfy it.

The modern door is: *make yourself*. Build your platform. Curate your image. Climb the ladder. Earn the recognition. The door promises that if you walk through it long enough, the ache will be satisfied. You will *matter*.

But the door is a trick.

Because the ache is for *jaʿala* — to be *constituted* — and the modern door offers only *ṣāra* — to *become* through your own striving. The door cannot deliver what the ache asks for. The ache wants to be *given* significance by Someone above. The door offers only significance you scrape together yourself. So you walk through the door, you do everything it asks, and at the end you find that the ache has not been satisfied. *You scraped together importance, but no One looked down and said "I have made you something."* And so you go back, and you scrape again, and the cycle eats your life.

This is what the ayah is naming when it begins *jaʿalnāhum*.

Ibrāhīm was put through fire. Migrated. Lost a homeland. Was given children only after being told to almost lose them. *He did not enter the modern door.* He had no platform. His audience for years was a single nephew and an old wife. By every modern metric, his life was a failure. And then — at the end of all of it — Allah said: *We constituted them imams.* And one Ibrāhīm has now mattered, by Allah's *jaʿala*, more than every emperor of his era, *combined*, who walked through the modern door.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. Because this is one of the most freeing things in the whole Quran.

You do not have to make yourself matter. The work of *making you matter* is Allah's, not yours. Your work is something else entirely — and the ayah is going to name it for us in its closing phrase. But your work is not the building of significance. *Ja'ala* is not on your to-do list. It is on His.

What changes when this lands?

The relentless self-promotion eases. The exhausting curation eases. The crushing comparison to people more "successful" eases. Because you finally understand that you were trying to do, with your own hand, what only Allah's hand can do. And He has not stopped doing it. He still constitutes imams. He still raises people. He just does not raise people *the way the door promises*. He raises the unwatched. He raises the unrecognized. He raises the ones who, while no camera was rolling, *were doing the doing*.

And that is the practical change this theme demands.

**Stop building. Start receiving.** That is the work. Stop trying to make yourself into something the world will see. Start trying to make yourself into something Allah might see worth bestowing. The first is exhausting and ends nowhere. The second is the entire prophetic path.

What does that look like tomorrow morning?

It looks like one prayer prayed when no one is watching, with the same care you would pray it in front of a thousand. It looks like one piece of your wealth given without even your own left hand being told. It looks like one sentence of remembrance whispered in the car alone, *because the audience you care about is the One who is always present and never visible*. The shift is from external metric to internal posture.

The internal posture is the *jaʿala* you make yourself eligible for. The external recognition is, and will remain, Allah's to bestow or not bestow. And the freedom in this — the freedom to live without the ache running your life — is enormous.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two — *Lanā ʿĀbidīn* — The Quiet Center That Makes Everything Else Possible

Now we come to the closing phrase of the ayah. And this is where the entire architecture lands.

*Wa-kānū lanā ʿābidīn* — "and **to Us alone** they were worshippers."

Let me show you something about Arabic word order before we open this phrase.

In standard Arabic, you would expect: *wa-kānū ʿābidīna lanā* — "and they were worshippers to Us." The predicate (*ʿābidīna*, "worshippers") usually comes before its prepositional complement (*lanā*, "to Us").

But the ayah does the opposite. It says *lanā ʿābidīn*. It moves *lanā* — "to Us" — *forward*, in front of *ʿābidīn*. The preposition jumps to the front.

In Arabic grammar this fronting is called *taqdīm*, and the classical grammarians — Sībawayh, al-Zamakhsharī, the entire chain — agree that *taqdīm* of a prepositional phrase typically yields *ikhtiṣāṣ* — *exclusivity*. By moving *lanā* forward, the sentence is no longer saying "they worshipped, and the worship was to Us." It is saying "**it was to Us, and to no one else, that they were worshippers**."

That is what the translation tries to capture by saying "**to Us alone** they were worshippers."

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment. Because of all the things this ayah could have ended with — and look at the heights it has climbed, *imams, guides, recipients of revelation, establishers of prayer, givers of zakāh* — the closing word is not any of those. The closing word is *ʿābidīn*. *Worshippers.* Servants. Slaves.

The ayah has descended from the most outer (imamate) to the most inner (servanthood). And the closing word is the smallest, plainest, most unglamorous word in the entire verse.

Why?

Because *that is the engine*.

Everything we have read in this ayah — the imamate, the guidance, the prayer, the zakāh — is the *visible architecture* of these prophets' lives. *ʿAbd*, slave-of-Allah, is the *invisible engine* that powered every visible piece. And the ayah, by ending here with the *taqdīm* of *lanā*, is making one final claim: **the engine had to be exclusive to Him.** Not "they worshipped Allah and also some other things." Not "they served Allah primarily but also their reputation." *Lanā* — to Us alone. Nothing else got a piece of the engine.

This is the secret of the imam.

Look at what an imam does outside. He stands in front. He guides. He is seen. He is followed. *Every one of those is a soul-corroding pressure*. To stand in front and not let it become a stage. To guide and not let it become control. To be seen and not let it become craving. To be followed and not let it become an empire. The outside of being an imam is the most spiritually dangerous position a human being can occupy.

What protects the imam from being destroyed by his own role?

*Lanā ʿābidīn.* The exclusive, fronted, emphatic worship of Allah at the *core* of his being. So that when the praise comes, it does not stick — because there is no one home to receive it; he is already, exclusively, *lanā*. When the criticism comes, it does not crush — because his measure of his life is not what anyone said; his measure is whether he was *lanā*. When the followers swell, it does not intoxicate. When the followers vanish, it does not devastate. The exclusive *lanā* is what allows a human to bear the weight of being made an imam without the weight crushing him into a Pharaoh.

[PAUSE]

This is the difference, again, between *yahdūna bi-amrinā* and *yadʿūna ilā n-nār*. Pharaoh stood in front too. Pharaoh was followed too. Pharaoh's *outside* and Ibrāhīm's *outside* would, to a foreign visitor, have looked structurally similar — a man, with people, leading. But Pharaoh had no *lanā ʿābidīn* engine. He was *lahu ʿābidīn* — he was a worshipper to himself. To his own image. To his own throne. And so the same outside became, on the inside, fire instead of light.

This is why the ayah ends here. The fronted *lanā* is the *single point of difference* between the prophet and the tyrant when both are doing visible leadership. The visible work can be identical. The hidden orientation is what divides them — for eternity.

What does this mean for us?

We will probably never be made into the kind of imam Ibrāhīm was made into. But every one of us is a small imam in some small space. A parent leading a child. A teacher leading a class. An older sibling leading a younger one. A friend whose words shape the thinking of another friend. A worker whose example shapes a junior. Every one of us *is followed by someone in something*.

And so every one of us has the same engine question.

Are you, in that small position, *lanā ʿābidīn*? Is the engine of your small leadership the exclusive worship of Allah? Or is the engine the praise of those who follow you — even just the silent praise, the way they look at you, the small lift of being important to them?

The honest answer for most of us is: the engine is mixed. Some *lanā*, some *lanafsī* (to myself). Some *for Him*, some *for the warmth of being looked up to*. And the ayah is showing us, very precisely, that the prophets had purified the engine. Not eliminated the role — they were imams, in front, being followed — but purified the engine inside the role. *Lanā.* Exclusive. Fronted. Nothing else gets a share.

The practical change this theme demands is the most internal of all.

**Audit the engine.** Once a day, in some private moment, ask: *for what did I do that?* The teaching, the helping, the kind word, the quiet act of leadership — *for what?* If the honest answer is "partly for Him, partly for the way it makes me look," then the ayah is asking you to do, today, one small re-purification. One act done so privately, so unwitnessed, so undocumented, that there is *nothing in it for the self*. Just *lanā*. Just to Him. Build that muscle. Because that muscle is the engine that will determine whether, on the day Allah may or may not constitute you as an imam in some small or large space, the position destroys you or sanctifies you.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now look at what the ayah has done.

It opens with the most outer thing — the public role, the imamate. *Wa-jaʿalnāhum aʾimmatan.* The world sees this.

Then it moves one layer in. Where does the imam's *guidance* come from? Not from himself — *yahdūna bi-amrinā*. The source is above.

Then another layer in. *What* exactly was given to them? Not just information. The *doing*. *Awḥaynā ilayhim fiʿla l-khayrāt.* The act itself was inhabited.

Then the specific actions named — *iqām al-ṣalāh, ītāʾ al-zakāh.* Prayer that stands. Zakāh that comes. The visible practice.

And then, finally, the deepest layer — the one phrase that made all the others possible.

*Wa-kānū lanā ʿābidīn.*

To Us alone they were worshippers.

The ayah has descended from the visible to the invisible, from the outside to the inside, from what people see to what only Allah sees. And the architectural argument it has built, by the end, is irreversible: **the outer is real because the inner was real.** The imamate was real because the guidance was sourced. The guidance was real because the *doing* was revealed. The doing was real because the engine was *lanā*.

Take away the closing phrase, and the whole tower falls. The imamate without exclusive worship is Pharaoh. The guidance without exclusive worship is misdirection. The prayer without exclusive worship is a body bending in space. The ayah is teaching us, by its very shape, that the visible Islamic life is *worth what its hidden engine is worth*. Nothing more. Nothing less.

### Bringing It Together

The two journeys — linguistic and thematic — were the same insight from two directions.

Linguistically, three walls: *jaʿalnāhum* (constitution from above), *bi-amrinā* (source from above), *fiʿla l-khayrāt* (doing as revelation).

Thematically, two truths: the ache to matter is satisfied only by *jaʿala*, not by self-making; and the visible life is sustained only by the hidden engine of *lanā ʿābidīn*.

Both are saying the same thing from opposite sides of the same wall. **The Islamic life is one in which you stop trying to make yourself, and you start trying to be makeable.** You stop building toward visibility, and you start building toward worship. And then *whatever* Allah decides to do with you, on the outside, you are at peace with — because the work was never the outside. The work was always the engine.

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you. Sit with them. Don't rush to answer.

1. Where in my life am I trying to perform *jaʿala* with my own hand — to *make myself* into something — when the ayah is telling me that work was never mine to do?

2. The voices I follow — by what *bāʾ* are they sourced, and toward what *ilā* are they pointing? Have I ever asked these two questions of the people I learn from?

3. When was the last time I learned a *fiʿl* — a doing — by sitting alongside someone who was already inhabited by it? And if I cannot name a person, what is missing in my path?

4. If I audit my engine honestly — for what did I do that? — how much of it was *lanā*, exclusively to Him, and how much was for the warmth of being seen as someone who did it?

5. Is there one small act today that I could do so privately, so unwitnessed, that nothing in it lands anywhere except in His knowledge alone?

### One Sentence

*The imam is not the one who built himself; the imam is the one whose engine was so exclusively for Allah that Allah constituted him to be seen.*

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not, in the end, a description of distant prophets. It is a blueprint of a kind of life. A life in which your work is interior — purifying the engine, *lanā*, again and again — and the exterior is *His* work, not yours. A life in which the ache to matter is finally, mercifully, redirected from the door it cannot satisfy to the door it was always made for.

You will probably not become Ibrāhīm. But you can take a single line from his architecture — *wa-kānū lanā ʿābidīn* — and let it become the standard against which you measure every act of your day. And in the measuring, you become slowly more makeable. And whatever Allah decides to make of you, you will be at peace with, because your peace was never in the outcome. Your peace was in the engine.

### A Du'ā from These Words

O Allah, You who are the One who *constitutes* — *jāʿil* — make us, in whatever small space You have placed us, into people whose engines are exclusively *to You*. Source our guidance from Your command, not from our own. Reveal to us not just the knowledge of good but the *doing* of it. And let our worship of You be so fronted, so exclusive, that whatever role You bestow upon us — large or small, seen or unseen — does not corrupt us, but sanctifies us. Make us *makeable*. And let our final word in every act be *lanā* — to You, and to no one else.

*Āmīn.*
