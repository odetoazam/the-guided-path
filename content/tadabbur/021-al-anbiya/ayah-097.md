# When the Eyes Finally Open

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 97

> **وَاقْتَرَبَ الْوَعْدُ الْحَقُّ فَإِذَا هِيَ شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا يَا وَيْلَنَا قَدْ كُنَّا فِي غَفْلَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا بَلْ كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ**

*Wa-qtaraba al-waʿdu al-ḥaqq, fa-idhā hiya shākhiṣatun abṣāru alladhīna kafarū. Yā waylanā qad kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā, bal kunnā ẓālimīn.*

> "And the True Promise has drawn near; then suddenly, fixed and staring are the eyes of those who disbelieved: *O woe to us — we had indeed been in heedlessness of this; rather, we were wrongdoers.*"

---

## Introduction: The World That Has To Be Rebuilt

Before we open this ayah, we have to remember where we are in the surah.

Surah Al-Anbiya does not start gently. It opens like a door kicked in.

> **اقْتَرَبَ لِلنَّاسِ حِسَابُهُمْ وَهُمْ فِي غَفْلَةٍ مُّعْرِضُونَ**
>
> *"The reckoning has drawn near for the people, and they are in heedlessness, turning away."* (21:1)

That is the first sentence of the surah. Not a greeting. Not a story. A diagnosis. **The reckoning has come close — and you don't even know it.**

Then the surah does something remarkable. It does not lecture. It does not threaten further. It tells stories. It walks us through Ibrāhīm holding the fire as cool. Through Mūsā and Hārūn given the criterion. Through Dāwūd and Sulaymān, mountains and birds praising with them. Through Ayyūb crying out, *"harm has touched me."* Through Yūnus in three layers of darkness saying, *"there is no god but You — glory be to You — I have been of the wrongdoers."* Through Zakariyyā, who feared he had no inheritor, calling out at midnight. Through Maryam, whose chastity Allah names. A long, sustained gallery — every figure marked by the same quality.

**They held the unseen close.** They lived as if the Promise were already at the door. That is what made them prophets. That is the entire architecture of *iman* the surah is showing us.

And then, after walking us through them all — after we have spent the surah inside the inner life of those who saw rightly — the surah turns to those who saw wrongly. And it does something extraordinary. It returns to its opening line. It picks up the very word it began with — *iqtaraba* — and it lets the heedless themselves now speak it.

That is verse 97. The bracket of the surah closes. What Allah declared in the first sentence — the reckoning is near, and they are heedless — is now being spoken aloud by the heedless about themselves.

But before we trace only the literary arc, we need to hold what the classical commentators keep at the center of this verse. Verse 97 does not arrive in isolation. It sits immediately after verse 96, which describes a specific eschatological event: the breach of the barrier holding back Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj — when they surge from every elevation of the earth and spread swiftly across it. Al-Ṭabarī is explicit on this: *wa-qtaraba al-waʿdu al-ḥaqq* names the moment *after* that event — the promise Allah made to His servants that He would resurrect them from their graves for judgment, reward, and punishment. This is not a general approaching. It is a *triggered* approach. Once the barrier opens, the Promise is in its final stages of arrival. Al-Ṭabarī preserves a narration from Ḥudhayfah on precisely this point: *"If a man were to wean a foal after the emergence of Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj, he would not ride it before the Day of Resurrection"* — the interval from that trigger to the Hour is too compressed for even the simplest human project to complete. Al-Muyassar follows the same sequence: the barrier opens, they surge from the elevated places of the earth and spread swiftly across its sides, the Day of Resurrection draws near and its terrors appear — and then the frozen eyes. The surah's literary bracket and this eschatological trigger are not two competing readings. They illuminate the same moment from two different angles. The bracket reveals the surah's architecture. The Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj sequence reveals the specific occasion on which that architecture arrives at its close.

They are finally agreeing with the diagnosis.

But the diagnosis is being spoken on the wrong side of the door.

[PAUSE]

So here is what we have to ask before we even enter the words:

**What does it cost a person to spend a life calling something far when it was always near? And what does the moment of finally seeing it look like — when the seeing is no longer of any use?**

The whole surah has been preparing us to see this scene. Let us now look at it carefully.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to walk through three small features of this ayah — three places where the Arabic is doing something the translation cannot quite carry. Each one will open a window. Stay with each for as long as it takes.

---

### Element 1: *Iqtaraba* — The Perfect Tense for an Event Still to Come

Let us start with the very first word of the ayah. **Wa-qtaraba**. *And it has drawn near.*

We need to slow down here, because Arabic is doing something we have to teach before we can hear it.

In Arabic, verbs have two basic tenses: the *māḍī* (perfect — completed action) and the *muḍāriʿ* (imperfect — ongoing or future action). If you want to say *"the Hour will come,"* the natural form is the imperfect: *taʾtī al-sāʿah*. If you want to say *"the Hour has come,"* you use the perfect: *atat al-sāʿah*.

But Arabic does something the English ear does not expect. **It uses the perfect tense for future events whose occurrence is so certain that it is rhetorically described as if already accomplished.** Grammarians call this *al-māḍī al-muḥaqqaq* — the "perfect of certainty." It is not a grammatical mistake. It is a deliberate compression of time. The future event is brought into the past by the force of its inevitability.

When Allah opens Surah al-Naḥl with *atā amru-llāh* — *"the command of Allah has come"* — the Hour has not literally arrived. But its arrival is so guaranteed that it is spoken in the past. The grammar itself collapses the gap between what is going to happen and what has happened.

Now. Look at the first word of our ayah.

**اقْتَرَبَ** — *iqtaraba*. *Has drawn near.*

This is not in the imperfect. Not *yaqtaribu* — "is approaching." It is in the **perfect**. The closeness is not in process. The closeness has already happened.

[PAUSE]

But notice something even more particular. The verb is not the simple Form I — *qaruba* (to be near). It is Form VIII — *iqtaraba*. Form VIII in Arabic carries a reflexive or intensifying force. *Qaruba* means "it is near." *Iqtaraba* means something closer to *"it has brought itself near"* — the closeness is intrinsic, not external. Not "someone is dragging the Hour toward us." The Hour is, by its own nature, drawing itself closer with every breath.

So we have a verb that combines two intensifications:
- The **perfect tense** says: this is so certain, treat it as already done.
- **Form VIII** says: this is not a passive being-pushed-near. The thing is moving on its own gravity. Closing the distance under its own weight.

Hold that for a moment. The Promise — the entire architecture of accountability, judgment, justice — is described not as a far horizon but as a thing that is **walking toward you**.

[PAUSE]

Now let us widen the lens.

This same word — *iqtaraba* — opens the surah. Verse 1: *iqtaraba lin-nāsi ḥisābuhum*. The reckoning has drawn near for the people. And here, near the surah's end, verse 97: *wa-qtaraba al-waʿdu al-ḥaqq*. The True Promise has drawn near.

The surah is bracketed by the same verb. The architecture is deliberate.

Notice what changes inside the bracket. In verse 1, the *object* is *al-ḥisāb* — the reckoning. In verse 97, the object is *al-waʿd al-ḥaqq* — **the True Promise**. The same approaching reality is named two different ways. In the surah's opening warning, it is called what people fear: *the reckoning*. In its closing scene, when the heedless are seeing it, it is called what it really is: *the True Promise*. Allah promised it. Allah's promise is true. That is its essence. The reckoning is what we feel when it arrives. The True Promise is what it actually is.

And one more thing. The word **al-ḥaqq**. Often translated *"the true."* But *ḥaqq* in Arabic means more than "true." It means **what is real, what is rightly owed, what corresponds to actual reality**. *Al-waʿdu al-ḥaqq* is not just *"the true promise"* — it is *the promise that is reality itself*. The Promise that is more real than what you can touch right now. The Promise that, when it arrives, will make this world look like the dream that ends when you wake up.

The classical anchoring of this verse to the Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj trigger sharpens the grammar still further. The *perfect of certainty* and the *triggered occasion* work together: the Promise is not approaching in the abstract; it is approaching because a specific event has occurred. The grammar's certainty has, in the classical reading, a concrete moment behind it.

So what has the first word done? It has placed us inside a universe where:

> **What you cannot see is closer than what you can see. What you treat as far is what is actually near. And the reality you treat as solid is the one that will dissolve.**

That is the entire ontology of the surah, sitting in one verb.

### Element 2: *Fa-idhā hiya shākhiṣah* — The Pronoun That Comes Before Its Meaning

Now we move to one of the strangest constructions in the ayah. Read the second half slowly:

> **فَإِذَا هِيَ شَاخِصَةٌ أَبْصَارُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا**
>
> *Fa-idhā hiya shākhiṣatun abṣāru alladhīna kafarū.*

Word for word: *Then suddenly — she/it — staring — eyes of those who disbelieved.*

Listen to that order. **The pronoun "hiya" — "she/it" — appears before we know what it refers to.** In Arabic this is unusual. Normally a pronoun comes after its referent has been established. You say "the eyes" first, and then later when you mention them again you can say "they."

But here, Allah places the pronoun *first*. *Fa-idhā hiya...* — *"and suddenly, she..."* — and we, the listener, are forced to wait. *She* what? *It* what? Who?

Then the next word: *shākhiṣah* — *staring, frozen.*

Still we don't know what is staring.

Only on the next word — *abṣār*, *eyes* — do we finally find out.

This construction has a name in Arabic grammar. Some grammarians call it the *ḍamīr al-shaʾn* — the *"pronoun of the matter"* — though more precisely here it functions as an anticipatory pronoun, a forward-pointing *hiya* that creates a small grammatical suspense. The pronoun arrives before its meaning. The mind is held one beat in not-yet-knowing.

[PAUSE]

Why? Why force us to wait?

Because **the grammar enacts the shock**.

Think about what is happening on the Day. The disbelievers are seeing something. We do not yet know what their face looks like. The *fa-idhā* — *"then suddenly"* — has set us up for a reveal. And then Allah delays the reveal by one word. *Hiya.* "She." "It." We the listener are now in the same suspended moment they are in. We are also waiting. We are also held in a half-second of *what is happening?*

And then: *shākhiṣah*. **Frozen. Staring. Transfixed.**

[PAUSE]

The word *shākhiṣah* comes from the root *sh-kh-ṣ*. In its concrete form *shakhṣ* refers to a figure that stands out, that rises up, that is fixed in its place — a silhouette on the horizon that does not move. Applied to eyes, *shākhiṣah* means **eyes that have locked open and will not blink**. Eyes that have stopped doing what eyes do. Frozen in the open position. Stupefied.

Al-Muyassar glosses this with precise, physiological language: these are eyes opened *min shiddat al-fazaʿ* — from the sheer intensity of terror — *lā takādu taṭrif*: barely blinking. Not the unfocused gaze of someone lost in thought. The eye's own reflex has been overridden. The body has crossed a threshold past which normal function does not return.

This is not the description of someone weeping. Weeping comes from grief that is being processed, however painfully. This is the moment **before** weeping. The moment when the mind has not yet caught up to what the eye is seeing. The moment when the body has frozen because the categories of reality the person was using have just collapsed.

You have seen this look on a human face once or twice in your life — perhaps in a hospital corridor when terrible news arrives, perhaps at the scene of an accident. The body stops. The eyes lock. The person is, for one suspended second, no longer entirely in the room. Their interior architecture is being demolished by what they have just seen, and there is no expression yet — because expression requires the architecture to still be standing.

[PAUSE]

Now consider what they are seeing. Not pain yet. They are not yet inside the punishment. They are seeing **the thing they spent a lifetime calling far**. They are seeing it close. The True Promise — *al-waʿdu al-ḥaqq* — is in front of their eyes. And it does not look the way they imagined it. It does not look like a metaphor, or a story, or "what some preacher said." It is here. It has weight. It has presence. It is *real* in a way the dunya never was.

The frozen eye is the face of someone whose **map of reality has just been overturned**. They had organized their lives around a different geometry — what is near is the world I can touch, what is far is the world I cannot. And now the geometry has been inverted. What they could touch is gone. What they could not see is the only thing left.

Notice the gender concord of the Arabic. *Shākhiṣah* is feminine — because in Arabic, broken plurals like *abṣār* take feminine concord. So the adjective and the noun match. But there is something else. By placing *shākhiṣah* — the staring — *before* *abṣār* — the eyes — Allah inverts the natural order. We see the staring before we see the eyes that are staring. **The state arrives before its location.** The condition is named before the body that bears it.

It is as if Allah is saying: *what is most important here is not whose eyes — it is the staring itself.* Whatever face you put on it, this is the look.

[PAUSE]

So what has this construction done?

It has placed us, the listener, into the same suspended beat the disbelievers are in. The grammar has taught us, by making us wait one word, what it feels like to have your reality paused. We waited for *hiya* to resolve. They are waiting for what they are seeing to resolve.

Except for them, it never does.

### Element 3: *Bal Kunnā Ẓālimīn* — The Word That Cracks the Self-Deception

We now arrive at the most psychologically devastating phrase in the ayah. Listen to it as a sequence:

> *"Yā waylanā — qad kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā — **bal** kunnā ẓālimīn."*
>
> *"Woe to us — we had certainly been in heedlessness of this — **rather**, we were wrongdoers."*

Three movements. *Woe to us. We were heedless. Rather — we were wrongdoers.*

Let me teach you a small Arabic particle that holds the whole thing together: **بَلْ** — *bal*.

*Bal* in Arabic is a particle of *iḍrāb* — of *retraction and correction*. When you say something, and then you realize what you said wasn't quite right, and you want to **correct yourself in mid-sentence**, you say *bal*. It is the Arabic of *"actually, no — it was more than that."* It is the moment a person revises their own statement upward in honesty.

So what are they revising?

They have said: *qad kunnā fī ghaflatin min hādhā* — *we had certainly been in heedlessness of this*. That is the first statement. A description that might carry, if not excuse, at least some measure of mitigation. *Ghaflah* is unawareness. *Ghaflah* could mean: it was hard to see. We were distracted. We didn't realize.

But *bal* will not let them keep it there.

**بَلْ كُنَّا ظَالِمِينَ.** *Rather — we were wrongdoers.*

They have upgraded the charge against themselves.

[PAUSE]

Notice the shift. *Ghaflah* — heedlessness — describes a relationship with *knowledge*. You didn't know, or you forgot. But *ẓulm* — wrongdoing — describes a relationship with *will*. You chose wrongly. You placed something in the wrong place. The root *ẓ-l-m* in Arabic carries the meaning of putting a thing where it does not belong — displacing something from its rightful position. The wrongdoer is someone who has disordered what should have been ordered.

And by saying *bal kunnā ẓālimīn*, they are confessing: it wasn't just ignorance. We knew. The signs were there. The message came. The warnings were given. And we *chose* the wrong geometry — we chose to treat the far as near and the near as far.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar's commentary names the specific content of their ghaflah with precision: they were *ghāfilīna ʿan hādhā al-yawm wa-ʿan al-iʿdād lahu* — heedless of this day and of *preparing for it*. The word *iʿdād* — preparation — is the operative one. Their ghaflah was not merely a philosophical error about the nature of reality. It was a practical failure to act on what they had been told. They had time. They had warnings. They heard the call. They chose not to prepare. That is what transforms negligence into wrongdoing.

That is what makes it *ẓulm*. That is what makes the *bal* an accusation, not merely a clarification. They cannot stand at the edge of eternity and say simply, *"we didn't know."* Their conscience — even on that day — corrects the record.

[PAUSE]

And notice when this correction happens.

This is not the *tawbah* — the repentance — of the dunya. Repentance in the dunya is a door. The door has weight and resistance and uncertainty — but it is *open*. What we are watching here is not repentance. The door is behind them. What we are watching is **honest accounting after the door has closed.**

The *bal* arrives too late to help. But it arrives with full honesty.

This is perhaps the most devastating detail in the whole ayah. They are still morally clear. Their conscience still knows what is right. They still have the capacity to say: *no — it was more than heedlessness — we were wrongdoers.* This capacity for honest self-accusation does not die. It survives the collapse of every other category. It survives the frozen eyes. It survives the scene of the Hour itself.

What did not survive was the time in which honest self-accusation could still become something.

[PAUSE]

There is one more dimension to the *kunnā*. They do not say: *kuntu* — "I was." They say *kunnā*. *We were.* Even in the moment of self-indictment they are speaking as a community. The ghaflah was not only a private failure — it was a collective architecture. They built a world together in which the Promise was treated as peripheral: a world in which the busy, the tangible, the immediate crowded out the coming reality until it disappeared from view entirely. And now they stand together in what that world cost them. The plural is not incidental. It names the full weight of what shared heedlessness produces.

## Part 2: The Architecture of Approach

We have walked through three linguistic windows. Let us step back and see the whole building.

The surah is called *Al-Anbiyāʾ* — The Prophets. It does not focus on any single prophet's story. It gives us a gallery. Ibrāhīm. Lūṭ. Isḥāq and Yaʿqūb. Nūḥ. Dāwūd and Sulaymān. Ayyūb. Ismāʿīl and Idrīs. Dhū al-Kifl. Dhū al-Nūn — Yūnus — in the darkness of three layered darknesses. Zakariyyā. Maryam. A sustained procession.

They are not all the same. Their circumstances are radically different. Ayyūb is in pain. Yūnus is inside a whale. Zakariyyā is afraid of dying without an inheritor. Ibrāhīm is being thrown into fire. Each is in a different kind of crisis.

But they share one thing. **They did not treat the Promise as far.**

Ayyūb cried *"harm has touched me"* — directly to Allah. Not to the world, not to people, not to circumstance. To Allah. Which means he knew there was someone to cry to. Which means the unseen address was more real to him than the visible pain.

Yūnus said *"there is no god but You — glory be to You — I have been of the wrongdoers."* In three layers of darkness, with no human to hear him, with no prospect of immediate rescue — he spoke. He spoke because he knew the speech was received. He held the Promise close even when it looked like it had abandoned him entirely.

Zakariyyā called out *"my Lord, do not leave me alone,"* and the surah tells us: *"and We responded to him."* The responsiveness of the unseen was not theoretical for Zakariyyā. It was the operative reality of his life.

This is the surah's silent argument: **those who lived inside the nearness of the Promise were given what the Promise promises.** Not ease — Ayyūb was not given ease. Not quick rescue — Yūnus spent real time in that darkness. But *response*. Connection. The reality of a living God who receives what is sent to Him.

And then, at the surah's close, we see the other kind of life. The life organized around the assumption that the Promise is peripheral. *Woe to us — we were in heedlessness.* The same capacity for honest speech that the prophets used to pray is the capacity the disbelievers use to indict themselves. The voice is the same. The direction is the same. But the door is different.

[PAUSE]

There is a question worth sitting with here.

The prophets in the gallery were not supernaturally immune to ghaflah. They were not born already awake. What made them who they were is precisely that they chose, moment after moment, to hold the Promise closer than their circumstances. Ayyūb could have organized his pain around the conclusion that the Promise was a lie. Yūnus could have organized his darkness around the conclusion that the Promise had forgotten him. They chose differently. They chose to treat the invisible as more structurally real than the visible.

We are not being shown this gallery to make us feel distant from the prophets. We are being shown it to make us understand what *iman* looks like when it is alive — and what ghaflah looks like when it wins. The surah gives us both ends. We can still choose which architecture we want to live inside.

## Part 3: The Open Door

We are still in the opening of the surah. We are still inside the *ghaflah* warning. The barrier of Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj has not yet been breached. The eyes have not yet frozen. We are not yet inside the *wa-qtaraba* event — we are inside the long space the surah spent the entire prophet-gallery building: the space where choosing differently is still possible.

Which means the *bal* we just watched them say — we can still say it now.

Not as an accusation at the sealed door. As a correction. As a turn. As what the surah has been building toward — a person who hears the diagnosis in verse 1, walks through the gallery of the prophets, arrives at verse 97, and chooses, before the freeze, to correct the geometry.

[PAUSE]

The *iʿdād* — the preparation — that al-Muyassar names as the specific content of their missed ghaflah: that is still possible for us. Preparation for the Day is not a single dramatic act. It is an orientation. It is the decision, made quietly and repeatedly, to treat what is unseen as more load-bearing than what is seen. To pray as if the address is received. To give as if the record is being kept. To speak honestly as if the account will be opened. This is precisely what each prophet in the gallery was doing — not in moments of obvious religiosity, but in the middle of their specific crises, their specific pressures, their specific forms of the human condition.

The architecture is available. The question is whether we will take it seriously before the Perfect Tense arrives.

[PAUSE]

Here is a way to sit with this ayah over the coming days.

The *bal* turn is one of the shortest corrections in the Arabic language. One word. Three letters. But in this context it moves a person from *"I didn't know"* to *"I chose wrong."* From passive victim of circumstance to honest agent.

Ask yourself: where in your own life are you using the language of ghaflah — *"I didn't realize," "I forgot," "I got distracted"* — when the more honest word is *ẓulm*? Where are you using the softer charge when you know, quietly, that the harder one is closer to the truth?

The *bal* is not punishment. It is clarity. And clarity, while the door is still open, is a form of mercy.

[PAUSE]

Notice something else. The ayah ends with *ẓālimīn* — not *ẓālamū anfusahum* ("they wronged themselves") but the simple nominal form: *they were wrongdoers.* The identity, not only the act. But the *kunnā* places it in the past — "we *were*" — which means even at the moment of confession they are reporting on a life already completed. A condition already sealed.

We are not there yet. The present tense is still available to us. We can still say not *kunnā* but *naḥnu* — "we are" — and we can still correct the architecture while correction still changes something.

## Conclusion: The Bracket and the Door

Return to the opening image for a moment.

The surah opens with a diagnosis: *the reckoning has drawn near, and they are in heedlessness, turning away.* It closes — through the gallery of every prophet who saw rightly — by letting the heedless speak the diagnosis themselves. The bracket closes. The argument is complete.

But notice what the bracket *contains*. It is not only a warning about those who failed. It is a portrait of every person who succeeded. Ibrāhīm. Ayyūb. Yūnus. Zakariyyā. Each of them, in the middle of their own version of the human condition, chose not to turn away. Each of them found the opening in the bracket — the space between the diagnosis and the closing scene — and chose to live there differently.

We are inside that space now. The bracket has not closed on us.

The Promise is drawing near — *iqtaraba*, Form VIII, by its own weight — and we know it, because we have been told. The prophets knew it because it was their lived reality, moment to moment. We know it because the surah has told us, and because every generation before us heard the same message and was given the same choice.

The question is not whether the Promise is near. The question is whether we will have frozen eyes when it arrives — or whether we will be among those who had already been living inside its nearness long before the barrier opened.

*Yā waylanā* is the cry of those who finally see it and have nothing to do with the seeing.

The prophets are the portrait of those who had always been seeing — and spent every ordinary day building something inside that sight.

The surah gives us both portraits. It will not choose for us.
