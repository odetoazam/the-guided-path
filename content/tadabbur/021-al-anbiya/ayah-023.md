# The One Who Stands Outside the Courtroom

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 23

> [ayah:21:23] لَا يُسْـَٔلُ عَمَّا يَفْعَلُ وَهُمْ يُسْـَٔلُونَ
>
> *Lā yus'alu ʿammā yafʿalu wa hum yus'alūn.*
>
> "He is not questioned about what He does — but they will be questioned."

---

## Introduction: The World Behind This Sentence

Before we open this ayah, sit with the world it stepped into.

Surah Al-Anbiya is a surah about the prophets — those who carried the message across centuries, who were mocked, who were called madmen, who watched their own people accuse them of forging Allah's words. The whole surah is a reckoning. It opens with: **"Their reckoning has drawn near for the people, while they turn away in heedlessness"** (21:1). The clock is ticking. The accounting is approaching.

And in this surah, the people of Makkah are doing something specific: they are questioning. They are interrogating. They are demanding. *Why this messenger? Why not an angel? Why this sign? Why this Quran?* They have placed Muhammad ﷺ in the dock. They have placed his message in the dock. They are behaving — and this is the heart of it — they are behaving as though the entire revelation must justify itself before their tribunal.

Then comes ayah 22 — the famous proof: **"Had there been within them gods besides Allah, they both would have been ruined."** A perfect logical thunderclap. If two ultimate authorities existed, neither would be ultimate. Reality could not hold.

And then this ayah lands.

It is one short line. Eight words in Arabic. But it is the line that closes the courtroom forever.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Watch what Allah does with one root, repeated twice. Watch what He hides, and what He reveals. Watch how a single grammatical mirror — the same word said two ways — re-draws the entire map of who is allowed to ask whom about what.

Because what's underneath this ayah is not just a statement about Allah. It is a statement about you. About what kind of being you are. About why your every act has weight. About the strange dignity of being a creature who *can* be questioned.

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: One Word, Two Destinies — *Yus'alu* and *Yus'alūn*

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch this ayah.

In Arabic, when you take a verb and put it in the **passive voice**, something interesting happens. The action is no longer about who is *doing* it. The action becomes about who is *receiving* it. *He hit* becomes *he was hit*. *They asked* becomes *they were asked*. The spotlight moves from the actor to the one acted upon.

Now in English, passive voice is often considered weak. "Mistakes were made." Politicians use it to hide responsibility. But in Arabic, especially Quranic Arabic, the passive voice is precise. It strips away who is doing the asking, the hitting, the judging — because in many contexts, **the identity of the actor is irrelevant compared to the position of the one receiving the action.**

Hold that.

Now look at the ayah:

> **لَا يُسْـَٔلُ عَمَّا يَفْعَلُ وَهُمْ يُسْـَٔلُونَ**

There are two main verbs. Both come from the same root: **س-أ-ل** *(sa'ala)* — to ask, to question, to interrogate, to demand an account.

The first verb: **يُسْـَٔلُ** *(yus'alu)* — passive — *"He is asked / questioned."*

The second verb: **يُسْـَٔلُونَ** *(yus'alūn)* — passive — *"They are asked / questioned."*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. These two words are almost identical. *Yus'alu* and *yus'alūn*. The second is just the first with a plural ending. They share the same root, the same form, the same passive voice. They are mirror images of each other.

But one of them has a *lā* — a negation — pinned to its forehead.

And the other does not.

**Same word. Same form. Same grammar.** The only difference is one word: *lā* — *"not."*

Let's sit with this.

Allah did not say: *"I act however I will, and they will give an account."* He could have. The meaning would have been similar. But He chose to use **the exact same verb in the exact same form** for both — and let the entire weight of the difference fall on a single particle of negation.

What does that achieve?

It achieves a **grammatical mirror**. It places "Him" and "them" in identical syntactic positions, and then tilts the mirror so one side reflects nothing and the other reflects everything. The form is symmetrical; the destinies are absolute opposites.

Here's what's remarkable: the ayah does not say Allah *will not be questioned*, as though there's some scenario where the question could even arise and He simply escapes it. The Arabic *lā yus'alu* is a present-tense, generic negation. **The very category of being-questioned does not apply to Him.** It is not that He passes the audit. It is that there is no audit, has never been an audit, will never be an audit.

And on the other side — *hum yus'alūn* — the same passive, but now it stands. **They will be questioned.** Not "He will question them" — Allah did not even need to name Himself as the asker. The passive leaves the questioner unnamed because the questioner does not need to be named. **They are simply, structurally, in the position of being-asked.** That is what they are. That is what kind of being they are.

[PAUSE]

So let me name what one root, said twice, is doing.

It is showing us that the difference between Allah and creation is not a difference of *degree* — Allah being more wise, more powerful, more able to defend His decisions. The difference is a difference of **category**. They occupy the same grammatical slot — *yus'alu* — and one of them is simply, by His very nature, outside that slot. He fills the verb only as a thing to be negated. He cannot really inhabit *yus'alu*. The word slides off Him.

But the same word — the exact same word — settles on creation like a permanent garment.

This is the **grammar of tawhid**. Not just "there is no god but Allah" as a slogan. *Lā ilāha illa Allāh* in its deepest form is: *there is no being for whom the question "why did you do that?" is structurally impossible — except Him.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The One Who Is Not Named, and the Ones Who Are Pinned in Place

Now look at something else.

In the first half of the ayah — *lā yus'alu ʿammā yafʿalu* — **Allah is not named.** There is no "Allah" in this clause. There is no "He" either as an explicit pronoun — only the implicit *huwa* hidden inside the verb conjugation.

But in the second half — *wa hum yus'alūn* — there is an explicit pronoun: **هُمْ** *(hum)* — *"they."* It stands there, pinned to the front of the clause, drawn out into visible existence.

Let me teach you something about Arabic pronouns.

In Arabic, every verb already contains a pronoun inside its conjugation. *Yaf'alu* already means "he does." You do not need to add "he." So when Arabic *adds* a separate, explicit pronoun — *huwa yaf'alu*, *hum yus'alūn* — it is doing it deliberately. It is **emphasizing**. It is dragging the subject into the spotlight and saying: *yes, them, specifically, these ones.*

So here we have an asymmetry of presence:

- Allah's identity is **hidden inside the verb**, not pinned to the surface. He is referenced obliquely, by absence, by what cannot be done to Him.
- *Their* identity is **explicit, exposed, pinned**. *Hum.* Them. Drawn out and named.

Stay with me. Why?

Because **being-named is part of being-accountable.** When you stand before any tribunal, the first thing the tribunal does is establish: *who are you?* It pins your identity. It names you. It places you in the record. The accountable being must be findable, must be locatable, must have an address.

The One who cannot be questioned has no such address. **He is not in the record. He is the One who keeps the record.** His identity is not pinned to the front of the verb because His identity does not stand at any tribunal. He stands behind every tribunal, holding the gavel that the tribunal does not even know it has borrowed from Him.

So Allah does not name Himself in this clause not from modesty but from sublimity. **Naming Him in the position of the unquestionable would have implied He could have been named in the position of the questionable.** And He cannot. There is no scenario in which the question is even put. So the verb simply carries Him as an implicit interior, while *they* are pinned to the outside, faces toward the bench.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to your reading. In the very grammar of the sentence, Allah recedes into implicitness — beyond reach of the question, beyond the categories of audit and tribunal — while creation is pulled forward, made explicit, made findable, made answerable.

That is not a stylistic flourish. That is **a map of reality drawn in pronoun choice.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Unspecified *Mā* — "About What"

Now look at one more thing. The first half says: *lā yus'alu **ʿammā yafʿalu*** — *"He is not questioned about **what He does**."*

That phrase — **ʿammā yafʿal** — literally: *about what He does* — uses the relative pronoun **مَا** *(mā)* — *"what."*

Let me teach you something. In Arabic, when you want to talk about *something specific* that someone does, you can specify it. *ʿan al-amri lladhī yafʿaluh* — *"about the specific matter that he does."* You can narrow the field. You can put walls around what's being asked about.

Here, Allah does not. He uses **mā** — the broadest, most unrestricted relative pronoun in the language. *Whatever* He does. *Anything* He does. *All* of what He does. **The scope is left wide open.**

Why does this matter?

Because the human heart, when it grapples with the difficulty of divine action, almost always wants to **carve exceptions**. *Yes, Allah is not questioned… about most things… but surely about this particular thing — this loss, this injustice as I see it, this child taken too young, this prayer unanswered — surely this can be questioned.* The nafs builds a small courtroom around a specific grievance and asks Allah to enter that one room.

The ayah forecloses that move with **mā**.

*ʿAmmā yafʿal* — *about whatever He does.* Not about most of what He does. Not about the easy parts. Not about what we already understand. **Every act, every decree, every withholding, every delay, every loss, every gift — all of it falls under the same wide *mā*.** And the same *lā* — the same negation — covers all of it.

[PAUSE]

This is hard. I will not pretend it is not hard. There are sufferings in this world that the human mind, on its own, cannot file under "wisdom we just don't see." There are losses that ache for a justification we are never given.

The ayah does not soften this. It does not say *Allah will explain it later.* (Though He may, and other ayahs suggest some illumination comes.) **It says: He is not in the position of explaining at all.** The category of "Allah owes a justification for X" — the ayah dismantles that category, root and branch, by leaving the *mā* unrestricted.

But notice something. This is not a tyrant's "because I said so." Other ayahs make abundantly clear that **He is الْحَكِيمُ** — *the All-Wise.* That **He does not wrong even by an atom's weight.** That He is **الرَّحْمَٰنُ الرَّحِيمُ** — the Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful. The ayah is not denying that wisdom and mercy underlie His actions. It is denying that those qualities ever stand under our review. **The wisdom is not on trial. The mercy is not on trial. He is not on trial.** Because nothing greater than Him exists to convene the court.

And here is what is remarkable, and what classical scholars like al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī and others noticed in passages like this: the absence of being-questioned is not an absence of *meaning*. Allah's actions are saturated with meaning, with purpose, with hidden good. The ayah is not saying *His actions are arbitrary.* It is saying *the meaning is not extracted from Him by interrogation; it is held by Him in unquestionable wisdom.* He is not silent under questioning — He is silent of questioning altogether.

That is a totally different kind of silence.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From the Mirror of Words to the Mirror of Reality

So we've seen three things in this ayah's grammar:

**One** — the same word, *yus'alu*, said twice, with one negation that re-routes destiny. Same form, opposite truths. The grammar of tawhid as mirror.

**Two** — Allah hidden inside the verb, hidden from the spotlight, while *hum* — they — stand pinned and explicit. The accountable are named; the Unaccountable does not even need to be named. Being-named, being-pinned, is part of what it is to be answerable.

**Three** — *ʿammā yafʿal* — the unrestricted *mā* that sweeps every divine act under the same negation, leaving no exception, no carve-out, no small courtroom for our specific grievances.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about you, and about the strange dignity that the second half of this ayah hands you.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Exhaustion of Justification — and the One Who Stands Outside It

Have you ever noticed how much of your life is justification?

You wake up and decide what to wear. Some part of your mind is already running an internal narrative: *if so-and-so sees me in this, they'll think…* You arrive at work. You make a decision. You spend the rest of the meeting framing it. Defending it. Pre-emptively explaining it. You speak with your parents. You explain why you took this job. You explain why you didn't get married yet. You explain why you did get married. You scroll your phone. You craft a sentence in your head, deleting it before you post it, because *what will they say.*

The economy of modern life is, at root, an economy of justification. Every act is shadowed by an audit. Every choice is shadowed by an explanation. We are exhausted not just because we work too much, but because we are constantly **on the witness stand.** Sometimes the prosecution is our family. Sometimes it is our colleagues. Sometimes it is our own internal critic — the harshest of all. **You answer to a courtroom that never adjourns.**

And in our age — the age of social media, of public-facing personas — the courtroom has become global. It is not just your family who watches you now. It is potentially everyone. Every move you make can be scrutinized. Every old tweet can resurface. Every act can be reviewed by anyone, at any time.

That is a kind of suffocation our ancestors did not know in this scale. It produces a particular modern ache — the ache of **being answerable to a tribunal that never declares the case closed.**

Now hear the ayah again.

*Lā yus'alu ʿammā yafʿal.*

He is not questioned about what He does.

[PAUSE]

When you say this — when you let it land — something inside you exhales.

Because here is what the ayah is doing for you, on top of everything else: it is **showing you that the entire architecture of justification you live under is not the deepest layer of reality.** Beneath all the courtrooms, beneath all the auditors, beneath all the asking — there is **One who is not in the dock.** There is One who is not justifying Himself. There is One whose every act is unimpeachable not because He has the best lawyer but because **the very category of impeachment does not apply to Him.**

And — this is the door — when you put your forehead on the ground and say *Allāhu Akbar*, you are not addressing another, larger entity inside the tribunal of being. You are addressing the One who is **outside** the tribunal. **You are placing your case in the hands of the only One who is not also a defendant.**

Do you see what that does?

It means your prayer is not lobbying. It is not arguing. It is not making your case before a higher court. **It is laying yourself down before the One who has no case to make.** And there, finally, you can stop justifying. You can stop framing. You can stop pre-empting. You can simply be a creature, in the open, before the only One who does not need anything explained.

The Sufis spoke of *al-iftiqār* — the recognition of poverty before Allah, the dropping of the constant defense of self. This ayah is the theological root of that station. **You can drop the defense because you have arrived in the only chamber where defense is not required of anyone — neither of you (you can finally stop), nor of Him (because He never had to begin).**

[PAUSE]

But notice — the ayah does not stop there. It does not say *He is not questioned, and you are not questioned either, so relax.* It says: *He is not questioned **— but they will be questioned.***

There is a *wa* — a *but* — that turns the ayah back toward you.

So we have to ask: what does it mean to be the kind of being who *will* be questioned?

That is the second theme.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Strange Dignity of Being a Being Who Can Be Questioned

There is a temptation — a deeply modern temptation — to read *"they will be questioned"* as a threat. As a punishment. As something heavy, and grim, and to be feared.

Some of that fear is appropriate. The Day of Reckoning is real. The accounting is real. The Quran does not soften this anywhere.

But hold the other side too. **Being a being who can be questioned is also a kind of dignity.**

Think about this. **Stones are not questioned about what they do.** A boulder rolls down a hill and crushes a tree, and we do not put the boulder on trial. **Rivers are not questioned.** A river floods and destroys a village, and we do not call the river morally responsible. **Even animals are not questioned in the full moral sense** — we do not place a wolf on trial for hunting.

Why? Because none of these beings has the kind of interior — the kind of will, the kind of awareness, the kind of capacity for deliberation — that makes the question *"why did you do that?"* meaningful.

But you. **You are questioned.** *Hum yus'alūn.*

That means Allah has placed inside you something so weighty, so meaningful, so capable, that **your acts can be brought into a courtroom.** Your acts have moral density. Your choices are not the fall of a leaf — they are the deliberate movement of a soul that knows what it is doing and is held to know what it is doing.

Stay with me. This is the strange flip the ayah performs.

The first half — *lā yus'alu ʿammā yafʿal* — tells you Allah is so high, so unmatched, that no audit can be convened over Him.

The second half — *wa hum yus'alūn* — tells you that **you are so significant that an audit will be convened over you.**

You are not the boulder. You are not the river. You are not even the wolf. **You are a being whose every act ascends with weight, whose every moment registers, whose every choice is recorded.**

That is a terrifying thing. It is also an honoring thing. **Allah took the dust of the earth and breathed into it of His Spirit, and what He produced was a creature heavy enough to be questioned.** That weight is *karāma* — dignity. That weight is what makes you not interchangeable with grass.

[PAUSE]

Now hold both halves together.

He is outside the audit. You are inside it.

What does that produce, when both truths are held simultaneously?

It produces **the right kind of seriousness, and the right kind of relief, in the same heart.**

The seriousness: **your actions matter.** They will be brought up. They will be weighed. The smallest sincerity, the smallest betrayal, the smallest moment of patience, the smallest indulgence of envy — all of it is registered. You cannot shrug your way through your one life like a leaf in the wind. You are not a leaf. You are *hum yus'alūn*. **You will be asked.**

The relief: **Allah's actions don't need defending by you.** When something happens in your life that hurts — a loss, a delay, an opacity — the temptation is to put Allah Himself in your private courtroom and ask Him to justify Himself before you can keep worshipping Him. The ayah forecloses that, gently and absolutely. **He is not in your courtroom. He is not in any courtroom.** Your discomfort with His decree does not constitute a subpoena. He is not standing trial. He never has been. He never will be.

Once you really let that land, an enormous burden lifts. You no longer have to *make sense of everything Allah does* before you can keep worshipping. **You can let some things remain mysterious without it threatening His Lordship**, because Lordship was never going to be established by your understanding in the first place.

But the seriousness stays. **Your own actions remain weighable.** You are still the questioned. You still walk forward into a Day where every speck will be brought out.

[PAUSE]

So the right posture is: **stop justifying Allah's actions to yourself, and start preparing to justify your own to Him.**

That is the swap the ayah is asking for.

Most of us, especially when we suffer, do the opposite. We spend enormous mental energy demanding that Allah's actions explain themselves to us, while spending almost no energy preparing the explanation we ourselves will have to give. **The ayah inverts the courtroom.** *Lā yus'alu ʿammā yafʿal* — He is not in the dock. *Wa hum yus'alūn* — you are.

What if you walked through tomorrow with that inversion?

What if, when something hurt and the *why?* rose up in your chest about Allah, you let the *why?* settle — not by silencing it, but by recognizing it had been pointed at the wrong defendant — and turned it instead toward yourself? *What did I do today that I will be questioned about? What am I building, what am I saying, what am I withholding, what am I indulging — that I will one day be brought to account for?*

That is not a way of avoiding the hard questions about suffering. The Quran has many ayahs that lean into those — *Sabr*, the eventual unveiling, the overall purpose. **It is a way of putting the courtroom back where it actually is.**

You — questioned.
Him — beyond the question.

Walk like a creature who knows both.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Now look at the ayah one more time, with everything we've uncovered:

> **لَا يُسْـَٔلُ عَمَّا يَفْعَلُ ۚ وَهُمْ يُسْـَٔلُونَ**
>
> *Lā yus'alu ʿammā yafʿalu wa hum yus'alūn.*

See its structure. The same root — *s-'-l* — bookends both halves. The same passive form — *yus'al* — sits at the heart of both. **One single particle of negation — *lā* — flips the destiny.** And one single, exposed pronoun — *hum* — pulls creation into the spotlight while leaving Allah implicit and hidden in the verb.

It is a **chiasmus of identity**. Allah, unnamed, unfindable in the surface grammar, behind every audit. *Hum*, named, exposed, before every audit. The verb between them, identical in form, negated for One, affirmed for the other.

This is not just a sentence. **It is a map.** A map of who stands where in the architecture of being. The Lord behind the bench. The creature in front of it. The same word in the room, doing different work.

And it is the perfect closure to ayah 22's logical proof. *If there were two gods, both would be ruined.* And then, ayah 23: *the One that remains is so utterly unique that He is not even in the category of beings who can be brought to justify themselves. They are. He is not. That is the deepest mark of His Godhood.*

[PAUSE]

### Bringing the Linguistic and the Thematic Together

Notice: the linguistic discovery and the thematic discovery are the same insight, said two ways.

**The linguistic insight:** The grammar of the ayah places Allah outside the verb of being-questioned. Allah is hidden inside the verb conjugation, while *hum* — they — are pinned in front of it, named, exposed.

**The thematic insight:** Allah is outside your tribunal forever. You are inside His forever.

Same truth. Once it is grammar. Once it is your life.

That is the way the Quran works. **The architecture of the sentence is the architecture of reality.** The position of the pronoun in the line is the position of the soul before the throne.

### Questions to Carry

1. *Where in my life am I exhausted from being on the witness stand — and have I yet brought that exhaustion to the One who is not on any stand?*

2. *Where in my life have I been quietly placing Allah in a courtroom, demanding that He justify a particular pain or loss before I will keep worshipping Him fully? What would it mean to release Him from a courtroom He was never in?*

3. *If today I were called and asked about even one hour of my day, what would I most want to be holding in my hands when the question came?*

4. *Do I treat my acts as though they have weight — or as though they are leaves in the wind? What does my schedule say about which one I really believe?*

5. *Have I confused "I do not understand why Allah did this" with "Allah owes me an explanation"? Are those actually the same statement, or have I quietly let one slide into the other?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

> **Allah is the One whose actions stand outside every courtroom; you are the one whose actions stand inside His — so stop interrogating Him and start preparing your own answer.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to feel a great surge of resolve after this ayah. You do not have to fix anything in your life by tonight. But there is a small shift the ayah is inviting.

The next time you find yourself silently demanding that Allah justify some part of your life before you continue trusting Him — pause. Not to suppress the pain. Not to deny that the pain is real. But to notice the courtroom you have built, and to recognize that the One you have summoned to it does not appear there. He cannot. By His very nature, He cannot.

And the next time you find yourself wasting an hour, an afternoon, a stretch of life — pause for the other reason. Because *hum yus'alūn*. **You will be asked.** Not as a threat — as a recognition of how much weight Allah has placed in your every breath.

Walk forward as a creature who knows both:
The Lord beyond the audit.
Yourself inside it.
And the strange, holy gravity of being made by Him precisely so that your acts could matter.

### Du'a

اللَّهُمَّ يَا مَنْ لَا يُسْأَلُ عَمَّا يَفْعَلُ، ارْفَعْ عَنْ قَلْبِي مَحْكَمَةً مَا كَانَ يَنْبَغِي لِي أَنْ أُقِيمَهَا عَلَيْكَ، وَأَعِنِّي عَلَى مَحْكَمَتِكَ الَّتِي لَا بُدَّ لِي أَنْ أَقِفَ فِيهَا.

*O Allah, O You who is not questioned about what He does — lift from my heart the courtroom I should never have convened against You, and ready me for the courtroom in which I must one day stand before You.*

*O Allah, let me be a creature heavy enough to be asked, and humble enough to no longer ask of You what You owe to no one.*

*Āmīn.*
