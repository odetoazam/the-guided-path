# Every Soul Is a Taster of Death — and Both Ease and Hardship Are the Refining Fire

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 35

> كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَائِقَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ ۗ وَنَبْلُوكُم بِٱلشَّرِّ وَٱلْخَيْرِ فِتْنَةً ۖ وَإِلَيْنَا تُرْجَعُونَ

> *Kullu nafsin dhā'iqatu'l-mawt. Wa nablūkum bi'sh-sharri wa'l-khayri fitnah. Wa ilaynā turjaʿūn.*

> **Every soul is a taster of death. And We test you with evil and good as a trial. And to Us you will be returned.**

---

## Introduction: The World Behind This Verse

Before we open this ayah, we have to know what it is answering.

The Prophet ﷺ has been mocked. Not for what he taught — for what he *was*. They said: *what kind of messenger is this? He eats food. He walks in markets. If he were really sent from God, wouldn't an angel come down with him?* Their objection was his mortality. They imagined that a true emissary from the Unseen would be deathless.

So the ayah right before this one (21:34) opens a door:

> *"We did not grant any human before you immortality. So if you were to die, will they live forever?"*

That is a sharp, almost ironic line. They mock the Prophet for being mortal — but **they themselves are mortal too**. Their critique condemns them.

Then comes our ayah. And here is where it stops being a debate and becomes something else entirely.

[PAUSE]

Allah does not just say: *yes, prophets die.* He says: **every soul tastes death**. Then He adds something the mockers were not asking about — He pivots and says: *and We are testing you. With evil. And with good. As a fitnah.* And then He closes: *and to Us, you will be returned.*

Watch what He has done. The opponents asked a question about prophethood. He has answered with a description of *their entire existence*. He has reached past their objection and pointed to the floor under their feet. **You are inside a test right now. You did not notice. You will be returned to give an account.**

There is something stunning in this ayah that I want us to find. It is hidden in three places: in a single verb form that almost never gets translated correctly, in the order of two simple words, and in the grammatical voice of the very last word. By the time we have unpacked them, the ayah will not feel like a verse you have heard a hundred times. It will feel like a mirror.

Let's begin.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Dhā'iqah* — Why "Taster," Not "Will Taste"

Here is the first thing almost every translation does to this ayah. It says:

> *Every soul **will taste** death.*

Future tense. Something that happens later. A coming event.

But that is not what the Arabic says.

The Arabic says: *kullu nafsin **dhā'iqatu**'l-mawt*. And **dhā'iqah** is not a verb. It is what Arabic grammarians call an **ism fā'il** — an active participle. It does not name an action that occurs at a particular time. It names a *quality of being*. A standing description.

Let me teach this distinction, because it changes everything.

In English, we have something close to it. There is a difference between saying "she runs" and "she is a runner." The first names an event. The second names what kind of person she is. "She is a runner" is true while she is sleeping. It is true while she is sitting on the couch. It is a description of what she *is*, not of what she is *doing*.

Arabic does this with the active participle. *Kātib* is not "he wrote" or "he writes" — it is **a writer**, one who has writing as a quality. *Sāmiʿ* is not "he hears" — it is **a hearer**, one whose nature includes hearing.

So **dhā'iqatu'l-mawt** is not "will taste death." It is: **a taster of death**. A *being-that-tastes-death*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that.

The ayah is not telling you about a future event that lies somewhere ahead of you. It is telling you what you **are** — right now, as you breathe, as you read this. **A taster of death.** That is your category of being. That is the type of creature you are.

Notice what this does to the timeline. If death were just a future event, you could put it on a shelf. *I'll deal with that later. For now I have things to do.* But the ayah refuses that. It says: the tasting is not coming — the tasting is your nature. **You are the kind of thing that ends.**

Now stay with me, because there is more in this word.

The verb chosen here is *dhāqa* — to taste. The root is **dh-w-q**. Why this verb? Allah could have said *yarā'l-mawt* — every soul will *see* death. Or *yalqā'l-mawt* — will *encounter* death. Or *yajidu'l-mawt* — will *find* death. Why specifically **taste**?

Here is what the classical lexicographers tell us. In *Lisān al-ʿArab* and in al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt*, the root **dh-w-q** carries three weights at once:

First, **direct sensory contact**. You cannot taste something at a distance. You cannot taste something abstractly. The mouth must close around it. There is no way to taste from across the room. Tasting is *intimate*.

Second, **brief but undeniable**. A taste is not a long act. It is a moment. But in that moment, you know exactly what the thing is. Bitter is bitter. Salt is salt. There is no debating the verdict of the tongue.

Third — and this is what classical scholars highlight — the Arabic verb *dhāqa* is used metaphorically for **knowing something by direct experience rather than by description**. To taste victory. To taste defeat. To taste fear. The Arabic does not just say this for poetry — it says it because that is how the verb actually functions. *Dhawq* is the opposite of *ʿilm* in one specific way: ʿilm can be theoretical; dhawq cannot.

So when Allah says *every soul is a taster of death*, He is making three claims at once.

**One:** Death is something you will know intimately. Up close. No distance. The tongue, not the eye.

**Two:** Death is a moment, not a state. You will not live in death — you will *taste* it. Brief, decisive, and then gone. Which means whatever is on the other side is not death. Death is just the threshold the soul crosses.

**Three:** No one will explain death to you afterwards. You will know it the way you know salt — by the unmediated verdict of being there.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. We spend our lives trying to *think about* death. To philosophize it. To insulate ourselves with abstractions. To make it a topic. But Allah says: *no, it is not a topic. It is a taste.* You will not think your way through it. You will not be told about it. You will *be* there, and you will *know*.

And then — pay attention — the ayah uses the active participle, not the verb, to tell you: **this is already what you are**. The tasting is in you. Your soul is already structured around this fact. Death is not waiting for you outside your house. It is woven into the kind of creature you were created to be.

One more thing on this word. Look at what *tastes*. Not "every body." Not "every person." **Every nafs.** Every *soul-self*. The body decomposes — that is biology. But Allah is not pointing to biology here. He is pointing to the inner subject, the *I* behind the eyes, the one who experiences. **That** is the one who will taste. Death is not a thing that happens to your body while you stand by and watch. It is a thing that **you yourself**, the inner self, will experience directly, with full presence.

Which means: there is no escape into observation. You cannot stand outside your own death. The taster and the tasted meet in the same self.

[PAUSE — major pause]

That is one word.

### Element Two: *Bish-sharri wa'l-khayr* — Why Evil Comes Before Good

Now look at the next phrase: *wa nablūkum **bish-sharri wa'l-khayri** fitnah*.

> *And We test you with evil and good as a trial.*

Most people read past this. *Sure, we're tested with hard things and easy things, fine.* But there is a deliberate choice here that almost no one notices.

**Evil is mentioned first. Then good.**

In Arabic, when two paired things are mentioned together, the order is rarely accidental. We say *ar-rajul wa'l-mar'a* (the man and the woman) or *as-samāwāt wa'l-arḍ* (the heavens and the earth) — these orderings carry weight in classical balāghah. So when the Quran lists *evil and good* in that order — and especially when it is talking about **what tests you** — we should ask why.

Let me first set up the conceptual ground.

If I asked you: *what is testing you in your life right now?* — what would you list?

You would probably list the hard things. The financial pressure. The illness. The strained relationship. The grief. The failure. **The difficulties.** This is what we instinctively call a "test." We say *Allah is testing me* and we mean *something hurts*.

This is the universal human reflex. Test = pain. Trial = suffering. *Fitnah* = the bad stuff.

Now look at the ayah. Allah lists evil first — yes, that matches your intuition. But then He keeps going. *Wa'l-khayr.* **And the good.** And He bundles them together under one label: *fitnatan*. **A single trial.** Both of them. The same test.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The order **bish-sharri wa'l-khayr** — evil then good — actually mimics the order of human awareness. We notice the test when it hurts. We do not notice the test when it is pleasant. So Allah meets us where our perception already is — *yes, the difficult thing is testing you, you were right* — and then immediately He widens it: *but so is the comfortable thing*. So is the salary. So is the health. So is the attention. So is the validation.

The order is pedagogical. He starts at the test you already know and adds the test you were missing.

Now let's open the word **fitnah**.

The root **f-t-n** in classical Arabic — and this is documented in al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt* explicitly — comes from **the smelting of metal in fire to separate the gold from what is not gold**. *Fattana adh-dhahab* means: he put the gold in the furnace to refine it. The original meaning is not "test" in the abstract. The original meaning is *fire applied to ore so that what is precious can be separated from what is impure*.

So when Allah calls both evil and good a *fitnah*, He is saying: **both are furnaces**. Both burn off the dross. Both reveal what is gold in you and what is not.

Hardship is a furnace because it asks: *will you trust Me when nothing makes sense?* Ease is a furnace because it asks: *will you remember Me when everything is fine?*

And here is what is stunning. **Most people fail the second furnace, because they do not know it is a furnace.**

When the difficulty hits, you turn to Allah. You weep. You make duʿā. You read Quran late at night. The fire is obvious — and the fire does its work, and the gold separates. The difficult fitnah is loud about being a fitnah.

But when the comfort comes — the promotion, the marriage, the good month, the answered prayer, the quiet season — you exhale. You think: *finally, the test is over. Now I can rest.* You stop watching yourself. You forget Allah was the one who gave it to you. You start to assume you earned it. And the gold and the dross stay mixed, because **you took the ore out of the furnace before the work was done**.

This is why Allah said *bish-sharri wa'l-khayr*. Because the human heart accepts the first as a test and treats the second as a vacation. And the verse refuses that. *Both,* it says. *One trial. Same fire.*

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer in *fitnah*. Al-Rāghib notes that fitnah, in its widest classical sense, includes **anything that distracts the heart from what it should be on**. Whether the distraction is shaped like pain or shaped like pleasure does not matter. The fire of pain can pull your heart away in despair. The fire of pleasure can pull your heart away in distraction. **The dross is the same. Only the heat looks different.**

So the question the ayah leaves open is not *am I being tested?* The question is: **which furnace am I in right now — and what is being burned off?**

Most of us, if we are honest, are in the easier furnace right now. We are warm. We have food. We have screens. We have problems but we have margins. **The fire of comfort is on, and we are not paying attention.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

That is the second move.

### Element Three: *Turjaʿūn* — The Voice You Cannot Choose

Now look at the last word of the ayah.

*Wa ilaynā **turjaʿūn**.* **And to Us you will be returned.**

In English we usually translate this as "to Us you will return." But that is not what the Arabic says. The Arabic *turjaʿūn* is a **passive verb**. *Yarjiʿūn* would be active — "they return." *Turjaʿūn* is passive — **"you are returned."** Or more precisely: **"you will be returned."**

Let me teach this distinction first.

In any language, the choice between active and passive voice is not just stylistic. It tells you who has agency and who is being acted upon. *I closed the door* and *the door was closed* describe the same physical event but with different subjects. The active sentence centers the doer. The passive sentence centers the recipient. In one, you are the agent. In the other, something happens *to* you.

Now: when human beings think about death, what do we tell ourselves? *I'll go back to my Lord. I'll meet Allah. I'll stand before Him.* We use active language. Because active language preserves the illusion that we are the ones moving — that we have time, that we are choosing the route, that we are the subject of the verb.

But Allah closes this ayah by stripping that away. He does not say: *and to Us you will return*. He says: **and to Us you will be returned.**

[PAUSE]

You are not the agent of this verb. You are the object of it. You are the one being moved.

Stay with this. Think about your life right now. You feel like the protagonist. You feel like the one making choices. You feel like the one going places — *I'm going to work. I'm going to school. I'm going to the gym. I'm going on a trip. I'm going home.* All active. You as subject. You as mover.

The ayah says: at the end of all those goings, **you will be brought back**. *Turjaʿūn*. The active life ends in a passive arrival. You did not choose to come into existence. You did not choose your soul. You will not choose the moment of the return. Someone else closes the loop.

This is not depressing — it is clarifying. Because if you are the one driving the whole journey, then the destination is whatever you decide. But if you will be **returned** — by an unseen hand, on an unannounced day, to a Lord you did not negotiate with — then **the route matters in a way you cannot escape**.

Notice how perfectly this stitches the ayah together.

In word one — *dhā'iqah* — Allah told you what kind of being you are: **a taster of death**.
In the middle — *bish-sharri wa'l-khayr fitnah* — He told you what is happening to you while you live: **you are inside a furnace, both kinds**.
In the last word — *turjaʿūn* — He told you what is at the end: **you will be brought back, not by your own legs, but by a hand you do not control**.

Three pillars. The kind of being you are. The condition you are inside. The end you are heading toward. **None of which you authored.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

That is the third move. Now let's ask what this whole construction reveals not just about words — but about the human condition itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Furnace You Did Not Notice Was On

Let me start far from the ayah, with something modern.

There is a concept in psychology called **hedonic adaptation**. The simple version is this: when something good happens to you — a raise, a promotion, a new house, a relationship — your happiness spikes. And then, within a remarkably short time, your baseline mood returns to where it was before. The new thing becomes the new normal. The car you dreamed about for years becomes the car you grumble about washing. The promotion you celebrated becomes the job you complain about on Sundays. The marriage you prayed for becomes a relationship with the same friction every other relationship has.

The reason this happens is that the human nervous system is wired for **comparison**, not absolutes. It does not register *"I have a working body, food, shelter, safety, and people who love me."* It registers *"this is better/worse than yesterday."* So when comfort is constant, the system stops registering it as comfort at all.

What this means in practice is: **most of your life right now is a blessing your nervous system has stopped noticing**.

The water is hot. The ceiling does not leak. Your child sleeps in a warm room. Your phone connects you to people across continents. You have read more books than 99% of humans who have ever lived. Most of your meals are not survival meals. Your medical pain is being managed. Your fears are mostly imagined and not present.

And yet, when you ask yourself *am I being tested?*, you list the thing that hurts. Because the hurt is what your nervous system can still see.

Now bring the ayah back into the room.

*Wa nablūkum **bish-sharri wa'l-khayr** fitnah.*

Allah is not just naming a theological fact. He is naming the exact place where the human heart lies to itself. He is saying: **the test you cannot see is the test most likely to break you**.

The hard test breaks people *visibly*. They lose faith out loud. They walk away angrily. We can see it. They can sometimes see it themselves, and they can repent.

The soft test breaks people *invisibly*. They do not lose faith — they just slowly stop being interested. They do not abandon prayer — they just gradually become someone who *gets to it when they can*. They do not deny Allah — they just live as if He is one consideration among many. The dross does not get burned off because the fire was not loud enough to alarm them.

Think about your own life through this lens. Think about the last six months. Where were you most spiritually present? Almost certainly in a moment of difficulty. The night something fell apart. The week you were waiting on a result. The conversation you didn't know how you would survive. **Those nights, you prayed.**

Now think about the last good month. The month nothing was wrong. Did you pray with the same weight? Did you read with the same hunger? Or did you coast?

If you coasted, the ayah is not condemning you. It is *naming* what happened. It is saying: that was not a vacation. That was a furnace. The fire just felt like a hot bath.

[PAUSE]

There is a hadith — I will not analyze it here as authentication, but the meaning is well-known and aligns directly with this ayah — in which the Prophet ﷺ says that when Allah loves a servant, He sends them difficulty so they will turn to Him. The implication that troubles people is: does that mean the comfortable life is a sign of being abandoned?

Not exactly. The ayah we are reading clarifies. Comfort is not abandonment — **it is the harder furnace**. Allah did not stop testing the comfortable. He just turned the heat on a setting they cannot see. The question is whether they have the spiritual sensitivity to notice the fire is still on.

This is what *fitnah* really teaches. Not "Allah only loves you when you suffer." But: *both kinds of life are calling out gold*. The suffering soul is being asked: *will you trust?* The comfortable soul is being asked: *will you remember?* Both questions are real. Both refusals are catastrophic. Only the first one is loud.

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means: when something is going well, **that is when the work begins, not when it ends**. The good month is not the rest between the tests. It *is* a test. And the way you pass it is by turning to Allah *as if* you were in difficulty — not because anything is wrong, but because you are aware of the furnace you are inside.

A practical translation. Do this once a week, in a moment of comfort: stop, and ask, *what would I be praying for right now if I knew this comfort were temporary and I had to give an account for it?* Pray that prayer. While you have margin. While the fire is quiet.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Soul as Taster, Not Spectator

Now let's go back to the first word — *dhā'iqah* — and let it open something deeper.

There is a habit modern humans have that the ancients did not. We **observe our lives from outside**. We do this constantly. We narrate our experiences as if to an imagined audience. We curate our reactions to events. We watch ourselves grieving and notice ourselves grieving and post about ourselves grieving. The phenomenon has been called *spectatorship of the self*. We are rarely fully *inside* our own lives.

The smartphone made this radically worse but it did not invent it. The human heart has always been able to retreat from its own experience by abstracting it. We turn pain into stories. We turn fear into philosophy. We turn questions about death into discussions about death. **We do this because direct experience is overwhelming and abstraction is safe.**

Now hear the ayah again:

*Kullu nafsin **dhā'iqatu**'l-mawt.*

Allah did not say *every soul will see death*. Sight is what spectators do. He did not say *every soul will know death*. Knowing can be theoretical. He said: **every soul will taste it**. Tasting is the one sense you cannot do from outside. **You cannot taste from a distance.** You cannot taste through a screen. You cannot taste by reading. You cannot taste by watching someone else taste. The tongue must close around the thing.

The choice of verb is itself a refutation of the spectator self. Allah is saying: at the moment death comes, **all the distance collapses**. You will not narrate this. You will not abstract this. You will not philosophize this. You will not film it. You will be *in it*. The full presence you have spent your whole life dodging will arrive whether you have practiced it or not.

[PAUSE]

This is the hidden mercy in the verb.

The mercy is: **Allah is warning you that direct experience is coming**, so that you might begin the work of being directly present *now*. While there is still time. While there is still margin to learn. The soul that has spent decades in spectator-mode and then suddenly arrives at *dhawq* — direct, unmediated tasting — arrives at it untrained.

The believer's whole project is the slow, daily, tiny rehearsal of full presence. Salah, when done well, is presence rehearsal. *Stand. Now you are speaking to the Living, who is here. You are not narrating this prayer. You are not watching yourself pray. You are praying.* Quran recitation, when done well, is presence rehearsal. *These words are addressed to you, in this moment, by the One who placed you in this chair.* Dhikr, when done well, is presence rehearsal. *Your tongue is moving with His name. Your heart is meant to follow.*

These practices are not random rituals. They are training the soul to *taste* rather than spectate. So that when the final tasting comes, the tongue is not unfamiliar with presence.

This connects directly to a striking detail. Allah uses the active participle — *dhā'iqah*, "a taster" — in the present tense of being. **You already are this.** The tasting has not started yet, but the *quality* of being a taster is already in you. So the work begins now. The soul that learns to *taste* this life — to be inside its prayers, inside its meals, inside its conversations, inside its grief, inside its joy — is being prepared for the great tasting at the end.

The soul that lives life entirely as a spectator arrives at the great tasting having never tasted anything fully before, and is overwhelmed.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means: **your prayers are dress rehearsals for a presence you cannot avoid**. The way you pray today is the way you will arrive at the moment of truth. If your prayer is done while watching yourself pray — narrated, half-attended, performed — you are training the muscle of distance. If your prayer is done with the tongue closing around the words, with attention on the One being addressed, you are training the muscle of *dhawq*.

A practical translation. In your next prayer, ask yourself just before you say *Allāhu akbar*: *am I about to perform this prayer, or am I about to taste it?* Then taste it. Even if it is one rakʿah. Even if it is one ayah. The soul learns by repetition; one tasted ayah today makes tomorrow's tasting easier.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at how the ayah is built.

> *Every soul is **a taster of death**.*
> *And We **test you** with evil and good as a trial.*
> *And to Us you will be **returned**.*

Three pillars in a row.

The first pillar — *dhā'iqatu'l-mawt* — defines the **kind of being you are**. A taster. A creature whose nature includes ending.

The second pillar — *nablūkum bish-sharri wa'l-khayri fitnah* — defines the **condition you are inside while you live**. A furnace. Both kinds of fire. One trial.

The third pillar — *turjaʿūn* — defines the **end you are heading toward**. A return that is not your own doing. A passive arrival.

Look at what is at the center. The middle pillar — the test — is the only one *active in this life*. The first pillar tells you what you are; the third tells you what you will be returned to. **Both are out of your hands.** But the middle pillar — the furnace — is the part where your own work happens. And that is the part Allah lingered on, with the deliberate phrasing of *evil and good*, both of them, *as a trial*.

The architecture is saying: *you did not choose to be the kind of being that tastes death. You will not choose the moment you are returned. But what you do inside the furnace — that is the meaning of your existence.*

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic and the thematic say the same thing.

The active participle *dhā'iqah* says: presence cannot be dodged forever. The spectator collapses at the moment of taste.

The order *evil and good* says: presence in the easy furnace is harder than presence in the hard furnace, and most people fail it without knowing.

The passive *turjaʿūn* says: the route is yours to walk; the arrival is not yours to negotiate.

All three converge on a single demand: **wake up inside your own life now**. Not later. Not after the next milestone. Not when things calm down. **Now**, while the fire is hot in whatever way it is hot, while the route is still under your feet.

### Questions to Carry

1. If I treated the next comfortable week of my life as a *furnace I could not see*, what would I do differently in it?

2. Which moments of my prayer in the last month did I actually *taste*, and which did I narrate? What is the difference between the two for me?

3. The ayah is built around things outside my control: my mortality, my return. Inside it sits the one thing I do control — what I do in the test. Am I living as if that center pillar matters more than it does, or less than it does?

4. When something good happens to me, what is my first instinct — to thank the One who gave it, or to assume I earned it? What does my answer say about the furnace I'm currently in?

5. If I had to give a verdict tonight on whether I am tasting or spectating my own life — which would I give?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**You are already a taster of death, you are inside a fire that uses both pleasure and pain to refine you, and at the end you will not return — you will be returned.**

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to be afraid of death. The Quran does not weaponize mortality as a threat — it uses it as a *clarifier*. The invitation is simpler than fear: come into your own life. Stop watching it from a balcony. Stop telling yourself that the comfortable seasons are a break from the test. Let the prayer you pray tonight be a prayer you *taste*, even if it is only two rakʿahs, even if your mind wandered for half of it. The soul that practices presence in small things is the soul that arrives at the great presence — the *turjaʿūn* — already familiar with the shape of being there.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who made our souls tasters and not spectators — let us not arrive at the great tasting having never tasted Your presence in this life. Awaken us inside the comfortable furnace before its fire burns away what we did not know we were losing. And when You return us, let us be returned to a Lord we have already begun to recognize.*

*Āmīn.*
