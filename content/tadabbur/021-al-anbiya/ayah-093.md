# What We Cut, He Reassembles

## On Fragmentation and the Gravity of Return — Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 93

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen carefully to what Allah has just done.

For nearly seventy ayahs, Surah Al-Anbiya has been parading the prophets before us. Ibrahim arguing with idols. Lut delivered from a city of corruption. Nuh saved from drowning. Dawud and Sulayman judging a field. Ayyub crying out from his suffering. Ismail and Idris and Dhul-Kifl, men of patience. Yunus calling from inside the dark. Zakariyya asking for a child against the laws of biology. Maryam, that tremendous sign.

Different centuries. Different geographies. Different languages. Different challenges. Egyptian. Babylonian. Levantine. Hijazi.

And then, after all of that, Allah says something that should stop us in our tracks. Ayah 92:

> **إِنَّ هَٰذِهِۦٓ أُمَّتُكُمْ أُمَّةً وَٰحِدَةً وَأَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمْ فَٱعْبُدُونِ**

*"Indeed, this umma of yours is one umma. And I am your Lord, so worship Me."*

One umma. All those prophets, all those struggles, across all those centuries — **one community.** One message. One Lord. The diversity of the prophetic stories was the diversity of *settings*, never of *substance*.

That is the declaration of ayah 92.

Now, the very next breath — ayah 93 — tells us what humans did with that gift.

> [ayah:21:93] وَتَقَطَّعُوٓا۟ أَمْرَهُم بَيْنَهُمْ ۖ كُلٌّ إِلَيْنَا رَٰجِعُونَ

*"But they cut their affair among themselves into pieces. All — to Us — are returning."*

Seven words. That is the entire ayah.

Seven words to describe the entire human story between revelation and resurrection. Between the moment Allah said "you are one" and the moment we will all return to find out what we did with that oneness.

Stay with me here. Because the most stunning thing about this ayah is not what it accuses us of. It is what it tells us about the structure of reality itself — about what stays cut, and what doesn't.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Cuts Itself

Before we open the ayah, I need to teach you something about Arabic that English doesn't have.

In English, when I say "they broke it," there is a *they* and an *it* — two separate things. The breaking happens to something else.

But Arabic has a special verb form — Form V — that, for roots of action, often folds the action back onto the actor. The doing happens to the doer. It's a grammatical mirror — and it operates here with full force.

Now add another layer. Arabic also has *intensive* verbs — Form II — that don't just do the action once, but repeatedly, thoroughly. Not just *cut*, but *cut and cut and cut into many pieces*.

Form V, the verb in our ayah, functions here as the reflexive of the intensive. It means: **the doer thoroughly, repeatedly, did the action to himself.**

Hold that in mind.

The verb Allah chose here is **تَقَطَّعُوٓا۟** — *taqaṭṭaʿū*.

The root is ق-ط-ع — *qaṭ-ʿa* — to cut. To sever. To slice. The bare verb (Form I) is what you do with scissors.

Move to Form II — *qaṭṭaʿa* — and you are no longer cutting once. You are cutting *into pieces*. Many pieces. Hacking. Shredding.

Now move to Form V — *taqaṭṭaʿū* — and you have something that should stop your breath:

**They thoroughly hacked themselves into pieces.**

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say *"qaṭaʿahum"* — *He cut them.* The verb is not done to them. It is done by them, to themselves.

He did not say *"qaṭaʿa baʿḍuhum baʿḍan"* — *some of them cut others.* That would have made it tribal warfare, intercommunal violence.

He said: ***they cut themselves.***

Let's sit with this.

When a community fragments — when an umma that was one becomes seventy-three sects, when a single masjid becomes three masjids on three different streets each with three different signs in the window — Allah's grammar tells us something we usually refuse to admit:

**The cutter and the cut are the same body.**

The tongue that excommunicates is part of the same mouth as the tongue that gets excommunicated. The hand that draws the line is part of the same body as the hand on the other side of the line. We are not divided *by* something external. We divide *ourselves*. The wound is self-inflicted.

Here's what's remarkable. The Arabic verb itself enacts what it describes. The sound of *taqaṭṭaʿū* — that sharp, emphatic ط hammered twice in the middle of the word — *qaṭ-ṭa-ʿū* — sounds like cutting. Listen: *taq-ṭa-ʿū*. You can hear the blade. You can hear the snap.

And then the sound of what comes after — *kullun ilaynā rājiʿūn* — is smooth, rolling, unbroken. *Kul-lun-i-lay-nā-rā-ji-ʿūn.* No emphatic letters. No sharp edges. The return *sounds* like the smooth pull of gravity. The cutting sounds like cutting. The returning sounds like flowing.

The ayah's phonetics are doing the ayah's theology.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: One Word, Many Pieces

Now, look at what they cut.

> **أَمْرَهُم** — *amrahum* — *their affair.*

The word is **singular**. Not *"their affairs."* Not *"their many religions."* Not *"their varying schools."*

Their **affair**. One thing.

This is not a casual choice. The grammatical singular is doing argumentative work.

In Arabic, *amr* is one of the heaviest words in the language. It means matter, command, affair, decree, business, situation, the whole of one's case. When the Quran says *amr Allah* — *the affair of Allah* — it means the totality of His decree. When it says *ulū al-amr* — *those of the affair* — it means those who hold authority. *Amr* is the word for the unified ordering of something. The wholeness of a matter.

So when Allah says they cut **their amr** — *singular* — He is telling us:

**They had one thing. They turned it into many.**

Al-Tabari names what those many things became. In his reading of this ayah, the single cutting produced three distinct groups: the Jews Judaized, the Christians Christianized, and idols were worshipped. One message, three parties. And notice the verb forms he reaches for even in his prose — not "they became Jewish" but *hawwadati l-yahūd*, the Jews actively Judaized; not "they became Christian" but *tanassarati l-naṣārā*, the Christians actively Christianized. Even in his description of the outcome, the grammar mirrors the Quranic text: these groups enacted it upon themselves. The reflexive enactment runs all the way down through the centuries of commentary.

The grammar enacts the loss. We expect, after a verb of cutting-into-pieces, to find something plural — *they cut their things*. Instead we find a stubborn singular. *Their affair.* Because in reality, in Allah's view, it was always one. The pieces are an illusion the cutters maintain.

Notice something else. We have spent decades, centuries, oceans of ink, mountains of fatwas debating which pieces are correct and which are wrong. Which sect. Which madhhab. Which jamaat. Which interpretation. Which scholar.

Allah does not engage that debate in this ayah. He simply names the underlying violation: there was *an affair* — and you broke it.

The pieces are not what He's grading. The breaking is.

This should reorient us. We tend to think the religious crime of our age is *being on the wrong piece.* Allah's ayah suggests the religious crime is *participating in the breaking* — regardless of which piece you ended up holding.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Word "Among Themselves"

> **بَيْنَهُمْ** — *baynahum* — *among themselves.*

Here is the third precision.

Where did the cutting happen? Allah does not say *"between them and the disbelievers."* He does not say *"between truth and falsehood."* He does not say *"between Allah and them."*

He says: **بَيْنَهُمْ** — *among themselves.*

The location of the fragmentation is *internal to the community.* Inside the umma. Among the people who already share the kalima. Among the people praying toward the same qibla.

Stay with this. Because this single preposition is the difference between two completely different kinds of conflict.

There is a conflict between truth and falsehood. The Quran spends much of itself describing that conflict. It is real. It must be named.

But there is another kind of conflict — the conflict of brothers turning on each other after their father has died. The conflict of inheritors fighting over which of them gets to hold the inheritance. That conflict is not between truth and falsehood. It is *baynahum* — among themselves.

This ayah is naming that second kind.

The implicit picture: the prophets came. Each one delivered the same message. They all left. And then the inheritors — the people who held the message after the prophets returned to Allah — turned on *each other*.

Not on the message. On each other. Over the message.

That is *baynahum.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Four: The Asymmetry Between Cutting and Returning

Now hold all three pieces in mind:

- They thoroughly fragmented themselves (taqaṭṭaʿū)
- One affair (amrahum singular)
- Among themselves (baynahum)

And then the second half of the ayah arrives:

> **كُلٌّ إِلَيْنَا رَٰجِعُونَ**

*"All — to Us — are returning."*

Here's what's remarkable. Look at the architecture.

The first half of the ayah moves *horizontally*: they cut, among themselves, into pieces. The motion is sideways. People drawing lines between people on the same plane.

The second half moves *vertically*: all — to Us — returning. The motion is upward. Or rather, the motion is *toward* — toward the One who is the destination of all motion.

The grammar performs this. *Kullun* — *all* — comes first. Totalizing. No one excluded. *Ilaynā* — *to Us* — comes second. The destination is named before the verb. *Rājiʿūn* — *returning ones* — comes last. An active participle, not a past tense. They are not "will return" or "returned." They **are returning.** Right now. The motion is in progress. The gravitational pull is on, this very moment, beneath every fragmentation we participate in.

[PAUSE]

What the ayah is showing us is this:

**The cutting is real. The returning is more real.**

The fragmentation lasts as long as this dunya lasts. The return is the structure of reality.

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say "all to Us are *returned*" — past tense, finished. He said "*returning*" — ongoing, present, in motion. The return is not just an event at the end of time. It is the *direction* in which everything is currently traveling, even when it appears to be moving sideways.

You can cut sideways all you want. The vertical pull is still on.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## BRIDGE

So what have we found?

A verb that names self-inflicted, thorough, repeated cutting — *taqaṭṭaʿū*. A singular noun that quietly insists on the original oneness — *amrahum*. A preposition that locates the wound inside the body — *baynahum*. And then a vertical pull that overrides all of it — *kullun ilaynā rājiʿūn*.

The linguistic structure tells us what reality is structured like.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how to live in a fragmented umma without becoming part of the cutting.

---

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Illusion of Pieces

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You have, perhaps, watched a family tear itself apart over an inheritance. Or you have watched friends separate over a misunderstanding that, twenty years later, no one can quite remember the original cause of. Or you have watched a community split over something that, with hindsight, looks much smaller than the splitting deserved.

What you noticed, if you watched closely, is this:

**The closer the original tie, the sharper the cut.**

Strangers don't cut each other. There's nothing to cut. You can't fragment what was never one. Cutting requires unity as its precondition. The deeper the original bond, the more violent its severance.

Now apply this to the umma.

What our ayah is saying — and this should change how you read every news headline about Muslim disunity — is that the very pain of our fragmentation is *evidence of an original oneness.* If we were never one, the cutting wouldn't hurt. The fact that it hurts, the fact that you feel something when a Muslim community splits, the fact that something in you recognizes Muslim-on-Muslim violence as a category of grief separate from other griefs — that is *taqaṭṭaʿū* registering in your soul.

You feel the cutting because Allah named the affair as *one.*

Here's where this opens.

When you encounter a Muslim from a different background — a different madhhab, a different geography, a different orientation toward the deen — the grammar of this ayah quietly trains you. The default is not "different from me." The default, per Allah's noun, is **same affair.** *Amr* singular. The differences are pieces of the cutting we did. The unity is the original Allah Himself declared.

This does not mean every disagreement is illegitimate. Classical scholarship preserves real, principled difference (*ikhtilāf*) as a mercy. The Companions disagreed. The schools disagreed. That is not what this ayah names.

What it names is something else: *taqaṭṭuʿ* — fragmentation that turns difference into severance. Disagreement that hardens into excommunication. Diversity that becomes division.

There is a line between the two. The umma has crossed it many times.

The practical question this ayah leaves us with: when you encounter difference, are you *recognizing pieces of one amr* — or *participating in the cutting?*

[PAUSE]

The first stance asks: what does this brother or sister *also* hold that I hold?

The second stance asks: what does this brother or sister hold that *disqualifies* them?

These are completely different stances. They lead to completely different communities.

This ayah is not giving us a fatwa on which positions are correct. It is naming the disposition of the cutter. And it is telling us: that disposition itself is the disease, prior to any specific position the cutter holds.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Gravity of Return

Now to the other half. Because if the ayah ended at *baynahum*, we would be left with despair. Look at the umma across history — all those prophets delivering the same message, all those inheritors turning on each other. If you stop there, the story is tragedy and nothing else.

But the ayah doesn't stop there.

*Kullun ilaynā rājiʿūn.*

All — to Us — are returning.

Al-Tabari draws three things from this clause, and all three deserve to be heard.

First: the return encompasses everyone. The cutter who drew the lines of excommunication. The sectarian who never once questioned his own position. The scholar who weaponized his learning against his brothers. *Kullun.* All. Without exception. No one gets to claim they have already arrived, already passed beyond the reckoning.

Second — and this is where the ayah's apparent comfort becomes weight — the return carries a warning. Al-Tabari explicitly reads it as a threat directed at those of deviation, informing them that Allah lies in wait: *bil-mirṣād.* The word *mirsād* — a lookout post, a place of ambush from which nothing slips past unseen — is the Quran's image for comprehensive divine attention. You can draw your horizontal lines, build your walls among yourselves, rearrange the pieces any way you like. The vertical gaze has seen all of it. Nothing disappears in the horizontal plane. It merely waits to be answered for vertically.

Third: the return is not erasure. It is precise accounting. Al-Tabari states it plainly — He will recompense the doer of good with his good, and the doer of evil with his evil. The return is *differentiated.* Every person carries their piece to the One who holds the whole, and will be asked: what was your relationship to the amr you were given? Did you recognize it? Or did you participate in its cutting?

This means *kullun ilaynā rājiʿūn* is not equally comforting to everyone who hears it. It is a mercy to those who spent their lives trying to hold things together — to recognize the original unity beneath the accumulated cuts. And it is a warning to those who spent their lives drawing lines.

[PAUSE]

There is one more grammatical detail that should stop you.

Notice: *ilaynā* — *to Us* — is placed before *rājiʿūn* — *returning.* In Arabic, when the destination is fronted before the verb, it emphasizes where over the act of going. The point is not the returning. The point is *to Whom* they are returning. We know the destination. The question is only what we are carrying when we arrive.

And then: *rājiʿūn.* Not *yarjiʿūna* — will return. Not *rajaʿū* — returned. But *rājiʿūn* — active participle. Present. Ongoing.

Every person alive is currently returning. The motion is already in progress. It did not begin at death; it began at birth. Every heartbeat is one step closer. Every breath is part of the return.

You are already on the way back.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Three: The Pieces Fill With Something

But we cannot leave the fragmentation without asking one more question. When something breaks, the pieces don't remain suspended in neutral space. They move. They attach somewhere.

The classical scholars noticed what they attach to. Al-Muyassar, summarizing this ayah's consequence, observes that those who fragmented from the amr wahid did not simply drift into abstention — *faʿabadū al-makhlūqīn wa-l-ahwāʾ.* They worshipped created things and desires. The cutting did not yield freedom or innocent pluralism. It yielded new objects of devotion. New centers of gravity. The pieces, cut loose from the original orbit, found other things to orbit around.

Created things. And whims.

This is the spiritual physics of fragmentation: nature abhors a devotional vacuum. Cut yourself from the source, and something else moves in.

Stay with this. Because it is far more diagnostic than a historical observation.

The fragmentations we participate in — and we all participate in some — are not merely organizational problems. They are devotional redirections. When we cut ourselves off from part of the umma, something fills the space left behind. Sometimes it is a personality. Sometimes it is a tradition isolated from its root. Sometimes — and this is the most subtle — it is our own *hawā*, our own inclination, dressed in religious language.

*Faʿabadū al-makhlūqīn wa-l-ahwāʾ.* Created things and desires. The cutting is not theologically neutral. It opens a space, and something always fills it. The question is what fills it.

This is why the ayah's implicit remedy is not "agree with each other" or "flatten all differences." That is neither possible nor desirable, and the Quran does not ask for it. The remedy is more surgical: keep the amr singular in your perception, even as you engage with the diversity of its expressions. Hold the noun the way Allah held it — as one. Let your differences be differences *within* the amr, not severances *from* it.

## SYNTHESIS

What this ayah teaches, finally, is a kind of depth perception — about history, and about the self.

On history: we look at the record of religious division and we see tragedy. The prophetic communities fracturing. The inheritors fighting over the inheritance. And it is tragedy. But beneath the tragedy is an unchanging grammar. Allah named the affair as *one*. No amount of cutting changes the noun. The pieces are pieces. The amr endures.

On the self: every human being is currently in motion toward a destination they did not choose and cannot avoid. *Kullun ilaynā rājiʿūn.* The question is not whether you are returning. The question is what you are carrying when you arrive. And beneath that question is a simpler one:

Were you a cutter, or were you a holder?

Did you find in this umma's diversity reasons to excommunicate, to separate, to subtract — or did you practice the grammatical mercy of the Quran and recognize the singular noun beneath the plural cuts?

There is a tradition grounded in the prophetic way of gathering around what is shared before pointing at what differs. Al-Hasan al-Basri, commenting on ayah 92, named it with precision: "Your path is one path. This is your shari'a." He was pointing not to a sentiment but to a method — the way that precedes every particular formulation of it. The amr preceded the pieces. The path preceded the paths. The way existed before any community fragmented it into ways. That sequence has never reversed.

The amr is still intact. It has not been cut — only our perception of it has been.

## A DU'A TO CLOSE WITH

*Rabbana, You declared us one umma, and we have spent centuries dividing what You named whole.*

*We ask You by the gravity You placed in all of creation — the pull that keeps every atom oriented toward You — to pull our hearts away from the scissors and toward the return.*

*Let us not be among those who cut. Let us be among those who, when they encounter a piece of what was broken, recognize the amr it came from.*

*Let us carry, when we arrive before You, not the weight of having multiplied the wounds, but the testimony of having tried to hold what You named one.*

*Kullun ilaynā rājiʿūn. And we ask that our return be to Your mercy.*

*Ameen.*
