# What Runs Ahead of You

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayahs 101-102

> إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ سَبَقَتْ لَهُم مِّنَّا ٱلْحُسْنَىٰٓ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ عَنْهَا مُبْعَدُونَ
> لَا يَسْمَعُونَ حَسِيسَهَا ۖ وَهُمْ فِى مَا ٱشْتَهَتْ أَنفُسُهُمْ خَـٰلِدُونَ

> *Inna alladhīna sabaqat lahum minnā al-ḥusnā, ulā'ika ʿanhā mubʿadūn. Lā yasmaʿūna ḥasīsahā, wa hum fī mā ishtahat anfusuhum khālidūn.*

> "Indeed, those for whom the most beautiful [reward] has gone forth ahead from Us — those are kept far away from it. They will not hear even its faintest hiss, and they are in whatever their souls themselves desire, abiding forever."

---

## Introduction: The World Just Before This Verse

Stay with me for a moment, because where this ayah lands depends entirely on what just happened in the verses before it.

Three ayahs earlier, Surah Al-Anbiya describes a moment so terrible it almost feels physical to read. The disbelievers and what they worshipped besides Allah — those carved gods, those idols people poured their lives into — all of them, the worshipper and the worshipped, are made the fuel of Hell. *Ḥaṣabu jahannam*. The text says they will *enter* it. The text says they will *hear* it. The text says they will be in it for an extended time.

Listen to that. **They will hear it.**

The surah has placed us inside the auditory experience of the damned. The roar of fire is in their ears. They cannot turn away from it. They cannot lower the volume. The sound itself becomes part of the punishment.

And then — without transition, without softening — the surah pivots:

> *Inna alladhīna sabaqat lahum minnā al-ḥusnā...*

"Indeed, those for whom the most beautiful has gone forth ahead from Us..."

A different group. A different sound. A different distance.

What I want us to see in this reflection is something that the architecture of these two ayahs makes almost violently clear: paradise in this passage is not first described as a garden. It is described as a **distance**. It is described as a **silence**. And then — only then — it is described as **what your own soul finally, rightly wants**.

There is a stunning logic at work here. Before we tell you what paradise *is*, we tell you what paradise *isn't*. Before we tell you what you receive, we tell you what you escape. And before we tell you what you'll be given, we tell you that, by then, you will want the right things.

Let's unpack it.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Sabaqat lahum minnā al-ḥusnā* — "The Best Has Gone Forth Ahead from Us"

Let me first teach you a small thing about Arabic that will change how you read this entire verse.

In English, when we want to say "they got the reward," we put the people first. *They received it.* The subject of the sentence is the human. The reward is the object — passive, waiting, received.

Arabic does this sometimes. But not here. Not in this ayah.

Here, Allah does something subtle and astonishing. He makes **the reward itself** the subject of the verb. He makes **al-ḥusnā** — "the most beautiful" — the actor. And the verb is not "given." The verb is *سَبَقَتْ — sabaqat*.

*Sabaqa* means "to go ahead, to outrun, to precede, to get there first." It is a racing word. It is the word you use when one runner crosses the finish line before another. It is the word you use when a herald arrives before the king. It is the word for what runs ahead.

So Allah is not saying: "These people earned a reward." He is saying something far stranger and far more beautiful:

**"The most beautiful — *al-ḥusnā* — ran ahead of them. From Us. Reached the destination before they did. Was already there when they arrived."**

Notice something. Stay with the picture for a moment.

Imagine a child running home from school. The child is tired, the road is long, the child does not know if anyone is waiting. But unbeknownst to the child, the father has already left work early, has already arrived home, has already prepared the meal, has already opened the door. Before the child took the first step toward home, the welcome was already running ahead of him.

The child *thinks* he is the one moving. But the welcome moved first.

This is the picture Allah is drawing. *Al-ḥusnā* — the best, the most beautiful — *sabaqat lahum*. It went ahead of them. From Us, *minnā*. Out of the divine, the goodness ran ahead, took its place, and is waiting before the runner has even crossed the line.

The classical commentators add a dimension that makes this image even stranger and more overwhelming. Al-Muyassar glosses this same construction as the precedence of happiness "in Our knowledge" — *fī ʿilminā* — of these people being among the people of Paradise. The "going ahead" is rooted not merely in sequence, not merely in time, but in the divine ʿilm itself. This is not a reward that moves swiftly through the world and arrives early. It is a knowing that was always already there: before the road existed, before the runner was born, before the first step was taken. The child's image has to shift entirely. It is not just that the father arrived home early. It is that the welcome was prepared before the child's name was given.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where it becomes overwhelming. The Prophet ﷺ taught us, in a hadith preserved in Bukhari and Muslim, that Allah said: *سَبَقَتْ رَحْمَتِي غَضَبِي* — *"My mercy has gone ahead of My wrath."*

The same verb. *Sabaqat.*

This is not coincidence. This is the same architectural word. In the hadith, Allah's mercy is described as having *outrun* His wrath — gotten there first, taken its place ahead of judgment. And here, in this ayah, *al-ḥusnā* — which is mercy made specific, mercy made personal, mercy made yours — has *sabaqat lahum*. Run ahead of them.

So when the verse says these people had *al-ḥusnā* go forth ahead of them, it is invoking the deepest architectural truth about how Allah deals with creation. **Mercy moves first.** Mercy gets there before you. Mercy is not your reaction's reward; it is the structure into which your effort is placed.

Notice what Allah does not say. He does not say: "Those who earned al-ḥusnā." He does not say: "Those who deserved al-ḥusnā." He does not say: "Those whose deeds purchased al-ḥusnā." He says: those *for whom* al-ḥusnā ran ahead.

This is not a denial of effort. The surah is full of the prophets striving. But the framing is precise: even your striving runs into a mercy that already arrived.

[PAUSE]

And one more thing — *al-ḥusnā* is itself unspecified.

What is it? The word is the feminine elative of *ḥasan* — "good, beautiful." It means "the most beautiful, the best, the supreme good." But Allah does not tell us *which* good. He does not say "Paradise" here, though He could have. He does not say "His pleasure," though that is also true. He leaves it open.

The classical scholars said: it is the kalimah, the word of mercy. It is paradise. It is His promise. It is His grace. It is the vision of His face.

Each scholar was right. They were all describing different facets of one unspecified gift.

Here the classical tradition pauses to note something the reflection must not pass over. Al-Tabari opens his commentary on this very verse by recording that "the scholars of tafsir disagreed about who is meant by it." One group read it broadly: everyone for whom Allah had decreed happiness (*saʿāda*) among His creation — that general, cosmic promise to all those covered by the divine mercy. But al-Tabari also preserves a transmission that is striking in its specificity: that Ali ibn Abi Talib (RA), while delivering a sermon, recited this very ayah and applied it to ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān (RA) by name. The Companions, it seems, read this verse not only as a theological principle. They also heard in it a word they could speak over particular beloved individuals — people whose fates they commended to the divine mercy by invoking this promise directly. The general and the particular hold together: a cosmic assurance to all those for whom the *sabaqat al-ḥusnā* ran ahead, and a word early Muslims placed on specific souls they loved.

By leaving *al-ḥusnā* unnamed, Allah lets it hold every form of the most beautiful — every form a soul could long for — without locking it down to one. The most beautiful, in whatever shape that soul most needs to receive it, ran ahead of them.

What this single word reveals: **the structure of grace is not transactional. It is preceding. The best gets there before you do.**

### Element Two: *Mubʿadūn* — "Kept Distanced" (in the Passive)

Now look at the second half of ayah 101. *Ulā'ika ʿanhā mubʿadūn.* "Those — from it — kept distanced."

The word *mubʿadūn* is a passive participle from Form IV of the root ب-ع-د (distance, far away). Form IV in Arabic carries a causative meaning. The simple verb *baʿuda* means "to be far." *Abʿada* — Form IV — means "to make something far, to remove, to keep distanced."

The passive participle *mubʿadūn* is therefore: "those upon whom the action of being-distanced has been performed."

Listen carefully to what is *not* in the verse.

Allah does not say: *they distanced themselves*. He does not say: *they ran from it*. He does not say: *they avoided it*. The verb is passive. They are kept far. They are *being distanced* by an unnamed agent.

Who is the unnamed agent?

The whole surah has been telling us. The same *Us* who sent al-ḥusnā ahead is the *Us* who keeps them distanced. The mercy that ran ahead also keeps them away from the fire. The grace is one motion: arriving for them, removing them.

Here is what's remarkable. In our minds, when we imagine paradise, we usually imagine *arriving somewhere*. The gardens. The rivers. The tree. But the verse leads with something else first. Before it tells us what they enter, it tells us what they are kept *away* from.

Why?

Because the original audience — the audience of these verses in Mecca, surrounded by the threat of fire just three ayahs above — needed first to know: **you will be removed.**

Sometimes the deepest gift is not what is added. It is what is taken away. The trauma you no longer carry. The fear you no longer have. The proximity to harm that has been *broken*. Allah tells the believer first: **the distance has been arranged.** That distance is itself a mercy. It is its own gift, before any other gift.

The classical commentators are precise on the degree of this removal. Al-Muyassar specifies two distinct negations: they will not enter it — and they will not even be near it. Both entry and proximity are foreclosed. The distinction matters, because nearness to fire is itself already a form of suffering — the heat reaches you, the fear finds you, the awareness of it presses in. Allah forecloses both: the entrance and the closeness. The distance is total, not approximate.

The passive matters. They do not have to maintain the distance through their own effort. They are kept. They are held away. The same divine motion that moved al-ḥusnā ahead of them now holds the fire behind them.

What this construction reveals: **paradise begins with a separation, not an arrival. And that separation is performed for you, not by you.**

### Element Three: *Lā yasmaʿūna ḥasīsahā* — "They Will Not Hear Even Its Faintest Hiss"

Now we move into ayah 102. And here Allah does something extraordinary with sound.

The word *ḥasīs* comes from the root ح-س-س, which means perception — but at its faintest threshold. *Ḥiss* is sensation. *Ḥasīs* is the ghost of sensation. It is the rustle in the leaves you barely catch. It is the whisper at the edge of hearing. It is the faintest, weakest, most attenuated possible sound.

Lexicographers describe *ḥasīs* as the sound of fire crackling in the distance, the hiss of something at the threshold of audibility. The word itself is onomatopoeic — say it out loud. *Ḥasīs.* The s-s-s at the end is the hiss. The word *sounds* like what it means.

Now hear the sentence again. *Lā yasmaʿūna ḥasīsahā.* "They do not hear even its hiss."

Allah could have said: *they do not hear it.* That alone would be a complete sentence. *Lā yasmaʿūnahā.* Three words. Done.

But that is not what Allah says. He adds the word *ḥasīs*. He doesn't just say "they don't hear it" — He specifies the *kind* of sound they don't hear: the absolute faintest one. The one a person would have to strain to catch. The one that exists only because they are trying to hear it.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter?

Because compare it to what was said three ayahs earlier about the disbelievers in Hell: they *hear it*. They are inside its sound. The roar fills their ears.

Here, Allah is not just saying believers are absent from Hell. He is saying they are so *absolutely* removed from it that not even the faintest sonic echo of it reaches them. They are not on the porch hearing distant noise. They are not in the next valley. They are at a remove so complete that the very lowest threshold of sound — the *ḥasīs* — does not reach them.

Stay with this. There is a kind of trauma in this world that comes not from being hurt directly, but from *hearing* others being hurt. Soldiers come back from war and they hear sounds even when there is no sound. People who have lived under bombs jump at distant fireworks years later. The ear remembers what the body experienced. The hiss of suffering, even at a distance, stays in you.

What Allah is promising is not just "you won't be in the fire." It is: **the very faintest residue of the fire — the auditory echo, the distant rumor, the smallest ghost of the suffering — will not reach you.** You will not even know what you escaped. You will not be haunted by it. You will be fully delivered from its sonic, sensory existence.

And lest this remain abstract — the classical commentators make it visceral. Al-Muyassar names precisely what this *ḥasīs* is: the sound of the flames, and the burning of bodies within it. Not a vague distant roaring. The particular sound of burning. That is what the word carries. And that is what will not reach them.

Al-Tabari, meditating on this same verse, pauses to raise what looks like an apparent difficulty. Narrations in the tradition describe the Day of Judgment itself as a moment when Hell lets out a roar so terrifying that even the nearest angels and the sent prophets fall to their knees in fear. How, then, can this verse promise that believers will not hear even the faintest hiss? His answer is careful: those are two different states, two different moments. The terror of that roar belongs to the gathering, the assembly — the trembling that accompanies the Day before destinations are settled. But the state described here, when not even the ḥasīs reaches them, is a later state: after the believers have been settled in their dwellings in Paradise, fully arrived, fully received. Once they are there — settled, dwelling, abiding — the fire's voice cannot find them. There is a day that trembles, even for the righteous. And then there is a place that is perfectly, permanently still.

The word *ḥasīs*, that little hissing word, is doing enormous theological work. It tells us paradise is not adjacent to suffering. It is in a different sonic universe. The fire's voice cannot find them.

What this single word reveals: **the gift is not just safety from harm. The gift is safety from even the rumor of harm. Even the faintest hiss does not reach.**

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Three words have done the architectural work: *sabaqat* (the best ran ahead), *mubʿadūn* (they are kept distanced), *ḥasīs* (the faintest hiss they do not hear).

Together they describe paradise not as a place you enter but as a *motion that has already happened on your behalf*. Mercy moved first. Distance was performed for you. Sound itself was filtered out before it could reach you.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.
