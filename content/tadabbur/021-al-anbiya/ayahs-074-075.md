# From the Town, Into the Mercy

## Lut and the Two Prepositions of Rescue

---

## INTRODUCTION

> **وَلُوطًا آتَيْنَاهُ حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا وَنَجَّيْنَاهُ مِنَ ٱلْقَرْيَةِ ٱلَّتِى كَانَت تَّعْمَلُ ٱلْخَبَـٰٓئِثَ ۗ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ قَوْمَ سَوْءٍۢ فَـٰسِقِينَ • وَأَدْخَلْنَـٰهُ فِى رَحْمَتِنَآ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ**

> *"And to Lut — We gave him judgment and knowledge, and We delivered him from the town that was doing the foul things. Indeed, they were a people of evil, breaking out of obedience. And We admitted him into Our mercy. Indeed, he was among the righteous."*

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

Imagine you are sitting in Makkah in the middle of the Prophet's ﷺ early years. The city around you is the city you grew up in — the streets you played in as a child, the tribal alliances that fed your family, the language you learned to dream in. And it has become unlivable.

The idols outside the Kaʿbah are not only stone. They are an entire economy, an entire moral architecture. Female infants are being buried alive. The poor are being eaten by the rich. The strong are doing what the strong have always done. And the few who have begun to see it — to *really* see it — are being dragged through the dust.

You are one of those few. You love your city. You also know, in a way that wakes you at night, that what is happening here cannot continue. And you have nowhere else to go.

Into this air, Allah recites a sequence of prophets. Each one is given a few lines. Ibrahim gets a long passage. Then Lut.

And Lut's section — listen carefully — is among the shortest of all of them. Just two ayahs — a compression he shares with Nūḥ, who follows immediately after, which suggests this brevity is not a gap but a pattern. He doesn't get the architecture of Ibrahim's argument with his father. He doesn't get the cry of Nuh. He doesn't get the kingship of Dawud. He gets two ayahs.

[PAUSE]

There is a reason. When Allah compresses a story to its smallest possible shape, what remains is **architecture**. Strip the incident, the dialogue, the names. What is left is the *structure of how Allah saves one of His own from a place that has gone foul.*

That structure is what we are going to read tonight.

### The Hook

There are two prepositions in these verses. They look small. They sit between the verbs almost invisibly. One says **FROM**. The other says **INTO**.

And between those two prepositions — between *min* and *fī* — there opens, if we read carefully enough, the full architecture of what salvation actually looks like. Not as a proposition to be memorized. As a structure to be inhabited.

Most of us have prayed to be rescued from something. Few of us have understood what Allah does after the rescue. These two ayahs answer that question. And the answer reframes what we are even asking for when we ask Him to save us.

Stay with me.

[PAUSE — major]

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ḥukman wa ʿilman* — The Gift That Came First

Before we look at the rescue, we have to look at what came before it. The ayah doesn't begin with deliverance. It begins with an inner gift.

> **آتَيْنَاهُ حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا**
> *"We gave him judgment and knowledge."*

Two words. Both indefinite — *ḥukman*, *ʿilman*. Not "the judgment" or "the knowledge." A judgment, a knowledge — qualitative gifts, not specific items.

Let's slow down on what these two words actually are.

**ʿIlm** is the easier one. Knowledge. Knowing the way things are. Knowing right from wrong as a content. Knowing what Allah loves, what He hates, what is and what isn't.

**Ḥukm** is harder. The root is ḥ-k-m. Same root as *ḥikma* (wisdom), *ḥākim* (ruler), *muḥkam* (firmly fixed). Originally — and this is documented in Arabic lexicography — the root carries the sense of *restraining*. The bridle on a horse is called *ḥakama* because it *restrains*. *Ḥukm* is the inner faculty that *restrains* you. The ability to look at a situation and *decide* — to apply knowledge, to rule between options, to hold yourself back from one and toward the other.

Classical commentators read *ḥukm* in this particular ayah with a notably practical sharpness. Al-Tabari glosses it as the capacity for *arbitrating between disputants* — the ability to look at a conflict between people and adjudicate it justly. This is not wisdom in the abstract. It is applied discernment under pressure, with real stakes, when people bring their grievances and their competing stories and expect a verdict. Al-Muyassar goes further: for him, this gift is inseparable from *prophethood itself* — the full prophetic mantle, the authority to guide and adjudicate as one who has been entrusted.

So *ʿilm* is the content. *Ḥukm* is the operating system that uses the content. You can have all the knowledge in the world and no *ḥukm*, and the knowledge sits in you like an unread book. You can have *ḥukm* without *ʿilm* and you'll decide confidently in the wrong direction.

Allah gave Lut both.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something. Notice the order of the ayah.

He didn't say: *"We rescued Lut from the town, and We gave him judgment and knowledge."*

He said: ***first*** *we gave him judgment and knowledge,* ***then*** *we rescued him from the town.*

Sit with this. Why this order?

Because — and this is the first quiet teaching — **you cannot be rescued from what you have not first been distinguished from internally.**

If Allah had pulled Lut out of the town without first giving him *ḥukm* and *ʿilm*, Lut would have walked five miles into the desert and recreated the town in his head. The town would have come with him. The town wasn't only outside. It was a way of seeing.

The internal gift had to come first. **The inner extraction is logically prior to the outer one.** Geography doesn't save anyone whose discernment hasn't been built.

Notice also: this same phrase — *ḥukman wa ʿilman* — is used for Yusuf when he reaches maturity (12:22) and for Musa when he comes of age (28:14). It is a signature. It marks the moment a prophet receives the inner equipment that makes everything else possible. It is, you might say, the *prophetic operating system*.

Lut got it. Then — and only then — Allah said *najjaynāhu*.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: *Al-Khabāʾith* — The Foulness That Will Not Be Named

> **مِنَ ٱلْقَرْيَةِ ٱلَّتِى كَانَت تَّعْمَلُ ٱلْخَبَـٰٓئِثَ**
> *"from the town that was doing the foul things."*

There is a word here that the listener has to hear with their *ears*, not just read with their eyes.

**Al-khabāʾith.**

Say it slowly. *Al-kha-bāʾ-ith.* Three heavy consonants — *kh*, *b*, *th*. The throat scrape of *kh*. The hard *b*. The teeth-tongue *th*. The word doesn't sound like anything beautiful. It sounds like what it means.

The root is **kh-b-th**. *Khabīth* means foul, filthy, putrid, contaminated. It is used for spoiled meat, for stagnant water, for impure earnings. It is one of the strongest words in Arabic for *moral disgust*. When the Prophet ﷺ described the ḥadīth of "the good word as a good tree" he used *kalima ṭayyiba* — clean, fragrant, pure — and the opposite was *khabītha* — foul, decayed, rotten.

Now notice: the ayah uses the **plural**. *Al-khabāʾith* — foul **things**, multiple kinds. Not one act. A *category* of acts. The town wasn't doing one thing. It was doing many things, all of which had the same quality of foulness.

The classical commentators, in their longer treatments, do identify what the plural covers: the sexual violation of men who came as travelers to Sodom, and what the tradition also describes as highway terror — hurling stones at passersby, terrorizing those who used their roads. The plural *khabāʾith* is not rhetorical vagueness. It names an actual multiplicity: Lut's town was failing on more than one axis, in its treatment of bodies and in its treatment of strangers, in what it did to those who came to it as guests and what it did to those who simply passed through. Many-headed, comprehensive foulness.

And notice what the ayah does **not** do.

[PAUSE]

It does not name the acts.

The longer narratives in Surahs Hud, Al-Naml, and Al-ʿAnkabūt do specify. But here, in this ayah, in this surah, in this compressed form — Allah does not list. He simply names the **quality**: foul, multiple, habitual.

Why?

Because this surah is not interested in the catalogue. It is interested in the **architecture**. Whatever the foul thing is — in any town, in any age — the structure is the same. There is a *quality* of life that is *khabīth*. It contaminates everything it touches. It is plural; it is many-headed. And when it has taken over a place, that place must either be left or it will leave you.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing. Look at the verb that frames *al-khabāʾith*:

> **كَانَتْ تَعْمَلُ**
> *"[the town] was [continuously] doing"*

This is *kāna* + the imperfect — the Arabic past habitual. It does not mean "the town did it once." It means **the town was in the constant, repeated practice of it.** It was the air people breathed. It was the unmarked default. It was what one did because one was from there.

Foulness, when it becomes habitual, stops being noticed. The town didn't experience itself as foul. It experienced itself as *normal*. That is the most dangerous condition a place can reach.

This is what *al-khabāʾith* — plural, unspecified, habitually-done — names: not a *deed* that the town committed, but a *climate* the town breathed.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: *Min* and *Fī* — Two Prepositions, One Architecture

Now we are ready for the heart of these ayahs. Stay with me carefully here, because what we are about to see is small in size and large in consequence.

Two verbs. Two prepositions.

> **نَجَّيْنَاهُ ✨ مِنَ ✨ ٱلْقَرْيَةِ**
> *"We delivered him **from** the town."*

> **أَدْخَلْنَـٰهُ ✨ فِى ✨ رَحْمَتِنَا**
> *"We admitted him **into** Our mercy."*

[PAUSE]

Look at what is happening.

The first verb — *najjaynāhu* — is form II of n-j-w. The simpler form would have been *anjaynāhu* (form IV). Both can mean "to save." But *najjā* — the doubled, intensive form — carries the sense of **decisive, complete, urgent rescue**. It is the same form Allah uses for Nūḥ in the very next ayah. It is the language of being pulled, decisively, from peril.

And it is paired with **min** — the preposition of *out-of-ness*. From. Away from. Out from inside.

The second verb — *adkhalnāhu* — comes from d-kh-l, "to enter, to be admitted into an enclosed space." It is form IV — *causative*. He didn't merely enter. Allah **admitted him into**. He was let in.

And it is paired with **fī** — the preposition of *into-ness*, of being inside something.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what these two ayahs are doing?

They are giving you the *two halves* of what salvation looks like when traced through the structure the Quran is laying down here.

Salvation is not a single act of removal. **Salvation is a relocation.** You are extracted from one container and placed inside another container. The first container is the town that practices the foul things. The second container is the *mercy of Allah*.

If you only have the first half — *najjaynāhu min* — you have *escape*. You have homelessness. You have someone pulled from a burning building and left standing in the parking lot.

If you only have the second half — *adkhalnāhu fī* — you have… well, you can't have only the second half. Because you cannot be admitted into mercy while you are still living inside the foul.

The two halves are one motion. **He pulls you out, and He places you in.** The pulling-out is meaningless without the placing-in. The placing-in is impossible without the pulling-out.

[PAUSE]

And now look what *raḥma* becomes in this construction.

Throughout the Qur'an, mercy is most often a *thing given* — *raḥmatan minnā* (a mercy from Us), *yarḥamukum* (He has mercy on you). Mercy comes *to* you. Mercy is *bestowed*.

But in this ayah — *adkhalnāhu fī raḥmatinā* — mercy is **a place**. A space. An enclosure. Lut isn't given mercy as a possession. He is **moved into mercy** as a residence.

Stay with this. This is enormous.

When you pray for mercy in your sujūd — *yā Raḥmān, irḥamnī* — you may have been imagining mercy as a kind of warm cloud that descends on you wherever you happen to be standing. That is one valid understanding.

But this ayah opens a second understanding. **Mercy is somewhere.** Mercy is an address. The ṣāliḥīn — the righteous — don't simply *receive* mercy. They are *re-housed* into mercy. They live there.

What you are praying for, when you pray for mercy in the deep way, is not a feeling that visits you at your old address. **You are praying to be moved.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

We have looked at three small things. The inner gift that came first. The plural foulness that was never specified by name. The two prepositions that hold the architecture of rescue.

Now let us ask what these three are showing us — not just about words, but about the structure of a life.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Cannot Be Rescued From What You Have Not First Been Distinguished From

Let me tell you something many people learn the hard way.

A person feels a marriage is toxic. They leave. Within eighteen months, they are in another marriage with someone different in surface and identical in substance. They cannot understand why.

A person feels a job is destroying their soul. They quit. Within a year, they have engineered their new role to look exactly like the one they left. They cannot understand why.

A person feels a city is bad for them. They move. Within two years, they have rebuilt — in a new city, with new friends — the exact same patterns. They cannot understand why.

There is a reason. **The geographical extraction does not save anyone whose internal discernment has not been built.** The town comes with you. Not because it is following you — but because it is *in* you. You are walking in its shape.

Now read the ayah again with this in your ear:

> *"And to Lut — **We gave him judgment and knowledge** — and **then** We delivered him from the town."*

The order is not decorative. The order is *causal*. The inner gift had to come first because **without it, the outer rescue would have been cosmetic.**

Lut had to be able to *look* at *al-khabāʾith* and see it as *khabāʾith* — foul. Not normal. Not just-the-way-things-are. And this is harder than it sounds. When the foul has become habitual — when *kānat taʿmalu* is the rhythm of a place — the foul stops announcing itself. It stops wearing a sign. It presents as ordinary. It presents as the weather.

What ḥukm and ʿilm together give you is the capacity to see through the habitualness. To look at the normal and ask: *but is it good?* Al-Tabari describes ḥukm in this ayah as the capacity to arbitrate — to look at competing claims, competing pressures, competing versions of what is acceptable, and *decide*. Not to be swept along. To *decide*. With your eyes open, with the awareness that what the crowd calls settled may be precisely what requires judgment.

Lut's town had built a world in which its practices were simply *the practices of this place*. Everyone did them. The town had enough social weight to feel like reality. Against that weight, against that gravitational pull, Lut needed something strong enough to hold him — a *ḥukm* that could look at the town's version of normal and say: this is not normal. This is foul.

[PAUSE]

The practical implication is serious.

If you feel yourself wanting rescue — from a relationship, a context, a community, a way of life — the first question to ask is not *how do I get out?* The first question is *do I have the inner equipment that will prevent me from immediately reconstructing what I am leaving?*

Because if you don't have the ḥukm, you will carry the khabāʾith with you. You will unpack it in your new city. You will hire for it in your new workplace. You will attract it in your new relationships. Not because you are bad — because you have not yet been given, or cultivated, the eye that sees it for what it is.

The prayer to ask Allah for, before the prayer for rescue, is the prayer for *ḥukm* — for the inner faculty to see clearly. For the discernment that has a name for the foul, that is not confused by habitualness, that can judge justly even when the evidence is socially expensive to acknowledge.

*Then* the rescue can mean something.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: Among the Righteous — The Company Mercy Admits You Into

> **إِنَّهُۥ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحِينَ**
> *"Indeed, he was among the righteous."*

Notice the preposition here too. Not *huwa ṣāliḥ* — he *is* righteous. Not *huwa al-ṣāliḥ* — he is *the* righteous one. But **min al-ṣāliḥīn** — he is *among* the righteous.

This is a membership statement. It is not a superlative.

And this matters enormously.

If you have been reading these two ayahs and thinking: *Lut was a prophet, this rescue is reserved for prophets, I have nothing to do with this architecture* — the ayah is quietly pushing back. The *ṣāliḥīn* is not a guild of the exceptional. Al-Tabari, in his commentary on this verse, describes them as those who *act in obedience to Allah's commands and remain within His prohibitions* — those who do what they are told and hold at the boundaries. This is not a mystical elite. It is a behavioral description: consistent action, consistent restraint.

Lut was *among* them. He was in a company. A company that includes, across human time, every person who received *ḥukm* and *ʿilm* and chose to use them — who saw the foul for what it was and did not make peace with it, who held at the prohibition even when the prohibition was socially costly.

[PAUSE]

And it is because he was in this company that Allah admitted him into mercy.

Notice the logic of the closing of 21:75. It does not say: *"We admitted him into Our mercy because he had left the town."* It does not say: *"because he had suffered."* It does not say: *"because he was a prophet."*

It says: *"because he was among the ṣāliḥīn."*

The admission into mercy is the consequence of a *mode of living*, not a *biographical event*. Lut is admitted because of what he consistently was — a person who obeyed the command and held at the prohibition, day after day inside a town that did neither — not simply because of what happened to him at the end.

Al-Muyassar describes the admission into mercy as Allah *completing His favor* on Lut — the language of completion, of a story that arrived at its right conclusion because the foundations were right. The rescue was not the gift. The rescue was the opening of the gift. The gift is the life of *min al-ṣāliḥīn* that the rescue makes possible.

This is a redistribution of comfort. It means the structure these ayahs describe is not available only to prophets who are dramatically rescued from destroyed cities. It is available to anyone whose daily life is characterized by acting on the command and staying within the prohibition. If those foundations are real — if the daily practice of *ṣalāḥ* is genuine — then the admission into mercy is not an exception waiting at the far end of heroic events. It is a completion toward which a life of *ṣalāḥ* naturally arrives.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Three: Mercy Is an Address — What It Means to Live There

Let us return to the architecture of these two prepositions one final time. Because there is something we have not yet fully opened.

We said: mercy is an address. Lut is not given mercy as a possession — he is moved into mercy as a residence. The preposition *fī* — into, inside — turns *raḥma* from an object to a location.

But what does it mean to *live inside mercy?*

One of the earliest commentators whose reading al-Tabari preserved — Ibn Zayd — read the phrase *fī raḥmatinā* with a concrete specificity that reorients everything. Into Our mercy, he said, means *into the submission* — into the complete surrender and right relation with Allah that is the meaning of *islām* in its full Quranic sense. Not a denominational label. The state in which the self has oriented correctly toward what it was made for.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment before deciding what to do with it.

In this reading, mercy and correct orientation are not two separate things. They are the same thing seen from two different angles. To be in Allah's mercy is to be in right relation with Him. To be in right relation with Him is to have accepted, acted on, and lived by what He has revealed. The *ṣāliḥīn* — those who act on the command and hold within the prohibition — are not *earning* mercy as a wage. They are *inhabiting* the relationship from which mercy cannot be separated. The admission into mercy is the formal name for what has been growing inside them through every act of obedience.

Notice also how this illuminates the contrast with the *fāsiqīn* the ayah names just before. *Fisq*, at its root, means *breaking out of* — leaving the boundary. The fāsiqīn of Lut's town had broken out of obedience, broken out of the correct relationship with Allah, broken out of what al-Muyassar calls "submission to His command." They lived, in the deepest sense, outside. And so they could not be admitted into the mercy that is, in Ibn Zayd's reading, the inside of that relationship.

The town broke out. Lut stayed in. And so, when the *min* came — when the rescue pulled him from the town — there was somewhere for the *fī* to take him. The *fī* was not a new destination. It was the formal confirmation of where he had always been living.

[PAUSE]

And now look at where this places the person who has survived their own version of the town.

You have been through a time — or a place, or a relationship, or a chapter — that was *khabīth*. You have been extracted. You feel the relief of *min* — of having come out from it. But there is a question that stays with you in the quiet after the rescue: *now what?*

The ayah answers.

*Now, fī raḥmatinā.* Now, the admission. Now, the relocation into a dwelling that is not a city and not a house but a mode of being — a daily practice of *ṣalāḥ*, a consistent returning to what you know is true, a refusal to call the foul normal. Now, the completing of the favor. The rescue was the beginning of a gift. The gift is the life that happens in the mercy. The life of *min al-ṣāliḥīn*.

This is what you are being moved into.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING

These two ayahs take thirty words. They describe a man whose story takes chapters in other surahs, but here receives only the minimum structure needed to communicate the architecture.

And the architecture is this:

**First, a gift. Then, a rescue. Then, an admission.**

The gift is inner — *ḥukm* and *ʿilm*, the faculty to adjudicate and the content to adjudicate with. The rescue is outer — *najjaynāhu min*, the decisive pulling-out, the severing from a climate of *khabāʾith*. The admission is both — *adkhalnāhu fī raḥmatinā*, the re-housing, the new address, the completing of the favor.

None of the three stands alone. The gift without the rescue would be discernment inside a climate of foulness — the painful loneliness of seeing clearly in a place that punishes clarity. The rescue without the gift would be cosmetic geography. The admission without the rescue is impossible. The rescue without the admission is incomplete.

All three. In this order.

[PAUSE]

If you are in a place that has gone foul — not dramatically destroyed, but slowly, habitually foul — the sequence is your map.

Ask first for the inner gift: the *ḥukm* that can look at what has become normal and call it what it is. Ask for the *ʿilm* that makes the ḥukm accurate rather than merely confident.

Then — when the way is prepared — the rescue becomes meaningful. The *min* has somewhere to take you from and someone equipped enough to survive the journey.

And the *fī* — the admission into the mercy — is not a reward waiting at the end of a process. It is the daily practice that makes you a person to whom the admission is, in the language of the classical commentators, a *completion*. A natural conclusion to a life of *ṣalāḥ*.

[PAUSE — major]

Lut received thirty words in this surah. No narrative. No dialogue. No burning city described, no angels named, no escape route detailed. Only this:

He got the gift. He got the rescue. He got the admission.

And he was — not the greatest, not the foremost, not the most praised — he was *among* the righteous. *Min al-ṣāliḥīn.*

Which is exactly what we are hoping to become.

[PAUSE — final]

*"And We admitted him into Our mercy. Indeed, he was among the righteous."*

يَا رَبِّ، آتِنَا حُكْمًا وَعِلْمًا، وَنَجِّنَا مِمَّا يُفْسِدُنَا، وَأَدْخِلْنَا فِي رَحْمَتِكَ، وَاجْعَلْنَا مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

*Our Lord, grant us judgment and knowledge, rescue us from what corrupts us, admit us into Your mercy, and make us among the righteous.*
