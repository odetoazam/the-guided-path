# No Denial for Your Effort

### A Tadabbur on Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 94

---

> [ayah:21:94] فَمَن يَعْمَلْ مِنَ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَلَا كُفْرَانَ لِسَعْيِهِۦ وَإِنَّا لَهُۥ كَـٰتِبُونَ
>
> *Faman yaʿmal mina-ṣ-ṣāliḥāti wa huwa muʾminun falā kufrāna li-saʿyihi wa innā lahu kātibūn.*
>
> **"So whoever does righteous deeds while he is a believer — there will be no denial of his effort, and indeed, We are recording it for him."**

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Words

I want you to feel where you are standing when this ayah arrives, because the ayah does not arrive into a calm room. It arrives at the end of a storm.

For ninety-one verses, Surah Al-Anbiya has been marching through the story of prophet after prophet. Ibrahim standing alone before the fire. Nuh calling until his voice broke. Ayyub in his affliction. Yunus in the belly of the darkness. Zakariyya crying out for a child in his old age. Prophet after prophet after prophet — and behind every one of them, a people. And behind so many of those people, the same ending: they were warned, they turned away, and they were swept off the earth like they had never been there at all.

Then, just before our verse, Allah says something that gathers all of it into one sentence. *Indeed, this nation of yours is one single nation, and I am your Lord, so worship Me.* One message. One rope. One Lord. And then, immediately: **they tore their affair to pieces between themselves.** They took the one thing and shattered it into a thousand quarreling fragments.

So picture the emotional temperature of the listener at this moment. You have just watched entire civilizations get erased. You have just heard that the one message meant to save everyone was broken apart by the very people it came to. If you are a believer sitting in Makkah — small in number, mocked in the streets, watching the powerful sneer at everything you hold sacred — a very specific fear begins to rise in your chest.

Not the fear of death. A quieter, more corrosive fear.

*What if none of it counts?*

What if all this striving — the prayers whispered in secret, the wealth given away when you had so little, the insults absorbed, the nights spent awake — what if it all just... evaporates? What if I am pouring my life into something and the universe simply is not keeping track? The disbelievers are laughing. They seem fine. And here I am, exhausting myself. Does anyone even *see* it?

[PAUSE]

That is the moment. That is the room the ayah walks into. And into that fear — that ancient, universal, deeply human fear that our effort might be unwitnessed and unrecorded — Allah does not send a threat. He does not send another destroyed city. He reaches down and places a single, quiet, unbreakable promise into the trembling hands of the believer.

And here is what I need you to sit with before we go a word further. In this promise, Allah uses two words that come from opposite ends of the entire Qur'an's vocabulary — a word for *covering something up so it disappears*, and a word for *carving something in so it can never disappear.* He places them a breath apart. And what He does with those two words is the most reassuring thing a striving, tired, unseen human being could ever hear.

Let me show you.

## Part One: The Linguistic Journey

### The Farmer Who Buries the Seed

Before we touch the ayah, I want to teach you one word — because if you understand this one word, the whole verse cracks open like a nut.

The word is *kufr*.

Now, when you hear *kufr*, your mind probably goes straight to "disbelief." A theological category. The opposite of Islam. And that is true — but it is not where the word *starts*. In its bones, in the oldest layer of the Arabic language, the root *k-f-r* does not mean "disbelief" at all.

It means **to cover something so that it is hidden.**

The Arabs called the farmer a *kāfir*. Not as an insult — as a description. Because what does the farmer do? He takes the seed and he *buries* it. He covers it over with dark earth until you cannot see it anymore. He is the one who covers. The night was called *kāfir* by the poets, because the night covers the world in darkness until the shapes of things vanish. To cover, to conceal, to bury out of sight — that is the raw material of this root.

And *this* is why disbelief is called *kufr*. The disbeliever is not someone who lacks information. The disbeliever is someone who *takes the truth and buries it* — who covers over what he knows deep down, shovels dirt over the clear signs of his Lord until he can pretend not to see them. *Kufr* is an act of burial.

Hold that image. A hand shoveling earth over something until it disappears.

Now hold this second word, because it is the one that will answer it: *k-t-b*. To write. To inscribe. To carve into stone, to record in a register, to make permanent. When something is *maktūb* — written — it is fixed. It cannot be quietly forgotten. It is *there*, in the record, forever.

So now you have the two opposites. On one side, the hand that buries something until it vanishes. On the other side, the hand that carves something in until it can never vanish. **Cover versus inscribe. Bury versus record.**

Keep both hands in your mind. Watch what Allah does with them.

### The Word He Chose for "Denial"

Look now at the center of the verse: *falā kufrāna li-saʿyihi.*

Every translation you will read renders this something like "there will be no denial of his effort" or "no ingratitude for his striving." And that is correct. But notice the word Allah chose to carry that meaning. He did not use a neutral word for "loss." He did not say *there will be no waste* using some ordinary vocabulary. He reached for the root *k-f-r* — the burial root — and He built from it the word *kufrān*.

Do you feel what just happened? The exact same root that describes the disbeliever burying the truth — Allah takes that root and He *negates it over the believer's effort.*

Let me say that as plainly as I can. The verse says: *the believer's striving will never be — buried.* It will never be covered over. It will never be shoveled under the dark earth of forgetting where no one can find it again. The one thing the disbeliever does to the truth — cover it, hide it, make it vanish — that is the one thing that will **never** happen to *your* effort.

And notice *who* is being promised this. Look at the word two beats earlier: *wa huwa muʾmin* — "while he is a believer." *Muʾmin*. That word comes from the root *ʾ-m-n*, the root of *īmān*, of *amān* — safety, security, trust. So inside this single short verse, Allah has placed the two great opposites of the human soul side by side: the one who *trusts and is secure* — the *muʾmin* — and the *burial* of *kufrān*. And He arranges them so that the burial can never touch the one who trusts.

The believer is defined by *amn* — security. And so Allah secures the one thing that believer is most afraid of losing.

[PAUSE]

### "No" — But How Big a "No"?

Stay with me, because there is a small word here doing enormous work, and if we walk past it we lose half the comfort.

*Falā kufrāna.* That *lā*.

In Arabic, there are softer ways to say "no" and there are total ways to say "no." There is the "no" that means "not this time," and there is the "no" that slams a door on an entire category of thing so that not a single member of that category can ever come through. Grammarians call this second one *lā* of absolute negation — the *lā* that denies the whole genus, root and branch, without exception.

And that is the *lā* Allah uses here.

He is not saying, "there will be *little* denial of his effort." He is not saying, "*most* of his effort will be honored." He is saying: **of the entire category called 'denial of effort,' not one single instance will ever exist for this person.** Zero. Not the effort that succeeded and not the effort that failed. Not the deed everyone saw and not the deed no one saw. Not the good you did before dawn when you almost went back to sleep, and not the good you did that got misunderstood and thrown back in your face. The whole genus of "your striving being buried" — Allah takes it off the table entirely.

Think about how rare that is in your life. Almost everything you are promised comes with fine print. Every guarantee has an asterisk. And here is your Lord, closing off an entire universe of possibility with one small letter, and leaving no crack for it to sneak back in.

There is no version of the future in which your sincere striving is buried. There simply is not one.

### The Word That Is Not "Deed"

Now I want to slow us down on a word that is easy to skate past, because Allah made a very deliberate choice here and the choice is *tender*.

He does not say *lā kufrāna li-ʿamalihi* — no denial of his *deed*. He says *li-saʿyihi* — no denial of his *saʿy*.

What is *saʿy*?

*Saʿy* is not the finished deed. *Saʿy* is the **striving** — the exertion, the running, the effort of the trying. It is the same word used for the running between Ṣafā and Marwah, the frantic back-and-forth of Hajar searching the empty desert for water for her child. *Saʿy* is the sweat, not the harvest. It is the reaching, not the grasping.

Do you understand why this should undo you a little?

If Allah had said "no deed will be denied," you might still lie awake at night thinking: *but what about the deeds that didn't work out? The charity I gave that got misused? The kindness I extended that was thrown in my face? The project for His sake that collapsed? The child I raised in dīn who walked away anyway? The years I poured into something that produced nothing I can point to?* Those weren't "deeds" in the sense of accomplishments. Those were just... effort. Effort that seemed to go nowhere.

And Allah, knowing that this is *exactly* where your heart would go, does not promise to honor your *results*. He promises to honor your *striving*. Your *saʿy*. Hajar ran between those two hills seven times and found nothing. Not a drop. The water came from a completely different place, from beneath her baby's own feet. Her running did not "produce" the well. And yet Allah made her running into a pillar of Islam that a billion people re-enact every single year until the end of time.

**That** is what *saʿy* means. He is not counting your trophies. He is counting your trying.

And notice — He attaches it to you personally: *li-saʿyi-hi*. *His* effort. Yours. Not effort in the abstract. The specific striving of this specific tired human being, with a possessive pronoun wrapped around it like an arm around a shoulder.

Everything you reached for, for His sake — even the reaching that closed on empty air — none of it is buried.

### The Hand That Inscribes

And now we arrive at the closing of the verse, and this is where the two hands from the very beginning finally meet.

*Wa innā lahu kātibūn.* "And indeed We are, for him, recording."

Feel the force gathering in these words. First, the address shifts. All through the verse we have been in the third person — *whoever does... while he is... his effort.* We have been watching from a distance, as if Allah is describing a general rule. And then, suddenly, at the very end, He steps forward and speaks in the first person: **innā** — "*indeed We.*"

The camera turns. It is no longer a rule about "him." It is now a declaration from the Sovereign Himself, directly. And He does not say it softly. He piles emphasis on emphasis. *Innā* — with the heavy particle of certainty, *inna*, that in Arabic is the sound of a fist coming down on a table: *no doubt about this whatsoever.* And then, even that is not enough, so He adds the emphatic *lā-hu* — "for him," with the emphatic *lām* — fronting it, putting *for-him* **before** the verb, so the whole weight of the sentence lands on: it is FOR HIM, in HIS favor, that this is being done.

And then the final word. He does not say *sa-naktub* — "We *will* write it." A future verb would mean: someday, later, at the reckoning, We will get around to recording this. No. He uses a noun of ongoing state: *kātibūn* — "We are Writers. We are Recorders. Recording is what We are doing, right now, continuously, as a settled and permanent reality."

Now look at the two hands.

The verse opened by taking the burying hand — *kufrān* — and pushing it away forever. And the verse closes by bringing forward the *opposite* hand — *kātibūn*, from *k-t-b*, to inscribe, to carve in, to make permanent. **He does not merely promise not to bury your effort. He promises to do the exact reverse: to inscribe it.**

There is the whole architecture of the verse in two hands. On the left, the hand shoveling earth over the seed until it vanishes — and Allah says: *never, not for the one who trusts Me.* On the right, the hand carving your name and your striving into a record that time cannot erase — and Allah says: *this, this is what We are doing, right now, for you, and there is no doubt about it at all.*

Buried, or inscribed. Those were always the only two options for your effort. And your Lord has chosen the pen.

### The Missing Object

One last thing before we leave the words, and it is a thing that is *not there*.

*Wa innā lahu kātibūn* — "We are recording..." Recording *what*? In Arabic, *kātibūn* is a word that wants an object. It reaches out for a "what." *We are recording — the effort? The deeds? The intention? The tears?*

And Allah leaves it blank.

He does not fill in the object. He just says: We are, for him, Recording. Full stop.

And that silence is not a gap in the meaning — it *is* the meaning. Because the moment you specify the object, you *limit* it. "We are recording his deeds" would leave out his intentions. "We are recording his effort" would leave out the good he wanted to do but never got the chance. By leaving the object of "recording" completely open, Allah is telling you that the recording has no edges. All of it. The seen and the unseen. The done and the merely intended. The prayer and the ache behind the prayer. Everything you offered Him, in any form — it is all being caught by the pen, and none of it is escaping through some loophole in the sentence.

The absence *is* the abundance.

So that is what the words are doing. Now let us ask what they mean not for grammar — but for the life you have to walk back into tomorrow.

## Part Two: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Terror of the Unwitnessed Life

Let me tell you about a particular kind of exhaustion. You may know it.

It is not the exhaustion of hard work. Hard work, when it is *seen*, when it is *counted*, is almost invigorating. You can run on very little sleep if you know the effort is landing somewhere.

The exhaustion I mean is different. It is the exhaustion of the *unwitnessed* life. It is the mother at 3 a.m. with a sick child, doing the ten-thousandth invisible act of a decade that no performance review will ever measure, wondering in the dark if any of it registers anywhere at all. It is the man who has worked a job for twenty years, done it honestly, and watched louder and lazier people get promoted past him. It is the caregiver tending a parent who no longer recognizes them. It is the person who quietly gave up something enormous — a relationship, a fortune, a reputation — to do the right thing, and got nothing for it but silence.

Our entire modern world is built to intensify this specific terror. We now live inside machines designed to *count* us — likes, views, followers, metrics, dashboards. And these machines have quietly re-trained the human heart to believe something monstrous: that **if a thing is not counted, it did not happen.** That the unmeasured effort is a wasted effort. That the good deed with no audience is somehow less real than the mediocre one that went viral.

And so people burn out — not because they are doing too much, but because they suspect, in a place they don't say out loud, that it all disappears into a void. That they are pouring water into sand. There is a word psychologists now use for a piece of this — "moral injury," the specific wound of striving hard for what is right in a system that does not honor it. It corrodes people. It hollows them from the inside.

Now here is where the ayah walks into the room.

Allah does not tell the exhausted, unwitnessed believer, "work harder." He does not say, "get better metrics." He does not even say, "one day it will pay off." He says something far deeper. He says: *your problem was never that your effort might fail. Your problem was that you feared your effort might be buried and unseen. And I am telling you — falā kufrāna li-saʿyihi — it will never, ever be buried. Not one atom of it. Because there was always a Witness. There was always a pen. The audience you were desperate for existed the whole time — and it was the only Audience that could never look away.*

Think about what that does to the 3 a.m. mother. The act no human being will ever see, thank her for, or record — the God of the heavens and the earth is, at that very moment, *inscribing it.* The dashboard she was never on — she was on His the entire time, in ink that outlasts the stars.

[PAUSE]

The tragedy was never that our striving goes unrewarded. The tragedy is how many of us have quietly *stopped* striving in the private, unwitnessed places — the good deed no one will see, the kindness with no return — because somewhere along the way we absorbed the lie that if it isn't counted, it doesn't count. This ayah takes that lie and buries *it*.

So here is what changes tomorrow. The next time you do something good that no one will ever know about — and you feel that little pang, that flicker of *what's the point, no one will see* — I want you to hear the verse answer back before the thought even finishes: *We are, for you, recording.* And then do the deed *precisely because* it is unwitnessed. Because the unwitnessed deed is the purest proof that you believe the pen is real.

### Theme Two: The One Word That Changes Everything — *While He Is a Believer*

Now I have to be honest with you, because there is a condition sitting in the middle of this verse, and if we skip it we will have told you a beautiful half-truth.

Allah does not say, "whoever does righteous deeds, no effort of theirs is buried." He says, "whoever does righteous deeds — *wa huwa muʾmin* — **while he is a believer** — no effort of theirs is buried."

There is a hinge in this verse, and the hinge is *faith*. And I need to show you why that hinge is not a restriction. It is the very thing that makes the whole promise possible.

Let me take you into the modern world to feel it, because you already know this truth from your own life — you have just never named it.

Consider two people who do the exact same good deed. Both donate the same amount to the same cause. Both work the same hours. From the outside, the two acts are identical — you could film them side by side and not tell them apart. But one of them is doing it to be *seen* — for the tax write-off, the photo, the reputation, the warm feeling of being admired. And the other is doing it anchored to something eternal — for the sake of the One who will never fade.

Now run the clock forward fifty years. The first person's deed — what happens to it? The reputation fades. The people who admired it forget. The warm feeling evaporates by the following Tuesday. The photo yellows. The whole thing was built on sand — on the applause of creatures who themselves turn to dust. It *scatters.* The Qur'an has a devastating image for this elsewhere: deeds like *scattered dust* — you thought you had something, and when you reach for it there is nothing in your hand.

And the second person's identical deed? It was anchored, by faith, to the one thing in existence that does not fade. And so it does not fade with everything else. It is inscribed on something permanent, because it was *for* Something permanent.

**That is what *wa huwa muʾmin* is doing.** *Īmān* is not a password that unlocks the reward. *Īmān* is the *anchor line* that ties your fleeting little deed to the eternal, so that when everything else in the universe washes away, your deed does not wash away with it. Faith is what makes an act made of dust into an act made of something that lasts forever.

Take away that anchor, and it does not matter how spectacular the deed was — it is tied to nothing, and so it drifts off with all the other temporary things. This is why the same passage of Al-Anbiya that promises the believer his effort is *inscribed*, has just finished describing nations whose entire civilizations were *erased.* They built empires. They *strove* on a scale you and I cannot imagine. And it all went under the earth without a trace — because none of it was tied to the One who lasts.

The difference between the erased civilization and the inscribed believer was never the *size* of the striving. It was the *direction* of the heart.

[PAUSE]

So sit with the strange comfort of this. You do not need your deeds to be big. Al-Anbiya just showed you that *big* gets buried all the time. You need your deeds to be *anchored*. A small act of good, done by a heart tied to Allah, outlasts a monument built by a heart tied to nothing.

And here is where it comes home, concretely, tomorrow. It means that before you chase the *bigger* deed, you tend the *anchor*. It means the quiet work of protecting your *īmān* — the prayer that keeps the line taut, the honesty with yourself about *why* you're really doing what you do — is not separate from your good works. It is what keeps your good works from scattering. Check the rope before you admire the load. A believing heart doing small things is building something eternal. A faithless heart doing enormous things is building sandcastles at low tide.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Now let me show you the whole design, because I think it is more beautiful than any single piece we have held.

Watch the verse move from beginning to end. It opens with a heart — *wa huwa muʾmin*, "while he is a believer" — a heart anchored in *ʾ-m-n*, in trust and security. Then it comes to the great fear, and it names that fear with the burial-root, *k-f-r*: *kufrān*, the shoveling of earth over your striving until it vanishes. And it slams that door: *falā* — no, never, not one instance of it. And then, having pushed away the *burying* hand, the verse reaches out with the opposite hand — *k-t-b*, *kātibūn*, the *inscribing* hand — and carves your effort into permanence.

Do you see the arc? The verse travels from the *heart that trusts*, through the *fear of being buried*, to the *promise of being inscribed.* From *ʾ-m-n* to a negated *k-f-r* to an affirmed *k-t-b*. From faith, past the fear, to the pen.

And notice that the two roots at the hinges — *k-f-r* and *k-t-b* — are near-mirror images of each other, differing by a single letter, and yet they are the two great opposites of what can happen to a human effort. One covers until it is gone. The other carves until it cannot go. Allah placed both in a single breath, and then — for the one who trusts Him — He crossed out the first and underlined the second.

That is not decoration. That is the entire spiritual condition of a human being, compressed into eleven words: *you are afraid your life is being buried; I am telling you it is being written.*

### The Two Roads Meeting

And now look at how our two journeys — the words and the themes — turn out to be the same journey.

The linguistic journey ended at two hands: the hand that buries and the hand that inscribes. The thematic journey ended at two hearts: the heart chasing what fades and the heart anchored to what lasts. And they are the *same truth* from two directions. The deed of the unanchored heart is a seed the disbeliever buries — it goes under the earth and rots. The deed of the anchored heart is a word Allah inscribes — it is carved into forever. What faith does, in the language of the roots, is take your striving *out of the ground* and *into the register.* It moves your life from the buried column to the written column.

We started this reflection in a room full of fear — the fear of the small, mocked, unwitnessed believer that none of it counts. We end it holding the one thing that answers that fear at its root. Not "your effort will succeed." Effort often doesn't. But "*your effort will never be buried, and it is being inscribed right now, by Me, for you, without the slightest doubt.*"

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Do not answer them quickly. Let them find the places in you that are tired.

1. Where in your life have you quietly *stopped striving* — in some private, unseen corner — because you decided that since no one would see it, it didn't count? What would you begin doing again tomorrow if you truly believed the pen was real?

2. When you do something good, be honest: which *hand* is your deed reaching toward — the applause of creatures that fades, or the record of the One who lasts? How would you *know* the difference in your own chest?

3. Is there a striving of yours that seemed to *fail* — a good you tried to do that produced nothing you can point to? What changes when you learn that Allah promised to honor your *saʿy*, your running, and not your results?

4. The verse leaves *what* is being recorded completely open. If everything — your intentions, your aches, the good you meant but never managed — is being caught by that pen, what have you been carrying as "wasted" that was never wasted at all?

5. What does it do to your fear of being forgotten to know that the God of the heavens and the earth is, at this very moment, *a Recorder* — and that recording you is not a task He will get to, but a state He is already in?

### The One Sentence to Carry

**The God who could have let your striving be buried in silence has instead taken up the pen — so no sincere effort of a trusting heart is ever lost, ever forgotten, ever denied; all of it is being written, right now, for you.**

### The Invitation

So here is the invitation — not the obligation, the invitation. Go and do the unwitnessed good. Do the deed with no audience, the kindness with no return, the striving that may never "succeed." Do it *because* no one is watching — and let that be your quiet declaration that you believe the pen is real. Stop measuring your life by the dashboards that were built to make you feel invisible. You were never invisible. You were being *inscribed* the entire time.

Tend the anchor. Guard the *īmān* that ties your small deeds to the eternal. And then strive — freely, lightly, without the crushing anxiety that it might all evaporate — because your Lord has closed off, with one small word, the entire universe in which your effort could ever be buried.

### A Closing Duʿā

*O Allah, You who are the Recorder that never sleeps and never forgets — we believe You. We believe that not one atom of what we offered You in trust has been buried, and that even now, for us, You are writing. Anchor our hearts in faith so that our small deeds are tied to what lasts, and let us strive for You in the unseen places without fear, knowing that the only Witness who could never look away has never once looked away from us. Do not let our striving be scattered like dust — inscribe it, and inscribe us among those whose effort You honored. Āmīn.*
