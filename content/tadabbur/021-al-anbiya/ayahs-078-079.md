# Two Prophets, One Case

## When Sulayman Understood and Dawud Was Not Diminished

> وَدَاوُۥدَ وَسُلَيۡمَٰنَ إِذۡ يَحۡكُمَانِ فِى ٱلۡحَرۡثِ إِذۡ نَفَشَتۡ فِيهِ غَنَمُ ٱلۡقَوۡمِ وَكُنَّا لِحُكۡمِهِمۡ شَٰهِدِينَ ۝ فَفَهَّمۡنَٰهَا سُلَيۡمَٰنَۚ وَكُلًّا ءَاتَيۡنَا حُكۡمًا وَعِلۡمًاۚ وَسَخَّرۡنَا مَعَ دَاوُۥدَ ٱلۡجِبَالَ يُسَبِّحۡنَ وَٱلطَّيۡرَۚ وَكُنَّا فَٰعِلِينَ

> *Wa dāwūda wa sulaymāna idh yaḥkumāni fī'l-ḥarthi idh nafashat fīhi ghanamu'l-qawmi wa kunnā li-ḥukmihim shāhidīn. Fa-fahhamnāhā sulaymān. Wa kullan ātaynā ḥukman wa ʿilman. Wa sakhkharnā maʿa dāwūda'l-jibāla yusabbiḥna wa'ṭ-ṭayr. Wa kunnā fāʿilīn.*

> "And [mention] Dawud and Sulayman, when they were judging concerning the field — when the sheep of certain people had grazed through it at night. And We were witness to their judgment. So We gave Sulayman understanding of it. And to each [of them] We gave wisdom and knowledge. And We subjected the mountains to exalt [Us] along with Dawud, and the birds also. And We were the ones doing it."

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are in the Surah

Surah Al-Anbiya has been moving through a dense gallery. Ibrahim arguing with idols. Ibrahim thrown into a fire that becomes coolness and peace. Lut delivered out of a corrupt city. Nuh calling, and Allah answering, saving him from "the great distress." It has been one prophet after another, mostly in trial, mostly besieged.

And then — quietly, almost in passing — the surah turns. The mode shifts. We're no longer hearing about prophets *under siege*. We're hearing about prophets *being honored*. Mountains move with one of them. The wind serves the other. Iron softens in the hand. Birds gather to glorify. Knowledge, judgment, kingship, craft. The surah opens its second wing.

And the first sentence in this new mode is not about a miracle. It's about a courtroom.

Two prophets — a father and his son — sitting before a case. Sheep have grazed through a field at night. The owner of the field is there. The owners of the sheep are there. And somehow, between these two judges who are both prophets of Allah, **a disagreement appears**.

Classical commentators were themselves divided about what the *ḥarth* — the field, the crop — actually held. Ibn Masʿud, transmitted via Abu Ishaq, understood it to be a vineyard, grapes with hanging bunches. Qatadah described it as standing grain. Al-Tabari preserves both positions without resolving them. The Quran does not adjudicate between these readings. What all agreed upon was the verb Allah chose for the sheep's movement through it: *nafashat* — a word that Ibn Abbas, Shurayh, al-Zuhri, and Qatadah all understood as specifically nighttime grazing, in contrast to *al-haml*, which is daytime grazing. The owner of the field could not watch. The damage was done in the dark, without witness — or so it seemed.

### The Hook

Listen carefully to what Allah does next. Because what He does is one of the strangest things in the Quran.

He tells us one of them was given the deeper understanding of the case.

But He never tells us what the case turned on. He never tells us what either prophet ruled. He never tells us which ruling Allah taught Sulayman to see beyond. And — here is what's stunning — He never says that Dawud was wrong.

In fact, in the very next breath, Allah will say something remarkable — something that holds two truths at once rather than collapsing them. He'll bestow *ḥukm* and *ʿilm* on *both*, without diminishing Dawud by name or by implication. And yet *fa-fahhamnāhā sulaymān* stands unretracted. One was given a deeper view of this particular case. Both were given prophethood and knowledge. The Quran does not smooth over this distinction — it is the distinction. And understanding how these two things coexist is, it turns out, the entire teaching.

[PAUSE]

We live in a culture that has weaponized "being right." We need someone to be wrong before someone else can be right. Disagreement, in our hands, almost always becomes diminishment.

But Allah, in two short verses, is going to teach us a way of disagreeing that we have almost completely lost. Where one party can be given a deeper view of a particular case, and the other party not only is not humiliated — he is named first, his judgment is witnessed by Allah Himself, and the gift of *ḥukm* and *ʿilm* is given to him in the very same breath as it's given to the one who saw further.

This is the grammar of *ikhtilāf* — disagreement — among the people Allah loves. It is almost completely absent from how we currently fight on the internet, in our families, in our communities, in our hearts.

Let's see if we can recover even a piece of it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to dwell on **three** linguistic moments. Three small features of these two short verses. Each will open onto something larger than itself.

---

### Element One: The Witness — *shāhidīn*

Let me ask you a strange question first.

When you imagine a courtroom — say, a serious one, a high-stakes one, a case being argued by people who actually understand law — who do you imagine in the room?

You imagine the judge. You imagine the parties. You imagine, perhaps, witnesses to the original event. Maybe a clerk, a stenographer, a small audience.

But here is what you do not, by default, imagine: **you do not imagine someone watching the judges themselves.** Watching how they reason. Watching what they choose to weigh and what they choose to ignore. Watching whether they listened well, or whether they listened only to the part of the argument that confirmed what they already thought. Watching their hearts.

We almost never picture a watcher of the watchers.

Now look at how Allah closes the first verse.

> وَكُنَّا لِحُكۡمِهِمۡ شَٰهِدِينَ
>
> *wa kunnā li-ḥukmihim shāhidīn* — "And We were, for their judgment, witnessing."

Notice something. The word is **shāhidīn**, the active participle of the root *sh-h-d*. In Arabic, *shāhid* doesn't just mean "knower." Allah has many names for "knower": *al-ʿAlīm*, the One who knows. *al-Khabīr*, the One intimately aware. *ar-Raqīb*, the One who watches over.

*Shāhid* is something more specific. *Shahida* means: to be present at the event itself. To witness it directly, in real time, with one's own eyes — not to be told about it after, not to know it from afar, but to be **in the room.**

This is why a witness in court is called a *shāhid*. Not because they know what happened, but because they were *there*.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what Allah is saying.

A father and son are working through a difficult case. The owner of the field has a grievance. The owners of the sheep are explaining. The two prophets are listening, weighing, reasoning toward a ruling. And in that small room — wherever it was, sitting on cushions, perhaps at the edge of the field itself, perhaps in the modest court of a king who slept on a reed mat — **Allah was in the room.**

Not from a distance. Not as the One who would later be told about what happened. *Shāhidīn.* Present. Attending. Witnessing not the verdict alone but **the entire process** of how they got there.

Now let me ask you to do something.

Stay with that image for a moment, but transpose it. Don't picture Dawud's courtroom. Picture the last difficult conversation you had where you were trying to be fair. The last argument with a sibling about your aging parents. The last meeting where you had to decide between two team members. The last time you sat as judge — even informally — over your own children when one accused the other.

Picture all of it again. And now place into that room a *shāhid*. Not someone reviewing the file later. Someone *in the chair across from you, watching*.

Watching what you weighed. Watching what you ignored. Watching how you listened to the one you secretly favored, and how you barely listened to the one you secretly resented.

[PAUSE]

This is what *shāhidīn* does to a courtroom.

It does not change the facts. It does not change the law. It does not change what is owed to whom.

But it changes **the judge**.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah doesn't say to Dawud and Sulayman, "And We knew of their judgment." That would have been *ʿalimnā*. He doesn't say, "We were aware of their judgment." That would have been *ʿarafnā* or, in His attribute, *al-Khabīr*. He chooses the verb of presence. He says: We were in the room.

For two prophets. For a small case about a field. About sheep that wandered at night.

What does this say about the cases we adjudicate? About the WhatsApp argument we just had? About the time we spoke to our wife with that edge in our voice and told ourselves it was justified? About the way we ruled on the character of someone we have never met based on three lines of a tweet?

We have been imagining we were alone in those rooms.

Allah is telling us: there has never been a room.

There has only ever been Allah's presence, with us inside it.

[PAUSE]

This single word — *shāhidīn* — quietly destroys the entire fiction that any of our judgments are private. The husband who rules on his wife's intentions. The mother who rules on her teenage son's character. The Muslim who rules on the niyyah of another Muslim he has never met. Each of those rulings was uttered in a room. In each of those rooms, Allah was already seated.

### Element Two: The Verb of Deepening — *fa-fahhamnāhā*

Now move to the very first word of the second verse. This is going to be the heart of what we're trying to recover.

> فَفَهَّمۡنَٰهَا سُلَيۡمَٰنَ
>
> *fa-fahhamnāhā sulaymān*

Translators give us: "So We made Sulayman understand it." That's correct, but it doesn't carry the full weight of the Arabic. Let me teach you the structure first, with an everyday example, before we open the word itself.

#### Teaching the form: How Arabic intensifies a verb

In Arabic, almost every verb has a **basic form**, which we call Form I, and then several **derived forms** — Forms II through X — that take that basic verb and do something to it. They intensify it. They make it causative. They make it reciprocal. They make it reflexive. They take a single root and unfold from it a whole tree of related meanings.

Form II — the form we're looking at now — does two things. It makes a verb **causative** ("to make someone do X") and it makes it **intensive** ("to do X repeatedly, or thoroughly, or deeply").

Take a simple example. The root *ʿ-l-m* means "to know." Form I, *ʿalima*, means "he knew." Form II, *ʿallama*, means something more: "he taught" — that is, he caused another person to know, *and* he did it thoroughly, repeatedly, the way a real teacher teaches: not by saying it once, but by saying it until it lands.

Or take the root *k-l-m*: "to speak." Form I, *kalima*, doesn't really exist much in Arabic. Form II, *kallama*, means "to speak with someone deliberately, thoroughly, in conversation." This is the form Allah uses about His speech to Musa: *wa kallama'llāhu mūsā taklīmā* — "And Allah spoke with Musa, a deliberate speaking" (4:164). Form II is not casual speech. It's *engaged* speech.

[PAUSE]

Now we're ready.

#### Opening the word

The root here is *f-h-m*. The basic meaning, in Form I, is "to understand." Simple.

But Allah does not say *fahima sulaymān* — "Sulayman understood." He does not even say *aʿlamnā sulaymān* — "We informed Sulayman." He uses Form II of *fahima*: **fahhama**.

*Fahhamnā*. We made him understand. Causatively, *and* thoroughly. We sat with him, as it were, until the understanding sank in.

But there's more. Look at what comes after the verb: *fahhamnā-**hā***. That little *hā* at the end is a feminine pronoun. "We made him understand **it**."

What is "it"? Grammatically, *hā* refers back to *al-ḥukm* — the judgment, the case, the matter at hand.

This may seem small. But it is doing significant work.

Stay with me.

Allah is not saying, "We taught Sulayman more in general." He is not saying, "We gave Sulayman an additional gift of intelligence." He is saying: **on this specific case, in this specific moment, We gave him deeper understanding of the matter at hand.**

The *hā* is precise. It localizes the gift.

Al-Tabari states this with characteristic directness: *fa-fahhamnā'l-qaḍiyata sulaymāna dūna dāwūd* — "We gave Sulayman understanding of the case, to the exclusion of Dawud." The phrase *dūna dāwūd* is not softened in his reading. On this case. On this occasion. The deeper understanding was Sulayman's alone. And then — in the very same breath, without pause — comes *wa kullan ātaynā ḥukman wa ʿilman*. Al-Tabari reads *ḥukm* in that clause not merely as judicial capacity but as *nubuwwa* — prophethood itself. And *wa kullan*, in his reading, extends even beyond this father and son: it sweeps in all the prophets named in this surah. The case-specific gift belongs to Sulayman on this occasion. The prophetic mantle and knowledge of Allah's rulings belong to all of them. These are not competing registers of divine giving. They simply operate at entirely different scales.

#### Why this precision matters

This is where the verse opens. Because if Allah had said, "We made Sulayman understand" (without the *hā*), the implication might have drifted. We might have heard: Sulayman generally understood things better than Dawud. Sulayman was the smarter one. Dawud was the lesser one.

But the *hā* anchors the gift to **this case alone.**

It is saying: in this particular matter, on this particular night, Sulayman saw further. That is what was given to him. *On this matter.*

It is not saying — it is going out of its way *not* to say — that Sulayman was in general the better judge, or that Dawud was generally less. The Arabic refuses that conclusion. It pins the gift to the case.

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter?

Because we, when we disagree, almost always do the opposite. When someone in a discussion gets a point we missed, we do not say, "He saw this particular point further than I did." We say, "He is smarter than me." Or, more often, we say, "He thinks he's smarter than me."

We generalize the local victory into a global hierarchy. Or we generalize the local defeat into a global humiliation.

The Quran refuses both moves.

*Fahhamnāhā sulaymān.* On *this*. We taught him *this*.

#### The gift was teaching, not winning

And there's still more. Notice — *fahhamnā* is in Form II. It is Allah causing Sulayman to understand. It is not Sulayman pulling something out of his own intellect. It is not Sulayman being cleverer than his father. It is Allah, **the same Allah who was witnessing in the previous verse**, *teaching* Sulayman in this verse.

And what did Sulayman arrive at, having been taught? The narrations preserved by classical commentators give us the detail the Quran itself withholds by name. Dawud had ruled that the sheep should transfer to the field owner in full compensation — their value equaling what was destroyed, a clean and terminal settlement. Sulayman reached a different conclusion: let the sheep owner tend and restore the damaged crop while, during that period, the field owner draws benefit from the sheep — their milk, their wool, their produce. When the restoration is complete and the values have balanced, each owner takes back what is his. Al-Muyassar names Sulayman's principle explicitly: *muraʿāt maṣlaḥat al-ṭarafayn maʿa al-ʿadl* — attending to the interests of both parties, together with justice. Dawud's ruling closes the transaction. Sulayman's holds the relationship open long enough for repair.
