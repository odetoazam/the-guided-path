# Introduction

**The Ayahs:**

> قَالُوا۟ يَـٰوَيْلَنَآ إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَـٰلِمِينَ
> *Qālū yā waylanā innā kunnā ẓālimīn*
> "They said, 'O our destruction — indeed we were wrongdoers.'"

> فَمَا زَالَت تِّلْكَ دَعْوَىٰهُمْ حَتَّىٰ جَعَلْنَـٰهُمْ حَصِيدًا خَـٰمِدِينَ
> *Famā zālat tilka daʿwāhum ḥattā jaʿalnāhum ḥaṣīdan khāmidīn*
> "And that cry of theirs did not cease until We made them as a reaped harvest, extinguished."

## Where We Are

Step back into the surah for a moment. Surah Al-Anbiya opens with a haunting line: *"the reckoning of the people has drawn near, and they, in heedlessness, are turning away."* That word — heedlessness — is the climate of the entire opening. The elites of Quraysh are leaning back in their dwellings, joking about the verses, calling the Prophet ﷺ a poet, a dreamer, a man with confused dreams.

Then the surah does something stunning. It zooms out. It tells us: *We have destroyed before them many a township that was wronging itself.* And right at the moment those townships sensed the punishment coming — the moment the ground began to shift under them, the moment they realized — they tried to flee.

And what does Allah say to them? In ayah 13, with a sarcasm sharper than any human voice could carry:

> *"Don't flee. Return to your luxury and your dwellings — perhaps you will be questioned."*

Go back. Sit on your cushions. The servants you mocked the messenger in front of — they're waiting to take your orders. Maybe they have a question for you. Maybe.

[PAUSE]

That is the moment we are in. Ayah 13 has just been delivered. The sarcasm is hanging in the air. The mocked are now being mocked. And then — these two ayahs, fourteen and fifteen, tell us what they actually said. What sound came out of them when they finally understood.

## The Hook

There is a sound a person makes only once. Not in sadness. Not even in pain. In finality. It is the sound of someone who has just discovered, in a single instant, what they always were.

The Quran captures that sound here, in three Arabic words. And then — and this is the part that should keep you up at night — the Quran tells you what happened to those three words. They didn't stop. They kept being said. They were said and said and said. **Until the people saying them were silenced.**

Stay with me. Because the question this passage is asking is not "what happens to disbelievers?" The question is: *what is the difference between a confession that saves you and a confession that doesn't?* They are using the same words. They are saying the right thing. And it doesn't matter.

Why?

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Yā Waylanā* — The Vocative of Calamity

Before we touch this phrase, let me teach you something about Arabic that English doesn't quite have.

In Arabic, the particle **yā** is what's called a vocative — it's how you call out to someone. *Yā Muḥammad*. *Yā Rabb*. *Yā ʿAbdallāh*. You use it to address a person. You use it to direct your speech *toward* somebody who can hear you.

But Arabic does something else with this particle. It allows you to call out to abstractions. To things that aren't people. *Yā layl* — "O night." *Yā dahr* — "O time." *Yā ardu iblaʿī māʾaki* — "O earth, swallow your water." When the Arabs wanted to express the kind of overwhelming emotion that needs a recipient — when the feeling was too big to just *describe* — they would address it. They would make it a person. They would speak to it.

Now look at what they say.

> *Yā waylanā*

They do not say "we are doomed." They do not even say "we are destroyed." They say: **"O our destruction!"**

They are calling out *to their own ruin*. They are addressing it as if it were a being standing in front of them. They are personifying the thing that is happening to them — and pleading with it.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. When you call out *yā* to something, what you're really saying is *I need you to hear me*. Vocatives are about being heard. So what does it mean that the only thing left for them to address is the destruction itself?

It means there is no one else to call.

The messenger, who had been *yā*-ing them for years — *yā qawmi*, "O my people" — they had ignored. The God who had been calling them through every sunrise and every breath — they had turned their backs on. And now, at the moment they finally need to call out to *something*, the only audience they have left is **their own annihilation**. The destruction has become the only listener.

This is what the linguistic structure is showing us: *they outlived every voice that could save them, and the last voice they have left is the one that is killing them*.

There is more. The grammarians of Arabic note that *wayl* is one of those rare nouns that linguistically functions almost as a curse you place on yourself — it's a word the Arabs used in moments of irreversible disaster. So when you put *yā* in front of it, you are not just acknowledging your destruction. You are summoning it. You are saying: *come, take me, I see you now.*

Their first word in this scene is not denial. It is not bargaining. It is summoning the very thing that is consuming them — because they finally recognize it, and they have nothing else to address.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Kunnā Ẓālimīn* — The Saddest Grammar in the Qurʾān

Now the second half of their statement. *Innā kunnā ẓālimīn.* "Indeed, we were wrongdoers."

In English you might read this and think nothing of it. It seems like a normal confession. But Arabic has a choice here that English doesn't, and Allah made it deliberately.

Let me show you the choice. They could have said *innā ẓālimūn* — "we are wrongdoers." A simple nominal sentence. Present tense. *We are this thing.* That would have been a confession in the present moment.

Instead they say *innā **kunnā** ẓālimīn* — using *kāna*, the past tense verb of being, plus the active participle. The construction *kāna* + active participle in Arabic generally communicates **a state that was held over time, now being looked back on from a different vantage point**. *We were being wrongdoers.* *We had been wrongdoers.* *That is what we used to be.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. They are not saying "we are wrongdoers right now." They are saying "we *were*."

Why?

Because by the time they are speaking these words, **the time in which you can be a wrongdoer is over**. The time in which you can act, choose, repent, change — all of that is in the past tense for them. The category of *being a wrongdoer* requires a category of *being someone who could still choose otherwise*. And that has expired.

This is what I have come to think of as the saddest grammar in the entire Qurʾān. Their confession is *theologically perfect*. Every word is correct. *Innā* — emphatic certainty. *Kunnā ẓālimīn* — full ownership of the wrong. If a believer said this in this life, it would be a moment of breakthrough. A turning point. The beginning of return.

But they are saying it in past tense — because there is no present left for them to be anything in.

The verb *kāna* in Arabic doesn't just mean "was." It often carries the sense of *finalization* — a state that has been concluded. So when they say *kunnā ẓālimīn*, they are inadvertently testifying to their own sealing. The book has been closed. The category has been finalized. There will be no further entries.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this is teaching us. **Confession is not magic.** The words "I was wrong" do not have intrinsic power. What gives those words power is the *time* in which they are said. The same syllables, said five minutes earlier, would have been the beginning of a life. Said now, they are only the eulogy of one.

The grammar is performing the very thing it is describing. They confess in past tense, because they have themselves become past tense.

## Element Three: *Ḥaṣīdan Khāmidīn* — Two Metaphors Stacked

Now the closing image of the second ayah. Allah tells us what He did to them. And He does not use one image. He uses two.

> *ḥattā jaʿalnāhum **ḥaṣīdan khāmidīn***

"Until We made them a reaped harvest, extinguished."

Two words. Two images. Both about ending. But they are not the same kind of ending — and the Qurʾān is doing something extraordinary by placing them side by side.

### *Ḥaṣīd* — The Cut Crop

The root *ḥ-ṣ-d* in Arabic is the root of harvesting. The verb *ḥaṣada* means to reap, to mow, to cut down standing grain with a scythe. *Ḥaṣīd* is the noun form — the cut crop, the mown wheat, the field after the blade has passed through it.

Picture the image. A field of wheat at sunset. Tall, golden, full of itself, swaying in the wind, indistinguishable from any other field that has ever existed or ever will. And then a man walks through it with a scythe. Not in anger. Not even with attention. Just doing the work that needed to be done. And in a few hours, the entire field is *flat*.

The wheat did not see the scythe coming. The wheat did not negotiate. There was no transition between standing and lying down. **One swing.**

This is the first image of their ending. They were a crop. The harvest came. They are now flat.

Notice something about the agricultural metaphor. *Ḥaṣīd* is grammatically singular here — *jaʿalnāhum ḥaṣīdan*, "We made them *a* harvest." Not "harvests." Not "many cut things." One harvest. Because in the eyes of the reaper, an entire field of wheat is *one event*. The individuality of each stalk does not register. They were many people, each thinking themselves singular, each leaning back in their own dwelling, each mocking on their own terms — but to the reckoning, they are *one mown field*.

The crop does not get to be individual at the harvest.

[PAUSE]

### *Khāmid* — The Dying Embers

Now the second image. *Khāmid* comes from the root *kh-m-d*, which lexicographers connect to the dying down of fire. When a fire has burned through its fuel and there is no more flame — when the embers are still glowing faintly but no longer producing heat — when the last red light is fading from the coals — *that* is *khumūd*. The fire has not been extinguished by water. It has simply *run out*.

The Arabic verb *khamada* is what you use when a fire goes quiet because it has nothing left to burn.

Now look at what Allah is saying. After the harvest — after the cutting — He calls them *khāmidīn*. **Extinguished. Faded out. Quiet.**

This is a different image from *ḥaṣīd*. *Ḥaṣīd* is sudden — the scythe falls. *Khāmid* is slow — the embers cool. *Ḥaṣīd* is violent — the cutting. *Khāmid* is gentle — the silence.

[PAUSE]

Why both?

Because death is not one event. **Death is two motions.**

There is the cutting — the moment the body stops, the moment the choice ends, the moment the field goes flat. That is the *ḥaṣīd*.

But after the cutting, there is a longer quieting. The voice that was loud, the noise that was the self, the announcing of *I am here, I matter, I have positions, I have luxury, I have dwellings* — all of that activity, the friction of a person who thought they were eternal — that fades like embers. Slowly. Without anyone there to listen.

The Qurʾān is not just telling you they died. It is telling you they **went quiet**. The thing that made them feel like a person — the noise of being a self — went out like a fire whose fuel was finally exhausted.

### The Number Shift

And here is the detail that should stay with you. *Ḥaṣīd* is singular. *Khāmidīn* is plural.

When they were cut, they were cut as *one harvest*. The reaping does not respect individuals. But after the cutting — when the slow extinguishment begins — they become *khāmidīn*, plural. Each ember dying on its own. Each fire fading without an audience.

Together they were a crop. Alone they are extinguished embers.

This is the geometry of how a community of the heedless ends: **gathered for the cutting, scattered for the silence**.

## Bridge

What has the linguistic construction shown us?

That when this people finally spoke, they spoke a confession that was correct in every word and useless in every syllable. That the grammar they used betrayed them — they confessed in past tense because there was no present left. That their last sound was a vocative addressed to their own destruction, because nothing else was listening anymore. And that what happened to them was not one death but two — the cutting of the harvest, and the slow extinguishment of the embers.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of regret itself, and about the noise that we make in our own lives.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Geometry of Regret — When Words Mean Nothing

Let me start somewhere familiar.

You have, at some point in your life, sent a message you should not have sent. The instant your finger lifted from the screen, you knew. The realization arrived a half-second too late, and that half-second was the *entire* difference between a life that contained the message and a life that did not. You did not change. The message did not change. *The timing changed*. And it changed everything.

Or maybe it was the wrong exit on the highway. The instant you passed it, you knew. The knowing was the same knowing you would have had a quarter-mile earlier. But a quarter-mile earlier the knowing would have meant *taking the exit*. Now it means *being on the wrong road*. Same knowing. Different geometry.

Or — and this is the deepest version — the moment in an argument when you said the unforgivable thing. You were watching yourself say it. You knew, mid-sentence, that you should not finish the sentence. But you finished it. And the instant the last word left your mouth, you would have given anything to have it back. *The same realization, ten seconds earlier, would have stopped you*. Ten seconds later, it is only a witness to what you've already destroyed.

This is what regret is. **Regret is the right knowing in the wrong time.**

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again.

> *They said: O our destruction — indeed we were wrongdoers.*

Their words are theologically flawless. *Yā waylanā* — they recognize their state. *Innā kunnā ẓālimīn* — they take ownership. They use the emphatic *inna*. They name themselves correctly. If a Muslim said this in the masjid tonight, between *ʿishāʾ* and sleep — if you said this on your prayer mat in the last third of the night — *it would change your life*. The malāʾika would carry these words upward. The *du'āʾ* would be answered. This is the formula.

But they say it in the wrong geometry.

And here is what is hard to absorb: **the words themselves are not the variable**. The variable is *when you said them*. The same syllables, in a different time, are the beginning of return. In *this* time, they are only the inscription on a tombstone.

This is what Allah is teaching us by recording their words. Not because their words are exotic. Because their words are *familiar*. Because we have all said this. We have all had the moment of recognition. The question is not whether the words come — the question is whether they come *while there is still a present in which to say them*.

Their tragedy is not that they refused to confess. **Their tragedy is that they confessed.**

[PAUSE]

There is something in the Qurʾān's depiction of the Hereafter that this ayah connects to. Allah tells us, again and again, that those in the fire will say *yā layta* — "O if only." If only we had listened. If only we had not taken so-and-so as a friend. If only we had been dust. The Hereafter is full of perfect confessions. Spiritually accurate self-assessments. Theologically immaculate diagnoses of where one went wrong.

And none of them count.

Because the technology of repentance does not run on accuracy. It runs on **time**.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ taught us that the gates of repentance close at two moments — at the appearance of the sun from its place of setting, and at the moment the soul reaches the throat. *Reaching the throat* — that vivid image of the *rūḥ* in transit, the body becoming a corridor it is exiting — that is when the geometry locks. After that, every word you say is correct, and none of them work.

So what is the practical weight of this for tomorrow?

It is this: **the only confession that matters is the one made while you still have the ability to act differently after making it**. If you have been carrying a wrong — a debt, a relationship you have damaged, a habit you have hidden, a duty you have neglected — the *yā waylanā* moment will come. The question is whether it comes now, when you can still pivot, or later, when it can only describe.

Don't wait until you can only narrate your wrong. Confess it while you can still leave it.

That is what these two ayahs were placed in your hands to teach you.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: The Two Deaths — On the Noise of Being a Self

Now to the closing image, and what it teaches about something subtler than death itself.

We tend to think of dying as a single event. There is a *before* — a person — and an *after* — not a person. One line. One moment. The light turns off.

But the Qurʾān here describes ending as **two motions** — and the gap between them tells us something the language of "death" cannot.

The first motion is *ḥaṣīd*. The cutting. The harvest. This is the part we recognize. The body stops. The choice ends. The field that was standing yesterday is flat today. This is the death we know how to fear.

But the Qurʾān adds *khāmidīn*. **Extinguished. Faded.** And this is a different kind of ending — the slow ending of all the *noise* that the self was generating.

Stay with me, because this is the part that should reach into your life today.

What does it mean to be a person? Among other things, it means to be an engine of noise. Not necessarily literal sound — though there is plenty of that. The noise of opinions. The noise of *being someone* in a room. The noise of declaring your tastes, your preferences, your loyalties, your offenses. The noise of *the dwellings* — going back to ayah 13 — the noise of *being someone with a place, a status, a luxury, a position*. The friction of a self that thinks it is permanent.

A living person *radiates*. Like a fire. Heat, light, motion, interference with the air. To be alive is to *make a difference to the room you are in*.

And what the Qurʾān is telling us with *khāmidīn* is that the cutting is not the end of all of that. The cutting is only the end of the body. There is something else that fades more slowly — the *radiation* of the self, the heat that the person generated. The "I" that was loud. That goes out like embers. Quietly. With no one obligated to notice.

[PAUSE]

Read the sequence again.

*"And that cry of theirs did not cease until We made them a reaped harvest, extinguished."*

Their cry — *yā waylanā* — kept being said. They kept saying it. Over and over. Not because they were repeating a strategy that might work, but because they were *radiating* the only thing left to radiate. The noise of a self at its end. And the verse tells us that this cry kept being said *until*.

Until what?

Until *jaʿalnāhum ḥaṣīdan khāmidīn*. Until We cut them as a harvest, *and they went quiet*.

The fire of the self — the noise of the *I* — is what *khumūd* extinguishes. **Their last sound is regret. And then nothing.**

[PAUSE]

Now ask yourself what this means for the noise *you* are making.

Right now, today, this hour — your life is generating a particular kind of fire. The arguments you are involved in. The positions you are defending. The grudges you are nursing. The status you are protecting. The *being someone* of your everyday existence. All of that is *radiating*. It feels permanent. It feels like the texture of being alive.

The Qurʾān is telling you, in the ending of these people, what becomes of all of it. **It goes out.** Not violently — quietly. Not as a statement — as a fading. The arguments stop mattering not because they are resolved but because there is no one left who is invested in them. The status protects itself for a while after the body stops, like coals glowing red after the flame has died, and then it too cools.

You are a fire. Every fire either gets re-fueled or runs out.

The question this ayah leaves you with is: *what is the fuel you are burning?*

If your life is fueled by the things that end at the harvest — the dwellings, the luxury, the status, the noise of being someone — then your *khumūd* is already coming. The embers will fade. The heat will go. And the silence that follows will not be peaceful. It will be the silence of a confession that came too late, said over and over into a room with no one listening.

But if your life is fueled by what does not end at the harvest — by the remembrance of Allah, by the deeds that the angels carry up, by the love you put into the people whose souls outlast their bodies, by the words of the Qurʾān that you let into the inside of you — then the cutting still comes. The body still stops. But the heat does not fade in the same way. Because that fire was being fed from a different source.

The contrast Allah is offering, by showing us *khāmidīn* at the end of these wrongdoers, is exactly the contrast that the surah will deliver later in the lives of the prophets. They face hardship. They are tested. They die. **But they do not go *khāmid*.** Their light keeps coming. Through their words, through their stories, through the surahs named after them, through the *du'āʾ* of every Muslim who has ever asked Allah to be made among the salihīn the way He made them. Their fire was being fed from somewhere the harvest cannot touch.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you let yourself get loud about. It changes what you let yourself burn fuel on. It is a question to ask in the small moments — when you feel the urge to assert, to argue, to defend, to be seen, to make a noise that proves you exist: *what is this fire being fueled by? Will it go khāmid at the harvest, or will it keep giving heat after?*

The Prophet ﷺ used to ask Allah to make him remember Him *kathīran* — much. And to be grateful to Him *kathīran*. He was, in effect, asking to be a fire fed from a source that the *ḥaṣīd* cannot reach. He was asking to die without going *khāmid*.

That is the *du'āʾ* this ayah teaches us to make.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Step back and see what these two ayahs have done, in only thirteen words of Arabic.

The first ayah gave us the *sound* of the end — a vocative addressed to destruction, and a confession spoken in the past tense because there is no present left. It is the moment the self finally sees itself, and the moment is too late by a hair's breadth and an eternity at the same time.

The second ayah gave us the *image* of the end — first the harvest, then the embers. First the cutting, then the silence. First the moment, then the long fading.

And do you see how the pieces fit?

The vocative *yā waylanā* — calling out to destruction — *is itself the radiation of a fire that is about to go khāmid*. The confession *innā kunnā ẓālimīn* — in past tense — is itself an inscription of someone who has already become *ḥaṣīd*, even before the description of the cutting comes.

The first ayah is *sound*. The second ayah is *what becomes of sound*. **First the cry, then the silence.** That is the architecture.

The whole micro-passage is one teaching: *you have a window in which your sound matters; outside of that window, the same sound is only a record of what you were*.

## Questions to Carry

1. Is there a confession I have been postponing — to myself, to Allah, to a person I have wronged — until a moment that may not be granted to me?

2. What is the fuel of my fire today? When my body stops, will the heat I have been generating be the kind that fades into *khumūd*, or the kind that the harvest cannot reach?

3. Who am I addressing when I am most distressed? The vocatives I reach for in my hardest moments — *yā* whom? — what does that reveal about which voices I have kept alive in my life?

4. If I had to write my confession in *kāna* + active participle form right now — *kuntu* what? — what would the verse read? And do I have time to make sure that is not the verse Allah inscribes about me?

5. The people of these ayahs spoke the right words at the wrong time. Where in my life right now am I waiting for permission, when in fact I already know exactly what to say and exactly when to say it?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Their last word was the right confession at the wrong time, and the silence that followed was not peace but extinguishment — and Allah recorded it so that I would say my confession while there is still a *me* to say it.**

## The Closing Invitation

Do not wait for the harvest to teach you how to confess. The exact words you need are already in your mouth. The grammar you need is the present, not the past — *innī ẓālim*, not *innī kuntu ẓālim*. The audience you need is the One who *can still hear*, not the destruction that will only consume.

Open your hands tonight, while your *rūḥ* is still in the middle of you and not at the threshold of your throat, and say what these people said — but say it in the time when it works.

That is the entire mercy of being given this ayah while you are still alive to read it.

## Du'ā'

*Allāhumma*, do not let me be among those whose confessions arrive after the cutting. Make my *yā waylanā* a *yā Rabb*, and make it in a time when You can still answer. Let my fire be fed from a source the harvest cannot reach, so that when the body stops, the light You placed in me does not go *khāmid* — but returns to You as something You recognize as Yours. Āmīn.
