# Return to Yourself

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayahs 62–64

> قَالُوٓا۟ ءَأَنتَ فَعَلْتَ هَـٰذَا بِـَٔالِهَتِنَا يَـٰٓإِبْرَٰهِيمُ
>
> قَالَ بَلْ فَعَلَهُۥ كَبِيرُهُمْ هَـٰذَا فَسْـَٔلُوهُمْ إِن كَانُوا۟ يَنطِقُونَ
>
> فَرَجَعُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ فَقَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّكُمْ أَنتُمُ ٱلظَّـٰلِمُونَ

*"They said: 'Did you do this to our gods, O Ibrahim?'*
*He said: 'Rather, this big one of them did it — so ask them, if they can speak.'*
*So they returned to themselves and said: 'Indeed, it is you who are the wrongdoers.'"*

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world we have to enter

Imagine a city. Not a small one. A city built around a temple. Generations of architecture, generations of priesthood, generations of stone-carvers whose entire economy depends on the gods that fill that temple. The idols are not just objects. They are the city's identity card. They are what makes this people *this* people. Everyone you know, everyone your parents knew, everyone in the cemetery — they all bowed to these stones.

And then, on a festival day when the city has emptied out into the fields for celebration, one young man stays behind. He walks into the temple. He picks up an axe. And he reduces every god in that room to rubble — except one. The biggest. He leaves the axe leaning against its shoulder.

Then he goes home and waits.

When the city returns, the scream that goes up from that temple is not just religious horror. It is civic. It is economic. It is existential. *Who would do this?* The investigation begins immediately. And someone says — there's a young man, Ibrahim, who has been speaking strangely about these gods for some time.

So they bring him in. Classical commentators record, on the authority of Ibn Ishaq, that this was no ordinary public hearing — Ibrahim was brought before Nimrod himself, the king. The entire community had gathered before their sovereign. This is not a neighborhood tribunal. The whole city is watching, and at the center of the room sits the ruler who will decide what happens next.

### What this passage actually is

This is the trial scene. But what makes these three ayahs one of the most psychologically dazzling passages in the entire Quran is what Ibrahim *doesn't* do. He doesn't preach. He doesn't lecture them about *tawḥīd*. He doesn't quote anyone. He gives them, instead, a single sentence that is almost a joke — and then he steps back and lets the room itself do the work.

And something happens that is described in the strangest, most haunting phrase in the whole episode: *"fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim"* — **so they returned to themselves**.

[PAUSE]

Where had they been, that they needed to return?

That is the question this entire reflection is going to live inside.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to walk through three linguistic moments. The first is the accusation. The second is Ibrahim's response. The third is the phrase that may be the most psychologically rich four words in this entire surah.

### Element One: The shape of an accusation — *"ʾa-anta faʿalta hādhā?"*

Listen to how the people open this trial. In English, the translation reads almost neutrally: *"Did you do this to our gods, O Ibrahim?"* But Arabic has an architecture that English cannot show you, and that architecture is doing real work.

In Arabic, the normal way to ask "Did you do this?" would be: *"hal faʿalta hādhā?"* — *hal* is the standard yes-or-no question particle. Calm. Procedural. The question a detective asks.

That is not what they say.

They say: **"ʾa-anta faʿalta hādhā?"** — and that opening *hamza* (*ʾa-*) is a different particle. It carries a built-in tone of incredulity, of accusation, of the question that is already half a verdict. And then look at what comes next: not the verb, but the **pronoun**. *Anta*. *You*. They put **you** before **did**.

Here's why that matters. In Arabic, when you front the pronoun in a question — when you put *anta* before the verb — you are no longer asking *whether* the action happened. You are asking *whether **you** were the one who did it*. The shock has shifted from the deed to the doer.

Stay with me. Imagine you find your living room destroyed and you walk into the kitchen where your teenager is standing. You don't say *"Did the living room get destroyed?"* You say: *"**You** did this?"* — voice rising, eyes locked, the whole sentence collapsing onto that one word: *you*.

That is *ʾa-anta faʿalta hādhā*.

And then the final word: **"yā Ibrāhīm"** — *O Ibrahim*. They name him. In a public space, this is not affection. This is the name spoken into a record. *We see you. We have a witness. The name is now attached to the act.*

Notice something else, almost invisible. They don't say *"to the gods."* They say **"bi-ālihatinā"** — *to **our** gods*. The possessive pronoun is doing emotional work. *These were ours. You took something that belonged to **us**.* The whole question is built on a possession claim — and that possession claim is exactly what Ibrahim is about to detonate.

[PAUSE]

So before Ibrahim has spoken a single word, the accusation has already declared:
- The deed is shocking (the *hamza* of incredulity).
- The accused is identified (the fronted *anta* + the named *Ibrāhīm*).
- The victims are *theirs* — a community whose ownership is now wounded.

Ibrahim is being given a script. He is supposed to either confess or deny. Either way, the conversation stays about *him* — *did he do it, did he not do it, what should be done with him*.

He refuses the script.

### Element Two: *"bal faʿalahu kabīruhum hādhā"* — the answer that isn't an answer

Look at how Ibrahim opens his reply. **"Bal."**

*Bal* in Arabic is a particle of contradiction, of pivot, of *"no — rather."* It is the word you use when someone has presented a frame and you intend to break the frame entirely. They asked, *Did you do this?* He answers with a word that doesn't say yes and doesn't say no — it says: *the very direction your question is going is wrong*.

Then: **"faʿalahu kabīruhum hādhā"** — *this big one of theirs did it*.

We have to slow down here, because there is a centuries-long question in the tafsir tradition about this sentence. Did Ibrahim lie? Did the prophet of God, whom the Quran elsewhere calls *ṣiddīq* — utterly truthful — speak a literal falsehood?

The classical scholars work this out from several angles, and each angle adds a layer to the same insight.

One reading: Ibrahim is speaking the truth in a sense his audience is refusing to hear. He is saying — *if you really believe these are gods, then the biggest one is the one who would have done this, because the smaller ones could not stop him. So go ahead. Ask him.* It is a conditional truth, hung on the absurdity of their own claim.

Another reading: Ibrahim is using a rhetorical structure the Arabs themselves understood — a *taʿrīḍ*, a pointed indirection. The statement is not false because no honest hearer would take it as a literal report. It is closer to what we today would recognize as a logical *reductio* — *let's accept your premise and see where it leads*.

A third reading attends to the word **"hādhā"** — *this one*. Some scholars note that in Arabic, *hādhā* can be read as belonging to the next clause: *"fa-faʿalahu kabīruhum — hādhā fas'alūhum…"* — *the big one did it. **Now,** ask them.* Read this way, *hādhā* becomes a discourse marker, not a finger pointing at the idol but a turn-marker in the speech.

And classical commentators add a fourth register entirely. Al-Tabari records, in the chain through Ibn Ishaq, that Ibrahim was voicing the inner logic of the idol-theology itself — that the great idol, if it were indeed a god, would have been *angered* that lesser stones were worshipped alongside it when it was greater. Ibrahim is not inventing a claim; he is reasoning from within the very premises of the belief system he is dismantling, taking polytheism's own hierarchy seriously enough to follow it to its absurd conclusion. Al-Muyassar calls this completing what Ibrahim "wanted of exposing their foolishness before their own eyes" — the *taʿrīḍ* aimed squarely at their *ghabāwah*, their obtuseness.

These are not four competing answers. They are four lenses on the same act. Ibrahim is staging a scene whose entire purpose is to make his audience hear how their own theology sounds when spoken back to them. He is not transmitting information. He is **holding up a mirror made of their own words**.

And then he closes with the trap: **"fas'alūhum in kānū yanṭiqūn"** — *so ask them, if they can speak*.

Listen to that conditional. *In kānū yanṭiqūn*. The word *yanṭiqūn* comes from a root *N-Ṭ-Q* that does not just mean "to make sound." It means **articulate speech** — the speech of a *nāṭiq*, a rational speaker. The word for "logic" in Arabic — *manṭiq* — comes from this same root.

So Ibrahim is not just saying *if they have voices*. He is saying *if they are capable of rational, articulate response — go interrogate them*. The condition he attaches is the condition of personhood itself. Speech as the marker of being a someone rather than a something.

And every person in that courtroom knows the answer. *They do not speak. They cannot speak. They never have spoken.*

Ibrahim has done something extraordinary. He has not refuted their theology. He has refused to participate in the assumption that there is a theology to refute. He has handed them a question and walked out of the room.

[PAUSE]

This is *istidlāl bi'l-maḥsūs* in its sharpest form — argument from what the senses can verify. He doesn't argue that the idols are not gods. He stages a scene in which their own ears hear the silence.

### Element Three: *"fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim"* — the four words that change everything

And now we arrive at the phrase we have been walking toward this entire reflection.

**"Fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim."**

Translation: *"So they returned to themselves."*

Stop here. Sit with it. Because in those four words is one of the most precise descriptions of the human conscience ever recorded.

Let me first show you the grammar, slowly.

**Fa-** — *and so, and then*. A consequence-marker. Whatever Ibrahim just said *caused* this next thing.

**Rajaʿū** — *they returned*. From the root *R-J-ʿ*. This is the same root from which the Quran builds words like *rajʿah* (return) and *marjiʿ* (place of return) and *innā lillāhi wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* — *to Him we return*. *Rujūʿ* is not casual returning. It is going back to a place where you originally were.

**Ilā anfusihim** — *to their selves*. *Nafs* is one of the deepest words in the Quranic vocabulary. It is the inner self, the soul, the *I* that you address when you talk to yourself in a quiet room.

Put it together: *So they returned to their own selves*.

Now the question that should haunt us: **where had they been?**

Because the sentence presupposes an absence. You cannot *return* somewhere unless you had been gone. So where were these people, that hearing one sentence from Ibrahim sent them *back* to themselves?

[PAUSE]

Here is what classical commentators understood — and what every honest human being can verify in their own life. There is a place we live most of the time that is **outside ourselves**. We live in the inherited story. We live in what the tribe believes. We live in the collective performance of certainty. The Arabic tradition has a beautiful word for this — *taqlīd* — *imitation*, the borrowing of a position you have not yourself examined.

These people had spent their entire lives bowing to stones — not because they had ever asked themselves *do these stones hear me?* — but because everyone bowed. The shape of their lives had been outsourced. The deepest questions had been answered for them, by ancestors, by priests, by social architecture.

And then a young man with an axe — who refuses to confess, who refuses to deny, who simply hands them back their own theology in the form of a question — does something that no sermon could do.

He sends them home. To themselves.

And the heart of this verse is that **the truth was already there waiting**. They didn't have to go learn anything new. They didn't have to hear an argument. They simply had to come back to the *nafs* — the inner witness — that had been there the entire time, gently knowing that stones do not speak.

This is the *fitrah*. The inborn knowledge of God that every human being carries and most human beings spend their lives drowning out with the noise of the crowd.

And the moment they return to it — listen to what they say.

**"Innakum antum aẓ-ẓālimūn."**

Three layers of emphasis stacked on top of each other.

*Inna* — *indeed, truly*. The particle of certainty.
*Kum* — *you* (plural, as a suffix on *inna*).
*Antum* — *you* (the same plural pronoun, said *again* as an independent word).
*Aẓ-ẓālimūn* — *the wrongdoers* — and notice the definite article *al-*. Not *some* wrongdoers. *The* wrongdoers. The category itself.

In Arabic rhetoric, when you say *inna* + a pronoun + *the same pronoun again* + a definite predicate, you have constructed maximum emphasis. The closest English can come is something like: *"It is **you** — **you yourselves** — who are **the** wrongdoers."*

But here is where it becomes vertiginous. **Who are they talking to?**

The grammar says *antum* — *you all*. Second person plural. They are addressing somebody as *you*. But the verse just told us they have *returned to themselves*. They are alone in their own interior. There is no one in front of them.

[PAUSE]

The grammar enacts the moment of conscience.

When a human being returns to themselves and finally sees what they have done, the inner voice does not say *"I am the wrongdoer."* It says *"**you** are the wrongdoer."* The self splits — into a witness and an accused. The conscience always speaks in the second person.

You have felt this. You have lain awake at three in the morning replaying something you did and heard a voice in your own head say: *what is wrong with you?* That voice did not say *what is wrong with me?* It said *you*. The conscience always addresses the self as *another*.

And that is exactly what is happening here. *"Innakum antum aẓ-ẓālimūn"* — the people of Ibrahim are looking into the mirror of their own *nafs* and saying to themselves: *you. you, yes you, are the wrongdoers.*

But here we should pause and acknowledge something the classical tradition carefully preserves. Ibn Kathir notes that when they "returned to themselves," the specific content of their self-reproach was not immediately a recognition that the idols were false — they were blaming each other, and themselves, for having *neglected to guard the gods*, for failing to protect their shrines. "You are the wrongdoers," Ibn Kathir explains, was in the first moment an accusation of negligence: *because you left them unguarded*.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. Because it actually makes the passage more profound, not less.

The conscience activated. The inner witness woke up. The machine of self-examination turned on. But it processed what it found through the only framework it had — the framework of the very idolatry it had not yet left behind. The *nafs* returned home, but home was still furnished with false gods.

And this is why the very word they chose — *ẓālimūn* — is so haunting. The root *Ẓ-L-M* does not simply mean "wrongdoer" in the abstract. In the classical lexicons, *ẓulm* means to place a thing in a position that does not belong to it — to disorder the right relation between things. *Ẓulmah* is darkness because it covers what should be illuminated. Wrongdoing is *ẓulm* because it violates the created order. And the Quran elsewhere names the greatest wrongdoing with this exact root: *inna al-shirka la-ẓulmun ʿaẓīm* — *associating partners with God is a tremendous ẓulm*.

So when these people, standing in their own interior, said *you are the ẓālimūn* — they used the precise Quranic word for what they were. The conscience found the right word. It just could not yet find the right object for that word. They spoke *ẓulm* when they thought they meant *negligence*. But the word itself was a confession they did not yet know they were making.

## PART 2: THE REVERSAL

And then comes what follows — the verse that cannot be separated from this one.

*"ثُمَّ نُكِسُوا عَلَى رُءُوسِهِمْ"* — **Then they were turned upside-down on their heads.**

*Nukisū*. Passive voice. They were *overturned*. The word *nakasa* means to be flipped, to have what was on top placed on the bottom. This is not the language of honest reconsideration. This is the language of being capsized.

They went in — they *returned to themselves* — and in that interior space they saw something true. And then they came back out, into the public room, with the king watching, with the crowd watching, with the priests watching, with the full social architecture pressing down on them — and something flipped.

What did they say when they flipped? *"Laqad ʿalimta mā hāʾulāʾi yanṭiqūn"* — *"You already know that these cannot speak."*

[PAUSE]

Read that carefully. They are not arguing that Ibrahim is wrong. They are *conceding* his argument. They are saying: *you know as well as we do that the idols cannot speak*. And their conclusion from this concession is not *"therefore we should stop worshipping them."* Their conclusion is: *"therefore you have no case."*

This is the anatomy of a refusal that cannot argue. When you cannot defeat a logic, you don't engage the logic — you dismiss the logician. *You already knew. So this was just a stratagem. Therefore, we don't have to reckon with it.*

Ibn Kathir's reading of their "return to themselves" becomes even more important in this light. They did not return to themselves and find monotheism. They returned to themselves and found guilt — the wrong kind of guilt, guilt for negligence rather than for shirk. And that wrong guilt was not strong enough to withstand the weight of the king's gaze.

The conscience worked. But the conscience was not given enough time, or enough silence, or enough courage, to do its full work.

[PAUSE]

This is the tragedy the Quran is documenting here. Not a people who are too obtuse to see — but a people who *do* see, for a flash, and then look away. The return happened. The inner witness spoke. The right word surfaced. And then the crowd pulled them back.

The question the Quran is putting to every reader who encounters this passage is not only about Ibrahim's contemporaries. It is: **what are you looking away from right now?**

## PART 3: WHAT IBRAHIM TEACHES US ABOUT THE MIRROR

Here is what Ibrahim did *not* do.

He did not make them feel stupid. He did not condescend. He did not compile a list of everything wrong with their theology and present it in a logical progression. He did not argue from outside their framework. He did not invoke authority. He did not, at this moment, even testify to his own faith.

He gave them a question. And then he waited.

*Fas'alūhum in kānū yanṭiqūn.* Ask them, if they can speak.

The entire strategy rests on one insight: **the truth they needed was already inside them**. He did not have to carry it in. He just had to create the conditions in which their own inner witness could be heard.

This is a kind of *daʿwah* — invitation — that the Quran models through the prophets again and again. Not a lecture but a mirror. Not *here is what you are wrong about* but *here is your own premise, followed to its honest conclusion*. Al-Muyassar calls it *iḥtijāj ʿalayhim maʿa al-taʿrīḍ bi-ghabāwatihim* — argument against them coupled with a pointed gesture toward their own blindness. The blindness was always theirs to see.

And the mirror works, for a moment. They return to themselves. The *nafs* speaks. The word *ẓulm* surfaces — the right word, even if attached to the wrong object.

[PAUSE]

This is what *fitrah* actually looks like in practice. It is not a clean, dramatic awakening. It is a glimmer. A word rising from somewhere deeper than the inherited story. A flicker of second-person address — *you are the wrongdoer* — that surfaces before the crowd reasserts itself.

The Quran is not telling us that Ibrahim's strategy *succeeded* in the sense we might want. In a few verses they will throw him in the fire. The people revert. The brief return is overturned.

But the Quran is telling us something more important: **it happened**. For one moment they were home. The inner witness activated. The word came. Even in a city built around idols, even under the gaze of a king, even under the full social pressure of generations of worship — a young man with an axe and a question produced a moment of genuine interior return.

And the Quran records it. Preserves it. Makes it part of the permanent sacred text.

Because moments like that — even flickers — matter. Because *fa-rajaʿū ilā anfusihim* is not a footnote in this story. It is the point of the story. The idols will be rebuilt. The fire will be lit. But the fact that the people *could* return — that the path back to the nafs was still open, still passable, that one well-placed question could send an entire crowd home to themselves — that is the evidence the Quran is entering into the record.

The *fitrah* is not destroyed by years of *taqlīd*. It is dormant. It is waiting. It only needs a question it cannot escape.

## DISTILLATION

Ibrahim did not argue his way into their conscience; he handed them a silence loud enough that their own *nafs* had to speak — and for one moment, before the crowd pulled them back, it did.

## DU'A — A PRAYER FROM THESE VERSES

*O Allah — You created us with the fitrah, the inborn compass that knows You. You placed the inner witness inside us before we could protect it or damage it. You built the conscience into the architecture of the nafs.*

*But we have spent so much of our lives outside ourselves — in inherited certainties we never examined, in the crowd's verdict, in what everyone else believes, in the noise that drowns the inner voice.*

*Bring us back. As You brought Ibrahim's people back — even for a moment — to themselves.*

*Let us hear what the nafs already knows. Let us not be overturned on our heads the way they were — seeing the truth for a flash and then looking away when the gaze of the room grows heavy. Give us the courage that comes after the return — the courage to stay home, to follow the recognition through, to let the right word find its right object.*

*And when we encounter those who are living outside themselves — make us less like debaters and more like Ibrahim: holders of a question, not professors of an argument. Mirrors, not hammers.*

*Āmīn.*
