# When He Responds, He Doesn't Restore — He Multiplies

## The Ayah

> فَٱسْتَجَبْنَا لَهُۥ فَكَشَفْنَا مَا بِهِۦ مِن ضُرٍّ ۖ وَءَاتَيْنَـٰهُ أَهْلَهُۥ وَمِثْلَهُم مَّعَهُمْ رَحْمَةً مِّنْ عِندِنَا وَذِكْرَىٰ لِلْعَـٰبِدِينَ

**Fastajabnā lahu fa-kashafnā mā bihi min ḍurr, wa-ātaynāhu ahlahu wa-mithlahum ma'ahum, raḥmatan min 'indinā wa-dhikrā lil-'ābidīn.**

> "So We responded to him and lifted from him what was upon him of harm. And We gave him back his family — and their like along with them — as a mercy from Us and a reminder for the worshippers."

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World Around This Ayah

Before we open this verse, let's stand for a moment in the world the original listener inhabited.

This Surah, Al-Anbiya, is moving through prophet after prophet at remarkable speed. Each one is sketched in two or three ayahs and we move on. Why? Because the Surah is doing something specific. It is showing the people of Makkah — who were ridiculing the Prophet ﷺ and asking, "If he is really sent by God, why is he being treated this way?" — that this is precisely how prophets are treated. The procession of suffering and rescue is the *signature* of prophethood, not the contradiction of it.

So when we arrive at Ayyub, we have already met Ibrahim thrown into fire, Lut among a destroyed city, Yunus swallowed by darkness. The Surah is teaching us a grammar: the way Allah handles those He loves most.

And in that grammar, Ayyub is the most extreme case. He is the prophet who lost everything — wealth, children, health — and stayed in his place. Stayed worshipping. Did not curse. Did not bargain.

In the previous ayah, 21:83, Allah quotes his entire du'a in fifteen Arabic words:

> "*Wa-Ayyūba idh nādā rabbahu annī massaniya-ḍ-ḍurru wa-anta arḥamu-r-rāḥimīn.*"
> "And Ayyub, when he called upon his Lord: 'Harm has touched me, and You are the most merciful of the merciful.'"

[PAUSE]

Notice what is not in that du'a. There is no request. There is no "remove this from me." There is no list of what he wants restored. There is just: *here is my condition. And here is who You are.*

He places his pain on one side of the sentence and Allah's mercy on the other. He doesn't even draw the conclusion. He lets Allah draw it.

That is what the next ayah — our ayah — responds to.

**Here is the question I want you to carry into this reflection:** when a slave whispers his pain to his Lord without demanding anything, how does the Lord respond? What is the *grammar* of His reply? Because you are about to see that the reply is not what you would expect. It is not equal to the request. It is not even proportional to it. It overflows the request in three directions at once — and ends with a sentence that turns the entire ordeal into something larger than Ayyub.

Let's open it slowly.

---

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Fastajabnā* — The Difference Between Answering and Responding

Let me first teach a small piece of Arabic grammar, because without it the next several minutes won't land.

In Arabic, verbs come in different "forms" — usually numbered I through X. Form I is the basic form. The other forms add meanings — intensification, causation, reflexivity, mutuality, *seeking*, and so on.

There is a verb in Arabic, *jāba* (root j-w-b), that means roughly "to traverse, to cross, to come back." From this root come several related verbs:

- **Form IV:** *ajāba* — "he answered." This is the standard verb for a basic reply. You ask, I answer. The transaction is complete. *Ajāba* is what you do when someone calls your name and you say, "Yes?"
- **Form X:** *istajāba* — "he responded." Form X often carries the meaning of *seeking* or of *fully receiving and responding*. The word doesn't just mean "he replied." It means "he received the call into himself and responded with his whole orientation."

Both verbs translate into English as some version of "answered" or "responded." But in Arabic they are not the same.

Now look at the verse.

Allah does not say *ajabnā lahu* — "We answered him." Allah says **fastajabnā lahu** — "We responded to him fully."

[PAUSE]

Sit with this for a moment.

Ayyub did not ask for anything. He just whispered his condition and named Allah's mercy. There was no request to "answer." There was a presence to *respond to*. And Allah doesn't deal with what was not said by ignoring it — He responds to the *whole posture* of the slave.

Here's the analogy. Imagine someone you love walks into the room. They don't say a word. They just sit down. There is something heavy on their face. Now: there are two ways you can react.

You can say, "Did you need something?" That's *ajāba*. You're answering a question — even one that wasn't quite asked.

Or you can stop what you're doing. Turn your whole body toward them. Set down your phone. Look at them and *be* with what they are carrying — before they have explained it, before they have asked you for anything. That's *istajāba*. That's responding to a person, not answering a query.

**Allah dealt with Ayyub the second way.**

Notice something: Ayyub never said *help me*. The whole architecture of his du'a was *I am here in this state, and You are who You are.* And Allah's first move in His reply is a verb that *receives the whole of him* — not just the words he said, but the silence around the words, the unasked thing, the dignity in not asking, the trust embedded in not bargaining.

Here's what's remarkable. The classical lexicographers note that *istajāba* in the Qur'an is almost always paired with this exact dynamic — a slave who turned to Allah, often without articulated demand, and Allah's response is described in this Form X. It is the divine grammar for *meeting the slave where he is*.

Stay with me. Because this means something for you.

When you are in a place where you cannot even form the request — when grief has flattened your sentences, when shame has shut your mouth, when the trial has gone on so long you've stopped knowing what to ask for — and all you can do is sit in front of Allah and *be* in front of Him — He does not require you to file a complaint in the proper format.

He responds to the *posture*.

He responds to the *being-there*.

The Form X is His promise that the whole of you — including what you cannot articulate — is being received.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Mā Bihi Min Ḍurr* — The Harm That Was On Him, Not Of Him

Now look at how Allah describes what He removed.

In Arabic, if you wanted to say "We removed his harm," the simplest construction would be: *kashafnā ḍurrahu* — "We uncovered/removed his harm." Two words. Possessive. The harm belonged to him.

But Allah doesn't say that. Allah says: **kashafnā mā bihi min ḍurr** — literally, "We lifted *what was upon him of harm*."

Five words. A relative clause. An *external* construction.

Notice what this construction is doing. It is placing the harm *on* Ayyub, not *in* him. It speaks of the harm as something that had alighted upon him — like a cloak, like dust, like a shadow — not as something that was *of his essence*.

Here's the analogy. Imagine your phone falls in mud. You can describe what happened in two ways. You can say "the phone is dirty" — making dirt a property of the phone. Or you can say "there is mud on the phone" — making mud something exterior to it, sitting on top of it, separable from it.

The two sentences describe the same scene. But they imply very different relationships between the phone and the mud. The first relationship is descriptive — "this is how the phone is now." The second is about *layering* — "the phone is itself; the mud is something else."

[PAUSE]

When Allah describes Ayyub's suffering, He uses the second kind of construction. *What was upon him of harm.* The harm is not who Ayyub is. It is something that had settled upon him for a season.

And the verb chosen — *kashafa* — is the verb you use for *lifting a covering*. You lift a cloth off something to reveal what's underneath. The verb assumes there is something *underneath* the harm that was always there, untouched.

What this invites us to consider is this: when you are deep in a trial, the trial feels like it has become *you*. The illness feels like your identity. The grief feels like your face. The depression feels like the inside of your chest. You begin to forget there was ever a you that wasn't this.

Allah's grammar pushes back on this.

He describes Ayyub — at the height of his ordeal, after years of it — as someone who had harm *upon him*, not someone who *was* the harm. The Ayyub underneath was still there. Still intact. Still the slave Allah loved.

And the rescue is described as *kashf* — *uncovering*. Not *creation*. Allah did not have to make a new Ayyub. He had to *uncover* the one who had always been there.

Stay with this. Because if you have ever been in a long trial — long enough that you stopped recognizing yourself — Allah is telling you something here. The you that He loves is still under there. The trial is the cloak, not the body.

### Element Three: *Mithlahum Ma'ahum* — Their Like, *With* Them, Not *Instead of* Them

Now we come to the most stunning two words in the verse. And to see them, we need to think carefully about what we *expect* the verse to say.

If you were writing a story of restoration — if you were the screenwriter tasked with giving Ayyub his happy ending — what would you write?

You would write: *and We gave him a new family in place of the one he had lost.* In Arabic, that would be **ahlan badala ahlihi** or **mithlahum badalahum** — "the like of them *instead of* them."

That is the natural restoration ending. The slate is wiped, the loss is replaced, life resumes.

But Allah does not say that.

Allah says: **wa-ātaynāhu ahlahu wa-mithlahum ma'ahum** — "And We gave him *his family* — and *their like* — *with them*."

[PAUSE]

Look at the word *ma'ahum*. Not *badalahum* (in place of them). Not *ghayrahum* (other than them). **Ma'ahum.** *With* them.

The grammar of replacement is rejected. The grammar of *addition* is chosen.

The classical mufassirūn discuss what this means and they offer different angles — and the right way to hold their opinions is not as alternatives but as *camera angles* on the same astonishing reality.

Some say: Allah restored to Ayyub the very family members he had lost — and gave him another family of equal size in addition. So he ended with two families' worth of loved ones.

Others say: Allah gave him so many descendants — children and grandchildren — that they exceeded what he had originally had, and that this overflow is what *mithlahum ma'ahum* is pointing toward.

Others, citing other narrations, hold that some of his family had survived and were given back to him along with new generations on top.

The opinions differ on the mechanism. **They agree on the grammar.** The grammar insists: *what came after the trial was greater than what was lost.* Not equal. Not restored. *Multiplied.*

Here's the analogy. Suppose you lent a friend a hundred dollars and they couldn't pay it back for years. Then one day they show up at your door. They hand you a hundred dollars — your money returned. And then they hand you another hundred. They say, "I owed you this. I'm giving you this also."

That second hundred is *ma'ahum*. It is the part that breaks the math.

Restoration says: I make you whole. *Mithlahum ma'ahum* says: I make you whole, *and then I do more*.

[PAUSE]

This is the grammar of Allah's response that you are meant to see.

He doesn't return the slave to baseline. He doesn't restore the original state of affairs. He responds to the long silence of the patient slave by *overflowing the original ledger*.

And notice — there is something deeper still. The construction *ma'ahum* implies that the original *was not erased*. It is not as though Allah says, "I'll replace what you lost with something better, but the loss itself stays in your story as a wound." The two-word *ma'ahum* asserts that the original was given back — *and* added to.

What this invites us to consider is this: in Allah's economy, your trial is not a tax on your future. The losses you sustained while turning toward Him do not get subtracted from your final tally. They get added back, and then more is laid on top of them.

That's what *with them* means.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### A Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

So far we have seen three things in this short verse.

We have seen that Allah's response to Ayyub is described with **istajāba** — Form X — the verb that means *responding to the whole posture of a slave*, not just to his articulated request.

We have seen that the harm itself is described as **mā bihi min ḍurr** — *what was upon him of harm* — placing the suffering *on* Ayyub, not *of* him, and the rescue as a *lifting* of a cover from a self that was always there underneath.

We have seen that the family is restored with **mithlahum ma'ahum** — *their like, with them* — the grammar of multiplication, not replacement.

These three things, taken together, sketch the *shape* of how Allah deals with the slave who turns to Him in extremity without demanding. He receives the whole of you. He treats your suffering as exterior to your essence. He responds with overflow, not equivalence.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way the universe is actually arranged for the worshipper.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Arithmetic of Divine Response

Let me start where you live.

Most of us, somewhere inside, are running a quiet calculation about our own trials. The calculation goes something like this: *if I just endure this long enough, eventually I'll get back to where I was before this happened.* The marriage will heal. The body will recover. The career will resume. The grief will fade. We are aiming at *baseline*.

Endurance, in our private mathematics, is the price we pay to *get back to even*.

Now look again at what just happened in this ayah.

Ayyub was not given baseline.

Allah did not return him to "where things were before the trial." Allah returned him to *more*. The verse insists on this with two words — *mithlahum ma'ahum* — that we are still chewing on.

[PAUSE]

This is not how human beings reward endurance. Human beings, in our best moments, give back what was lost. We restore. We make whole. We say "as good as new."

Allah does not deal in *as good as new*. Allah deals in *better than before.*

Here is something striking. The Prophet ﷺ said that no calamity befalls a believer — not even a thorn that pricks him — except that Allah expiates sins by it or raises his rank. The hadith is well known. But notice what it is doing. It is taking the human concept of *cost* — the idea that pain is something you *pay* — and inverting it. In Allah's economy, the pain is not a cost. The pain is the *currency with which something larger is purchased*.

The widow who buries her husband patiently is not being subtracted from. She is being deposited into.

The man whose business collapses and who does not curse the time it took to build, is not in deficit. He is being credited.

The mother whose body is failing and who keeps her tongue from complaint — every silent hour of her struggle is being weighed in a scale we cannot see, against a reward we cannot price.

And when Allah finally responds — and He always does, in this life or the next — the response is not equal to the request. It is *raḥmatan min 'indinā* — mercy from *His own presence*.

Look at that phrase. **Min 'indinā.** Not just *minnā* — "from Us" — but *min 'indinā* — "from with Us," "from Our presence."

In Arabic, *'inda* implies a treasury, an interior, a place of storage. *Min 'indinā* points toward something Allah keeps near Himself, in His own immediate possession. The mercy that comes for the patient slave is drawn from Allah's *own private treasury*. It is not generic mercy dispensed from inventory. It is mercy with the King's seal on it, drawn from where the King keeps his most precious things.

This is the analogy. Imagine two kinds of gift. One kind is the gift purchased on the way: the bouquet from the gas station. It is fine. It is a gift. The other kind is the gift the giver took *off their own person*: the watch from the wrist, the ring from the hand, the heirloom from the drawer. The second gift is *min 'indī* — *from with me*. Its value is not the object. Its value is the *intimacy of its provenance*.

[PAUSE]

When Allah finally answers your long silent waiting — He does not hand you a generic outcome. He hands you something drawn from *His own with-ness*.

What does this change for tomorrow?

It changes the calculation. You are not waiting for *baseline*. You are not enduring to *get back to even*. The patience is not the price of restoration. The patience is the substrate on which something *more* will be built — not by you, but by Him, from a source that is His own.

This means: the next time the trial drags on past the point where you can see the end, you are not in the *deficit* phase of your life. You are in the *deposit* phase. Something is being stored up that you have not yet been shown.

### Theme Two: A Reminder for the *Worshippers*, Not the *Patient*

Now we come to the final phrase. And it does something so unexpected that if you have been reading the Qur'an carelessly, you will miss it entirely.

Allah ends the verse: **wa-dhikrā lil-'ābidīn** — "and a reminder for *the worshippers*."

Stop here.

Whose story is this? Whose story is this *most famously* in the entire Qur'an?

This is the story of Ayyub. And in the Islamic tradition, in the Bible's tradition, in the popular consciousness across continents, Ayyub is the icon of one specific virtue. He is the icon of *patience*. *Ṣabr*. He is, in fact, the prophet who is named in the proverb-language of the Arabs and the Muslims as *the most patient of the patient.*

So when the verse arrives at the closing — *and a reminder for...* — the word you expect, the word the rhythm of the verse practically demands, the word any human writer would supply, is **ṣābirīn**. *The patient.*

"And a reminder *for the patient.*"

That would be the perfect rhetorical close. The Surah has already invoked patience as an explicit theme. The Ayyub story is *about* patience. The audience is primed.

[PAUSE]

But Allah does not say *ṣābirīn*. Allah says **'ābidīn**. The *worshippers*.

Why?

This is not a small word swap. This is Allah re-titling the entire story.

Here is what *ṣabr* is. Ṣabr is a posture toward calamity. It is what you do *with respect to the trial.* You hold steady. You do not curse. You do not collapse. Ṣabr is admirable. It is one of the highest praised qualities in the Qur'an. But notice — ṣabr is fundamentally *about the trial*. The calamity is the subject; ṣabr is the response.

Here is what *'ibādah* is. 'Ibādah is a posture toward *Allah*. It is what you do *with respect to your Lord.* The subject is no longer the calamity. The subject is the relationship. *'Ibādah* is the bowing, the calling, the remembering, the trust, the love.

Do you see the difference?

A man can have ṣabr toward his disease and never turn toward Allah. He can grit his teeth, refuse to complain to people, manage his pain with stoic dignity — and Allah barely enters into it. That is ṣabr without 'ibādah.

A man can have ṣabr toward his disease *and* keep praying *and* keep dhikr *and* keep saying "You are the most merciful of the merciful" — that is ṣabr in service of 'ibādah. The trial is no longer the subject. *Allah* is the subject. The trial is just the room he is doing it in.

[PAUSE]

What Allah is saying in this final word — *'ābidīn* — is that **the Ayyub story was never primarily about endurance. It was about whether you continued to worship while in pain.**

The miracle of Ayyub is not that he survived. It is that he stayed *facing Allah* the entire time. The trial did not redirect his worship. The trial *was the place where his worship was happening.*

Here is the analogy. Imagine two runners caught in a sudden rainstorm during a marathon. One of them slows down, hunches his shoulders, puts his head down, and grimly endures until the rain passes. He is patient. He does not quit. He gets through it.

The other runner does not slow down. He does not change his form. He keeps running exactly as he was running — in the same rhythm, with the same breath, with the same focus on the finish line. He is *also* getting wet. He is *also* enduring. But the rain is not interrupting him. The rain is just the weather his running is happening *in*.

Both have ṣabr. Only one is still *running*.

[PAUSE]

Ayyub was the second runner. The disease did not interrupt his worship. The losses did not interrupt his worship. The disease was simply the weather his worship was happening in.

And this — *this* — is what Allah wants the worshippers of every age to remember.

Here is what this changes.

It changes what you think the trial is *for*.

Most of us think a trial is an *interruption* of our religious life. "I'll get back to consistent prayer once this is over." "I'll start tahajjud again when I'm not so tired." "I'll do dhikr more when I'm in a better headspace." We treat the difficulty as the *enemy* of the worship.

This ayah re-frames it. The trial is not an interruption of your worship. *The trial is where your worship is being forged.* The thirty seconds of dhikr you whispered through tears in the hospital bathroom — that is not lesser worship done in spite of the pain. That is *the worship Allah preserved this entire story to point toward.*

When Allah closes Ayyub's story with *dhikrā lil-'ābidīn* — *a reminder for the worshippers* — He is telling every reader of the Qur'an, in every language, in every century: *this is for you, if you keep facing Me. The story is not given to you because you have a long trial ahead. The story is given to you because no matter what comes, the question has never been "will you survive?" The question has always been "will you keep worshipping?" And if you do — what happened to him is the shape of what is coming for you.*

That is what the closing word does.

What this changes about how you live tomorrow: the next time you find yourself in pain and your prayer feels small, do not be ashamed of the smallness. Do not wait for a "better" moment to worship. The small whisper from the hard place is not a downgraded version of the worship you'll do later. It is *the worship the entire Ayyub story exists to validate.*

You are an *'ābid*, not a *ṣābir*. The pain is just the weather.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture We Just Walked Through

If you stand back from this single ayah, you can see now that it is built from three matching gestures.

**Istajāba** — Allah received the whole of him.
**Kashafnā mā bihi min ḍurr** — Allah lifted what was on him, leaving the him underneath untouched.
**Mithlahum ma'ahum** — Allah multiplied, not restored.
**Min 'indinā** — Allah gave from His own presence, not from inventory.
**Lil-'ābidīn** — Allah re-titled the story away from endurance and toward worship.

Each of these moves the same direction. Each refuses the smaller frame. Each insists on something larger than what we expected.

This is the *grammar* of how Allah handles the slave who turns to Him in extremity without demanding.

He does not do *equal*. He does not do *fair*. He does not do *baseline*.

He does *overflow*.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar of the verse — the Form X verb, the externalizing construction, the *ma'ahum*, the *min 'indinā*, the *'ābidīn* — is not decorative. It is the *visible signature* of a divine pattern that is invisible elsewhere. Allah is letting us read off, from these word choices, the way the unseen world is actually arranged.

And the way it is arranged is this: *the worshipper who keeps worshipping in the dark is not in deficit. He is in deposit. And when the response comes — and it will come — it will not match his ledger. It will overflow it.*

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Do not rush.

1. Is there a trial in your life right now where you have been *enduring* but not *worshipping*? What would it mean to start treating the trial as the *room* of your worship, not the obstacle to it?

2. Are you running a calculation in which you are aiming at *baseline* — at "getting back to where things were"? What would it cost you to stop aiming there?

3. When was the last time you turned to Allah in du'a *without* a list of requests — just to be in front of Him, like Ayyub? What stops you?

4. Is there a way you have come to identify *with* your suffering, rather than seeing it as something *upon* you? What would it look like to remember the you underneath?

5. If your trial is a *deposit phase* and not a *deficit phase* — what do you stop doing? What do you start doing?

### One Sentence

When the slave whispers his pain without bargaining, Allah does not restore him — He multiplies what was lost, draws the multiplication from His own presence, and turns the entire ordeal into a textbook for everyone who keeps worshipping in the dark.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not change your trial. You probably cannot. That is not what this ayah is asking.

What this ayah is inviting is something smaller and harder: to stop treating the trial as an interruption of your relationship with Allah, and start treating it as the place where the relationship is being deepened in ways you cannot yet see.

You are not a *ṣābir* waiting for the rain to stop. You are an *'ābid* running through the rain. The finish line is the same. And what waits for you there is not your old life restored. It is *mithlahum ma'ahum* — what was, *and more than what was, with it* — drawn from His own treasury, with His own seal on it.

### Brief Du'a

Yā Allah — *istajib lanā* — receive us as we are, before our requests are formed. Lift from us what is upon us of harm, and let us recognize, underneath it, the slave You shaped. Make us of those who do not merely endure the dark, but worship inside it. And when You respond, give us not what we lost — but what is *min 'indika*: from Your own presence, with Your own seal on it. Let our ordeal be a *dhikrā* — a reminder — for someone who comes after us and does not yet know that the rain is just the weather. Āmīn.
