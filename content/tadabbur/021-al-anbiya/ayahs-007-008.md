# Why the Prophet Eats: The Body That Carries Revelation

## The Ayahs

> وَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَا قَبْلَكَ إِلَّا رِجَالًۭا نُّوحِىٓ إِلَيْهِمْ ۖ فَسْـَٔلُوٓا۟ أَهْلَ ٱلذِّكْرِ إِن كُنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ
>
> *Wa mā arsalnā qablaka illā rijālan nūḥī ilayhim, fas'alū ahla-dh-dhikri in kuntum lā taʿlamūn.*
>
> **And We sent not before you except men to whom We revealed [Our message] — so ask the people of the Reminder if you do not know.**
>
> وَمَا جَعَلْنَـٰهُمْ جَسَدًۭا لَّا يَأْكُلُونَ ٱلطَّعَامَ وَمَا كَانُوا۟ خَـٰلِدِينَ
>
> *Wa mā jaʿalnāhum jasadan lā ya'kulūna-ṭ-ṭaʿāma wa mā kānū khālidīn.*
>
> **And We did not make them a body that did not eat food, nor were they immortal.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Where We Are

We are at the beginning of Surah Al-Anbiya — the Surah of the Prophets. Notice the name. The surah is going to take us through a long line of messengers: Musa and Harun, Ibrahim, Lut, Nuh, Dawud, Sulayman, Ayyub, Yunus, Zakariyya, Yahya, Maryam, ʿIsa. A whole gallery of prophets is about to unfold.

But before Allah opens that gallery, He has to answer an objection. A specific objection. The same objection that every generation of deniers has raised, in slightly different words. The objection sounds reasonable. The objection sounds like fairness. The objection is this:

**"If God really wanted to send us guidance, He could have sent something more impressive than this man."**

In a different surah, the deniers say it openly: *"What is the matter with this messenger that he eats food and walks in the markets?"* (25:7). Why does he get hungry? Why does he need to sleep? Why does he have a wife? Why does he look exactly like us?

If he's special, why does he look so... unspecial?

### What This Moment Felt Like

Imagine you are sitting in Makkah in the early years of revelation. You know Muhammad ﷺ. You've known him your whole life. You've watched him grow up. You've seen him laugh. You've seen him bargain in the market. You've seen him eat dates and drink water. He is, by every visible measure, a man. Then he stands up and says: *I bring you a message from the Lord of the heavens and the earth.*

And something inside you resists. Not because what he says is unreasonable. But because **HE** seems too reasonable. Too human. Too local. If God wanted to speak, wouldn't He send something with wings? Something that doesn't sweat? Something you couldn't have grown up with?

This is the resistance Allah is responding to. And His response is one of the most quietly devastating answers in the Quran.

### The Hook

Watch what Allah does in these two ayahs. He does not say: *"Trust Me, this messenger is special even though he looks ordinary."* He says something far more startling. He says: **his ordinariness is the design.** The eating, the mortality, the hunger, the body — these are not flaws Allah was unable to remove. These are features Allah deliberately built in.

[PAUSE]

The body that eats food is not a problem the messenger has to overcome. **The body that eats food is the messenger.**

Stay with me. Let's see how Allah builds this argument with the precision of a master architect.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Restrictive Construction — مَا... إِلَّا

Before we read the ayah, let me teach you a structure in Arabic that English doesn't have.

In English, when we want to say something is exclusive — "only this, nothing else" — we use the word "only." *Only men were sent.* It's a single word, easy to skim past, easy to soften.

Arabic does it differently. Arabic builds a frame around the exclusive thing. It first denies everything — *"We did not send"* — and then carves out a single exception — *"except men."* The structure is **negate-everything-then-allow-one-thing**. Grammarians call this *ḥaṣr* — restriction, confinement, walling-in.

The effect is dramatic. You think the door is closed. Then a single door opens. And only that door.

Hear it now:

> وَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَا قَبْلَكَ إِلَّا رِجَالًۭا
>
> *We did NOT send before you... EXCEPT men.*

Notice something. Allah does not say: "We sent men before you." That would be a normal statement of fact. He says: *We did not send anything except men.* Every other possibility — angels, jinn, voices from the sky, beings of fire — every other channel of guidance — Allah is closing those doors first. Then opening only one door. The human one.

Why does this matter?

Because the deniers were essentially saying: *We would have accepted an angel. We would have accepted a being of light. We would have accepted anything more than this man.* And Allah's response is not, "I chose to send a man." His response is, **"I have never sent anything else."**

[PAUSE]

This is not a one-time choice. This is a pattern stretching across all of history. Every prophet you've ever heard of — Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, ʿIsa — every single one was a man. Not because Allah couldn't do otherwise. Because human guidance requires a human guide.

Think about what this implies. If Allah had ever once sent a non-human messenger, the deniers' demand would be reasonable. They could say: *You did it for them, do it for us.* But Allah is saying: **There is no precedent for what you're asking. You're not demanding fairness. You're demanding something that has never been on the table.**

The walls of the *ḥaṣr* close in. The only door open is the one they refuse to walk through.

### Element Two: رِجَالًا — Men, Indefinite

The word Allah uses is رِجَالًا — *rijālan*, "men." But notice — it's **indefinite**.

In Arabic, you can say *the men* (الرجال) or *men* (رجال) — definite or indefinite. The choice is never accidental. The definite form would point to a specific, known group. The indefinite form opens up something else.

When Allah says *rijālan* — indefinite men — He is saying: *just men. Ordinary men. The kind you'd pass in a market. The kind you wouldn't look at twice. Men of various kinds, men of various backgrounds, men whose distinguishing feature is not their distinction.*

This is the deflation the deniers cannot tolerate. They wanted a category — *The Sent Ones, The Chosen, The Distinct.* Allah gives them a category they cannot stomach: *just some men.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. The next word, نُّوحِي إِلَيْهِمْ — *to whom We were revealing* — uses the present tense. Not *to whom We revealed* (past). *To whom We were and are revealing* — ongoing, continuous.

Watch the structure: *We sent* (past) men *to whom We are revealing* (present). The sending is a completed event in history. The revealing is portrayed as a perpetual current — a stream that keeps flowing through the human channel.

The men were ordinary. The current passing through them was extraordinary. **The vessel was indefinite. The water was definite.**

This is the inversion the deniers cannot perform. They want the vessel to be glorious so they can trust the water. Allah is saying: *The vessel is one of you. Trust the water because it is from Me, not because the cup looks special.*

### Element Three: جَسَدًا — A Single Body

Now we come to ayah eight, and to a word that has puzzled grammarians for fourteen hundred years.

> وَمَا جَعَلْنَـٰهُمْ جَسَدًۭا لَّا يَأْكُلُونَ ٱلطَّعَامَ
>
> *And We did not make them a body that did not eat food.*

Look carefully at the word جَسَدًا — *jasadan*, **a body**. Singular. One body.

But the verse is talking about all the prophets — plural. The pronoun is جَعَلْنَاهُمْ (We made *them*) — plural. And then suddenly the word is جَسَدًا — *a body*, singular.

Classical grammarians explain this in two converging ways. First, this is what's called a *collective singular* — using one body-form to stand for the entire pattern of prophetic embodiment. As if Allah is saying: *We did not make their bodily form one that doesn't eat food.* The single word *jasad* names the category, not a number. Second, the singular emphasizes the unity of the pattern — every prophet had **the same kind** of body, the same vulnerable, hungry, sleeping, dying form.

Now hear what Allah is denying. He is denying that He made them جَسَدًا لَّا يَأْكُلُونَ ٱلطَّعَامَ — *a body that does not eat food*. **A body that does not eat food.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that phrase. *A body that does not eat food.* What is that? A body that does not eat food is not really a body in the human sense. It's a sculpture. It's an apparition. It's a thing that looks like a body but doesn't function like one. A facade.

What the deniers wanted — what every generation has wanted — was a messenger who **looked** human enough to talk to but didn't carry the **weight** of being human. A body without hunger. A body without exhaustion. A body without need.

And Allah is saying: *I did not make My prophets that. I did not give them statue-bodies. I gave them eating-bodies.*

This is the heart of it. The eating is not a concession. The eating is the proof.

A body that doesn't eat doesn't experience the dependence of needing. A body that doesn't eat doesn't know what fasting means. A body that doesn't eat cannot teach you what gratitude tastes like, because it has never had a tongue that received the date after a long day. **A body that doesn't eat cannot be your model, because it has never been you.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Four: وَمَا كَانُوا خَالِدِينَ — Nor Were They Immortal

The final phrase. *And they were not immortal.*

The word خَالِدِينَ comes from the root خ-ل-د — *khulūd*, abiding eternally. This is the same root that names paradise: *Jannāt al-Khuld* — the Gardens of Eternity. *Khulūd* is the divine quality that human beings will receive only in the next life, only after death has been crossed.

What is Allah denying here? He is denying that any prophet was given that *khulūd* in this world. Every prophet died.

Every. Prophet. Died.

Adam died. Nuh died — even after nine hundred and fifty years of preaching. Ibrahim died. Musa died. ʿIsa was raised, but his earthly mission ended. Muhammad ﷺ died.

[PAUSE]

Why is this one of the most theologically important sentences in the Quran?

Because the death of the prophet is the final test. As long as the messenger is alive, the followers can lean on the messenger. The messenger becomes the shrine. The messenger becomes the destination. The moment the messenger dies, the followers are forced to do what the message was always asking them to do: **turn to Allah directly.**

When Muhammad ﷺ died, Abu Bakr stood up and said: *"Whoever worshipped Muhammad — Muhammad has died. Whoever worshipped Allah — Allah is alive and never dies."* And then he recited: *"Muhammad is no more than a messenger; messengers have passed away before him..."* (3:144).

The mortality of the prophet is the **vaccine** Allah builds into prophethood. It prevents the message from collapsing into messenger-worship. It forces every generation to receive the revelation as revelation, not as the personality of the man who delivered it.

If a prophet had been given *khulūd* in this world — eternal life — the entire human race would have eventually worshipped him. The denial of *khālidīn* is not a deprivation. **It is a protection.**

### Bridge

So look at what we have. The restrictive structure (*we sent nothing except men*). The indefinite *rijālan* (just men, ordinary men). The collective singular *jasad* (a body that eats). The denial of *khulūd* (a body that dies).

Four linguistic moves, each one closing off an escape route. Each one denying the deniers a category they would prefer. The messenger is human, the messenger eats, the messenger dies — and **none of these is a flaw.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the very design of guidance itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Pure Leader Fantasy

Here is something true about every human heart, including yours and mine.

When we look for guidance, we don't actually want it from someone who shares our weaknesses. We want it from someone who has somehow transcended them. We want the teacher who is never tired. The parent who never doubts. The shaykh who never gets angry. The leader who never has private struggles.

The moment we discover that our hero eats food — the moment we find out the imam is in debt, the moment we learn that the mujāhid argued with his wife last night, the moment we read that the saint fell asleep during a meeting — something inside us deflates. *Oh. He's just a person.*

That deflation is the same deflation the people of Makkah felt. They saw Muhammad ﷺ in the market, eating dates, and they thought: *Oh. He's just a person. He cannot be the messenger of God.*

[PAUSE]

This is one of the deepest human errors, and Allah is exposing it in two short ayahs.

The error goes like this: *Authority can only come from someone untouched by my weaknesses.* So if I am hungry, my teacher must never get hungry. If I am afraid of death, my teacher must never die. If I have desires, my teacher must have transcended desire. Otherwise — what does he have to teach me? He's just like me.

But think about this for a moment. **A teacher who has never been hungry cannot teach you about fasting.** A teacher who has never feared cannot teach you about courage. A teacher who has never died cannot teach you how to face death. A guide who has never walked the road cannot guide you down it.

The pure leader fantasy is a self-defeating fantasy. The very purity we demand from our guide is the purity that would disqualify him as a guide.

When Allah sent prophets who ate food and died, He was not lowering the bar. He was making guidance possible at all.

### The Modern Bridge

You see this in our own time, in a sad, secular form. We elevate a politician, a celebrity, a thinker. We project onto them a kind of purity they never claimed. Then we discover they are a person — they had an affair, they have a temper, they hold an unfashionable opinion — and we destroy them. We don't just lower our estimate. We invert it. *They lied to us. They were never special.*

But they never claimed to be non-human. **We needed them to be non-human, and we are angry that they are not.**

The Quran is gently pointing out that this same dynamic killed prophets in every age. The pagans of Makkah needed Muhammad ﷺ to be inhuman before they could trust him. When they discovered he was human, they did not adjust their model of prophethood. They rejected the prophet.

Allah is asking us, through this ayah, to do the harder thing. **Adjust the model.** Accept that guidance was always going to come through someone who eats food and walks in markets. Accept that this is the design. Accept that the human-ness of the messenger is not the cost of revelation. **It is the medium of revelation.**

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes how I receive teachers. It changes what I forgive in my parents. It changes what I expect from my imam. It changes what I demand from myself.

If even prophets ate food and died — if even prophets had bodies that hungered and bodies that ended — then perhaps my hunger is not a flaw to escape. Perhaps my mortality is not a defect. Perhaps the fact that I am a body that needs is not the obstacle to my closeness with Allah. **Perhaps it is the very surface on which that closeness gets written.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: Embodied Revelation as Mercy

Now let's go deeper. Not just to the psychology of denial — but to the architecture of mercy itself.

Why did Allah, who could have written the Quran across the sky in letters of fire, choose to deliver it through a man who got tongue-tied, who sweated when revelation came down, who feared he might die when the angel first appeared?

The answer is hidden in the word جَسَدًا — body — and in what a body actually is.

A body is the surface on which experience gets written. When you are hungry, your body remembers hunger. When you fast, your body learns about restraint. When you stand for prayer, your body practices stillness. When you prostrate, your body teaches your soul about smallness before Allah.

**Revelation that does not pass through a body cannot be lived.** It can only be admired from a distance.

If Allah had sent the Quran through an angel directly to all of humanity — every soul receiving the words simultaneously without any human intermediary — the words would have been received, but they would never have been **performed.** There would be no Sunnah. No demonstration of what *patience* looks like when your child dies. No demonstration of what *forgiveness* looks like when the people who tortured you for thirteen years are now standing helpless before you. No demonstration of what *gratitude* looks like when you have nothing but a torn cloak and a few dates.

The body of the Prophet ﷺ was the **canvas** on which the Quran was painted in three dimensions. The eating, the sleeping, the marrying, the grieving, the laughing, the bleeding — these were not interruptions of the message. **They were the message.**

[PAUSE]

Think about your own life. The teachings that changed you — were they words on a page? Or were they someone living in front of you?

The mother who lost her son and still prayed Fajr — that taught you about sabr more than any chapter on patience. The father who held his anger when he was wronged — that taught you about ḥilm more than any sermon. The teacher who stayed after class to help the slowest student — that taught you about ikhlāṣ more than any discourse on intention.

This is how guidance works. **Through bodies. Through lives. Through the ordinary substance of one human being walking in front of another.**

Allah did not give us an angel-prophet because He wanted us guided, not just informed. The angel could have informed us. Only the man could guide us. And the man could only guide us by being a man — by hungering, by hurting, by dying.

### The Asking

There is one more thing in 21:7 we have not lingered on. After Allah establishes that all prophets were men, He gives a command:

> فَسْـَٔلُوٓا۟ أَهْلَ ٱلذِّكْرِ إِن كُنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ
>
> *So ask the people of the Reminder if you do not know.*

Notice the structure. Allah does not say: *Believe Me when I tell you all prophets were men.* He says: **Go ask.** Go to the people who have a record. Go to the Jews who remember Musa. Go to the Christians who remember ʿIsa. Go to whoever remembers any prophet at all. Ask them: did your prophet eat food? Did your prophet die?

The answer in every case will be yes.

Allah is inviting evidence. He is opening His own claim to verification. He is saying: *if you don't know, go find out.*

This is a remarkable confidence. It tells you that the human-ness of every previous prophet is so well-attested in human memory that the deniers cannot escape it through ignorance. They can only escape it through willful denial.

**The argument is not that the human messenger is unique. The argument is that the human messenger is universal.**

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes how I read the lives of the prophets in this very surah. As Surah Al-Anbiya unfolds — Ibrahim arguing with his father, Musa carrying the staff, Yunus in the belly of the fish, Ayyub in his illness, Zakariyya weeping for a child — I am not reading about beings of light. **I am reading about bodies.** Bodies that ate. Bodies that bled. Bodies that died.

And every one of those bodies carried something Allah placed inside it. A trust. A message. A possibility. The same possibility Allah has placed in **my** body, even though I am not a prophet — the possibility of carrying His remembrance through ordinary days, through the eating of food and the breaking of fasts, through the small obediences and the large griefs, until the body returns to dust and the soul returns to Him.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now we can see what Allah built in these two short ayahs.

Ayah 7 establishes the pattern — *every prophet was a man.* It then gives the verification — *go ask the people of the Reminder.* The argument is universal and falsifiable.

Ayah 8 closes off the two escape routes the deniers might still try. **Maybe the prophets were special bodies that didn't really need food** — *no, they ate.* **Maybe they were given some kind of immortality that put them above us** — *no, they died.*

Two ayahs. Four moves. Every door of objection sealed.

And then — only then — does the surah open the gallery. *Remember Musa. Remember Ibrahim. Remember Lut. Remember Nuh.* Ayah after ayah, prophet after prophet. But now the listener is reading correctly. Now the listener knows: these are bodies. These are men. These are mortals. **And that is precisely why their guidance can reach me.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The restrictive *mā... illā* and the indefinite *rijālan* — these are Allah closing the door on the demand for a non-human messenger.

The collective singular *jasad* and the denied *khālidīn* — these are Allah closing the door on the demand for a non-eating, non-dying messenger.

The thematic insight is the same. **Guidance had to be embodied to be guidance.** The pure leader fantasy is the enemy of actual guidance. The body that hungers is not the obstacle to revelation. It is the surface on which revelation gets written.

### Questions to Carry

1. What teachers in my life did I reject the moment I discovered they were human? What guidance did I lose because I needed the guide to be more than a guide?

2. Where in myself do I treat my own body — its hunger, its tiredness, its needs — as an obstacle to closeness with Allah, rather than as the very canvas on which that closeness gets painted?

3. The Prophet ﷺ ate dates. He laughed. He grieved. He died. Which of his ordinary humannesses do I quietly skip over when I imagine him? What would change if I let him be as human as Allah let him be?

4. Allah said *go ask*. When I have a question about my faith, do I go ask? Or do I sit in private doubt because going to ask would feel like weakness?

5. If even prophets were not given *khulūd* in this world — if even Muhammad ﷺ died — what am I waiting for to begin the work of preparing for my own return?

### One Sentence

Allah designed prophethood to come through bodies that hunger and bodies that die, because guidance has to be embodied to be guidance — and an angel-messenger is one you cannot follow.

### The Closing Invitation

There is something to surrender here. The expectation that guidance should arrive in a form that has no relation to your weakness. The expectation that your teachers should be free of the things you struggle with. The expectation that your own body — its needs, its mortality, its smallness — is the obstacle to your faith, rather than the soil in which your faith is supposed to grow.

The Prophet ﷺ ate food. He drank water. He slept. He aged. He died. And Allah called this **the design.**

Walk into tomorrow knowing your body is not a problem to be solved. It is the trust on which Allah is writing your life. The hunger you feel before iftar, the exhaustion you feel after a long day, the ache you feel when grief comes — these are not interruptions of your walk with Allah. **They are the walk.**

### A Du'a

*O Allah, You who sent every prophet as a man who ate food and died — let me stop demanding from my teachers what You did not give to Your own messengers. Let me stop demanding from myself what You did not build into Your prophets. Let my body, with its hunger and its mortality, be the canvas on which Your remembrance is written, until the day this body is returned to the earth and this soul is returned to You. Āmīn.*
