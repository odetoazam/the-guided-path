# The Day That Walks Toward You While You Walk Away

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayah 1

> [ayah:21:1] ٱقْتَرَبَ لِلنَّاسِ حِسَابُهُمْ وَهُمْ فِى غَفْلَةٍ مُّعْرِضُونَ
>
> *Iqtaraba lin-nāsi ḥisābuhum wa hum fī ghaflatin muʿriḍūn*
>
> *"Their reckoning has drawn near for mankind — while they, in heedlessness, are turning away."*

---

## Introduction — Building the Floor

Imagine a courtroom. The judge has not yet entered. The seats are filled. Everyone in the room knows there will be a verdict — knows it the way you know the sun is hot, the way you know water is wet. It is not a hidden fact. It is the plainest fact in the room.

But notice what happens in the seats.

People are reading newspapers. People are scrolling. People are leaning over to whisper jokes. People are arguing about parking. People are checking the score. Some have fallen asleep with their mouths open. A few are arranging their hair. The bailiff calls to order. They do not hear. The clock above the bench ticks louder. They do not look up.

This is the world Surah Al-Anbiya opens onto.

The surah is going to spend its next hundred ayahs telling you stories of prophets — stories of fire that became cool, of seas that split, of birds that returned, of fish that gave up the man it had swallowed. Stories of arrivals. Things that were promised, then came.

But before any of those stories — before a single name — Allah opens the curtain on this courtroom. He says one sentence. Seven Arabic words. And in those seven words He tells you exactly where you are sitting and exactly what is happening in the room.

You are sitting in a courtroom whose verdict is already on its way.

And you are looking the other direction.

[PAUSE]

The Meccans who first heard this surah were obsessed with what they could see. Their gods were tangible. Their wealth was countable. Their sons were named. The afterlife was something Muḥammad ﷺ kept describing, and they kept laughing at, because — they reasoned — *we don't see it. So how close can it really be?*

This ayah does not argue with them. It does not say, "the reckoning will come." It does not even say, "the reckoning is coming." It says something stranger than both. It says, in the perfect tense, in the past-completed form of the verb — **it has already drawn near.**

Drawn near while they are not looking.

Drawn near while they are turning away.

There is something in this opening that stops you, if you let it. Because the verb is doing one thing. And the people are doing the opposite thing. And those two motions are not happening in two different rooms. They are happening in the same room. The same air. The same instant.

What I want to do tonight is open this sentence very slowly. We are going to spend our time on three small things in the Arabic — the verb of approach, the absence of a fixed amount, and the pairing of two words that English usually treats as one. And then we are going to ask what those three small things show us about a phenomenon every single one of us is living inside, right now: **the geometry of avoidance.**

Stay with me. This sentence is short. Its floor is very deep.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Closes Its Own Distance

The first word of the surah is *iqtaraba* — ٱقْتَرَبَ.

Before we go inside the word, let me show you something about Arabic grammar that English does not have. In Arabic, verbs come in different *forms* — different shapes the root can take. The root for "near" is ق-ر-ب, *q-r-b*. The simplest, most basic verb shape from this root is *qaruba* — "it was near, it became near."

But Arabic can take that root and pour it into different molds. Each mold changes what is happening with the action. There is a mold that means "to bring something close" — *qarraba*. There is a mold that means "to bring oneself close to someone" — *qāraba*. There is the mold that produced our word — *iqtaraba*. This is what classical grammarians call **Form VIII** — the *iftaʿala* pattern.

Form VIII has a specific signature in Arabic. It often carries a **reflexive intensification**. The action turns back on its own subject. "It did the thing *to itself.*" Or: "It did the thing *with effort, of its own initiative.*"

I want you to feel that before we apply it.

Think of the Arabic verb *qatala* — "he killed." Now in Form VIII it becomes *iqtatala* — which doesn't just mean "killed" — it means *the people killed each other, in a back-and-forth combat that drew them in.* The action curls back on the actors themselves.

So when Allah says about the day of reckoning *iqtaraba* — He is not just saying "it has approached." He is saying something with more force than that. He is saying:

**The reckoning has brought itself near.**

It is not lying out at a distance, waiting for time to drag it forward. It is *closing the distance under its own power.* It is moving toward you — actively, of its own initiative.

[PAUSE]

Now stay with me, because there is a second feature of this verb that, when you put it together with the first, will change how you read this whole sentence.

The verb is in the **perfect tense** — what Arabic calls *al-māḍī*. The form used for things that have already happened. "He went." "She wrote." "It fell."

But the reckoning has not happened yet.

The Day of Judgment is in the future — every Muslim knows this, the Meccans knew this. So why does Allah use the past-tense form to describe a future event?

Classical scholars give this a name: they call it the *māḍī of certainty* — the certainty-perfect. When something is so absolutely going to happen that there is no possibility, no contingency, no maybe, no perhaps — Arabic speaks of it as if it had already happened. The grammar bypasses the future tense altogether, because the future tense, in human thought, smuggles in the possibility that the thing might not arrive.

Allah will not give you that smuggled possibility.

So He puts the future event in the past tense. *Iqtaraba* — it has already drawn near. As far as God is concerned, the closing of the distance is not in the future. It is a fact that has already become real.

Now combine the two things. **A reflexive verb of self-driven approach. In a tense reserved for completed certainty.**

What you have, in this single word, is this:

**The Day is moving itself toward you with its own power, and from God's view, it has already arrived.**

Not "will arrive."

Has arrived.

The only thing standing between you and it is the soft cushion of your own perception of time. Pull that cushion away, and you will see what the verb is showing you.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the listener. Most warnings work by making the future feel close. *Be careful, this might happen, you don't know when.* This ayah does not work that way. This ayah operates by treating the warning as already-completed news. It is not warning you about something that *might* arrive. It is reporting on something that *has* arrived, and is now rolling toward your awareness like a wave that the shore has not yet noticed.

The wave does not get any larger or smaller depending on whether the shore is paying attention.

That is the work of *iqtaraba*.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Two: The Reckoning That Belongs to You

The second piece of grammar I want you to see is small. So small that translations smooth right over it. But once you see it, the sentence will read differently forever.

The word for "reckoning" in this ayah is *ḥisāb* — حِسَاب. From the root ح-س-ب, which gives us words for *counting, calculating, accounting, summing up*. A *ḥisāb* in Arabic is what an accountant produces at the end of the year. It is also what the merchant gives the customer at the end of a transaction. It is the bill. The total. The figure that captures everything that was bought and owed.

Allah does not just say *al-ḥisāb* — "the reckoning." He says *ḥisābu-hum* — **their reckoning.**

That little suffix at the end — *-hum*, "their" — is the most intimate possessive in Arabic. It binds the reckoning to the people. The reckoning is not an abstract event hovering somewhere in the cosmos. It is theirs. It belongs to them. It has their name on the file.

Think about what happens when you receive a generic email. *Dear Customer.* You skim it. You delete it. It does not feel addressed to you, even if technically it is.

Now think about what happens when you receive an envelope at your doorstep with your full name handwritten on it, your address typed correctly, and the words *Final Notice* in red on the front.

That is the difference between *al-ḥisāb* and *ḥisābu-hum*.

The reckoning is not the world's reckoning. It is not abstract. It is *yours.* It has been prepared for you, item by item, line by line, and it is being delivered to your address.

[PAUSE]

And notice — Allah says *li-n-nās*. "For mankind." Not "for the disbelievers." Not "for the wrongdoers." Not "for the Meccans." For *al-nās* — humanity, full stop. Every human being walking the earth is the addressee of this sentence.

So the grammar of this ayah, taken as a whole, says: *the reckoning that has your name on it is closing the distance to you, and you are one of an-nās, so this is for you.*

There is no exit clause in the grammar. There is no carved-out group that gets to say, "well, this isn't about us." It is about you. It has been written specifically for you. It is being walked, by its own power, toward your address.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Two Words English Treats as One

Now we come to the second half of the ayah, and this is where the deepest reading lives.

*Wa hum fī ghaflatin muʿriḍūn.*

Most English translations render this as something like: *"while they are heedless"* or *"while they are turning away in heedlessness."* And that's not wrong. But it collapses two distinct words into one English mood. In Arabic, two different things are happening simultaneously, and the ayah is showing you both.

The first word is *ghaflah* — غَفْلَة. This comes from the root غ-ف-ل, which means *to be unaware, to be unconscious of, to fail to notice.* Classical lexicographers describe it as the *forgetting that comes from inattention* — not the forgetting of someone who once knew and lost, but the unawareness of someone whose attention is simply not on the thing.

*Ghaflah* is **the soft fog of not noticing.**

It is the state you are in when you walk past your friend at the grocery store and don't see them, because you are thinking about something else. It is the state you are in when an entire afternoon passes and you cannot account for the hours. It is what the Quran elsewhere calls *al-nāsīn* — the forgetting people. *Ghaflah* is the texture of unconsciousness.

Now notice — *ghaflah* in this ayah is **indefinite**. Not *al-ghaflah* (the heedlessness). Just *ghaflah*. In Arabic, the indefinite often carries a sense of scope, of unspecified depth. *Fī ghaflatin* — "in *some* heedlessness," "in *a* heedlessness," meaning a heedlessness whose dimensions are not announced. A fog whose density is not specified. It could be a thin haze. It could be cataract-thick.

The grammar leaves the depth open because **the depth varies from person to person, and from hour to hour within the same person.**

[PAUSE]

That is the first word. Now the second.

*Muʿriḍūn* — مُّعْرِضُون. From the root ع-ر-ض, which carries the meaning of *side, breadth, what is presented in front of you.* The Form IV of this root, *aʿraḍa*, means **to actively turn one's side away from something — to present one's back, to refuse to face.**

This is not a passive word. This is a posture.

If *ghaflah* is the fog, *iʿrāḍ* is the body language. *Iʿrāḍ* is what you are doing with your shoulders. *Iʿrāḍ* is what you are doing with your gaze. *Iʿrāḍ* is the deliberate angling of your attention away from something that is in front of you.

You can be *ghāfil* (heedless) without being *muʿriḍ* (turning away). A child engrossed in play is *ghāfil* — they don't notice the time. But they are not turning away from anything. There is no thing they are angling their body against.

But you cannot be *muʿriḍ* without there being something specific you are turning from. The word demands an object. To turn one's side away from — *what?* The thing presented to you. The truth that has been raised in front of your face.

So when Allah pairs these two words — *fī ghaflatin muʿriḍūn* — He is telling you something very precise about the human condition that this ayah is diagnosing:

**They are simultaneously fogged AND positioned.**

They are not just unaware. They are unaware *and oriented away.*

The *ghaflah* would be tragic enough on its own — a fog that prevents seeing. But the ayah does not let us blame the fog. Because alongside the fog is a posture. The body has been turned. The shoulders have been angled. Even within the fog, the person has positioned themselves *away* from the thing the fog is hiding.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most psychologically precise observations in the entire Quran.

Because what it is describing — and we will sit with this in part two — is the way human beings produce their own heedlessness through their own turning. The fog is not a weather event that happened to us. The fog is being maintained by the angle of our gaze. We turn away first. The fog thickens to fit the angle of the turn.

The Arabic does not let you separate them. The two words sit on either side of the preposition *fī*. *In a fog, turning away.* They are simultaneous. They are entangled. They feed each other.

[PAUSE — major]

### The Bridge

Three pieces of grammar. Let me bring them into a single sentence before we walk across to part two:

**A future so certain it is described as completed. Brought toward you under its own initiative. Carrying your name on it. While you are simultaneously fogged and angled away.**

Now let's ask what this is showing us, not just about words, but about a reality you are living inside right now.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Geometry of Avoidance

I want to begin where you live.

You have, somewhere in your life right now, a thing you know is coming.

It might be a medical appointment. It might be a difficult conversation. It might be a debt that has been quietly compounding. It might be a deadline at work that you have been pushing further into the back of your calendar. It might be a phone call to a parent. It might be a deeper question — whether the life you are building is the life you actually want.

You know it is coming.

That knowledge sits in you, low and steady, like the hum of an old refrigerator. You can ignore it for hours. You cannot quite turn it off.

Now notice what you do.

When the thing gets closer — when the appointment is next week instead of next month, when the deadline is Monday instead of vague — what happens to your behavior? Does it move *toward* the thing? Does it move *away*?

In almost every case, what happens to most of us is that **the behavior moves away in proportion to how close the thing has come.**

The night before the difficult conversation, you find yourself binge-watching something you would never normally watch. The morning of the medical appointment, you are unusually preoccupied with reorganizing a shelf. The week of the deadline, you discover seventeen emails that suddenly feel urgent. The closer the reckoning comes, the more energetically the body angles itself elsewhere.

This is *iʿrāḍ*. Not metaphorical *iʿrāḍ*. Literal *iʿrāḍ*. The shoulders turning. The gaze redirected. And the busier the turning becomes, the thicker the fog gets — because the activity of turning produces enough mental noise to obscure the thing being turned from. The *muʿriḍūn* manufacture their own *ghaflah*.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what the ayah shows you that the modern psychology of avoidance does not show you:

**The thing does not slow down because you are turning away from it.**

Avoidance behavior, in psychology, is described as a coping mechanism. And in the short term, it functions. It reduces the immediate anxiety. The conversation you avoided does not happen tonight. The deadline you ignored does not require work tonight. The bill you did not open does not have to be paid tonight. So the avoidance feels — for a few hours — like *control*. As if you have made the thing smaller by looking away from it.

But the ayah's verb is *iqtaraba*. The thing is closing the distance under its own power. The thing is not slowed by your inattention. The thing is not deterred by your turn. Whether you face it or not, the gap between you and it is contracting.

Here is the geometry the ayah is teaching:

**You are walking south. The thing is walking north. The space between you is filled with your distractions. Every distraction you place between yourself and the thing makes the south-walk feel more productive — but does not move the thing one inch back to the north.**

The meeting is not negotiable. The only question is what shape you are in when it arrives.

[PAUSE]

I want you to see how cruel — and how merciful — this ayah is at the same time.

It is cruel because it removes the comfort of avoidance. It tells you, in seven words, that the strategy you have been using to manage your largest fears is not actually managing them. It is only managing your perception of them. The fears themselves are arriving on schedule.

It is merciful because — and this is the gift hidden in the ayah's opening — *Allah is the one telling you this.*

Think about that for a moment.

If the reckoning were truly close and Allah said nothing, the heedlessness would be tragic. We would arrive at the courtroom not knowing it existed. The fog would be unbroken. The turn would be uncorrected.

But the reckoning is close, and Allah opened the surah by *announcing* it. The very existence of this sentence is the breaking of the fog. The very fact that you are reading this ayah right now is Allah, in His mercy, walking up behind you in the courtroom and **tapping you on the shoulder.**

The ayah does not just describe the heedlessness. **It interrupts it.**

That is the structure. Allah says: *the meeting is coming, and you are turning away from it* — and the saying of it is the doorway out of the turning. The moment you read these words and *recognize yourself* in the *muʿriḍūn*, you have, in that recognition, already begun to face. Iʿrāḍ has begun to reverse.

The ayah is its own escape hatch.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It means: in any area of my life where I notice the texture of avoidance — where I notice the shoulders turning, the fog thickening, the mind manufacturing urgent distractions — I should treat that noticing as a gift. The noticing itself is not heedlessness anymore. The noticing is the first ayah of Surah Al-Anbiya operating on me. The noticing is the tap on the shoulder.

And the right response to the tap is not panic. The right response is to slowly turn back toward the thing.

Open the email.

Make the call.

Look at the number.

Sit with the question.

The reckoning is closing the distance whether I face it or not. So I might as well face it while there is still room to prepare for it.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: Heedlessness in the Age of Infinite Scroll

Let me end with something close to home.

The Meccan disbelievers had certain tools for *iʿrāḍ*. They had wealth. They had social status. They had idols. They had family pressure. They had the small comforts of trade routes and tribal alliances. These were the angles their bodies could be turned to.

We have more.

We have a small rectangle in our pockets that has been engineered, by some of the most expensively trained minds in human history, to be exactly the right shape and weight and brightness to hold a human gaze and prevent it from turning toward anything else. The entire economy of attention runs on the cultivation and exploitation of *iʿrāḍ*. Whole industries — billions of dollars — are devoted to making sure the angle of your shoulders stays where they want it to stay.

You did not invent your heedlessness. You inherited an infrastructure designed to produce it.

That does not absolve you. *Iʿrāḍ* is still a posture, and you still hold it. But it should at least make you tender with yourself when you notice how thick the fog has become. The fog is no accident. The fog is being supplied to you, hour by hour, by systems whose business model is the preservation of the fog.

[PAUSE]

But notice something about modern *ghaflah* that the ayah's diagnosis cuts through with surgical accuracy.

Modern distraction is unique in that it is **distraction about ultimate things.**

The Meccans were heedless of the reckoning while preoccupied with their actual lives — their trade, their families, their tribes. There is a kind of moral weight to that. They were heedless because they were busy.

We are heedless while preoccupied with **other people's heedlessness.** We scroll past clips of strangers, fragments of strangers' children, opinions on strangers' breakfasts, outrage over strangers' politics. We are not turning away from the reckoning to face our own actual lives. We are turning away from the reckoning to face *nothing in particular* — to face an algorithmic foam of curated nothings.

This is *ghaflah* on a new level. *Ghaflah* without an alibi. *Ghaflah* that even the *ghāfil* cannot defend.

And the ayah's verb still applies. The reckoning is still *iqtaraba* — closing the distance under its own power. It is not slowed by the algorithm. It is not deterred by the next reel. It is not bargained with by the dopamine cycle.

[PAUSE]

Here is the practice the ayah suggests, if you want one.

Once a day — even once a week — let there be a moment where you stop the scroll, put the device down, and ask one question:

**If the reckoning has already drawn near, what part of my life have I been positioning my shoulders away from?**

Don't answer it quickly. Sit with it until the honest answer comes up. It will come up. You already know what it is. You have always known. The fog is thinner than you think — it just needs you to stop adding to it.

Whatever the answer is — that is the place where your tomorrow begins.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Two Halves of the Sentence Are Doing the Same Thing

Look at the architecture of the ayah one more time:

> *Iqtaraba lin-nāsi ḥisābuhum* — **drawing near.**
>
> *Wa hum fī ghaflatin muʿriḍūn* — **turning away.**

The whole ayah is a study in opposing motion. The reckoning moves *toward.* The people move *from.* And these two motions are not parallel. They are pointed at each other.

The geometry is not stable. Two things pointed at each other do not stay equidistant. They meet. The only variable is *when*.

Allah has placed the entire surah on the foundation of this geometry. Every prophet's story that follows is going to be a case study of the same physics. Ibrahim was thrown into the fire — and the fire was commanded to be *bardan wa salāman*, cool and peace. The promise arrived. Yunus was swallowed by the fish — and from the belly of the fish, his cry was answered. The promise arrived. Zakariyya called from the depth of his old age, *rabbi lā tadharnī fardan* — and Yahya was given to him. The promise arrived.

Surah Al-Anbiya is a one-hundred-ayah meditation on the fact that **what Allah has put in motion does not stop.** Promises walk to their destinations. Reckonings close their distances. The verb is *iqtaraba*, in the certainty-perfect, and once that grammar is in motion, no human turn can reverse it.

The opening ayah is not just an introduction. It is the thesis. The rest of the surah is the proof.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the thing in my life right now that I know is coming, and have been positioning my shoulders away from?

2. If I imagine my reckoning as already-drawn-near — already in the same room with me, just outside my line of sight — does that make me afraid, or does it make me focused?

3. When I notice the texture of avoidance in myself, do I treat that noticing as a problem, or do I receive it as Allah's mercy tapping me on the shoulder?

4. What is the *iʿrāḍ-infrastructure* in my life — the apps, the rooms, the relationships, the habits — that is most efficiently producing my fog? What would it cost me to disable one of them?

5. If the entire surah of Al-Anbiya is built on the principle that *what Allah puts in motion arrives,* what promise of His am I quietly disbelieving in by the way I am living?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**The Day you fear is closing the distance to you under its own power, while you spend your strength turning away from it — and the ayah's mercy is that the saying of this is itself the breaking of the fog.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to fix everything tonight.

You only have to do one thing.

You have to **face** — for one moment, for one breath — the thing you have been turning from. You do not have to solve it. You do not have to finish anything. You only have to look directly at it, with your shoulders square, and let it be in front of you.

That single turn — from *muʿriḍ* to *muqbil*, from facing-away to facing-toward — is the entire ayah's invitation. Everything else in the deen is built on that turn. Tawbah is that turn. Salah is that turn. Reflection is that turn. The whole architecture of the religion is the slow, repeated, patient practice of straightening the shoulders.

The ayah is not asking you to be perfect. It is asking you to stop angling away.

### Du'a

*Yā Allāh, You who told us in seven words what we spend our whole lives refusing to hear — break our fog gently. Untangle the angles of our turning. Let us face what is closing its distance to us, before the distance is closed. And when we do face it, do not let us face it alone — face it with us, with Your mercy walking out in front of us. Āmīn.*
