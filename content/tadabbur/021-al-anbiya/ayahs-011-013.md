# Run Where? — The Mockery of Fleeing Comfort

## Surah Al-Anbiya, Ayahs 11–13

> وَكَمْ قَصَمْنَا مِن قَرْيَةٍ كَانَتْ ظَالِمَةً وَأَنشَأْنَا بَعْدَهَا قَوْمًا آخَرِينَ ۝ فَلَمَّآ أَحَسُّوا۟ بَأْسَنَآ إِذَا هُم مِّنْهَا يَرْكُضُونَ ۝ لَا تَرْكُضُوا۟ وَٱرْجِعُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰ مَآ أُتْرِفْتُمْ فِيهِ وَمَسَـٰكِنِكُمْ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُسْـَٔلُونَ

*Wa kam qaṣamnā min qaryatin kānat ẓālimah, wa anshaʾnā baʿdahā qawman ākharīn. Fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūn. Lā tarkuḍū wa-rjiʿū ilā mā utriftum fīhi wa-masākinikum, laʿallakum tusʾalūn.*

> "And how many a town that was wrongdoing have We shattered, and brought up after it another people. Then, when they sensed Our wrath — suddenly they were galloping away from it. *Do not flee. Return to the luxuries you were given and to your dwellings — perhaps you will be questioned.*"

## Introduction: The Surah's Pause Before the Prophets

Al-Anbiya — "The Prophets" — is named for the gallery it builds in its second half. Ibrahim with the idols. Musa with the staff. Dawud judging between the disputants. Sulayman with the wind. Yunus in the belly of the whale. Zakariyya pleading for a son. Maryam preserving her chastity. The surah will eventually become a gallery of prophets — Ibrahim, Musa, Dawud, Sulayman, Yunus, Zakariyya, Isa, and more than a dozen others.

But before it lets us into that gallery — it stops us at the entrance.

It says: *I want to show you something else first. Look down. At the rubble.*

Because before Al-Anbiya tells you about the men Allah sent, it tells you about the cities Allah ended.

The surah opened by diagnosing us. *Iqtaraba li-n-nāsi ḥisābuhum wa hum fī ghaflatin muʿriḍūn* — "their reckoning has drawn near to them, and they are in heedlessness, turning away." The audience has been described. Now, before the prophets are introduced, the surah asks one terrifying question:

**Do you think you are the first people to feel safe?**

And then, in three short ayahs, it shows us a time-lapse. An entire civilization. From its peak — to its panic — to its end. In about thirty Arabic words, the Quran fits the rise and fall of a world.

Stay with that. Thirty words. An entire civilization.

There's a hook here that won't be obvious until we open the language. Let me just tease it: at the climax of this passage, when the city is collapsing and the people are fleeing — Allah does not address them with anger.

He addresses them with mockery.

And not the kind of mockery you might expect. This is the kind of mockery that uses your own comfort against you. The kind that points at your house and tells you to go sit in it. The kind that uses one of the most hopeful particles in the Arabic language and twists it into the cruelest irony.

We have to earn that moment. Let's start with the first word that does the work.

## PART 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Word **Qaṣamnā** — The Sound of Beyond Repair

> *Wa kam **qaṣamnā** min qaryatin kānat ẓālimah*
> "And how many a town that was wrongdoing have We **shattered**..."

Before we touch this word, let's notice something about Arabic that English speakers often miss.

In English, when something breaks, we have basically one verb: *break*. We add adverbs and contexts to specify. *He broke the plate. He broke the law. He broke her heart.* The verb is the same.

Arabic doesn't work this way. Arabic refuses to use one verb for what are, in reality, very different kinds of breaking. There's a verb for *kasara* — to break, possibly into pieces that could be glued back. There's *fasama* — to split or crack. There's *thalama* — to chip or notch. There's *hashama* — to crush. Each verb is a slightly different *quality* of breaking.

And then there is the verb in this ayah.

*Qaṣama.*

Listen to it: *qa-ṣa-ma.* Two of the heaviest sounds in the Arabic language locked into one syllable — the *qāf*, articulated from the deep back of the throat, and the *ṣād*, an emphatic pressed against the upper palate. The word is built like a hammer.

*Qaṣama* carries a different character from all of these.

Al-Tabari notes that the root — *qāf-ṣād-mīm* — is at its foundation a form of *kasara*, breaking, and that the operative meaning the classical mufassirūn assigned to it here is *ahlakna*: destroyed, annihilated. Mujahid's transmitted reading says simply, *"we destroyed them."* But not every destruction is equal, and the lexical tradition (Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib) preserves a qualitative distinction: *kasara* may leave debris that can be reassembled; *qaṣama* is the kind of break from which the original wholeness is not recovered. The tafsir gives us the theological verdict — annihilation — and the lexical character of the verb shows us why this word, rather than a gentler one, was chosen to deliver it.

This is the verb Allah uses for the civilizations He has ended.

Not *kasara* — not "we broke them and they might rebuild." *Qaṣamnā* — the word that holds annihilation in a single stroke.

Notice, too, what preceded the *qaṣm*. The town is described as *kānat ẓālimah* — "was wrongdoing." Classical commentators did not leave this vague. Al-Muyassar specifies: the wrongdoing was the people's disbelief in what their messengers brought them — prophetic guidance rejected. They were not destroyed for poverty of resources; they were destroyed for poverty of response to *rusul*. This is why these three ayahs stand precisely where they stand. The surah is about to take you through a gallery of messengers. First, it shows you the rubble of those who refused them.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. We can stand in the ruins of those civilizations *right now*. We can fly to Egypt and walk through Karnak. We can stand in the rubble of Pompeii. We can drive through the abandoned cities of the Anasazi in the American Southwest. The stones are still there. The walls are still there.

But the civilization is gone. *Qaṣama* gone.

Because *qaṣama* is not the breaking of stones. It is the breaking of something more delicate than stone — the living web of a society. The shared assumptions. The habits. The languages that wove people together. The unspoken trust that this is *how things work*. Once that web is shattered, you can preserve every brick, and the civilization will not return.

Now stay with this. Let the word do its other work.

What in your own life have you tried to put back together that the universe was telling you was *qaṣama*? Not *kasara* — broken, repairable — but *qaṣama* — broken in the way that does not come back?

There are some breakings the heart wants to deny. We sit at the rubble and we say *next year, next year, I'll rebuild it.* Allah is showing us something hard: there are some breakings He calls final. The mercy of His final breaking is that it ends our delusion of repair.

But notice — He does not stop the verse there.

> *wa anshaʾnā baʿdahā qawman ākharīn*
> "...and We **brought up** after it another people."

The same verb root — *n-sh-ʾ* — used elsewhere in the Quran for raising a child, for bringing up a plant from the soil, for the dawn breaking, for the original creation of the human being from his mother's belly.

The same hand that *qaṣama*'s — *anshaʾu*'s.

The same hand that annihilates — *grows*.

This is Allah's signature on history. He does not just shatter. He shatters and then *cultivates*. The death of one civilization is the soil of the next. The end is not the end of *people*; it is the end of *that arrangement*. A new people grows up in the ruins. New languages. New mosques and temples and markets. New children laughing on the same earth where another civilization fell.

This is the rhythm we are about to forget. The next two ayahs will narrow in on the moment of shattering — but the frame Allah has set is wider. Even *qaṣama* is followed by *anshaʾnā*. Even the most final breaking is followed by life.

Hold that. We will need it.

### Element Two: The Suddenness Particle — *Idhā Hum Minhā Yarkuḍūn*

> *Fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā **idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūn***
> "Then, when they sensed Our wrath — **suddenly, they are galloping away from it.**"

Arabic has a particle that English does not have an equivalent for. It's the small word *idhā* — and you have to teach yourself to feel it.

In English, when we want to express surprise inside a sentence, we have to break the grammar. We have to say *"I opened the door — and there she was!"* The dash and the exclamation do the work. We pause, we redirect, we mark the surprise.

Arabic builds the surprise *into the grammar itself*. The particle *idhā* — sometimes called *idhā al-fujāʾiyyah*, "the suddenness *idhā*" — is a single word that means: *and at that very instant, behold!*

It is the grammatical equivalent of a sharp inhale. It tells the reader: *what comes next is unexpected, unprepared, and right now.*

Allah uses it here.

> *Fa-lammā aḥassū baʾsanā* — "Then, when they sensed Our wrath..."
> *idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūn* — "...behold! they are galloping from it."

Notice the verb tense shift, too. The first half is past tense — *aḥassū*, "they sensed." The second half jumps to present tense — *yarkuḍūn*, "they are galloping." The narrator is no longer recounting a past event. The narrator has placed the camera *inside the moment*. We are watching it happen now. This is a cinematic technique that Arabic does effortlessly: the past collapses into the present at the moment of crisis.

So the grammar is doing three things at once:
- *fa-lammā* says: *when this happened*
- *idhā* says: *suddenly!*
- *yarkuḍūn* (present tense) says: *and now we are watching them*

The whole construction enacts panic. You read the verse and the verse itself accelerates.

Now let's open *yarkuḍūn*.

The root is *r-k-ḍ*. Al-Tabari traces its etymology precisely: *"rakada fulānun farasahu idhā kaddahu siyāqatahu"* — "he drove his horse hard in running it." It is the verb of the rider who kicks his mount into a gallop. By extension, it means simply *to run hard*, but its origin is the cavalry image.

In the desert, when a man needed to flee — when raiders had crested the hill, when a sandstorm was rolling in, when the enemy was in pursuit — what did he do? He drove his heel into the side of his horse and bolted. *Yarkuḍ.*

So this is not the verb of someone calmly leaving a room. This is the verb of an animal-mounted human being driving every nerve in his body to escape. Al-Tabari's chains describe them as *"yuhribūna sirāʿan ʿajlā, yaʿdūna munhazimīn"* — fleeing quickly, in haste, running in defeat. The word *munhazimīn* — in defeat — is telling. The rout has already happened; the running is its symptom, not its cause.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. The body senses divine wrath approaching, and its instinct is flight.

Al-Muyassar is precise about what *aḥassū* actually describes: what they sensed was not the full arrival of the punishment but its *bawādir* — its first signs, its harbingers. The punishment had not yet landed in full when the flight began. The first cracks in the familiar sky were enough. The verse is recording something exact about human responsiveness to divine warning: when the early signs appear, the instinct is not to reckon — it is to bolt. They could not even wait for the thing itself.

And here is the bitter genius of the verse. The grammar contains a small preposition: *minhā* — "from it." Grammatically, the *hā* refers back to the city, the *qaryah*. So the surface meaning is: "they are galloping from the town."

But on the deeper level, what they are fleeing is the *baʾs* — Allah's wrath itself. They have *sensed* the wrath, and the response of the body is to run.

Stay with that. The body senses divine wrath approaching, and its instinct is *flight*.

Why is this the instinct? Because every other danger we have ever faced in our evolutionary history has had a *direction*. The lion is over there, so I run *that* way. The flood is to the east, so I move *west*. Danger has always come from somewhere, which means safety is always *somewhere else*.

The body is built for a universe in which danger is local.

But Allah's *baʾs* is not local.

There is no "somewhere else" with respect to it. There is no *that* way to run. The wrath of the One who fills all directions cannot be outrun in any direction. The human bodies in this verse are doing what bodies have always done — they are spurring their horses, they are *yarkuḍūn* — but they are doing it in a geometry where running has no meaning.

This is what makes the next ayah so devastating.

### Element Three: The Most Painful *Laʿalla* in the Quran

> ***Lā tarkuḍū** wa-rjiʿū ilā mā **utriftum** fīhi wa-masākinikum, **laʿallakum tusʾalūn***
> "**Do not flee.** Return to the luxuries **you were given** and to your dwellings — **perhaps you will be questioned.**"

Allah does not name who is speaking. The classical mufassirūn debate it — angels, the divine voice itself, a chorus of those who saw their fall. The text, in a remarkable choice, leaves the speaker faceless. The mockery hangs in the air without a body to attach to. The whole universe seems to be the one speaking.

And what it says is shattering.

Let's break it apart slowly.

**"Lā tarkuḍū" — "Do not flee."**

The same verb that just panicked across the previous ayah is now placed under a prohibition. *Stop running.* It is the voice of someone watching a man flail in panic and saying, *calm down — there is nowhere to go anyway.*

If a friend is panicking, "calm down" is mercy.

If your enemy is panicking and the trap has already closed, "calm down" is the worst thing they can hear.

**"Wa-rjiʿū ilā mā utriftum fīhi" — "Return to what you were given as luxury."**

Now we open the word *utriftum*. The root is *t-r-f*. *Tarafa* in Arabic means *to enjoy abundance*, but with a specific connotation. It is not the wealth of the prophet Sulayman, who used wealth as service. It is not the wealth of Ibrahim, who saw it as trust.

*Taraf* is the wealth that *makes you soft*. It is the comfort that breeds heedlessness. The Quran consistently uses this word for the rich whose riches dulled them — *al-mutrafīn* — those whose luxury became the *cause* of their disregard for Allah.

And notice — the verb here is in the *passive voice*. *Utriftum* — "you were given luxury." Not *atraftum* — "you yourselves accumulated luxury." The grammar quietly strips them of agency.

Al-Tabari's gloss illuminates this further: he reads *utriftum* in parallel with *unʿimtum* — "you were blessed." The luxury was not merely distributed to them; it was a *niʿma*, a divine gift. And every gift carries the weight of recognition. What was sent as blessing, they received as entitlement.

They thought their wealth was theirs.

The grammar reveals it was a loan.

[PAUSE]

**"Wa-masākinikum" — "and to your dwellings."**

Their *houses*. The specific buildings. The ones with their names on the door. The ones whose architecture they were proud of. The ones they would invite their friends into to show off.

*Go sit in those houses.*

This is one of the most quietly cruel lines in the Quran. Not because Allah is cruel — but because the universe is showing them something about themselves they had refused to see. *You loved your house. You thought your house was you. Fine. Go to it. Sit in it. See what it does for you now.*

Now the climax.

**"Laʿallakum tusʾalūn" — "perhaps you will be questioned."**

The word *laʿalla* is one of the most beautiful particles in the Arabic language. It signals *hopeful possibility*. The Quran uses it dozens of times in its most tender contexts:

*Laʿallakum tashkurūn* — "perhaps you will be grateful."
*Laʿallakum tattaqūn* — "perhaps you will be God-conscious."
*Laʿallakum tahtadūn* — "perhaps you will be guided."
*Laʿallakum tarḥamūn* — "perhaps you will be shown mercy."

*Laʿalla* is the particle of Allah's hope for us. It is the word that opens the door of possibility. It is the grammatical heart of mercy.

And here — Allah uses it as a knife.

Because what comes after *laʿalla* is not *guidance*, not *gratitude*, not *mercy*. It is *tusʾalūn* — *you will be questioned.*

But notice the second layer. The verb *tusʾalūn* in the passive voice has a double meaning. It means *to be asked*, but in classical Arabic it can mean two very different things:

1. *To be asked for one's opinion* — to be consulted as an authority. The way a chief is asked. The way a tribal elder is approached. *Sulta yusʾal* — "an authority who is consulted."

2. *To be interrogated* — to be questioned about one's deeds. The way a defendant is examined. *Yawma yusʾalūn* — "the day they will be questioned."

These were *al-mutrafīn*. The luxurious ones. The elites. Their entire status had been built on being *the ones consulted*. People came to their dwellings to seek their counsel. Their wealth was the wealth of the consulted. Their houses were houses of decision.

And now the voice from somewhere says: *go back to your dwellings — perhaps you will be consulted.*

The mockery has at least three layers stacked on top of each other. *Laʿalla* — the hope-particle — is poisoned. *Consultation* — the source of their pride — is invoked at the moment it has become impossible. And *consultation* itself, in the eschatological key, slides into *interrogation*. The same verb.

You wanted to be the asked. Fine. You will be the asked. But not in the courtyard of your status. In the court of your Lord.

This is the cruelest sentence in the surah, and it is delivered with grammatical economy that would be impossible in English. *Laʿallakum tusʾalūn.* Three Arabic words. Three layers of irony. One ruined civilization.

The classical tradition did not flatten this into a single reading without debate. Al-Muyassar is explicit: the line is delivered *ʿalā wajh al-sukhriyya wa-l-istizhāʾ bihim* — on a tone of mockery and derision. And yet some classical readers held that *laʿalla*, even here, retains its grammatical identity as a particle of genuine possibility — and that the cruelty lies not in the particle being false, but in the particle being true. The only remaining door is *questioning*. That door is real. But what was once a door of honor — being summoned as the consulted, the powerful — has become the threshold of accountability. The sarcasm is in the transformation of meaning, not necessarily in the impossibility of the event. Whether the reader follows the strong sarcasm reading or the darker-still version where the invitation is genuine but the destination is reckoning — the verdict is the same. The *mutrafīn* cannot be saved by their houses.

### Bridge

So here is what the language has shown us, all together:

A civilization that thought it was permanent (*qaṣamnā*'s opposite, the assumption of repair).

A moment of sensing wrath (*aḥassū baʾsanā*) — not the punishment in full, but its harbingers alone.

A panicked flight (*idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūn*) — bodies trained on a universe of local danger, encountering a wrath without a direction, fleeing in defeat before the blow fully lands.

A voice from nowhere (*lā tarkuḍū*) saying: *stop running, there is nowhere to go.*

And then the killing line: *go back to the luxury you were given as a blessing — the niʿma you mistook for your own — the houses you were proud of — perhaps your status will save you. Perhaps you will be consulted.*

[PAUSE]

The surface of this passage is a historical observation. *Civilizations have fallen. They will fall again.* But underneath, the language is asking us something else. Something more personal.

It is asking what happens to *us* when the comforts we have organized our lives around are stripped away.

Let's go there.

## PART 2 — The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Geometry of Flight — Why Running From Allah Is Geometrically Impossible

I want to start far from the verse. Stay with me.

When you were a child, where did you run when you were scared?

You ran to a parent. You ran into a closet. You ran under the bed. You ran into the bathroom and locked the door. You went somewhere *small and contained* — somewhere where the threat became geometrically excluded by walls. The wall is between you and the bad thing. You are *here*, the bad thing is *there*. The geometry is the safety.

This is how the human nervous system has always worked. We run *from* something *to* something. Every nerve in your spine is calibrated for a universe in which safety is *somewhere else*.

Now ask yourself: what would it feel like to be afraid of something with no *somewhere else*?

What if the thing you feared was the medium itself?

This is what these *yarkuḍūn* — these galloping fugitives — are running into. Allah's *baʾs* is not a force in space. *Baʾs* is the disclosure of a reality that was already filling all of space. There is no direction in which Allah's authority does not extend. There is no closet in the universe big enough.

The horse is running, but the rider's destination — *outside Allah's dominion* — does not exist. There is no such place. The map of safety, to which the body is so faithfully trying to get, has been revealed as fiction.

This is what makes the *idhā hum minhā yarkuḍūn* so quietly devastating. The body is *still running*. The instinct hasn't caught up to the metaphysics. The man on the horse is doing the only thing his body knows how to do, in a geometry where it does not work.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this home.

You are not a citizen of a destroyed civilization. But your nervous system runs on the same instinct.

When something scares you in your life — a diagnosis, a layoff, a betrayal, a moral failure of your own that you cannot un-do — what does your body try to do?

It tries to *run somewhere*. Sometimes the running is literal. Sometimes the running is digital. *Where is my phone — let me scroll.* Sometimes the running is into work. Sometimes it is into food. Sometimes it is into another person's body. Sometimes it is into rage. The running is the body trying to put walls between you and the fear.

And much of the time, this works. Many fears in life *are* local. The phone *does* distract you. The food *does* dampen the anxiety. The closet *does* contain the parent's argument.

But there is one fear for which the running does not work. There is one disclosure where every direction is the wrong direction.

That is the fear of meeting the One you were avoiding all along.

You cannot scroll your way out of standing before Allah. You cannot eat your way out. You cannot work your way out. You cannot sleep your way out. The body, faithful to its instinct, will *try* — *yarkuḍūn* — but the running will produce nothing because there is no *to*.

This is why the Quran's medicine, throughout, is the *opposite* of the body's instinct. Where the body wants to run *from* Allah, the Quran says — *firrū ilā Allāh* — "flee *to* Allah" (Surat Adh-Dhāriyāt 51:50).

Notice what this does. *Firrū* — flee. The same instinct. *To* Allah — the destination is reversed.

The cure for the geometry of fleeing-from is the geometry of fleeing-to. You take the same body that wants to run, and you turn it around. The running is not the problem. The *direction* is the problem.

The destroyed civilizations of *qaṣamnā* ran *from*. Their bodies were doing what bodies do. But their direction was the lie.

The believing heart runs *to*. Same legs. Same panic. Different destination. Different end.

What in your life right now are you running from that you should be running to? What ruler are you flailing in front of as if He were not the only place safety exists?

### Theme Two: The Luxury Trap — When Comfort Itself Becomes the Indictment

Let me start with something modern. Stay with me again.

Imagine a successful executive. He has built a career over twenty years. He has a corner office, a beautiful home, a calendar full of important meetings, a network full of important names. He is *consulted*. People call him for his opinion. His luxury is not just possessions — it is the social fact of *mattering*.

Now imagine that overnight, his company collapses. Fraud is uncovered. The board is fired. The building is shuttered.

He sits in his living room the morning after, in his expensive chair, in his beautiful house, in his silk robe. His phone is silent. The people who used to call him for his opinion are now calling each other to distance themselves from him.

A voice in his head says: *go back to the office.*

He thinks: *what office? It is closed.*

The voice says: *go anyway. Sit at your desk. Maybe someone will call you.*

This is the cruelty of *laʿallakum tusʾalūn*. The comforts you organized your life around are still *there* — the chair is still there, the house is still there, the desk is still there — but the *meaning* they had has been pulled out from underneath them. The objects remain. The status that animated them does not.

This is what comfort becomes when it has been built on the wrong foundation. It does not just fail you. It *mocks* you. It sits there, unchanged in form, as the visible monument to an arrangement that has collapsed.

[PAUSE]

This is what *utriftum* names.

*Taraf* is not wealth in itself. The Quran does not condemn possessions. *Taraf* is the *softening* effect of ease — the way comfort, over time, dulls the parts of us that should stay awake. The way being secure for too long makes us forget we were ever fragile. The way being consulted for too long makes us think we are the source of wisdom rather than its borrower.

*Taraf* is the moral biography of the well-fed.

And the verb is in the *passive voice*. *Utriftum* — "you were *given* this." Not *atraftum* — "you yourselves accumulated luxury." The grammar quietly strips them of agency. Al-Tabari reads this as *unʿimtum* — "you were blessed." The luxury was not merely distributed to them; it was a *niʿma*, a gift from Allah. And every gift carries the weight of recognition. What was sent as blessing, they received as entitlement.

Stay with this. It applies to you and to me.

What in your life have you started to think of as *yours*?

Your health — is it yours, or is it something you were given?

Your intelligence — is it yours, or is it something you were given?

Your spouse — is that person yours, or is that a person you were given?

Your time — is it yours, or is it something you are being given right now, this minute, breath by breath?

The grammar of *utriftum* says: every comfort you call *mine* is, in the deeper grammar, a *was-given*. The active voice in which you live is a translation; the original is in the passive.

This is not a debasement. This is a liberation. Because once you remember that the comforts were given, two things happen:

1. You stop being terrified of losing them. The thing that was given was never something you held onto by force.
2. You start to see them as gifts that point at the Giver. Each pleasure becomes a small note from Allah that says, *I'm here. I gave you this.*

The civilizations of *qaṣamnā* could not see their comforts this way. Their grammar was *atraftum* — *we ourselves accumulated*. The corrective came too late. By the time the divine voice said *utriftum* — *you were given* — the houses were already empty.

The mercy of receiving this verse now, from far away, is that we can re-grammar our lives *before* the empty house. We can wake up tomorrow and look at our coffee, our family, our work, our breath, and say — *utriftu* — *I was given this.*

That single re-grammar is the difference between being *al-mutrafīn* and being among those who, when they were given, said *al-ḥamdu li-llāh*.

What in your life, in the next twenty-four hours, can you re-grammar from *mine* to *given*?

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture We Did Not Notice

Step back from the three ayahs and look at their shape.

Ayah 11 is wide-angle. It is the camera at fifty thousand feet, looking down at *kam qaṣamnā* — *how many we have annihilated.* It speaks of civilizations as nouns. It is the historian's view.

Ayah 12 is mid-range. It is the camera at street level. We can now *see* the bodies running. We can see the horses kicking. We are inside a moment.

Ayah 13 is close-up. It is the camera in the face of the fugitive. We can hear the voice that mocks him. We can almost smell the dust of his stopped horse.

Allah has zoomed in. From civilization, to crowd, to face.

And the closing word — *tusʾalūn* — *you will be questioned* — is not aimed at the dead. It is aimed at *us*. The Arabic verb is in the second person plural. *You.*

The ones being addressed in the final word of the passage are not the destroyed civilizations of the past. They are the listeners in front of the Quran. *You* will be questioned.

The whole passage was a setup for that pronoun.

### The One Sentence

When Allah's wrath is disclosed, the very comforts we trusted to protect us become the things the universe uses to mock us — and the running our bodies were built for has nowhere to go because the One we are running from fills every direction we could run toward.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What in your life are you running *from* that you should be running *to*? What is the closet you keep flinging yourself into to avoid the One who fills the closet too?

2. Which of your comforts are you holding in the active voice — *I built this, I earned this, I deserved this* — that the Quran would re-grammar into the passive: *you were given this*?

3. When you imagine the moment of standing before Allah, does your nervous system reach for *yarkuḍūn* — flight — or for *firrū ilā Allāh* — running toward Him? Which set of muscles have you been training?

4. If your house, your job, your status, your network — your *mā utriftum fīhi* — were taken in the morning, what would remain that you would still call yours? Is *that* what you are actually building your life on?

5. *Laʿallakum tusʾalūn* — "perhaps you will be questioned." If you were questioned today, by Allah, about the comforts you have been given — could you answer?

---

### The Closing Invitation

Do not finish this reflection by being afraid of Allah's *baʾs*. That is the response of the *mutraf*, the soft one whose first instinct in the face of God is to run.

Finish it by being a different kind of person. The kind who, hearing about *qaṣamnā*, looks at his own house and says: *thank You for letting me sit in this house tonight. It is not mine. I was given it. Let me sit in it as Your guest.*

The kind who, hearing about *yarkuḍūn*, takes his body — its instinct to run — and turns it around. *Firrū ilā Allāh.* I will flee *to* You from You. I will run *to* the closet that turns out to be the chest of the One I was avoiding.

The kind who, hearing *laʿallakum tusʾalūn*, does not wait for the question.

The kind who answers it now.
