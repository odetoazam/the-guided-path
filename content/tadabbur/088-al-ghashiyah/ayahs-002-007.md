[ayah:88:3] عَامِلَةٌ نَّاصِبَةٌ

[ayah:88:4] تَصْلَىٰ نَارًا حَامِيَةً

[ayah:88:5] تُسْقَىٰ مِنْ عَيْنٍ ءَانِيَةٍ

[ayah:88:6] لَّيْسَ لَهُمْ طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِن ضَرِيعٍ

[ayah:88:7] لَّا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِى مِن جُوعٍ

*Wujūhun yawma'idhin khāshi'ah. 'Āmilatun nāṣibah. Taṣlā nāran ḥāmiyah. Tusqā min 'aynin āniyah. Laysa lahum ṭa'āmun illā min ḍarī'. Lā yusminu wa lā yughnī min jū'.*

Faces that Day will be humbled — working hard, exhausted — entering blazing fire — given drink from a boiling spring — no food for them except bitter thorns — which neither nourish nor satisfy hunger.

---

Allah just asked: هل أتاك حديث الغاشية — has the report of the Overwhelming reached you? And now He answers. And the first thing He shows you is not a landscape. Not a fire. Not a gate. **Faces.**

Not people. Not bodies. Not names. *Faces*.

This is deliberate. In Arabic culture — in all human culture — the face is where dignity lives. It's where pride shows. Where defiance sits. Where identity is most readable. And the first thing you see on the Day of the Overwhelming is faces that have lost all of that.

But before the fire, before the boiling water, before the thorns — there's a phrase so devastating that the classical scholars split on its meaning, and both readings destroy you in different ways. عَامِلَةٌ نَّاصِبَةٌ. Two words. And they contain either the image of futile punishment or the image of a wasted life — depending on which scholar you follow. We'll sit with both. Because both are grounded in the tradition, and both are real.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Faces, Not People

وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ خَـٰشِعَةٌ — "Faces, that Day, humbled."

The word وُجُوه — faces — is doing something the word "people" couldn't. When the Quran says "faces," it puts the camera on the most visible part of the human being. Not the hands that sinned. Not the feet that walked away. The *face* — the thing you present to the world. The thing that carried your smile, your smirk, your look of dismissal when someone mentioned the Day of Judgment.

And خَاشِعَة — humbled. From the root خشع — to lower, to bow, to submit. The mufassirūn read it here with a hard edge: Qatādah, in al-Ṭabarī's report, glosses خاشعة plainly as *ذليلة* — abased, brought low, stripped of standing — and specifies "*khāshi'atun fī al-nār*," humbled *in the Fire itself*. خشوع is what your face is supposed to do in prayer, voluntarily. The face that refused to bow in prayer is now bowed involuntarily. The humility that was invited in this life is enforced in the next.

Notice: the ayah doesn't say *their* faces. Just: وجوه. Indefinite. Unidentified. These could be anyone's faces. This anonymity is itself a punishment — on a Day when everything is exposed, these faces have no names. No reputation. No legacy. No "do you know who I am?" Just: faces. Abased.

[PAUSE]

### The Two Readings of عَامِلَةٌ نَّاصِبَةٌ

Now we arrive at one of the most haunting phrases in the surah — and the classical scholars read it in two ways, and both are devastating.

عَامِلَةٌ — working, laboring, active. From عمل — to do, to work, to act.
نَّاصِبَةٌ — exhausted, fatigued, toiling in futility. From نصب — to be weary, to labor to the point of exhaustion.

**Reading One** — and this is the reading al-Ṭabarī himself favors, reporting it from Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah, and noting that *"the people of interpretation said the like of what we have said."* These faces are working hard and exhausted *in the Fire itself*. Ibn ʿAbbās puts it in the barest terms: *"taʿmalu wa tanṣabu min al-nār"* — it labors and is worn down by the Fire. The punishment is not just burning — it is *labor*. They toil. They are forced to work. And the work produces nothing. Exhaustion without rest, effort without result, movement without progress. Qatādah sharpens it further: these were faces that *were arrogant in this life* (تكبرت في الدنيا), and so they are set to strain and toil in the Fire. The pride that once held the face high now bends it over endless, fruitless work. This is what the Fire does: it doesn't just hurt. It *wastes*. It makes everything you do amount to nothing, over and over, forever.

**Reading Two** — al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī bridges the two worlds. Reading عاملة ناصبة, he said: *"lam taʿmal lillāhi fī al-dunyā, fa-aʿmalahā fī al-nār"* — *it did not labor for Allah in this world, so He set it to labor in the Fire.* The exhaustion of the next life is the mirror-image of a refusal in this one. Later commentators drew out the natural extension: these were not necessarily idle people. Many of them were busy — active, straining, exhausting themselves — but not for Allah, not on the صراط المستقيم. They worked 80-hour weeks. They built companies, collected accolades, achieved milestones. And they were ناصبة — worn out by all of it. But none of that effort was directed toward Allah. And so all of that work, all of that exhaustion — *amounted to nothing on the Day it mattered*.

[PAUSE]

Let both readings sit together. Because the composite image is worse than either reading alone. On al-Ḥasan's bridge, these faces refused to spend themselves for Allah *in this life* — and now they spend themselves *in the next life*, hard, endlessly, to the point of exhaustion, for nothing at all. The refusal to labor rightly becomes the sentence to labor uselessly. The futility answers the failure. They would not work for Him; now they cannot stop working, and none of it counts.

This isn't a portrait of criminals. This isn't a description of the obviously evil. On Qatādah's reading it is a portrait of the *arrogant* — the busy, the accomplished, the self-important — whose pride is finally bent to fruitless toil.

[PAUSE]

### The Anti-Feast

Now the passage shifts from state to sustenance — and every detail is a precise inversion of the thing the Arab listener valued most: *hospitality*.

In pre-Islamic Arabia, honor was measured by how you treated your guest. A guest arrived, and you gave them the best you had: cool water from your freshest spring. Abundant food. Comfort. Rest. A guest in your tent was sacred. To fail in hospitality was to fail as a human being.

Now watch what the Fire offers.

تُسْقَىٰ مِنْ عَيْنٍ ءَانِيَةٍ — "They are given drink from a boiling spring."

A spring — عَيْن — is supposed to be the source of life, of cool relief. In the desert, a spring is salvation. But this spring is آنية — *boiling*; al-Muyassar renders it a spring *شديدة الحرارة*, of intense, extreme heat. The word comes from the root أني, meaning to reach the utmost point of heat. The thing that was supposed to save you *burns* you. The source of relief becomes the instrument of pain.

And notice the verb: تُسْقَىٰ — passive voice. "They are *given to drink*." Not "they drink." They don't choose this. In this life, they chose what to consume — what to pour into their minds, their hearts, their bodies. They chose heedlessness. They chose distraction. They chose everything except what would nourish their souls. And now choice is stripped from them. They are *made* to drink. The agency they misused is removed.

[PAUSE]

لَّيْسَ لَهُمْ طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِن ضَرِيعٍ — "No food for them except from *ḍarī'*."

ضَرِيع. This word appears *nowhere else in the entire Quran*. A hapax legomenon — a word used exactly once. al-Muyassar describes it as *نبت ذي شوك لاصق بالأرض* — a thorny growth that clings low to the ground — and calls it flatly *مِن شر الطعام وأخبثه*, among the worst and foulest of foods. The lexicographers add that it is called شِبْرِق when green and ضريع when dry: a plant so bitter and thorny that, they say, even *camels* turn from it once it dries. And camels eat almost anything.

Allah chose a word that appears only once — a word so rare that even some early Arabs struggled with it — to name the food of the Fire. The rarity of the word matches the extremity of the reality. This isn't just bad food. This is food that exists at the outer edge of what language can describe. The Quran needed a word that sat alone, without echoes, without comparison, to capture something that has no parallel — and it named the lowest thing that grows: a thorn clinging to the dirt.

And then, in case any doubt remains about what this food does, the next ayah eliminates it:

لَّا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِى مِن جُوعٍ — "It neither nourishes nor satisfies hunger."

Both verbs — يُسْمِنُ and يُغْنِى — are Form IV: the causative form. This food does not *cause* weight gain (nourishment). It does not *cause* sufficiency against hunger (satisfaction). al-Muyassar unpacks exactly this: it does not fatten the body against its emaciation (*من الهُزال*), nor does it block the hunger and the last flicker of life (*لا يسدّ جوعه ورمقه*). Food exists for exactly two purposes: to build the body and to relieve hunger. This food does neither. It is food that fails at being food. It occupies the category of nourishment while delivering none of its content — the guest is fed, and stays starving.

[PAUSE]

Step back and see the complete anti-feast:

| Hospitality norm | What the Fire offers |
|---|---|
| Cool water from a spring | Boiling water from a spring |
| Abundant, quality food | Thorns clinging to the dirt, even camels reject |
| Nourishment for the body | Neither nourishes the wasted body |
| Satisfaction of hunger | Nor blocks hunger or the last of life |
| Guest chooses what to eat/drink | Guest is *made* to drink (passive) |

Every element is present. Nothing is missing. Spring: present. Food: present. But every element is *inverted*. The form of hospitality exists. The substance is its opposite. This is not the absence of provision. It is **provision turned against itself**.

[PAUSE]

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Busy Life That Amounted to Nothing

There's a particular modern anxiety that maps directly onto عاملة ناصبة, and most people who experience it don't have a name for it.

It's the feeling of being exhausted but not fulfilled. Of having a full calendar and an empty heart. Of achieving everything your culture told you to achieve — the career, the house, the status, the followers — and lying awake at 2 a.m. with the quiet suspicion that none of it *mattered*.

The burnout epidemic isn't just about overwork. It's about overwork *in the wrong direction*. The body breaks down not because it worked too hard but because the work didn't nourish the soul. You can run a marathon and feel alive. You can sit in meetings for ten hours and feel dead. The exhaustion of عاملة ناصبة isn't only physical. It's existential. It's the tiredness that comes from spending your life's energy on things that will not survive the Day.

[PAUSE]

al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's bridge is the seed of this: *they did not labor for Allah in this world, so He set them to labor in the Fire.* The commentators who followed him drew out what that means for the busy — and it is unsparing. These were not lazy people. They may well have been the most active, the most productive, the most visibly successful. عاملة — workers, doers, achievers. And ناصبة — exhausted by all of it. But on the Day that matters, their faces are abased. Not because they didn't work. Because they didn't work *for Him*.

The Quran isn't against ambition. It isn't against building. It's against building without foundation. Against running without direction. Against exhausting yourself on a road that doesn't go anywhere — and calling that exhaustion "success."

The question عاملة ناصبة plants in you is not "Am I working hard enough?" You're probably working too hard. The question is: **"Is any of this work being done for Allah — and will it survive the Day when everything is uncovered?"**

### Theme 2: When Nourishment Stops Working

We nourish ourselves with many things. Food, obviously. But also entertainment, validation, status, relationships, distraction, consumption. Every human being has a system of sustenance — things they turn to when they're hungry for meaning, for comfort, for peace.

And most of those systems work, for a while. The scroll relieves the boredom. The purchase relieves the emptiness. The achievement relieves the self-doubt. For a while. And then the hunger returns. And you consume again. And the hunger returns. And you consume again.

لا يسمن ولا يغني من جوع. It neither fattens the wasted body nor blocks the hunger.

[PAUSE]

This is the Quran's description of the Fire's food. But read it again as a description of every false source of nourishment in this life. The entertainment that passes time but doesn't feed the soul — لا يسمن. The approval of people that feels good for an hour but leaves you needing more by morning — ولا يغني من جوع. The consumption that fills the stomach but starves the heart — that leaves you, in al-Muyassar's phrase, still gaunt with هُزال, still down to the last رمق.

The anti-feast doesn't start in the Fire. The anti-feast starts every time you feed yourself something that cannot actually nourish you — and the hunger returns, unchanged, an hour later. The Fire simply makes permanent and visible what was already happening invisibly in this life.

The ضريع — the thorns — are the final form of every substitute. The lowest thing that grows, clinging to the dirt. Everything you consumed instead of what would have actually fed you. The junk that took the place of the real. And on that Day, the substitutes are all that's left. No more access to the real thing. Only the thorns.

[PAUSE]

## Closing Synthesis

### Architectural Reveal

These six ayahs cascade in a precise sequence — from identity to state to sustenance:

**Who:** وُجُوهٌ — faces (identity, anonymized)
**When:** يَوْمَئِذٍ — that Day (the Overwhelming, الغاشية)
**State:** خَاشِعَةٌ عَامِلَةٌ نَّاصِبَةٌ — abased, laboring, worn out
**Setting:** تَصْلَىٰ نَارًا حَامِيَةً — entering a blazing Fire
**Drink:** تُسْقَىٰ مِنْ عَيْنٍ ءَانِيَةٍ — a boiling spring, poured on them (passive)
**Food:** لَيْسَ لَهُمْ طَعَامٌ إِلَّا مِن ضَرِيعٍ — nothing but thorns
**Result:** لَا يُسْمِنُ وَلَا يُغْنِى مِن جُوعٍ — neither nourishes nor satisfies

Read top to bottom, the passage narrows like a funnel. It begins with the whole self — the face — and ends with the smallest, most humiliating detail: a thorn that cannot even do the one thing food is for. Every stage strips something away. The name is gone (just *faces*). The dignity is gone (*abased*). The rest is gone (*laboring, exhausted*). The relief is gone (*boiling*). The nourishment is gone (*thorns that feed nothing*). What's left at the bottom of the funnel is a being that works forever and is fed nothing — the pure distillation of a life that ran hard in the wrong direction.

### Where This Lands

Ibn Kathīr opens this surah by recording that the Prophet ﷺ used to recite Al-Aʿlā and Al-Ghāshiyah together in the Friday and Eid prayers — on the authority of an-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr. Sit with that. This scene — the abased faces, the futile labor, the boiling spring, the thorns — was not meant to be encountered once and shelved. The Prophet ﷺ placed it in front of the community *every week*, in the largest gathering of the week, standing shoulder to shoulder. The question that opens the surah — *has the report of the Overwhelming reached you?* — was designed to reach you again and again, before the weekend, before the work, before you poured your one life into another seven days.

So let it ask now. The faces in these ayahs were busy. They labored. They exhausted themselves. Their tragedy was not idleness — it was direction. They fed themselves what could not nourish and worked at what could not last, and the Day uncovered all of it.

The mercy hidden inside the warning is this: you are still choosing. The spring is not yet boiling. The food is not yet thorns. The agency that تُسْقَىٰ strips away in the next world is, right now, still in your hands. You can still choose what you consume — for your eyes, your heart, your soul. You can still turn your labor toward the One for whom labor is never wasted.

*Yā Allāh, make our faces of those that are bright on that Day, not abased. Let our work be for You, so that it is never صار ناصبة — never toil that amounts to nothing. And feed our hearts, in this life, with what actually nourishes: Your remembrance, Your Book, Your nearness. Āmīn.*
