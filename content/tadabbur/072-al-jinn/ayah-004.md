# Our Foolish One: The Confession of a Lie Carried Too Far

> وَأَنَّهُۥ كَانَ يَقُولُ سَفِيهُنَا عَلَى ٱللَّهِ شَطَطًا
>
> *wa-annahu kāna yaqūlu safīhunā ʿalā-llāhi shaṭaṭā*
>
> "And that the foolish one among us used to speak about Allah an outrageous transgression."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sound of a People Waking Up

I want you to imagine a group coming home from somewhere that changed them.

They had gone out into the night the way they always did. And on this particular night, they heard something. Not music. Not a poem. Not a fortune-teller's rhyme. They heard the Quran being recited — and something in them cracked open. The text tells us, just a few verses earlier, that they could not stop listening. *"Indeed, we have heard a wondrous Recitation"* (72:1).

Now they are back among their own people. And they begin to speak.

This is one of the rare passages in the entire Quran that is not addressed *to* anyone — not a command, not a warning, not "O you who believe." It is a **report**. Allah is letting us listen in on the jinn talking to each other. We are overhearing a community in the act of waking up.

And here is what I want you to notice before we even open the words. When people wake up from something — a bad relationship, a false belief, a movement they gave years to — there is a predictable sequence. First comes the new truth: "This is real, this is right." Then comes something harder. Then they have to turn around and look at what they used to be.

The verse just before this one is the new truth. The jinn declare: *"Exalted is the majesty of our Lord — He has taken no wife and no son"* (72:3). That is the light coming on.

And then — *this* verse. The moment they turn around and look back at the dark room they had been living in.

[PAUSE]

Listen to what they say. They do not say, "We were lied to." They do not say, "Someone deceived us." They say something far more honest, far more costly, and far stranger than that.

They say: **"Our foolish one used to speak about Allah an outrageous transgression."**

*Our* foolish one. Not "a" liar. Not "the" deceiver. **Ours.**

What does it mean to wake up and discover that the lie you believed was not imposed on you from the outside — but was speaking from inside your own house, in a voice you had claimed as one of your own? That is the question this verse is going to open. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: سَفِيهُنَا — "Our Foolish One"

#### Teaching the structure first

Let me start with a small piece of Arabic that does enormous work here. In Arabic, you can attach a possessive pronoun to the end of a noun. *Kitāb* is "a book." *Kitābunā* is "our book." That little suffix — **‑nā** — means "us, ours, belonging to us."

It is the most ordinary thing in the language. We do it in English too: "my brother," "our team." But here is the thing about that little word "our." It is not just descriptive. It is a claim of belonging. When you say "our team lost," you are not reporting a fact about a team across town. You are confessing a connection. You are putting yourself inside the thing.

Now hold that.

#### Opening the word

The jinn could have used many words for the source of the falsehood. The Arabic language gave them options. They could have said *al-kādhib* — "the liar." They could have said *ʿaduwwunā* — "our enemy." They could have named Iblis directly and pushed him away from themselves.

Instead they say:

> سَفِيهُنَا — *safīhunā*

The word *safīh* comes from the root **s-f-h** (سفه). And the classical lexicons are precise about what this root means. It is not merely "stupid" in the sense of low intelligence. *Safah* is **lightness, instability, a deficiency of sound judgment** — the opposite of *ḥilm* (gravity, forbearance) and the opposite of *rushd* (right-mindedness, maturity). The Arabs used it for a rope that is loose and flapping, for a garment that is thin and flimsy, for a mind that has no weight to it, that blows wherever the wind of desire pushes it.

So a *safīh* is not someone who lacks information. A *safīh* is someone who lacks **ballast**. Someone whose judgment has no anchor.

And the imams of tafsir tell us who this *safīh* is. Mujāhid, Qatāda, and Ibn Jurayj — as recorded by al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī — say the foolish one here is **Iblīs himself**. Al-Baghawī glosses *safīhunā* simply as *jāhilunā* — "our ignorant one." Al-Qurṭubī then preserves a second reading from Qatāda: that it refers to **the foolish among the jinn** more broadly — "the foolish of the jinn disobeyed Him just as the foolish of mankind disobeyed Him."

Notice these are not two competing claims fighting for the same chair. They are two camera angles on one reality. Whether the chief fool is Iblīs at the head, or the rank-and-file fools who followed him — the structure is the same. **Foolishness was speaking, and a community was listening.**

#### Sitting with the suffix

But return now to that little ‑nā. *Our* foolish one.

Here is what is remarkable. The jinn have just had the most clarifying experience of their existence. They have heard the Quran. They have grasped *tawḥīd*. In that moment of clarity, the easiest thing in the world — the *human* thing, the *nafs* thing — would be to draw a clean line. "*Those* fools deceived us. *We* are not like them anymore. We have crossed over to the side of truth, and we leave the liars behind on the far side."

That is what we do. When we leave a mistake behind, we exile it. We make it foreign. We say "the person I used to be" as if he were a stranger we are not responsible for.

The jinn do not do this. [PAUSE]

In the very breath where they discover the truth, they reach back and they **claim the fool as their own**. *Safīhunā.* He is one of us. He came from among us. We let him speak for us. The line of belonging is not cut. It is confessed.

Let's sit with what that costs. To say "our foolish one" is to admit: *the lie was not done to me. The lie was, in some measure, done by us, and I was inside the "us."* It is the difference between "I was a victim" and "I was complicit." One of those is comfortable. The other is the beginning of real repentance.

And do you see how much more honest this is? Because it is **true**. No one is purely deceived. We give the fool his platform. We nod along. We repeat what he said at our own gatherings. The falsehood about Allah did not float in from nowhere — it had a voice, and the community had handed that voice a microphone and called it "ours."

What this single suffix reveals is the anatomy of an honest awakening: **the first thing a truly awakened heart does is stop outsourcing the blame.**

### Element Two: شَطَطًا — Speech That Drifts to the Far Shore

#### Opening the word

Now look at the object of all this foolish speaking. What exactly was the *safīh* saying about Allah?

> شَطَطًا — *shaṭaṭā*

Translators reach for words like "an outrage," "a lie," "an excess," "transgression." All of these are gesturing at something the single English word cannot hold. Let me take you into the root, because the root is a physical image — and remember, in the Quran the physical image is almost always a stepping stone toward something unseen.

The root **sh-ṭ-ṭ** (شطط) carries, at its base, the idea of **distance and remoteness**. The lexicographers connect it to *shaṭṭ* — the **far bank of a river, the distant shore**. *Shaṭaṭ* is "going far," exceeding the proper limit, traveling out past the bounds. To *yashuṭṭu* is to go to a faraway place, to overshoot.

So when the classical commentators define it, watch how they reach for the language of **excess** and **going-too-far**. Al-Jalālayn: *ghuluww fī l-kadhib* — "extremity in the lie." Al-Muyassar: *qawlan baʿīdan ʿan al-ḥaqq* — "a saying **far** from the truth." Al-Qurṭubī: *al-shaṭaṭ wa-l-ishtiṭāṭ: al-ghuluww fī l-kufr* — "shaṭaṭ is extremity in disbelief," and he adds a second gloss from Abū Mālik: *al-jawr* — deviation, swerving off the road.

#### Teaching the image

Do you feel the physical picture underneath all of these?

A lie about Allah is not pictured here as a small thing, a stain, a flaw. It is pictured as a **distance**. As **drift**. As a boat that slips its mooring and floats, slowly, away from the bank — until the shore of truth is just a thin line on the horizon and you cannot get back without enormous effort.

This is the genius of the word the Quran chooses. Because that is precisely how false belief about God actually works. **No one drifts to the far shore in a single afternoon.** You do not wake up one morning and decide to attribute a wife and a son to the Lord of the worlds. It happens by inches. One claim. Then one claim built on that claim. Then a story to support the second claim. Then a song. Then a festival. Then it is simply "what we believe," and the shore you started from is so far behind you that you have forgotten there was ever a shore at all.

*Shaṭaṭ* is the name for arriving at the far bank without ever feeling the journey.

#### The indefinite — an excess without measure

Now notice one more thing, and this is the kind of detail the Arabic gives that translation quietly drops. The word is *shaṭaṭā* — with the **tanwīn**, the indefinite ending, the *‑an* sound. It is not "the transgression." It is not "a specific named lie." It is left **indefinite, unbounded** — *some* transgression, an excess whose dimensions are not given.

In Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite like this, in a context like this, opens it up rather than closing it down. It refuses to put a fence around the falsehood. As if to say: how far did he drift? You cannot measure it. The distance is beyond accounting. **An outrageousness without an edge.**

And here is the link back to Element One that I want you to feel. The fool — the *safīh* — was defined as the one without ballast, without anchor. And what does a boat without an anchor do? **It drifts. It goes shaṭaṭ.** The two words are one diagnosis: the unanchored judgment (*safah*) inevitably produces the unbounded drift (*shaṭaṭ*). Take away the ballast, and distance from the truth is not a risk — it is a certainty.

#### Listen to the word

One last thing, and this you can hear with your ears even if you know no Arabic. Say it: *shaṭaṭā.*

It begins with the hiss of **shīn**, and then it strikes two heavy, emphatic **ṭāʾ** sounds in a row — *ṭa-ṭā* — like two stumbles, like a stone skipping wrong, like spitting. It is a harsh, jarring, **ugly-sounding** word. Set it beside the soft slide of *safīh* — *sa-fī*, almost gentle, almost slipping past you.

That is not an accident. The foolishness sounds *easy* — it slips by, soft, unnoticed. The transgression it produces sounds **violent** — it lands on the ear like a hammer. The very sound of the verse is teaching you: the soft thing and the hard thing are the same thing, just at the beginning and the end of the drift.

And this is the only other place in the Quran the word arrives with this exact shape — *shaṭaṭā* — outside the mouths of the believing youth of the Cave, who said: *"If we called upon any god besides Him, we would then have said an outrageous transgression — shaṭaṭā"* (18:14). The young men of the Cave **named the far shore so they would never drift toward it.** The jinn here name the far shore having just been pulled back from it. Same word. One spoken in prevention, one spoken in repentance. **Both spoken by people who finally understood that the worst thing a tongue can do is travel far from the truth about God.**

### Element Three: كَانَ يَقُولُ — The "Used To" That Is Finally Over

#### Teaching the tense

Here is a quiet grammatical feature that carries the whole emotional weight of the verse. In Arabic, when you want to express a **habitual, ongoing action in the past** — something that *used to* happen, repeatedly, as a settled state of affairs — you pair the verb *kāna* ("was") with a present-tense verb after it.

> كَانَ يَقُولُ — *kāna yaqūlu* — "he **used to** keep saying," "he was continually saying"

This is not "he said it once." This is the grammar of a **standing condition**. Al-Qurṭubī even pauses on the word *kāna* here, discussing its grammatical function in the sentence — it is doing real structural work. The construction tells us this foolish speech against Allah was not a single slip. It was the **background noise of an entire era**. It went on, and on, and the community lived inside the sound of it like people who no longer hear the traffic outside their window.

#### Why the tense is the whole point

Now feel why this matters so much, sitting where it sits.

The jinn are speaking in the **past habitual**. "He *used to* say." And that small tense is doing something enormous: it is announcing that **the era is over**. You only say "he *used to*" about a thing that has stopped. The very grammar is a door closing. By framing the foolish one's speech as *kāna yaqūlu* — a thing that *used to* be the case — the jinn are declaring, without saying it directly, that **it is no longer the case for us.**

This is the sound of a line being drawn through time. Not a line between "us" and "them" — we already saw they refused that line. A line between **then** and **now**. Between the long era when the unanchored voice spoke for everyone, and this moment, this verse, where the recitation has come and the era has ended.

There is something almost unbearably hopeful in a verb tense. The drift to the far shore went on for ages — *kāna yaqūlu*, it kept being said. And then it stopped. **The "used to" is the proof that even a falsehood that has been speaking your whole life can become a thing you speak about in the past tense.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words have built, together, in seven words of Arabic.

A community names the source of its falsehood and refuses to disown it — *safīhunā*, **our** foolish one. They name the falsehood itself not as a stain but as a **distance**, an unmeasured drift to the far shore — *shaṭaṭā*. And they place the whole thing in the **past habitual** — *kāna yaqūlu* — the grammar of an era that has just, finally, ended.

That is not a description of the jinn. That is a description of every human awakening that has ever happened.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way falsehood actually lives in us, and the way it finally dies.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Foolishness Is Not Stupidity — It Is Speaking Without Weight

Let me start where you live.

Think about the most confident wrong person you have ever encountered. Not the quiet ignorant person — the *loud* one. The one who held forth at the dinner table about a thing they had never studied. The one whose certainty rose in exact proportion to how little they actually knew. There is a strange law in human life: **the less ballast a claim has, the more loudly it tends to announce itself.** Real expertise is full of "it depends" and "I'm not sure." Foolishness has no such hesitation. It speaks fast, speaks far, speaks with total conviction.

The Quran has a precise word for this, and it is the word in our verse: **safah.** Lightness. The absence of weight. We translate it "foolishness," but that makes us picture a low IQ, and that is exactly wrong. Iblīs — named by the imams as the *safīh* here — is not unintelligent. He out-argued, he out-maneuvered, he constructed a theology of disobedience. He is brilliant. And he is the *safīh*. Because **safah is not a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of gravity.** It is a sharp mind with no anchor, brilliance with nothing to hold it down, so that it floats free and drifts to the far shore.

Now bring this home, because here is where it stops being about the jinn. Where in your life is there a voice that speaks about Allah, or about the deen, with great confidence and no ballast? Sometimes it is a voice out there — a clever personality, an online presence, someone whose certainty is magnetic precisely because it is unburdened by knowledge or fear of God. But sometimes — and this is the harder one — **the safīh is a voice inside you.**

There is a part of every one of us that wants to speak about Allah on our own terms. To say, quietly, in the chamber of the heart: *Surely He won't mind. Surely He understands. Surely this thing I want is fine. Surely He is not really watching this closely.* That voice feels reasonable. It feels soft. It slips by — *safīh*, soft as the sound of the word. And every one of those quiet revisions is a sentence of **shaṭaṭ**: a small drift, a few feet farther from the shore.

The Quran elsewhere names the gravest version of this: *"And do not say about Allah except the truth"* (4:171). *"Say, my Lord has only forbidden... saying about Allah that which you do not know"* (7:33). Did you notice where that last verse places it? Saying about Allah what you do not know is listed in the same breath as the gravest sins. Because it is the engine under all of them. **Every drift to the far shore begins with one unanchored sentence about God.**

So what does this change about tomorrow? It is very concrete. It is this: develop a holy suspicion of your own certainty *whenever your certainty happens to align with your desire.* When you find yourself confidently concluding that Allah surely permits exactly the thing your *nafs* was already craving — pause. That is the precise location where the *safīh* speaks. Not in your moments of doubt. In your moments of convenient confidence. **Put an anchor down right there, before the drift begins** — by going back to what He actually said, through people who actually know, rather than what the light, weightless voice inside assures you He must have meant.

### Theme Two: The First Act of an Awakened Heart Is to Own the Lie

Now I want to take you into the deepest thing in this verse — that little suffix, *‑nā*. "**Our** foolish one."

Here is a pattern you have seen, maybe in yourself, maybe in someone you love. A person leaves something — a destructive ideology, a bad crowd, a long error. And there are two completely different ways to leave.

The first way is to leave by **exile**. You get out, and you slam the door, and you spend the rest of your life describing how *they* did it to you. *They* manipulated me. *They* brainwashed me. *I* was the victim. *I* was clean; *they* were the poison. This kind of leaving feels like freedom, but watch what it actually does: it keeps you a passive object. It says the most important things that happened in your life were done *to* you by other people. And it quietly preserves your innocence at the cost of your agency — because if *they* did it all, then you never really chose anything, which means you have nothing to actually repent of, which means nothing in you has actually changed.

The second way to leave is the way the jinn leave. You get out, and then you turn around, and you say: **"That fool was ours. We housed him. We handed him the microphone. We are the 'us' he spoke for."**

This second way is agony. It is so much harder. Because it refuses you the comfort of victimhood. But stay with me — look at what it gives you in exchange. [PAUSE] It gives you back **yourself**. It says: I was not a leaf blown by other people's wind. I was a participant. I gave my consent. And if I was capable of giving my consent to the lie, then — and only then — am I genuinely capable of withdrawing it for the truth. **You can only renounce what you first admit you owned.**

This is why the jinn's confession is not weakness — it is the precise sound of power returning to a soul. The man who says "they ruined me" stays ruined, because he has located the steering wheel of his life in someone else's hands. The man who says "I let the fool speak for me" has just taken the wheel back. The whole future opens at the exact moment he stops being a victim and becomes a confessor.

And here is the mercy folded into this. Notice what the jinn do **not** say. They do not grovel. They do not catalogue their shame for ten verses. They name it — *our foolish one used to say outrageous things* — cleanly, honestly, in one breath, and then in the next breath they keep telling the truth. The confession is **owned but not wallowed in.** This is the prophetic balance. Real repentance is not endless self-flagellation, and it is not slick self-exoneration. It is the clean, dignified ownership of "that was ours" — and then walking forward toward the shore.

So what shifts tomorrow? Find the one thing in your past you have been describing in the passive voice. The relationship that "happened to" you. The years you "were led" into. The version of your deen you "were raised" in and blame for everything. Just for one of them, try the harder sentence. Not "they did this to me" but "this was mine; I had a hand in it." Feel how much it costs. And then feel what it returns to you: **the standing to actually change it.** You cannot steer a life you insist you were only a passenger in.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this verse turn out to be one insight approached from two sides.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a machine with three parts: an unanchored voice (*safīh*), producing unmeasured drift (*shaṭaṭ*), sustained across a whole era (*kāna yaqūlu*). Pull the ballast out of judgment and distance from the truth becomes inevitable, and it compounds quietly for as long as no one names it.

The **thematic journey** showed us how that machine is switched off. It is switched off the instant a heart does two things at once: it sees the *safīh* for what he is — not unintelligent, but weightless — **and** it claims him with that unbearable little word, *‑nā*. Ours.

And here is where the two meet. The reason the *kāna yaqūlu* — the "used to" — could finally become past tense, the reason the era could finally end, is *because* the community stopped exiling the fool and started owning him. **The drift stops at the exact moment the drifter admits the boat was his.** You cannot turn a boat you claim you were never steering. The confession in the suffix is what makes the past tense in the verb possible. Owning the lie is what lets you finally speak of it in the past.

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now stand back and see the shape of these seven words, because the order is the argument:

**سَفِيهُنَا** — the source named, and *owned* (our fool)
**عَلَى ٱللَّهِ** — the target named (against Allah — the gravest possible direction)
**شَطَطًا** — the result named (drift to the far shore, without measure)

The community moves from **who** spoke, to **against whom**, to **how far it carried them**. And the whole sentence sits inside *kāna yaqūlu* — the past habitual — like a closed parenthesis around a finished era. The verse is built as a confession that is already, in its very grammar, a leaving-behind.

### Questions to Carry

- Where in my life does a voice speak about Allah with great confidence and no weight — and is that voice out there, or is it in here?
- What conclusion about what Allah "surely allows" have I reached recently that happened to be exactly what I already wanted?
- What in my past am I still describing in the passive voice — "it happened to me" — that I have never once owned with "it was mine"?
- If I am honest, who is *my* foolish one? What have I let speak for me, and called it part of myself?
- What "used to" in my life is ready, finally, to become past tense?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The first thing an awakened heart does is stop outsourcing the blame — it names the weightless voice that spoke about Allah, claims that voice as its own, and by owning the drift to the far shore, finally earns the right to say it in the past tense.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to be a victim of your own past. That is the strange gift hiding in this confession of the jinn. The most freeing sentence available to you is not "they did this to me." It is "this was ours, and I am turning the boat around." Somewhere in you, perhaps, a *safīh* has been speaking for a long time — softly, reasonably, slipping by. You are allowed to name him today. You are allowed to claim him as yours. And in that same breath, you are allowed to leave him in the past tense, and turn your face back toward the shore.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, anchor our judgment in the weight of Your truth, so that no light and weightless voice within us drifts us out toward the far shore. Let us never speak about You except what You have said of Yourself. And where we have already drifted, give us the honesty to say "this was ours" — and the mercy of finding the shore again. Āmīn.
