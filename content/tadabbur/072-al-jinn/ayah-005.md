# The Lie We Never Thought to Doubt

### Surah Al-Jinn, Ayah 5

> وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّآ أَن لَّن تَقُولَ ٱلْإِنسُ وَٱلْجِنُّ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ كَذِبًا
>
> *wa annā ẓanannā an lan taqūla l-insu wa-l-jinnu ʿalā llāhi kadhibā*
>
> **"And we had assumed that humans and jinn would never speak a lie against Allah."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Confession From the Dark

Let me set the scene, because this verse is not Allah speaking *to* us in the ordinary way. This verse is a quotation. Allah is telling His Prophet ﷺ — and through him, telling us — what a group of jinn said *to each other* after they encountered the Quran for the first time.

Picture it. A band of jinn, going about whatever it is they do in the unseen, suddenly catch the sound of recitation. The opening of the surah captures their shock: *innā samiʿnā qurʾānan ʿajabā* — "we have heard an astonishing recitation" (72:1). It guides to right conduct, they say. We believed in it. We will never associate anyone with our Lord.

And then — this verse. After the wonder, comes the reckoning. They turn to one another and they make a confession. Not a confession of a sin they committed actively. A confession of something quieter and more unsettling: **a confession about something they had simply assumed.**

Here is what makes this moment worth sitting with for the better part of an hour. These are not naive creatures. The jinn in the Quran are shrewd. Some of them, by their own admission later in the surah, used to eavesdrop on the heavens (72:8-9). They are not gullible. They are not foolish.

And yet they were deceived for a very long time. They believed that Allah had a consort and a child. They held a corrupted theology. And in this verse, they finally diagnose *how* it happened. They put their finger on the exact mechanism of their own deception.

[PAUSE]

And the diagnosis is devastating — because the thing that deceived them was not a vice. It was something that looks, on its surface, like a virtue.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows: **How does a wise, shrewd, careful mind end up believing something completely false — not through stupidity, but through a kind of decency?** How does goodness itself become the door that deception walks through?

The jinn are about to tell us. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ẓanannā* — The Quiet Word That Carries the Whole Collapse

Let me begin with a small grammar lesson, because the entire verse hinges on a single word, and unless we understand that word precisely, the confession is invisible.

In Arabic, there is a spectrum of knowing. At one end you have *yaqīn* — certainty, the kind of knowing you would stake your life on, knowledge that has no crack in it. At the other end you have pure ignorance. And in the vast middle, there is a word for the territory of *thinking-without-knowing*: **ẓann.**

*Ẓann* is supposition. It is assumption. It is "I figured." It is the conclusion you reach not because you verified it, but because it seemed reasonable, because everyone around you held it, because you had no reason to doubt it. *Ẓann* can be strong or weak — sometimes it leans toward likelihood, sometimes it is barely a guess — but it is always, by definition, **short of certainty.** It is a belief you are holding without having earned it.

We live in *ẓann* constantly. You assume the chair will hold you. You assume the person who said they'd call will call. You assume the ground under the city is solid. Most of these assumptions are harmless and necessary; we could not function without them.

Now look at how the jinn open their confession.

> **وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّآ** — *wa annā ẓanannā* — "And we had **assumed**..."

Not "we knew." Not "we were taught and it was proven." **We *assumed*.**

[PAUSE]

Sit with the honesty of that word for a moment. They are not saying, "We were lied to and it wasn't our fault." They are not saying, "We had compelling evidence." They are reaching back, examining the foundation of a belief they held for who-knows-how-long, and they find — at the very bottom of it — not bedrock. **Sand.** *Ẓann.* An assumption they never tested.

Here is what is remarkable. The corruption in their theology — believing Allah had a consort and child, which is among the gravest falsehoods a creature can hold — did not rest on a sophisticated argument. It rested on a *feeling that something was probably true.* That is all. The whole tower of their false belief was balanced on one untested assumption.

And notice the sound of the word itself: *ẓa-nan-nā.* The doubled *nūn* in the middle makes it murmur, almost soften, like something half-spoken, half-believed. It does not sound like a verb of certainty. It sounds like what it means — a soft, rolling supposition, a thing muttered rather than declared.

This is the first thing the verse reveals: **the gravest errors of a life are rarely built on grand, examined convictions. They are built on quiet assumptions we never thought to question.** The jinn's catastrophe began with a *ẓanannā.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Lan* — Absolute Certainty Resting on Nothing

Now watch what the jinn assumed. And here the verse does something almost paradoxical, something you could easily read past a hundred times.

> ظَنَنَّآ **أَن لَّن تَقُولَ** — *ẓanannā an **lan** taqūla* — "we assumed that they would **never** say..."

Let me teach you the word *lan* (لَّن). In Arabic, there are softer and harder ways to negate the future. You can say something "will not" happen. Or you can reach for *lan* — which is the emphatic, the categorical negation of the future. *Lan* does not mean "probably won't." It means **"never. Not ever. There is no future in which this occurs."** When you want to slam a door on a possibility entirely, you use *lan.* It is the strongest tool the language gives you for saying *no* to what is to come.

Now feel the collision.

The verb is *ẓanannā* — **"we assumed,"** the language of supposition, the soft sand.

But what they assumed is framed with *lan* — **"never,"** the language of absolute, door-slamming certainty.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? **They were *absolutely certain* about something they had only *assumed.*** They held a categorical, no-exceptions conviction — *they would NEVER lie about God* — and underneath that iron certainty there was nothing but a guess.

This is the precise anatomy of self-deception, captured in two adjacent words. The danger was never that they were uncertain. The danger was the opposite: they felt *too* certain. They had taken an assumption and, somewhere along the way, promoted it to an unquestionable law. *Of course no one would lie about God. That's not even a possibility worth considering.* The door was slammed shut — *lan* — on the very possibility that could have saved them.

And here is the trap, the thing I want you to really feel: **the more certain you are that something could never happen, the less you watch for it.** Their *lan* was not protection. Their *lan* was the blindfold. The absolute confidence that "no one would ever lie about God" is exactly what made them defenseless against the people who did.

A belief that you are certain about but never examined is more dangerous than a belief you know to be shaky — because you have stopped guarding it.

### Element Three: *Al-Insu wa-l-Jinn* — Why They Included Themselves

There is a third detail, and it is easy to slide past because it sounds like a simple list.

> أَن لَّن تَقُولَ **ٱلْإِنسُ وَٱلْجِنُّ** — *an lan taqūla **l-insu wa-l-jinnu*** — "that **humans and jinn** would never say..."

The jinn, speaking, name two categories of creature who they assumed would never lie about Allah: humankind — *al-ins* — **and the jinn themselves** — *al-jinn.*

Stay with this. They are not standing outside the picture, pointing at deceitful humans. They fold *their own kind* into the assumption. *We assumed that neither humans nor jinn — not them, and not us — would ever dare lie against God.*

Why does this matter? Because it tells you what the assumption was really *about.* It was not an assumption about a particular group's trustworthiness. It was an assumption about **the sheer gravity of the act itself.** They reasoned, in effect: *lying about God is so enormous, so heavy, so unthinkable a transgression, that surely no created being — of any kind — would actually do it.* The subject seemed too sacred to defile. The crime seemed too great to commit.

[PAUSE]

And that — *that* — is the exact reasoning that left the door open.

Here is the chilling logic, made plain: **they could not imagine the lie precisely because the lie was so serious.** The very sacredness of the subject became the cover for the falsehood. *Nobody would lie about something this important* — and so when someone did, it sailed straight through, unexamined, because it never occurred to them that anyone would commit so grave an act.

Notice the verb tied to all of this — *taqūl,* from the root *q-w-l,* simply "to say, to speak." In the recitation preserved from Yaʿqūb (and reported in classical commentary from al-Jaḥdarī and Ibn Abī Isḥāq), the word is read as *taqawwal* — a more intensive form carrying the sense of **fabricating, concocting, inventing speech deliberately** and pinning it on someone. Al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both preserve this reading. Taken together as a composite, the two readings deepen each other: the jinn assumed no one would so much as *say* a lie about Allah, let alone *manufacture* one and attribute it to Him. The act they could not conceive of was not a careless slip — it was a deliberate forgery against the Divine. And it was happening the whole time.

### The Bridge

So here is what the language has shown us, three words doing the work of an entire confession:

*Ẓanannā* — the whole edifice rested on an untested assumption.
*Lan* — they held that assumption with door-slamming, blindfolding certainty.
*Al-insu wa-l-jinn* — and the assumption was rooted in something that *felt like reverence*: surely no one would defile something this sacred.

Three words. A complete autopsy of how a wise creature gets fooled.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the grammar of a confession — but about how our own minds work. Because the jinn are not telling us a story about jinn. They are handing us a mirror.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: When Decency Becomes the Door

Let me start where you actually live.

Think about how a con artist works. Not the clumsy kind — the good kind. The skilled deceiver does not target your weaknesses. **The skilled deceiver targets your decency.** They lean on the assumption that *you* would never do what *they* are doing. "Surely no one would lie about a charity for sick children." "Surely no one would forge a doctor's credentials." "Surely no one would fake their own father's death for sympathy." And precisely *because* the act is so low, so unthinkable to a decent person, the decent person does not see it coming.

This is the open secret of every great fraud. The fraud does not survive in spite of how outrageous it is. The fraud survives **because** of how outrageous it is. Your mind reasons: *that's too far, nobody would actually do that* — and that reasoning is the very gap the liar slips through.

Now go back to the jinn. *We assumed that humans and jinn would never speak a lie against Allah.* It is the same mechanism, raised to its highest possible power. They applied their own sense of reverence to everyone else. *I would never dare lie about God — so surely no one would.* They projected their decency outward, and that projection became a blindfold.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hard part. We tend to think the lesson is "be more cynical." But that is not the Quranic lesson, and it is not what the jinn conclude. The problem was never that they had *ḥusn al-ẓann* — a good opinion of others. A good opinion of people is, in general, a beautiful thing; the believer is taught to assume the best of others.

The problem was *where they placed it.* They placed their good assumption **at the foundation of their theology.** They let "people are probably honest" stand in for "I have verified what is true about God." They allowed a social instinct — trust — to do the work that only knowledge should do.

And that is the precise error so many of us make. We hold our most important beliefs — about God, about the purpose of our existence, about what is permitted and forbidden — not because we have examined them, but because of *who told us.* "My family always believed this." "Everyone I respect holds this view." "That teacher seemed so sincere; surely he wouldn't misguide me." These are all forms of *ẓanannā.* They may even be forms of decency. But decency is not verification.

The jinn are warning us across the centuries: **be most careful exactly where you feel most certain that no one would deceive you.** The places where you think "this is too sacred for anyone to corrupt" are the places where corruption is most likely to be sitting comfortably, undetected, precisely because you stopped checking.

What does this change about tomorrow? It is this: take one belief — one — that you hold about your faith *only* because someone you trusted told you, and that you have never actually traced back to its source. And trace it. Not out of cynicism toward the one who taught you. Out of the recognition that the jinn confessed too late: trust in a teacher is not the same as standing on the truth. The most important things in your life deserve to rest on *yaqīn,* not on *ẓann.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Living on Borrowed Belief — Until the Quran Arrived

Let me ask you something. How much of what you believe is actually *yours*?

I don't mean believed sincerely. I mean *yours* — examined, owned, arrived at, the kind of conviction that would survive a serious challenge because you have already met the challenge and walked through it. As opposed to belief you are simply *carrying* — handed to you, held faithfully, but never opened. Secondhand. Inherited. Borrowed.

Most of us live on borrowed belief for most of our lives. And there is no shame in that as a starting point — we all begin by trusting those who raised us. But borrowed belief has a hidden fragility. Because you never built it, you do not know its weak points. Because you never tested it, you do not know what it can bear. It works perfectly — right up until the day something hits it that it was never reinforced to withstand.

This is the jinn's entire condition before this surah. They had a theology — Allah has a consort, Allah has a child — and they carried it faithfully, *ẓanannā,* never opening it, never testing it. It was borrowed belief all the way down. And it was wrong all the way down. And the terrifying thing is: nothing in their own reasoning ever flagged the error. They could have carried it forever.

So what changed? What finally cracked it open?

Go back to the start of the surah and the verses around this one. *We heard an astonishing recitation* (72:1). *Until we heard the Quran* — as al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both gloss the moment — they kept believing the liars. **The Quran is what moved them from *ẓann* to *yaqīn.***

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that means. For untold time, their false belief sat untouched because nothing had the *weight* to dislodge it. Then the Quran arrives, and within the space of a recitation, an assumption they had held with door-slamming *lan*-certainty simply **dissolves.** Not because someone argued them out of it. Because they encountered speech of a different order entirely — speech that did not *ask* to be believed but *compelled* recognition. The classical commentators describe their state as *tabayyun* — the truth becoming *clear,* distinct, unmistakable. The fog of *ẓann* burning off in the light of *yaqīn.*

This is what the Quran is *for.* Not merely to add information to what you already assume. To **reach underneath your assumptions and test the foundation.** Every time you open it honestly, it is doing to you what it did to the jinn: it is auditing the sand at the bottom of your beliefs, asking, *is this true, or did you just assume it?*

And here is the part that should unsettle the comfortable among us. The jinn were *fortunate.* The Quran reached them and they responded — *fa-āmannā bih,* "so we believed in it" (72:2). They had the humility, in the moment of exposure, to say *ẓanannā* — "we assumed" — rather than clinging to their certainty. The tragedy is not when an assumption is exposed. The tragedy is when it is exposed and the person *defends the sand anyway,* because admitting "I only assumed" is too humiliating.

So the practical question for tomorrow is sharp: **When you read the Quran, do you let it audit you — or do you only let it confirm you?** Do you come to it with the jinn's openness — ready to discover that something you were sure of was only ever a guess — or do you come holding your *lan,* your door already slammed, reading only to find what you already decided?

The difference between those two postures is the difference between the jinn who were saved and the people whose lie they had been believing.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this reflection are really one insight seen from two sides.

The **language** showed us a structure: an untested assumption (*ẓanannā*) wearing the mask of absolute certainty (*lan*), rooted in something that felt like reverence (*surely no creature would defile what is sacred*).

The **themes** showed us that same structure living inside us: decency becoming the door a deceiver walks through, and borrowed belief sitting unexamined until something with real weight finally tests it.

They are the same truth. The jinn's confession is a diagram of how any mind — yours, mine, a shrewd creature of the unseen — comes to hold something false with total confidence. And the Quran's arrival in this very surah is the demonstration of the only thing strong enough to fix it: not more cleverness, but exposure to the truth, met with the humility to say *"we assumed."*

### Questions to Carry

1. What do I believe about Allah, about my religion, about right and wrong — *only* because someone told me, and have never traced to its source?
2. Where in my life have I slammed the door of certainty (*lan*) on a possibility I never actually examined?
3. Has my respect for someone, or my sense that "they would never deceive me," ever stood in the place where real verification should have been?
4. When I open the Quran, am I letting it *audit* my assumptions — or only letting it *confirm* them?
5. If an assumption of mine were exposed as mere *ẓann* tomorrow, would I have the humility of the jinn to say "I assumed" — or would I defend the sand?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The jinn confess that their good assumption — *surely no creature would dare lie about God* — was the very door through which deception entered, and only the Quran had the weight to turn their untested *ẓann* into clear *yaqīn.*

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to become cynical. The jinn were not punished for thinking well of others. The invitation is gentler and harder than cynicism: **let your most important beliefs rest on something more than a guess.** Find one assumption you have never examined — especially one you are *most* sure about, especially one that feels too sacred to question — and bring it to the Quran. Let it be audited. And if it turns out you had only ever assumed, have the courage the jinn had, and say so.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who turned the assumption of the jinn into certainty by Your speech — reach beneath the beliefs we have never examined and show us the sand from the bedrock. Save us from the false confidence that stops us looking, and grant us the humility to say *"we only assumed"* before it is too late to say it. Let Your Book audit us, and let us love the truth more than we love being right.

*Āmīn.*
