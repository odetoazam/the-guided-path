# One Letter Between the Just and the Lost

## INTRODUCTION

Let's begin by hearing them.

> **وَأَنَّا مِنَّا ٱلْمُسْلِمُونَ وَمِنَّا ٱلْقَـٰسِطُونَ ۖ فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا۟ رَشَدًا • وَأَمَّا ٱلْقَـٰسِطُونَ فَكَانُوا۟ لِجَهَنَّمَ حَطَبًا**

> *Wa annā minnā l-muslimūna wa minnā l-qāsiṭūn. Fa-man aslama fa-ulā'ika taḥarraw rashadā. Wa ammā l-qāsiṭūna fa-kānū li-jahannama ḥaṭabā.*

> "And among us are those who have submitted, and among us are the deviators. So whoever submitted — those have sought out right guidance. But as for the deviators, they have become firewood for Hell."

I want you to notice, before anything else, **who is speaking.**

This is not Allah describing the jinn. This is not the Prophet ﷺ reporting on them from the outside. For fifteen verses now, Surah Al-Jinn has been handing us the microphone of a creature we cannot see. A company of the jinn passed by, overheard the Qur'an being recited, and something cracked open in them. *"Indeed, we have heard a wondrous recitation"* (72:1). And they went back to their own people — and the entire opening of this surah is **their report.** Their theology rearranging itself in real time. *We used to think no one would ever lie about Allah. We used to take shelter with certain spirits and they only increased us in burden. We used to sit in the heavens to listen — and now we find it guarded.*

And here, at verse fourteen, they reach their conclusion. They have heard the truth. And now they do the most honest thing a community can do.

**They sort themselves.**

Not "the humans split into two groups." Not "out there, somewhere, are believers and disbelievers." They say *minnā… minnā* — **among us, and among us.** The line does not run between us and them. The line runs straight through our own community. Some of us submitted. Some of us deviated.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook I want you to carry into the next hour. There is a word in this verse — *al-qāsiṭūn*, the deviators — and it is built from three Arabic letters. And those exact three letters, with a single tiny addition, spell **its own opposite.** The same root that gives us the word for the one who twists away from justice gives us the word for the one who is perfectly just. One breath of a letter stands between them.

The jinn are telling us that the difference between being saved and being firewood is not a vast canyon. It is, linguistically, almost nothing at all. And we need to understand why that is the most frightening and the most hopeful thing in these two verses.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *al-Qāsiṭūn* — The Word That Contains Its Own Enemy

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about Arabic that has no real equivalent in English.

In English, when we want to reverse a word, we usually bolt something onto the front. *Just* becomes *unjust*. *Honest* becomes *dishonest*. The opposite is the original word plus a "not." The negation is visible, external, tacked on.

Arabic can do something stranger and more beautiful. Arabic builds words by pouring a three-letter root into a mold — a *form* — and the **mold itself** can change the meaning. Sometimes the very same root, poured into two slightly different molds, comes out meaning two **opposite** things. Arabic grammarians have a name for a word that holds two contrary meanings inside it: *aḍdād*. A word at war with itself.

Hold that idea. Now look at the word.

> **ٱلْقَـٰسِطُونَ** — *al-qāsiṭūn*

The root is **ق-س-ط**, *q-s-ṭ*. And here is the thing you must sit with.

From this exact root comes **qisṭ** — and *qisṭ* is one of the great words of the Qur'an for **justice, balance, the perfectly even scale.** *"Wa aqīmū l-wazna bi-l-qisṭ"* — establish the weighing with *qisṭ*, with perfect equity (55:9). When Allah praises those who are just, He calls them **al-muqsiṭūn**: *"Indeed Allah loves al-muqsiṭīn"* (49:9), the ones who deal in *qisṭ*.

So how can *al-qāsiṭūn* — from the very same letters — mean the **deviators, the unjust, those bound for Hell?**

Let's sit with this, because the answer is exact, and al-Qurtubi lays it out for us with a grammarian's precision. He writes: *al-qāsiṭ* is the one who **swerves away** from the truth — *jā'ir* — because **he deviates *from* what is right.** *Al-muqsiṭ* is the just one, **because he deviates *toward* what is right.** Then he gives us the rule plainly: *qasaṭa* (the simple Form I verb) means **to deviate, to be unjust.** *Aqsaṭa* (Form IV, with one added letter — the hamza, the little alif of causation) means **to act justly.**

Do you feel what just happened?

[PAUSE]

The root **ق-س-ط** by itself means something like *to incline, to lean off the straight.* And the entire moral universe turns on **which way you lean** and **whether you add that one letter.**

- *Qāsiṭ* — the active participle of the bare verb — is the one who leans away from the scale. He had the balance in front of him and he tipped it.
- *Muqsiṭ* — the active participle with that single added hamza — is the one who leans **into** the scale. He took the inclination of his soul and bent it back toward true.

**One letter.** One small breath of a hamza is the difference between the man Allah loves and the man who becomes kindling. The same raw material — the same inclining heart — and everything depends on the direction of the lean.

This is why I said the line runs *through* us, not around us. The qāsiṭ is not a different species from the muqsiṭ. He is not made of darker clay. He is the muqsiṭ who leaned the wrong way. The capacity for justice and the capacity for deviation **share a root.** They are the same energy of the soul, the same restless inclination — pointed in opposite directions.

And now hear what al-Baghawi adds, reporting from Ibn ʿAbbās — and let me be precise, this is an *athar*, a saying of the Companion, not a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ. Ibn ʿAbbās said the *qāsiṭūn* here are *"those who set up a rival to Allah"* — *alladhīna jaʿalū lillāhi nidda.* Look at what shirk is, in the light of the root. Shirk is the ultimate *deviation of the scale.* It takes the worship that belongs entirely on one side of the balance — Allah's side — and tips a portion of it onto something else. The idolater is the supreme *qāsiṭ* because he has un-balanced the most important scale there is. He had *tawḥīd* — the perfect equity of giving the Only One His due — and he leaned.

So when the jinn say *"among us are al-qāsiṭūn,"* they are not pointing at cartoon villains. They are confessing: among us are those who took the same inclining heart you all have — and tipped it away from the One.

[PAUSE — let it land]

### Element Two: *Taḥarraw* — Guidance Is Something You Hunt

Now watch what the jinn say about the *other* group. And here a second word is going to do something I think will surprise you.

> **فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا۟ رَشَدًا**
> "So whoever submitted — those have **sought out** right guidance."

Let me first teach the structure, because the form of this verb is carrying the whole weight of it.

In Arabic, the simplest verb form — Form I — is just *the bare action*. To do the thing. But Arabic has higher forms that **intensify** and add the dimension of *effort* and *deliberate self-involvement.* Form V — the pattern *tafaʿʿala* — takes a root, doubles its middle and folds the action **back onto the doer with strain and repetition.** It is the form of *trying hard at something, over and over, with your whole self bent toward it.* Think of how *ʿalima* means "he knew," but *taʿallama* — Form V — means "he **studied**," he labored at knowing, he worked for it across time.

Now the word:

> **تَحَرَّوْا** — *taḥarraw*

This is Form V, from the root **ح-ر-ي**. And *taḥarrā* means: to **aim**, to **seek diligently**, to **pursue something painstakingly until you home in on it.** Al-Qurtubi gives us the most beautiful illustration. He says it is the very word used for *taḥarrī al-qibla* — **the act of carefully working out the direction of prayer** when you cannot see the Kaʿba. Picture the traveler at night, with no certainty of which way Makkah lies. He studies the stars. He weighs the signs. He turns, and adjusts, and turns again, straining toward a direction he cannot see — until he is as sure as a seeking heart can be. **That** is *taḥarrā.*

Stay with this, because it overturns something we quietly believe.

We tend to imagine guidance as a thing that **happens to** people. A lightning bolt. A passive reception — you were sitting there and suddenly *poof*, you were guided. But look at how the jinn describe the saved ones. They do not say "those upon whom guidance fell." They say *taḥarraw rashadan* — **they hunted for rightness. They aimed. They strained toward a direction they could not yet see.**

And here is what's remarkable about the grammar of the whole sentence. Read the order again: *fa-man aslama fa-ulā'ika taḥarraw rashadan* — "whoever **submitted**, *those* **sought out** right guidance." Submission is named first; the seeking is named second. As if the submission came before the search. That seems backwards — don't you search *first* and submit *after*?

Al-Muyassar resolves it exactly, and notice the chain: *"whoever submitted to Allah in obedience — those are the ones who **aimed for the path of truth, and strove to choose it**, so Allah guided them to it."* The submission and the seeking are not two events in sequence. **They are the same act seen from two sides.** Your submission *is* your seeking made visible. The outward act of *aslama* is the inward *taḥarrā* taking a body. You don't seek and then submit; **your submitting is what seeking looks like from the outside.**

[PAUSE]

So now lay the two words side by side and feel the symmetry the jinn have built.

The *qāsiṭ* **leaned away** — *qasaṭa*, a verb of swerving off course.
The *muslim* **strained toward** — *taḥarrā*, a verb of homing in on course.

Both are in motion. Neither is standing still. **Guidance is not a place you stand; it is a direction you lean.** One soul tipped away from the scale. One soul turned, like a traveler reading the stars, and aimed itself at a rightness it could not yet fully see — and Allah closed the distance.

### Element Three: *Rashadan* and *Ḥaṭaban* — The Two Destinies That Rhyme

Now I want to show you something that only appears when you let both verses sound together — something in the very *music* of the ending.

Look at how verse 14 closes, and how verse 15 closes:

> *…taḥarraw **rashadā.*** (14)
> *…fa-kānū li-jahannama **ḥaṭabā.*** (15)

> **رَشَدًا** … **حَطَبًا**

Say them out loud. *Rashadā… ḥaṭabā.* Hear it? Both words are **indefinite** — not *the* guidance, not *the* firewood, but *a* rightness, *some* kindling. Both are in the same accusative case. Both land on that same open, falling *-aā* with the *tanwīn*. **The two destinies rhyme.**

This is not decoration. The jinn have given the two ends of the human and jinn story a matching cadence, so that you cannot hear one without the echo of the other. Two roads. Two final words. The same length, the same shape, the same musical fall — and at the bottom of one is light, and at the bottom of the other is fire.

Now look at the two destination-words themselves, because the *contrast* in their meaning is total.

**رَشَدًا — *rashadan*.** From the root **ر-ش-د**, which means *right-guidance, soundness, maturity, the clear-eyed knowing of the right course.* It is the word of arrival, of a mind and heart that have come into their full, true direction. It is **alive.** It grows. *Rushd* is what an orphan reaches when he becomes mature enough to be trusted with his wealth (4:6) — it is ripeness, fullness, the thing coming into what it was meant to be.

And against it:

**حَطَبًا — *ḥaṭaban*.** Firewood.

Let's sit with this word, because the jinn could have chosen others. They did not say the deviators became *waqūd* (fuel, the more general word used elsewhere, 2:24, 66:6). They said **ḥaṭab** — *firewood.* Cut wood. Gathered sticks. And think about what firewood actually **is.**

[PAUSE]

Firewood is a tree that has been **cut off from its root.** A living tree is rooted, drinking, green — it gives shade, it gives fruit, it shelters birds, it breathes out the air we breathe in. **Firewood is that same tree after it has been severed from its source, dried out, stripped of every living thing it once did, and gathered into a pile for one purpose only: to be consumed.**

Do you see the stepping-stone Allah has laid down? *Qasaṭa* — the deviator's whole crime — means **to swerve away.** Away from what? Away from the source. Away from the path. Away from the One. And what happens to a branch that swerves away from its root, that cuts itself off from the trunk? **It dries. It dies. It becomes wood good for nothing but burning.**

The deviation *is* the drying out. The *qāsiṭ* did not commit one sin called "leaning away" and then receive a separate, unrelated punishment called "firewood." **The leaning away and the becoming-firewood are the same process.** To cut yourself off from the Source of life is, by that very act, to begin to become kindling. The fire in verse 15 is not an arbitrary sentence bolted onto the crime. It is the crime, **grown to its full and visible form.** A branch severed from the tree does not need to be condemned to dryness. The severing *was* the dryness.

And notice one more thing — the cruelest dignity stripped away. The firewood is not even the *fire's victim*. It is the fire's **raw material.** The *qāsiṭūn* do not merely *go into* Hell; verse 15 says *li-jahannama ḥaṭaban* — they become firewood **for** Hell, the very stuff that keeps it burning. Anonymous. Indefinite. A heap of dried sticks that has lost its name, lost its shade, lost its fruit, lost everything that made it a living thing — reduced to the lowest possible function in the universe: to feed a flame.

[PAUSE]

One letter separated *qāsiṭ* from *muqsiṭ*. And here, at the end, are the two harvests of that one letter. **Rashad** — ripeness, arrival, the soul come into its full green life. **Ḥaṭab** — the soul dried to a stick, severed from the root, burning. The two words even rhyme, so you can never forget that they grew from the same soil.

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things, and they are really one thing.

The *qāsiṭ* and the *muqsiṭ* share a root — the same inclining heart, leaning two ways. The *muslim* didn't passively receive guidance; he *taḥarrā*, he hunted it, aiming through the dark like a man finding the qibla by the stars. And the two destinations — *rashad* and *ḥaṭab* — rhyme like two branches of one tree, one ripening, one drying to firewood.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of a human life. About *your* life. Because you are, right now, a heart that is leaning **some** direction.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Line Runs Through *Us* — The Courage of Self-Categorization

Let me start where you already live.

There is a move the human mind makes so reflexively that we barely notice it. When something goes wrong in the world — when there is cruelty, or corruption, or violence done in the name of God — we instinctively draw a circle. And we draw it so that *we* are inside it and *they* are outside it. "There are good people and bad people." "There's our community, and then there's *those* people who give us a bad name." The bad actors are always **out there.** A separate kind. Not us.

It feels like clarity. It is actually the most comfortable lie we tell ourselves. Because if evil is a *kind of person* — a category I am safely outside of — then I never have to check my own scale. The danger is always somebody else's problem.

Now listen again to how the jinn speak.

> *Wa annā **minnā** l-muslimūna **wa minnā** l-qāsiṭūn.*
> "And among **us** are the submitters, and among **us** are the deviators."

They had every opportunity to externalize. They are reporting back to their own people after a world-altering encounter. They could have said, "Out there among the wicked jinn are deviators, but **we** — we who heard the Qur'an — we are the good ones." Instead, twice, they say **minnā. minnā.** *From among us. From among us.* The deviators are **not a separate tribe.** They are sitting in the same gathering. They heard the same recitation. They are *kin.* The line that divides the saved from the firewood does not run around the community — **it runs straight through the middle of it.**

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most spiritually mature sentences any community has ever spoken about itself. And it is the exact opposite of how we usually protect our egos.

Think about what it would mean to internalize this. It would mean that when you hear of evil done by people who share your faith, your name, your skin, your nation — your first instinct is not "they are not really one of us." Your first instinct is the jinn's instinct: *minnā.* The capacity that produced that deviation is a capacity that shares a root with the capacity in me. The *qāsiṭ* and the *muqsiṭ* are the same clay leaning two ways — and I am that clay too.

And here is why this is not despair but the beginning of safety. **You cannot guard a border you refuse to admit exists.** As long as the deviator is "out there," I will never inspect the lean of my own heart, because I have already filed myself under "submitter" by default. The jinn refused that default. They said: among the very ones who heard the truth, some still tipped the wrong way. *Hearing is not the same as submitting.* Sitting in the gathering is not the same as aiming for the qibla. Some of us were in the room and still leaned away.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It means the most important sorting in the universe is not a sorting you do to others. It is a sorting you must, with terror and honesty, perform on **yourself.** Tonight, ask the jinn's question of your own soul — not *who are the deviators*, but *which way am I leaning?* On the scale of this one relationship, this one habit, this one place where I give to other-than-Allah what belongs to Allah alone — **am I a muqsiṭ or a qāsiṭ?** The line runs through *me.* The honest soul stops drawing circles around its enemies and starts checking the tilt of its own balance.

### Theme Two: You Do Not Wait for Guidance — You Hunt It

Now let me take you to a different place you already live: the experience of waiting.

So many people are *waiting* to be guided. Waiting to "feel" faith. Waiting for the moment when belief will descend on them fully-formed, when prayer will suddenly feel sweet, when doubt will lift on its own like fog burning off. They treat *hidāyah* — guidance — like weather. Something that arrives, or doesn't, on its own schedule. And while they wait, they do not move. *"When I feel it, I'll act. When my heart is ready, I'll change."*

The jinn's word for the saved ones demolishes this.

*Taḥarraw rashadan.* **They sought out right guidance.** They did not wait for it. They *hunted* it. Remember the image al-Qurtubi gave us — *taḥarrī al-qibla* — the traveler in the dark, who does not sit down and wait for the Kaʿba to announce its direction. He **strains.** He reads what signs he has. He turns himself, adjusts, turns again, aiming his whole body at a certainty he cannot yet see. The guidance was not a feeling that fell on him. It was a **direction he leaned into with effort** — and then, al-Muyassar tells us, *fa-hadāhumullāhu ilayhi* — **so Allah guided them to it.**

Here is the whole architecture of guidance in one verse, and it is the most hopeful thing I know. **Guidance is the meeting of two motions.** A servant who *aims* — *taḥarrā* — and a God who *closes the distance* — *hadā.* The servant cannot complete the journey by himself; he is in the dark, he cannot see the Kaʿba, his aiming alone would never get him there. But Allah does not guide the one who refuses to turn. The aiming is the servant's part. The arriving is Allah's gift. **You provide the lean. He provides the light.**

[PAUSE]

And notice the staggering generosity hidden in this. The verse does not say "those who *attained* perfect guidance." It says those who *sought* it — *taḥarraw.* The reward is attached to the **seeking**, not to the flawless arrival. The traveler who turns toward the qibla with sincere effort and gets the direction slightly wrong is, in the eyes of the Law, in a wholly different category from the one who never turned at all. **Allah honors the aim.** He meets the heart that leans toward Him, even when that heart is fumbling in the dark, even when it cannot yet see what it is reaching for.

This is the answer to everyone who has ever said *"I don't feel faithful enough, so I'm waiting until I do."* You have it backwards. You do not wait for the feeling and then aim. **You aim, and the feeling — the rashad, the ripeness, the green life of guidance — is what Allah grows in the soul that aimed.** The submission comes first in the verse precisely because the *act* of turning is what you owe; the *arrival* is what He gives.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means guidance has a doorway, and the doorway is an action you can take **right now, in the dark, without waiting to feel ready.** Pick the one direction you have been waiting to "feel" ready to turn toward — the prayer you keep postponing, the wrong you keep meaning to set down, the relationship with the Book you keep planning to begin. Do not wait for the sun. **Turn toward the qibla in the dark.** Lean. Aim. Make the move that says *I am seeking,* however unsteady — and trust the promise buried in this verse: the One you are aiming at will close the distance you cannot cross alone.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how these two verses are built. Because the jinn have constructed a perfect, delayed symmetry — and the delay itself is a sermon.

Watch the order:

- Verse 14 opens by naming **both** groups together: *al-muslimūn* **and** *al-qāsiṭūn.* They stand side by side, kin, in the same gathering.
- Then verse 14 gives us the fate of **only the first group**: *fa-man aslama fa-ulā'ika taḥarraw rashadā.* The submitters seek, and find ripeness.
- And the deviators? Verse 14 says **nothing about their end.** Their fate is left hanging. Suspended. The sentence simply… moves on, as if there is still time, as if the door has not yet closed.
- Then — only in verse 15, after a pause, after a delay — the other shoe drops: *wa ammā l-qāsiṭūna fa-kānū li-jahannama ḥaṭabā.*

That **delay** is mercy made into grammar. The submitters' reward comes immediately, joined right to their submission, as if Allah cannot wait to record it. The deviators' sentence is **held back**, given its own separate verse, pronounced last — as though even the syntax is reluctant, as though the space between verse 14 and verse 15 is one more moment in which the lean could still be corrected.

And notice the chosen tense of the final blow: *fa-**kānū*** — "they **were** / they **have become** firewood." A *past* tense, for a fate that lies in the future. The jinn speak of the Fire as though it has **already happened** — because for the one who dies leaning the wrong way, it is as good as done. The certainty is so total that the future is reported in the past.

### Bringing It Together

The language and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar told us the *qāsiṭ* and the *muqsiṭ* share one root — and the theme told us the line runs through *us*, not around us, because the deviator and the submitter are the same clay leaning two ways.

The grammar told us *taḥarraw* is a verb of straining, hunting, aiming through the dark — and the theme told us guidance is not weather you wait for but a direction you lean into, met halfway by a God who closes the distance.

And the two destinies *rhyme* — *rashadā, ḥaṭabā* — because they grew from the same soil. One soul leaned toward the Source and ripened into green, living guidance. One soul leaned away from the Source, dried out, and became firewood. **The deviation and the drying were the same act.** The fire was never a sentence imposed from outside; it was the swerving-away, grown to its full and visible form.

One letter. One lean. That is all that ever stood between the just and the lost.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I hear of evil done by those who share my faith or my name, is my first instinct *"they are not one of us"* — or the jinn's instinct, *minnā,* *that capacity shares a root with something in me?*
2. On the scales I actually live by — my time, my love, my fear, my hope — where am I a *muqsiṭ*, leaning toward the One, and where am I quietly a *qāsiṭ*, tipping the weight onto other-than-Him?
3. Am I *waiting* to feel guided — or am I *aiming*, turning toward the qibla in the dark before the sun comes up?
4. Is there a branch of my life that has cut itself off from the Source and begun, quietly, to dry?
5. If my fate were reported tonight in the past tense — *fa-kāna* — which of the two rhyming words would close my verse?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The same inclining heart becomes either ripe guidance or dry firewood depending on a single lean — toward the Source, or away from it — and the saved are not those upon whom guidance fell, but those who hunted it in the dark.

### The Closing Invitation

You are, at this moment, a heart that is leaning. That is simply what it is to be a soul — you are never neutral, never standing perfectly still. The jinn, who saw their own community split clean down the middle, are not asking you to feel guilty. They are inviting you to do the one thing that actually changes a destiny: to stop sorting the world into us and them, to turn the scale around and look honestly at your own tilt — and then, gently, deliberately, like a traveler finding the qibla by the stars, to lean back toward the One. You provide the aim. He has promised the light.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You who love *al-muqsiṭīn* — straighten the lean of our hearts. Where we have swerved, turn us back to the Source before we dry; where we are aiming through the dark, close the distance we cannot cross alone. Make us of those who *sought*, and let the seeking be enough in Your sight to grow in us the green, living *rashad* — and never, ever let us become firewood for a fire we were warned about by creatures we could not even see. Āmīn.
