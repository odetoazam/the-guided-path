# "I Do Not Know" — The Sentence Allah Put in the Mouth of the Man Who Knew the Most

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah three times, so it settles in three different places — in the ear, on the tongue, and in the mind.

In Arabic:

> **قُلْ إِنْ أَدْرِىٓ أَقَرِيبٌ مَّا تُوعَدُونَ أَمْ يَجْعَلُ لَهُۥ رَبِّىٓ أَمَدًا**

In transliteration:

> *Qul in adrī a-qarībun mā tūʿadūna am yajʿalu lahu rabbī amadā.*

And in plain English:

> **Say: "I do not know whether what you are promised is near, or whether my Lord will appoint for it a distant term."**

Now I want you to do something before we go a single step further. I want you to feel the strangeness of this verse — because if it doesn't feel strange to you yet, it means we've grown numb to something astonishing.

### Rebuild the world

Picture Makkah. The message has been preached for years now. And one of the loudest, most repeated taunts thrown at the Prophet ﷺ is a question disguised as a challenge: *When?* When is this punishment you keep warning us about? When is this Hour, this resurrection, this reckoning? You say it's coming — so name the day. Put a date on it. Show us.

It is the oldest trick of the scoffer. You don't refute a warning; you just ask it to be specific, and when it won't, you laugh. *He can't even tell us when. How real can it be?*

The verse right before this one — verse 24 — has just answered them with a quiet menace: *"Until, when they see what they are promised, then they will know who is weaker in helpers and fewer in number."* In other words: keep counting your numbers, keep feeling strong. One day you will see it with your own eyes, and on that day the math will reverse.

So the deniers have been told *that* it is coming. The threat is firm. And now — at the very moment when you would expect the warner to press his advantage, to add weight, to project authority — comes this verse.

And what is the Prophet ﷺ commanded to say?

Not "It is near." Not "It is far." Not "Wait and see." He is commanded to say: **"I do not know."**

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to sit with as we begin. This is the man who was taken by night through the seven heavens. This is the man to whom Jibrīl descended with revelation, who saw Paradise and the Fire, who was given knowledge that no human being before or after has been given. And of all the things he could be made to say to people demanding answers — Allah chooses, for him, to put on his tongue the words: **I do not know.**

Why would Allah make His most beloved, His most knowledgeable creation, stand before mockers and confess ignorance? Why is "I don't know" not a weakness here but the single most precise, most powerful thing he could possibly say?

And there is a second mystery buried in the grammar that most of us read straight past. When Allah offers the two possibilities — near, or far — He does not say them the way you and I would. The asymmetry between the two halves of this verse is going to tell us something about who actually owns time.

Let's earn it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: قُلْ — The Command to Confess Not-Knowing

Let me start with the very first word, because we skip it every time. **قُلْ** — *Qul.* "Say."

Now, before we touch what comes after it, let's understand what this little word *is* and what it does — as a general feature of language, before we apply it here.

When someone hands you a script, two things are true at once. The first is obvious: the words coming out of your mouth are not your own — they were given to you. But the second is subtler and more powerful: by speaking them, you make them yours. The script becomes your voice. You stand behind it.

That double reality is exactly what قُلْ does in the Quran. It appears more than three hundred times. And every single time, it carries this doubleness: *these are not your words, Muhammad — and yet you are the one who must say them.* The content descends from above; the voice that delivers it is the Prophet's own.

So now look at what he is being commanded to voice.

> **قُلْ إِنْ أَدْرِىٓ**
> *Say: "I do not know."*

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with the sheer weight of this for a moment. Allah — who knows the hour, the second, the exact appointed moment of every promised thing — is dictating a script. And in that divinely authored script, the line He writes for His Prophet is a confession of ignorance.

Notice something. Allah could have left the Prophet ﷺ to simply *not answer* the mockers. Silence would have been easy. But silence can look like evasion. Silence lets the scoffer say, *See, he has no reply.* Instead, Allah does something far more deliberate. He turns the not-knowing into a *spoken declaration.* He makes the ignorance public, owned, deliberate, and commanded.

This is the opposite of how power normally behaves. The powerful man, cornered with a question he cannot answer, bluffs. He projects. He says something vague and authoritative to keep the room. The last thing a leader wants to do in front of his detractors is say "I don't know" — because we have been trained to believe that not-knowing is the same as not-having-authority.

And here is the heart of it. The Prophet's authority does not come from knowing the date. **His authority comes from his relationship with the One who set the date.** So he loses nothing by confessing he doesn't know the hour — because his job was never to be the keeper of the timetable. His job was to be the truthful relayer of the message. And a truthful relayer who says "I know" when he doesn't would destroy the very thing — *truthfulness* — that makes him worth listening to.

Do you see the inversion? The "I don't know" is not a crack in his credibility. It *is* his credibility. It is the proof that everything else he reports, he reports because he truly received it — because here, where he did *not* receive the knowledge, he says so plainly.

[PAUSE]

What this single command reveals: the most honest man who ever lived was made to model, for all of us, that confessing the limits of your knowledge is not the failure of your authority — when your authority rests on truthfulness, it is the *guarantee* of it.

### Element Two: إِنْ أَدْرِى — The Denial Placed at the Front

Now look closely at the two words that form the confession itself: **إِنْ أَدْرِى** — *in adrī.*

In ordinary Arabic, if you wanted to say "I do not know," the natural words would be *mā adrī* (مَا أَدْرِي) or *lā adrī* (لَا أَدْرِي). But here the verse uses a different particle: **إِنْ** — *in*.

The classical commentators are unanimous on what it means. Al-Jalālayn glosses it directly: *in* here means *mā* — "not." Al-Baghawī says the same: *"in adrī" — meaning "mā adrī," I do not know.* Al-Qurtubī confirms it: *in* is functioning here in the sense of *mā* or *lā*, the negation. So the meaning is settled and certain: **"I do not know."**

But linguists notice something about the *flavor* of this negating *in*. It is the lighter, more emphatic, more categorical particle of denial — the one that drops the negation crisply onto the verb. There is a reason poets and the Quran reach for it: it has a finality to it, a clean shutting of the door.

And here is what I want you to notice about the *order*. The negation comes first. *In* — "not" — lands before *adrī* — "I know." The denial is fronted. The very first thing established, before the knowing is even mentioned, is its absence.

Stay with me, because this matters. The structure of the sentence enacts its meaning. The not-knowing isn't tacked on at the end as an afterthought, a mumbled "...I guess I'm not sure." It is placed *at the head* of the confession. The ignorance is declared boldly, up front, with the crisp particle of denial — not whispered, but announced.

[PAUSE]

What this reveals: there is a way of saying "I don't know" that is small and embarrassed, and there is a way that is upright and clean. The grammar of this verse models the second. The believer's "I do not know" is not a stammer. It is a *stance.*

### Element Three: أَقَرِيبٌ ... أَمْ يَجْعَلُ — The Asymmetry of the Two Possibilities

Now we come to the feature that I promised you at the start — the one hidden in plain sight. Let me first teach the structure as a general thing, and then we'll watch it do its work.

In Arabic there is a construction for posing a genuine either/or question: you open with the questioning *hamza* — the little *a-* sound — and then later you place **أَمْ** — *am* — which means "or." *A...am...* — "Is it this... or is it that?" It frames two alternatives and holds them open. English does the same with "whether... or."

So you would expect the two alternatives in our verse to be perfectly parallel. Symmetrical. Balanced. Like: *whether it is near, or whether it is far.* Two adjectives. Two matching halves of a scale.

Now read what Allah actually wrote:

> **أَقَرِيبٌ مَّا تُوعَدُونَ أَمْ يَجْعَلُ لَهُۥ رَبِّىٓ أَمَدًا**
> *whether near is what you are promised, or [whether] my Lord will appoint for it a term.*

Look at the two halves. They are **not** parallel.

The first half is a simple description: *qarīb* — "near." A quiet adjective. A state. It just sits there: *Is it near?*

But the second half is not an adjective at all. It is a whole action with a named actor. It does not say *am baʿīd* — "or far." It says: **"or my Lord will appoint for it a term."**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel that? The two sides of this scale are made of different material. One side is a flat description — *near*. The other side is a *sentence with God in it* — *my Lord will appoint a term.*

Let's sit with why. Because this asymmetry is doing enormous theological work in total silence.

If Allah had written "near or far," He would have presented the timing of the reckoning as a fact floating out in the world — a date sitting somewhere on a calendar, near one end or far the other, an impersonal *when*. As if time were a road and the only question is whether the destination is close or distant.

But that is not how this verse sees time. Watch the second half again. The "far" possibility is not described as a *distance*; it is described as **an act of the Lord**: *"yajʿalu lahu rabbī"* — *my Lord will make / appoint for it* a term. The verb is *yajʿal*, from the root that means to *make, set, place, ordain*. Far-ness, here, is not a property of the event. It is a **decision of the Rabb.**

So the verse quietly refuses the symmetry our minds expect. It will not let "far" be a neutral fact on a map. It insists that any extension of time, any delay, any stretching out of the term — is not the universe drifting; it is **my Lord, actively, deliberately, appointing.**

And notice *whose* Lord. **رَبِّى** — *rabbī*, "**my** Lord." Not "the Lord." Not "God." The possessive. The One who has nurtured me, raised me, sustained me, brought me to this moment — *He* is the one who sets the term. The confession of not-knowing is wrapped, in its very last breath, inside an affirmation of intimate trust: *I don't know the time, but I know the One who keeps the time, and He is mine, and I am His.*

[PAUSE]

Let me show you the full shape of it now. The verse moves like this:

- *I do not know* — the human limit.
- *Is it near* — possibility one, a bare fact.
- *or will my Lord appoint a term* — possibility two, but now it is no longer a fact; it is an act of God.

The structure carries you from human ignorance, through an open question, and lands you — not on an answer — but on **the Lord who holds the answer.** The verse doesn't resolve the *when*. It resolves the *Who*.

What this asymmetry reveals: the timing of the reckoning was never a thing out in the world for us to locate on a map. It is a sealed decision held in the hand of "my Lord." And so the right response to not knowing the *when* is not to keep hunting for the *when* — it is to turn toward the *Whom*.

### Bridge

So let's name what the grammar has shown us. The command *Qul* taught us that confessing the limit of knowledge can be the very proof of truthfulness. The fronted *in adrī* taught us that "I don't know" can be spoken with an upright spine, not a bowed head. And the broken symmetry of *near... or my Lord will appoint a term* taught us that delay is not drift — it is decision; that behind the hidden *when* stands a known and trusted *Who*.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about the strange, anxious relationship every human being has with not knowing what is coming.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Dignity of Not-Knowing in a World That Cannot Bear It

Let me begin where you actually live.

We live in the age of the forecast. Think about how much of modern life is built on the desperate attempt to *know what's coming.* The weather forecast. The stock forecast. The election prediction. The "people who bought this also bought." The algorithm that tells you what you'll want next. We have built entire industries — fortunes — on the single human craving to *not be surprised.* To see the future before it arrives. To close the gap of uncertainty.

And underneath all of it is a quiet, constant anxiety. Have you felt it? That low hum of *not knowing* — about your health, your livelihood, your children, the thing the doctor said he'd call back about. The mind cannot stand an open question. It circles it. It refreshes the page. It runs the scenarios at two in the morning. We treat *not knowing* as a wound that has to be closed, and we will accept almost any false certainty over the discomfort of leaving it open.

Now bring that anxious, forecast-addicted heart back to the verse. And watch what Allah does to it.

He takes the most knowledgeable human being who ever lived — the one person who would have every excuse to claim certainty — and He has him say, calmly, in public, to people *demanding* a forecast: **"I do not know."**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what this does? It takes the thing we are most ashamed of — not knowing — and it places it on the noblest tongue in creation, as a *commanded* act of worship. Not-knowing, here, is not the anxious wound we treat it as. It is dignified. It is upright. It is *obedience.*

There is a profound freedom hidden in this, and I want you to taste it. The mockers wanted to trap the Prophet ﷺ between two bad options: either *claim* knowledge of the timing (and risk being wrong, exposed as false) or *admit* he doesn't know (and look weak). It's the same trap our own egos set for us a hundred times a week — the trap of feeling that we must have an answer, must seem certain, must never be caught not-knowing.

And the verse breaks the trap entirely. Because it reveals a third option the mockers never imagined: **to not know, and to be completely at peace.** To say "I don't know" not as a defeated mumble but as a free man's truth — because his security was never located in *knowing the future.* It was located in *trusting the One who holds it.*

Stay with this, because it's the turn. The reason we cannot bear not-knowing is that we are secretly trying to be our own lords of time. We want to hold the calendar ourselves. We refresh, we forecast, we predict, because deep down we do not trust that the future is in safe hands — so we try to grab it into our own. And the verse gently pries our fingers open. It says: *the term belongs to my Lord. He appoints it. You were never meant to carry it.*

The anxiety of not-knowing is, at root, a crisis of trust. And the cure is not more information. **The cure is a better Lord.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical application, and it is concrete. The next time your mind seizes on an open question it cannot close — the diagnosis, the decision, the outcome you cannot see — try saying, out loud if you can, the Prophet's own commanded words: *I do not know.* And then finish the verse the way the verse finishes itself — not on the not-knowing, but on the Lord: *I do not know... but my Lord appoints the term.* Let the sentence carry you, as it carried the Prophet ﷺ, from the open question to the trusted Hand. You are not required to close every gap. You are required to trust the One who holds the gaps.

### Theme Two: The Mercy of the Hidden Deadline

Now let's go to the second theme, and again, let me begin where you live.

Imagine you were told the exact date and hour of your death. Stamped, certain, on the calendar. Some part of you thinks you'd want to know. But sit with it honestly. What would that knowledge *do* to you?

If the date were far, you would relax into it. You would say, *I have decades — I'll change later, I'll pray later, I'll repair that relationship later, I'll come back to my Lord later.* The far deadline breeds postponement. It anaesthetizes. You would sleepwalk toward it, always intending to wake up "closer to the time."

And if the date were near, it might crush you. You might collapse under the weight of the countdown, paralyzed, unable to live at all.

A *known* deadline, it turns out, is a kind of poison either way. It either lulls you to sleep or it crushes you flat.

Now look again at what the verse does with the timing of the promised reckoning. Al-Jalālayn and al-Baghawī both explain *amadan* as *a term, an end-point* whose measure **no one knows but Allah.** Al-Qurtubī adds that *mā tūʿadūn* — "what you are promised" — was understood by the early commentators as either the punishment or the coming of the Hour itself, and that its timing is *ghayb* — unseen — *known to none but Allah.* The hiddenness is not an accident of the verse. It is the *point* of the verse.

And here is the question that opens it all up: **why** does Allah keep it hidden? If He knows the term — and He does — why does He veil it even from His own Prophet?

[PAUSE]

Because the hidden deadline does the one thing the known deadline cannot: **it keeps the heart awake without crushing it.**

Think about how this works. Because you do not know whether the reckoning — or your own death, which is your personal Hour — is near or far, you can never safely postpone *and* you are never crushed by a countdown. The hiddenness holds you in the only posture that produces a living faith: **constant readiness.** Awake, but not paralyzed. Alert, but able to live, to build, to love, to plant the tree even today.

Recall the asymmetry we uncovered in the grammar. The "far" possibility was not a neutral fact — it was *my Lord will appoint a term.* Now you can feel why that mattered so much. If delay were just the universe drifting, then a long delay would be permission to sleep. But delay is not drift. **Delay is my Lord, appointing.** Even the long term is a deliberate, merciful decision — a gift of *time to return* — not an empty stretch of permission to forget. The believer reads the delay not as "I have time to waste" but as "my Lord has given me time to come home."

This is the mercy folded inside the hiddenness. Allah veils the *when* so that the *now* stays alive. Every moment becomes the moment that might be the last one before the term arrives — and so every moment is worth living rightly. The unknown deadline turns your whole life into a standing invitation: *return now, because you do not know, and the not-knowing is itself the mercy that keeps the door open.*

[PAUSE]

And notice the gentleness toward the mockers, too. They demanded the date so they could mock it. Allah does not give them the date — and in withholding it, He is *still* giving them something: more time. The undisclosed term is, for the one who demanded it in scorn, a reprieve he didn't even know he was receiving. The very hiddenness they ridiculed was the mercy extending their chance to turn back.

What does this change about tomorrow? Here is the concrete application. Stop asking the mockers' question — *when?* — and start asking the believer's question — *am I ready?* The hidden term means the only honest preparation is the kind that's always current. So make one thing right today that you have been postponing "until later." A prayer you keep delaying. A debt of forgiveness you keep deferring. A return you keep telling yourself you'll make "closer to the time." The verse has just told you: **there is no "closer to the time," because the time is hidden — which means the time is now.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two journeys — the linguistic and the thematic — turn out to be the same single insight approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us a verse that refuses to resolve the *when* and instead lands us on the *Who* — *my Lord will appoint the term.* The themes showed us a heart that is freed from the tyranny of not-knowing precisely *because* it has stopped trying to be its own lord of time and has handed the calendar back to its true Owner.

The asymmetry we found — *near* as a bare fact, but *far* as an act of "my Lord" — is the whole spiritual lesson in miniature. Your peace does not come from learning *which* it is, near or far. Your peace comes from knowing that *either way, it is in His hands.* The "I do not know" and the "my Lord appoints" are not two separate statements. They are one breath. The not-knowing is only bearable — is even *beautiful* — because of the Lord that the sentence ends on.

This is the architecture of the verse, and it is the architecture of faith itself: human limit at the front, divine ownership at the close, and an open question held trustingly in between.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I exhausting myself trying to *know* what is coming, when I have not yet learned to *trust* the One who holds it?
2. Have I been treating "I don't know" as a shame to hide — or could it become, on my tongue too, an upright act of honesty before Allah?
3. If the deadline of my own life is hidden from me precisely as a mercy, what am I postponing "until closer to the time" — a time I will never actually be able to see coming?
4. Do I read the *delays* in my life as the universe drifting, or as my Lord deliberately appointing a term — giving me more time to return?
5. When I am cornered by an open question I cannot close, does my sentence end where the verse ends — on "my Lord"?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The most knowing man was commanded to say "I do not know" — so that we would learn that peace is never found by seizing the hidden *when*, but by trusting the known *Who* who appoints it.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is the shift this verse invites — gently, not as a burden but as a relief. You are allowed to not know. You were never appointed the keeper of the calendar. The future was never your weight to carry. The Prophet ﷺ, the most beloved of all creation, was made to stand before mockers and say *I do not know* — and lost nothing, because what he kept was infinitely greater than a date: he kept his *Lord.* You can lay down the exhausting work of forecasting your own future and pick up, instead, the lighter and truer work of being ready *now* — because the term is hidden, and the hiddenness is the mercy, and the One who holds it is *yours.*

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who alone know the term and the time — teach our hearts the dignity of "I do not know," and the peace that lives on the other side of it. We cannot see what is coming, so let us not exhaust ourselves trying; instead let us trust the Hand that appoints it. Keep us awake without crushing us, ready without despairing, and bring us home to You before our hidden term arrives — for it is **Yours**, and so are we. Āmīn.
