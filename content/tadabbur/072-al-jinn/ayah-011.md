# The Unnamed Group and the Strips of a Single Hide

## Surah al-Jinn, Ayah 11

> **وَأَنَّا مِنَّا ٱلصَّـٰلِحُونَ وَمِنَّا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ كُنَّا طَرَآئِقَ قِدَدًا**
>
> *Wa-annā minnā ṣ-ṣāliḥūn, wa-minnā dūna dhālik, kunnā ṭarāʾiqa qidadā.*
>
> "And among us are the righteous, and among us are those below that; we were [of] separate, divided ways."

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me ask you to do something before we read a single word closely.

I want you to imagine a creature you have never seen, that has been alive far longer than you, that has watched human beings come and go for ages — and that, on a certain night, was passing through the air over a valley and heard something it had never heard in all its long existence.

The Qur'an, being recited.

That is the dramatic situation of this entire surah. Surah al-Jinn is almost unique in the Qur'an because for most of its length **Allah is quoting somebody else.** He is reporting, word for word, the speech of the jinn — a whole people of the unseen — as they react in real time to overhearing revelation. The surah opens: *Qul ūḥiya ilayya annahu-stamaʿa nafarun mina l-jinn* — "Say: it has been revealed to me that a company of the jinn listened." And from there, verse after verse begins with the same small word: **wa annā** — "and that we…", "and that we…" — the jinn testifying about themselves.

So picture the scene. A group of these beings — old, intelligent, set in their ways, carrying centuries of inherited assumptions about how the universe works — encounter the speech of Allah for the first time. And it undoes them. They had a whole cosmology, and the recitation walks straight through it. They had believed certain things about the heavens, about who could approach the divine, about their own importance. And one by one, in this surah, they recant.

Now, here is the moment we have arrived at. After all their confessions about Allah and the heavens, the jinn turn the camera around. For the first time they stop describing the cosmos and start describing **themselves.** Their own people. Their own community.

And what is the very first true thing they manage to say about themselves, now that the light has been switched on?

Not "we are a great nation." Not "we have always been guided." Not even "some of us did wrong and we're sorry."

The first honest sentence is this: *among us are the righteous, and among us are those below that — we were divided ways, cut into strips.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. I want you to notice something strange about how Allah has them say it, and I will not resolve it yet — I just want to plant it. When the jinn name the good among them, Allah gives them a full, dignified, *named* noun: **al-ṣāliḥūn**, "the righteous," complete with the definite article, the way you'd name a known and honored group. But when they come to the others — the not-righteous — Allah does not give them a name at all. He leaves a hole in the sentence. He just says **dūna dhālik** — "below that," "lesser than that," "other than that." A blank where a noun should be.

Why would the *good* be named and the *not-good* be left nameless?

Hold that question. By the end, I think it will open into something about mercy that you did not expect to find in a verse about division.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

There are three things in this short verse that I want to slow down on. A named group and an unnamed group. A past-tense verb that hides a whole story. And a frightening little word — *qidad* — that comes from the world of cutting leather.

Let's take them in turn, and let's earn each one.

### Element One: The Group That Is Named and the Group That Is Not

First, a small lesson in how language works — not Arabic yet, just language.

When we describe people we have feelings about, we reveal those feelings in *whether* and *how* we name them. Think of how a parent talks about two children to a stranger. "This is Maryam, she's studying medicine —" full name, full identity, the warmth audible. And then, of the other: "…and we have another one too." You can hear an entire relationship in that gap. Naming is honoring. Withholding a name is doing *something* — but what it does depends entirely on the heart behind it. Sometimes withholding a name is contempt. And sometimes — stay with this — withholding a name is **shielding.** A protective vagueness. "I'd rather not say."

Hold both possibilities, because Arabic is about to force the choice.

Now look at the verse: **wa-annā minnā ṣ-ṣāliḥūn** — "and among us are *al-ṣāliḥūn*, the righteous."

*Al-ṣāliḥūn* comes from the root **ṣ-l-ḥ** (صلح), and this is one of the great, load-bearing roots of the whole Qur'an. Its base meaning is not "holy" or "pious" in some floating spiritual sense. Its base meaning is **soundness, wholeness, being in good working order, being fit-for-purpose, being un-corrupted.** The same root gives us *iṣlāḥ* — repair, reconciliation, the mending of what was broken. *Ṣulḥ* — a peace treaty, two broken parties made whole again. To be *ṣāliḥ* is to be a thing that *functions as it was meant to function* — like a sound limb, a sound tool, a sound heart. The opposite, *fasād*, is rot, breakdown, a thing that has stopped working as designed.

So when the jinn name their good ones *al-ṣāliḥūn*, they are not merely saying "the pious among us." They are saying: **the ones who still work right. The un-rotted ones. The ones who are whole.** And Allah lets them say it with the definite article — *al*-ṣāliḥūn — the way you point to a known, recognizable, dignified class of people. They get a noun. They get an identity. They stand up in the sentence.

Now watch what happens to the others. **Wa-minnā dūna dhālik** — "and among us are *dūna dhālik*."

There is no noun here at all.

Notice it. Arabic *expects* a parallel here. The sentence has just given us a beautiful balanced frame: "among us are X… and among us are Y." Your ear waits for the second group to be named the way the first one was. *Al-ṣāliḥūn* and then — what? The wicked? *Al-fāsidūn*, the rotten? *Al-kāfirūn*, the deniers? Arabic has all these words ready and waiting. The slot is open. The grammar is leaning forward.

And Allah fills the slot with a **location word.** *Dūna* (دون) is not a name. It is a preposition of position — it means "below," "short of," "this side of," "less than," "other than." *Dūna dhālik* literally means **"short of that," "beneath that level," "on the lower side of that."** The classical commentators feel this immediately. Al-Baghawī glosses it plainly: *dūna ṣ-ṣāliḥīn* — "below the righteous," i.e., those who fall short of that station. Al-Qurtubī notes the same, and even prefers the reading that it means those *beneath the righteous in degree of righteousness* — not necessarily disbelievers, just **less.** Al-Jalālayn rounds it to "a people other than the righteous." And the Muyassar widens it to include the corrupt and the rebellious.

So put the asymmetry in front of you and just look at it:

The good ones: **named, definite, dignified, standing upright in the sentence** — *al-ṣāliḥūn*.

The others: **no name, no noun, just a direction — "down from there."**

[PAUSE]

Now — back to the question I planted at the start. Why? Why are the *good* honored with a name and the *not-good* left in this nameless, shadowed half of the sentence?

Here is what I want you to consider. There are two ways to leave a group unnamed, and the difference between them is the difference between cruelty and mercy.

You can leave a group unnamed in **contempt** — "they're not even worth naming." That is one kind of silence.

But you can also leave a group unnamed in **restraint** — a refusal to nail anyone to a label, a refusal to publish the full indictment, a leaving-of-the-door-open. And notice: *dūna dhālik*, "short of that," is not a verdict of doom. It does not say *the damned*. It does not say *the lost*. It says **"less than."** It measures a distance, not a fate. A distance can be closed. A person who is "short of righteous" is precisely a person who can still travel the remaining distance.

And remember *who is speaking.* This is the jinn describing **their own people** — *minnā*, "from among us," twice. These are not strangers being catalogued. This is a family being honest about itself. And when a community describes its own flawed members, the merciful move — the *ṣāliḥ* move, the un-rotted move — is exactly this: name the good plainly so virtue is honored, and veil the rest, because they are still *ours*, still *here*, still within reach of return.

The grammar is enacting mercy. The good are named because virtue deserves to be seen. The fallen are unnamed because a name is a sentence, and they have not yet been sentenced.

That is what one missing noun is doing in this verse.

### Element Two: *Kunnā* — The Past Tense That Hides a Conversion

Now to a single verb, and a lesson about time.

Languages mark time, and the tense a speaker chooses tells you where they are *standing* when they speak. "I smoke" and "I used to smoke" describe the same person — but the second sentence can only be spoken by someone who has crossed over to the other side. The past tense, *used to*, is the grammar of a person who has changed. You cannot say "I used to" about a thing you are still doing. The past tense is a quiet announcement: **I am no longer there.**

Hold that, and look at the verb the jinn reach for: **kunnā** — "we *were*."

*Kunnā* is from **k-w-n** (كون), the root of being itself, here in the perfect tense, first-person plural — completed, finished, sealed-off action. "We *were* divided ways."

Now feel the weight of that tense in this exact spot. The jinn have just been describing the present: "among us *are* the righteous, among us *are* those below that." Present tense — that is how their community looks *right now*. And then, the instant they characterize the *whole* of what they had been — the togetherness, the arrangement, the shape of their people — they slip into the **past**: *kunnā*. "We *were* divided ways, cut into strips."

Why the shift? Because something happened between *were* and *are*. Something happened in this very surah. **They heard the Qur'an.**

Go back two verses and you can watch the hinge turn. *Wa-annā lammā samiʿnā l-hudā āmannā bih* — "and when we heard the guidance, we believed in it" (72:13). The recitation has *already landed* by the time they say *kunnā*. So this past tense is not casual. It is the past tense of a people standing on the far bank of a river they have just crossed. "We *were* fragmented" — said by beings who can only call their fragmentation "the past" because something has just begun to gather them.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it is gentle and easy to miss. The jinn do not say "we *are* divided ways." They say "we *were*." The very act of telling the truth about their brokenness is spoken from a place *past* the brokenness. **Naming the fracture in the past tense is itself the first evidence of repair.**

This is something true about every honest confession you will ever make. You can only say "I *used to* be lost" from inside the beginning of being found. The diagnosis and the cure arrive together. The moment you can describe your fragmentation accurately — *we were strips, cut apart, running in parallel and never touching* — is the moment you are no longer fully inside it, because something whole enough to see the whole picture has begun to form in you.

The tense is the testimony. *Kunnā.* We were. Not anymore — or not entirely, not as before. Something heard us into wholeness.

### Element Three: *Ṭarāʾiqa Qidadā* — The Hide Cut Into Strips

Now the image at the heart of the verse — and I have saved it for the end of our linguistic journey because it gathers up everything.

**Kunnā ṭarāʾiqa qidadā.** "We were *ṭarāʾiq*, *qidad*."

Let's open both words.

*Ṭarāʾiq* (طرائق) is the plural of *ṭarīqa*, from the root **ṭ-r-q** (طرق) — a path, a road, a way. You know this root already even if you didn't know you did: a *ṭarīq* is a road; *al-ṭāriq* is the night-traveler who *knocks* on the road; the Ṣūfī *ṭarīqa* is a "path" of spiritual practice. So *ṭarāʾiq* is simply **"paths," "ways," "roads."** Already this tells us the jinn are describing themselves as plural — not one road but many roads. Diverging ways.

But it is the second word that makes the skin prickle. *Qidadā* (قددا), from the root **q-d-d** (قدد).

Listen to the word before you even know its meaning: *qi-da-dan*. The deep *qāf* at the back of the throat, and then that **doubled, hammering *dāl* — da-dan.** It is a chopping sound. The mouth itself performs a cutting motion. This is a word that *sounds like what it does.*

And what does it mean? The root **q-d-d** means **to cut lengthwise** — to slice a thing *along* its length into long strips. This is not the cutting of *q-ṭ-ʿ*, which severs across, snaps in two. *Qadd* is the specialized, deliberate cutting of the leather-worker: you take a single hide — *one whole skin* — and you draw the knife down its length again and again, slicing it into long parallel **thongs, straps, strips.** Al-Baghawī gives us exactly this etymology: *al-qiddatu l-qiṭʿatu mina sh-shayʾ* — "a *qidda* is a strip cut from a thing" — *wa aṣluhā mina l-qaddi wa huwa l-qaṭʿ* — "and its origin is from *al-qadd*, which is the lengthwise cut." And he gives the proverb the Arabs used: *ṣāra l-qawmu qidadā* — "the people became *qidad*" — said precisely **when a community's states and conditions splinter apart.**

[PAUSE]

Now let the image do its work, because this is the load-bearing center of the whole verse.

A hide. One animal's skin. **A single, continuous, whole thing.** That is what a community is *meant* to be — one piece.

And the knife comes down its length. *Qadd.* And again. *Qadd.* And the one skin becomes a fistful of long thin straps. Here is the terrible precision of the image: the strips are **all from the same hide.** They share an origin. They lie right next to each other. They run *parallel* — pointing the same direction, the same length, the same material. And yet they **never touch as one thing again.** They are adjacent and severed at the same time. Near, and separated. Same source, sliced apart.

*That* is what the jinn are confessing about themselves. Not "we were different." Difference is not the wound. The wound is that **they came from one skin and were cut into parallel strips** — together in origin, sundered in fact. Side by side, and utterly apart.

And the classical commentators, when they reach for what *qidad* actually meant in the life of the jinn, reach for the most painful possible examples — and notice, they reach for *our* vocabulary. Al-Suddī: *firaq shattā* — scattered sects. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk: *adyānan mukhtalifa* — differing religions. Qatāda: *ahwāʾan mutabāyina* — clashing desires, opposed cravings. And then al-Baghawī reports something that should stop us cold: al-Ḥasan and al-Suddī said the jinn are *like you* — *fa-minhum qadariyya wa-murjiʾa wa-rāfiḍa* — "among them are Qadarites and Murjiʾites and Rāfiḍites" — and Ibn Kaysān: *shiyaʿan wa-firaqan li-kulli firqatin hawā ka-ahwāʾi n-nās* — "factions and sects, each faction with its own craving, just like the cravings of human beings."

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? The jinn looked in the mirror, and the early scholars looked at the reflection, and said: **this is not a description of jinn. This is a description of every community that has ever held a truth and then cut itself into strips over it.** Same revelation. Same hide. And down comes the knife — this faction, that faction, this craving, that label — until the one skin is a bundle of parallel straps that share everything and touch nothing.

That is the linguistic journey. A named good and an unnamed fallen. A past tense spoken from the far bank. And a hide cut into strips.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Honesty That Becomes Possible Only in the Light

Let me begin not with the jinn but with you, on an ordinary day, talking about a group of people you don't belong to.

Notice how the mind works. "The atheists." "The liberals." "The traditionalists." "The other masjid." "That family." Notice that when we name a group we are *not* part of, the mind does something automatic and almost invisible: it **smooths them into one block.** They become a single, undifferentiated *they*. They all think the same. They all want the same. They are one solid thing, and that one solid thing is usually a threat. We can hold a hundred contradictions inside our own community — "well, *we're* not all like that" — while flattening the entire outside world into a single hostile shape.

Psychologists have a clumsy name for it: *out-group homogeneity.* We see *our* people in high resolution — every face distinct, every motive nuanced — and we see *their* people in low resolution, a gray mass. It is one of the most reliable distortions of the human mind, and it is the engine of nearly every contempt you have ever felt.

Now hold that ordinary distortion up against what the jinn do in this verse.

They have just been humbled. They have just heard the speech of Allah and had their entire cosmology corrected. The light has come on. And the *first* thing the light reveals — the very first true sentence they speak about their own people — is a refusal to flatten. **"Among us are the righteous, and among us are those below that."** They will not say "we jinn are righteous." They will not say "we jinn are wicked." They look at their own kind in *high resolution.* They confess complexity. They admit their own community contains the whole spectrum — the whole and the rotted, the upright and the fallen-short — and they refuse to claim the flattering version *or* to disown the unflattering one.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to see. **That high-resolution honesty was not available to them before the Qur'an.** It is no accident that this confession comes *after* "when we heard the guidance, we believed." Guidance is what makes accurate self-vision possible. In the dark, you can only tell flattering stories about yourself — "we are a great people," "we have always been guided." It takes light to see the strips. It takes the speech of Allah landing in a heart to make a person finally able to say, without flinching and without despair: *some of us are sound, and some of us are not, and I am honest enough now to hold both.*

This is the opposite of how we usually use group-identity. We use it to *escape* self-examination — "at least I'm not one of *them*." The jinn use the light to turn the examination *inward*, onto their own. And notice: this honesty is neither self-hatred nor self-flattery. They do not say "we are all corrupt" (despair) and they do not say "we are all righteous" (delusion). They say the true, difficult, in-between thing. *Among us are both.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

Tomorrow, you will think about some group — and you will feel the mind reach for the gray block, the flattening, the *they*. And I want you to remember that the first sentence intelligent creatures spoke after their hearts were opened by revelation was a sentence that refused exactly that move — and refused it *about their own people*, where honesty costs the most.

So the practice is precise. **Spend your honesty inward, and spend your charity outward.** When you describe your own community — your family, your masjid, your people — resist the flattering smoothing; admit the strips, name the soundness *and* the rot, in high resolution, the way the jinn did. And when you describe the group you fear — resist the *opposite* flattening; remember that *they* too are *ṭarāʾiqa qidadā*, also a hide cut into strips, also containing their righteous and their fallen-short, also in higher resolution than your fear wants to allow.

The light that opened the jinn does the same to anyone it reaches. It does not make you think you are better than everyone. It makes you able, at last, to see clearly — including the parts of your own that you had been protecting from view.

### Theme Two: One Hide, Many Strips — and the Voice That Gathers

Now the deeper wound, the one the knife-image opens.

Let me start where you live. Have you ever felt divided *inside yourself*? Not divided against another group — divided against *you*? Part of you wants the prayer and part of you wants the screen. Part of you is generous and part of you is grasping. Part of you believes and part of you, at 3 a.m., is not so sure. You make a sincere intention in the morning and betray it by noon — same person, same day, cut into strips that run parallel and never quite meet. You are, on most days, *ṭarāʾiqa qidadā* — a self that came from one soul and somehow got sliced lengthwise into competing straps.

And what is true of the single self is true of the community, only larger. The Muslims are *one umma* — Allah says so, *inna hādhihi ummatukum ummatan wāḥida*, "this, your community, is one community" (21:92). One hide. And look at us. *Firaq shattā.* Scattered sects. *Ahwāʾun mutabāyina.* Clashing cravings. The exact words the commentators used for the jinn. We share the *shahāda*, the same qibla, the same Book — same skin — and the knife has come down its length so many times that we lie side by side as strips that touch at every edge and unite at none.

So here is the question the verse forces. **What cuts the hide, and what gathers the strips back?**

[PAUSE]

Watch the order of events in the surah, because the order *is* the answer.

The jinn say *kunnā ṭarāʾiqa qidadā* — "we *were* cut into strips" — in the *past tense*. We established why: because by this point they had heard the guidance and believed. So the fragmentation is described as **finished, prior, the old state** — and what ended it was a single thing entering from outside: **the recited speech of Allah.**

Think about what that means. The strips cannot rejoin *themselves*. A cut strip of leather has no power to grow back into the hide. Fragmentation is not self-healing — that is the whole tragedy of *qidad*, the cutting is one-directional, the knife only ever divides. **Nothing inside the divided thing can undo the division.** If the strips are ever to be one skin again, something must come *from outside the strips* and gather them.

And in this surah, that something has a sound. It is *al-hudā*, the guidance — *lammā samiʿnā l-hudā* — the *heard* guidance, the recitation moving through the air over a valley and into the ears of beings who had been splintered for ages. The Qur'an is what walks into the bundle of severed straps and begins, impossibly, to make them one hide again.

This is exactly the medicine Allah prescribes for *us* in our fragmentation. *Wa-ʿtaṣimū bi-ḥabli-llāhi jamīʿan wa-lā tafarraqū* — "Hold fast, all of you together, to the rope of Allah, and do not become divided" (3:103). Notice the architecture of that command. He does not say "hold fast *to each other.*" Strips holding strips cannot make a hide. He says **hold fast to the *rope* — to the thing that comes down from Him** — *all of you together*, and *then* you are gathered, not by gripping one another but by all gripping the same descending cord. Unity is never horizontal first. We do not become one by negotiating with each other. We become one by each turning to the same vertical thing — His speech — and discovering, as we grip it, that we are now holding the same object, and so are joined.

[PAUSE]

Let the paradox sit, because the verse does not rush to resolve it and neither should we. The jinn confess their division *in the very breath* that proves it is ending. The brokenness and the mending are spoken in one sentence. *We were strips* — and the fact that they can now say "were" is the gathering already underway. You do not have to be whole before you turn to the Qur'an. You turn to it *as a bundle of strips*, and the turning is the first stitch.

So what changes tomorrow?

Two things, one inward and one outward.

Inward: the next time you feel the strips inside yourself — the divided will, the self that betrayed its own morning intention — do not try to *will* yourself back into one piece. Strips cannot rejoin strips. Instead, do what the jinn did: **go and hear the guidance.** Open the Book. Sit in the recitation. Let the thing from outside come in and gather what you cannot gather by force of resolve. The integration you cannot manufacture, the speech of Allah can perform.

Outward: when you grieve the fractured state of the umma — and you should grieve it — resist the instinct to fix it by getting everyone to agree with *your* strip. That is just one strap trying to be the hide. The verse points elsewhere. The gathering happens when each of us grips the same descending rope, not when we win the argument against the strip beside us. Be the kind of presence that points people to the rope, not to your faction. Honor the *ṣāliḥūn* wherever you find them — even on a strip that is not yours — and veil the failings of those *dūna dhālik*, exactly as the verse modeled, because they are still your hide.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this verse were saying one thing all along.

The **linguistic** discovery: a group named with honor (*al-ṣāliḥūn*), a group left mercifully unnamed (*dūna dhālik*), a verb of completed change (*kunnā*), and an image of a single hide cut into parallel strips (*ṭarāʾiqa qidadā*).

The **thematic** discovery: that the light of revelation makes honest, high-resolution self-vision possible — and that the fragmentation it reveals can only be healed by the very voice that revealed it.

These are the same insight from two directions. The grammar *enacts* the theme. The good are named and the fallen are veiled — because the speaker has been changed by mercy and now extends it. The verb is past tense — because the speaker is already on the far bank, already being gathered. And the strips are confessed in the same breath that proves them to be the *old* state — because the thing that lets you finally *see* your fragmentation is the same thing that has begun to end it.

A people sat in the dark, smooth and flattering to themselves, certain they were one thing. The recitation came through the air. And the first thing the light showed them was the strips — *and gave them the honesty to say so, and the mercy to say it gently, and the past tense to say it from the far side of the cut.*

### Questions to Carry

1. When I describe my *own* community, do I see it in high resolution — *al-ṣāliḥūn* and *dūna dhālik*, both — or do I flatten it into the flattering version?
2. When I describe a community I fear, do I grant *them* the same high resolution — or do I let my fear cut them into one gray block?
3. Where, exactly, am I *ṭarāʾiqa qidadā* inside myself — which two strips of me run parallel and never touch?
4. Am I trying to make myself whole by force of will — strips gripping strips — when the verse says the gathering comes from *outside*, from the heard guidance?
5. When I grieve the divisions of the umma, do I reach for the rope that descends — or do I just want everyone to move onto my strip?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the light finally reached them, the first true thing the jinn could say about themselves was that they had never been one thing — and the very fact that they could now call that fragmentation "the past" was the proof that the voice they had just heard had already begun to gather the strips back into a hide.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to arrive whole. The jinn did not. They came to the recitation as a bundle of severed straps — scattered sects, clashing cravings, a hide long since cut — and they did not first repair themselves and then believe. They heard, and the hearing *was* the first stitch. So the invitation is simply this: stop trying to will the strips of yourself back together, and go instead to where the gathering voice can be heard. Open the Book as the fragmented thing you actually are. Let the speech that gathered an ancient people of the unseen do its quiet, stitching work on you.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who gathered a scattered people of the jinn back into wholeness by the sound of Your speech — gather the strips of me. The part that intends and the part that betrays, the part that believes and the part that doubts at 3 a.m. — make them one hide again under the knife-less mending of Your guidance. And let me carry Your mercy the way this verse did: honoring every *ṣāliḥ* I meet, and veiling, never publishing, the failings of those who are merely *dūna dhālik* — for they, like me, are still Yours, and still within reach of return.

*Āmīn.*
