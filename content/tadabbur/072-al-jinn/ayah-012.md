## Introduction

**Arabic:**
[ayah:72:12] وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّآ أَن لَّن نُّعْجِزَ ٱللَّهَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَن نُّعْجِزَهُۥ هَرَبًا

**Transliteration:**
*Wa annā ẓanannā an lan nu'jiza Allāha fī l-arḍi wa lan nu'jizahu harabā*

**Translation:**
"And that we came to full certainty — we will never overcome Allah on earth, and we will never escape Him by flight."

### Rebuilding the World

We are inside the first fifteen ayahs of Surah Al-Jinn.

A group of jinn — beings made from smokeless fire, invisible to human eyes, moving through the world on their own routes and in their own ways — were passing through somewhere. They weren't invited. They weren't sought out. And then they heard something they hadn't been looking for.

They heard the Quran being recited.

The Prophet ﷺ was at a place called Nakhlah, in prayer. The scholars of tafsir, including al-Baghawi, report among the narrations that this occurred at one of the most painful stretches of the mission — after the rejection at Ta'if, after years of being turned away in Makkah. The city that was supposed to receive the message had closed its doors. The people most expected to respond had refused. And in that quiet, in that solitude, in what must have felt like the lowest point — unseen beings stopped. Gathered. Listened.

What happened to them?

The entire opening of Surah Al-Jinn is their answer. When they returned to their own people, they couldn't stop talking about it. *It guides to right conduct. It is astonishing. We believed in it.* (72:2) They confessed their own divisions — some of us are righteous, some of us have gone wrong. (72:11) They spoke with the urgency of people who had encountered something and been permanently changed by it.

And then they said this.

Ayah 12 is the theological anchor. Before they can invite their people to believe, before they can testify to the Quran's guidance, they have to state this. This is the ground they are standing on. The fact that has stopped them in their tracks. The recognition that no longer permits them to run.

Here is what is worth pausing on before we even enter the text:

The jinn are not announcing something new that Allah revealed to them. They are announcing something they came to *know* — something that the hearing of the Quran finally solidified in them, perhaps something they had long been circling around. And what they are announcing is not that Allah is frightening. Not that they are cornered. It is something more specific, and more interesting, than either of those things.

They are announcing that they cannot outrun what is true.

And that is a very different kind of statement.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element 1 — ظَنَنَّا: When Suspicion Becomes Certainty

---

Think about a word you use all the time in English: *think.*

"I think it's going to rain." That's a guess — mild, easy to reverse, held loosely. "I think she's upset with me." That's a reading of signals, not a conclusion. "I think I left my keys at work." Uncertainty, not conviction.

But now consider this sentence: "I think I'm going to die someday."

Same word. Completely different weight. There's no tentativeness in that sentence. You *know* it. The word that usually means *probably* is doing something entirely different — it's carrying a certainty so obvious, so fundamental, that to say it more emphatically would feel almost redundant. The word for "maybe" has become the vehicle for "absolutely."

Arabic has a word that works exactly this way.

**ظَنَّ** — *ẓanna.*

In its standard usage, ẓanna means to suppose, to suspect, to think without full confirmation. It's the word of incomplete evidence — you point in a direction, but you hold it loosely. The Quran uses it this way often. In verse 7 of this very surah, the jinn describe how human beings "thought, as you thought (ẓannū)" that Allah would never resurrect anyone — and there, it is precisely wrong conjecture, a false assumption held without warrant.

But in this ayah?

Every classical commentator stops at this word and says the same thing.

Al-Qurtubi writes, with characteristic precision: *"al-ẓann hāhunā bi-maʿnā l-ʿilm wa l-yaqīn"* — "the ẓann here carries the meaning of knowledge and certainty." He explains: it is certainty arrived at through reflection, through looking at the signs of Allah, through the process of thinking and concluding — and what the process arrives at is not opinion but conviction.

Al-Baghawi writes: *"ʿalimnā wa ayqannā"* — "we came to know, and we became certain."

Al-Muyassar renders it with one word: *"ayqannā"* — "we arrived at yaqin." Unshakeable conviction.

So the jinn use the word for "we thought" — but every scholar of the tradition hears them saying "we became certain."

[PAUSE]

Why use the word for "maybe" when you mean "absolutely"?

Here is what I want you to sit with.

The word ẓanna, at its root, carries a quality of something that *settles*. Not the sudden detonation of revelation — not a bolt-from-the-blue realization. Something that *settles* — the way sediment moves through water, slowly, continuously, until one day you look at the water and it is clear.

Think about the experiences in your own life that made you most certain of something. The certainties that actually changed you — not just intellectually but in what you do. Were they instantaneous? Or were they things that built quietly, over time, until one day you realized you couldn't argue against them anymore? That the exit you'd been using had quietly closed?

The jinn heard the Quran. They listened. Something moved in them that had perhaps been moving for a long time. And when they returned to their people and said *ẓanannā* — they were not announcing a sudden discovery. They were announcing that something had finished. The settling was complete. The water was clear.

[PAUSE]

There is a difference between *knowing* something and *knowing it in a way that changes what you do next.*

You might have known, in some abstract corner of yourself, that you will die. But the person who has just heard a serious diagnosis — who has just sat beside someone they loved as that person took a final breath — knows differently. Something has landed that cannot be unlanded. The knowing has moved from the periphery to the center. And you cannot un-see it.

The jinn heard the Quran, and something settled.

**ظَنَنَّا** — certainty that has arrived and will not leave.

But notice what they have become certain of. Not merely "Allah exists." Not simply "the Quran is true." They have become certain of something very specific — something that forecloses an entire way of being. Something that closes off not just a door but an entire category of option.

They have become certain that they cannot escape.

### Element 2 — نُّعْجِزَ ... نُّعْجِزَهُ: The Same Verb, Closing Every Door

---

Let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs are built before we enter the next word.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — what grammarians call *awzān*, or "forms." Think of them as architectural templates: each one takes a three-letter root and reshapes it into a specific semantic function. The first form is the most direct expression of the root's basic meaning. Subsequent forms add layers: intensity, transitivity, mutuality, reflexivity, direction.

The root here is **ع-ج-ز** — *ʿayn-jīm-zayn*.

In its base form, this root means to be unable, to be weak, to fail at something. Someone who is *ʿājiz* is someone who lacks the capacity to manage what is before them.

Form IV — which is active in both verb occurrences in this ayah — makes this root transitive and gives it a directional quality. The Form IV pattern: **أَعْجَزَ** (*aʿjaza*). This form, when applied to this root, carries two closely related functions:

First — *to render someone else incapable*: to overwhelm someone, to be so much more powerful than them that they fail in whatever they were trying to do.

Second — *to outstrip someone, to escape from a pursuer*: to move in such a way that the one chasing fails to catch you. The imagery is of a race — you make the chaser fail by being uncatchable.

Both functions are alive in this ayah. And here is what is structurally stunning:

The Quran uses this same verb — same root, same form, same tense, same first-person plural — **twice** in a single ayah. Back to back. Unmistakably deliberate.

**لَّن نُّعْجِزَ ٱللَّهَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ**
*Never will we cause Allah to fail — by existing on the earth.*

**وَلَن نُّعْجِزَهُ هَرَبًا**
*And never will we escape Him — by flight.*

[PAUSE]

The repetition is not redundancy. It is architecture. A door closing. And then another door closing.

Stand inside this structure and feel what it does.

The first clause describes a *state*: being somewhere. We are on the earth. We exist in a place. Can our existence in a location render Allah's authority over us null? Can we be somewhere that exempts us from what He wills?

No. *Lan nu'jiza Allāha fī l-arḍ.*

The second clause describes a *movement*: going somewhere. We flee. We take ourselves away from our current location and head toward another. Can our motion outstrip His reach?

No. *Wa lan nu'jizahu harabā.*

Between these two clauses, every possibility has been sealed. At any given moment, you are either somewhere or moving somewhere. Those are the only two modes of existence available to any created being. And the Quran has just closed both.

[PAUSE]

There is an image I want to offer you — not as decoration, but as the structure itself.

Imagine floating in an ocean. Not drowning — just suspended, submerged, held in water on all sides. Now try to escape the water.

You can swim horizontally — moving across the ocean, covering distance, arriving at a different location. But you are still in the water.

You can swim upward — pressing toward the surface, toward air, toward something above. But until you break through, you are still in the water.

The water is not a wall you can go around. It is not a guard you can distract. It is the medium — the very context of your movement. You cannot escape water by moving through it. Moving through it *is* being in it.

This is what the jinn are describing. Allah's authority is not a location you can leave. It is not a force stationed in one place that you can distance yourself from. It is the medium — the condition of existence itself. And to move is to move *within* it, not away from it.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something in the grammar itself.

First clause: **نُّعْجِزَ ٱللَّهَ** — "cause Allah to fail." Allah is named. The full, majestic name: **ٱللَّهَ**. Explicit. Direct. As if to say — we are speaking about *this*, about *Him specifically*.

Second clause: **نُّعْجِزَهُ** — "escape *Him*." Not the full name again — the pronoun *hū*. Just "Him."

In Arabic, a pronoun indicates something already established in the discourse, something the listener already knows and holds in mind. You don't need to name something again when it is already present and known.

But there is something else a pronoun implies: nearness. Proximity. You point with *hū* at something already there — already in the room, already in the sentence, already in the conversation.

So the second clause says: you cannot escape the One already indicated by a pronoun — the One who is so present in this sentence, so clearly near, that His name doesn't even need to be repeated.

The impossibility of escape is announced in the same breath as the intimacy of presence.

The One you cannot outrun is not distant and unfindable. He is close enough to be a pronoun.

There is something in that — if you sit with it — that is not terror. There is something in that that sounds almost like being known.

[PAUSE]

### Element 3 — هَرَبًا: The Flight That Fails

---

One more word before we turn to the themes.

**هَرَبًا** — *harabā*.

The root **ه-ر-ب** means flight. Running. Fleeing from something. *Al-hurūb* is the act of escape — the physical departure, the desperate leave-taking, the attempt to put distance between yourself and whatever is coming for you.

But what grammatical role is it playing in this sentence?

Al-Qurtubi, in his tafsir, stops here and offers a precise grammatical observation: *"harabā maṣdar fī mawḍiʿ al-ḥāl"* — a verbal noun functioning as a circumstantial clause. What does that mean?

It means the word *harabā* is not just naming the concept of flight. It is describing the *mode* of the second attempt. The sentence is not only saying "we cannot escape Him by running away." It is saying "we will never escape Him **while in the very act of fleeing**." The flight itself — the running in motion — is what accomplishes nothing.

The flight does not fail after a while. It fails *as* flight. The running, in the moment of running, is what comes to nothing.

[PAUSE]

I want you to think about a particular kind of running. Not physical. The kind we are more practiced at.

You know a conversation needs to happen, and you delay it. You know something is true about your life, and you fill your days with noise so you don't have to sit with it. You keep scrolling. You avoid the appointment. You tell yourself you'll think about it later, and later comes, and you find another reason for later.

This is *hurūb*. And we are experts at it.

The remarkable thing about this kind of running is that it works — in the short term. The thing you're avoiding stays at a distance. The noise keeps the silence at bay. The running produces a real, felt relief in the moment.

But the jinn are pointing to something about why it ultimately accomplishes nothing.

Because what you're running from is not behind you. It is not in a specific location that you can distance yourself from. It is already inside you, already with you, already present in the medium you move through. And so the flight — the full-force, exhausting, consuming act of flight — leaves you in exactly the same position you started.

Except more tired.

**Lan nu'jizahu harabā.**

Even the running. Even *that*. Even with everything you have — it doesn't move you one step further from the One you're trying to escape.

[PAUSE]

Listen to the sound of that word: **هَرَبًا** — *harabā.*

The breath of *hā*. The rolling rush of *rā*. The abrupt, stoppering close of *bā*. There is something in the phonetic texture of that word that feels like desperate movement — like breath shortened in a sprint, like the rhythm of feet hitting ground hard. The word sounds like what it means.

And the sentence places it at the end, exhaled: *harabā*. Like the last breath of a run that went nowhere.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge: What the Linguistic Structure Has Shown

Three words. Three doors into the same room.

**ẓanannā** showed us: this is certainty that has finished settling. Not argument, not proof-by-logic — a knowing that arrived through the process of looking and seeing, and has now become ground.

**nu'jiza / nu'jizahu** showed us: the same verb twice, sealing both modes of existence. Place and motion. Being somewhere and going somewhere. The two verbs together close every option the way a binary closes: you have covered all cases.

**harabā** showed us: the flight itself is what fails. Not "we tried to run and were stopped mid-flight." But something more complete: the running — in the very moment of running — accomplishes nothing.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1 — The Exhaustion of Running

---

Psychologists have a name for one of the most pervasive human patterns: *experiential avoidance*. The tendency to flee from internal states we find difficult — unwanted feelings, heavy memories, truths we'd rather not confront. We don't just run from external threats. We run from what's inside us.

And the finding that has stopped me every time I've encountered it:

**The running doesn't reduce the difficult thing. In almost every case, it amplifies it.**

The harder you try not to think about something, the more cognitively active it becomes. The more you avoid a necessary conversation, the heavier it weighs on you in the hours you're not having it. The more you fill your days to prevent sitting with a particular truth, the more that truth colonizes the gaps between your distractions — showing up in the quiet, in the moments just before sleep, in the spaces you didn't manage to fill.

You cannot outrun what you carry. Because what you're running from is not behind you.

And here's the particular cruelty of avoidance at full sophistication: the most accomplished avoiders — people who have constructed entire architectures of distraction, who have organized their lives around never having to stop — are often those who feel the hollowness most acutely. The running hasn't solved anything. It has simply deferred the reckoning while slowly draining the runner.

The jinn have just described this in seven words.

**لَن نُّعْجِزَهُ هَرَبًا.**

*We will never escape Him by flight.*

[PAUSE]

I want to be careful about something here.

The jinn are not describing Allah as a threat you flee from the way you run from danger. They are not positioning God as a pursuer and themselves as prey. What they are describing is something more fundamental: Allah is not at a location. His authority, His knowledge, His presence — these are not in one place that you can move away from. They are the condition of existence itself. They are the medium through which every flight passes.

And so to run "from" Him is not to move toward an exit. There is no exit from the medium you are inside. The running changes your position within the medium. It does not change your position relative to the medium.

What the running *does* change is this: your willingness to acknowledge where you are.

[PAUSE]

Think about the person — and perhaps you know this person well because you have been this person — who knows, in the bones, beneath all the noise, that there is a God. That accountability is real. That death is not a theoretical concept but a scheduled appointment. That the life they are living has consequences that extend past the visible.

And they have spent years — maybe decades — running from that knowing. Not the active work of argument and counter-argument. Not the intellectual labor of atheism. Just... filling every moment. Keeping the noise up. Deferring. Delaying. Maintaining a life sufficiently busy that the question never has time to surface and be looked at directly.

But the ẓanna is already there. The knowing is already inside them. The running is not moving them away from the knowing — the knowing travels with them. What the running does is refuse to let the knowing settle, refuse to let it become the ground they stand on, refuse to let it change what they do.

And this — this is what the jinn are announcing when they say *ẓanannā*. They stopped running. Something they had perhaps been circling for a long time was finally allowed to land. The Quran they overheard was the occasion, but the certainty was the arrival of something that had been in motion for a while.

They let it settle.

[PAUSE]

There is a quality the scholars of the heart describe as *sakīna* — often translated as tranquility, stillness, a descending peace. It comes in the Quran in moments of profound resting after strain. Something arrives in the heart and the storm settles.

I used to imagine sakīna as the cause. That peace comes first, and the running stops as a consequence.

I think the jinn are teaching the reverse order.

The running stops first. And the sakīna comes into the space that opens up.

Because the running itself — the perpetual, exhausting effort to be somewhere other than where you are spiritually — that is the source of the turbulence. The maintenance cost of avoidance is enormous. You have to keep the noise up. You have to keep moving. You have to keep finding new exits to try.

And when you stop — when you say, with the jinn, "ẓanannā, we know, we are in His grasp, there is nowhere else to go" — something settles. Not because the truth has become easier. But because the resistance to the truth has ended. And resistance to what you already know is among the most exhausting things a human being can do.

The moment you stop running, you discover that standing still is not harder than running. It is incomparably easier.

[PAUSE]

What does this mean for tomorrow?

There is something you are running from. I say this not as a rhetorical gesture — I say it because hurūb is one of the defining features of the nafs. We are all, in some form, in flight. From a conversation that needs to happen. From a realization about ourselves we've been circling. From a practice we know we should have begun. From a relationship with Allah that we know needs to be rebuilt but the rebuilding feels too large to start.

The jinn's declaration is not a threat. It is an invitation.

You can stop.

Not because the thing you've been running from will disappear. But because you were never going to outrun it. And knowing that — really knowing it, letting the ẓanna finish its settling into yaqin — is itself a form of release. The impossibility of escape is the permission to stop trying to escape.

Stop. Stand where you are. Let the certainty land.

### Theme 2 — Sovereignty Without Borders: What It Means That There Is Nowhere Else

---

There is a question that has come to me in many forms over the years — from students, from people wrestling with faith, from my own earlier self:

If Allah knows everything, if He is everywhere, if no action escapes His sight — why does that feel, sometimes, suffocating? Why does the declaration of divine omnipresence land in some hearts not as relief but as weight? Why does "there is nowhere to run" feel, to some people, like a cell door closing?

I think the answer has everything to do with what we imagine divine sovereignty to *look* like. What we unconsciously picture when we hear the word.

If you picture it as a surveillance system — cameras covering every angle, a warden watching every movement, a recorder that captures every transgression — then omnipresence is a prison. The fact that there's "nowhere to run" becomes something sinister. You are watched, and the watching cannot be escaped.

But what if sovereignty is something else entirely?

[PAUSE]

Let me approach this from a completely different direction.

Think about the worst loneliness you have experienced. Not isolation — not being physically alone. I mean the specific quality of loneliness where people are around you but no one actually sees you. Where you carry something heavy and there is no one to set it down with. Where you perform wellness in one interaction after another while something in you is quietly, continuously calling out.

In that moment — what would it mean to know, not just intellectually but in your bones, that you are not unseen? That the thing you are carrying is already known? That the calling out has been heard before you have even fully formed the words of it?

That changes the emotional register of "there is nowhere to run" completely.

If the One who is everywhere is a surveillance apparatus, then being known everywhere is exposure. If the One who is everywhere is the One who actually sees you — sees you in the way you most need to be seen — then being known everywhere is the answer to the deepest form of loneliness.

The impossibility of escape becomes the impossibility of being abandoned.

[PAUSE]

Al-Muyassar frames the jinn's certainty with a phrase I keep returning to: they recognized that they are "in His grasp and His authority" — *fī qabḍatihi wa sulṭānihi*.

The grasp.

**قَبْضَة** — *qabḍa*. In Arabic, this word carries range. A grasp can be the grip of a captor. But *qabḍa* is also the word for the physician's steady hand on the patient. The mother's palm around the newborn's head. The hold that does not let go because it does not want to let go.

The jinn are saying they are in a grasp they cannot escape. The character of that grasp — what it *means* to be held — depends entirely on who is holding.

And the jinn have just heard the Quran. They have heard the One whose grasp it is, speak. And what they heard, they said in verse 2, "guides to right conduct" — *yahdī ilā l-rushd*. What they heard, they said "we believed in it."

The certainty of inescapability becomes bearable — becomes more than bearable, becomes orientation — when you know something about the nature of the One from whom there is no escape.

This is why the jinn said what they said in verse 2 before they said verse 12. They named the guidance first. They established who Allah is — as revealed through His word — before they declared His total sovereignty. The sequence matters. The character of the One comes before the declaration of His inescapability.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the two clauses of this ayah again — but through this lens.

**لَن نُّعْجِزَ ٱللَّهَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** — we cannot render Allah's authority null by existing on the earth.

The earth is the entirety of the horizontal plane — everywhere you can be. The jinn are not just saying "we can't hide in one place." They are saying the *whole earth* offers no position of exemption. No corner. No altitude. No country. No circumstance. The earth, in its entirety, is within the scope of His authority.

**وَلَن نُّعْجِزَهُ هَرَبًا** — we cannot escape Him through flight.

Flight here is the vertical imagination — moving upward, moving outward, moving beyond the boundaries of the current plane. The jinn, who move through dimensions unavailable to human beings, are saying even their modes of movement, even the traversal of unseen realms, does not put them outside the scope.

Together: everywhere you can *be*, and every direction you can *go*. Covered.

Not with the anxious energy of someone checking every exit. With the quiet, settled tone of those who have been through every exit in their minds and have arrived at the same conclusion from each one.

[PAUSE]

The scholars of the heart describe a state called *al-istirāḥa min al-tadbīr* — resting from the burden of self-management. The relief that comes when you stop engineering your own protection. Not negligence — the opposite. It is the recognition that the One in charge sees further than you do, knows more than you do, and holds everything you cannot hold. And that your frantic designing of escape routes — from consequences, from outcomes, from what you know you owe — is not only exhausting but unnecessary.

When the jinn say "we cannot escape Allah" and then let it settle — what they are describing is not defeat. They are describing the end of a project. The exhausting project of positioning yourself outside divine reach, which was always impossible, is finally, fully over. They are simply here. Known. In the grasp.

And from there — from exactly that recognition — the very next verse follows. "And when we heard the guidance, we believed in it." (72:13)

Certainty of sovereignty opens the door to trust. Trust opens the door to belief. Belief changes everything.

[PAUSE]

There is a particular kind of anxiety that lives precisely in the gap between what we know and what we are willing to let act on us. The gap between certainty and submission. You know something is true — about what your soul needs, about what you owe, about the direction your life should face — and you have not yet let that knowing fully land and change you. The running maintains that gap. And the gap is the site of the exhaustion.

The jinn are showing us what it looks like to close the gap. The ẓanna has settled. The yaqin has arrived. They are not still negotiating with it. They are not holding it at arm's length in case some argument emerges that will let them put it down. They have let it fully land.

And having fully landed — the certainty is no longer a weight. It is the ground.

Stand on what you know. Stop running from it. Let the certainty hold you.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we have walked through the linguistic and the thematic — look at the structure of this single ayah from above.

It is a perfectly sealed binary.

لَّن نُّعْجِزَ ٱللَّهَ **فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** — never escape Him on **the earth** — *place: being somewhere*
وَلَن نُّعْجِزَهُ **هَرَبًا** — never escape Him through **flight** — *motion: going somewhere*

Where you are. Where you go.

At any moment you are in one of those two modes. And the Quran has sealed both — not with elaboration, not with argument, but with a clean parallel construction that closes like a hand.

The architecture does not feel like a trap. It feels like a proof. A proof that has been laid out by the jinn themselves — not told to them by an authority, but arrived at by them through the process of looking and seeing and letting what they saw settle.

And the linguistic and thematic work have been pointing in the same direction all along.

The double verb closed every geographical option. The meditation on running showed us that escape was never possible anyway — not because Allah intercepts, but because His authority is the medium through which every movement passes, not a location you can leave behind.

The word ẓanna taught us that certainty is a settling, not a detonation. The exploration of sovereignty taught us that what settles — when it fully lands — is not a cage. It is the end of running. And the end of running is the beginning of something else.

**These are not two different insights. They are the same insight from two angles.**

Certainty of divine sovereignty closes every exit — and in closing every exit, it ends the exhausting project of running — and in ending the running, it opens the possibility of finally being still.

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you. Don't answer them now. Let them work.

1. What are you currently in flight from — and how long has the running been going on? Has the thing you're running from gotten smaller with distance, or has it stayed exactly the same weight?

2. Is there a ẓanna inside you — a quiet knowing about something true — that you have not yet let settle into yaqin? What exactly are you still holding at arm's length, and why?

3. When you encounter the idea that there is nowhere to run from Allah's knowledge, what is the first emotion that moves in you? Fear? Relief? Something complicated? Whatever it is — what does that emotion tell you about your current relationship with Him?

4. The jinn said they became certain — and then, in the next verse, they said they believed. What is the relationship between certainty and belief in your own life? Are there things you are certain of that you have not yet allowed to become belief — because believing would require change?

5. If this ayah is true — if sovereignty is this total, if the medium is this encompassing — what specifically changes about how you spend the next 24 hours?

### The Closing Invitation

The jinn heard the Quran, and something settled in them they could no longer argue against.

You are hearing the Quran right now.

Perhaps something is settling in you too. Perhaps it has been settling for a while.

You do not have to have your entire relationship with faith resolved before you take one step toward it. You do not have to arrive at certainty about everything before you can stand on the certainty you already have.

But consider this honestly: every step you have taken in the direction of running — the avoidance, the delay, the distraction, the "I'll deal with this later" — how has that project been going? Has the thing you're carrying gotten lighter from the distance? Or are you more tired than when you started?

What would it feel like, just for tonight, to stop?

Not to have all the answers. Not to have reached some elevated spiritual state. Just to stop the flight. To stand where you are. To say — with the jinn, in your own way, in whatever words you have:

*Ẓanannā.* We know. We know where we are. We know in whose grasp we find ourselves. And we are not going to keep pretending otherwise.

That is not defeat. That is arrival.

### Duʿāʾ

*Allāhumma* — You told us through the words of those who heard Your book and could no longer run, that there is nowhere to go that is outside Your reach, nowhere to be that is beyond Your knowledge.

Let that truth — which we have half-known, and circled around, and filled our days to avoid sitting with — finally, fully settle in us. Let the ẓanna become yaqin. Let the yaqin become ground.

Grant us the quiet of those who have stopped running. The rest that comes when the exhausting project of escape is finally over. And make us among those who, having let Your sovereignty land — do not search for the exit, but walk through the door that was always open: toward You.

*Āmīn.*
