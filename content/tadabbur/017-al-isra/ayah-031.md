# Do Not Kill Your Children Out of Fear

**Surah Al-Isra, Ayah 31**

> وَلَا تَقْتُلُوٓا۟ أَوْلَـٰدَكُمْ خَشْيَةَ إِمْلَـٰقٍۢ ۖ نَّحْنُ نَرْزُقُهُمْ وَإِيَّاكُمْ ۚ إِنَّ قَتْلَهُمْ كَانَ خِطْـًۭٔا كَبِيرًۭا
>
> *Wa lā taqtulū awlādakum khashyata imlāq — naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum. Inna qatlahum kāna khiṭʾan kabīrā.*
>
> "And do not kill your children out of fear of destitution. It is We who provide for them — and for you. Indeed, their killing has ever been a great sin."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Surah's Quiet Decalogue

Before we touch this ayah, we have to know where it sits.

Surah Al-Isra opens with the night journey — the Prophet ﷺ taken in a single night from Makkah to Jerusalem, then through the heavens. A surah that begins with a journey across the sky descends, by its middle, into the most ordinary corners of human life. Verses 22 through 39 form a sequence so coherent that classical scholars sometimes call it the *waṣāyā* — the testaments. The ten testaments. A near-Decalogue.

It begins: do not associate anything with Allah.

Then: be good to your parents. Give the kin their right. Don't waste. Don't be miserly with your hand. Don't be extravagant. Then verse 30 pauses and says something the listener urgently needs to hear before what's coming next:

> *Inna rabbaka yabsuṭu al-rizqa li-man yashāʾu wa yaqdir — innahu kāna bi-ʿibādihi khabīran baṣīrā.*
>
> "Indeed, your Lord expands provision for whom He wills and constrains it. He has ever been, of His servants, All-Aware, All-Seeing."

And only THEN, immediately after that reminder of who controls rizq — only then does verse 31 land:

> "And do not kill your children out of fear of destitution."

[PAUSE]

So before you even hear the prohibition, the surah has done something to you. It has reminded you that provision is not yours to manufacture. It is loosened and tightened by a Hand you cannot see. And now — knowing that — listen to what some humans do anyway.

### The world this ayah descends into

In the Arabia of the Prophet's ﷺ time, the killing of children — particularly girls — was a documented social practice. The Quran names it elsewhere with searing brevity: *wa idhā al-mawʾūdatu suʾilat, bi-ayyi dhanbin qutilat* — "When the buried-alive girl is asked, for what sin was she killed?" (81:8-9).

Two motivations are recorded. One was *shame* — a daughter was a social vulnerability. That is the sin Allah calls out in Surah Al-Naḥl (16:58-59), where the man's face darkens when given the news of a daughter.

The other motivation — the one this ayah confronts — was *economic*. A child was another mouth. In a desert economy where food and water could vanish in a season, the calculation became: we cannot feed her. We cannot feed him. So they would not let the child live to need feeding.

The horror of it is not just the act. It is the **logic** that produced the act. A logic that began not with cruelty but with a kind of arithmetic. A father looking at his shrinking flocks. A mother watching the rains fail. And the spreadsheet of survival saying: there is not enough.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Watch what Allah does in this ayah. He does not say "do not kill your children because they are innocent." He does not say "do not kill them because life is sacred." Both would have been true. Both would have been sufficient.

But Allah, in His Wisdom, attacks something deeper than the act. He attacks the **premise** the act is built on.

He attacks the **fear**.

And in the way He does it — in a single word that distinguishes this ayah from its near-twin in Surah Al-Anʿām, in the order in which He arranges two pronouns, in a verb whose tense reaches into eternity — He gives us a diagnosis of one of the most relevant illnesses of our age.

Because the literal practice this ayah forbids has largely faded.

But the fear underneath it? The mental act of looking at the future and pre-emptively closing it down? The arithmetic of "I can't afford this, so it shouldn't exist"?

That fear is everywhere now. In our marriages we don't enter. In our children we don't have. In our dreams we won't risk. In the pre-emptive smallness of lives organized entirely around protection from imagined scarcity.

Let me show you what Allah placed in thirteen words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: خَشْيَةَ — *Khashyata*. The Fear, Not the Fact.

Let me start with a single Arabic word.

**خَشْيَةَ — *khashya*.** Fear. Dread. Apprehension.

Before we look at this word, I want to teach a small piece of Arabic grammar that you don't need to know technically — you just need to feel.

In Arabic, when a sentence describes an action, you can attach to that action a kind of footnote that says **why**. Grammarians call this the *mafʿūl li-ajlih* — "the object of cause." It's the slot for motivation. The reason behind the act.

So when a friend tells you "I went to the store" — that's the action. But "I went to the store **for milk**" — that "for milk" is the cause. In Arabic, that "for milk" sits in a particular grammatical case and a particular position, like a tag clipped to the verb.

Now look at the verse:

> *Wa lā taqtulū awlādakum **khashyata** imlāq.*
>
> "And do not kill your children — *out of fear of* destitution."

The word *khashyata* sits in exactly that slot. The "why-tag" attached to the act of killing.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah did NOT say.

He did not say: "Do not kill your children **because of** destitution." He did not say: "Do not kill your children **in** destitution."

He said: **Do not kill them out of FEAR of destitution.**

Stay with this.

The Arabs were not, in 99% of cases, killing children at the moment of starvation. By the time you are starving, you have other problems than family planning. The children were being killed in **anticipation**. In the calculation of a future. In the imagined arrival of a hunger that had not yet come.

Allah is not addressing the actual poor man with no food in his bowl. He is addressing the anxious man with food in his bowl who is afraid the bowl will be empty next year.

He is addressing the **fear**, not the fact.

And here is where a single comparison shatters everything open.

### The Twin Verse — and the One Word That Differs

Turn with me, in your mind, to Surah Al-Anʿām, verse 151. Allah says, in a verse that sounds like the same prohibition:

> *Wa lā taqtulū awlādakum **min** imlāqin — naḥnu narzuqukum wa-iyyāhum.*
>
> "And do not kill your children **from** destitution — We provide for **you** and for them."

Notice three differences. They look small. They are not.

**Difference one:** In Surah Al-Anʿām, the cause-tag is *min imlāq* — "from destitution." In Surah Al-Isra, it is *khashyata imlāq* — "out of fear of destitution."

*Min* means out of, from, because of an actual present condition. *Khashyata* means because of an anticipated, imagined, feared one.

**Difference two:** In Surah Al-Anʿām, Allah says *narzuqukum wa-iyyāhum* — "We provide for **you** AND for them." Parents first. Children second.

In Surah Al-Isra, Allah says *narzuquhum wa-iyyākum* — "We provide for **them** AND for you." Children first. Parents second.

**Difference three:** Different surahs, different audiences, different pastoral situations. And yet — same prohibition.

[PAUSE — sit with this]

Why this asymmetry? It cannot be coincidence. The Quran does not casually rearrange pronouns.

Here is what classical mufassirūn — scholars like al-Rāzī, al-Zamakhsharī, al-Bayḍāwī — drew out of this asymmetry. And what they noticed will reach into your chest.

In Surah Al-Anʿām, the parent is **already poor**. The destitution exists. The bowl is empty. So what does that parent need to hear from Allah? *Your* survival is on My ledger. I provide for **you** — and for them, too. Because the parent who is already in scarcity is anxious about himself first. Allah meets that fear: the parent first.

In Surah Al-Isra, the parent is **not yet poor**. The bowl has food. But the imagination has built a future where it doesn't. So this parent is not afraid for himself today. He is afraid for the child tomorrow. He is killing the child *because* he cannot bear the imagined moment of failing to provide for them.

So Allah says: I provide for **them** first — and for you, too.

Allah meets the actual location of the fear.

Look at the precision. The pronouns are not decoration. They are surgery. Each verse opens the chest at a different angle and sutures the specific wound that drove the act in that specific case.

[PAUSE]

This is the Quran reading the human heart back to itself. Not flattening human beings into one type. Recognizing that there are two parents — the one in present pain and the one in future-tense fear — and naming the **rizq promise** in the exact order each one needs to hear it.

That difference of one word — *khashyata* — and that flip of one pronoun pair, is the whole psychological architecture of this ayah.

It is not "do not kill out of poverty."

It is "do not kill out of the **anticipation** of poverty."

And once you see that — the ayah stops being about pre-Islamic Arabia and starts being about us.

### Element Two: نَحْنُ نَرْزُقُهُمْ — The Architecture of Provision

Now the second element, and I want you to feel this in your chest.

> *Naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum.*

Translate it word by word.

**Naḥnu** — "We."
**Narzuqu-hum** — "We provide for them."
**Wa-iyyākum** — "and you."

In Arabic, the verb *narzuq* already contains "we." The "we" is built into the verb conjugation. You don't need a separate pronoun. So when Allah adds *naḥnu* — "We" — at the beginning, before the verb, He is doing something the grammar does not require.

Arabic grammarians have a name for this. They call it *taqdīm li-l-takhṣīṣ* — fronting for restriction. When you put a pronoun in front of a verb that already contains it, you are doing the equivalent of pointing your finger and saying: **THIS one. Not anyone else.**

It is the difference between saying "I'll handle it" and saying "**I** will handle it."

The first is information. The second is a claim of ownership.

Allah does not say "we provide for them and for you." Allah says: **It is We — and We alone — who provide for them and for you.**

[PAUSE]

Now stay with what He just dismantled.

When the parent looks at the child and thinks "I cannot afford this child," there is a hidden assumption inside that thought. The hidden assumption is: **I am the source.** I am the channel. The food in this child's mouth comes from my hand. If my hand fails, the child starves. So if I cannot guarantee my hand, I cannot let the child exist.

The whole moral arithmetic depends on the parent thinking they are the provider.

Allah's response is not "you are wrong about the future." Allah's response is "you are wrong about the **causality**."

You were never the source.

The child does not eat because you have wealth. The child eats because Allah feeds the child. **And the food just happens to come through your hand.** And on the day your hand has nothing, food will come through some other means. *Naḥnu narzuquhum.* It is We who feed them.

Notice what this does to the entire conversation.

Suddenly the parent is not the provider. The parent is the **agent of distribution**. The wealth of the parent is not the cause of the child's eating; it is the current channel. And channels can change without the river running dry.

This is a shift so deep that if it actually lands in a heart, it ends an entire form of human anxiety.

### Element Three: كَانَ خِطْأً كَبِيرًا — The Eternal Verdict

The third linguistic element. The closing.

> *Inna qatlahum **kāna** khiṭʾan kabīrā.*
>
> "Indeed, their killing has ever been a great sin."

Two words I want you to look at: *kāna* and *khiṭʾ*.

**Kāna** is the past-tense form of "to be." Its surface meaning is "was." But when *kāna* is used in this Quranic register, paired with a verbal adjective and a predicate, it does not mean "was once and is no longer." It means **has always been.** It reaches both backward into the eternal past and forward into the permanent.

When Allah says about Himself *kāna ghafūran raḥīmā* — "He has ever been Forgiving, Merciful" — He is not saying He used to be. He is saying His mercy has no beginning and no end. The *kāna* in that register is the verb of timeless reality.

Now apply that here.

*Inna qatlahum kāna khiṭʾan kabīrā* — their killing **has ever been** a great sin.

This was not declared a sin in 7th-century Arabia. This sin existed before the Quran was revealed. It existed before the Prophet ﷺ was born. It existed in the rule book of every prophet from Adam onward. It existed in the moral structure of creation itself.

In other words: this was not new law. This was eternal law that the Quran was finally **naming aloud** to a society that had forgotten it.

And the word *khiṭʾ* — what does that mean?

Lexicographers like al-Rāghib distinguish *khiṭʾ* from related words. *Dhanb* is sin in the sense of consequence. *Ithm* is sin in the sense of taint. *Khiṭʾ* — from the root *kh-ṭ-ʾ*, which gives us the word *khaṭaʾ* (mistake, error, missing the target) — names the act as a **catastrophic missing of the mark**.

It says: this was an action where you did not just sin. You **inverted the entire purpose** of what a parent is for.

A parent exists to keep the child alive. The killing of one's own child is not just sin layered on a basically functioning relationship — it is the inversion of the relationship itself. It is missing the mark by 180 degrees.

And then *kabīr*. **Great.** Not just a missing of the mark. A great missing of the mark. A categorical, monumental inversion.

[PAUSE]

So what we have, in the closing of this verse, is Allah closing the file on this question forever.

**It has ever been.** No era escapes the verdict.
**A khiṭʾ.** Not just sinful — the inversion of purpose.
**Great.** Of the highest order.

The case is closed. There will be no era — pre-Islamic, Islamic, modern, post-modern — in which this is anything but what Allah has named it.

### Bridge

Three linguistic moves. Look at what they have done together.

The first — *khashyata* — diagnosed the cause. Not poverty. Fear of poverty. Imagined poverty. Anticipated poverty.

The second — *naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum* — dismantled the false premise underneath the fear. You are not the source. You were never the source.

The third — *kāna khiṭʾan kabīrā* — sealed the verdict in eternity. There is no temporal escape from this judgment.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Economics of Imagined Scarcity

Let me describe a man you may know.

He is thirty-four. He has a job. He has, by any historical standard, a great deal of food in his refrigerator. His parents survived on a fraction of what he earns. His grandparents survived on less than that. By the metrics of human history he is — and this is just a fact — wealthy.

He is also exhausted by the calculation. Every month he runs a spreadsheet in his head: rent, insurance, savings rate, the cost of childcare in his city, the cost of private school in fifteen years if it comes to that, the cost of college tuition in twenty years, the medical bill for the surgery he has not had and may never need.

He is, in his own description, "drowning."

He has a wife. They are, in their own description, "not ready for kids yet." When you ask why, he uses the language of math. We can't afford it. We need to wait until we can afford it. We don't want to bring a child into a situation where we can't give it what it needs.

The child, in this framing, is a financial event. Like a mortgage. Something you do not enter into without securing the means.

And so the years pass. Thirty-four becomes thirty-eight. Thirty-eight becomes forty-two. The window narrows. The arithmetic never resolves, because there is no number at which "enough" is reached. The arithmetic was never the problem.

The problem was the **fear**.

[PAUSE]

I am not telling you that financial planning is wrong. The Prophet ﷺ taught responsibility. The Quran condemns waste. There is a real, sober adulthood in counting the cost of an act before committing to it.

But what this ayah names — and what we have to be honest enough to hear — is that there is a kind of fear that **disguises itself as planning**.

That fear has a particular signature. Its signature is: **the calculation never ends, and the answer is always "not yet."**

If you find yourself in a calculation like that — about a marriage, about a child, about a move, about anything that involves trusting the future to provide — you have to ask the diagnostic question this ayah teaches you to ask.

**Am I responding to a fact, or to a fear?**

The fact has a shape. The fact says: I have $X coming in, $Y going out, here is the actual gap, here is what I would need to close it. The fact is finite. You can negotiate with it.

The fear has no shape. The fear keeps generating new categories of cost. The fear keeps moving the goalposts. The fear says "but what if" forever.

The fact is *imlāq*. The fear is *khashyatu imlāqin*.

And only one of those two is actually in front of you.

#### What Allah is doing in this ayah

He is not telling the parent to ignore the future. He is telling the parent that **the future is not the parent's to provision.**

The arrogance hidden inside chronic financial anxiety is a strange one. It looks like humility — "I'm just being responsible." But it contains a hidden assumption: **I am the one who has to make the future safe.** If I do not see how the food gets to the child in 2034, the food does not get to the child in 2034.

Allah's response is *naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum.*

You did not even feed yourself last week. You think you fed yourself. You earned a salary, you bought groceries, you cooked food. But the rain that grew the wheat — was that yours? The cells in your hand that gripped the knife — were those yours? The breath that allowed your body to digest what it ate — was that yours?

You have never fed yourself a single day of your life. You have only ever **received** food, through whatever channels Allah opened that day.

The same Allah who fed you yesterday will feed your child tomorrow. The mechanism of the feeding is His to design. You are not the engineer. You are a tool the engineer uses, gladly, when you make yourself available.

[PAUSE]

This does not make planning frivolous. It makes planning **light**. You plan from rest, not from terror. You make decisions about money the way you make decisions about traffic — practically, but without the assumption that one wrong move will end the world.

The world is not held together by your spreadsheet.

#### The practical shift

So here is what this theme asks of you, before tomorrow morning.

When the next financial fear comes — and it will, because you are alive in a culture engineered to manufacture financial fear at scale — try the diagnostic.

Pause. Ask: am I responding to a fact in front of me, or to a forecast inside me?

If the answer is "a forecast" — name it. *This is khashya, not imlāq.* And then return the matter to its actual owner: *naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum.*

Then act, calmly, on the fact only.

That is the disciplined freedom this ayah is teaching.

### Theme Two: You Are Not the Provider — and That Is the Mercy

I want to slow down on something that has been quietly humming under the whole ayah.

There is a particular pain that shows up in the lives of conscientious parents — and conscientious people generally — that we don't talk about enough.

It is the pain of believing **you are the only thing standing between your loved ones and disaster.**

Talk to a father who works ninety hours a week. Underneath the ambition there is often a story. A story that says: *if I stop, they fall.* My family eats because I work. My children's education is funded because I produce. The house, the safety, the future — all of it sits on my shoulders, and if my shoulders give out, everything collapses.

This story is so widespread that we treat it as a virtue. We call it "providing." We say of a man, approvingly: he is a provider.

But the Quran reserves that title for someone else.

*Allāhu huwa al-razzāq dhū al-quwwati al-matīn* — "Indeed, Allah — He is the Provider, the Possessor of Strength, the Firm" (51:58). The definite article. **The** Provider. Not *a* provider. **The**.

[PAUSE]

What does it do to the human heart to take a title that belongs to Allah and wear it as one's own?

It crushes the human. Because the title comes with a burden the human cannot carry. To be **the** provider for a family means: their lives are on you. Their futures are on you. Their wellbeing is the weight you carry to bed each night.

Nobody can carry that. So the human who tries to does one of two things. Either they break — silently, internally, year by year, in ways their family does not see. Or they refuse the situation that requires the carrying. They do not get married. They do not have children. They do not commit. Because the burden of being **the** provider is too crushing to take on, and they have not been told that nobody was ever asking them to.

This ayah is one of the places in the Quran where Allah gently, and firmly, takes the title back.

*Naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum.*

You are not the provider. You were never the provider. The child eats because **I** feed them. The food comes through your hand because that is the design today. But the child is My responsibility, not yours, and your responsibility is something else entirely — not to *be* the source, but to **show up** as the channel.

Show up for the child. Work, plan, exert yourself, save where you can save, give where you should give. But carry it the way a mailman carries a letter. The letter is not yours. You did not write it. You did not authorize it. You are entrusted with delivery for a stretch of road, and then someone else picks up where you left off.

[PAUSE]

This reframe matters because of a paradox that classical scholars noticed in this ayah.

The pre-Islamic Arab who killed his daughter out of *khashyata imlāq* believed he was being **responsible**. He was, in his own mind, sparing her a worse fate. He was preventing a future suffering by ending a present life. His logic, in his own ears, was the logic of a careful man.

This is what fear-disguised-as-responsibility looks like at its most extreme.

And Allah's response is to expose the flaw at the root: **you assumed you were responsible for an outcome that was never yours to determine.** You took on a weight no human shoulder is built for, and then you committed an atrocity in service of that weight.

The atrocity could not have happened without the weight.

Which means — and this is the unbearable, beautiful implication — the way you protect yourself from atrocities of *khashya* is by **putting down the weight you were never asked to carry.**

The man who knows in his bones that he is not the provider does not kill his child to spare them poverty. Because he knows poverty is not his to administer. The father who knows in his bones that he is not the provider does not work himself to a heart attack to "give them everything." Because he knows "everything" was never his to give. The mother who knows in her bones that she is not the provider does not collapse under the imagined weight of a future her child has not yet entered. Because she knows that future has its own Provider already on duty.

Putting down the weight is not the abdication of responsibility. It is the **right-sizing** of responsibility. A finite human carrying finite obligations under an Infinite Provider whose Hand is the actual support of the world.

#### The practical shift

Tomorrow, when you feel the weight of providing — for your spouse, your children, your aging parents, anyone who depends on you — say it out loud, even quietly to yourself.

*Naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum.*

I am not the provider. I am a tool the Provider is using. My job is to be a clean, available tool. The outcome is not mine.

Watch what happens to the weight.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Look at this ayah now from above, after we have walked through it.

Allah does something architectural here, and we can finally see it:

**He names the act.** *Lā taqtulū awlādakum* — do not kill your children.

**He diagnoses the cause.** *Khashyata imlāqin* — out of fear of destitution. Not destitution. *Fear of* it.

**He dismantles the premise.** *Naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum* — and notice the order: them first, you second. Calibrated to the exact location of the fear in this kind of parent.

**He seals the verdict.** *Kāna khiṭʾan kabīrā* — has ever been. No era of human history can rewrite this.

A four-step movement: act named, cause diagnosed, premise dismantled, verdict sealed. In thirteen words.

And when you place this verse beside its near-twin in Surah Al-Anʿām (6:151), you see something else. Allah has not given us one verse for one situation. He has given us **two verses for two situations**, with the pronouns flipped to address each one with precision. The parent in actual poverty hears "We provide for **you** and for them." The parent in imagined poverty hears "We provide for **them** and for you." The same God meeting two human conditions with the same promise but the order calibrated to where each soul needs to hear it first.

This is what Quranic precision looks like. Not flat repetition. Not aesthetic flourish. A clinician who knows two different patients and gives each the medicine in the order their symptoms require.

### The Two Halves of One Insight

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistics showed us *khashyata* — that Allah is naming **fear**, not fact, as the trigger of the act. And it showed us the fronted *naḥnu* — that the prohibition is paired with the dismantling of the false claim of human providership.

The themes showed us where this lives in our own lives. In the fear that disguises itself as planning. In the weight of providership we were never authorized to carry.

The two halves meet at one place: **the moment a human being mistakes a forecast for a fact, and mistakes themselves for a god.**

That is what this ayah is for. To unmake that double mistake before it produces its catastrophe.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life right now am I making decisions out of *khashyata imlāq* — fear of imagined scarcity — rather than response to actual scarcity? What am I refusing to do because of a future I have not yet met?

2. What title am I wearing that belongs to Allah? Where am I quietly believing I am **the** provider, **the** protector, **the** one who holds it all together — when the Quran reserves that title for Someone Else?

3. When I read *naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum*, where does it land in me? Do I believe Allah is the Provider in theory but live as if I am the provider in practice? What would change about tomorrow if I actually believed the words?

4. Are there relationships I have **not entered** — a marriage I have postponed, a child I have not had, a commitment I will not make — because I am running an arithmetic that has no termination point? What would it mean to plan from rest instead of from terror?

5. The verse closes with *kāna khiṭʾan kabīrā* — "has ever been a great sin." What forms of pre-emptive ending of life — life of relationships, life of dreams, life of possibilities — am I committing in milder forms because I cannot bear the imagined cost of letting them live?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When fear of an imagined poverty drives you to end something Allah authorized to live, the deepest sin is not the ending — it is the moment you mistook yourself for the source of provision.

### The Closing Invitation

If something in this ayah opens for you, let the shift be small.

Tomorrow, when the financial calculation begins in your chest — pause and ask: fact or fear? When the weight of providing for someone settles on your shoulders — pause and remember: the title is not yours. When the urge to pre-empt a future, to close down a possibility, to refuse a commitment because you cannot guarantee its provision — pause and let *naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum* do its quiet, dismantling work.

You do not have to feel certain. You only have to act lightly enough that the certainty of Allah is what carries the weight, not the certainty of your spreadsheet.

That is the freedom this ayah is offering. The freedom to let your children — your real children, the ones you have or might have, and the metaphorical children of your dreams and undertakings — **live**.

### Du'a

O Allah, You are *al-Razzāq*. We are not. Take from us the burden of a title that was never ours, and let us live as channels of Your provision instead of as terrified gods over outcomes we cannot govern. When fear of an imagined poverty rises in our chests, replace it with the trust that *naḥnu narzuquhum wa-iyyākum* — that You feed our beloveds and us, both, from a Hand that has never failed. Make us parents and partners and friends who do not pre-empt the futures You have authorized to live. *Āmīn.*
