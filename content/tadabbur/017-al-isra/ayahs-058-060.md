# When Signs Become Accountability, Fear Itself Can Expose the Heart

## Surah Al-Isra (17:58-60)

[ayah:17:58] وَإِن مِّن قَرْيَةٍ إِلَّا نَحْنُ مُهْلِكُوهَا قَبْلَ يَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَـٰمَةِ أَوْ مُعَذِّبُوهَا عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا ۚ كَانَ ذَٰلِكَ فِى ٱلْكِتَـٰبِ مَسْطُورًا

## Introduction

Human beings often ask for signs as though signs are harmless.

Show me more. Make it undeniable. If Allah really wanted people to believe, why not send a miracle so clear that no one could resist?

These verses answer that desire with history.

The problem with a sign is not that Allah is unable to send it. The problem is that a sign intensifies responsibility. Once a people asks for a decisive sign and then denies it, the sign they demanded becomes evidence against them. Thamud asked, received the she-camel, saw a sign they could not reduce to ordinary life, and then wronged it.

So the passage is not saying signs do not matter. It is saying they matter so much that they are not toys for a defiant people.

Then verse 60 brings that principle into the Prophet's own mission. The vision shown to him becomes a trial for people. The cursed tree in the Qur'an becomes a trial. Warning itself becomes a trial. The same frightening disclosure that should humble one heart can harden another.

That is the terrifying lesson: sometimes the problem is not absence of evidence, but the heart's response to evidence.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. `Wa-in min qaryatin illa`: No Community Is Outside the Written Horizon

The opening grammar is sweeping:

`wa-in min qaryatin illa`

There is no town except...

This construction creates a universal net. No `qaryah`, no settled human community, no built world of markets, homes, customs, alliances, and public confidence sits outside Allah's decree. The verse then gives two possibilities: destruction before the Day of Resurrection or severe punishment.

The force is not only eschatological. It is historical. Every community lives under an ending.

Then:

`kana dhalika fi al-kitabi mastura`

That is inscribed in the Book.

`Mastur` comes from writing in lines, something recorded, set down, not improvised. The fate of communities is not chaos. It belongs to a written order. Ibn Kathir specifies that this decree is not a vague sense of destiny but something already written in al-Lawh al-Mahfuz, the Preserved Tablet held with Allah, before the town ever rose; al-Muyassar names the same tablet, calling it a decree Allah has already settled and made binding so that its occurrence is inevitable. Al-Tabari keeps the meaning anchored in Allah's prior decree, and reports from the early commentators (as Mujahid transmits) that a measure of this — ruin or affliction — will touch *every* town on earth before the Hour. The ending of a community, then, is not an accident that befalls it; it is a line already written waiting to be read aloud in time.

### 2. `Wa-ma mana'ana ... illa an kadhdhaba biha al-awwalun`: Withheld Signs Are Mercy Against a Worse Accountability

Verse 59 begins with what sounds like restraint:

`wa-ma mana'ana an nursila bi-al-ayati illa an kadhdhaba biha al-awwalun`

Nothing prevented Us from sending the signs except that the former peoples denied them.

The verb `mana'a` means to prevent or hold back. But because the speaker is Allah, we must hear it carefully. Nothing external prevents Him. The restraint belongs to wisdom. He is not unable; He is choosing not to send certain demanded signs because history has already shown what happens when decisive signs meet defiant hearts.

Ibn Kathir preserves a striking narrative behind this verse, on the authority of Sa'id ibn Jubayr. The idolaters of Makkah pressed the Prophet ﷺ: you claim earlier prophets had the wind made subject to them, or could raise the dead — so ask your Lord to turn al-Safa into gold for us, and then we will believe. Allah revealed to His Messenger that He had heard them, and gave him a choice: the sign could be granted, *but* if they then disbelieved they would be destroyed as no community had been destroyed before; or the door could be left open to mercy and repentance. The Prophet ﷺ chose the door of mercy. Al-Tabari and al-Muyassar read the verse along the same line: the earlier nations asked for signs exactly as the Makkans did, received them, denied them anyway, and were overtaken at once — so to withhold the demanded sign from this community was to spare it that same swift finality.

That changes how we think about unanswered demands. Sometimes Allah does not give the thing a person demands because receiving it would not save them. It would only make their rejection more damning. The unanswered request can itself be an act of protection — a reprieve dressed as a refusal.

### 3. `Al-naqata mubsiratan fa-zalamu biha`: The She-Camel Was a Seeing Sign, and They Wronged Through It

Then Allah names the example:

`wa-atayna Thamuda al-naqata mubsiratan`

We gave Thamud the she-camel as `mubsirah`.

The word is usually rendered clear or visible, but the root is sight. It is a sign that makes things seen, a sign that should have produced insight. Al-Muyassar identifies Thamud as the people of the prophet Salih, and calls the she-camel a `mu'jizah wadihah` — a manifest miracle — not an argument to be debated but a wonder standing openly inside the daily life of the people.

Then:

`fa-zalamu biha`

The common English rendering, "they wronged her," is not wrong, but it quietly drops a preposition the mufassirun notice. The `bi` in `zalamu biha` is instrumental: they committed `zulm` *by means of her*, *through* her. The she-camel was the occasion and instrument of an injustice whose ultimate victims were themselves — they harmed her, and in doing so it was their own destruction they were writing. The `fa` makes the movement painfully immediate. Gift, then violation. Sign, then injustice. In the broader Qur'an, Thamud's story shows that the she-camel came with a moral boundary: leave her to drink, do not harm her. The test was not whether they could explain the sign. The test was whether they would restrain themselves before it.

This is what signs do. They do not merely increase information. They create responsibility.

### 4. `Wa-ma nursilu bi-al-ayati illa takhwifa`: Signs Are Sent to Frighten Awake, Not to Entertain Curiosity

The end of verse 59 gives the principle:

`wa-ma nursilu bi-al-ayati illa takhwifa`

We do not send the signs except as warning.

`Takhwif` is frightening, but not pointless terror. It is fear meant to wake the heart before the final consequence arrives. Al-Muyassar draws out the purpose exactly: the signs and lessons and miracles placed in the messengers' hands are sent as `takhwif li-l-'ibad` — a frightening of the servants *so that they might take heed and remember* (`li-ya'tabiru wa yatadhakkaru`). The fear is instrumental, aimed at return. The Qur'an does not treat fear as a primitive emotion to be discarded. It treats rightly directed fear as mercy when it interrupts arrogance.

But fear only helps if the heart is still willing to be corrected.

That is why verse 60 becomes so sobering. Allah says He frightens them, yet:

`fa-ma yaziduhum illa tughyanan kabira`

It only increases them in great transgression.

The same warning that should have restrained them becomes, through their disease, another occasion for rebellion.

### 5. `Ahata bi-al-nas ... al-ru'ya ... al-shajarah al-mal'unah`: Trial Can Come Through What Allah Shows and What Allah Names

Verse 60 opens with reassurance to the Prophet:

`inna rabbaka ahata bi-al-nas`

Your Lord has encompassed the people.

`Ahata` is surrounding, enclosing, comprehending. Allah surrounds them in knowledge, power, and decree. Their mockery is not outside Him. Their reaction to signs is not beyond Him. The word is a shield handed to the Messenger: whatever they say or scheme, they are already enclosed.

Then:

`wa-ma ja'alna al-ru'ya allati araynaka illa fitnatan li-l-nas`

We did not make the vision We showed you except as a trial for people.

Classical tafsir commonly connects this `ru'ya` to what the Prophet was shown on the Night Journey, though the commentators record other possibilities as well. The important point for the passage is clear: even what Allah shows His Messenger can become a trial for listeners. A truthful disclosure does not automatically soften every heart.

Then the verse names:

`wa-al-shajarata al-mal'unata fi al-Qur'an`

the cursed tree in the Qur'an.

Ibn Kathir and others identify this with the tree of Zaqqum, whose mention became a point of mockery for deniers — some scoffed that a tree could grow in a fire. Again, the content that should frighten awake becomes a test. Some hearts hear Jahannam and tremble. Others hear it and joke.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Demanding More Evidence Can Sometimes Be a Way of Avoiding the Evidence Already Given

There is a kind of question that is not really a question.

It asks for one more proof, one more sign, one more special event, one more impossible thing. But beneath the request is not hunger for truth. It is postponement. If this arrives, then maybe I will believe. If Allah shows me this, then I will change. If the proof becomes dramatic enough, then I will surrender.

The Qur'an is deeply suspicious of that posture.

Not because signs are weak, but because the human heart can turn even signs into delay. The former peoples denied signs. Thamud wronged the she-camel. The people around the Prophet turned vision and warning into fitnah. The Makkans who wanted al-Safa turned to gold would, Allah warned, have found in that very gold the ground of their ruin.

[PAUSE]

This is painfully modern. Many people do not need more information before obeying what they already know. They need honesty. They know enough to pray. Enough to stop the obvious sin. Enough to apologize. Enough to leave the haram income. Enough to ask forgiveness. But the nafs asks for another sign because another sign feels easier than immediate submission.

One mercy of this passage is that it redirects us from spectacle to response. The question is not: what additional sign would finally force me to become sincere? The question is: what have I already been shown, and what am I doing with it?

### Theme 2: Fear Is Mercy When It Arrives Before the Door Closes

The phrase `illa takhwifa` rescues fear from being misunderstood.

Fear in the Qur'an is not always despair. It is not always spiritual immaturity. When Allah frightens a heart awake before destruction, that fear is mercy wearing severity.

A doctor who calmly names a dangerous diagnosis is not being cruel. A parent who shouts when a child walks toward a fire is not being harsh in the blameworthy sense. A revelation that warns of punishment, destroyed towns, Thamud, Zaqqum, and the written fate of communities is not trying to traumatize the listener. It is trying to interrupt the trance.

Al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir both preserve the warning function of signs. The sign is not magic for entertainment; it is hujjah, proof and warning. Al-Muyassar's concise framing keeps the same meaning: signs are sent to make people fear so that they might take heed, remember, and return.

[PAUSE]

The question is whether we let fear do its merciful work.

If fear makes us pray, repent, return rights, repair speech, and stop walking toward harm, it has served us. But if fear makes us mock, numb, argue, or become more defiant, then the disease is deeper than lack of warning.

That is why the end of verse 60 is so frightening. `Nukhawwifuhum` - We frighten them. But it only increases them in `tughyanan kabira`, great transgression. The warning was real. The problem was what their hearts did with it.

### Theme 3: A Sign Is Not Only Something You See; It Is Something That Places You Under Responsibility

The she-camel of Thamud is one of the clearest examples of this principle.

She was not merely a wonder. She was a boundary. Her presence in the community created an obligation: do not harm her, give her her appointed drink. The miracle did not end the moment it was witnessed; it began there. To *see* the sign was to be enrolled, that same day, in a duty. This is precisely why the Qur'an calls her `mubsirah` — a sign that makes seen — and then reports, with the immediacy of the `fa`, that they wronged themselves through her. The clearer the sign, the sharper the accountability it lays on the one who receives it.

That is the frightening symmetry running through all three verses. A written decree over every town. A demanded miracle withheld as mercy. A living she-camel given and violated. A vision shown and a tree named — both turned into trials. In every case the sign is not neutral. It weighs the one who receives it. It does not merely inform; it obligates, and then it exposes.

[PAUSE]

Brought home, this dismantles a quiet fantasy — that if only we knew more, saw more, were shown more, we would surely be better. But every increase in what we are shown is an increase in what we are answerable for. The person who has heard the ayah, understood the boundary, felt the warning land — that person is now, like Thamud before the camel, standing under a duty they did not have an hour ago.

And over all of it stands `inna rabbaka ahata bi-al-nas`. Your Lord has encompassed the people. Nothing we do with the signs — restraint or violation, trembling or mockery — falls outside His knowing. The signs test us; His encompassing means the test is never unwitnessed.

So the prayer this passage leaves us with is not *show me more*. It is: let what You have already shown me do its merciful work — let it frighten me awake, not harden me; let it enroll me in duty, not in delay; and let me not be among those whom warning only increases in transgression.
