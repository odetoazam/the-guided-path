# The One Who Wants the Immediate Gets Only What Allah Wills, but the Hereafter-Seeker Finds His Striving Thanked

## Surah Al-Isra (17:18-21)

[ayah:17:18] مَّن كَانَ يُرِيدُ ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ عَجَّلْنَا لَهُۥ فِيهَا مَا نَشَآءُ لِمَن نُّرِيدُ ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَا لَهُۥ جَهَنَّمَ يَصْلَىٰهَا مَذْمُومًا مَّدْحُورًا

[ayah:17:19] وَمَنْ أَرَادَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ وَسَعَىٰ لَهَا سَعْيَهَا وَهُوَ مُؤْمِنٌ فَأُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ كَانَ سَعْيُهُم مَّشْكُورًا

[ayah:17:20] كُلًّا نُّمِدُّ هَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ وَهَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ مِنْ عَطَآءِ رَبِّكَ ۚ وَمَا كَانَ عَطَآءُ رَبِّكَ مَحْظُورًا

[ayah:17:21] ٱنظُرْ كَيْفَ فَضَّلْنَا بَعْضَهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ ۚ وَلَلْـَٔاخِرَةُ أَكْبَرُ دَرَجَـٰتٍ وَأَكْبَرُ تَفْضِيلًا

*Man kana yuridu al-'ajilata 'ajjalna lahu fiha ma nasha'u liman nurid, thumma ja'alna lahu Jahannama yaslaha madhmuman madhura.*

*Wa man arada al-akhirata wa sa'a laha sa'yaha wa huwa mu'minun fa-ula'ika kana sa'yuhum mashkura.*

**Whoever desires the immediate life, We hasten for him in it whatever We will, for whom We intend; then We appoint for him Jahannam, where he will burn, blamed and banished. But whoever desires the Hereafter and strives for it with the striving due to it while being a believer, those are the ones whose striving is appreciated. Each group We extend, these and those, from the gift of your Lord, and the gift of your Lord is never restricted. Look how We have favored some of them over others, but the Hereafter is greater in degrees and greater in distinction.**

## Introduction

One of the most dangerous confusions in human life is to mistake receipt for approval.

If I got it, I assume it was good.

If I rose, I assume I was right.

If doors opened, I assume Allah must be pleased.

[PAUSE]

These verses shatter that confusion. They tell us that the one seeking `al-'ajilah`, the immediate world, may indeed receive from it. But what he receives is only what Allah wills and only for whom He wills. Ibn Kathir draws attention to exactly this: not everyone who desires this world and its luxuries actually gets what he wants — that is granted only to those whom Allah wills, in the measure He wills. He notes that this verse narrows down the more general statements made elsewhere in the Qur'an about worldly provision. Provision is real, access is real, visible success is real, and none of that by itself proves final favor. By contrast, the one who wants the Hereafter, strives for it in the way it deserves, and remains a believer finds something astonishing: his striving is `mashkura`, thanked, appreciated, received by Allah.

Then the passage explains why our surface judgments so often fail: Allah extends to both groups from His gift. Dunya distribution is not the final scoreboard. The scoreboard that matters is in the Akhirah, where the degrees are greater and the distinction greater still.

The hook of the passage is this: **Al-Isra teaches that what ultimately defines a life is not what it managed to obtain in the immediate world, but what it truly wanted, how it strove for it, and whether that striving was carried by faith toward the Hereafter.**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### 1. **Man kana yuridu al-'ajilah**: The Problem Is Not Merely Possessing the Dunya, but Wanting the Immediate as the Final Horizon

The verse begins:

**man kana yuridu al-'ajilah**

Whoever desires the immediate.

Not merely the world as one sphere of human responsibility, but `al-'ajilah` - the rushed, the near, the quick-arriving, the immediate gratification horizon.

[PAUSE]

This is subtle and important. The Quran is not condemning lawful work, provision, family, or human flourishing in this life. It is exposing an orientation in which the heart wants what comes fastest and nearest as though that were the real measure of life.

It is worth being precise here about whom the classical scholars understood this verse to describe. Al-Tabari fills out the portrait: the one meant is he whose whole pursuit is the immediate world, who works and strives and craves for it alone, *"who is not certain of any return, and hopes for neither reward nor punishment from his Lord for his deeds."* Al-Muyassar reads it the same way — one *"who did not affirm the Hereafter and did not act for it."* So the primary referent of this ayah is not simply a distracted believer, but the person whose horizon has collapsed entirely into the here-and-now because he has cut the Hereafter out of his reckoning. The warning still reaches every heart that lets the immediate crowd out the eternal — but tadabbur honesty requires us to name that the verse's sharpest edge, as the classical commentators read it, falls on the one who lives for the near world *because* he does not truly believe in the far one.

Then comes the divine qualification:

**'ajjalna lahu fiha ma nasha'u liman nurid**

We hasten for him in it whatever We will, for whom We intend.

This destroys entitlement. Even the dunya-seeker does not obtain the world on his own terms. He gets only what Allah wills, in the amount Allah wills, for the person Allah wills. Al-Tabari underlines that "whatever We will" is genuinely open in both directions: it may mean the *expansion* of the world upon him, or its *restriction* — even his ruin through the punishments Allah decrees. Al-Muyassar ties it to the decree already written: what is hastened for him is only *"what Allah has recorded for him in the Preserved Tablet."* So the immediate world is not self-owned territory, and abundance is not even guaranteed to the one who chases it. It is still, entirely, under divine administration.

Then the terrifying end:

**thumma ja'alna lahu Jahannama yaslaha madhmuman madhura**

Then We appoint for him Jahannam, where he burns, blamed and banished.

`Madhmuman` means blameworthy, deserving censure — and al-Tabari specifies the ground of the blame: it is *for his ingratitude* and for the poor use he made of the very favors Allah had already placed in his hands in the dunya. `Madhura` (from the root د-ح-ر, to drive off) carries being expelled, pushed off, driven away — al-Tabari and al-Muyassar both gloss it as being *driven from the mercy* of Allah. The final result of living only for the immediate is not simply the loss of a better option. It is disgrace: censured for one's ingratitude, and expelled from the mercy one never reached for.

### 2. **Wa sa'a laha sa'yaha wa huwa mu'min**: The Hereafter Is Not Reached by Wishfulness, but by Fitting Striving Carried by Faith

Then the contrasting path:

**wa man arada al-akhirah**

Whoever desires the Hereafter.

Desire is still present. Islam does not kill wanting. It reorders it.

[PAUSE]

But wanting alone is not enough:

**wa sa'a laha sa'yaha**

and strives for it with the striving due to it.

This is a powerful formulation. The Hereafter has a `sa'y` that belongs to it. Al-Tabari reads "the striving due to it" as concretely *the obedience of Allah and what earns His pleasure* — it must be sought in the mode appropriate to it: obedience, repentance, sincerity, patience, worship, moral struggle, and the long work of inward reordering. You cannot want Akhirah with a dunya-shaped effort.

Then:

**wa huwa mu'min**

while he is a believer.

Faith is not an accessory added after effort. It is the living ground that makes the striving truly oriented. And notice how precisely this word answers the first man: al-Tabari points out that `mu'min` here means *one who affirms the reward of Allah and the greatness of His recompense — not denying it as the seeker of the immediate denied it.* The believer's faith is the exact mirror-image of the first person's disbelief. Where the dunya-seeker's horizon collapsed because he did not credit the Hereafter, the believer's striving stands upright because he does.

Then comes one of the most tender words in this part of the surah:

**fa-ula'ika kana sa'yuhum mashkura**

their striving is appreciated, thanked.

Imagine that. Allah speaks of the believer's labor as `mashkur`. The One who needs nothing receives the servant's stumbling, sincere, imperfect effort with gratitude-language. Al-Muyassar adds a beautiful dimension to what "thanked" means in practice: their work is *accepted and stored up — treasured (`mudakhkhar`) for them with their Lord* — kept safe until the day they will be rewarded for it in full. Their striving is not only received; it is banked.

### 3. **Kullan numiddu ... wa la-al-akhiratu akbaru darajatin**: Dunya Distribution Is Wide, but It Is Not the Final Measure

Then the passage clarifies the worldly scene:

**kullan numiddu ha'ula'i wa ha'ula'i min 'ata'i rabbik**

Each group We extend - these and those - from the gift of your Lord.

[PAUSE]

This is the ayah that stops us from making simplistic conclusions. The dunya-seeker may be given. The Akhirah-seeker may also be given. Provision crosses categories. Allah's `ata'` is broad.

Then:

**wa ma kana 'ata'u rabbika mahzura**

The gift of your Lord is not restricted.

So worldly spread, resources, openings, and capacities are not themselves proof of closeness.

Then comes the interpretive key:

**unzur kayfa faddalna ba'dahum 'ala ba'd**

Look how We favored some over others.

Yes, there are visible gradations in the world. But then the ayah immediately corrects our scale:

**wa la-al-akhiratu akbaru darajatin wa akbaru tafdila**

The Hereafter is greater in degrees and greater in distinction.

The world's differentials are real. They are just not ultimate.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: The Deep Question Is Not "What Did You Get?" but "What Were You Living For?"

These verses bring moral clarity to a world obsessed with outcomes.

We are trained to ask: what did you achieve? what did you accumulate? what doors opened? what status did you reach?

[PAUSE]

The Quran asks a prior question: what did you actually want?

`Man kana yuridu al-'ajilah.`  
`Wa man arada al-akhirah.`

The axis is desire before result.

That is sobering, because two people can outwardly perform similar actions while inwardly moving toward opposite worlds. One can work hard because he wants to be seen, secured, praised, envied, or soothed by the immediate. Another can work hard because he wants Allah, wants the Akhirah, wants his striving to survive death.

And as the classical readings make clear, desire in this passage runs all the way down to belief itself. The seeker of the immediate, in al-Tabari's and al-Muyassar's portrait, does not merely prefer the world — he has stopped counting on the Hereafter at all. The seeker of the Akhirah does not merely add a good deed — he *affirms* the reward, and that affirmation is what makes his labor eligible to be thanked. What you want, at the deepest level, is inseparable from what you believe is real.

The dunya often hides that distinction because both people may receive something. But the ayah tells us that distribution is not disclosure. Final disclosure belongs to the Hereafter.

### Theme 2: Allah Can Give the World Broadly Without Giving It the Meaning We Project Onto It

One of the great sources of fitnah is that Allah gives widely in the dunya.

These and those.

The righteous and the rebellious.

The grateful and the heedless.

[PAUSE]

That means dunya is not a clean mirror of rank.

It is a field of test.

This is why people become spiritually confused by wealth, fame, influence, talent, visibility, and access. They assume that if Allah gave it, it must mean He endorsed the whole inner orientation behind it. But the ayah refuses that reading. And recall al-Tabari's warning from the first verse: even what looks like abundance is only ever "what We will" — it can be expansion or contraction, sustenance or the slow rope of a punishment. The gift itself carries no verdict.

He gives. Broadly.

Yet `wa la-al-akhiratu akbaru darajatin wa akbaru tafdila`.

So the believer learns to loosen his obsession with comparative worldly placement. Yes, Allah has favored some over others in visible things. The ayah itself tells you to look at that. But it tells you to look at it in order to be corrected, not seduced. The greater hierarchy has not yet appeared.

This is deeply freeing. It means you do not need the dunya to prove your worth. You need to know what you are seeking and whether your striving belongs to the Hereafter.

## Closing Synthesis

These verses rescue us from one of the oldest illusions: that immediate attainment tells us the truth about a life.

It does not.

[PAUSE]

Some are given because Allah willed to give them in this world.

Some are denied because Allah willed to deny them in this world.

Some who are given are moving toward loss.

Some who struggle quietly are moving toward a `sa'y mashkur`, a striving thanked by Allah — and, as al-Muyassar reminds us, treasured up with their Lord until the day it is repaid.

So the passage teaches us to re-read our own lives.

Not first by asking what came quickly.

Not first by asking what others received.

Not first by asking whether our station looks impressive in the eyes of people.

But by asking:

What do I actually want?

Am I seeking `al-'ajilah` as my horizon?

Or do I want the Akhirah enough to strive for it with the striving it deserves — and to believe in it enough that the striving is real?

[PAUSE]

Because one day the visible rankings of this world will feel very small.

The true degrees will appear.

And in that unveiling, one of the sweetest possibilities named in these ayahs will be this:

that a life of sincere striving, perhaps unimpressive to many eyes, is found by Allah to have been `mashkura`.
