# If He Wills, Or He Turns

## A reflection on Surah Al-Ahzab, ayah 24

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

> لِّيَجْزِىَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ بِصِدْقِهِمْ وَيُعَذِّبَ ٱلْمُنَـٰفِقِينَ إِن شَآءَ أَوْ يَتُوبَ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًۭا رَّحِيمًۭا
>
> *Li-yajziya llāhu ṣ-ṣādiqīna bi-ṣidqihim wa-yuʿadhdhiba l-munāfiqīna in shāʾa aw yatūba ʿalayhim. Inna llāha kāna ghafūran raḥīman.*
>
> So that Allah may reward the truthful for their truthfulness, and punish the hypocrites — if He wills — or turn to them in mercy. Indeed, Allah has always been Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

---

### The world this ayah landed in

Year five after the Hijra. Spring. The city of Medina is a small island of belief in a sea that has decided, finally, to drown it.

Ten thousand fighters have marched. Quraysh from Mecca. Ghatafan from the east. The Jewish tribe of Banu Qurayza inside the city has just broken its treaty. The plan is simple: surround the city, crush it, end the matter.

The Muslims number perhaps three thousand. Many are starving — they tie stones to their stomachs to dull the hunger. Salman al-Farsi proposes something no Arab army has done: dig a trench around the exposed flank of the city. So they dig. For weeks. In the cold. With nothing in their bellies. The Prophet ﷺ digs with them.

And then the siege begins. Twenty-seven days. The wind is freezing. The arrows fly across the trench at night. Sleep is impossible. Hunger is permanent. The mothers and children behind them are unprotected if the line breaks.

And inside the city, something else is happening. The hypocrites — those who had said *lā ilāha illa llāh* with their tongues but had never let it reach their hearts — begin to leak excuses. **"Our houses are exposed,"** some of them say, asking permission to leave the line (the surah records this in 33:13). Some whisper that Muhammad ﷺ promised victory but delivered only this trench, this cold, this fear. The strand of doubt that had always been thin inside them — under the pressure of the siege, it begins to widen into a chasm.

And in the same city, in the same trench, in the same cold — others are doing something else entirely. Anas ibn al-Naḍr, who had missed Badr and grieved over it, walking toward the enemy and saying, *"By Allah, I find the fragrance of Jannah on the other side of Uḥud" —* that same spirit was here. Men like Saʿd ibn Muʿādh, like Salman, like the youngest companions, whose faith was being *increased* by the very pressure that was breaking the hypocrites.

Two groups of people. Same trench. Same cold. Same hunger. Same enemy. **Same outward Islam.**

And the pressure was sorting them.

Then this ayah came down.

---

### The hook

Read the ayah again. Listen for what is *symmetric* and what is *asymmetric* between the two groups.

The truthful — Allah rewards them. Full stop.
The hypocrites — Allah punishes them *if He wills*. Or He turns to them.

There is something hidden in this asymmetry that, if you let it open all the way, will change how you read the entire siege — and how you read every difficulty Allah has ever placed in your life.

Stay with me.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will sit with three things in this ayah. Each is small. Each is doing structural work.

---

### 1. The *lām* at the very beginning

The ayah opens with one letter. A single *lām*: **لِـ**

In Arabic, this is the *lām al-taʿlīl* — the *lām* of purpose. It means "*so that*," "*in order that*." It does not begin a new sentence — it links back to whatever just happened, and explains *why* it happened.

Think about it like this. Imagine a friend tells you a long story about an exhausting, painful experience — and at the very end, they say: *"…so that I would finally understand."* That tiny phrase at the end changes everything. It tells you the meaning was not in the experience. The meaning was in what the experience *was for*.

Now look back at what just happened in the surah. Twenty-three ayahs about the siege. The hearts in the throats (33:10). The hypocrites making excuses (33:13). The believers whose faith was strengthened when they saw the confederates (33:22). The men true to their pledge, some martyred, some still waiting (33:23).

And then, one letter: **لِـ**

*So that.*

Notice what Allah is doing. He is not telling us *what happened* at the trench. He told us that already. He is telling us *what the trench was for*.

[PAUSE]

The siege was not just military history. The siege was a *purpose-clause*. It was an instrument. The cold, the hunger, the fear, the betrayal — all of it had a *li-*.

And the *li-* opens onto two things: reward the truthful, expose the hypocrites.

Here is the contemplative weight of this. **Allah did not bring the ahzab to Medina because the ahzab were strong.** He brought them because the inner reality of those inside Medina needed to be *drawn out*. Some of those people needed their truth to be rewarded. Others needed their hypocrisy to be revealed — even to themselves.

The siege was a mirror. The *lām* tells us this directly. Not by argument. By a single letter at the start of a sentence.

[PAUSE]

---

### 2. The doubling: *al-ṣādiqīna bi-ṣidqihim*

Now look at the next words: **اَلصَّـٰدِقِينَ بِصِدْقِهِمْ** — *the truthful, for their truthfulness.*

Notice something. The word for "truthful" and the word for "their truthfulness" are from the **same root**: ṣ-d-q. Allah did not have to say it this way. He could have said *li-yajziya llāhu ṣ-ṣādiqīna* — "so that Allah may reward the truthful." Period. The reward would be understood.

But He did not stop there. He added *bi-ṣidqihim* — "*for their truthfulness*," "*because of their truthfulness*."

In Arabic grammar, that little *bā'* preposition has many functions. One of them is what the grammarians call the *bā' al-sababiyyah* — the *bā'* of *cause*. It means: this reward is not just *given to* them. It is given *because of* something inside them.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

If Allah had only said *yajzī al-ṣādiqīn* — *He rewards the truthful* — we would have understood: there is a category called "the truthful," and Allah rewards that category. The reward and the people are external to each other.

But when He says *yajzī al-ṣādiqīn bi-ṣidqihim* — *He rewards the truthful because of their truthfulness* — He is saying something different. He is saying: **the reward is being drawn out of the very thing inside them.** The *ṣidq* is the cause. The reward is the effect.

Here is what this means. The reward of the truthful is not arbitrary. It is not Allah choosing to be generous to some people while withholding from others. It is Allah honoring something that *exists*. The ṣādiq carries the cause of his own reward *inside himself*. The reward is not from outside him — it is the inevitable outflow of the truth that was already there.

This is why the verse before this one (33:23) is so striking. Allah describes men who were *true to what they pledged*. Some died fulfilling that pledge. Others are still waiting. **And they have not altered in the least.** That is what *ṣidq* is. It is alignment between the inside and the outside. Between the word said and the deed done. Between the pledge made and the life lived.

And when that alignment is real — when there is no gap between the heart and the tongue — Allah does not even need to add anything to it from outside. **He just rewards it for being what it is.**

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators all converge here in their composite reading. Al-Baghawi explains *ṣidqihim* directly: *"and their truthfulness is the fulfillment of the pledge."* Al-Muyassar adds the dimension of trial: *"the people of truth, because of their truthfulness and their tested endurance."* Al-Qurtubi and al-Jalalayn read the *bā'* as causal: *they are rewarded because of their ṣidq.*

So now look at the trench again. The companions in the cold. Their tongues had said one thing — *we believe* — and their bodies were now living that thing. The pledge and the action were the same shape. **The ṣidq was the alignment.**

And Allah, the moment He saw this alignment, was already rewarding it. Not for some other reason. *Bi-ṣidqihim.* Because of it.

---

### 3. The asymmetric absence — and what it reveals

This is the heart of the ayah. Stay with me, because once you see this, you will not be able to unsee it.

Read the two halves of the ayah back to back, carefully:

> **(A)** *li-yajziya llāhu ṣ-ṣādiqīna bi-ṣidqihim*
> So that Allah may reward the truthful for their truthfulness.
>
> **(B)** *wa-yuʿadhdhiba l-munāfiqīna in shāʾa aw yatūba ʿalayhim.*
> And punish the hypocrites — if He wills — or turn to them in mercy.

Now notice what is *present* in (B) that is *absent* in (A).

In (A), the reward of the truthful: **no condition.** No *"if He wills."* No alternative. Just — He rewards them.

In (B), the punishment of the hypocrites: **a condition appears.** *In shāʾa* — *if He wills.* And then — an *alternative.* *Aw yatūba ʿalayhim* — *or He turns to them in mercy.*

[PAUSE]

This asymmetry is the entire teaching.

Let me show you what is happening here.

In Arabic, what is *not said* speaks just as loudly as what *is said*. Allah is not in the business of accidental phrasing. When two parallel structures are deliberately made *non-parallel*, the gap is doing work.

Here, the gap is doing this: **the reward of the truthful is presented as if it is already done.** It has no conditions because it is not in question. The truth is there. The reward is its shadow. They move together.

But the punishment of the hypocrites is presented as **suspended.** Held above the page. Allah names that He *could* punish them — and immediately He names the alternative. He could punish. *Or* He could turn back to them.

This is staggering when you remember who the hypocrites in this surah are. These are not strangers. These are people inside Medina, who said the shahāda, who prayed behind the Prophet ﷺ, and who — at the moment of greatest danger — made excuses, leaked the line, whispered against the mission. These are people whose betrayal was *active* and *known*.

And the ayah, addressing them — these very people — says:

*If He wills, He punishes you.*
*Or He turns to you.*

[PAUSE]

This is what the grammarians notice but rarely say out loud: the verse on the *failed* in the trench *does not slam a door.*

Look at what is absent in (B) that you might have expected:
- No "and He will punish them" with certainty.
- No "their punishment is decreed."
- No closing of the option.

Instead, two doors. One door: punishment if He wills. Another door: He turns to them.

And then look at how the ayah *ends*.

It does not end with the punishment. It does not end with the hypocrites being condemned. It ends with: **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ كَانَ غَفُورًۭا رَّحِيمًۭا** — *indeed, Allah has always been Oft-Forgiving, Most Merciful.*

The verse on the siege of the ahzab — the verse on the people who tried to abandon Islam at its most fragile moment — *closes with mercy.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the linguistic honesty. Grammatically, *in shāʾa* and *aw yatūba ʿalayhim* are documented features of the verse — they are not interpretive embellishments. What this *invites us to consider* is the theological reading: that Allah is here describing His standing posture toward even the failed. Al-Qurtubi reads it this way directly: *"if He wills, He has not opened for them the way of repentance; and if He does not will to punish them, He turns to them before death."* Al-Muyassar reads it through the lens of *tawfīq* — the granting (or withholding) of the success to repent. Al-Baghawi reads *yatūba ʿalayhim* as *"He guides them to faith."*

All of them are reading what the verse itself signals: **the door of return for the hypocrite of the trench is not closed.** Not even on the day of the trench. Not even after that betrayal.

---

### Bridging into the deeper question

We have looked at three things.

The *lām* of purpose told us the trench was an *instrument* — Allah brought the siege so that something inside the people of Medina could be drawn out.

The doubling *al-ṣādiqīn bi-ṣidqihim* told us the reward of the truthful is the *outflow* of what was already inside them — it is not arbitrary, it is the natural shadow of the truth they were already carrying.

The asymmetric absence — *in shāʾa, aw yatūba ʿalayhim* — told us that even the failed in the trench are addressed by a sentence that *does not close.* The verse ends not with their punishment but with God's mercy.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

---

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme 1: Pressure does not *make* you. It *reveals* you.

Here is a small experience almost everyone has had.

You meet someone in good times. They are kind. They are agreeable. They say all the right things. You think you know them. You spend years thinking you know them.

Then a crisis comes. Maybe it is a death. Maybe it is a financial collapse. Maybe it is a betrayal. And suddenly, this person you thought you knew does something you would never have predicted. Sometimes for the better — they rise into something you didn't know was inside them. Sometimes for the worse — they shrink into something you didn't know was there either.

And the truth that hits you is this: **you did not see who they were in the good times.** The good times had a kind of soft light that touched everyone the same way. Only the pressure showed you what was inside the room.

This is a universal human fact. We do not know ourselves in comfort. We learn ourselves under load.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah is doing at the trench.

The Muslim community in Medina, before the siege, looked from the outside like *one* community. The same prayers behind the same imām. The same words on the same tongues. The same outward signs of belief. Even Abdullah ibn Ubayy — the leader of the hypocrites — prayed in the masjid.

In comfort, the *ṣādiq* and the *munāfiq* are not distinguishable from the outside. They look the same. They sound the same. Sometimes they don't even know which one they themselves are.

And then Allah brings ten thousand fighters to the gates.

[PAUSE]

Notice — and this is important — that Allah did not bring the ahzab to *make* anyone become a hypocrite. The *lām* of purpose tells us the trench was so that the truthful would be *rewarded for what they were* and the hypocrites would be *exposed for what they were.* The pressure did not *create* the inner state. The pressure *revealed* it.

Think of it like a kiln. Clay sits on the potter's wheel, and you can shape it into anything — into the form of a vessel. But until you put it in the fire, you don't know if it's pottery or if it's mud. The fire does not *make* the clay. The fire reveals what the clay already was. Pottery comes out hardened, ringing, holding water. Mud comes out cracked, crumbling, holding nothing.

The believers at the trench did not become believers *at* the trench. They had been believers all along. The trench just hardened them into something that *rang.*

The hypocrites at the trench did not become hypocrites *at* the trench. They had been hypocrites all along. The trench just cracked them open and showed everyone — including, painfully, themselves — what was actually inside.

[PAUSE]

And here is the bridge to your life.

Every difficulty Allah has placed in your path is — at one level — a *lām of purpose*. It is happening *so that.* So that something inside you can be drawn out.

This is not a sentimental claim. It is a structural one. The ayah is teaching us how to read the events of our lives. They are not random shocks. They are not pure punishment. They are not pure test in some abstract academic sense. **They are sorting mechanisms — for the heart's own benefit.**

What you say *you believe* in comfort is one thing. What comes out of your mouth when you are exhausted, terrified, betrayed, hungry, and uncertain — that is the *ṣidq* or the *nifāq* the kiln is exposing.

And the question the ayah is asking you is: when your trench comes — when the cold sets in and the line is thin and the excuses are tempting — **which one will the fire reveal?**

This is not a question to make you anxious. This is a question to make you *prepare.* Because the alignment between tongue and heart — the *ṣidq* — is not built in the trench. It is built *before* the trench, day by day, in the small choices nobody sees. The trench just shows what those small choices added up to.

**The practical application:** What is one small unaligned thing in your life right now — one place where what you say and what you do are slightly different shapes? The trench will eventually find that crack. The mercy of Allah is that He often shows you the crack *before* the trench, in small revealing moments — a difficult conversation, a financial squeeze, a sudden temptation — so you can fix it in the small pressure before the large pressure comes. Treat every small pressure as the kiln's gentle preview. Fix the crack now.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme 2: The asymmetry of the door — and how God's mercy ends every sentence

Here is a deeper teaching, hidden in the asymmetric absence we noticed.

Imagine for a moment a courtroom. The judge sits at a high bench, and on the bench are two pens. One pen writes rewards. The other pen writes punishments.

When the case of the truthful comes before the bench, the judge does not hesitate. The pen of reward moves. There is no qualifier. There is no *"if I feel like it."* The reward is the natural recognition of what is already true. The truthful do not have to fear the judge.

When the case of the hypocrite comes before the bench — the one who actively betrayed, the one who undermined, the one who tried to abandon the ship — watch carefully what the judge does.

The judge takes the pen of punishment. He holds it above the page. **He pauses.** And out loud, He says: *"If I will."*

Then He puts the pen down and picks up something else — His hand extended in return. And He says: *"Or — turn back."*

[PAUSE]

This is staggering. This is *not* what we would write.

If we were writing the verse, the verse would read: *"So that Allah may reward the truthful for their truthfulness — and punish the hypocrites for their hypocrisy."* That would be symmetric. That would be just. That would be how a human court works.

But Allah does not write symmetric. He writes *toward mercy.*

He inserts *in shāʾa* — *if He wills.* He inserts *aw yatūba ʿalayhim* — *or He turns to them.* And He ends the verse — the verse, remember, on the hypocrites of the trench, the most dangerous betrayers — with *ghafūran raḥīman*.

The last word in the verse on these people is **mercy.**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with the *form* of this for a moment, because the form is the message.

In rhetoric, the position of the final word carries weight. It is what lingers. It is what the listener carries away. Allah could have ended this verse on a punishment word — *makrūhan*, *mughḍaban*, anything menacing. He did not. He ended on *raḥīman.*

What does this tell you about how Allah relates to even those who failed Him most painfully?

It tells you this: **God's mercy is not a clause appended to His justice. God's mercy is the room His justice stands inside.** The justice is real — *in shāʾa* — He could punish, He has the right, the punishment would be deserved. But the room around the justice is mercy. And the mercy is what remains when the sentence finishes.

[PAUSE]

Now, this raises a hard question, and we should not duck it. Does this mean the hypocrites get away with what they did? Does this mean nifāq has no consequence?

No. The composite of the commentators is careful here. Al-Qurtubi notes that "if He wills to punish them, He does not grant them the way to repentance." That is — the punishment, when it falls, is precisely the *withdrawal* of the mercy of being granted repentance. Al-Muyassar is even more specific: the punishment is *that they are not given tawfīq to make sincere tawba before death*, so they die on their nifāq. The punishment is the *closing* of the door from inside their own hearts, because they refused to walk through it.

This is the structural insight. **The door is open. The punishment is what happens when someone walks the other way for so long that they no longer see the door.**

The verse is not saying nifāq is harmless. The verse is saying: *as long as you are breathing, the door is open.* Allah is announcing His posture toward you, on the very day of your betrayal, while the trench is still cold — *in shāʾa aw yatūba ʿalayhim.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the modern psychological bridge.

In our lives, we accumulate shame. We do things — small or large — that misalign us. We say one thing and live another. We betray people we said we wouldn't. We let down our own selves. Each failure stacks on top of the last, and the inner voice begins to whisper: *too late. The door is closed. You are now this kind of person, and you cannot un-be it.*

That voice is lying.

And the proof that it is lying is in this verse. **The hypocrites of the trench** — people whose betrayal endangered the entire community, people who tried to walk away from Islam at its most fragile moment, people whose names were leaking through the lines in the cold — *these people* are addressed in the same sentence with: *or He turns to them.*

If the door is not closed for them, on the day of the trench, by the One whose mission they betrayed —

**the door is not closed for you.**

Not for what you did last week. Not for what you have been carrying for ten years. Not for what no one knows about. Not for what everyone knows about.

The structural logic of the verse is: *while there is sentence-time left, there is mercy.* And in your case, there is sentence-time left. You are still reading.

[PAUSE]

**The practical application:** Right now, there is something specific in your life that the *shayṭān* tells you Allah will never forgive you for, or never accept your return on. Name it to yourself, silently. Then read the final five words of the ayah aloud — *inna llāha kāna ghafūran raḥīman.* Notice the verb. *Kāna* — He has *always been.* Not "He will be." Not "He might be." He has *always been* this, before you ever sinned, while you were sinning, and right now. The door has never been closed. You have been standing in front of it imagining a wall. Walk through.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we have walked through, look at the shape of the ayah whole.

> **A.** *Li-yajziya llāhu ṣ-ṣādiqīna bi-ṣidqihim* — reward, unconditional, drawn from what was inside them.
>
> **B.** *Wa-yuʿadhdhiba l-munāfiqīna in shāʾa* — punishment, conditional, suspended.
>
> **B'.** *Aw yatūba ʿalayhim* — return, alternative, door open.
>
> **C.** *Inna llāha kāna ghafūran raḥīman* — mercy as the closing seal.

Notice the architecture. The ayah is structurally weighted toward mercy.

- One clause of reward.
- One clause of *possible* punishment.
- One clause of return.
- One clause of mercy as the divine attribute that contains all of it.

Three out of four clauses are mercy-tilted. Even the punishment clause is conditional. The verse is built so that the listener, walking through it from first letter to last, *ends in mercy* — even when the verse is about the worst category of people in the surah.

This is the form. This is the message. **The whole sentence tilts toward the door.**

---

### Bringing it together

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes are the same insight from two directions.

The *lām of purpose* and Theme 1 are saying: the trench was an *instrument*. Allah uses pressure to *reveal* what was inside. Don't fear the kiln — fear being a cracked vessel that no kiln ever exposed because no kiln ever came. The kiln is mercy in disguise. It shows you the cracks while you still have time to repair them.

The asymmetric absence and Theme 2 are saying: even when the kiln exposes the worst, the verse does not slam shut. The door of return is built into the structure of the universe. Even at the trench, even at the moment of betrayal, the sentence ends in mercy.

Together: **Allah brings the pressure to reveal you, and offers the return so that the revelation does not destroy you.**

The trench is for both. The reward and the door.

### Questions to carry

1. What in my life right now is the small kiln — the present pressure that is revealing a crack in my alignment between tongue and heart?

2. If a great pressure came tomorrow — a death, a financial collapse, a public scandal, a betrayal — what would it reveal was inside me? What is the gap between who I say I am and who would emerge under load?

3. Is there a door I have stopped seeing because I have been walking the other way for so long? What would it take to turn?

4. Who in my life is currently in their trench, and have I been treating them with the asymmetric mercy of this ayah — holding back judgment, holding open the door — or with the human symmetry of immediate condemnation?

5. When was the last time I let *inna llāha kāna ghafūran raḥīman* land on something specific in my own history, instead of leaving it abstract?

### One-sentence distillation

The trench was an instrument designed to reward the truthful for what was already inside them and to expose the failed — and even in exposing the failed, the verse on the siege closes not with their punishment but with the standing open door of Allah's mercy.

---

### The closing invitation

There are two shifts this ayah invites.

The first is the way you read your difficulties. They are *lām*-clauses. They have a *so that.* They are not just happening to you — they are happening *for the revealing of something inside you that needs to be revealed while there is still time to repair it.* Sit with your present pressures and ask: *what is this drawing out of me? Which version of me is the fire showing?*

The second is the way you read your failures. The verse will not let you close the door that Allah refused to close. If He held open the door for the hypocrites of the trench — and He did — He has held it open for you. The mercy at the end of this verse is not decoration. It is the room everything else stands in. Walk through it.

### A du'a from these words

O Allah — You who have always been *ghafūran raḥīman* — let the pressures of our lives reveal in us *ṣidq*, not *nifāq*. And where they reveal cracks, send Your kiln gently, in small revealing moments, before the great pressure comes. And for what they have already revealed — the failures we carry, the door we have stopped seeing — turn to us, *yā tawwāb*, before our sentence-time runs out. Let the last word in our story be the last word in Your ayah: *raḥīman*. Āmīn.
