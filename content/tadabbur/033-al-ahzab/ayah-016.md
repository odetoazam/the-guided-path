# The Arithmetic of Flight

## Surah Al-Ahzab — Ayah 16

> قُل لَّن يَنفَعَكُمُ ٱلْفِرَارُ إِن فَرَرْتُم مِّنَ ٱلْمَوْتِ أَوِ ٱلْقَتْلِ وَإِذًا لَّا تُمَتَّعُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا
>
> *Qul lan yanfaʿakumu-l-firāru in farartum mina-l-mawti awi-l-qatli wa idhan lā tumattaʿūna illā qalīlā*
>
> **Say: Fleeing will never benefit you — if you flee from death or being killed; and then you would not be given enjoyment except for a little.**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Walk Into the Trench

Before this ayah can do its work, you have to be standing inside the moment it spoke into.

Madinah, year five of the *Hijrah*. The city has been encircled. Not by one enemy — by a coalition. Quraysh from Makkah, Ghaṭafān from Najd, Banū Naḍīr in exile working the negotiations, and inside the walls a Jewish tribe — Banū Qurayẓa — whose treaty with the Muslims has just been violated under pressure from the besiegers. The *aḥzāb*, the **confederates**, the name this surah carries.

Numbers vary in the sources, but the picture is consistent: roughly ten thousand fighters on the outside. Three thousand defenders on the inside. The trench dug by the suggestion of Salmān al-Fārisī (raḍiya-llāhu ʿanhu) has held the cavalry off, but only barely. Cold. Hunger so deep the Prophet ﷺ tied a stone against his stomach. Weeks of siege.

And inside Madinah — a population doing what populations do under siege.

[PAUSE]

Some are at the lines. Some are in their homes. Some are doing something else — they are talking to each other quietly. Verses 12 through 15 of this surah, just before our ayah, preserve their words with the precision of a recording:

> *"Allah and His Messenger have promised us nothing but delusion."* (33:12)

> *"O people of Yathrib! There is no place for you here — go back."* (33:13)

> *"Our homes are exposed."* (33:13)

And the line that the ayah hangs on:

> *"They had already pledged to Allah before that they would not turn their backs."* (33:15)

So the situation is this: a body of people had given their word that they would not flee. The siege got hard. They started looking for the exit. They started **asking permission** to leave the line — *yastaʾdhinu farīqun minhumu-n-nabiyy* — "a group among them is seeking permission from the Prophet" (33:13). And underneath the request was a calculation. **If we leave, we survive. If we stay, we die.**

That is the arithmetic this ayah enters. Not abstract theology. A whispered cost-benefit equation inside a city under siege.

[PAUSE]

And here is the hook, because the ayah is not what you would expect.

You would expect Allah to threaten them. *Don't flee, because if you flee I will punish you in the Hereafter.* You would expect Him to shame them. *Don't flee, because the believers will look at you with contempt.* You would expect Him to remind them of paradise — fight, and you win paradise.

He does none of that here.

He does something stranger. He does **their own math**. He sits down inside their own equation and reruns the numbers. *Even if your flight worked exactly as you imagined it would — what would you have actually gained?*

That is the move of this ayah. And once you see it, you will see it operating inside almost every fear you have ever fled from.

Let us go word by word.

# PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: الْفِرَارُ + فَرَرْتُمْ — the doubled root, the doubled flight

Look at the first half of the ayah carefully:

> *Qul lan yanfaʿakumu-l-**firār**u in **farartum**...*

The root **ف ر ر** (*f-r-r*) — to flee, to bolt, to take to one's heels — appears **twice in five words.** Once as a noun, *al-firār*. Once as a verb, *farartum*.

Before we look at what that doubling does, let me teach you the grammatical structure underneath it, because Arabic gives a writer this option in a way English does not.

Arabic has a category called the *maṣdar* — the verbal noun. Every verb in Arabic has a noun-form that names the action itself. *Daraba* (he struck) gives you *ḍarb* (the act of striking). *Kataba* (he wrote) gives you *kitāba* (writing). *Farra* (he fled) gives you *firār* (the fleeing).

So a writer in Arabic has a choice. You can refer to an action with the verb (active, in motion, attached to a subject), or you can refer to it with the *maṣdar* (the action itself, abstracted, standing alone like an object you can pick up and examine). And every time Arabic uses both — verb AND *maṣdar* of the same root in close proximity — something is happening. The classical rhetoricians called this kind of doubling *taʾkīd* — emphasis, intensification. It is the language putting its weight on a single point.

Here is an everyday analogy. In English, if I say *"running won't help you if you run from this,"* the doubled root *run / running* tightens the sentence. It locks you in. You cannot read the sentence without the act of running being present twice — once as a thing, once as a doing. The sentence does not let you off the hook.

Now open the ayah.

> *Lan yanfaʿakumu-**l-firār**u in **farartum**...*
>
> **The fleeing** will never benefit you, if **you flee**...

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah is not just saying *if you flee, it will not help you.* He is saying *the fleeing itself* — *al-firār*, with the definite article, *the* fleeing — *will never benefit you, if it is the case that you actually flee.*

The *maṣdar* with the definite article — *al-firār* — names the action like a thing. It is on the table. We are examining it. We are not yet committing anyone to doing it; we are looking at it. *Here is this object: the fleeing. Will it benefit you?* No. *Will it never benefit you?* Never. *Lan*. The strongest future negation Arabic has.

And then — *in farartum* — *if* you actually do it.

Stay with me, because the order matters. Allah does not say *"if you flee, the fleeing will not help you."* He says **"the fleeing will not help you, IF you flee."** He puts the verdict first and the condition second. The act is condemned before it is even imagined as happening. The arithmetic is done before they get to the door.

And here is what the doubling does that a single use of the root could not have done.

A single noun — *fleeing will not help you* — would be a general principle. A single verb — *if you flee, it will not help you* — would be a specific accusation. The doubled form does both at once. It states the universal principle (the fleeing — categorically — does not benefit), AND it pins the universal to *these specific people in this specific moment*: *if you flee*.

[PAUSE]

Now listen to the sound.

*Al-firār*. *Farartum*. **Ferārrr. Farartum.** The doubled *rāʾ* in classical Arabic recitation rolls — it is one of the *qalqala*-adjacent letters that vibrates. *Ferārrr.* You can hear the sound of running in the word itself. Feet on the ground. A pulse repeating. And then the very next thing your tongue has to do is hit the heavy, closed consonants of *al-mawt* — **death.** *m-w-t*. Closed. Final. A thud.

The sentence enacts what it describes. The rolling escape collides with the immovable wall. *Ferārrrrr* — *mawt.* You run, and the running ends in the same syllable it was trying to outrun.

That is not decoration. That is the sound of the verse doing the thing the verse is teaching.

**What this single element reveals:** when Allah doubles a root in this way, the language is closing the loop. There is no version of fleeing that escapes the verdict on fleeing. The act and the actor are bound together inside the same line.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: مِّنَ ٱلْمَوْتِ أَوِ ٱلْقَتْلِ — the "or" that closes both exits

> *...if you flee **from death or being killed**...*

Read that phrase slowly. *Mina-l-**mawt**i awi-l-**qatl**.* From *death*, or from *killing*.

Two distinct words. Two distinct roots. **ٱلْمَوْتِ** — *al-mawt*, from the root م و ت — death, the cessation of life. The thing that happens when the appointed term arrives, regardless of cause. **ٱلْقَتْلِ** — *al-qatl*, from the root ق ت ل — killing, slaying, death-by-the-hand-of-another. The thing that happens specifically in battle.

In the four classical commentaries we have on this ayah — al-Jalalayn, al-Muyassar, al-Qurtubi, and al-Baghawi — there is a remarkable agreement on what this pairing is doing. Al-Baghawi phrases it most directly: *li-anna man ḥaḍara ajaluhu māta aw qutila* — *because whoever's appointed time has arrived, either dies or is killed.* Al-Qurtubi mirrors this almost word for word. The pairing is not poetic redundancy. It is a closing of every possible exit.

Let me show you why this is structurally devastating to the calculation they are running.

The man inside the trench thinking about leaving has a model of reality in his head. Inside that model, **danger has a location.** The danger is *there*, at the line, where the swords are. *Death-by-killing* lives at the trench. If I get away from the trench, I get away from *al-qatl*. I get away from the swords.

But what about *al-mawt*? What about the kind of death that finds you in your bed? The fever? The fall? The arrow that comes from nowhere on the road home? *That* kind of death has no location. It can find you anywhere.

So the calculation is: *if I flee, I get out of the path of al-qatl, and as for al-mawt, well, al-mawt is in Allah's hands wherever I am, so I lose nothing.* The flight makes sense **inside that model** because the man thinks he is only running from one of the two doors. The other door, he believes, follows him wherever he goes — so leaving costs him nothing extra.

[PAUSE]

Watch what the ayah does to this model.

*Mina-l-mawti **awi**-l-qatl.* **Or.** The disjunction yokes them together as a pair from which one cannot be partially escaped. Allah is not saying *"flee from killing, but death will still find you."* He is saying *the very act of fleeing — whichever of the two exits it is aimed at — will not benefit you.*

Why? Because both doors are governed by the same decree. The *ajal* — the appointed term — does not distinguish whether you die by sword or by sickness. When your term arrives, **one of the two will arrive with it.** And when your term has not arrived, **neither will arrive**, even if you stand in the middle of the battlefield.

So the man who flees the trench is not actually choosing between two outcomes. He is not choosing *al-qatl-or-no-death-today.* He is choosing — if his time has come — *al-mawt-at-the-place-he-fled-to* instead of *al-qatl-at-the-trench*. The location changes. The fact does not.

And if his time has not come, then *he could have stayed, and nothing would have touched him.*

This is what al-Qurtubi is pointing to when he writes *kullu mā huwa ātin fa-qarīb* — *everything that is coming is near.* The flight does not delay the appointment. It only changes the address where the appointment is kept.

[PAUSE]

Now notice something else. There is a quiet **absence** in this phrase. Look at where the *min* — "from" — leads. *From* death. *From* killing.

Where does the flight lead **to**? The ayah does not say.

Arabic could easily have specified. *Flee from the trench TO your homes. Flee from the city TO the desert.* But there is no *ilā* — no "to." The *firār* is grammatically destinationless. They are running, but the ayah refuses to name what they are running toward. As if to say: *you think you are running to safety, but the safety you imagine does not exist as a place. There is no `to` for this flight. There is only `from`.*

We will come back to this absence in Part Two, because it opens onto something much larger.

**What this second element reveals:** the "or" closes both exits at the same time. The man fleeing the trench is not actually escaping a category of death; he is moving an appointment from one location to another. The decree is unchanged. The flight buys nothing.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: تُمَتَّعُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا — the math of what fleeing actually buys

Here is the third move, and it is the one that breaks the calculation completely.

> *...wa idhan lā **tumattaʿūna** illā **qalīlā**.*
>
> *...and then you would not be given enjoyment except for a little.*

Look at the verb. **تُمَتَّعُونَ** — *tumattaʿūn*. Let me unpack this carefully because the form is doing real work.

The root is م ت ع — *m-t-ʿ* — connected to enjoyment, provision, temporary benefit, the kind of comfort one is granted for a period. The Leeds corpus confirms this verb is in **Form II** — *mattaʿa / yumattiʿu* — and here it appears in the **passive imperfect, second person plural masculine**: *tumattaʿūna*.

Two grammatical observations matter.

First, **Form II.** Now — and this is an important precision — Form II in Arabic is not automatically "intensive" or "causative" the way introductory grammars sometimes summarize it. Each Form II verb has to be examined on its own merits. For *mattaʿa* specifically, the Form II carries a clear **transitive/factitive** sense: *to grant enjoyment to someone.* The simple root in Form I means roughly *to enjoy / to derive benefit*; the Form II makes someone else the recipient of granted enjoyment. *Mattaʿahu-llāhu bi-kadhā* — *Allah granted him enjoyment by means of such-and-such.*

Second, **passive voice.** *Tumattaʿūna* — *you will be given enjoyment.* Not *you will enjoy.* Not *you will live well.* **You will be granted enjoyment by an unstated agent.** The agent is unstated grammatically — but theologically, of course, the agent is unambiguous. It is Allah.

Stay with me on this, because the passive is not incidental. The hypocrites are about to be told what their flight buys them. The verb construction makes sure they cannot imagine that they themselves are the source of the buying. *Even what little you would gain — you would not give yourselves. It would still be given to you. And the giver is the one whose decree you thought you were escaping.*

[PAUSE]

Now place that verb against its restriction: *illā qalīlā* — *except a little.*

**قَلِيلًا** — *qalīlan*, from the root ق ل ل — a little, a small amount. Notice that it is **indefinite**. Not *the little.* Not *the specified little.* Just — *little.* Unmeasured. Unspecified.

Why indefinite? Because Allah is not telling them *how much* extra time their flight would buy. He is saying: *whatever it is, it is little.* A day is little. A year is little. A decade is little. Forty years is little. By the measure of what they imagine they are gaining, and the measure of what they are weighing it against, every figure is *qalīl*.

And the indefiniteness has another rhetorical function. When you do not name the quantity, you let the reader supply it. The man calculating his flight thinks he is buying *a long time.* The ayah refuses to dignify his number. *Whatever number you have in your head — call it little.*

[PAUSE]

Now let us put the third element together with the first two, because this is where the entire arithmetic collapses.

The calculation the hypocrite is running in his head is this:

| Option | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Stay at the trench | Possibly die |
| Flee | Live |

That table has TWO columns and FOUR cells. The flight, on this table, is straightforwardly the better choice. Stay = risk. Flee = safety. Easy math.

The ayah replaces his table with a different one.

| Option | If your time has come | If your time has not come |
|---|---|---|
| Stay at the trench | You die — and Allah is pleased | You live |
| Flee | You die anyway — and Allah is angry | You live a little, then die |

[PAUSE]

Look at that bottom-right cell. *Flee, your time has not come — you live a little, then die.* That is what *tumattaʿūna illā qalīlā* is doing. It does not promise the flight will fail at saving the life. **It concedes** the flight may, in some narrow sense, "succeed." The man may indeed make it home. He may indeed survive the siege. *Wa idhan* — *and then, in that case, granting you what you wanted*...

*...lā tumattaʿūna illā qalīlā.* You are given enjoyment, *only a little.*

The flight, even on its best day, only delays. It does not change the destination. It cannot. The appointment is the same; only the address moved.

And then the question the ayah leaves you to ask yourself is the question that ends the calculation: **what did you actually buy?**

You traded your word — the pledge in 33:15 not to turn your backs. You traded your standing with the Messenger ﷺ. You traded your place among the believers. You traded — if you are honest — the meaning of your death, the only thing about your death you had any control over. And what did you receive in exchange?

*Qalīlan.* A little.

That is not an unfavorable trade. That is **a disaster of a trade.** And the cruelty of the trade is that it could only be seen as a good trade by someone who had stopped thinking about death honestly.

**What this third element reveals:** the ayah does not refute fear by telling the listener that flight is impossible. It refutes fear by completing the calculation honestly. Even if the flight gives you exactly what you imagined it would — *you bought almost nothing for everything you spent.*

## Bridge

Three things, then, have come into view from inside the Arabic.

The doubled root locks the fleer to his flight — there is no abstract "fleeing" he can claim he didn't really do.

The "or" between *al-mawt* and *al-qatl* closes both exits — he is not choosing a category of death, he is choosing the location of an appointment.

And the passive *tumattaʿūna* with the indefinite *qalīlan* finishes the math — even granting his premise, what he buys is little.

Now let us ask what this construction reveals not just about the verse — but about how human beings actually live with fear, and what they are running from when they run.

# PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Arithmetic Fear Refuses to Finish

Here is something every adult human knows.

When you are anxious, your mind does **half** of a math problem.

It does the part that quantifies the danger. *If I stay in this conversation, I will be humiliated. If I stay in this marriage, I will be unhappy. If I stay at this job, I will be miserable. If I stay in this country, I will not be safe.* The fearful mind is **excellent** at the first column of the table. It can write that column with full color and high resolution and exquisite detail.

And then it stops.

It does not write the second column.

It does not finish the question: *and if I leave, what will I have?* It does not ask: *and how long will what I have last?* It does not ask: *and is what I gain proportional to what I am about to spend to get it?*

It does not write that column because writing that column is the thing that makes fear release its grip. Fear stays in power exactly to the extent that you let it write only the first half of the table.

[PAUSE]

I want you to sit with this, because I think most of us have lived this exact thing.

You are in a hard conversation. The person across from you has said something that wounds you. Your body floods. You want to leave the room. The fear says: *if you stay, more pain.* You stand up. You walk out.

You feel, for thirty seconds, free.

And then you sit down somewhere else, and you realize the thing you ran from has come with you. The conversation is unfinished. The relationship is now worse. The fear is still inside your chest, only now you are alone with it instead of in front of the person who could have helped you understand it.

You did not escape. You moved an appointment.

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah is doing for the hypocrites at the trench, and through them for every person who reads it after.

It is sitting down inside their calculation and *finishing it for them.* It is saying: *you've done the first column beautifully. You know exactly what you'll suffer if you stay. Now let's do the second column. If you flee — where will you go? For how long? At what cost to who you are? And when the appointed time arrives — and it will arrive, because that is what appointments do — what will you have built with the time you bought?*

*Qalīlan.* A little.

The fear had told them they were running toward years. The ayah tells them: you are running toward *qalīl*.

And here is the kindness of the ayah, because we have to name it as kindness. **Allah is not punishing them by making them stay.** He is **showing them their own arithmetic** so they can choose differently. He is not threatening; He is teaching. *Here is the math you didn't finish. Look at it. Now choose.*

The hypocrite who reads this ayah and stays is not staying because he was scared into staying. He is staying because he was shown the second column of the table and realized he had been miscounting.

[PAUSE]

Apply this to your own life. Not at the trench. At your trench.

Where are you running? What are you running from? Have you done the second column? Have you asked — if my flight works exactly as I imagine it will, what will I actually have? For how long?

I will say something hard: in my experience, almost every flight from a hard, righteous thing produces *qalīlan*. A little quiet. A little relief. A little easier sleep for a stretch. And then the appointment that was waiting at the original address shows up at the new address, and you discover that you traded something real for something little.

This is the first thing the ayah is teaching, before it teaches anything about death.

It is teaching you to finish the second column.

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes **one specific habit**: before you flee from something, write the second column. Take five minutes. Actually write it. *If I leave this conversation, I will have... for... and then...* If the second column reads *qalīl*, the fear was lying to you. Stay.

## Theme Two: Fleeing Without Knowing Where You Are Going

Earlier I pointed at a small absence in the Arabic, and I promised we would come back to it. Here is where we come back.

Look at the ayah one more time:

> *Lan yanfaʿakumu-l-firāru in farartum **mina**-l-mawti awi-l-qatl.*

The flight has a *from*. *Mina* — from death. From killing.

The flight has **no** *to*. There is no *ilā*. The ayah names what they are running away from and refuses to name what they are running toward.

Now ask: why?

You could say *it's elliptical, the listener fills in the destination from context, the original audience knew they were running to their homes.* That is true at the literal level. But the literal level does not exhaust what the text is doing. In a text this precise, an absence this prominent **is itself the teaching**.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I think the absence is doing.

It is showing you that **you don't actually have a destination.** You only have an exit.

The hypocrite at the trench thinks he is running TO his home, TO safety, TO his family, TO the parts of his life that the siege has interrupted. But the ayah quietly strips those off. It names only what he is fleeing FROM, because **what he is fleeing FROM is the only thing that is actually real to him in this moment.** The rest is a fantasy he is using to dignify the exit.

Watch this in yourself. The next time you want to leave something hard — a marriage, a job, a city, a community, a relationship with a parent, a hard conversation, a long-overdue confession — ask yourself: *what am I going TO?*

You will notice, if you are honest, that the *to* is usually thinner than the *from*. The *from* is in 4K resolution. The *to* is a vague feeling. *I'll be... I'll feel... I'll have... room. Space. Peace. Something better.* When you press it, it dissolves.

That is because most flights are not motions toward something. They are **escapes from a feeling**. The destination is **the absence of the feeling.** And the absence of a feeling is not a place. **You cannot live in the absence of a feeling, because feelings come back.**

[PAUSE]

This is what the ayah is exposing, beneath the surface of the literal flight from the trench. It is showing the hypocrite that the very grammar of his decision is broken. He has a *from*. He does not have a *to*. He is not moving toward anything. He is moving *away from* a fear — and away-from-a-fear is not a destination, because the fear travels with the man.

Al-Qurtubi has a phrase here that I keep returning to: *kullu mā huwa ātin fa-qarīb.* **Everything that is coming is near.** What he is naming is the strange acceleration of time when you flee. The day you are running from feels far away when you stand at the trench; it feels right next to you when you are sitting in your home a week later. Time collapses the distance you thought you bought with your feet.

[PAUSE]

Now flip the lens.

What does a real *to* look like? In the moral life — what does a flight WITH a destination look like, instead of a flight from a feeling?

The Quran gives us the answer in another ayah, deliberately using the same root:

> *Fa-firrū ilā-llāh* — **so flee to Allah.** (51:50)

Same root — *frr*, fleeing. But this time the flight HAS its *ilā*. It has its *to*. *Flee TO Allah.* And the moment you supply the *to*, the entire grammar of fleeing changes. **It is no longer escape; it is approach.** The same act becomes the opposite act because the destination is named.

So the question is not *should I run or should I stay?* The question is *what is my destination?* If I have a destination — a real one, a true one, Allah — then "running" toward it is faithful. If I have no destination and am only running from a feeling, then "running" is the trap the ayah is naming.

[PAUSE]

Apply this practically.

When you next feel the urge to flee something, do not ask *do I have a good reason to leave?* That question is too easy — the reason to leave is the feeling, and you will always have a feeling. Ask instead: *if I leave, what am I moving TOWARD?* Name it in one sentence. *I am moving toward...*

If the sentence completes — *I am moving toward a marriage that allows mercy, I am moving toward work that I can do without sin, I am moving toward a city where my children can pray* — then you are not fleeing. You are *journeying*. The *firār ilā* is permitted. The Quran itself commands it.

But if the sentence will not complete — if the only honest version of it is *I am moving toward... not feeling this anymore* — then you are running without a *to*, and the ayah is speaking to you. Whatever you buy with this flight will be *qalīl*, and the feeling you are fleeing will be waiting at the next address.

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

**Refuse to make any large decision until you can name its *ilā*.** If you can't say what you're moving toward in one true sentence, you are not deciding — you are escaping. And the ayah has already done that math for you.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## Bring it together

The two halves of this reflection are the same insight from opposite directions.

The linguistic journey showed us that **the ayah completes a calculation the fearful mind refused to finish.** The doubled root locks the fleer to his flight. The "or" closes both exits. The passive *tumattaʿūna* with the indefinite *qalīlan* finishes the math by showing what the flight, even on its best day, actually buys.

The thematic journey showed us that **the absence of a *to* in the Arabic exposes the structure of fearful flight in every human life.** We run with a *from* and without an *ilā*. We move away from feelings and call it a destination. The Quran offers the alternative — *firrū ilā-llāh* — flee with a destination named, and the entire moral grammar of fleeing changes.

The same teaching from two angles. The fearful mind does half the math and forgets to name where it is going. The ayah finishes the math and exposes the missing destination, and the same act that was condemned — *firār* — becomes commanded the moment Allah names what we are running toward.

---

## Questions to carry

Sit with these. Let them be uncomfortable.

1. **Where in my life right now am I running with a clear *from* and no real *to*?** Not in theory. Specifically. Name the situation.

2. **When I last fled something hard, what did I actually buy with the flight?** If I'm honest — was it *qalīl*?

3. **The hypocrites' calculation in 33:13-15 assumed that fleeing changed the outcome. What in my life today rests on the same assumption — that motion away from danger creates safety, when in fact it only changes the address of an appointment that Allah has already set?**

4. **If I have to flee something — and sometimes we do — am I willing to do the work of naming my *ilā* before I move my feet?**

5. **What would change in me if I treated my next hard decision not as a question of *stay vs. leave*, but as a question of *do I have a destination worth moving toward*?**

---

## One-sentence distillation

**The ayah is not commanding you to stay where you are; it is demanding that you finish the arithmetic before you move, and naming the truth your fear keeps trying to hide — that flight without a destination buys only a little, at a cost that is not little.**

## The closing invitation

You will face a trench this week. It will not be ten thousand soldiers outside a city wall. It will be a conversation you do not want to have, a habit you do not want to break, a sin you do not want to leave, a hard duty you do not want to accept, a person you do not want to forgive, a truth you do not want to say.

And you will feel the pull of *firār*. You will feel your feet start to move.

The invitation of this ayah is small and exact: **before you move, finish the second column.** Ask what you are buying. Ask for how long. Ask what your *ilā* is.

And if your *ilā* is not real, sit back down at the trench. Not because staying is heroic. Because *qalīlan* is not worth the trade.

And if you must move — let your motion be *firār ilā-llāh*. Let it have a destination. Let it be approach, not escape. Then the running becomes prayer, and the same root that condemned the hypocrites in 33:16 becomes the very thing Allah commands in 51:50.

The choice is not between motion and stillness. The choice is between motion *from* and motion *toward*.
