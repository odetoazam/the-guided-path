# Forever Without a Friend
## A Tadabbur on Surah Al-Ahzab, Ayah 65

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> [ayah:33:65] خَـٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا ۖ لَّا يَجِدُونَ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

**Transliteration:**
> *Khālidīna fīhā abadā — lā yajidūna waliyyan wa-lā naṣīrā.*

**Translation:**
> "Abiding therein forever — they will find no protector and no helper."

---

### Where We Are

Before we even open the words, let's locate ourselves.

Surah Al-Ahzab — the surah of *al-aḥzāb*, "the confederates," "the allied factions." The whole surah is haunted by one historical moment: the Battle of the Trench. A coalition of tribes had marched on Madinah, surrounded it, and the believers found themselves looking around at every face they knew, asking the same question:

**Who is actually with us?**

That is what the surah is *about*. Not war. Not politics. Loyalty. The surah names the hypocrites who melted away. It names the believers who stood firm. It names the household of the Prophet ﷺ. It names the wives of the Prophet ﷺ. It names the orphan Zayd. It rewrites the laws of who-belongs-to-whom, who-can-be-called-by-whose-name, who-stands-with-whom on the Day everything is exposed.

The surah is, from beginning to end, a vast inventory of **alliances.**

And then we reach the closing movement. Allah has just said that the rejecters are cursed in this world and the next, that He has prepared for them *saʿīrā* — a blazing Fire. And then comes this ayah.

Classical commentators frame the preceding context in terms that sharpen what this ayah will name: Allah has *expelled* the disbelievers — *ṭaradahum* — from His mercy in both worlds. The Fire, in this reading, is not punishment grafted onto people who still carry some residual claim on mercy. It is the final form of a mercy-expulsion already operative in this life, already underway in the world where they refused. When we read the two absences named in this ayah, we are reading what that expulsion looks like from the inside.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Eight words. That's all this ayah is. Eight Arabic words, and three of them are particles — *fī*, *lā*, *wa-lā*. The content words are five.

But sit with what those five words choose to name.

The ayah does not describe the heat of the Fire. It does not describe the chains, the fuel, the screaming, the regret — all of which the Quran describes elsewhere with terrifying detail.

This ayah, at the climax of the surah of alliances, names something else as the defining feature of that punishment.

**Two absences.**

Not the presence of pain. The **absence** of two specific things. And the two things Allah chooses to name as absent are the two things this entire surah has been about.

What does Allah place at the very heart of what makes the Fire the Fire? Not what is there. What is *not* there.

Stay with that question. We are going to spend the next hour unfolding it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to walk through this ayah slowly. Three elements. Each one earned from the grammar itself.

---

### Element One: *Khālidīna fīhā abadan* — and the strange addition of "forever" to "forever"

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, a participle like *khālid* (خالد) comes from the root **خ-ل-د**, which the classical lexicons define as *long-lasting*, *enduring*, *remaining*. A *khālid* is one who abides, who stays. The plural *khālidūn* or, in this case, the accusative *khālidīna* means "those abiding."

Now, the root خ-ل-د in Arabic — and this is important — does not actually require infinity. Lisān al-ʿArab notes that pre-Islamic Arabs would use *khulūd* to describe a king whose reign was long, a mountain that had stood for ages, a person who had lived an extraordinarily long life. *Khulūd* meant something closer to **"enduring beyond ordinary measure"** — not necessarily *without end*.

That detail matters. Because of what comes next.

#### Opening the word

Look at the ayah:

> خَـٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ **أَبَدًاۖ**

*Khālidīna fīhā* — "abiding therein."

And then Allah adds: **أَبَدًا** — *abadā*.

#### Sitting with it

If *khulūd* by itself already meant "to remain for an extended duration," then why does the Quran add *abadan* — "forever" — on top of it?

Let's sit with this. Notice what's happening.

The word *abad* (أبد) is the strongest Arabic word for boundless time. It is what you use when you want to seal a duration against any possibility of escape. Lisān al-ʿArab defines it as *al-dahr al-ṭawīl alladhī laysa bi-maḥdūd* — "the long age that has no boundary."

So when Allah writes *khālidīna fīhā* — "abiding therein" — the original Arab listener might have heard something like: a very, very long stay. A reign. An age. Perhaps with some sliver of imagination still able to whisper, "but every king eventually dies, every mountain eventually crumbles."

And then Allah, knowing exactly the shape of that whispered hope, places *abadan* on top of the word.

[PAUSE]

**He closes the door the language left open.**

#### Here's what's remarkable

Look at the word order. Allah does not say *abadan khālidīna* — putting forever first. He says *khālidīna fīhā abadan*. He lets the listener taste *khulūd* first, with all of its old human resonances of long ages and long reigns. He lets them feel the duration as something measurable, even if vast.

And then He drops *abadan* at the end like a seal.

It is the difference between saying "you will stay a long time" and saying "you will stay a long time — *and that time has no end.*"

The word *abadan* in this position is doing the work of a lock placed on a door that was already closed. Why lock a closed door? Because the human heart, even in the Fire, will still be looking for the crack. Will still be measuring. Will still be telling itself, *surely this is almost over.* And Allah, who knows the heart He created, names *abadan* explicitly so that even the hope of an ending is removed.

This single grammatical choice tells us something terrifying about what Allah knows of us: **even in the Fire, we will still be calculating the way out.**

Al-Tabari, in his gloss of this phrase, renders *abadā* as *ilā ghayri nihāya* — "to no end, to no limit." That reading from the classical tradition confirms what the grammar itself demands: this is not merely a very large quantity of time with a distant endpoint somewhere beyond imagination. The earliest exegetes heard in this single adverb exactly what its position in the sentence requires — the categorical removal of a horizon. A duration that cannot be measured because it has no far wall.

### Element Two: *Lā yajidūna* — the verb that doesn't say what we expected

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, when you want to say someone *does not have* something, you have many options. You can say *laysa lahum* — "there is not for them." You can say *mā lahum* — "they have no..." You can say *laysū bi-...* — "they are not with..."

Each of these constructions has its own shade.

There is also another option: the verb **و-ج-د** (*wajada*), which the Leeds morphological corpus confirms is the verb Allah uses here in its imperfect form *yajidūna* — "they find."

In classical Arabic, *wajada* is the verb of **searching and discovering.** It is what you do when you are looking for something. *Wajadtu kitābī* — "I found my book." It implies effort, scanning, reaching. Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Rāghib's Mufradāt both note that *wajada* in its primary sense involves *iṣābat al-maṭlūb* — "hitting upon the thing sought."

So *wajada* is not the verb of static possession. It is the verb of **active looking.**

#### Opening the word

Now look at what Allah chose:

> **لَّا يَجِدُونَ** وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

*Lā yajidūna* — "they will not find."

Not *laysa lahum* — "there is not for them."
Not *mā lahum* — "they have no..."

But *lā yajidūna* — **"they will not find."**

#### Sitting with it

Notice something. Notice what this verb assumes about its subject.

You only "find" or "fail to find" something if you are **looking for it.**

The grammar embeds, inside itself, a picture of someone in the Fire **searching.** Scanning the horizon. Turning. Calling out. Looking from face to face. *Wajada* requires a seeker.

If Allah had said *laysa lahum waliyyun wa-lā naṣīr* — "they have no protector and no helper" — it would have been a flat declarative statement. A fact about their condition.

But by saying *lā yajidūna* — "they do not find" — Allah is not just describing a condition. He is describing **an action that never lands.**

[PAUSE]

They are looking. They are searching. The eye is scanning, the heart is reaching, the hand is extending toward some imagined face — *and finding nothing.*

This is the difference between being told "there is no door in this room" and being told "they search the walls for a door and find none." The first is information. The second is **anguish.**

#### Here's what's remarkable

Think about what *wajada* implies about the psychology of the rejecter in the Fire.

In the world, this person had walīs — people they considered protectors. Status, money, family, tribe, ideology, faction. In the world, when trouble came, they turned to those walīs and the walīs answered. That muscle of *turning toward an ally* was trained into them for a lifetime.

In the Fire, that muscle still fires. The instinct to look for a friend is the last thing to die — and Allah is telling us, in the choice of this verb, that it does not die. They still look. The neck still turns. The eyes still scan.

**They just never find anyone.**

The cruelty of the verb is that it preserves the search and removes the discovery. The reaching is intact. The arriving is gone.

This is grammar telling us something about the soul: *the habits of alliance survive the world that made them.* And the soul in the Fire is still running the program of "find someone to stand with me" — except the program now returns nothing, *abadan*, forever.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The two indefinites — *waliyyan wa-lā naṣīrā*

#### Teaching the structure first

In Arabic, when you negate a noun, you can negate it in two distinctly different ways. You can negate it as definite — *lā yajidūna al-waliyya* — "they will not find *the* protector." Or you can negate it as indefinite, with *tanwīn* — *lā yajidūna waliyyan* — "they will not find *any* protector."

The classical grammarians, including Sibawayh, note that **indefinite negation in Arabic is a categorical denial.** It does not merely say "the specific protector they had in mind is missing." It says *waliyyan* in the broadest possible sense, *any* at all, of any kind, anywhere — is missing.

The *tanwīn* on *waliyyan* and *naṣīrā* — that final *-an* sound — is the grammatical marker doing this work. It opens the negation to *every* member of the category.

#### Opening the words

Now look:

> لَّا يَجِدُونَ **وَلِيًّا** وَلَا **نَصِيرًا**

*Waliyyan* — any protector. *Naṣīrā* — any helper.

Both indefinite. Both with that final *tanwīn* opening them to total negation.

#### Sitting with it

But before we go into the categorical denial, we have to ask the harder question:

**Why these two words, specifically?**

Allah could have named many things absent from the Fire. Food. Water. Light. Cool air. Mercy. Sleep. A second chance. The Quran elsewhere names many of these. Here, in this ayah, in the climactic moment of the surah of alliances, Allah names exactly two:

*Walī* and *naṣīr.*

And these are not random words. These are the **two key vocabulary items of the entire surah.**

#### Stay with me

A *walī*, from the root **و-ل-ي**, is one who is **near** to you, who has *walāyah* over you — the relationship of standing beside, of guardianship, of being the one in the loop. A *walī* is your inner circle. Your kin. Your ride-or-die. Your default. The person whose number you call first when the call goes out.

A *naṣīr*, from the root **ن-ص-ر**, is one who comes to your **aid** when called. The *naṣīr* is not necessarily inner-circle — they could be a distant tribe, an ally of convenience, a hired sword, a fair-weather friend. The *naṣīr* is the **second ring** outward from you. Not as intimate as the *walī*, but still — when you cry out, they come.

In the battle this surah is named after — the Trench — the believers were besieged by *aḥzāb*, confederate factions. They survived because, when called, certain people *came*. Some came as walīs — the inner circle, the Muhajirun and Ansar, the household. Some came as nasirs — allies bound by treaty or interest.

When the surah names the rejecters in the Fire as having *no walī and no naṣīr,* it is naming the **complete collapse of the social architecture of survival.**

[PAUSE]

Classical commentators were precise about what makes this collapse so absolute, and their precision sharpens the picture considerably. The inner-circle/outer-circle framing used above is a tadabbur interpretive frame, a way of organizing the relational distinction; the classical exegetes were pointing at something more operational. Al-Tabari defines each absent figure not in terms of what they would *feel* but what they would *do.* The absent *walī* is one who would take charge of them to **rescue them from the blazing Fire** — al-Tabari uses *istinqādh* for this operation, the verb of extraction, of physically pulling someone from a place they cannot leave on their own. The absent *naṣīr* is one who would aid them to **deliver them from Allah's punishment**. Classical commentators add a further precision: the *walī* would *yudāfiʿu ʿanhum* — actively **defend** them, pushing the punishment back from them — while the *naṣīr* would bring them **out** of the Fire entirely.

Notice what this means. The absent figures are defined not by their warmth or loyalty but by their *operative capacity.* What is absent from the Fire is not primarily companionship — it is the ability to **change where they are.** No *walī* to hold the punishment back. No *naṣīr* to extract them. Two different rescue operations, functioning at two different registers — and both will not be launched.

The absence is total not because no one loved them, but because **no one can touch what is being done to them.**

#### Here's what's remarkable — and here's what the grammar adds

Now layer the *tanwīn* back in.

*Lā waliyyan* — not even one *walī*. Not a *walī* you used to have. Not a *walī* you might have made. Not even a *walī* whose existence you can imagine. The *tanwīn* opens the door of imagination to every possible protector — past, present, future, conceivable, inconceivable — and slams it on **all of them at once.**

*Wa-lā naṣīrā* — and not even one *naṣīr*. Not even the cheap help. Not even the help that comes with conditions, that demands payment, that arrives late. Not even the worst, most reluctant, most opportunistic helper. **None.**

Read it again with the absences fully open:

> They will not find — *any* protector, of any kind, in any form, at any time — and they will not find — *any* helper, of any kind, with any motivation, for any price.

[PAUSE]

And here is the grammatical detail that takes the breath: **both nouns are indefinite, and there is no specification of who would have been the walī or the nasir.** Allah does not say "they will not find a walī *from their tribe*" or "a helper *from their friends*." The grammar leaves the source completely unspecified. **Anyone, from anywhere — is not coming.**

The absence is total because the search is total.

### Bridge to Part 2

Three elements: *abadan* sealing the duration, *yajidūna* preserving the search, *waliyyan wa-lā naṣīrā* naming the two empty rings.

And the surah does not leave the scene at this ayah. The very next verse (33:66) — linked directly to this one by the classical commentators — completes the picture: those faces turned in the Fire, saying in regret and confusion, *"Would that we had obeyed Allah and obeyed His Messenger."* In the surah of alliances, where the test of the Trench was precisely that — whether you would obey the Prophet's command or scatter, whether you would hold your post or seek an exit — this regret lands with terrible precision. The search that the verb *yajidūna* embeds continues even inside the Fire: the neck still turns, the eyes still scan, and what they finally land on is the memory of a path that was available and was not taken. The one alliance that would have held — and was refused.
