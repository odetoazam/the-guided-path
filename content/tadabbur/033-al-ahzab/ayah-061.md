# The Geography of a Curse — When Speech Earns Its Echo
## Surah Al-Ahzab, Ayah 61

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> مَّلْعُونِينَ ۖ أَيْنَمَا ثُقِفُوٓا۟ أُخِذُوا۟ وَقُتِّلُوا۟ تَقْتِيلًۭا

**Transliteration:**
> *Malʿūnīna aynamā thuqifū ukhidhū wa-quttilū taqtīlā.*

**Translation:**
> Accursed — wherever they are encountered, they are seized and utterly put to death.

---

### Rebuilding the World

Before we go anywhere near these words, we have to inhabit the city they fell into.

Madinah, year five or six after the Hijrah. The Battle of the Trench has just ended. The largest coalition ever assembled against the Muslim community — ten thousand men encircling a city of barely three thousand — has just dissolved without a single decisive military engagement. Allah turned them back with a wind.

But the city is exhausted. The trenches are still freshly dug. The graves of those who died in the siege are still soft. And in the streets, a different kind of warfare is underway.

Three groups are at work. The first, the **munāfiqūn** — those who showed up to prayer with the believers but mocked them privately. The second, those Allah describes throughout this surah as **alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍ** — those in whose hearts is disease. Not full hypocrites. Wavering souls. Doubting souls. Souls whose orientation flickered with every new pressure.

And the third group — this is the group most modern listeners have never heard named. The **murjifūn**.

Listen to the root of that word. *Rajafa* — to quake. To cause an earthquake. The same root used in Surah Al-Muzzammil where Allah says *yawma tarjufu al-arḍ* — the Day the earth will quake. A **murjif** is not just a liar. A liar tells you something untrue. A **murjif** tells you something specifically designed to make the ground move under your feet.

What were they doing in Madinah? They were spreading terror-inducing reports. "The army is coming back." "The Prophet has been killed." "Madinah will fall tonight." *Irjāf* in its fullest form — language designed not to inform, but to destabilize. The munāfiqūn and those with diseased hearts carried a separate charge in the classical sources: harassing believing women in the streets at night, and when confronted, claiming "We thought she was a slave-woman, we didn't know" — the behavior behind the jilbab command of the immediately preceding verse, 33:59. Different crimes, named in the same breath, rooted in the same orientation: the deliberate unraveling of a community from the inside.

Three groups. One pattern. Words and deeds wielded to destabilize a sacred community from within.

Ayah 60, just before our ayah, gives them the warning: *If they do not desist, We will surely incite you against them.* Then ayah 61 — our ayah — describes what will happen if that warning is ignored.

And ayah 62, right after, names this as **sunnat Allāh fī alladhīna khalaw min qabl** — the pattern of Allah with those who came before. Not arbitrary. Not vengeful. A pattern. A law.

---

### The Hook

Six words. Three verbs. An escalation that begins with a curse, passes through capture, and ends with a verbal form so intensive that Arabic itself bends to carry the weight.

We have to ask: why does Allah speak about words with this much weight? What did these murjifūn actually do — that the response in divine speech itself adopts a grammar this heavy?

And there is a question underneath the question. Where is the curse actually being announced? On their bodies? Or on something much harder to see?

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مَّلْعُونِينَ — The Word That Opens Without a Subject

Notice how this ayah begins.

It does not begin with *they are*. It does not begin with *those who*. There is no nominative subject. The ayah opens in mid-air with a word in the accusative case — a state, a condition, a description hanging suspended.

**مَّلْعُونِينَ** — *malʿūnīn*. Accursed ones.

Before we look at what this word means, look at its grammatical shape. Al-Ṭabarī in his tafsīr notes that *malʿūnīn* stands in the accusative *ʿalā al-shatm* — for contempt and rebuke, the accusative of moral condemnation rather than grammatical predicate. He transmits from Qatadah a reading that sharpens this further: *malʿūnīna ʿalā kulli ḥāl* — accursed in every condition, in every state, without exception. The classical grammarians also debated whether *malʿūnīn* functions as a **ḥāl** — a circumstantial accusative describing the *state* in which they are seized — or whether it functions as a kind of standalone exclamation, an utterance of contempt detached from the verb structure that follows.

Both readings sit on top of each other. Allah is describing *what they are* and *the state in which they are caught.* The accursedness is not what *happens* to them after capture. It is what they *already are* at the moment of capture.

Now the word itself.

**Malʿūn** is the passive participle of the verb **laʿana**. The root is **ل-ع-ن**. In classical Arabic — Lisān al-ʿArab is explicit on this — the original sense of **laʿana** is **al-ṭard wa-l-ibʿād min al-khayr**. To drive away. To distance from goodness. The word originally described what shepherds did to an animal they were expelling from the flock.

Al-Ṭabarī himself renders *malʿūnīn* in his tafsīr as **maṭrūdīn manfiyyīn** — expelled and banished. Not divine ill-wishing. Not imprecation. *Removal.* The same physical vocabulary used for banishment from a city, from a tribe, from a flock. Al-Muyassar follows this exactly: *maṭrūdīn min raḥmat Allāh* — driven away from the mercy of Allah. The word, in both sources, names a spatial and relational fact before it names any punishment.

A curse, in Quranic Arabic, is not a magical incantation. It is not Allah speaking ill of someone. **It is distance.** It is removal from mercy. It is the state of a soul that has been driven out from the field where divine grace falls.

Stay with this. We modern listeners hear the word *curse* and we picture an active hand from above striking down. The Quranic concept — confirmed by both al-Ṭabarī's rendering and the lexical tradition — is different. The picture is of a soul that has *placed itself* at such a distance from the source of mercy that the rain no longer reaches it. The mercy is still falling. The soul has walked out from under it.

This is the unseen reality the ayah is teaching us to see. The physical sentence — *they are seized and killed* — is the stepping stone. The destination, the thing that cannot be touched or photographed, is **a state of soul outside the reach of rain.**

[PAUSE]

And notice — Allah does not say *malʿūnūn*, the nominative plural, which would be the simple statement of fact. He says *malʿūnīn*, the accusative. They are not standing somewhere being labeled. They are being *encountered in this state.* The grammar itself carries motion. They are caught mid-stride, already in the condition they made themselves.

The curse is not the verdict at the end of the trial. **The curse is who they were when the judgment found them.**

---

### Element Two: ثُقِفُوٓا۟ — The Verb of Skilled Apprehension

Now look at the next word.

**أَيْنَمَا ثُقِفُوٓا۟** — *aynamā thuqifū*. Wherever they are... here the standard translations all say *found*. And something is lost.

The root of **thuqifū** is **ث-ق-ف**. And this is one of those Quranic words where the choice of root tells you something profound about how the encounter is happening.

Let me teach the root before I use it.

In classical Arabic, **thaqifa** — and the verbal noun **thaqāfa** — does not primarily mean "to find." Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Rāghib's Mufradāt both attest to the original semantic field: **skill, sharpness, capability, deftness.** The word **thaqāfa** in modern Arabic, which we translate as *culture*, comes from this root — a person of **thaqāfa** is someone who has been *shaped*, *sharpened*, brought to a kind of cultivated edge. The verb **thaqifa al-rumḥa** in classical poetry means *he straightened the spear* — he made it true and sharp.

So when the root **ث-ق-ف** is used for encountering a person, it does not mean *stumbled upon*. It means **encountered with capability** — apprehended by one who possesses the skill to apprehend.

Allah uses this root in Quranic warfare contexts elsewhere — in Surah Al-Baqarah, in Surah Al-Anfāl, in Surah An-Nisā. In every case, the listener is being told something specific: **the one doing the catching is skilled enough not to miss.**

Now hold this against the previous word.

**Malʿūnīn** — accursed, driven from the field of mercy.
**Aynamā thuqifū** — wherever they are *skillfully apprehended.*

What is the picture? A soul that has placed itself outside the reach of mercy *and* a pursuer whose skill does not fail. Two facts in tension. They cannot escape because they have already left the only sanctuary that existed. Outside that sanctuary, there is no terrain where the skilled apprehender does not reach.

[PAUSE]

This is the geography the ayah is teaching us to see.

We tend to imagine consequence as something that comes *toward* the wrongdoer. The Quranic picture inverts this. Consequence is what is left when the wrongdoer has walked out of the only place where mercy intervened on their behalf. The pursuer is not chasing. The pursuer is simply *present everywhere outside that one place.*

Notice something. Allah does not specify *who* does the catching in this ayah. The verb is passive — **thuqifū**, *they are caught*. The agent is unnamed. This is one of those Quranic absences worth reading carefully. The agent is unnamed because **the agent is not the point.** The point is the geography. The point is that there is no longer anywhere they are not findable.

The next ayah, 33:62, names what kind of reality this is: **sunnat Allāh** — the pattern of Allah. This is a *law*, not a vendetta. A soul that chooses to corrode the trust of a sacred community has, by that very choice, walked out from under mercy's roof and into a region where any storm finds it.

### Element Three: قُتِّلُوا۟ تَقْتِيلًۭا — When Grammar Bends to Carry the Weight

Now we come to the last two words, and here Arabic itself is doing something the listener has to be taught to see.

**أُخِذُوا۟ وَقُتِّلُوا۟ تَقْتِيلًۭا** — *ukhidhū wa-quttilū taqtīlā*.

Let me build this slowly because the construction is doing real structural work.

First: there are two verbs side by side. **Ukhidhū** — they are seized. From the root **أ-خ-ذ**, simple seizure, the ordinary verb. Form I. Nothing unusual in its shape.

Then: **quttilū**. They are killed.

But notice the doubling of the middle radical. *Quttilū*, not *qutilū*. This is the difference between Form I and Form II of the Arabic verb. Form I, **qatala**, is "to kill" — singular, complete. Form II, **qattala**, carries one of two related senses depending on context: it can intensify the verb (a more thorough killing), or it can distribute it (killing carried out across many — what we would call *massacre*, the killing of many one after the other).

The classical grammarians flag this Form II structure here with care. Al-Ṭabarī's tafsīr preserves the full construction, reading *quttilū taqtīlā* as a totalizing, intensified act — and the grammatical architecture carries that weight. The shift from **qatala** to **qattala** is not stylistic. It is a load-bearing grammatical decision.

But Allah doesn't stop there.

The verb **quttilū** is followed by the verbal noun **taqtīlā** — its own cognate noun, taken from the same Form II structure (**taf'īl** is the standard maṣdar pattern for Form II). This construction has a name in Arabic grammar: **al-mafʿūl al-muṭlaq**, the absolute object, or as it's sometimes called, the cognate accusative.

Let me teach this construction before I use it.

In English we have nothing quite like this. The closest we come is doubling for emphasis — "she ran a hard run," "he died a hard death." Arabic formalizes this. The grammar allows you to take the verb you just used and produce a noun from the same root, place it in the accusative, and use it to **confirm**, **specify**, **or intensify** the action you just named.

There are three classical functions of the **mafʿūl muṭlaq**:
- *Li-l-ta'kīd* — for emphasis: making the verb absolutely certain.
- *Li-bayān al-nawʿ* — to specify the kind of action.
- *Li-bayān al-ʿadad* — to specify the number.

Which one is operating here?

Read the construction again: **quttilū taqtīlā** — they were killed with a [thorough/distributive] killing.

It is all three at once. The intensification of Form II already builds *kind* into the verb. The cognate noun in the accusative builds *certainty* on top. The shape of the Arabic itself is bending under the weight of what is being described.

[PAUSE]

This is where you have to ask honestly: why?

Why does Allah, who could have said *qutilū* — they were killed — instead bend the grammar three times? Form II for intensification. Cognate accusative for certainty. The shape of the maṣdar **taqtīlā** lingering on the ear with its long final vowel.

Here is what the construction is doing. The murjifūn used words to do violence to the community. They spread reports designed to make the ground shake. Their crime was not physical at the moment they committed it — it was spoken. Whispered. Rumored.

**And the divine response, when it comes, takes the form of speech that itself has weight.** Form II. Cognate accusative. Maṣdar. The grammar carries the moral arithmetic. *The speech that destabilized a city is answered by speech whose own shape cannot be ignored.*

This is not divine retaliation in kind. This is divine speech arranging itself so that no listener can pretend the matter is light. The weight in the words is the weight of the reality being named.

### Bridge

We have walked through three linguistic features:

A passive participle hanging in the accusative — **malʿūnīn** — describing a state of soul outside mercy's reach, which al-Ṭabarī renders as *maṭrūdīn manfiyyīn* and Qatadah reads as *ʿalā kulli ḥāl* — in every condition, without exception.

A verb of skilled apprehension — **thuqifū** — whose passive voice leaves the agent unnamed because the agent isn't the point. The geography is the point.

And a final construction — **quttilū taqtīlā** — where Form II, the cognate accusative, and the absolute object stack one on top of the other so that Arabic itself buckles under the weight.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Murjif — When Speech Becomes a Weapon Against the Sacred

Before we touch this theme through the ayah, let me start where you actually live.

You have a phone in your pocket. On that phone, every few hours, you see a screenshot. A clipped video. A "leaked" memo. An anonymous account claiming insider knowledge. A "I'm hearing from sources that..." A "wake up, everyone." A "they don't want you to know this." A breathless warning that something terrible is about to happen, or has just happened, or is happening right now and no one is talking about it.

You have felt the small lurch in your stomach. You have felt the ground move slightly. That is precisely the effect. That is what a **murjif** does. That is the work the word names.

And we are not just consumers of this anymore. Most of us, in some small way, have *participated*. Forwarded a thing without checking. Said the dramatic version of a story we half-knew. Spoken in a way designed to elevate our own status by lowering someone else's. Stayed silent when we could have steadied the ground, because steadying the ground was less interesting than letting it shake.

The murjifūn of Madinah were not exotic. They were ordinary people who had discovered that **speech is a lever**. That a sentence whispered at the right moment can move a city. That language, used a certain way, can compress fear into other people's chests and walk away looking innocent.

Now read the ayah again with that in mind.

*Malʿūnīn — accursed — wherever they are skillfully apprehended, they are seized and utterly put to death.*

What the ayah is naming, beneath the violence of its physical sentence, is the **moral seriousness of language used to corrode a community**. The murjifūn are not killed because Allah is offended by their existence. They are placed beyond mercy because **they made it their work to undo the very thing mercy was building**.

A community held together by trust, by Prophetic guidance, by the slow construction of a place where the most vulnerable can sleep — they were taking that apart, sentence by sentence.

[PAUSE]

This is the modern psychological reality the ancient grammar is pointing toward. Every community you belong to — your family, your mosque, your workplace, your neighborhood, your online circle — is held together by something fragile. Trust. The willingness to assume good of one another. The slow-built sense that this is a place where someone vulnerable can be safe.

A murjif disassembles that with words. Not always loudly. Sometimes through innuendo. Sometimes through "concerns." Sometimes through "have you heard?" Sometimes through the carefully shared story whose effect is to make a person, or a group, or a leader, look untrustworthy in the eye of the listener — without the speaker ever having to make a direct accusation.

The classical scholars when they discussed this ayah — al-Baghawī, the position attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and others reported in the standard tafāsīr — kept returning to one observation. **The murjifūn ranked, in the moral architecture of the city, alongside the open hypocrites.** Their tongues did what swords could not. They were named in the same breath, given the same warning, promised the same end.

And the classical commentators were precise about what the murjifūn intended. Al-Muyassar names it directly: *bi-gharaḍ al-fitna wa-l-fasād* — with the deliberate aim of **fitna** and **fasād**. Not accidental alarm-raising. Not careless gossip. **Fitna**: discord, the fracturing of trust between people. **Fasād**: active ruin, the corruption of what is sound. The murjif has a purpose. The speech is not incidental — it is a tool toward a specific end: the destruction of community cohesion. Al-Ṭabarī adds another layer, noting that the intensified consequence attaches to the murjifūn *likufrihim billāh* — for their disbelief in Allah. What expressed itself outward as irjāf — as the deliberate quaking of a city — was rooted inward in a rupture that had already occurred at the level of faith. The social crime and the theological condition were the same reality viewed from two angles.

And then comes the unbearable line in the next ayah: this is **sunnat Allāh**. The pattern. Not specific to Madinah. Not specific to the seventh century. A pattern.

What does it ask of us tomorrow morning? Specifically?

It asks us to audit our own speech. Not for whether what we said was *technically true* — that is the question of an amateur. But for whether what we said **stabilized the ground or shook it.** For whether the foreseeable effect of our words on the listener was *trust* or *tremor*. For whether the community is more held together because we spoke, or less.

That is the test. And it is a test we administer to ourselves before any other test administers itself to us.

### Theme Two: The "Wherever" of Consequence — Inner Geography

Now to the second theme, and the door we enter through is again your own life.

Most of us have had the experience of carrying something with us. A pattern. A way of being. A reflex. And we have noticed — sometimes with a quiet horror — that *it follows us everywhere*. We moved cities to escape it. We changed jobs. We changed marriages. We changed mosques. And there it was, the same pattern, present in the new city, the new job, the new community, as soon as we arrived.

We learned, slowly, that **some things are not about where you are.** They are about who you are when you walk into a room. The room cannot be the variable. *You are the constant.*

This is the deeper meaning of **aynamā thuqifū** — *wherever they are skillfully apprehended*.

The surface reading is geographic. They cannot escape physical capture. True.

But read it as the ayah is teaching you to read — through the physical to the unseen — and a second reading opens. **Aynamā** is also the geography of the soul.

A person who has trained themselves to be a murjif — to destabilize, to corrode, to whisper, to shake — does not stop being one when they cross a border. They walk into the next community and do the same work. They carry the pattern in their tongue. They carry it in their reflexes. They carry it in the small calculation they perform a hundred times a day about how to position themselves above someone else by lowering that someone in the eyes of a third party.

There is no city they enter where they are not, by virtue of who they have made themselves, **catchable**. The grammar of **thuqifū** — the passive of skilled apprehension — names this. The skilled apprehender does not need to chase. The skilled apprehender is **the moral structure of reality itself**, which sooner or later closes around what cannot keep itself hidden.

[PAUSE]

This is what the curse means in the deepest sense. **Malʿūnīn** — driven from mercy. Not because mercy was withdrawn. Because the soul cultivated itself into a shape that mercy could no longer fit inside.

Mercy is a kind of geometry. It enters certain hearts and not others — not because the gate is selective, but because some hearts have grown the shape of a closed fist, and a closed fist cannot receive what an open hand can.

Sit with this. The chilling thing about this ayah is not the physical violence at the end of it. The chilling thing is the first word. **Malʿūnīn.** They were already that, before they were caught. The catching was the recognition. The judgment was the naming of a condition that had already been true.

And the question that lives underneath this — the question we have to be honest about — is: **how does a soul drift into this geography without noticing?**

The classical commentators on this passage — and the patterns we can read in Surah Al-Munāfiqūn, Surah Al-Baqarah's opening, the description of the qulūb maraḍ throughout Al-Ahzab itself — keep returning to a single dynamic. **It begins with the misuse of speech.** Not with grand crimes. With small ones. With the moment a person discovers that a slightly shaded sentence accomplishes something a direct sentence cannot. With the moment language becomes a tool for advantage rather than truth. With the moment the listener becomes a target rather than a person.

Drift. That is the word. There is no border crossing. There is no moment when the soul announces, "I am now a murjif." There is only a slow recalibration of speech-habits, over years, until one day the speaker discovers that their tongue moves toward destabilization the way water moves downhill.

The classical reading holds a mercy inside this reality. Al-Muyassar renders the consequence as attaching *mā dāmū muqīmīna* — *as long as they persist.* The geography of consequence is not determined by a single misused sentence. It is determined by *persistence* — by the repeated choice, until choice hardens into nature. A soul that notices the first crooked sentence and does not say it has not yet built the geography. The door is still open. The rain still reaches.

And by then — *aynamā thuqifū* — there is no city that does not catch them.

What does this ayah ask of us, practically, tomorrow morning?

It asks us to notice the **first** crooked sentence. Not the hundredth. The first. The one we are about to say in five minutes that we know, somewhere underneath thought, is shaped to shake rather than to steady. To name it inside ourselves. To not say it.

Because **the geography is set early.** A soul that catches itself at the first crooked sentence is a soul still under mercy's roof. A soul that has practiced the crooked sentence for ten thousand repetitions has built its own location outside that roof — and the rain no longer reaches.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now, with the work done, look at the shape of the ayah.

Three verbs in sequence. **Malʿūnīn** — they *are* (state). **Ukhidhū** — they are *seized* (event). **Quttilū taqtīlā** — they are *utterly killed* (consequence, with the grammar itself escalating to carry the weight).

State → event → consequence.

But notice — the state comes **first**. The state is already true *before* the event. The grammar of the ḥāl, the accusative *malʿūnīn* hanging in the air before any verb has been uttered, is telling us something about how divine judgment works.

The judgment is the recognition of who you already are. The seizure and the consequence that follow are not what *makes* the soul accursed. They are what *makes visible* the curse the soul had already entered.

This is the architecture. And the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **linguistic** side: a passive participle of state, rendered by al-Ṭabarī as *expelled and banished*, read by Qatadah as *accursed in every condition*; a passive verb of skilled apprehension; and a cognate accusative whose grammar bends to carry weight.

The **thematic** side: a soul that drifted out from under mercy's roof through the slow misuse of speech — with the deliberate aim, named by the classical sources, of fitna and fasād — and a pursuer, the moral structure of reality, whose skill does not miss; and a consequence whose weight is the weight of what was actually done.

Both sides say the same thing. *The state is the destination. The capture and the killing are only the moment the state became visible.*

### Questions to Carry

1. When I spoke this week — in person, in a message, in a voice note, in a forwarded post — was the foreseeable effect of my speech to *steady* the ground under another person, or to *shake* it?

2. Is there a community in my life that is more whole because I am in it, or less? What did my last three weeks of speech actually do to the trust in that community?

3. Have I ever caught myself in a crooked sentence — a sentence shaped to lower someone in a listener's eyes — and *not* said it? When was the last time?

4. The word *murjif* names a soul that has trained its tongue toward destabilization. Where in my speech-habits is that training underway, even slightly? What is the *first* sentence I am about to say tomorrow that I should stop?

5. **Malʿūnīn** means *driven from mercy* — maṭrūdīn manfiyyīn, expelled and banished. If mercy is a geometry that fits certain shapes of soul and not others — what shape is my soul taking? And how do I know?

### The Closing Invitation

This is a hard ayah. We should not pretend otherwise. We should also not soften it into something it is not, because softening it costs us the precision of what Allah is actually teaching.

What should shift after this is not your view of *other* people. The temptation with verses like this — the temptation Allah's enemies count on you taking — is to read it outward, to find the accursed in someone else, to wield the verse as a weapon.

But the ayah is structured to be read **inward**. The grammar opens with a state — *malʿūnīn* — before any agent is named. You are invited to ask: *am I, in any small way, in the room where this state takes shape? Have I trained my tongue, in any small way, toward the work of the murjif?*

If the answer is even a quiet *yes, slightly* — then the mercy of this ayah is that it came **before** the catching. You are still under the roof. The rain is still falling. The condition for consequence is persistence, not a single slip. Al-Muyassar's *mā dāmū muqīmīna* — *as long as they persist* — is the opening the ayah leaves for anyone who has not yet made destabilization their permanent home. The first crooked sentence you do not say tomorrow morning is the beginning of an inner migration back to where the rain reaches.

That is the invitation.

### Du'a

O Allah — keep our tongues steady where they would shake the ground. Place our speech in the service of the trust You are building between Your servants. And if we have drifted, in even the smallest way, into the geography of the murjif — pull us back under the roof of Your mercy before the rain stops reaching us.

Āmīn.
