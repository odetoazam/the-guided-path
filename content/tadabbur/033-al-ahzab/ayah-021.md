# The Example That Binds Wounds

## Surah Al-Ahzab, Ayah 21

> لَّقَدْ كَانَ لَكُمْ فِى رَسُولِ ٱللَّهِ أُسْوَةٌ حَسَنَةٌ لِّمَن كَانَ يَرْجُو ٱللَّهَ وَٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَ وَذَكَرَ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًا

*Laqad kāna lakum fī rasūli-Llāhi uswatun ḥasanatun li-man kāna yarjū-Llāha wa-l-yawma-l-ākhira wa-dhakara-Llāha kathīrā.*

**"Indeed, in the Messenger of Allah you have an excellent example — for whoever hopes in Allah and the Last Day and remembers Allah often."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this verse walked into

Before we can hear this ayah properly, we have to stand inside the moment Allah spoke it. Because this is one of the most quoted verses in the entire Quran — you have probably heard it a hundred times, on minbars, in books on character, on calligraphy on someone's wall. *"The Messenger of Allah is the best example."* We have heard it so often that we have almost stopped hearing it.

But Allah did not reveal it as a poster. He revealed it inside a siege.

The year is the fifth year after the migration. The city of Madinah is small — a few thousand souls scattered across a cluster of neighbourhoods and date orchards. And around the northern edge of the city, the believers have spent weeks digging a trench. The Prophet ﷺ digs with them. He carries stones with them. He ties stones to his stomach when there is no food, the same way they do.

Coming toward them is not an army. It is a confederation. **Al-Aḥzāb** — *the confederates*, *the parties*, *the leagues*. The Quraysh of Mecca have come with their full force. The Ghaṭafān have joined them. Bedouin tribes from across the peninsula have joined them. Surah Al-Ahzab opens with the number Allah Himself reports: ten thousand. Ten thousand against a community whose entire fighting strength is around three thousand.

And then, while the believers are pinned against the trench in the north, the news arrives that Banū Qurayẓah — a tribe inside Madinah, sworn by treaty to defend the city — has broken faith and joined the enemy. Now there is an army outside the trench, and there is a knife at the back of every Muslim home, where their women and children are sheltering. Look at how Allah describes what that did to people, a few verses earlier in this same surah:

> *"When they came at you from above you and from below you, and when the eyes shifted in terror and the hearts reached the throats…"* (33:10)

That is the climate. Eyes that cannot focus. Hearts physically rising in the chest. The body in revolt against itself.

And then — almost as if a camera that has been shaking finally steadies — Allah says one sentence:

> *Laqad kāna lakum fī rasūli-Llāhi uswatun ḥasanah.*

### The hook

When you put a verse about "the beautiful example" on a poster, you imagine it being spoken in a moment of calm reflection. But this verse was not spoken in calm. It was spoken when the wind was tearing the tents apart. It was spoken when one group of believers was making excuses to leave, when another group was being tested past the edge of what they thought they could bear.

So what does Allah actually mean here? Because the word He chooses for "example" — **uswah** — does not primarily mean "role model" the way we use that phrase. The root of this word, in classical Arabic, has nothing to do with imitation. It has to do with **binding wounds**.

Stay with that. We are about to walk into a verse that, when you read its grammar in Arabic, says something stunning: in a siege, when the wounds of fear and abandonment and exhaustion are being inflicted on you, *the binding of those wounds is in the person of the Messenger himself* — but only for the kind of heart that can see it.

Let's open it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word أُسْوَة (*uswah*) — what it actually means

Before we step into the Arabic, let's establish a principle, because this is going to matter for the whole verse.

In Arabic, **words are not arbitrary**. Every word carries a root — usually three letters — and that root has a *core image*. From that core image, all the derived words radiate outward. Words that share a root share that core image, even when their surface meanings have drifted apart. So if you want to know what a word *really* means, you do not look only at how it is used. You look at the root, you find the core image, and then you ask: what is this particular word's relationship to that image?

Here is a simple example in English. The word "sympathy" sounds emotional. But its root, *sym-pathos*, means *to suffer together*. So when you say "I sympathise," what you are actually saying, at the root level, is *I am suffering with you*. The grammatical sense has drifted from the root — but the root is still doing work underneath.

Arabic does this far more aggressively than English. The root almost never disappears.

Now look at the word Allah uses here:

> **أُسْوَةٌ** — *uswah*

Most translations render it as "example" or "pattern." That is correct on the surface. But the root is أ-س-و, and **the core image of this root is not imitation. It is the binding of a wound.**

Open any classical Arabic dictionary — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib, Tāj al-ʿArūs — and you will find the same family of meanings clustering around this root:

- *Asā al-jurḥ* — to treat and bind a wound.
- *Al-āsī* — the physician who treats injuries.
- *Muwāsāh* — to console someone in their grief; to share in their suffering; to give of what you have so that the burden falls equally on both of you.

Notice something. Notice something carefully.

**The root has nothing to do with copying. It has everything to do with the closing of pain.**

So when Allah says "*lakum fī rasūli-Llāhi uswah*," at the root level, what He is saying is closer to: *"For you, in the Messenger of Allah, there is a binding of your wound."*

Now stop. Remember the moment we are inside. The trench. The wind. Ten thousand outside the gate. Banū Qurayẓah behind you. The hypocrites slipping away. *Hearts in throats.*

Of all the words Allah could have used for "example" — and Arabic has several — He chooses the one whose root means *to treat your injury*.

[PAUSE]

He is not saying, in this moment, "Now go and try to imitate his prayer style." He is saying something much more tender. He is saying: *the medicine for what is happening to you right now is to look at him. The bandage for the wound that the siege is inflicting is the sight of his face among you.*

And that is why classical scholars, when they unpacked this verse, did not race to the question *"What did the Prophet do that we should copy?"* They began with the question, *"What did the Prophet's presence do to a believer's heart in that hour?"* Imam al-Rāzī notes that the verse is placed exactly where it is placed — between two descriptions of fear — because the example is being offered as relief from fear, not as a curriculum.

The example is medicine before it is a model.

So before we ever get to *what* we are to imitate, the verse is asking us to feel *that* we are to be consoled. The Prophet ﷺ is not, first, a syllabus. He is, first, a comfort.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The shape of the sentence — what comes first, and what is left bare

Now we are going to look not at a word, but at the shape of the sentence. Because Arabic, even more than English, uses *word order* as a tool. When something is moved out of its expected position, it is being emphasised. When something is left without an article — without "the" — that bareness is itself a statement.

Let's begin with a small principle. In English, you can say:

> "I gave the book to him."

Or:

> "To him I gave the book."

The second one sounds strange — but you can hear that something has shifted. The emphasis has moved. *Him* is now the focus. The grammar is doing emotional work.

Arabic does this constantly, and it does it with precision.

The standard, neutral order in Arabic for this sentence would be:

> *Kāna fī rasūli-Llāhi lakum uswatun ḥasanah.*
> "There was, in the Messenger of Allah, for you, a beautiful example."

But that is not what Allah said. He said:

> *Kāna **lakum** fī rasūli-Llāhi uswatun ḥasanah.*
> "There was — **for you** — in the Messenger of Allah, a beautiful example."

The "for you" — **lakum** — has been pulled forward, placed before the source. In Arabic rhetoric, this kind of fronting is called *taqdīm*, and its function is *takhṣīṣ* and *ihtimām* — restriction and emphasis. Translated into plain English, the effect is: *"This is for **you** — you specifically, you the besieged believers, you who are reading this verse right now."*

Notice something. In a moment of terror, what does a person feel? *Forgotten.* *Overlooked.* *That no one knows what I am going through.* The very first thing the structure of the sentence does — before it gives you the noun, before it tells you what the example is — is to grab you by the shoulders and say *lakum*. **For you.** This is addressed to you. You have not been left out of God's gaze.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that. Because there is a second feature of this sentence that does even more work, and it is so easy to miss that most readers miss it for their entire lives.

The word **uswah** is indefinite. There is no "the" in front of it. In Arabic this is shown by the *tanwīn* — the *un* sound at the end: *uswat**un**.* It is *an* example, not *the* example.

Now ask yourself: doesn't that seem strange? If you wanted to say "the Prophet is *the* perfect example," you would put "al-" in front of it. *Al-uswah al-ḥasanah.* "*The* beautiful example."

But Allah does not say that. He says *uswatun ḥasanah*. **A** beautiful example.

Why?

In classical Arabic balāghah, an indefinite noun used in this way carries the meaning of *ta'ẓīm* — *magnification*. You can hear something like this in English when someone says: *"There is a man — a man like you have never met."* The indefiniteness is not a downgrade. It is the opposite. It is the speaker saying: *the category is too large for the definite article to contain it. I will not call him "the man" because that would limit him. I will say "a man" — and you will understand that the kind of man I mean is beyond what "the man" could ever capture.*

That is what *uswatun ḥasanah* is doing. It is not a smaller claim than "the example." It is a vaster one. **"An example — and what an example. An example so comprehensive that no article could pin it down."**

And then look at what comes next to it. *Ḥasanah.* "Beautiful." Also indefinite. Also feminine to agree with *uswah*. Two soft, open vowels closing the phrase — *us-wa-tun ḥa-sa-na-tun* — like a hand laid gently on a forehead.

Put it all together. The shape of the sentence does three things at once:

1. It places **"for you"** before anything else — so that the wounded person knows they are the addressee.
2. It leaves **"example"** indefinite — so that the example is *too large* to be reduced to a definition.
3. It pairs it with **"beautiful"** — softening the texture of the sentence at the exact point where the human heart most needs a soft texture.

[PAUSE]

This is not the grammar of a lecture. This is the grammar of a hand on a shoulder.

### Element Three: The three coordinates — who can actually see the example

And now we come to the third linguistic feature, and this one will reframe everything.

After Allah says "*for you, in the Messenger of Allah, there is a beautiful example,*" He immediately adds a restriction:

> *li-man kāna yarjū-Llāha wa-l-yawma-l-ākhira wa-dhakara-Llāha kathīrā*

> **"…for whoever hopes in Allah and the Last Day, and remembers Allah often."**

That **li-man** — "for the one who" — is doing something theologically and psychologically enormous. It is telling you that the example is not automatically available to every set of eyes that looks at it. There is a condition. Or rather, there are three conditions. And only the kind of heart that meets these three conditions can actually *see* what is there.

Think about that. The Prophet ﷺ was physically visible to thousands of people in his lifetime. People who hated him saw him. People who fought him saw him. Some of his own relatives, who saw him every day, never followed him. So *physical sight of the example* and *receiving the example as healing* are two different things. The first is given to all. The second is given only to certain hearts.

What are those three conditions? Allah lists them with surgical care.

**One: *yarjū-Llāh* — hoping in Allah.**

The root is ر-ج-و, and *rajāʾ* in classical lexicons is not "wish." It is *hope that has an expectation underneath it*. It is the hope of someone who has put effort in and now leans toward the outcome they have been working for. It is not "I hope I win the lottery." It is "I hope this seed I planted will grow." Hope with a tilt of the whole self toward Allah.

**Two: *al-yawma-l-ākhir* — and the Last Day.**

The second coordinate is *time*. Hoping in Allah is the vertical axis — pointed upward. The Last Day is the horizontal axis — pointed forward. You are oriented not only to Who is above you, but to what is coming toward you. The life of this world is no longer the whole story; it is the opening chapter.

**Three: *dhakara-Llāha kathīrā* — and he remembered Allah often.**

The third is the practice. Not "thought about Allah." Not "believed in Allah." But ***remembered***. *Dhikr*. From a root whose core image is *bringing to mind what is otherwise forgotten*. And the qualifier — *kathīran*, "much" — does enormous work. Allah did not say "he remembered Allah." He said *he remembered Allah **much**.* Frequently. Habitually. As a feature of his life, not a feature of his Friday.

So three coordinates: a hope that leans, a horizon that extends, a tongue that returns.

Now stop and notice the architecture. **These three are the eyes with which the example becomes visible as an example.**

[PAUSE]

Look around in the world. Many people have studied the Prophet's life ﷺ as historians. Many have analysed his battles, his treaties, his speeches. They have all looked at the same data. But the data has done different things to different hearts. To some, he is a fascinating seventh-century figure. To some, he is a model statesman. To some — the ones who carry these three coordinates — he is the binding of wounds, the medicine in the siege, the example whose presence pulls them through.

The difference is not in the data. The difference is in the eyes.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge into the themes

We have now seen three things. We have seen that the word *uswah* is, at its root, the binding of a wound — that the Prophet ﷺ is given to us first as consolation, not first as curriculum. We have seen that the very shape of the sentence — "for you" forward, "an example" indefinite, "beautiful" attached — is doing the work of a hand laid on a shoulder. And we have seen that the example is not automatically visible to every viewer; it requires three coordinates — hope, the Last Day, much remembrance — to come into focus.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we actually live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How we choose who to look up to — and why Allah's choice is different

Let's start where you live. Think about how the modern world chooses role models.

You scroll a feed. Someone has built a company. Someone has a body you would like to have. Someone speaks with a confidence you do not have. Someone has a marriage that looks the way you wish yours looked. And so you bookmark them. You watch their morning routine. You buy the book. You try to imitate.

What is the underlying logic? *I want their outcome. I will copy their inputs. Maybe I will get their output.*

This is what the world means by "role model." A role model is a person whose life is functioning at a level you aspire to, and who therefore becomes a template. The role model is *useful*. They are *productive*. They tell you what to do in the morning.

Now hear what Allah offers, and feel the difference.

He does not offer a *role model*. He offers an **uswah**. And we have seen what *uswah* means at the root. It does not mean *the person whose life you should imitate so you can get their results*. It means *the person whose presence treats your wound*.

This is a completely different category. Because notice — when do you need a role model? When you want to grow, when you want to optimise, when you want to perform better. When do you need an *uswah*? When you are bleeding.

The two are not the same. One is upgrade. The other is healing.

And here is the heart of it. The Quran is revealing the Prophet ﷺ in the second category, not the first. He is not, first, a productivity hack. He is, first, a consolation. The whole reason he is named *Rasūl Allāh* — the Messenger of Allah — in this very verse, rather than by his personal name, is that the title carries the consolation. *You are not alone. The One who made you sent someone to you. That someone is in your sight. Hold onto him.*

[PAUSE]

Now translate this into a life. Most of us, in our pain, are looking for an upgrade. We are looking for the productivity guru who will tell us how to handle our anxiety, how to optimise our sleep, how to fix our marriage. We approach the Prophet's life the same way — as a kind of seventh-century optimisation manual. *What did he eat. What time did he wake. How did he train.*

And there is a place for that. But this verse, in the context of the trench, is telling us something different. It is telling us that long before he is your manual, **he is your medicine**. Long before he tells you how to sleep, his presence in your awareness *lets you sleep*. Long before he tells you how to handle fear, the fact that *Allah sent him to you* is, itself, a treatment for the fear.

What does this change about how you live?

It changes the *posture* in which you read his life. You do not read his sīrah the way you read a self-help book. You read it the way someone in grief sits with a friend who has carried the same grief. You are not, in those moments, taking notes. You are taking comfort. The notes will come later, when the wound has closed enough that you can think again.

This is why the great scholars of the heart said: *recitation of the description of the Prophet ﷺ — the shamāʾil — is itself a form of healing.* Not because of any magic in the words, but because his being is constituted, by God's choice, as *uswah* — and an *uswah* is, at its root, a healer of wounds.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The three coordinates — why some people see and some do not

Now to the second theme, and this is the harder one.

Allah does not offer the example automatically. He says: *the example is there, for those who hope in Allah and the Last Day, and remember Allah often.*

Why this condition? Why are these three things, specifically, the eyes through which the example becomes visible?

Imagine someone hands you a beautifully written book in a language you do not read. The book is there. The meaning is there. The wisdom is there. But for you, in this moment, it is closed. The book has not failed. *You do not yet have the language.*

The Prophet's life ﷺ is exactly like that. The data of his life — the sīrah, the ḥadīth, the descriptions — is available to anyone with a search engine. But the *meaning* of that data, the way it can heal a wound, requires a language. And Allah tells us what the three letters of that language are.

**The first letter is *rajāʾ* — hope.**

If your heart does not lean toward Allah with expectation, the Prophet's life will not console you. It will inform you. It might impress you. But it will not bind your wound. Because consolation requires a heart that is *reaching* — and *rajāʾ* is precisely that reaching posture. Without it, the example bounces off the surface of you.

How is your *rajāʾ*? Not your belief that Allah exists. Not your knowledge that He is merciful. But your daily, leaning, expectant reach toward Him — the way a plant in a dim room turns its leaves toward the window. Is your heart turned toward Him that way?

**The second letter is *al-yawm al-ākhir* — the Last Day.**

If your imagination ends at this life, the Prophet's life will look like a story that ended in the seventh century. Closed. Finished. A historical figure. But if your imagination extends to the Last Day, suddenly his life is not *behind* you. It is *with* you. He is, in the language of the Qur'an, a *witness*. You will see him. He will see you. The example is alive because the *examplar* is, in the most real sense, your future.

How is your sense of the Last Day? Not your theological agreement that it exists. But the felt sense that what you are doing right now will be replayed there. Is it close? Or has it drifted to the edge of your awareness?

**The third letter is *dhakara-Llāha kathīrā* — remembered Allah much.**

This is the one we most overlook. Notice the qualifier. *Kathīran.* Not "remembered Allah." But "remembered Allah **often**." Frequently. Habitually. As a default state.

Why is this the third coordinate? Because without dhikr, the other two collapse. Hope drains away when the mind is not refilled. The Last Day fades into the background when nothing in your day says the name of God. **The remembrance is the maintenance of the other two.** It is the tongue's way of keeping the heart's posture from slumping.

How much do you remember Allah? Not on Fridays. Not when you sit on the prayer mat. But *kathīran* — in the car, in the queue, between meetings, lying awake at 3am? Is His name a frequent visitor on your tongue, or a stranger?

[PAUSE]

Here is what the verse is telling you, with quiet seriousness. **You can read the same sīrah that everyone else reads, and have it do nothing for you, if you do not carry these three coordinates.** You can know the dates of every battle. You can quote every ḥadīth. And still be untouched, still be unconsoled, still be unable to see the example as anything but a story.

But conversely — and this is the merciful side of the same coin — **if you carry these three coordinates, even a small encounter with his life can heal you.** A single ḥadīth, read with these eyes, can rearrange your whole week. A single image of his face among the trench-diggers — stones tied to his stomach, hands black with earth — can pull a tear out of a heart that thought it was beyond crying.

The example does not change. The eyes change.

What does this change about how you live?

It changes the *order of operations*. Most of us, when we want to draw closer to the Prophet ﷺ, start by reading more about him. But this verse suggests a different sequence. *Start with the eyes.* Cultivate the hope. Pull the Last Day forward in your imagination. Put his Master's name on your tongue. **Then** open the sīrah. You will find a different book than you read last time.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture of a single sentence

Now look back at what we have walked through, and watch how every piece fits together.

The verse begins with **emphasis** — *laqad*, "indeed" — preparing the wounded heart to receive something certain in a moment of total uncertainty.

It places **"for you"** before everything else — telling the besieged believer that they specifically are the addressee.

It names the source as **"the Messenger of Allah"** — using the title that carries the consolation, *the one Allah sent to you*.

It chooses the word **uswah** — from a root that means *the binding of a wound* — at the exact moment wounds are being inflicted.

It leaves *uswah* **indefinite** — so the example cannot be pinned down to one definition.

It pairs it with **beautiful** — softening the texture of the sentence at the exact place a soft touch is needed.

And then — only *then*, after all of this consolation has been offered — it adds the three coordinates: **hope, Last Day, much remembrance** — the eyes through which the consolation can be received as consolation.

This is not a verse. **This is a piece of architecture.** Every word is load-bearing. Every word is doing the work of treating a wound that the next ayāt of the surah will describe in detail.

### Questions to carry

Take these with you. They are not rhetorical. Sit with them in your own time.

1. When you think of the Prophet ﷺ, do you think of him first as a *model* you should imitate — or first as a *consolation* Allah sent for your wounds? What would change in your reading of his life if the order were reversed?

2. The verse offers the example to those who hope, who orient to the Last Day, and who remember Allah often. Of those three coordinates, which is the weakest in your life right now? What would it look like to strengthen it, even by a little?

3. Allah revealed this verse in the middle of a siege — not in a moment of calm. What is the siege in your life right now? And what would it mean to look at the Prophet ﷺ for binding of that wound, rather than for solutions to the problem?

4. The example is grammatically indefinite — *an* example, not *the* example — because no definite article could contain him. What part of his life have you not yet seen, because you have not yet had the eyes for it?

5. *Dhakara-Llāha kathīran* — *remembered Allah often*. Not Friday-much. *Often.* If someone followed you around for a day with a counter, how many times would your tongue or your heart return to Allah's name?

### One-sentence distillation

**In the hour the wound is being inflicted, the binding of the wound is in the person of the Messenger ﷺ — but only the eyes of hope, of the Last Day, and of constant remembrance can see the bandage that is already in their hand.**

### The closing invitation

You do not need to change everything tomorrow. You need to change the *posture* in which you approach his life ﷺ.

The next time you open his sīrah, or read a ḥadīth, or hear a story about him — do not approach it as someone trying to optimise. Approach it as someone in a siege approaching a healer. Let the data of his life *touch* the data of your life. Let his stones at the trench sit next to your exhaustion at the desk. Let his hunger sit next to your hunger. Let his face — described in the books as *kāna kal-qamari laylata-l-badr*, "like the moon on the night of fullness" — sit in your mind's eye when the wind of your own *aḥzāb* is tearing at your tent.

That is what Allah is offering in this verse. Not a syllabus. A medicine.

And ask, in your own way, that He give you the three coordinates by which the medicine becomes visible.

### A brief du'a

*Allāhumma*, You named Your Messenger ﷺ a binding for our wounds. Bind ours. Give us the hope that leans toward You, the imagination that holds the Last Day close, and a tongue that returns to Your name in the small hours. And when the *aḥzāb* of our lives close in — let our eyes find him, and through him, find You.

*Āmīn.*
