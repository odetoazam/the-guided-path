# When the People Ask About the Hour

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
يَسْـَٔلُكَ ٱلنَّاسُ عَنِ ٱلسَّاعَةِ ۖ قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ تَكُونُ قَرِيبًا

**Transliteration:**
*Yas'aluka an-nāsu ʿani s-sāʿah. Qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh. Wa mā yudrīka laʿalla s-sāʿata takūnu qarīban.*

**Translation:**
The people ask you about the Hour. Say: Its knowledge is only with Allah. And what would make you know? Perhaps the Hour is near.

---

## Introduction

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to understand the room it was spoken into.

Surah Al-Ahzab has been building toward a confrontation. For most of the surah, Allah has been speaking to a community under siege — literally, during the Battle of the Trench, and metaphorically, by a network of hypocrites and "those with disease in their hearts" who whispered against the Prophet ﷺ, harassed the believing women, and circulated rumors meant to destabilize the community from within.

Just three ayāt before our verse, Allah issued an extraordinary warning. Ayah 60 says: *"If the hypocrites and those with disease in their hearts and the agitators in the city do not stop, We will surely incite you against them..."* — and then ayah 62 closes that warning with the heavy phrase: *"the way of Allah with those who passed before — and you will never find any change in the way of Allah."*

That is the moment our ayah enters.

Allah has just said: **a reckoning is coming. The pattern is fixed. There will be consequences.**

And the response from the questioners — the ones with disease in their hearts, the ones who heard the warning and felt its edge — is not repentance. It is not silence. It is a question:

*"Okay then. When? When exactly is this 'Hour' you keep threatening us with? Give us a date."*

Imagine someone telling a young man heading down a dangerous path, "If you don't change, you will destroy your life." And the young man, instead of considering whether to change, says with a smirk: "Oh yeah? When? What day? What time? Be specific."

The question is not a question. It is a deflection. It is a way of avoiding the warning by demanding a precision the warner cannot give, so that the failure to produce that precision becomes an excuse to dismiss everything that was said.

That is the energy this ayah responds to.

### The Hook

Here is what's strange. The Prophet ﷺ is being asked a question. And Allah does not tell him to answer it.

Allah tells him to respond — but the response is not an answer. The response is a redirection that, if you read it slowly, actually contains *three different moves* in fifteen words. It refuses the question, it humbles the questioner, and then — at the very end — it does something that should make the questioners' blood run cold.

It gives them an answer they didn't see coming.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Yas'aluka an-nās* — "The People Ask You"

Let's begin with the opening phrase. In Arabic: **يَسْـَٔلُكَ ٱلنَّاسُ** — *yas'aluka an-nās* — "the people ask you."

Before we dive in, let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work, because it matters here.

In Arabic, there are two main verb tenses: the *māḍī* (past, completed action) and the *muḍāriʿ* (present-continuous, ongoing or repeated action). The choice between them is not just about *when* something happened. It's about *how* it's happening — whether it's a finished event or a recurring pattern.

If I want to say "He asked you," in past tense, I would say *sa'alaka*. One event. Done.

But here, Allah uses *yas'aluka* — the *muḍāriʿ*, the imperfect. **"They keep asking you. They are continuously asking you."**

Notice what this means. This is not one questioner who came up to the Prophet ﷺ once and asked, "Hey, when is the Hour?" This is an ongoing pattern. A drumbeat. The question keeps coming. Day after day. Person after person.

Stay with that for a moment.

Now look at *who* is asking. Allah says **ٱلنَّاسُ** — *an-nās* — "the people." Not "the disbelievers." Not "the hypocrites." Not "those with disease in their hearts." Just... *the people*.

Why?

Here's what's remarkable. Throughout Surah Al-Ahzab, Allah has named the groups very specifically — *al-munāfiqūn* (the hypocrites), *alladhīna fī qulūbihim maraḍ* (those with disease in their hearts), *al-murjifūn fī l-madīnah* (the agitators in the city). He has been precise. He has been pointed.

But here, suddenly, **the specificity drops away.** "*The people* ask you."

Why generalize at the very moment of confrontation?

Let's sit with this. One reading — and the classical commentators including al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi point this out — is that the question about the Hour was so widespread, so endemic, that it had become a shared posture. The hypocrites asked it sneeringly. The doubters asked it cynically. Even some of the curious among the broader population asked it because the conversation was in the air. The disease of *"prove it to me on my terms"* had become a kind of cultural water that everyone was drinking.

So Allah doesn't say "the hypocrites ask you." He says **"the people ask you"** — because the disease has spread beyond the diseased.

Notice something else. By using *an-nās*, Allah also strips the questioners of any title that would dignify their question. They are not "the seekers of truth" or "the inquirers." They are just... *people*. A faceless crowd asking the same tired question.

[PAUSE]

That is the first thing this ayah does. Before it says anything about the Hour, it places the question in a frame: this is a recurring noise from an undifferentiated crowd, asking something they should not be asking.

### Element Two: *Innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh* — "Its Knowledge Is Only With Allah"

Now look at the response Allah commands. **قُلْ إِنَّمَا عِلْمُهَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** — *Qul innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh* — "Say: its knowledge is only with Allah."

I want you to slow down on one tiny word: **إِنَّمَا** — *innamā*.

In Arabic grammar, *innamā* is called a **particle of restriction** (*adāt al-ḥaṣr*). Its job is to fence something in — to say "this and *only* this; nothing else."

In English we say "only" and it sounds soft. In Arabic, *innamā* is a wall. It is the grammatical equivalent of slamming a door.

Think of it this way. If I say to you, "The key is in my pocket," that's information. But if I say, "The key is *only* in my pocket — nowhere else, with no one else, not in any drawer, not in any other person's hand" — now I have not just told you where the key is. I have closed off every other possibility.

That is what *innamā* does here.

Allah does not just say *ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh* — "its knowledge is with Allah." That would be true and complete. He says *innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh* — **"its knowledge is *only* with Allah. Nowhere else. With no one else. Not with any prophet. Not with any angel. Not in any book. Not anywhere except Allah Himself."**

Why is the wall so high? Because the question being asked is a *trap*. The questioners are not asking for information. They are asking because they assume the Prophet ﷺ either knows and won't tell, or doesn't know and is therefore a fraud. They have set up a dilemma. Allah's response, through *innamā*, blows up both horns of the dilemma at once: **"The Prophet doesn't know — and the fact that he doesn't know is not a defect in him. It is a feature of the knowledge itself. This knowledge is *only* with Allah. By design."**

Now look at the next word: **عِندَ** — *ʿinda*. "With."

Arabic has multiple words that can be translated as "with." *Maʿa* means "alongside, accompanying." *Ladā* means "in someone's presence." *ʿInda* means "with, in the possession of, in the custody of" — and it carries the sense of something *belonging to* the one who holds it.

So *ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh* is not just "the knowledge is hanging out near Allah." It is **"this knowledge is in the possession of Allah, belonging to Him, kept by Him, custodied by Him."**

Put these together: *innamā ʿilmuhā ʿinda Llāh* says — **the knowledge of the Hour is the exclusive, custodied, withheld property of Allah, kept by no one else, accessible to no one else, possessed by no one else.**

That is one phrase doing the work of an entire theological doctrine.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa Mā Yudrīka* — "And What Would Make You Know?"

Now we come to the most extraordinary phrase in the ayah. Maybe in the whole surah.

**وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ** — *Wa mā yudrīka.*

Translated literally: "And what would make you know?" Or even more bluntly: "What could possibly cause you to know?"

The verb is *yudrīka* — from the root *d-r-y*, which means "to know through evidence, to perceive through clues, to figure out." The morphology here is **Form IV** (*adrā*), which functions transitively — *to cause someone to know, to make someone aware.*

So *mā yudrīka* is literally: "What would cause-you-to-know?" or "What would make-you-aware?"

And the question is rhetorical. The implied answer is: **nothing**. Nothing would make you know. There is no clue, no evidence, no piece of information that could possibly inform you of when the Hour will arrive — because the knowledge is *innamā ʿinda Llāh*, locked exclusively with Allah.

But here is where you need to slow way down.

**Who is Allah addressing with *wa mā yudrīka*?**

Look at the grammar. The *-ka* at the end of *yudrīka* is the singular masculine pronoun — *you* (one person). And we have just been told, in the previous phrase, **قُلْ** — *qul* — "Say." Who is being told to say? The Prophet ﷺ.

So grammatically, the *-ka* in *yudrīka* should refer to the Prophet ﷺ.

But pause. Listen to what this means.

Allah is telling the Prophet ﷺ to relay this message to the people, and then, in the middle of that very message, Allah turns and says to the Prophet himself: *"And what would make **you** — Muhammad — know?"*

Let's sit with that.

This is the most beloved human being in creation. The one whom Allah took up through the heavens on the Night of Ascent. The one who received the Quran. The one whom Allah calls *raḥmatan li-l-ʿālamīn* — a mercy to all the worlds.

And Allah says to him, in front of his own followers, **"And what would make even *you* know?"**

This is staggering humility imposed on the Prophet ﷺ — not as a humiliation, but as a *liberation*. Allah is publicly removing from the Prophet's shoulders any expectation that he should know. He is preempting the trap. The questioners were hoping to pin the Prophet ﷺ as either a knower-who-won't-tell or a fraud-who-can't-know. Allah lifts the entire false dichotomy off him: *"He is the Prophet. He is not the Hour-knower. No one is the Hour-knower except Me."*

Now here is where it gets even more remarkable.

If the *-ka* is the Prophet ﷺ, then by extension — and the classical commentators including al-Jalalayn read it this way — the address radiates outward. *If even the Prophet ﷺ is told "what would make you know," then certainly anyone asking the question gets the same answer with a hundred times the force.* If the most-knowing human being in creation does not know, what makes you, the questioner, think you have a right to demand the answer?

The verse silences the questioners not by attacking them, but by humbling the one whom they were trying to catch out. And in his humbling, they are humbled a thousand times more.

[PAUSE]

But there is still one more layer. The deepest one.

Some classical commentators — particularly in the layers preserved by al-Qurtubi — note that *wa mā yudrīka* is a phrase Allah uses elsewhere in the Quran in a very specific pattern. When Allah says *wa mā adrāka* (the past-tense form, "what made you know"), it is almost always followed by an *explanation*. "And what would make you know what the Hour of striking is? *Al-qāriʿah*..." — and then a description follows.

But when Allah says *wa mā yudrīka* (the imperfect, "what *would* make you know") — He almost always **leaves it unanswered.** The question dangles. The veil stays drawn. The Prophet ﷺ is told, and through him the people are told: *"This is one of those things that even the asking does not lead to the answering."*

[PAUSE]

So we have three linguistic moves so far. The questioners have been generalized into a faceless crowd (*an-nās*). The knowledge has been sealed exclusively with Allah (*innamā ʿinda Llāh*). And the Prophet himself has been publicly relieved of any expectation to know (*wa mā yudrīka*).

Now let's see what Allah does with the silence He has created.

### Element Four: *Laʿalla s-Sāʿata Takūnu Qarīban* — "Perhaps the Hour Is Near"

The final phrase. **لَعَلَّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ تَكُونُ قَرِيبًا** — *laʿalla s-sāʿata takūnu qarīban* — "Perhaps the Hour is near."

Let me teach you the word *laʿalla*.

In Arabic grammar, *laʿalla* is a particle that classically carries the meaning **"perhaps, it may be that, hopefully."** It expresses *expectation* or *anticipation*. In human speech, it usually carries uncertainty — "perhaps it will rain tomorrow" — because the speaker doesn't actually know.

But here is the question that the classical scholars wrestled with: **how can Allah, who knows everything, say "perhaps"?** Doesn't *laʿalla* imply uncertainty? And doesn't uncertainty contradict divine omniscience?

The classical answer, preserved in tafsir tradition, is profound. When *laʿalla* comes from Allah, it does not express uncertainty in the speaker. It expresses **certainty that the listener should treat as imminent.** It is *laʿalla* directed at the human heart — "you should live as if it is near, because from your standpoint, it is."

In other words: Allah is not saying "I'm not sure when it is." Allah is saying **"You should consider it near."**

Now look at the word *qarīban* — "near."

In Arabic, *qarīb* describes nearness. But *qarīban* here is unspecified. Allah doesn't say "near in time" or "near in distance." He just says **near**. And the word, by its bareness, opens onto every kind of nearness: temporal, experiential, existential.

Now combine this final phrase with everything that came before it, and watch what just happened.

The questioners came in mockery. *"When is your Hour?"* They were trying to use the absence of a date as proof that the Hour was a fabrication.

Allah replies: *"The knowledge is sealed with Me. Even the Prophet doesn't have access to it."*

And then — instead of leaving them with that silence — He turns the silence into a sword.

**"But perhaps it is near."**

Stay with me here. The questioners assumed that not-knowing-when meant the Hour was safely *far*. Allah inverts this. He says: the fact that you don't know when does not mean it is far. It might be tomorrow. It might be in the next breath. **Your ignorance of timing is not a license for delay. It is a reason for urgency.**

This is one of the most devastating rhetorical moves in the Quran. The questioners tried to use the unknown to dismiss the warning. Allah uses the same unknown to *intensify* the warning.

*"You don't know when? Good. Then act as if it is at the door."*

[PAUSE]

There is one more layer. The word *takūn* — "it will be" or "it is." Imperfect tense. Ongoing.

So *laʿalla s-sāʿata takūnu qarīban* is more textured than English shows. It is not just "perhaps the Hour is near" as a static fact. It is "**perhaps the Hour is in the process of being near, of becoming near, of unfolding into nearness.**" The Hour is not a static date sitting somewhere on a calendar waiting to be marked. It is, in a sense, *approaching*. And it might be — at this very moment — closer than it was a moment ago.

That is what Allah leaves the questioners with.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So look at what fifteen words have done.

In the space of one ayah, Allah has reframed the question, generalized the questioners, sealed the knowledge of the Hour exclusively with Himself, publicly relieved the Prophet of the burden of knowing, refused to give the date — and then weaponized His refusal into a more terrifying warning than any specific date could have produced.

The questioners asked "when?" hoping the absence of an answer would prove the Hour wasn't real.

Allah's response: **the absence of an answer is exactly why the Hour might happen to you tonight.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Disease of the Wrong Question

Let me start with something you may have experienced.

Have you ever tried to give someone serious advice — about their health, their marriage, their faith, the direction of their life — and instead of engaging with the substance, they responded with a question designed to derail you?

You say to a friend who's been drinking too much: "Brother, this is hurting you." And he says: "Oh, so you think I'm an alcoholic now? Define alcoholism for me. What's the clinical definition? At what number of drinks per week does a person become an alcoholic? Be precise."

Notice what just happened. You raised a substantive concern. He responded with a demand for definitional precision that you cannot meet. And then, when you fail to produce that precision, he gets to dismiss everything you said.

The technique is ancient. Philosophers call it **"motivated questioning"** — asking not to understand, but to destabilize. The question is not a search for truth. The question is a weapon to avoid the truth.

This is exactly what the questioners in Surah Al-Ahzab are doing.

Allah has just warned them — three ayāt earlier — that a reckoning is coming. Their response is not "let me reconsider my life." Their response is: **"When? Be precise. Give us the date."**

They are not asking because they want to know. They are asking because they want a reason to dismiss.

Now look at how Allah handles this.

He does not get drawn into the false frame. He does not produce a date. He does not even hint at a date. He does something far more disorienting to the questioner: **He validates that no human will ever know the date — and then says the unknown date is exactly the reason to take the warning seriously.**

Think about what this teaches us.

When someone tries to derail a serious matter with a motivated question, the response is not to play their game. The response is to **change the frame**. Allah does not say "fine, here's an approximate date." He does not even concede that the question is reasonable. He says: *the knowledge is only with Me; and what you should be thinking about is not when, but the fact that you don't get to know when.*

Now turn this inward, because this is where it gets uncomfortable.

How often do we ask the wrong question to avoid the right one?

You feel guilty about a sin. Instead of repenting, you ask: "But what's the exact ḥukm? Is this technically ḥarām or just makrūh?" The question is real. But often, it is a way to avoid the deeper reality, which is: *Allah is watching, and your heart already knows.*

You feel called to give more to charity. Instead of giving, you ask: "But what's the exact percentage required? What's the minimum?" Again — a real question. But often a way to fence off generosity behind a technical minimum so you don't have to feel the pull toward more.

You feel the pull toward Allah. Instead of responding, you ask: "But what proof do I have? Show me, scientifically, the existence of God." Sometimes a real inquiry. But often a way to delay the response your heart already wants to give.

This is the disease the ayah is diagnosing. **The question that is really a deflection.**

And Allah's response is not to feed the deflection. It is to call it out — gently, but with finality — and then redirect the questioner to what actually matters.

What actually matters? Not the timing. Not the precision. **The proximity.** *Laʿalla s-sāʿata takūnu qarīban.*

The practical application is this. The next time you find yourself asking a question that, if answered, would *not actually change what you do*, ask yourself: **am I seeking knowledge, or am I avoiding action?**

If the answer is "avoiding action," then the question is not your real question. Your real question is the one you are afraid to ask. And the real question is almost always some version of: *"What is Allah asking of me, right now, that I am pretending not to hear?"*

### Theme Two: The Withholding That Is a Mercy

I want to ask you a strange question.

Imagine, for a moment, that Allah had answered the questioners. Imagine the Prophet ﷺ had said: *"The Hour will come on the 14th of Ramadan in the year 7,000 of the Hijri calendar."*

What would have happened?

Take a moment with that. Really sit with it.

I'll tell you what would have happened. Every single human being who lived between the time of the Prophet ﷺ and the year 7,000 would have known they would not see the Hour in their lifetime. They would have known, with certainty, that the Final Reckoning was not their concern. They could die in peace. Their personal accountability could be detached from the cosmic accountability.

And what would happen on a Tuesday afternoon in the year 5,432 H, when someone is tempted toward a sin? They would think: *"The Hour isn't coming for another 1,568 years. I have time."*

Knowledge of when the Hour will be would have **destroyed the urgency of every Hour in every human life.**

This is the divine wisdom in withholding.

Allah did not say "no one knows" because of some technical limitation. He said "no one knows" because **if anyone knew, the spiritual machinery of accountability would break.** The not-knowing is not a deprivation. It is the engine that keeps the believer awake.

Now zoom in on your own life.

You don't know when you will die.

Stop and feel that. Really.

You don't know if you will die at 90 surrounded by grandchildren, or at 34 in a car accident on the way home tonight. You don't know if you have 50 years or 50 minutes.

And let me ask you: **is that a deprivation, or is that a mercy?**

If you knew you had 50 years, what would you do tonight? You would go to bed without praying. You would scroll your phone for another hour. You would say "I'll start tomorrow" — and you would mean it, because tomorrow is one of 18,250 tomorrows you have left.

If you knew you had 50 minutes, what would you do? You would panic. You would scramble. You would not be able to bear the weight.

But what you have is neither certainty. You have *laʿalla qarīban* — **perhaps it is near.**

That word "perhaps" is the most ethically generative word in your life. It is what keeps you awake. It is what keeps you tender. It is what keeps you choosing prayer over the phone, mercy over harshness, repentance over delay. **The unknown date is the architecture of your heart's responsiveness.**

Allah did not hide the Hour from you because He doesn't care about you. He hid it from you because if you knew, you would no longer be the kind of being capable of being saved.

[PAUSE]

There is something else hidden here, and it's worth naming.

Notice that when Allah refuses to give the date of the Hour to the questioners, He is, in that very act, **showing them a sign of His existence**. Because think about it: who else could *credibly* withhold the answer like this?

A false prophet would have given a date. He would have had to. The pressure to produce specifics, the desire to seem like an authority, the lure of impressing the audience — all of these would push him toward fabrication. *"The Hour will come in 100 years."* *"In 1,000 years."* *"On the 17th of Muharram."*

The hallmark of revelation is the willingness to say: **"I will not tell you that, because the One who sent me has not told me, and the One who has not told me has His reasons."**

The refusal to answer is itself an answer. It is a signature. It says: **this message comes from a Source that is not trying to impress you. This Source does not need your approval. This Source will not flatter your curiosity. This Source will tell you exactly what serves your soul, and not one syllable more.**

That is what real authority sounds like. Not the authority that produces dates on demand, but the authority that holds back what would harm you.

The practical application is this. Live in a state of *laʿalla*.

When you wake up, ask: *laʿalla — perhaps today.*
When you make a small choice that no one will see, ask: *laʿalla — perhaps this is the choice that gets weighed.*
When you delay a repentance, ask: *laʿalla — perhaps the door closes tonight.*

Not in dread. In wakefulness.

The believer is not afraid of the Hour. The believer is *prepared* for it. And preparation, in the Quranic vocabulary, is another word for life lived in the shadow of *laʿalla qarīban*.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Look at the structure of what Allah has built in fifteen words.

- He names a **persistent problem** (the people keep asking).
- He **closes a door** (the knowledge is exclusively with Allah).
- He **humbles the messenger** (and what would make *you* — Muhammad — know?).
- He **opens a different door** (perhaps it is near).

The shape is masterful. Allah does not just refuse the question. He uses the refusal to redirect the questioners toward a more urgent reality. He turns a question about cosmic timing into a question about personal preparedness. He turns "when" into "now."

The linguistic discoveries we made — *yas'aluka* as ongoing pattern, *an-nās* as faceless crowd, *innamā* as the wall of exclusivity, *wa mā yudrīka* as the public humbling of even the Prophet, *laʿalla qarīban* as the redirection from timing to urgency — these are not separate insights. They are the same insight from different angles.

And the themes — the disease of the wrong question, and the mercy of the withholding — are themselves the same insight viewed from two directions.

**The mercy of not knowing is the cure for the disease of wanting to know on your own terms.**

The questioners wanted to know in order to dismiss. Allah refused to tell them, in order to save them. The unknowing they hated is the unknowing that gave them — and gives you — a chance.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the question I keep asking that, if I'm honest with myself, is really a way of avoiding what I already know I need to do?

2. If I knew, with certainty, that I had exactly 30 days to live — what would I do differently? And given that I might *actually* have only 30 days to live, why am I not doing it?

3. Where in my life have I confused "no specific deadline" with "no urgency"? What am I postponing on the assumption that I have time I do not actually have?

4. Can I sit with *laʿalla* — with "perhaps" — as a spiritual posture, rather than rushing to resolve every uncertainty into either dismissal or panic?

5. Allah refused to flatter the questioners with a specific date. Where in my life am I demanding specifics from Allah that He has chosen, in His wisdom, to withhold? And can I receive His withholding as the gift it is?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The Hour is hidden so that the heart stays awake — and the only question worth asking is not *when* but *how am I living, now, while the door is still open*.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, ask yourself one thing.

If the Hour came tomorrow morning, would the way you spent today be the way you wanted to be found?

Not because you are required to live in fear. The believer does not live in fear. But the believer lives in **wakefulness** — in the soft, quiet awareness that *laʿalla qarīban*. Perhaps near. Perhaps soon. Perhaps tonight.

The unknown is not your enemy. The unknown is the gift that keeps the heart capable of returning. Don't ask Allah for the date. Ask Allah, instead, that whenever the date comes, He finds you in a state He loves.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You have kept the knowledge of the Hour with Yourself, and we know this is from Your mercy. Make us of those who live as if it is near — not in panic, but in wakefulness. Let us not waste the gift of not knowing by sleeping through the days You have given. And when the Hour arrives — whether for the world or for us, whichever comes first — find us in a state You love.*

*Āmīn.*
