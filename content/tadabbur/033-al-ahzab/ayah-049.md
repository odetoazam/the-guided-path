# Sarāḥan Jamīlā: When Allah Makes Beauty a Legal Requirement

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓاْ إِذَا نَكَحْتُمُ ٱلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتِ ثُمَّ طَلَّقْتُمُوهُنَّ مِن قَبْلِ أَن تَمَسُّوهُنَّ فَمَا لَكُمْ عَلَيْهِنَّ مِنْ عِدَّةٍ تَعْتَدُّونَهَاۖ فَمَتِّعُوهُنَّ وَسَرِّحُوهُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا

**Transliteration:**
Yā ayyuhalladhīna āmanū idhā nakaḥtumul-muʾmināti thumma ṭallaqtumūhunna min qabli an tamassūhunna famā lakum ʿalayhinna min ʿiddatin taʿtaddūnahā — famattiʿūhunna wa sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlā.

**Translation:**
O you who believe! When you marry believing women and then divorce them before you have touched them, then there is no waiting period upon them that you should count. So provide for them and release them with a beautiful release.

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Words

To hear this ayah the way its first listeners heard it, you have to imagine a culture in which the ending of a marriage was a transaction with no manners.

In pre-Islamic Arabia — and frankly, in much of the world before and after — when a man wanted to walk away from a woman, he walked. The contract was severable. The accounting was hers to do. If he had given her nothing of his life, she was owed nothing of his name. If she had carried no child of his, she had earned no claim on his future. The cleaner the cut, the colder the exit. In many pre-Islamic customs, the most efficient ending was the most respected one.

This is the world the ayah enters.

And it enters at a very specific moment in the surah. Surah Al-Ahzab is the surah of trial. The trench has been dug, the confederates have been turned back, the inner life of the Prophet's household has been opened to the community for instruction. Just a few ayahs earlier, Allah resolved the affair of Zayd and Zaynab — a marriage that had ended in pain and a new one that had begun by divine command. Al-Qurtubi notes that **after the case of Zayd's divorce of Zaynab, Allah turned to address the believing men generally about the legal shape of divorce.** A surah saturated with marriage, prophetic household, social etiquette — and into this saturated air, a single ayah falls about the most quiet, most unwitnessed, most administratively easy ending a marriage can have:

**The marriage that is divorced before it is ever consummated.**

The contract was signed. The names were exchanged. And then, before he ever took her into his life, he ended it.

This is the moment where, by every cultural logic available to the seventh-century Arabian, the man owed nothing. The dowry — if he had named one — could be returned. There was no child. There was no household. There was no time invested. Cleanest of clean breaks.

[PAUSE]

Listen to what Allah does in this ayah.

He does not say "you may walk away." He does not even say "be fair." He commands two things — and the second one is not legal in any other tradition I am aware of. He commands provision. And He commands a particular kind of release.

A release that is **beautiful**.

Hold that word. Allah is about to put beauty into law. Not into virtue. Not into recommendation. Into the binding imperative form of an instruction to the believing community.

We are going to spend our time in this ayah asking a question that the rest of the world has never been forced to ask: **what does it mean for Allah to legislate beauty into the act of letting someone go?**

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Mirror of Form II

In Arabic, verbs have shapes. The bare root of a verb — three letters — sits at rest. Then the language can take that root and pour it into different molds, each mold doing a different kind of work. Some molds intensify the action. Some make it causative. Some make it reciprocal. The shape of the verb is doing semantic work before any meaning even enters.

Let me give you an everyday example. In English, "to break" and "to shatter" are not the same. "Shatter" is "break" with intensification poured into it. Arabic does this not with different words — but with different shapes of the same word. The same root reshaped into Form II carries that intensification, or deliberateness, or formal completion of the action, built right into the morphology.

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah.

There are three Form II verbs in this verse. Three. In a verse of only twenty-two words.

**ṭallaqtumūhunna** — Form II of طلق. "You divorced them." Not "you let them go." Not the loose Form I sense of release. Form II — the formal, legal, deliberate, intensive act of pronouncing divorce.

**mattiʿūhunna** — Form II of متع. "Provide them with enjoyment." Form I just means "to enjoy." Form II — make them enjoy. Cause them benefit. Deliberately, intensively, do this for them.

**sarriḥūhunna** — Form II of سرح. Form I of this root literally means to let livestock out into open pasture in the morning, to let them graze freely. Form II — formally, deliberately, with full intention — release them.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me on this. There is a pattern Allah is showing us in the very shape of the language.

The same grammatical intensity that you brought to the act of ending the marriage — that formal Form II ṭallaqtumūhunna — Allah is demanding that you bring to **the provisioning** and to **the releasing**.

You did not just let her go. You **divorced** her — Form II. Then, says Allah: you will not just give her something. You will **provide** her — Form II. You will not just let her drift away. You will **release** her — Form II.

The Form II symmetry is doing structural work. **Whatever deliberateness, whatever formality, whatever solemnity you put into the act of ending — Allah requires that same weight in the act of healing.**

Notice what this destroys. It destroys the entire cultural logic that says: the formality is in the entering, and the casualness is in the leaving. Allah inverts it. The man who entered the marriage with formality and signed papers and named witnesses and made announcements — that same man, says Allah, will leave the marriage with the same level of intention.

The shape of the language is the lesson. Form II in, Form II out.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable about this. The Form II structure makes it linguistically impossible for the divorce to be the dominant gesture. The verse contains three Form II verbs — one of divorce, two of repair. The repair *outnumbers* the rupture, in the very grammar.

You did one Form II thing to her. Allah commands you to do two Form II things in response.

### Element Two: The Word for Touch

There is a word in this verse that I want you to notice for what it does not say.

**tamassūhunna** — "before you have touched them."

The root is مسس — simply, to touch. Allah is talking about marital intimacy. There are stronger, more explicit, more anatomically direct verbs in Arabic for this. The Quran elsewhere uses fuller language. But in *this* verse — in a verse about a man who is severing a relationship that never became physical — Allah uses the gentlest possible word.

He says: before you have **touched** her.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The verse contains, in its first half, the harshest cluster of words the verse can contain: marriage, then divorce, then a clause about non-touching, then the cancellation of the waiting period. Every one of these is an interruption, a not-yet, a no-longer. And right in the middle of that cluster, where Allah could have used a more explicit verb for the marital act that did not happen, He uses *touch*.

The gentleness of the language is itself an instruction.

Even when speaking of what did *not* happen, Allah does not speak coarsely about the woman's body. The body is referred to with the lightest possible Arabic verb. A whisper of a verb. **The dignity of the language about her is preserved even in the very act of legislating her departure.**

Now: is this just a euphemism? Arabic uses euphemism in legal contexts the way English does in legal contracts. Yes. But notice what the euphemism is *doing* in this particular ayah.

The ayah is about a man who has the legal right to walk away with nothing owed. He could, in principle, walk away with a story to tell. He could, in principle, describe what did or did not happen between them. The world is full of men describing women they did not touch.

Allah uses *tamassūhunna* — and in so doing, He sets the linguistic ceiling for how the man may even *think* about this woman.

She is "the one I did not touch." Not "the one I did not have." Not "the one who was not mine." Not any of the possessive or anatomical or transactional vocabulary the man might reach for. Just: the one I did not touch.

[PAUSE]

The instruction is hidden in the verb. **The way Allah refers to her is the way you must refer to her.**

This is what it means that the Quran teaches by its very surface. The lesson is in the choice of word.

---

### Element Three: Sarāḥan Jamīlā — Beauty as a Legal Object

Now we reach the phrase that has stopped scholars for centuries.

**wa sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlā** — "and release them with a beautiful release."

Three things to notice in this phrase. Each of them is doing work.

**First — the construction itself.** In Arabic, there is a rhetorical device called the *maṣdar muʾakkid* or cognate accusative: you take a verb, and then you place the noun-form of that same verb right after it, to intensify and certify the action. *Sarriḥū* is the verb. *Sarāḥan* is the noun. To release with a releasing. The construction tells you: this is not casual release. This is release with the full weight of release behind it. *Be sure you are actually releasing — really releasing, fully releasing.*

If you have ever experienced a relationship that someone "ended" but kept calling, kept appearing, kept hovering at the edges, you understand why Allah builds this redundancy into the grammar. He is closing every loophole. **You will release her, with a release. The completion of the action is in the noun.**

**Second — the adjective.** Allah qualifies the release. Not just any release. Not adequate release, not lawful release, not minimum-standard release. **Jamīl.** Beautiful.

Now: the root جمل in Arabic — the noun *jamāl* — does not mean "pretty." It means a beauty that is fitting, that is harmonious, that is *appropriate to the dignity of what it adorns.* When the Prophet ﷺ said *Allāhu jamīlun yuḥibbul-jamāl*, "Allah is beautiful and loves beauty," this is the same root. It is a beauty that comes from things being in their right form, their right station, their right balance.

So Allah is not saying: release her with style. He is saying: release her in a way that has integrity. A way that is fitting. A way in which nothing in the act is uglier than it had to be.

**Third — and this is what stops me.** Allah is putting *jamāl* — beauty — into a legal command. Into the imperative form. Into the binding instruction to the believing man.

[PAUSE]

In most legal systems, the law sets the floor. Beauty is the ceiling — what the virtuous person aspires to on top of the law. The law says: don't hit her. Beauty says: be tender. The law says: pay what you owe. Beauty says: pay generously.

Here Allah does something different. **He makes the beauty the floor.** Not aspirational. Mandatory. *Sarriḥū* is in the imperative.

Al-Jalalayn glosses this concisely: *khallū sabīlahunna min ghayr iḍrār* — "free their path, without harm." Al-Muyassar expands: *wa khallū sabīlahunna maʿa al-satril-jamīl, dūna adhan aw ḍarar* — "free their path with beautiful covering, without harm or injury." The classical commentators converge on a vision in which the release is not merely allowed — it is *covered with beauty.*

Al-Qurtubi spends considerable time on this verse, and one of his recurring concerns is precisely this: the release must contain no element of degradation, no public shaming, no withholding of what is due, no cruelty in the manner. The release must be *jamīl* — and *jamīl* is incompatible with cruelty.

[PAUSE]

So Allah has placed beauty into law. In one of the most administratively-easy acts a man can perform — the divorce of a woman he has not even touched — Allah has refused to let the man have the easy thing.

The easy thing would be: end it, move on, owe nothing, say nothing, mean nothing.
The required thing is: end it with provision, release with beauty, leave nothing ugly behind you.

### Bridge

So we have three findings from the language. The Form II symmetry — the formality of the entry is mirrored in the formality of the exit. The dignity of the verb — even her body is referred to with the gentlest available Arabic word. And the construction *sarāḥan jamīlā* — beauty made into a binding legal object.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

What is Allah teaching us about how a covenant ends? What is He teaching us about the soul of the one who must do the ending?

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Way You End Is the Truth of How You Loved

Begin with something most of us have lived.

There is a saying that has circulated in modern psychology for the better part of fifty years: *the way a relationship ends tells you what the relationship was.* It is a hard saying. It is the kind of thing said by people who have been through many endings and have stopped pretending.

Because anyone can be loving when love is easy. Anyone can be generous when the other person is delighting them. Anyone can be respectful when they want something from the other person.

The truth of who you were in the relationship comes out at the moment you no longer need anything from the other person.

The breakup text. The closed door. The unanswered call. The cold settlement. The disappearance from the friend group. The story told to your friends in which she is the villain and you are the hero. **The exit is the audit.** Everything you said you were — patient, kind, just, considerate, generous — gets tested on the way out, when the only person watching is Allah.

This is what Surah Al-Ahzab's ayah confronts.

The case Allah picks is the case most likely to expose the worst in a man. A marriage contracted but never consummated. A woman with no claim on him by intimacy, no child, no history, no shared household. The case in which every cultural and economic incentive points toward: walk away with nothing. The case in which his ego is most likely to want to *win* the ending. The case in which his social cost is lowest and his temptation to be cold is highest.

And in *this* case — the case of maximum permission to be ugly — Allah commands beauty.

[PAUSE]

Here's what this reveals. Allah is not making law for the easy cases. Allah is making law for the cases where the *nafs* — the lower self — will pull hardest toward smallness. He is reaching into the most psychologically vulnerable moment a man will have in marriage — the moment of severance without sunk cost — and He is saying: *this is exactly where I will measure your faith.*

The ayah opens with *yā ayyuhalladhīna āmanū* — "O you who have believed." It does not say "O husbands." It does not say "O men." It says: O you who have made a claim of faith.

The address is itself the warning. **If you are who you say you are, the ending will be beautiful.** If the ending is ugly, the claim of faith was being financed by the relationship — and now that the relationship is over, the faith has nothing to show for itself.

This is not a soft theme. This is the diagnostic. You can tell what someone's *imān* is actually made of by watching how they end something they no longer need to be in.

Some have shown beauty in their marriages and ugliness in their divorces. Some have shown beauty in their friendships and ugliness in their fallings-out. Some have shown beauty in their employment and ugliness in their resignations. The same person. The same claim of faith. **But two different selves, depending on whether the relationship still served them.**

Allah is closing this gap. He is saying: the believer is not the one whose faith only operates when something is gained. The believer is the one whose faith **also operates on the way out.**

[PAUSE]

There is a practical instruction in this for every one of us. It is not about marriage only.

When you are about to leave something — a job, a friendship, a community, a chapter — pause before the exit. Ask: am I about to do something now that I would have refused to do at any earlier point in this relationship? Am I about to take a freedom from this ending that I never took during the relationship? Am I about to be smaller, colder, sharper, more unkind, because the cost of being so has dropped?

If yes — that is the *nafs* taking advantage of the exit. That is the trap Allah is teaching you to refuse. The believer does not collect the dividend of ugliness that endings offer. The believer leaves the same way the believer entered. **Form II in, Form II out.**

### Theme Two: When Allah Refuses to Let Mercy Be Optional

Now let me draw your attention to something easy to miss.

There are two things Allah commands in this verse, not one. **Provide** for them — *mattiʿūhunna*. And **release** them beautifully — *sarriḥūhunna sarāḥan jamīlā*.

Notice this. The man, by every legal logic, owes nothing. The marriage was unconsummated. There was no child, no shared household, no relational history to compensate. In most legal systems then and now, the dissolution of an unconsummated marriage means each party walks away with whatever they came with.

Allah inserts an obligation that no human legal logic requires. The man must give her something.

The classical commentators discuss what this *mutʿa* — this provision — looks like. Al-Jalalayn, citing Ibn Abbas and the position adopted by al-Shafiʿi, notes that if no dowry was named in the contract, then the *mutʿa* itself is required as a meaningful gift; if a dowry *was* named, then half of the named dowry is owed (as Allah will later legislate in Surat al-Baqarah 2:237). Al-Muyassar describes the gift in terms of *jabran li-khawāṭirihinna* — **a mending of their hearts.** Al-Baghawi places the verse in the larger discussion of how divorce attaches to marriage, and the surrounding fiqh of pre-consummation divorce.

The scholars differ on the exact amount and whether the *mutʿa* is obligatory in every case or only in the no-dowry case — this is a long-standing point of ikhtilāf among the legal schools. But what they do not differ on, and what the ayah says with full clarity, is this:

**Allah will not let her leave the marriage with nothing in her hand.**

Even though no legal logic requires it. Even though no human relationship demands it. Even though, by every accounting principle the world recognizes, she could be released with no compensation at all — Allah inserts a duty of provision.

[PAUSE]

Let that sit. Allah is doing something here that overrides the logic of contractual fairness. He is saying: *the fact that she has no legal claim against you does not mean she should leave your life empty-handed.*

Why?

Because something happened. A marriage was contracted. Her name was joined to his. A future was, however briefly, imagined. The community knew. The families knew. The woman, in the cultural context of the time and in our context now, carries the trace of that moment in ways the man does not. Allah, in commanding the *mutʿa*, is recognizing something that legal logic ignores: **a relationship leaves a mark even when nothing physical occurred.**

The mark is in her social standing, in her time, in the imagined future, in the public knowledge. And for that mark — the mark that no court would award damages for — Allah commands provision.

This is what I want to call **the fiqh of mercy.** It is the layer of obligation Allah inserts on top of bare legal logic. It is the legal recognition of harm that no court would compensate. It is Allah refusing to let the technically-permissible become the morally-acceptable.

[PAUSE]

Here's the universal in this. We live in a world in which the dominant ethical question has become: *what am I legally required to do?* This is the language of contracts, of HR policy, of terms of service, of relationship boundaries scripted by self-help culture. People narrow themselves to the bare floor of obligation and call it integrity.

Allah is refusing this. He is saying: there is a layer of duty that sits *above* the bare legal floor — and a believer is held to that layer. Even when you owe her nothing, you owe her something. Even when you can leave without provision, you provide. Even when the law would let you be silent, you speak with beauty.

This is mercy as a legal category. **Not mercy as a private virtue you might choose to display. Mercy as something Allah builds into the contract itself.**

The application for our lives is direct. When a relationship ends — any relationship — the question is not "what am I obligated to do?" The question is "what is Allah's *additional* layer of obligation, the layer that mends hearts and covers ugliness with beauty?"

The friend you are no longer close to — does she leave the friendship empty-handed? Or did you, before the distance set in, give her a word of appreciation, a gesture of recognition, something that mends the heart on the way out?

The employee you are letting go — does he leave with nothing? Or do you give him more than the law requires, more than the severance demands, because *mattiʿūhunna* is a principle, not just a marriage rule?

The chapter of your life you are closing — did you mark it with beauty, or did you let it dissolve into bitterness?

**Every ending in your life is a *sarāḥan jamīlā* opportunity.**

You will be asked, at the end of your own life, what you did at the end of every relationship you closed. Not whether you were legally clear. Whether you were *jamīl*.

## Closing Synthesis

Now we can see how the linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The Form II symmetry told us: the weight of entry must equal the weight of exit. The grammar refused to let the leaving be casual. The themes told us: the believer's faith is tested on the way out, not the way in. **Same insight. The first proves it in the morphology. The second proves it in the soul.**

The gentleness of *tamassūhunna* told us: even her body is referred to with linguistic dignity. The themes told us: Allah's law includes a layer of mercy above the bare floor of fairness. **Same insight. The first proves it in the vocabulary. The second proves it in the legal structure.**

The cognate accusative *sarāḥan jamīlā* told us: Allah has placed beauty into the imperative. The themes told us: beauty is not the optional virtue on top of the law — it is the floor of the law for the believer. **Same insight. The first proves it in the syntax. The second proves it in the ethics.**

And the architecture of the ayah itself is the proof. Three Form II verbs, surrounding a euphemism for the unconsummated, closing on an adjective of beauty. Twenty-two words. A complete legal-ethical-aesthetic instruction that no other legal tradition I have encountered attempts in this form.

### Questions to Carry

- When was the last time you ended something? A friendship, a job, a contract, a chapter of life. Was the ending *jamīl*?

- Is there a person in your life whom you released, but not beautifully? Is there a *sarāḥan jamīlā* you still owe?

- Where in your life are you operating at the legal floor — what is technically permissible — rather than at the level of *mutʿa*, the additional provision that mends hearts?

- If a hidden observer were to grade your endings — not your beginnings, not your peaks, but your endings — what would they see? What is the pattern of your exits?

- Allah used the gentlest word for her body. What is the vocabulary you use about people you have left behind? Is it as gentle as Allah's vocabulary about a woman who was never even touched?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah refuses to let the easy ending be the ugly ending: the same formality you brought to the entering, the same care you would have shown if she had stayed, the same beauty you would want for yourself — that is the floor Allah sets for how the believer leaves.

### The Closing Invitation

There is an ending you are sitting on right now. Most of us are. A relationship that has cooled, a chapter you have not formally closed, a person you stopped responding to, a community you drifted out of, a name in your phone you no longer call.

The invitation of this ayah is not to revive every ending. Some things genuinely must end. Allah Himself is legislating the *ending* here, not the prevention of endings.

The invitation is: **before the next ending closes, do the Form II thing.** Make the provision. Speak the word of recognition. Send the message of acknowledgment. Cover the closure with something *jamīl*.

You will not regret an ending you made beautiful. You will, almost certainly, regret an ending you let curdle.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who have placed beauty into Your own law — let the endings of our lives be beautiful. Let no soul leave our presence emptier than they came. And when the final ending arrives — the parting of our own souls from this world — receive us with the *sarāḥan jamīlā* we tried to give to others. Āmīn.
