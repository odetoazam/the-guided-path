# When Allah Asks You to Join Him in Honoring His Beloved

## The Ayah

> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ وَمَلَـٰٓئِكَتَهُۥ يُصَلُّونَ عَلَى ٱلنَّبِىِّ ۚ يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ صَلُّوا۟ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلِّمُوا۟ تَسْلِيمًۭا

> *Inna-Llāha wa malā'ikatahu yuṣallūna ʿalā-n-nabiyy. Yā ayyuha-lladhīna āmanū ṣallū ʿalayhi wa sallimū taslīmā.*

> Indeed, Allah and His angels send blessings upon the Prophet. O you who have believed, send blessings upon him and greet him with [a worthy] greeting.

---

## Introduction: The Room Before the Ayah

Before we open this verse, we have to stand inside the room it was revealed into.

Surah Al-Ahzab is the surah of the Confederates — the surah of the Trench, of the siege of Madinah, of the hypocrites who watched the believers from the inside while the enemy gathered on the outside. But by the time we reach ayah 56, the battle has receded. What's left is something more intimate. More painful, in a different way.

The Prophet ﷺ is a man. He has a household. He has wives. He has a door people knock on, a meal he eats, a private life that ends where his public life begins. And in these surrounding ayahs — the ones right before and after this one — Allah is regulating *how people behave around him*. Don't enter his house without permission. Don't sit and talk endlessly after a meal. Don't ask his wives anything except from behind a screen. Don't marry his wives after him. Don't hurt him.

[PAUSE]

That last one is heavy. *Don't hurt him.*

Because he was being hurt. Not just by enemies on the battlefield. By neighbors. By guests who overstayed. By whispers about his household. By people who wore the name "Muslim" but whose tongues didn't match. The Quran here is not abstract. It's responding to specific moments where a man — the most beloved man Allah ever made — was being made uncomfortable in his own home by his own community.

And right there, in the middle of all that, Allah does something extraordinary. He pulls the camera away from the earth entirely. He shows us what is happening *somewhere else* — somewhere we cannot see — about the same man.

While some of you on earth are troubling him, **let me tell you what We are doing in the heavens.**

That is the room this ayah enters. Hold that room in your mind. Because everything we're about to discover depends on standing inside it.

So here's the question I want you to carry: What does it mean that Allah felt the need to *tell us* what He is doing about His Prophet — and then to *invite us into it*? What is happening in this verse that isn't happening anywhere else in the Quran in quite the same way?

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Two Tenses — *yuṣallūna* and *ṣallū*

Let me start with something so quiet most readers walk past it.

There are two verbs in this ayah from the same root — ص ل و. The first one describes what Allah and the angels do. The second one describes what we are commanded to do.

In English they read almost identical. *Send blessings.* And *send blessings.*

But in Arabic, they are not the same form of the verb. They are not even the same *kind* of verb. And that difference is the whole architecture of the ayah.

Let me teach you the structure first, before we open the word.

[PAUSE]

In Arabic, every verb lives on a kind of time-axis. There are essentially three positions:

The **māḍī** — the past. The thing that has happened. Completed. Closed.

The **muḍāriʿ** — the imperfect. The thing that is happening, or that keeps happening, or that will happen. Ongoing. Open. Not yet finished. It carries continuity in its very shape.

And the **amr** — the imperative. The command. "Do this." The verb of instruction.

Think of these like three different angles a camera can take on an action. The māḍī shows you the action *complete* — already done. The muḍāriʿ shows you the action *in motion* — still happening as you watch. The amr doesn't show you the action at all — it puts the action in *your* hands and says: now you do it.

Now hold those three angles in mind, and look at the ayah.

When Allah describes what He and His angels do for the Prophet, He uses — *yuṣallūna*. **The muḍāriʿ.** The continuous, the ongoing, the still-in-motion.

When Allah commands us, He uses — *ṣallū*. **The amr.** The imperative.

Notice what just happened.

Allah did not say *ṣallā-Llāhu wa malā'ikatuhu ʿalā-n-nabiyy* — "Allah and His angels *sent* blessings on the Prophet" — past tense, finished, completed. He chose the continuous form. The form that says: this is happening now, was happening before now, will be happening after now. It does not stop.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this.

In the heavens, at this very moment, there is an act being performed upon the Prophet ﷺ that has no beginning we can locate and no ending we can imagine. It is not a memory. It is not a thing that *was* done. It is a thing that *is* being done. As you read this sentence. As your eyes move across these words. The verb is alive.

And then — only then — does Allah turn to us.

*Yā ayyuha-lladhīna āmanū ṣallū ʿalayhi.*

"O you who believe — *ṣallū* — send blessings upon him."

Imperative. Command. Action placed into your hands.

But notice what He did *not* say. He did not say: "O you who believe, start sending blessings upon him." He did not say: "begin." He did not introduce the act to us as something new.

He introduced it to us as something **already in motion** — and then He invited us in.

Here is the analogy. Imagine you walk into a room where the most important people you can imagine — the people whose opinion would shape your entire life — are all standing in a circle, doing one thing together. Saying one name with love. Lifting one person up. And the host of the room turns to you at the door and says: *come in. Join us. We're already doing this. We have been doing this. We don't need you to do it — but we want you to.*

That is the difference between *yuṣallūna* and *ṣallū*.

You are not being commanded to initiate something for the Prophet ﷺ. **You are being commanded to synchronize with something already happening.** Your salawat is not a gift you give him from your own resources. It is a chance to put your small human voice into a song that the angels are already singing and that the Lord of the angels is already conducting.

This is what the muḍāriʿ buys you. The continuous tense doesn't just describe the timing of the verb. It tells you what kind of act you are entering. You are joining. You are not starting.

And here's what's remarkable — *and* heart-breaking, in a beautiful way: you can fall silent for years, and the song does not stop. You can forget for a decade, and *yuṣallūna* keeps happening above your head. The continuity does not depend on you. The honor of the Prophet ﷺ does not need your participation to be sustained.

But the door of the room is open. *Ṣallū.* Come in.

[PAUSE]

That is the first thing this ayah is doing — and we haven't even passed the third word.

---

### Element Two: One Verb. Three Realities.

Here is the second thing — and you have to stay with me for this one, because it requires patience.

The verb يُصَلُّونَ — *yuṣallūna* — has *one* subject in Arabic grammar, but in this ayah it carries *two actors*: Allah and the angels. Allah and His angels both *yuṣallūna* upon the Prophet.

Then a few words later, the same root appears in the command to us: *ṣallū*.

So in fourteen words, the root ص ل و is used by three different actors: **Allah, the angels, and the believers.**

Same word. Same root. Same syllable on the tongue.

But the same reality?

[PAUSE]

This is where classical Arabic does something modern English cannot. The root ص ل و carries a base meaning that the lexicographers describe as *connection*, *attention*, *facing toward*. Something or someone is being turned toward. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, Mufradāt al-Rāghib, Tāj al-ʿArūs — circle around this same idea. To do *ṣalāh* on someone is to *turn toward them* in a particular way that lifts them up.

But what does it mean for *Allah* to turn toward someone? What does it mean for *angels* to turn toward someone? What does it mean for *you* to turn toward someone?

The classical scholars saw this immediately. The same verb cannot possibly mean the same thing across these three subjects, because the actors are not the same kind of being. Allah is not a creature among creatures. The angels are not lords. We are not angels.

So they said: this is one Arabic verb pointing toward **three different realities** that share a family resemblance.

When *Allah* sends *ṣalāh* upon the Prophet, the classical commentators describe it as **rahma**, mercy — but a specific kind. Some say it is His praise of him in the highest gathering, the *al-malaʾ al-aʿlā*. Some say it is His exaltation of his name and his rank. Some say it is His mercy that descends specifically and continuously upon this one servant. The lexicons make clear we are inside a meaning that no English word fully holds.

When the *angels* send *ṣalāh* upon the Prophet, the classical commentators say it is **du'a** — they are asking. They are interceding for him. They are turning toward him with their voices raised, asking Allah to elevate him further.

And when *we* are commanded to send *ṣalāh* upon him, our act is also du'a — we are asking Allah to do what He already does. We are asking the One who is already exalting him to exalt him.

[PAUSE]

Notice what just happened. **One verb. Three realities. Same root, vastly different actions.**

Why would Allah do that?

Here is the analogy. Think of a single word like "love." When a parent says they love their child, and when a child says they love their parent, and when the child says they love ice cream — three radically different things are happening. The word is the same. The action behind the word is unrecognizable across the three.

But here is what is interesting: the human language *forces* us to use the same word, because at some deep level we know these three things are *related*. They share a family resemblance. They all involve a turning-toward, a giving-of-attention, a willing-of-good.

Now do this with the Arabic verb *ṣalāh*. At its deepest, it means: turning toward someone with the intention to lift them. To raise them in standing. To give them attention that exalts.

So when Allah does it, the lifting is *infinite*, because He is the one who *gives* standing in the first place. When the angels do it, the lifting is *intercessory*, because they have access to the throne. When we do it, the lifting is *participatory* — we are asking the One who lifts to keep lifting.

But all three are the same fundamental act seen from three different altitudes.

Here is what this gives us:

**When you send salawat on the Prophet ﷺ, you are doing — at your own scale — the same kind of act Allah Himself is doing.**

That should stop you.

I do not mean you become equivalent to Allah. *Subḥānahu wa taʿālā ʿammā yaqūlūn.* You do not become like Him. You do not match Him. The verb is not equal across actors. But you are *participating in a kind of action that He performs*. You are turning toward the Prophet ﷺ with the intent to lift him — and Allah, in the same ayah, just described Himself as doing precisely this.

There is no other action in the Quran where this happens quite like this. There is no other ayah where Allah describes Himself doing an action and then turns to you in the same sentence and says: *and you do it too*. He is not commanding you to imitate Him in a metaphor. He is commanding you to take part in an act He has just identified Himself as performing.

[PAUSE]

The ordinary translation hides this entirely. "Allah blesses the Prophet — you bless him too." It reads like two separate things. But in Arabic, it's the same root, the same shape. The same *act* — recalibrated to each actor's altitude.

This is what makes the ayah staggering. **You are being asked to do, in your own small way, what Allah is doing.**

That is the second thing.

---

### Element Three: The Strange Asymmetry — Why *taslīmā* But No *ṣalātan*?

Now, the third thing — and this one comes from listening to what Allah *does not* say.

The believers are given two commands here, not one.

*Ṣallū ʿalayhi* — send blessings upon him.

*Wa sallimū taslīmā* — and greet him with greeting.

Two verbs, side by side. From two different roots. ص ل و and س ل م.

You would expect symmetry. You would expect Allah to either intensify both or intensify neither. In Arabic, there is a particular tool for intensifying a command — it's called *maf'ul muṭlaq*, the absolute object. It's when you follow a verb with its own verbal noun: "do the doing." It functions almost like saying in English "really and truly do this." It doubles down. It removes any wiggle room. It signals: I mean this fully.

If I tell you in English: "wash the car" — that's a normal instruction.

If I tell you: "wash the car a thorough washing" — I've intensified it. I've removed your option to do a quick spray and call it done. I've told you: do this in the way that the word *wash* fully deserves.

That's what *taslīmā* does to *sallimū*. The verbal noun intensifies the imperative. *Wa sallimū taslīmā* means roughly: "and greet him with the kind of greeting that the verb 'to greet' fully deserves." It removes the option of a half-greeting. It maxes out the verb.

Now here is the question: **why did Allah do this for *sallimū* but not for *ṣallū*?**

He could have said: *ṣallū ʿalayhi ṣalātan wa sallimū taslīmā* — "send blessings upon him a thorough sending of blessings, and greet him with a thorough greeting."

He didn't. He intensified the salam. He left the salah without an intensifier.

[PAUSE]

The classical scholars saw this asymmetry and spent ink on it. Their answers, taken together, form a remarkable composite picture.

One angle: the *ṣalāh* upon the Prophet — the act of asking Allah to lift him — has no ceiling. You cannot intensify it because *human capacity cannot exhaust it*. Whatever amount you produce, it is dwarfed by what Allah and the angels are already producing. To say "do a thorough ṣalāh" would be to suggest that there is a complete-enough version a human could reach. There isn't. You will never reach the depth of what is owed. The verb is left, almost humbly, without a ceiling word — because the ceiling is uncatchable.

Another angle: *salam* — peace, safety, well-wishing — *is* something a human can fully extend. Your heart can wholly turn toward someone with the wish that they be safe, kept, sound, at peace. That is a complete act within human range. So Allah intensifies *that*. He says: when you turn toward him with peace, turn fully. Don't half-greet him. Don't extend a clipped salam. Let your whole heart go.

A third angle: the *ṣalāh* is the action Allah and the angels share with you. The *salam* is the action *you* alone perform on the human plane. Allah's *salam* on the Prophet works differently than His *ṣalāh*. The angels' *salam* works differently. The *salam* you give him from your tongue and heart is something distinctly *yours* — and so it is *that* one Allah intensifies, because that one *you* can max out.

All three readings, taken together, give us this:

**The verb without the intensifier is the verb you cannot complete. The verb with the intensifier is the verb you can — so do it fully.**

[PAUSE]

Let me give you the analogy that I think makes this concrete.

Imagine a child writing a letter to a great king. The child is told: *honor the king, and greet the king*.

If the child is told "honor the king *fully*" — the child is set up for impossibility. No child can honor a king the way a king is owed honor. So the instruction is wisely left without that ceiling: just *honor him*. Bring what you can. The fullness is not on you.

But "greet the king" — *that* a child can do completely. The child can put his whole small self into a greeting. He can mean it entirely. So *that* is where the instructor adds: "and greet him *fully*. Don't mumble. Don't half-bow. Mean the greeting."

That is what Allah is doing with us. He knows our scale. He is not asking us to fill a glass we cannot reach the rim of. But where we *can* be complete — in our salam, our turning to the Prophet ﷺ with the heart's full peace and reverence — there He says: be complete.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing, and then I will stop with the linguistic. Notice the word *sallimū* itself, from س ل م. This is the same root as Islam. The same root as Muslim. The same root as salām, peace. To do *taslīm* is — at the deepest level — to *hand over*. To submit. To enter a state of peace by giving something fully.

So when Allah commands "*sallimū taslīmā*" — He is not just commanding a greeting in the social sense. He is commanding a *handing-over* of the heart toward the Prophet ﷺ. Greet him by *giving yourself in greeting* — fully. Not partially.

And He intensifies *that*. Because that is the one a human can actually complete.

### Bridge

Look what the language has shown us in three small movements:

You are joining an action already happening in the heavens — not starting one.

You are participating, at your scale, in an act Allah Himself performs.

And where your participation can be *full*, Allah commands you to make it so.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what it means to be a believer who has the privilege of being told what is happening above the ceiling.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Spiritual Psychology of Joining vs. Initiating

There is a difference between two postures in life that almost no one names, but every one of us has felt.

The first is the posture of the *initiator*. You wake up in the morning and you have to begin. Nothing is moving yet. You have to push the boulder up the hill from a dead stop. Whatever good is going to exist in your day, it has to come from you — your willpower, your effort, your discipline, your fuel. The world is asleep and you have to wake it.

The second is the posture of the *joiner*. You wake up in the morning and something is already in motion. The sun is already up. The birds have already started. A current is already flowing through the day and you only have to *step into it*. You're not lighting the fire. You're warming yourself by a fire someone else lit before you arrived.

Most of us live in the first posture and don't know there's another one available.

We treat ibādah this way. We treat dhikr this way. We treat dua this way. As if everything spiritual has to be initiated by us, generated by us, summoned by us out of our own willpower. So when our energy is low, we feel like the whole spiritual world has gone dim. As if the lights only come on when *we* flip the switch.

[PAUSE]

This ayah comes and dismantles that.

It says: there is at least one act in the universe that is **already happening at full intensity, every moment, with or without you**. The *yuṣallūna* on the Prophet ﷺ is ongoing. The angels are doing it as you read this. Allah Himself is doing it as you breathe.

And you have been invited to step into it.

Look at what that does to your spiritual psychology.

When you are exhausted, when your soul is dry, when you cannot summon a single moment of fresh devotion — *ṣallū ʿalayhi* is the easiest doorway in the universe. Because **you are not the engine**. You are not generating heat. You are stepping into heat that is already there. You are putting your cold hands by a fire the angels have been tending since before you were born.

This is mercy. This is one of the deepest mercies in the religion. Allah has built into the day an act you can do that does not require you to start anything. It only requires you to *join*.

Say it. *Allāhumma ṣalli ʿalā Muhammad.* And you are inside the song. The song was already playing. You did not turn on the music. You only opened your mouth and let your voice find the note that the angels were already singing.

[PAUSE]

And here is what's beautiful — and this comes directly from ayah 56 of this same surah's surrounding context, where Allah says, *huwa-lladhī yuṣallī ʿalaykum wa malā'ikatuhu* — He is the One who sends ṣalāh upon *you*, and His angels too. Earlier in this very surah, Allah has already told us: I do this for you. I send my ṣalāh upon you, believer.

So we are not just joining the angels in their ṣalāh on the Prophet ﷺ. We are also entering a cosmic logic where Allah's ṣalāh is *circulating*. It flows from Him onto the Prophet. It flows from Him onto the believers. It flows from the believers back onto the Prophet. The whole sky is full of *ṣalāh*, and we have been given a doorway in.

The shift this should produce in you is concrete. Tomorrow when you wake — *especially* on the mornings when your soul is heavy — try this. Don't try to begin something. Try to **join** something. Say "Allāhumma ṣalli ʿalā Muhammad" once, with the awareness that you have just put your voice into the angels' voices. You did not start the act. You walked into a room where the act was already happening.

This is the first practical inheritance of this ayah. **Salawat is the spiritual act that asks nothing from you except your willingness to step inside.**

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Mercy Hidden Inside the Command

Now I want to ask a harder question. A question that, when you sit with it, opens the ayah completely.

Why did Allah command this at all?

Think about it. Allah just told us, in the first half of the ayah, that He and His angels are sending blessings upon the Prophet ﷺ continuously. The action is already happening. It is not lacking. It is not waiting for our participation to complete it.

So why turn to us and say *ṣallū*?

Allah does not need our salawat for His Prophet. The honor of the Prophet ﷺ is not pending the arrival of our small words. Whether or not you ever say *Allāhumma ṣalli ʿalā Muhammad* in your entire life, the angels will keep singing. Allah will keep doing what He is doing. The cosmic dignity of His messenger is not held together by our participation.

So why command us?

[PAUSE]

The answer is so important you should let it sit.

**The command is for *us*, not for him.**

Allah is not commanding salawat because He needs it for the Prophet ﷺ. He is commanding it because *we* need it. Because *we* needed a way to be tied to the most beloved created being. Because *we* needed an action small enough that we could do it a hundred times a day, accessible enough that it doesn't require wuḍūʾ or qiblah or specific time, and powerful enough that it links our small lives to the cosmic act Allah Himself is performing.

The command, in other words, is a **mercy disguised as an obligation.**

When a great teacher invites a small student to help him grade papers, the teacher does not need the help. The teacher could grade them faster alone. But the invitation transforms the student. It tells the student: *you have been counted as worth bringing close*. The honor flows downward, not upward.

When Allah commands you to send salawat on His Prophet, the same thing is happening. He doesn't need your participation. *You* need to be counted as someone whose voice was invited into the heavenly chorus. *You* need to walk through your day with a thread running between your tongue and the *al-malaʾ al-aʿlā*. *You* need to know that something you say in your car on the way to work is reaching into the same act the angels are performing.

The command lifts *you*. The verb is about the Prophet ﷺ; the gift is for the one obeying.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back to the room we entered at the beginning. The Prophet ﷺ was being mistreated. Slandered. His house disturbed. The community had members who weren't behaving as they should.

Look at what Allah does. He does *not* respond by punishing them on the spot. He does not lower a stone from the sky. Instead, He shows the believers — the ones who *do* love the Prophet — a way to align themselves with the heavenly response. He says: while *they* are doing what they are doing, *you* can do this.

In the very moment when some are turning *against* the Prophet ﷺ, Allah opens a doorway for those who want to turn *toward* him. And He tells you: when you walk through this doorway, you are not alone. The angels are already there. I Myself am already there.

This is no small thing. This is one of the most psychologically generous moves in the entire Quran. **He gave us, in a moment of communal failure around His Prophet, a personal way to align ourselves with His own honoring of him.** You don't need to fix the community. You need to step into the room the angels are in.

And every time you say it — in your car, walking to work, before sleep, during your prayer — you are doing exactly that. You are choosing the heavenly response over the earthly one. You are siding with the angels and with Allah, against the slanderers and against the indifferent.

[PAUSE]

This is what changes about your tomorrow.

The next time you say *Allāhumma ṣalli ʿalā Muhammad*, do not say it as if you are doing a religious duty. Say it as if Allah, the Lord of the worlds, has just *invited you into a room* where He and His angels are honoring the most beloved created being — and you have accepted the invitation.

Because that is exactly what is happening.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Look at how this ayah is built.

It opens with **the cosmic** — Allah and the angels, *yuṣallūna*, the continuous heavenly act.

It moves to **the call** — *yā ayyuha-lladhīna āmanū*, the address to us.

It places **the command** — *ṣallū ʿalayhi*, join the act.

And it ends with **the intensification** — *wa sallimū taslīmā*, the one piece you can do completely.

[PAUSE]

The structure is not flat. It is a *descent and an ascent*. It descends from the heavens (Allah, the angels) down to us (the believers, the call), places an action in our hands (the imperative), and then asks us to send something back up (the salam, fully extended).

The whole ayah is shaped like a doorway. The doorway is opened from above and we are invited to walk through it from below. And the action that crosses the threshold — what you give as you walk through — is your *taslīm*. Your full handing-over of greeting and peace.

The architecture is the proof. This is not a verse about a duty. This is a verse about an *invitation*, and the structure itself shows you which way the invitation flows and what you are asked to bring with you.

---

### Bring It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The continuous tense *yuṣallūna* told us the act is already in motion — and the theme of *joining vs. initiating* gave us the spiritual psychology of stepping into something rather than starting it.

The single root spanning three actors told us your salawat is participation in an act Allah Himself performs — and the theme of *mercy hidden in the command* told us why He invited you in: not because He needs it, but because *you* needed to be counted.

The asymmetric intensifier on *taslīm* told us where your fullness is asked — and the architecture told us the salam is what you carry across the threshold of the doorway Allah opened.

All of it converges on this:

**You have been invited, at no cost to yourself, into a cosmic act of love that Allah Himself is performing.**

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. Don't rush to answer them.

1. When I have said *Allāhumma ṣalli ʿalā Muhammad* in my life, have I been *initiating* something — or have I known I was *joining* something? What changes if I say it tomorrow as a joining?

2. Allah did not need to invite me into this. He chose to. What does it mean that the Lord of the worlds wanted my small voice in the chorus that honors His beloved?

3. The command is the mercy. Where else in my life has Allah given me an obligation that, when I look closely, is actually a gift to me hidden inside an instruction to me?

4. The Prophet ﷺ was being mistreated when this was revealed. When the community around me is failing someone I should be honoring — a parent, a teacher, a friend, a leader — am I aligning with the indifferent majority or stepping into the room where the few are honoring them?

5. *Wa sallimū taslīmā* — where in my engagement with the Prophet ﷺ am I half-greeting him? Where could I extend a fuller salam, a more complete handing-over of my heart?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah is already honoring His Prophet, continuously and cosmically — and the command to send salawat is the merciful door He opened so that your small voice could join that heavenly act.**

---

### The Closing Invitation

Something should shift after this. Not as an obligation — as an invitation.

Tomorrow, somewhere in your day, say *Allāhumma ṣalli ʿalā Muhammad* once. Just once. And as you say it, hold this in your awareness: *the angels are already saying this. Allah is already doing this. I am not starting anything. I am stepping inside something that is already in motion. My voice is being placed inside a song that has been playing since before time was time.*

That is all.

If the awareness shifts even once — if even one time you say it not as the initiator of an act but as a joiner of an act — this ayah will have done its work in you.

And if you can manage it: when you say the salam — *ʿalayhi-ṣ-ṣalātu wa-s-salām* — let your heart go fully. *Taslīmā.* That part Allah asked you to complete. Complete it.

---

### Closing Du'a

Allāhumma, You who *yuṣallūna* upon Your Prophet ﷺ at every moment with Your mercy and Your angels' du'a — count our small voices among the voices that turn toward him in love. Let our salawat not be a duty we perform but a doorway we walk through into the act You are already performing. And when we say *ʿalayhi-ṣ-ṣalātu wa-s-salām*, let our hearts hand themselves over fully — *taslīmā* — the way You commanded those who believe. Āmīn.
