# The Ten Pairs: When a Woman Asked, and Allah Answered with a Mirror

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

> [ayah:33:35] إِنَّ ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ وَٱلْمُسْلِمَـٰتِ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَـٰتِ وَٱلْقَـٰنِتِينَ وَٱلْقَـٰنِتَـٰتِ وَٱلصَّـٰدِقِينَ وَٱلصَّـٰدِقَـٰتِ وَٱلصَّـٰبِرِينَ وَٱلصَّـٰبِرَٰتِ وَٱلْخَـٰشِعِينَ وَٱلْخَـٰشِعَـٰتِ وَٱلْمُتَصَدِّقِينَ وَٱلْمُتَصَدِّقَـٰتِ وَٱلصَّـٰٓئِمِينَ وَٱلصَّـٰٓئِمَـٰتِ وَٱلْحَـٰفِظِينَ فُرُوجَهُمْ وَٱلْحَـٰفِظَـٰتِ وَٱلذَّٰكِرِينَ ٱللَّهَ كَثِيرًا وَٱلذَّٰكِرَٰتِ أَعَدَّ ٱللَّهُ لَهُم مَّغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

**Translation:**

> Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing women, the devoutly obedient men and devoutly obedient women, the truthful men and truthful women, the patient men and patient women, the humble men and humble women, the charitable men and charitable women, the fasting men and fasting women, the men who guard their private parts and the women who do so, and the men who remember Allah often and the women who do so — Allah has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward.

---

### Rebuild the World

Before we open a single word of this ayah, we have to walk into the room where it was first heard.

Madinah. Sometime after the Battle of the Trench. The community is exhausted, traumatized, freshly reorganized. Surah Al-Ahzab has been descending in pieces — pieces about war, about households, about the Prophet's wives, about who is family and who is not, about adoption, about hijab. It is a surah that keeps reaching into the most intimate corners of life and rearranging them.

And in the middle of all this — a woman walks up to the Prophet ﷺ with a question.

Her name is **Umm Salamah**, radiyAllahu 'anha. She is one of the wives of the Prophet ﷺ. And she notices something. She notices that when the Quran promises reward, it tends to say "the believers" — using a grammatical form that, in Arabic, technically includes women but linguistically *sounds* male. The masculine plural in Arabic is the default. It absorbs the feminine. It can mean "all of them" or it can mean "just the men" — and the listener has to infer which from context.

Umm Salamah notices. And she asks.

Different narrations preserve the question slightly differently. In one, she says to the Prophet ﷺ: *"Why does Allah mention the men and not the women?"* In another: *"I do not hear Allah mention women in anything of what He has revealed regarding emigration."* The question is, in essence: **Do we count? Are we seen? Is the reward also for us?**

[PAUSE]

Stay with that for a moment. A woman, in the seventh century, walks up to a Prophet and asks a question about her own inclusion. And she is not rebuked. She is not told to be patient. She is not told that the masculine includes the feminine and she should stop being difficult.

She is told to wait.

And then — Allah Himself answers.

The Heavens open. Jibrīl descends. And what comes down is not a one-line clarification. It is not a footnote. It is one of the longest, most rhythmically deliberate ayahs in the entire Quran — a sentence with ten elements named, each one given twice, once for him and once for her, all the way down to a final reward that is identical for both.

This is the world we are walking into.

---

### The Hook

A woman asks a question. And Allah does not just answer her — He builds a mirror. Ten qualities. Each named twice. Once in his form. Once in hers. Why ten? Why doubled? And why — when the Quran usually trusts a single word to carry the meaning — does it slow down here, almost ceremonially, and refuse the shortcut?

Let's find out.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We are going to look at three things in this ayah. Not ten. Three.

The first is the structure itself — the doubling. The second is one specific pair inside it that does something the others don't. The third is the closing — the reward — and the small word that determines who it belongs to.

---

### Element One: The Architecture of Naming Twice

#### Teach the concept first

In Arabic — as in many languages — the masculine plural is what grammarians call the **unmarked default**. If you have a room of one hundred women and one man, the verb and the noun used to describe them collectively will technically take the masculine form. The feminine is the **marked** form. It is specified. The masculine is the assumed.

Think of how, in older English, "mankind" was supposed to include women. Or how "you guys" addresses a mixed group. The masculine carries the weight of the universal, and the feminine — when it appears — is felt as an addition, a specification, sometimes an afterthought.

That is the linguistic background. That is the default.

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah.

#### Open the structure

He says:

> **al-muslimīna wa al-muslimāt**
> **wa al-muʾminīna wa al-muʾmināt**
> **wa al-qānitīna wa al-qānitāt**
> **wa al-ṣādiqīna wa al-ṣādiqāt**
> **wa al-ṣābirīna wa al-ṣābirāt**
> **wa al-khāshiʿīna wa al-khāshiʿāt**
> **wa al-mutaṣaddiqīna wa al-mutaṣaddiqāt**
> **wa al-ṣāʾimīna wa al-ṣāʾimāt**
> **wa al-ḥāfiẓīna furūjahum wa al-ḥāfiẓāt**
> **wa al-dhākirīna llāha kathīran wa al-dhākirāt**

Ten qualities. Each named in its masculine plural form. Each followed by its feminine plural form. The grammatical default — the one that could have done all the work by itself — is refused, ten times in a row.

#### Let's sit with this

Allah could have said: *Indeed the Muslims, the believers, the devoutly obedient, the truthful, the patient, the humble, the charitable, the fasting, those who guard themselves, and those who remember Allah often — for them is forgiveness and a great reward.*

In Arabic, that sentence is grammatically complete. It is theologically complete. It includes the women. Every classical grammarian would tell you the masculine plural, by default, absorbs the feminine.

So why doesn't Allah trust the default?

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. Umm Salamah's question was, in its bones, a question about *being seen*. Not about being technically included. Not about whether a footnote in a grammar book counted her. She wanted to know if Allah was *speaking to her*.

And Allah's response is to refuse the shortcut. To refuse the technicality. To refuse the linguistic convention that would have left her, accurately but invisibly, inside the male form.

Notice something: when something is the default, you don't notice it. When something is named, you do. By breaking the default and naming the feminine ten consecutive times, Allah is doing the linguistic equivalent of looking someone in the eye while He speaks to them.

This is not redundancy. **This is recognition.**

#### Deepen

There is something else happening in the rhythm. Read it aloud. Even if you don't know Arabic, listen:

*muslimīna wa muslimāt... muʾminīna wa muʾmināt... qānitīna wa qānitāt...*

Each pair ends in **-āt**. The feminine plural ending. The pattern hammers itself into the ear. Every single line lands on her. The feminine ending is not just present — it is the *resolution* of the rhythm. The masculine sets it up; the feminine completes it.

If you were sitting there, in that room, hearing this ayah for the first time, you would not be hearing "and the women too." You would be hearing the women named as the *cadence* of the entire revelation. The closing note. The thing that resolves the line.

[PAUSE]

#### What this single feature reveals

The doubling is not a concession. It is a correction of how language itself was hiding her. Allah did not just answer Umm Salamah's question. He restructured the sentence so the answer could not be missed, could not be assumed away, could not be lost in the default.

**Being included is not the same as being named. Being named is not the same as being addressed. And Allah is addressing her.**

[PAUSE]

---

### Element Two: One Pair Does Not Match the Others

Now look more closely. Inside the ten pairs, something happens at pair number nine that does not happen anywhere else.

#### Read it carefully

Pair nine: **wa al-ḥāfiẓīna furūjahum wa al-ḥāfiẓāt**

"The men who guard their private parts — and the women who guard."

#### What is missing?

In every other pair, the structure is symmetrical. Same word, masculine and feminine. *Muslimīna / muslimāt.* *Ṣādiqīna / ṣādiqāt.* *Ṣāʾimīna / ṣāʾimāt.*

But here, the object — *furūjahum*, "their private parts" — is mentioned for the men. And then, when it comes to the women, the object is left unsaid. Just **al-ḥāfiẓāt**. "The guarding women." Guarding *what*? The text does not repeat it.

#### Teach the concept

In Arabic rhetoric, there is a phenomenon called **ḥadhf** — deletion. When a word is omitted that the listener can easily supply from context, that omission is not laziness. It is rhetorical economy. The classical rhetoricians — al-Jurjānī, al-Zamakhsharī — taught that ḥadhf, properly used, *increases* the force of meaning because it forces the listener to participate in completing it.

But it can also do something else. It can be a form of **veiling**.

#### Stay with this

Notice what Allah does. For the men, He specifies the object. *Ḥāfiẓīna furūjahum.* The word is on the page. For the women, He withholds the explicit object. Just *al-ḥāfiẓāt*. The guarding ones, feminine.

The meaning is the same. The grammar requires the listener to supply the same object. But the word itself — the specific, intimate noun — is not pronounced over the women. It is veiled in the very ayah that, more than any other, is concerned with their being seen.

[PAUSE]

This is staggering when you slow down to it. The surah this ayah sits in — Surah Al-Ahzab — is the surah of *hijab*. The surah that introduces the concept of veiling, of modesty as protection, of inner sanctum. And here, in the rhetorical structure itself, Allah enacts what the surah teaches. The women are fully named in their virtue — but the language about their bodies is treated with linguistic modesty. The same noun, the same meaning, but spoken with a different kind of care.

#### What this reveals

The mirror is not mechanical. Allah is not running a function that replaces every *-īna* with an *-āt*. He is composing each pair with attention to what each pair is about. When the topic is intimate, the rhetoric becomes intimate. When the topic is bodily, the language itself wears a veil over her.

**This is what it looks like when justice is not flatness, but precision. Equal in reward. Identical in dignity. Distinct in how each is honored.**

---

### Element Three: The Tenth Pair — Where the Mirror Breaks Open

We have nine pairs. Then comes the tenth — and the tenth is the longest, the most expanded, and the one that is doing something different.

#### Read it

**wa al-dhākirīna llāha kathīran wa al-dhākirāt**

"The men who remember Allah often — and the women who remember."

#### What's different

Look at all the other pairs. They name the quality and stop. *Muslimīna wa muslimāt.* Just the word. *Ṣābirīna wa ṣābirāt.* Just the word.

But here, in the tenth pair, Allah expands the masculine: *al-dhākirīna **llāha kathīran***. "Those who remember **Allah** **often**." The object is named: Allah. The intensifier is named: *kathīran*, "much, often, abundantly."

This is the only pair in the entire list where the masculine side is amplified with both an object *and* an adverb of quantity.

#### Why here?

Because *dhikr* — remembrance — is the quality that integrates all the others.

Think about what we have just been told. Submission. Faith. Devotion. Truthfulness. Patience. Humility. Charity. Fasting. Guarding the self. Each is a vertical column of practice. Each is a distinct way of being.

But what runs through all of them like a current?

**Constant awareness of Allah.**

You cannot truly submit without remembering Who you submit to. You cannot truly believe without remembering. You cannot be patient without remembering why. You cannot guard yourself without remembering Who is watching. Every other quality on this list quietly depends on this last one.

So when Allah comes to the tenth, He does not just name the quality — He gives it volume. *Kathīran.* Often. Abundantly. Not occasional. Not seasonal. Not when convenient.

The Quran is telling us: **of all ten, this is the one you have to do constantly.** The others have their times — fasting has its month, charity has its occasions, patience has its trials. But dhikr is the through-line. It is the breath underneath all the other limbs of practice.

#### And the feminine?

*wa al-dhākirāt.*

Just one word. No expansion. No object. No adverb.

#### What this reveals — and this is delicate

Two readings live together here, and both are honored in the tradition.

**The first reading:** the *kathīran* and the object *Allāha* are understood to apply to both genders. The rhetoric mentions them once for grammatical economy — but theologically, the women remember Allah just as often, just as much.

**The second reading:** there is a deliberate gentling for the women here. The men get the full weight of the imperative — remember **Allah**, remember Him **often**. The women, in a surah saturated with their being seen, are simply named as the rememberers. The word over them is not piled with intensifiers. They are simply *dhākirāt*.

Both readings are present in the classical commentary. And here is what is beautiful: you do not have to choose. They are both true at once. The reward is identical. The standard is identical. But the rhetoric, again, is not mechanical. It is composed.

[PAUSE]

#### One clear statement

By saving the expansion for the tenth pair, Allah is signaling which quality is the spine of the other nine. **Remembrance is not one virtue among ten. It is the thread that makes the other nine possible.**

And by placing it last, Allah leaves us on the note that we are supposed to leave the ayah carrying with us into our day.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. The doubling that refuses to let the feminine disappear into the default. The withheld noun that veils her even as it names her. The expanded tenth that makes remembrance the spine of the list.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: When You Ask to Be Seen, and the Answer Is a Sentence Built for You

#### Begin where you live

There is a particular kind of pain that comes from being technically included but functionally invisible.

You have felt this. It is the meeting where everyone agrees that "we all matter" but somehow your contribution never gets named. It is the family where "we love everyone equally" but somehow your sibling's wedding got the long toast and yours got a sentence. It is the policy that "applies to everyone" but somehow it was written for a body that is not yours, a schedule that is not yours, a life that is not yours.

The pain is not that you are excluded. It is that your inclusion is invisible, and you cannot prove it. Anyone you try to explain it to says: *but the rule includes you.* And technically, they are right. And you walk away knowing you have been seen — and not seen — at the same time.

#### Now bring the ayah to it

Umm Salamah, radiyAllahu 'anha, walked up with that exact pain. Not exclusion — *invisibility inside inclusion*. The Quran *included* her. She wasn't fighting that. She was naming the gap between technical inclusion and felt recognition.

And Allah's answer was not: *technically, you were always included.*

His answer was: *let Me say your name.*

Ten times.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepen

Think about what this means about Allah's relationship to your felt experience.

He did not tell her: *the default form was sufficient, your concern is misplaced.* He did not tell her: *grammatically you have nothing to complain about.* He answered the question *underneath* the question. The question on the surface was about grammar. The question underneath was: *am I seen?*

And He sent down an entire ayah to say: **yes.**

Now apply this to yourself. Not as a woman, not as a man, but as a human being who has, at some point, felt that gap between technical and real, between included and addressed, between counted and known.

The God who composed this ayah knows the difference. He knows that being included in the masculine plural is not the same as being named in the feminine. He knows that *being part of a group He blesses* is not the same as *hearing your specific form receive the blessing*.

And He composed His speech accordingly.

#### Carry this into your life

Tomorrow, when you say *al-ḥamdu lillāh*, sit with the fact that the God you are praising is the One who, when one woman asked to be seen, slowed His own revelation down to name her ten consecutive times. He is not impatient with the small ache of being unseen. He is the One who answers it.

And when *you* feel invisible — in your home, in your work, in your community — return here. Not to demand the same answer. But to remember that the God you worship is the kind of God who *gave* that answer. He is not blind to the ache. The ache is a thing He has, on the record of revelation, taken seriously.

### Theme Two: The List as a Mirror for the Self

#### Begin where you live

There is a habit modern life trains us into. The habit of self-evaluation through metrics. Steps walked. Calories tracked. Books read. Habits streaked. We have become accustomed to measuring our lives in numbers we can render in a graph.

But Allah is offering us something different here.

#### Look at the list

Read the ten qualities again, slowly:

1. Submission (*islām*)
2. Faith (*īmān*)
3. Devotion / obedience (*qunūt*)
4. Truthfulness (*ṣidq*)
5. Patience (*ṣabr*)
6. Humility / awe (*khushūʿ*)
7. Charity (*ṣadaqa*)
8. Fasting (*ṣiyām*)
9. Guarding the self (*ḥifẓ al-furūj*)
10. Constant remembrance (*dhikr kathīr*)

Notice what they share. None of them is a single act. None of them is a streak you can complete and move on from. They are all **conditions of being** — states the self inhabits, that are tested and renewed every day.

You are not "patient" because you survived one hard thing. You are *ṣābir* if patience is the shape of your responses to whatever comes. You are not "truthful" because you didn't lie today. You are *ṣādiq* if truth is the climate of your speech. You are not "humble" because you bowed in prayer this morning. You are *khāshiʿ* if there is a softness in your standing before Allah that does not leave you when you stand up.

These are not boxes. They are *aromas*.

[PAUSE]

#### Deepen

Notice also: the list moves from outward to inward.

It starts with the most visible: *islām* — the outward profession, the submission anyone can witness.
Then it moves deeper: *īmān* — the inward conviction.
Then deeper still: *qunūt* — the soul's habitual posture before its Lord.
Then *ṣidq* — the alignment of word with inner reality.
Then *ṣabr* — what holds when life pulls.
Then *khushūʿ* — the trembling softness of the heart.
Then *ṣadaqa* — the body acting on the heart's softness.
Then *ṣiyām* — the body refusing what it wants for the One it loves.
Then *ḥifẓ* — the protection of the self's most intimate ground.
Then, at the bottom of the well, *dhikr kathīr* — the unbroken thread of remembrance that runs through it all.

This is a topology of the self. From the outermost wall to the innermost room.

#### And what is at the center?

Remembrance.

Not knowledge. Not certainty. Not any particular act. **Remembrance.**

Because Allah is not asking you whether you know Him. He is asking whether you *remember* Him. Knowledge is a possession. Remembrance is a relationship. Knowledge is held in the mind. Remembrance is held in the breath.

#### Carry this into your life

Tonight, before you sleep, take the ten. Walk yourself through them — not to score yourself, but to see yourself.

*Am I a muslim today? Did I submit, somewhere, to something I would rather not have submitted to?*

*Am I a muʾmin today? Did I trust Him when something asked me not to trust Him?*

*Am I qānit? Was there a moment of voluntary obedience — not because I had to, but because I wanted to please Him?*

*Am I ṣādiq? Was there a moment today where the truth would have cost me something, and I told it anyway?*

*Am I ṣābir? When the small thing didn't go my way — the traffic, the comment, the news — did I hold the moment with steadiness?*

*Am I khāshiʿ? Did I bow today with a heart that knew it was bowing?*

*Am I mutaṣaddiq? Did anything leave my hand for the sake of someone who needed it more than I did?*

*Am I ṣāʾim? Did I refuse my nafs anything today — not just food, but anything it wanted that it didn't need?*

*Am I ḥāfiẓ? Did I guard the gates — eyes, hands, what I consumed, what I produced?*

*And — the question that gathers all the others — was Allah, today, present to me kathīran? Often? Or was He a thought I had once at fajr and forgot until maghrib?*

This is not a test. This is a mirror. The ayah is asking you to use it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal

Now that we have done the work, look at what Allah composed.

A woman asked: *am I seen?*

He answered with a list of ten qualities — the qualities by which any soul, of any gender, is measured before Him. He named each one twice — once for the form she came in, once for the form he came in. He veiled the most intimate of the qualities so that even in being named, she was honored with care. He saved the most universal of the qualities — remembrance — for the tenth position, and there He let the rhetoric expand, so that we would know which of the ten is the spine of the others.

And then, at the very end, He collapsed the whole edifice into one promise:

> **aʿadda llāhu lahum maghfiratan wa ajran ʿaẓīman**
> "Allah has prepared for *them* forgiveness and a great reward."

**Notice the pronoun.**

*Lahum.* "For them."

After ten pairs of insisting on the feminine — after ten consecutive resolutions of the rhythm into the *-āt* — when it comes time to give the reward, Allah does not split it. He does not say *for the men this reward and for the women that reward*. He uses one pronoun. *Lahum.*

The journey of the ayah is: distinguish carefully, then unite completely. Name him. Name her. Name him. Name her. Ten times. And then — when the matter is reward, when the matter is forgiveness, when the matter is what He is preparing in the unseen — there is no longer a him or a her. There is only *them*.

This is what the architecture proves. Recognition is not opposed to unity. **Distinct naming is what makes shared reward credible.** If He had collapsed them into *al-muslimīn* from the beginning, the *lahum* at the end would have hidden her. But because He named her ten times first, the *lahum* at the end can include her, fully, in a reward that is genuinely shared.

[PAUSE]

---

### Bringing It Together

The two themes — being seen, and the mirror of the self — are the same insight from two angles.

The first is Allah looking at *you*. The second is Allah giving you the tools to look at *yourself*.

In the first, He proves He sees. In the second, He shows you what He sees by.

And in both, the answer to Umm Salamah's question is also the answer to yours.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life have you mistaken being included for being seen — and where have you mistaken the absence of a name for the absence of love?

2. If you were to be described by one of the ten qualities — not aspirationally, but accurately — which would it be? And which is the one you most need to grow into?

3. The ayah saves *kathīran* for the tenth. If you measured your day by how often Allah was present in your awareness — how often, today, did you remember Him?

4. Allah names the feminine ten times in this ayah. Where in your speech, your home, your community — is there someone whose inclusion is technical but whose naming is missing?

5. The reward at the end is *one* reward, *lahum*, for them. What would it change about how you treat people of different forms and stations if you held in your chest the truth that the reward Allah is preparing is genuinely shared?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**When a woman asked Allah whether she was seen, He answered not with a clarification but with an architecture — ten qualities, each named twice, ending in a single shared reward, so that no listener of any form could miss that she was being addressed directly, by name, in her own grammatical body.**

### The Closing Invitation

Walk through your day tomorrow with two things in your chest.

The first: *I am seen.* Not technically. Not by default. Specifically. The God who composed Surah Al-Ahzab does not include you by accident. He names you on purpose.

The second: *the mirror has ten panels.* When you want to know how you are doing in front of Him — do not ask whether you are religious. Ask the ten. Submission. Faith. Devotion. Truth. Patience. Humility. Charity. Fasting. Guarding. And — running through it all — remembrance. *Kathīran.* Often.

If even one of the ten is alive in you today, you are on the list. If you are working to bring the others alive, you are walking toward *lahum* — the pronoun in which He prepares for you what no eye has seen and no ear has heard.
