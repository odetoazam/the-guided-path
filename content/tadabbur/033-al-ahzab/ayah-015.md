# Introduction

## The Ayah

> **وَلَقَدْ كَانُوا۟ عَـٰهَدُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مِن قَبْلُ لَا يُوَلُّونَ ٱلْأَدْبَـٰرَ ۚ وَكَانَ عَهْدُ ٱللَّهِ مَسْـُٔولًۭا**

*Wa laqad kānū ʿāhadū-llāha min qablu lā yuwallūna-l-adbāra ۚ wa kāna ʿahdu-llāhi mas'ūlā.*

**"And they had already promised Allah before that they would not turn their backs. And the promise to Allah is to be questioned about."**

---

## Rebuild the World

To feel what this ayah is doing, we have to put ourselves back in Medina in the fifth year after the Hijrah. A confederation of tribes — *al-aḥzāb*, the very word that names this surah — has gathered against the Muslim community. Ten thousand fighters. A trench dug around the city in panic. The cold. The hunger. The terror of children. The whisper that runs through the camp at night when no one is sure the morning will come.

In that moment, some hearts hold. And some hearts open at the seams.

This passage — running through verses 9 to 27 — is Allah pulling back the curtain on what was happening inside those hearts. Not the public speech. Not the words said in the masjid. The actual private interior. The shifting eyes. The men whispering, "Allah and His Messenger promised us nothing but delusion." The ones who said, "Our houses are exposed," when their houses were not exposed at all — they were just looking for an exit.

And here, at ayah 15, Allah does something that makes the whole passage land in a different way. He reaches back into the past. He reaches into a moment these people had probably forgotten. A moment when there was no fear. A moment when they had stood, in safety, and made a promise.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators tell us *who* this promise belonged to — and they don't fully agree. **Qatāda** says these were people who had been absent from Badr. They had watched, from a distance, the honor and the vindication that came to the men of Badr, and their hearts ached. So they swore to Allah: *if You ever give us a battle, we will fight.* **Yazīd ibn Rūmān** names them: Banū Ḥāritha, who almost broke ranks at Uhud alongside Banū Salima, and afterward swore to Allah they would never do that again. **Muqātil** preserves a similar narration.

Take all three together as one composite picture — because the *type* of person is identical in every version. Someone who pledged courage when courage cost nothing. Someone whose tongue moved faster than their nerve. Someone whose imagined future self was braver than their actual present self.

[PAUSE]

## The Hook

What Allah is about to do in this ayah is not what we expect. We expect a rebuke for cowardice in the present moment. Instead, He reaches into the past, retrieves a promise these people had probably stopped thinking about, and places it next to their current behavior. And then He says one short, devastating sentence at the end that we are going to spend a long time with — because that sentence is not just about *them*.

It is about every promise you have ever made to Allah when the test was not yet in the room.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

We are going to walk through three features of this ayah. None of them are exotic. All of them are sitting on the surface of the words. But each one, when we slow down, opens a door.

## Element One: The Shape of a Promise — عَاهَدُوا (ʿāhadū)

Before we look at the word, let's teach a piece of Arabic grammar — because without it, the word will pass by unnoticed.

Arabic verbs have a base form, sometimes called Form I. And then they have derived forms, numbered II through X, each of which adds a specific layer of meaning to the base. You don't need to memorize them. But you do need to know that one of these forms — **Form III**, built on the pattern *fāʿala* — has a particular function. It marks **reciprocity**. Mutual action between two parties.

Here is what I mean in everyday Arabic. The Form I verb *kataba* means "he wrote." But the Form III verb *kātaba* doesn't just mean "he wrote a lot" or "he wrote intensely." It means *he corresponded with*. Two people writing back and forth. Form III pulls a verb out of one direction and makes it into a relationship.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the word Allah chose:

> **عَاهَدُوا** — *ʿāhadū*

This is Form III. The root is ʿ-h-d, which has to do with covenant, promise, treaty. Form I (*ʿahida*) would mean simply *he made a promise, he committed*. But Allah does not use Form I here. He uses Form III.

Notice something. He is not saying *they made a promise to Allah* in the way you might write a personal vow in your journal. He is saying — **they entered into a mutual covenant with Allah.**

Stay with this. Because something theological just opened.

A Form III verb requires two parties. When *they* covenant with *Allah*, the verbal structure itself is telling you that this is not a one-sided thing. This is not a person muttering a vow to themselves and asking Allah to witness it. This is a contract. With clauses. With a counterparty. And that counterparty is **Allah**.

Here's what's remarkable. Allah's commentary on what these people did, in this single grammatical choice, refuses to demote the seriousness of what happened. He could have said *waʿadū-llāha* — "they made a one-sided promise to Allah," using a different root. He didn't. He used the form that says: *two parties were bound here.*

[PAUSE]

And now ask the obvious follow-up question. **If they bound themselves, what did Allah bind Himself to?**

The classical scholars don't shy away from this. Throughout the Quran, Allah's covenant with His servants carries reciprocity. You will find it in al-Baqara 2:40 — *awfū bi-ʿahdī ūfi bi-ʿahdikum* — "fulfill My covenant, I will fulfill yours." You will find it again in al-Mā'ida 5:7. The structure is everywhere.

What this invites us to consider — and now I am stepping past pure grammar into interpretive application, so I will mark that — is that when these people stood, in their safe moment, and said "We will not turn our backs," they weren't just making a unilateral commitment. They were entering into a structure in which Allah, by His own grace, was prepared to give them aid, fortitude, the angels at Badr, the calm that descends. Form III binds in both directions.

And then, when fear came, **they broke their side of a covenant Allah was still honoring on His side.**

That is not the breaking of a personal resolution. That is the breaking of a treaty.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Verb That Refuses to Even Face Backward — لَا يُوَلُّونَ ٱلْأَدْبَارَ

Now let's look at what they promised.

> **لَا يُوَلُّونَ ٱلْأَدْبَارَ** — *lā yuwallūna-l-adbār*

"That they would not turn their backs."

The English translation is fine, but it loses the precision. Let's open this slowly.

The verb here is *yuwallūna*, from the root w-l-y. The Leeds morphological corpus marks it explicitly as **Form II**. Form II is the *faʿʿala* pattern — the doubling of the middle radical. And one of the standard functions of Form II is to make a Form I verb transitive — to *cause* something to face a direction, to *make* something turn.

The Form I verb *waliya* means *to follow, to be near to, to be in charge of*. But the Form II verb *wallā* means *to cause to turn, to direct toward, to make face* something.

So the structure here is not just "they will not flee." It is more precise than that. It is: **they will not direct, will not orient, will not even cause to face — their backs — toward the enemy.**

Do you see the difference?

In English, "to flee" is a single action. In Arabic, what Allah is describing in their promise is the *very first geometric act of fleeing* — the rotation of the body so that the back is now what is presented to the threat. The promise is not just "we will stay." The promise is "**we will not even orient our backs in that direction**."

[PAUSE]

This is a maximalist promise. It is a promise that refuses to even *gesture* toward retreat. Not "we will not fully run." Not "we will not flee far." But "we will not even direct ourselves backward."

And then notice the tense. *Yuwallūna* is imperfect — *muḍāriʿ* — which in Arabic carries continuous, ongoing, habitual force, especially when paired with the negation *lā*. So the precise sense is not just "we will not turn our backs once." It is "**we will never, at any moment, turn our backs.**" A standing, perpetual commitment.

Stay with the audacity of this. These are people standing in safety, in the warm light of a moment when nothing is asked of them, and they are reaching for the maximum vocabulary of courage. *We will not even direct our bodies away from the fight.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's worth sitting with. There is a psychology embedded in this verbal choice that most of us know intimately. When the test is not present, we imagine ourselves in the maximalist form. We don't imagine ourselves doing the bare minimum — we imagine ourselves doing the saintly thing, the heroic thing, the thing the books describe.

And Allah, by quoting their own pledge back to them in its maximalist Form II shape, is doing something very specific. He is not adding to their guilt. He is using their *own* chosen words. He is saying: *these are not My words. These are your words. You picked them.*

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Sentence That Asks Without Telling You Who Is Asking — وَكَانَ عَهْدُ ٱللَّهِ مَسْـُٔولًۭا

Now we come to the final sentence of the ayah. And this is where the door opens widest.

> **وَكَانَ عَهْدُ ٱللَّهِ مَسْـُٔولًۭا**

"And the promise to Allah is to be questioned about."

The word we want to focus on is *masʾūlan*. Root s-ʾ-l, which means *to ask*. *Masʾūl* is the passive participle. *Asked about*. *Questioned about*. *Something concerning which a question will be raised.*

Now I want to teach a piece of grammar that most listeners have never been taught to notice but that does enormous theological work in the Quran. It is what scholars call the **agentless passive**.

In English, if I say "the cake was eaten," you immediately ask, *by whom?* In Arabic, the passive structure can be used in exactly that way — and very often, especially in the Quran, the agent is deliberately not named. The grammar leaves it open.

When Allah uses an agentless passive, the silence around *who is acting* is not a gap. It is a feature. It expands the action. It makes it universal.

[PAUSE]

So look at *masʾūlan* again.

Allah does not say *yas'aluhum Allāhu ʿan ʿahdihim* — "Allah will ask them about their promise." He could have said that. The grammar is available to Him. He chose not to.

Instead, He uses the passive participle. **The covenant is asked-about.** That's the structure.

By whom? **Unspecified.**

And because the agent is unspecified, the questioning expands. It is not narrowed to a single moment of judgment. It is not pinned to a single questioner. It becomes — and here is what this invites us to consider — a property of the covenant itself.

The covenant is *the kind of thing that gets asked about*. The promise to Allah is *intrinsically interrogative*. By its very nature, it carries a future question inside it. The promise itself is alive with a question that will, somewhere, somewhen, by someone, be put to you.

[PAUSE]

Let me try to make this concrete with an analogy. Think of a loan. The moment you sign for a loan, the loan does not just sit there. The loan is *the kind of object that, by its nature, contains a future question* — namely, *when will you pay it back?* That question is built into the document. You do not have to be reminded of it for it to exist. It exists from the moment of signing.

Now Allah is saying: *ʿahd Allāh* — the covenant with Allah — is exactly that kind of object. From the moment you make it, it contains within itself a future question. *Did you fulfill it?* That question does not need a questioner to make it real. It is real because the covenant is real.

[PAUSE]

And there is one more thing to notice in this last sentence that I do not want to miss.

The phrase is *ʿahd Allāh* — "the covenant of Allah" — using the construct state (*iḍāfa*) that binds two nouns into a relationship. In Arabic, that construct is genuinely ambiguous. *ʿahd Allāh* can mean:

- the covenant *to* Allah (Allah as the one to whom the promise is made),
- the covenant *from* Allah (Allah as the one giving the promise),
- the covenant *of* Allah (Allah as the owner of the structure).

And the classical commentators don't pick one and discard the others. They preserve the ambiguity. Because in a real covenant with Allah, all three are true at the same time. He is the recipient. He is the witness who guarantees the structure. And — going back to what Form III taught us — He is also a party.

So when Allah says "the covenant of Allah is asked-about," every dimension of that covenant is being placed under questioning. The promise you made to Him. The aid He extended in return. The structure that held the whole thing together. All of it. Questioned.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Bridge

Look at what these three features have shown us together.

The verb *ʿāhadū* told us this was not a one-sided vow — it was a binding contract with two parties. The verb *lā yuwallūna* told us they had reached for the most maximalist language available, refusing to even orient their backs toward retreat. And *masʾūlan* told us that a covenant with Allah is alive — it contains its own future question, regardless of whether anyone is currently asking it.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Imagined Self and the Actual Self

I want you to think of someone you knew in high school. Or college. Or yourself at twenty-two. Think of the version of yourself that existed before the test came.

That version of you knew exactly what kind of parent you would be. Exactly what kind of spouse. Exactly the kind of Muslim you would be at thirty-five. That version of you had it all figured out. You were going to be patient. Generous. Up before fajr. Never raise your voice. Always make time for your parents.

That version of you was *brave*.

[PAUSE]

Now sit with the version of you that actually exists. Right now. Today. The one who lost his temper this week. The one who skipped the dhikr she promised herself she would do every morning. The one whose tongue moves faster than his nerve.

There is a gap. The gap is universal. The gap is human.

Modern psychology has a name for this. It's called **affective forecasting error**. Researchers have shown, over and over, that human beings are systematically and predictably wrong about how they will feel and act in future emotional situations. We imagine our future selves as more virtuous, more disciplined, more courageous than our future selves turn out to be. The gap between the *imagined self* and the *acting self* is not a personal failing. It is a structural feature of being human.

[PAUSE]

And here is where this ayah opens onto something deep.

Allah is not introducing us to a new psychological insight. He is, in 1400-year-old Arabic, describing the same gap. People stood in safety. They imagined themselves brave. They reached for the maximalist Form II language — *we will not even orient our backs in that direction.* Their imagined self was a hero.

And then the trench came. The cold came. The whispers in the night came. And the actual self — the one who *actually* shows up when fear is in the room — turned out to be smaller than the imagined self.

[PAUSE]

The question this ayah is putting to us is not, *did the gap exist?* It is going to exist. The gap is human.

The question is — **what does the covenant do, knowing the gap exists?**

And here is where it gets interesting. Because notice — Allah is not, in this ayah, telling them they should have promised less. He is not saying *you were arrogant to promise something so maximalist*. He is not lowering the bar. He is holding them to the bar they themselves set.

What this invites us to consider is that the function of the covenant, in Islam, is *not* to be a description of who you already are. It is to be the **shape your becoming is reaching toward.** The covenant is the pull. The covenant is the gravity.

The danger is not in making a covenant your present self cannot keep. The danger is in making one and then *forgetting you made it*. The danger is when the imagined self disappears from your memory the moment fear walks in.

[PAUSE]

And this changes how we read every promise we have ever made to Allah. The promise you made during a hard moment — *Yā Allāh, if You get me through this, I will pray every fajr.* The promise you made the night you cried after a sin — *Yā Allāh, I will never do this again.* The promise you made when your child was sick — *Yā Allāh, I will be more present, I will give more, I will turn back.*

Those promises did not evaporate. They are not memories. They are not feelings.

They are **objects** — alive, intrinsically interrogative, carrying their own future question — that you placed into the universe in your name. And Form III tells us: Allah received them. And He has been responding on His side.

What does this change tomorrow? It changes whether you treat the next promise you make in a moment of fear or beauty as a *feeling* or as a *contract*. It changes whether you write it down, name it, hold it in front of you. Whether, when the test comes, you let your present self forget what your earlier self pledged — or whether you keep the imagined self in the room as a witness to what the acting self is supposed to do.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: The Covenant Is Alive — It Is Waiting To Speak

Let me ask you something. Where do the promises you have made — and broken — currently live?

In your memory, probably. Some of them. The others have fallen out of memory entirely. You couldn't list them if I asked you to. You made a promise to Allah in 2019 that you could not name today.

And we have a tendency to assume that *out of memory* equals *out of existence*. If I no longer think about it, it must no longer exist.

[PAUSE]

The last sentence of this ayah is taking a hammer to that assumption.

*Wa kāna ʿahdu-llāhi mas'ūlā.*

The covenant with Allah is **masʾūl** — asked-about, questioned, the kind of thing whose nature includes a future question. We saw the grammar. The agent is unspecified. The questioning is intrinsic to the covenant itself.

So here is the picture this gives us. Every promise you have ever made to Allah — every one — has not disappeared. It is sitting somewhere in the architecture of your existence. It is waiting. It contains, inside it, a future question that has not yet been asked but will be. And the moment of the asking is not under your control.

[PAUSE]

Let me try an analogy.

Imagine you walked into a room and signed a stack of contracts. Real contracts. Legally binding. And then you forgot about them. You went on with your life. You stopped thinking about them.

The contracts did not stop existing because you stopped remembering them. They are still in a filing cabinet somewhere. Each one is still *the kind of object that contains a future question*. One day someone is going to walk into that room, pull open the cabinet, and ask: *did you do what you said?*

[PAUSE]

Now scale that up. Because the architecture Allah is describing is bigger than any human filing cabinet. The covenants you made — *I will turn back*, *I will leave that habit*, *I will be present for my parents*, *I will pray*, *I will give*, *I will not return to that sin* — every one of them, the grammar of this ayah is telling you, has the property of *masʾūl* built into it.

You did not just make a wish. You did not just feel an emotion. You signed something. And Form III tells us Allah signed back.

[PAUSE]

There is, I want to be careful here, a way to hear this that becomes crushing. A way to hear it and think: *every broken promise I have ever made is hanging over me like a sword*. That is not actually what this ayah is doing. Notice the very next verses in this surah. Verse 23: *"Among the believers are men who have been true to what they promised Allah."* Allah is not done with the theme. He is, four verses later, going to show us the other half of the picture — the people whose covenant did get fulfilled.

So what this ayah is doing is not condemning. It is **dignifying**.

It is dignifying your promises by telling you they are not feelings. They are not mood. They are not journal entries. They are real objects with real weight that Allah Himself has received and is holding.

And here is the strange comfort in that. If your promises are real objects, then so is your *return* to them. If you made a covenant in 2019 and broke it and forgot it — and then, today, you remembered it, and you turned back, and you renewed it — that turning back is also a real act, witnessed by the same Allah who witnessed the original covenant. The structure that holds the question also holds the answer.

[PAUSE]

What this changes about how I live tomorrow is this: **I want to know what I have promised.** I want to do the slow work of remembering. Not to drown in regret — but because the covenant is alive whether I remember it or not, and I would rather meet it with my eyes open than be surprised by it when it speaks.

Sit, even for one evening, and try to remember. *What did I promise You, Yā Allāh, on the worst night of my life? What did I promise on my best night? What did I say in the hospital? What did I say at the funeral? What did I say in the prayer where I wept?*

Those are not memories. They are masʾūl. They are asking to be answered.

# Closing Synthesis

## Bringing It Together

Look at what we have walked through.

The verb *ʿāhadū* — Form III — told us that a promise to Allah is not a unilateral vow. It is a contract with a counterparty. Two parties, two sets of obligations.

The verb *lā yuwallūna* — Form II, in the imperfect — told us that the promise these people made was a maximalist promise, reaching for the highest language of courage. They picked the words. Allah held them to the words.

And *masʾūlan* — the agentless passive participle — told us that a covenant with Allah is intrinsically interrogative. It contains its own future question. The question lives in the covenant, not in the questioner.

And then, in our two themes, we asked: what does this mean for the gap between who we imagine ourselves to be and who we actually are when tested? And what does this mean for the promises we have made and forgotten?

These are not two separate insights. They are the same insight from two directions.

**The imagined self that made the promise is the one Allah is going to ask about. And the actual self that broke it is the one being given, right now, the chance to return.** The covenant is the bridge between them. The covenant is alive. The covenant is waiting to speak.

[PAUSE]

## Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time you made a real promise to Allah — not a wish, not a hope, but a covenant? Can you name what it was?

2. What does your imagined self — the one who knows exactly who you should be — currently demand of you that your actual self is not delivering?

3. Are there promises you made to Allah in a moment of fear, beauty, or weeping that you have stopped thinking about? If you sat for one hour tonight, what would you remember?

4. If every covenant is *masʾūl* — alive with its own question — what would change about how you make the next one?

5. The believers in 33:23 fulfilled what they promised. What separates them from the people in 33:15? Is it the size of the promise — or something about how the promise was held?

[PAUSE]

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The promise you made to Allah when no test was in the room is the same promise that will speak when every test reveals you — because a covenant with Allah is not a feeling that fades but a living object that carries its own question into the future.**

[PAUSE]

## The Closing Invitation

What I want to invite, rather than impose, is this. Take one evening. Maybe tonight. Maybe this week. Sit in a quiet room. And try to remember. Not to drown — to dignify.

*Yā Allāh, what have I promised You?*

Some of those promises you will be able to keep. Some you will not. Some you will need to renew. Some you will need to apologize for. But every single one of them is still real, still received, still held by the One you made it to.

And the same surah that opens this question, just a few verses later, shows us that some people *did* fulfill what they pledged. The door is not closed. It never was. The door is exactly the size of your willingness to remember what you said and walk back toward it.

[PAUSE]

## A Brief Du'a

Yā Allāh — You have received every covenant I have made, even the ones I have forgotten. Let me not be among those whose imagined self made promises my acting self did not honor. Bring back to my memory what I have buried, and give me the strength to fulfill what is still mine to fulfill. And on the day the question is asked, let my answer be the one You gave to those who were true.

Āmīn.
