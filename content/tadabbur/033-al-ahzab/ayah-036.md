# When the Matter Is Decided

## Surah Al-Ahzab, Ayah 36

---

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**

[ayah:33:36] وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ وَلَا مُؤْمِنَةٍ إِذَا قَضَى ٱللَّهُ وَرَسُولُهُۥٓ أَمْرًا أَن يَكُونَ لَهُمُ ٱلْخِيَرَةُ مِنْ أَمْرِهِمْ ۗ وَمَن يَعْصِ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ فَقَدْ ضَلَّ ضَلَـٰلًا مُّبِينًا

**Translation:**

"And it is not for a believing man or a believing woman, when Allah and His Messenger have decided a matter, that they should have any choice in their affair. And whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger has certainly strayed into clear error."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuild the World

Imagine, for a moment, you live in a city where the ground itself has shifted three times in the last few years.

You used to drink wine. Now you don't.
You used to face Jerusalem in prayer. Now you face Makkah.
You used to keep your slaves and your adopted sons under the same family name as your biological sons. Now Allah has just told you — *no, that adopted son is not your son, he is your brother. He carries his birth father's name.*

This is Madinah, somewhere around year 5 of the hijrah. The community is being *re-formed* — not just spiritually, but structurally. Every social convention that the Arabs took as eternal — every assumption about lineage, marriage, wealth, war, modesty — is being lifted up by Allah, examined, and either confirmed or shattered.

And the people are watching the Prophet ﷺ closely. Because everything that touches his household becomes a public matter. Everything that happens in his family becomes law for the rest of them.

Now imagine a specific situation. There is a woman in this community — Zaynab bint Jahsh — noble, devout, the Prophet's own cousin. There is a man — Zayd ibn Haritha — a former slave whom the Prophet ﷺ freed and adopted as a son. The Prophet ﷺ proposes that Zaynab marry Zayd.

And Zaynab does not want to.

Neither does her brother. *We are noble Quraysh,* they think. *And he is a freed slave. The categories do not match.*

[PAUSE]

This is the moment Allah is speaking into. Not into a vacuum. Into a *very specific resistance*. A resistance born of the old world, the world before Islam, the world where bloodline and status decided what was thinkable.

And before Allah even narrates how that marriage went, before He tells us what happened next — He stops the entire flow of the surah and lays down a principle.

A principle so absolute, so structural, that it doesn't just speak to Zaynab and Zayd. It speaks to every believing man and every believing woman who will live until the end of time.

### The Hook

There is a word in this ayah that, on the surface, looks like a small grammatical feature. A particle. A turn of phrase. But that word, if you sit with it, is doing something almost violent — it is *removing* something from the believer. Removing it in a way Arabic rarely removes anything.

And there is a pairing — *believing man and believing woman* — that Allah does not need to make grammatically, but chooses to make.

[PAUSE]

Why? When Arabic already covers both with one word, why does He name them both?

Stay with that question. Because by the time we come back to it, we will see that this ayah is not really about obedience.

It is about something the modern world has lost the ability to name.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Wa mā kāna* — The Death Sentence on a Possibility

Let me ask you something about the way you speak.

When you want to tell someone they shouldn't do something, you have options. You can say, *"Don't do that."* You can say, *"You're not allowed."* You can say, *"It's wrong."* Each of these is a kind of prohibition. But they all leave the action *possible* — the person could still, theoretically, do it.

But there is another mode of speech, in every language, where you don't just prohibit an action. You declare it *unthinkable*. You say, *"That's not who we are."* Or *"It's not in him to do that."* You're not telling someone not to do it. You're telling them the action and the identity *cannot coexist*.

In English we might say: *"A doctor does not abandon a dying patient."* That's not a rule. It's a statement about what a doctor *is*. If a doctor did that, you wouldn't say they broke a rule. You'd say they *stopped being a doctor in that moment.*

Hold onto that. Because Arabic has a construction that does this with surgical precision.

[PAUSE]

The ayah opens with: **وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ وَلَا مُؤْمِنَةٍ** — *wa mā kāna li-mu'minin wa-lā mu'minah.*

Literally: *And it has not been for a believing man, nor for a believing woman...*

The construction is **مَا كَانَ + لِ** — *mā kāna* followed by the *lām* of belonging.

*Mā kāna* is the past-tense negation of the verb *to be*. *Lām* is the particle of possession, of "for" or "belonging to."

In English we might naturally translate this as: *"It is not for them..."* or *"They have no right..."*

But that translation, while technically correct, misses the *temperature* of the Arabic.

Here is what *mā kāna li-* does in classical Arabic. It does not merely prohibit. It declares an action *incompatible with the nature of the subject*. It is the construction Allah uses when He wants to say: *this thing and this identity have never gone together, do not now go together, and will never go together.*

Look at how the Quran deploys it elsewhere:

**مَا كَانَ لِلَّهِ أَن يَتَّخِذَ مِن وَلَدٍ** — *"It is not for Allah to take a son."* (Maryam 19:35)

That's not Allah saying *"I have decided not to."* That's Allah saying *taking a son is incompatible with being Allah.* The two cannot be predicated of the same subject.

Or: **مَا كَانَ لِنَبِيٍّ أَن يَكُونَ لَهُۥٓ أَسْرَىٰ** — *"It was not for a Prophet to have captives..."* (Al-Anfal 8:67)

The construction says: the role of prophet and the act being described cannot coexist in the way being suggested.

So now hear it again: **وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ وَلَا مُؤْمِنَةٍ ... أَن يَكُونَ لَهُمُ ٱلْخِيَرَةُ**

"It is not — it has never been — for a believing man or a believing woman... that they should have *al-khiyarah* in their affair."

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Allah is not saying, "Believers, don't claim choice when I decide." He is saying something much more radical: *the very category of 'believer who claims choice when Allah has decided' does not exist.*

It is a logical impossibility. A square circle. To be a believer is *already* to have surrendered that claim. The moment you reclaim it, you have not broken a rule — you have stepped outside the definition.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Because most of us — and I include myself — when we hear "submission," we picture *deciding to obey.* We picture a moment of choice, where we lay down our preference and pick up Allah's command. As if islām is an ongoing decision we make again and again.

And it is, in one sense. But this ayah is reaching beneath that.

This ayah is saying: at the level where you actually *are* a believer — not the level where you behave like one, but the level where the word *believer* is true of you — there is no faculty left in you that contests Allah's decision. The faculty has been *given away.* That's what it meant to enter into iman in the first place.

The Arabic construction is not describing a behavior to adopt. It is describing a state to inhabit.

And here is the linguistic precision: Allah does not say *lā yanbaghī* (it is not appropriate) or *lā yajūz* (it is not permitted). He says *mā kāna* — *it has never been the case, in the order of being itself, that this is so.*

This is metaphysics expressed as grammar.

### Element Two: *Al-Khiyarah* — Not "A Choice," But *The* Choice

Now look at the second half of the construction: **أَن يَكُونَ لَهُمُ ٱلْخِيَرَةُ مِنْ أَمْرِهِمْ**

"...that there should be, for them, *al-khiyarah* concerning their affair."

The word is *al-khiyarah* — الْخِيَرَةُ — from the root **kh-y-r**, which is also the root of *khayr* (goodness), *ikhtiyār* (choosing, selecting), *takhayyur* (preferring).

But notice three features.

**First**, the word here is not *ikhtiyār*, the verbal noun for the *act* of choosing. It is *al-khiyarah* — a different morphological form, weightier, more substantive. *Al-khiyarah* is closer to *the prerogative of choice*. The standing right to select. Not the act, but the *authority* behind the act.

**Second**, it is definite — *al-khiyarah*, with the *al-* of definition. Not *a* choice. *The* choice. The faculty itself. The whole thing.

**Third**, it is followed by **مِنْ أَمْرِهِمْ** — *min amrihim* — "from / regarding their affair." The *min* here is partitive but, in context, comprehensive. It marks the entire domain in which they might have wanted to assert preference.

Put those together and what Allah is removing from the believer is not the right to express a preference. Not the right to feel an inclination. Not the right to have an opinion.

What He is removing is **the standing prerogative to determine the outcome.**

[PAUSE]

This is exquisite. Think about why this distinction matters.

Zaynab, when the proposal came, could feel resistance. That feeling was not sin. Zayd, in his own marriage, could feel difficulty. That feeling was not sin. The Prophet ﷺ himself, in many decisions Allah commanded, had visible emotional responses — grief, hesitation, weight.

The ayah is not asking the believer to be a hollow puppet who feels nothing. The Arabic is too precise for that.

What is being removed is *al-khiyarah*. The *standing*. The position of one who decides. The throne, if you will, of personal sovereignty over the matter.

You may feel. You may struggle. You may weep. You may not understand. But the *seat of decision* — the throne on which the verdict is rendered — that seat is not yours.

It was never yours. *Mā kāna lakum.*

[PAUSE]

And here is where the Arabic does something quietly devastating. Look at the word *amrihim* — "their affair." Allah is calling it *their* affair. The matter being decided concerns them — their marriage, their household, their life. By every worldly logic, it is *their* business.

But Allah has just said: even though it is *their* affair, the prerogative of choice in it is *not theirs.*

[PAUSE]

Read that again. *Even in your own affair* — the affair where every framework of modernity would tell you *"this is your call, this is your life, this is your body, this is your decision"* — even there, when Allah and His Messenger have decided, *al-khiyarah is not yours.*

Why? Because the moment you became a believer, you handed Allah a key. And the key was the key to *al-khiyarah*. Not the key to your feelings. Not the key to your preferences. The key to your *standing as the final decider.*

That key is what *islām* means. Not the act of obedience — the *transfer of jurisdiction*.

### Element Three: The Pairing — *Mu'min wa Mu'minah*

Now I want to take you to the most quietly radical feature of this ayah.

In Arabic, the default convention is that the masculine plural — or the masculine singular used generically — covers both men and women. If Allah had said simply *wa mā kāna li-mu'minin* — "it is not for a believing man" — every Arabic-speaker, then and now, would have understood this to apply equally to women.

There is no grammatical necessity to mention the woman separately. The masculine, in classical Arabic, is the inclusive default.

And yet here, Allah does not use the default. He pauses the sentence and says: **وَمَا كَانَ لِمُؤْمِنٍ وَلَا مُؤْمِنَةٍ**

Notice the second *lā*. *Wa lā mu'minatin*. "And not for a believing woman."

That extra particle is not grammatically required. It is rhetorically chosen.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this.

In a moment, this surah is going to tell the story of Zaynab. A woman. A specific woman, who is going to receive a specific decree. And the surah is going to ask the entire community to accept what Allah has decided for her — not just her, but the principle of how marriages and lineage and adoption work in Islam.

If Allah had said only *mu'min*, "believing man," a listener could quietly think: *yes, of course men should submit, but Zaynab's resistance was understandable, she was the one being asked to marry someone she didn't want.*

By saying *mu'min wa mu'minah*, Allah closes that door. He is saying: the principle of *al-khiyarah* belonging to Allah and not to you — this is not gendered. The woman, in her own marriage, in her own affair, in her own body — she too has surrendered *al-khiyarah*. The same standing. The same surrender.

[PAUSE]

But here is what's stunning. This same gesture — naming the woman explicitly — *also lifts her up.*

In a culture where women's spiritual standing was contested, where it was a live question whether women even had souls in some neighboring traditions — Allah pairs her with the man in the exact same construction, with the exact same standing before Him, with the exact same *level of accountability and dignity.*

Notice the strange beauty of this. The pairing simultaneously:
1. **Closes** the loophole that would let the woman think "this principle is for men."
2. **Opens** the door of equal address — *she* is being spoken to, directly, by the Lord of the worlds.

She is not collateral to a male addressee. She is *a co-equal addressee*.

And the verb of the surrender — that her *al-khiyarah* is gone — applies to her with the same weight as it applies to him.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider: in the moment Allah is asking for the deepest surrender of personal sovereignty that any community has ever been asked for, He simultaneously dignifies the woman as a full spiritual agent. The two moves are not in tension. They are the same move.

She is asked to surrender because she has something to surrender. She has *standing*. And that standing is what is being placed at Allah's feet.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So look at what we have.

We have *mā kāna* — a construction that doesn't prohibit but *unthinks* a possibility. A believer who claims *al-khiyarah* against Allah's decree is not breaking a rule; that combination of words is grammatically incoherent in the world Allah is describing.

We have *al-khiyarah* — not "a choice" but "the standing prerogative" — the throne of final say.

And we have *mu'min wa mu'minah* — the deliberate naming of both, so that no one can think this surrender is for the other gender, the other category, the other person.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Death of the Throne

There is a concept in modern psychology — let's call it *the locus of evaluation*. The therapist Carl Rogers wrote about it. The question of *where, inside a person, does the final judgment about their life live?*

A young person grows up looking outward for that locus. *Did Mom approve? Did Dad? Does my teacher think I did well?* The locus of evaluation is outside.

Adolescence and adulthood, in the modern frame, are about *internalizing* that locus. Bringing the throne of judgment inside yourself. *I decide what's right for me. I am the author of my life. My truth is mine to define.*

This is, in many ways, the foundational story of the modern self. The whole architecture of contemporary identity rests on it. You are sovereign. You are the final court of appeal in your own affairs. Anyone who would take that from you is an oppressor.

And it is the most exhausting throne ever built.

[PAUSE]

I want you to think about someone you know — or maybe yourself — who is exhausted by being the final decider. The one for whom every choice carries the weight of *what if I'm wrong, what if this isn't right for me, what if I'm missing the better path.* The one who lies awake at 2 a.m. running scenarios because no matter how the decision goes, the burden of having made it sits squarely on their shoulders.

This is what it feels like to sit on the throne of *al-khiyarah*.

Now hear Allah, in this ayah, doing something that the modern world would call oppression — and that the believer's heart, properly tuned, will hear as a *release.*

He is taking the throne away.

Not the throne of feeling. You can still feel. Not the throne of preference. You can still prefer. Not the throne of asking, of consulting, of weighing.

He is taking the throne of *final say.*

[PAUSE]

Imagine the relief of this. Truly imagine it.

You wake up in the morning. Today there are a hundred small decisions. And then there are two or three that touch on what Allah has clearly decided — *salah on time, honesty in business, kindness to parents, restraint of the tongue.*

For all the others, you exercise your prudence, your *ikhtiyār*, your judgment. You ask Allah to bless what you choose.

But for those two or three — the ones where Allah has spoken — there is no judgment to render. No throne to sit on. *Al-khiyarah* is not yours, and you are *free.*

The believer is not someone who has lost their freedom. The believer is someone who has been *relieved of the impossible job of being God.*

That is what this ayah is doing. It is taking the impossible job out of your hands.

[PAUSE]

But here is the human door. Most of us, when we read this ayah, do not feel relief. We feel resistance.

Why?

Because we are still, somewhere inside, attached to the throne. Even when the throne is killing us. Even when sitting on it has us exhausted, anxious, second-guessing every decision. We still want to *hold the key.* We tell ourselves we'd give it up — but only to a God who would do exactly what we'd do if we were Him.

That is not islām. That is the throne wearing a mask of submission.

The real test of this ayah is the moment Allah decides *something you would not have decided.* The moment His verdict cuts against your inclination. The Zaynab moment. The marriage you would not have arranged. The hijab you would not have worn. The inheritance you would not have divided. The sin you would not have left.

In that moment, the ayah comes down: *mā kāna li-mu'minin wa lā mu'minah*. It has never been your call.

And the relief on the other side of that surrender — when it actually lands — is the closest thing in this life to the peace of the next.

**Practical application:** Tomorrow, pick one matter — small, specific — where Allah has decided and you have been negotiating. Maybe a prayer you've been delaying. Maybe a word you've been justifying. Maybe a relationship boundary you've been blurring. And simply, quietly, say to yourself: *Al-khiyarah is not mine in this. The throne is not mine here.* And step off it. Notice what happens when you do.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Architecture of Clear Error

The ayah does not end with the principle. It ends with a warning.

**وَمَن يَعْصِ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ فَقَدْ ضَلَّ ضَلَـٰلًۭا مُّبِينًۭا**

*"And whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger has certainly strayed into clear error."*

Look at the verb: *ḍalla* — strayed, gone off course. From the root **ḍ-l-l**, which carries the image of *losing the path*, *wandering*, *being unable to find one's way home*.

And the modifier: *ḍalālan mubīnan* — *a clear straying*. *Mubīn* from **b-y-n**, meaning *clear, evident, distinguished from what surrounds it*.

I want to ask you something. Why this word — *ḍalla*? Why not *ʿaṣā* — disobeyed, again? Why not *kafara* — covered up the truth? Why does Allah end this ayah with the language of *being lost*?

[PAUSE]

Because to disobey Allah's decree, after Allah has spoken, is not just a violation. It is *to walk off the map.*

Here is the analogy. Imagine you are in a vast, trackless desert at night. You have no compass. No stars are visible. There are no landmarks. Every direction looks the same.

And then a guide appears. A guide who *knows the desert.* Who knows where the water is, where the cliffs are, where the way home runs. The guide says: *walk this way.*

If you turn and walk the other way, you have not just *disobeyed* the guide. You have *lost your way.* The disobedience and the lostness are the *same act.*

Allah is telling us, at the end of this ayah, that the believer who claims *al-khiyarah* against the decree of Allah and His Messenger is not just guilty of disobedience. They have *unmoored themselves from the only orientation system that exists.*

[PAUSE]

There is a thought experiment I want you to try.

Take any decision in your life right now where you are uncertain. Let it be small. Now ask: *if I were in a featureless desert at night, who would I trust to point me the right way?* The guide who has crossed this desert ten thousand times? Or my own sense of direction, in a place I have never been before?

The answer is obvious. We would trust the guide.

But somehow, in the spiritual desert — the one we have *also* never crossed — we trust our own sense of direction over the One who designed the desert.

This is *ḍalāl mubīn*. Not a hidden error. Not a subtle drift. A *clear, obvious, evident* straying. Clear to anyone whose vision has not been clouded by attachment to the throne.

[PAUSE]

And look at the structure of the whole ayah now. It is a perfect arc.

- First half: *mā kāna li-mu'minin wa lā mu'minah* — the believer's nature is incompatible with claiming *al-khiyarah* against Allah's decree.
- Second half: *wa man yaʿṣi Allāha wa rasūlahu fa-qad ḍalla ḍalālan mubīnan* — whoever does it has clearly lost the path.

The first half tells you what it *means* to be a believer. The second half tells you what happens when you act as though that meaning didn't apply. You don't just sin. You step outside the category. You become *one who is lost.*

[PAUSE]

Notice one more thing. The pairing of "Allah and His Messenger" appears twice. *Allah and His Messenger decided.* *Whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger.*

In a moment when the community might be tempted to think *"this is just the Prophet's preference, this is a human matter, Allah didn't really speak,"* the ayah refuses the separation. The decree of the Messenger ﷺ in matters of revelation is the decree of Allah. You cannot pick one to obey and the other to negotiate with.

This is what makes the *ḍalāl* so *mubīn* — so obvious. Because the believer has been told plainly: *these two are joined.* To slip between them and obey one while contesting the other is not nuance. It is a clear losing of the way.

**Practical application:** When you find yourself wanting to obey the parts of revelation that match your preferences and renegotiate the parts that don't — recognize that move as the throne reasserting itself. The unified address — *Allah and His Messenger* — is precisely designed to close that escape route. Ask yourself: *am I receiving the decree, or am I shopping it?*

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Look at the ayah one more time and see what Allah has built.

**Opening clause:** *Wa mā kāna li-mu'minin wa lā mu'minah* — the impossibility of the position itself.

**Conditional middle:** *idhā qaḍā Allāhu wa rasūluhū amran* — the trigger: *when Allah and His Messenger have decided a matter.*

**The thing removed:** *an yakūna lahum al-khiyaratu min amrihim* — the standing prerogative.

**Closing clause:** *wa man yaʿṣi Allāha wa rasūlahu fa-qad ḍalla ḍalālan mubīnan* — the consequence: not just sin, but lostness.

The opening and closing mirror each other. The opening says: *to claim al-khiyarah is incompatible with being a believer.* The closing says: *to act on that claim is to walk off the map.*

These are the same insight from two directions. The first is structural — *you cannot do this and be what you are.* The second is consequential — *if you try, you will be lost.*

And right at the center, surrounded by the two halves of the structure, sits the moment of decision: *idhā qaḍā Allāhu wa rasūluhu amran.* The moment when Allah speaks. The moment the throne must be vacated.

The architecture itself is teaching you: this moment is the hinge of who you are.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The linguistic side showed us: *mā kāna* is not a prohibition but a declaration of incompatibility. *Al-khiyarah* is not "a choice" but the standing prerogative. *Mu'min wa mu'minah* names both genders explicitly, refusing any loophole.

The thematic side showed us: the throne of final decision is the most exhausting throne ever built, and Allah, in His mercy, is *relieving* the believer of it. And: to refuse that relief is not just disobedience — it is to lose the only map there is.

Both sides converge on one truth. To be a believer is to have transferred jurisdiction. Not to have stopped feeling. Not to have stopped preferring. But to have *handed the gavel* — the final ruling — to the only One whose ruling is wisdom itself.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the matter in your life right now where Allah has clearly decided and you are still negotiating? Not breaking — *negotiating.* Reframing. Postponing. Reinterpreting until it fits your preference. What would it cost to stop?

2. When you imagine "submission" to Allah, do you imagine grim duty — or do you imagine *relief*? If grim duty, what does that tell you about which throne you are still sitting on?

3. Is there a place where you have made *al-khiyarah* yours in someone else's life — a spouse, a child, a sibling — and pushed your preference against Allah's clear word? What does it mean that *their* surrender is between them and Allah, not between them and you?

4. The ayah pairs *Allah and His Messenger* twice. Where in your engagement with Islam do you find yourself splitting them — accepting the Quran while bracketing the Sunnah, or accepting principles while shopping the applications? What is the throne behind that split?

5. The word *ḍalla* — "strayed" — does not mean *broke a rule.* It means *got lost.* When you imagine the consequence of refusing Allah's clear decree, do you imagine it as punishment, or as *no longer knowing where you are*? Which framing better tracks the ayah?

### One-Sentence Distillation

To be a believer is to have already handed Allah the throne of final decision in your own affairs — and to take it back, when He has clearly spoken, is not to break a rule but to step outside the very definition of the one you claim to be.

### The Closing Invitation

If something in you has resisted this ayah while you have been reading — sit with that resistance. Don't argue with it. Don't shame it. Just notice where it lives.

Because that resistance is the throne. It is the part of you that still wants to be the final decider, even after declaring *lā ilāha illa Allāh* — *there is no deity but Allah.*

And the gentle invitation of this ayah is not to crush that part of yourself. It is to *show it where it has been hiding.* So that, one decision at a time, one decree at a time, you can place it where it has always belonged.

At the feet of the One who designed the desert. The One who knows the way home.

### Du'a

*O Allah, You who have decreed and whose decree is wisdom itself — relieve me of the impossible throne of being the final decider in my own affair. Teach me to feel without claiming, to prefer without grasping, to walk through this world with my hands open to whatever You have placed in them. And when Your decree cuts against my inclination, do not let me mistake the cut for cruelty — let me recognize it as the touch of the only One who knows the way home. Āmīn.*
