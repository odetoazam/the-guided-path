# Two Gardens, One Command

## The Sign That Was Sabaʾ — Surah 34, Ayah 15

### The Ayah

**Arabic:**
> لَقَدْ كَانَ لِسَبَإٍ فِى مَسْكَنِهِمْ ءَايَةٌ ۖ جَنَّتَانِ عَن يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ ۖ كُلُوا۟ مِن رِّزْقِ رَبِّكُمْ وَٱشْكُرُوا۟ لَهُۥ ۚ بَلْدَةٌ طَيِّبَةٌ وَرَبٌّ غَفُورٌ

**Transliteration:**
*La-qad kāna li-Sabaʾin fī maskanihim āyah; jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl; kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lah; baldatun ṭayyibatun wa-rabbun ghafūr.*

**Translation:**
*Certainly there was for Sabaʾ in their dwelling place a sign: two gardens on the right and on the left. "Eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him." A good land, and a Forgiving Lord.*

---

## Rebuilding the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to walk into Sabaʾ.

Sabaʾ was not a metaphor. It was a real kingdom, in what is now Yemen, that flourished for a thousand years before the time of the Prophet ﷺ. They built one of the engineering wonders of the ancient world — the Maʾrib Dam — a massive structure that captured the seasonal monsoon waters pouring down the mountain valleys and distributed them across the plain through an intricate canal system.

The result was something almost mythological. Travellers' accounts speak of a land so fertile, so densely cultivated, that you could walk for days under a continuous canopy of fruit trees. Classical commentators preserve descriptions of a woman walking through Sabaʾ with a basket on her head — and by the end of her journey, the basket would be full of fruit that had simply fallen into it from the branches above. She didn't pick anything. Gravity did the harvesting.

Al-Jalalayn preserves a striking detail from the tradition about this land: no marshes, no mosquitoes, no flies, no fleas, no scorpions — *ليس فيها سباخ ولا بعوضة ولا ذبابة ولا برغوث ولا عقرب*. Some classical narrations add: a stranger could walk into Sabaʾ with lice in his clothes — and the lice would die from the purity of the air. This detail circulates in the tafsir tradition though its chain is weak; the al-Jalalayn text itself stops at the scorpion.

[PAUSE]

Hold that picture in your mind. Not because the historical details matter for their own sake — but because **the listener of the Quran is being shown what a divinely-arranged life looks like at its peak.** Climate, soil, water, harvest, even the absence of pests — every variable in the equation, tuned in their favor.

And this is the moment Allah chooses to speak. Not when they are suffering. Not when they are crying out. **When they have everything.**

The strand running through this surah is a quiet comparison. A few ayahs earlier, we met Dāwūd and Sulaymān — given mountains that praised with them, iron that softened in their hands, wind that carried them, jinn that built for them. And how were they introduced? *"Work, O family of Dāwūd, in gratitude — and few of My servants are grateful"* (34:13). Abundance, met with gratitude.

Now Allah turns to Sabaʾ. Also given abundance. Also given a sign in their very dwelling place. And the surah is about to show us what they did with it.

This ayah is the doorway. **The hook is this:** in twenty Arabic words, Allah will name what Sabaʾ was given, give them the entire prescription for how a human being is supposed to relate to abundance, and end with two words about Himself that are so tender they almost break the symmetry of the verse.

Twenty words. We will spend an hour with them.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Āyah* — When the Map Becomes the Sign

Let's start with a question.

When you walk into a beautiful place — a valley, a coastline, a forest that takes your breath away — what is the first thing your mind does? It tries to *receive* the beauty. To take it in. To photograph it, maybe, or to remember it.

But there is a second thing the mind can do, which most of us rarely reach. It can ask: *what is this pointing toward?* The beauty is not the destination. It is a sign. Of what?

This second motion is what the Quran calls **the recognition of an *āyah*.**

In Arabic, *āyah* (ءَايَة) — from the root *Ayy* — means a sign, a marker, an indicator. It is the same word the Quran uses for its own verses. Every verse is an *āyah* — a sign — and every sign is a verse. The world and the Book are made of the same kind of thing.

But notice something extraordinary about the construction here. Allah doesn't say: *"in their lives there was a sign,"* or *"in their wealth there was a sign,"* or *"in their kingdom there was a sign."*

He says: **فِى مَسْكَنِهِمْ — *in their dwelling place* — there was a sign.**

The root of *maskan* is *s-k-n*, which is also the root of *sakīnah* — stillness, tranquility, the place where the heart rests. A *maskan* is not just a house. It is the place where you settle. The place where you stop moving. The place that, for most of us, becomes so familiar that we stop *seeing* it.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Allah is locating the sign **precisely in the place where the human eye stops looking.**

Think about your own home. The walk from your bedroom to the kitchen. The view from your window. The faces of the people you live with. There was a day, when you first moved in or when you first met them, that you noticed everything. The light through the curtain. The particular way someone laughed. Now? Most of it has disappeared into the background. It has become *maskan*. It has become the place where you have stopped seeing.

And this is exactly where Allah says the sign was for Sabaʾ. **In their maskan.** In the most ordinary, most settled, most invisible part of their lives.

The implication is staggering. The greatest sign was not on a mountaintop. Not in a thunderclap. Not in a miraculous event that interrupted their routine. It was woven into the wallpaper of their daily existence. It was the thing they walked past every morning.

And the question this puts to us — the question that hangs in the air after we read this ayah — is: **what āyahs has Allah placed in our maskan that we have stopped seeing?**

The face we see every day. The body that has carried us this long. The pillow we wake up on. The light that comes through the window without our permission. These are not background. **These are the text of the message.**

[PAUSE — long]

### Element Two: *Jannatāni ʿan Yamīnin wa-Shimāl* — The Dual That Encloses

Now look at how Allah unfolds what the sign was: **جَنَّتَانِ عَن يَمِينٍ وَشِمَالٍ — two gardens, on the right and on the left.**

I want to teach you something about Arabic before we go further. Arabic has three numerical forms: singular, dual, and plural. English collapses dual into plural — we just say "gardens." But Arabic has a specific form for *exactly two*: the suffix *-āni*. *Jannah* is one garden. *Jannāt* is many gardens. *Jannatāni* is **specifically two.**

This is not a poetic flourish. The dual form is precise. Allah is telling us: not many gardens, not one garden — *two.*

Why two? And why on the right and on the left?

The commentators tell us the historical reality matched this exactly: the Sabaʾ civilization was built around a valley, and the cultivated land lay in two great expanses, one to the right of the valley and one to the left, irrigated by the Maʾrib dam in the middle.

But notice what this geometry does to the human standing in the middle.

[PAUSE]

If you are standing in Sabaʾ, in your maskan, **wherever you turn, you face a garden.** Look right — garden. Look left — garden. You are not adjacent to the blessing. You are not at the edge of it. **You are surrounded by it.**

The dual form here is doing something the singular could never do. A single garden would mean: there is a blessed place, and you can visit it. Many gardens would mean: there are scattered blessings out in the world. But *jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl* means: **the blessing is the shape of the room you live in.**

This is the architecture of being held.

And here is what is remarkable. In modern psychology, we now have a name for what happens to a person who is surrounded by something for a long time: **habituation.** The brain, by design, stops processing constant stimuli. If a sound never changes, you stop hearing it. If a smell is constant, you stop smelling it. If a blessing is always there, you stop seeing it as a blessing.

The very thing that should have made gratitude inevitable — the constant presence of beauty on both sides — was the thing that made gratitude difficult. **The blessing's totality was its disguise.**

[PAUSE]

And now read the ayah again, with this in mind. Allah says: *in their maskan — the place where they had stopped looking — there was an āyah — a sign — two gardens, on the right and on the left.*

Every word is pressing on the same nerve. **The sign was everywhere. Which is why they couldn't see it.**

### Element Three: *Kulū... wa-Shkurū* — Two Commands That Are One

Now we come to the moment in the ayah where the grammar shifts. Allah has been describing — *there was a sign, two gardens.* And suddenly the voice turns. He addresses them directly:

**كُلُوا۟ مِن رِّزْقِ رَبِّكُمْ وَٱشْكُرُوا۟ لَهُۥ**
*Eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him.*

Two imperative verbs. *Kulū* — eat. *Ushkurū* — be grateful. The Leeds morphology confirms both are imperatives, commands. And they are joined by a single *wāw* — "and."

Now let me teach you something about Arabic rhetoric. When two commands are joined by a *wāw* like this, the rhetorical tradition treats them as **a single integrated act, not two separate tasks.** It's not: do this, then do that. It's: this *is* that.

Think of how we say in English, "wash and dry your hands." We don't mean two events. We mean one completed act of cleaning. The *and* is not separating — it is binding.

When Allah says *kulū wa-shkurū* — eat and be grateful — He is not giving us two assignments. **He is defining what eating actually is, when done correctly.**

[PAUSE]

To eat without gratitude is, in the Quranic vision, to do half an act. To consume the rizq and miss the *rabb* who placed it before you. To take the gift and forget the giver. The mouth received, but the heart never registered the transaction.

And notice the deliberate phrasing: **مِن رِّزْقِ رَبِّكُمْ — from the provision of *your Lord*.**

Not *from the gardens.* Not *from the earth.* Not *from your harvest.* From the *rizq* of *rabbikum.* The grammar yanks the eye off the visible producer — the soil, the irrigation, the fruit — and points to the invisible source. *Your Lord.*

Because here is what habituation does to a heart over generations. The first generation in Sabaʾ knew: this is from Allah. The second generation knew it intellectually. By the fifth generation, the gardens *feel* like they are simply *there.* They are the wallpaper. They became the inheritance. They became *ours.*

And the moment a blessing stops being *His* and starts being *ours*, two things die at once. Gratitude dies. And humility dies.

[PAUSE]

The grammar is performing the correction. *Min rizqi rabbikum.* Trace the food backward. Through the fruit. Through the branch. Through the root. Through the soil. Through the rain. Through the cloud. Through the wind that brought the cloud. Through the heat that raised the moisture. Through the sun. **All the way back to the One who said *kun*.**

Eating is a chain of dependencies. Gratitude is what happens when you walk the chain consciously.

### Bridge

So we have three linguistic discoveries. *Āyah in maskan* — the sign placed precisely where the eye stops looking. *Jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl* — the dual form that places the human inside the blessing rather than beside it. *Kulū wa-shkurū* — eating and gratitude as one integrated act, with the source named to defeat the illusion that blessings are self-generating.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Invisibility of Constant Blessing

Let me ask you a question. When was the last time you were grateful for your eyes?

Not your vision in general. Not the fact that you can see beautiful things. Your *eyes.* The two organs in your head that you have been using continuously since you woke up this morning, that have been performing tens of millions of micro-adjustments since you started reading this — focal length, pupil dilation, blink reflex, tear coating — without a single instruction from you.

If you have not thought about your eyes today, you are not unusual. **You are Sabaʾ.**

This is the human condition. We are wired to notice change, not constancy. The brain is an efficient predictor — it allocates attention to deviations from baseline. Anything that has been there all along becomes baseline. And baseline, by definition, drops out of awareness.

This is why a person can lose their hearing for a week, regain it, and weep with gratitude — and then within a month, stop hearing the gratitude. The blessing has returned to baseline. It has become *maskan.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is what is staggering about the ayah we are reading. Allah is not addressing people who lost their gardens and got them back. He is addressing people who **never lost them.** The gardens were *always* there. On the right. On the left. Generation after generation.

And He is saying — through the very word *āyah* — *that* is the problem you need to solve. Not loss. **Constancy.**

This is the spiritual difficulty that most religious frameworks barely name. We are taught how to be patient in hardship. We are taught how to be grateful when something good happens. But almost nothing in our spiritual training prepares us for the much subtler challenge of staying awake to a blessing that never goes away.

The Sabaʾ problem is the modern problem. We live in maskan-blessings. Running water. Electricity that doesn't blink. Refrigeration. Antibiotics. The fact that the floor will be there when we put our foot down. The fact that the next breath will come. The fact that the people we love are, right now, still in this world.

How do we wake up to what we never lost?

The ayah gives us the answer in the imperative: ***kulū wa-shkurū.*** Eating itself becomes the practice. Every meal is a built-in opportunity to walk the chain backward — from the food to the *rabb*. The Prophet ﷺ taught us *bismillāh* at the start and *al-ḥamdulillāh* at the end of every meal. That bracket is not ceremonial. **It is the technology of staying awake to constancy.**

Three to five times a day, the body forces you to confront a *rizq* you did not create. The dhikr around the meal is Allah's way of inserting an alarm clock into the baseline. If you remembered Him at every meal, you would remember Him at the times of greatest forgetting — the times of greatest *ease.*

This is what changes tomorrow. Pick one constant blessing — your eyes, your bed, the floor, the air — and before you use it, name it as His. Just once. Just to break the wallpaper. To say: *this was placed here. It was not generated by my expectations.*

### Theme Two: The Tenderness at the End of the Ayah

Now I want you to look at how this ayah ends. Because it does something I want you to feel before I explain it.

**بَلْدَةٌ طَيِّبَةٌ وَرَبٌّ غَفُورٌ**
*A good land, and a Forgiving Lord.*

Four words. Two pairs. And in those four words, the entire emotional posture of the ayah turns.

The first part of the ayah was descriptive and instructional. *There was a sign. Two gardens. Eat. Be grateful.* The voice has been steady, almost stately. Then suddenly — at the end — the ayah collapses into something that reads almost like a sigh. Like Allah pausing and saying, quietly: *baldatun ṭayyibatun wa-rabbun ghafūr.*

A good land. And a Forgiving Lord.

Notice what is *not* in those words. There is no verb. There is no command. There is no warning. There are just two beautiful realities placed next to each other, **almost as if Allah is whispering to them the deal they are being offered.**

[PAUSE]

Look at the symmetry. *Baldah ṭayyibah* — a good land. *Rabb ghafūr* — a Forgiving Lord. The land and the Lord. Pair them. They are the two coordinates of the entire human situation. **Where you live, and Who owns it.**

But notice the asymmetry inside the symmetry. The land is described as *ṭayyibah* — good, pure, wholesome. A neutral, beautiful adjective. The Lord is described as *ghafūr* — Forgiving.

Why *ghafūr* here? They have been given a good land, they have been told to eat and be grateful — why does this ayah end with *forgiveness*?

The classical commentators noticed this and they sat with it. *Al-Qurtubi* preserves the discussion. Because the surrounding context is about people who will, in the very next ayah, **turn away.** Allah is naming Himself *ghafūr* at the exact moment He is delivering them the abundance they will fail to honor.

In other words: **He is telling them, before they fall, that the door is open.**

[PAUSE]

Let that land.

This is not a Lord standing with arms crossed, watching to see if they will earn the gardens. This is not a Lord delivering an ultimatum. This is a Lord who, in the same breath that He gives them the blessing and asks for their gratitude, *already names His forgiveness.* As if to say: I know what is coming. I know that the human heart, when surrounded by constant blessing, forgets. **And I am telling you now — *while you still have it* — that I am ghafūr.**

The placement of *ghafūr* at the end of this ayah is one of the most tender things in the Quran. It is grace built into the foundation of the warning. It is the safety net stitched into the trapeze before the act begins.

And here is what is heartbreaking. The next ayah — 34:16 — opens with the Arabic phrase *fa-aʿraḍū* — *but they turned away.* They received the gardens. They received the command to be grateful. They received the news that their Lord was *ghafūr.* And they turned away.

[PAUSE]

But — and this is what the ayah teaches us — *the door He named was still there.* The *ghafūr* He pre-announced did not retract when they turned. It was waiting. It is the *ghafūr* of every human being who has ever lived in unexamined abundance and woken up too late.

Which is to say: **it is the *ghafūr* available to us right now.**

Because we are living, every one of us, in some version of Sabaʾ. We have gardens we have stopped seeing. We have *rizq* we have stopped tracing. We have *baldah ṭayyibah* — the good land of our lives — without daily wonder. And the ayah is telling us, before the warning ever reaches us: **your Lord is still ghafūr.**

The path back is not closed. The asking has not expired. The fact that we have neglected the *kulū wa-shkurū* for years — that itself is what *ghafūr* is for.

What does this change about tomorrow? It removes the most common excuse for not turning back: *it is too late, I have squandered too much.* The ending of this ayah is Allah's preemptive refutation of that excuse. He named His forgiveness **before** they failed. Which means it was never contingent on their performance. It was the structure of the relationship itself.

You do not need to deserve the return. You only need to begin it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found in twenty words.

The linguistic journey showed us a sign placed in the most ordinary part of life (*āyah fī maskanihim*), a blessing structured to surround rather than confront us (*jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa-shimāl*), and a command that fuses consumption with consciousness (*kulū wa-shkurū min rizqi rabbikum*).

The thematic journey showed us that the deepest spiritual difficulty is not loss but constancy — and that even when constancy lulls us to sleep, the ayah ends on the breath of a Lord who pre-announces His forgiveness.

These are the same insight from two directions. **The sign is everywhere. The forgetting is built into us. And the forgiveness is built into Him.**

Sabaʾ is not a story about a far-away kingdom. It is a mirror. The gardens are still there. The command is still standing. And the *rabb ghafūr* is still ghafūr.

### The Architecture

Look at the shape of the ayah now:

> *There was for Sabaʾ in their dwelling place a sign:*
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*two gardens — on the right and on the left.*
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*"Eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful to Him."*
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*A good land — and a Forgiving Lord.*

Notice the chiastic shape. The outer frame is description — *sign* and *good land.* The middle is the gardens and the Lord. And at the absolute center is the imperative: *eat and be grateful.*

The blessing is placed on both sides of the command — gardens framing the call. **The verbal structure mirrors the geographical structure.** Just as Sabaʾ was surrounded on the right and the left by the gardens, the command to eat and give thanks is surrounded on the right and the left by the proof of why it is owed.

Allah did not just describe their world. **He built the ayah in the shape of their world.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life has become *maskan* — so settled, so familiar that I no longer see it as a sign?

2. When I eat today, will I do half the act, or the whole act?

3. What blessing surrounds me so completely that its totality has become its disguise?

4. If Allah named Himself *ghafūr* before Sabaʾ turned away — what is He naming Himself before I turn back?

5. What is the chain of dependencies behind my next breath, and have I ever walked it all the way back?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The sign is in the place where you have stopped looking, the command is woven into the meal you are about to eat, and the door of return was opened before you ever needed it.**

### The Closing Invitation

Pick one ordinary thing today. The water from the tap. The bread on the counter. The face across the table. Stop, for ten seconds, and trace it backward to the *rabb* who placed it there.

You are not adding a religious ritual to your day. **You are doing what eating was supposed to be in the first place.**

### Du'a

O Allah, You who placed signs in our dwellings and forgiveness at the end of every warning — wake us up to what we have stopped seeing. Let our eating be remembering, let our breathing be witnessing, and when we have forgotten, meet us — as You met Sabaʾ — with the door of *ghafūr* still open. Make us of *the few* of Your servants who are grateful. Āmīn.
