# He Knows What Penetrates — and He Names Himself Merciful

## Introduction

[ayah:34:2] يَعْلَمُ مَا يَلِجُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا يَخْرُجُ مِنْهَا وَمَا يَنزِلُ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَمَا يَعْرُجُ فِيهَا ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلرَّحِيمُ ٱلْغَفُورُ

*Ya'lamu mā yaliju fī'l-arḍi wa mā yakhruju minhā wa mā yanzilu mina's-samā'i wa mā ya'ruju fīhā, wa huwa'r-Raḥīmu'l-Ghafūr.*

*"He knows what penetrates into the earth and what comes out from it, and what descends from the heaven and what ascends therein. And He is the Most Merciful, the Oft-Forgiving."*

### Where We Are Standing

Surah Saba is a Makkan surah. The setting is a community of skeptics — Quraysh and others — who could not accept the resurrection. Their core argument, which the surah will quote in ayah 7, was this:

*"Shall we point you to a man who tells you that when you have been completely torn apart, you will be created anew?"*

In other words: how can a God reassemble dust that has been scattered for centuries? How can He find a body whose particles have soaked into rivers, been eaten by worms, dissolved into earth?

The first ayah of the surah opens with hamd — praise — and establishes Allah as Owner of the heavens and the earth. *Lahu'l-ḥamdu fī'l-ākhirah* — and to Him be praise in the next life too. The skeptics will be answered in the next life whether they accept the answer now or not.

Then comes our ayah. Before any direct argument about resurrection, before any rebuke to the deniers, Allah simply states a fact about Himself: **He knows what goes into the earth.** He knows what comes out. He knows what falls from the sky. He knows what rises back up.

This is not a poetic flourish. This is the foundation of the surah's entire argument. If He knows the location of every speck — every drop of water that seeped down, every seed that pushed up, every drop of rain that fell, every prayer that rose — then how could anyone hide their scattered atoms from Him?

### The Hook

There is something strange about the end of this ayah. After listing four kinds of all-encompassing knowledge — down into the earth, up out of it, down from heaven, up into heaven — we expect Allah to seal the statement with names that match knowledge. *Al-'Alīm. Al-Khabīr. Al-Baṣīr.* The Knowing. The Aware. The All-Seeing.

But that is not what happens.

After this exhaustive surveillance — He knows *everything* that moves anywhere in His creation — Allah names Himself **al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr.** The Most Merciful. The Oft-Forgiving.

Why?

Stay with that question. Hold it. Because the answer to that single asymmetry will reframe how you have ever thought about being seen.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

There are three things in this ayah that we must open carefully. A single verb. A pattern of motion. And an absence — a missing word where you expected one.

### Element One: The Word *Yaliju* — To Thread Through a Narrow Opening

Before we touch this verb, let me give you something to compare it to.

In English, there are different words for "to enter." You can *enter* a room — that is just walking in through an open door. You can *invade* — that is forcing your way against resistance. You can *infiltrate* — that is sneaking in through tiny gaps where you weren't expected. You can *penetrate* — that is pushing through dense matter. And you can *thread* — that is fitting through an impossibly narrow space.

These all live in different categories. Arabic does the same thing — but more precisely.

Now look at the verb Allah chose: **يَلِجُ — *yaliju*.**

The root is **و-ل-ج (w-l-j).** It is not the generic verb for "to enter" — that would be *dakhala*. This root carries a specific texture: to enter through a *narrow* opening. To thread. To slip into something tight. To infiltrate.

You may have heard this root before, in Surah Al-A'raf, ayah 40, where Allah describes the disbelievers entering paradise:

*"They will not enter paradise until the camel threads through the eye of the needle — حَتَّىٰ يَلِجَ ٱلْجَمَلُ فِى سَمِّ ٱلْخِيَاطِ."*

That verb — *yalija* — same root, same texture. Threading a camel through the eye of a needle. That is what *waliyaja* means. To enter through an impossibly narrow opening.

So when Allah says, in our ayah, **يَعْلَمُ مَا يَلِجُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** — He is not saying "He knows what enters the earth." He is saying something far more intimate.

He knows what **threads** into the earth.

He knows what infiltrates the tightest crevices.

He knows what penetrates the densest matter.

Notice something. The classical commentators list examples of what is intended. Al-Baghawi says: "from water, and the dead." Al-Qurtubi says: "from drops of water, and treasures, and buried things, and the dead." Al-Muyassar says: "every drop of water that enters the earth, every plant and mineral and water source." These are reading the most obvious applications.

But the verb itself points further. The drops of water that *thread* through compressed soil. The roots that *infiltrate* between grains. The body that, over centuries, *slips* atom by atom into different vessels — eaten by worms, taken up by plants, carried by rivers, redistributed across continents.

Allah knows where every thread went.

Stay with me, because *yaliju* is not only physical.

Think about the things that thread into your own self. The thought you didn't invite. The whisper that slid in through a gap in your attention. The doubt that found a tiny crack in your certainty and made it bigger. The intention that you can barely admit to yourself. The half-formed worry that you wouldn't say out loud.

These also *thread* in.

The Quran will later say, in Surah Qaf:

*"We know what his soul whispers to him — وَنَعْلَمُ مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِ نَفْسُهُ."*

That is *waliyaja* applied to the inner life. The thing that *threads* into the heart through the narrowest opening — Allah knows it.

[PAUSE]

This is what the verb is doing. It is not a list of obvious things that enter visible earth. It is the claim that **whatever can find a tiny gap to slip through anywhere in creation — He knows it.**

That includes the gaps in you.

### Element Two: The Architecture of Motion — Four Directions, One Frame

Now look at the structure of the whole ayah. There are four verbs. All four are imperfect tense — *mudāri'* — meaning continuous, ongoing, never-ceasing. Not "what entered" past tense. Not "what will enter" future tense. **What enters, what exits, what descends, what ascends — now, this instant, and every instant.**

Watch the directions:

1. **يَلِجُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** — threads INTO the earth (going down)
2. **يَخْرُجُ مِنْهَا** — comes OUT from it (going up)
3. **يَنزِلُ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ** — descends FROM the heaven (going down)
4. **يَعْرُجُ فِيهَا** — ascends INTO it (going up)

The pattern is: down, up, down, up.

But notice something else. The first two are about the earth. The second two are about the heaven. So the structure is also: **earth-pair, heaven-pair.**

And inside each pair, there is a complete cycle. Things go into the earth and things come out. Things come down from the sky and things go back up. **No motion in creation falls outside these four categories.**

This is what classical Arabic rhetoric calls *istiqsā'* — exhaustion of categories. Allah is not listing specific items. He is mapping every possible directionality. He is saying: name a motion, and it belongs to one of these four. There is nowhere for anything to move that He hasn't accounted for.

Now let's get more specific about each direction.

**What threads into the earth.** Water seeping. Seeds burrowing. Bodies dissolving. Treasure buried. Roots infiltrating. *And* — secrets confided to graves. Tears soaked into prayer mats. Words spoken into the dust during sujūd. The earth is the receiver of everything we hide.

**What comes out of the earth.** Plants. Springs. Insects. *And* — the dead, when they are gathered. *And* — every word ever spoken into the dust, on the Day when the earth will testify (Surah Az-Zalzala 99:4). The earth is the witness who has been recording.

**What descends from the heaven.** Rain. Sustenance. Angels. Revelation. *And* — every grace, every answered prayer arriving back, every divine decree taking shape in a person's life.

**What ascends into it.** Angels returning. The deeds of human beings. Words. *Ilā Allāhi yaṣ'adu'l-kalimu'ṭ-ṭayyibu wa'l-'amalu'ṣ-ṣāliḥu yarfa'uhu* — "To Him ascend the goodly words, and righteous deeds He lifts up" (35:10).

Al-Baghawi captures this perfectly: *"and what ascends therein — meaning the angels and the deeds of the servants."*

So look at what this architecture creates.

You are standing in the middle. Below you is earth. Above you is sky. And right now, in every direction around you, motion is happening:

Drops of water are threading down into soil somewhere.
Seeds are pushing up out of soil somewhere.
Rain or revelation or a grace is descending from above somewhere.
A prayer or a deed or an angel is rising up somewhere.

**Every one of those movements — every single one — is fully known to Him.**

And this is happening **continuously** because of the imperfect tense. Not a snapshot. A motion picture without a single frame missed.

### Element Three: The Absence — What Allah Did Not Say

Here is the most important linguistic feature of this ayah. And it is something you would miss if you only read the translation.

Look at how the ayah ends:

**وَهُوَ ٱلرَّحِيمُ ٱلْغَفُورُ**

"And He is the Most Merciful, the Oft-Forgiving."

Now ask yourself a question: based on everything that came before this clause, what divine names did you *expect*?

We just heard four claims about Allah's exhaustive, real-time, all-directional knowledge of every motion in creation. What divine names are conventionally paired with that kind of statement throughout the Quran?

Look:

- *"He is the All-Knowing, the All-Aware"* — *al-'Alīm al-Khabīr* (6:73, 67:14)
- *"He is the All-Hearing, the All-Seeing"* — *al-Samī' al-Baṣīr*
- *"He is the All-Knowing, the All-Wise"* — *al-'Alīm al-Ḥakīm*

These are the expected pairings. After demonstrating knowledge, Allah typically names Himself by knowledge-related attributes. **It is the pattern of the entire Quran.**

But not here.

Here, after the most exhaustive knowledge claim He could make — after the listing of every direction motion can take in His creation — Allah names Himself as:

**ٱلرَّحِيمُ** — al-Raḥīm — the One whose mercy specifically reaches the believers.

**ٱلْغَفُورُ** — al-Ghafūr — the One who covers and forgives sins.

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is the asymmetry. This is the place where the ayah breaks its own expected pattern. And in tadabbur, this is where you stop and ask: **why?**

Why, after telling me He sees what I cannot hide, does He not name Himself the Knowing or the Aware? Why, instead, mercy and forgiveness?

Because Allah is anticipating what happens in the heart of the listener who has *truly* heard the previous clauses.

If you have heard them. If you have actually let it sink in that He knows the thought that just threaded through your mind a second ago, the secret you have not told anyone, the intention you have not even admitted to yourself — if you have heard that — then there is only one emotion possible.

Fear.

Specifically: the fear of being seen.

And Allah, in the very same breath, before the listener can spiral, says: **and the One who knows all of that is the One who is merciful, the One who covers.**

This is the asymmetric absence speaking. The missing divine names — al-'Alīm, al-Khabīr — would have completed the rhetorical pattern. But they would have *crushed* the listener. So Allah withholds the expected and gives the unexpected. He names Himself by the very attributes the trembling heart needs.

Bridge: We've seen what *yaliju* does — the threading-in of motion. We've seen the four-direction architecture that maps every possible movement. And we've seen Allah replace the expected divine names with mercy and forgiveness, at exactly the moment when the listener might break under the weight of being known.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about what it actually means to be seen by Him.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Modern Terror of Being Seen — and the Correction

We live in a strange moment in history. Probably the most surveilled moment that has ever existed.

There are cameras in your pocket, on your laptop, on every street corner, in every store. Your messages are stored. Your searches are logged. Your location is tracked. Algorithms learn what you click on at 2am and adjust your feed accordingly. People upload themselves voluntarily, then panic about what they've shown.

The result, psychologically, is something new in human experience: a low-grade, chronic anxiety about being seen.

We have all developed surveillance trauma. We feel watched even when we are not. We perform even when we are alone. We have begun to associate being known with being exposed, being humiliated, being judged, being canceled.

And here is the danger: when we then come to a Quranic ayah like this one — *"He knows what threads into the earth and what comes out, what descends from the sky and what ascends"* — we project the surveillance trauma onto Allah.

We hear: *He is watching me, just like everyone else is watching me, and He is keeping a file, and one day He will publish it.*

That is not what this ayah says. That is what a person damaged by modern surveillance hears when they read this ayah.

[PAUSE]

So watch what Allah does. He gives us the maximally invasive knowledge claim — He knows what threads into the *gaps*, the most hidden places — and then He immediately says: *and He is the Most Merciful, the Oft-Forgiving.*

This is the correction.

Being seen by an algorithm is dangerous, because an algorithm has no mercy.
Being seen by a state is dangerous, because a state has no forgiveness.
Being seen by a stranger online is dangerous, because they do not love you.

**Being seen by Allah is not dangerous in the same way, because the One seeing you is named al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr.**

Al-Qurtubi notes the standard interpretation: *al-Raḥīm bi-'ibādih* — the One whose mercy reaches His servants. *Al-Ghafūr li-dhunūbihim* — the One who forgives their sins. These are not abstract divine attributes hovering somewhere. They are the *response* to the previous knowledge claim.

Imagine someone walks into your private room while you are at your most vulnerable — crying, broken, ashamed. The first kind of person is a journalist with a camera — and you are destroyed. The second kind of person is a stranger who turns away and pretends not to see — and you are grateful but still alone. The third kind of person is a mother. She enters, sees, kneels down, covers you, and forgives whatever it is you are ashamed of — without needing to know the details.

Allah is the third. Except His mercy is infinitely greater than any mother's. The Prophet ﷺ once watched a mother frantically searching for her lost child, and when she found him, she pulled him to her chest and never let him go. He asked his companions: do you think this woman would throw her child into the fire? They said: no, never. He said: *Allah is more merciful to His servants than this mother is to her child.*

So the omniscience claim in this ayah is not a threat. It is the foundation of an intimacy that no human relationship can match. **He sees you completely — and He has chosen to cover you anyway.**

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you pray when you are too ashamed to speak. Because you remember: He already knows. You are not informing Him. You are responding to being already known. Prayer is the act of meeting Him at the place where He already met you.

It changes how you read your own thoughts. The intrusive thought, the awful thought, the petty thought — the thought that *threaded* in unbidden — He knows it. He also knows that it was not the deepest part of you. The depth of you is the part that is *uncomfortable* with the intrusive thought.

It changes how you exist when no one else is around. Because you are never actually alone. And the one who is with you is al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr.

### Theme Two: Knowledge as the Foundation for Resurrection

Now let's zoom out and ask: why is this ayah where it is in the surah?

Remember the context. Surah Saba is, in large part, an argument with the deniers of resurrection. In ayah 3 — the very next ayah — Allah will say:

*"Those who disbelieve say: 'The Hour will not come upon us.' Say: yes, by my Lord, it will surely come upon you — the Knower of the Unseen. Not even the weight of an atom escapes Him in the heavens or in the earth..."*

So our ayah is the setup for that argument.

The deniers said: how can a God reassemble dust that has been scattered for centuries? The body decomposed. The atoms dispersed. Some became plant. Some became river. Some became another body. Some became air. How can He find them?

And our ayah — without rebutting them directly — simply states the premise that makes their objection collapse.

**He knows what threaded into the earth.**

He knows where every atom of you went. The water that left your body and rejoined the cycle — He knows where it is. The carbon that became part of a tree — He knows which tree. The minerals that became part of another person's bones — He knows whose bones.

This is what classical Arabic rhetoric does sometimes: it does not refute an objection. It states a fact about Allah that, once internalized, makes the objection embarrassing to have ever raised.

The deniers were operating with an implicit assumption that "scattered" means "lost." For human beings, this is true. If you scatter a manuscript into the wind, you cannot find every page. But Allah's relationship to the universe is not the relationship of a human to a manuscript. He is the One whose knowledge *constituted* every atom. To Him, the atoms are not scattered. They are exactly where He knows they are.

Al-Muyassar captures this beautifully in his explanation. He writes that Allah knows *qaṭarāt al-mā'* — the *droplets* of water — that enter the earth. Not the water in general. Each droplet, individually. This is the level of detail the verb *yaliju* implies.

And once you grant this — once you grant that He knows each droplet — then *resurrection is just a logistical claim, not a metaphysical one.* The hard part is the knowledge. The reassembly is trivial for One whose knowledge already holds every coordinate.

This is why mercy follows. Because resurrection, framed as exposure, is terrifying. But resurrection framed as the return to the One whose knowledge of you is paired with mercy and forgiveness — that is hope. That is the whole reason the surah opens with *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* Praise to the One who will bring me back, because the One who will bring me back is al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you think about death. Death is the moment something threads into the earth. *Wa mā yaliju fī'l-arḍ* — you are one of the things that threads in. But His knowledge of you does not end when your body enters. It accompanies the threading. And His knowledge of you accompanies the coming out, when *mā yakhruju minhā* applies to your reassembly.

It changes how you read the loss of those you love. They have not been lost. They have been threaded into the earth in a place fully known to the One who is merciful to them.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture Revealed

Let me now show you how the pieces fit together.

This ayah is built like a chamber with four entries and one center.

The four entries are the four verbs of motion:
- يَلِجُ — what threads down into the earth
- يَخْرُجُ — what comes up out of it
- يَنزِلُ — what descends from heaven
- يَعْرُجُ — what ascends back into it

These four directions exhaust every possible movement in creation. Wherever anything goes, anywhere in His domain, it is one of these four.

The center of the chamber is one word: **يَعْلَمُ** — *He knows.* This single verb governs all four directions. There is no escape from it. There is no movement that occurs unwitnessed.

But then — at the end of the chamber, the wall opposite the door you came in — is the great surprise. The names He gives Himself.

Not *al-'Alīm.* Not *al-Khabīr.* Not *al-Baṣīr.*

**Al-Raḥīm. Al-Ghafūr.**

The chamber is built so that you enter through the realization that you are fully known — and you exit through the realization that you are fully covered.

That is the architecture. The knowledge clause is the journey in. The mercy clause is the door out.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight from two directions.

The verb *yaliju* — to thread through narrow openings — told us that He sees the most hidden movements. The four-direction architecture told us that no motion in creation falls outside His knowledge. The asymmetric absence of *al-'Alīm* in favor of *al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr* told us why this knowledge is not surveillance — because the One holding it is merciful.

The themes told us the same thing from the angle of lived experience. Modern surveillance trauma tempts us to read divine knowledge as threat — but the names of Allah at the end of the ayah explicitly refuse that reading. And resurrection — the great fear of the deniers and the great hope of the believers — rests on this same exhaustive knowledge. He knows where you are even when you are scattered.

The whole ayah teaches one thing: **Being known by Him is the precondition of being saved by Him.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When I imagine being seen by Allah, do I imagine a watchful eye or a covering hand? What does my honest answer tell me about which divine name I have internalized?

2. What is the most private thing about me — the thought, the memory, the secret — that I forget Allah already knows? What changes if I pray *into* that knowledge instead of around it?

3. The classical commentators said *yaliju* means water threading into soil and plants pushing up. What else, in my own life right now, is threading down and being pushed up? What is descending toward me and what is rising from me?

4. If resurrection is just the *coming-out* of what threaded *into* the earth — and Allah knows the location of every thread — what does this change about my grief for those who have died?

5. Why did Allah pair omniscience with mercy in this verse, instead of with judgment? What is He trying to protect me from misunderstanding?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The God who threads His knowledge into the narrowest gaps of your inner life and the deepest layers of His earth — the God to whom every motion is visible in every direction at every instant — has named Himself, at the end of that knowledge claim, not the Watcher but the Merciful and the Forgiving.

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, before you sleep, try this. Lie down and let your mind do what it does — wander, remember, accuse, regret. And as each of those threads in, say quietly: *He already knows this one. And He is al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr.*

You are not informing Him. You are remembering His names.

The first half of the ayah is true whether you accept it or not — He knows. The second half of the ayah is only an experience you have if you receive it — *al-Raḥīm al-Ghafūr.*

Receive it.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who know what threads down into the deepest gaps of earth and the deepest gaps of my heart — You have named Yourself, after that knowing, the Most Merciful and the Oft-Forgiving. Let me not be the one who hears the first half of Your ayah and forgets the second. Cover what You see, forgive what You know, and gather me back to You when this body threads into the earth, with mercy as my only credential. Ameen.
