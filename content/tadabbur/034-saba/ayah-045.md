# The Tenth They Never Reached

## A Warning Carved from Ruins — Surah Saba, Ayah 45

---

## INTRODUCTION

**The Arabic:**

وَكَذَّبَ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ وَمَا بَلَغُوا۟ مِعْشَارَ مَآ ءَاتَيْنَـٰهُمْ فَكَذَّبُوا۟ رُسُلِى ۖ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ نَكِيرِ

**Transliteration:**

*Wa kadhdhaba alladhīna min qablihim wa mā balaghū miʿshāra mā ātaynāhum, fa-kadhdhabū rusulī, fa-kayfa kāna nakīr.*

**Accessible English:**

"And those before them denied — and these have not reached even a tenth of what We had given them — yet they denied My messengers. So how was My rejection?"

---

### Rebuild the World

Before we open this ayah, we have to put ourselves inside the room where it was first heard.

The audience is Quraysh. They are not a poor people. They are the custodians of the Kaʿba. They run the great trade route — the *riḥlat al-shitāʾ wa-l-ṣayf* — the winter caravan to Yemen, the summer caravan to Sham. Their city is a sanctuary. Their economy is stable. Their lineage is proud.

And this surah — Surah Saba — has been doing something specific to them for forty-four ayahs. It has been holding up mirrors. Not flat mirrors. Magnifying mirrors.

Look at Dawud, Allah says. **Mountains and birds joined him in praise.** Look at Sulayman. **The wind ran for him a month's journey in a morning. Jinn built for him whatever he wanted — towering structures, sculptures, basins like reservoirs, cauldrons fixed in place.** Look at Saba. **Two gardens, right and left. Eat from the provision of your Lord and give thanks.**

These were not myths to the Quraysh. The ruins of Mārib were on their trade route. They walked past them. The collapsed dam, the dried-out gardens, the abandoned cities — these were tourist sites. You could put your hand on the stone.

And then the surah turns. *We sent against them the flood of the dam. And We replaced their two gardens with two gardens bearing bitter fruit, tamarisk, and a little of the lote tree.* Saba had everything. Saba lost everything. Because Saba **turned away.**

Now — forty-four ayahs into this argument — the Quraysh are standing in front of the speaker, arms crossed. *We are not them. We are doing fine. Our gods serve us. Our trade works. Our city is sacred. Your warnings are for other people.*

And the ayah lands.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what the ayah is about to do — and you have to listen for it because it is almost easy to miss.

Allah is not going to argue with the Quraysh about whether they are powerful. He is going to do something far more devastating. He is going to **measure them.** He is going to put them on a scale next to the civilizations that came before them — civilizations they themselves had walked through the ruins of — and He is going to give them a number.

And the number is going to be brutal.

Then He is going to ask one question. Not a question He needs the answer to. A question He wants you to feel.

Let's open the ayah.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We will follow three threads through this ayah. Each one is small. Each one opens a room.

---

### Thread One: The Doubled Denial — *kadhdhaba* twice

Look at the ayah's bones. Two verbs of denial. The same verb. Used twice.

**وَكَذَّبَ** — and they denied.
**فَكَذَّبُوا۟** — so they denied.

In Arabic, there is a difference between the bare verb form — Form I — and what grammarians call Form II. Form I is the simple action: *kataba* means he wrote. Form II is the same root with the middle letter doubled: *kattaba*. That doubling does not just emphasize. It often **intensifies** the action, makes it deliberate, makes it repeated, or makes the act of causing the action central.

Take an everyday example in English. There is a difference between someone saying "I disagreed with him" and "I rejected him outright, repeatedly, in the face." Both are negative responses. One is a position. The other is a posture. A whole stance toward another human being.

*Kadhdhaba* is Form II of the root *k-dh-b* — the root of lying. Form I — *kadhaba* — would mean: he lied. But Form II — *kadhdhaba* — means he called another person a liar. He looked the messenger in the face and said: **you are lying.** It is not a private intellectual error. It is a public, declarative, repeated act of accusation. It is denial as confrontation.

The Quranic Arabic Corpus confirms both verbs in this ayah are Form II. Both. Not casual. Not one-time. The verb itself carries the texture of someone who looked Hud in the face. Looked Salih in the face. Looked Lut in the face. Looked Shuʿayb in the face. And said: *you are a liar.*

Now — notice something. Allah uses this verb twice in a single ayah. Once at the beginning. Once near the end.

**وَكَذَّبَ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ** — those before them denied.
**فَكَذَّبُوا۟ رُسُلِى** — so they denied My messengers.

Why say it twice?

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Because at first glance it looks redundant. He said they denied. Why say it again?

Here is what the second *kadhdhabū* does. The first one is the announcement of the fact. *They denied.* But then Allah inserts something between the two denials — He inserts the measurement. *And these have not reached even a tenth of what We had given them.* Then He returns to the denial. And this time, the second denial is doing something different. It is the denial seen **after** you have absorbed the measurement.

The first time you hear "they denied," you might think of it as a category. Some people, somewhere, in history, said no. The second time — after Allah has reminded you how vast they were, how much they had, how much greater their power was than yours — the second time the word lands with weight. *Those people. With all of that. Denied My messengers.*

Al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi both note that those prior civilizations were *ashaddu minhum baṭshan* — mightier than them in force — and *akthar amwālan wa-awlādan* — greater in wealth and progeny. The doubled denial frames the catastrophe of the verb. It is not that small people denied. It is that **the largest people denied.** And the verb itself — *kadhdhaba* — refuses to soften.

One word repeated. And the repetition is the argument.

[PAUSE]

---

### Thread Two: *Miʿshār* — the cruelty of a precise number

Now look at the middle of the ayah. The measurement.

**وَمَا بَلَغُوا۟ مِعْشَارَ مَآ ءَاتَيْنَـٰهُمْ**

"And they have not reached **miʿshār** of what We had given them."

The word *miʿshār* comes from the root *ʿ-sh-r* — ten. Ten. The number.

Before we touch this word, sit with what Allah is doing. He is making a quantitative claim. Allah, the Lord of all the worlds, the One who needs no rhetorical flourish, the One whose every word is measured — Allah is giving the Quraysh a **number.**

He could have said: *they were greater than you.* He could have said: *you are nothing compared to them.* He could have used vague comparison. But He didn't. He chose a measuring word. A fraction word.

In everyday English, when we want to humble someone, we say things like "you don't even have a fraction of what they had." It is impressive rhetoric, but it is also escape rhetoric. A "fraction" could mean half. A "fraction" could mean a third. The phrase is technically true no matter what the real proportion is.

Allah does not let the Quraysh escape into a fraction. He nails the fraction down.

*Miʿshār.* A tenth.

Al-Qurtubi pauses here. He notes that there is a discussion among the lexicographers — *al-miʿshār wa-l-ʿushr sawāʾ* — the *miʿshār* and the *ʿushr* are the same: a tenth. And then he reports another view: *al-miʿshār ʿushr al-ʿushr* — the *miʿshār* is the tenth of the tenth. A hundredth. Al-Jawharī, the great lexicographer, narrows it further: *miʿshār al-shayʾ ʿushruh, wa-lā yaqūlūn hādhā fī shayʾin siwā al-ʿushr* — the *miʿshār* of a thing is its tenth, and the Arabs do not use this construction *miʿ-* for any fraction except the tenth.

Notice what that grammatical detail does to the ayah. The Arabic language has a special, dedicated construction for "one tenth" — and only for one tenth. Not for half. Not for quarter. Not for ninth or eleventh. As if the language itself, in its bones, knew that the tenth has a particular psychological weight. It is the threshold below which a quantity stops feeling like "a portion of" and starts feeling like "almost nothing." Below a tenth, the comparison breaks down.

And that is exactly where Allah places the Quraysh.

[PAUSE]

This is not a poetic flourish. This is a verdict.

You — Quraysh — with your sanctuary, your trade, your prestige, your two caravans, your standing among the Arabs — **you have not even reached one-tenth** of what those prior nations were given. The civilizations whose ruins you walk past on the way to Yemen — they had ten times what you have. Ten times the wealth. Ten times the power. Ten times the wonders. *Wa mā balaghū miʿshāra mā ātaynāhum.*

And here is the cruelty of the precise number. Vague comparisons let the ego survive. *I'm not as good as them.* Sure, fine, but *as good* is elastic. *As good* can be massaged.

But *miʿshār* cannot be massaged. A tenth is a tenth. A tenth means: if you took everything you have — your house, your caravan, your gold, your prestige, your network, your lineage — and you multiplied it by ten, you would only then equal **one** of the civilizations that Allah destroyed.

Notice another absence in the ayah. Allah does not say "they had ten times your wealth." He does not specify what they had ten times more of. The object is left wide open. *Mā ātaynāhum* — "what We gave them." Whatever it was. Wealth, lifespan, power, knowledge, kingdom, glory — whatever measurement you would like to apply, you are at less than a tenth.

And — this is where the verb *ātaynāhum* matters. It is Form IV of the root *ʾ-t-y* — to come. Form IV here is causative-transitive: to cause something to come, that is, to give. And it is in the first-person plural — *ātaynā* — **We gave.** The royal plural of divinity.

The civilizations the Quraysh are being compared to were not self-made. *Ātaynāhum* — **We gave them.** Their greatness was not earned. It was a gift. A divine grant. And the same is true of Quraysh themselves — everything they own, their sanctuary, their trade, their stability — *given.* Both sides of the comparison are recipients. The only difference is the size of the gift. And the gift to the prior nations was at least ten times larger.

This destroys a very specific posture. The posture of the self-made man. The posture of *I earned this, therefore I deserve this, therefore I am safe.* No, says the ayah. You are not even a tenth of what was given to people who were also not self-made. And those people were destroyed. So.

[PAUSE]

That "so" is the next word.

### Thread Three: *Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — the question that is not a question

The ayah ends with this:

**فَكَيْفَ كَانَ نَكِيرِ**

*Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr.*

"So how was My rejection?"

This is one of the most haunting endings in the Quran, and we have to slow down to feel why.

First, the grammar. *Fa* — so, then, therefore. The conjunction that ties cause to consequence. *Kayfa* — how. The interrogative. *Kāna* — was. Past tense. *Nakīr* — My rejection, My disowning, My refusal-to-acknowledge.

The word *nakīr* comes from the root *n-k-r* — the root that gives us *munkar* (the rejected, the disgraceful) and *inkār* (denial, refusal). When Allah uses *nakīr* about Himself, He is using the language of *refusing to acknowledge* — the very thing the disbelievers did to His messengers. They refused to acknowledge His messengers as His. So *He* refused to acknowledge them as His.

The mirror in the language is perfect. *Kadhdhabū rusulī* — they denied My messengers. *Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — so how was My disowning. They disowned His. He disowned theirs.

And notice the spelling. *Nakīr* in the muṣḥaf is written **نَكِيرِ** — with a kasra at the end, where the *yāʾ* of the first-person possessive has been dropped. In Quranic orthography, this dropping of the final *yāʾ* of "my" happens in specific, weighty contexts — at the end of ayahs, at the close of arguments, where the rhythm demands a single open syllable to ring out and decay. Listen to the sound. *Na-kīr.* The vowel hangs in the air.

[PAUSE]

Now — the question itself. *Kayfa.* How.

This is what Arabic grammarians call an *istifhām* — a question. But it is not a question of inquiry. Allah does not need information. It is what is called *istifhām taqrīrī* — a question whose purpose is to make the listener affirm an answer they already know. Like when a parent says to a child who just broke a vase: "Now what did I tell you?" The parent does not need new information. The parent is forcing the child to **complete the thought themselves.**

So when Allah asks **fa-kayfa kāna nakīr** — "so how was My rejection?" — He is not asking. He is forcing the Quraysh — and us — to fill in the answer ourselves.

And here is where the ayah does its most stunning work. Because **Allah does not describe the punishment.**

Pay attention to this absence. After forty-four ayahs of imagery — gardens turned bitter, dams bursting, kingdoms falling, jinn building palaces, mountains glorifying — at the climax of the warning, Allah goes **silent on the details**.

He does not say: "I destroyed them with a flood." He does not say: "I struck them with a wind." He does not say: "I shattered their cities into dust." He says: *how was My rejection?*

[PAUSE]

Why?

Because the Quraysh **already know**. They have walked through the ruins. They have seen Mārib. They have heard the stories of ʿĀd. They have passed the dwellings of Thamūd carved into the cliffs of al-Ḥijr on their way north. They have all the data. Allah does not need to repeat the catastrophe. He needs them to **remember** it. The question *kayfa kāna nakīr* is a finger pointed at every ruin they have ever seen, every collapsed wall, every drained oasis. He says: **look. Don't you have eyes? My disowning — how did it look?**

Stay with the structure of this. The ayah moves like this:

— *They denied.*
— *And you are not a tenth of them.*
— *So they denied My messengers.*
— *And My disowning — how was it?*

The first part is data. The second part is measurement. The third part is the consequence. The fourth part is the **silence that is louder than any description.** Because the ruins themselves are the answer.

Al-Jalalayn captures this in two words: *wāqiʿ mawqiʿah* — "it falls exactly where it should fall." His brief gloss is saying: the punishment landed exactly on the spot it was meant to land. The question is not asking *whether* — it is asking *how precisely the verdict matched the crime.*

And that is the answer the listener has to give themselves. To say *kayfa kāna nakīr* out loud is to be forced to remember. To remember is to know that what happened to them can happen to you. To know that what happened to them can happen to you is to look at your own life and ask: **am I living as if I am a tenth of them — and yet doing what they did?**

### Bridging Out

So three threads:

A doubled denial that turns *kadhdhaba* into a posture — not a slip but a confrontation.
A precise fraction that strips away the comfort of vague comparison and assigns a number to your relative size.
A question with no answer — because the answer is etched into ruins you have already seen.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the Quraysh — but about how power, denial, and divine warning actually work. In every age. Including ours.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

We will follow two themes. Each one starts somewhere the listener already lives.

---

### Theme One: The Tyranny of Comparison — and Why Allah Uses It Anyway

Begin here.

Most of us spend a quiet, daily portion of our lives in comparison. We open a screen and we compare. Our home to their home. Our income to their income. Our marriage to their marriage. Our children to their children. The modern psychological literature has a term for this. They call it *social comparison theory* — the observation that human beings cannot evaluate themselves in a vacuum. We need a reference group. We need someone to be next to so we can know who we are.

The pain of this is well-documented. Comparison upward — looking at people who have more — produces envy, inadequacy, depression. Comparison downward — looking at people who have less — produces a fleeting, dirty kind of satisfaction that does not last. Most spiritual traditions try to talk us out of comparison entirely. *Don't compare. Just be yourself. Stay in your lane.*

And here is the Quran. Here is Allah. In the climactic ayah of a forty-five-ayah argument, doing what?

**Comparing.**

Look how that pulls against everything we have been told. The Quran does not say to the Quraysh: *don't compare yourselves to others; just look at your own relationship with God.* It says: *compare. Look at them. You are less than a tenth of them.*

[PAUSE]

So what is going on?

Here is what is going on. There are two kinds of comparison, and modernity has confused them.

One kind of comparison is **horizontal** — across your peers, for status. *Am I doing better than my cousin? Am I doing better than my neighbor? Am I doing better than the person whose feed I am scrolling?* This comparison is poison. Because the people you compare yourself to are not the ones who will judge you, and the standards you measure by are not the ones that matter. Horizontal comparison turns life into a race no one asked you to run.

The other kind of comparison is **vertical** — across time, against the warnings of history. *Did people more powerful than I am think they were safe? Did people richer than I am think they were untouchable? What happened to them? What does that mean for me?* This comparison is not poison. This comparison is **medicine.** It is the medicine that the Quran prescribes again and again, in the great refrain *afa-lam yasīrū fī-l-arḍi* — have they not traveled the earth and seen the end of those before them. The Quran is constantly asking us to walk among the ruins.

Vertical comparison kills pride. Horizontal comparison feeds it. The same act — looking at someone else — does opposite things depending on whether you are looking sideways or backward.

[PAUSE]

So when Allah tells the Quraysh, *you are not even a tenth of them* — He is not playing the status game. He is yanking them out of horizontal comparison — *we are the best of the Arabs, we are richer than the Bedouins, our trade beats their trade* — and forcing them into vertical comparison. *Forget your peers. Look up the timeline. ʿĀd dwarfed you. Thamūd dwarfed you. Iram of the columns dwarfed you. And they are dust.*

Notice what this does. Horizontal comparison says: *because I am bigger than my neighbor, I am secure.* Vertical comparison says: *because I am smaller than my predecessors who were destroyed, I am not secure at all.*

The Quran is not anti-comparison. It is anti-**the wrong direction** of comparison.

And here is the application that should land. Tonight, when you next pick up your phone and find yourself comparing — pause. Ask: am I looking sideways or am I looking backward? If you are looking sideways, you are feeding a hunger that will not be filled. If you are looking backward — through the lens of the Quran, through the lens of ruins, through the lens of those-who-had-more-and-lost-it — you are doing what Surah Saba is teaching you to do. You are using comparison to rinse the heart of pride.

[PAUSE]

Try this practice. Every time you find yourself measuring your life against your peers, replace the peer with someone from the Quran. Are you proud of your wealth? Compare it to Qarun's. Are you proud of your power? Compare it to Firʿawn's. Are you proud of your security? Compare it to Saba's. The size of what you have shrinks immediately. And so does the size of your certainty that you have it figured out.

This is the first thing the ayah does. It does not abolish comparison. It redirects it.

### Theme Two: The Silence at the End — and the Pedagogy of the Unfinished Sentence

Begin here, in another place the listener already lives.

You are watching a film. The villain has done something monstrous. The hero is closing in. The music is rising. The hero raises a hand to deliver the verdict — and the film cuts to black. No closure. No final blow. Just silence.

You walk out of the theater complaining. *They didn't show me what happened.* But three days later, you cannot stop thinking about that film. The film that **did** show you the final blow is forgotten by Sunday. The one that cut to black lives in your head for weeks.

This is a known pedagogy. Storytellers, teachers, parents, prophets — anyone who wants something to take root in another mind eventually learns: **do not finish the sentence for the listener. Leave them to finish it themselves. What the listener completes, the listener owns.**

Allah is the supreme pedagogue.

Look at how this ayah ends. *Fa-kayfa kāna nakīr.* How was My rejection. He does not answer.

He spent the surah painting Saba's destruction in vivid detail. He told us about the dam. He told us about the bitter fruit. He told us about the tamarisk and the lote. He gave us the picture. And then, when the moment comes to close the case — when the rhetoric demands a final detailed image of what He does to the deniers — He **withholds.**

[PAUSE]

Why?

Because if He had described it, you would have read the description and walked away thinking about the description. But by withholding it, He forces you to write the description yourself. You have to summon, from your own memory of the Quran, of history, of the world — what does the *nakīr* of Allah look like? You have to picture it. You have to populate the silence.

And the moment you populate the silence, you are doing tadabbur. You have stopped being a reader and started being a participant. The ayah has moved from being words on a page to being a question your mind is now answering.

The doctors of language call this *iktifāʾ* — sufficiency. Allah leaves the predicate unstated because mentioning it would be redundant after the entire surah has done the work. And there is something even deeper than that. By leaving the answer to you, He makes the answer **yours**. You will remember what He destroyed those people with, because **you** said it, not because He said it to you.

This is also the pedagogy of every parent's "*you tell me what you did wrong.*" The child, forced to say it out loud, internalizes the verdict in a way that no parental lecture ever could.

[PAUSE]

The application: when you are reading Quran, learn to read the silences. Learn to feel where Allah has stopped speaking and is now waiting for **you** to speak. The unfinished question. The undefined object. The unspecified outcome. These are not gaps in the divine speech. These are invitations.

When you reach **fa-kayfa kāna nakīr** at the end of this ayah, do not move on. Stop. Answer. Out loud, in your heart, in your imagination — picture it. Picture Mārib's dam bursting. Picture ʿĀd's tents flying in the wind. Picture Thamūd's mountain houses silent at dawn. Picture every empire whose end you have read about. And let that be the answer.

Now ask the next, harder question. *And me? What does my life look like if measured against people who were ten times more, and were swept away anyway?*

That question is the silence the ayah hands you. Live in it for a moment.

[PAUSE — long]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now, having walked the linguistic threads and the themes, look at the bones of the ayah one more time:

> *And those before them denied — and they have not reached a tenth of what We had given them — yet they denied My messengers — so how was My rejection?*

Four moves.

**Move 1:** They denied. (The verdict on the past.)
**Move 2:** You are less than a tenth. (The measurement that exposes the present.)
**Move 3:** They denied My messengers. (The repetition that makes you taste the catastrophe.)
**Move 4:** How was My rejection? (The silence that hands the verdict to you.)

The ayah moves from announcement, to measurement, to repetition, to silence. From speech, into more speech, into more speech, into the listener's own voice. By the end of the ayah, **Allah is no longer speaking. You are.**

This is the architecture. The ayah is built so that its final word is yours. *Kayfa kāna nakīr.* And what you whisper back is the warning.

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic and thematic threads converge:

The doubled *kadhdhaba* (the Form II verb of confrontational denial) is the linguistic shape of the **horizontal comparison** trap. The deniers were sure they were on top — they looked at their peers, they looked at their wealth, they were certain. That certainty is what made denying a messenger feel safe. *Kadhdhaba* is what arrogance sounds like.

The *miʿshār* (the precise tenth) is the **vertical comparison** that Allah forces on them. It does not let them stay in their peer group. It puts them next to giants. And it is the only direction of comparison that produces *taqwā* — God-consciousness, the awareness of one's actual smallness.

And the *kayfa kāna nakīr* — the silence at the end — is what makes the whole thing land in **your** mouth and not just your ears. Because warnings that you read are forgotten. Warnings you have to **speak the conclusion of** become part of you.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions. The ayah is teaching you: do not deny what you have not measured; do not measure horizontally when Allah is offering you the vertical; and when Allah leaves a silence at the end of a warning — fill it yourself, because that is where the warning lives.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. Where in my life am I comparing horizontally — to my peers, to my feed, to my neighbors — when Allah is asking me to compare vertically — to those whose ruins my ancestors walked past?

2. If everything I own is *given* — not earned — what does my gratitude actually look like in proportion to what I have been given?

3. When I read warnings in the Quran, do I read them as warnings about *other people* — or do I let them measure me?

4. What is the unfinished sentence in my own life — the silence Allah is leaving for me to fill — that I have been avoiding filling?

5. If I am less than a tenth of those who were destroyed, what right do I have to my current certainty?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> Allah measures the Quraysh against giants they themselves know are ruins — and then asks a question He does not answer, so that the answer must come from your own mouth.

### The Closing Invitation

Read this ayah at least once a month for a year. Not as a passage. As a measurement. Each time, place yourself in front of it. Each time, fill in the silence at the end with whatever you have most recently seen — a fallen empire on the news, a billionaire's collapse, a celebrity's disgrace, an ancient ruin you visited, a friend whose certainty in their own future shattered overnight.

Let the question *kayfa kāna nakīr* never become rhetorical. Keep it alive. Keep answering it.

And every time you answer it, ask the silent companion question that the ayah is too dignified to ask out loud:

*And what about me?*

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

Ya Allah — You who measure with the precision of *miʿshār*, the One whose rejection is the only rejection that matters — make us of those who measure ourselves against ruins, not against rivals.

You who left the silence at the end of Your warning so that we would speak it ourselves — let us speak it, and let the speaking soften our hearts before the day when no speaking softens anything at all.

And make us not of those who, given less than a tenth of what was given before, still found ten times their pride.

Āmīn.
