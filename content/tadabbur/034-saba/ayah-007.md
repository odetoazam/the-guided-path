# Introduction: The World Before the Words

Before we open this ayah, sit with where we are.

Surah Saba opens with a declaration about Allah's knowledge that is almost dizzying in its scope. *Not even the weight of an atom escapes Him* — not in the heavens, not in the earth, not smaller, not larger — *everything is in a clear record* (34:3). The surah begins by establishing that reality itself is **fully accounted for** in Allah's knowledge. Nothing leaks out.

And then immediately — verse 7 — we are dropped into a room where men are laughing.

We have to feel this contrast. Allah has just spoken about the precision of His knowledge. The reach of His record-keeping. The completeness of His grasp on creation. And the response of those who reject Him is not argument. Not counter-evidence. Not even silence.

It is **a joke**.

> "Shall we direct you to a man who tells you that when you have been torn apart with every kind of tearing, you will surely be in a new creation?"

Picture the scene. A group of Quraysh in Makkah. Perhaps in the shade of the Kaʿbah. Perhaps in a courtyard. They are talking about him — the one who keeps saying things they find ridiculous. And one of them turns to the others, gestures, and says: *Want me to show you someone? There's this man. You won't believe what he claims.*

The whole sentence is shaped like the opening of a comedy routine. The setup. The pause. The punchline. They are not refuting Muhammad ﷺ. They are **performing his absurdity for each other.**

[PAUSE]

And here is the strange thing. The strange, almost vertiginous thing.

Allah preserves their joke. He does not paraphrase it. He does not sanitize it. He quotes them — every syllable — and places those exact syllables inside His revelation, where they will be recited until the end of time. The very mockery they thought would discredit Muhammad ﷺ becomes one of the most-recited specimens of mockery in human history. Recited by the descendants of the very people they were mocking.

This is the hook. **Allah keeps the joke.** Why? What is He showing us?

We are going to listen to the language they chose. Because every word they picked is doing something. And every word betrays them.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: "Shall we *guide* you?" — the inversion of the most sacred word

Let me first teach you something about how Arabic builds the concept of "guidance."

In Arabic, there are several verbs that cluster around the idea of showing someone the way. There is *hadā* (هدى) — to give right guidance. There is *arshada* (أرشد) — to direct rightly. And there is *dalla* (دلّ) — to point toward, to indicate, to lead someone *to* something. The noun *dalīl* (دليل) — proof, evidence, guide — comes from this root. A *dalīl* is what you follow in the desert when you cannot see the road. It is also the technical word, in Islamic scholarship, for **evidence** — a *dalīl sharʿī* is a textual proof.

So this root — د-ل-ل — is one of the architectural roots of the entire vocabulary of guidance. To "*dalla* someone" is to **show them what to follow**.

Now hold that in your mind, and listen to what the disbelievers say:

> هَلْ نَدُلُّكُمْ — *hal nadullukum* — "Shall we **guide** you?"

[PAUSE]

They are using the verb of *guiding*. The verb that, elsewhere in the Quran, is used when the believers are invited to truth. The verb that lives at the heart of the Muslim's entire vocabulary of seeking — *the path, the proof, the guide.*

And they are using it to **point at a guide they consider a fool.**

Stay with me here. This is a specific kind of irony — not the soft irony of a passing remark, but a deliberate inversion. They have taken the language of *hidāyah* — guidance — and they have turned it into the language of ridicule. *Shall we guide you... to someone you should laugh at?*

Notice what this reveals about disbelief. It is not the absence of religious vocabulary. It is **religious vocabulary inverted**. The disbelievers in this ayah are not crude pagans grunting against the Prophet. They are sophisticated. They are using the very framework of guidance to construct a mockery of guidance.

This is, by the way, almost universally how denial works. The atheist who says, "Let me *enlighten* you about religion." The cynic who says, "Let me *show you* what real wisdom looks like." Every age recycles this move — the inversion of the sacred word, used now to mock the sacred.

Notice something else. The verb is in the **imperfect, first person plural**: *nadullukum* — "**we** shall guide you." Not "I." This is **collective mockery**. They are not speaking as one individual. They are constituting themselves as a group — *we, the ones who know better* — pointing together at a single man.

Here is what is remarkable: Allah does not strip the verb away from them. He does not have them say, *"Shall we show you a deluded man?"* or *"Shall we expose you to a liar?"* He lets them keep the dignified verb of guidance. He lets them say *nadullu* — we guide.

Why?

[PAUSE]

Because **Allah is allowing them to incriminate themselves with their own borrowed vocabulary**. They reached for the language of the very thing they were rejecting. And the moment they reached for it, they admitted — without meaning to — that the framework of guidance is real. That there is a *direction* toward which one can be pointed. That truth is the kind of thing one can be *led to*.

Their joke depends on the legitimacy of what they are mocking. You cannot parody guidance unless guidance is a real category. The very structure of the sentence is a tribute to the existence of what they are denying.

What this single word reveals: **mockery of revelation always borrows the vocabulary of revelation, and in borrowing it, confesses it.**

## Element Two: "...to *a man*" — the grammar of refusal

Now look at what they say next.

> عَلَىٰ رَجُلٍ — *ʿalā rajulin* — "to **a** man."

In Arabic, the absence of the definite article — *al-* — is not a small thing. A noun without *al-* is **indefinite**. It is *some* man. *A* man. **One among many. Nameless.**

If they had said *ʿalā al-rajul* — *that man* — there would be specificity. If they had said *Muḥammad* — there would be identification. But they did not. They refused to name him.

Notice what they are doing. They are not unaware of his name. The whole city of Makkah knows his name. He is *al-Amīn* — the trustworthy one. They have known him since childhood. They have given him their valuables for safekeeping. And yet here, at the moment they want to mock him, they will not let his name cross their lips.

This is a specific rhetorical move that human beings make in every language, in every era. **When you want to diminish someone, you stop naming them.** You say *that guy*. *Some person*. *A man*. You strip the name away because the name carries weight — and you do not want to grant the weight.

Watch any political opponent refuse to say their rival's name. Watch any estranged family member refer to "that one." Watch how grief sometimes works — when you cannot bear to say a name, you say *him*, *her*, *the one we lost*. **Naming is intimacy. Refusing to name is distance.**

And the disbelievers, in this ayah, are constructing distance.

But — and here is where the ayah turns on them — *Allah is the one quoting them*. So in the Quran, **Muhammad ﷺ is recorded as having been called "a man"**. The indefinite. The unnamed.

[PAUSE]

And what does Allah do, fifteen verses later in this same surah? He says:

> قُلْ إِنَّمَا أَعِظُكُم بِوَٰحِدَةٍ ۖ أَن تَقُومُوا۟ لِلَّهِ مَثْنَىٰ وَفُرَٰدَىٰ ثُمَّ تَتَفَكَّرُوا۟ ۚ مَا بِصَاحِبِكُم مِّن جِنَّةٍ
> *Say: I only counsel you in one thing — that you stand for Allah in pairs and singly, then reflect. There is no madness in your companion...* (34:46)

Watch what Allah does. They called him *a man*. Allah calls him *ṣāḥib* — **your companion**. The one who *accompanies* you. The one who has been beside you. The familiar one. The intimate one.

They reached for distance. Allah reached for closeness.

They said *a man*. Allah said *your companion*.

This is the response of the Quran to their refusal to name him: it does not give him a name *back* — it gives him **a relationship**. The disbelievers thought they were diminishing him by stripping the article. Allah honors him by restoring the relational word: *your companion*. You know him. He is yours. He has always been here.

What this single absence reveals: **when human beings refuse to name what they want to dismiss, Allah responds by naming the relationship they have been pretending does not exist.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: "...torn apart with every kind of tearing" — when the sound becomes the meaning

Now we come to the strangest, most physically violent phrase in the ayah.

> إِذَا مُزِّقْتُمْ كُلَّ مُمَزَّقٍ — *idhā muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — "when you have been **torn apart with every kind of tearing**."

Let me teach you two grammatical features before we step inside this phrase.

**First — Form II verbs.** Arabic verbs come in numbered forms. Form I is the basic verb. Forms II through X add layers of meaning. Form II, in particular, often carries **intensification** or **repetition** — doing something thoroughly, again and again. The simple verb *mazaqa* would mean to tear. The Form II *mazzaqa* — with the doubled middle consonant — means **to tear apart utterly, to tear into pieces, to tear and tear and tear**.

The Leeds morphological corpus confirms: the verb here, *muzziqtum*, is Form II — and it is passive. Not *you tore* but *you were torn — repeatedly, intensively, completely.*

**Second — the *mafʿūl muṭlaq*.** This is one of the most beautiful structures in Arabic. It is the use of a noun derived from the same root as the verb, placed after the verb, to **emphasize the action itself**. In English we might say "I love you, *truly*." In Arabic you say, "I love you, *a loving*." The repetition of the root word doubles down. It makes the action total.

Now hear the phrase again:

> *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — "you were torn-apart **every torn-apart-ness**."

The verb is intensive Form II. The object is its own intensive Form II cognate. The word *kull* — "every" — sits between them. The whole phrase is **a tower of tearing**. *Torn — every kind of torn — torn-apart-ness*.

[PAUSE]

Now I want you to hear something the page cannot show you. Say these sounds out loud:

> *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq*

Notice the sounds your mouth has to make. The *m* hum. The *z* — a sharp, hissing consonant. The *q* — pulled from the back of the throat, almost a click. *Mz-qq. Mz-qq.* It is **harsh**. It is **broken**. The sounds of the word are the sounds of something being ripped.

This is what scholars of Arabic rhetoric call *muḥākāt* — phonetic mimesis. The sound of the word imitates the meaning of the word. Allah did not choose any verb for "decay." He chose a verb whose very phonetics tear at the throat.

Compare what comes next:

> إِنَّكُمْ لَفِى خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ — *innakum la-fī khalqin jadīd* — "indeed, you will be in a new creation."

Notice the sounds. *Khalqin jadīd*. The *kh* opens softly. The *j* — gentle, almost breathy. The *dīd* — long, opening, sustained. After the harsh, broken phonetics of *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq*, the phrase *khalqin jadīd* feels like **dawn after a storm**.

Allah has hidden the entire argument of resurrection inside the **acoustic shape** of the sentence. The disbelievers are using their words to describe how absurd it is that broken-apart-people could be remade whole. But Allah has chosen the words so that the very sound of the sentence enacts the journey: **the harsh tearing of *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* gives way to the soft opening of *khalqin jadīd*.**

[PAUSE]

But notice something even deeper.

The disbelievers chose *muzziqtum* — Form II, intensive — to make resurrection sound impossible. *Not just torn, but torn into every kind of pieces.* They piled the language of disintegration as high as it could go, because they thought: *the more torn we are, the more impossible it is to be remade.*

But what does that pile of intensification actually accomplish, inside Allah's speech?

It accomplishes the opposite of what they intended.

**The more thoroughly torn they describe themselves as being, the greater the demonstration of Allah's power when He remakes them.** Their own rhetorical inflation — the *kull*, the Form II, the *mafʿūl muṭlaq* — becomes the testimony to Allah's grandeur. They piled up the difficulty. Allah lets the difficulty stand. Because the difficulty is no difficulty to Him.

You can almost hear Allah's response — given a few surahs later, in Yasin:

> قُلْ يُحْيِيهَا الَّذِي أَنشَأَهَا أَوَّلَ مَرَّةٍ
> *Say: He will give them life who created them the first time* (36:79)

Their intensification did not stretch Allah's power. **It only stretched their own confession of His task** — and the task, however large they made it, is nothing before the One who made them from nothing the first time.

What this phrase reveals: **the sounds of revelation are not accidental. Allah chooses words whose phonetic texture enacts what they describe. And when disbelievers try to make a denial vivid, the vividness becomes the proof of what they are denying.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

## The Bridge

So look at what we have found in the language so far.

They used the verb of **guidance** — and confessed that guidance is real.

They refused to **name him** — and Allah called him *your companion*.

They piled up the **sounds of disintegration** — and the sounds themselves built the testimony of His power.

Every move they made in mockery, Allah turned into a move that incriminated their mockery.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about their words — but about a deeper reality. About the architecture of denial itself. About what is really at stake when someone laughs at resurrection.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Architecture of Mockery — why ridicule is the last shape of resistance

I want you to think about a moment in your own life when you were laughed at for something you knew was true.

Not corrected. Not argued with. **Laughed at.**

Maybe it was your faith. Maybe it was a moral position. Maybe it was a decision about your life that didn't fit what people around you considered reasonable. Maybe it was something as small as praying when your friends thought you should be sleeping in.

Notice what mockery does that argument doesn't do. Argument engages you. Argument takes you seriously. Argument says *your position is wrong* — but it acknowledges that your position is a position. Mockery says **your position is not even worth being wrong**. It refuses to grant you the dignity of being engaged.

This is why mockery is so much harder to bear than argument. And this is why mockery is the **final form** of resistance when someone cannot answer you on the merits.

Now look at the disbelievers in this ayah.

They had every opportunity to argue with Muhammad ﷺ. They had wealth, scholars, poets, leaders. They could have produced surahs to rival his — the Quran itself dares them to. They could have produced philosophical counter-arguments. They could have refuted resurrection with any reasoning they had.

What did they do?

**They told a joke.**

This is not a sign of strength. **It is a sign that the argument has been lost.** When a person has the truth, they make a case. When a person has only social power, they make a joke. The joke is the weapon of the one who knows, somewhere underneath, that the case cannot be made.

This is the first and oldest lesson of this ayah for us. **The presence of mockery is often the proof of the absence of refutation.**

[PAUSE]

But there is something deeper here, and we have to be careful.

The mockery is not just a sign of intellectual surrender. It is a *social* weapon. Look at what the disbelievers do — *hal nadullukum* — "shall **we** direct **you**." This is not one person speaking to themselves. **It is the construction of a group around a shared joke.**

Have you ever noticed how social pressure crystallizes around ridicule? In a workplace, in a school, in a family — when a group wants to enforce its norms, it does not produce a position paper. It produces a *joke*. A nickname. A face. A glance. The job of the joke is to make anyone in the group who secretly agrees with the mocked person **terrified to admit it**, because to admit it would be to risk becoming the next target.

This is what the disbelievers in 34:7 are doing. They are not just describing what they think of Muhammad ﷺ. They are **defining the in-group** by making him the joke. *Anyone who finds him credible is one of us no longer.*

And this is why mockery — across centuries — has been the most effective recruiter for falsehood. Not because the falsehood is convincing. But because **the cost of being publicly counted as someone who is mocked** is one of the highest social costs a human being can pay.

Stay here a moment.

What does this mean for you?

It means that when you find yourself in a room where the sacred is being mocked, the question is not "can I out-argue them?" The question is: **can I tolerate being adjacent to the mocked?** Because the moment you defend the position, you become the next item in the joke.

This is the test of every believer in every age.

[PAUSE]

And now look at how Allah responds.

He does not delete their joke. He does not have the Prophet ﷺ retaliate with a counter-joke. He **preserves the joke and continues the conversation**.

Verse 8 immediately follows: *"Has he invented a lie against Allah, or is there madness in him?"* — even *their* mockery is recorded, then *quoted*, then **answered**. Verse 9: *"Have they not seen what is before them and behind them of the heaven and the earth?"* — answered with the gesture toward the sky, toward the earth, toward what they have been ignoring while they were laughing.

Allah does not stoop to their rhetorical register. **He simply keeps speaking past it.**

And this is the second lesson. When you are mocked for something true — you do not need to win the joke war. You need to **keep speaking**. Keep doing the thing. Keep praying. Keep believing. Keep telling the truth. The joke will burn itself out, because jokes have a half-life. But the truth, spoken plainly and lived out, lasts.

Look at what happened. The Quraysh's joke about *a man* lasted years. Muhammad's ﷺ patient continuation lasted centuries. Today, more than fourteen hundred years later, **the joke is preserved only because the Quran preserved it**. The mockers are nameless. The man is named in five daily prayers across every continent on earth.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It means: when you are mocked for a position rooted in truth, **the most powerful response is not the wittiest comeback**. The most powerful response is *continuing*. The disbelievers in 34:7 had social power, wit, and the home advantage in Makkah. They had every weapon. What they did not have was *the ability to outlast a man who would not stop walking the path*.

Mockery has a short life. **Steadfastness has a long life.** When you are facing ridicule for something you know is true, the question is not *can I win this exchange?* The question is *can I outlast this exchange by quietly continuing?*

## Theme Two: Why Resurrection? — the question that sorts every human being

Notice something about the mockery. It is not generic.

The disbelievers are not just laughing at Muhammad ﷺ for being a prophet. They are not even laughing at him for monotheism. They are laughing at him for **one specific claim**: *that the dead will be remade*.

This is striking. Of all the things in the message, **why is resurrection the focal point of their ridicule?**

This is a pattern across the Quran. Look at 36:78 — *"He says: Who will give life to bones when they are decayed?"* Look at 13:5 — *"And if you are amazed, then truly amazing is their saying: When we are dust, shall we then be in a new creation?"* Look at 17:49 — *"They say: When we are bones and dust, shall we really be raised up as a new creation?"* Look at 23:82, 37:16, 50:3. **Again and again**, the disbelievers' core objection is the same. Not God. Not prophecy. **Resurrection.**

Why?

Begin where the listener lives. Think about what you can deny without changing your life, and what you cannot.

You can deny the existence of distant galaxies and your day continues exactly as before. You can deny historical facts about ancient civilizations and your life carries on. You can hold all kinds of metaphysical opinions about the nature of consciousness, and your morning routine does not shift.

**But you cannot deny resurrection without changing the meaning of every single thing you do.**

If you will not be raised, then the moral weight of your actions is whatever you negotiate it to be. The man who cheated you walks free. The injustice unanswered will stay unanswered. The wealth you accumulated is yours to spend as you like. The body's appetites have no court of appeal. **The end of you is the end of the case.**

But if you *will* be raised — then **every act has a witness**. The hidden gesture, the private cruelty, the secret charity, the unseen prayer — all of it is held. None of it disappears.

This is why the disbelievers cluster around resurrection. **Resurrection is the doctrine that makes every other doctrine consequential.** You can entertain belief in God in the abstract. You can even agree that there is a creator. What you cannot do — without changing your life — is believe that you are going to stand before Him.

[PAUSE]

So when they mock *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — "torn apart with every kind of tearing" — listen to what they are actually saying.

They are not making an argument about physics. They are not, really, asking how decomposed bones can be reassembled. They are *asserting their freedom*. They are saying: *the body that decays into the earth takes with it every claim against me. Once I am torn apart, I am beyond accountability.*

The *kulla mumazzaq* is not a scientific observation. **It is a hope.** It is a prayer in reverse — a prayer that there is no remaking. That the tearing apart is final. That what is undone cannot be redone.

Notice how often, in the modern world, the same hope wears different clothes. "When you die, you just rot." "Death is the end." "We came from nothing, we return to nothing." Listen carefully: these are not always *conclusions*. Often they are **wishes**. Wishes that the case will close when the body closes.

The disbeliever in 34:7 and the secular materialist in the modern coffee shop are, structurally, performing the same act. **They are asserting that disintegration will save them from accounting.**

And the Quran's answer is short, almost casual:

> إِنَّكُمْ لَفِى خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ — *innakum la-fī khalqin jadīd*

Notice the *innakum* — "indeed, you." Notice the *la-* — the emphatic lām. **Indeed, you will surely be in a new creation.**

The Quran does not even argue with them in this verse. It quotes their objection, lets the heaviness of *kulla mumazzaq* land, and then — with the lightest grammar of certainty — says **yes, you will**.

[PAUSE]

What does this mean for how you live tomorrow?

It means that you can locate the **fault line in your own heart** by paying attention to where, exactly, the doctrine of resurrection makes you uncomfortable.

If the thought of standing before Allah with everything you did and said visible makes you genuinely uneasy — then **you have located something to work on**. Not something to deny. Not something to laugh away. Something to *attend to*.

The disbelievers in Saba 7 made their uneasiness into a joke because they could not bear to make it into a question. **Don't make their move.** When the unease comes, let it become a question: *what am I doing now that I would not want to see played back?* Then change that one thing.

Resurrection is not, as the disbelievers thought, the most fantastical claim in revelation. It is the **most practical**. It is the doctrine that asks you to live as though every act has consequence — because it does.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture of the Ayah

Now look back at what Allah has done in sixteen words.

He has preserved a joke. He has preserved its setup — *hal nadullukum*. He has preserved its dismissive grammar — *rajulin*. He has preserved its rhetorical climax — *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq*. He has preserved its punchline — *innakum la-fī khalqin jadīd*.

And in preserving it precisely, He has built a trap.

The setup uses the verb of guidance — confessing that guidance is a real thing.

The dismissive grammar refuses to name a man — and Allah responds, in the same surah, by naming him *your companion*.

The rhetorical climax piles up the difficulty of remaking what is broken — and the more they pile it up, **the more they testify to the size of His power.**

The punchline they meant as the absurdity is, in fact, **the entire promise of the Quran** — *innakum la-fī khalqin jadīd*. **Indeed, you will surely be in a new creation.**

The disbelievers wrote, without knowing it, **the most economical statement of the resurrection doctrine in the Quran**. Their mockery composed the headline of the message they were rejecting.

## Bring It Together

The linguistic and the thematic are the same insight from two directions.

**Linguistically**: mockery of revelation always borrows the vocabulary of revelation, names what it pretends not to name, and intensifies what it wants to deny — and in each move, confesses what it is fleeing.

**Thematically**: the human being who laughs at accountability is performing the laugh **because** accountability is the one thing whose denial requires laughter. You can deny much by argument. **Resurrection you can only deny by mockery — because the moment you take it seriously, your life changes.**

## Questions to Carry

1. When something true has been said in a room you are in, and the room responds with a joke — what do you do? Do you join the joke? Stay quiet? Or speak?

2. Whose name do *you* refuse to say? Who has been turned, in your private vocabulary, into *that one*, *some person*, *a man*? What would it mean to give them back their name?

3. Where is the doctrine of resurrection making you secretly uneasy? What one act of yours would you most like to be unseen at the gathering of accounts?

4. If your life were quoted back to you the way Allah quotes the disbelievers in this ayah — with every word preserved exactly, no editing, no softening — what would you wish to take back?

5. What in your life is the equivalent of *muzziqtum kulla mumazzaq* — the thing you have piled up so high in your mind that you have convinced yourself even Allah cannot remake it? And what would it look like to say back to that pile, *innakum la-fī khalqin jadīd*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Mockery of revelation, even at its most confident, is always **borrowing the words of the truth it is fleeing** — and the truth it is fleeing, most often, is the one that would make every other thing it says consequential.

## The Closing Invitation

What should shift after this is small and precise.

The next time you are tempted to dismiss something true with a joke — pause. Notice the move. Notice what your laughter is trying to keep at a distance. **Then ask whether you would rather keep the distance, or close it.**

The next time someone makes a joke at the expense of the sacred — and you are in the room — pause again. Notice what costs you would pay to gently *not laugh*. **Pay one of those costs.** Just one. See what happens.

And the next time you feel the unease of resurrection — the sense that you are accountable, that the case is not closed — **do not turn it into a joke**. Let it stay as a question. The question is a mercy. The question is the door.

## A Du'a from this Ayah

*O Allah — You who quoted Your enemies precisely and let their mockery testify to Your truth — preserve in us the courage to be named by You, even when others refuse to name us. Let our piled-up failures become only a measure of Your remaking. And on the day You assemble what was torn, gather us with Your companion — the one they called only "a man" — and let our names be said by You.*

*Āmīn.*
