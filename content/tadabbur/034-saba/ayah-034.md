# The Tenants Who Forgot the Landlord

## Surah Saba 34:34

> [ayah:34:34] وَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَا فِى قَرْيَةٍ مِّن نَّذِيرٍ إِلَّا قَالَ مُتْرَفُوهَآ إِنَّا بِمَآ أُرْسِلْتُم بِهِۦ كَـٰفِرُونَ

> *Wa mā arsalnā fī qaryatin min nadhīrin illā qāla mutrafūhā innā bimā ursiltum bihi kāfirūn*

> **And We never sent a warner into any town except that its luxury-soaked ones said: "We — in whatever you've been sent with — are disbelievers."**

---

## Introduction

### Before we open the ayah, let's stand somewhere

Imagine you are the Prophet ﷺ in the middle period of the Meccan revelation. You are not a king. You are not surrounded by armies. You walk through the streets of Mecca, and the people who decide whether your community lives or dies, whether your followers eat or starve, whether you yourself will be allowed to keep breathing — those people are not the poor.

The people who decide are the rich.

The chieftains of Quraysh. The men whose caravans go to Syria in summer and to Yemen in winter. The owners of the springs. The protectors of the trade routes. The custodians of the Kaʿba and the keepers of its idols — which, by the way, are *also* a business. Pilgrimage means commerce. Tribal status means contracts. Religion in Mecca, as you stand there, is not separate from the economy. **The economy is the religion.**

And you, Muhammad ﷺ, have arrived with a message that threatens all of it. You are saying: *there is one God.* You are saying: *the orphan matters as much as the chief.* You are saying: *what you have was given to you, and what was given can be taken, and you will stand and answer for it.*

Of course they refuse you.

But here's the thing that's hurting your heart, Messenger of Allah ﷺ. It's not the philosophical disagreement. It's the *pattern* you're starting to notice. The slaves believe. The widows believe. The young people whose fathers haven't yet trapped them in inheritance believe. **It is the comfortable who refuse.** It is always, always the comfortable. And you wonder if this is your failure — if you have somehow not found the right words for the powerful.

It is into this wound that Allah speaks Surah Saba 34:34.

### The Hook

In a single sentence, Allah is going to tell the Prophet ﷺ — and tell us — something that turns history from a series of accidents into a law. He is going to use one word, a word that most translations soften into "the wealthy" or "the affluent," but a word that in Arabic carries a quiet accusation buried inside its very grammar.

The word is **مُتْرَفُوهَا** — *mutrafūhā*. And it is built in the passive.

Stay with that. The passive. Not the people who *made* themselves wealthy. The people who were *made* wealthy. By Whom?

That's where this ayah opens up. Let's go in.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element 1: The universal-negation-exception structure — *mā... illā*

Before we touch the word *mutrafūhā*, we need to look at the shape of the whole sentence. Because Allah is not telling us a story here. He is stating **a law**.

In Arabic, when you want to say something is *always* the case — without a single exception in all of history — you don't say "this is always true." You build a sentence that has two pieces locked together: a total negation, and then an exception.

Let me show you what this sounds like in everyday speech, because we actually use this exact structure in English without noticing.

If I say: "I never go to that restaurant except when my mother visits" — what have I just told you? I have told you two things at once. First: I do not go there. Second: when my mother visits, I do. But the *force* of the sentence isn't on either piece individually. The force is on the **inevitability of the pairing**. My mother visits → I go there. Every single time. No exceptions.

Arabic builds this same structure with two particles: **مَا** *(mā)* — "not" — and **إِلَّا** *(illā)* — "except." Together they create what grammarians call a *restriction*. The sentence says one thing happens, and only one thing, and it happens *every single time* the condition is met.

Now read the ayah again with this in your ears:

**وَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَا فِى قَرْيَةٍۢ مِّن نَّذِيرٍ — إِلَّا قَالَ مُتْرَفُوهَآ**

*"We did not send into any town a warner — EXCEPT that its luxury-soaked ones said..."*

Allah is not telling us a sad fact about most warners. He is telling us about every warner. From Nūḥ to Hūd to Ṣāliḥ to Lūṭ to Shuʿayb to Mūsā to ʿĪsā to Muhammad ﷺ — there has never been a moment in human history where Allah sent a warner into a town and the comfortable people of that town did *not* respond by rejecting him.

Notice something. This isn't an observation. It is **legislation about reality**. Allah is saying: *this is how the social physics of revelation works.* When light enters a settled community, the ones who have the most invested in the darkness shout first.

[PAUSE]

And there's a further subtlety in the wording. Allah doesn't say "a town" definitively — He says **قَرْيَةٍ** *(qaryatin)*, indefinite. *Any* town. He doesn't say "the warner" — He says **نَّذِيرٍ** *(nadhīrin)*, indefinite. *Any* warner. The indefiniteness here is doing real work: Allah is universalizing. **Take any town. Take any warner. Run the experiment. The result will be the same.**

This is the first thing the ayah does. It lifts the Prophet's experience ﷺ out of the particular sting of Mecca and shows him that what he is feeling is the friction of a universal law against his shoulders. Not his failure. **The pattern.**

[PAUSE]

### Element 2: The word مُتْرَفُوهَا — and the silent confession inside its grammar

Now let's go to the word itself. **مُتْرَفُوهَا** — *mutrafūhā*. Most translations give you "its affluent ones" or "its wealthy" or "its rich elite." These are correct, but they hide the thing the Arabic is showing us in broad daylight.

Let's build this word from the inside.

The root is **ت ر ف** *(t-r-f)*. It means luxury, ease, soft living, the kind of abundance that goes beyond need into indulgence. In classical Arabic, *taraf* described the soft tip of a fresh palm shoot — the part that requires no effort to eat. Tender. Easy. **Given to you.**

From this root, Arabic builds a verb in **Form IV** — *atrafa* — which functions transitively. It means "to bestow luxury *upon* someone." Not "to live in luxury." Not "to enjoy luxury." But to *give* luxury to a recipient.

Now here is where it gets stunning.

The word *mutraf* — the form Allah uses here — is the **passive participle** of that Form IV verb. In English grammatical terms, it means "one upon whom luxury has been bestowed." Not one who acquired it. Not one who earned it. Not one who built it. **One who was *given* it.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me here, because this is the heart of the ayah.

When Allah names the people who reject every warner, He does not call them *aghniyāʾ* — the rich. That word exists. He does not call them *mulūk* — the kings. That word exists. He does not call them *kubarāʾ* — the great ones. That word exists.

He calls them **mutrafūn** — the ones-who-were-given-luxury.

In the very name He gives them, **He records the verdict against them.**

Because the passive form has an implied agent. If someone "was given" something, *someone gave it*. Who? Who gave the rich of Mecca their wealth? Who gave Pharaoh his throne? Who gave the chieftains of ʿĀd and Thamūd their gardens and their stone-cut cities? **He did. Allah did.** The same Allah Whom they now refuse to acknowledge.

So when Allah says *mutrafūhā qālū* — "its given-luxury ones said" — He is doing something staggering. He is putting their entire ingratitude into a single grammatical form. He is saying: *the people who turned and said "we reject what you've been sent with" — they are, by their own structure, the recipients of gifts. They cannot even be named without the trace of My giving being on them.*

[PAUSE]

And then there's the suffix. **-hā**. "Its." *Mutrafū-hā* — "her luxury-soaked ones." Her — meaning belonging to the *qaryah*, the town. They are *of the town*. They are not transcendent. They are not above. They are an organ of a particular place. The town's wealth produced them. They will fall with the town.

Look at what Allah has done in one word. He has:
1. Named the rejecters by the very gift they refused to acknowledge.
2. Tied them grammatically to the town that will be judged.
3. Removed any pretense that their position is self-made.

This is not a description. This is a court record.

### Element 3: The mirror — *arsalnā* and *ursiltum*

Now look at how the ayah opens and how it closes.

It opens with **أَرْسَلْنَا** — *arsalnā* — "We sent." Form IV from the root *r-s-l*. Active. First person plural of majesty. Allah Himself, by His own act, sent.

It closes with **أُرْسِلْتُم** — *ursiltum* — "you were sent." The same root, the same Form IV, but now in the **passive**, addressed to the messengers in the plural. "You — plural messengers across history — were sent."

[PAUSE]

Notice what has happened in the structure of the ayah. The word for "sending" appears twice, framing the entire verse. And here is the thing that is so easy to miss:

**The rejecters themselves use the language of sending.**

They don't say "we reject *you*." They say *bimā ursiltum bihi kāfirūn* — "we reject *what you were sent with*." In the very act of refusing the message, they acknowledge that a sending has occurred. They acknowledge that the messengers did not come of their own accord. The passive form *ursiltum* — "you-were-sent" — assumes an agent. Someone sent them.

They know.

That's what the grammar is whispering. **They know.** They know these men did not invent what they brought. They know there is a Sender behind the sent. And yet they declare, in the next breath, *kāfirūn* — "we reject."

This is not the rejection of ignorance. It is the rejection of those who saw and chose anyway.

[PAUSE]

And the framing — *arsalnā* at the start, *ursiltum* at the end — does something else. It surrounds the rejection of the *mutrafūn* with the divine act of sending on both sides. Allah's sending opens the sentence; the messengers' having-been-sent closes it. The rejection of the comfortable is **boxed in** by the sovereignty of the Sender. They speak from the middle, in their pride. But the One Who sent and the act of sending close around them on both sides. They are not in control of the frame. **He is.**

### The bridge between linguistic and thematic

So in one sentence, Allah has shown us three things through the grammar alone:

A **universal law** — *mā... illā* — encoded in the structure of the sentence itself.

A **silent indictment** — *mutrafūhā* — that records the gift inside the name of the ingrate.

A **divine framing** — *arsalnā... ursiltum* — that boxes in the rejecters with the sovereignty of the Sender they refuse to name.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. What is happening in the soul of the *mutraf* that makes him always, always the first to refuse?

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme 1: Why comfort is the most reliable veil

I want you to imagine something.

A man is renting an apartment. For the first year, he is grateful. He thanks the landlord. He pays rent on time. He treats the walls like they belong to someone else. He keeps the place clean. He doesn't drive nails into the plaster without asking.

Year two passes. He is still living there. The rent is automatic now. He doesn't think about the landlord much. He has put up a few pictures. He has started to think of the kitchen as *his* kitchen.

Year five. He has lived in this apartment longer than anywhere else. His friends come over. They call it "your place." He has stopped saying *the* couch and started saying *my* couch — though the couch came with the apartment. He has stopped saying *the* view and started saying *my* view. He has even, without noticing, started to feel a small resentment when the building does maintenance, because they are interrupting *his* life in *his* apartment.

Year ten. The landlord knocks on the door. He says, gently: "I've come to remind you that this is my property, and you are a guest here, and one day you will need to leave."

What does the tenant feel?

If the tenant is honest, he feels **outrage**.

How dare you. How dare you come into *my* home and tell me I don't own it. I have lived here. I have made this place what it is. The pictures on the wall are mine. The carpet I picked. The plants I watered. **Who are you to tell me this isn't mine?**

The longer the tenancy, the deeper the forgetting. The deeper the forgetting, the more violent the reaction when the landlord arrives.

[PAUSE]

This is the spiritual physics of the *mutrafūn*. Allah gave them a town. He gave them caravans. He gave them gold and slaves and palaces and the springs of Mecca and the bones of their ancestors buried in the soil. He gave them every single thing. And for so long that they forgot it was given. They began to say *my* wealth. *My* status. *My* tribe. *My* honor. And when the warner arrived to remind them — *you are tenants here, and the Landlord is coming* — they didn't hear a reminder. They heard a threat. They heard a trespasser. They heard someone trying to take away what was theirs.

**That is why the *mutrafūn* always reject first.** Not because they are stupid. Not because they haven't heard the argument. They reject first because they have the most to forget. They have lived in the apartment the longest. Their hands are gripping the door frame the hardest.

The poor man who hears the warner has nothing in his hand to grip. He looks at the warner and says: *yes. Of course. I always knew this wasn't mine.* The slave looks at the warner and says: *yes. There is a Master. I have always served one — what difference does it make that He is now named?*

But the chief? The chief has spent forty years pretending to be the master. And you cannot, in one sermon, undo forty years of practiced sovereignty.

[PAUSE]

So here is the question the ayah pushes into your chest:

**Where in your life have you been a tenant long enough that you've started to believe you own the place?**

Your body. Your time. Your children. Your intelligence. Your reputation. Your accumulated savings. Your beauty. Your good health. Your network of friendships. Your professional skill. Your tribe. Your country.

How much of what you call *mine* was given to you in a form so steady that you stopped noticing the giving?

And when the warner comes — in the form of an ayah, in the form of a sudden loss, in the form of a sickness that reminds you that even your cells are on loan — **do you greet him? Or do you greet him the way the *mutrafūn* did?**

The ayah is not just about seventh-century Mecca. The ayah is a mirror. *Mutraf* is a spiritual category, not a tax bracket. You can be poor by the world's standards and still be a *mutraf* — if you've started believing that the small thing you have is yours by right.

And you can be wealthy and *not* be a *mutraf* — if every time you look at what you have, you feel the trembling gratitude of someone who knows the keys belong to Someone else.

**The question is not how much you have. The question is whether you remember it was given.**

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Tomorrow, when you wake up, before you open your phone, before you check your messages, sit for sixty seconds and name three things in your life you have stopped feeling gratitude for. Three things you have started saying "mine" about without trembling.

Then name the Giver of each one.

Do this every morning for one week. Not because gratitude is a self-help technique. Because forgetting is the disease the ayah is diagnosing in you, and remembering is the only treatment.

### Theme 2: The consolation buried in the indictment

There is a second voice in this ayah, and it is speaking to a different person. It is not speaking to the *mutrafūn*. It is speaking to the warner.

Imagine you are the Prophet ﷺ. You have been calling people for years. Some have come. Many have refused. Some have spat at you. Some have thrown stones at your feet. Some have plotted to kill you. And the ones who have caused you the most pain are the ones with the most influence — the chiefs, the wealthy, the men who could have, with one nod, opened the doors of Mecca to your message.

And you, being a perfect human, ﷺ, sometimes look at this in the privacy of your own heart and wonder: *what could I have said differently? Did I phrase something wrong? Is there a version of me that would have reached them?*

This is the wound. This is what Allah is addressing.

Allah does not say: *Muhammad, you're doing fine, don't worry about it.* Allah does something deeper. Allah lifts the camera up off the rooftops of Mecca and shows the Prophet ﷺ the whole sweep of human history. He says: *look. Look at every prophet I have ever sent. Look at every town that has ever existed. Look at the pattern.*

**"We did not send into any town a warner — except that its luxury-soaked ones said: we reject what you were sent with."**

[PAUSE]

What is Allah doing here?

He is telling the Prophet ﷺ — and through him, every person who has ever tried to call others to truth and failed — *this is not your failure. This is the law.*

Nūḥ called his people for nine hundred and fifty years. The *mutrafūn* refused him. Hūd called ʿĀd, who had built pillars no one had seen the like of. The *mutrafūn* refused him. Ṣāliḥ called Thamūd, who had carved palaces from mountains. The *mutrafūn* refused him. Mūsā walked into the court of Pharaoh — the most *mutraf* of all *mutrafūn* — and Pharaoh said: *we reject what you've been sent with.*

You, Muhammad ﷺ, are not deficient. **You are continuous.** You are inside a chain of warners stretching back through every generation, and the chain has always been met with the same refusal at the same address. The mansion door. The palace gate. The chieftain's tent.

There is no version of you that would have been accepted by them. **They are not refusing you. They are refusing the One Who sent you. And He has been refused before.**

[PAUSE]

I want to sit with what this does to the heart of someone who has tried to call others — to faith, to truth, to a better way — and failed.

Maybe you are a parent whose adult child has walked away from the dīn, and you have spent years agonizing about what you said wrong.

Maybe you are a teacher whose student keeps slipping, and you wonder if you taught the lesson well enough.

Maybe you are someone whose spouse refuses to pray, refuses to listen, refuses to soften, and you have begged Allah for them in every prostration.

Maybe you are a daʿwah worker, or an imam, or just a friend who once tried to share the dīn with someone you loved, and you felt the door close on you and you walked home feeling like a failure.

To every one of you, the ayah is speaking. **The pattern is older than you.** The people you are trying to reach — if they have something in this world that they are gripping with both hands — they will not let go because you spoke beautifully. They will let go because Allah opens their fingers. And until He does, your job is not to succeed. Your job is to *warn*. The warning, not the result, is what was assigned to you.

This is liberating. Because if your job were to succeed, you would be crushed by every rejection. But if your job is to warn — to deliver — then every rejection is the *recipient's* problem, not the warner's. You have done what you were sent to do. The rest is between them and Allah.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes about tomorrow

Tomorrow, if there is someone in your life you have been trying to call to good, and you have felt the weight of their refusal sitting in your chest — release it. Make duʿāʾ for them. Continue to call them, gently, when the moment opens. But stop carrying their rejection as if it were your debt.

The Prophet ﷺ did not carry the rejection of Quraysh as his debt. Allah took the weight off his shoulders by showing him the pattern. Let Allah do the same for you. **The pattern is ancient. The rejection is theirs. The warning is yours. The opening of hearts is His.**

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing it together

Look at what we have found.

The grammar of the ayah — the *mā... illā* structure — encoded a **universal law**, not a particular complaint. Allah was not describing Mecca. He was describing the social physics of every town that has ever heard a warner.

The word *mutrafūhā* — passive participle, recipient of luxury — encoded an **indictment inside a name**. The very label Allah gives the rejecters records the gift they refused to acknowledge. You cannot pronounce their identity without conjuring the Giver they denied.

The mirror of *arsalnā* and *ursiltum* — Allah's sending at the start, the messengers' having-been-sent at the end — **framed the rejection between two acts of divine sovereignty**. The rejecters spoke from the middle. But the One Who sent surrounds them.

And these three grammatical features pointed to two unseen realities:

**One**, that comfort is the most reliable veil — that the longer one is given, the more one forgets the giving, and the more one forgets, the more violently one resists the reminder.

**Two**, that the rejection a warner faces is not his failure — it is the friction of a universal law against his shoulders, and the One Who set the law is also the One Who comforts him.

These are not two separate insights. They are the same insight from two directions. The *mutrafūn* reject because comfort makes you forget. The warner is consoled because remembering you are inside a pattern lifts the weight of personal failure. **The same forgetting that condemns one, releases the other.**

### Questions to carry

1. Where in your life have you started saying "mine" about something you originally received with trembling?

2. If a warner came to your door today, what specifically would you need to give up — what comfort, what status, what arrangement — to listen to him fully? And does the size of that thing tell you anything about why the *mutrafūn* of every age have refused?

3. Is there someone you have been trying to call to good, whose rejection you have been carrying as your own failure? What would it mean to release that and trust the pattern?

4. The passive form *mutraf* records the Giver inside the name of the ingrate. If you were named today by the structure of your relationship to your blessings, what name would Allah give you?

5. The poor and the slaves believed the warners first. They had nothing to grip. What in your life are you gripping that you could quietly, today, begin to loosen?

### One-sentence distillation

**The loudest rejection of every warner has always come from those whom Allah Himself most lavishly blessed — because the soul that has been given much, for long enough, begins to mistake the gift for an entitlement, and entitlement cannot tolerate a Master.**

### The closing invitation

If after sitting with this ayah you walk away with one thing, let it be this:

The disease the ayah is diagnosing is not wealth. It is forgetting that wealth was given. And the cure is not poverty. The cure is **remembering**.

You can be a multimillionaire and not be a *mutraf*, if every dollar in your hand passes through the awareness that it is loaned. You can have nothing in this world and *be* a *mutraf*, if the small things you have are gripped in your fist as if they were yours by right.

This week, find one thing in your life — one specific, named thing — that you have been quietly possessing as if it were not given. Maybe it's your morning coffee. Maybe it's your spouse. Maybe it's your ability to walk up the stairs. And every time you encounter it this week, before you use it, say internally: *this was given. This can be taken. Thank you, my Lord.*

Do this until it stops being a script and becomes a way of seeing. That is the antidote to *taraf*. Not deprivation. **Recognition.**

### Duʿāʾ

Yā Allah, You Who gave before we asked, and Who gave still when we forgot to thank — protect us from the forgetting that lives inside long-given gifts. Make our hands hold what You have given us the way a guest holds the keys of a borrowed house: gratefully, lightly, and ready to return them. And when Your warners come to us in any form — an ayah, a loss, a moment of clarity — open our doors to them, not the way the *mutrafūn* opened theirs, but the way the slaves and the widows and the children of every prophet's town opened theirs. Āmīn.
