# Surah Saba 34:35 — The Argument from Abundance

## The Ayah

**Arabic:**
[ayah:34:35] وَقَالُوا۟ نَحْنُ أَكْثَرُ أَمْوَٰلًا وَأَوْلَـٰدًا وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُعَذَّبِينَ

**Transliteration:**
Wa qālū naḥnu aktharu amwālan wa awlādan wa mā naḥnu bi-muʿadhdhabīn

**Translation:**
"And they said: 'We have more wealth and children, and we will not be punished.'"

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuild the World

Before we touch a single word, let's place ourselves inside the moment this ayah is describing.

A messenger has been sent. He has stood in the marketplace, in the gathering places, in the courts of his people. He has said something simple and devastating: *there is a Day. There is an accounting. The way you are living matters.*

And every time — **every time** — a particular kind of voice rises to answer him. Not the voice of the poor. Not the voice of the broken. Not the voice of those who have nothing to lose.

The voice that always rises against the messenger is the voice of those who have the most.

The Quran has a name for them. It calls them **al-mutrafūn** — "those who live in soft affluence." The ones whose tables are full. Whose houses are large. Whose children are many. Whose name carries weight in the city.

And Surah Saba, just two ayahs before ours, has already told us the pattern. In 34:34, Allah says: *"We never sent a warner to a town except that its mutrafūn said: We disbelieve in what you have been sent with."*

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah is doing. He's not describing one moment. He's describing a **pattern across all of human history**. Every time. Every messenger. Every town. The same people stand up and say the same thing.

And then comes our ayah — 34:35 — which gives us the **argument** they make. The reason they believe their rejection is justified. The theology that lets them sleep at night.

It is one sentence. Eight words in Arabic. And inside those eight words lives one of the oldest, most seductive, most spiritually toxic arguments that human beings have ever made against a prophet.

### The Hook

Here's what I want you to sit with before we begin.

The argument in this ayah is not a primitive one. It is not the argument of someone who hasn't thought. It is the argument of someone who has thought *very carefully* — and arrived at a conclusion that feels so obvious to them, so backed by the evidence of their own lives, that they cannot understand why anyone would disagree.

And here is what should disturb us: **this argument is alive in the world right now.** It is being made in boardrooms, in pulpits of certain prosperity preachers, in the private justifications of people in every faith and no faith. It is being made, sometimes, in our own hearts.

What is the argument exactly? How does it work? Why is it so durable? And what is the surgical thing this ayah does to expose it?

That's what we're here to discover.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three linguistic doorways into this ayah, and each one opens onto something different. Let's take them one at a time.

---

### Element One: The Comparative without a Comparison — **أَكْثَرُ** (aktharu)

#### Teaching the concept first

In every language, there is a difference between saying *"I have a lot"* and saying *"I have more."*

Imagine two scenes. In the first, a man tells you: *"I have a lot of money."* He is describing himself. He's looking at his own bank account. He might be grateful. He might be content. He's making a statement about his own situation.

Now picture the second scene. The same man says: *"I have more money."*

Stop. Something has changed. The moment he uses the word **"more,"** another person has walked into the sentence. There is now a *you* somewhere in the picture — someone he is being measured against. **"More"** is never a solo word. It always brings a second party with it, even when that second party is unnamed.

This is a structural feature of Arabic too. The form **أَكْثَرُ** — *aktharu* — is what grammarians call *ism al-tafḍīl*, the noun of preference. It is a comparative. It cannot stand alone. It demands an *other*.

Hold onto that. Now let's enter the ayah.

#### Opening the word

Allah does not record them saying *naḥnu kathīrū al-amwāl* — "we are abundant in wealth." That would have been a self-description.

He records them saying: **نَحْنُ أَكْثَرُ أَمْوَٰلًا** — *"We are more in wealth."*

Let's sit with this.

The moment they open their mouths, they have not actually said anything about themselves. They have positioned themselves *against* the messenger and his followers. The believers walk into the sentence the second the word *aktharu* is spoken. Invisible, unnamed, but present.

Notice something. This is not a verse about a self-satisfied man marveling at his own gardens. The Quran has those verses too — and they are very different in tone. This verse is about a man whose entire sense of his own worth is built on the fact that *somebody else has less.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. In English we have a phrase: *"keeping up with the Joneses."* It describes a person whose happiness depends not on what they have, but on having *more than the neighbor*. Modern psychologists have a term for it: **positional goods**. A positional good is something whose value to you comes not from the thing itself, but from your rank relative to others. A bigger house is not just a bigger house — it's a *bigger-than-his* house.

The mutrafūn in this ayah are not describing wealth. They are describing **rank**. They are not satisfied. They are *winning*.

And the word *aktharu* is the linguistic signature of a heart that cannot stop measuring itself against others.

Stay with that for a moment. Because the disease this ayah is diagnosing begins not with greed, but with **comparison**.

[PAUSE]

#### The deeper layer

Now ask yourself something even harder. If their argument is *theological* — if they're trying to prove that they are favored by Allah — why does their evidence need a comparison at all?

If Allah loves you, He loves you. That's not a relative claim. That's an absolute one.

But notice: their proof requires that *someone else have less.* Their certainty about their own standing with Allah is parasitic on the deprivation of others.

This is what makes the argument so spiritually dangerous. It cannot exist in a vacuum. It needs a poor man to point at. It needs a believer with fewer children. It needs a community of the dispossessed in the background of the photograph, so that the wealthy man in the foreground can stand taller by comparison.

What this invites us to consider: **whenever your sense of being loved by Allah depends on someone else looking lesser than you, the love you think you're feeling is not from Allah. It's from your nafs.**

---

### Element Two: The Pairing — **أَمْوَٰلًا وَأَوْلَـٰدًا** (Wealth and Children)

#### Teaching the concept first

In every language, certain pairs of words travel together. *Bread and butter. Health and happiness. Time and money.* When two words are constantly paired in a culture's speech, it means the culture has decided that these two things, together, form something complete. A single unit of well-being.

Arabic has many such pairs. And there is one pair that recurs throughout the Quran like a refrain. It appears here in 34:35. It appears in Sūrat al-Kahf when the man boasts of his garden. It appears in Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn warning believers not to let it distract them. It appears in Sūrat al-Tawbah describing the deceptive favor given to the disbelievers.

**أَمْوَٰلٌ وَأَوْلَادٌ** — *amwāl wa awlād* — *wealth and children.*

Why these two? Why this pair?

#### Opening the words

Let's think about what each one actually does in a human life.

**Wealth — *amwāl*** — from the root م-و-ل. The root carries the meaning of *that which sustains and supports*. Wealth is **horizontal** security. It spreads out across your present life. It says: *I am safe today. I am safe this month. I have what I need.* Wealth is the security of *now*.

**Children — *awlād*** — from the root و-ل-د, the root of birth and descent. Children are **vertical** security. They extend across time. They are the part of you that survives your death. In a tribal society especially, your children are your name continuing. Your blood line. Your insurance against being forgotten. Children are the security of *forever*.

Put them together. *Amwāl wa awlād.* **Wealth and children.** The security of *now* plus the security of *forever*. Horizontal protection plus vertical protection. Together they form, in the human imagination, **complete coverage**. Total insulation. Nothing can touch you.

[PAUSE]

This is why these two words travel together. Between them, they cover every dimension of human anxiety. If you have wealth, you don't fear today. If you have children, you don't fear tomorrow. If you have both — **what is left to be afraid of?**

That is exactly the position the mutrafūn are speaking from. They are not just rich. They are *comprehensively secured*. They have plugged every hole through which fear might enter their lives.

And from that position of completeness, they look at the messenger and his ragged band of poor followers and they think: *"What can this Day you keep speaking of possibly do to me? I have built walls on every side."*

#### The deeper layer

Now notice something about the Quran's response, here and elsewhere.

When Allah confronts this argument, He doesn't tell the mutrafūn that wealth and children are *bad*. Wealth is a gift. Children are a gift. He is the One who gives them.

What He confronts is the **mistake of treating them as proof**.

Wealth proves you can buy things. It does not prove Allah loves you.
Children prove your lineage continues. They do not prove Allah is pleased.

This is the great categorical confusion at the heart of the mutraf's argument. He has taken something that exists in the realm of **provision** (*rizq*) — which Allah gives to whomever He wills, righteous or wicked, beloved or distant — and he has imported it into the realm of **standing** (*manzilah*) — which is determined by faith and deeds, not by bank balances and bloodlines.

It is a category error. And the Quran will name it directly in just a moment, when ayah 34:37 explodes the argument: *"It is not your wealth or your children that bring you near to Us..."*

But before we get there, the architecture of this ayah is itself doing the work of exposure. By recording the mutrafūn pairing these two words, Allah is showing us the **complete logic of their false security**. They think they have covered every base. They have not. The base they have left exposed is the only one that ever mattered.

[PAUSE]

What this invites us to consider: **the things that make you feel safest are often the things that distract you most from the only real safety there is.**

---

### Element Three: The Double Denial — **وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُعَذَّبِينَ** (And we will not be punished)

#### Teaching the concept first

In English, when we want to negate something strongly, we add words. *I am not going. I am absolutely not going. There is no way I am going.* Each version is stronger than the last.

Arabic has a different mechanism for emphasis. It can negate something with a single word: *lā* or *mā*. But when it wants to *intensify* a negation — when it wants to slam the door — it doesn't add more words. It does something subtler. It inserts the particle **bi-** before the negated thing. The "bi" of emphasis. The *bāʾ al-zāʾidah*, as the grammarians call it. The "extra" *bāʾ*.

It does not translate. You cannot point to a word in English that captures it. But what it does is make the negation feel **complete, settled, irreversible**.

Compare two ways the sentence could have been built:
- **وَمَا نَحْنُ مُعَذَّبِينَ** — "And we are not punished ones."
- **وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُعَذَّبِينَ** — "And we are *by no means* punished ones."

Same words. One small extra particle. But the second one is **the door slamming shut.**

The first version describes a state. The second version *forecloses a possibility*. It says: *this is not on the table. This is not even a question. This is settled.*

#### Opening the words

Now look at what Allah has them saying. He records them not in the lighter form but in the heavier form. **وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمُعَذَّبِينَ.** They are not merely denying punishment. They are **refusing to entertain the possibility** of punishment.

Stay with me on this.

A man who says "I don't think I'll be punished" is still a man inside a conversation. He is offering an opinion. He could be persuaded. He has left the door cracked open.

A man who says "I will *by no means* be punished" — using the language of foreclosure — has done something different. He has decided that the question is *not even worth asking*. He is not arguing against a possibility. He is denying it the dignity of being a possibility.

That is the spiritual posture this small particle reveals. Total certainty. Total closure. Total contempt for the question itself.

[PAUSE]

And now look at the *form* of the word **مُعَذَّبِينَ** (*muʿadhdhabīn*). The root is ʿ-dh-b. But the form Allah uses here — confirmed by the morphological corpus — is the **Form II passive participle**. Form II in Arabic intensifies the root. It is the form that means *repeatedly, thoroughly, severely*.

*Muʿadhdhab* is not just "one who is punished." It is "one who is *thoroughly subjected to punishment*." The form itself carries weight, intensity, repetition.

So watch what they're actually saying. They are using the heaviest form of the verb — the form that means *severe, repeated, comprehensive punishment* — and they are slamming the door on it with the heaviest form of negation. They are not saying "we won't get a slap." They are saying **"the severest, most thorough punishment is by no means coming for us."**

This is theology stated as bravado. The strongest possible "no" attached to the strongest possible "yes" — *you will be thoroughly punished* — that the messenger had warned them about.

#### The deeper layer

Now ask yourself: where does this certainty come from?

They are not prophets. They have not received revelation that they are safe. They have no scripture in their hand saying *the wealthy of this town are exempt.* What is the source of their certainty?

The first half of the ayah tells you. **"We have more wealth and children."** That is their evidence. That is their entire epistemology of salvation.

The logic, made explicit, runs like this:

1. Allah gives blessings to those He loves.
2. We have many blessings.
3. Therefore Allah loves us.
4. Therefore we will not be punished.

It looks like a syllogism. It feels like reasoning. But it has a hole in it the size of the universe.

[PAUSE]

The hole is premise one.

Allah gives blessings to those He loves — *and to those He is testing, and to those He is gradually leading away, and to those whose punishment He is increasing by giving them more rope.* The classical tafsīr tradition — al-Qurtubi and al-Baghawi here — both make this point. Al-Baghawi paraphrases the mutrafūn's logic exactly: *"If Allah were not pleased with our religion and our deeds, He would not have entrusted us with wealth and children."* They have built a complete theology on a premise they never proved.

The Quran will respond elsewhere with the concept of **istidrāj** — being drawn gradually toward destruction by ever-increasing favors. *"We will lead them on from where they do not know."* Sometimes the cup keeps filling not because Allah is pleased, but because He has decreed that they should drink it to the dregs.

But the mutrafūn cannot see this. Their certainty has foreclosed the question. The double denial — *mā naḥnu bi-muʿadhdhabīn* — is not the conclusion of an argument. It is the **refusal to allow the argument to take place**.

What this invites us to consider: **the most dangerous spiritual state is not doubt. It is the certainty that has stopped asking questions.**

---

### Bridge

Three words. *Aktharu.* The comparative that exposes a heart addicted to comparison. *Amwālan wa awlādan.* The pair that reveals a soul trying to plug every hole except the one that matters. *Bi-muʿadhdhabīn.* The slammed door of a certainty that has stopped reasoning.

Together, these eight words give us an X-ray of a particular kind of soul. Not the soul of a rebel. Not the soul of an atheist. The soul of a person who *believes in Allah*, who *thinks Allah is on his side*, and who has used that belief to wall himself off from the only message that could have saved him.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Mistake of Reading Outcomes as Verdicts

Let's start somewhere everyone has been.

You apply for a job. You get it. *Alhamdulillah, Allah loves me.*
A friend applies for a job. They don't get it. *Allah must have something better.*

You marry. *Allah honored me.*
A friend stays single past thirty. *Allah is testing them.*

You make money. *Barakah.*
Someone loses their business. *Did they do something wrong?*

Stop. Notice what we just did.

We took two outcomes — one good, one painful — and we did something subtle and ugly with them. We treated **the good outcome as a verdict from Allah in our favor**, and **the painful outcome as a verdict against the other person.**

That is the mutraf's logic. We have just performed it on a small scale, in our own heart, about our own life.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most universal human habits. The intellect knows that bad things happen to good people and that good things happen to bad people — we say this all the time, we read it in books, we observe it in the world. And yet, the moment something good happens to *us*, we forget every example we have ever seen and immediately interpret it as a personal endorsement from the Lord of the Worlds. And the moment something painful happens to *someone else*, we look for what they must have done.

It is the mutraf's argument running on lower voltage. But it is the same circuit.

What the ayah is showing us is that this logic, when it metastasizes, ends in catastrophe. The mutraf is not someone who occasionally makes this mistake. The mutraf is someone whose **entire life** is governed by this mistake. He has read every outcome as a verdict. He has read his wealth as proof of his goodness. He has read his children as proof of his standing. He has read the poverty of the believers as proof of their inferiority. And he has read all of this as proof that the Day the messenger warns about will not touch him.

Outcomes were never meant to be read as verdicts. Outcomes are *tests*. Sometimes the test is the gift. Sometimes the test is the loss. The verdict is reserved for a Day that has not yet come.

The classical scholars, including al-Qurtubi commenting directly on this ayah, made this point with a precision worth dwelling on. He notes that the mutrafūn argued: *"If our Lord were not pleased with the religion and excellence we are upon, He would not have entrusted us with all of this."* And Allah's response, beginning in the next ayah, is the dismantling of this exact category error.

What does it look like to walk free of this in our own lives?

It looks like learning to receive a blessing **with trembling**. Not with the smug satisfaction of one who has been validated, but with the awareness of one who has been *tested*. *"O Allah, You have given me this. I do not know what You mean by it. Help me not to mistake Your generosity for Your approval."*

It looks like learning to look at the suffering of another and **refuse to convert it into a verdict**. To say: *I do not know why this is in their life. I do not know what it is doing in them. The one thing I do know is that my comfort gives me no right to assess their soul.*

It looks like a daily practice of **uncoupling outcomes from verdicts**. Every time a good thing happens, ask: *what is this testing in me?* Every time a painful thing happens to someone else, ask: *what is this asking of me — to help, to pray, to not look away?*

That is the beginning of escape from the mutraf's argument. Not by becoming poor. Not by despising your blessings. But by **refusing to confuse what you have with where you stand.**

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Architecture of False Safety

Now let's go somewhere harder.

We live in a culture that is *obsessed* with safety. Insurance. Investment portfolios. Diversified assets. Retirement accounts. Estate planning. Generational wealth. We build elaborate financial structures whose purpose, when you peel back the layers, is the same purpose the mutraf had: *to plug every hole through which fear could enter our lives.*

Now hear me carefully. The Quran is not against planning. It is not against saving. It is not against providing for your children. These are good things. The Prophet ﷺ himself emphasized leaving one's heirs independent rather than impoverished.

What the Quran is exposing here is something different. It is exposing the *spiritual posture* that grows in a person when they believe their structures of safety have actually *succeeded*.

Let me name it directly. There is a state of mind that the mutraf has reached, and that many of us are reaching, in which the question *"what if I am wrong about my standing with God?"* has stopped being a live question. Not because we have resolved it. But because we have buried it under so many layers of horizontal and vertical security that it can no longer reach us.

[PAUSE]

This is what *amwālan wa awlādan* — wealth and children — does when it is unaccompanied by *taqwā*. It does not just provide for you. It **anesthetizes** you.

There is a beautiful, terrifying ḥadīth in which the Prophet ﷺ said that the things he feared most for his ummah were not poverty, but the *opposite*: that the world would be opened up for them as it had been for those before them, and that they would compete in it as those before them had competed, and that it would destroy them as it had destroyed those before them.

He did not fear our deprivation. He feared our *comfort*.

Because comfort, when it is total, has a specific spiritual effect. It does not just make you forget Allah. It makes you forget *that you could forget Allah*. It removes the trembling. It removes the asking. It removes the urgency. It builds a soft, padded room around the heart, and inside that room, the voice of the messenger — *there is a Day, there is an accounting* — sounds quaint. Almost embarrassing. The kind of thing the poor and the desperate need, but not us. We're fine.

That is the mutraf's exact posture. And many of us — myself first — are closer to it than we want to admit.

So what do we do?

We do something the mutraf could not bring himself to do. We **let the question back in**. We say: *Yā Rabb, all of this could be istidrāj. All of this could be You giving me more rope. I cannot read the gifts in my life as verdicts in my favor. I cannot use my comfort as evidence that I am safe.*

We let our **dhikr** include the possibility that we are wrong. Not in a paralyzing way. In a softening way. The way a person walks more carefully when they know the ground might be unstable.

We re-introduce the trembling that the mutraf has anesthetized. Not constant fear — that is not what the Prophet ﷺ taught. But a posture of *not knowing*. A refusal to claim a verdict that has not been issued.

Here is a specific practical application. The next time you find yourself instinctively reading a good thing in your life as a sign that *Allah is pleased with you* — pause. Catch the thought. Replace it with a different thought: *Allah has given me this. I do not know what He means by it. Let me not be one of those who confused His gifts with His approval.*

That single substitution, practiced enough times, **rewires the circuit** that this ayah is exposing.

It is small. It is silent. No one will see you do it.

But it is the difference between the mutraf and the muʾmin.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at what we have found in these eight words.

The grammar gave us a soul addicted to comparison — *aktharu*.
The pairing gave us a soul that has bought complete insurance against fear — *amwālan wa awlādan*.
The double denial gave us a soul that has stopped allowing the most important question to be asked — *bi-muʿadhdhabīn*.

The themes gave us the mistake of reading outcomes as verdicts, and the architecture of a false safety that anesthetizes the trembling soul.

These are not separate findings. They are **one finding from two directions**.

The linguistic discoveries showed us the *anatomy* of the mutraf — what he is made of, word by word. The thematic depths showed us the *life* of the mutraf — how this anatomy expresses itself in the way a person lives, day by day.

The ayah is a portrait. The portrait has a frame (the comparative *aktharu*), a body (the wealth-and-children pair), and a slammed door behind it (the double denial). And inside the frame is a face we have seen before. It is the face of every prosperous rejecter in human history. And sometimes, when the light is right, it is a face we glimpse in our own mirror.

### Questions to Carry

1. When something good happens to you, what is the *first* sentence that forms in your mind about what it means? Is it gratitude — or is it verdict?

2. Whose "lack" are you using, quietly, to confirm your own "enough"? Whose absence of something has become, in your mental map, a sign of your superiority?

3. What are the "amwāl" and "awlād" of your life — the structures of horizontal and vertical safety you have built? Are any of them whispering to you that the Day is no longer a live question?

4. When was the last time the possibility *"what if I am wrong about my standing with Allah?"* genuinely visited your heart? What does it mean that it has been so long?

5. If Allah suddenly removed the comforts you read as proof of His favor — would your love for Him survive, or was the love always for the comforts?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The mutraf's argument is the eternal temptation of the comfortable to mistake the gifts of Allah for the verdict of Allah, and to use that mistake to slam shut the door through which a messenger was sent to save them.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to become poor. It is not asking you to feel guilty about your blessings. It is not asking you to interpret every gift in your life as a punishment in disguise.

It is asking you to **uncouple two things** that the mutraf has fused together: *what you have* and *where you stand*.

It is asking you to receive your wealth and your children — and your health, and your safety, and your ease — as **gifts**, not as **verdicts**. To love the Giver more than the gifts. And to let the question *"what if I am wrong?"* live somewhere in your heart, not as a paralysis, but as a softening — the very softening that the mutraf in this ayah has anesthetized himself against.

This is one of the smallest distances in the universe and one of the largest. Between the mutraf and the muʾmin, with the same amount of wealth and the same number of children, the only difference is **what they believe their abundance means**.

May we be of those who, when asked *what does your wealth prove?*, answer: *that my Lord is generous — not that I am safe.*

### Du'a

Yā Rabb, You have given us more than we have earned and more than we deserve. Do not let us mistake Your generosity for Your approval. Do not let us slam shut the door of trembling that the mutrafūn slammed shut. Keep alive in our hearts the question they refused to ask, so that we may live as those who *hope* in Your mercy rather than as those who *assume* it. Āmīn.
