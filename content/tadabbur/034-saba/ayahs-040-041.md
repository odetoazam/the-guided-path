# Surah Saba 34:40–41 — The Courtroom Where the Accused Never Speaks

---

## Introduction

Before we open this ayah, I want to take you somewhere.

Imagine a courtroom. The judge has called everyone in. The accused is standing there — silent. The judge does not turn to the accused. The judge turns to a third party in the room — the one the accused has spent his whole life invoking, defending, claiming to follow. And the judge asks that third party, in front of the accused, a single question:

*"Did this man ever speak for you?"*

The third party answers — not the accused. The accused stands there as the conversation about his life happens over his head. He has no voice recorded in this exchange. The Quran gives him none. The classical commentators — al-Tabari, al-Muyassar, Ibn Kathir — read the scene as *tawbikh*: divine rebuke directed *at* the mushrikeen *through* this very interrogation. Al-Muyassar is explicit: Allah poses the question "on the grounds of rebuke for those who worshipped them." The rebuke is structured so that it happens over the head of the one being rebuked — he is the subject of the scene, not a participant in it. The two parties speaking are the Judge and the witness. The accused is what they are speaking about.

That is the scene Allah is showing us.

Listen to the Arabic.

> وَيَوْمَ يَحْشُرُهُمْ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ يَقُولُ لِلْمَلَـٰٓئِكَةِ أَهَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ إِيَّاكُمْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْبُدُونَ ۝ قَالُوا۟ سُبْحَـٰنَكَ أَنتَ وَلِيُّنَا مِن دُونِهِم ۖ بَلْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْبُدُونَ ٱلْجِنَّ ۖ أَكْثَرُهُم بِهِم مُّؤْمِنُونَ

*Wa-yawma yaḥshuruhum jamīʿan, thumma yaqūlu lil-malāʾikati a-hāʾulāʾi iyyākum kānū yaʿbudūn. Qālū subḥānaka anta waliyyunā min dūnihim, bal kānū yaʿbudūna l-jinna, aktharuhum bihim muʾminūn.*

*"And the Day He will gather them all, then He will say to the angels: 'Was it you these used to worship?' They will say: 'Glory be to You! You are our protector — not them. Rather, they used to worship the jinn. Most of them were believers in them.'"*

Step back into Surah Saba for a moment, because this scene does not arrive out of nowhere. This whole surah has been building one argument. Earlier in the surah Allah turns to the people who worship anything besides Him and says — *qulil-d'ū lladhīna zaʿamtum* — "call those you claim besides Allah. They don't own an atom's weight in the heavens or the earth. They have no share in either. He has no helper among them." The surah keeps returning to one challenge: name what you worship besides Me, and tell me what it actually owns.

The audience for that challenge — the Quraysh and the broader pagan Arabian world — worshipped, among other things, the angels. They called the angels Allah's daughters. They believed the angels were intermediaries — closer to Allah than they could ever be, and so worth invoking. The argument felt sophisticated to them. They weren't atheists. They weren't even denying Allah. They were just adding a layer.

Ibn Kathir notes the precise form of that layer: they claimed their idols were made in the image of the angels, and that through these forms the angels could bring them nearer to Allah. It was a theology of access — not raw polytheism but a mediated system. Noble beings, noble goals, a constructed architecture of closeness.

And these two ayahs are the final scene in Allah's response to that whole logic. The Day comes. The worshippers are gathered. The angels are summoned. And then — the question.

[PAUSE]

Here is what we are going to discover together. The strangest fact about this courtroom is not what is said. It is that the accused has no voice in it — the Quran records his silence here, and that silence within the structure of *tawbikh* is itself a kind of answer. And the strangest word in the angels' response is not the word you would expect. It is a word that sounds like praise, but that al-Tabari reads as simultaneously a declaration of transcendence *and* a disavowal — a clearing of Allah from everything the mushrikeen had attributed to Him.

Let's earn it.

## Part 1 — The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The Question That Isn't About Worship

Before I show you what Allah's question actually does, I want you to do something with me in plain English first.

Imagine two ways of asking the same thing.

The first: *"Were these people worshipping you?"*

The second: *"Was it **you** these people were worshipping?"*

Read them again. They look like the same question. They are not.

The first question is about an action — was worship happening, yes or no. The second question is about an *addressee* — assuming worship was happening, was it directed to *you* specifically?

In English we feel that shift by where the stress falls. We pronounce the word "you" with weight, and suddenly the whole question changes its target. We move from asking about the existence of an act to asking about who the act was aimed at.

Arabic does this not with stress but with *position*. It moves words. When Arabic wants to put pressure on a specific element in a sentence — wants to make that element the actual center of the question, not a side detail — it pulls that element forward, out of its expected slot, and puts it in front.

Now look at what Allah does.

> أَهَـٰٓؤُلَآءِ إِيَّاكُمْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْبُدُونَ

*A-hāʾulāʾi iyyākum kānū yaʿbudūn.*

The normal Arabic word order for "Were these worshipping you?" would put the verb first and the object pronoun attached or after it. The natural shape would be something like *a-kānū yaʿbudūnakum hāʾulāʾi* — "were these worshipping you." But that is not what Allah said. Allah took the word **إِيَّاكُمْ — *iyyākum*, "you"** — and pulled it forward. Out of its expected place. He placed it *before* the verb كَانُوا يَعْبُدُونَ, "they were worshipping."

The construction we end up with is closer to: *"Was it **you** specifically these people were worshipping?"*

And I want us to stay with this, because it changes everything.

Notice something. Allah does not ask the angels *did worship happen*. Allah does not ask *were these people sincere*. Allah does not ask *did they pray, did they call out, did they sacrifice*. None of those questions are on the table. The worship is presupposed. The question is *the address line on the envelope*. Did this devotion, which clearly happened, arrive at *you*?

Ibn Kathir draws out a further dimension here: the question, he explains, is asking not only whether worship reached the angels but whether the angels ever *commanded* it — whether they authorized it, whether this devotion had their permission at all. Two separate failures are exposed at once by the construction. The act never arrived at the right recipient. And the supposed recipient never sanctioned the act to begin with.

Al-Tabari records from Qatāda in his *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* that the question here is *istifhām taqrīr* — an interrogation not for information, but for establishment. A rhetorical structure where the questioner already knows the answer, and is asking to make the truth land publicly. Allah knows whether worship reached the angels. The question is staged for the worshippers standing there. It is the prosecutor turning to the witness and saying, in front of the accused: "Did this defendant ever once speak for you?"

Qatāda — as al-Tabari reports him — compares this construction directly to another scene in the Quran where Allah asks ʿĪsā ibn Maryam: *a-anta qulta linnāsi t-takhidhūnī wa ummiya ilāhayni min dūni'llāh* — "Was it **you** who told the people to take me and my mother as gods besides Allah?" Same structure. Same pulled-forward "you." Same rhetorical move. In both cases the supposed object of worship is summoned, and the question that is asked is not about the act of worship but about *whether the worshipper's claim about the object's permission was ever true*.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this opens up.

When Allah questions the disbelievers' shirk, He does not start by interrogating *whether* they had devotion. He interrogates *the direction* of their devotion. The whole prosecution turns on one fact: every act of worship has a recipient, and the recipient has to be the right one, and the act itself is not the saving thing — only the address line is.

Think about what this does to a certain modern instinct. We often hear, and sometimes say: "What matters is sincerity. What matters is that someone is *trying*. What matters is the action." The first thing Allah's question makes clear is that this framing is incomplete. The worship of these people was real. Their sincerity was real. Their devotion was real. Allah does not deny any of it. Allah just asks: *who was it for?*

The act, alone, will not save anyone. The act with the wrong addressee is a letter that goes to the wrong house. It does not matter how beautiful the letter is, how passionate the words inside, how many years you spent writing it. If the address is wrong, no one you intended receives it.

This is the first thing the construction is telling us. The question of judgment is not whether you worshipped. It is whether you worshipped *Him*.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The First Word Out of the Angels' Mouths

Now watch what the angels say.

They could have answered the question directly. The question is yes-or-no. Were these people worshipping you? The simple answer would be: *no, they weren't*. Two words. Done.

That is not what the angels say.

> قَالُوا۟ سُبْحَـٰنَكَ

*Qālū subḥānaka.*

*"They said: Glory be to You."*

Before the denial — *subḥānaka*. Before "no, they weren't worshipping us" — *subḥānaka*. Before any defense of themselves, before any clearing of their name, before any setting of the record straight — the angels' first instinct is to declare Allah's incomparability.

Stay with how strange that is.

The question was about them. The question put them in the spotlight. The question was, in a sense, a kind of inappropriate suggestion — *did people direct worship to you?* If we received that question, our first reaction would be defensive. *No, no, I never asked for that. I never wanted that. Don't pin that on me.* We would protect ourselves first, then maybe, if we remembered, glorify Allah second.

The angels invert that order completely. They glorify *first*. They defend themselves *second*. And actually if you look closely, they barely defend themselves at all. The whole answer pivots around Allah. *Glory be to You. You are our protector, not them.* They never spend a single word on their own innocence. They only spend words on His exclusivity.

What is *subḥānaka* doing here?

The word *subḥān* comes from a root that carries a sense of distancing — of declaring something far above, free from, incomparable to. When a Muslim says *subḥānAllāh*, what they are really saying is: *Allah is utterly beyond — beyond any imperfection, beyond any partner, beyond any peer, beyond any of the categories my mind keeps trying to fit Him into*.

Al-Tabari's gloss on the angels' *subḥānaka* is precise: he reads it as *tanzīhan laka wa tabriʾatan mimmā aḍāfa ilayka hāʾulāʾi min al-shurakāʾi wa al-andād* — "a declaration of Your transcendence, and a clearing from what these people attributed to You of partners and equals." The word carries both movements simultaneously. It is *tanzīh* — raising Allah above — and *tabriʾa* — disavowing, clearing Him of the attribution. By saying *subḥānaka*, the angels are not only praising; they are repudiating. The glorification and the refutation arrive fused in a single word.

So when the angels say *subḥānaka* — what they are really saying is something like this: *the very suggestion that worship could be appropriately directed to anyone other than You is a suggestion against Your nature. The premise of the question, taken seriously as a possibility about reality, is something we have to push back from before we can even answer it. And in pushing back from it, we are clearing You — not just ourselves — from the claim these people spent their lives making.*

It is a *theological* response before it is a *self-protective* one.

[PAUSE]

And I want you to feel the contrast.

The worshippers spent their lives saying: *the angels are intermediaries, the angels are closer to Allah than us, the angels can carry our requests*. They built a whole sophisticated theology around the angels' nearness to Allah. They believed they were honoring the angels.

But here is what real nearness to Allah looks like. The angels, when given a chance to claim the devotion of these worshippers — when given a chance to take credit, to accept the role those worshippers tried to give them — *recoil*. They will not even speak of themselves first. The first word they say is about Him.

The worshippers thought the angels' nearness to Allah made the angels worth worshipping. The angels' actual nearness to Allah is precisely what made them refuse it.

This is the irony at the center of every form of shirk. The thing we elevate is, if it is genuinely good, the very thing that would refuse our elevation. A truly righteous person does not accept being worshipped — that is part of what makes them righteous. A truly noble angel does not accept worship — that is part of what makes them an angel. A truly close prophet does not accept worship — that is part of what makes him close. The closer the creature is to Allah, the more vehemently it would refuse what the shirk-makers try to give it.

The very *qualification* that the false worshippers used to justify their direction of worship is the qualification that makes the recipient refuse the worship.

[PAUSE]

And then notice what comes after *subḥānaka*. The angels do not just glorify. They claim something.

> أَنتَ وَلِيُّنَا مِن دُونِهِمْ

*Anta waliyyunā min dūnihim.*

*"You are our protector — instead of them."*

The word *walī* is one of the deepest words in the Quran. It is not just "friend." It is not just "protector." It is the one who has *walāyah* over you — the one whose claim on you is total, the one who covers you, the one whose side you are on, the one whose side is on you. *Walī* is the relationship word. It names allegiance.

Al-Muyassar unpacks what the angels are declaring: *You are our walī — the One we obey and worship alone.* The *walāyah* they are claiming is not passive loyalty. It is active, exclusive worship. When the angels say "You are our *walī*," they mean: we are already entirely His, we always were, and that is why what the mushrikeen tried to project onto us was always a fiction. There was no gap in our allegiance where their devotion could enter.

The angels say: *You are our walī. Not them.*

They are not denying neutrally. They are declaring sides. They are saying: *we belong to You. Whatever the worshippers thought, whatever side they tried to put us on, we were never on theirs. We were always Yours.*

And the phrase *min dūnihim* — "rather than them," "instead of them" — places a wall between two groups. There is the angels' group, where Allah is the walī. And there is the worshippers' group, where the angels were merely being invoked. The angels never crossed the wall. The worshippers, even when invoking the angels' names, were never on the same side as them.

This is what every act of false worship gets wrong: it imagines that the object of worship and the worshipper are on the same team. That by elevating this thing, we have aligned with it. The Day of Gathering reveals the truth — the false object of worship was never on the same team as the worshipper. The wall was there the whole time.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Word *bal* and the Worship No One Thought They Were Doing

Now the angels say something devastating.

Having declared *subḥānaka* — clearing Allah of every attribution — and having claimed *walāyah* with Him alone, they could have stopped. The disavowal is complete. *We were never the recipients. We were never on their side.* That would have been enough.

But the angels do not stop there. They pivot. And the pivot is a single word:

> بَلْ

*Bal.*

In Arabic, *bal* is a contrastive particle — translated "rather," or "on the contrary." What it does structurally is set aside what came before and replace it with something closer to the truth. It is a correction in motion. You have been thinking X; *bal* — here is what is actually the case.

The angels have just said: *we were not the ones being worshipped by them.* Then — *bal*. Rather. *Kānū yaʿbudūna l-jinn* — they were worshipping the jinn.

This is the revelation inside the denial. The worship the mushrikeen believed they were directing upward — the intermediary devotion, the sophisticated layered theology of angelic nearness — was not landing on the angels. The angels testify to where it was landing instead.

*The jinn.*

Al-Muyassar is explicit about what this means: the jinn here are the shayatin — the devils. *Kānū yaʿbudūna l-shayāṭīn, aktharuhum bihim muṣaddiqūn wa muṭīʿūn* — "they were worshipping the devils; most of them were affirming them and obeying them." Al-Tabari, in his *Jāmiʿ*, confirms the same reading: *bal kānū yaʿbudūna l-jinn* is the substance beneath the surface claim of angel-veneration. What looked from the outside like elevated devotion was, at the level of its actual spiritual mechanics, obedience to the shayatin. Because the shayatin are the ones who told them to direct that worship. They are the ones who whispered that the angels were Allah's daughters, that intermediaries were needed, that the layered theology was valid. The mushrikeen trusted that argument. And in trusting the argument, they were obeying the ones who built it.

Think about the structure of this for a moment. The mushrikeen believed they were worshipping upward — toward beings of light, nearer to Allah. The angels, when asked, report the opposite: the worship was flowing to beings of darkness, who had been constructing the very theological layer the mushrikeen were trusting. The shayatin built the argument. The mushrikeen trusted the argument. And in trusting the argument, they were in allegiance with the architects.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the final clause.

> أَكْثَرُهُم بِهِم مُّؤْمِنُونَ

*Aktharuhum bihim muʾminūn.*

*"Most of them were believers in them."*

The word is *muʾminūn*. Believers. The exact same word used for the faithful — for those who have *iman*. Here it is deployed for the mushrikeen's relationship to the jinn.

The word *iman* comes from a root that means to trust, to give security to, to affirm. When someone has *iman* in something, they are giving it their fundamental trust. They are making it the thing they rely on, the thing they affirm as real, the thing they go back to when they need grounding.

The angels are saying: most of them had *iman* — in the jinn. They trusted the jinn. They gave the jinn the position of the trusted one. They affirmed the jinn's authority. They went back to what the jinn told them when they needed grounding.

The capacity for *iman* is one of the most profound things a human being has. It is the faculty that, when properly aimed, produces *tawḥīd* — the declaration that Allah alone is the Real. When misdirected, it produces the same structure of trust, the same intensity of reliance, the same returning — but aimed at something that will answer for it on the Day of Gathering.

This is *iman* misdirected. Not the absence of faith. The presence of faith in the wrong place.

[PAUSE]

And notice the word *aktharuhum* — "most of them." Not all. Most.

The Quran does not flatten the crowd into one uniform mass. Even in the gathering of the mushrikeen, individual calibration is preserved. Not every person in that assembly had the same depth of allegiance to the jinn — most did, in the sense of full spiritual affirmation and obedience, but not all. Perhaps some performed inherited practices without full conviction. Perhaps some had genuine confusion about what they were trusting. The angels, in their testimony, are precise. *Most* of them were *muʾminūn* in the jinn. The testimony does not exceed what is true.

## Part 2 — The Deeper Reading

What do we carry from this?

I want you to notice what these two ayahs teach together, because neither one makes full sense without the other.

The first ayah stages a question: *was worship reaching the angels?* The second contains the angels' answer, and that answer has three movements.

The first movement is *subḥānaka* — placing Allah before everything, declaring His transcendence and disavowing the attribution as the first response to any question about any false recipient of worship.

The second movement is *walāyah* — the angels' declaration that they are entirely His, and always were. Not available. Not neutral. Already claimed, completely.

The third movement is *bal kānū yaʿbudūna l-jinn* — the corrective that dismantles the mushrikeen's theological architecture from inside. You thought you were worshipping upward through a noble layer. You were being fed an argument by the very entities who would receive your misdirection.

Put them together and what you have is a complete account of how shirk works. Not by brute force. Not by someone standing over a believer and demanding idol-worship. But by a constructed theological layer — something that sounds elevated, something that invokes genuine entities (the angels are real, their nearness to Allah is real), something with internal logic and apparent sophistication. And underneath that layer, the ones actually receiving the devotion are the ones who built the layer.

[PAUSE]

There is something in this that speaks past the Quraysh.

The Quraysh are not the only people in history who worshipped upward while being channeled downward. Every generation has its version of the constructed theological layer. Every generation has frameworks that appear to elevate, to bring us nearer to the Real, but whose actual mechanics — when examined at the level of what they ask us to trust, what they ask us to obey, what they ask us to return to — point elsewhere.

The question Allah stages is not unique to Judgment Day. It is the question we can ask in every age: *who actually authorized this? Does the being I'm invoking — if it is genuinely good — consent to being invoked this way? Or is the invitation coming from somewhere else?*

The test is in the response of the supposed recipient. The angels recoil. They will not accept the role. They spend their first word on *His* transcendence, clearing Him before clearing themselves. If what we are returning to accepts the worship — demands it, feeds on it, grows in authority from it — that is precisely the sign that the constructed layer is not leading to the angels. It is leading to the ones who built the argument for why that layer was needed.

[PAUSE]

And then there is the word *muʾminūn*.

I keep coming back to it. *Most of them were believers in them.*

Because the capacity for *iman* is not the problem. The direction is the problem. These people had enough trust to be called *muʾminūn*. They had enough conviction to build theology around it. They had enough sincerity to keep coming back to what they trusted. All of that capacity — real, human, profound — aimed at something that on the Day of Gathering will refuse them, deny them, and hand them over.

The ache in this is not that they were faithless. It is that they were full of faith.

That capacity doesn't disappear when someone comes to the Quran. It comes with them. The question these ayahs ask of every person who reads them is not *do you have iman?* It is: *in what?* The instrument is there. It has always been there. The Quran is asking us to check the address line before we send the letter.

[PAUSE]

One more thing. The angels say *anta waliyyunā min dūnihim* — You are our *walī*, not them. And this declaration is made in front of the mushrikeen who spent their lives trying to invoke the angels as their own intermediaries. The angels disown them publicly. *We were never your wali. We were always His.*

In the Islamic tradition, *walāyah* — the relationship of closeness, protection, and allegiance — runs in a specific direction. The wali does not just protect from harm. The wali shapes who you become, what you belong to, which side you are on. When someone worships something, they are declaring that thing their wali. They are putting their walāyah with it.

The Day of Gathering reveals who was actually anyone's wali. And the revelation is not gentle. The very beings the mushrikeen tried to claim as their protectors are the first ones to testify that the relationship never existed.

The believer who has spent his life saying *anta waliyyunā* — *You* are our wali — will, on that Day, find the One he addressed turning toward him. The mushrik who spent his life saying it to something other than Allah will find that thing turning away.

This is what the two ayahs are really about. Not just the mechanics of shirk. Not just the structure of the question. Not even just the theology of the angels' response. They are about the most fundamental question in human life:

*Who do you belong to?*

## Closing

There is a moment in this passage that I want to leave you with.

The angels, asked whether worship reached them, say *subḥānaka* first. Not "we didn't authorize it." Not "we weren't consulted." Not "we reject the accusation." Just — *subḥānaka.*

Al-Tabari spent careful attention on it, and he saw two things fused together: *tanzīh* — raising Allah above everything — and *tabriʾa* — disavowal, clearing, a repudiation of what has been attributed. Both at once, in a single word. But even that analysis, as precise as it is, doesn't capture what it means to be the one saying it in that moment.

To say *subḥānaka* first — before your own defense, before the crowd's verdict, before anything else — is to have already settled something inside yourself. It is to have the orientation already in place. It is to have answered the question *who do you belong to* so thoroughly that even being put at the center of a scene about someone else's misdirected worship doesn't move you from your position. The first word is still about Him.

The angels were never confused about where their walāyah lay. And so they were never tempted by the role the mushrikeen tried to give them.

*Subḥānaka.* And from that — everything else follows.
