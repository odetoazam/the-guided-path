# When "You" Becomes "Some": The Day the Network of Human Power Dissolves

## INTRODUCTION

**The Arabic:**

فَالْيَوْمَ لَا يَمْلِكُ بَعْضُكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ نَفْعًا وَلَا ضَرًّا وَنَقُولُ لِلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا ذُوقُوا عَذَابَ النَّارِ الَّتِي كُنتُم بِهَا تُكَذِّبُونَ

**Transliteration:**

*Fa-l-yawma lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā, wa naqūlu lilladhīna ẓalamū dhūqū ʿadhāba an-nāri allatī kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn.*

**Translation:**

*"So today none of you possesses for any other any benefit or any harm. And We will say to those who wronged: Taste the punishment of the Fire which you used to deny."*

---

### Rebuilding the World

To feel what this ayah does, we have to first feel the world it answers.

Surah Saba is not a random collection of stories. From its opening, it is meditating on one question: **what do humans actually own?** It begins with the great kingdom of Saba — a civilization so prosperous the Quran describes it as a garden between two rows of fruits, walking distance between blessed cities. Then it ends. A flood, a dam break, and a vanished people. Then we are taken to Sulayman — perhaps the most powerful human king in history. The wind in his service. The jinn in his service. He sits to rest leaning on his staff. And he dies. And no one notices. The jinn keep working. Until a worm eats through his staff, and his body falls, and the truth is revealed: the king has been gone for who knows how long, and the empire of jinn was still bowing to a corpse.

Saba is a surah about **possessing**. About thinking you possess. About all the kingdoms that thought they possessed something — Saba's wealth, Sulayman's command, the idols' worshippers, the wealthy's patronage networks — and the moment when it is revealed that they possessed nothing.

The surah has used a specific verb for this all along: **مَلَكَ — to possess, to own, to have power over.** In ayah 22, Allah commands: *"Call those you claim besides Allah — they do not possess (lā yamlikūna) the weight of an atom in the heavens or in the earth."* In ayah 23, *"intercession will not benefit (lā tanfaʿu) with Him."*

Then a few ayahs before our verse, in 34:40-41, Allah stages a courtroom. He gathers the polytheists with the angels they used to invoke. He asks the angels: *"Were these worshipping you?"* And the angels recoil. *"Glory be to You — You are our protector, not them. They were worshipping jinn. Most of them believed in those."*

Picture this scene. The worshippers stand there. Their alleged intercessors have just turned and said: *we have nothing to do with you.* The entire architecture they spent their lives building — *"these are the ones close to Allah, these will speak for me, these will lift my case"* — collapses in two sentences.

Then comes our ayah.

### The Hook

Notice the word Allah chooses for the worshippers and their worshipped, now standing equally exposed in the courtroom: **بَعْضُكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ** — *"some of you for some others."*

Not "you and them." Not "the deceived and the deceivers." Not "the weak and the strong." Just — **some... for some.**

Listen to what that word is doing. And listen to what comes a few breaths later, when Allah turns to the wrongdoers and uses one specific sensory verb that should stop us cold: **ذُوقُوا — taste.**

Why *taste*? Of all the senses, why the most intimate one? They denied with their tongues. Allah responds with their tongues.

There is an exactness here that is going to take us a while to feel. Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: **بَعْضُكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ** — When the Network Collapses Into Indefiniteness

Before we touch the Arabic, let me ask you to picture something from your own life.

Picture the web you live inside. Not just family — the whole web. Your boss who can promote you or block you. The colleague who could vouch for you. The friend who could introduce you. The relative whose call you take immediately because someday you might need them. The professor who writes your recommendation. The official who can expedite your paperwork. The person whose feelings you manage because their bad opinion costs you. The person whose good opinion you cultivate because their endorsement opens doors.

You don't usually map this out, but it's there. A whole geometry of perceived benefit and harm. Who lifts you. Who can damage you. Who do you owe. Who owes you.

Most of human life is spent managing this map.

Now, here is something Arabic does that English doesn't quite do the same way. In Arabic, when you want to talk about *a part of a group*, you use the word **بَعْض — baʿḍ** — "some." And here is the key: *baʿḍ* is grammatically indefinite. It has no article. It is the most non-specific word for "part of a group" in the language. It doesn't name. It doesn't pick out. It just says: *some.*

Allah could have said many things. He could have said *"the worshipper has no power for the worshipped."* He could have said *"the weak has no power for the powerful."* He could have specified. He could have named.

Instead He says:

**فَالْيَوْمَ لَا يَمْلِكُ بَعْضُكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ**

*Some of you... for some others.*

Sit with this for a moment. The first *baʿḍ* has the pronoun *-kum* attached — *baʿḍukum*, "some of you." The second *baʿḍ* has nothing — *li-baʿḍin*, just "for some." Even the second group is left ungrouped. The indefiniteness is total.

Notice what just happened. The careful map you spent your life maintaining — *this one helps, this one harms, this one is high, this one is low, this one I court, this one I avoid* — Allah doesn't even bother naming the categories. He says: *today, this whole map is just "some... for some."*

Here's what's remarkable: the very feature of the day is that **the categories you built no longer exist.** The day arrives and what was a stratified web of patrons and clients and superiors and inferiors collapses into one undifferentiated mass: *baʿḍ li-baʿḍ.* Some. For some.

Stay with the analogy. Imagine children playing pretend. One is the king, one is the soldier, one is the courtier. In the game, there is a real hierarchy. The king issues commands. The soldier obeys. The courtier flatters. There is real social power inside the game. The soldier really cannot speak when the king is speaking. The courtier really has to bow.

Now mom calls them in for dinner.

The game ends.

What happens to the king's authority? It evaporates. Not "diminishes." Evaporates. There is no king. There is no soldier. There is no courtier. There are only — *some children with some other children.* The game's architecture, which felt absolute thirty seconds ago, doesn't survive the real voice from outside.

Allah is saying: *fa-l-yawma — so today, this day —* the real voice has called you in. Everything you built inside the game, every status you sweated for, every patron you cultivated, every enemy you feared — all of it now is just *baʿḍ li-baʿḍ.* Some, for some.

And here is what makes this surgical: the surah we're inside has been preparing this. Recall — in 34:22 Allah said the false gods *do not possess (lā yamlikūna) an atom's weight.* The same verb. *Yamliku* — to possess, to own, to have authority over. Saba is a surah obsessed with the verb *malaka*. Kingdoms. Possession. Authority. And here, at the climax, Allah uses that verb in its strongest negation: **لَا يَمْلِكُ بَعْضُكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ** — *no one of you possesses anything for anyone.*

The verb *yamliku* is doing very particular work. *Malaka* doesn't just mean "to help." It means **to have ownership, to have command, to have the authority to dispose of something.** What Allah is negating is not just the ability to help — it is the ability to *control any outcome at all* for another being. To possess any disposal-power over their fate. None. For none.

And then notice the two objects: **نَفْعًا وَلَا ضَرًّا** — *any benefit, any harm.* Both indefinite. Both stretched to their smallest unit. Not "the great benefit" — *any* benefit. Not "ultimate harm" — *any* harm. Even the most microscopic favor — gone. Even the smallest threat — gone. The patron cannot help. The enemy cannot hurt.

Let's sit with this. Every relationship in your life right now is operating on one of two principles: *they could benefit me* or *they could harm me.* If they could do neither, your whole posture toward them would change. The flattery, the avoidance, the calculation — all of it is downstream of *nafʿ wa ḍarr.* Benefit and harm.

Allah says: on that day, **the only two motors of human social calculation will be exposed as having been switched off all along.**

[PAUSE]

What this single phrase reveals: every social map you built in this life — every IOU, every patron, every enemy, every protector — is *baʿḍ li-baʿḍ.* Indefinite. Ungrouped. Powerless. *The day arrives and the only voice in the room is the One whose voice you spent your life trying not to hear.*

### Element Two: **وَنَقُولُ** — The Voice That Replaces Every Voice

Now notice something in the next phrase. The grammar shifts.

**وَنَقُولُ لِلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا** — *"And We will say to those who wronged..."*

*Naqūlu.* "We say." First person plural. The royal *We* of divine majesty.

Why does this matter? Step back two verses. In 34:40, Allah asks the angels a question. In 34:41, the angels reply. The angels speak. They disavow. *"They were not worshipping us."* The worshippers have just heard their alleged advocates publicly walk away from them.

Now in our ayah, after the silence of the abandoned, Allah Himself speaks.

**وَنَقُولُ** — *and We say.*

In dunya, these people built a whole architecture so they would *never have to deal directly with Allah.* The whole point of intercessors, idols, patron-figures was: *I don't approach Him directly. I go through them. They speak to Him. They handle Him.* The intercessor was a buffer. A translator. A go-between.

And on this Day, every buffer has been stripped away. The angels said *we have nothing to do with this.* And now the worshipper, who spent his life trying to manage his relationship with Allah through proxies, hears the unmediated voice for the first time.

*Wa naqūl.*

There is no buffer. There is no intercessor. There is no patron stepping forward. There is only Allah, addressing them directly.

This is the structural mirror of what *baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin* did. *Baʿḍ li-baʿḍ* says: horizontally, your network is gone — no one in the human realm can help you. *Wa naqūl* says: vertically, your buffers are gone — no one between you and Allah is going to mediate this conversation.

The day is a day of **direct address.** And the direct address comes from the One you spent your life addressing indirectly.

### Element Three: **ذُوقُوا... كُنتُمْ بِهَا تُكَذِّبُونَ** — The Mouth That Denied Becomes the Mouth That Tastes

Now Allah turns to the wrongdoers, and gives them an imperative.

**ذُوقُوا عَذَابَ النَّارِ** — *"Taste the punishment of the Fire."*

The verb is *dhūqū.* From *dhāqa, yadhūqu* — to taste. It is one of the most common verbs in the Quran for describing the entry into punishment. It appears dozens of times.

We rush past it because we are used to it. But let's stop and ask: of all the verbs available to describe the experience of punishment, why does Allah so consistently choose *taste*?

He could have said: *enter, see, feel, suffer, endure.* All of these are available in Arabic. But again and again the Quran chooses *dhūqū.* Taste.

Now think about what taste is, as a sense.

Sight is at a distance. You see something — it can be miles away.

Hearing is at a distance. The sound traveled to you, but the source is not in you.

Smell is closer — the molecules are in your nose — but it's still external.

Touch is closer still — skin to skin — but it's surface.

**Taste is the most intimate of the senses.** To taste a thing, the thing must be *inside* you. It must be on your tongue, against the most sensitive tissue of your body, releasing its molecules into the receptors that map to the deepest parts of your brain. You cannot taste at a distance. You cannot taste an idea. To taste a thing is to admit it into the interior.

This is the verb Allah chooses.

Why?

Because of what the second half of the ayah says.

**الَّتِي كُنتُمْ بِهَا تُكَذِّبُونَ** — *"...which you used to deny."*

Two grammatical features here are critical. First: **kuntum tukadhdhibūn.** *Kāna* (past tense) + imperfect verb. In Arabic, this construction is the past habitual continuous. Not "you denied once." Not "you denied for a moment." **You used to deny, continually, as your habit, as your default posture, day after day after day.**

Second: **tukadhdhibūn.** This is Form II of the root *k-dh-b.* The basic Form I would be *kadhabtum* — "you lied." Form II — *kadhdhabtum / tukadhdhibūn* — has a doubled middle consonant and carries an intensive meaning here. It is not just *saying a lie.* It is **the active, repeated declaring of something to be a lie.** Pointing at the truth and calling it false. Pointing again. Pointing again. Pointing for years.

Put it together: **kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn** — *you used to, habitually, day after day, declare this very thing to be a lie.*

How did they declare it a lie? With their tongues.

Their tongues did the denying. Their mouths formed the words: *there is no Fire. There is no Day. This Quran is fabricated. Muhammad is a poet.* The mouth was the instrument of denial. The taste buds, the tongue, the lips — these were the apparatus of *takdhīb*.

Now Allah responds. And what does He give them as their punishment-encounter?

**ذُوقُوا — taste.**

The mouth that denied is the mouth that tastes. The instrument of *takdhīb* becomes the instrument of *ʿadhāb.* Allah's reply is so exact it is almost frightening. *You used your tongue to say: this is not real. I will let your tongue, the very tongue, be the first to know that it is real.*

There is a principle here that runs through the Quran but lands with terrible precision in this ayah. **The thing you used to deny becomes the thing you cannot escape.** The medium of denial becomes the medium of confrontation. What was at a distance — denied in words about something far away — is brought to the most intimate possible place: into your mouth.

Notice the cinematography. The denier in dunya pointed *outward* — *that, over there, is false.* The denial assumes distance. *That far thing, that abstract claim, that future event — I declare it a lie.* But on this Day, that far thing is on his tongue. The distance has collapsed. What was a verbal assertion at arm's length is now the temperature in his mouth.

There is no longer any distance from which to deny. **You cannot call a lie what is currently on your tongue.**

Let's sit with one more layer. The relative pronoun in the phrase: *al-latī kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn* — *which you used to deny.* The "which" — *al-latī* — refers back to the Fire. They denied the Fire specifically. Not a generic concept. *The* Fire. *This* Fire. The very Fire that is now on the tongue is the very Fire that they used to point at and call non-existent.

The grammar makes the identification exact. *This one. This very one you denied. Taste it.*

[PAUSE]

What this final phrase reveals: Allah's responses are not arbitrary. The instrument of your rebellion becomes the location of your reckoning. The faculty you used to push the truth away becomes the faculty by which the truth enters you.

### Bridge

We have looked at three things. *Baʿḍukum li-baʿḍ* — the horizontal network of human power collapses into indefinite, undifferentiated nothingness. *Wa naqūl* — the vertical mediators evaporate and Allah speaks unbuffered. *Dhūqū... kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn* — the very mouth that performed the denial becomes the mouth that receives the consequence.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the architecture of the life we are living right now.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Map You Maintain Is Not the Real Map

Let me start where you live.

Open your phone. Look at the last week of messages. Look at who you replied to within minutes and who you let sit for days. Look at whose names made your stomach tighten and whose names made your shoulders relax. Look at the favors you remembered to do and the favors you remembered to extract.

If you are honest, there is a map there. Not one you drew on paper, but one you maintain in your nervous system, every day, all day. **Who can lift me. Who can sink me. Who do I owe. Who owes me. Who must I please. Who can I ignore. Who must I never offend. Who can I afford to disappoint.**

This map is so deeply embedded that we don't usually notice it. It's just *how the world works.* You wouldn't take that client meeting at 9pm if you didn't think the client mattered. You wouldn't laugh at that joke if you didn't think the speaker had power. You wouldn't return that call if you didn't think the caller could affect your life.

The whole map is built on two beams: **nafʿ wa ḍarr.** Benefit. Harm.

Now Allah, in one phrase — *fa-l-yawma lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā* — does something to that map that is hard to express.

He doesn't say *the map will be redrawn.* He doesn't say *some people will move from high to low.* He says: **the map never existed.** The day arrives and what is exposed is that none of those people, none of them, ever actually possessed *nafʿ* or *ḍarr* for you. The patron's promotion was never his to give. The enemy's threat was never his to enforce. The grant came not from the giver but through the giver. The harm came not from the harmer but through the harmer. The intermediate causes that you took as the *real* causes were, the whole time, *baʿḍ li-baʿḍ* — some-with-some — never possessing, never disposing, never the source.

This is not philosophy. This is the testimony of the day that comes.

Notice what this does to how you breathe.

Right now, much of your psychological energy is going into managing your relationship with the people on your map. The ones who can hurt you, you appease. The ones who can help you, you cultivate. The ones who threaten your standing, you watch. The ones whose approval you need, you perform for. Every act of social calculation costs energy. Most of your tiredness, if you trace it, comes from this map.

And Allah is saying: **the map is not real. The patron is** *baʿḍ.* **The enemy is** *baʿḍ.* **The judge of your reputation is** *baʿḍ.* They cannot, in the absolute sense, give you what you fear losing or take what you fear losing. *Lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā.*

The Prophet ﷺ told Ibn Abbas in a famous tradition: *"If the entire ummah were gathered to benefit you with something, they would not benefit you except with something Allah had already written for you. And if they were gathered to harm you with something, they would not harm you except with something Allah had already written against you."*

This is the Surah Saba 42 logic, articulated as a way of living *now.* The hadith doesn't ask you to wait for the Day. It says: **start treating the map as already what it will be revealed to be.**

What does this change about tomorrow?

It does not mean you become rude to your boss or ignore your obligations. It does not mean you stop being polite. The Prophet ﷺ was the most refined human in his social conduct. *Baʿḍ li-baʿḍ* is not a license to abandon courtesy.

What it changes is **the location of fear and the location of hope.** Right now, your fear and your hope are distributed across the network. Some of it sits in your boss. Some of it sits in your father-in-law. Some of it sits in the person whose call you fear. Allah is asking: bring all of it home. Bring fear and hope to their actual source. *Lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā* — so the One who *does* possess *nafʿ* and *ḍarr*, the One whose voice is the *naqūl* of that day, fear Him and hope in Him directly.

When the children come in for dinner, the soldier doesn't fear the king anymore. He fears mom. Properly. Real fear. Real authority. *Wa-llāhu fawqahum* — *and Allah is above them.* The map evaporates. The mother is in the room.

The practical shift, if you'll take it: **for one week, when you feel the tightening of social fear or the tightening of social hope toward some person on your map, name it.** *Baʿḍ li-baʿḍ.* This person possesses for me no benefit and no harm except what Allah has already written. Then ask Allah directly for what you were going to ask the person to grant you. Then go do what was right anyway.

You will notice your shoulders drop.

### Theme Two: The Mouth That Denies Becomes the Mouth That Knows

There is something Allah does in this ayah that you will see if you watch carefully, and it will not leave you once you've seen it.

In dunya, the deniers used a specific bodily faculty to deny. The tongue. The mouth. *Kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn* — you used to keep calling this a lie. Calling — *qawl* — *takdhīb* — these are mouth-actions. Lips, tongue, breath.

The denial itself assumes **distance.** *That far thing — that Fire I cannot see, that Day I cannot see, that Account I cannot see — I declare to be unreal.* You can only call a thing a lie if you take yourself to be standing somewhere other than where the thing is. The denier needs distance from the denied to make the denial possible. *You can't call a lie what is sitting on your tongue.*

So what does Allah do?

He collapses the distance. **Dhūqū.** Taste it. Not "see it from a distance." Not "approach it." *Put it in your mouth.* The most intimate sense. The one that requires the thing to be *inside* you.

Stay with this. There is a principle here that runs all the way down into our daily psychological life.

**The thing we deny most intensely is often the thing we already sense most acutely.** Psychologists notice this. The fiercest denial is rarely casual. People do not expend enormous energy denying things that are obviously irrelevant. The energy of the denial is itself a confession that the thing is close. You don't argue against a stranger's ghost. You argue against a presence.

The denial of the Fire was not casual. *Tukadhdhibūn* — Form II, intensive, repeated, habitual. They worked hard at the denial. They came back to it. They argued it. They made jokes about it. They wrote poetry against it. They labored — because somewhere, the soul that Allah created upon the *fiṭra* could sense it was real, and the labor of *takdhīb* was the labor of pushing it back down.

And on this Day, Allah doesn't argue with them. He doesn't refute their poetry. He doesn't list the proofs. He simply places, on the very tongue that did the pushing, the very thing that was being pushed.

**The faculty of denial becomes the faculty of confession.** Not in words. In sensation. The tongue that said *this is not real* now knows, by direct contact, that this is real. There is no need for argument. The mouth knows.

This is not just about disbelievers and the Fire. The principle is wider, and we should let it touch us.

In your own life, ask: what am I currently in *takdhīb* about? Not necessarily about a Quranic truth, though that is the primary frame. About anything Allah has said, anything the *fiṭra* whispers, anything I have been told and have been working hard to deny. The pull I feel toward something I know is wrong, but I'm telling myself doesn't matter. The relationship I know I am damaging, but I keep saying is fine. The prayer I know I am avoiding, but I keep saying I'm too tired for. The *dhikr* I know my heart is calcifying without, but I keep telling myself I'm not the type. The reckoning I know is coming, but I keep telling myself can be delayed.

Wherever there is *takdhīb* now — *intensive, repeated, habitual denial* — there is a *dhūqū* waiting. Not as a threat. As a description of the mechanics of the universe Allah has built. *You cannot push a thing forever. At some point, the thing arrives where the pushing came from.*

The mercy in this ayah, hidden in its terror, is this: **you do not have to wait for the *dhūqū* to encounter the reality.** You can taste it now, in the form of *dhikr*, in the form of *istighfār*, in the form of opening the mushaf and letting words touch the same tongue. You can let the tongue be a place of acknowledgment now, voluntarily, while the door of repentance is open. Better the sweet taste of *lā ilāha illa-llāh* on the tongue now than the bitter taste later.

The Companions understood this. *ʿUmar (r.a.)* used to weep until lines were drawn on his cheeks. Why? He was not weeping out of weakness. He was tasting now — in the form of fearful remembrance — what others would taste later in the form of fearful arrival. The tongue that could have been used in *takdhīb* he flooded with *dhikr.* The mouth that could have been a denier he made a confessor.

**The faculty was the same. The direction was different.** That is the entire spiritual life, in one image.

[PAUSE]

What this changes for tomorrow: notice, in one specific area of your life, where *takdhīb* is operating. Where are you working hard to declare something unreal that you know is real? Then, with the same mouth, say one true thing. Out loud. To Allah. *"O Allah, You know I have been denying X. I admit it now, on the tongue I was using to deny it. Accept this from me before the day You accept nothing else."*

That is *dhūqū*, mercy-side.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now look at the ayah's shape.

It opens with **fa-l-yawma** — *so, this day.* It is built around two collapses:

**The horizontal collapse:** *baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā* — the human network is leveled.

**The vertical collapse:** *wa naqūlu lilladhīna ẓalamū* — the divine voice speaks unmediated, with no intercessor in the gap.

And it ends with **the body of the wrongdoer becoming the location of the verdict** — *dhūqū... kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn.* The very faculty that built the denial becomes the faculty that receives the answer.

So the ayah moves: *outside (network) → vertical (the buffer to Allah) → inside (the body itself).* Allah closes the distance in three steps. The network goes. The intercessor goes. The skin goes. By the end of the ayah, what was once held at arm's length is on the tongue.

This is not coincidence. This is the architecture of the Day.

### Bringing It Together

The horizontal phrase and the final imperative are saying the same thing from two directions.

*Baʿḍ li-baʿḍ* says: **the things you trusted to mediate between you and Allah do not, and never did, possess anything.** Therefore the soul stands directly before Him.

*Dhūqū... kuntum bihā tukadhdhibūn* says: **the things you used to push Allah's word away from you cannot, on that day, perform any further pushing.** The very tongue is occupied with the thing it once denied. Therefore the soul stands directly within the consequence.

Both are descriptions of one event: **all distance from Allah is collapsed.** The patron-distance, the intercessor-distance, the language-distance, the denial-distance — all gone. The soul, the One, the truth. Nothing between.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is one node in my social map — one person — toward whom I have given more *nafʿ wa ḍarr* than belongs to them? What would it look like to address Allah directly instead?

2. When I notice myself laboring to deny something — about Allah, about myself, about a wrong I am committing — what is the energy of the denial telling me about how close the thing actually is?

3. If the tongue is the apparatus of *takdhīb* for some, and the apparatus of *dhikr* for others, what is my tongue mostly doing this week?

4. If *baʿḍ li-baʿḍ* is the eventual truth, what would my prayer life look like if I prayed to Allah for the things I currently ask of *baʿḍ?*

5. What thing am I currently calling unreal — postponing, minimizing, joking past — that I would speak about differently if I knew it was on my tongue tomorrow?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**On the Day when every human network collapses into indefinite "some-for-some" and every buffer between the soul and Allah is stripped away, the very tongue that performed the denial becomes the location where the denied truth is finally tasted.**

### The Closing Invitation

Do not wait for the *dhūqū.*

The ayah is not given to you to terrify you. It is given to you to let you begin, today, the inward migration that the Day will impose on everyone. Begin to bring fear and hope back to their true Owner. Begin to thin out the social map. Begin to use the tongue that could have been an instrument of denial as an instrument of remembrance. *Dhikr* now, while it is voluntary, is the same faculty being trained for the only conversation that will matter.

The *baʿḍ* around you are not your enemies and not your saviors. They are, like you, *baʿḍ.* Love them as such. Forgive them as such. Stop fearing them as such. The One you fear and hope in is the One whose voice comes through *wa naqūl.* Address Him now, and you will know His address then.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who said *fa-l-yawma lā yamliku baʿḍukum li-baʿḍin nafʿan wa lā ḍarrā,* break in our hearts the illusion of human power — the patron we feared more than we feared You, the enemy we feared more than we feared You — and return all fear and all hope to their rightful place in You. And O Allah, do not let our tongues be tongues of *takdhīb* on the Day; let them be tongues that tasted *lā ilāha illa-llāh* before they tasted anything else. Make the mouth that could have denied a mouth that remembers, until the day we meet You unmediated, on a day no buffer remains.

*Āmīn.*
