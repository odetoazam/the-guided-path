# The Race Against the Uncatchable

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to these words first. Let the sound land before the meaning does.

**وَٱلَّذِينَ يَسْعَوْنَ فِىٓ ءَايَـٰتِنَا مُعَـٰجِزِينَ أُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ فِى ٱلْعَذَابِ مُحْضَرُونَ**

*Walladhīna yasʿawna fī āyātinā muʿājizīna ulā'ika fī al-ʿadhābi muḥḍarūn.*

"And those who strive against Our signs, seeking to cause them to fail — those will be brought into the punishment."

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this ayah, you have to step into Mecca during the years when Surah Saba was revealed. The Quran is not yet a book in anyone's hand. It is a voice — verses arriving in fragments, then in waves, then in sustained passages that seem to know what is happening in the room before anyone has spoken. And around the Prophet ﷺ, a strange and exhausting kind of opposition has formed.

It is not lazy opposition. It is not the opposition of people who shrug and walk away. It is **busy** opposition. Active. Sweating. The leaders of Quraysh hold meetings. They draft strategies. They plant hecklers in marketplaces to drown out the recitation. They travel — to Yathrib, to the Jewish tribes, to learned men — asking, *what questions can we ask this man that will trip him up?* They commission poets. They organize boycotts. They spend money. They spend nights awake.

They are **working** at this. Hard. The Quran does not describe them as resting in disbelief. It describes them as **running** in it.

And Surah Saba is the surah that pulls back the curtain on this whole performance. The surah opens with the lineage of the people of Saba — a civilization that had everything, that thought its dam and its gardens and its trade routes made it untouchable, and then watched it all wash away in a single flood. The surah moves through David and Solomon, through wealth and power, and keeps asking the same question in different voices: *what exactly do you think you're protected by?*

This ayah arrives in the middle of that interrogation. It is the portrait of the runner. The one who has not just rejected the signs but has organized his life around defeating them. And in nine Arabic words, Allah shows us what that race actually looks like — from the only vantage point that matters.

### The Hook

Something strange is happening in this verse that you will miss on the first reading. Allah is describing people who are *moving fast*. They are striving, sprinting, scheming. And then in the second half of the ayah, the verbs do something they should not do. They go completely still. The runners stop running. And the way they stop — the single word Allah chooses for it — contains the entire theology of this ayah.

Stay with me. We are going to unpack what kind of race this is, who is actually chasing whom, and why the destination is not arrived at but *delivered to.*

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: يَسْعَوْنَ — The Word for Running

Before we look at this word in the ayah, let's understand what it normally means in Arabic.

The root س-ع-ى (s-ʿ-y) is a physical root. It describes a particular kind of motion — faster than walking, not quite a full run. It is the gait of someone with somewhere to be. When you have an appointment and you are almost late, that movement — that focused, forward-leaning, *I have a destination* movement — that is *saʿy*.

This is the same word Allah uses for what Hajar did between Safa and Marwa, searching for water for her son. It is the word used in Surah al-Jumuʿah — *fas-ʿaw ilā dhikri-llāh* — "hasten to the remembrance of Allah." It is the word for **purposeful, urgent motion toward something you want.**

Hold that meaning. Hasty. Urgent. Aimed.

Now look at what Allah does in this ayah:

**وَٱلَّذِينَ يَسْعَوْنَ فِىٓ ءَايَـٰتِنَا**

"And those who *run* — into Our signs."

Notice something. This is the verb of someone hurrying *toward* a destination. But Allah has turned it. They are hurrying — but the hurrying is *into Our signs*. Their urgency is **directed against the very thing that should be their destination.**

Let's sit with this. A human being has a finite amount of *saʿy* in them. A finite amount of urgency, of caring-enough-to-run. Most of life is spent walking. Saʿy is what you save for the things that actually matter. And here is a category of person who has located their *saʿy* — their fastest, most invested, most awake version of themselves — and they have aimed it at undoing the speech of God.

This is not casual rejection. The Quran does not describe these people as *yamshūn* (walking) against the signs, or *yajlisūn* (sitting) against them. The verb is *yasʿawn*. They are giving this their best energy.

Here's what's remarkable: when a person opposes a truth with this much velocity, the velocity itself is a confession. You do not run from something that does not threaten you. You do not organize your week around discrediting a thing you actually think is nothing. The very speed of the opposition reveals that something has been touched. Something in them already knows.

[PAUSE]

And then there is the preposition. *Fī* āyātinā. Not *ʿalā* (upon). Not *ḍidda* (against, in the sense of opposition). *Fī* — *in, into, within*. They are running *inside* the signs. As though the signs themselves are the arena of their running. As though they have stepped onto the field of Allah's revelation and chosen to expend their energy there — turning the very stadium of divine speech into the place they exhaust themselves trying to discredit.

The classical commentators — al-Qurtubi, al-Baghawi, al-Jalalayn — all explain this *fī āyātinā* the same way: *fī ibṭāli ḥujajinā* — "in the nullification of Our proofs." The running is aimed at *cancellation.* They want the signs to stop functioning as signs.

What this single word reveals: there is a category of human energy that is invested with full *saʿy* not toward Allah but toward the project of making His signs not count.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: مُعَاجِزِينَ — The Race They Think They Are Running

Now we come to the word that opens the whole ayah. *Muʿājizīn.*

To feel this word, let's first teach the grammar. In Arabic, verbs have ten possible "forms" — different stems built from the same root, each adding a layer of meaning. The root here is ع-ج-ز (ʿ-j-z), which in its simplest form means *to be incapable, to be powerless, to fall short.* From this same root comes the word *muʿjizah* — a miracle — meaning literally "that which renders others incapable of producing its like."

The word in our ayah is Form III: *muʿājiz.* Form III in Arabic almost always describes **a relationship between two parties** — an interaction, a contest, a mutual engagement. *Qātala* (Form III of *qatala*) doesn't mean "to kill," it means *to fight* — to engage in killing-against-someone. *Sābaqa* doesn't mean "to race" in the abstract, it means *to race against another runner.*

So *muʿājiz* is not just "one who is unable" or "one who makes unable." It is **one who is in a contest with another, trying to render that other incapable.** It is the posture of an athlete who has identified an opponent and is now actively trying to outstrip him, to outrun him, to leave him behind, to make him *fall short.*

Now read the ayah again.

**وَٱلَّذِينَ يَسْعَوْنَ فِىٓ ءَايَـٰتِنَا مُعَـٰجِزِينَ**

"And those who run inside Our signs — *as competitors trying to outstrip*."

Outstrip *whom*?

This is the moment the ayah opens up. Al-Qurtubi explains: *yaḥsabūna annahum yafūtūnanā bi-anfusihim* — "they reckon that they will escape Us, that they will get away from Us by their own selves." Al-Baghawi: *yaḥsabūna annahum yuʿjizūnanā wa yafūtūnanā* — "they reckon that they will render Us incapable and will escape Us." Al-Jalalayn: *muqaddirīna ʿajzanā wa annahum yafūtūnanā* — "estimating Our incapacity and that they will get past Us."

The picture is now devastatingly clear. These are not just deniers. These are people who have entered into what they imagine to be a **race with God.** They have positioned themselves as runners, and they have located their opponent. They are sprinting, in their own minds, *ahead* of divine reach. They are the athlete who looks back over his shoulder, sees the other runner falling behind, and grins.

Let's sit with the sheer pretension of this for a moment. Notice something. There are two things you have to believe simultaneously to assume the posture of *muʿājiz* toward Allah:

First, that there is a finite distance, a finite arena, in which the race takes place — so that you can, in principle, run beyond it. Second, that your speed is sufficient to traverse that distance before the other runner catches you.

Both of these beliefs require you to have *measured* Allah. To have estimated His reach as finite. To have estimated His speed as something that can be exceeded. And here is what is breathtaking: the Quran does not even bother to argue with the second belief. It just stages the race and shows you the finish line.

Here is what is remarkable about Form III when it is used in this direction — by a creature toward the Creator. Every other Form III contest in human life has some meaning. *Sābaqa* between two runners — meaningful, one is actually faster. *Qātala* between two armies — meaningful, one can actually win. But *ʿājaza* directed at the One who is *al-Qādir* — the contest is structurally void. There is no track. There is no finish line. There is no *ahead* in which to be.

What Allah is showing us is not just disbelief. It is **disbelief that has imagined itself into a competitive posture with God.** It has framed its own life as a contest it can win.

And here is where the Arabic does something incredible. The word *muʿājizīn* is a *ḥāl* — a circumstantial accusative — describing the state in which they are running. So a fuller translation would be:

"And those who run inside Our signs — *in the state of being competitors, in the posture of those trying to outstrip.*"

The word does not say they *are* outstripping. It says they are *acting as if they are* — running in that mode, that posture, that imagination of themselves. The whole race exists inside their own assumption.

[PAUSE]

What this word reveals: there is a particular sickness in which a human being, faced with signs of God too clear to ignore, does not respond by surrendering and does not respond by walking away. He responds by sprinting — *in the imagined posture of someone who is winning a race against the Real.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: مُحْضَرُونَ — The Word That Stops the Race

And now we come to the word that breaks the entire ayah open.

After all this running — all this *saʿy*, all this *ʿājaza*, all this imagined velocity — Allah ends the ayah with one word:

**أُوْلَـٰٓئِكَ فِى ٱلْعَذَابِ مُحْضَرُونَ**

"Those — into the punishment — *muḥḍarūn.*"

The root is ح-ض-ر (ḥ-ḍ-r), meaning *to be present, to attend.* The basic verb *ḥaḍara* means "he was present, he attended." The word *ḥāḍir* means "one who is present." The word *ḥaḍrah* means "a presence, a court, the immediate vicinity of someone important."

But the word in our ayah is not *ḥāḍir.* It is *muḥḍar.* Form IV passive participle. *One who has been brought into presence.* One who has been made to attend. One who has been **summoned and delivered.**

Stay with the grammar here, because this is the whole point. *Ḥāḍir* is what you are when you walk into a room on your own feet. *Muḥḍar* is what you are when you are *brought* into the room — whether you wanted to come or not. *Ḥāḍir* has agency. *Muḥḍar* does not.

Now read the second half of the ayah again.

The runners. The competitors. The ones who built their entire identity around *saʿy* and *muʿājaza* — around motion, against the signs of God. The ones who imagined themselves as the fastest runners in the field.

And the ayah ends with them — **standing perfectly still.**

Not arriving at the punishment. Not walking into it. Not even being thrown into it. **Brought into it.** As one is brought into a court. As one is brought into the presence of a king. Delivered.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has done. The first half of the ayah is *all verbs of self-motion.* They strive, they compete, they outstrip — all active, all theirs. The second half of the ayah is *one passive participle.* They are brought. Their agency, which they were so sure of, which they were investing so heavily in, is **simply removed from them** in the construction of the sentence itself.

The grammar performs the theology.

And then notice the second small wonder. The verse doesn't say *yuḥḍarūna ilā al-ʿadhāb* — "they will be brought *to* the punishment." It says *fī al-ʿadhābi muḥḍarūn* — "*in* the punishment, brought." The preposition *fī* — the same preposition that described their running *fī āyātinā* — comes back, but now they are not running inside His signs. They are stationary, *inside* the punishment.

The two *fī*s mirror each other. *Fī āyātinā* — the arena they chose to run in. *Fī al-ʿadhāb* — the arena they ended up standing in. Both arenas were always Allah's. They only got to choose which one they used up their *saʿy* trying to navigate.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing in this word. *Muḥḍar* — to be brought into presence — implies that *someone is doing the bringing.* The verb form is passive: the doer is hidden from the surface of the sentence. But the doer is implicit. They are brought. *By whom?*

The mufassirūn answer: *taḥḍuruhumu al-zabāniyah* — the angels of punishment, the keepers of the Fire, bring them. Al-Muyassar adds: *fa-lā yakhrujūna minhā* — "and they will not exit from it." The runners who could not be caught, by their own estimate, now cannot leave.

What this word reveals: every imagined contest with God ends the same way — not with a verdict reached after a long pursuit, but with the runner suddenly *standing still*, having been brought to the only destination there ever was. The race was always inside the courtroom.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three words have done together.

*Yasʿawn* — they ran with everything they had.
*Muʿājizīn* — they imagined themselves outstripping.
*Muḥḍarūn* — they were brought, standing still, into the presence of what they ran from.

Three words. A complete arc. The Arabic itself enacts the theology: motion, motion, *stop*. Self-driven verbs followed by a passive participle. An arena they chose to enter followed by an arena they were placed into. The grammar is doing the work.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the disbelievers of Mecca — but about a tendency that lives in every human heart, in some form, at some moment.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Imagined Race Against Reality

Begin not with the ayah. Begin with something familiar.

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from running from a truth you already know. Anyone who has ever delayed an important conversation — the one with the doctor, the one with the spouse, the one with the parent who is getting older — knows this exhaustion. You are not at rest. You are *expending energy* to maintain the illusion that the conversation does not need to happen. You change the subject. You schedule around it. You become busy with other things, *strategically* busy, in a way that the busy-ness is itself a fence around the thing you will not look at.

Psychologists have a term for this: *experiential avoidance.* It is the active work of not-knowing what you already know. And here is what the research shows: it costs more energy than acceptance. The person avoiding the truth is more tired than the person facing it. Because reality keeps arriving — through reminders, through dreams, through chance encounters — and every arrival has to be re-deflected. Every deflection takes work.

Now hear the ayah again, with this in your ears.

**وَٱلَّذِينَ يَسْعَوْنَ فِىٓ ءَايَـٰتِنَا مُعَـٰجِزِينَ**

"And those who run inside Our signs, as competitors trying to outstrip."

This is the cosmic version of experiential avoidance. It is what experiential avoidance looks like when it is scaled up from a personal truth to the truth of God Himself.

Stay with me here. The leaders of Quraysh were not lazy. They were *the most invested* people in Mecca with respect to the message of the Prophet ﷺ. They thought about it more than his followers did. They strategized about it more than he did. They spent more sleepless nights on it than the believers spent in prayer. **The opposition was a full-time occupation.**

And the Quran's diagnosis is brutal in its precision: this was not strength. This was *saʿy in the wrong direction.* This was a finite resource — human energy, human focus, human ingenuity — invested entirely in the project of not-arriving where they already knew they had to go.

Here is the human universal. Every one of us has a *muʿājiz* mode somewhere in our life. Maybe it is not directed at the signs of God in the same way as Quraysh's was. Maybe it is the relationship we run from. Maybe it is the prayer we keep delaying. Maybe it is the recovery we keep avoiding. Maybe it is the act of forgiveness we keep building elaborate reasons not to perform. **The race we are running is the proof that we already know where the finish line is.**

The ayah is not just describing them. It is showing us the mechanism. And then it is showing us the end of the mechanism.

Because here is the paradox the ayah quietly opens: *the more energy you invest in outrunning a reality, the more certain it is that you have located the reality.* You cannot run from what is not there. The fastest runners against the Quran were the ones whose hearts had been most struck by it. Walid ibn al-Mughirah, after hearing it, said the famous words — *innahu la-yaʿlū wa lā yuʿlā ʿalayh* — "it ascends and is not ascended over." And then went and led the opposition. He had *felt* it. And his response to the feeling was to run faster.

Here is what changes when this ayah lands in the heart: you start to notice your own running. You start to notice where you have invested *saʿy* into the avoidance of a thing that, somewhere in you, you already know is true. And the question that begins to form is the one every honest person eventually asks of himself: *what would it mean to stop running, and just stand?*

Because here is what the ayah is whispering underneath its warning: the standing-still is coming either way. *Muḥḍar* is the destination of every runner. The only question is whether you arrive standing on your own feet, brought by your own *saʿy* toward Allah, or whether you arrive having been *brought* — extracted from a race you could not actually win, into a presence you could not actually escape.

**Practical application:** Tonight, before you sleep, identify one piece of *saʿy* in your life that is aimed in the wrong direction. One thing you are running against. One avoidance that is costing you energy. Name it. Just name it. Do not yet try to stop the running. Just see it. The ayah is a mirror before it is anything else — let it mirror first.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Theology of Passive Arrival

Now let's go to the second half of the ayah, and what it reveals about the architecture of accountability itself.

Start here. There is a difference, in every human encounter, between *coming* and *being brought.* Think of a wedding. The groom arrives at his own wedding — but he *comes* to it. He chose the day, he chose the bride, he is walking in on his own feet, smiling. Now think of a defendant arriving at his sentencing. He is in the same courtroom. But he is *brought.* The bailiff is at his elbow. His agency is paused. The motion that put him in the room is not his motion.

The difference is not the room. The room is the same. The difference is the verb.

The Quran, in this ayah, is making a precise distinction. The believers, in many other ayahs, are described with verbs of arrival that they own. They *come* (*atā*), they *enter* (*dakhala*), they *are admitted* in honor (*udkhilū*). But here, for the runners, for the competitors against the signs, the verb is *muḥḍarūn.* Brought. Delivered. Summoned. Their agency is removed from the construction.

Notice what this reveals. The people who lived their whole life insisting on their own agency — *we will outrun, we will outstrip, we will render-incapable* — end the ayah *with no agency at all.* They are grammatical objects. The doer is hidden. The angels bring them, but the angels are not named, because the point is not who brings them. The point is that **they are no longer the ones moving.**

This is the theology underneath the grammar: **agency that is used to flee from Allah is the agency that gets removed in the end.** Agency that is used to walk *toward* Allah is the agency that gets honored — the believer is named as the doer of his arrival. Agency that is used to *muʿājaza* against Him gets quietly stripped away in the passive participle, and the runner is set down, standing, in the courtroom he thought he was outrunning.

Stay with this for a moment. Because there is something almost merciful in how Allah constructs this. He does not say in this ayah that they will be *dragged*, that they will be *thrown*, that they will be *crushed* into the punishment — though other ayahs in the Quran use precisely those verbs for other categories of the rejecters. Here, the verb is gentle in its surface — *they are brought.* Brought as one brings a guest. Brought as one brings the accused before the judge. There is no violence in *muḥḍar* itself. The violence is in the **silence of their agency.**

Here is the thing about the imagined race against God. It is not refuted by being lost. It is refuted by being *cancelled.* The competitor does not finish the race in second place. The competitor wakes up to find that there was no race — only a courtroom, and he is now standing in it.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is devastating: the cancellation is not announced. There is no moment in the ayah where Allah says, "and they realized there was never a race." The ayah simply *performs* the cancellation by switching grammatical voice. The runner becomes the brought. The active becomes passive. The chase becomes the standing-still. The verse itself enacts what the verse is describing.

Notice the connection to the broader surah. Surah Saba is built around this exact theme — the gap between the human estimate of one's own position and the reality of it. The people of Saba estimated themselves invulnerable. The wealthy estimated their wealth as proximity to God (the surah just rebutted this two verses earlier — *wa mā amwālukum wa lā awlādukum bi-llatī tuqarribukum ʿindanā zulfā*). And here, the runners estimate their speed as escape. The whole surah is a sustained correction of human self-estimation.

And the correction is always the same. Reality is one. Your estimate of it is another. The gap between them is the space in which all human delusion lives. And one day, the gap closes — not because you closed it, but because the courtroom opens.

**Practical application:** Reframe one daily act tomorrow. When you stand for ṣalāh, do not just stand for ṣalāh. Stand consciously as one who is *coming* to be in the presence of Allah — *ḥāḍir*, not *muḥḍar.* Choose the courtroom now. Walk into it on your own feet. Five times a day, you are given the chance to be the believer who *comes* rather than the one who is *brought.* Notice that gift. Use it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture of the Ayah

Now that we have walked through it, look at the structure of these nine words.

| First half | Second half |
|---|---|
| Active verb (*yasʿawn*) | Passive participle (*muḥḍarūn*) |
| They are moving | They are still |
| *Fī āyātinā* — inside His signs | *Fī al-ʿadhāb* — inside His punishment |
| Their imagined arena | The actual arena |
| Agency present | Agency removed |
| Competitive posture (*muʿājizīn*) | Delivered posture (*muḥḍarūn*) |

The ayah is structurally a hinge. The pivot is the demonstrative *ulā'ika* — "those." Everything before the pivot is the human imagination of the race. Everything after the pivot is the divine resolution of it. The pivot itself is the moment Allah's vantage point takes over from theirs.

This is not just a verse about the punishment of the disbelievers. It is a verse about **the structure of every human delusion about Allah's reach.** First, the running. Then, the imagined outstripping. Then, the pivot. Then, the silence of having been brought.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the thematic depths are the same insight, approached from two directions.

From the linguistic side: Arabic grammar enacted the theology. Active verbs collapsed into a passive participle. Self-chosen prepositions (*fī āyātinā*) inverted into delivered prepositions (*fī al-ʿadhāb*). The competitor posture (*muʿājiz*) dissolved into the delivered posture (*muḥḍar*).

From the thematic side: human energy invested in outrunning Allah is the proof that Allah has been located. The race is the confession. And the race ends not with a verdict but with a cancellation — the runner standing still in the courtroom he was sure he had outrun.

Both directions arrive at the same single insight:

**You cannot outrun the One who sees the racetrack from outside it.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in your life is your *saʿy* — your urgent, hurrying, focused energy — currently aimed? Is it aimed *toward* Allah, or is it aimed *against* something you already know is true?

2. What conversation, what prayer, what act of repentance, what reconciliation have you been busy *not having*? What is the energy of that not-having costing you?

3. The believer is invited to come; the rejector is brought. In your standing for prayer today, are you *coming* or being *brought*? What would it take to come on your own feet, with your agency intact?

4. The ayah ends with a hidden doer — *they are brought,* but the bringer is not named. What does it do to your heart to know that you are, in this moment, walking somewhere — and the One who will eventually escort the lost is also the One who, right now, you can walk *toward* freely?

5. The opposition to the Prophet ﷺ was the most invested, hardest-working response to the Quran in 7th-century Mecca. What does it tell you that the most exhausted people in the world are often the ones running *from* what would give them rest?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Every human energy spent outrunning the signs of Allah is energy spent constructing a race that does not exist, ending in the moment the runner is set down — standing, silent, agency removed — inside the courtroom he was sprinting away from.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not just a warning to the disbelievers of Mecca. It is a mirror held up to a specific kind of motion that every human being is capable of. The motion of running *against* what you already, somewhere, know to be true.

The invitation of the ayah is not to stop moving. *Saʿy* is a gift; the capacity for urgent, focused, hurrying energy is what makes a believer's life possible. The invitation is to **turn the direction of the running.** To take the same energy you have been spending on avoidance, on argument, on the elaborate construction of reasons to not-arrive — and to aim it the other way.

The ayah after this one — verse 39 — is the open door. *Wa mā anfaqtum min shay'in fa-huwa yukhlifuhū* — "whatever you spend, He will replace it." The Quran does not leave you with the closed door of verse 38. It opens the next one immediately. The runners of verse 38 are brought; the spenders of verse 39 are replenished. **Same surah. Same Allah. Different direction of the saʿy.**

Choose the direction. That is the whole invitation.

### Du'a

O Allah, who sees every racetrack from outside it: catch our *saʿy* before it spends itself in the wrong direction. Wherever we are running from You, turn our feet. Let us come to Your courtroom on our own feet, drawn by love, not delivered in silence. And on the day that the brought are brought, let us be among those who *came.*

Āmīn.
