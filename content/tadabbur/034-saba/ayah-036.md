# The Test Disguised as a Blessing

## Surah Saba 34:36

> قُلْ إِنَّ رَبِّى يَبْسُطُ ٱلرِّزْقَ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيَقْدِرُ وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ ٱلنَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

> *Qul inna rabbī yabsuṭu r-rizqa li-man yashā'u wa yaqdir, wa lākinna aktharan-nāsi lā yaʿlamūn.*

> **Say: Indeed my Lord extends provision for whom He wills and restricts it — but most people do not know.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World Before the Words

Picture Mecca in the years just before the Hijra. The Quraysh leaders are standing in the shade of the Kaʿbah, their caravans returning from Syria and Yemen, their storehouses full. They look at Muhammad ﷺ — a man without a tribe powerful enough to protect him, surrounded by followers who are slaves, freed slaves, the young, the poor. Bilal. Suhayb. ʿAmmār. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd.

And the Quraysh laugh. Not because they cannot understand his message. Because they think they have a knockdown argument.

*If your God truly favored you, you'd be rich. Look at us. Look at our wealth. Look at our position. The fact that we have everything and you have nothing — isn't that the verdict? Hasn't reality already spoken?*

This is the climate of Surah Saba. The whole surah is engaged in dismantling one specific argument — the argument that **worldly success is a divine signature, and worldly hardship is a divine rebuke**. It walks us through Dawud with his iron and his psalms. Sulayman with his jinn and his wind. Saba with their gardens and their dams. And in every single case, the surah shows us: the prosperity was not what they thought it was. It was a stage in a longer story. It was a test in disguise.

Two ayahs before our verse, in 34:34-35, the Quraysh — through the mouth of every haughty civilization the Prophets faced — say: *"We are more in wealth and children, and we will not be punished."* The pattern is so consistent it's almost a script. Power reads itself as permission.

And then, into that scene, comes our ayah. A single command. Eight Arabic words of substance. **Say.** Here is what to say back.

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

But notice something strange before we even open the text. The ayah doesn't say *"my Lord extends provision for the righteous and restricts it from the wicked."* That would have been a clean rebuttal. It would have flipped the Quraysh's logic against them — *no, actually, the opposite signal is the true one.*

Instead, Allah cuts the link entirely. He severs wealth from worth. He severs poverty from punishment. He removes the entire signal-reading apparatus from your hands. And then, at the end, He says something quietly devastating: **but most people do not know.**

What is it that most people do not know? Why is this one of the most repeated patterns in the entire Quran — that wealth is a test, not a verdict — and yet still, somehow, most people do not know it? What is the gravity that keeps pulling the human heart back into this misreading, no matter how many times it's corrected?

Let's open the words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Qul* — The Ayah That Begins With a Command to the Prophet ﷺ

Before we even reach the content, the very first word does something we need to slow down for.

**قُلْ** — *Qul.* Say.

Let's teach the concept before we use it. In ordinary speech, when you tell someone to "say" something, you're putting words in their mouth — but more than that, you're handing them an answer they didn't quite have on their own. A father tells his young son who's been wronged at school: *"Tomorrow, when he says that to you again, say this back."* The "say" carries the weight of someone older, someone who's seen more, someone equipping the younger person with the right reply for a moment that's coming.

Now look at where *Qul* appears in the Quran. It appears nearly 300 times. In almost every case, the Prophet ﷺ is being given language he is to deliver — not language he is composing. He is the messenger of the message, not just the messenger of the meaning. The exact words matter.

Stay with this. The Quraysh have just made an argument. They have said — paraphrasing the surrounding ayahs — *we have more, therefore we are favored.* Now the Prophet ﷺ stands in front of them. He has to respond. And Allah does not say *"O Muhammad, tell them this idea."* Allah says: **Qul.** Say. These words. In this order.

Here is what's remarkable. The Prophet ﷺ is the most eloquent of the Arabs. He could have answered the Quraysh in a hundred ways from his own tongue. But the answer to a theological misreading is not given to the eloquence of even the most eloquent. It is given by Allah Himself, scripted, and handed across. Because **the misreading of wealth as favor is so deep, so primal, that only revelation can dislodge it.**

You and I — when we encounter this argument in our own lives, from a relative who says *"if Islam were true, why are the Muslims so poor?"*, or from inside our own chest when we wonder *"if Allah loved me, would He let this happen?"* — we are not equipped to answer it from our own reasoning. We need the *Qul.* We need the scripted reply. The whole logic of the misreading is so seductive that even the Prophet ﷺ is given the words.

[PAUSE]

That's what *Qul* does here. It tells you: this is not an argument the Prophet ﷺ is making. This is an answer Allah is delivering through him. Which means it is not optional. It is not a perspective. It is the correction.

---

### Element Two: *Yabsuṭu* and *Yaqdiru* — The Two Hands of Provision

Now to the heart of the ayah. Two verbs, both in the present-tense imperfect form, both with Allah as the subject:

**يَبْسُطُ** — *yabsuṭu* — He extends, He spreads out, He expands
**يَقْدِرُ** — *yaqdiru* — He restricts, He measures out, He tightens

Let's teach the concept before we open it. Arabic verbs in the *muḍāriʿ* — the imperfect tense — carry continuity. They do not describe a thing that happened once. They describe a thing that is **happening, ongoing, characteristic.** When you say in English *"He extends provision,"* it sits flat. In Arabic, *yabsuṭu* means *He is, right now, in this moment and in every moment, extending it.* The action never stopped.

Now the two verbs.

**Basṭ** — from the root ب-س-ط — has a striking lexical field. The root means *to spread out, to lay flat, to unfold, to make wide and open.* The same root gives us *bisāṭ* — a carpet, a rug, the thing you unroll across a floor. It gives us *basīṭ* — simple, smooth, unobstructed. When Allah uses *yabsuṭu* for provision, He is not picturing money being handed out coin by coin. He is picturing **a carpet being unrolled.** Sustenance laid flat in front of someone, spread wide, no obstacles, easy to walk across.

Notice what that physical image is doing. It is the image of a path made smooth. A surface made wide. Doors opening. A life where things… flow.

**Qadr** — from the root ق-د-ر — means *to measure, to determine, to set the exact amount.* It is the same root from which we get *al-Qadar*, divine decree. But here, used opposite to *basṭ*, it specifically means **to tighten the measure, to make the portion precise and small.** The image is not of a carpet but of a careful cup — exactly this much, no more.

So the two pictures: the unrolled carpet and the carefully measured cup. The expansive path and the narrow ration.

[PAUSE]

Now read the absence. Because here is what's stunning, and you might have missed it.

**Allah does not name who gets which.**

He says *yabsuṭu r-rizqa li-man yashā'u* — "He extends provision for whomever He wills." And then He says *wa yaqdiru* — *"and He restricts."* For whom? For whom does He restrict?

The English translations supply it — "and restricts it for whom He wills" — to make the sentence read smoothly. But in the Arabic, the second *li-man yashā'u* is **gapped.** It is not there. The verb *yaqdiru* sits alone, with no object specified.

Why?

Because Allah is doing something rhetorically extraordinary. He has just told you that the extending is according to *mashī'a* — His will. He could have said it again for the restricting and the sentence would be symmetrical. But by not saying it the second time, He is doing two things at once.

First, He is telling you the two actions are governed by the **same** *mashī'a*. They are not two different processes. They are one process — divine will allocating provision — and *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdiru* are just the two directions that single will moves in.

Second, and this is what catches the breath: by leaving the second *li-man yashā'u* unsaid, **Allah refuses to give either side of the equation a moral identity.** He will not say "He extends for those He loves and restricts from those He disregards." He will not say "He extends as reward and restricts as punishment." He simply will not let you build that grammar.

The wealthy person cannot say *"He extended this for me because of who I am."* And the poor person cannot say *"He restricted this from me because of who I am."* The grammar itself blocks both readings.

Stay with that. The grammar itself is the theology.

[PAUSE]

And classical commentary confirms it. Al-Baghawī says directly: *"Allah extends provision and restricts it as a trial and a test — the extending does not indicate Allah's pleasure with him, nor does the restriction indicate His displeasure."* Al-Qurṭubī adds the consequence: *"The breadth of provision in this world does not indicate happiness in the Hereafter — so do not think your wealth and your children will avail you anything tomorrow."* Al-Muyassar names the cause explicitly: *"not out of love and not out of hatred, but He does this as a test."*

Four classical sources, four cameras on the same reality. None of them read wealth as a divine signature. All of them read it as a stage on which the human being is tested.

---

### Element Three: *Lā yaʿlamūn* — The Knowing That Doesn't Stick

The ayah closes with a phrase that, at first reading, feels like a familiar Quranic refrain:

**وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ ٱلنَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ** — *but most people do not know.*

But notice something. Notice what's missing. Allah does not say *"most people do not believe"* — *lā yu'minūn.* He does not say *"most people do not understand"* — *lā yafqahūn.* He does not say *"most people do not reflect"* — *lā yatafakkarūn.*

He says **lā yaʿlamūn.** They do not *know.*

The root ع-ل-م — *ʿilm* — is the root for knowledge in its plainest form. Information. Awareness. Recognition of how things actually are. When Allah negates *ʿilm* here, He is not saying these people refuse to believe. He is saying: **they do not even have the basic informational grasp of how provision works.** The fact has not even reached them yet, let alone been accepted.

Stay with this. Because it raises a question that the ayah is quietly forcing on us.

How can this be one of the most repeated patterns in the entire Quran — wealth as test, not as verdict — and yet *lā yaʿlamūn?* How can the information itself not have landed?

Here's what's remarkable. Al-Qurṭubī gives the answer in one phrase. He explains *lā yaʿlamūn* like this: **لأنهم لا يتأملون** — *because they do not reflect.* They do not engage in *taʾammul* — careful, patient looking at what's in front of them.

So the *ʿilm* that's absent is not information. The information is everywhere. The Quran has said this dozens of times. Every prophet said it. Every wise person has said it. The information is in the air the listener breathes.

What's absent is the *taʾammul* — the willingness to sit with it long enough to let it actually settle as knowledge. People hear it and walk past it. They nod and forget. They affirm it on Friday and forget by Sunday morning when the neighbor pulls up in the new car.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most precise diagnoses in the entire Quran of what we now call *the gap between knowing and knowing.* You know it intellectually. The neurons fire. You could answer the question correctly on a test. But the knowledge has not penetrated to the layer of your seeing. When you walk past the mansion, you still feel something. When you see the homeless man, you still feel something. The lower brain is still running the misreading even though the upper brain has the correction.

That gap — between the upper brain's affirmation and the lower brain's interpretation — is what *lā yaʿlamūn* is naming. And the cure, per al-Qurṭubī, is not more information. It is *taʾammul.* It is what we are doing right now. Slow, sustained, deliberate looking.

---

### Bridge

So the linguistic structure has shown us three things.

The *Qul* tells us this answer is so important it had to be scripted by Allah Himself.

The *yabsuṭu / yaqdiru* pairing — with its gapped second clause — refuses to let either wealth or poverty carry moral signature.

The *lā yaʿlamūn* tells us this isn't an information problem; it's a reflection problem.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Misread Signal — Why the Heart Keeps Returning to the Wrong Reading

Imagine you are walking through an airport. You see a man walking briskly through the priority lane, suit tailored, watch heavy. And then, in the next lane over, you see a woman in cleaning uniform pushing a mop bucket. Be honest with yourself about what your first, fastest, pre-rational response is.

You do not think *"two souls, each on a path the Almighty has measured for them, neither of which tells me anything about their worth before God."* You think — flickeringly, in the half-second before your conscious mind catches up — *the man is doing well, and the woman is not.*

That's the misread signal. And it does not appear because you are a bad person. It appears because **you are a human being living in a world where almost every signaling system is correlated with worth in some way.** Better grades signal better effort. Better health signals better habits. Better relationships signal better character — usually. The brain is a pattern-finding machine, and over millions of years it has learned to read external states as evidence of internal ones.

Wealth slips into that system like it belongs there. **It looks like a signal.** It walks like a signal. It quacks like a signal. And the brain reads it as such before the soul can intervene.

This is why *lā yaʿlamūn* is not a moral failing — it's a structural one. The misreading is the default setting of the pattern-recognition system. You have to *override* the default, every time, with deliberate *taʾammul*, with the ayah's own correction running in the background, or the default will reassert itself.

And here is where the ayah gets surgical. Because notice what Allah is NOT doing. He is not saying *"do not be impressed by wealth."* He is not saying *"wealth is bad."* He is doing something far more precise. He is saying: **the signal you think you're reading isn't actually there.** It's not that the signal is bad. It's that there is no signal.

The man in the suit might be on the cusp of Hellfire and not know it. The woman with the mop might be among the friends of Allah and not need to know it. The clothing has nothing to say about the soul. The clothing is a costume the divine will has dressed each of them in for a stage of a play whose meaning will only be clear at the end.

[PAUSE]

Now hear how the surah itself dramatizes this. Just before our ayah, the Quraysh elite say *"we have more wealth and children, we will not be punished."* And the surah's answer is not just *Qul inna rabbī yabsuṭu.* The surah's whole architecture is the answer.

It tells you about **Saba** — a people whose gardens were so abundant that their land was called *baldatun ṭayyibatun wa rabbun ghafūr* — *a good land and a forgiving Lord.* And what happened to them? The flood of al-ʿArim. Their gardens replaced with bitter fruit and thorn. **The basṭ became qadr in their own lifetime.** The signal they had been reading reversed itself in front of their eyes.

It tells you about **Sulayman** — the wealthiest of any human being who ever lived, with jinn building, wind carrying, and the entire created world bowing to his command. And what happened? He died standing, leaning on a staff, and the jinn — who supposedly knew the unseen — did not notice he was dead until the staff broke. **All his wealth could not give him knowledge of his own death.** The basṭ was real but it was telling no truth about what was coming next.

In every story the surah hands us, the prosperity was a test, not a verdict. Saba failed the test and the gardens vanished. Sulayman passed the test and the wealth was lifted from him gently, even his corpse held upright until the work was done. **Same external signal. Different inner reality. Different ending.**

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

Here is the application. When you next encounter someone who has more than you — a colleague who was promoted past you, a relative whose business is thriving while yours struggles, a stranger whose Instagram is glowing while yours is gray — your soul will produce a flicker. Either envy, or the inverse: a quiet self-rebuke, *I must be displeasing Allah.* Both of those flickers come from the same misreading. They both treat the external as a signal.

The discipline this ayah is asking for is not to suppress the flicker. It is to **answer it.** To say back to your own nafs, in the same scripted way the Prophet ﷺ was given the *Qul:* *Inna rabbī yabsuṭu r-rizqa li-man yashā'u wa yaqdiru.* The carpet is unrolled and the cup is measured. Neither says anything about who is loved. I am not reading what I think I am reading.

You may need to say it many times. *Lā yaʿlamūn* implies that even those who technically know it, do not actually know it — until the *taʾammul* sinks the knowing past the surface.

### Theme Two: When Restriction Itself Is the Provision

We have spoken about wealth as misread blessing. But there is something deeper hidden in this ayah, and it lives in the gapped clause.

Look again at *yabsuṭu r-rizqa li-man yashā'u wa yaqdiru.* Notice once more that *yaqdiru* — He restricts — sits alone, without an object. We said earlier that this prevents the reader from giving either side a moral identity.

But there is a second thing the gap does.

It **refuses to call the restriction a deprivation.**

If Allah had said *"He extends provision for whom He wills and withholds it from whom He wills,"* the symmetry would have implied: extension is the giving, withholding is the not-giving. Provision in one case, absence-of-provision in the other.

But that's not what *yaqdiru* means. *Yaqdiru* means *measures it out, sets a precise amount.* It is not the negation of provision. **It is provision in a different shape.**

Stay with this. The carpet is unrolled. That is rizq. The cup is precisely measured. **That is also rizq.**

Both are giving. Both are from the same Hand. One is wide, one is narrow, but neither is absence. *Yaqdiru* is not the closed fist of withholding. It is the careful fingertip that hands you exactly the portion you need.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most consoling absences in the Quran. By refusing to symmetrically pair *yabsuṭu* with a verb of withholding, Allah is telling the person whose rizq has been measured tight: **you have not been given less. You have been given measured.** The amount in your cup is the amount your soul could carry. The amount in someone else's carpet is the amount their stage requires. Both are exact. Both are deliberate. Both are mercy.

Think about what this means in your own life. There is, almost certainly, some area in which you feel restricted right now. Money. Time. Health. A relationship. Recognition. The opportunity you keep almost getting. The peace that keeps slipping away. There is some narrowness you are carrying, and somewhere in your chest there is a quiet accusation — *Allah, why for them and not for me?*

The ayah turns the accusation back into a question. **What if the narrowness IS what He measured for you?** What if it is not a withholding but a portion — the exact portion you needed to walk this particular path?

Hear what the surah is telling you through its own logic. Sulayman with all the wealth in creation died leaning on a stick. The poor companion in Mecca, with nothing, walked behind the Prophet ﷺ and saw the face of the messenger of God every morning. **Which one received more?** The vocabulary of *more* and *less* depends entirely on what we are measuring.

The companion whose rizq was *yaqdiru* — measured narrow in dirhams — received it widely in *baraka,* in proximity, in tranquility, in the chance to be among those whose hearts were attached to nothing in this world. The carpet was rolled tight on his floor, and that compression became the very thing that pushed his soul upward.

[PAUSE]

There is an athar — a saying attributed to one of the early Muslims — that captures this exactly. They said: *"How many a person was destroyed by what was given to them, and how many was saved by what was withheld."* That is the entire ayah in one sentence. The basṭ that destroys; the qadr that saves.

So the practical question becomes: **what is the narrowness in your life right now, and have you sat with it long enough to ask what it might be giving you?**

Not what it is taking from you. What it is *giving.* What the cup contains that the unrolled carpet does not.

Maybe the restriction in your finances is the very thing keeping your marriage together because you have not had the disposable income to drift apart. Maybe the restriction in your career is the very thing keeping your prayers humble. Maybe the restriction in your health is what made you turn back to Allah after years of forgetting. Maybe the restriction in your social life is the silence in which you can finally hear yourself think.

I do not know what your narrowness is. But I know that the ayah refuses to call it deprivation. The grammar refuses. The lexicon refuses. The classical scholars refuse. Al-Qurṭubī says it is *imtiḥān* — a test. Al-Muyassar says it is *ikhtibār* — an examination. And every test is also an opportunity. The narrowness is your scripted role in a play. Sit with what it is asking of you. That sitting is the *taʾammul* the ayah is calling for.

The application: pick one place in your life where you feel restricted. Tonight, before you sleep, instead of asking *"why is this restricted?"* — ask, *"what has being restricted here let me become that being expanded would not have?"* And then let the question sit. Do not rush to answer. The answer often arrives weeks later, in an unexpected moment. That delayed arrival is the *taʾammul* doing its work.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic structure and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar told us: the extending and the restricting come from one will, with no moral signature attached to either. The theme told us: this is why the heart's pattern-reading apparatus keeps failing.

The grammar told us: *yaqdiru* is not the negation of *yabsuṭu* — it is a different shape of the same giving. The theme told us: this is why your restriction may itself be your provision.

The grammar told us: *lā yaʿlamūn* is not unbelief — it is the failure to reflect. The theme told us: this is why even the most well-known truth in the Quran has to be re-learned every time you walk past a mansion or open Instagram.

The whole ayah is doing one thing. It is dismantling the reading system through which you misperceive your own life and the lives of others. And it is replacing that system with a single scripted *Qul* — a sentence so important that Allah did not trust even the most eloquent of His messengers to compose it from his own tongue.

### Questions to Carry

1. When was the last time you felt that someone with more — money, status, recognition — was being shown more favor by Allah than you? What would it look like to sit with that flicker and answer it, instead of either suppressing it or believing it?

2. What is the narrowness in your life that you keep asking Allah to remove? Have you ever asked Him, instead, what it is giving you?

3. The ayah says *most people do not know.* Where in your own life is there a piece of Quranic truth that you intellectually affirm but do not yet live by? What would *taʾammul* on that one truth look like this week?

4. If wealth is not a divine signature, what becomes of the way you have read your own past — the seasons of abundance and the seasons of scarcity? Have they been telling you a story that is not actually there?

5. The Prophet ﷺ was given the exact words to deliver. What scripted reply do you need to memorize for the next time your own nafs makes the misreading?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The same Hand that unrolls the carpet measures the cup, and neither act is what your eye thinks it is.

---

### The Closing Invitation

You will not stop reading worldly signals as signs of worth. The pattern-recognition system is too old and too deep for a single ayah to disable. But you can begin to **answer** the misreading every time it appears. Carry the *Qul* in your chest. Carry the gap between *yabsuṭu* and *yaqdiru* — the gap that refuses to call either one a verdict. And carry the diagnosis of *lā yaʿlamūn* — that knowing is not enough until you have sat with what you know.

The shift this ayah is inviting is not toward indifference to wealth or sentimentality about poverty. It is toward **a quieter eye.** An eye that sees the man in the suit and the woman with the mop and does not assume it has been told anything about either of their standings before Allah. An eye that looks at its own narrow cup and does not assume it has been told anything either, except that the One who measured it knows the size of the hand that will hold it.

---

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah — You who unroll the carpet and measure the cup, whose extending is not favor and whose restricting is not rebuke — teach our hearts to read what You have written, not what our eyes invent. Let us not be among those of whom You said *most people do not know.* Place us instead among those who sit with what You have given, until the knowing settles past our minds and into our seeing.

Āmīn.
