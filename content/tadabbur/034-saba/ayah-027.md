# Show Me What You Attached

## Surah Saba 34:27

> قُلْ أَرُونِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَلْحَقْتُم بِهِۦ شُرَكَآءَۖ كَلَّاۚ بَلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

> *Qul arūniya alladhīna alḥaqtum bihi shurakāʾ. Kallā. Bal huwa Allāhu al-ʿAzīzu al-Ḥakīm.*

> "Say: 'Show me those whom you have **attached** to Him as partners.' **By no means!** Rather, He is Allah, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Imagine you are sitting in a courtroom. A man is on the stand, and for the last hour the prosecution has been quietly removing every leg from beneath his story. Not loudly. Not theatrically. Just question after question, each one taking away something he had assumed was solid. The room where the alleged crime took place — gone. The witnesses he named — none of them were there. The motive — exposed as something he constructed afterward. The opportunity — impossible by the timeline.

And now there's a silence. Everyone in the room knows the case is finished. There's nothing left of the story he came in with. But the prosecutor stands up one more time, walks to the center of the room, and says only this:

*"Sir. Show me the man."*

Not "explain the crime." Not "tell me again." Just — produce him. Bring him forward. Point to him. The whole story you've been telling depends on this person actually existing. **Show me where he is.**

That is the rhetorical position of ayah 27 in Surah Saba.

[PAUSE]

For five ayahs before this, Allah has been quietly dismantling the cosmology of the mushrikūn — the people who attached partners to Him. In 34:22 He told the Prophet ﷺ to challenge them: call upon those partners; they don't own an atom in the heavens or the earth, they have no share in either, and Allah has no helper among them. In 34:23 He closed the door on intercession — none can intercede except by His permission. In 34:24 He pressed further: ask them *who* gives them sustenance from the heavens and the earth, and then *answer for them* — "It is Allah."

By the time we arrive at 34:27, the case is made. There is nothing left structurally for a partner to do. He doesn't own anything. He doesn't sustain anything. He can't intercede without permission. He has no share. He has no portion.

And then Allah says: **"Show me."**

This is not a request for information. This is the finishing move. This is what you say when you have already proven that the thing your opponent is defending does not exist — and you want them to feel that emptiness themselves.

But here is what's stunning. The verb Allah chooses for "you took these partners" is not "you worshipped" — it is not "you took" — it is not "you believed in." It is a verb so precise, so revealing about the nature of shirk itself, that once you see it, you cannot un-see it. It will change how you understand not just the partners of the polytheists of Mecca, but every quiet partner you may have attached to Allah in your own heart without ever naming it that.

Stay with me. Let's open this slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَرُونِىَ — The Grammar of a Challenge

Before we enter this word, let's understand the kind of grammar at work.

In Arabic, there is a verb form called **Form IV** — built on the pattern *afʿala*. Many verbs in Arabic have a basic form (Form I) which describes a state or a simple action, and then a Form IV which transforms that verb into something *transitive* — something done *to* someone, *for* someone, *upon* someone. The simplest function of Form IV is to take a one-person verb and make it two-person. To take an internal event and make it external.

So the root *ra-ʾā* (ر-أ-ى) in its basic form means **"he saw."** It is something that happens inside one person. Their eye, their object. One participant.

But Form IV — *arā* (أَرَى) — means **"he made another see."** It pulls a second person into the verb. Now there are two participants: the one who shows and the one who is shown.

In English we don't have a single word for this. We have to use two words: *show me*, *make me see*, *let me see*. The Arabic does it in one tight construction.

Now look at the word Allah uses:

> أَرُونِىَ
> *arūniya*

**"You all — make me see."**

The "you" here is plural — Allah is addressing all the mushrikūn at once. And the "me" is the speaker. The Prophet ﷺ is being commanded to say this, but the structure of the demand is universal: bring this into view. Pull what is in your minds out into the open. Put it in front of me where I can examine it.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with what kind of speech act this is.

When you say to someone *"show me,"* you are doing one of two things. You are either asking because you genuinely don't know — *show me your new car, show me the photos from your trip* — or you are issuing a challenge because you suspect the thing doesn't exist as the person claims. *Show me where it says that in the contract. Show me the receipt. Show me one example.*

The first kind comes from curiosity. The second kind comes from certainty. The second *"show me"* already knows the answer. It's not asking. It's exposing.

Allah is the All-Knowing. He is not asking the polytheists for information. He knows exactly what they think their partners are. He created them and He created the idols they bowed before. The challenge is not for Him to learn — it is for *them* to discover that they cannot produce what they have been defending.

This is what classical scholars call a *taḥaddī* — a challenge framed as a request. And here, al-Qurṭubī notices something extraordinary about the verb. He says: this *arūnī* could be understood in two senses. It could be *ruʾyat al-baṣar* — vision of the eye — *show me physically what these partners are.* Or it could be *ruʾyat al-qalb* — vision of the heart, of the intellect — *show me conceptually, define for me, identify for me — make me understand who you mean.*

Don't choose between these. Hold both.

Because what Allah is really saying is: **whether you appeal to the eye or to the mind — produce them. By any faculty you possess. Pull them into view.**

And the listener, if they are honest, suddenly realizes — *I can't.* I can point to a stone, but I cannot point to what makes it a partner. I can name a name, but I cannot show what makes that name divine. The thing I have been defending has no physical address and no conceptual coordinates. It exists only in the moment I am not being asked to produce it.

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable. This is the same rhetorical structure Allah uses elsewhere in the Quran — *arūnī* in Surah Fatir 35:40, *ayna shurakāʾī* in Surah al-Naḥl 16:27 and Fuṣṣilat 41:47. Each time the verb of *seeing* or the question of *where* is deployed against a claim that cannot survive being looked at.

Shirk lives in the dark. The moment it is asked to stand in light, it cannot.

That is what this single word — *arūnī* — does. It turns on the light.

### Element Two: أَلْحَقْتُم — The Verb That Reveals What Shirk Actually Is

Now we come to the word that, if you have only one word from this ayah to carry away, this should be it.

> أَلْحَقْتُم
> *alḥaqtum*

It is built on the root *l-ḥ-q* (ل-ح-ق). In its basic form, the verb *laḥiqa* means **to follow, to catch up to, to overtake, to reach, to come up alongside.** When someone walks ahead of you and you run to join them, *laḥiqahum* — you caught up to them, you joined them, you came up with them.

So *laḥaqa* is a verb of one thing coming after and reaching another. The natural meaning is movement: one thing closing the gap toward another.

Now apply Form IV: *alḥaqa*. This makes it transitive — you take something, and *you* cause it to catch up to, *you* make it reach, *you* affix it, *you* attach it. The lexical sense is *to append, to join on, to make follow.*

A classical example: in Arabic legal language, *alḥaqa al-walada bi-abīhi* means "he attached the child to his father" — meaning, he formally affixed lineage. He made a connection that required an act of attachment.

Another usage: *alḥaqa al-bayta bi-baytihi* — he annexed the house onto his house. He joined a structure that was previously separate. He built an extension. He stuck one thing onto another.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again with this in mind:

> أَرُونِىَ ٱلَّذِينَ **أَلْحَقْتُم** بِهِۦ شُرَكَآءَ
> "Show me those whom **you have attached** to Him as partners."

Listen to what Allah is *not* saying.

He is not saying "those whom you worshipped." He is not saying "those whom you believed in." He is not saying "those whom you took as gods." He is not saying "those who became partners with Him."

He is saying: *"those whom you appended. Those whom you affixed. Those whom you stuck onto Him. Those whom you yourselves had to join to His reality, because they were never naturally there."*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The grammar of this verb places *all the agency* on the polytheists. **They** are the ones who did the attaching. The partners did not become partners. The partners did not assert themselves. The partners did not naturally accrue to Allah's reality. The partners had to be *manually appended* by human hands.

This is the entire theology of shirk in a single morphological choice.

Shirk is not the discovery of additional gods. Shirk is the *attachment*, by human will, of a fictional partnership onto a reality that is whole without it. The thing you call a partner is not a partner. It is something you *attached* and called a partner. The attaching is yours. The connection is yours. The categorization is yours. None of it is from the side of Allah, and none of it is from the side of the thing you attached.

Imam al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī, and al-Jalālayn all read *alḥaqtum* with the same essential force: *jaʿaltumūhum shurakāʾ* — "you *made them* partners." The making is the human action. The partnership has no existence outside of your action of making it.

Now let's stay here.

[PAUSE]

Think about what it means in everyday language when we say someone *appended* something. You append a footnote to a paper. You append a clause to a contract. You append a signature to a document. The thing being appended is *not part of the original.* It is added. It is stuck on after the fact. It depends entirely on your act of attaching it.

If someone tomorrow appended a thirteenth point to the Bill of Rights with a pen, and we walked in and saw it, we would not say *"look, the Bill of Rights has thirteen points now."* We would say *"someone added that. That's not part of it."*

This is exactly what Allah is doing. He is naming the partner-claim as *a thing humans appended.* Not a thing that exists. A footnote stuck onto a document where no footnote was ever needed.

And then, into the silence of that exposure, comes the word that does the demolition.

### Element Three: كَلَّا — The Word That Slams the Door

> كَلَّا
> *Kallā*

This is one of the strongest negations in the Arabic language. It is not the soft *lā* of ordinary "no." It is not the *laysa* of factual denial. It is *kallā* — a particle that classical grammarians describe as *ḥarf radʿ wa-zajr* — **a particle of deterrent rebuke.**

What does that mean?

A *radʿ* is when you not only refuse a claim but you push the claim back at the one who made it. Imagine someone walks toward you with an idea, and you don't just say *"I disagree"* — you put your hand up and physically push them back. *Stop. Get this thought out of your mouth. Get this thought out of your head. Do not bring this to me again.*

That is *kallā*. It is the verbal equivalent of a hand raised to push something back.

[PAUSE]

al-Jalālayn comments on this exact word in this exact ayah:

> «كَلَّا» — ردع لهم عن اعتقاد شريك له
> "*Kallā* — a deterrent rebuke against them, against believing in a partner for Him."

It is not just a "no" to the proposition. It is a deterrent against ever *entertaining* the proposition. It is a slamming shut of the entire mental category from which the idea came.

And notice where it falls in the ayah. Allah has just said *"Show me those you attached as partners."* He has issued the challenge. He has invited them to produce what they claim. And then — without waiting for the answer, without giving them even the rhetorical space to stammer a reply — He himself answers on their behalf: **kallā.**

Not "kallā" *because they failed to produce.* But "kallā" *because there was never anything to produce.* The rebuke is preemptive. He doesn't even let the bad idea finish forming.

[PAUSE]

Here's what is remarkable about the placement.

If the ayah had said *"Show me those whom you attached as partners — and you will find that you cannot,"* it would have been an argument. A logical demonstration. *You try, you fail, conclusion follows.*

But Allah does not argue. He challenges, and then He slams the door before the answer even arrives. This is the speech of someone who knows. Someone who is not in conversation but in instruction. *I am giving you the chance to look — but I am not pretending there is any genuine doubt about what you will find.*

The *kallā* is the difference between a teacher who lets the student fumble and a teacher who knows the student well enough to cut off the fumbling for their own sake.

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

We have looked at three things.

We saw that **arūnī** is a challenge that exposes — Allah asking for the production of what has no address.

We saw that **alḥaqtum** is the verb that names shirk for what it really is — not the discovery of partners, but the human act of appending fictional ones.

We saw that **kallā** is the deterrent that slams the door — not arguing the case but ending it.

Three movements. The light is turned on. The artifice is named. The category is closed.

[PAUSE]

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about the words on the page — but about reality itself, and about the quiet places in our own hearts where we may be doing the very thing this ayah is exposing.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Audit of an Attached Belief

There is an experience in modern life that I want you to recall. It happens in interviews, in cross-examinations, in those moments when someone you respect asks you to *defend* a position you have held for years without ever being asked to defend it.

You feel a particular kind of vertigo. You've believed this thing for a long time. You've acted on it. You've made decisions around it. You've never doubted it, exactly. But you've also never *examined* it.

And now someone is asking you to lay it out. To produce it. To show them the basis for it. To name the source. To articulate the logic. To bring forward the evidence.

And in that moment, sometimes — not always, but sometimes — you discover something strange. The belief that felt solid when it was unexamined becomes suddenly hollow when it is asked to stand in the light. You realize you cannot quite name *why* you believed it. You cannot find the original source. You cannot reconstruct the argument. You have been carrying around something that has no foundation, and the foundation only became absent when you turned to look for it.

This is the experience of being audited.

[PAUSE]

Allah, in this ayah, is auditing the polytheists. He is asking them to produce what they have been carrying. *Show me. Bring it forward. Let me see what these partners are.*

And the entire force of the ayah depends on the fact that **they cannot.** Not because they refuse, but because the thing they were carrying never had a substance to begin with. It survived only in the dark of habit, of inheritance, of social pressure, of the thousand small reasons we accept things without examining them. Once asked to produce, it evaporated.

Now here is the question — and I want you to actually ask it, not just hear it.

What have *you* attached to Allah in your own life that would evaporate if you were asked to produce it?

[PAUSE]

I don't mean the obvious idols of the Meccans — the stones and the statues. The Quran is not addressing only their specific shirk. The Quran is addressing the *grammar* of shirk, which is the human tendency to append things to the divine — sources of security, sources of identity, sources of decision-making, sources of fear, sources of hope — that we silently treat as partners even while we say with our tongue *lā ilāha illa Allāh.*

The job we believe is providing for us — have we attached it to Him as a co-provider? If tomorrow He asked us *"Show me that job — show me how it provides independently of My will"* — could we produce it as a partner? Or would it evaporate into His reality, which was always the only reality?

The reputation we have built — have we attached it to Him as a co-honor? If asked to produce it as a partner, could we? Or does it dissolve the moment we look directly at it?

The person whose approval we crave — have we attached them to Him as a co-decider of our worth? Could we produce them as an independent source of value? Or do they too collapse into the truth that *huwa Allāh — He is Allah*?

[PAUSE]

Here is the merciful thing about this ayah. The audit is not happening on the Day of Judgment — yet. It is being offered to the polytheists in this life, in this moment, while there is still time to look and to discover that the partner you attached has no substance — and to *de-attach* it. To take the footnote off the document. To return to the original.

The *alḥaqtum* is in the perfect tense — *you have attached.* What is attached can be detached. The verb that names the problem also names its undoing. You attached it; you can also remove the attachment. The text that you appended to is whole; remove your appendage and what was always whole returns to view.

This is the gift of the audit. It does not destroy the auditee — it exposes what was never really there, so that what *is* there can be seen.

Tomorrow morning — and this is the practical move — name one thing you have been quietly attaching. Not necessarily an idol. Perhaps an outcome you cannot release. Perhaps a person whose approval you treat as the verdict on your worth. Perhaps a future scenario you keep running because you have made it the floor of your peace. Name it. Hold it in front of yourself for one breath. And then ask: *if Allah said to me right now "show me this partner you have attached to Me," could I produce it as anything but a thing I made up?*

That moment of inability is the *kallā* working inside your own heart.

### Theme Two: The Asymmetry of Attachment — Why the Verb is on You

The second thing I want us to sit with is the asymmetry that this ayah quietly reveals.

In a normal partnership — a business partnership, a marriage, a co-authorship — both parties have to consent. There is a meeting of two wills. Both parties bring something. Both parties are present at the formation of the bond. The partnership is real because both sides participate in making it real.

Now look at the shirk that Allah is naming.

**Allah did not participate in the attaching.** He did not consent. He did not become a partner. He did not even acknowledge the partner. The verb is entirely on the human side: *alḥaqtum — you* attached. Allah is grammatically passive in the partnership. He is the destination of the attachment, not its co-creator.

And the alleged partner — the idol, the stone, the figure, the inheritance, the imagined intercessor — also did not participate. The idol did not request the partnership. The idol cannot. The stone is just a stone. The dead saint did not nominate themselves. The constellation did not advocate for divinity.

So the entire "partnership" exists in **one place only** — the mind and act of the human being who appended it. It is a contract with one signatory pretending to be three.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the loneliness of that.

When a person commits shirk, they are not entering into a relationship. They are *imagining* a relationship that exists nowhere except in their own attribution. The idol does not love them back. The system does not love them back. The career does not love them back. The image they have built does not love them back. None of these things even know the person exists.

This is what makes shirk so quietly tragic. It is not just theologically wrong. It is *relationally barren.* You are pouring devotion into things that are not even aware of you. You are appending yourself to objects that have no capacity to append themselves to you.

The only place in this entire arrangement where there is genuine awareness, genuine knowing, genuine relationship — is Allah. Who has been waiting the whole time. Who knows you. Who created you. Who has been calling you to a relationship that is mutual, that is alive, that goes both ways.

And the person committing shirk is standing with their back to the only door that opens — and bowing to a wall that doesn't know they're there.

[PAUSE]

This is why the ayah does not end at *kallā*. The rebuke is necessary, but it is not the whole message. The whole message is what comes next:

> بَلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ
> *Bal huwa Allāhu al-ʿAzīzu al-Ḥakīm.*
> "**Rather** — He is Allah, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise."

The *bal* is a particle of redirection. It says: *do not stay in the negation. Do not just leave with "no." Look here. Turn your face. This is what is actually true.*

And what is actually true is named with two attributes that are extraordinary when paired:

**al-ʿAzīz** — the All-Mighty. The One whose power cannot be overcome, whose decree cannot be diverted, whose will is not subject to any other will. The One who *cannot be added to* because He is already the totality of strength. There is no room in *ʿizz* for a co-strength. To be al-ʿAzīz is to be the only one in that category.

**al-Ḥakīm** — the All-Wise. The One whose every decree is placed exactly where it belongs, whose every action serves a purpose, who never errs in placement, who never miscalculates, who never needs to revise. The One who *cannot be advised* because He is already the source of all wisdom. There is no room in *ḥikma* for a co-wisdom. To be al-Ḥakīm is to be the only one in that category.

Now hear them together: *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm.* All-Mighty *and* All-Wise.

Why this pairing, specifically, after a refutation of shirk?

Because shirk usually rests on one of two fears. Either you fear that Allah is *not powerful enough* to handle what you need — so you attach a secondary power as backup — or you fear that Allah is *not wise enough* to understand your particular case — so you attach a secondary intercessor who you imagine will understand you better, plead for you better, see you better than He does.

al-ʿAzīz removes the first fear. There is no shortage of power that requires a backup.

al-Ḥakīm removes the second fear. There is no gap in understanding that requires a translator.

Together, these two names close every door through which a partner could be smuggled in. He is enough in power. He is enough in wisdom. There is no role left for any partner to fill — and any partner you have attached, you attached not because there was a vacancy, but because *you imagined a vacancy that was never there.*

[PAUSE]

This is the deepest movement of the ayah. The challenge exposes. The rebuke deters. And then the divine names *fill the space* with the truth that was always present.

You do not just leave shirk by being told it is wrong. You leave shirk by being shown that Allah is enough — so completely, so totally, so without remainder — that the very idea of a partner becomes laughable.

The practical move from this theme is simple but heavy. Every time you feel the impulse to attach something — a person, a system, an outcome — pause. And before you attach, ask: *am I attaching this because there is a real vacancy in Allah's care for me? Or am I attaching it because I have failed to know al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm well enough to trust that there is no vacancy at all?*

The answer will almost always be the second. And in that recognition, the attachment loses its grip.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look back at the ayah with what we have learned, and see how the architecture works.

> **قُلْ أَرُونِىَ** — *Say: Show Me*
> ↓
> ٱلَّذِينَ أَلْحَقْتُم بِهِۦ شُرَكَآءَ — *those whom you attached to Him as partners*
> ↓
> **كَلَّا** — *By no means!*
> ↓
> بَلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ — *Rather, He is Allah, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise*

The ayah moves in four beats:

1. **The challenge** — produce what you claim.
2. **The naming** — what you claim is not discovery but attachment, a thing you yourselves appended.
3. **The slam** — the door closes on the entire category.
4. **The replacement** — into the empty space, the truth that was always there.

Each beat does work the others cannot do. The challenge alone would be cruel without the naming — they would not understand what they are being asked to produce. The naming alone would be intellectual without the slam — they could keep entertaining the category. The slam alone would be a void — leaving them with nothing. The replacement alone would not have been heard — without the prior emptying, the divine names would not have been able to land.

This is how the ayah works *on a human heart.* It does not merely tell you not to commit shirk. It walks you through the experience of having your shirk exposed, named, demolished, and then replaced — in the space of twelve words.

### Bringing the Strands Together

The linguistic and the thematic are one insight from two angles.

The linguistic insight is that *alḥaqtum* is the human act of appending what was never there. The thematic insight is that the audit of belief exposes that what was appended cannot be produced. Same insight: shirk is the manufacture of a partnership that exists nowhere except in the manufacturer's mind.

The linguistic insight is that *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* leaves no vacancy for a partner. The thematic insight is that every appended partner is filling a vacancy we imagined but that never existed. Same insight: Allah is enough, and the partner is the symptom of our failure to know that.

### Questions to Carry

1. What have I, perhaps without naming it, *appended* to Allah in my decision-making — a job, a person, an outcome, a system — that I treat as a co-source of my security?

2. If Allah said to me right now *"arūnī — show Me this partner you have attached"* — could I produce it as anything other than something I made up?

3. Of the two divine names that close this ayah — *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Ḥakīm* — which one corrects the specific fear that drives my attachment? Is it that I doubt His power, or that I doubt His wisdom?

4. The *kallā* of this ayah is a door slammed. What thought-pattern in my own life needs a *kallā* — not a polite negotiation, but a deterrent that does not let the bad idea finish forming?

5. The verb *alḥaqtum* is in the perfect tense — *you have attached.* What is attached can be detached. What attachment will I begin to remove this week?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah challenges the polytheist to produce a partner who exists only in the act of being attached — and into the silence of that impossibility, places the truth that He is, all by Himself, complete in power and complete in wisdom.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to feel guilty. It is asking you to be honest. The same mind that quietly appended a partner is the mind that can quietly detach one. The same hand that pinned a footnote to a whole document can take it off.

Tonight, before you sleep, sit for one minute. Name one thing you have attached. Hold it in front of yourself the way Allah is asking the mushrik to hold their idol up. Look at it directly. Ask whether it would survive being shown. And when it dissolves — as it will — let the words *huwa Allāh al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* fill the space where it stood.

This is not loss. This is the return of vision. This is what tawḥīd feels like in the body — not a doctrine memorized but a clutter cleared.

### Du'a

اللَّهُمَّ يَا عَزِيزُ يَا حَكِيمُ — O Allah, the All-Mighty, the All-Wise — show us the partners we have quietly attached to You in our hearts without ever naming them, and give us the courage to detach what we have appended. Let our trust have no co-trust beside it, and let our hope have no co-hope, and let no thing in Your creation ever stand where only You should stand. **Kallā** — let it never be — that we live and die having attached to You what You never asked us to. Āmīn.
