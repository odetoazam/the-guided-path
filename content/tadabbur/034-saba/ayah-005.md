# Running From the One Whom Nothing Escapes

## Surah Saba 34:5

> وَٱلَّذِينَ سَعَوْ فِىٓ ءَايَٰتِنَا مُعَٰجِزِينَ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ أَلِيمٌ

> *Wa'lladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīna ulā'ika lahum ʿadhābun min rijzin alīm.*

> "And those who strove against Our verses, seeking to outstrip [Us] — for those, there is a punishment of vile defilement, painful."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The world this ayah enters

To feel this ayah, we have to put ourselves back inside late Meccan air.

The Prophet ﷺ is not yet in Madinah. He is a man in a city that mostly hates what he is saying. Every day he stands and recites verses about a coming Hour, a coming reckoning, a coming day when the secret things in the chest will be turned out into the open. And every day a particular kind of opposition meets him.

It is not the opposition of a person who simply disagrees. It is not even the opposition of someone who walks away. It is something more *active* than that.

Some of them go out to the trade caravans before the caravans reach Mecca and warn them: *don't listen to him, his words are sorcery.* Some of them stand near him while he recites and raise their voices, clap, whistle, make noise — anything to drown the sound out. Some commission poetry against the Quran. Some buy stories from Persian merchants to compete with the stories he tells. Some sit in the gatherings around the Kaʿba and laugh on cue whenever he passes.

They are not passive disbelievers. They are *working*. They are pouring effort into the dismantling of revelation. They are running.

[PAUSE]

It is this very specific kind of person Allah is naming in this ayah. Not "those who disbelieved." Not "those who turned away." A more precise category: *those who ran against Our verses, trying to render [Us] incapable*.

And the surah has already, two ayahs earlier, sworn an oath about the Knower of the unseen — that not the weight of an atom escapes Him in the heavens or the earth. So when this ayah describes people *trying to outstrip Him*, the picture is already painted. A creature is sprinting in front of the One from whom nothing has ever, in any heaven or any earth, escaped.

### The hook

Look at the ayah just before this one. 34:4 says: *that He may reward those who believed and did righteous deeds — for them is forgiveness and noble provision.*

That is the positive half. Now you would expect the negative half to read, simply, "and those who disbelieved — for them is punishment."

That is not what Allah says.

He says: *and those who **ran** against Our verses, **trying to render [Us] incapable** — for them is a punishment of **rijz**, **painful**.*

Why the running verb? Why the strange Form III construction *muʿājizīn*? And why the doubled descriptor for the punishment — not just ʿadhāb, but ʿadhāb *min rijz alīm*?

Three small linguistic choices. Each one is doing structural work. Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: سَعَوْ — "they ran"

Before we open this word, let me ask you something about your own life.

Think of the last time you really *exerted* yourself toward something. Not a thing you did because it was on a list. A thing you ran toward. Maybe a relationship you were trying to build. Maybe a deal you were trying to close. Maybe a body you were trying to get into the gym every morning at five. There is a particular Arabic word for that energy — not "doing," not "working," but *running*. Sustained, urgent, body-leaned-forward motion.

That word is **سَعْي** — saʿy.

You meet it elsewhere in the Quran in completely beautiful places. When the call for the Friday prayer is given, Allah says: *fasʿaw ilā dhikri'llāh* — *run to the remembrance of Allah* (62:9). When Hajar searches for water for her infant in the desert, what she does between Safa and Marwa is called *saʿy* to this day — the ritual of the running woman. When the boy in Surah al-Ṣāffāt grew old enough to walk beside his father, the Quran says *fa lammā balagha maʿahu al-saʿy* — *when he reached the age of running with him*.

Saʿy is a noble word. Saʿy is the body in motion toward what it loves.

[PAUSE]

Now look what Allah does with it here.

> وَٱلَّذِينَ **سَعَوْ** فِىٓ ءَايَٰتِنَا

*Those who **ran** in Our signs.*

The same beautiful verb. The same body-leaned-forward motion. But this time the destination is the *dismantling* of revelation. They are not lazy disbelievers. They are not casual disbelievers. They are *running*.

Notice something. Allah does not say *kafarū* (they disbelieved) here. He does not say *aʿraḍū* (they turned away). He uses the verb of athletic, deliberate, sustained motion. Because the people He is describing are not failing to believe — they are *succeeding* at something. They are succeeding at sabotage. Their lives are organized around it. Their mornings begin with it. Their evenings end with it.

Stay with me. This is the first thing the ayah is doing: it is dignifying their effort — and then revealing the horror of what they have spent it on.

Every human being has a finite amount of saʿy in them. A finite number of mornings. A finite quantity of leaned-forward energy. The question the ayah is asking is not *will you run* — you will. The question is *toward what*.

Hajar ran for water. The believer runs to Friday prayer. These people ran *fī āyātinā* — *into / against / inside Our signs* — to break them.

The same verb. Opposite destinations.

This is what we mean by linguistic honesty: Allah is not denying the seriousness of their work. He is naming it. He is calling it saʿy. And then He is letting the picture of saʿy poured *against* revelation stand by itself — without the verb itself condemning them. The condemnation is in the *fī āyātinā*. The condemnation is in the direction.

Here is what this single word reveals: **the moral weight of a life is not the magnitude of its effort. It is the orientation of its running.** A person who exerts immense effort against the truth is not less guilty for having tried hard. They are more pitiable for it. They have spent the one currency a human gets — their breath, their morning, their attention — and they have spent it building something that will be torn down on a day that is already named.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: مُعَاجِزِينَ — Form III, and the audacity it preserves

Now we come to the strangest word in the ayah.

*Muʿājizīn.*

Most translations render it as "to cause failure" or "thinking they can escape." That is correct. But the grammar of the word is so specific that it is worth slowing down and learning a small piece of Arabic morphology — because once you see it, you will never read this word the same way.

In Arabic, verbs are not just words. They are *patterns*. Every Arabic verb is built on a three-letter root, and then poured into a mold — like sand poured into a cast — that gives it its specific shape and meaning. The mold is called a *Form*. There are roughly ten of them. Each Form does a particular kind of work.

Form I is the basic action. *Kataba* — he wrote.

Form II intensifies it or causes it. *Kassara* — he smashed (not just broke).

And Form III — the one in this ayah — is the one we need.

Form III, *mufāʿala* (مُفَاعَلَة), almost always means doing an action *to* or *with* or *against* another party. It is the Form of *mutuality* or *attempted action upon someone else*. *Qātala* — he fought *against* him. *Sābaqa* — he raced *against* him. *Khāsama* — he disputed *with* him.

You cannot make a Form III verb without there being another party in the picture, even if that party is not named.

Now the root.

The root of *muʿājizīn* is **ع ج ز** — ʿ-j-z. The basic meaning of this root is *incapacity, weakness, inability*. *ʿAjz* is when an old person cannot stand up. *ʿAjaztu* — *I was unable*. *Muʿjiza* — a miracle, called that because it leaves the would-be challenger *incapable*.

So if you pour the root ع ج ز into the mold of Form III — *mufāʿil* — you get the active participle مُعَاجِز, which literally means:

> *One who is attempting to render the other party incapable. One who is trying to outstrip the other party. One who is trying to leave the other party behind, unable to catch up.*

[PAUSE]

Read the ayah again with that grammar in your hand.

> وَٱلَّذِينَ سَعَوْ فِىٓ ءَايَٰتِنَا **مُعَاجِزِينَ**

*And those who ran against Our verses, **trying to render [the other party] incapable**.*

Who is the other party?

The grammar leaves the object unspoken — and that absence is itself doing work. Form III demands another party. The party is not named because *naming Him would shatter the audacity*. The construction preserves the obscenity of what they imagined: they were running against Allah, trying to outstrip Him, trying to leave Him behind unable to catch them up.

Al-Jalalayn and al-Qurtubi both gloss this exactly: *yaḥsabūna annahum yafūtūnanā* — *they imagine that they will outrun Us, escape Us, slip past Us*. Al-Baghawi gives the same reading. Al-Muyassar adds the angle of *mughālibīn* — trying to defeat Allah, trying to outdo Him.

This is not a small claim about a few Meccan opposers. This is a diagnostic of a permanent human posture.

Notice what they imagined. They did not imagine they could *defeat* Allah in a direct contest — even they were not that delusional. They imagined something subtler. They imagined they could *outrun* Him. They imagined the consequences would not catch up. They imagined that by the time the reckoning arrived they would already be safely past.

That is what saboteurs of revelation always think. *We'll get away with it. We will move fast enough that nothing will reach us. We will be smart enough, well-placed enough, well-defended enough, that the moral logic of the universe will not arrive at our door.*

The Quran calls this *muʿājaza*. The attempt to outrun God.

And remember — two ayahs earlier, Allah established: *not the weight of an atom escapes Him in the heavens or the earth* (34:3). He is not a force you outrun. There is no past Him to get to. There is no jurisdiction He does not cover.

So what does *muʿājaza* actually look like, when the One you are running from is omnipresent? It looks like a man running in a circle inside a room, convinced he is escaping the room.

[PAUSE]

What this Form III reveals: **the disbeliever's fantasy is not that there is no God. The disbeliever's fantasy is that there is a God who can be slipped past.** That He is a sheriff in one town and we can ride to the next. That He is a camera at one intersection and we can take a different street. That the consequences are slow enough, distractible enough, weak enough that a fast enough person can stay ahead of them.

Every sin you and I have ever committed contained a small version of this same delusion. *I will not get caught. The fallout will not reach me. By the time the bill arrives I'll be somewhere else.*

The ayah is naming that exact posture and giving it its true name: *muʿājaza* — the attempted *outstripping of the One whom no atom outstrips*.

### Element Three: عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ أَلِيمٌ — the doubled punishment

Now look at the back half of the ayah.

> أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ مِّن رِّجْزٍ أَلِيمٌ

*For those, a punishment, of rijz, painful.*

Here is something to notice. In Arabic, you almost never need to say *more than one thing* about a punishment. *ʿAdhāb* alone is enough. *ʿAdhāb ʿaẓīm* — great punishment. *ʿAdhāb shadīd* — severe punishment. Single descriptor, single noun, done.

But here Allah does something heavier. He says *ʿadhāb* — and then He does not modify it directly. He says *ʿadhāb **min rijzin** **alīm***. A punishment **of the genus of rijz, painful**.

He has stacked two descriptors. He has gone out of His way to weight this sentence. Why?

Let me teach you the word *rijz*.

In classical Arabic, *rijz* (رِجْز) is not just "punishment." It is a much heavier, much more specific word. Its core sense is *foulness, defilement, abomination, that which is filthy and that which causes filth*.

The Quran uses it in very specific places. In Surah al-Aʿrāf (7:134-135), the plague that was sent on Pharaoh's people — the locusts, the lice, the frogs, the blood — is called *rijz*. *"And when the rijz fell upon them, they said: O Mūsā, supplicate for us your Lord."* In Surah al-Mā'ida (5:90), intoxicants and gambling and idol-stones are called *rijz min ʿamali al-shayṭān* — *defilement from the work of Satan*. In Surah al-Mudaththir (74:5), when the Prophet ﷺ is first commanded to begin his mission, he is told: *wa al-rujza fa'hjur* — *and the abomination, forsake it.*

So *rijz* is not a generic punishment word. It is *contaminating* punishment. It is punishment that is itself *unclean*. Punishment that is of the same texture as the plagues. Punishment as defilement.

Qatāda, as al-Baghawi reports, glosses *rijz* here as *sū' al-ʿadhāb* — *the foul kind of punishment*. The worst kind. The kind that does not just hurt — the kind that *contaminates*.

And then Allah adds *alīm* — *painful*. (Both readings of *alīm* are preserved in the qirā'āt: it can grammatically attach to *rijz* or to *ʿadhāb*. Either way, the weight stacks.)

Listen to the sound of the word itself.

*Rijz.* رِجْز.

The *rā* with a kasra, then the *jīm* with a sukūn slamming into the *zā*. There is a guttural friction in it. It sounds like something heavy being dragged. It sounds like something foul.

Compare it to a soft word like *raḥma* — رَحْمَة — where the *ḥā* and *mā* open the mouth and let air flow. *Rijz* does the opposite. It closes the throat.

This is the sonic signature of the word. Even before you know what it means, your mouth knows. The ayah is using a *physically heavy* word to describe a *spiritually heavy* destiny.

[PAUSE]

Why does the punishment for these specific people get described in this doubled, weighted way?

Because their crime was doubled.

If a man simply disbelieves, the punishment is severe but it is the punishment of a *passive* sin. He did not believe; he did not act for or against.

But these people — these *muʿājizīn* — did not just disbelieve. They *ran*. They organized their lives around the dismantling of revelation. They poured saʿy into it. They wanted to render Allah Himself incapable. They wanted to make the signs *fail*.

For that doubled exertion against revelation, the response is a doubled descriptor of punishment. *ʿAdhāb* — punishment. *Min rijz* — of the genus of contaminating filth. *Alīm* — and painful with it.

It is as if Allah is saying: you wanted to *defile* My signs in the eyes of people. So the destiny that meets you will share the texture of what you tried to do. *Rijz* for those who tried to make *āyāt* into *rijz*.

[PAUSE]

### Bridging into the themes

So here is what these three small linguistic choices have, together, revealed.

They ran — but the running was the wrong direction. They tried to outstrip — but the One they ran from cannot be outstripped. And the punishment that meets them is described in a doubled, sonically heavy way, because the crime is *active sabotage*, not passive denial.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about a few late Meccan opposers — but about a posture that lives, in smaller and quieter forms, inside almost every human being.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The fantasy of outrunning consequences

Let me start somewhere ordinary.

Almost every person who has ever told a serious lie has had a single recurring thought. It is so common we don't even notice it anymore. The thought is: *I just need to get past the next conversation. Get past tonight. Get past this week. After that it will be fine.*

The lie is not built on the belief that there are no consequences. The lie is built on the belief that the consequences will be *slow enough* that we will be safely past them by the time they arrive.

Notice what kind of fantasy that is. It is not the fantasy of a world without justice. It is the fantasy of a world whose justice is *out of breath* — limping, late, distracted, behind us by a few steps.

This is *muʿājaza*. In its small form. In its everyday form.

The addict who tells himself: *one more time and then I'll quit. The damage hasn't caught up to me yet.* The cheating spouse who tells himself: *she still doesn't know. I'm still ahead.* The accountant who shaves a number and tells herself: *no one is looking that closely.* The man who hurts his children with his words and tells himself: *they're young, they'll forget, by the time they're adults this will all be water under the bridge.*

In each case, there is a presupposition. The presupposition is that consequence is a *runner* — a runner who can be left behind if you sprint fast enough.

The Quran is making a metaphysical claim against that whole picture.

[PAUSE]

The claim is this: there is no *behind* and *ahead* with Allah. There is no head start. There is no jurisdiction He has not entered yet. *Lā yaʿzubu ʿanhu mithqālu dharratin fī al-samāwāti wa lā fī al-arḍ* — not the weight of an atom in the heavens or the earth escapes Him.

The ayah we are sitting with names the people who did not believe this — *muʿājizīn*, trying to outstrip Him — and calls their punishment *rijz*. Defilement. Plague. The punishment that contaminates.

But notice: the same diagnosis applies to *us* in the moments we are living out the smaller versions of this fantasy. Every time you and I have told ourselves *the consequences won't catch up*, we have been doing muʿājaza in miniature. We have been, in a small and quiet way, betting that Allah can be outrun.

This is not a metaphor I'm imposing on the ayah. This is the diagnostic the ayah is offering. It is naming the *fantasy of outpacing the divine* and saying: that fantasy is what active disbelief is *built on*.

The believer's posture is the exact inverse. The believer does not run *from* the consequences; he turns toward them. He says: *I cannot outrun You, so let me run toward You.* The Friday prayer call — *fasʿaw ilā dhikri'llāh* — uses the same verb of saʿy. Same running. Opposite direction.

So here is the practical edge of this theme. In the small decisions of this week — the lie you are considering, the conversation you are avoiding, the apology you are postponing, the desire you are about to indulge — there will be a moment where a voice in your chest says *just get past this and you'll be fine*. That voice is muʿājaza. That voice is the small inheritance of the people in this ayah.

Naming it does not make it disappear. But naming it strips it of its disguise. It is not a voice of pragmatism. It is a voice that is asking you to believe in a slower God than the One who exists.

Practical application: this week, when that voice arrives — and it will — pause and put a name on it. *I am about to do muʿājaza.* Then make the smaller, harder choice that does not require Allah to be slow.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: A life that runs — toward what?

Notice something we touched on earlier and now need to sit inside.

Allah did not say *those who disbelieved*. He said *those who ran*.

This means the ayah is not just talking about the disbeliever. It is talking about the *energetic* disbeliever. The *organized* disbeliever. The one whose mornings are structured. The one with a strategy.

And here is what is important: that energy — that saʿy — is itself morally neutral. It is what God gave every human being. It is the body's capacity to lean forward. It is the will's capacity to *push*. Hajar had it. The Prophet ﷺ had it. The disbelievers in this ayah had it. You have it.

The ayah is not condemning the energy. It is condemning what these particular people poured it into.

This raises a question that I think we do not ask ourselves often enough. **Where is my saʿy going?**

Not: *am I a believer or a disbeliever.* Most of us are, in some technical sense, believers. The deeper question is: when I track my actual leaned-forward energy across a week — the things I get up early for, the things I check first when I wake up, the things I refresh, the things I rehearse in my head while showering — *what shape does that map make?*

Because saʿy is the truest evidence of what we love. Words are cheap. Schedules are not. Where the body leans forward, the heart already is.

[PAUSE]

Now apply the diagnostic of the ayah carefully. The people in this ayah are condemned not because they ran but because they ran *fī āyātinā* — *against Allah's signs*. The mirror image is the believer who runs *ilā dhikri'llāh* — toward Allah's remembrance.

But there is a third category. The one most of us actually live in. We are not actively dismantling revelation. We are also not actively running toward it. We are running toward *other things*. A promotion. A house. A platform. A relationship. A body. A vacation. A reputation. We have not *fought* the signs. We have just spent our saʿy somewhere else.

The ayah does not address this third category directly. Surah Saba 34:4 addresses the believers; 34:5 addresses the active opposers. The middle is silent — and the silence is loud. Because the middle category has used up its currency on something that did not deserve it, and it has not even gotten the dramatic dignity of being named.

The most painful audit we can do is not *am I a believer or a disbeliever*. It is *whose name is on the bottom of the receipt of my saʿy*.

Hajar ran in the desert and her running became a ritual that millions of pilgrims repeat in her footsteps every year. Three thousand years of feet are still tracing her saʿy. That is what running in the right direction looks like — it outlives you.

The Meccan opposers in this ayah ran every day. They poured everything into it. And the only place their saʿy survives is as the negative example in a single ayah of the Quran — a permanent monument to wasted leaned-forward energy.

The question for you and me is not whether we are running. The question is: in three thousand years, what trace will our saʿy have left?

[PAUSE]

Practical application: take one inventory this week. Not of your beliefs. Not of your prayers. Of your saʿy. Where did your body lean forward? Where did your mornings start? What did you refresh, rehearse, return to? Then ask the harder question: *would I be willing for this map to be the report of how I spent the only mornings I will ever have?*

If yes, run on. If no, the work is not to run less. It is to redirect.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architectural reveal

Now we can see the architecture.

Surah Saba opens with praise to Allah, the Lord of everything in the heavens and the earth. Two ayahs in, the disbelievers' core claim is voiced: *the Hour will not come to us.* In the third ayah, Allah swears against that claim — and the *ground* of the oath is the divine attribute of total knowledge: *not the weight of an atom escapes Him in the heavens or the earth.*

Then the consequences fan out.

In 34:4: those who believed and did righteous deeds — forgiveness and noble provision.

In 34:5 — the ayah we have been sitting with — those who ran against the signs trying to outstrip Him — *ʿadhāb min rijz alīm*.

Look at the mirror.

In 34:3: *lā yaʿzubu* — *nothing escapes Him*.

In 34:5: *muʿājizīn* — *trying to make Him incapable, trying to escape Him*.

These are not two separate ayahs. They are one argument. The third ayah states the metaphysical truth — nothing escapes Him. The fifth ayah names the people who lived as if the opposite were true. The architecture is: *here is the reality. Here are the people who bet against it.*

And there is one more architectural beauty. Within Surah Saba itself, the exact formula *alladhīna saʿaw fī āyātinā muʿājizīn* appears twice — here in 34:5 and again in 34:38. The first time it is in the *perfect tense* (*saʿaw* — they ran, completed action). The second time it is in the *imperfect tense* (*yasʿawn* — they keep on running). The formula bookends the surah's central section on accountability. The first time, the act is described as complete. The second time, the act is described as ongoing. As if the surah is showing the same posture from two angles — first as a finished history, then as a continuing present-tense reality in every era.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the linguistic side: Allah uses the noble verb of *saʿy*, the audacious Form III of *muʿājaza*, and the contaminating noun *rijz*, in order to name a very specific kind of person — the one whose effort is real, whose strategy is to outrun God, and whose destiny is to be defiled by exactly what they tried to defile.

From the thematic side: the same diagnostic applies, in smaller form, to every human being. We all have saʿy. We all run somewhere. And every time we tell ourselves *the consequences won't catch up*, we are doing miniature muʿājaza — betting on a slower God than the One who is.

One claim, seen from two angles.

### Questions to carry

1. Where, in my actual week, is my saʿy going? If I made an honest map of my leaned-forward energy across the last seven days — what shape would it make, and whose name would be on the bottom of it?

2. In what areas of my life am I currently betting that the consequences won't catch up? What specific situation am I telling myself *I just need to get past this and I'll be fine*?

3. If saʿy is the truest evidence of what I love, and *rijz* is the consequence of running in the wrong direction — am I actually willing to redirect *one* thing this week, knowing the redirection will cost me?

4. The believer in Hajar's footsteps ran *for water for her child*. The opposer in this ayah ran *to dismantle a sign*. Both are described with the same verb. What in my life is the saʿy I would want my children to trace three thousand years from now?

5. Allah did not name the object of *muʿājaza* in this ayah — because naming Him would shatter the audacity. When I commit a sin in private and tell myself *no one is watching*, am I in fact doing the same thing the ayah leaves unsaid — running, in front of the One whom no atom outstrips?

### One-sentence distillation

**The people in this ayah are not condemned for failing to run; they are condemned for running with full force in the one direction that cannot be outrun.**

### The closing invitation

Do not leave this ayah resolving to *believe more*. That is not what it is asking of you.

It is asking you to notice, this week, the moments where you are tempted to bet on a slower God than the One who exists. The lie you were going to tell. The apology you were going to delay. The small dishonesty you were going to file safely past. In each of those moments, a tiny version of *muʿājizīn* lives in the chest. Name it. Then make the harder choice that does not require Him to be slow.

And then notice your saʿy itself. Notice the leaned-forward energy you have been given. It is precious. It is finite. It is the only currency a human being has. Allah did not create it so that we would withhold it — He created us as creatures that lean forward. The only question is *which way*.

Hajar leaned toward water for her child, and the universe still walks in her footsteps. The Meccan opposers leaned toward the unmaking of revelation, and survived only as a warning. Between those two destinies — between the saʿy that outlives you and the saʿy that outlives you only as a cautionary tale — sits every morning of your life.

Run. But run in the direction that the One whom nothing escapes is also walking.

### Du'a

اللهم اجعل سعينا إليك لا عنك، واجعل عجزنا أمامك سلامًا لا فضيحة، واحفظنا من ظنّ أنّ شيئًا يفوتك.

*O Allah, make our running toward You, never away from You. Make our incapacity before You a peace, not an exposure. And protect us from ever imagining — even for a moment — that anything we do slips past You.*
