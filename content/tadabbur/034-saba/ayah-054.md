# The Barrier of Wanting: When Desire Arrives After the Door Has Closed

## Introduction

Listen to these words. Let them land slowly.

[ayah:34:54] وَحِيلَ بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ مَا يَشْتَهُونَ كَمَا فُعِلَ بِأَشْيَاعِهِم مِّن قَبْلُ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا۟ فِى شَكٍّ مُّرِيبٍۭ

*Wa ḥīla baynahum wa bayna mā yashtahūn, kamā fuʿila bi-ashyāʿihim min qabl. Innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb.*

*"And a barrier will be placed between them and what they desire — as was done with their kind before. Indeed, they were in disquieting doubt."*

[PAUSE]

This is the last ayah of Surah Saba. The surah ends here. And the surah has been building toward this verdict for fifty-three ayat.

Rebuild the world for a moment. The surah opens with a hymn — *all praise is for Allah, to whom belongs everything in the heavens and the earth.* It moves through Dawud, whose mountains sang with him. Through Sulayman, whose wind carried him across kingdoms. Through the people of Saba — that lush civilization of two gardens, right and left, where you could walk for days under a single canopy of fruit. And then the surah tells you what happened to Saba. *They turned away.* The dam burst. The gardens became thorns. The civilization scattered into proverb: *"They became stories spoken about, scattered into total dispersion."*

From there the surah turns to a different audience. To those who heard the warning and shrugged. Those who said: *we have more wealth and more children — what will Allah do to us?* Those who delayed. Those who waited. Those who said: *let's see, let's think, we have time.*

And then this ayah closes the surah. It is the camera pulling back at the end of the film and showing you what happens to the people who waited too long. It is not a threat shouted at them. It is something quieter and far more terrifying.

**It is the sound of a door closing between a human being and the very thing they finally want.**

[PAUSE]

The hook is this: in this ayah, Allah does not tell us *what* they wanted. He leaves the object of their desire unspecified. He says only that something they desired was placed beyond a barrier. And the classical scholars have spent centuries asking — *what was it?* What did they want, in that final moment, that Allah refused to give them?

Stay with that question. Because the answer is going to require us to understand something about the heart that most of us have never been forced to confront.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: وَحِيلَ — The Passive Barrier

Before we open this word, let's understand what we're looking at.

In Arabic, there is a verb form called the **passive voice** — what grammarians call *al-fiʿl al-majhūl*, literally "the verb whose doer is unknown." In English we say "the window was broken." We don't say *who* broke it. The doer is hidden. The action stands alone.

In Arabic, the passive is even more striking, because Arabic normally loves to name the doer. Arabic is a language of agency — *who did it, to whom, with what, from where.* Every sentence wants a subject. So when Arabic chooses the passive, when it deliberately removes the actor, it is doing something significant. It is saying: *the action is what matters. The doer is either obvious, or being deliberately withheld for effect.*

Now look at the first word of this ayah.

**وَحِيلَ** — *wa ḥīla.* "And a barrier was placed."

This is the root **Ḥ-W-L** — to come between, to intervene, to separate. From this same root comes *ḥāʾil* — a wall, an obstacle, something that stands between two things. From this root comes *taḥawwul* — transformation, the thing that comes between what you were and what you became. From this root comes *ḥawl* — the year, because a year is what passes between one moment and another.

The root itself is about **interposition**. Something getting in the middle.

And here, the verb is in the passive. *Ḥīla.* "It was placed between." No doer named.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this. Why would Allah not say *"We placed a barrier between them"*? He has said exactly that elsewhere. In Surah al-Anfal, ayah 24, He says directly: *"And know that Allah comes between a person and his heart."* There, the doer is named. The action is owned. Allah claims it.

But here, at the end of Surah Saba, the doer is withheld. *A barrier was placed.* By whom? It hangs in the air.

Notice something. When Allah hides the doer in Arabic, He is often doing one of two things. Sometimes He is being merciful — softening the impact by not pointing at the agent. And sometimes He is doing the opposite. He is saying: *the barrier is so absolute, so final, so woven into the fabric of reality at this moment, that naming a doer would almost diminish it.* It is not a barrier that someone constructed. **It is a barrier that simply IS.**

Imagine you are running toward a door, and as you arrive the door is gone — not slammed shut, not locked, not guarded. *Gone.* The wall is solid. There was a door yesterday and there is no door today. Who removed it? The question almost doesn't apply. The reality of its absence is so total that asking *who* feels small.

That is what *ḥīla* does here. It removes the door without naming the hand.

And there is a second thing the passive does. It removes the possibility of negotiation. If the doer is named, you can plead with the doer. You can call out: *please, I'm sorry, let me through.* But when the action is done and the agent is hidden, **there is no one to address.** The barrier is just there. You cannot argue with it.

[PAUSE]

Now hear what the classical commentators put inside this barrier. Al-Qurtubi preserves multiple positions here, and they don't compete — they accumulate. **Some said:** a barrier between them and *escape from the punishment.* **Some said:** a barrier between them and the *wealth and family they loved in the world* — everything is suddenly inaccessible. **Qatada said:** a barrier between them and the *belief they now finally want to declare* — they see the punishment, they want to say *I believe,* and the belief is no longer available to them. Al-Baghawi adds the same layers — *faith, repentance, return to the world.* Al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar both center it on **faith itself** being refused.

Now — these are not four different theories competing to be right. They are four faces of one reality. **Everything they want is gone.** Escape gone. Wealth gone. Family gone. Faith gone. Repentance gone. The ability to go back gone. The barrier is not narrow. It is **total**.

That is what *ḥīla* — passive, agent-hidden, root meaning interposition — does in one word. It builds a wall the height of every possible desire.

### Element Two: مَا يَشْتَهُونَ — The Unspecified Object of Desire

Now look at what comes after the barrier.

**بَيْنَهُمْ وَبَيْنَ مَا يَشْتَهُونَ** — "between them, and between *what they desire.*"

Notice the doubled *bayn.* *Bayna-hum wa bayna mā.* In Arabic, when you say "between A and B," you can say it with one *bayna,* or you can repeat it. Repeating it puts a wall between each side and the middle. It makes you feel the gap. *Between them — and between what they desire.* The grammar itself is a barrier. The word *bayn* — meaning "between" — appears twice, like two pillars holding the wall up.

Now look at the verb of desire. **يَشْتَهُونَ** — *yashtahūn.* "They desire."

The root is **Sh-H-W**. From this root comes *shahwa* — appetite, craving, that pull toward something the soul wants. It is not neutral wanting. It is *hungering.* The verb is in **Form VIII** — *ishtahā* — which in Arabic adds reflexivity and intensity. *Shahā* would be "to desire." *Ishtahā* is something more inward, something the self does to itself. *To crave within oneself. To pull desire up from inside.*

And the tense is **imperfect** — *yashtahūn,* ongoing, present, continuous. *They are craving. They are wanting. They are reaching.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to notice. **Allah does not tell us what they are craving.** The object of the verb is *mā* — "what." The most generic, most empty word in the language. *Whatever they desire.*

This is what the methodology calls **reading absence as carefully as presence.** Arabic could have specified. Arabic loves to specify. The ayah could have said *they wanted faith,* or *they wanted escape,* or *they wanted to return.* Instead it says *what they desire.* It leaves the object empty.

Why?

Because the absence is doing theological work. *Whatever it is they crave in that moment, it is on the other side of the wall.* The barrier is not against a specific thing. The barrier is against **wanting itself reaching its object.**

This is why the classical scholars could not agree on what was behind the wall — *because Allah deliberately did not say.* The unspecified *mā* is what holds all the scholarly positions at once. It is faith. It is escape. It is wealth. It is family. It is the world. It is repentance. It is every reach of every craving. *Whatever* — *mā yashtahūn.*

[PAUSE]

Now stay with this. The verb is in the **present continuous**. *They are craving.* Not "they craved." Not "they wanted." *They are — right now, in that moment — reaching.* The wanting is alive. The hunger is active. The hand is extended.

**And the wall is there.**

This is what makes this image so devastating. It is not the image of someone who has stopped wanting. It is the image of someone who is *still wanting, more than they have ever wanted anything in their lives,* and who now sees that wanting has nothing to do with getting.

Most of us have lived our whole lives inside an unspoken assumption: *if I want something badly enough, eventually I'll get it. The wanting is the engine. The wanting will pull the thing to me.* This ayah is the demolition of that assumption. The wanting is intact. The wanting is intensified. **The wanting has been completely uncoupled from its object.**

Imagine reaching for a glass of water in a dream. Your hand goes through it. You reach again — through it again. The thirst is real. The reaching is real. The water is right there. But your hand will never close around it. Forever.

That is the picture this ayah is painting. And the verb is in the imperfect because the picture never ends.

### Element Three: بِأَشْيَاعِهِم — The Word "Kind"

There is one more word we need to open.

**كَمَا فُعِلَ بِأَشْيَاعِهِم مِّن قَبْلُ** — "as was done with their *ashyāʿ* before."

Look at the verb again. **فُعِلَ** — *fuʿila.* "It was done." Passive again. Another verb with the doer hidden. The same rhetorical choice the ayah opened with.

But the word I want to focus on is **أَشْيَاع** — *ashyāʿ.* The translation usually renders it as "kind," or "their like," or "their counterparts." But the word is richer than that.

The root is **Sh-Y-ʿ**. From this root comes *shīʿa* — a faction, a party, a group that follows the same leader or the same path. From the same root comes *shāʾiʿ* — widespread, common, the way a rumor "spreads." From the same root comes *tashayyuʿ* — to side with, to take a party. The root carries the idea of a group bound together by **shared direction.**

So when Allah says *ashyāʿihim* — "their *ashyāʿ"* — He is not just saying "people like them" in a generic way. He is saying *the ones who walked the same road. The faction of similar choices. The party of postponers.*

[PAUSE]

Here is the load-bearing analogy. Think of how a riverbed forms. Water doesn't flow randomly. It flows where water has flowed before. The first stream cuts a small channel. The second stream deepens it. By the thousandth stream, the channel is a canyon. New water arriving at the high ground doesn't have to think about where to go. **It joins the bed already cut.**

The people Surah Saba is addressing — the ones who delayed, who shrugged, who said *what will Allah do to us, we have wealth* — they thought they were making their own choices. They thought their postponement was personal, individual, theirs alone. The ayah says: *no. You are walking in a riverbed.* There is an *ashyāʿ* before you. The river of postponement is ancient. Every people who delayed went through this same door, hit this same wall, reached for this same unreachable thing.

This is what *ashyāʿ* does. It removes the illusion of originality from disbelief. You are not the first to delay. You are not the first to think you had time. You are not the first to assume the door would still be there when you finally turned around. *Ashyāʿukum* — your faction — walked this path. And what was done to them, the ayah says, is what will be done to you. *Kamā fuʿila* — *as it was done.*

The passive here matters too. *As was done.* Not "as We did." The doer hidden, again. Because the point is not who did it. The point is that **the pattern is the pattern.** The riverbed is the riverbed.

And notice something else. The phrase is **مِّن قَبْلُ** — *min qabl* — "from before." Just "before." Not "from a specific previous nation." Not "from the people of Saba" or "from the people of Nuh." Just *before.* Open-ended. Universal. It applies to every previous reach of human postponement, all of them. *Ashyāʿ from before* is everyone who ever delayed faith and found, at the end, that delay was a one-way road.

---

### Bridge

We have been with three words.

*Ḥīla* — a passive verb with the doer hidden, building a wall whose maker cannot be petitioned.

*Mā yashtahūn* — an unspecified object of an intensified, ongoing craving, so that the wall sits in front of *whatever the soul reaches for.*

*Ashyāʿihim* — a word that places the individual postponer inside a vast, ancient faction of people who walked the same path to the same wall.

Three words. A door without a doorkeeper. A thirst without water. A path well-worn by every traveler who ever told themselves they had time.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Cruelest Architecture — Desire That Outlives Its Object

Let me start somewhere familiar.

Have you ever loved someone who died? Not loved them in a soft, abstract way — loved them in a way that built every day around them. Their voice in the morning. Their laugh in the kitchen. The small accumulated rituals of two lives stitched together.

And then they are gone.

Here is what most people do not warn you about. *The love does not die when they die.* The love keeps reaching. Your hand still moves toward where their hand should be. You still turn to tell them something at dinner. Your body has not been told. Your body has been built around the assumption of their presence, and the building does not collapse just because the foundation has been removed. The love is intact. **The object is gone.**

This is grief. And it is one of the most painful experiences a human being can have, because the wanting and the having have been severed.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that experience in your mind and look at the ayah again.

*Wa ḥīla baynahum wa bayna mā yashtahūn.* "A barrier was placed between them and what they desire."

The horror of this ayah is not that Allah punishes them. The horror is that Allah preserves their wanting **and removes their access to it.** They are not numbed. They are not made to stop caring. The craving — *yashtahūn,* present continuous, ongoing, intensified Form VIII — is left fully alive. And the wall is dropped in front of it.

This is grief, but worse — because in human grief, the object is gone. In this ayah, the object is *right there.* On the other side of the wall. They can see it. They reach for it. **And they cannot reach it.**

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because this is going to open something.

Think about the way modern life has trained us. We live in an age of access. Almost everything we want, we can have, immediately. Hungry — food in twenty minutes. Lonely — a screen in our pocket with a thousand voices. Curious — every answer one question away. Bored — infinite content. We have been trained, every single day, to believe that **the gap between wanting and having is small, closeable, and our right.**

The ayah is showing us a state in which that gap becomes infinite.

And here is what is most terrible. The people in this ayah did not arrive at this state because Allah was cruel. They arrived because *they spent their entire lives treating the door as if it would always be open.* They lived inside the assumption that wanting was enough. That if they wanted faith later, they could have faith later. That if they wanted to return to Allah at the end, they could return at the end. That the relationship between desire and access — which their whole worldly life had taught them was reliable — would hold forever.

The ayah is the moment they discover it does not hold.

[PAUSE]

Now hear what Allah does NOT say in this ayah. He does not say *we punished them with fire.* He does not describe the physical torment. The closing ayah of Surah Saba — *the entire surah's verdict* — is not about flames. It is about **a wall in front of a wanting.**

That is His chosen image for what the loss of Him will feel like.

Not pain. Not heat. The eternal experience of reaching, and not arriving. Of every desire intact, and every object inaccessible. Forever.

And here is what makes this universal. Every one of us reaches. All day, every day. We reach for food, for rest, for love, for meaning, for status, for ease, for understanding. The reaching is what we do. The reaching is who we are. The ayah is asking: *what if, one day, the reaching continues and the object never comes? Not for a day. Not for a year. Forever.*

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes this. **The reaching itself is not the goal.** The reaching is a vehicle, and the vehicle is meant to deliver you somewhere. If you spend your whole life reaching for things that will not be there when you arrive — wealth that will be on the other side of the wall, status that will not survive the closing of the door, validation from people who will be unable to validate you on the day it counts — then you have spent your reaching on objects that the wall will swallow.

The only safe place to put your reaching, the ayah is whispering, is *toward the only One who never becomes inaccessible.* Allah does not put a wall between Himself and the one who reaches for Him in this life. **He only does that to the one who waited until the wall was already built.**

So reach now. While the door is open. Reach now, before the architecture of access changes. Reach now, while the wanting and the having are still on the same side of the wall.

### Theme Two: The Doubt That Makes the Wall

The ayah does not end with the wall. It tells us *why.*

**إِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا فِي شَكٍّ مُّرِيبٍ** — *Innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb.* "Indeed, they were in disquieting doubt."

Begin where you live. Doubt, in our world, has been made into a virtue. *Question everything. Trust no one. The rational mind doubts.* We have built an entire intellectual culture around the idea that doubt is sophistication and conviction is naïveté. The thoughtful person doubts. The unsophisticated person believes.

The ayah is going to challenge that. Carefully. Because Islam does not condemn questioning — the Quran is full of *think, reflect, ponder, look.* What this ayah condemns is something specific. **A specific kind of doubt.** And we have to look at the word to see which kind.

[PAUSE]

The word is **شَكٍّ** — *shakk.* The root **Sh-K-K** means to be pierced through, to be uncertain, to have something stuck through you that you cannot resolve. *Shakk* is doubt — but doubt as a wound, doubt as something lodged inside.

But that word alone is not yet the indictment. Doubt happens. The thoughtful believer has doubted. Ibrahim asked Allah to show him how He revives the dead — *"so that my heart may be at rest."* Doubt that **moves toward resolution** is not condemned. Doubt that knocks on the door is the beginning of faith.

The indictment is the next word. **مُّرِيبٍ** — *murīb.* From the root **R-Y-B**, which is a sister-root to *shakk* but darker. *Rayb* is not just doubt — it is doubt that produces unease, anxiety, restlessness. It is doubt that destabilizes. *Murīb* is the **Form IV** active participle — it means "that which causes unease in others, that which produces restlessness."

Look at the construction. *Fī shakkin murīb.* "In a doubt that produces unease." The doubt itself is described as something that *creates* its own anxiety. It is doubt that **never resolves** — doubt that, instead of pushing the doubter toward inquiry and resolution, only generates more disquiet.

This is the doubt the ayah condemns. Not the doubt that asks. The doubt that **stays.** The doubt that becomes a permanent state. The doubt that uses its own restlessness as an excuse to never settle anywhere.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the analogy.

There is a kind of person — and many of us have been this person at moments in our lives — who, when faced with a big decision, will not decide. They will research. They will research more. They will list pros and cons. They will get opinions. They will sleep on it. They will revisit it. They will tell themselves they are being careful. But the careful person eventually decides. **The person in *shakk murīb* never decides** — because the deciding would end the doubt, and the doubt has become comfortable. The doubt has become the answer.

Doubt as a destination. Doubt as a home. Doubt as the reason I do not have to commit to anything, ever.

This is what the ayah is naming. And it is naming it as the *cause* of the wall.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the verb. **كَانُوا** — *kānū.* "They *were.*" Past tense. Perfect. *Kāna* — the verb of being that, in Arabic, attaches to the entire stretch of their worldly life. They *were,* throughout, *in shakk murīb.*

This is a description of a life. Not a moment of doubt. Not a season of questioning. A whole life lived inside an unresolved restlessness. They never settled. They kept the door of faith deliberately ajar — never opening it, never closing it — so that they could always say *I haven't decided yet.*

And what the ayah reveals is this: **the wall in front of their desire was built, brick by brick, by the doubt they refused to resolve.**

You cannot live a life of *I'm not sure yet* and then, at the end, suddenly become sure. Because the muscle of decision has atrophied. Because the heart has been trained, for decades, that *not committing* is the safest option. The "I'll figure it out later" became the answer. And at the end — when finally the punishment is in view, when finally the truth is undeniable, when finally the *wanting* arrives — there is no faculty left to act on the wanting.

The wall is not *placed* on them, in some external sense. The wall is **the shape of their own un-resolved life made permanent.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about how I live tomorrow?

It changes this. **Doubt is a state to pass through, not a state to camp in.** If you have a question about Allah, about faith, about the Quran — pursue it. Read. Ask. Sit with people who have wrestled with the question. Read what the scholars have said. Pray for clarity. *Move.* The question is meant to drive you somewhere.

But do not treat your unresolved questions as a personality. Do not say *I am a doubter* as if doubt were an identity. Do not let *shakk* become a permanent room with comfortable furniture. Because the ayah is telling you what permanent doubt becomes: it becomes the architect of the wall.

Every day you live with a question unresolved, you are training your heart that questions don't have to be resolved. And one day, when it really matters, the heart will not know how to resolve.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture

Look at how this ayah is built. The whole surah ends here, and the structure of these fifteen words is precise.

It opens with a **wall** — *ḥīla.* A passive verb, doer hidden, action absolute.

It places the wall in front of a **wanting** — *mā yashtahūn.* Object unspecified, verb intensified, tense ongoing.

It anchors the moment in a **pattern** — *kamā fuʿila bi-ashyāʿihim min qabl.* This is not new. This is the riverbed. The faction. The path well-worn.

And it closes by telling you the **soil from which the wall grew** — *innahum kānū fī shakkin murīb.* A whole life lived inside an unresolved disquiet.

The architecture moves from *effect* to *pattern* to *cause.* It shows you the wall. It tells you the wall is ancient. And then it whispers the secret of who built the wall: *they did. With their own unresolved doubt. Brick by brick. Decade by decade. They built the wall the moment they decided not to decide.*

### Bringing the Pieces Together

The three linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **linguistic discoveries** — the passive verbs that hide the doer, the unspecified object of desire, the word *ashyāʿ* placing the individual inside a faction — all converge on one truth: *what is happening here is not something done to them. It is the natural shape of what they did to themselves, revealed at the moment when the shape becomes permanent.*

The **thematic depths** — desire that outlives its object, doubt that becomes a home — show you what this looks like from inside. From the inside, it feels like reaching for water that your hand passes through. From the inside, it feels like the comfortable doubt that finally has nowhere to go.

The wall is them. The wall is what *kānū fī shakkin murīb* hardens into when the moment of softness is over.

### Questions to Carry

1. *What am I reaching for today, that I cannot guarantee will be on the same side of the wall as me when the wall comes?*

2. *Is there a question about Allah, about my faith, about my relationship with the Quran, that I have been allowing to sit unresolved for years — not because the answer is unavailable, but because the unresolved state is comfortable?*

3. *When I imagine my own death — not the dramatic kind, but the ordinary, certain kind — what do I think I will be wanting in that moment? And am I building my life around getting it now, while the wanting and the access are still on the same side?*

4. *Where in my life have I treated "later" as if it were guaranteed?*

5. *If doubt has become my identity, who would I be if I let myself decide?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

The wall that stands between a soul and what it finally wants on the Last Day is not built by Allah in that moment — it is the hardened shape of a whole life lived inside doubt that was never allowed to resolve.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not asking you to be afraid. It is asking you to be **decided.** Not certain about every theological question — no human has that. But decided about the direction of your reaching. Decided that, whatever else you doubt, you are not going to camp inside the doubt about Him.

The door is open right now. The wanting and the having are on the same side of the wall right now. *Reach.* While the architecture of access still permits it.

Do not wait until the wall is built to discover what you wanted.

### Du'a

*Yā Allah, do not let me be among those whose doubt becomes a wall between them and what they want from You. Soften my reaching while my reaching can still arrive. Resolve in me what I have allowed to remain restless, before the day when restlessness becomes permanent. Place me on the side of the door where Your nearness is still reachable, and keep me reaching for You until the moment I meet You.*

*Āmīn.*
