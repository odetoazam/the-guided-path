# The Assumption That Required Our Help

## Surah Saba, Ayah 20

> **وَلَقَدْ صَدَّقَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِبْلِيسُ ظَنَّهُۥ فَٱتَّبَعُوهُ إِلَّا فَرِيقًا مِّنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ**

> *Wa laqad ṣaddaqa ʿalayhim Iblīsu ẓannahu fa-ttabaʿūhu illā farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn.*

> **"And certainly Iblis found his assumption about them true — so they followed him — except a group from the believers."**

---

## INTRODUCTION

### The World This Ayah Steps Into

Before this verse arrives, the surah has spent five ayahs telling you about a civilization. Saba. A real people, in what is now Yemen — a kingdom so prosperous that the classical commentaries describe it as a place where a woman could walk between two cities with a basket on her head, and by the time she arrived at the second city, the basket was full of fruit. She didn't pick anything. The fruit fell into her basket from the trees as she walked beneath them.

That is what abundance looked like.

Two gardens, the Quran says — one on the right, one on the left. *Kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lah*. Eat from the provision of your Lord and be grateful. A good land. A forgiving Lord.

And then — and this is the part you have to feel — they turned away. Not dramatically. Not in some spectacular rebellion. They just stopped being grateful. They got used to the fruit falling into the basket. They began to think it was the basket that mattered, not the One who filled it. And so Allah sent the flood of al-ʿArim — the breaking of their great dam — and scattered them across the land until they became, in the Quran's chilling phrase, *aḥādīth*. **Stories.** People you tell tales about. A cautionary phrase. A footnote in someone else's lecture.

That's where we are when ayah 20 arrives.

The camera has been on Saba. And then in the space of a single sentence, the camera pulls back. Way back. Until you realize this isn't only about Saba. It's about a much older pattern — a pattern that started before Saba existed, and that has been running, quietly, through every civilization that ever had a basket of fruit and forgot Who filled it.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to sit with before we open a single word.

Iblis — the one who swore he would mislead all of humanity — never once had certainty that he could. He never had power over you. He never had knowledge that you would fall. He only had a **guess**. A hunch. A suspicion.

He guessed you would follow him.

And you — and I — and the civilizations before us — proved his guess right.

[PAUSE]

What this ayah is going to do is the most uncomfortable thing the Quran ever does to us. It is going to take what we usually frame as **Iblis's victory** and reframe it as **our verification of his theory**. He didn't win. We confirmed his hypothesis.

The whole drama of human history, from this ayah's vantage point, is the story of a being who made an educated guess about us — and a species that spent ten thousand years gathering evidence that he was right.

Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word He Did Not Use — and the Word He Did

Before we open the word itself, let's teach ourselves a category of Arabic that English flattens.

In Arabic, there is a clean separation between three kinds of mental states.

There is **ʿilm** (عِلْم) — knowledge. You know something. It is confirmed. It is verified. You stand on it.

There is **yaqīn** (يَقِين) — certainty. Even firmer than knowledge — the kind of knowing where doubt cannot enter.

And then there is **ẓann** (ظَنّ) — and this is the word that lives in our ayah. **Ẓann** is something else entirely. It is *assumption*. It is *conjecture*. It is *suspicion*. It is the mental state of someone who thinks something might be true but cannot prove it. It is the state of a person who is guessing.

In English we collapse all of this into "thought" or "believed." In Arabic, choosing **ẓann** over **ʿilm** is a deliberate, surgical act. It tells you: *this person did not know. They suspected.*

Now look at what Allah says about Iblis.

> **وَلَقَدْ صَدَّقَ عَلَيْهِمْ إِبْلِيسُ ظَنَّهُۥ**

The word for what Iblis had about us is **ẓannahu** — *his ẓann*. **His assumption. His conjecture. His hunch.**

Allah does not say "Iblis's knowledge proved true." He does not say "Iblis verified his certainty." He says: *his ẓann*. The guess he made. The bet he placed without seeing the cards.

Stay with this. Because it changes everything we thought we knew about the cosmic drama.

When Iblis stood before Allah after refusing to bow to Adam, and he made his great boast — *By Your might, I will mislead them all* — he was not speaking from knowledge. He was making a prediction. A hypothesis about human nature. He was saying: *I think they'll follow me. I think they're weak in the places I can press. I think their loyalty to You is shallower than it looks. I bet I can prove it.*

That was his ẓann.

He had no power over us. He has no power over us. The Quran says this repeatedly in other places — that he has no *sulṭān*, no authority, no leverage. **All he had was a theory.** A theory he could not test until we cooperated.

Now feel the weight of this word.

[PAUSE]

The being who has caused, by his own boast, the misguidance of nations — was not operating from intelligence about us. He was operating from a guess. And his guess required something to become true. **It required us.**

A guess is not a destiny. A theory is not a verdict. Until someone tests it, a hypothesis hangs in the air, weightless, waiting.

What the word **ẓann** does in this ayah is tear the cloak off of Iblis. The Quran is saying: *Look at him clearly. He is not your superior. He is not your seer. He is a being who placed a bet about you. The bet just keeps winning.*

And the question this ayah forces us to ask — quietly, the kind of question you have to sit with for hours — is: **whose fault is it that the bet keeps winning?**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: He Verified It *Against* Them

Now look at the verb. **Ṣaddaqa**. *He verified.* *He found true.* *He made-true.*

This is from the root **ṣ-d-q** — the same root that gives us **ṣidq** (truthfulness), **ṣiddīq** (the deeply truthful one), and **ṣadaqah** (charity that confirms your sincerity).

But the verb here is in Form II — **ṣaddaqa**, with a doubled middle root letter. In Arabic, Form II is the form that intensifies, declares, or causes. When you take a word that means "true" and put it in Form II, you don't just get "to be true." You get something more active: **to find true, to declare true, to verify, to confirm**.

So **ṣaddaqa ẓannahu** — *he verified his assumption*. He took his guess — and **made it true**. Or: he saw it become true. Or: he found, when the test was run, that he had guessed correctly.

But notice what comes right after the verb. **ʿAlayhim** — *upon them.* *About them.* *Against them.*

This is the load-bearing preposition in the whole sentence. **ʿAlā**. *Upon.* In Arabic, when you say something proved true *ʿalā* someone, you don't just mean it was true *about* them. You mean it was proved *against* them. *To their detriment.* *Upon their account.*

Imagine a trial. A man stands accused. The prosecutor has a theory. The trial runs. The verdict comes back. **Ṣaddaqa ʿalayhim** — *the theory was verified against them.* The case closed. The hypothesis became the conviction.

That preposition is the courtroom in this ayah.

[PAUSE]

Now layer this. Iblis had a **ẓann** — a guess. He could not prove his guess. The proof had to come from somewhere. The proof had to come **from them**. From us. Our actions, our compliance, our slow drift toward what he said we would do — *that* was the evidence. *That* was what made his hypothesis become a verdict.

You and I, in our small daily betrayals, in the moments we knew better and did otherwise, in the times we shrugged and said *I'm only human* — we were the witnesses. The witnesses for Iblis's case. And the witnesses said: *yes, he was right about us.*

The verb **ṣaddaqa** here is not describing something Iblis did to humanity. It is describing something humanity did to Iblis's prediction. **We made his guess credible.** We promoted it from a theory to a fact.

The classical commentators noted two readings of this verb in the recitation tradition — one with the doubled middle letter (**ṣaddaqa**, what the Kufan readers preferred) and one without (**ṣadaqa**). Both are present in the qiraʾāt. Al-Baghawi explains them together: in one reading, Iblis "made his assumption true through what he did to them and they followed him"; in the other, "his assumption was found accurate about them." Two camera angles on the same scene. In one, Iblis brings the assumption to fulfillment by working on us; in the other, his assumption is simply found, when tested, to match what we already were. Either way the verdict is the same — and either way, **we are the ones who supplied the proof**.

Let's mark what this single word and its preposition have done. **Ṣaddaqa ʿalayhim** — *he verified [his guess] against them*. The verb tells you the guess became a verdict. The preposition tells you who bore the cost of that verdict.

It was not Iblis who paid for being right. It was them.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Exception That Names a Universe

We now have to look at the four words at the end. And I want to teach a small grammatical idea before we do.

In Arabic, when you make a general statement and then say **illā** — *except* — what comes after **illā** is called the *mustathnā*, "the excepted thing." The grammatical logic is this: you first establish a category, then you carve out a piece of it and say *this piece is different*. The structure assumes the category is the default and the exception is the rare.

This is built into how the sentence breathes. *Everyone died — except one survivor.* The default is death; the exception is the survivor.

Now look at our ayah.

> **فَٱتَّبَعُوهُ إِلَّا فَرِيقًا مِّنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ**
>
> *So they followed him — except a group from the believers.*

The fa- at the start (**fa-ttabaʿūhu**) means "consequently." So the structure is: Iblis's guess was verified → **so consequently** they followed him → **except** a group from the believers.

Notice the architecture. The default is following Iblis. The exception is the believers.

Sit with the discomfort of that for a moment.

In the way most of us frame the world, we assume the default is doing fine, behaving reasonably, walking the middle road — and the exception is the deviant who falls off into the ditch. The Quran inverts this. From the cosmic vantage point of this ayah, **the deviant — the abnormal one — is the believer who refuses to follow Iblis.** The mass is being followed. The faithful are the carved-out exception.

This is not insulting to humanity. It is liberating. Because it means: **if you ever feel like the strange one** for not going along with what everyone is doing — for not numbing yourself the way they numb themselves, for not chasing what they chase, for not letting Iblis's hypothesis pull you in — *you are exactly where this ayah locates the believer.* In the exception. Outside the gravity well. Carved out of the mass by an act of grammatical mercy.

Now look at the word for the exception: **farīq**. **A party. A group.**

The root is **f-r-q** — the same root that gives us **furqān** (criterion, that which separates), **firāq** (separation), **iftirāq** (parting). A **farīq** is, etymologically, *a group that has been separated off*. They are a faction. They are a piece that has been pulled away from the mass. They are not the mass.

So Allah does not just say *the believers were the exception*. He says **farīqan** — *a group that has been separated*. There is a *separation* baked into the word. Believers, in this ayah, are not described as the majority who happened to be sensible. They are described as the **separated** — the ones who, by an act of distinguishing, were pulled away from the trajectory of the mass.

And that word **farīqan** is grammatically indefinite. Not *the* group. *A* group. There is a smallness in it. A modesty. *A faction. A minority. A handful.*

If you have ever, in your own life, felt small — *what difference can my little practice make, what difference can my small prayer make, who am I in the face of this whole world drifting* — let this word be a balm. **Farīq.** *A small group, separated off, named in the divine record, exempted from the verdict that fell on everyone else.*

You don't need to be the majority. You only need to be a **farīq**.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

Three words. **Ẓann**. **Ṣaddaqa ʿalayhim**. **Farīq.**

The first told us Iblis had only a guess.

The second told us we made his guess into a conviction — against ourselves.

The third told us that the exception clause is real, that the carve-out is named, that being one of the carved-out is what the believer is.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Prophecy That Could Not Run Without You

There is a phenomenon in social psychology — well-documented, studied for decades — called the **self-fulfilling prophecy**. The idea is simple: when someone is told a certain thing is going to happen, and they organize their behavior around the expectation, the prophecy comes true *through* their behavior, even though, in the absence of the prediction, it might never have come true at all.

The original experiments studied teachers. Teachers were told certain students were "bloomers" — destined to perform well — when in fact those students had been chosen at random. By the end of the year, those students *had* performed well. Not because of any innate ability. Because of how they had been treated. The expectation became the engine. The prediction wrote the future.

Now hold that beside our ayah.

Iblis's **ẓann** is a self-fulfilling prophecy on the scale of human history.

He made a prediction about us. The prediction was: *they will follow me, they will be ungrateful, they will not be the kind of beings who hold a covenant.* He did not have proof of this. He had a hunch. He had a guess that, given how Allah made us, given what we are pulled toward, given the architecture of nafs — *given enough time and enough whispering, they will drift.*

And what makes the prophecy run is **not Iblis**. The prophecy runs **through us**. Through our compliance. Through our small daily concessions. Through the moments we shrug and say *that's just how I am*. Through the way we underestimate the seriousness of a small bad habit. Through the way we say *next year* instead of *today*. Through every individual collaboration with the theory that humans are weak.

He didn't make the prophecy come true. **We did.** He just had a hypothesis. We supplied the proof.

This is uncomfortable. It should be uncomfortable. Because the uncomfortable part is the agency it returns to us. If Iblis had a destiny over us, we could grieve and surrender. But this ayah does not give us a destiny. It gives us a *guess that we kept verifying*.

And here is what's quietly revolutionary about the ayah's grammar: every time we **break the script**, we are unverifying his guess. We are, at the scale of one human life, gathering evidence *against* his hypothesis. Every prayer that arrives on its time when no one is watching. Every dollar of charity given when you could have kept it. Every honest word in a context that rewards lies. Every refusal to follow a habit you've been told *defines* you. **Each of these is a small piece of falsification of Iblis's theory of you.**

He bet you wouldn't do it.

You did it.

That is what worship is, in part — the *embarrassment of Iblis's data set*. The pieces of your life that don't fit his model. The actions that, if anyone tried to predict them from your worst patterns, would not have been predicted.

You don't have to be perfect to be inside the exception. You just have to be unpredictable in the direction of God.

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?** It changes one thing: it makes every small good deed *cosmically significant*. Because every small good deed is, at the level of metaphysics, *the unverification of a satanic conjecture*. Pray on time tomorrow — not because of the reward in the next life only, but because tomorrow's prayer is a piece of evidence in the cosmic court that says: *his hypothesis was wrong about this human.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Architecture of Being Carved Out

Now we turn to the second half of the ayah. **Illā farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn.** *Except a group from the believers.*

There is a phenomenon you have probably felt in your life. You are in a room. Everyone is laughing at a joke that should not be funny. Or everyone is enthusiastic about a thing that should not be enthusiastic about. Or everyone is pretending something terrible is fine. And you sit there, feeling like the broken one — the one who didn't get the memo, the one who is too sensitive, the one who is taking it too seriously.

You feel like the deviant. The strange one.

Here is what this ayah does to that feeling. It says: **you are not the deviant. You are the farīq.** You are the one who, by some act of mercy, was separated from the trajectory of the mass. You are not failing to fit in. You are succeeding at being carved out.

The believer, in this ayah, is not the conformist. The believer is the exception clause.

Now there are two ways to live inside this exception, and the difference matters.

The first way is to feel **isolated** inside it. To feel that being the carved-out is a punishment. That you have been pulled away from the warmth of the mass, that you are alone, that you are looking at everyone else having fun while you eat ash. This is a real temptation. Iblis whispers it. *Look at them. They are free. You are constrained. They are happy. You are dutiful. They are the ones living.*

The second way is to feel **chosen** inside it. To realize that the mass is heading somewhere, and that being pulled out of the trajectory is a mercy. That **farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn** is not a punishment of separation but a gift of separation. That the same word — **farq** — that names what divides also names what *distinguishes*. The **furqān** (criterion) is given to the believer not as a wall but as a lens.

The classical commentators, when they reach this phrase, do not linger on triumphalism. They simply note: this is the group. This is the carved-out. Al-Muyassar puts it plainly — *they remained steadfast in obedience to Allah.* That is all. The carve-out is not theatrical. It is quiet. It is people who, in the moments that mattered, kept going. Who didn't outsource their behavior to the mass.

Here is the practical movement of this theme. When you next feel **strange** for being a believer — strange for praying when no one prays, strange for fasting in a culture that snacks all day, strange for not laughing at a joke that everyone else laughed at, strange for being uncomfortable with a thing that everyone has decided is fine — **do not interpret that strangeness as failure.** That strangeness is what *farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn* feels like from the inside.

The exception clause does not feel triumphant. It feels lonely sometimes. It feels small. It feels like you are the only one. *Farīq* is an indefinite, modest, small-sized word. It is meant to feel modest. **It is not meant to feel like you are part of a winning team.** It is meant to feel like you are part of a saved one.

And here is what's strange — and beautiful — about the construction *mina-l-muʾminīn*. The classical grammarians read it largely as *bayāniyya* — explanatory. Meaning: *a group, which is the believers*. The exception isn't *some of the believers*. The exception is *what the believers are*. To be a believer, in the grammar of this ayah, is to *be* the exception. The carve-out is not a subset of the believers. The carve-out **defines** them.

So if you want to know what it means to be a believer, this ayah has an answer that bypasses everything we usually say. A believer, in 34:20, is **the human whose behavior Iblis could not predict.**

**What does this change about how I live tomorrow?** It changes how you interpret the loneliness of practice. The loneliness is the texture of the exception. Don't run from it. Don't try to make your practice popular or palatable or universally cheerful. The exception is *small*. The exception is *quiet*. The exception, by definition, is *not the mass*. Live tomorrow without needing the mass to validate what you do. Their validation is structurally not coming. The validation is in being named in this ayah.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal

Now that we have done the work, let me show you something the ayah is doing that you could not have seen at the beginning.

This ayah is not a free-standing statement. It is the **closing of a case file**.

Earlier in the Quran — chronologically much earlier, in Surahs like al-Aʿrāf (7:16-17) and Ṣād (38:82-83) — Iblis is given the chance to speak. He makes his great boasts. *I will lie in wait for them on Your straight path. I will come at them from front and behind, from their right and from their left. You will not find most of them grateful. By Your might, I will mislead them all — except Your chosen servants.*

Those were his **ẓann statements**. His conjectures. The hypotheses he advanced when he was given the floor.

Now look at our ayah again. **Wa laqad ṣaddaqa ʿalayhim Iblīsu ẓannahu — fa-ttabaʿūhu illā farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn.**

This is the **report back**. This is the case officer's verdict. This is Allah closing the file on Iblis's claim and saying — *yes, the prediction he made? It was verified. They did follow him. Except a group from the believers.*

Notice — and this should be felt in the chest — that the exception clause in *our* ayah mirrors the exception clause Iblis himself spoke. In 38:83, Iblis said: *except Your chosen servants.* In 34:20, Allah confirms: *except a group from the believers.*

**The exception clause is held intact.** Iblis's prediction included a carve-out. Allah's report acknowledges the carve-out. Even in the verifying of his guess, the *farīq* survives. Even when the prosecution wins most of its case, this clause stands.

The architecture of the ayah, then, is the architecture of a courtroom closing — referring back to evidence given long ago, certifying that the prosecution's prediction held — and yet preserving the small group that was always going to be excepted from the verdict.

You feel the whole Quran behind a single sentence now.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes have been converging on one thing.

The word **ẓann** told us Iblis only had a guess. The theme of the self-fulfilling prophecy told us that the guess required our participation. The verb **ṣaddaqa ʿalayhim** told us that we supplied the proof against ourselves. The exception **farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn** told us that the carve-out exists, that the carve-out is small, that the carve-out is the believer.

Two directions, one truth: **Iblis's claim on you is only as strong as the evidence you give him.** Stop giving him evidence and you have, by the grammar of this ayah, walked yourself into the *illā*.

### Questions to Carry

1. When Iblis made his guess about you specifically, what did he guess? What is the pattern in you he is betting on? Name it. *That is the hypothesis you are about to falsify.*

2. Where in your life have you been **supplying him evidence** — confirming his ẓann with small daily compliances that you wave off as "just how I am"?

3. When was the last time you felt **strange** for being a believer — too sensitive, too restrained, too uncool? Can you receive that strangeness as the texture of the *farīq* rather than as a sign that something is wrong with you?

4. The exception clause is named in the Quran. **You are named in the Quran**, if you are inside it. What does it mean to live a day as someone *named in a divine carve-out*?

5. Is there one act tomorrow — small, unobserved — that would constitute *the unverification of Iblis's theory of you*? Could you do that act?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> **Iblis only had a guess about us — and the whole of human history is the question of whether we will keep proving him right, or step into the small, named, carved-out group whose lives quietly fail to fit his theory.**

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to fix everything tomorrow. You don't have to become a different person.

You only have to do **one thing** that, if Iblis had been asked to predict it, he would have gotten wrong.

One prayer he didn't expect.
One word of patience he bet you wouldn't say.
One refusal to look at something his hypothesis assumed you'd look at.
One quiet act of gratitude in a room where no one was watching.

That single thing — that one act — is your name being entered into the **farīq**. Not theatrically. Not loudly. The way the believers in this ayah are entered: quietly, modestly, in a single clause at the end of a verdict.

Be the data point that breaks his model.

### Du'a

*O Allah — You knew, before the world began, what Iblis would guess about us. You knew the bet he placed. You also wrote the exception clause. Let me live my life inside that exception. Let one of my small acts tomorrow be a piece of evidence against his theory. And let me die, by Your mercy, named among the* **farīqan mina-l-muʾminīn**. *Āmīn.*
