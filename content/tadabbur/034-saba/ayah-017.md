# That We Requited Them With, Because They Disbelieved

## Surah Saba 34:17

> [ayah:34:17] ذَٰلِكَ جَزَيْنَـٰهُم بِمَا كَفَرُوا۟ ۖ وَهَلْ نُجَـٰزِىٓ إِلَّا ٱلْكَفُورَ
>
> *Dhālika jazaynāhum bimā kafarū, wa hal nujāzī illā-l-kafūr.*
>
> **"That We requited them with, because they disbelieved. And do We requite [in this way] anyone but the deeply ungrateful?"**

---

## Introduction: The Verdict Spoken Over the Rubble

Before we touch this ayah, you have to stand where the original listener stood.

Imagine a valley — a long, fertile strip running between two ridges of mountains. On the right, gardens. On the left, gardens. The Quran tells us this two ayat earlier: *jannatāni ʿan yamīnin wa shimāl* — two gardens on the right and the left. So lush, so heavy with fruit, that a woman could walk from one end of the kingdom to the other with a basket on her head, and the basket would fill itself from the branches as she passed beneath them. That's how the early commentators describe it. No labor. Just walking under abundance.

At the top of the valley, a dam. A miracle of engineering. It held the rains of the southern mountains, and from that reservoir the gardens were watered. The Quran calls this place a *baldatun ṭayyibatun wa rabbun ghafūr* — a good land, and a forgiving Lord. Two ayat back. That's the whole offer. A good land, and a forgiving Lord. All they had to do was take from His provision and thank Him for it.

*Kulū min rizqi rabbikum wa-shkurū lah.*

Eat. And be grateful.

[PAUSE]

They didn't.

The Quran is sparse about the *how* of their turning away. It doesn't give us a list of crimes. It just says *fa-aʿraḍū* — they turned away. They turned their faces. From what? From gratitude. From the instruction that came with the gift. And then comes one of the most quietly devastating images in the entire Quran: *fa-arsalnā ʿalayhim sayla-l-ʿarim* — so We sent upon them the flood of the dam. The dam they had built to hold their abundance became the instrument of its destruction. The walls broke. The water that had been their life poured down the valley as their death. And when the water receded, the gardens were gone. In their place: *jannatayni dhawātay ukulin khamṭin wa athlin wa shayʾin min sidrin qalīl* — two gardens of bitter fruit, tamarisk, and a few stunted lote-trees.

Read that slowly. The replacement is not a desert. That would be easier to bear. The replacement is *the same word* — *jannatayn,* two gardens — but emptied of everything that made a garden a garden. The shape of blessing remained. The substance was gone. They still walked between two rows of trees. The trees just gave them bitter fruit and thorns.

[PAUSE]

And now, after all of this — after the dam, the flood, the bitter harvest — comes our ayah. Eight words. A verdict spoken over the rubble. And then, at the end of those eight words, a question that Allah leaves hanging in the air like smoke after a fire.

**That We requited them with, because they disbelieved. And do We requite anyone — but the deeply ungrateful?**

Here is what I want you to feel before we go in. The question at the end is not rhetorical the way English rhetorical questions are. It is not closing the case. It is opening a door — a door that the original listener was meant to walk through and look down the hallway of their own life. Because the verb in that question is doing something the verb in the first half is not doing. The word for "ungrateful" at the end is doing something the word for "disbelieved" in the middle is not doing. And the entire ayah hinges on a distinction so subtle that most translations cannot carry it across.

By the time we finish, you will hear this ayah differently. You will hear what Allah is **withholding** in it, which is more merciful than what He is saying. And you will hear what the question is really asking — not of them, but of you.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: Two Verbs From The Same Root — *Jazaynā* and *Nujāzī*

Let me show you something almost no English translation captures.

Look at the Arabic again. The ayah uses the verb "to requite" — *j-z-y* — **twice**. Once in the first half, once in the second. Two times from the same root. But Allah does not use the same form. He shifts.

In the first half: *jazaynāhum* — "We requited them." That is Form I. The simple, base form of the verb. To recompense. To pay back what is owed. It's the everyday word. You did this; I am giving you the matching response.

In the second half: *nujāzī* — "Do We requite." That is Form III. *Mujāzāh.* The pattern *fāʿala.* Same root, different form.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me. Because this is where the ayah opens.

Let me teach you a general principle about Arabic verb forms first, so the move I'm about to show you lands. Arabic verbs work like a tree. There's a root — three consonants — and then there are *forms* built off that root. Form I is the bare trunk. The forms after it — II, III, IV, V, and so on — are branches. Each branch carries a meaning the trunk doesn't have. Form II often intensifies. Form III often involves a reciprocal or mutual dimension — doing something *with* or *toward* another party. Form IV often introduces a causative element. And so on.

So when Allah uses Form I in one breath and Form III in the next — using the *same root* — He is doing the verbal equivalent of pointing at the same object from two different angles. The object hasn't changed. The angle has. And the angle is the message.

Now back to our ayah.

*Jazaynāhum* — Form I — is the straightforward word. We gave them what their actions warranted. The transaction is clean. They did X; We responded with Y.

But *nujāzī* — Form III — carries a different texture. The classical commentators noticed this immediately. Listen to what Mujahid says, preserved in al-Baghawi's tafsir: *yujāzā ay yuʿāqab* — "to be *jūziya* is to be *punished* with strictness. There is a difference," he says, "between *jazā* and *jāzā.*" One is recompense. The other is **strict reckoning** — the kind of recompense where every detail is brought to account, where the meeting between you and your deed is exact and unrelenting.

Al-Jalalayn glosses the second verb with one word: *yunāqash* — "to be put to strict accounting, audited line by line, with nothing waived." That is the Form III. That is what Allah uses in the question.

[PAUSE]

Notice something remarkable.

Allah uses the gentler verb when describing what He *did* to Saba. *Jazaynāhum* — We recompensed them. He uses the harsher verb — the *mujāzāh,* the strict reckoning — only in the rhetorical question that follows.

And what does the question say?

*Wa hal nujāzī illā al-kafūr.* "And do We strictly recompense — anyone but the deeply ungrateful?"

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Allah is telling us, in the very grammar of the ayah, that **what He did to Saba was not the strict reckoning.** The flood that swept their gardens — that was *jazā,* the base form. The recompense. Even that was restrained. The strict reckoning — the line-by-line audit, the unwaived reckoning — is reserved. It comes only for one category of person. And that category is named in the next word.

Let that sit for a moment. The destruction of an entire civilization — gardens swallowed, dam shattered, people scattered (the Quran later says Allah *made them tales,* *jaʿalnāhum aḥādīth,* "we turned them into stories people tell") — and Allah is signaling, through the choice of verbal form, that even this was not the full weight of the divine reckoning. The full weight is held back. It is reserved for one named figure.

Who?

### Element Two: The Word *Al-Kafūr* — When the Pattern Becomes the Person

Look at the last word of the ayah. *Al-kafūr.*

You will read translations that render this simply "the ungrateful" or "the disbeliever." Both translations are pointing at something real, but both are missing what the morphology is doing.

Arabic has a family of noun patterns that grammarians call the *ṣīghat al-mubālaghah* — the patterns of intensification. These are forms built off a verb that don't just describe someone who *does* the action. They describe someone in whom the action has become **constitutive.** The action has stopped being something they do and started being something they *are.*

Let me teach you with English. Compare these three:
- *He drinks.* (a verb — describes an action)
- *He is a drinker.* (a noun — describes a person who does this)
- *He is a drunkard.* (an intensive — describes a person for whom this has become identity)

Arabic does this with vowel patterns. The pattern *faʿūl* — the pattern of *al-kafūr* — is one of the most powerful intensive patterns in the language. It does not describe someone who has, on some occasion, been ungrateful. It describes someone for whom ingratitude has become the constitution of their being. *Kafūr* is not "he denied a blessing." *Kafūr* is "he is the *one who denies blessings,* habitually, deeply, by nature."

[PAUSE]

Now hold this against the first half of the ayah.

In the first half, Allah uses a verb: *kafarū* — "they disbelieved" or "they were ungrateful." A verb. An action. Something they did. The people of Saba *kafarū.* They committed the act.

In the second half, Allah uses the intensive noun: *al-kafūr.* Not the act. The constitution.

Look at what Allah is doing. He describes the people of Saba with the verb — *kafarū,* they did the action. Then He asks the question with the noun — *al-kafūr,* the one whose entire being is ingratitude.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this means.

It is possible to *do* the action without yet *becoming* the noun. The people of Saba did the act. The question Allah leaves hanging is whether they had become the noun — and whether *you* have. The verb is a moment. The noun is a state. The verb is what happens on a Tuesday afternoon when you forget. The noun is what happens when forgetting has become your atmosphere.

This is, I think, the most merciful possible framing of the warning. Allah is saying: *I do not bring the strict reckoning down on every act of ingratitude. If I did, no one would stand. I bring it down only when the act has crystallized into a self. Only when the verb has become the noun.*

This is why the ayah is a question and not a statement. A statement would close the case. A question opens it. The question is being asked of *you* — *which one are you?* The verb, or the noun? The Tuesday-afternoon forgetting, or the atmosphere?

### Element Three: The Word You Did Not Notice — *Kafara* in the Quran Does Not Always Mean Disbelief

Now let's go back to the middle of the ayah, to the phrase *bimā kafarū* — "because they disbelieved."

Almost every English translation renders this as a statement about *belief.* They disbelieved. They denied. As though the people of Saba had been confronted with a doctrinal proposition and rejected it.

But look at the context. What did the people of Saba actually do? The Quran does not, in the surrounding ayat, describe them rejecting a creed. It does not describe them denying God's existence. It describes them turning away from gratitude. *Wa-shkurū lah,* the instruction had been — "and be grateful to Him." *Fa-aʿraḍū,* the response was — "but they turned away."

The root *k-f-r* in Arabic does not, in its base sense, mean "to disbelieve." It means **to cover.** A farmer is called a *kāfir* in pre-Islamic poetry — not because he denies God but because he *covers* the seed with earth. The night is called *kāfir* because it *covers* the daylight. To do *kufr* to a thing is, at root, to **cover it up,** to bury it, to refuse to acknowledge what is there.

When the Quran uses *kafara* about disbelief, it is using a metaphor. The disbeliever is one who *covers up* the truth that has reached him. He is not, primarily, ignorant. He is *concealing.*

[PAUSE]

Now apply this to Saba.

What did they cover? They covered the *blessing.* They walked beneath gardens that filled their baskets and they refused to see the Hand that filled them. They drank from a dam that held their abundance and they refused to thank the One who sent the rain. They lived inside a *baldatun ṭayyibatun wa rabbun ghafūr* — a good land and a forgiving Lord — and they covered both. They buried the good land under their entitlement. They buried the forgiving Lord under their forgetting.

*Bimā kafarū* — because they covered. Because they refused to see.

This is why the second half of the ayah uses *al-kafūr* and not, say, *al-mushrik* (the polytheist) or *al-jāḥid* (the explicit denier). The category Allah is naming is not the philosophical atheist. It is **the chronic blessing-coverer.** The person whose habitual posture toward what they have been given is *not-seeing.*

And here is the linguistic point that should make you flinch. *Al-kafūr,* the intensive noun, is built on the same root as the everyday verb *kafara.* Which means the line between the everyday act of forgetting a blessing and the deep, constitutive state of being a *blessing-coverer* — that line is not drawn anywhere obvious. It is drawn by **accumulation.** The verb repeated enough times *becomes* the noun. The act of covering, done day after day after day until the covering is no longer something you do but something you *are.*

[PAUSE]

Now we have our anchor points. Let me name what the grammar has shown us — three things sitting on top of each other:

**First** — there are two different verbs of recompense in this ayah. The gentler is used for what happened to Saba. The harsher is reserved for the one named at the end.

**Second** — the people of Saba are described with a verb (*kafarū*). The one for whom the strict reckoning is reserved is described with an intensive noun (*al-kafūr*). The grammar itself draws a line between the act and the identity.

**Third** — the root underneath both is *covering,* not *denying.* The verdict over Saba is not a verdict about doctrine. It is a verdict about a posture toward blessing.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Mercy of the Threshold

I want you to think about something completely modern for a moment.

You know how, in any relationship that is alive, there is a difference between an offense and a betrayal? Your spouse forgets your birthday — that is an offense. It hurts. You talk about it. They apologize. The marriage absorbs it and goes on. But if your spouse has been forgetting your birthday for fifteen years, and forgetting the anniversary, and forgetting the small daily acts of recognition that say *I see you* — at some point the marriage is no longer absorbing an offense. It is registering a state. The forgetting has stopped being an act and started being an atmosphere.

What changed? Not the action. The action is the same — they forgot. What changed is the **accumulation.** The verb has become the noun.

And here is the thing every long-married person knows: the marriage that survives the first kind of forgetting often does not survive the second. Not because the second is, in any single instance, worse. But because the second has become *who you are to each other.* The marriage is no longer trying to fix an act. It is trying to fix an identity. And identities are harder.

[PAUSE]

Now read the ayah again.

*Dhālika jazaynāhum bimā kafarū wa hal nujāzī illā al-kafūr.*

Allah is telling us how the divine relationship works. There is *kafara,* the act — and there is *al-kafūr,* the state. There is the offense, and there is the identity. And the strict reckoning — the *mujāzāh,* the line-by-line audit of every blessing forgotten — Allah is telling us, by the very structure of the ayah, that He does not bring this down on the act. He brings it down on the state.

This is the *mercy of the threshold.*

If Allah brought the full reckoning down on every *kafara* — every instance of a blessing forgotten, every moment of failing to thank, every breath taken without praise — no creature would survive a single morning. None of us. The verb in everyday Arabic prayer is *astaghfirullāh* — *I seek forgiveness from Allah* — and the Prophet ﷺ said it seventy times a day, more than seventy by some reports. Why? Because the act of forgetting is constant. It runs underneath ordinary life like groundwater. No human being clears even an afternoon without some *kafara* of some blessing.

Allah knows this. The ayah tells us He knows. The Form I verb *jazā* covers many things — including the relatively contained recompense that fell on Saba, devastating as it was. The Form III verb *jāzā* — the strict reckoning, the unwaived audit — is held in reserve. Not because Allah lacks the right to apply it. The ayah is not denying His right. It is announcing His **restraint.**

[PAUSE]

This is the texture of divine mercy you do not usually hear about. Mercy is not usually the absence of consequence. Mercy is usually the **deferral of strict reckoning** — the gap Allah leaves between what He could exact and what He actually exacts. The space between *jazā* and *jāzā.* That space is where every human life is lived. We live in that space because Allah holds the strict reckoning back.

And here is what this means for you tomorrow morning. When you forget a blessing — and you will, before noon — the right response is not panic. The right response is not "I have become *al-kafūr.*" Allah has told you, in this ayah, that the verb is not yet the noun. You have time. The act of forgetting can still be turned, today, by the act of remembering. The accumulation has not yet become an atmosphere. Not yet.

But you also know — because you know your own life — that the threshold is real. There is some number of days of forgetting after which the forgetting becomes the texture of who you are. The mercy is that Allah does not tell you what that number is. You don't know which forgotten blessing is the one that tips the scale. Which is precisely the point. The not-knowing is meant to keep you tender. *Today's* forgetting could be the one. So today's remembering matters. Right now. Before the next breath, which is itself a blessing you have not yet thanked Him for.

That is what the gap between *jazā* and *jāzā* is asking you to live inside. Awake.

### Theme Two: The Posture That Buries the Garden

Now let me bring the second theme through a different door.

Think about how human attention works. You walk into a new house. The first morning, you notice the light coming through the kitchen window. You notice the sound of the floor underfoot. You notice the way the air feels. You are *seeing* the house.

By month three, you walk through the kitchen and you do not notice the light. You do not notice the floor. The house has become *invisible* to you. Not because the house has changed. Because your attention has *covered* it. You have built, in the months of habituation, a layer of *not-seeing* over what is still, materially, right there.

This is what attention does. It defaults to invisibility. The new becomes the normal becomes the unseen. The blessing becomes the background.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the root *k-f-r* again. *To cover.* The pre-Islamic farmer who covers the seed with earth. The night that covers the day.

The people of Saba did not, in any explicit moment, *deny* their gardens. They did not stand on a hill and declare, "There are no gardens here." That would be a strange kind of madness. What they did was far more ordinary. They *stopped seeing them.* They walked beneath the trees that filled their baskets and they thought about other things. The gardens became the background. The dam became infrastructure. The good land became real estate. The forgiving Lord became a folk memory.

The covering was not a doctrine. It was an *attentional drift.* It was the slow burial of what was right in front of them under the soft soil of normality.

And this is why the ayah is so terrifying when you hear it correctly. Because the question is not asking, *"Are you a militant atheist?"* The question is asking, *"Are you a person whose attention has buried your blessings?"* And the honest answer, for most of us, most of the time, is — partly. Yes. Partly. I have buried the blessing of my eyes under my phone. I have buried the blessing of my children under my fatigue. I have buried the blessing of my next breath under my anxiety about the breath after it. I am, in small constant ways, *covering.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what makes the ayah merciful even in this. The ayah does not say *al-kāfir* — the simple noun "one who covers." It says *al-kafūr,* the intensive. The one for whom covering has become a totalizing state. There is enormous room, in the grammar of the ayah, for the person who is *occasionally* a coverer and is trying not to be. That person is named by the verb *kafarū,* which the people of Saba shared with most of humanity. That person can still walk out of the verb before the verb becomes the noun.

How do you walk out of the verb?

By the act that is the *exact opposite of covering.* The Arabic word for it is *shukr.* Gratitude. But *shukr* in Arabic does not mean what "thank you" means in English. *Shukr* means **to make manifest.** To bring into view. To *uncover.* When you do *shukr* of a blessing, you are literally undoing the *kufr* of it — pulling the soil off the seed, lifting the night off the day, returning the blessing to the field of your active vision.

Look at where the surah began. *Wa-shkurū lah.* "And be grateful to Him." The people of Saba covered, when they could have been uncovering. The whole surah turns on this single hinge.

This is what changes about your life tomorrow if this ayah has reached you. You are going to walk through a house you have stopped seeing. You are going to eat food whose source you have forgotten. You are going to take breath after breath without registering that the breath is being given. The *kafara* will happen — quietly, constantly, all day. The question is whether you will, somewhere in the day, do the small act of *uncovering* — naming one blessing out loud, letting your eyes rest on it long enough that it returns to view, saying *al-ḥamdu lillāh* and *meaning it about something specific.* Not a generic thank-you. A specific uncovering. *Thank You for this water. Thank You for the fact that my back did not hurt this morning. Thank You for the person who is across the room from me.* One act of *shukr* a day pulls the soil off one seed. It keeps the verb from becoming the noun. It keeps you in the space between *jazā* and *jāzā,* where mercy lives.

That is the practical heart of this ayah. The flood will not come for the person who is *uncovering.* The flood comes — eventually, somewhere, in some form — for the one who has made *covering* their atmosphere.

## Closing Synthesis

### Bringing It Together

Look at what the linguistic and the thematic have shown us. They are the same insight, arriving from two directions.

The linguistics showed us that Allah uses a verb for what Saba did (*kafarū*) and an intensive noun for what triggers the strict reckoning (*al-kafūr*). The themes showed us why: because Allah is the Lord of the threshold, the One who distinguishes between the act and the identity, between the offense and the betrayal, between the forgotten blessing and the buried blessing. The grammar *is* the theology. Allah is using verbal morphology to teach you the architecture of His own mercy.

The linguistics showed us that the root underneath all of this is *covering* — not denying. The themes showed us why this matters: because the human spiritual condition is rarely active denial. It is, almost always, attentional drift. The forgetting that buries the seen.

The linguistics showed us two verbs of recompense — *jazā* and *jāzā,* one milder, one strict. The themes showed us that we live our lives in the *gap* between them. The gap is mercy. The gap is held open by every act of *shukr* and slowly closed by every act of *kufr.*

### Questions to Carry

1. What blessing in your life, right now, have you stopped seeing? Not lost — *stopped seeing.*

2. Of all the *kafara* you have done this week — the small daily forgettings — has any of it begun to feel like atmosphere rather than action?

3. If today you had to *uncover* one buried blessing — to do the *shukr* that pulls the soil off the seed — which would you choose?

4. Is there a relationship in your life where the verb has become the noun? Where what you do to someone has crystallized into who you are to them? What would it cost to walk back out of the noun?

5. When you hear the question *wa hal nujāzī illā al-kafūr,* and you let it land on *you* — what does the honest answer feel like in your chest?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah, in this ayah, draws a line between the act of forgetting a blessing and the identity of being a blessing-burier — and tells you, through the very grammar of His speech, that His full reckoning is reserved only for the second, which means today you are still standing in the space where one act of remembering can keep you out of it.

### The Closing Invitation

Do not leave this ayah only with the warning. The warning is real, but it is not the deepest thing here. The deepest thing here is the *restraint.* The fact that Allah, having every right to bring the line-by-line audit down on every breath you fail to thank Him for, has instead reserved that audit for the one who has made covering a totalizing identity — that is not the speech of a Lord looking for a reason to destroy you. That is the speech of a Lord looking for a reason **not** to. He has built the reason into the grammar. The verb is not yet the noun. You have today.

Walk into today as someone who knows that. And uncover something.

### Du'a

O Allah, You who have held the strict reckoning back from us breath after breath — keep us in the space You have opened between *jazā* and *jāzā.* Lift the soil off the seeds we have buried. Return our blessings to our vision before we become the ones whose vision has been taken. And do not let the verb of our forgetting harden into the noun of our being. Āmīn.
