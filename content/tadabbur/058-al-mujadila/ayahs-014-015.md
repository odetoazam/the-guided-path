# Belonging to Neither: The Homelessness of the Divided Heart

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first, the way they fall on the ear before the mind has a chance to organize them.

> [ayah:58:14] ۞ أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ تَوَلَّوْا۟ قَوْمًا غَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِم مَّا هُم مِّنكُمْ وَلَا مِنْهُمْ وَيَحْلِفُونَ عَلَى ٱلْكَذِبِ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ
>
> *Alam tara ilā alladhīna tawallaw qawman ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhim, mā hum minkum wa lā minhum, wa yaḥlifūna ʿalā al-kadhibi wa hum yaʿlamūn.*
>
> **"Have you not seen those who turned toward a people upon whom Allah's wrath has fallen? They are neither of you nor of them, and they swear upon a lie while they know."**

And then the verdict, one ayah later:

> [ayah:58:15] أَعَدَّ ٱللَّهُ لَهُمْ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا ۖ إِنَّهُمْ سَآءَ مَا كَانُوا۟ يَعْمَلُونَ
>
> *Aʿadda Allāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdan, innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn.*
>
> **"Allah has prepared for them a severe punishment. Truly, evil is that which they used to do."**

### Let's rebuild the world

To feel the weight of these words, you have to stand in Madina in the years after the *hijra*.

The city is no longer a place where you are simply a tribe or a clan. A new thing has appeared — a community gathered not by blood but by belief. And the moment a community gathers around something true, a particular kind of person appears at its edges. Not the open enemy. The open enemy is almost a relief; you know where he stands. No — the person who appears is the one who **wants the benefits of belonging without the cost of commitment.**

These were the *munāfiqūn*, the hypocrites. By daylight they sat in the Prophet's gatherings ﷺ. They prayed in the same rows. They said the words. And then, the classical commentators tell us — al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī citing Qatāda — they would carry the secrets of the believers out the back door, into the homes of those who opposed the Prophet, and quietly tell them everything. Among the narrations for this passage, al-Suddī and Muqātil report it concerned specific men of Madina — one tradition names ʿAbdullāh ibn Nabtal — who would sit with the Prophet ﷺ and then relay his words to his opponents. The commentators differ on the exact occasion, and we should hold that lightly. What they agree on is the *shape* of the thing: a person facing two directions at once.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the strange thing. We expect the Qur'an, at this moment, to expose these people as enemies. To say: *they are against you.* But that is not what it says. It says something far stranger, and far more terrifying.

It says: **they are not of you — and they are not of them either.**

### The Hook

Think about that. You would assume that if someone betrays one side, they at least *belong* to the other side. The traitor at least has a home in the camp he sold you out to.

But the Qur'an refuses them even that. It describes a person who has betrayed his way out of *every* belonging. Who is homeless in the deepest sense the human soul can be homeless — not without a house, but without a people. Without a *we*.

How does a person arrive there? How do you end up belonging to no one — not even to the side you chose? The answer is hidden in a single verb, and once we see it, we will not be able to unsee it. Stay with me.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One — *Tawallaw*: The Verb of Turning Yourself

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning, because this verb is doing work that an English translation simply cannot carry.

In Arabic, a root — a cluster of usually three consonants — is like a seed. From one seed you can grow many different plants by pouring it into different *patterns*, called forms. There are ten common patterns. And each pattern doesn't just change the word; it changes the *kind of action* the word describes.

Here is the everyday version. Imagine the idea of "learning." In one pattern it means simply *to know*. Pour the same root into another pattern and it means *to teach* — to cause someone else to know. Pour it into yet another pattern and it means *to make yourself learn, to take learning upon yourself with effort* — what we'd call studying. Same seed. Three different plants. The pattern tells you who is doing what to whom, and how deliberately.

Now hold that idea, and let's look at our verb.

The root here is **و-ل-ي** *(w-l-y)*. This is one of the great roots of the entire Qur'an. At its core it means **nearness** — to be so close to something that nothing comes between you and it. From this single root grows *walī* (an intimate protector, a guardian, a close friend), *mawlā* (a patron, a master, the one you belong to), and *wilāya* (allegiance, guardianship, devoted loyalty). When you make someone your *walī*, you are not casually liking them. You are pulling them in close enough that they stand *between you and the world.*

Now — the word in our ayah is **تَوَلَّوْا** *(tawallaw)*.

[PAUSE]

This is the root **w-l-y** poured into **Form V** — the pattern of *taking something upon yourself*, the reflexive, effortful pattern. The corpus confirms it: a Form V verb, in the past tense.

So *tawallaw* does not mean they were assigned this loyalty, or drifted into it, or were born into it. Form V is the pattern of the **deliberate self.** It means: **they turned their own selves toward these people. They took them, with effort, as their intimate protectors. They made them their *walī*.**

Notice something. The plainest physical meaning of this root is simply the turning of the body — to turn your face *toward* one thing is, in the same motion, to turn your back *on* another. You cannot face two directions. And this is the genius of the image: a physical act everyone has done a thousand times — turning toward someone you trust — is being used to point at an invisible act of the heart. **An allegiance is a direction the soul faces.** When you make something your *walī*, your whole inner self orients toward it, the way a face orients toward a voice.

Here's what's remarkable. The grammar tells us this was not a slip. It was a *turning* — chosen, reflexive, owned. They worked at this. And what did they turn toward? *Qawman ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhim* — "a people upon whom Allah's wrath has fallen."

Of all the directions a soul could face, they deliberately oriented themselves toward the one direction the verse marks as already under divine displeasure. The grammar of self-investment, aimed at the worst possible target. That is the first stone laid in the diagnosis.

Let this settle: **the tragedy described here is not an accident of fate. It is the long, slow result of where a person keeps choosing to turn.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Mā hum minkum wa lā minhum*: The Architecture of Belonging to No One

Now we come to the heart of these words. And to feel it, I want to teach you about a tiny preposition that carries an enormous weight in Arabic: **مِنْ** *(min)*.

We usually translate *min* as "from" or "of." But there is a particular use of it called *min al-tabʿīḍiyya* — the *min* of "being part of." When you say someone is *min* a people, you are not saying they are near them or allied with them. You are saying they are **made of the same substance as them.** That they are a piece *of* that whole. It is the *min* of belonging in the marrow — like saying "she is one of us," meaning she is cut from our cloth, she is part of our body.

Now listen to the structure Allah builds:

**مَّا هُم مِّنكُمْ** — *mā hum minkum* — "They are **not** of you."

**وَلَا مِنْهُمْ** — *wa lā minhum* — "**and not** of them."

Two negations. *Mā* and *lā* — two different negating particles, stacked, sealing off two different exits. The first slams shut the door to the community of believers — *you.* The second slams shut the door to the people they actually ran toward — *them.*

Read the absence here. Read it slowly, because **what is missing from this verse is the whole point.**

The verse offers a person two possible homes and then, with surgical care, takes both away. It does not say "they are of them, not of you." That would at least be a verdict you could live inside — a defection, a clean switch of teams. It does not even say "they are confused about which side they are on." It says something with no soft edge at all: **you are part of no whole. There is no *we* that contains you.**

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to notice about the precision of it. The verse could have stopped at "they are not of you." That alone would be a devastating expulsion. But Allah adds the second negation — *and not of them* — and in doing so He tells us something the hypocrites themselves did not know. They *thought* they had a fallback. They thought: if this community of faith falls, we still have our secret allies; we'll be fine; we have somewhere to land. The second *lā* removes the ground from under that landing. **The people you betrayed your soul to befriend will not hold you either.** Betrayal does not buy you a seat at the other table. It only burns the chair you were sitting in.

The Qur'an has a single word for this condition. It is not in our verse, but it is the verse's twin, in Sūrat an-Nisāʾ (4:143): *mudhabdhabīn* — and al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both reach for it to explain our ayah. *Mudhabdhab.* The word sounds like what it means — a kind of stuttering, swinging, suspended thing. Picture a bucket on a fraying rope swinging in a deep well: not down at the water, not up at the rim, just swinging in the dark in between, *dhabdhaba, dhabdhaba*, belonging to neither the depth nor the height. That is the sonic texture of the condition. Suspension. No floor. No resting place.

So the structure of this little phrase — *not of you, and not of them* — is not a description of where the hypocrite stands. **It is a description of the fact that he stands nowhere at all.**

And now connect it back to our first verb. *Tawallaw* — they *turned.* A turning is a direction. But what the second ayah-fragment reveals is that the direction they turned never actually arrived anywhere. They faced away from faith, but they never truly faced into the other camp either. They spent their whole turning *between.* The motion was real; the destination was a void.

Sit with that one clear truth: **the man who will not fully commit to the truth does not thereby gain the world. He loses the truth without gaining the world. He ends with neither.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three — *Yaḥlifūna ʿalā al-kadhibi wa hum yaʿlamūn*: Standing On a Lie You Can See

There is one more piece of language here, and it is the most chilling, because it shows us *how* a person maintains a life with no floor under it.

Three roots cluster together: **ح-ل-ف** *(ḥ-l-f)* — to swear an oath; **ك-ذ-ب** *(k-dh-b)* — falsehood, the lie; and **ع-ل-م** *(ʿ-l-m)* — to know. Watch how they are arranged.

First: **وَيَحْلِفُونَ** — *wa yaḥlifūna* — "and they swear." The corpus marks this as a present-tense verb, and that tense matters. It is not "they swore, once." It is **they keep swearing, habitually, on and on.** An oath in that world was the heaviest currency of truth a person had. To swear by Allah was to stake your sincerity on the most sacred name there is. It was the thing you reached for when your bare word was not enough — the final guarantee.

Now the second piece: what are they swearing *on*? **عَلَى الْكَذِبِ** — *ʿalā al-kadhib* — "**upon** the lie."

Notice the preposition: *ʿalā*, "upon," "on top of." In Arabic, as in English, you stand *ʿalā* a surface — *ʿalā the ground*, *ʿalā a foundation.* The image the verse builds is that **the lie has become the platform they stand on.** They have planted their oaths *on top of* falsehood. The most sacred act they perform — invoking the name of Allah — is built on a floor of fabrication. And remember what we just established: they have no real ground beneath them, no *we* to stand inside. So this is what they do instead. **They manufacture a standing-place out of oaths. They swear themselves into a belonging they do not have.**

This is the engine of the whole condition. A man with no genuine home must *perform* one. And the more hollow the belonging, the louder the oath required to prop it up. Al-Jalālayn and al-Muyassar both tell us exactly what they were swearing: *that they were believers* — "we are with you, we are of you, we believe." The very claim the verse already demolished with *mā hum minkum.* They stood on the lie that they belonged, and swore to it by the name of the One who had just declared they did not.

[PAUSE]

And now the phrase that should make every one of us go quiet. **وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ** — *wa hum yaʿlamūn* — "**while they know.**"

This is the door slamming shut on every excuse.

We can forgive the person who is mistaken. We make room for the one who is confused, who genuinely believes the false thing he says. But the verse cuts off that mercy with three words. They were not confused. They were not self-deceived in the comforting way we often are, where the lie has crawled so deep we half-believe it ourselves. No. *Wa hum yaʿlamūn.* **They knew.** At the very moment the sacred oath left their lips, a clear voice inside them said: *this is false, and I know it is false.*

Think about what that costs a soul. There is a kind of liar who has lost the truth — he no longer knows up from down, and there is a terrible mercy even in that fog. And then there is this far rarer, far colder thing: the person who keeps the truth perfectly intact *inside* and lies *against* it anyway. He has not lost his sight. He sees clearly, and chooses, with open eyes, to swear to the thing he sees is false. The light is on. He acts in the dark anyway.

That is the most awake a conscience can be while a person betrays it. And the Qur'an leaves it there, naked, with no softening: *wa hum yaʿlamūn.*

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

Look at what these three pieces of language have shown us, laid end to end.

A verb of turning — *tawallaw* — the soul deliberately facing the wrong direction.

A double negation — *mā hum minkum wa lā minhum* — that turning arriving nowhere, leaving the person with no people, no home, no floor.

And then a habit of false oaths sworn knowingly — *yaḥlifūna ʿalā al-kadhibi wa hum yaʿlamūn* — the homeless soul manufacturing a fake belonging out of sacred words it knows to be empty.

That is not three observations about three words. **It is one portrait, drawn three times.** It is the anatomy of a divided heart, from the first wrong turn to the final hollow performance.

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about words, and not just about a handful of men in Madina fourteen centuries ago — but about reality itself, and about something you and I carry in our own chests.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One — The Homelessness of Trying to Belong Everywhere

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You know the experience of becoming a slightly different person depending on the room you walk into. With your family, one voice. With your colleagues, another. With your old friends, a third; with the people you're trying to impress, a fourth. We all do this a little — it's the ordinary social grease of life, and in small doses it's harmless, even kind.

But you have also met — maybe you have, at moments, *been* — the person who does this all the way down. The one who has no fixed self underneath the performances. Who agrees with whoever is in the room. Who tells each group what it wants to hear, holds no opinion that might cost them a friend, and tailors their loyalties to whoever holds power that day. From the outside it can look like remarkable social skill. They fit in *everywhere.*

And here is the thing modern psychology has a hundred names for and the Qur'an named in four words: **the person who fits in everywhere belongs nowhere.**

[PAUSE]

There is a hollowing-out that happens to such a person, and it is exactly what our verse describes. *Mā hum minkum wa lā minhum.* When you give a slightly different self to every group in order to be accepted by all of them, no single group ever actually meets *you.* They meet the version you built for them. And so even when you are surrounded — even when you are, by every external measure, *included* — you are profoundly, structurally alone. Because the *you* they accepted was never real, their acceptance cannot reach the real you. It lands on the mask and goes no further.

The hypocrites of Madina are the extreme case of a universal disease. They wanted to keep their standing with the believers *and* keep their insurance with the opposition. They refused to pay the price of either belonging. They wanted the warmth of the fire without standing close enough to be seen by its light. And the result was not the best of both worlds. It was the loss of both. **A foot in two boats is not twice the journey. It is the splits, and then the water.**

Let me push this one layer deeper, because there is a paradox here worth sitting in. We usually think the divided person is *protecting* themselves — keeping their options open, avoiding the risk of being wrong, staying flexible. That is the whole psychological appeal of never fully committing: it feels *safe.* If I never plant my flag, I can never lose the ground I planted it on.

But the verse exposes the lie in that. **The refusal to belong is not a safe middle ground between two risks. It is its own catastrophe — the one risk worse than either of the others.** Choose faith and be tested by the opponents: at least you have a home and a people and a direction. Choose the other side openly: at least you have *a* place to stand. But refuse to choose, swing forever in the well like the *mudhabdhab* bucket, and you have engineered the one outcome with no home at all. The flexibility you thought was protecting you was quietly amputating you from every "we" that could have held you.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. The next time you feel yourself shape-shifting to win a room's approval — softening a conviction you actually hold, laughing at what you actually find wrong, going silent where you should speak, performing an enthusiasm you don't feel — pause and ask one question: *Am I building a bridge, or am I dissolving myself?* Some adaptation is love; you really do speak gently to a grieving friend and firmly to a wandering child, and that is integrity, not division. But there is a line, and you can feel it. On one side, you flex *how* you say a true thing. On the other side, you erase *whether* you'll say it at all, depending on who's listening. The first builds connection. The second, repeated long enough, builds a person who is *minkum wa lā minhum* — accepted everywhere, at home nowhere, increasingly unsure there is anyone real left inside.

The cure is not rigidity. The cure is a center. A few things you will not move on — your *qibla*, the direction your soul faces — so that everything else can flex *around* something solid instead of flexing all the way down into nothing.

### Theme Two — The Oath as the Last Refuge: When We Lie Against What We Know

Now the harder theme. The one that comes closer to home than we'd like.

Begin with an ordinary moment. You have done something — small, maybe — that you know was wrong. And someone asks you about it. And in the half-second before you answer, you feel the truth sitting right there, fully visible to you, and you watch yourself reach past it and say the other thing. The smooth thing. The thing that protects you. And — this is the part — *you know.* You know exactly what you're doing as you do it. There is no fog. The light is on.

That is *wa hum yaʿlamūn,* in miniature, in your own kitchen.

The verse is describing the munāfiqūn at full scale, but the mechanism it exposes is one we all carry. And the most unsettling detail is *what they reached for to protect the lie.* They didn't just lie. **They swore.** They reached for the most sacred thing available — an oath in the name of Allah — and used it as a shield for falsehood.

Stay with how perverse that is. The oath exists *because* of the lie. We invent oaths, promises, "I swear to God," "wallāhi," precisely for the moments when bare words aren't trusted — to borrow the weight of the sacred and press it down onto our claim. So when a person swears to a lie *knowingly,* they are doing something worse than lying. **They are conscripting the holy into the service of the false.** They take the very instrument designed to guarantee truth and wrap it around a lie, so that the lie can wear truth's clothing.

[PAUSE]

Why does the soul do this? Here is the connection back to the first theme, and it's the thing that makes these two verses one verse. The person with no real ground beneath them — *mā hum minkum wa lā minhum* — must manufacture ground. And the loudest, fastest way to manufacture the *appearance* of belonging is to swear to it. *I'm with you, wallāhi, I'm one of you, I swear it.* The emptier the belonging, the more emphatic the oath has to be to cover the hollowness. **The volume of our protest is often a measure of the lie underneath it.** The person who actually belongs rarely has to keep swearing they belong. It's the bucket swinging in the well that has to keep shouting that it's reached the water.

And notice the exhaustion built into this. A true self is restful — you simply *are* what you are, in every room, and there is nothing to maintain. But a performed belonging held up by oaths is a full-time job. Every oath written a check the next oath has to cover. You can never relax, because relaxing for a moment might let the real self show, and the real self is the thing you've sworn does not exist. The munāfiq is the most tired man in Madina. He is doing the exhausting labor of being someone he is not, in front of two audiences at once, forever.

This is why the verdict in the next ayah lands the way it does. Listen again:

> أَعَدَّ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا
>
> "Allah has prepared for them a severe punishment."

The verb — *aʿadda* — is in the past tense. *Has prepared.* Not *will* prepare. **It is already done.** And here is the quiet, devastating symmetry the architecture reveals: these people spent their lives *preparing* — preparing their oaths, preparing their cover stories, preparing their secret messages carried out the back door, preparing their fallback with the other side. All that preparation. All that exhausting labor of the divided life. And while they were busy preparing their schemes, the verse says, **Allah had already finished His preparation.** Their effort versus His. They prepared a thousand lies; He had already, calmly, in the past tense, prepared what those lies were earning. The whole frantic enterprise of the double life was racing toward something that was sitting there, *ready,* the entire time.

[PAUSE]

And then the final word on it: *innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — "truly, evil is that which they used to do." That last phrase, *kānū yaʿmalūn,* carries the sense of ongoing, habitual action — not one evil deed but a *way of life,* a settled practice. The verdict is not on a moment of weakness. It is on a *career* of division. On the steady, daily craft of facing two ways at once.

So what does this change about tomorrow? Two things, very concretely.

First: **guard the oath.** Notice how easily *wallāhi* falls out of our mouths — to win an argument, to dodge blame, to add weight to a story we're exaggerating. The verse should make us flinch a little before we reach for the sacred to prop up something less than fully true. Let your *wallāhi* be so rare and so honest that no one ever doubts it. Don't spend the holy on the trivial, and never, ever spend it on the false.

Second, and deeper: **listen to the moment of "while they know."** That clean inner voice that says *this is false* even as you reach past it — that voice is not your enemy. It is a mercy. It is the last piece of you that is still awake. The munāfiq's tragedy is not that he heard that voice; it's that he heard it and swore over the top of it until, presumably, he could barely hear it anymore. The moment you still feel the friction between what you know and what you're about to say — that friction is the door back. Most people lose their way not in one dramatic betrayal but by slowly sanding down that friction until it's smooth. Keep it rough. Let it hurt a little. That ache is your *fiṭra* refusing to die.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now stand back and see the whole shape of these two ayahs, because the structure itself is the final argument.

Ayah 14 is a **diagnosis,** delivered in three movements that descend like steps into a basement:

1. *Tawallaw* — they turned. **The wrong direction was chosen.**
2. *Mā hum minkum wa lā minhum* — they belong to neither. **The chosen direction arrived nowhere.**
3. *Yaḥlifūna ʿalā al-kadhibi wa hum yaʿlamūn* — they swear knowingly to a lie. **The homeless soul fakes a home, against its own knowledge.**

Turn — land nowhere — perform. Each step is the consequence of the one before. The wrong turning produces the homelessness; the homelessness produces the desperate, knowing oaths.

And ayah 15 is the **sentence,** mirroring it:

4. *Aʿadda Allāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdan* — Allah has *already prepared.* **Against all their preparing, His was finished first.**
5. *Innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn* — evil is what they *used to do.* **The verdict is on the whole habit, not a single act.**

The human beings in this passage are frantic with motion — turning, swinging, swearing, scheming, carrying secrets. And the divine response is utterly still: *has prepared.* Past tense. Done. The contrast between the noise of the divided life and the silence of the settled verdict *is* the meaning.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey are the same insight approached from two directions.

From the words, we learned that *tawalla* is a turning, that belonging is a *min* — a being-made-of, that an oath is a borrowed weight pressed onto a claim, and that *yaʿlamūn* leaves no room for the excuse of ignorance.

From the themes, we learned that the soul which refuses to fully belong does not stay safe in the middle — it loses every home; and that such a soul will manufacture a counterfeit belonging out of sacred oaths it knows are empty, exhausting itself in a performance whose verdict is already, quietly, prepared.

They meet at one place. **The integrity of the heart is not a luxury or a private virtue. It is the only ground there is to stand on.** Refuse it, and you don't get a safer life. You get the swinging bucket, the false oath, the two slammed doors, and a punishment that finished being readied while you were still busy pretending.

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you, not to answer cleverly but to sit with honestly.

1. Is there a room in my life where I become someone I am not — and have I started to mistake that performance for who I really am?
2. When I reach for "*wallāhi*," what am I usually trying to cover? Is my oath protecting truth, or propping up something I'd rather not examine?
3. Where is the friction in me — the place where I still feel *wa hum yaʿlamūn,* the clean voice saying *you know this isn't true* — and have I been quietly sanding it smooth?
4. Do I have a center, a *qibla* my soul faces, that I will not move for any room's approval? Or do I flex all the way down?
5. If all my preparing — my image-management, my hedging, my keeping-options-open — were set beside what Allah has already prepared, would I be comforted or unsettled?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The soul that tries to belong to two opposed worlds at once ends up belonging to neither, and the oaths it swears to hide its emptiness — knowing they are false — only seal a punishment that was finished being prepared while it schemed.

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked, by these verses, to become rigid or harsh or to wear your convictions like a weapon. You are being invited to do something gentler and far harder: **to be one person.** To let the self that prays be the self that speaks, the self that's alone be the self that's in the crowd. To have a few unmoving things at your center so that your kindness can flex without your soul dissolving. To stop the exhausting labor of facing two ways, and simply — finally — *face one.* There is rest there. The whole-hearted are the only ones who ever truly get to put their burdens down.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who already know the direction our souls truly face — make us *of* Your people, in the marrow and not in the mask. Save us from the swinging in-between, from the oath that covers a lie, from the friction we have learned to ignore. Let us be one heart facing one way, so that when we stand before what You have prepared, we find we belonged to You all along. *Āmīn.*
