# Make Room and Be Made Spacious

## The Economy of Yielding — Surah Al-Mujadila, Ayah 11

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓاْ إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمْ تَفَسَّحُواْ فِى ٱلْمَجَٰلِسِ فَٱفْسَحُواْ يَفْسَحِ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ ۖ وَإِذَا قِيلَ ٱنشُزُواْ فَٱنشُزُواْ يَرْفَعِ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ مِنكُمْ وَٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُواْ ٱلْعِلْمَ دَرَجَٰتٍ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ**

Roughly, in English:

> *"O you who believe! When it is said to you, 'Make room in the assemblies,' then make room — Allah will make room for you. And when it is said, 'Rise up,' then rise up — Allah will raise those who believe among you, and those who were given knowledge, by degrees. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do."*

Now, I want you to notice what we are about to spend nearly an hour on. Not a battle. Not the laws of inheritance. Not the fate of nations.

A crowded room.

That's it. People sitting close together, and someone asks you to slide over and let another person in.

### Rebuilding the world

You have to feel where this lands to understand why it matters. Picture the assembly of the Prophet ﷺ. It is not a stadium. It is a modest space — a circle of bodies, knee to knee, in the heat of Madinah. And to sit close to the Prophet ﷺ was not like sitting close to a celebrity. It was *light*. Every inch nearer was nearer to the source of revelation, nearer to the voice that was reshaping eternity in real time. People did not jostle for those seats out of vanity. They jostled because they were thirsty.

Among the narrations preserved for this ayah — and the classical commentators record more than one occasion — al-Baghawī relates from Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān that the Prophet ﷺ used to honor the people of Badr. One day a group of these veterans of Badr arrived after the gathering had already filled. They came, they gave salām to the Prophet ﷺ, they gave salām to the people seated — and then they stood there. Standing on their feet. Waiting. Hoping someone would notice and shift over to make space. And no one moved. The narration says this weighed heavily on the Prophet ﷺ.

[PAUSE]

Stay in that moment. There are men standing — men who had walked into the valley of Badr when the whole future of Islam hung on a few hundred lives — and they are left on their feet in a crowded room because the people already seated would not give up their place.

That is the world this ayah walks into. Not an abstract ethics seminar. A real, specific, slightly awkward, deeply human moment that every single one of us has lived: *the moment someone needs you to move, and moving costs you something you wanted to keep.*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hold onto as we go. This ayah is going to take that tiny, almost embarrassingly small act — sliding over a few inches on a bench — and it is going to tie it directly to the architecture of how Allah elevates a human being.

It is going to claim something that runs against everything your survival instinct tells you. It is going to say: **the way up is down. The way to be enlarged is to make yourself smaller. The way to be raised is to give up your seat.**

And it's going to say this not as poetry — but as a transaction, with a verb on your side and the *same root verb* on Allah's side. Watch what He does with the language. Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Tafassaḥū* — the verb that loops back on itself

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the word, because the whole insight lives in the machinery.

Arabic builds meaning out of a three-letter root, and then it bends that root into different *forms* — patterns — and each pattern does a different job. Think of the root as raw clay and the forms as molds. The same clay, pressed into different molds, comes out doing different work.

There is a mold in Arabic — grammarians call it Form V — that has a special character. It is **reflexive and reciprocal**. That means the action curves back onto the doers themselves, and it happens *among them, between them, mutually*. It's not one person doing something to another. It's a group doing something with and for one another, and the result settling back over the whole group.

Hold that idea: *an action the group does to itself, for itself, among itself.*

Now the word. When Allah issues the command, He says:

> **تَفَسَّحُوا فِى ٱلْمَجَٰلِسِ**
> *tafassaḥū fī al-majālis* — "make room in the assemblies."

The root here is **f-s-ḥ (فسح)**. And what does this root mean? Spaciousness. Openness. Room. *Fusḥa* in Arabic is open ground, a clearing, breathing space — a place where you are not crowded, where the chest can expand. When something is *fasīḥ*, it is wide and unconstricted.

And the verb **tafassaḥū** is sitting in that Form V mold — the reflexive, reciprocal one. So this is not merely "make space." It is, almost untranslatably: *spread the spaciousness around among yourselves. Create room mutually. Let openness circulate through the whole gathering.*

[PAUSE]

Now notice something, and stay with me, because this is the heart of it.

Listen to the chain of verbs Allah lays down, all from this one root f-s-ḥ:

> *tafassaḥū* — (you all) make room among yourselves
> *fa-fsaḥū* — so make room
> *yafsaḥ-illāhu lakum* — **Allah will make room for you.**

Three uses of the *same root*, one after another. Your action, your action again, and then — **God's action, in the exact same vocabulary.** You perform *fasḥ*. He performs *fasḥ*. The verb you do to a stranger in a crowded room is the verb Allah turns around and does to *you*.

This is not a vague promise of reward. Allah does not say "make room and I will give you something nice." He says: *make room — and I will make room for you.* He answers your verb with His verb. He matches the act, mirror to mirror. You widen the world by a few inches for someone; **He widens existence for you.**

Here's what's remarkable. Look at what Allah *leaves out*. You made room in a specific place — *fī al-majālis*, in the assemblies, the sitting-places. When Allah promises to make room for you, He says simply: *yafsaḥillāhu lakum* — Allah will make room *for you*.

For you — where?

He doesn't say.

The object is missing. The location is unspecified. And in Arabic, when a phrase is left open like that, it does not mean *less* — it means *unbounded*. You gave a defined, bordered, measurable thing: six inches on a bench. He responds with an undefined, borderless, immeasurable thing: room — everywhere, in everything, with no edge named.

The commentators reach for what this openness might mean. Al-Jalālayn says: room *in Paradise*. Al-Muyassar widens it further: Allah will make room for you *in this world and the next*. And these aren't competing answers — they're camera angles on a single reality. He left it open precisely so that it could not be confined to one room. Room in your grave. Room in your chest. Room in your provision. Room in your hour of dying. Room on the Day when the earth itself will be too tight for the guilty. **You gave inches. He gives a spaciousness with no walls.**

What this one word reveals: *the smallness you accept is repaid in a vastness you cannot measure.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Unshuzū* — the harder command

Now Allah raises the difficulty. And He does it with a deliberately harder word.

The first command was gentle: slide over, *spread out a little*. But the second is sharper:

> **وَإِذَا قِيلَ ٱنشُزُوا فَٱنشُزُوا**
> *wa-idhā qīla-nshuzū fa-nshuzū* — "and when it is said, 'Rise up,' then rise up."

The root is **n-sh-z (نشز)**. Feel the sound of it first — *nashaza*. It is not soft. It has that sharp *shīn* in the middle and the buzzing *zāy* at the end. It catches in the mouth. And the meaning matches the sound: this root describes *ground that rises up* — a raised hummock, a swell of earth that stands higher than its surroundings. *Nashaz* is elevated terrain. To do *nushūz* is to *rise, to get up, to lift yourself out of your place.*

Notice the escalation. The first command asked you to *compress* — to make yourself a little smaller so another fits beside you. You stay seated; you just narrow. But this command asks for something more: *get up entirely. Stand. Leave the spot.*

Whether it means "rise up to make full room for someone," or "rise to go and do good — to prayer, to battle, to need" — and the commentators include both, al-Muyassar reading it as rising "for some matter in which there is good for you" — the demand on the self is the same. You are being asked to *vacate*. To surrender not an inch but the whole place. To stand up and let it go.

[PAUSE]

And here is the structural beauty. Allah builds the two commands as twin pillars, identical in shape:

> *When it is said to you, "make room"* → so make room → **Allah makes room for you.**
> *When it is said, "rise up"* → so rise up → **Allah raises you.**

Two conditions. Two human actions. Two divine responses. And in each case, the divine response is *built from the same kind of verb as the demand.* You make room — He makes room. You rise — **He raises.** The second response uses *yarfaʿ* — He lifts, He elevates — which is the verbal twin of the rising you just did. You lifted yourself up out of a seat for the sake of another; He lifts you up in ranks for the sake of eternity.

The human being rises a few feet off a cushion. Allah raises him through *darajāt* — degrees, levels, stations — that climb toward Him.

You see the logic now: **every act of self-lowering in this ayah is met by an act of divine raising. The verbs on your side go down; the verbs on His side go up. And they are tied together by the same roots, so you cannot miss that one causes the other.**

What this word reveals: *the willingness to lose your place is the exact mechanism by which Allah grants you a higher one.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Ūtū al-ʿilm* — knowledge that was *given*

Now we arrive at the most quietly stunning word in the entire ayah. And to see it, we have to read what Allah does *and* what He pointedly does not do.

> **يَرْفَعِ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ مِنكُمْ وَٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُواْ ٱلْعِلْمَ دَرَجَٰتٍ**
> *yarfaʿillāhu alladhīna āmanū minkum wa-alladhīna ūtū al-ʿilma darajāt*
> "Allah will raise those who believe among you, and those who were given knowledge, by degrees."

Two groups are lifted: those who *believe*, and those who *were given knowledge*. And faith and knowledge are bound together here — the elevation belongs to the believer whose faith is joined to knowledge. That alone is worth a lifetime of reflection: of all the things Allah could have named as the engine of being raised in rank — wealth, lineage, strength, age, eloquence — He names *faith* and *knowledge*.

But look closer. Look at the verb attached to knowledge.

He does not say *alladhīna ʿalimū* — "those who knew," "those who acquired knowledge," "those who studied hard." He says **ūtū al-ʿilm** — "those who *were given* knowledge."

It is in the **passive voice.** And it comes from the root *a-t-y* in a form that means *to give, to hand over, to deliver*. The grammar is unmistakable: **the knowledge was placed into their hands.** They are the receivers, not the producers. Knowledge came *to* them; they did not generate it.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this, because it overturns something.

The single most common disease that attaches itself to knowledge is *pride*. The more a person knows, the more they are tempted to feel they have *built* something, that their understanding is a monument to their own intellect, that they have *earned* their height and may now look down. And Allah, in the very breath where He *elevates* the people of knowledge, slips in one passive verb that dismantles the entire foundation of that pride.

*You were given it.*

The mind that received the knowledge — who made that mind? The capacity to understand — where did it come from? The teacher who taught you, the book that reached you, the moment of clarity when a hard thing suddenly became simple — was any of that *yours*? Allah pulls the verb into the passive to say: even at the summit of human elevation, even among *those whom I am raising by degrees*, the thing that elevates them is a gift I placed in their hands, not a wage they extracted from the universe.

So the very people most at risk of arrogance are handed, inside the same sentence, the cure for it. **You are raised — by something you were given.** Knowledge humbles in the same instant that it elevates. The higher you go, the more clearly you should see whose hands lifted you.

And notice the last word — *darajāt*. Degrees. It is left **indefinite and plural** — not "two degrees," not "the degrees," but simply *darajāt*, an open, uncounted plural. In Arabic that indefiniteness signals magnitude beyond reckoning. Al-Muyassar reads it as *"many degrees"* in reward and stations of pleasure. How many? Unnumbered. Just as He left *"make room for you"* without a place, He leaves the raising without a ceiling. **The descent you accept is finite — one seat, one moment. The ascent He grants is innumerable.**

What this word reveals: *the highest thing a human can be lifted by is the one thing he must admit he did not earn.*

### The bridge

Look at what the language alone has shown us. A root that loops your action back as Allah's action — *fasḥ* for *fasḥ*. A pair of twin commands where every lowering of the self is answered by a raising from God. And a single passive verb that humbles the proud at the exact peak of their elevation.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way reality itself is built.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The economy of scarcity is a lie

Let me start where you actually live.

Walk into any crowded place — a packed train, a full meeting room, a busy parking lot — and watch what happens inside your chest. There is a low, automatic calculation running, beneath your awareness: *space is limited. If I give some up, I have less. If someone moves in, I lose out.* This is the scarcity reflex, and it is ancient. It is wired into us from a world where the seat by the fire, the spot near the chief, the place at the table really did mean survival. Less room for you literally meant less life for you.

We carry that reflex everywhere now, long after it stopped being about survival. We carry it into status. We carry it into attention. We feel that if a colleague is praised, there is somehow less praise left for me. If someone else is honored, my honor shrinks. If I yield my place, I will be left behind. The whole modern anxiety machine runs on this one assumption: **life is a fixed pie, and every slice you give away is a slice you'll never see again.**

Now hear the ayah again against that reflex.

*Make room — Allah will make room for you.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the violence it does to the scarcity logic? The ayah is not asking you to make a noble sacrifice and accept the loss. It is telling you the loss *is not real.* The room you give away does not leave the world — it loops back to you, multiplied, from a Source that cannot run out. You thought you were operating inside a closed economy where giving means having less. Allah informs you that you are actually standing inside an *open* economy, where the One who owns all the space has tied His giving to your giving.

This is *istidlāl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah using the thing you can physically feel, the crowded bench, to point you to a thing you cannot see: that the entire structure of provision in your life does not behave the way scarcity tells you it behaves. The believer who has internalized this verse moves through the world differently. He is the one who lets the other car merge. He is the one who gives up the better seat. He is the one who introduces a rival to the opportunity he wanted for himself. Not because he is naive about loss — but because he has been let in on a secret: **in Allah's economy, what you release toward others is the very channel through which He releases toward you.**

And it reaches far past benches. It reaches the thing you are most afraid to give up. The credit. The recognition. The last word in the argument. The grudge you are entitled to keep. Every one of those is a "seat" you are clutching. And the ayah whispers the same equation over every one of them: *loosen your grip, make room, and watch the One with no scarcity make room for you in places you didn't know were tight.*

What this changes about tomorrow: the next time you feel the grip tighten — the reflex to keep, to hold, to not let them in — treat it as a doorway, not a threat. Make the small room. Let the person in. And do it specifically *as a transaction with Allah* — not "I'm being nice," but "I am making room because the One who owns all room has told me what He does in return."

### Theme Two: The two ladders — and why only one is steep

Now look at *who* gets raised, and let it land on something tender in the modern soul.

We are obsessed with rising. The entire architecture of contemporary life is a set of ladders — career ladders, follower counts, net worth, the curated highlight reel that says *I have ascended, look how high I am.* And the exhausting thing about every one of those ladders is that the rising is *visible, comparative, and contested.* You go up by climbing over. Your degree is measured against someone else's. Their rise is your fall. It is the scarcity reflex again, dressed as ambition.

Allah names two things that lift a person: *faith* and *knowledge*. And He attaches the open-ended *darajāt* — degrees without number — to them. But look at *how* this rising works in the ayah, because it is the opposite of every worldly ladder.

On the worldly ladder, you rise by *taking* a higher place. In this ayah, you rise by *giving up* your place — *unshuzū, fa-nshuzū*, get up, vacate. The mechanism of ascent is descent. You don't climb over the person who needs the seat. You *stand up for them* — and **Allah** does the lifting. *Yarfaʿillāh* — it is His verb, not yours. On the worldly ladder you must elevate yourself, and the exhaustion of self-elevation never ends. On Allah's ladder, you simply lower yourself in the right way, and the raising is done *to* you, by Him, in degrees you cannot count and cannot lose.

[PAUSE]

And then the passive verb returns to finish the lesson. *Those who were given knowledge.* Even the ladder of knowledge — the one ascent we are most tempted to brag about — is built from something you were *handed.* So there is no room on this ladder for the thing that poisons every worldly ladder: the looking-down. You cannot look down from a height you were *given* without the absurdity of it striking you. The man raised by a gift, sneering at the man below — it collapses under its own contradiction.

This is the second great lie the ayah dismantles. The first lie was *scarcity* — that giving means losing. The second lie is *self-made height* — that your elevation is your achievement and your possession. Faith reminds you the height is granted. Knowledge, *ūtū*, reminds you it was placed in your hands. So the higher the believer climbs on Allah's ladder, the *lower* his head bows — because every degree up is one more piece of evidence of how much he was given.

And then the seal: **wa-llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīr** — "And Allah is All-Aware of what you do." The root *kh-b-r* is not surface-knowledge; it is knowledge of the *inner reality* of a thing. Why does this seal the ayah? Because the whole verse is about acts that *look* small and invisible. Sliding over on a bench. Standing up quietly so a tired man can sit. No one records these. No one applauds. The worldly ladder ignores them entirely. And Allah closes by saying: *I see the inner reality of every one of these unnoticed acts.* The seat you gave up that no one thanked you for — *Khabīr.* He knew. He was lifting you the whole time.

What this changes about tomorrow: stop measuring your rise against anyone. The two ladders that actually carry weight — faith and knowledge — are climbed by *yielding* and held by *humility*, and the climbing is done by Allah, in private, where *Khabīr* sees what the crowd misses. Seek a degree no one can take from you, because no one but Allah handed it to you in the first place.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape, because the ayah was built with a symmetry you can only appreciate now that we've walked through it.

Two commands, two responses, sealed by one divine attribute:

- **"Make room"** *(tafassaḥū)* → you make room *(fa-fsaḥū)* → **Allah makes room for you** *(yafsaḥillāhu lakum)* — room without a named place, so room without limit.
- **"Rise up"** *(unshuzū)* → you rise *(fa-nshuzū)* → **Allah raises you** *(yarfaʿillāh)* — by degrees without a named number, so degrees without ceiling.
- **Seal:** *And Allah is All-Aware of what you do* — He sees the inner reality of every small, unwitnessed act.

The structure *is* the argument. On the left side, every verb is a lowering — compress, vacate, yield. On the right side, every verb is a raising — He widens, He lifts. And the same roots bridge the two sides so you cannot pretend the connection is loose. **Your descent is literally the cause of His elevation of you.** The ayah doesn't just say it. It is *shaped* like it.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery approached from two sides.

The language showed you a verb that loops your action back as Allah's action, a structure where lowering is answered by raising, and a passive verb that humbles at the height of elevation. The themes showed you that scarcity is a lie and self-made height is a lie. **These are one truth:** in the kingdom of Allah, the human being rises by descending, is enlarged by making himself small, and is most honored precisely where he admits he was given everything. The crowded room was never about the room. It was Allah teaching you the physics of the unseen world through six inches of space on a bench.

### Questions to carry

1. Where in your life are you clutching a "seat" — a credit, a recognition, a place — that Allah may be asking you to make room around?
2. When you give something up for another, do you experience it as a *loss*, or as a *transaction with the One who has no scarcity? What would change if you truly believed the second?*
3. The knowledge, the faith, the capacity you are proud of — can you name the hands that *gave* it to you?
4. If Allah is *Khabīr* — aware of the inner reality of your smallest unwitnessed acts — which act of quiet yielding would you do today if you fully believed He was watching it lift you?
5. Which ladder are you actually climbing — the visible one you must defend, or the hidden one He raises you on while you bow?

### One-sentence distillation

When you make yourself smaller to give another room, Allah enlarges you in a vastness without walls — and the only height that truly lifts a human being is built from faith and knowledge he must admit he was *given*.

### The closing invitation

You will be in a crowded space before this day is over — physically, or in the crowded space of your own ambition, where it feels like there isn't enough honor to go around. When that tightness comes, I invite you to remember that you have been let in on the secret of how the unseen economy actually runs. Make the room. Stand up. Yield the place. Not as a loss you bravely absorb — but as a door you knowingly open, through which the One who owns all space and all rank does His own work. Let the small descent be deliberate. Then leave the raising to Him. He is, after all, *Khabīr.*

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You who make room without limit and raise without ceiling — teach our hearts that to yield is not to lose. Loosen our grip on the seats we clutch, and make us of those who make room, so that You make room for us in our graves, in our chests, and on the Day the earth grows tight. And what knowledge and faith You give us, let it bow our heads even as it lifts our rank — for we were only ever *given*. Āmīn.
