# The Word That Could Not Remake the World

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read these words to you first, and then I want us to walk all the way back to the moment they answered.

> **ٱلَّذِينَ يُظَـٰهِرُونَ مِنكُم مِّن نِّسَآئِهِم مَّا هُنَّ أُمَّهَـٰتِهِمْ**
>
> *Alladhīna yuẓāhirūna minkum min nisā'ihim mā hunna ummahātihim*
>
> "Those among you who pronounce *zihar* against their wives — they are not their mothers."

To feel what this verse is doing, you have to understand the cruelty it walked into.

### Rebuilding the world

In the Arabia before Islam, there was a thing a man could say to his wife — five words, more or less — that would ruin her life without releasing her from it. He would say: *anti ʿalayya ka-ẓahri ummī* — "You are to me like the back of my mother."

Think about what that sentence was engineered to do. By comparing his wife to his mother's back, the man declared her permanently forbidden to him — as unlawful as his own mother. But here is the cruelty: he did not *divorce* her. He simply suspended her. She was now forbidden to him, so he would never touch her again. But she was not free, so she could never leave, never remarry, never build another life. She was locked in a doorway — not inside the marriage, not outside it. A wife who was not a wife. A free woman who was not free. The early authorities preserve exactly this: relaying from Abū Qilāba, classical commentators note that in the Jahiliyya *zihar was a kind of divorce* — and yet a divorce stripped of every mercy that divorce is supposed to carry. No clean release. No waiting period that ends in freedom. No remarriage. Just the severance, with the door bolted from both sides.

And it took five words. The husband spoke, and a woman's entire future closed like a door.

Now meet the woman this surah is named after. Her name was **Khawla bint Thaʿlaba** — the sources preserve her name with slight variations, but her voice they preserve exactly. Her husband, Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit, an older man, said these words to her in a moment of anger. And then he regretted it. But the custom had no exit. There was no undo.

So Khawla did something almost no one in her world would have thought to do. She went to the Prophet ﷺ — and she *argued*. She pleaded. She disputed. She said, in effect: *He has eaten my youth, I have borne his children, and now that I am old he discards me like this — is there nothing?* And the Prophet ﷺ, who had no revelation on the matter yet, kept telling her gently: *I have not been commanded anything about your case.* And she kept turning back to him, and then she turned upward — she raised her complaint to Allah Himself.

[PAUSE]

And the surah opens — the verse right before ours:

> **قَدْ سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ قَوْلَ ٱلَّتِى تُجَـٰدِلُكَ فِى زَوْجِهَا**
>
> "Allah has certainly heard the speech of the woman who disputes with you concerning her husband."

The Lord of the worlds halts the cosmos to record that He *heard a poor woman arguing about her marriage.* ʿA'isha later said: "Blessed is the One whose hearing encompasses all things. I could hear some of what Khawla was saying, and some I could not — and Allah heard all of it from above the seven heavens."

That is the room we are standing in. A woman the world would not hear. A formula designed to silence her. And then — Allah speaks.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to hold as we go. The husband believed his words had the power to *remake reality* — to turn a wife into a mother, to dissolve a marriage with a sentence. And Allah's response is going to do something extraordinary. He is not merely going to overrule the custom. He is going to expose something about the relationship between *what we say* and *what is real* — something that will, at the same time, humble every one of us and set us free.

And then — in the very same breath that He condemns these words as a lie and an outrage — He is going to say something that should not, by any human logic, come next.

Let's find out what Allah placed in here.

## PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yuẓāhirūn* — the back, and the word that keeps repeating

Let me teach you something small about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the shape of the verb is carrying half its meaning.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — ten main "forms," and each form bends the basic meaning in a particular direction. The first form is the plain action. But there is a form — grammarians call it **Form III** — that takes the action and *aims it at someone*. It is the form of doing-something-toward-another, of engaging a second party in the act. When the plain verb *qatala* means "he killed," the Form III *qātala* means "he fought *against* someone" — the action now has a target, a counterpart, a person on the receiving end.

Now hold that, and look at the word Allah chose:

> **يُظَـٰهِرُونَ** — *yuẓāhirūn*

It comes from the root **ẓ-h-r** (ظهر), and that root means *the back* — the literal back of a body. The word is built on exactly that Form III pattern. And here is the first thing to notice: the verb is not "they recite a formula" or "they make a declaration." Grammatically, it is an act *aimed at* someone — *min nisā'ihim*, "**against** their wives." The man does not simply utter words into the air. He does something *to her*. He takes the image of his mother's back and he lays it over his living wife like a shroud.

(The early grammarians noticed something here worth a breath. The reciters preserved this verb in more than one shape — the reading we recite, *yuẓāhirūn*, is the Form III "aiming-at-another" that we have been tracing, while another reading goes back to *yataẓahharūn*, a reflexive form in which the man *brings the thing down on himself* — he is the one who makes himself say it, who implicates his own tongue. Whichever way the tongue shapes it, the verb describes the same ruinous pronouncement. I point this out only because it is a small window onto how carefully this one word has been handled for fourteen centuries.)

Sit with the choice of *the back*. Of all the things he could have invoked, the custom reached for the **ẓahr** — the back. Why the back?

The back is what you see when someone has turned away from you. The back is the opposite of the face — and the face is where intimacy lives, where recognition lives, where you are *seen*. To make your wife "like a back" is to turn her into the part of a person you face when love has ended. He is not even looking at her anymore. He has reduced a whole human being — a wife, the mother of his children — to a turned shoulder, a thing you walk away from.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the second thing, and this is where the grammar opens up something I want you to feel. Notice the *tense* of this verb. *Yuẓāhirūn* is in the **imperfect** — the tense of the ongoing, the repeated, the unfinished. And a few words later, when Allah describes their speech again, He says **لَيَقُولُونَ** — *la-yaqūlūn*, "they surely keep saying" — again the imperfect, again the tense of the thing that goes round and round.

Stay with me, because this is the design. The man's words are in the tense of *churning*. Round and round, repeated, ongoing — and, as we are about to see, completely empty. He can say it a thousand times. He can say it in the imperfect forever. The repetition adds nothing to its reality, because **saying a thing more often does not make it more true.**

Hold that thought about tense. We are going to return to it in a moment, and when we do, it is going to break something open.

What this one word reveals: the husband's *zihar* is not a neutral pronouncement. It is an act of contempt aimed at a person, reducing her to a turned back — and it lives in the grammar of empty repetition.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Mā hunna... illā allā'ī waladnahum* — reality answers the word

Watch how Allah responds. He does not argue. He does not negotiate with the custom. He states a fact — twice, from two directions — and then He anchors it in something no word can touch.

First the negation:

> **مَّا هُنَّ أُمَّهَـٰتِهِمْ** — *mā hunna ummahātihim* — "They are **not** their mothers."

Flat. Total. The particle *mā* here is the *mā* of absolute negation. Not "they are not quite their mothers," not "they shouldn't be treated as mothers." They are *not* their mothers. The thing you said is simply false.

Then — and this is a feature of Arabic eloquence — Allah comes at the same truth from the opposite side, using a structure called **restriction**: a negation followed by an exception, which together fence a truth in on all sides.

> **إِنْ أُمَّهَـٰتُهُمْ إِلَّا ٱلَّـٰٓـِٔى وَلَدْنَهُمْ**
>
> *In ummahātuhum illā allā'ī waladnahum*
>
> "Their mothers are **none but** those who gave them birth."

Here *in* is another negative ("their mothers are not...") and *illā* is the exception ("...except"). Put them together and you get a sealed statement: nothing qualifies as "mother" *except* this one thing. The structure doesn't just say what a mother is. It slams every other door. There is no other category. There is no second route to motherhood.

And now — *now* — comes the word I promised we would return to. What is the one thing that makes a woman your mother? Allah says: **ٱلَّـٰٓـِٔى وَلَدْنَهُمْ** — "those who **gave them birth.**"

> **وَلَدْنَهُمْ** — *waladnahum*

Look at the tense.

After all those imperfect verbs — *yuẓāhirūn*, *la-yaqūlūn*, the churning, repeating, empty speech — Allah reaches for the **perfect** tense. *Waladnahum.* "They gave birth to them." Done. Completed. Settled in the past and unchangeable forever.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the architecture now?

The man's claim lives in the **imperfect** — ongoing, repeated, never quite landing, because it has nothing real to land on. He can keep saying it. It stays unfinished because it is unfinishable; words alone cannot complete the act they pretend to do.

But motherhood lives in the **perfect** — a single, accomplished, biological fact. *She gave birth to you.* That happened. It is sealed. No sentence can create it where it didn't happen, and no sentence can erase it where it did.

This is the heart of it. The grammar itself is staging the collision: **the imperfect verb of human speech** crashing against **the perfect verb of created reality** — and reality does not move. Your wife is your wife because of a real bond. Your mother is your mother because she carried you and bore you — a thing that *happened*, in the perfect tense, beyond the reach of any tongue. You cannot speak your wife into your mother. You cannot speak a fact into being, and you cannot speak a fact out of being.

What these words reveal: motherhood is not a status you assign with your mouth; it is a reality Allah authored in the body, settled and finished. And the moment you understand that, the entire formula of zihar collapses into what it always was — *noise.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Munkaran... wa zūran* — then "Ever-Pardoning"

Allah now names the speech. And He names it on two separate axes — two charges, not one.

> **وَإِنَّهُمْ لَيَقُولُونَ مُنكَرًۭا مِّنَ ٱلْقَوْلِ وَزُورًۭا**
>
> "And indeed they are uttering something **rejected** — an objectionable word — **and a falsehood.**"

The first word: **مُنكَرًا** — *munkaran*. From the root *n-k-r*, and built as a passive form meaning "that which is **disowned, rejected, refused.**" It is the exact opposite of *maʿrūf* — the "known," the recognized-as-good. *Munkar* is what the sound heart pushes away the moment it sees it. So the first charge is *moral*: this speech is something creation itself recoils from.

The second word: **زُورًا** — *zūran*. From a root that carries the sense of *leaning, deviating, twisting away from straight.* *Zūr* is falsehood — but specifically falsehood as a *bending* of what is true, a thing tilted off its real axis. So the second charge is *factual*: this speech is not just ugly, it is *false* — it bends reality, claims a thing that is not so.

Feel the precision. Allah convicts the *zihar* on both counts at once. It is **munkar** — morally rejected. And it is **zūr** — factually crooked. It fails the heart and it fails the truth, simultaneously. There is no angle from which the words survive.

[PAUSE]

And now — having pronounced this speech a moral outrage *and* a lie — what should come next?

By every human reflex, what comes next is the verdict. The punishment. The closing of the door.

Listen to what Allah says instead:

> **وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌۭ**
>
> "Yet indeed Allah is **Ever-Pardoning, Ever-Forgiving.**"

Read absence here. Where is the threat? Where is the "and Allah is severe in punishment" that we might brace for? It is *withheld.* In its place, two names of mercy — and they are not the same mercy twice. Look closely.

**عَفُوّ** — *ʿAfuww*, from a root that means *to erase, to efface, to wipe away until no trace remains.* The Arabs used it for the wind that erases footprints in the sand until you cannot tell anyone walked there. *ʿAfw* is the mercy that **deletes the record** — as if the sin was never written.

**غَفُور** — *Ghafūr*, from a root that means *to cover, to shield, to veil over.* The *mighfar* is the helmet that covers the head in battle. *Maghfira* is the mercy that **covers you** — shields you from the consequence, drapes the sin so it cannot harm you.

So one name *erases* the trace, and the other name *covers* you from the fallout. Allah does not simply offer leniency. He offers two distinct gifts at once: the act wiped clean, *and* the actor shielded.

[PAUSE]

And He offers this in the very same verse where He called their words *munkar* and *zūr.* Sit with that placement, because it is not an accident — it is the whole posture of your Lord toward you. He will name your wrong with total honesty. He will not pretend the *zihar* was fine, will not soften "lie" into "misunderstanding." And in the same breath, before you have even asked, He tells you the door is open. **The naming of the sin and the offer of mercy arrive together, in one verse, in one breath.**

And the classical commentators are careful to name the hinge on which the erasing turns: this *ʿafw* and *maghfira*, al-Muyassar explains, meet the one who lets some lapse slip from him *and then catches it with sincere repentance* — *tawba naṣūḥ.* The door is open, but it is a door you walk back through. The mercy is not indifference to the wrong; it is the reward held out to the one who turns.

What this reveals: with Allah, exposure is not the end of the road — it is often the beginning of the return. He shows you exactly what you did, precisely so you know exactly what He is willing to erase.

### Bridge

Look at what the construction has shown us. A verb that aims contempt at a wife and reduces her to a turned back. A grammar that pits the churning imperfect of human speech against the settled perfect of created reality — and reality wins. Two charges, moral and factual, that leave the lie no exit. And then, fused to the condemnation, two mercies that erase and cover.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about the kind of God who heard a woman arguing in the dark.

## PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Your words are powerful enough to be sin — and not powerful enough to remake the world

Let me start where you already live.

There is a quiet belief most of us carry without ever examining it: that *saying* something makes it so. We declare things over ourselves and over the people we love, and somewhere underneath we treat the declaration as if it were a creation.

A parent, in a moment of fury, says to a child: *"You're worthless. You'll never amount to anything."* And the words land like a verdict — as if the parent had the authority to *define* the child's worth into existence.

A man, in the wreckage of a fight, says: *"You're dead to me."* As if a sentence could perform a funeral.

We say: *"I'm broken." "I'm unlovable." "We're finished."* And we live inside those sentences as though we had spoken reality into a new shape.

This is exactly the spiritual disease underneath *zihar.* The husband believed his five words had *world-making power* — that he could speak a wife into a mother, speak intimacy into prohibition, redraw the map of what is real with his tongue. He treated his speech as if it were *kun fa-yakūn* — "Be, and it is." As if creation answered to *him.*

[PAUSE]

And here is Allah's response, and notice that it cuts in two directions at once.

**First, He takes our words seriously — more seriously than we do.** He does not say "they were just words, no harm done." He calls them *munkar* and *zūr.* The man will owe an expiation — freeing a slave, or fasting two months, or feeding sixty — for something he merely *said.* So this is not a faith that shrugs at speech. Your tongue can incur a debt before Allah. Your words have *weight.*

**But, second — and this is the liberation — your words do not have the weight you fear they have.** They are heavy enough to be sin. They are *not* heavy enough to remake what Allah authored. *Mā hunna ummahātihim* — "they are not their mothers." Whatever you said, *she is still your wife.* The reality did not bend. The man's contempt was real as a wrong, but powerless as a *fact.* He could not actually transform her. He only managed to harm her — and to indict himself.

Do you feel how both edges of this serve you?

To the one who has *wounded* with words: take it seriously. It was real. It incurred something. Go and make it right — there is an expiation, there is a path. You are not let off the hook.

But to the one who has *been* wounded — the child told they were worthless, the spouse told they were dead, the person who absorbed someone else's cruel verdict and started living inside it — hear this: **that sentence did not have the authority it claimed.** No human tongue can speak your reality into a new shape. They called you worthless; you are not worthless because someone *said* it, any more than Khawla became a "mother" because a man *said* it. Their words were heavy enough to be *their* sin. They were never heavy enough to remake *you.* The only One whose word *creates* reality is the One who said *kun.* And He has not said over you what they said over you.

[PAUSE]

What this changes tomorrow: there are sentences you have been living inside — verdicts handed to you by people who had no authority to issue them, and verdicts you handed yourself in your worst hours. Name one of them. And then say, with the certainty this verse gives you: *That was munkar. That was zūr. Heavy enough to be a wound. Never heavy enough to be true.* And there are words on your own tongue, aimed at people you love — be careful with them. Not because they can remake reality. Because they can wound it, and you will owe something for the wound.

### Theme Two: The God who bends down to the unheard

Now let me take you back to Khawla in the doorway of the Prophet's ﷺ home, arguing, weeping, refusing to be dismissed.

Think about how power normally flows in this world. The voices that get answered are loud, or rich, or male, or connected, or many. The voice of an aging woman with no standing, complaining about her private marriage, is precisely the kind of voice the world is *built to ignore.* It is background noise. It does not move institutions. It does not bend custom. It is, by every earthly measure, *the least likely speech to be heard.*

And the surah opens: **قَدْ سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ** — "Allah has *certainly* heard."

[PAUSE]

Stay with the scandal of that, because we have heard it too many times to feel it. The Sovereign of all existence, the One *to whom* the seven heavens make tasbīḥ, the One before whom kings are nothing — *that* One leans down into a small house in Madinah, and the speech He chooses to enshrine in eternal scripture is *the argument of a woman about her husband.*

He could have ignored her. By every law of how power works, He *should* have, if power worked the way we think it does. Instead He overturns an entire social custom — a custom centuries old, woven into the fabric of Arabian marriage — *for her.* He does not send her a private comfort. He legislates. He changes the law of the land because one unheard woman raised her voice to Him.

And notice *how* He changes it — because this is where His justice and His mercy fuse. He does not simply abolish *zihar* and leave the couple stranded. Look at what verses 3 and 4 build:

> "...they must free a slave **before the two may touch one another**... whoever cannot find one, then **a fast of two consecutive months**... whoever is unable, then **the feeding of sixty needy persons.**"

Before we climb the staircase, notice precisely *when* it is owed. The verse does not say "those who pronounce zihar must free a slave." It says: those who pronounce it *thumma yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū* — "**and then go back on what they said.**" The classical scholars differed over exactly what that "going back" means — al-Tabari records the *ikhtilaf* openly — but the meaning that the commentators settle on is striking: the expiation is triggered not by the ugly words alone, but by the man's *decision to reverse them* — to hold on to the wife he tried to forbid, to undo the prohibition and return to the marriage. In other words, the kaffāra is the price of *coming back.* The whole legal apparatus switches on at the exact moment the husband turns back toward her. The law is built around the return.

This is a *staircase.* Allah does not impose one impossible standard and let people fall off the edge of it. He builds a descending ladder of mercy: *if you can, free a human being from bondage* — and the commentators specify a *believing* soul, a *raqaba mu'mina*, so that the act of liberation restores a life to faith and not merely to the open air; *if you cannot, fast two unbroken months; if you cannot do that, feed sixty of the poor* — and even here the law stays concrete and humane, the classical authorities measuring it out as a *mudd* of the staple food of the land for each needy mouth. Each rung reaches the person the rung above could not. **No one is left without a path home.** The law is hard enough to teach the tongue to fear, and merciful enough that everyone — rich or poor, strong or weak — can actually walk it.

And do not miss the strange little word that explains *why* the ladder descends. After laying out the easing — slave, then fast, then food — Allah says *dhālika li-tu'minū bi-llāhi wa rasūlih*, "**that** is so you may believe in Allah and His Messenger." The classical commentators ask: *that* — what? And al-Jalalayn answers plainly: *dhālika* points back to **the lightening itself** — the very fact that the burden steps down to meet you is meant to *deepen your faith.* Mercy is not a concession wrung out of the law against its will. The graduated ease *is* the teaching. God lightens the load precisely so that, feeling how gently He carries you, you would believe in Him more.

And look *where* the staircase leads. Twice Allah says the expiation comes *min qabli an yatamāssā* — "before the two may touch one another." The whole apparatus is engineered not to *end* the marriage but to *restore* it. The man who tried to wall his wife off forever is given a road back *to* her — through repair. He wronged a relationship; the kaffāra mends the relationship. And there is a quiet poetry in the first rung: the man who tried to *imprison* a woman in a non-marriage atones by *freeing* a captive — *taḥrīr raqabah*, the liberation of a neck. The one who bound, unbinds.

[PAUSE]

So here is the God these verses reveal. Not a distant Legislator handing down rules from a height. A Lord who *hears the whisper of the powerless*, who *answers the unanswerable voice*, who *overturns the cruelty of the strong* — and who, even as He establishes His limits, builds them as a ladder so that no one falls.

What this changes tomorrow: there is a complaint you have stopped voicing because you decided no one is listening — a private grief, an injustice too small to matter to anyone, a thing you only ever say into your pillow. Khawla's surah exists to tell you that the One who matters most has *already* heard it. *Qad samiʿa.* He heard it before you finished the sentence. So raise it to Him — not because He needs the information, but because you need to remember who is listening. And when *you* hold a little power over someone with no power over you — an employee, a child, someone who depends on you — remember that the Lord who bent down for Khawla is listening to *their* whisper too.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now stand back and see how these three verses are built, because the shape is the proof.

- **Verse 2** is the *lie and the mercy.* It names the crime (*munkar*, *zūr*), demolishes it with reality (*mā hunna ummahātihim... illā allā'ī waladnahum*), and — in the same breath — opens the door (*ʿafuww, ghafūr*).
- **Verse 3** is the *first step home.* Free a neck, before you touch. And: "Allah is fully aware of what you do" — the kaffāra is not a loophole; He sees whether your return is sincere.
- **Verse 4** is the *ladder.* Can't free a slave? Fast. Can't fast? Feed. And then: "These are the limits of Allah" — *tilka ḥudūd Allāh.*

Watch the movement of the whole passage: it begins with a *destructive word* and ends with a *restored marriage.* It takes the most ruinous sentence a man could speak and builds, rung by rung, a staircase that leads him back to the wife he tried to cast off. The arc bends from *zūr* — crooked falsehood — to *ḥudūd Allāh* — the straight, firm limits of God. From a word that bent reality to limits that hold reality straight.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discovery and the thematic discovery are the same insight, seen from two sides.

The *grammar* told us: human speech is the imperfect tense — churning, repeating, never landing — while created reality is the perfect tense, settled and done. The man's word could not finish the act it pretended to perform.

The *themes* told us: your words are heavy enough to wound and to incur sin, but never heavy enough to remake what Allah authored — and the God who authored it is the same God who bends down to hear the wound and build the road back.

One truth: **Only one Speaker's word creates reality. When He says *kun*, it *is.* When we speak, we can bless or we can wound — but we cannot remake the world He made. And that same Speaker heard the smallest voice in Madinah, and answered her in words that have been recited for fourteen centuries.**

### Questions to carry

1. What sentence — spoken over you by someone with no authority to define you — have you been living inside as though it were true?
2. What words have you aimed at someone you love, treating your tongue as if it had the power of *kun*? Is there an expiation you owe?
3. Where in your life are you treating *saying* a thing as the same as *making* it so — about yourself, about your past, about someone you've written off?
4. What is the complaint you've stopped voicing because you decided no one is listening? What would change if you truly believed *qad samiʿa Allāh* — He has already heard?
5. When you hold even a little power over someone who has none over you, do you remember that their whisper reaches the same Throne that Khawla's did?

### One-sentence distillation

Your words are real enough to wound and to be judged, but they cannot remake the reality Allah authored — and the One who authored it is the same One who heard a discarded woman's whisper and rebuilt her dignity into law.

### The closing invitation

Let this surah change the weight you give to two things you may have had backwards. Give your *own words* more weight than you do — they can wound, and you will answer for them. And give *other people's words about you* far less power than you have let them have — no human sentence can remake what Allah made of you. And underneath both, let the certainty settle in: the Lord who stopped to hear Khawla is bent toward your unheard voice right now, and the door He keeps open is described in the same breath as the sin — *ʿafuww, ghafūr*: He erases the trace, and He covers you from the fall.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, You who heard the woman who disputed before she finished her plea — hear the words I cannot say to anyone but You. Guard my tongue from the* munkar *and the* zūr*, and where I have wounded with my words, open for me the road back, for You are* ʿAfuww*, who erases the trace, and* Ghafūr*, who covers what You have erased. Let me never mistake my speech for Your* kun*, and never forget that the smallest voice reaches You.* Ameen.
