# Made New, Not Mended

## Surah Al-Waqi'ah, Ayahs 35–38

> إِنَّآ أَنشَأْنَٰهُنَّ إِنشَآءً ۝ فَجَعَلْنَٰهُنَّ أَبْكَارًا ۝ عُرُبًا أَتْرَابًا ۝ لِّأَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْيَمِينِ

*Innā anshaʾnāhunna inshāʾā · fa-jaʿalnāhunna abkārā · ʿuruban atrābā · li-aṣḥābi l-yamīn*

**"Indeed, We brought them into being — a whole new bringing-into-being — and made them ever-fresh, lovingly devoted, all of one age, for the Companions of the Right."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Let me ask you to do something before we open a single word of this passage.

Picture an old woman.

Not a stranger. Picture someone you have actually known — a grandmother, a great-aunt, an elderly neighbor who is gone now. Picture the hands first, because the hands tell the truth fastest: the skin thinned to paper, the knuckles swollen, the veins risen up like roots near the surface of soil. Picture the slowness of her. Picture how she had to be helped up from a chair. Picture how, somewhere in a drawer, there was a photograph of her at nineteen — and how, when you looked at the photograph and then looked at her, you could *almost* find the same face, but not quite, because seventy years had moved through it like weather moves through stone.

Hold that person in your mind. Because there is a report — we will come back to how to weigh it — that an old woman once came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked him to pray that Allah admit her into the Garden. And the answer she received was not a simple yes. The answer turned her grief inside out. We will earn that answer slowly.

Now widen the picture. **Surah Al-Waqi'ah is a surah about a day when everything that seemed permanent comes undone.** It opens with the earth shaking with a shaking, the mountains crumbled to scattered dust, and humanity sorted — not into nations or races or generations — but into three groups. The Foremost. The Companions of the Right. And the Companions of the Left. The whole surah is a sorting.

And right in the middle of describing the second group — the great wide company of the saved, the **Companions of the Right** — the surah pauses on the people who will be *in* that garden with them. Not the fruit. Not the rivers. The companions. And about these companions Allah says something so structurally strange that the classical scholars stopped and pointed at it: He describes *them* before He has told us who *they* are. A pronoun arrives with no name in front of it. "We originated **them**." Them who?

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Hold onto it for the next hour.

The deniers in this very surah will be challenged with a single argument for resurrection — the argument of the *first* creation. "You already know," Allah will say a few verses later, "the **first** origination — so why will you not remember?" The word He uses there is from the exact same root as the word at the heart of our passage today.

So these four small verses about the companions of the Garden are not a side-note about Paradise. **They are a rehearsal of resurrection itself.** They are Allah showing us, in miniature, what He means when He says He will bring the dead back. And what He means is going to overturn everything you assumed the word "back" could mean.

Let's go slowly. Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: أَنشَأْنَا — The Verb That Does Not Mean "Repair"

Before we touch the word, let me teach you a distinction that your whole life runs on, even though you have probably never named it.

There are two ways to deal with something that has broken down.

The first is **repair**. You take the broken thing and you work *on* it. You keep the original object. You replace the cracked part, sand the rough edge, glue the split seam. Repair is conservative — it preserves the thing and labors against its decay. But repair always carries the memory of the damage. The mended bowl shows its crack-line. The rebuilt knee aches before rain. Repair is always *the old thing, patched*.

The second is **origination**. You don't work on the old object at all. You bring a new thing into being from the ground up. Not the same object made better — a fresh act of creation.

Hold that distinction in one hand.

Now the word. Allah does not say "We *fixed* them." He does not say "We *returned* them." He says:

> إِنَّآ أَنشَأْنَٰهُنَّ
> *Innā anshaʾnāhunna* — "Indeed, **We originated them**."

The verb is **أَنشَأَ** *(anshaʾa)*, from the root **ن-ش-أ** *(nūn–shīn–hamza)*. And I want you to feel what lives in this root, because the lexicons are unanimous about it. This root is not a manufacturing word. It is a *growing* word. From this same root comes **النَّشْء** — the young, the rising generation, the ones coming up. From it comes **نَشَأَ** — for a child to grow up, for a cloud to rise and build on the horizon, for something to *come into being and develop*. The root means **to originate something and bring it forth into growth.**

Let's sit with that. When you originate by this root, you are not assembling a static object. You are starting a *living thing on its way*. A نَشْأة *(nashʾah)* is an upbringing, a coming-of-age, a whole arc of becoming.

Notice something. The corpus confirms this verb is **Form IV** — the *af'ala* pattern, أَفْعَلَ. Now, I want to be careful here, because it is easy to slap the label "causative" on every Form IV and move on. That is a habit to resist. Form IV does many things. But here its work is precise and beautiful: it takes the root's idea of *coming-into-being-and-growing* and makes Allah the One who **brings that whole becoming into existence.** Not "He caused them to grow" as if they were already there. **He authored the entire arc of their existence as a single deliberate act.** He is the One from whom the growing-thing rises.

This is why the translators reach for "produced," "brought into being," "originated" — and why none of them is quite enough. The closest English would be something like: *"We brought them up into a whole new existence."*

[PAUSE]

Here is what is remarkable, and here is where the root pays off everything.

Al-Qurtubi, commenting on this verse, lays out two readings that the early community held — and watch how the verb decides between nothing and reconciles everything. One reading — the one al-Jalalayn states flatly — is that *these are the ḥūr al-ʿīn,* **مِنْ غَيْرِ وِلادَة**, *brought into being without ever passing through birth into this world at all.* The other reading, which al-Baghawi attributes to Ibn ʿAbbās: *these are the women of this world — the very ones who grew old here* — **خَلَقْنَاهُنَّ خَلْقًا جَدِيدًا**, "We created them as a new creation," meaning, in Ibn ʿAbbās's words as al-Baghawi narrates them, *the aged women, the gray-haired ones — We brought them into being again, after old age, as another creation.*

Do you see why the *verb* makes both readings sing the same note? Whether she was created fresh in the Garden, or whether she is the grandmother whose paper-thin hands you held — **the verb is the same.** أَنشَأْنَا. Originated. Because for Allah there is no difference in difficulty between making something the first time and making it again. The woman who never lived and the woman who lived ninety years and was buried — both are, to Him, simply *anshaʾnā*. **Originated.** This is exactly the argument the surah will press on the deniers: the first origination and the second are, for the One who does them, the same word.

So this is not repair. The grandmother in the photograph is not patched back toward the photograph. She is **originated** — and the photograph, it turns out, was the lesser version all along.

One clear statement, and then we breathe: **This verb tells us that what Allah does to His people after death is not restoration of an old self, but the authoring of a new and living one.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: إِنشَاءً — The Word That Repeats Itself to Seal the Promise

Now look at what comes immediately after the verb:

> أَنشَأْنَٰهُنَّ **إِنشَآءً**

He says *anshaʾnāhunna* — "We originated them" — and then He adds **إِنشَآءً** *(inshāʾā)*, the noun from the very same root. Literally: "We originated them **an origination**."

In English this sounds redundant, almost clumsy. *We produced them a production.* But in Arabic this is one of the language's most powerful and deliberate constructions. The grammarians call it the **مَفْعُول مُطْلَق**, the "absolute object" — and what it does is take a verb and then drop the verb's own root-noun beside it like a hammer-blow on a nail already driven.

Let me teach the device with something from ordinary speech. In English, when we want to insist that we *truly, fully, no-half-measures* did a thing, we sometimes double it: "I thanked her — *really* thanked her." "He cried — he *wept*." Arabic has a clean, grammatical machine for exactly this. You say the verb, then you name the verb's own action again as a noun: "I love you a loving," "they were shaken a shaking." It is not redundancy. **It is the grammar of emphasis and completeness.** It says: *this action happened to its absolute full measure, leave no doubt, imagine no lesser version.*

So when Allah says *anshaʾnāhunna inshāʾā* — "We originated them, an origination" — He is not stuttering. He is **sealing**. He is saying: do not picture a partial making. Do not picture a touch-up, a freshening, a cosmetic improvement. Picture **a total, complete, root-and-branch bringing-into-being, to the absolute full of what that word can mean.**

Stay with this, because it answers a fear you may not have known you carried.

When we imagine being "made young again," some quiet part of us suspects a catch. The cosmetic surgery that leaves the eyes looking startled. The restored painting where you can still see where the restorer's brush met the master's. The reunion that is *almost* like old times but everyone can feel the strain. We have learned, in this world, that the second version is *always* a little less. The copy degrades. The sequel disappoints. **Entropy taxes everything, and the tax is always paid in authenticity.**

The مَفْعُول مُطْلَق exists in this verse to kill that suspicion. *Inshāʾā.* A complete origination. Al-Muyassar reads precisely this completeness into it, and notice he frames it as a *different kind* of making before he calls it complete: **نشأة غير النشأة التي كانت في الدنيا، نشأة كاملة لا تقبل الفناء** — "an origination *unlike the origination that was in this world* — a *complete* origination that does not admit of perishing." Not the worldly making run a second time. A making of another order entirely. Not a younger-looking old age. Not youth with an expiration sticker. A making so total that decay has no foothold in it anywhere.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I find almost unbearable in its tenderness. Allah did not *need* to add the second word. "We originated them" is already a complete sentence. The *inshāʾā* is grammatically optional. It is there for one reason: **to reassure.** It is Allah anticipating the doubt of the worn-down soul — the soul that has been disappointed by every "good as new" this world ever promised — and saying, before that soul can even voice its fear: *No. Not good as new. New. Completely. I am telling you twice so that you will believe Me once.*

One clear statement: **The doubled word is mercy in the form of grammar — Allah emphasizing the totality of the new creation precisely because He knows how little we have learned to trust a second chance.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: هُنَّ — The "Them" With No Name In Front Of It

Now the strangest thing in the passage. And this is a case of reading an **absence** — a gap the Arabic itself leaves open on purpose.

Look at the very first verb again: *anshaʾnā**hunna***. That **-hunna** ending is a pronoun: "them," feminine, plural. Now, a pronoun is a stand-in. It points back to a noun already named. "I saw the woman; *she* was tired." You cannot normally say "*she* was tired" with no one named first — the listener would stop you: *she who?*

And yet here, that is exactly what Allah does. The pronoun **هُنَّ** arrives, and if you scan backwards through the verses, **there is no noun it refers to.** No "women" has been mentioned. No "ḥūr." No antecedent at all. The "them" floats free.

Al-Qurtubi stops on exactly this. He says: **وأُضْمِرْنَ ولم يتقدم ذكرهنّ** — "they are referred to by a pronoun, though no prior mention of them has come." A pronoun without an antecedent. Grammatically, this should not work. So why is it here? What is the gap doing?

Let's not rush to fill it. Let's feel it first.

A pronoun-without-a-name does something to the listener: **it assumes you already know who is meant.** It is the grammar of intimacy. When you walk into your home and your spouse says, without preface, "*They* called again" — you know who "they" is, because you share a world. The missing name is not a failure of communication. It is *proof of a shared world.* You only drop the name with someone who is already inside the story with you.

So whom is Allah treating as already-known, already-inside-the-story?

Al-Qurtubi gives the answer, and it lands like a key turning: **لأنهنّ قد دخلن في أصحاب اليمين** — "because they have *already entered into* the Companions of the Right." The "them" needs no introduction because **they are already counted among the people the whole passage is about.** They are not a new cast of characters wheeled in for the men's reward. They were *already inside* "the Companions of the Right" — that great company the surah has been describing all along. The women are not an amenity of the Garden. **They are its inhabitants.** The pronoun has no separate antecedent because they were never separate.

And the early commentary anchors this from another angle. Al-Tabari preserves a narration — through ʿUthmān ibn ʿAbdillāh ibn Surāqah, commenting on the three groups of the surah — that distributes the Garden's companions deliberately: *the ḥūr al-ʿīn for the Foremost, and the ʿurub atrāb* — the very ones described here — *for the Companions of the Right.* So the floating "them" is doubly tethered. It is not only that they have entered into the Companions of the Right grammatically; the classical reading assigns *these* spouses, with these exact qualities, to *this* group by name. The pronoun was always pointing at the verse's last word.

[PAUSE]

And now feel how the passage is *built*. It opens with a pronoun whose name is withheld — *them* — and it travels through four verses, freshness, devotion, equal-age — and only at the very end, in the final verse, does it deliver the name the pronoun was always pointing at:

> [ayah:56:38] لِّأَصْحَـٰبِ ٱلْيَمِينِ
> "...**for the Companions of the Right.**"

The whole passage is shaped like a held breath. *Them.* Them who? Wait. Made new. Made fresh. Made devoted. Made equal. *Them who?* — and then, exhale — **the Companions of the Right.** The name arrives last, and when it arrives it does not just name the recipients; it retroactively names the women too, folding everyone into one company. The grammar enacts the theology: **in that Garden there is no separate class of "the rewarded" and "the rewards." There is one people, the People of the Right, and they are made new *together.***

One clear statement: **The missing name is not an oversight — it is Allah speaking of the people of the Garden the way you speak of those already inside your own home: no introduction needed, because they were never strangers to the promise.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three features have shown us, standing together.

A verb that means *originate-and-grow*, not repair. A doubled word that seals the origination as *total*, immune to decay. And a pronoun that treats the people of the Garden as already-known, already-inside, never a separate class.

Three small grammatical facts. And every one of them points the same direction: **away from the world of patching, fading, and second-rate second-chances — and toward a creation that is whole, living, and shared.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what death actually is. And about the two fears these verses were sent to heal.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Will Not Be Mended — You Will Be Made

Let me start where you already live.

You have, somewhere in your body right now, the evidence of time. Maybe it is small still — a knee that speaks up on the stairs, a name that takes a second longer to surface, a face in the mirror that has started, almost imperceptibly, to belong to one of your parents. Or maybe it is not small. Maybe you are reading this from inside a body that has been seriously betrayed — by illness, by an accident, by the simple long arithmetic of decades. Maybe you have already watched someone you love be slowly subtracted by time until the person inside became hard to reach.

Here is the deep human ache underneath all of it. **We were built to sense that we are more than this decline, and we have no evidence that the decline can be reversed.** Every restoration we have ever seen is partial. The recovered patient is never *quite* who they were before the diagnosis. The renovated house is not the original. We *long* for renewal and we have only ever been shown repair — and repair, we have learned, is decline that has been politely slowed.

This is why the word أَنشَأْنَا is not decoration. It is a different physics.

Listen to how the Garden's promise refuses the entire logic of repair. The old woman is not given a treatment. She is not de-aged like a photograph run backwards. **She is originated.** The verb skips over the whole worn-out object and starts a new living thing in its place — and, crucially, it is still *her*, because the pronoun never let her go. *Anshaʾnā**hunna*** — We originated **them**. The continuity is in the pronoun; the renewal is in the verb. **She is kept and she is remade at the same time** — the one thing this world has never once let us have.

Now come back to that report we left at the door an hour ago. Al-Baghawi narrates — and let me be honest about its standing: this comes through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, which makes it a *mursal* report, a narration with a gap in its chain, so we lean on it as a beautiful illustration and not as bedrock law — al-Baghawi narrates that an old woman asked the Prophet ﷺ to pray she enter the Garden, and he said, in effect, *no old woman enters the Garden* — and she turned away weeping — and he told them to tell her: *not as an old woman.* Because, he said, Allah says: **إِنَّآ أَنشَأْنَٰهُنَّ إِنشَآءً فَجَعَلْنَٰهُنَّ أَبْكَارًا.** "Indeed We originated them, an origination, and made them ever-fresh."

Sit with the shape of that mercy. Her grief was real. She heard "no old woman enters" and it cracked her heart, because she *was* an old woman and the door seemed to be closing on exactly what she was. And the answer did not say "you are wrong to grieve your age." The answer said: **the thing you grieve is the thing that will not be carried across.** The old age is not admitted — *because it is not repaired, it is left behind entirely, and you are made new.* Her tears were not corrected. They were *outdone.*

And look at the word the Prophet ﷺ reached for as the answer: **أَبْكَارًا** *(abkāran)*, which the translators soften to "virgins" but whose root **ب-ك-ر** is, at bottom, the root of *earliness* — of **البُكْرَة** *(al-bukrah)*, the first light of morning, the freshness of a day not yet spent. To be *bikr* is to stand at one's own daybreak, never carried past it into the long afternoon. So *abkāran* is not only a statement about the body; it is a statement about *time itself*: a people fixed at their own dawn. Al-Jalalayn draws out the practical edge of it without flinching — *every time their spouses come to them they find them ever-new, and no pain* (وَلا وَجَع) — a freshness that does not deplete with use, the way every good thing in this world depletes. The old woman feared she was all dusk. The verse answers her with daybreak — and a daybreak that never moves toward noon.

[PAUSE]

And here is the verse reaching past the women in the narration to every single one of us, men and women alike, because this is the law of the resurrection itself. Three verses later in this surah the same root returns as an argument: you already know **النَّشْأَةَ الْأُولَىٰ**, "the first origination" (56:62) — so how do you find the second impossible? **The freshness promised to the people of the Garden is the same act that raises all of us from the dust.** What is done to the aged woman is a close-up of what is done to every buried body: not mended, *made.*

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes your relationship to your own decline. The Muslim is not asked to pretend the body does not fail — Islam is too honest for denial. The Muslim is asked to hold the failing with a strange and steady hope: **this version of me is not the one being saved. This one is the seed; that one is the tree.** The graying, the slowing, the scars — these are not the erasure of you. By the logic of أَنشَأْنَا, they are the *first* origination, on its way to the second. You are allowed to grieve them. You are not required to despair of them. **They are not the end of the sentence; they are the comma before إِنشَآءً.**

Practical application, concrete and small: the next time the mirror or the body tells you the truth about time — the new ache, the new line, the slowness — let it become a *cue*, not a wound. Let it move your tongue to a single sentence: *Ya Allah, this is the first origination; I trust You for the second.* Turn the evidence of decay into the trigger for trust. That is what these verses train.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: A World With No One Ahead and No One Behind

Now the second fear — and this one is quieter, but it runs through almost every human relationship you have.

[ayah:56:37] عُرُبًا أَتْرَابًا

Let me open *atrāb*, because there is something in its very root that most readers walk right past.

The root is **ت-ر-ب** *(tāʾ–rāʾ–bāʾ)*. And from this exact root comes one of the most ordinary words in the Arabic language: **تُرَاب** *(turāb)* — **dust. Soil. The earth itself.** The lexicographers connect them through a homely, almost heartbreaking image: *atrāb* are peers because they are the children who **played together in the same dust.** Coevals are those who, as little ones, sat in the same dirt of the same courtyard in the same season of the world. To be someone's *tirb* is to have been small at the same time they were small. (I offer the dust-image as the lexicons offer it — as the picture beneath the word — and the settled meaning, "those of one age," stands regardless.)

Now feel why that buried connection is devastating in *this* surah.

This is Al-Waqi'ah. The surah of the dust. The mountains were just **بُسَّتْ بَسًّا** — crumbled to scattering dust. We come *from* dust — *turāb* — and we return to it, and from it we are originated again. And here, in the description of the people of the Garden, Allah reaches for a word whose own root is **dust** — *atrāb* — to describe the abolition of age. **The very soil that aged us and buried us becomes the root of the word for our eternal equality.** The dust that was the great leveler — that takes the young and the old, the powerful and the weak, and flattens them all into the same ground — that same dust-root names the state where everyone is finally, permanently, *the same age.* From the dust, peers; in the Garden, peers forever.

[PAUSE]

Now let me bring this down into the room you actually live in.

Almost every relationship you have is quietly distorted by **time-position** — by who is ahead and who is behind. The friend who married first and now lives in a different world than you. The sibling who is a decade older and was always, somehow, *more*. The spouse who is aging at a different rate, so that one of you fears becoming a burden and the other fears becoming a nurse. The whole grinding economy of comparison runs on the fact that **we are never on the same step of the staircase.** Someone is always further up, further down, ahead, behind, peaking, declining. We compete because we are staggered in time. We envy because someone got there first. We grieve because someone is being left behind.

*Atrāb* deletes the staircase.

In that Garden, no one is ahead of you and no one is behind you. There is no "she got there first." There is no "he is past his prime and I am in mine." There is no anxious arithmetic of who is aging faster. **Everyone is of one age, in a creation that does not age** — which means the entire machinery of comparison, the staircase that this whole world is built on, simply has no floor to stand on. You cannot envy someone's position in time when time has no positions left.

And notice that *atrāb* does not come alone. It comes married to *ʿurub* — restored, devoted love. Because here is the secret the two words tell together: **most of what poisons love is time-mismatch.** The fading of attraction is time. The drift of two people aging into strangers is time. The resentment of "I gave you my best years" is time. Strip out the staggering of time — make everyone *atrāb*, perfectly coeval, none declining — and what is left? *ʿurub.* Love with nothing pulling it out of shape. Devotion with no clock running underneath it. **The Garden does not just preserve love; it removes the very thing that was always eroding it.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It exposes the lie underneath your comparison. Every time you measure yourself against where someone else is in life — further along, falling behind — you are treating the *staircase of time* as if it were ultimate reality. *Atrāb* tells you it is not even permanent. The final arrangement of the people Allah loves is **one age, no hierarchy, no one ahead, no one behind.** The staircase you are exhausting yourself on is temporary scaffolding around a building that, when finished, has no stairs at all.

Practical application, concrete: pick the relationship most distorted by time-position in your life right now — the one where comparison or aging or "ahead and behind" has lodged like a splinter. The successful peer. The aging parent. The spouse you fear time is changing. And practice seeing them, deliberately, as a future *tirb* — someone who, in the creation that does not decay, will be exactly of your age, beside you, none ahead, none behind. Let the eternal equality reach back and soften the temporary inequality. You are not behind them. You are not ahead of them. You are, in the end, **atrāb** — and that ending can start governing how you treat them now.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at how these four verses are built, because the structure is the proof of everything we have said.

The passage is a single arc from a withheld name to a delivered one, and along the way it does three things in sequence:

- **35 — أَنشَأْنَٰهُنَّ إِنشَآءً** — *origination, sealed twice.* What is the new existence? Total, complete, decay-proof.
[ayah:56:36] فَجَعَلْنَـٰهُنَّ أَبْكَارًا
- **37 — عُرُبًا أَتْرَابًا** — *devoted, of one age.* The new existence has no time-staircase, so love has nothing to erode it.
- **38 — لِّأَصْحَٰبِ ٱلْيَمِينِ** — *the name, at last.* And the floating "them" finally lands: they were the People of the Right all along.

Do you see the shape? **It begins with a creation and ends with a community.** It opens on what Allah *does* (originates them) and closes on who they *are* (the Companions of the Right). And the bridge between the act and the identity is freshness and equality — the two things this world could never keep. The verb at the start and the name at the end were never two topics. **They were one promise, stated from both ends.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

From the words: a verb of *origination-not-repair*, a doubling that seals it as *total*, a pronoun that *folds the people of the Garden into one company*.

From the themes: a renewal that *makes rather than mends*, and a community with *no one ahead and no one behind*.

They meet in one place. **The world you live in is the world of repair and the staircase — everything fades and everyone is staggered in time.** The promise of these verses is a world that runs on the opposite physics: **made not mended, level not staggered, fresh not fading, together not sorted.** And the grammar is not describing that world from the outside. The grammar *is* that world, leaking into our language — the doubled word that refuses decay, the nameless pronoun that refuses separation.

### Questions to Carry

1. Which version of yourself have you been quietly mourning as if it were the final one — and what would change if you treated it as the *first* origination, not the last?
2. Where in your life is repair masquerading as renewal — a thing you keep patching that you secretly know needs to be *made new* instead?
3. Who in your life are you measuring by the staircase of time — ahead of you or behind you — when their final station is to be *atrāb*, exactly beside you?
4. When the next ache, line, or slowness arrives in your body, what sentence will it trigger on your tongue?
5. If love is most eroded by time-mismatch, what would it look like to love someone *as a future coeval* — right now, in this staggered world?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**What time wears down, Allah does not patch — He originates anew, completely and forever, folding the worn and the forgotten into one ageless company where no one is ahead, no one is behind, and nothing is left to erode love.**

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to stop grieving what time takes. Islam never asked you to lie about decline. The invitation is smaller and stranger than denial: to start reading your own fading the way these verses read it — as the comma before إِنشَآءً, not the period at the end of the sentence. To let the staircase you are exhausting yourself on lose a little of its authority, because you have seen the building it is wrapped around, and the building has no stairs. To treat the worn-down — in yourself, in the people you love — not as the saved version *failing*, but as the seed of the saved version *arriving.*

Made, not mended. That is the offer. Let it change what you do with the evidence of time the next time it speaks to you.

### A Brief Du'a

*Ya Allah, You who originate and do not merely repair — we are worn by the time You set running, and we feel it in our bodies and our faces and the people we are losing. Let this fading be, for us, the first origination and not the last word. Make us, do not merely mend us. And gather us — ageless, beside one another, none ahead and none behind — among the Companions of the Right. Āmīn.*
