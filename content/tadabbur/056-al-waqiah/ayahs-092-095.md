# The Welcome-Meal of the Fire

### *Surah Al-Waqi'ah, verses 92–95*

> [ayah:56:92] وَأَمَّآ إِن كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ ٱلضَّآلِّينَ
> [ayah:56:93] فَنُزُلٌ مِّنْ حَمِيمٍ
> [ayah:56:94] وَتَصْلِيَةُ جَحِيمٍ
> [ayah:56:95] إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ حَقُّ ٱلْيَقِينِ

*Wa ammā in kāna mina-l-mukadhdhibīna-ḍ-ḍāllīn. Fa-nuzulun min ḥamīm. Wa taṣliyatu jaḥīm. Inna hādhā la-huwa ḥaqqu-l-yaqīn.*

**"But if he was one of the deniers, the astray — then a welcome of scalding water, and a roasting in the Blazing Fire. Indeed, this — it is the very truth of certainty."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Curtain at the Throat

Let me put you in the room.

Surah Al-Waqi'ah begins enormous. It opens with the cosmos coming apart — *idhā waqaʿati-l-wāqiʿah* — when the Inevitable befalls, when the earth is shaken, when the mountains are scattered into floating dust. The whole sky is the stage. And across that vast canvas, the surah does something almost surgical: it sorts every human being who has ever lived into three groups. The Foremost, the brought-near — *as-sābiqūn*. The People of the Right — *aṣḥāb al-yamīn*. And the People of the Left — those the surah will call, by the end, *the deniers, the astray*.

Then, having shown you the sorting on a cosmic scale, the surah does something tender and terrifying. In verse 83, it suddenly shrinks the whole drama down to one bed, one body, one breath:

*Falawlā idhā balaghati-l-ḥulqūm* — **"Then why, when the soul reaches the throat...?"**

The camera leaves the splitting sky and comes to rest on a single dying person. The breath has climbed to the throat. The family is gathered around, helpless. And the Quran says: *you are watching at that moment, but you cannot see.* The curtain that has hung in front of every human eye for an entire lifetime is, right then, being drawn back.

And in that instant — the instant the curtain lifts — the dying soul is sorted. Verse 88: if he was of the brought-near, then *rest, and fragrance, and a garden of bliss*. Verse 90: if he was of the People of the Right, then *peace be unto you* from the People of the Right. And then comes our passage. The third door. The last possibility.

**Here is the question I want you to carry into everything that follows.**

When the curtain lifts at the throat, the believer is met with a word of welcome — *salām*, peace, a greeting, someone speaking to him. So what, exactly, is waiting on the other side of that curtain for the one who spent his whole life insisting there was no other side? [PAUSE]

The answer the Quran gives is not the answer you expect. It does not say *punishment*. It does not say *torment* — not first. The very first word it uses for what waits him is a word of **hospitality**.

Stay with me. We are going to find out why.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Al-Mukadhdhibīn* — Denial That You Have to *Work* At

Before we open the word, let me teach you a small piece of how Arabic builds its verbs, because the whole weight of this verse hangs on it.

Arabic takes a three-letter root and pours it into different molds, and each mold changes the *intensity* of the action. There is a basic mold — Form I — and then there is a heavier one, Form II, which doubles the middle letter and, very often, means the action is done *repeatedly, deliberately, with force, again and again.*

Here is an everyday example. The root *k-s-r* means "to break." In the basic form, *kasara* — he broke something. One act. A cup slips, it breaks. But put it in Form II — *kassara* — and now it means *he smashed it to pieces.* Not one break. A shattering. Repeated, violent, thorough. The doubled middle letter is the sound of the action happening over and over.

Now hold that, and look at the word Allah chooses for this third group:

**ٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ — *al-mukadhdhibīn*.**

The root is *k-dh-b*, which means *to lie, falsehood.* And the basic verb, *kadhaba*, just means "he lied" — he told an untruth. But that is **not** the word here. The word here is Form II — *kadhdhaba* — with the doubled middle letter, the *dh* under pressure, geminated, held.

And Form II of this root does something specific. It doesn't mean *to tell a lie.* It means **to declare something a lie. To brand the truth as false. To meet a reality and call it fake — actively, repeatedly, to your face.**

Do you feel the difference? [PAUSE]

A liar tells an untruth. A *mukadhdhib* takes the truth — the warning of the messenger, the signs of the resurrection, the whisper of his own conscience — and stamps it: *false.* And the Form II intensity tells you this is not one stamp. This is a man at a stamping machine, all day, every day, for a lifetime. Every sign that came to him, he branded it a lie. Every warning, *rejected.* Every quiet 3 a.m. doubt that *maybe this is real* — stamped down, *false*, and moved past.

Listen to the word itself. *Mu-kadh-dhi-bīn.* It clogs in the mouth. The doubled *dh* makes you stop, push, repeat — the very sound of effort, of something being forced. **Denial, the Quran is showing you in the texture of the word, is not effortless.** The believer is not the one who works hard and the denier the one who relaxes. It is the reverse. To keep the truth branded *false* for an entire life, against the grain of every sign, takes relentless, exhausting, daily labor. The denier is the one straining at the machine.

Now notice what Allah pairs it with — and notice what He leaves *out* between them.

**ٱلضَّآلِّينَ — *aḍ-ḍāllīn*** — the astray. The lost. The ones who wandered off the path. The root *ḍ-l-l* is the root of being lost in a trackless desert, of losing your way so completely you no longer even know which direction is home.

And here is what your ear should catch: **there is no "and" between them.** Arabic could easily have said *al-mukadhdhibīn wa-ḍ-ḍāllīn* — "the deniers *and* the astray," two boxes, two crimes. It does not. It says *al-mukadhdhibīn aḍ-ḍāllīn* — the deniers, the astray — laid skin to skin, with no conjunction at all. In Arabic, when you drop the "and" like that, you are not adding a second group. **You are describing the same group twice. You are saying: the second word *is* the first word, seen from the inside.**

So the verse is teaching you the anatomy of a single condition. The *takdhīb* — the active branding of truth as false — is what they *did.* The *ḍalāl* — the lostness — is what they *became.* You do not get lost first and then deny. You deny first, deliberately, at the stamping machine — and the lostness grows over you like a second skin until you genuinely cannot find the way anymore.

And this is exactly what the classical commentators saw in it. Al-Qurṭubī reads *aḍ-ḍāllīn* as those who went astray *"from guidance and the road of truth"* — not lost by accident, but lost off a road that was right there. Al-Baghawī adds the chilling structural note: *"and they are the People of the Ill-Omen"* — *aṣḥāb al-mashʾamah* — the People of the Left from the start of the surah. The denial and the lostness are two names for the people of the left hand.

And the commentators are precise about *what*, above all, was being branded false. Al-Muyassar glosses the pair tightly: the *mukadhdhibīn* here are *"the deniers of the Resurrection"* — *al-mukadhdhibīn bi-l-baʿth* — and the *ḍāllīn* are *"those astray from guidance"* — *aḍ-ḍāllīn ʿani-l-hudā*. That is the spine of the matter. Of all the signs a man might stamp false, the one at issue in this surah of the *wāqiʿah*, the Inevitable, is the rising itself — the claim that this is not the end, that there is a door at the throat and something waiting on the other side of it. He spent his life insisting the door did not exist. Now it opens.

**One word reveals it.** The man at the throat is not being punished for a single bad bet. He is meeting the cumulative weight of ten thousand small refusals, each one a stamp, until the stamping *was* his life. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Nuzul* — The Hospitality of Hell

Now we come to the word I promised you in the introduction. The word that does not behave the way you expect.

Let me first build the world this word lives in.

In the culture this Quran first descended into, there was no institution more sacred than hospitality. A guest — a *ḍayf* — arriving at your tent was a trust from God. And there was a specific word for the thing you prepared the moment a guest arrived: the welcome-meal. The reception. The food and drink set out *first*, before anything else, to honor the one who has come. That word is **نُزُل — *nuzul*.**

*Nuzul* is not just "food." *Nuzul* is the *hosting.* It is what is made ready for the one who has just *arrived* — the root carries the sense of *coming down, alighting, settling in as a guest.* When an honored traveler dismounts at your door, the *nuzul* is the spread you rush to lay before him. It is generosity made edible. It is the message: *you are welcome here, and we have prepared for you.*

And the Quran *knows* this word as the believer's word. This is the stunning part. Elsewhere, the gardens of the righteous are called their *nuzul* — *"the Gardens of Firdaws as a nuzul,"* a welcome from their Lord. The reward of the God-conscious is *"a nuzul from their Lord."* Across the Quran, *nuzul* is the honored guest's welcome-feast laid out by a generous Host.

Now read verse 93 again, knowing all of that:

**فَنُزُلٌۭ مِّنْ حَمِيمٍۢ — *fa-nuzulun min ḥamīm*.**

**"Then — a welcome-meal of scalding water."**

[PAUSE]

Sit with what just happened. The same word that crowns the believer — *nuzul*, the honored reception — is laid before the denier. But the spread on his table is *ḥamīm*: water heated to its absolute, screaming limit, boiling, scalding. The hospitality is real. The hosting is real. The *form* of honor is fully present. And it is filled with fire.

And this is not a modern reading pressed onto the word from outside. The early commentators heard the hospitality in it too — and reached for the plain language of guest-reception to render it. Al-Muyassar glosses verse 93 with the word *ḍiyāfah* — the formal hosting of a guest: *"a hosting from the boiling drink of Hell, at the very extremity of heat."* Ibn Kathir's rendering keeps the same register, calling it *"an entertainment with Ḥamīm"* — an entertainment, a reception laid on for one who has arrived. The tradition names the welcome *as* a welcome, with full deliberation, even as it names what fills the cup: fire.

This is one of the most devastating rhetorical strokes in the Quran, and it is not cruelty for its own sake. The bitter irony is making a precise argument. Let me unfold it slowly.

A *nuzul* is what you receive *according to what you brought.* The host reads the guest and lays out a welcome that matches him. So when the Quran calls the scalding water a *nuzul*, it is telling you: **this is not an arbitrary sentence dropped on him from outside. This is the welcome that exactly fits the guest who arrived.** He spent a lifetime at the stamping machine, branding the Host's signs as lies. He arrives at the Host's door at last — and is received. He *is* hosted. The reception is laid out. It is simply the reception he himself furnished, dish by dish, denial by denial, across all his years.

Notice the indefiniteness, too. It is not *the* scalding water, a known thing — it is *ḥamīm*, indefinite, *some* scalding water, a *hamim* whose nature you are left to dread without measure. Arabic uses this indefiniteness to make a thing loom larger, more shapeless, more terrible than any named quantity could.

And then verse 94 completes the table setting: **وَتَصْلِيَةُ جَحِيمٍ — *wa taṣliyatu jaḥīm*** — "and a roasting in the Blazing Fire." *Taṣliyah* is, again, a Form II word — the intensive mold — built from the root of *roasting, searing on flame.* Not warming. Not singeing. A thorough, sustained roasting. And *jaḥīm* — the Blaze — is the fire described by the very sound of it, the harsh *j* and *ḥ* catching in the throat like the crackle of it.

So the menu of the welcome is complete: the drink is boiling, the seat is the flame.

But here is the door I want you to walk through. Go back to the believers, two verses up. When the soul of the brought-near reaches the throat, what is his *nuzul*? Verse 89: *rawḥun wa rayḥānun wa jannatu naʿīm* — **rest, and fragrance, and a garden of bliss.** And the People of the Right? Verse 91: *salāmun laka* — **"Peace be unto you."** Someone *speaks* to them. A greeting. A relationship. A voice that turns toward them and says *peace.*

**Now read the deniers' verses again and find the missing thing.** [PAUSE]

There is no greeting. No one turns toward him and speaks. There is no *salāmun laka* for the *mukadhdhib*. There is a meal — a thing, set down — and a fire, and the chapter moves on. The deepest poverty of his hosting is not only what is *on* the table. It is that **no one says hello.** The believer is met by a Person — *peace be unto you.* The denier is met by an arrangement. He spent his life refusing to be in relationship with his Host, insisting the Host was not there, branding His every message *false* — and so, at the threshold, he is hosted in exactly the relationship he chose: none. A table, and silence. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Ḥaqq al-Yaqīn* — The Certainty That Comes Only by Tasting

And then the whole surah turns to look you in the eye.

Verse 95: **إِنَّ هَـٰذَا لَهُوَ حَقُّ ٱلْيَقِينِ — *inna hādhā la-huwa ḥaqqu-l-yaqīn*** — "Indeed, this — it is the very truth of certainty."

Look at how heavily this sentence is bolted down. *Inna* — "indeed," the particle of emphasis. *Hādhā* — "this," pointing. Then *la* — the emphatic *lām*, an extra "truly." Then *huwa* — "it," a standalone pronoun thrown in not because the grammar needs it but because the emphasis does. Four separate hammers of certainty before we even reach the phrase. The sentence is built like a vault door slamming shut. And the sound seals it: *ḥaqqu-l-yaqīn* — two hard *qāf* stops, the most closed-throat consonant in the language, thudding down twice like a bolt driven home.

Now the phrase itself: *ḥaqq al-yaqīn.* The truth of certainty. To understand why this is the perfect seal for the whole chapter, you need to know that the Quran recognizes that certainty comes in *levels* — that there is a ladder of knowing.

Let me give you the ladder with something from your own life.

Imagine someone tells you there is a fire in the next room. You *believe* them — you smell smoke, you see the orange glow flickering under the door. You are certain there is a fire. That is the first rung: **ʿilm al-yaqīn — the certainty of knowledge.** You know it because the evidence is overwhelming. The Quran names this rung in Surah al-Takāthur: *"Nay, if you knew with the certainty of knowledge..."*

Now you open the door and you *see* the fire. The flames are right there in front of your eyes. You no longer need the smoke and the glow — you are looking straight at it. That is the second rung: **ʿayn al-yaqīn — the certainty of sight.** The same surah climbs to it two verses later: *"Then you shall surely see it with the certainty of the eye."*

But there is a third rung. There is a kind of certainty even sight cannot give you. It is the certainty of the one who has *walked into the fire* — who is no longer looking at it but is *in* it, who knows the fire not by report and not even by sight but by the unmediated touch of it on his own skin. That is **ḥaqq al-yaqīn — the truth of certainty.** The certainty of *tasting.* The certainty that has stopped being information about a thing and has become *experience of* the thing. There is nothing left between you and the fact. You *are* the evidence now.

[PAUSE]

That is the phrase Allah places here, at the threshold, in the denier's verses. *Inna hādhā la-huwa ḥaqqu-l-yaqīn.* **This — the scalding water, the blaze, the silent table — this is the truth of certainty.**

And feel the devastating precision of *which* certainty it is. The denier could have had the *first* kind — *ʿilm al-yaqīn* — his whole life, for free. The signs were everywhere; the smoke was under every door. He had only to reflect, and he would have *known.* He refused. He stamped it *false.* And so the certainty he denied himself by knowledge, he now receives by *tasting.* He climbs the ladder the hard way — skipping the rung of reflection he was offered for nothing, and arriving at the top rung, *ḥaqq al-yaqīn*, in the only way left: by walking into the room. By becoming the evidence himself.

He was offered certainty as *light to read by.* He gets it as *fire to be roasted by.* The same certainty. Two utterly different ways to receive it. **And the only thing that decided which way he would receive it was whether he was willing to know it before he had to taste it.** [PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So step back and see what these three words, laid end to end, have built.

*Al-mukadhdhibīn* showed us that denial is not a passive mistake but an active, lifelong labor — a man at a stamping machine, branding truth as false until the lostness grows over him like skin. *Nuzul* showed us that what waits him is not arbitrary punishment but *hospitality* — the precise welcome his own labor furnished, a table he set himself, in the silent, relationless solitude he insisted on. And *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* showed us that the reality he spent his life branding *false* is, in fact, the most certain thing there is — and that he was always going to reach that certainty; the only question was whether he would read it as light or taste it as fire.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about us. About the way a human being constructs his own destiny one small refusal at a time, and the way certainty is sitting in front of every one of us right now, waiting to be *known* before it has to be *tasted.*

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Are Building the Person Who Will Die

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a well-documented thing the human mind does, and modern psychology has a dozen names for it: motivated reasoning, confirmation bias, cognitive dissonance. Strip the jargon and it is very simple. **When a truth is inconvenient, we do not usually argue with it. We *file* it.** We brand it — *not now, not real, not for me* — and we move on. Quietly. Constantly. All day.

You know this from the inside. The medical symptom you decided was nothing, because looking into it would be frightening. The pattern in your behavior a friend named gently, and you laughed off, because admitting it would mean changing. The quiet, recurring sense — maybe at a funeral, maybe at 3 a.m. — that *this life is not the whole story, and you are not ready* — and how fast, how expertly, you changed the subject in your own head.

That move — *truth arrives, and you stamp it "false" and walk on* — that is *takdhīb* in miniature. And here is what the Form II intensity of *al-mukadhdhibīn* was trying to tell us: **the danger is not the single stamp. The danger is that the stamping becomes a self.**

This is the part we get wrong. We imagine the denier of verse 92 as a cartoon villain who, one day, shook his fist at the sky and shouted *I reject God.* Almost no one is that. The denier is built the way a riverbed is carved — not by one flood, but by water running the same way ten thousand times until the channel is so deep the water *cannot* run anywhere else. That is why the Quran fused *mukadhdhibīn* and *ḍāllīn* with no "and" between them. The lostness is not a separate event. **The lostness is what a lifetime of small stampings hardens into.** You deny by choice for years, and then one day you discover you have lost the *capacity* to believe even when you want to. The riverbed is too deep. You are *ḍāll* now. Astray. And you carved the channel yourself, a refusal at a time, each one feeling at the moment like nothing at all.

Let this land, because it cuts both ways, and the other way is hope. If a self can be *carved* toward lostness by small repeated refusals, then a self can be carved toward guidance by small repeated *acceptances.* Every time a truth arrives and you do *not* stamp it false — every time you let an uncomfortable reality sit, and look at it, and say *yes, even this, even though it costs me* — you are running the water the other way. You are carving a different channel. **The person who will lie on that final bed, with the breath at his throat, is being built right now, today, by which truths you are willing to not look away from.**

So here is tomorrow's application, and it is concrete. Find the one truth you have been filing. You know what it is — it came to mind before I finished the sentence. The symptom, the habit, the relationship, the state of your soul. Do not resolve it today. Just *refuse to stamp it false.* Take it out of the drawer and let it sit on the table where you can see it. That single act — not looking away — is you carving the riverbed toward *yamīn*, toward the right hand, toward *salāmun laka.* [PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Certainty Is in the Room With You Right Now

Now the second door, and it opens off the first.

We treated *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* as a destination — the certainty you reach by tasting. But I want you to feel the strange mercy hidden in the fact that the Quran *told* you about it.

Think about what verse 95 actually does. It reaches out of the page and says: *this is the truth of certainty.* But it says it to you **now** — while you are still reading, still breathing, still on the right side of the curtain. And the moment it does that, it hands you the gift the denier threw away. **It offers you ʿilm al-yaqīn — certainty by knowledge — about the very thing he will only get by tasting.**

This is the whole quiet argument of the passage, and it is breathtaking once you see it. The Quran could have simply described Paradise and the Fire and left you to find out which is true on your own. Instead it describes them and then turns and says, *this is certain — I am telling you now, so that you do not have to learn it the other way.* The verse is not gloating over the denier. **The verse is a hand extended to *you*, before your curtain lifts.**

Here is the human picture. Every one of us is, right now, somewhere on that ladder of certainty about the most important fact of our existence. Some of us are at the very bottom — we have heard there might be a fire in the next room, and we have filed it. Some of us have smelled the smoke — we sense it is real but have not let it change how we live. And the entire point of *tadabbur*, the entire point of sitting with verses like these, is to climb — deliberately, while you still can — from *hearing* to *knowing*, to let the reality become so vivid in the heart that it functions like certainty, like something you have nearly seen, *before* the day you have no choice but to touch it.

Because there is a brutal asymmetry the passage is built on, and you must not miss it. **Certainty after death changes nothing. Certainty before death changes everything.** The denier reaches *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* — absolute, total, unanswerable certainty — at the exact moment that certainty can no longer save him. It arrives precisely one second too late to be of any use. That is the tragedy. He finally *knows*, with his whole scorched being, and the knowing is worth nothing to him now.

The certainty available to you tonight is worth everything. It is the same fact. It is just offered to you while it can still redirect your feet.

So the application here is not an action — it is a posture. When you read of the Garden and the Fire, of the *nuzul* of fragrance and the *nuzul* of scalding water, do not read it as *information to agree with.* Read it as *a reality to stand inside.* Slow down. Let the table of the deniers become real enough that you can see the steam off the *ḥamīm*; let the *salāmun laka* of the People of the Right become real enough that you ache to be greeted that way. **That ache, that vividness — that is your *ʿilm al-yaqīn* deepening toward what feels like sight.** That is you climbing the ladder by reflection — which is the one and only way to climb it that does not require walking into the room. [PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at how the whole final movement of the surah is built, because the structure is itself the argument.

From verse 88, Allah lays out three identical doorframes — *fa-ammā in kāna...* / *wa ammā in kāna...* — "if he was of..." Three times the same grammatical frame, and through each frame a different soul passes at the moment of death:

- **If he was of the Brought-Near** → *rest, fragrance, a garden of bliss.*
- **If he was of the People of the Right** → *peace be unto you.*
- **If he was of the deniers, the astray** → *a welcome of scalding water, a roasting in the blaze.*

Three guests. Three receptions. **One Host.** And the structure quietly insists on the thing we discovered through *nuzul*: the difference between the three is not in the Host's generosity — He prepares a *nuzul*, a welcome, for each. The difference is entirely in *what each guest brought to the door.* The frame is identical; only the soul passing through it differs. You are not waiting to find out what God will decide about you. You are deciding, right now, which frame you will walk through — because the soul that arrives is the soul you spent a lifetime building.

And then verse 95 seals all three at once: *inna hādhā la-huwa ḥaqqu-l-yaqīn.* This — all of it, every doorframe — is the truth of certainty. The vault door slams shut on the whole chapter. And the very next verse, 96, tells you the only sane response to standing in front of a truth this certain: *fa-sabbiḥ bismi rabbika-l-ʿaẓīm* — **so glorify the name of your Lord, the Magnificent.**

### Bringing It Together

See now that our linguistic discoveries and our two themes were one insight all along, approached from two sides.

The word *mukadhdhibīn* and the theme of self-construction are the same truth: **a destiny is built, not assigned** — stamp by stamp, refusal by refusal, until the riverbed is carved and the soul that arrives at the door is the soul the labor made. And the word *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* and the theme of certainty-now are the same truth: **the reality is fixed and the only variable is the timing of your knowing** — light tonight, or fire too late. The denier's tragedy is that he spent the effort of *takdhīb* — relentless, exhausting effort — to avoid the small cost of *ʿilm al-yaqīn*, and so purchased, at the highest possible price, the *ḥaqq al-yaqīn* he could have had for free.

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the one truth I have been *filing* — branding "not now, not real" — and what would it cost me to take it out of the drawer and simply look at it?
2. If the person who will die is being built today, what did *today's* small choices build?
3. Do I treat the Garden and the Fire as *information I agree with*, or as *realities I stand inside*? What would change if I let them become vivid?
4. The believer is met with *"peace be unto you"* — a greeting, a relationship. Am I building a relationship with my Host now, or only an arrangement?
5. Where in my life am I spending the exhausting effort of denial to avoid the smaller cost of facing something true?

### One-Sentence Distillation

At the threshold of death every soul is hosted according to the welcome it spent a lifetime preparing — and the denier discovers, one second too late to help him, that the reality he labored to brand a lie is the very truth of certainty.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to wait for the curtain to lift to know what is behind it. That is the mercy folded inside this terrifying passage. The certainty that will one day arrive as fire is being offered to you tonight as light. The invitation is not to be afraid — it is to *climb*: to let these realities become real enough, now, while it can still turn your feet, that you walk through the door of your own making toward *salāmun laka*, and not toward the silent table. Let the truth sit on the table where you can see it. Do not stamp it false. That single refusal to look away is the whole path.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who prepare a welcome for every soul that comes to Your door — let us not be among those who brand Your signs false until we taste what we denied. Grant us the certainty of knowing now, before the certainty of tasting then. And when our breath reaches our throat and the curtain lifts, let us be among those who are met with *salāmun laka* — peace be unto you. *Āmīn.*
