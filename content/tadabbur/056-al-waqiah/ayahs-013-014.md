# A Company and a Few: Why the Foremost Came Early

## Introduction: The Counting of the Saved

Let me begin by placing these seven words in your hand.

> ثُلَّةٌ مِّنَ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ وَقَلِيلٌ مِّنَ ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ
>
> *Thullatun mina-l-awwalīn. Wa qalīlun mina-l-ākhirīn.*
>
> **"A company of the former peoples, and a few of the later peoples."**

That is the whole of it. Two short clauses. A group, and a smaller group. A *many* and a *few*. On the page it looks almost like an accountant's note in the margin of something grander.

But stay with me. Because I want to show you that these two clauses are doing something so precise, so quietly devastating, that once you see it you will not be able to read the rest of this surah — or count your own place in history — the same way again.

**Rebuild the world for a moment.**

Surah Al-Waqi'ah is the surah of the great Event — *al-wāqi'ah*, the Inevitable, the thing that *falls* and cannot be turned back. Picture the moment it describes: the earth shaken, the mountains crumbled to scattered dust, and then humanity — all of humanity, from the first man to the last child born before the Hour — sorted. Not by nation. Not by century. Not by language or wealth or fame. Sorted into three.

The Companions of the Right. The Companions of the Left. And then, lifted above both, named first and named twice for emphasis:

> وَٱلسَّٰبِقُونَ ٱلسَّٰبِقُونَ ۝ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ ٱلْمُقَرَّبُونَ
>
> **"And the Foremost, the Foremost — those are the ones brought near."** (56:10-11)

*As-Sabiqun.* The Forerunners. The ones who ran ahead. And what is their reward? Not just Gardens — but *nearness*. *Al-Muqarrabun.* The ones drawn close to the Throne of the Most Merciful. This is the highest human destiny the Qur'an describes. Above the gardens of the righteous is the *proximity* of the King.

And now — *now*, at the very summit of human possibility — Allah pauses to take a census.

Who makes it here? To the highest place? How many?

**Here is the hook.** When Allah counts the Foremost, He says: *most from the former, few from the later.* But when He counts the Companions of the Right, just twenty-six verses on, He changes exactly **one word** — and the entire arithmetic of hope flips open. One word. Hold that question. We will earn the answer.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: ثُلَّة — The Word for an Uncountable Crowd

Let me teach you a small thing about how Arabic counts before we look at the word Allah chose.

In English, when we want to say "a lot of people," we reach for numbers or near-numbers — *thousands, multitudes, a great many.* Our instinct is to gesture at a quantity. Arabic has a different and older instinct. It has words that name a crowd not by *how many* but by *what kind of mass it is* — a *fawj* is a surging wave of people, a *zumra* is a troop moving together, a *thullah* is...

Let's open the word.

> ثُلَّةٌ — **thullah.**

The root is **ث-ل-ل** (*th-l-l*). And here is the first beautiful thing. In the speech of the Arabs, before the Qur'an ever used it for the people of Paradise, *thullah* was a word from the world of **the shepherd**. A *thullah* was a portion of a flock — a body of sheep cut off from the larger herd and moving as one. The root carries the sense of a *piece sheared from a greater whole*, a gathered mass with no fence around its number.

Notice what that does. Allah did not say "a thousand from the former" or "a great number." He reached for a word that means **a crowd you cannot put a fence around.**

Listen to what the scholars do with this. Al-Baghawi, reading *thullah*, stops and defines it precisely for us:

> *"wa-th-thullatu: jamā'atun ghayru maḥṣūrati-l-'adad"* —
> **"And the *thullah* is a group whose number is not enclosed, not bounded, not countable."**

Sit with that phrase: *ghayru maḥṣūrati-l-'adad.* A multitude that escapes the census even as Allah is taking one. He counts them by telling you they cannot be counted. The very word is a crowd overflowing its own container.

[PAUSE]

And now feel the sound of it. *Thullah.* The breath of the *th* — soft, exhaled, almost like wind over the flock. Then the doubled *l*, the *shadda*, the tongue lingering — *thul-lah* — the sound itself pools and gathers, the way the people it names pool and gather. The word does not click shut like a number. It spreads.

Here is what this single word reveals before we have even moved on: **the highest rank of Paradise is not a narrow elite.** When you first hear "the Foremost, the brought-near," your mind builds a picture of a tiny, exclusive few — a handful of saints. And Allah's very first word about their number tears that picture down. *Thullah.* A flock without a fence. An uncountable company.

Remember that — because in the next breath, He is going to say something that sounds like the opposite.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Two: قَلِيل — And Then, the Word That Stings

Now the second clause.

> [ayah:56:14] وَقَلِيلٌ مِّنَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِينَ
>
> **"And a few of the later peoples."**

> قَلِيل — **qalīl.** *Few. Scant. A small number.*

After the great overflowing *thullah*, this word lands like a single drop. And the contrast is not an accident of vocabulary — it is the entire point. Allah has built these two verses as a perfect seesaw:

- A **company** — from the **former**.
- A **few** — from the **later**.

The weight is all on one side. *Thullah* lifts up the *awwalīn*. *Qalīl* presses down on the *ākhirīn*. Two scales, and the early generations sit heavy, the later generations sit light.

Now — who feels the sting of this? **You do. I do.** Because we are not the *awwalīn*. We are, by any reckoning, among the *ākhirīn* — the later peoples, born at the tail of history, fourteen centuries downstream from the Prophet ﷺ. When Allah says "and few of the later," He is, in some real sense, counting *us* — and the number is small.

And here is something I want you to hold, because it is not abstract to me as I read it, and it was not abstract to the first community that heard it. Al-Qurtubi preserves the moment this verse landed on the Companions. He reports — and his text breaks off right at the wound — that when this was revealed, *"shaqqa 'alā aṣḥāb..."* — **it weighed heavily, it pressed painfully, upon the Companions.** They heard "and few of the later," and they understood: *the door to the very front rank is closing behind the first generations, and we are arriving late.*

Feel that with them for a moment. These are the very people we would call the *sābiqūn* of this ummah. And they heard a verse that seemed to say: *the foremost of the foremost will mostly be others, older than you, gone before you.* It grieved them.

[PAUSE]

Why did it have to be a *few*? Hold that grief. We do not resolve it yet. We let it sit — because the surah itself does not leave it sitting, and neither will we.

## Element Three: ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ and ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ — Who Are "the Former" and "the Later"?

Before we can heal that wound, we have to be honest about a real difference among the scholars — not as competing answers to pick between, but as **two camera angles on one scene.**

The two words are:

> ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ — **al-awwalīn**, from the root **ء-و-ل**, "the first ones, the former."
> ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ — **al-ākhirīn**, from the root **ء-خ-ر**, "the last ones, the later."

The question the mufassirūn ask is: *former and later relative to what?* And they give us two lenses.

**The first lens — across all of human history.** Al-Baghawi reads *al-awwalīn* at the widest possible scale:

> *"min al-umami-l-māḍiyah min ladun Ādama 'alayhi-s-salām ilā zamāni Nabiyyinā ﷺ"* —
> **"the former are the bygone nations, from the time of Adam, peace be upon him, all the way to the era of our Prophet ﷺ."**

On this reading, *al-awwalīn* is everyone who came before this ummah — every believer of every prophet across thousands of years, Adam to 'Isa. And *al-ākhirīn* is this final ummah, the community of Muhammad ﷺ. The math becomes almost obvious: of *course* the forerunners are mostly "former" — they are drawn from the entire span of human history before us, every nation that ever followed a messenger, while the "later" are drawn from one ummah alone.

Al-Qurtubi sharpens exactly this logic, and it is worth hearing his reasoning in full because it is a comfort hiding inside an arithmetic:

> The earlier ones were called *many*, and these *few*, **only by comparison** to those before them — *"li-anna-l-anbiyā'a-l-mutaqaddimīna kathurū fa-kathura-s-sābiqūna ilā-l-īmāni minhum"* — **"because the earlier prophets were many, so the forerunners-to-faith among them were many,"** more than the number who raced first to belief from our ummah.

Do you see what he just did? *Qalīl* — "few" — is not a verdict that our ummah produced few great souls. It is a statement of **scale.** One ummah measured against the sum of all prior ummahs and all their prophets will, of course, look smaller. The "few" is few *only beside an ocean.*

**The second lens — within this ummah itself.** Now hear al-Muyassar and the great reading of al-Hasan al-Basri, which Qurtubi also preserves. On this lens, *awwalīn* and *ākhirīn* are both *inside* the ummah of Muhammad ﷺ:

> Al-Muyassar: a large company — *"min ṣadri hādhihi-l-ummah"* — **"from the first generations of this ummah,"** and others from earlier nations — *"wa qalīlun min ākhiri hādhihi-l-ummah"* — **"and few from the last of this ummah."**

> And al-Hasan al-Basri, in Qurtubi: *"thullatun mimman qad maḍā qabla hādhihi-l-ummah, wa qalīlun min aṣḥābi Muḥammadin ﷺ"* — a company from those before us, and few even from the very Companions — and then al-Hasan breaks into du'a: **"Allāhumma-j'alnā minhum bi-karamik"** — *"O Allah, make us among them, by Your generosity."***

[PAUSE]

Don't pass over that. One of the masters of the early generation reads this verse, understands that the front rank is mostly already taken — and his response is not despair but *prayer*: *make me one of the few, by Your generosity.* He turned a verse of arithmetic into a verse of asking.

So the composite picture — both cameras at once — is this: **the supreme rank of nearness was filled, overwhelmingly, early.** Whether you read "early" as the prophets-and-nations before us, or as the first generations of this ummah, the shape is the same. The vanguard fills up at the front of the line. The later you arrive, the fewer the open places at the very summit.

That is the linguistic construction. *Thullah* lifts the former; *qalīl* presses the later; *awwalīn* and *ākhirīn* set the two on a timeline. The grammar has handed us a hard truth about time and arrival.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Mathematics of Being Early

Let me start where you already live.

You know, instinctively, that **being early costs more and is worth more.** The person who stood up for an unpopular truth *before* it was safe is honored differently than the person who agreed once everyone agreed. The one who funded the cause when it was a rumor in a back room is remembered differently than the one who donated after it was famous. We have a word for this in every domain — *pioneer, founder, first responder, early adopter.* And we know, without being told, that those words carry a weight that "joined later" never carries, no matter how sincere the latecomer.

Why? Because **earliness is expensive in a currency you cannot earn back: risk taken in the dark.** The early believer in Mecca said *lā ilāha illā Allāh* when saying it could cost him his life, his family, his standing — and with *no crowd around him,* no momentum, no certainty it would ever be safe. The one who believes today says the same words inside a civilization of a billion-plus, with mosques on the skyline and the matter long settled. The words are identical. The *cost*, and therefore the *rank*, are not.

This is exactly what the Qur'an says elsewhere, in plain speech:

> لَا يَسْتَوِى مِنكُم مَّنْ أَنفَقَ مِن قَبْلِ ٱلْفَتْحِ وَقَٰتَلَ ۚ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ أَعْظَمُ دَرَجَةً
>
> **"Not equal among you are those who spent and fought *before* the Conquest. Those are greater in degree..."** (57:10)

Before the conquest — when it was still dangerous, still uncertain — is *not equal* to after. The Qur'an itself ranks by earliness.

So now return to our verse with new eyes. **"A company of the former, and a few of the later"** is not Allah being stingy with the latecomers. It is Allah being *just* about a cost the early ones paid and the later ones cannot. The forerunner's rank is high precisely because the front of the line is the most dangerous place to stand. The *thullah* is large at the front because the front is where the price was highest — and the *qalīl* is small at the back not because the back is unworthy, but because the back is *safe*, and safety rarely produces forerunners.

[PAUSE]

But here is where I want to press, because there is a door even here. *Qalīl* is **few** — but it is **not zero.** Allah did not say "and none of the later." He left the door of the *supreme* rank cracked open for every generation that would come after, including ours. The race to the front did not end when Mecca's danger ended. In every age, in every safe and comfortable century, there are still a *few* who manufacture, with their own hands, the kind of cost that earliness used to charge — who fast when no one fasts, who stand at night when the whole world sleeps, who carry the truth where it is *newly* unpopular again. The *qalīl* is the standing reservation, in every era, for those willing to be early *to something.*

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It means the question is not "Was I born too late to be a forerunner?" The question is: *Where, in my own time, is the front of the line — the place where the truth is costly and the crowd is thin — and am I willing to stand there?* The *qalīl* is hiring in every generation. It is small. It is open.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: The Wideness of Salvation and the Narrowness of Nearness

Now I have to give you the second key, because if Theme One were the whole story, the *ākhirīn* — you, me — would close this surah in quiet grief, like the Companions did when al-Qurtubi tells us *"it weighed heavily upon them."*

But the surah does not let the grief stand. And to feel how it lifts the weight, we need the modern intuition first.

Think about the difference between **finishing a race** and **standing on the podium.** In any great race, the podium — first, second, third — fills up fast and fills up *once.* Once those three places are taken, they are taken; no one in the crowd behind can have them. But *finishing the race* — crossing the line, completing it, being counted among those who made it home — that remains open to every single runner until the very last one crosses. The podium is narrow. The finish line is wide.

The Qur'an, with surgical precision, distinguishes these two things — and it does it *inside this very surah*, with the change of a single word. Watch.

Here, for **As-Sabiqun**, the Foremost, the brought-near — the *podium* — Allah says (56:13-14):

> ثُلَّةٌ مِّنَ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ **وَقَلِيلٌ** مِّنَ ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ
> A company of the former, **and a *few*** of the later.

But twenty-six verses later, describing **Aṣḥāb al-Yamīn**, the Companions of the Right — the vast saved multitude, the *finish line* — Allah says (56:39-40):

> ثُلَّةٌ مِّنَ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ **وَثُلَّةٌ** مِّنَ ٱلْءَاخِرِينَ
> A company of the former, **and a *company*** of the later.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? Do you see what moved?

The verse about the *former* is **word-for-word identical** in both passages: *thullatun mina-l-awwalīn.* A company, both times. The early generations are a great company at the podium *and* a great company at the finish line.

The only thing Allah changed — the single hinge on which all the hope of the later generations turns — is the word for **us, the later ones.** At the podium of *nearness*, the *Sabiqun*: we are **qalīl**, few. At the finish line of *salvation*, the *Aṣḥāb al-Yamīn*: we are **thullah** — a full, uncountable, fenceless company, exactly as many as the early ones.

This is the answer to the grief. This is why the wound does not stay open.

[PAUSE]

What the surah is teaching, through one swapped word, is the most liberating distinction in all of eschatology: **najāh is wide; qurb is narrow.** *Salvation* — being among the saved, the right-hand company bound for the Garden — is thrown open to the later generations in exactly the same overflowing measure as to the first. There, we are no fewer than the *awwalīn*. The door of being *saved* never narrows with the centuries. It is *thullah* to the very end of time.

But *nearness* — *al-qurb*, the maqām of the *Muqarrabīn*, the place at the front by the Throne — *that* narrows. That is the podium, and the podium is *qalīl* for the latecomer. Not closed — *qalīl,* remember, is not zero — but narrow.

And there is a mercy folded inside even *that* narrowness. Why must the highest rank be earned by the few and the early? Because if it were handed out as widely as salvation, it would mean nothing. A summit that everyone stands on is no longer a summit. The *qalīl* protects the *value* of nearness — it keeps it precious, keeps it worth a whole life's race, keeps al-Hasan al-Basri whispering *"Allāhumma-j'alnā minhum"* across the centuries to us.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Two things, and you must hold both or you will fall off one side. First: **rest.** If you are exhausted, if you arrived to faith late, if your past is heavy — the *finish line* is wide open to you, *thullah*, no fewer than the greatest generations. You are not consigned to the scraps. Salvation does not ration itself to the latecomer. Second: **reach.** Do not let the wideness of salvation make you lazy about the narrowness of nearness. The *qalīl* is still hiring. There is still a podium, and there is still — in your own ordinary, comfortable century — a way to be early to something costly enough to climb it. Rest in the *thullah*. Reach for the *qalīl*.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture Revealed

Now let me show you the whole structure at once, because the proof was hiding in plain sight all along, split across two halves of one surah.

Allah describes two destinies with what is almost the same couplet:

> **The Foremost (the near):**  *thullah* of the former  /  ***qalīl*** of the later
> **The Right Hand (the saved):**  *thullah* of the former  /  ***thullah*** of the later

Three of the four lines are identical. The architecture is a deliberate near-mirror with **one fault line** running through it — and that fault line falls *exactly* on the word for the later peoples, *exactly* on us. Allah built two almost-twin statements so that the *one* difference between them would be impossible to miss and impossible to misread. He is not merely telling us facts about Paradise's demographics. He is using the *structure itself* — the held-constant *thullah* of the former, the *changed* word for the later — to teach us that **as time runs on, the door of nearness narrows while the door of salvation stays flung wide.** The form *is* the lesson. The repetition *is* the argument.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same insight approached from two directions.

From the *words*: *thullah* is an uncountable overflowing crowd; *qalīl* is a single counted drop; and Allah placed the overflow on the former and the drop on the later — *but only for the rank of nearness.*

From the *life*: being early costs a price the latecomer cannot pay, so the summit fills up at the front — *yet salvation itself was never the summit, and salvation stays wide for all of us to the end of time.*

One says it in grammar. One says it in the ache of arriving late and the relief of being caught anyway. They are the same truth: **the price of nearness rises with the centuries, but the gift of salvation never runs short.**

## Questions to Carry

1. When I feel that I arrived to this *deen* "too late" — late in my life, late in history, late after years of distraction — am I grieving the wideness of *salvation*, which is fully open to me, or only the narrowness of *nearness*, which I can still race toward?

2. Where, in *my* century and *my* ordinary life, is the front of the line — the place where the truth is costly and the crowd is thin? Have I ever once stood there?

3. The *qalīl* — the few of the later who still reach the highest rank — is not zero. What would I have to *manufacture* in my own comfortable life to become one of them, when the old dangers that made forerunners are gone?

4. Al-Hasan al-Basri read this verse and *prayed* instead of despairing: *"O Allah, make us among them."* When the Qur'an shows me a height I did not reach, is my reflex despair, or du'a?

5. If I am honest, am I content merely to *finish the race* — or do I still want the podium enough to pay for it?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**As the centuries run on, the place at the very front grows precious and few — but the door of the saved never once narrows, so the latecomer may rest in the wide company of the rescued even as he still races, with the few, for the nearness of the King.**

## The Closing Invitation

Let something shift. If you have read these two verses as a verdict against your generation — *too late, too few, too far back* — read them again. They are not a closed door; they are two doors of different widths. Walk, with relief, through the wide one: you are *thullah*, a full company of the saved, no fewer than the first. And then, having entered, *turn around and run* — quietly, in the dark, in the thin crowd at the front — for the narrow one. The *qalīl* is small. It is also still open. And it is the most worth-it race a human being can run.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, who made the saved a *company* without a fence — count us among them, by Your generosity, as Your servant al-Hasan begged before us. And O Allah, who kept the front rank narrow so it would stay precious — do not let us be content with merely arriving. Make us, though we are of the later and the few, among the forerunners brought near to You. *Allāhumma-j'alnā minhum bi-karamik.* Āmīn.
