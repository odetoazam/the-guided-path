# Look at Your Food

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read it to you first — in Arabic, then in a translation that tries to stay out of the way.

> فَلْيَنظُرِ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِهِۦ
>
> *Fal-yanẓuri l-insānu ilā ṭaʿāmih*
>
> **So let the human being look at his food.**

And then it unfolds:

> *How We poured the water down, a pouring; then We split the earth open, a splitting; and We caused to grow in it grain, and grapes and fresh herbage, and olives and date-palms, and densely-treed gardens, and fruit and pasture — provision for you and for your livestock.*

Now I want to rebuild the room you're standing in before we open this.

### The world this fell into

Surah Abasa does not begin gently. It begins with a frown.

A blind man — the tradition names him Ibn Umm Maktūm — comes to the Prophet ﷺ wanting to be taught. And the Prophet ﷺ, in that moment deep in conversation with the chiefs of Quraysh, the men of influence whose acceptance might open the city — turns slightly away. *ʿAbasa wa tawallā* — "He frowned and turned away." And the Quran corrects this. Gently, but firmly. Heaven leans down to say: the one you turned from is the one who was reaching for purification; the ones you leaned toward are the ones who feel no need at all.

So the surah is, from its opening breath, about a single dangerous inversion: **the human heart's tendency to honor what looks powerful and ignore what is actually seeking truth.** To be impressed by the wrong things.

And then the surah does something striking. It widens its lens off the specific scene and onto the whole species. *Qutila l-insān, mā akfarah* — "Cursed be the human, how intensely ungrateful he is!" Then it takes that proud creature by the collar and walks him backward through his own beginning: *from what did He create him? From a sperm-drop.* From a fluid you would step around on the ground. From almost nothing.

[PAUSE]

And it is *here*, with the human's arrogance exposed and his lowly origin laid bare, that Allah does not send him to a laboratory, or a mountaintop, or the edge of the ocean to be humbled.

He sends him to lunch.

### The hook

There is something almost embarrassing about how *close* this evidence is. Allah could have pointed the proud human anywhere in the cosmos. Instead He points at the plate in front of him — the most ordinary, most overlooked, most daily thing in a human life. Three times a day this creature handles a miracle and calls it a chore.

And the question this passage is quietly asking — the question I want us to sit inside for the next hour — is this:

**Why food? Of all the signs in the universe, why would Allah stake the argument here? And what exactly is He asking this man to "look" at — because it cannot simply be the meal.**

Stay with me. The destination is much larger than the plate.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — *Fal-yanẓur*: the command to *look*, and why your eyes are not enough

Let me teach you something about a single word before we touch it.

In English we have one tired verb — "look" — doing a dozen jobs. You look at your phone. You look for your keys. You look into a problem. The word has gone flat from overuse. Arabic does not let *naẓar* go flat. The root **ن-ظ-ر (n-ẓ-r)** — confirmed in the earliest corpora — carries a spectrum: it can mean the eye's glance, yes, but it also means *to wait*, *to consider*, *to weigh a matter*, *to reflect*. From the same root comes *naẓariyya* — a "theory," a considered way of seeing. *Naẓar* at its full strength is not what the eye does. It is what the mind does *through* the eye.

Now hold that, and let's open the word as it actually sits in the ayah.

> فَلْيَنظُرِ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ

Notice the little **lām** fastened to the front — *fal-yanẓur*. Grammarians call it the *lām al-amr*, the *lām* of command. This is not a suggestion. It is not "the human might want to consider." It is an *imperative* — issued by Allah, about you, in the third person, as if He is turning to all of existence and saying of this proud creature: **"Let him look. Make him look."**

[PAUSE]

So the first thing to register is the *force* of it. Heaven is commanding an act of attention.

But attention to what — and of what kind?

Here the classical commentators do something I want you to see, because they are not disagreeing; they are turning the same diamond in the light. Al-Qurṭubī is the most explicit. He says of *fal-yanẓur*: **«وهذا النظر نظر القلب بالفكر»** — "this looking is the looking of the *heart*, by way of *thought*." Not the looking of the eye. *Naẓar al-qalb.* The gaze of the heart. He continues: *ay li-yatadabbar* — "that is, let him *reflect deeply* on how Allah created his food." Al-Muyassar opens the ayah with exactly that word: *fa-l-yatadabbar al-insān* — "let the human engage in *tadabbur*." And al-Jalālayn pins it precisely: *naẓara ʿtibār* — "a looking of *taking-a-lesson*," a seeing that draws a conclusion — and then completes the thought with the *object* of that seeing: *kayfa quddira wa dubbira lah* — "how it was *measured out* and *arranged* for him."

Al-Ṭabarī sharpens the same point and tells us *who* is standing in the dock. The one commanded to look is, in his words, *al-insān al-kāfir al-munkir tawḥīd Allāh* — the ungrateful human who *denies God's oneness* — and the thing he is sent to weigh is *kayfa dabbarah,* "how He managed it." Hold that verb, *dabbara,* for a moment: it is the root of *tadbīr,* the careful governing of a thing — measuring it, timing it, ordering it toward a purpose. So the looking is not only the looking of the heart; it has a destination. The food was not merely *made.* It was *administered* — proportioned to your body, timed to your seasons, and set in front of you on purpose. To trace it back honestly is to arrive at *one* arranging Hand. That is why al-Ṭabarī frames the whole command as an argument aimed at the man who denies God's oneness: look hard enough at a single meal, and the denial cannot survive the looking.

Do you feel what they are all circling?

[PAUSE]

There is a kind of seeing that registers nothing. You have done it a thousand times. You have looked *at* food — photographed it, even, arranged it for a screen — without ever once *seeing* it. Your retina caught it. Your heart was absent.

And here is the quiet devastation of placing this command *in this surah.* Remember how the surah opened — with a *blind* man who came *seeking*, and a *seeing* man who *turned away.* The blind man could not use his eyes at all, yet his heart was straining toward the light. And now Allah commands the seeing, sighted, arrogant human: **"Look."** As if to say: *you have the eyes he lacked, and you have used them on nothing. He could not see and was seeking. You can see and you are asleep.*

The whole surah is about the difference between the eye and the heart. And this verb sits at its hinge.

So when Allah says *fal-yanẓur*, He is not asking you to glance at your dinner. He is commanding you to do the one thing the proud human refuses to do: **trace the thing in front of him back to its Source.** To let the eye be a doorway the heart walks through.

One statement to carry from this word: **the command was never "see your food." It was "see *through* your food."**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two — *Ṭaʿāmihi*: the smallest letter in the verse carries the largest lie

Now look at the object of that looking.

> إِلَىٰ طَعَامِهِۦ — *ilā ṭaʿāmih* — "at **his** food."

The root is **ط-ع-م (ṭ-ʿ-m)**, confirmed in the corpus — it gives us *ṭaʿām*, food, and *ṭaʿm*, taste. Ordinary word. Nothing exotic. And that ordinariness is exactly the point — Allah deliberately chose the most domestic noun in the language.

And the earliest authorities refused to let us shrink it even to the plate. Mujāhid — among the first generation of commentators, his words preserved by al-Ṭabarī — glossed *ṭaʿāmihi* as *maʾkalihi wa mashrabih:* "his food *and his drink.*" Not one meal. The whole intake of the body, the eating and the drinking together. And al-Muyassar names what that intake actually *is* to you: *qiwām ḥayātih* — "the very *mainstay of his life*," the thing his continued existence stands upon. So before we have even touched the possessive suffix, the word has quietly widened in our hands. You are not being sent to admire a dish. You are being sent to look at the entire material basis of your staying-alive — and then to notice Whose it is.

But I want you to look not at the noun. I want you to look at the letter clinging to the end of it. The little *hāʾ* — *-hi*. **"His."**

His food.

Let that sit for one second, because it is about to come apart in your hands.

[PAUSE]

The human calls it *his*. My food. My groceries. My harvest. My dinner that I made. And then — *the instant* that possessive is spoken — Allah seizes the sentence and floods it with verbs that have a completely different owner:

> *An*na** ṣabab**nā** … shaqaq**nā** … anbat**nā***

**We** poured. **We** split. **We** grew.

Count them. Every single verb of *production* in this passage belongs to Allah. The pouring of the water — His. The cracking-open of the dead earth — His. The pushing of green life up through the soil — His. There is not one verb of making left over for the human. Not one.

And what is the human's verb? Go back. His only assigned verb in the whole passage is *yanẓur* — **"let him look."** That's it. The human's entire contribution to the meal he calls *"his"* is to *look at it after it arrives.*

[PAUSE]

Read the absence here, because this is where the verse cuts deepest. Arabic could easily have given the human a partnership clause — "the food you *tilled,*" "the harvest you *earned.*" The Quran does this elsewhere when it wants to. Here it withholds it *completely.* The human is grammatically permitted to *own* the food — *ṭaʿāmihi*, "his" — and grammatically forbidden from having *made* any part of it. The possessive is granted; the production is denied. And that gap between the two — between *"mine"* and *"I made none of it"* — **that gap is the exact size of human arrogance.**

This is the same human the surah cursed a few lines up — *mā akfarah*, "how ungrateful." And now we see the mechanism of the ingratitude in a single suffix. *Kufr* in Arabic, at its root, means *to cover over.* The farmer is even called *kāffār* because he covers seed with soil. And here is the human, covering over the whole chain of divine action with one possessive pronoun. He drops the word *"my"* over a mountain of God's labor like a sheet over furniture, and walks out of the room.

The one statement to carry: **you have never once eaten "your" food. You have eaten His, and called it yours — and the lie was so small it fit inside a single letter.**

[PAUSE — let this one land]

### Element Three — *Ṣabban, shaqqan*: the sound of the earth breaking open

Now I want to do something different. I want you to stop reading the verse and *listen* to it.

Here is a feature of Arabic grammar — let me teach it plainly first. Sometimes a verb is followed by a noun built from the *very same root* as the verb itself. You say the action, and then you say the action's own name again, in the accusative. Grammarians call it the **mafʿūl muṭlaq** — the "absolute object," or the cognate accusative. We have a faded echo of it in English: "He smiled a *smile*." "She slept a deep *sleep*." In Arabic it is not faded at all — it is a hammer. Its job is **intensification and certainty**: it says *this action happened, fully, really, with its whole nature present.* No metaphor. No half-measure.

Now listen to what Allah does — twice, back to back:

> صَبَبْنَا ٱلْمَآءَ **صَبًّۭا** — *ṣababnā l-māʾa **ṣabban*** — "We poured the water — *a pouring.*"
>
> شَقَقْنَا ٱلْأَرْضَ **شَقًّۭا** — *shaqaqnā l-arḍa **shaqqan*** — "We split the earth — *a splitting.*"

Twice He uses the absolute object. So before we even reach the meaning, the grammar is already shouting: *this was no gentle, half-imagined process. The water REALLY came down. The earth REALLY broke open.*

[PAUSE]

Now — *listen* to the consonants. *Ṣabban. Shaqqan.* Then keep going down the list, because the whole passage is built on it: *ḥabban* (grain), *qaḍban* (herbage), *ghulban* (dense trees), *abban* (pasture). Hear the shape? Short, struck, doubled consonants slammed shut with a hard *-an*. **Ṣab-ban. Shaq-qan. Ḥab-ban. Ab-ban.** These are not flowing, liquid sounds. They are *percussive.* They are the sound of something being *struck and broken.*

The Arabic is doing with its *mouth* what the verse describes with its *meaning.* Water cracking down. Earth splitting apart. Seeds bursting their shells. The phonetics *are* the event. You can hear the soil breaking open in the back of your throat when you say *shaqqan.*

And notice the sequence the verbs trace — because it is the sequence of a *violence* that produces life. First the water is *poured* — *ṣabban*, hard, total. Al-Jalālayn fixes its source in three words: *min al-saḥāb* — "from the clouds." It does not seep up from beneath your control; it is *sent down,* from a height no hand of yours can reach. Then — and the word *thumma* marks a real pause, a "then, after a while" — the earth is *split.* *Shaqqan.* And here the classical commentators add a detail worth catching, because it changes who is doing the breaking. Al-Jalālayn glosses *shaqaqnā l-arḍa* with two words — *bi-l-nabāt* — "by the vegetation": the earth is split open *by the very plant pushing up through it.* Al-Muyassar reads it the same way, *bimā akhrajnā minhā min nabāt shattā,* "split by the diverse growth We drew out of it." So the thing that cracks the locked ground is not a tool from above — it is the fragile green shoot rising from below. The Quran is showing you that life does not arrive softly. The seed must be *broken* for the shoot to escape, and then that same emerging shoot *becomes* the blade that tears the ground. The ground must be *torn* for the green to climb out — and it is the green itself, frail as it looks, that does the tearing. **Resurrection — even the small daily resurrection of a field in spring — requires a breaking-open.**

Hold onto that word. *Shaqq.* The splitting. We are going to need it before the end.

The one statement to carry: **the grammar intensifies and the sounds break, because Allah is not describing a quiet garden — He is describing the violent, total, unmistakable act of dragging life up out of the dead.**

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Fal-yanẓur* commanded us to look *through* the food, not at it. *Ṭaʿāmihi* exposed the small lie of "mine" laid over a mountain of His doing. And *ṣabban / shaqqan* let us *hear* the act itself — the breaking-open that life always costs.

Three words. A command, a possessive suffix, and an echo. And already the plate in front of us has stopped being lunch.

Now let's ask what all this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The illusion of ownership, and the creature whose name means "the one who forgets"

Let me begin where you actually live.

You walk into a kitchen. You open a door, and there is light and cold and food. You take out eggs, bread, fruit. Twenty minutes later there is a meal on a plate, and you say — without a flicker of doubt — *"I made dinner."*

Now trace one egg backward. The egg came from a hen you never met, fed on grain you never planted, grown in soil you never tilled, watered by rain you did not summon, warmed by a sun you did not light, on a planet tilted at an angle you did not set, orbiting in a system you did not design. You participated in *the last four feet* of a supply chain that stretches back to the beginning of the cosmos — and you took credit for the whole thing because you *cracked it into a pan.*

[PAUSE]

This is not a moral failing unique to you. The Quran tells you it is *structural.* It is built into the name of the species.

Look at what Allah calls the human here. Not *ʿabd* (servant). Not *bashar* (flesh-being). He says **ٱلْإِنسَٰن — *al-insān.*** And the root — **أ-ن-س (ʾ-n-s)**, confirmed in the corpus — is one of the most loaded roots in the language. The lexicographers connect *insān* to two things at once. To *uns* — intimacy, familiarity, the warmth of companionship. And, in a reading the classical scholars repeatedly note, to *nisyān* — **forgetting.** *Al-insān: the one who forgets.* The creature so familiar with his blessings that he forgets they were ever given.

Do you see how perfectly the name fits the crime? *Familiarity* is exactly what *breeds the forgetting.* The food is so *daily,* so *close,* so *reliably there* that the human stops seeing it as a gift at all. He has eaten ten thousand meals and thanked the Provider for almost none of them — *not* because he is evil, but because he is *insān.* Because the things nearest to us are the things we stop seeing first.

[PAUSE]

And this is the genius of *why food.* Go back to the question from the start. Why would Allah, wanting to humble the proud human, point not at a galaxy but at a grain of wheat?

*Because the galaxy was never the problem.* No one forgets to be awed by a galaxy. We forget to be awed by *lunch.* The proof had to be placed *exactly* where the forgetting lives — in the most ordinary corner of the day — or it would prove nothing. Allah does not meet the arrogant human at the edge of the universe. He meets him at the kitchen table, where the man is least defended, and says: *look. Even here. Especially here. You did not make this.*

So what shifts tomorrow?

Here is the practice, and it is almost embarrassingly small — which is the whole point. Before you eat, *for the length of one breath,* run the chain backward. Not a long meditation. One breath. The water — *poured.* The earth — *split.* The green thing on this plate — *grown,* by a hand that was not mine, while I did nothing but show up at the end with a fork. That single breath is *naẓar al-qalb.* It is the heart doing what the surah commanded the eyes to make possible. And a person who takes that one breath three times a day has thanked his Lord a thousand times a year for a thing he used to swallow in silence.

*Al-insān* forgets. The remedy is not to become a different creature. The remedy is one breath of remembering, pressed against the most forgettable moment of the day.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two — There is a resurrection hidden in your lunch

Now I want to ask the question we have been circling since the introduction, the one that unlocks the whole passage. *Why does Surah Abasa spend nine verses on agriculture?*

Look at where this surah is *going.* A few lines after our passage, the sky breaks: *fa-idhā jāʾati ṣ-ṣākhkhah* — "and when the Deafening Blast comes." The Day of Resurrection. The Day a man flees from his brother, his mother, his father, his own children — each soul that day with a concern that swallows everything else. The surah ends in the graveyard of the cosmos, with the dead rising and the faces splitting into two camps: lit and dust-covered.

So this passage about food is *not* a pleasant nature interlude dropped between two terrors. It is the **bridge** between them. It is the *argument.* And here is how it works — and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

[PAUSE]

Stand in a field in the dead of winter. The ground is hard, brown, locked. Nothing in it. By every appearance it is *finished* — a flat expanse of death. A person standing there who had never seen a spring would swear, on everything, that nothing could ever come out of that ground again.

Then the water comes. *Ṣabban* — poured, total. And the earth — *shaqqan* — **splits open.** And up out of what was provably, visibly *dead* comes grain, and vines, and olive trees, and date-palms heavy with fruit, and whole gardens *ghulban* — so densely treed they tangle into each other. Out of the dead ground. Every single year. In front of your eyes. *You have personally witnessed the dead being brought back to life so many times that you stopped finding it remarkable.*

Now hear the word again — the one I told you to hold. *Shaqq.* The splitting. The Quran uses that *same root* for the great Day: *idhā s-samāʾu nshaqqat* — "when the sky is *split open*"; the earth too is split, *shuqqiqat,* for the dead to climb out. **The splitting of the ground for a seed and the splitting of the ground for a body are described with the same verb — because in the sight of Allah they are the same act.** And remember what the commentators told us about *who* does the splitting in the field: the earth is torn open *by the life rising through it.* On the great Day it will be no different — the ground will be split not by the buried staying buried, but by the dead being pushed up into the light.

[PAUSE]

This is the logic the surrounding surahs make explicit. In Sūrat Qāf, Allah lists this exact sequence — the water, the grain, the gardens, the tall palms — and then closes it with five words that detonate the whole image: *kadhālika l-khurūj* — **"*Thus* is the coming-forth"** (50:11). *That* — the rising of crops from dead soil — *that is what your resurrection looks like.* In Sūrat Fuṣṣilat: "you see the earth barren, then when We send water on it, it stirs and swells… the One who gave *it* life is the One who gives the dead life" (41:39). Same argument, again and again.

So watch what Allah has actually done in our passage. He told the proud human, *fal-yanẓur* — look at your food. The man thinks he has been sent to consider his *lunch.* And he has been, without realizing it, **walked straight up to the edge of his own grave and shown the way out of it.** The evidence for the thing he most denies — that the dead can rise — *he eats it.* It is on his plate. It was in the field. It happens every spring whether he believes in it or not. **He has been swallowing the proof of the resurrection his entire life and calling it dinner.**

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what a meal *means.* The next time something grown comes to your table — a grape, an olive, a date, a piece of bread — let it testify. It came up out of dead ground. It is a small, edible rehearsal of the Day you will be raised. And the same Power that did not find it hard to push that green shoot up through locked winter soil will not find it hard to bring *you* up through the earth. The doubt the human carries — *"how could I possibly be brought back once I am dust?"* — is answered, three times a day, on his own plate. **You do not need to imagine the resurrection. You need only to notice your food.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and look at the shape of the whole thing, because the structure is itself the proof.

The passage opens with the human and a command — *fal-yanẓur* — and a possessive: *his* food. It then floods the middle with the action of God — *We* poured, *We* split, *We* grew — eight gifts spilling out in a percussive list: grain, grapes, herbage, olives, palms, gardens, fruit, pasture. And it closes on a single word that resolves everything: **مَّتَٰعًۭا لَّكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ — "a provision for *you* and for your *livestock.*"**

Look at that ending. And notice that the cattle's share was not sprung on us at the last word — it was seeded halfway up the list. *Qaḍb,* which we rendered "fresh herbage," al-Muyassar glosses precisely as *ʿalaf li-l-dawābb,* "fodder for the grazing animals," and al-Jalālayn pins it tighter still as *al-qatt al-raṭb* — fresh, moist clover, the green cut for beasts. So while the human was reading the list as a menu of *his* delicacies — al-Jalālayn even names the *ḥabb* for him, *ka-l-ḥinṭa wa-l-shaʿīr,* "like wheat and barley" — the field was, the whole time, also quietly growing the cattle's dinner. And the word right before the close, *abb,* is one the classical scholars openly differed over: al-Jalālayn records both readings — *mā tarʿāhu al-bahāʾim,* "what the grazing animals pasture on," and then, with an honest *wa qīla,* "and it is said," the alternative *al-tibn,* the straw or hay. The exact botany is contested; the direction is not. Whichever way you read it, the word is feeding the animals. The closing word *li-anʿāmikum* only says out loud what the passage had been providing all along.

The human began the passage standing *over* his food, calling it *"mine."* He ends the passage standing *beside the cattle* — *"for you, and for your grazing animals."* The same rain, the same earth, the same gift, feeds the proud human and the dumb beast from one table. The arrogant creature who frowned at a blind man, who thought himself too important for the seeker — Allah seats him at the end of this passage *next to the livestock,* both of them fed by a Hand that is not their own. The arrogance that opened the surah is dismantled by the last word of the passage.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes were one insight approached from two sides.

The word *fal-yanẓur* commanded a looking of the *heart.* The word *ṭaʿāmihi* exposed the lie of *"mine."* The sounds *ṣabban* and *shaqqan* let us *hear* the dead earth break open. And those three discoveries deliver the two truths whole: that you are *al-insān,* the forgetter, who calls God's gift his own — and that the cure and the proof are the same thing, sitting on your plate, where the daily resurrection of a field quietly rehearses your own.

One command. One possessive suffix. One echoing sound. And from them: the dismantling of human pride, and the demonstration of the Day of Standing — all without sending you one step further than your own dinner.

### Questions to carry

1. When did I last *look* at my food — *naẓar al-qalb,* the looking of the heart — instead of merely eating it?
2. How many things in my life do I call *"mine"* whose entire making belonged to Someone else?
3. What is the *familiarity* in my life that has bred *forgetting* — the blessing so reliable I have stopped seeing it as a blessing at all?
4. If a dead field returns to life in front of me every spring, where exactly does my doubt about my own resurrection come from?
5. Am I, in some quiet way, still the one who honors what looks powerful and turns from what is truly seeking — the inversion this whole surah came to correct?

### One-sentence distillation

Allah sent the proud, forgetful human no further than his own plate — because the food he calls "his" is built entirely by God's hand, and the dead earth that yields it is, three times a day, the proof of the resurrection he denies.

### The closing invitation

Tomorrow you will eat. You will eat something that, not long ago, was buried in dead ground, split open by a pouring of water you did not send, and pushed up into the light by a Power you did not lend. You can swallow it in silence, the way *al-insān* always has. Or you can take one breath — *look* at it — and let the most ordinary moment of your day become the moment you remember your Lord, your dependence, and your rising. The invitation is not to eat differently. It is to *see* what you were already eating.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, who poured the water and split the earth and brought living green out of the dead ground — open the eye of our heart, that we may *look* and not merely glance. Forgive us the small lie of "mine" that we have laid over the mountain of Your giving. And as You raise the seed from the locked winter soil, raise us on that Day among the faces that are lit, not among the faces covered in dust. Āmīn.
