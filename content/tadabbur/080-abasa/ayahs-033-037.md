# The Day You Flee from Everyone: The Deafening Blast and the Concern That Consumes

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **فَإِذَا جَآءَتِ ٱلصَّآخَّةُ ۝ يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ ٱلْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ ۝ وَأُمِّهِۦ وَأَبِيهِ ۝ وَصَٰحِبَتِهِۦ وَبَنِيهِ ۝ لِكُلِّ ٱمْرِئٍۢ مِّنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍۢ شَأْنٌۭ يُغْنِيهِ**

Transliteration:

> *Fa-idhā jāʾati ṣ-ṣākhkhah. Yawma yafirru l-marʾu min akhīh. Wa-ummihi wa-abīh. Wa-ṣāḥibatihi wa-banīh. Li-kulli mri'in minhum yawma'idhin sha'nun yughnīh.*

And in plain English:

> "But when the Deafening Blast comes — the Day a man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his companion and his children — for every person among them, that Day, there will be a concern enough to consume him."

### Let's rebuild the world this lands in

To feel these verses, you have to hear what comes immediately before them. Because the Quran does not drop this scene out of nowhere. It has just spent eight verses doing something gentle, almost tender.

Go back a few lines in this same surah. Allah turns the human being's gaze downward, to the plate in front of him: *"So let man look at his food."* And then He opens it up. **He** sent the rain pouring down. **He** split the earth open — cracked it, like a seed-case breaking. And out of that cracked ground He brought grain. And grapes. And greens. And olives and date-palms. And dense walled gardens. And fruit. And pasture. And then the closing phrase: *"a provision for you — and for your cattle."*

Sit in that for a moment. It is a scene of abundance. Of rain and green and harvest. Of a world arranged, down to the smallest detail, to *feed* you. To keep you and the animals you depend on alive. It is the most ordinary, comforting picture imaginable — the world as a table set for your sustenance.

And then — one word. **فَإِذَا.** *"But when."*

[PAUSE]

The verse pivots so hard you can almost feel the floor tilt. From the garden to the Blast. From the rain that feeds you to the Sound that ends you. al-Qurtubi catches exactly what is happening here. He says: *when Allah had mentioned the matter of the living — the food, the harvest, the sustenance — He then mentioned the matter of the return,* so that people would store up provision **of a different kind** for that Day. The grain and the olives were provision for the body. Now the surah asks: and what have you stored for the Sound that is coming?

There is one more layer to this world. Remember how this surah *opens*. It opens with a frown — *ʿabasa* — the Prophet ﷺ turning his face toward the influential men of Quraysh and away from a poor blind man who came seeking guidance. The whole surah is, in a sense, a correction of our instinct about **who matters** and **what matters**. We are pulled toward status, toward the well-connected, toward our people.

And the surah will end — right here, in these five verses — by showing us the Day when *all* of those connections, every single one of them, comes apart in our hands.

### The hook

So here is what I want you to carry into this. There is a single word in this passage for the Day of Resurrection — a word used nowhere else in the entire Quran in this form. It is a word that, the scholars tell us, does two opposite things at the same time: it **deafens** every ear, and it makes every ear **strain to listen**. A sound that shuts you down and seizes your full attention in the same instant.

And by the end of these five verses, you will meet a second thing that does *exactly* that to a human being — silences everything else and commands his total attention. And you will see that the Quran has built these verses so that the Sound and the Self mirror each other perfectly.

Let's go and find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: ٱلصَّآخَّة — the Sound that is also a name

Let me teach you something about how Arabic builds words before we open this one.

In English, when we want to name an event, we usually name *what happens*. "The explosion." "The collapse." We describe the event from outside. But Arabic has a habit the Quran uses with devastating effect: it will sometimes name a thing not by what it *is*, but by what it *does to you*. It will turn the effect into the name.

Here is an everyday example. Imagine you don't call a particular person "the speaker." You call him "the one who silences the room." You have named him by his effect on you. Now his name *contains* your experience. Every time you say it, you re-live being silenced.

That is what is happening in verse 33.

Allah does not say "when the Day comes," or "when the Resurrection comes." He says:

> **فَإِذَا جَآءَتِ ٱلصَّآخَّةُ**
> *"But when the **Ṣākhkhah** comes."*

Now. **ٱلصَّآخَّة.** Say it slowly. *Aṣ-Ṣākhkhah.*

[PAUSE]

Feel what your mouth does. It starts with the **ص** — that heavy, emphatic *Ṣ*, a sound that pushes from deep and tight in the throat. Then a long, stretched **آ**. And then — the heart of the word — a **doubled خّ**. The *kh*, doubled, held. That guttural scrape, twice. And it closes on a clipped *-ah*.

This is not a smooth word. You cannot say it gently. The throat constricts to produce it. It is a word that *scrapes*. And here is the principle we hold onto in this kind of reflection: in the Quran, words that are difficult and harsh to *say* very often name realities that are difficult and harsh to *endure*. The phonetic texture is not decoration. The word sounds like what it means. The Ṣākhkhah even *feels* like a blast in your own mouth.

Now let's open what it actually means, because the lexicon is extraordinary here.

The root is **ص-خ-خ**. The verb *ṣakhkha* means: to strike the hearing with a sound so violent it deafens. The form the Quran uses — ** صَآخَّة** — is an intensive pattern, the kind of form Arabic reserves for something whose very nature, whose *profession*, is to do this. Not a sound that happens to be loud. **The Deafener.** The one whose entire identity is to overwhelm the ears.

Listen to what the classical commentators say, and watch them, almost without meaning to, build a single composite picture from two directions.

al-Baghawi says: *it is the cry of the Resurrection, called the Ṣākhkhah because it "overwhelms the hearing until it nearly deafens it."* It pushes into the ears past the point they can hold.

al-Qurtubi gives that same meaning — *taṣukhkh al-asmāʿ*, it deafens the ears so that they hear nothing — and then he reports something beautiful from "a group of the commentators." They connect it to the verb *aṣākha*, which means **to strain to listen, to incline your ear toward something completely.** And he cites the narration that on the day of Jumuʿah, every creature is *muṣīkhah* — straining, listening, in dread of the Hour.

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened? Hold both of those in your hand at once.

The Ṣākhkhah **deafens you** — it blots out every other sound, every voice, every distraction, the entire ocean of noise you have lived your whole life inside. *And* the Ṣākhkhah **makes you strain to listen** — it seizes your hearing and bends it toward itself, so that you can attend to *nothing else*.

These are not two competing opinions where you pick one. This is one reality seen from two angles. A sound so total that it simultaneously **shuts down everything else** and **commands your complete attention.** Every other claim on your ears — gone. Only this.

Notice one more thing, the grammar of arrival. The verb is **جَآءَتِ** — *jāʾat* — "it came." Past tense. Perfect. But the event is in the future; it hasn't happened yet. Why the past?

This is one of the Quran's great moves. When Allah wants to tell you that a future event is *absolutely certain* — so certain that to His knowledge it is as good as already done — He speaks of it in the past. *"When it came."* Not "if it comes." Not "when it might come." It is reported as accomplished fact. The Ṣākhkhah is not a possibility on a distant horizon. In the grammar, it has already arrived.

And which blast is it? This matters more than it first appears. The Quran speaks of the trumpet being sounded more than once — a first blast at which everything living is struck down, and a second at which the dead are raised and rise to their feet (you can hear both in Surah al-Zumar, 39:68: *"the trumpet is blown and all in the heavens and earth fall dead, except whom Allah wills — then it is blown again, and at once they are standing, looking on"*). al-Jalalayn pins the Ṣākhkhah down to one of these precisely: it is *al-nafkha al-thāniya*, **the second blast** — not the one that ends life, but the one that *gives it back* in order to summon it. al-Muyassar names it the same way: *ṣayḥat al-baʿth*, the **Cry of Resurrection**, the sound "that deafens the hearing by its sheer terror." So sit with what that does to the word. This Deafener is not the noise of the world dying. It is the noise of the world being *called to stand*. The ear it overwhelms is an ear that has only just been given back its hearing — and given it back for one purpose: to answer.

So here is what this single word gives us before we move a step further: **The Day is named after the experience of total interruption.** A sound that ends the world of distractions and bends your whole being toward one thing. Remember that — *total absorption, everything else silenced* — because we are going to meet it again.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: the order of the fleeing

Now Allah does something the human heart does not expect. He tells us what we will *do* when that Sound comes. And it is not what you would guess.

You might expect: when the terror comes, people will run **to** each other. They will grab their children. They will look for their mother. We cling together in catastrophe — that is the deepest human reflex. When the building shakes, you reach for the person beside you.

The verse says the opposite.

> **يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ ٱلْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ**
> *"The Day a man **flees** — from his brother."*

The verb is **يَفِرُّ** — *yafirru*, from the root **ف-ر-ر**. This is not "walks away" or "leaves." *Firār* is the flight of someone running for his life — the way you flee from a fire, from a predator, from a collapsing wall. It is escape driven by terror. al-Muyassar makes the cause explicit: he flees *li-hawl dhālik al-yawm* — **from the sheer horror of that Day**. The verb is in the present tense, which in Arabic makes it vivid and ongoing — you *see* it happening, the running, right now, in front of you.

And he flees **from his brother**. From *akhīh*. The peer. The one who shared your childhood, your blood, your home.

Then watch the list unfold. Allah does not just say "his family." He names them, one by one, in a deliberate sequence, and the sequence is the whole point.

> *"and his **mother** and his **father**..."*
> **وَأُمِّهِۦ وَأَبِيهِ**

> *"and his **companion** and his **children**."*
> **وَصَٰحِبَتِهِۦ وَبَنِيهِ**

Let's sit with this order, because it is built with great care.

It begins with the **brother** — your peer, the one beside you, your equal. Then it rises to those *above* you: **mother**, then **father** — the two who made you, carried you, raised you, the source you came from. Then it moves to those who came *from* you and *to* you by your own choosing: your **companion**, the one you chose to walk through life with, and your **children**, the ones who came from you, who are in a sense the very extension of your own self into the future.

Many of the commentators note that this is an order of *ascending closeness and ascending love*. It does not move randomly. It moves toward the heart. The mother is mentioned before the father — and the one whose claim on tenderness is deepest, the mother, comes first among the parents. And the list lands, finally, on **بَنِيه** — *his children.* The ones for whom a human being will sacrifice anything. The last thing any of us would ever run *from*.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the cruelty of the design — and the mercy of it? The Quran walks you up the staircase of everyone you love, rung by rung, brother, mother, father, spouse, child — each one more beloved than the last — and at every single rung the verb is the same. *Yafirru. He flees. He flees. He flees.* Even from the child.

And notice the **absence**. Arabic has a perfectly good, common word for "wife" — *zawjah*. The Quran does not use it here. It uses **صَاحِبَة** — *ṣāḥibah*, from the root **ص-ح-ب**, the root of *companionship*. A *ṣāḥib* is the one who *accompanies* you, who is *with* you, the chosen partner of your road. Why this word and not "wife"?

Because *ṣāḥibah* names the **relationship of being-together-on-the-journey** itself. It is the most relational word available. And that is precisely the bond the verse is dissolving. Allah did not pick the legal word; He picked the word that means *the one who was always at your side* — and shows even *that* one being fled from. The deepest companionship of this world comes undone.

And here is a connection that will take your breath. Go to Surah al-Maʿārij, chapter 70, verses 11 through 14. There, Allah describes the guilty person on that same Day, and He lists almost the *exact same people*: *"he would wish to ransom himself from the punishment of that Day by his children, and his companion (ṣāḥibatihi), and his brother..."* The very same words — *banīh, ṣāḥibah, akh*.

But look at the difference. In Surah al-Maʿārij, he wants to give them *away* — to push them into the fire *in his place* — to ransom himself **with** them. Here, in Abasa, he simply **flees from** them. Put the two together and you have the full collapse: on that Day a human being will neither cling to those he loves, nor even keep them as something precious — he will run from them, and given the chance, hand them over to save himself.

[PAUSE]

So this second element gives us: **the Day undoes the very relationships out of which we built our entire sense of who we are.** Brother, parent, spouse, child — every line that connected you to another human being, severed. And the verb is not "left." It is *fled from* — in terror.

But *why*? What could possibly make a mother flee her own son? That is the question the final verse answers. And the answer is the most precise word in the whole passage.

### Element Three: شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ — the concern that makes him rich in his ruin

> **لِكُلِّ ٱمْرِئٍۢ مِّنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍۢ شَأْنٌۭ يُغْنِيهِ**
> *"For every person among them, that Day, there will be a **concern enough to consume him**."*

Two words carry this. Let's take them in turn.

First: **شَأْن** — *shaʾn*. From the root **ش-أ-ن**. A *shaʾn* is a *matter*, an *affair*, a *concern* — but specifically a matter of such weight and gravity that it occupies the whole of your attention. It is the word you'd use for "the thing he is utterly preoccupied with." Not a task on a list. *The* matter. The one consuming you.

And notice it is **شَأْنٌ** — indefinite. *A* concern. Unspecified. The Quran does not tell us *what* the concern is. Why leave it open? Because the concern is *different for every single person* — and because the human being on that Day will not be thinking in categories or explanations. He will simply be **inside** it, swallowed by it, his own particular dread. Each one has *his own* shaʾn. *Li-kulli mri'in minhum* — "for **each** person among them." The crowd has dissolved into a billion separate, sealed terrors, each one alone inside his own.

Now the word that detonates the whole passage: **يُغْنِيهِ** — *yughnīh*.

This is from the root **غ-ن-ي**. And I want you to know that root, because you already know it. It is the root of *ghinā* — **richness. wealth. self-sufficiency. having-enough-that-you-need-no-one.** It is the root behind one of Allah's own names: **al-Ghaniyy** — The Self-Sufficient, The One Free of All Need. When you say someone is *ghaniyy*, you mean: he is so full that he requires nothing from anyone.

The verb here is the *fourth form*, *aghnā / yughnī*, which carries the sense of *making someone sufficient, making them have-enough, occupying them so fully that they need nothing — and no one — else.*

So *shaʾnun yughnīh* means, on the surface, exactly what al-Muyassar and the commentators tell us: **a concern that occupies him and bars him from any concern with anyone else** — *amrun yamnaʿuhu min al-inshighāl bi-ghayrih*, a matter that prevents him from being occupied with anyone but himself. A preoccupation so total it leaves no room for another soul. *That* is why he flees his mother — not from hatred, but because there is **no attention left over for her.** She has fallen entirely outside the walls of his own consuming dread.

[PAUSE]

But now feel the terrible irony the *root* puts into this.

The root of **needing no one** — the root of **wealth** and **self-sufficiency** — is the very root chosen to describe the most **destitute** moment a human being will ever face. The person standing before his Lord with nothing, stripped of every relationship, every protector, every helper — and the Quran says his concern *yughnīh*: it "makes him sufficient unto it," it "leaves him needing nothing else."

He has finally become *self-sufficient* — but only in the sense that he has been emptied of everyone. He needs no one now, not because he is full, but because his terror has consumed every other need. It is *ghinā* turned inside out: the richness of the utterly bankrupt. The only thing he "has enough of" is his own dread.

And now — bring it back to where we started. To the **Ṣākhkhah.**

Remember what the Sound did? It **shut down every other sound** and **commanded his total attention.** And now look at his *shaʾn*: it **shuts out every other person** and **commands his total attention.** The Sound that opened the passage and the Self that closes it do the *exact same thing*. The Ṣākhkhah deafens him to the world; the shaʾn blinds him to everyone he loves. The outer reality and the inner reality are mirror images. The blast that no ear can hold becomes the concern that no heart can look past.

[PAUSE — major pause]

So that is the linguistic architecture. A Day named after a sound that overwhelms and commands — the very Cry that raises the dead to stand; a flight up the staircase of everyone we love; and a concern that, sharing its root with *self-sufficiency*, leaves a person rich only in his own ruin, needing no one because aware of no one.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the way you and I are living right now.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: the relationships we think are *us*

Let me start where you actually live.

Ask yourself, quietly: *who am I?* And notice how, almost instantly, the answer arrives wrapped in other people. I am someone's son or daughter. I am a mother, a father. I am his wife, her husband. I am the brother who shows up, the friend they call first. We do not experience ourselves as solitary points. We experience ourselves as **knots in a net** — and the net is the people we love and who love us. Pull on any thread and you feel it in your chest.

This is not a flaw. It is how Allah made us. *"And among His signs is that He created for you, from yourselves, mates, that you might find rest in them."* The companion, the *ṣāḥibah*, is a mercy. The child is *the adornment of this worldly life*. These bonds are real and they are gifts.

But here is what we *do* with the gift. We do not just *enjoy* the net — we **become** it. We make our relationships the floor we stand on. Our worth, our safety, our very sense of being a real and solid person — we quietly outsource all of it to the people around us. "As long as I have my family, I have everything." And so, without ever deciding to, we begin to lean the full weight of our soul on bonds that were never built to bear it.

Now read the verse again as a diagnosis, not a threat.

> *"The Day a man flees from his brother, and his mother and his father, and his companion and his children."*

Allah is not being cruel here. He is being *honest* with us about the load-bearing capacity of what we have leaned on. He walks us up the whole staircase — brother, mother, father, spouse, child — and shows us, rung by rung, that **not one of them will be standing under you on that Day.** Not because they don't love you. Your mother loved you with a love that is itself a sign of God. But on that Day she has her own *shaʾn*. She is inside her own consuming concern. The net you wove your identity into simply isn't there.

[PAUSE]

And feel the precision of *who* gets named. If the Quran had only said "his neighbors" or "his acquaintances," we would shrug — of course those fade. But it names the **innermost ring.** The people for whom you would, in this life, walk into fire. The verse is not telling you that your weak bonds will fail. It is telling you that your *strongest* bonds — the ones you were *certain* of — will not be where you stand on the only Day that finally matters.

There is a mercy buried in this terror, and I don't want you to miss it. If your relationships are not the floor — then losing them is not the end of you. The person who has understood verse 34 is *freed*, a little, from the tyranny of needing everyone's approval, everyone's presence, everyone's love, to feel real. Because he knows there is a deeper floor underneath the net. There is **One** who will not have a *shaʾn* that distracts Him from you. There is One whose attention does not run out, who does not flee, who is *al-Ghaniyy* — needing nothing from you — and yet turns toward you completely.

So what does this change tomorrow? Here is the practical edge. Tonight, take the one relationship you are most *afraid* of losing — the person whose absence would feel like the floor dropping out — and consciously hand them back to Allah. Not by loving them less. By loving them as a *trust* (amānah), a gift on loan, rather than as your foundation. Say it plainly: *"O Allah, this person is Yours. You are my floor; they are Your gift. Let me love them without leaning my whole soul on them."* That single reframe is the difference between a love that enslaves you to fear and a love that is free.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: the all-consuming concern — and which one will it be?

Let me start, again, with something you have actually felt.

Think of a time you were so consumed by a single overwhelming thing that the whole world went quiet around it. Maybe it was the night before a result that would decide your future. Maybe a phone call in the dark with terrible news. Maybe a moment of acute physical fear. And remember what it did to your attention. Someone could be talking to you and you would not hear them. Your own child could tug your sleeve and for a few seconds you would not register it. The crisis *filled the entire frame.* There was no room — literally no spare attention — for anything or anyone else.

We have a word for it in English now: we say we were "consumed." We say something "took over." We say "I couldn't think about anything else."

That experience — the one you already know — is the *exact* experience the verse names. **شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ.** *A concern that consumes him.* The Quran is not describing an alien state. It is taking the most overwhelming moment you have ever lived and saying: *that — but total, and for everyone, all at once.*

This is the answer to the question we left hanging. *Why* does the mother flee her son? Now you know. Not because love died. Because her *shaʾn yughnīhā* — her own concern has consumed every scrap of her attention. She is inside the crisis so completely that the son, whom she would have died for yesterday, has simply fallen outside the frame. There is no cruelty in it. There is only the terrible arithmetic of total preoccupation: when one concern takes *everything*, there is nothing left for anyone else.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the movement I want you to make with me, because this is where the verse stops being about *them* on that Day and becomes about *you* tonight.

That Day, **everyone** will have a *shaʾn* that consumes them. That is not in question. The only question — the *entire* question — is **which concern will it be.**

Because there are two completely different ways to be consumed on that Day.

There is the *shaʾn* of the one who arrives having stored no provision — the one the surah warned about when it pivoted from the harvest to the Blast. His consuming concern is raw terror. *What is happening to me. Where do I go. What did I do.* He is rich only in dread — *ghinā* turned to ruin. His total attention is bent toward his own catastrophe, and that is why he flees everyone.

And there is the *shaʾn* of the one whose whole attention, on that Day, is bent toward **his Lord** — consumed, yes, but consumed by *hope*, by standing before the Face he spent his life trying to turn toward, by the weight of *this is the moment everything was for.* He too is consumed. He too has no attention to spare. But what fills his frame is not horror. It is the One he has been walking toward.

Both are *yughnīh*. Both leave no room for anyone else. The difference between them is not decided on that Day. **It is decided now.** It is decided by what you let consume you in *this* life. Because the thing that fills your frame at the end will be the thing you trained your attention on at the beginning.

[PAUSE]

And this is exactly why the surah set the harvest right before the Blast. *"Let man look at his food."* The grain, the olives, the gardens — Allah was showing you how He prepared, in advance, every detail of your bodily provision *before* you ever needed it. The rain fell, the earth split, the seed grew — long before the meal. The lesson is not subtle: **provision is stored before the need arrives.** The body's provision was arranged before your hunger. And the soul's provision — the only thing that will determine *which shaʾn* consumes you — must be stored before the Ṣākhkhah, not after.

So the practical edge, the thing to carry into tomorrow: notice what currently *consumes* you. Notice what fills your frame when the world goes quiet — what your mind runs to the instant it is unoccupied, the concern that can make you not hear your own child tugging your sleeve. Because *that* — whatever it is — is what you are training to be your *shaʾn* on the Day of the Blast. If your consuming concern right now is provision, status, the opinion of "your people" — the very things Surah Abasa opened by correcting — then ask, gently and honestly: *am I storing the right provision? Is the thing that fills my frame the thing I want bent toward me at the end?* You still have time to change what consumes you. That is the entire mercy of being told this **before**.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and see how these five verses were built, because the structure *is* the argument.

The passage opens and closes on the **same shape**, and that is not an accident.

It **opens** with the **Ṣākhkhah** — a sound that does two things: it **deafens** you to everything else, and it **commands** your entire attention. Total interruption from *outside*.

It **closes** with the **shaʾn yughnīh** — a concern that does two things: it **shuts out** everyone else, and it **commands** your entire attention. Total interruption from *inside*.

And between those two mirror-images — the Sound and the Self — sits the **fleeing**: the staircase of everyone you love, brother to child, every bond severed by the verb *yafirru*. The outer Blast and the inner Concern close like two hands around that severing, and explain it. *Why* does he flee everyone he loves? Because the Sound has deafened him to the world and the Concern has consumed his every thought. The architecture answers its own question.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight seen from two directions.

The **root of yughnīh** — *ghinā*, self-sufficiency, needing no one — taught us, through pure morphology, that on that Day a person becomes "rich" only in the sense of being emptied of everyone. And the **theme** taught us, through lived experience, that we have spent our lives leaning our whole identity on the net of people around us — a net that, on that Day, will not be there.

Same insight. The word *yughnīh* is the grammar of it; your fear of losing the people you love is the felt experience of it. The verse takes the thing you most depend on — *other people* — and shows you the Day it returns to dust in your hands, so that you will, *while there is still time*, build on the only floor that holds: the One who is *al-Ghaniyy*, who needs nothing from you, and yet never flees.

### Questions to carry

- When I ask "who am I?", how much of my answer is borrowed from people who, on that Day, will have a *shaʾn* of their own?
- What consumes my attention right now — the moment the world goes quiet, what does my mind run to? Is that what I want bent toward me at the end?
- The surah opened by correcting *who* and *what* we prioritize. If I am honest, am I still turning toward the "influential" and away from what actually matters?
- Have I stored provision for the Ṣākhkhah the way Allah stored the harvest before my hunger — *in advance*, before the need arrives?
- Is there a person I love whom I have quietly made into my *floor* instead of my *gift*?

### One-sentence distillation

When the Sound comes that deafens every ear and seizes every heart, each soul will be so consumed by its own concern that it flees even those it loved most — so store now, while there is time, the only provision that decides *which* concern will consume you.

### The closing invitation

This is not a verse meant to leave you cold with fear. It is meant to *relocate your weight* — gently, off the net of people that was never built to hold a soul, and onto the One who alone holds. Love your brother, your mother, your father, your companion, your children — love them more, even, now that you know they are gifts on loan and not foundations. But lean your soul on the One who will not, on that Day, have a *shaʾn* that turns Him away from you. Begin, today, to make Him the thing that consumes your frame — so that when the Ṣākhkhah comes, your total attention is already bent, by long habit, toward the Face you were always meant to turn to.

### A brief du'a

O Allah — You who are *al-Ghaniyy*, free of all need, and yet never turn away — on the Day the Blast deafens every ear and every soul flees the ones it loved, let the concern that consumes me be **You.** Let my whole attention, on that Day, be bent toward Your Face and not toward my dread. And teach me, before that Day, to love the people You gave me as Your gifts, while leaning the full weight of my soul on You alone. *Āmīn.*
