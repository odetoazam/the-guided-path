# As If They Were Rubies and Coral

## The Ayah

> <!-- ayah:55:58 -->     كَأَنَّهُنَّ ٱلْيَاقُوتُ وَٱلْمَرْجَانُ

*Ka-annahunna al-yāqūtu wa al-marjān.*

**"As if they were rubies and coral."**

---

## Introduction: The Surah That Cannot Stop Counting

Before we come anywhere near these six words, we have to stand inside the surah they live in. Because *Ar-Rahman* is not like the surahs around it, and if you don't feel what makes it strange, this ayah will slide past you as a pretty line of jewelry — and it is so much more than that.

Sit for a moment with how this surah opens. Not with *Bismillah* alone, not with a story, not with a command. It opens with a single, staggering Name — **Ar-Rahman** — hanging by itself, and then a list begins. *He taught the Qur'an. He created the human being. He taught him clear expression.* The sun and the moon run by exact calculation. The star and the tree prostrate. He raised the sky and set down the balance. And then — before you've caught your breath — the first strike of the refrain:

*So which of the favors of your Lord would you two deny?*

And it comes again. And again. And again. Thirty-one times across this surah. Like a hand tapping on the table. Like a bell that will not let you drift off. *Which one? Which one will you deny? This one? Or this one?*

Here is what the classical scholars noticed, and what I want you to feel in your chest: the whole surah is a **counting**. Allah, *Ar-Rahman* — the One whose very Name means an overwhelming, spilling-over mercy — is walking His creation, item by item, through the ledger of what He has given. And the refrain is addressed, grammatically, to **two** — *tukadhdhibān*, "you two deny." The scholars say: the jinn and mankind. Two whole worlds of created beings, stood side by side, being asked the same relentless question about the same relentless gifts.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the pivot. Around the middle of the surah, the counting of *this* world — the sun, the sea, the pearls, the ships riding the waves like mountains — turns toward the next. Everything that stands upon the earth will perish (*kullu man 'alayhā fān*), and only the Face of your Lord remains, full of majesty and honor. And then the door opens: *"And for the one who feared the standing before his Lord — two gardens."* Two. Again two. Then two more below them. Fountains, in pairs. Fruits, of every kind, in pairs. And within these gardens, a description of companions begins — *qāṣirāt al-ṭarf*, those whose gaze is restrained, kept — whom no one has ever touched.

And it is right *there*, mid-description, that our ayah lands. Cradled between two identical strikes of the refrain — the bell rings, then the ayah, then the bell rings again — Allah says of them: *ka-annahunna al-yāqūtu wa al-marjān.* **As if they were rubies and coral.**

Here is the hook. I want you to hold one small word up to the light before we begin, and refuse to let it go: **"as if."**

Not *"they are* rubies and coral." *As if.* The One who made the ruby, who knows its every atom, who could describe it down to the light bending inside it — when He turns to describe the reward, He reaches for the word **"as if."** Why would the All-Knowing, the One whose speech is perfect, describe the greatest gift in His ledger with a word of *approximation*?

Stay with me. That small word is a door, and behind it is nearly everything.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### The First Element: كَأَنَّ — The Word That Admits a Limit

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the ayah, because you need the tool in your hand before I hand you the treasure.

In Arabic there is a family of little words whose entire job is to draw a line between two things and say *this resembles that.* The simplest is the letter *kāf* — you stick it onto the front of a word and it means "like." *Ka-l-asad* — "like a lion." Simple resemblance.

But our ayah does not use the simple *kāf*. It uses something heavier: **كَأَنَّ** — *ka-anna*. And this word is a fusion. It is the *kāf* of resemblance melted together with **أَنَّ**, the particle that means "*that*" — the same particle you use to assert something firmly, to say *"I am certain that..."* So *ka-anna* is a strange and beautiful hybrid: it carries the **firmness of assertion** and the **honesty of comparison** in the same breath. It is not a weak, throwaway "sort of like." Arabic grammarians call it a particle of *tashbīh* — likening — and they note that *ka-anna* is used specifically when the resemblance is *strong*, when you are almost, *almost* ready to say the two things are the same.

Think about how you actually use this in your own life. When something moves you past the edge of language, you don't say "that was nice." You say: *"I swear, it was as if the whole room stopped breathing."* You reach for *"as if"* not because you're being vague — but because the plain words underneath you have given out, and the only honest thing left to do is to point at something else and say *it was like that.* **"As if" is the sound a person makes at the border of what they can say.**

Now bring that back. *Ka-annahunna.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. Everywhere in this surah, Allah has been direct. *He created. He taught. He raised the sky. He set the balance.* Blunt, sovereign verbs — no hedging, no "as if." When *Ar-Rahman* speaks about *what He does*, there is no simile, because His action needs no comparison.

But the moment the subject becomes **the reward itself** — the substance of Paradise, the beauty of what waits — the grammar changes. He does not say *"they are* rubies." He says *ka-anna* — **as if.** And here is what that shift is telling us, and it is not a small thing: the reality of Paradise is not *less* than a ruby, requiring a generous comparison to lift it up. It is *more* than a ruby — so much more that a direct noun would actually *shrink* it. To say "they are rubies" would trap an unseen glory inside a stone you've already held in your hand. But to say *"as if they were rubies"* is to say: *here is a doorway your mind can walk up to — now understand that what is behind it exceeds the door.*

This is the honesty of *ka-anna*. It is Allah, in perfect speech, choosing the one word that refuses to let you mistake the picture for the thing. **The "as if" is not a limit on Allah's knowledge. It is a mercy placed on your imagination** — a handrail, not a wall.

The Prophet ﷺ told us the reality directly in the sacred narration: *"I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has never crossed the heart of a human being."* No eye has seen it. So when it comes to *you* — with your eyes, your ears, your heart — the only truthful bridge is a comparison. And Allah, who does not deal in falsehood, marks that bridge honestly with a single word: *as if.*

That is what this one particle reveals. **[PAUSE]**

### The Second Element: هُنَّ — The Ones Who Are Never Named

Look again at the word — *ka-anna-**hunna**.* There is a small suffix riding at the end of that first word: **-hunna.** In Arabic that is a single pronoun: *"them,"* feminine, plural. *They — the women, the many of them.*

Here is the tool I want you to hold. A pronoun is a word that **stands in the place of a name.** It only works if you already know who is being pointed at. If I walk into a room and simply say *"they finished it,"* you are lost — *who? finished what?* A pronoun leans backward onto something already spoken; grammarians call that thing the *antecedent*, the "one who came before."

So the natural question: in *this* ayah, who is *hunna*? Who are "they"?

And here is the quiet, striking thing — **the ayah does not tell you.** There is no noun in this verse for the pronoun to lean on. Not one. To find out who "they" are, you have to go *back*, past the refrain, to two ayahs earlier, where they were described — never *named*, only described — as *qāṣirāt al-ṭarf*: "those who restrain the gaze." Even there, they are not given a name. They are a description. And now, here, they are reduced further — to a single pronoun. *Them.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah has done, and notice it as an *absence*, because the absence is the whole point. Arabic could easily have supplied a noun here. It did not. The grammar leaves a gap exactly where a name would go — and into that gap rushes a quality that runs through this entire passage: **concealment.** These are companions *whose gaze is kept, whom no one has touched, whom no eye has fallen upon.* And the language *enacts* that. It refuses to display them with a clear noun. It veils them behind a pronoun. **The grammar is doing the same thing the meaning is describing** — it is guarding, keeping, not putting on show.

Now hold that against the two gems that *are* named in full: *al-yāqūt, al-marjān.* The one thing Allah *does* spell out plainly is not the person — it is the *comparison*. You are given the jewel in sharp focus and the one it describes left deliberately soft, veiled, unspoken. And that is exactly backwards from how our world works, where the person is exposed and the jewelry is what's kept in the safe.

One honest note, so we stay grounded: that the pronoun has no antecedent *inside this single ayah* is a plain grammatical fact — you must reach to 55:56 for its referent. That Allah's choice to veil rather than name carries the *meaning* of concealment is the way this invites us to sit with it — a reading, drawn from how the whole passage behaves, not a claim stamped on the pronoun itself. But once you've felt how the surah guards these companions everywhere else, the silence where a name should be stops feeling like an accident.

That is what this small suffix reveals. **[PAUSE]**

### The Third Element: ٱلْيَاقُوت وَٱلْمَرْجَان — Two Gems, and Why These Two

Now the two jewels themselves. And I want to do two things here: I want you to *hear* them, and then I want you to see why — of all the beautiful things in creation — Allah reached for *these* two, together.

First, hear them. Say the ayah slowly. *Ka-annahunna... al-yāāāqūūūt... wa-l-marjāāān.* Feel your mouth. Both words are built on long, open, resonant vowels — the long *ā*, the long *ū* — sounds that make you *hold* the breath and let it ring. *Yāqūt* opens the throat and glows; *marjān* rolls forward and lands on that final *nūn*, a nasal hum that keeps resonating after your lips have stopped. These are not sharp, clipped words. They are words that *shine* when you say them — words whose very sound is unhurried, spacious, luminous. **The verse doesn't just describe radiance. It sounds like radiance.** The mouth does what the meaning means.

Now — why *these* two?

Here the classical scholars did something I love, and I want to build it with you the way they built it, as layers on a single image rather than a menu you pick from. When they turned to *why ruby and why coral*, they did not disagree so much as each catch a different facet of the same light.

One angle: the scholars pointed to **the ruby's clarity** — *yāqūt* is a stone you can *see into*. Its color lives *inside* it, glowing outward through a body so pure it's almost transparent. Some of the narrations describe a purity so complete you could see through many layers to the light within. So the first facet caught is *ṣafā'* — purity, clarity, a beauty with nothing hidden or muddied in it.

A second angle: they pointed to *al-marjān* and read it as **whiteness** — the pale, soft luster of coral or small pearls. So the second facet is *bayāḍ* — a clean, gentle brightness.

And when you lay those two readings on top of each other — not choosing, *combining* — you get exactly the complexion the Arabs prized as the summit of beauty: **the clear glow of the ruby's inner fire held inside the soft whiteness of coral.** Radiance and purity, warmth and calm, the color that shines through and the fairness that surrounds it — fused into one. Neither gem alone says it. The beauty is precisely in the *pairing.*

[PAUSE]

And that pairing is not random in *this* surah of all surahs. Look what Allah has been doing this whole time: *two* gardens, then *two* more. Fountains in *pairs*. Fruits, *of every kind two*. The address to *two* worlds — jinn and mankind. A refrain phrased in the *dual* — *you two.* And now, when He describes the beauty of the reward, He does not give you one gem. He gives you **two**, and lets their qualities marry. *Ar-Rahman* is a surah that sees creation in complements — and even its picture of Paradise's beauty obeys that law. **Nothing in this surah stands alone. Not even a jewel.**

One honesty check, because we hold the line here: that *yāqūt* means ruby (or a red jacinth) and *marjān* means coral or small pearls, and that both are named, definite nouns joined by "and" — that is documented. The specific readings of *clarity* and *whiteness* come from the classical commentators reading the image; I give them to you as their layered sketch of the picture, not as something the two words *say* on their own. But the sketch is old, it is careful, and it is beautiful — and it is built by people who spent their lives inside these words.

That is what these two named gems reveal. **[PAUSE]**

### The Bridge

So look at what these six words have shown us, held together. A particle — *ka-anna* — that stands honestly at the border of language and says: *the reality is more than the picture.* A pronoun — *hunna* — that veils rather than displays, guarding the one described the way the whole passage guards her. And two gems — *yāqūt* and *marjān* — that only become the full picture of beauty *when they are joined*, in a surah that joins everything.

But this was never really about jewelry.

*Ar-Rahman* does not count its favors so that you can admire them from a distance like items in a museum case. The refrain is a question aimed at *you*: *which of these will you deny?* So let's now ask what these words reveal not about stones — but about the human heart that hears them.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### The First Depth: The Grammar of Longing

Let me start somewhere you've already been.

You come back from somewhere — a mountain at dawn, the birth of a child, a night of grief you barely survived, a moment of beauty so sharp it hurt — and someone who wasn't there asks, *"What was it like?"* And you open your mouth. And you find that everything you can say is *too small.* So you start reaching. *"It was like..."* *"You know when...?"* *"I can't describe it, but imagine..."* And finally, defeated and honest, you say the truest thing available: **"You had to be there."**

Notice what you did not do. You did not conclude that the experience wasn't real because you couldn't fit it into words. You did not decide the sunset was a disappointment because your sentence was inadequate. You understood, without being told, that **the failure was in the language, not in the thing.** The reality was *bigger* than the container. And so you reached for comparison — *"it was like"* — not to diminish it, but because comparison was the only bridge wide enough to carry any of it across to another person.

[PAUSE]

Now hear the ayah again with that in your bones. *Ka-annahunna* — *as if they were.* Allah, describing a reward that no eye has seen, does the exact thing your own heart does at the edge of the sayable. He reaches for *"as if."* Not because His knowledge falls short — nothing falls short of *Ar-Rahman* — but because *your* eye has never seen it, and a comparison is the only bridge that reaches *you.* When Allah says *"as if,"* He is not being vague with you. **He is being tender with you.** He is meeting the limits of the human heart exactly where they are and building a handrail there.

And here is what that does to the way a believer lives. Because when you truly understand that Paradise is described with *"as if"* — that even the ruby, the most radiant thing you can imagine, is offered to you as a *doorway* and not the room — something shifts. You stop mistaking the gifts of *this* world for the destination. Every beautiful thing here — a face you love, light on water, the first cool air of a morning — stops being a resting place and becomes a *rumor.* A whisper. An "as if." A small, held-in-the-hand ruby that says: *there is a room, and this is only the door.*

This is what the scholars meant when they spoke of *tadabbur* moving the heart and not just the mind. It would be easy to learn what *ka-anna* means and walk away *informed.* But sit with it until it changes how you look at your own longings. You know that ache you get sometimes — the one that even good things don't fully answer, the one that's still there the morning after the wedding, still there when you finally got the thing you wanted? That ache is not a defect. **That ache is the "as if" working in you.** It is the part of you that has always known, dimly, that everything here is a comparison pointing at a reality you have not yet seen. Faith is not the killing of that longing. Faith is learning *whose door* it is knocking on.

So here is the concrete thing, for tomorrow. The next time something in this world is beautiful enough to make your chest tighten — don't grab it and try to make it the whole point. Let it be an *as if.* Let it be Allah handing you a ruby-sized piece of a door, and let the longing it stirs turn, quietly, toward the One who is keeping the room. That is a small practice. Do it for a week and watch what it does to your heart's center of gravity.

### The Second Depth: The Honor of the Hidden

Now let me take you somewhere our whole age is confused about.

We live inside a machine built to *expose.* Everything is meant to be seen — your meal, your face, your opinion, your morning, your grief, your body, your children, your worship. The unspoken law of the age is: *if it wasn't seen, it didn't count.* Value is measured in views. A moment un-posted feels almost like a moment un-lived. And under that pressure, something in us quietly starts to believe that **to be hidden is to be worthless** — that the guarded thing, the kept thing, the un-displayed thing, is somehow lesser, a failure to be noticed.

Now go and look at a ruby.

Where does a ruby *form*? Not in the open air. It grows in the crushing dark, deep inside rock, under heat and pressure, unseen for an age, touched by no one. And the coral, the pearl — *marjān* — where does *that* form? At the bottom of the sea, in the black quiet under the weight of all that water, sealed away where no eye goes. **The two most radiant things Allah could name are the two things that are made in total hiddenness.** Their glory and their concealment are not opposites. Their glory *comes from* their concealment. The pressure that hides them is the pressure that makes them.

[PAUSE]

And now hear how tightly the ayah ties this to a *person.* The companions compared to these gems were introduced, two ayahs before, as *qāṣirāt al-ṭarf* — those whose gaze is *restrained*, *kept.* Untouched. Un-looked-upon. And the grammar itself, as we saw, refuses even to *name* them — veiling them behind a pronoun. So the whole picture rhymes: a *guarded* person, described in *veiling* grammar, compared to gems that form *in hiding.* The message underneath is unmistakable and it cuts straight against the age: **in the sight of Ar-Rahman, the hidden thing is not the worthless thing. The hidden thing is the treasure.**

And this is not only about companions in Paradise. This is a law about *your* life, right now. Because remember *who* earns this whole garden in the first place: *"the one who feared the standing before his Lord."* Feared it *where?* In private. In the dark. In the un-posted, un-witnessed places — the tears no one saw, the sin you walked away from when no camera and no crowd would ever have known, the prayer in the empty room, the charity given with one hand hidden from the other. **The reward described in hidden-formed gems is the reward for a fear that formed in hidden places.** The concealment of the ruby is the concealment of your best, quietest deeds — and Allah is telling you what He does with things kept away from every eye but His. *He turns them into rubies.*

[PAUSE]

So when the world tells you that your unseen life doesn't count — the faithfulness no one applauds, the restraint no one filmed, the worship with no audience — I want you to remember where the ruby grows. I want you to remember that *Ar-Rahman*, counting His favors, chose to describe His deepest reward with the one kind of beauty that is *made in the dark and kept from every gaze.* Your hidden life is not your leftover life. In the economy of the One who sees what no one sees, **it may be the only part that is quietly turning into a jewel.**

The concrete thing, for tomorrow: find one good deed this week and *hide* it. Deliberately. Something you would normally, half-consciously, let be seen — and instead keep it entirely between you and Allah. Tell no one. Post nothing. Let it form in the dark like coral on the sea floor. And notice the strange, clean weight of it — the way a thing kept only for Him sits differently in the chest. That weight is you learning, in your body, what this ayah knows: that the guarded thing is the precious thing.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Jewel in Its Setting

Now let me show you something I held back, because it only becomes proof at the *end* — after we've done the work.

Go and look at where this ayah physically *sits* on the page of the Qur'an. Ayah 57: *"So which of the favors of your Lord would you two deny?"* Ayah 58: *"as if they were rubies and coral."* Ayah 59: *"So which of the favors of your Lord would you two deny?"* The exact same refrain, word for word, on **both sides.**

Do you see it?

The ayah about the jewel is **mounted like a jewel.** It is set between two identical refrains the way a gem is held between two matching prongs of a ring. Allah did not merely *describe* a jewel — He *set* the description into the surah in the shape of a setting. The form of the Qur'an here *performs* its own content. The verse that says *"as if they were rubies"* is itself framed, cradled, held up to the light between two matching bands of speech. **[PAUSE]** That is not something a human author reaches for. That is design so quiet you can pass it thirty times and never see it — until you've learned what the jewel inside it is worth.

### Where We Started, Where We've Arrived

And now watch the two halves of everything we've done fold into a single thing.

The **linguistic journey** showed us three moves: *ka-anna*, the honest "as if" that admits the reality is bigger than the picture; *hunna*, the veiling that guards rather than displays; and the two joined gems whose beauty lives in their pairing and whose glory is made in hiding.

The **thematic journey** showed us a heart: a *longing* that reaches for "as if" because the truest things overflow our words, and an *honor of the hidden* that knows the treasure forms in the dark.

But those are the *same insight* approached from two directions. *Ka-anna* — "as if" — is the grammar of a heart that has been taught to *long* for what it cannot yet see. And the veiling of *hunna*, the gem grown in darkness, is the shape of a life that has been taught to *treasure* what no one sees. **Longing for the unseen, and treasuring the unseen — these are one posture, not two.** They are the posture of the servant who "feared the standing before his Lord" in private and now walks, aching and faithful, toward a room whose door he has only ever been handed the ruby of.

We began by holding one small word up to the light and refusing to let it go: *as if.* And here is where it has brought us. That word was never Allah falling short of describing Paradise. It was *Ar-Rahman* — the overflowing Mercy — handing you a jewel small enough to hold, so that your heart could begin walking toward the one it cannot yet see.

### Questions to Carry

- When something in this world is beautiful enough to ache — do you try to make it the destination, or can you let it be an *"as if,"* a door pointing past itself?
- Where in your life have you started to believe that the unseen parts — the un-applauded faithfulness, the private restraint — don't really count? What would change if you truly believed those were the parts turning into rubies?
- *Ar-Rahman* pairs everything and lets nothing stand alone. Where in your own life are you trying to be a single note, when Allah's design is harmony?
- The refrain asks *you*, thirty-one times: *which of these favors will you deny?* When you are honest — which one *are* you denying, right now, by not letting it move you?
- If your most hidden deed this week were pulled out and held up to the light — would it shine like something formed under pressure in the dark, or would it be nothing at all?

### One Sentence to Carry

**When the All-Knowing could only say "as if," He was not failing to describe Paradise — He was handing you a jewel small enough to hold, so your heart could start walking toward the room it cannot yet see.**

### The Invitation

So here is what this ayah invites — not demands, *invites*. It invites you to stop grabbing at the doorways of this world as though they were the room. It invites you to let your longings turn, gently, toward the One who keeps the reward. And it invites you to go and live a *hidden* life on purpose — to fear your Lord in the private dark, to guard your best deeds from every eye but His — trusting that *Ar-Rahman* does with hidden things what pressure and darkness do with carbon and sand: **He makes them shine.**

### A Closing Du'ā

O *Ar-Rahman*, You who count Your favors so that we would not deny them — teach our hearts to read every beauty here as an "as if," a door pointing home to You. Make our hidden lives our best lives; take the deeds we keep only for You, in the dark, and let them form into something that shines on the Day we stand before You. And do not let us be among those who, having been shown the ruby, denied the One who set it before them. Āmīn.
