# Which One Will You Call a Lie?

> [ayah:55:59] فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ
>
> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*
>
> **So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Sentence You Have Heard Before

Let me begin with something strange about the ayah in front of us.

If you have ever sat in a gathering where Surah Ar-Rahman was recited, you already know this sentence. You have heard it not once, not twice, but again and again, washing over the room like a wave that keeps returning to the same shore. Thirty-one times across this single surah, Allah turns to His listeners and asks the very same question: *which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

And here is the strange thing. We have a deep instinct — built into us by every essay, every lecture, every conversation we have ever had — that repetition is a weakness. That if you want to be taken seriously, you say a thing *once*, say it well, and move on. To say the same sentence thirty-one times would get you marked down in any classroom on earth.

[PAUSE]

So either this is a flaw in the most eloquent speech ever composed in the Arabic language — or our instinct about repetition is simply wrong, and Allah is doing something here that we have not yet learned to see.

Sit with that tension, because it is the doorway into this ayah.

### The world this question fell into

To feel this sentence, you have to feel the world it interrupted.

The Arabs who first heard Surah Ar-Rahman were a people who *worshipped* the spoken word. Poetry was their currency, their pride, their record of who they were. They could hear a single line of verse and tell you the tribe of the man who composed it. They hung their finest poems on the walls of the Kaʿba. And into that world — a world of connoisseurs, of experts, of people who could not be impressed by an amateur — came a surah that opens not with the name of the Prophet, not even with the name *Allah*, but with a Name: **Ar-Rahmān.** The Most Merciful. The All-Compassionate.

And then this Name begins to *teach*. He taught the Qur'an. He created the human being. He taught him to articulate. The sun and the moon by precise calculation. The star and the tree, both bowing. The sky, He raised. The balance, He set. And on, and on — gift stacked upon gift, the whole cosmos unrolled like a carpet of generosity.

And every few lines, the recitation stops and turns to *you* and asks: **so which of these favors of your Lord will you deny?**

### The moment this particular verse lands

Here is what makes *this* instance — ayah 59 — worth slowing down for.

Look at what comes right before it. The verses just preceding (55:56–58) have left the world of seas and stars and crossed over into the unseen. They describe the companions of the Garden — pure, modest, untouched, *as though they were rubies and coral*. We have moved from the blessings you can touch today to a blessing you have only been *promised*. A reward in a world you have not yet seen.

And it is *there*, standing at the edge of a promise, that Allah asks again: **which of your Lord's favors will you call a lie?**

[PAUSE]

Notice the quiet genius of the placement. It is one thing to be grateful for the sun on your face — you can feel that. It is another thing entirely to be grateful for a Garden you have never entered, on the word of a Lord you have never seen. This refrain, in this spot, is no longer only about *thanking*. It has become about *trusting*. About whether you will brand a promise a lie before it has had the chance to come true.

So here is our hook, and I want you to carry it through everything that follows:

**By the end, I want you to see that this is the one question in the Qur'an that is impossible to answer wrongly — and that its impossibility is the mercy.**

Let's earn that.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have only four words. Four. And I want to show you that each one is load-bearing — pull any of them out and the whole question collapses into something smaller. We will open three of them: the word for *favors*, the verb for *denial*, and the small marked ending that tells us **who** is being addressed.

---

### Element One: *Ālāʾ* — The Word That Refuses to Be Counted

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word itself.

In English, when we want to talk about good things given to us, we have a small handful of words — *blessing, gift, favor, grace* — and we mostly use them interchangeably. Arabic does not work that way. Arabic is a language of *precision under pressure*: it will hand you several different words for what looks like the same thing, and the choice between them carries the meaning.

The most common Qur'anic word for a blessing is **niʿma** — root *n-ʿ-m*. You have heard it: *anʿamta ʿalayhim*, "those whom You have blessed," in Surah Al-Fātiha. Niʿma is the everyday, intimate word for a favor. It is the word you would expect here.

But Allah did **not** choose niʿma.

He chose **ālāʾ** — *ءَالَآء* — from the root *ʾ-l-w* (Leeds corpus confirms the root, *Alw*; the classical lexicons, Lisān al-ʿArab and al-Rāghib's Mufradāt, gloss ālāʾ simply as *al-niʿam*, the favors). And al-Muyassar, in plain Arabic, renders the word exactly that way: *niʿam rabbikumā* — the favors of your Lord.

So if they mean the same thing, why the switch? Stay with me, because the difference is not in the dictionary definition — it is in the *shape* of the word.

[PAUSE]

**Ālāʾ is a plural.** It has no singular living comfortably inside the sentence; the grammarians debated for centuries what its singular even *is* — *ilan? alan? ily?* — and could not fully agree. Think about what that means. This is a word that arrives already *multiple*. You cannot say it in the singular without a fight. It is a word built to overflow.

Now watch what Allah places in front of this overflowing plural. He places **ayy** — *which one?* A singular interrogative. *Which single one.*

Do you feel the trap closing?

He takes a word that means *favors-too-many-to-count* and asks you to reach into that uncountable flood and pull out **one** — just one — that you are prepared to stand over and call false.

> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi…* — "So which single one, out of the countless…"

This is not a casual question. It is a structure designed to defeat you. The grammar itself makes ingratitude *un-performable*. To answer, you would have to isolate one gift — the air in your lungs, the sight in your eyes, the name *Ar-Rahmān* you just heard — hold it up, and say: *this one. This one is a lie.* And the moment you try, you discover you cannot do it without exposing yourself.

[PAUSE]

There is one more thing the lexicon gives us, and I want to offer it carefully as the scholars do, not beyond what the word warrants. Several classical authorities note that **ālāʾ** carries a flavor that niʿma does not quite have — a sense of the *manifest*, the *displayed*, the blessing that is **out in the open** where it can be seen. Niʿma can be a quiet, private grace. Ālāʾ tends to be the favor that is on *show* — evident, undeniable, in front of your eyes.

And if that flavor is in the word — and the lexicons invite us to hear it — then the question sharpens further. He is not asking, "which hidden grace will you deny?" He is asking, **"which of these favors — these obvious, visible, can't-miss-them favors — will you call a lie?"** The denial is not even made difficult. It is made *embarrassing*.

So what does this single word reveal? That before the question is asked, the answer has already been arranged. Ālāʾ is a word that cannot be counted, asked about with a *which* that demands you count. The favor outnumbers the denial before the denial can open its mouth.

[PAUSE — major]

---

### Element Two: *Tukadhdhibān* — The Verb That Is Worse Than Lying

Now to the engine of the sentence: the word translated "deny."

Again, let me set the ground before we step on it. Arabic verbs are built on patterns called *forms* — ten of them — and the form is not decoration. The form *changes the action*. Take a simple root and you can run it through these forms the way you run light through a prism: each angle throws a different color.

Our root here is **k-dh-b**, the root of *lying*, falsehood.

In its simplest shape — **Form I, *kadhaba*** — it means *he lied*. He told an untruth. That is the everyday word. A person who *kadhaba* has spoken a falsehood.

But that is **not** the word in our ayah. Our word is **tukadhdhibān** — and the Leeds corpus marks it precisely: **Form II.** Form II is built by doubling the middle letter — you can hear it in the recitation, the *dh* held and stressed: *tu-kadh-dhi-bān*. And Form II does something specific to this root.

It does not mean *to lie.*

It means **to brand something a lie. To call the truth false. To give the lie to a speaker who is telling you the truth.**

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference, because everything turns on it.

When a person **kadhaba** (Form I), they have a problem with the *truth inside themselves* — they spoke an untruth. But when a person **kadhdhaba** (Form II), they have a problem with *the truth in front of them* — they took something real, something given, something true, and they pronounced over it the verdict: *false.* They became a judge. They looked at a gift and ruled it a fraud.

This is why the Qur'an reserves *takdhīb* — this Form II — for the gravest spiritual posture there is: *kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā*, "they branded Our signs as lies." It is not the sin of weakness. It is the sin of *verdict*. It is standing in front of the sun and ruling that there is no light.

And notice — this is not the passive, sleepy ingratitude of a person who simply *forgets* to say thank you. The form is active, declarative, intensive. To *kadhdhaba* a favor you must rouse yourself, take a position, and pass sentence on it. Ingratitude, in this word, is not a lapse. It is a *court ruling against reality.*

[PAUSE]

So now layer the two words we have opened, one on top of the other, and hear the full sentence breathe:

*Out of favors beyond counting* — **ālāʾ** —
*which single one* — **ayy** —
*will you haul into court and convict of being a lie?* — **tukadhdhibān.**

There is even a mercy hidden in the harshness of the sound. Listen to the two key words against each other. **Ālāʾ** — open, flowing, the mouth falls open to say it, *aa-laa*, like a sigh of relief. **Tukadhdhibān** — clenched, doubled, the tongue catching on that stressed *dh*, a word you almost have to *force* out. The gift slides off the tongue like water. The denial *snags*. Even in the body, even in the mouth, the favor is easy and the denial is hard work. Allah made the lie physically more difficult to say than the thank-you.

What does this verb reveal? That Allah is not asking whether you *feel* grateful. He is asking whether you are willing to stand as a *judge* against your own Sustainer — and the word He uses makes that posture sound exactly as absurd as it is.

[PAUSE — major]

---

### Element Three: The Ending That Means "You Two" — *Rabbikumā… Tukadhdhibān*

Now the smallest thing in the ayah, and somehow the most intimate. Look at two endings: **rabbi-kumā** ("your Lord") and **tukadhdhibā-n** ("you two deny"). Both carry a marker we do not have in English at all.

In English we have *you* — and it is the same whether I speak to one person or a thousand. Arabic is more careful. Arabic has a singular *you*, a plural *you*… and, sitting between them, a **dual** — a special form reserved for exactly *two*. And every single time this refrain returns across the surah, it is in the **dual**. *Your Lord* — Lord of you *two*. *Will you deny* — you *two*.

So the first question the grammar forces on us is simply: *two what?*

The scholars are clear, and al-Qurtubī lays it out for us. This is the address to **ath-thaqalān** — "the two weighty ones": **humankind and the jinn.** It is the consensus position, the *qawl al-jumhūr*, and Qurtubī even points to the moment later in the very same surah where Allah names them outright: *yā maʿshara l-jinni wa l-ins* — "O company of jinn and humankind" (55:33). The whole surah, he shows, is built to hold both species in its address. Two created peoples, both given speech, both given law, both given a reckoning — and so both are stood together before this question.

[PAUSE]

Now, here is why this matters for *you*, sitting wherever you are sitting.

Most of the Qur'an addresses *you* as a believer, *you* as a people, *you* as humankind — and there is comfort in the crowd. But the dual does something the plural cannot. It is *small*. It narrows the room. When you address two, you are no longer addressing a faceless multitude — you are nearly addressing an *individual*, with one companion beside them. The dual has an intimacy the plural loses. It leans in.

And then look at the word He chooses for Himself in this intimate address. Not *Allāh*. Not *Ar-Rahmān*, even — though that is the Name presiding over the surah. Here, in the question itself, He calls Himself **Rabb** — from the root *r-b-b* — *your Lord.*

And *Rabb* is not a distant, throne-room word. Its root reaches into *raising, nurturing, bringing-to-maturity*. The *rabb* of a child is the one who feeds it, washes it, watches it grow, corrects it, provides for it across years. It is the most *hands-on* of the divine names. So when He asks the question, He does not say, *which favor of the Almighty will you deny?* He says, *which favor of the One who raised you, fed you, grew you from nothing — which of His favors will you two call a lie?*

[PAUSE]

Put the three discoveries together now and the sentence is no longer four flat words. It is a face leaning close to two people and asking, gently, devastatingly:

*Out of the countless favors* (**ālāʾ**)
*of the One who has nurtured you your whole life* (**rabbikumā**),
*which single one* (**ayy**)
*will the two of you drag into court and convict of being false?* (**tukadhdhibān**).

---

### Bridge

Look at what the construction has done. It has taken the most overflowing word for blessing, asked us to find *one* deniable item inside an uncountable flood, named the denial with a verb that means *passing a false verdict on the truth*, and addressed the whole thing in the intimate dual of *the One who raised you.* The grammar has not described ingratitude. The grammar has made ingratitude **unspeakable** — literally, you cannot complete the sentence as a denial without exposing the absurdity of the attempt.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange machinery of the human heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: Why We Stop Seeing the Things That Keep Us Alive

Let me start somewhere you already live.

You have a sense of smell. Right now, unless something is unusual, you are not aware of it — but it is one of the quiet miracles that lets you taste your food, detect danger, recognize the people you love. Now imagine you lost it for a week. Suddenly you would *grieve* it — food turned to cardboard, a whole channel to the world gone dark. And then, if it came back, within a few days you would stop noticing it again. Completely.

Psychologists have a name for this. They call it **hedonic adaptation** — the mind's relentless habit of taking whatever is *stable and good* and dropping it beneath the threshold of attention. We are wired to notice *change*, not *constancy*. A new blessing thrills us for a week and then becomes wallpaper. The roof over your head, the water from the tap, the heart that has beaten — what is it now, two, three billion times? — without your permission or your thanks. The most reliable favors become the *most invisible* ones. Constancy is the cloak that good things hide behind.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that beside our ayah, because the Qur'an diagnosed this about us fourteen centuries before psychology gave it a name.

When Allah asks *which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?* — He is not, in the first place, accusing us of standing up and shouting "I deny God's gifts!" Almost no one does that. The denial He is reaching for is *quieter* and far more common. It is the denial of **not-seeing**. It is the favor that has become wallpaper. It is the heart that beats unthanked, the eyes that read this line unthanked, the *ālāʾ* — those *manifest, on-show, can't-miss-them* favors we opened earlier — that have been so constant for so long that they have gone invisible, and invisibility is its own kind of lie we tell ourselves about reality.

And do you see now why Allah built the question as a **trap of specificity**? *Which one?* He will not let you answer in the abstract. Hedonic adaptation thrives on the general — "yeah, yeah, I'm grateful for everything" — because *everything* is a fog you never have to look at. But the moment He says *which single one*, the fog is forbidden. You are forced to reach in and name an item — and the instant you name it, you *see* it again. You cannot pick up the favor to deny it without, in that very act, finally *looking* at it.

[PAUSE]

So watch the question turn inside out in your hands.

It arrives looking like an *accusation* — "how dare you deny?" But it operates like a **cure**. It is the exact opposite of how we usually try to be grateful. We try to feel thankful for *everything* and end up feeling thankful for nothing, because the mind cannot hold a fog. Allah does the reverse. He hands us a *flood* — ālāʾ — and then forces us to pull out *one item at a time* and look at it. The repetition we puzzled over at the very beginning — thirty-one times — is not redundancy. It is the *method.* Thirty-one times He stops the recitation and makes you reach into the flood again. Reach in, pull one out, see it, fail to deny it. Reach in again. The surah is not lecturing you about gratitude. It is *training* you in it, one forced act of attention at a time.

What does this change about tomorrow? Something small and specific. Do not try to "be more grateful" — that is a fog, and the fog defeats you by lunchtime. Instead, borrow Allah's own method: once a day, reach into the flood and pull out **one** — one favor so constant you had stopped seeing it — and hold it long enough to honestly ask yourself, *would I dare call this a lie?* You will not be able to. And in that inability, the favor comes back into view. That is the surah's training drill. Thirty-one repetitions were the model. Give Him one a day.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: The Strange Mercy of Being Asked Instead of Told

Here is a thing about being human that we rarely say out loud.

We do not like being *told*. Tell a person — even a true thing, even a thing for their own good — *you are ungrateful, you should be ashamed, look at all you've been given* — and watch what happens inside them. The walls go up. The defenses muster. Something stiffens and resists, even when the speaker is *right*. We are built to push back against the verdict handed down *at* us.

But ask a person a question — a real one — and something entirely different happens. The question does not arrive *at* them. It opens up *inside* them. They have to go and find the answer themselves, and whatever they find, *they* found it; it is theirs; there is nothing to push back against, because no one imposed it.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah did not do in this ayah.

He could so easily have said: *You have denied the favors of your Lord.* A flat statement. A verdict. And every defense in us would have risen to meet it. We would have argued, qualified, explained, looked away.

He did not do that. He asked. *Which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?* And by asking, He handed the verdict to **you**. He made you walk into the room, reach into the flood, hold up a single favor, and pronounce — out of your own mouth — that you *cannot* call it false. He did not convict you of gratitude. He arranged it so that *you* would arrive at your own inability to deny. The truth that would have bounced off you as an accusation goes straight to the center of you as a question.

This is the *adab* — the courtesy — of how Allah deals with the human heart. He knows exactly how we are built. He knows the verdict-handed-down hardens us and the question-asked opens us. And so the Most Merciful, *Ar-Rahmān* whose Name presides over this entire surah, chooses the gentler instrument — not because the harsh one would be untrue, but because the gentle one would *work.* He is not trying to win an argument with you. He is trying to bring you home.

[PAUSE]

And remember where this particular instance falls. Right after the promise of the Garden — a reward not yet seen. So the question here is not only "which seen favor will you deny?" It is also, tenderly, "which *promised* one will you call a lie before I have even given it to you?" And by phrasing the promise as a *question rather than a demand for belief*, He does the same merciful thing again: He does not order you to trust Him. He invites you to notice that the One promising the unseen Garden is the very same One who already gave you the sun, the sea, the speech in your mouth, the heart in your chest — every one of which you would be ashamed to call a lie. *That* is the argument. And He lets you assemble it yourself.

What does this change about tomorrow? Two things. First, in your own heart: when you catch yourself in ingratitude, do not flog yourself with the verdict — *you ungrateful wretch.* That hardens the very heart you are trying to soften. Instead, do what your Lord does: ask. *Which of these would I really call a lie?* Lead yourself home the gentle way. And second, with the people you love and are responsible for: notice that the most merciful Teacher in existence taught by *asking*, not by *telling.* If you want to move a heart — your child's, your spouse's, your friend's — the question that lets them find the truth themselves will do what the lecture never can.

[PAUSE — major]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now I can show you the thing I held back at the start.

Remember our opening puzzle — thirty-one repetitions of one sentence, against every instinct we have about good speech. Here is what that repetition is *doing*.

This refrain is the **spine of the entire surah.** Surah Ar-Rahman is not a flowing essay; it is a series of *galleries.* First a gallery of creation — sun, moon, star, tree, sky, sea. Then the refrain. Then a gallery of the human and jinn condition — made from clay, made from fire, the two seas, the pearl and coral. Then the refrain. Then judgment, then the Fire, then the two Gardens, then the companions like rubies and coral — and after *each* gallery, the same voice turns and asks the same question. *Which of your Lord's favors will you deny?*

The refrain is the heartbeat between the chambers. It is the hand on your shoulder after every single room, making sure you did not just *walk through* the gift but actually *received* it. And our ayah — 59 — is the hand on the shoulder after the room where He showed you a reward you have not yet been given. The architecture *is* the argument: He will not let you tour His generosity like a museum. After every exhibit, He stops you and makes you answer for what you saw.

[PAUSE]

### Bringing It Together

See now that the linguistic discoveries and the human themes were the same insight arriving from two directions.

The grammar made denial **unspeakable** — an uncountable flood (*ālāʾ*) asked about one item at a time (*ayy*), with a verb that means *passing a false verdict on the truth* (*tukadhdhibān*), spoken intimately to *the two of you* by *the One who raised you* (*rabbikumā*). And the human themes showed us *why* He built it that way: because we deny by *not-seeing*, and the only cure for not-seeing is to be made to look at *one thing at a time*; and because we harden under verdicts and soften under questions, so the Most Merciful chose to *ask*.

The structure of the sentence and the structure of the human heart were designed to fit each other exactly. The question is shaped like the lock it was made to open.

### Questions to Carry

- If Allah stopped *you* right now, after the room of your own life, and asked *which one?* — which favor would your hand reach for, and would you dare?
- Which of your most constant blessings has gone invisible precisely *because* it never failed you?
- Where in your life are you handing down *verdicts* — to yourself, to others — when a *question* would open the door the verdict keeps slamming shut?
- The Garden in 55:56–58 had not yet been given when this question was asked. What promise of your Lord are you tempted to call a lie simply because you have not seen it *yet*?
- If the favor slides off the tongue and the denial snags — why do we still, so often, choose the harder word?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When the One who raised you lists gift upon countless gift and asks which single one you would convict of being a lie, the question itself is the mercy — because it makes your ingratitude impossible to say out loud without hearing how absurd it sounds.

### The Closing Invitation

You do not have to manufacture a flood of gratitude tonight. The flood is already there — it always was; that is what *ālāʾ* means. All that is asked of you is to do what the surah trained you to do thirty-one times: reach in, pull out *one*, hold it up to the light, and answer honestly. Not "am I grateful for everything?" — that fog will defeat you. Just: *this one. Would I call this one a lie?*

You already know you wouldn't. Let that small, honest *no* be the beginning.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

O Ar-Rahmān, You who raised us before we could ask, who set the favors before us in the open where they cannot be missed — do not let us deny by forgetting. Make our hearts soft enough to be asked, and honest enough to answer. And of all Your countless ālāʾ, let there not be one — seen or yet promised — that we ever call a lie. Āmīn.
