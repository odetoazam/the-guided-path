# Which One Would You Deny? — The Question That Counts You

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Hold it in your ear before we touch a single word of it.

> [ayah:55:55] فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ
>
> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*
>
> **So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?**

You may already know this line. If you have ever heard Surah Ar-Rahman recited, you have heard it come back, and come back, and come back — thirty-one times across this one surah. It is the most repeated refrain in the entire Quran. It returns after the heavens are described. It returns after the two seas. It returns after the human being shaped from sounding clay. It returns after the fire. And here, in our ayah, it returns after Allah has described the people of the garden — reclining on couches lined with brocade, the fruit of two gardens hanging *near at hand* (55:54).

And then: *so which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

Now here is what I want you to notice before we go anywhere. When something repeats thirty-one times, the human ear does something dangerous to it. It **stops hearing it.** It becomes wallpaper. It becomes the chorus you hum without listening. And that — that exact reflex, the reflex by which a thing becomes invisible precisely because it is always there — is not a flaw in how you're listening tonight.

[PAUSE]

It is the *subject of the ayah.*

Let me rebuild the room this verse was first spoken into. The surah opens with a Name — Ar-Raḥmān, The Entirely Merciful — and then, without a verb of praise, without a "say" or a "tell them," it simply begins to *unspool* the universe. He taught the Quran. He created the human. He taught him eloquence. The sun and the moon by precise calculation. The star and the tree prostrating. The sky raised, the balance set. And on, and on — gift after gift after gift, each one followed by this question turning back on you like a face: *so which of these will you deny?*

There is a narration, reported by al-Qurtubi and others and recorded by al-Tirmidhī, that when the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah to his companions and they sat silent, he said that the jinn had given a better response than they had — that every time he reached *fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān,* the jinn would answer: *"Not one of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny — so to You belongs all praise."*

So hold that. The question is not rhetorical wallpaper. It is a question that *expects an answer.* Someone, somewhere, was answering it out loud.

Tonight, I want us to be among those who answer.

**The hook:** there are three small things in this short line — one word for "favors," one hidden listener the grammar keeps pointing at, and one verb that does not mean what most translations make it mean. And when those three open, this verse stops being a chorus you've heard before. It becomes a question with your name in it. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: ālā' — the word that has no comfortable singular

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we look at the word.

In English, when we want to talk about "a blessing," we can say it cleanly in the singular. *A blessing. A favor. A gift.* The singular is the natural home of the word, and the plural is just the singular stacked up. *One gift, two gifts, three gifts.*

Arabic does not always work like that. There is a category of words in Arabic whose natural home is the **plural** — words that live in the collective, words whose singular is so rarely used that the lexicographers actually argue about what it even is. You reach for the singular and your hand closes on air.

Now look at the word Allah chose:

> **ءَالَآءِ** — *ālā'* — the favors.

This is from the root Alw. And here is the remarkable thing the classical lexicographers tell us: the singular of *ālā'* is contested. They offer you several possibilities — *ilā,* *alan,* *iliyy* — and they do not fully agree, because in living usage the word **comes plural.** It almost refuses the singular. You do not naturally say "an ālā." You say *ālā'* — favors, plural, pouring.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because it is not a grammatical curiosity. It is a theology hidden inside a vowel pattern.

Listen to the *sound* of the word. *Ālā'.* Three open syllables, two long alifs, the mouth opening and opening and opening — *aaa-laaa.* There is no way to say this word with a closed, tight mouth. The word itself is a mouth held open in receiving. The phonetics are doing the work the meaning is doing: **abundance that will not be made small.**

And here is why the missing singular matters. Allah is not asking, *"which one favor will you deny?"* — as though His blessings arrived one at a time, in a line, each waiting its turn. The word He chose carries the truth that **a single, isolated blessing is almost a fiction.** You did not receive "a" favor. You are *standing inside* favor — submerged in it, the way a fish is submerged in water and therefore cannot point to the water. Your heartbeat is a favor sitting on top of your breath, which is a favor sitting on top of the light reaching your eye, which is a favor sitting on top of the ground holding your weight. There is no *one.* There is only the flood.

This is the first stepping stone. The favors named all through this surah are physical — fruit you can taste, pearls you can hold, two seas you can sail, a sun you can feel on your skin. But the word *ālā'* is pointing past the physical fruit toward an unseen reality: that you have been living, this entire time, **immersed** in a giving so total that you lost the ability to see it. The fish cannot deny the water. It can only forget it is wet.

Now — watch what Allah does next, because it is exquisite.

He floods you with a plural — *ālā',* favors beyond counting — and then He interrogates you with a **singular.** *Bi-ayyi* — "by *which*." *Which* favor. Singular. Out of the uncountable flood, He turns to you and says: name one. Pick a single one out of the ocean and tell Me, to My face, that this one is not real. That this one I did not give.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the trap closing, gently? The plural makes denial impossible by sheer volume — there are too many to reject. And then the singular makes denial *personal* — because now you are not arguing with a category, you are being asked to point at one specific gift and call it a lie. The sight in your eye, right now, reading these words. *That one.* Will you deny *that one?*

What this single word reveals: **Allah did not ask you to be grateful for everything at once, which is impossible. He asked you whether there is even one thing you would dare to disown.** And the moment you go looking for that one thing — you find your hands full of His giving instead.

### Element Two: the dual that has been speaking to two of you the whole time

Here is the second thing, and it is hiding in plain sight in two of the words.

Let me teach the concept first. Arabic has something English lost: a **dual.** English has singular ("you") and plural ("you all"), but nothing in between. Arabic has a dedicated grammatical form for *exactly two.* Not one, not many — two. It has its own pronoun endings, its own verb endings. When Arabic uses the dual, it is being precise: I am speaking to two parties, and I want you both to know I see you both.

Now look at our ayah. Two words carry it:

> **رَبِّكُمَا** — *Rabbi-kumā* — "your Lord," where *kumā* is the dual: the Lord of **you two.**
>
> **تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhibāni* — "you two deny," the verb ending in that dual *-āni.*

Twice in a five-word verse, the grammar grabs you by the shoulder and says: *I am talking to two of you.*

So the question every classical commentator asks is: **who are the two?**

And here the scholars give us not competing answers but a converging one. Al-Qurtubi reports that this is the address to *al-ins wa al-jinn* — humankind and the jinn — and he tells us this is the position of the *jumhūr,* the majority. Al-Muyassar names them directly: *ayyuhā al-thaqalān* — "O you two weighty ones." Al-Qurtubi builds the case from the surah itself: Allah said "He created the human" (55:3) and "He created the jinn from a smokeless flame" (55:15) — both creations named — and earlier said *"We shall attend to you, O you two weighty ones"* (55:31), and *"O company of jinn and humankind"* (55:33). The whole surah has been holding two audiences in its two hands the entire time.

*Al-thaqalān.* The two weighty ones. Sit with that title for a moment. Why *weighty?* Because these two species — humans and jinn — are the two creations burdened with the trust, weighed down with choice, heavy with accountability. The sun is not weighty; it simply obeys. The tree is not weighty; it prostrates without deliberation. But you — you carry weight. You can say yes and you can say no. You are *thaqīl,* heavy, because something has been placed on you that the mountains, the Quran tells us elsewhere, declined to carry.

[PAUSE]

Now here is what I want you to feel, and it is the whole reason the dual matters.

When you read this ayah, you naturally hear it as addressed to *you* — singular, alone, just you and the page. But the grammar is quietly correcting you. It is not *you, alone.* It is *you two.* You are being addressed as a **member of a species standing before its Lord.** You are not an individual having a private spiritual moment. You are one of *al-thaqalān,* summoned alongside an entire order of creation, and asked, together, in the dual: which of His favors will you deny?

Notice what this does. It takes the question *out of your head* and puts it into the open court of creation. This is not a feeling you can have privately and then set down. It is a question put to you *as a witness,* in a courtroom where the other species is also standing and — per that narration — already answering better than you are.

And notice the tenderness folded inside the weight. The word is not just "your Lord" — it is *Rabbikumā,* and *Rabb* (root rbb) does not mean a distant sovereign. *Rabb* is the one who **nurtures, sustains, raises a thing stage by stage to its completion** — the same root from which a mother raising a child draws her word. So the dual address is not a prosecutor reading charges. It is the One who *raised you,* who fed you stage by stage from a clot to a child to whatever you are tonight, turning to the two creations He nurtured and asking — almost wounded by the possibility — *which of the favors of the One who raised you will you two deny?*

What this dual reveals: **you were never reading this alone, and you were never standing before a stranger.** You are one of the weighty two, addressed by the One who nurtured you into being. The question has witnesses, and the Questioner has a claim on you older than your memory.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: tukadhdhibān — not "fail to thank," but "actively call it a lie"

Now the third thing, and this is the one that, when it opens, changes the temperature of the whole verse.

Most of us, hearing "which of the favors of your Lord will you deny," translate "deny" in our hearts as something soft. We hear: *which will you fail to appreciate? Which will you take for granted? Which will you forget to thank Him for?* And those are real sins, and they are gentle sins, the sins of the distracted and the busy.

But that is **not what the verb says.** Let me teach you the form.

The root is k-dh-b — *kadhaba* — and in its simplest form (Form I), it means *to lie,* to speak a falsehood. That is the base.

But the word in our ayah is not Form I. The Leeds corpus and every grammarian confirm it is **Form II** — *kadhdhaba,* with that doubled, stressed middle letter, *tukadhdhibān.* And Form II in Arabic is not just a louder Form I. It transforms the meaning. Form II here is **declarative and intensive-transitive** — it does not mean "to lie." It means **to actively declare something to be a lie. To cry "false!" against something. To take a truth that is standing in front of you and reject it to its face.**

Do you hear the difference?

- *Kadhaba* (Form I) — *you* speak a lie.
- *Kadhdhaba* (Form II) — you take something *true* and you **brand it false.** You denounce it. You accuse the truth of being a liar.

[PAUSE]

This is an enormous shift, and I need you to feel its full weight. The ayah is not asking, "which favor did you neglect to be grateful for?" That would be the language of forgetfulness. The ayah uses the language of **active rejection.** *Which of these favors will you stand in front of and denounce as false? Which of these gifts will you point to and say: this is not real, this did not come from Him, this is a lie?*

And here is the devastating, quiet thing. The doubled letter in Form II often carries *repetition, insistence, the doing of a thing again and again.* *Tukadhdhibān* is not a single act of denial. It has the texture of a *posture,* a way of standing in the world — denying, and denying, and denying.

Now let's bring this back to the human reality, because this is the bridge to everything that follows. We rarely deny Allah's favors with our *mouths.* Almost no one says out loud, "the sun is not a gift." We are far too sophisticated for that. We commit *takdhīb* — active denial — with our **lives**: by living as though what we have is ours by right, by attributing to our own hands what came from His, by receiving the gift and handing the acknowledgment to chance or effort or luck. The Form II verb is asking us not just about what we say. It is asking about the posture of our whole existence.
