# Which One Would You Pick?
### A Reflection on Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 28

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah three times before we begin.

In Arabic:

> **فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ**

In transliteration:

> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*

And in English, as close as English can come:

> **"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

You may already know this ayah. Not because you studied it — but because you've *heard* it. It repeats thirty-one times in this one surah. It is the most repeated single sentence in the entire Quran placed back-to-back with itself. If you have ever listened to Surah Ar-Rahman recited, this line is the wave that keeps returning to the shore. *Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.* Again. And again. And again.

And here is the strange thing about a sentence you've heard thirty-one times.

You stop hearing it.

[PAUSE]

This is the first trap we have to escape before we can even enter this ayah. A repeated thing becomes invisible. The refrain becomes background music. The mind files it under "I already know this one" and moves on, hunting for something new. And so the most repeated verse in the Quran becomes, for many of us, the *least* reflected-upon — precisely because it is everywhere.

I want to ask you to do something difficult today. I want you to pretend you have never heard this ayah. Because hidden inside this sentence — this short, familiar, easily-skipped sentence — is one of the most surgically constructed questions in all of revelation. A question designed so that you *cannot answer it.* A question that, if you sit with it honestly, leaves you with nothing to say.

### Rebuilding the World

To feel the weight of where this ayah sits, you have to know what comes right before it.

Two verses earlier, Allah says something that should stop the heart:

> **كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ** — "Everyone upon it will perish." (55:26)
>
> **وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو ٱلْجَلَٰلِ وَٱلْإِكْرَامِ** — "And there will remain the Face of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor." (55:27)

Everyone. Everything. Gone. The earth and everyone standing on it — finished. Every face you love, every face you fear, your own face in the mirror — *fānin*, perishing, already in the process of vanishing. And one thing remains. One. The Face of your Lord.

[PAUSE]

And then — *immediately* — without a breath between them, comes our ayah:

> **"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

Do you feel the strangeness of that? Allah has just told you that you will die, that everyone will die, that the whole standing world is on its way to nothing. And in the very next breath He calls it — *a favor.* He frames the announcement of universal death, and the survival of His Face, as something belonging to the list of *ālā'* — His bounties, His gifts.

That is the world we have to enter. A world where the question "which of His favors will you deny?" is being asked *over the grave.* Not in a garden full of fruit. Not by a flowing river. But standing at the edge of your own ending.

### The Hook

So here is what we are going to chase today.

This ayah is built as a question. And questions are meant to be answered. So let's take it seriously and try to answer it. Allah asks: *which one will you deny?* So — pick one. Name a single favor you would be willing to stand up and call a lie.

Go ahead. Try.

I promise you that by the time we are done, you will understand why the question was built this way — and why every creature that has ever tried to answer it has fallen silent.

Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Is Plural With No Single Form — *ālā'*

Let's start with the favors themselves. The word is **ءَالَآء** — *ālā'.*

Now, before we open this word, I want to teach you something about Arabic that will make this land.

In English, when we want to make a word plural, we usually just add an *s.* One favor, two favors. The plural is built *out of* the singular — the singular is the home base, and the plural is the singular, multiplied. You always know what the "one" looks like.

Arabic mostly works the same way — there is usually a singular you can point to. But Arabic also has a rare and beautiful category of words where the plural is, in a sense, *primary* — where the "one" is so contested, so faint, that the grammarians themselves argued about what a single unit even looks like. The word arrives already in the plural. It comes to you as a *collection.* As an abundance. You cannot easily hold up *one* of it.

**Ālā' is one of these words.**

The lexicographers — in Lisān al-ʿArab, in al-Rāghib's Mufradāt — record that the singular of *ālā'* is genuinely disputed. Was it *ilā*? *Alan*? *Iluww*? *Ily*? They could not settle it. The word for "Allah's favors" comes into the language already multiplied, already overflowing, as if a single one of them was never the point.

Sit with that for a moment.

[PAUSE]

Allah did not say *fa-bi-ayyi niʿmati Rabbikumā* — "which *blessing* (singular) of your Lord." He could have. *Niʿma* is a clean word with a clear singular. He chose instead a word that **resists being made singular** — a word whose very grammar is abundance, plurality, a flood you cannot count down to one.

And now look at what He pairs it with.

He pairs this uncountable plural with the interrogative **بِأَىِّ** — *bi-ayyi* — "which *one*?"

Do you see the trap closing? He uses a singular, pinpointing question — *which one?* — and aims it at a word that **has no comfortable one.** He asks you to select a single item from a category that refuses to be reduced to single items. The grammar itself is the argument. Before you have even thought about an answer, the structure of the sentence has already shown you that the answer cannot be given. You are being asked to pull one drop out of an ocean and label it — and the word for "ocean" here won't even let you see a drop.

**The favors are so total that the language built to name them forgot how to count to one.**

[PAUSE]

There is more in the sound of it. Say it slowly: *ā-lā'.* Two long, open vowels. A soft glottal stop. It opens the mouth wide — *aaa* — like the involuntary sound a person makes when something is poured over them, when they receive without effort. It is not a clipped, transactional word. It is a word that *spills.*

This is what this single word reveals: **Allah's giving is not a series of countable gifts you could itemize and audit. It is a condition you are submerged in.** And you are about to be asked to deny it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: The Grammar of *Two* — *Rabbikumā* and *Tukadhdhibān*

Now look at who is being asked.

Read the ayah again and listen for a particular sound — the sound at the *end* of the words:

> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i **Rabbikumā** tukadhdhi**bān**.*

**رَبِّكُمَا** — *Rabbi-kumā.* "Your Lord" — but *your* in a very specific form. Not *Rabbikum* (your Lord, addressing many). Not *Rabbika* (your Lord, addressing one). *Rabbi-**kumā.*** The *-kumā* ending is the **dual** — the grammatical form Arabic reserves for *exactly two.*

And the verb agrees: **تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhi-**bān.*** That *-āni* ending is again the dual. "Will *you two* deny."

Let me teach you why this matters, because most languages on earth do not have this.

English has singular and plural — *you* (one) and *you* (many). That's it. If I am speaking to two specific parties, English shrugs and uses the same "you" it uses for a stadium. But Arabic has a *third* number. It has a grammatical form that exists **only** for two. Not one, not three, not a crowd — *two.* When Arabic uses the dual, it is not being casual. It is pointing a finger at *two distinct parties* and saying: *I mean you. And I mean you. The two of you.*

So the question becomes unavoidable: **who are the two?**

Throughout this surah the question keeps appearing in the dual — *Rabbikumā,* "your Lord" of you two. And the jumhūr — the majority of scholars, as al-Qurtubī records — hold that the two addressed are **al-ins wa al-jinn**: mankind and the jinn. The two weighty species. Later in the very same surah, Allah removes all doubt and names them outright: *"sa-nafrughu lakum **ayyuha al-thaqalān**"* — "We will attend to you, O **two weighty ones**" (55:31) — and *"yā maʿshara al-jinni wa al-ins"* — "O company of jinn and mankind" (55:33). Al-Qurtubī notes this is the position of the jumhūr, supported by the report that the jinn, too, were given this surah to hear.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this does to you as the listener.

You are not alone in this room.

When you read "which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny," you are not the sole addressee being singled out. You are being addressed *alongside an entire creation you cannot see.* A second species — made of a different substance, living in a register your eyes cannot reach — is standing in the same dock, hearing the same question, at the same moment. The dual *-kumā* binds you to them. You and they are *the two.* You and they share a Lord, share the flood of favors, share the accounting.

There is something humbling in this almost beyond words. We tend to imagine ourselves as the center — humanity, the audience of revelation, the point of the cosmos. The dual quietly corrects us. *You are one of two.* The question is not "human, will you deny?" It is "the two of you — will you deny?" Your significance is real, and it is *shared.* You are addressed with magnificent dignity — directly, by your Lord — and in the very same word you are told you are not the only ones He made, not the only ones He provides for, not the only ones called to account.

[PAUSE]

And there is a tenderness even inside the word "Lord." *Rabb* — from the root *r-b-b* — is not a cold title of ownership. The *Rabb* is the one who nurtures a thing in stages, who takes it from one state to a more complete state, who raises and sustains and brings to maturity. When Allah asks "the favors of your *Rabb,*" He is not asking "the favors of your distant Master." He is asking: *the favors of the One who has been growing you, feeding you, raising you toward completion your entire existence — which of those will you deny?* The intimacy of the question is built into the noun. He could have said "the favors of *Allah.*" He said "the favors of your *Rabb* — the one nurturing the two of you."

**The dual tells you that you are addressed with dignity and with company; the word *Rabb* tells you the One addressing you has been raising you the whole time.** That is who is asking the question. That is whose favors are on trial.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: The Verb of Active Denial — *Tukadhdhibān*

Now the last word. The verb. The thing you are accused of being *capable* of doing: **تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhibān.* "Will you two deny / belie / call a lie."

I need to teach you one feature of Arabic verbs, and then this will open completely.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — the grammarians call them *forms,* numbered I through X. The base form, **Form I**, is the simple action. From the root *k-dh-b,* Form I — *kadhaba* — simply means "he lied." He, himself, told an untruth.

But our word is not Form I. The Leeds morphological corpus confirms it is **Form II** — *kadhdhaba* — built by doubling the middle letter. And Form II does something specific: it is the form of **intensity and of doing the action** ***to*** **something.** It transforms "to lie" into "**to charge something as a lie.** To actively declare a thing false. To stand in front of a truth and call it a fabrication."

Feel the difference. Form I, *kadhaba* — *you* tell a lie; the lie is inside you. Form II, *kadhdhaba* — you take something that is *true and standing in front of you* and you **brand it false.** You point at a real thing and shout "fake." It is not a private failing; it is an *act aimed at the truth.*

[PAUSE]

So look at the precision of the accusation. Allah did not ask: *fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā **takfurān*** — "which of the favors will you two **disbelieve in**?" *Kufr* is to cover, to be ungrateful, to refuse. That would already be severe. But He chose something sharper still.

He chose *takdhīb* — the active, intensive **branding of a truth as a lie.**

Why is this the perfect word? Because a favor is not an *idea* you can quietly disbelieve. A favor is a **standing fact.** The air in your lungs right now is not a theological proposition you can take or leave — it is *happening,* a present and undeniable event. So to reject a favor is not merely to "disbelieve" it in your head. It is to look at something *visibly, physically, undeniably given* — and call the giving a lie. To stand in the rain and say "it is dry." To eat and say "there is no food." That is *takdhīb.* That is the only word violent enough to name what rejecting a real, present gift actually *is.*

[PAUSE]

And there is a quiet mercy buried even here, in the grammar of the tense.

*Tukadhdhibān* is the **imperfect** — the present-and-future tense, the tense of ongoing and not-yet-finished action. Allah does not say *kadhdhabtumā* — "you two *have denied*," in the past, the verdict sealed. He says *tukadhdhibān* — "**will you two** deny," "**are you** denying," ongoing, open, unfinished. The door is still open. The question is not a sentence pronounced over a closed case. It is a question asked *while you can still change your answer.* The verb form itself is holding the door open for you to walk through.

**The favors are real and present (so the only word for rejecting them is "to call a truth a lie"); and the tense is open (so you are being asked, not yet condemned).** That is the whole tension of the ayah held in one verb.

---

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Ālā'* — a flood of favors so total the language cannot count one of them. *Rabbikumā* — addressed to two, in dignity and company, by the One who has been raising you the whole time. *Tukadhdhibān* — and the only honest word for rejecting a present, standing gift is "to call a truth a lie," asked in a tense that still leaves the door open.

The grammar has already made its case. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the strange way the human heart actually works.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why You Cannot Answer the Question

I want to start not with the Quran, but with your own kitchen.

There is a phenomenon that psychologists have a name for. They call it **hedonic adaptation** — though you have lived it long before you had a word for it. It is this: *we stop perceiving the things that are constant.*

The first sip of cold water when you are parched is ecstasy. The tenth glass that day, you don't taste at all. The first night in a home with heat after being cold is gratitude that brings you to tears. Six months later you adjust the thermostat with irritation because it's a degree off. The hand you have held for twenty years becomes, to your attention, almost *invisible* — not because you love less, but because your perception has a cruel feature: **it is built to notice change, and to delete the constant.**

Your nervous system is, quite literally, a *difference-detector.* It alerts you to what is new, what moved, what threatens. And it *suppresses* what is steady. This is useful for survival. It is catastrophic for gratitude. Because it means that **the more reliably Allah gives you something, the less you are able to see it.** The favors that never fail — the beating heart, the rising sun, the breath you did not have to request — fall *below the threshold of your attention* precisely *because* they never fail.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back to the ayah. *Which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

Here is why you cannot answer that question — and it is not for the reason you first think.

Your first instinct is: "I cannot answer because there are too many favors to deny." True. But there is a deeper reason, and it is darker. You cannot answer because **you cannot even** ***see*** **most of them.** They have been deleted by your own perception. You are being asked to pick one favor to call a lie — but the favors most worth defending have become *invisible to you,* dissolved into the background of "normal," so woven into the constant texture of your life that you have stopped registering them as gifts at all.

This is the genius of the refrain repeating thirty-one times. Do you see it now?

The ayah's *form mirrors its content.* It repeats and repeats and repeats — and your instinct, as we said at the very start, is to *stop hearing it.* The refrain enacts the very disease it is curing. It floods you with itself until you go numb to it — *exactly* the way Allah's favors flood you until you go numb to *them.* And every time it returns, it is reaching out to shake you awake: *don't let this become background. Don't let Me become background. Look again. Which one. Name it.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the spiritual work is not, first, to *thank* Allah for the favors. It is something prior and harder: **to** ***perceive*** **them again.** To defeat the difference-detector. To deliberately drag a constant favor back above the threshold of attention.

Here is a practice. Tonight, before you sleep, take **one** thing your perception has deleted — your breathing, your eyesight, the fact that you were not in pain today, the fact that you knew where your next meal was coming from — and *deny your perception's deletion of it.* Hold it up. Look at it as though it just arrived, as though you had been without it for a year and it was handed back to you this morning. Feel what you would feel if it were *new.*

That act — making the invisible favor visible again, for even thirty seconds — is the entire reflexive force of this ayah turned into a habit. The refrain is asking you, thirty-one times, to refuse to let His giving become wallpaper. **You answer the question not by naming a favor to deny — but by rescuing one favor, each day, from the oblivion of the ordinary.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Favor Hidden in the Grave

Now I want to take you to the hardest place this ayah goes. Remember where it sits.

It comes right after: *"Everyone upon it will perish; and there will remain the Face of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor."* And then: *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*

Let me ask you the question that this placement forces. Allah has just announced *death.* Universal, total, the erasure of everyone and everything. And He files it under *favors.* He calls the announcement of your annihilation an *ālā'.*

How can your own ending be a gift?

[PAUSE]

Let me come at this through something you already know.

Imagine you are reading a story — a novel you cannot put down. And imagine you discover, partway through, that this story *never ends.* It just continues. Forever. The same characters, the same world, no final page, no resolution, no meaning gathered up and handed back to you. At first that sounds like a dream — your favorite story, unending. But sit with it longer and a horror creeps in. A story with no ending has no *shape.* Nothing in it can *matter,* because nothing is ever final, nothing is ever weighed, nothing ever *counts.* It is the ending that makes the middle mean something. **It is the last page that gives the first page its weight.**

Now look at your life.

The reason your moments *matter* — the reason a kind word costs you something and is therefore worth something, the reason a sin is a real betrayal and a prayer is a real reaching, the reason love given *now* is precious — is that you are *finite.* *Kullu man ʿalayhā fānin.* You will perish. And it is *that* — your perishing — that pours weight into every hour you are given. A creature that could not die could not *matter.* Its choices would be weightless, infinitely revisable, never final. **Your mortality is the very thing that makes your life a life and not a smear.**

So when Allah announces death and calls it a favor — He is telling a truth your heart already half-knows. *The ending is a gift.* The fact that this is not forever is *why it is precious.*

[PAUSE]

But that is only half of the placement. Look at what He pairs death with: *"and there will remain the Face of your Lord."* He does not leave you with mere annihilation. He sets your perishing right beside *His permanence.* Everything that fades is set against the One who does not. And *that* is the deeper favor.

Think of what it would mean if *everything* perished — including the One who gives. Then death would be the last word, and the ending would not give your life meaning, it would *steal* it, because there would be nothing on the far side to receive what your life *meant.* But Allah pairs *fānin* with *yabqā* — *perishing* with *remaining.* You perish *toward* something. Your transience is not a fall into nothing; it is a **return to the One who remains.** The favor is not only that you end. The favor is that **He doesn't** — that there is a permanent Face to which your impermanent life is traveling, a *Rabb* who has been raising you, in stages, *for exactly this return.*

[PAUSE]

And here is where the two halves of this ayah lock together. Remember the verb. *Tukadhdhibān* — to look at a real, present gift and brand it a lie.

So Allah is asking, right over the open grave: *Even this — even your death, even the perishing of everything you can see, set beside the Face that remains — even* ***this*** *favor, will you call it a lie?* Will you stand at the edge of your own ending, where the difference-detector finally fails and the curtain of "normal" is ripped away, and *still* refuse to see the gift?

Because notice — death is the one favor your perception *cannot* delete. You can grow numb to your breath. You cannot grow numb to your grave. It is the one *ālā'* that hedonic adaptation can never bury, the one favor guaranteed to break through the background and demand to be seen. Perhaps that is *why* the refrain is anchored here, in these two verses, more pointed than in all its other placements: Allah has placed the question beside the one truth no human heart can render invisible.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means that your fear of endings — and you have them; we all do — can be reread. The ending of this stage of your life. The aging of your body. The losses you can already see coming. The simple, daily fact that time is *moving* and you cannot hold it. Your instinct is to grieve these as the *withdrawal* of favor. This ayah asks you to consider that they are favor *itself* — that transience is the very thing giving your days their weight, and that all of it is moving you toward the only Face that remains.

Tomorrow, when you feel the ache of something ending — let it become, for one moment, the thing this ayah names it: not a theft, but an *ālā'.* A favor. The shape that makes your life mean something, and the current that carries you home.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see how the pieces were placed.

This ayah did not arrive randomly in the surah. It was set, deliberately, into a frame:

- **55:26** — *Everyone upon it will perish.* (The favor of the ending.)
- **55:27** — *And there will remain the Face of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor.* (The favor of the One who does not end.)
- **55:28** — *So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?* (The question, asked in the gap between your perishing and His permanence.)

The refrain is the *response* the soul is meant to give after seeing death and seeing the eternal Face side by side. First Allah shows you the two greatest realities — that you go, and that He stays. Then He turns and asks: *now — which favor will you call a lie?* The architecture itself is the argument: place a human being between their own grave and the Eternal Face, and the question answers itself.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same insight approached from two directions.

The **grammar** showed us a question engineered to be unanswerable: a flooding plural (*ālā'*) that won't reduce to one, pinned by a singular "which?"; a dual address that places you, in dignity, beside an unseen creation, asked by the *Rabb* who has raised you the whole time; and a verb (*tukadhdhibān*) so sharp that rejecting a present gift can only be called *branding a truth a lie* — held open in a tense that still lets you change your answer.

The **themes** showed us *why* it cannot be answered: because your perception has deleted the very favors most worth defending — and because even the favor you most fear, your own ending, turns out, when set beside the Face that remains, to be the gift that gives your whole life its weight.

Both arrive at one place. **You cannot name a favor to deny — not because you would not dare, but because once you truly see them, denial becomes the one impossible thing.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Which favor in your life has fallen so far below the threshold of your attention that you would have to *work* to see it again? What is the most deleted gift you own?
2. When the refrain returns the thirtieth time and you feel yourself stop hearing it — what is that numbness teaching you about how you receive *Him*?
3. If you set your own ending beside the Face that remains, the way this ayah does — does your fear of endings change shape? What would it mean to read a loss as an *ālā'*?
4. The question is asked to "you two." What does it do to your sense of self to be addressed not as the center, but as *one of two* weighty creations before one Lord?
5. The verb is open — *will you* deny, not *you have* denied. What answer are you giving with your life, today, while the door is still open?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Standing between your own perishing and the Face that remains, you are asked to name a single one of His countless, present, ungraspable gifts that you would dare to call a lie — and the question is built so that, once you truly see, you cannot.

### The Closing Invitation

You came in able to skip this ayah because you'd heard it thirty-one times. I hope you leave unable to hear it without stopping. Let the refrain do to you what it was built to do: every time it returns, let it be a hand on your shoulder turning your face back toward a favor you'd stopped seeing. Don't answer the question by naming a gift to deny. Answer it by rescuing one — today, and tomorrow, and the day after — from the oblivion of the ordinary. That is the only honest reply a creature drowning in *ālā'* can give.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Rabb who has raised us in stages and never let one breath fail — pull our deleted favors back into the light, and let us never grow so numb to Your giving that we cannot see it. You who remain when all faces perish — make our own ending sweet to us, because it carries us to You. And of all Your countless favors, let us never, with word or heart or silence, call a single one a lie.*

*Āmīn.*
