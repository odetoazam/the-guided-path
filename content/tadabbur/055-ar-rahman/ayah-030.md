# Which One Will You Call a Lie?

## فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION — Standing Inside the Most Repeated Question in the Quran

There is a sentence in the Quran that Allah asks you thirty-one times in a single surah. Thirty-one times. He does not vary it. He does not soften it. He does not, after the tenth time, assume you have understood and move on. He asks it again. And again. And again.

We do not do this with people. When we ask someone a question and they hear us, we let it go. To repeat a question thirty-one times would be, between human beings, almost unbearable — a kind of pressing that we instinctively avoid. And yet here is Allah, in *Sūrat al-Raḥmān*, the surah named after the most overflowing of His names, asking the same four words over and over until the question stops being something you read and becomes something you live inside.

Let me rebuild the room you are standing in.

Imagine the surah as a long, slow walk through everything Allah has made. He begins — *al-Raḥmān, ʿallama al-Qur'ān, khalaqa al-insān* — the Most Merciful, taught the Qur'an, created the human being. Then He walks you outside. Here is the sun and the moon, moving by precise reckoning. Here are the stars and the trees, prostrating. Here is the sky, raised. Here is the balance, set down so you would not transgress in the balance. Here is the earth, laid out for all living things — with its fruits, its date-palms in their sheaths, its grain with its husk, its fragrant herbs. Here are the two seas meeting, with a barrier between them, and from them come pearl and coral. Here are ships sailing like mountains.

And after each thing He shows you — after the sun, after the seas, after the fruit, after the pearls — He turns to you and asks the same thing. *So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

It is the rhythm of a host walking a guest through a house he has built for them, room by room, and at the doorway of each room, quietly turning and asking: *this one too — will you say I did not give you this?*

[PAUSE]

In our verse — *this* instance, 55:30 — the question lands right after something staggering. The verse just before it says: *"Whoever is in the heavens and the earth asks Him; every day He is bringing about a matter"* (55:29). Every single day, every moment, the entire heavens and earth are turned toward Allah, asking — and He is, without pause, attending, deciding, sustaining, raising one and lowering another, healing one and taking another, answering an ocean of need that never empties. And *then*, on the heel of that — having just shown you that you are alive only because He is, this very second, choosing to keep you alive — He asks:

**So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?**

Here is the hook. Notice He does not ask, "Are you grateful?" He does not ask, "Will you thank Me?" He asks something far more pointed, far more difficult to wriggle out of. He asks you to *name the lie*. He hands you the entire catalogue of existence and says: point to one. Point to a single favor and tell Me it isn't real. Tell Me I didn't give it.

Why would Allah frame gratitude as a dare to find a lie? Why "deny" and not "forget"? Why "which" — as if waiting for an answer? And who is the *second person* in that sentence — because the Arabic is not speaking to one of you. It is speaking to two.

Let us not rush. Let us earn this.

# PART ONE — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: تُكَذِّبَانِ — Why Ingratitude Has to Become an Accusation

Before we open the word, let me put a small idea in your hands — something from ordinary life.

There is a difference between *not thanking* someone and *accusing* them. Picture a friend who quietly paid off a debt for you when you were drowning. Now imagine two failures. In the first, you simply forget to thank them — you got busy, life moved on, you never said the words. That is a failure of gratitude. It is real, it is a flaw, but it is *passive*. In the second, your friend reminds you gently, and you look at them and say: *"You never did that. That didn't happen. I owe you nothing."* That is a different category of thing entirely. That is not forgetting. That is calling them a liar to their face about their own kindness.

Hold that distinction. Because Arabic has a way of marking it, and Allah chose it deliberately.

Now look at the word. The verb is **تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhibān*.

Its root is *kāf–dhāl–bā* (ك-ذ-ب), the root of *kadhib*, a lie. The simplest verb from this root is *kadhaba* (Form I) — "to lie," "to speak falsehood." But that is not the form Allah used. He used **Form II** — *kadhdhaba* — which the Leeds morphological corpus confirms, and which classical grammar describes as the intensive and *declarative* form. Form II doubles the middle letter, and doubling the letter doubles the force. *Kadhaba* is "to lie." *Kadhdhaba* is something more active and more aggressive: **to pronounce something false, to charge it with falsehood, to treat it as a lie.**

Sit with this. The difference between *kadhaba* and *kadhdhaba* is exactly the difference between our two failures.

*Kadhaba* would be the person who simply speaks an untruth. But *kadhdhaba* — the word Allah actually used — is the person who takes something true and *declares it false*. It is not lying. It is *belying*. It is looking at a reality and saying, "No. That is not real." It is the verb the Quran uses again and again for those who *rejected* the Messengers — they did not merely doubt them; they stood before the truth and named it a lie.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened to the verse?

Allah did not ask, *"Which of your Lord's favors will you fail to thank Me for?"* That would have been the passive failure — forgetting, neglecting. He asked, *"Which of your Lord's favors will you* ***belie*** *— will you stand before and declare false?"*

**He has made ingratitude impossible to do quietly.**

This is the precision of it. Because of this one verb form, you cannot be a casual ingrate. The favors are too obvious, too constant, too undeniably *there* — the breath in your chest right now, the light reaching your eyes, the ground holding your weight. To be ungrateful for something that real, you cannot simply look away. You have to *deny* it. You have to commit yourself to a falsehood. You have to look at the sun and say it was never lit for you, look at the water and say it was never made to be swallowed, look at the heart beating without your permission and say no one set it beating.

Ingratitude, Allah is telling you, is not a gap. **It is a claim.** And it is a false one.

And there is one more layer the verb tucks in. *Kadhdhaba* — to charge with falsehood — is, in its bones, an accusation against a *person*. You do not "belie" a rock. You belie a *speaker*, a *witness*, someone who has made a claim. So when the verse asks *which favor will you belie*, embedded in the grammar is the recognition that the favor is a kind of testimony — and behind every favor stands the One who gave it. To call the favor false is to call its Giver a liar.

That is why the verse is so quietly devastating. Ingratitude, dressed in the verb *tukadhdhibān*, is revealed for what it actually is: not a missing "thank you," but a charge of dishonesty laid against *al-Raḥmān* Himself.

**One word — and the entire weight of the verse changes. To be ungrateful is to accuse God of lying about His own generosity.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: آلَاء — The Word That Forces You to Count, One by One

Now to the thing being denied. The favors. The Arabic is **آلَاء** — *ālā'*.

But before the word, let me give you its shape — because *how* this word is built is the second half of the verse's argument.

There is a kind of question that is not really looking for an answer — it is looking for an *admission*. A teacher returns a test and says to a student, "Which of these answers did I mark unfairly?" — and goes silent, waiting. The student scans the page. Every red mark is correct. There is nothing to point to. The silence does the teaching. The question was never about finding an unfair mark; it was about making the student *look at every single one* and find none.

That is the engine of our verse, and it runs on one small word: **أَيّ** — *ayy*, "which." *Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā* — "So by *which* of the favors of your Lord...". The interrogative *ayy* demands a *specific item* in answer. Not "are you grateful in general," but "*which one* — name it." And the moment you try to answer, you are forced to do the very thing ingratitude can never survive: you have to **go through them one at a time.**

[PAUSE]

Now the word *ālā'* itself.

*Ālā'* is a plural — favors, bounties, graces. Lexicographers note something striking about it: it is a plural whose singular is debated and slippery — variously given as *ily*, *aly*, *ilā*. The point for us is not the technical dispute. The point is that **this word almost does not exist in the singular.** It comes to you, by its very nature, as a *multitude*. You cannot easily hold one *alā* in your hand; the word arrives already plural, already overflowing, already more than you can separate into pieces.

Feel how perfectly that fits a question that says *which one*. Allah asks you to single out one favor to deny — using a word that resists being made singular. He invites you to pick one thread from a fabric so densely woven that you cannot find where one favor ends and the next begins. Is the breath you just took one favor, or is it the lungs, the air, the oxygen, the brain that triggered it, the centuries of a beating heart behind it, the mother who carried you to your first breath? *Which one?* You cannot even isolate it to deny it.

This is the same reality Allah states plainly elsewhere: *"And if you should count the favor of Allah, you could not enumerate it"* (16:18, 14:34). *Ālā'* is that uncountability made into a single word, and then placed inside a question that demands you count.

[PAUSE]

And listen — actually *listen* — to the sound of this word against the verb beside it. *Ālā'* — open, soft, flowing vowels: *aa–laa*. It sounds like what it means: ease, grace, gentleness, a thing poured out generously. Now the verb: *tukadhdhibān* — *tu-kadh-dhi-baan* — clustered, hard consonants, the doubled *dh* catching in the throat. It is a harsh word. It *sounds* like an obstruction, a refusal, something jamming.

So the verse itself, in pure sound, stages the drama it describes: the *softness* of the gift meeting the *harshness* of the denial. The flowing *ālā'* and the choking *tukadhdhibān*. You can almost hear, in the phonetics alone, how unnatural, how *ugly*, it is to take something poured out so gently and answer it with a refusal so hard.

**Allah names the favors with a word too generous to be singular, then asks you to pick one to deny — and the very sound of the asking tells you it cannot be done.**

## Element Three: The Dual — رَبِّكُمَا and تُكَذِّبَانِ — You Were Never the Only One Asked

Here is the feature most easily missed in English, and it may be the most humbling of all.

In English, "you" hides a multitude. "Will *you* deny" — is that one person or a crowd? You cannot tell. But Arabic does not let this stay vague. Arabic has a *singular* "you," a *plural* "you," and — uniquely — a *dual* "you": a grammatical form reserved for **exactly two.**

And every address in this verse is in the dual.

*Rabbi-**kumā*** — "your Lord," where *-kumā* means "of you *two*." Not *kum* (you all), not *ka* (you, one man). *Kumā* — you two. And the verb *tukadhdhibā**n*** — that final *-ān* is the dual ending: "will you *two* deny." The Leeds corpus marks it: second person, dual.

So the question is not, as we instinctively read it, aimed at *me alone* in my private moment of reflection. It is aimed at *two*. Who?

The classical commentators are nearly unanimous. Al-Muyassar names them directly: *"O you two weighty ones"* — *ayyuha al-thaqalān* — **the jinn and mankind**, the two species of beings created with will, with the freedom to obey or rebel, the two who will be gathered and questioned. Al-Qurtubi records that this is the position of the overwhelming majority (*al-jumhūr*), and he marshals the evidence from within the surah itself: Allah said *"He created the human being"* (55:3) *and* *"He created the jinn"* (55:15); and a few verses on, He will say plainly *"We will attend to you, O you two weighty ones"* (55:31), and then *"O company of jinn and mankind"* (55:33). The whole surah is a conversation held with *two* species at once.

[PAUSE]

Let me make this land. Step back into that house Allah is walking you through, room by room. You thought the host was speaking to you alone. And then you notice — there is *someone else beside you*. Another whole creation, made of fire as you were made of clay, standing in the same hall, hearing the same question, just as accountable, just as unable to point to a favor and call it false.

What does this do to you?

First, it *dignifies* the question. This is not small talk. This is not a private nicety between you and God. This is a cosmic address, delivered to every being in creation who was given the terrible gift of choice. You are being spoken to as a member of one of only two peoples in all existence who *can* answer — and who therefore *must*.

Second, it *exposes* the strangeness of ingratitude. Al-Qurtubi preserves a narration he traces through al-Tirmidhi: the Prophet ﷺ recited *Sūrat al-Raḥmān* to his Companions, and they received it in silence. He said that the jinn had given a better response — that each time he reached *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*, **they answered**: *"Not for anything of Your favors, our Lord, do we declare falsehood — so to You belongs all praise."* (I cite this as al-Qurtubi reports it through al-Tirmidhi; it is a transmitted narration, not of the highest grade, but its meaning is unmistakable.)

Sit with that picture. The unseen creation — the jinn, whom we tend to associate with mischief and rebellion — *answered the question*. They did not let the refrain pass over them as background music. Each time Allah asked, they replied: *no, Lord — none of it will we call false.*

And the human beings, to whom the surah equally speaks, were silent.

[PAUSE]

The dual is not a grammatical footnote. It is a mirror held up beside you. *You are not the only one being asked. And right now, the other one may be answering better than you are.*

**The question was never rhetorical. It is dual because it expects a reply — and somewhere, the reply is already being given. The only question left is whether yours joins it.**

---

### Bridge

Look at what these four words have shown us. A verb — *tukadhdhibān* — that turns silent ingratitude into a spoken accusation, and makes you the accuser of *al-Raḥmān*. A noun — *ālā'* — too generous to be made singular, set inside a "which" that demands you count what cannot be counted. And a grammar of *two*, revealing that this most intimate question is also the most universal, posed to every willed creature in existence, and answered — by some of them — out loud.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words, but about the strange architecture of the human heart — about why we, of all creatures, find it so easy to swim in an ocean of gifts and feel nothing at all.

# PART TWO — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Why the Gift You Live Inside Is the One You Cannot See

Begin not with the verse, but with something you have felt this week and did not notice you felt.

Psychologists have a name for one of the most reliable features of the human mind: *hedonic adaptation*. It means this — whatever good arrives in your life, however large, your mind absorbs it, adjusts its baseline, and then stops registering it. The promotion that thrilled you becomes Tuesday. The home you longed for becomes the walls you ignore. The person you prayed to marry becomes the one you forget to look at. We do not lose these things. We lose our *perception* of them. They slide beneath the threshold of notice, and the mind, ever hungry, turns to scan the horizon for the next thing it does not have.

Here is the cruel mechanism inside it: **the bigger and more constant a blessing is, the more completely it disappears from view.** You will notice a stone in your shoe before you notice the foot it sits in. You will feel a single missed breath — a moment of choking — far more sharply than the eighteen thousand effortless breaths you took today. The favors most essential to your existence are precisely the ones most invisible to your attention, *because they never stop.* Constancy is camouflage.

Now bring the verse back. *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why Allah had to *ask*? And why He had to ask thirty-one times?

A question does what a statement cannot. A statement — "Allah has given you many favors" — slides past you, agreed-to and unfelt, filed away as something you already know. But a *question* reaches into your mind and *forces it to move*. *Which one?* Your attention, which had drifted off to the horizon, is yanked back to the foot inside the shoe. You are made to look — at the breath, at the light, at the water, at the heartbeat — at the very blessings that hedonic adaptation had rendered invisible. The refrain is not Allah informing you. **It is Allah un-numbing you.** It is the divine antidote to a mind built to forget.

And He does it *thirty-one times* for the same reason. One question, you would adapt to. You would read it once, nod, and move on, and the numbness would close back over you like water. So He asks after the sun — and before you can numb to that, after the seas — and before you numb, after the fruit — after the pearls — after the daily sustaining of all creation. He does not let the numbness re-form. **The repetition is the cure for the very adaptation it describes.** Each return of the refrain is a hand on your shoulder, turning you back to look again at what you had already stopped seeing.

This reframes what gratitude even *is*. We tend to think of gratitude as an emotion — a warm feeling we either have or lack. But the verse teaches that gratitude begins one step earlier, as an act of *attention*. Before you can thank, you must *see*. And seeing, for a mind like ours, does not happen on its own. It must be summoned. It must be asked for.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tomorrow, you will take roughly twenty thousand breaths, and adaptation will hide every one. So borrow the verse's method. Once — just once, deliberately — stop, and answer the question with a single favor you had stopped seeing. The breath. The eyes reading these words. The name of someone who loves you. Name it, and silently refuse to call it false. That is not a small thing. That is you doing, by choice, what Allah designed the refrain to make you do: lifting one blessing back above the threshold of sight. Do it once a day and you have begun to defeat the deepest reflex of your own mind.

## Theme Two: The Favor That Is Happening Right Now — رَبّ and the Verse Before

Now let us go back to where this particular refrain stands — because *where* Allah placed it tells us *which* favor He most wanted us to see.

This instance, 55:30, comes immediately after: *"Whoever is in the heavens and the earth asks Him; every day He is bringing about a matter"* (55:29). *Kulla yawmin huwa fī sha'n.* Every day — every instant — He is in a new affair. Raising and lowering. Giving and withholding. Forgiving here, granting there, taking a soul, knitting a bone, splitting a seed, answering a whispered prayer in a language no one else heard.

And then: *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*

Here is what the placement does. Most of the surah's favors are *finished* things you can point to — the sun *is* lit, the seas *are* set flowing, the fruit *is* hanging in the palm. They are gifts already given, standing there for you to notice. But the favor named just before *this* refrain is of a completely different kind. It is not a gift He gave. **It is a gift He is giving — right now, without pause, as the condition of this very moment continuing to exist.**

[PAUSE]

Let me make it physical, then point past the physical.

You think of yourself as a thing that exists — solidly, by default — the way a statue stands whether or not anyone is watching it. You imagine your existence as something you possess, something that would simply *continue* even if Allah turned His attention elsewhere. *"Every day He is bringing about a matter"* says: no. You are not a statue. You are more like a note being sung. A note does not "exist" the way a stone does; it exists only as long as the singer keeps breathing it out. Stop the breath, and the note does not fall to the ground — it simply ceases. There was never a note there to fall. There was only the singing.

Your existence is like that. You are being *sung* into being, this instant and the next, by a Lord who is *"every day in a new affair."* The favor is not that He made you, past tense. The favor is that He is *keeping* you, present tense, continuously, a sustaining so constant and so total that — by the very law of adaptation we just named — it is the one favor you are guaranteed never to feel.

And *that* is the favor Allah parks the refrain right beside. As if to say: of all the things you might deny, will you deny *this one* — the one happening inside you as you read? The fact that you are still here, still reading, still breathing, is not the background to the favors. **It is the favor.** It is *al-Raḥmān* choosing, in this exact second, to continue you.

[PAUSE]

And now the name. The verse does not say *"the favors of Allah"* or *"the favors of al-Qahhār,"* the Subduer. It says *ālā'i **Rabbi**kumā* — the favors of **your Lord.** The root of *Rabb* (ر-ب-ب) carries the meaning not just of a master, but of a *nurturer* — one who owns, yes, but who owns the way a gardener owns a seedling: by tending it, watering it, raising it stage by stage toward the fullness it was made for. To say *Rabb* is to say the One whose ownership *expresses itself as care*.

So the favors do not fall from a distant authority. They flow from *rabbūbiyya* — from the active, intimate, day-by-day nurturing of a Lord who is, this very moment, *"in a new affair"* for your sake. The constancy you cannot feel is not cold machinery. It is tending. It is a Gardener who has not, for one instant of your life, looked away from the plant He is raising.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time you feel that low, formless anxiety — *will I be okay, will it hold together, can I keep this up* — answer it with the verse before the refrain. You are not holding yourself up. You never were. *"Every day He is bringing about a matter."* The One who has sung you into being through every second you have ever lived is not about to fall silent now. Let the favor you cannot feel become the security you can rest in. That is what it means to *not* call this one a lie: to actually live as though you are held.

[PAUSE]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture — Why It Repeats

Now, having done the work, look at the surah from above, and the structure reveals its argument.

*Sūrat al-Raḥmān* is built as an alternation: a cluster of named favors, then the refrain; another cluster, then the refrain again; on and on, thirty-one times, all the way through this world's gifts and into the gardens of the next. We might have read this repetition as mere ornament — a beautiful chorus. But after the linguistic and thematic journey, we can see it for what it is.

The repetition *is the meaning.* Allah is describing a reality — His favors — that arrives without pause, wave after wave, never stopping, *"every day in a new affair."* How do you represent an *unceasing* inflow in the structure of a text? You make the text itself unceasing. You let the refrain return and return until the reader *feels*, in the very rhythm of the words, the relentlessness of the generosity they describe. The form of the surah is a model of the favor it names. The architecture is the proof: grace that does not stop, asked about in a question that does not stop.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The *grammar* showed us that ingratitude cannot be passive — the verb *tukadhdhibān* makes it an active accusation, a charge of falsehood against the Giver. The *psychology* showed us why we nonetheless slip into it so easily — because adaptation hides our largest gifts beneath the threshold of sight, until we are denying them not with malice but with sheer *numbness*. Put the two together and you arrive at the quiet terror, and the quiet mercy, of the verse: **what feels to us like merely "not noticing" is, in the courtroom of reality, a verdict of "false" delivered against al-Raḥmān — and the refrain exists to wake us before the verdict hardens.**

The *grammar* showed us the dual — that the question is posed to two species and *answered* by one of them. The *theme* showed us what the right answer sounds like: not a feeling, but an act of attention, a deliberate refusal to call the breath, the light, the daily sustaining, a lie. The jinn's reply — *"not for anything of Your favors do we declare falsehood; to You belongs all praise"* — is simply a heart that has stopped being numb, said out loud.

## Questions to Carry

1. If Allah asked you, right now, *"which one?"* — which favor would you be most ashamed to discover you had stopped seeing?
2. The other species was answering while the humans sat silent. When the refrain washes over *you* — in salah, in recitation — does your heart answer, or does it let the words pass as background?
3. You instinctively feel that your existence is *yours*, a default you possess. What would change in your anxieties if you truly absorbed *"every day He is bringing about a matter"* — that you are being sung, not standing on your own?
4. Ingratitude, the verb tells us, is a kind of accusation. Is there a favor in your life you have quietly been treating as *owed* to you — and is that, dressed up, a soft form of calling it false?
5. What is one favor so constant in your life that, like the foot inside the shoe, you have never once thanked Allah for it specifically?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**Every gift Allah gives is a question He is asking, and because the gifts never stop, the question never stops — and the only way to be ungrateful is to look at the undeniable and call it a lie.**

## The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked, by this verse, to manufacture a feeling. Feelings come and go and cannot be commanded. You are being asked to do something smaller and far more durable: to *look*, and having looked, to *refuse the lie*. To let the refrain do its work on you — to let it un-numb you, once a day, for one breath, one mercy, one undeniable gift — and to answer the way the unseen creation answered. Not for *anything* of Your favors, our Lord, will I declare falsehood. The numbness is the default. The looking is the choice. And the choice is available to you in the next breath you take — the one you were about to take without noticing.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who are every day in a new affair for our sake, forgive us for the favors we have lived inside and never seen — the breath, the light, the holding-together of all that we are. Open our eyes to what You pour out without ceasing, and let our hearts answer Your question the way Your creation answered it: that not for anything of Your favors will we ever call You false — so to You, our Lord, belongs all praise.* Āmīn.
