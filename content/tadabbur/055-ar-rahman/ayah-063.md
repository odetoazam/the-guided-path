# The Question With Only One Honest Answer

## Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 63

> فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: A Question Asked Thirty-One Times

I want to begin not with the words, but with a strange experience.

Imagine someone you love sits you down. And instead of telling you they love you, they begin to list. Quietly. One thing at a time.

*Remember when you were sick and I stayed up.* Pause. *Remember when you had nothing and I gave.* Pause. *Remember the roof, the food, the morning you woke up breathing without asking to.* Pause.

And after each item, they don't say "you're welcome." They ask you a question. The same question. Every single time.

*So tell me — which of these are you going to call a lie?*

You would have no answer. Not because you're being stubborn. Because the question is built so that honesty has only one direction. You cannot point to a single gift and say *that one — that one didn't happen, that one wasn't real.*

[PAUSE]

This is Surah Ar-Rahman. The scholars called it **ʿArūs al-Qur'ān** — the Bride of the Qur'an — for its beauty. And the most famous thing about it is this one sentence, repeated **thirty-one times** from beginning to end. Not as a chorus you skip past. As a heartbeat. As a refrain that returns after the sun and the moon, after the seas and the pearls, after the description of the Fire, after garden upon garden upon garden.

Today we are sitting with the thirty-first occurrence's neighbor — ayah 63 — and I want to tell you up front: this is a four-word sentence. Four words. And we are going to spend nearly an hour inside it, because every one of those four words is doing something that, once you see it, you will not be able to un-see.

Let me rebuild the world this question was first asked in.

The Prophet ﷺ recited this surah aloud. And in a narration that al-Qurtubi brings in his commentary — reported through al-Tirmidhi — the Prophet ﷺ said that when he recited Surah Ar-Rahman to the jinn, **they gave a better response than you (humans) did.** Every time he reached *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*, they answered: *"Not one of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny — so all praise be to You."*

Sit with that image. A whole order of creation, unseen, answering a refrain out loud. And the quiet rebuke folded inside it: *they answered. You went silent.*

So here is the question I want you to carry into this hour, and I am not going to answer it yet. **Why does Allah ask "which favor will you deny?" — instead of simply saying "do not deny My favors"? Why a question with no possible answer? And why, of all the words in Arabic, does He choose the verb that doesn't mean "forget" or "ignore," but means something far more violent — to call a gift a lie?**

Let's go find out.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: ءَالَآء — The Word He Chose for "Favors"

Let me teach you something about the Qur'an before we touch this word.

Arabic has more than one word for a blessing. The most common is **niʿmah** (نعمة) — from the root that gives us *naʿīm*, comfort, ease. When you read elsewhere "*and as for the favor of your Lord, proclaim it*" (93:11), that's *niʿmah*. It's the warm, intimate, familiar word for a gift.

But that is **not** the word in our refrain.

The word here is **ءَالَآء** — *ālā'*. Look at it. Listen to it. *Aa-laa-eh.* It opens with a long, stretched alif and lands on a soft, open ending. The mouth has to *widen* to say it.

Now — the morphology. *Ālā'* (root أ-ل-و) is a **plural**. The lexicographers debate its singular — *ilā, alan, uluww* — but they do not debate its meaning: **favors, bounties, the displayed gifts of God.** And here is the first thing I want you to notice. Allah did not say *fa-bi-ayyi niʿmati Rabbikumā* — "which comfort of your Lord." He said *ālā'* — the plural of the grand, the manifest, the **displayed** bounty.

Let's sit with the difference.

A *niʿmah* is a kindness you receive. An *ālā'* — in the texture the classical usage gives it — is a kindness *put on display*, a bounty held up to be witnessed. Think of the difference between a gift left quietly on your doorstep, and a gift unwrapped slowly in front of you, item by item, so that you *see* it being given.

That is the entire mode of this surah. Allah is not just blessing — He is **unwrapping**. The sun, on display. The moon, on display. The two seas, the coral, the pearl, the gardens with their fruit hanging low — each one held up. *Look. Look. Look.*

[PAUSE]

And here is what the plural does that the singular never could. He did not ask "which **favor**." He asked "which of the **favors** — *ālā'*." The plural is already a flood. Before you have even reached the verb at the end of the sentence, the noun has already buried you. The question assumes not one gift you might overlook, but **gifts beyond counting**, every one of them visible, every one of them undeniable.

This is why the same four words can return thirty-one times and never feel repetitive. Each time, the *ālā'* before it is different. Here at ayah 63, the favor on display is what came just before in ayah 62: *"and besides those two, two other gardens."* **Besides** the highest paradise — *more.* Gardens beyond the gardens.

Al-Tabari notes that the classical commentators were not in full agreement about what *min dūnihimā* — "from below those two" — carries here. Some held these are gardens **lower in rank** (*fī al-daraj*), a degree beneath the first exalted pair — not lesser in beauty, but a step below the summit. Others read *dūn* as simply meaning *besides* or *other than*, with no implication of inferior station. The refrain at ayah 63 holds in either reading. But in the ranked reading, something quietly devastating surfaces: God does not wait until the highest gift to ask. He asks about **each tier of His generosity as it descends** — not only "will you deny the summit?" but "will you deny *even the overflow below it?*" The question is equally unanswerable at every level.

So when the refrain lands at 63, it is pointing at a very specific bounty: not the gift of *enough*, but the gift of **more than enough.** The overflow. The excess. God's generosity spilling past the line where it could have honorably stopped.

What the verses immediately following reveal about these second gardens makes the point visceral. They are *mudhāmmatān* — a word for a depth of green so saturating it approaches darkness, gardens drunk so deeply on provision that their color has deepened past ordinary lushness. And their springs are described as *naddakhatan* — gushing, welling up with force — a different word from *tajriyān*, the steadily "flowing" springs of the first pair (55:50). Where the higher gardens have springs that run, these surge and overflow. The gifts do not diminish as God moves through His tiers of generosity. The color intensifies. The springs overflow with more force, not less. *Ālā'* — the displayed bounty — keeps accumulating, each naming more extravagant than a fair Creator would have owed.

*Which of these displayed favors — even the ones beyond what you needed, even the second-tier overflow — will you call false?*

One word reveals it: **He is not asking you to be grateful for sufficiency. He is asking you to reckon with abundance.**

## Element Two: تُكَذِّبَانِ — The Verb That Should Stop Your Heart

Now to the word that, the first time I understood it, genuinely changed how I read this entire surah.

The verb at the end is **تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhibān*. And I need to teach you the grammar carefully, because everything turns on it.

The root is **ك-ذ-ب** (*k-dh-b*) — to lie. In its simplest form, *kadhaba*, it means: he lied, he spoke an untruth.

But that is **not** the form here. The form here is **Form II** — *kadhdhaba* — and you can hear it in the doubled, hammered middle letter: *ka-DH-dhaba*. In Arabic morphology, Form II (the *tafʿīl* pattern) characteristically **intensifies** the root and turns it outward onto an object. So *kadhaba* — "he lied" — becomes *kadhdhaba* — and this is the crucial shift — **"he declared something false. He gave the lie to it. He called it a falsehood to its face."**

Do you feel the difference? *Kadhaba* is a lie you tell. *Kadhdhaba* — *takdhīb* — is a verdict you pass. It is not the absence of belief. It is an act. It is taking something true and standing in front of it and pronouncing: *false.*

[PAUSE]

Now hold the whole sentence together and feel the collision.

Allah lists His **ālā'** — His displayed, undeniable, overflowing bounties. The seas. The pearls. The gardens beyond the gardens. And then He chooses, for the human and jinn response, not the verb *tansawn* ("you forget"), not *tajhalūn* ("you are ignorant of"), not *taʿrūn* ("you turn away"). He chooses **tukadhdhibān** — *you give the lie to.*

Think about what that exposes. Allah is telling us that the real nature of ingratitude is not a soft thing. It is not forgetfulness. **Ingratitude is calling a gift a lie.** When you receive breath, eyesight, a body that works, a roof, a child's face — and you live as though none of it were given, as though it all simply *is* — you are not merely forgetting. In the eyes of this verb, you are standing in front of a real, displayed, witnessed bounty and pronouncing the verdict: *false. Not a gift. Owed to me. Mine by right. No one to thank.*

That is *takdhīb.* And the surah is too honest to call it anything gentler.

[PAUSE]

Now here is where the impossibility of the question becomes total. The structure is *fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — and the *bi-* on *bi-ayyi* is the instrumental, the "by means of which." So the literal weight is: **"So by means of which of your Lord's favors do the two of you pass the verdict of falsehood?"**

Read that slowly. The question doesn't ask *whether* you deny. It assumes the denying and asks you to *name the gift you're using to do it.* And you cannot. Because the very breath you would use to call a blessing false **is itself** one of the blessings. The tongue that would say *"none of this is from God"* is itself an *ālā'*. You would have to deny the gift using the gift. The question is a closed circle, and there is no honest way out of it except the jinn's answer: *not one. Not one of them, our Lord, do we deny.*

What this single verb reveals: **the opposite of gratitude is not forgetting — it is a verdict, and it is a verdict that refutes itself the moment it is spoken.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: رَبِّكُمَا — The "You" That Is Two

We've looked at the gift and at the denial. Now look at *who is being asked.* Because there is a grammatical feature running through this entire surah that an English reader walks straight past, and it is one of the most extraordinary things in the Book.

Look at two pieces of the ayah: **Rabbi-kumā** and **tukadhdhib-ān.**

That ending — **-kumā** on "your Lord," and the **-ān** on the verb — is not the plural. Arabic has a separate grammatical number that most languages have lost entirely: the **dual.** Not "you" (one). Not "you all" (many). But **"you two."** A form reserved for exactly two parties.

So the refrain does not say "which of the favors of your Lord will *you* deny," addressed loosely to whoever is listening. It says, with surgical precision: **"will *you two* deny."** Thirty-one times. The whole surah is built on a "you" that is exactly two.

Who are the two?

Al-Qurtubi tells us plainly, and calls it the position of the majority: this is an address to **al-ins wa-l-jinn** — humankind and the jinn. He grounds it in the surah itself: Allah says *"He created the human..."* (55:3) and *"and He created the jānn..."* (55:15); He says *"We shall attend to you, O **two weighty ones** (al-thaqalān)"* (55:31); and He calls out openly, *"O **assembly of jinn and humankind**..."* (55:33). The dual is not loose. It is the two species who carry the burden of being accountable — *al-thaqalān*, the two weighty ones — addressed together, side by side, thirty-one times.

[PAUSE]

Now let this land, because it changes the room you thought you were standing in.

When you read this surah, you are not the only audience. There is another whole order of creation being addressed in the exact same breath, with the exact same dual ending wrapping you both together. You and they are *-kumā*. You and they are the *thaqalān.* And — go back to the narration we opened with — **when this question was recited, they answered, and your kind did not.**

The dual is a mirror held up at an angle. It says: *you are not the center. You are one of two. And the other one passed a test you tend to fail.*

But the dual is doing something tender, too — and this is the part I cannot get past. Look at the word it attaches to: not "Allah," not "the Sovereign," but **Rabb.** *Rabbi-kumā* — "the Lord *of you two.*" The root **ر-ب-ب** carries the meaning of the one who *nurtures, sustains, raises, brings to maturity stage by stage.* This is the intimate name. The name of the caretaker, not the distant king.

So the dual does not lump you into an anonymous crowd. It binds you and the jinn into a *small, specific belonging.* He is *Rabb of you two* — your two kinds, the only two carrying this weight — the One who has been raising and sustaining you both, gift by gift, the whole time. The question is sharp, yes. But it is asked by the One who has been feeding you while He asks it.

What this single grammatical form reveals: **you are being addressed as one of two accountable creations, by the One who personally sustains you both — and the intimacy of "your Lord" is precisely what makes the denial unthinkable.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these four words, opened one at a time, have built.

A noun — *ālā'* — that floods you with **displayed** bounty before you can object. A verb — *tukadhdhibān* — that names ingratitude not as forgetting but as **a verdict of falsehood, spoken with the very gift you'd deny.** And a dual ending — *Rabbikumā ... -ān* — that pins you as **one of two accountable kinds, addressed by your own Sustainer.**

The construction itself is a sealed room. There is no exit.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of the human heart, and about a God who keeps asking instead of simply telling.
