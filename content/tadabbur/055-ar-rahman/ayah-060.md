# The Word That Comes Back to You

## Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 60

> هَلْ جَزَآءُ ٱلْإِحْسَـٰنِ إِلَّا ٱلْإِحْسَـٰنُ
>
> *Hal jazā'u-l-iḥsāni illā-l-iḥsān.*
>
> **"Is the reward for good anything but good?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing Inside the Garden

Before we open these five words — and there are only five — I want you to feel where you are standing when you hear them.

You are deep inside Surah Ar-Rahman. By the time you arrive at this line, the surah has already done something to you. It has a heartbeat. Thirty-one times across its length, a single refrain returns like a wave returning to the shore: *fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — **"So which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?"** Again. And again. And again. Addressed to two audiences at once — humankind and the jinn — the *thaqalān*, the two weighty species.

And in the verses just before this one, the surah has stopped cataloguing the cosmos — the sun and moon on their reckoning, the sea where two waters meet, the mountains anchored like landmarks — and it has turned to the reward. It has begun to describe the gardens. For the one who *feared the standing before his Lord* — there are two gardens. Branches heavy with fruit. Springs running. Couches lined with rich brocade. Fruit hanging low enough to take. Companions of restraint and beauty.

Verse after verse of overwhelming generosity. The listener is almost saturated. The senses are full.

And then — right in the middle of all this description — the surah does something unexpected. It stops describing. It asks a question.

[PAUSE]

Not "here is more fruit." Not "here is another spring." Instead: **"Is the reward for good anything but good?"**

Why pause the tour of Paradise to ask a question? Why interrupt the gift to state a principle?

Here is the hook. I want you to notice something strange about this verse before we even begin. It is built almost entirely out of **one word, said twice**. The same word — *al-iḥsān* — appears at the start and at the end. The reward and the deed are called by the **identical name**. As if Allah reached for the whole dictionary of the Arabic language to describe what He gives back to you — and chose to give it back *the same word you gave Him*.

What does it mean that the reward wears the same name as the deed?

Stay with that question. Because the answer is going to turn out to be one of the most tender things in the entire Qur'an. Let's earn it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Question That Is Not a Question

Let me teach you something about how language works before we touch the Arabic — something you already do every day without naming it.

Imagine your friend has just done something extraordinarily kind for you. They drove three hours in the rain to help you move. And afterward, someone asks you, "Are you going to thank them?" And you say:

**"How could I not?"**

Now notice — that was a question in its *grammar*. It has a question mark. But it was not a question in its *meaning*. You were not asking for information. You were not uncertain. You were making the strongest possible statement — *of course I will* — and you dressed it as a question precisely **because a question is more forceful than a statement.** A flat "I will thank them" is a fact. "How could I not?" is a conviction. The question form pulls the listener in and makes them answer it themselves, in their own heart, and arrive where you already are.

Arabic does this with surgical precision, and it has a name for it. The word here is **هَلْ** — *hal*. Normally *hal* is a simple interrogative: "Is...?" "Does...?" But the classical commentators noticed that *hal* does not always ask.

Listen to what al-Qurtubī documents. He says *hal* in the Arabic of the Qur'an moves across **four registers**. Sometimes it means *qad* — "indeed, already" — as in *hal atā ʿalā-l-insān* ("there has indeed come upon man..."). Sometimes it is a true question. Sometimes it carries the force of a **command** — *fa-hal antum muntahūn* ("will you not then desist?"). And sometimes — and this is our verse — *hal* carries the meaning of **مَا** in the mode of *al-jaḥd*, of **negation**. Al-Jalālayn confirms this reading directly: *hal* here means *mā* — "not." The verse is not interrogating; it is declaring.

[PAUSE]

Let that land. In this verse, *hal* does not mean "is...?" It means **"there is not."**

So the sentence, read at its grammatical foundation, is not asking whether good is rewarded with good. It is **declaring**: *There is no reward for good — except good.*

And now look at the partner of *hal* in this verse: **إِلَّا** — *illā* — "except." This is the exceptive particle. And here is the engine of the whole line. When you combine a negation with an exception — *nothing... except...* — Arabic rhetoricians call this construction **qaṣr**, restriction, or **ḥaṣr**, confinement. It is the most emphatic affirmation the language can produce. *Nothing... but this one thing* is far stronger than simply saying *this thing*.

Think of how you do it in English. "I want dessert" is a preference. "I want **nothing but** dessert" — now the whole field has been cleared and only one thing is left standing. Negate everything, then carve out the single exception, and that exception blazes.

So watch what the structure actually accomplishes. Allah does not say, "Good is rewarded with good." A plain statement. He says — through *hal* and *illā* — **"Is the reward for good anything but good?"** Which means: *I have searched the entire range of possible responses to your good, and I have eliminated every one of them — except this.* The good you did has exactly **one** possible destiny. It cannot be lost. It cannot be ignored. It cannot return as anything lesser. The grammar has **sealed off every exit** except the return of good.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the difference? A statement informs you. This construction *guarantees* you. The rhetorical question does not leave room for doubt — it **closes** the doubt, the way a key closes a lock. There is no universe in this grammar where your good goes unanswered.

That is what *hal... illā* reveals. Not a question. A promise with every loophole removed.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Word Said Twice

Now to the heart of it. The two words that are the same.

**ٱلْإِحْسَـٰنِ ... ٱلْإِحْسَـٰنُ** — *al-iḥsān... al-iḥsān.* The deed is *iḥsān*. The reward is *iḥsān*. Identical letters. Identical root.

Let me first open the word itself, because everything depends on it.

The root is **Ḥ-S-N** (ح-س-ن), and at its base it means **beauty, goodness, that which is lovely and fitting.** *Ḥusn* is beauty. *Ḥasan* is good, fine, beautiful. But our word is not *ḥusn*. It is *iḥsān* — and that prefix, that lengthening, matters enormously.

Here is a small grammatical key. In Arabic, you can take a simple root and put it onto a pattern that means **"to actively cause, to do, to make happen."** Grammarians call it **Form IV** — the pattern *afʿala*. The morphology data confirms it: *iḥsān* is the verbal noun of *aḥsana*, Form IV. So *ḥusn* is beauty as a *quality* — something is beautiful. But *iḥsān* is beauty as an **action** — you actively **make** something beautiful. You **do** good. You **produce** excellence where there was none.

So *iḥsān* is not merely "being good." It is the *doing* of beauty. It is taking your action — your prayer, your charity, your patience, your treatment of a stranger — and not just completing it, but **making it beautiful.** Doing it as fully, as gently, as excellently as it can possibly be done.

This is exactly what the Prophet ﷺ named when the angel Jibrīl came to him in human form and asked, "What is *iḥsān*?" And he ﷺ answered — and this is a *marfūʿ* ḥadith, traced directly to him in the rigorous collections — **"That you worship Allah as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then [know] that He sees you."** That is *iḥsān*. Not just worship. Worship made beautiful by the awareness of being seen. The highest of the three stations: Islām, then Īmān, then *Iḥsān* at the summit.

Now hold that meaning, and come back to the verse. *Iḥsān*, that summit, that beautiful-doing — what is its reward?

**Al-iḥsān.** The exact same word.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this, because there are at least two ways the classical scholars opened it, and they do not contradict — they are two camera angles on one reality.

The first reading — al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar, and al-Jalālayn all gather here. The reward of the one who *did beautifully in this world* is that **he is dealt with beautifully in the next.** Al-Muyassar makes it concrete: the reward of the one who did good through his deeds in the *dunyā* is nothing but *iḥsān* shown to him — *bi-l-jannah* — by being given the Garden in the *ākhira*. Al-Jalālayn adds a precision worth pausing on: the first *iḥsān* is *bi-l-ṭāʿa* — good done *through worship and obedience* — and the second is *bi-l-naʿīm* — iḥsān returned *through bliss*. The pairing is not incidental. The quality of the *ṭāʿa*, the specific texture of your obedience in this life, maps directly onto the quality of the *naʿīm* — the specific character of your delight in the next. Worship lived with beauty returns as bliss lived with beauty. You did beauty; beauty is done back to you. You worshipped as if you saw Him; now you are given to actually *see* Him. The structure of the verse mirrors the structure of reality: input and output share a name because they share a nature.

And there is a second reading, narrated by al-Baghawī and al-Qurtubī from Ibn ʿAbbās and ʿIkrima — and notice this is an *athar*, a report from the Companions and their students, not a saying of the Prophet ﷺ himself, so we hold it as the precious commentary it is. On this reading, the first *iḥsān* is the deepest beauty a human heart can do: **to say *lā ilāha illā Allāh*** — to declare that there is no god but God — and to live by what Muḥammad ﷺ brought. And the reward — the second *iḥsān* — is **the Garden** itself. The most beautiful word answered by the most beautiful home.

Put the two readings together and you have a composite far richer than either alone: *iḥsān* is the whole arc of a beautiful life — the beautiful creed of *tawḥīd*, lived out as beautiful deeds, made beautiful by the awareness that Allah sees — and its single, sealed, guaranteed destiny is *iḥsān* returned: to be dealt with beautifully, forever, in a beautiful place, by the Most Beautiful.

Here's what's remarkable. Notice what Allah did **not** do. He had the entire Arabic language available. He could have said: the reward of *iḥsān* is *jannah*, garden. Or *naʿīm*, bliss. Or *riḍwān*, His pleasure. Each of those is named elsewhere in the Qur'an. But *here*, in this verse, He chose to answer the word with **itself**. To let your own action echo back to you wearing its own name.

Why? Because it tells you something the other words could not. It tells you the reward is **of the same kind** as the deed. That when you do beauty, what comes back to you is not a *substitute* for your beauty, not a *payment* in some foreign currency — it is your own beauty, magnified, returning home. The word coming back to you is the most intimate possible way of saying: *what you sent out is what you will receive.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Asymmetry Inside the Symmetry

But now I have to show you something, and this is where the verse becomes almost unbearably generous. Stay with me very closely.

The word is the same. But the **two who perform it are not the same.**

The first *iḥsān* is **yours**. A human being's. And what is human *iḥsān*? It is real, it is precious — but it is *finite*. It is a few decades of struggle. It is prayers offered through tiredness. It is charity from a limited purse. It is patience that frays and is rebuilt. It is beauty done by a weak creature who forgets, who sins, who returns. Your *iḥsān* has a ceiling, because *you* have a ceiling.

The second *iḥsān* is **His.** And here the same word means something with **no ceiling at all.** Because when *Ar-Raḥmān* — and remember, that is the very name presiding over this entire surah — when *Ar-Raḥmān* does *iḥsān*, He does it with the resources of the One who owns the gardens and the springs and the eternity in which they last. His *iḥsān* is not bounded by decades; it is forever. It is not bounded by a purse; He owns everything. It is not weakened by forgetfulness or fatigue.

So look at what the grammar is hiding in plain sight. The verse uses **one word** to make the deed and the reward look *equal* — symmetrical, balanced, fair. *Iḥsān* for *iḥsān*. That is justice, *ʿadl*, and it would already be enough.

But because the **two actors are infinitely unequal**, the very sameness of the word becomes the vehicle for an **infinite overflow.** Your finite, flickering beauty is answered by His infinite, unending beauty — and both are called by the identical name. The equality of the *word* is the disguise; the inequality of the *givers* is the gift.

[PAUSE]

This is the genius of choosing the same word. If Allah had used a *bigger* word for the reward — if He had said "your small good earns My enormous garden" — you would have felt the gap, the distance, the sense that you are being *over*paid. Instead, by using the *same* word, He folds the infinity inside a whisper. He says, with total tenderness: *you did good; good is what returns.* And only when you sit with it do you realize that "good" from your hand and "good" from His hand are separated by an ocean — and He let you keep your dignity by calling them the same.

And notice one more absence. He says *al-iḥsān* — **the** good, with the definite article, in the abstract. Not "your good," not *iḥsānukum*. He did not chain it to you and your smallness. He left it open, universal, as a **law of reality**: that wherever beauty is done, beauty returns. He could have made it personal and limited. He made it cosmic and unlimited, and then let you walk inside it.

### The Bridge

So look at what the five words have built.

A guarantee, not a question — sealed shut with *hal* and *illā*, every loophole closed. A law named with a single word, repeated — *iḥsān* calling back to *iḥsān* across the gap between earth and heaven. And an asymmetry hidden inside a symmetry — the same word used by unequal hands, which means the equality of the name conceals an infinity of excess.

Now al-Jalālayn's precise mapping comes into full focus: the first *iḥsān* is *bi-l-ṭāʿa* — through worship, through obedience, through the orienting of your life toward Him. The second *iḥsān* is *bi-l-naʿīm* — through bliss, through delight, through a beauty that has no end. The verse maps *ṭāʿa* to *naʿīm*: worship becomes bliss. And notice what that specific pairing reveals. The verse is not merely about justice — the settling of accounts, the balancing of a ledger. It is about **resonance**. About what happens when a creature, out of all its possible orientations, chooses beauty. When the quality of that choice — its depth, its sincerity, the degree to which it inhabits the awareness of being seen — flows directly into the quality of what it receives.

[PAUSE]

And this is why the verse had to use the same word twice. Any other pairing — *ʿibāda* rewarded with *jannah*, *ṭāʿa* rewarded with *naʿīm* — would have named a transaction. *Iḥsān* → *iḥsān* does something different. It names a **return**. What you gave comes home. The deed wears the name of the reward and the reward wears the name of the deed, and they share that name because they share a *kind* — just at different scales, from different hands, across different worlds.

You sent beauty ahead. Beauty is what comes back to you. Five words. Everything sealed.

## PART 2: THE LAW BEHIND THE GARDEN

### Why the Surah Stops Describing

Let me return to the question I raised in the introduction: why does Surah Ar-Rahman interrupt its vision of paradise to state a principle?

If you were writing a tour of the greatest gift ever given, you would describe it. You would give more details. You would keep the listener in the experience. And the surah *does* do that — on both sides of this verse. In verses 58 and 59, immediately before our line, al-Tabari describes how the companions of the garden are likened to *yāqūt* — ruby, luminous, through which the thread inside can be seen from without — and to *marjān*, coral or pearl, carrying their own light. The imagery is staggering: beings of such beauty that they are simultaneously transparent in one dimension and radiant in another. The listener has been saturated with the visual. The ears are full of wonder.

And then, between that image and the next refrain, comes this principle.

[PAUSE]

Why? Because the surah is doing something architecturally profound. It is not merely giving a gift. It is naming the *logic* of the gift. It pauses in the middle of showing you the garden to tell you **why the garden exists at all** — so that when you return to the description, you hear it differently. The companions of *yāqūt* and *marjān* are not random generosity. The springs are not decoration. The fruit hanging within reach is not surplus. They are *precisely what was coming to you*, because of precisely what you sent ahead.

The garden is *iḥsān* returned. That is what verse 60 says. And once you know that, every image of the garden that follows is not just beautiful — it is **legible**. You can read it. You can trace each gift back to the deed that earned it, because the deed and the gift share the same name. The garden is not a mystery gift from an inscrutable God. It is your own *iḥsān*, glorified, magnified, and returned to you wearing the name you gave it.

The surah stops describing so that you will understand what you are being described.

### The Name Over the Surah

There is something about the placement of this verse that I do not want to pass over.

This is Surah **Ar-Raḥmān**. The surah of the Most Merciful. Before any content — before the description of creation, before the listing of favors, before the two gardens — this surah names itself after one of His names. And it is not merely a name. It is a **declaration of character**.

*Ar-Raḥmān* — the One whose mercy is vast, encompassing, prior to any deed. The One who gives before you ask. The One whose generosity is not triggered by your achievement but by His nature.

And now listen: this surah of the All-Merciful, in the middle of its vision of paradise, pauses to say that good is answered with good. Here is the deepest layer. *Iḥsān* is itself a divine attribute — Allah is *al-Muḥsin*, the One who does good, the One who makes things beautiful. When the verse says the reward of *iḥsān* is *iḥsān*, it is also saying: **the reward of your beautiful-doing is to be received by the Beautiful.** The reward of your small, earnest attempt at beauty is to be enveloped in the *iḥsān* of the One who invented beauty — whose *iḥsān* is *bi-l-naʿīm*, al-Jalālayn tells us, bliss without measure.

That is *Ar-Raḥmān* naming the law by which He operates. Not as a legal transaction, but as a revelation of His own nature. This is how He is. This is who He is. And the entire surah — every refrain, every favor catalogued, every garden described — is evidence that the law is already in operation.

[PAUSE]

He has been doing *iḥsān* to you from before you could do *iḥsān* to anyone. He created you with beauty. He taught you speech — *ʿallamahu-l-bayān* (55:4). He gave you the sun and moon on their reckoning, the sea that does not transgress its boundary, the earth spread out for your standing. His *iḥsān* came first. It came before your deed, before your awareness, before your first breath.

And so verse 60 is not only forward-looking — *your good will be rewarded with good in the garden*. It is also backward-looking. It explains everything that already happened to you. You were created, sustained, guided, forgiven — not because you earned it, but because *Ar-Raḥmān* operates on the principle that beauty goes out and beauty returns. And it was His *iḥsān* that started the chain. Before you gave anything, He gave. And now He is telling you: when you *do* give — when you join the chain, when you orient your actions toward Him with genuine presence — it **must** come back to you. It is not charity at that point. It is law.

## PART 3: THE WORD IN YOUR HANDS

### What Iḥsān Looks Like

We have spent a long time with the theology. Now let's come closer. What does *iḥsān* actually look like in a life?

The Jibril hadith gave us the definition: worship Allah as though you see Him. And that definition, when you hold it up to ordinary life, becomes very specific.

It is not a different *category* of action. It is not "do extraordinary things." It is doing the *same* things — prayer, charity, honesty, patience, kindness — but with a different *quality* of attention. *Iḥsān* is not about *what* you do but about *how fully you bring yourself* to what you do.

The prayer you would have prayed quickly, distracted — *iḥsān* means arriving at it as if you had an audience. Not performing for an audience. *Having* one. The difference is everything. A performance is still about you. An awareness you are genuinely inside changes you. You slow down. You choose words more carefully. You mean what you say.

The charity you were going to give and move on from — *iḥsān* means pausing to make it beautiful. Not just the amount, but the manner. With a kind word. Without the performance of generosity. Given in a way that protects the dignity of the one who receives it.

[PAUSE]

And notice: all of this flows from **awareness**. *Iḥsān* is, at its core, consciousness. The Prophet ﷺ said: *worship Him as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, then He sees you.* Even if you cannot achieve the first — the vivid, felt presence of seeing — you can hold the second. He sees. And that awareness, when it is real, when it has settled into the body and is not just an idea in the mind, beautifies everything it touches.

This is what the verse is calling you toward. Not perfection. Not the absence of struggle or failure. It is calling you toward a quality of *presence* in your actions — a bringing-of-yourself to what you do — that makes your deeds *iḥsān* rather than mere motion. Because not every action is *iḥsān*. You can pray without praying. You can give without giving. The body can move through the form while the heart is somewhere else entirely. That is not *iḥsān*. That is the shell of an act without its inhabiting.

*Iḥsān* is when you arrive. When you bring yourself — fully, present, aware — to what you are doing. When the deed is inhabited.

### The Verse and Its Companions in the Qur'an

It is worth pausing on how this principle echoes across the Qur'an — not as curiosity, but as evidence of how central it is.

In Surah Al-Naḥl (16:90), Allah commands *al-ʿadl wa-l-iḥsān* — justice *and* beautiful-doing. The command pairs them deliberately: justice (*ʿadl*) is giving exactly what is due; *iḥsān* is going beyond what is due, into generosity and beauty. The two are not the same. You can be just without being *muḥsin*. But the divine command reaches for both — as if to say that justice is the floor, and *iḥsān* is what builds upward from there.

And in Surah Yūnus (10:26): *"For those who have done good (ahsanū) is the best (al-ḥusnā) and more (ziyāda)."* Notice — the reward is not just *al-ḥusnā*, the beautiful recompense. It is *al-ḥusnā* and *ziyāda* — the good reward and then something beyond it. Classical commentators understood this *ziyāda*, this excess, as the vision of Allah Himself — the gift that surpasses every named gift, the reward that no word has yet been big enough to fully contain. So verse 10:26 tells you: the reward of *iḥsān* is beauty — and then something beyond beauty that only the Beautiful can give.

Verse 55:60 does not say all of this explicitly. But it gestures toward it by using the same word for deed and reward — because no word, finally, is large enough to name what *His iḥsān* actually means. Calling it by the same name as your deed is the most honest approximation available: it points toward the shape of the thing without pretending to capture its magnitude.

[PAUSE]

And there is Surah Al-Qaṣaṣ (28:84): *"Whoever comes with a good deed will have better than it."* Better. Not the same — *better*. Surah Ar-Rahman says *the same word* — and yet it means something better than your deed, because the giver of the reward is greater than you. These verses are not in tension. They are facets of the same reality, each showing a different angle of the same breathtaking truth: that what you send ahead of you is not merely safe — it is *growing*. It is becoming more than it was. The *iḥsān* you gave has been received by the One who does not diminish things. It has been received by the One who only adds.

## PART 4: THE QUESTION ADDRESSED TO YOU

### The Refrain That Follows

Let me now tell you something about the verse that comes *after* verse 60. It is the refrain — *fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — "So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"

This refrain has appeared across the surah many times already. You know it by now. It has become almost a rhythm in the hearing.

But do you see what happens when it follows verse 60?

Every other time the refrain appears, it follows a description of a *thing* — a favor, a gift, a creature, a garden. *Here*, it follows a *principle*. And that changes the weight of the question entirely.

The refrain is now asking: after being told that there is a law in this universe — a sealed, grammatically locked, divinely established law that your beauty will be met with beauty — **which part of that will you deny?**

Not "will you deny that the garden has fruit?" Not "will you deny that the springs run?" But: will you deny that the law holds? Will you deny that the equation closes? Will you deny that what you sent ahead is not lost?

[PAUSE]

This is the sharpest the refrain has been. Because the favors you might deny are usually concrete things — and you might, in a bad moment, doubt the garden, doubt the companions, doubt the springs. But this refrain is asking about something harder to deny: a *law of return*. You cannot look at your own genuine acts of *iḥsān* — at the times you were truly present, truly generous, truly aware — and believe they dissolved into nothing. Something in you knows they did not. The refrain is asking that *something* to stand up and be counted.

It is addressed to both the *ins* and the *jinn* — every thinking being who has ever wondered whether the good they did mattered. Whether the care they spent was noticed. Whether the beauty they put into the world had a destination.

The answer is built into the grammar of the verse that precedes the refrain. Not a question at all. A law. And the law says: **it was never wasted.**

## CONCLUSION: The Five Words Come Home

We began with a mystery. Why does the surah stop the tour of paradise to ask a question? Why interrupt the gift to state a principle?

By now you know it is not a question. It is a declaration, dressed as a question so that you can answer it in your own heart, arrive at the answer in your own voice, and know it more surely than if you had been told.

And the principle is this: *iḥsān* has one possible destiny. It cannot scatter. It cannot evaporate. It cannot return empty-handed. It is sealed by the grammar — *hal... illā* — into a single inevitable outcome: *iḥsān* returned.

Five words. Subject, exceptive particle, two identical nouns, and a guarantee built into the very structure of the sentence.

And inside those five words, everything:

The word is the same, so you feel the fairness of it — a deed met with a deed of the same name. But the actors are unequal, so the fairness is also an overflow — your small *iḥsān* of worship and obedience, al-Jalālayn tells us, met by His *iḥsān* of bliss without measure. The law is not abstract. It has a direction: from earth to heaven, from the texture of your *ṭāʿa* to the texture of your *naʿīm*, from your creaturely act of turning toward Him to the flood of beauty that comes pouring back.

[PAUSE]

There is a practice hidden inside this verse, and I want to leave you with it.

The next time you do something and wonder whether it matters — the next time you say a careful word to someone who is struggling, the next time you give from what you have rather than what you have left over, the next time you choose the slower, more present, more beautiful way of doing what you could have done quickly — I want you to hear this verse.

*Hal jazā'u-l-iḥsāni illā-l-iḥsān.*

Not as a reward system. Not as a transaction. But as a law of the universe, spoken by the One who made the universe, about the nature of beauty itself: **that it returns.**

Your *iḥsān* is not a letter dropped into an unmanned box. It is addressed. It is received. And the One who receives it operates on one principle, named across the Qur'an in more than one voice: that beauty sent out — beauty comes back. That whoever comes with a good deed will have better than it (28:84). That for those who did *iḥsān* is the best and more (10:26). And here, at the heart of the surah of the Most Merciful, in five words without decoration: *nothing but good, for good*.

[PAUSE]

The surah is named after *Ar-Raḥmān*. And *Ar-Raḥmān* paused His description of paradise to tell you this: that the garden you have been hearing about is not foreign to you. You are already building it — deed by deed, prayer by prayer, careful word by careful word. Every act of *iḥsān* is a word you are sending ahead, and the word that comes back to you — when you arrive — will be wearing your own name, spoken by a voice that makes it larger than you ever could.

*Hal jazā'u-l-iḥsāni illā-l-iḥsān.*

Is the reward for good anything but good?

The grammar says no. The law says no. The One who sealed the grammar and established the law says no.

What you send is what returns. And what returns — from His hand — is beyond what you sent.
