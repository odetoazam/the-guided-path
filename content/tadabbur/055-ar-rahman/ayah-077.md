# The Last Time the Question Is Asked

## Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 77

**فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ**

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Refrain That Will Not Let You Go

There is a verse in the Qur'an you have almost certainly heard, even if you have never opened a single page of tafsir. If you have ever sat in a masjid during Ramadan, or heard a recitation drift out of someone's car, or listened to a child memorizing in the back of a class, you have heard it. It returns, and returns, and returns. Thirty-one times in a single surah, the same eleven syllables come back like a wave that will not stop reaching the shore:

**فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ**

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

Most of us have heard it so many times that we have stopped hearing it. It has become wallpaper. Beautiful wallpaper — but wallpaper. The recitation washes over us and we feel something, a kind of rhythmic awe, and then it passes.

Today we are going to do something different. We are going to stop on a single one of those thirty-one repetitions. Not the first. Not a middle one. **The last one.** Ayah 77 — the thirty-first time the question is asked, after which it is never asked again in this surah. One verse later, the entire chapter closes and falls silent.

[PAUSE]

To understand why the *last* one is different from all the others, we have to rebuild the world this verse lives in.

### Rebuilding the World

Surah Ar-Rahman is unlike anything else in the Qur'an. It opens not with "In the name of Allah" as its first word of content, but with a *name* — **Ar-Rahman**. The Most Merciful. The Entirely Merciful. And then, without a verb, without a sentence connecting them, it simply begins listing:

*Ar-Rahman. Taught the Qur'an. Created the human being. Taught him eloquence.* (55:1–4)

The sun and the moon move by precise calculation. The stars and the trees bow down. He raised the sky and set the balance. He laid out the earth for all living things — fruit, date palms, grain, fragrant herbs.

And then, the first time, the question arrives: *So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

From there the surah becomes a tour. A guided walk through everything the Most Merciful has made and given. The two seas. The pearl and the coral. The ships that sail like mountains. Then a turn toward the end of all things — the Day when everything perishes and only the Face of your Lord remains, the Day of reckoning, the description of the Fire for those who denied. And then — the Gardens. Two gardens for those who feared standing before their Lord, with their flowing springs and fruits in pairs. And below them, two more gardens, dark green and dense, gushing with water, filled with fruit and palms and pomegranates and pure companions reclining on green cushions and fine carpets.

And it is **right here** — at the very end of that description of Paradise, with the listener's imagination still full of green and water and rest — that the question comes for the thirty-first and final time:

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

[PAUSE]

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to sit with before we begin. By ayah 77, the speaker has shown you *everything*. Creation, sustenance, the sea, the stars, justice, judgment, and finally Paradise itself. Every card is on the table. There is nothing left to reveal.

So why ask again?

If you ask a question thirty times and get no answer, why ask a thirty-first? What does the *last* asking know that the first one didn't? And why — this is the strangest part — why is the verb in this verse not "forget," not "ignore," not "fail to thank," but a far heavier, far more dangerous word: **to call a lie**?

Let us find out.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: تُكَذِّبَانِ — The Verb of the Disbeliever

Let me ask you to picture something ordinary first, before we touch the Arabic.

Imagine someone spends years quietly doing things for you. They pay for something when you weren't looking. They show up when you were sick. They wrote you a letter you never knew about. And then one day, in front of others, you say: *"You never did anything for me. None of that is real. You made it up."*

Notice that this is worse than forgetting. Forgetting is passive — the gift slips your mind. This is something else. This is taking the gift, looking the giver in the eye, and **declaring it false.** Calling it a lie. Turning a real thing into a fiction with your own mouth.

Hold that image. Because that — exactly that — is the verb Allah chose.

### Opening the Word

The verb at the end of our verse is **تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhibān*.

Its root is **ك-ذ-ب** (*k-dh-b*). And that root, in its simplest form, means *to lie*. *Kadhaba* — he lied, he spoke falsehood.

But our verb is not in that simple form. Let's look closely.

### Deepening

In Arabic, a verb's basic three-letter root can be reshaped into different *forms*, and each form bends the meaning in a specific direction. Our verb sits in what grammarians call **Form II** — *taf'īl* — the form made by doubling the middle letter. *Kadhaba* (he lied) becomes *kadhdhaba*.

Now here is the thing you must understand about this particular doubling. With many verbs, Form II intensifies an action — to do something hard, repeatedly, thoroughly. But with this verb, and a handful of others like it, Form II does something more precise. It makes the verb **declarative**. It doesn't mean *to tell a lie*. It means **to declare something else to be a lie. To brand a thing as false. To pronounce it untrue.**

Let me make this concrete with its exact opposite. The opposite of *kadhdhaba* is **صَدَّقَ** — *ṣaddaqa* — also a Form II verb. *Ṣaddaqa* means *to declare something true, to affirm it, to believe it.* When you have *īmān*, when you are a *muʾmin*, what are you doing? You are performing *taṣdīq* — you are looking at the message and saying, *this is true.*

And *tukadhdhibān* is the precise mirror image. To look at something and say: *this is false.*

[PAUSE]

So the question is not asking, *which favor will you forget?* It is not even asking, *which favor will you be ungrateful for?* It is asking something with the full weight of belief and disbelief behind it:

**Which favor of your Lord will you look at — and call a lie?**

### Deeper Still — Where Else This Verb Lives

Now stay with me, because this is where it opens.

This same verb — *kadhdhaba*, in this exact declarative sense — is the verb the Qur'an uses again and again for one specific group of people. The people who heard the Messengers and rejected them. *Kadhdhabat ʿĀdun al-mursalīn* — ʿĀd **denied** the messengers. *Kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — they **declared Our signs to be lies.**

Do you see what has just happened?

The Qur'an has reached into its vocabulary and pulled out, for the act of ingratitude toward a favor, **the exact same verb it uses for rejecting a prophet.**

This is not an accident of language. This is a teaching buried inside a grammatical choice. The surah is telling you, at the level of the verb itself, that **denying a gift and denying a messenger are the same kind of act.** Both are *takdhīb*. Both take something true and call it false. The ingrate and the disbeliever are doing the same thing with their hearts — one is denying a sunrise, the other is denying a prophet, but the *motion of the soul* is identical: to be handed truth and to answer, *this is a lie.*

[PAUSE]

That is why the verse does not say "forget." Forgetting is human and forgivable. *Takdhīb* is a verdict. It is the heart sitting as a judge over the gifts of God and ruling them counterfeit.

### What This One Word Reveals

*Tukadhdhibān* tells us that ingratitude, at its root, is not a memory problem. It is a **truth problem.** Every favor is a true thing — a real, solid, undeniable fact, like the warmth of the sun on your skin. To be ungrateful is to commit a kind of perjury against reality: to receive what is undeniably real and to deny it anyway.

## Element Two: رَبِّكُمَا — Whose Favors, and Who Is Being Asked

Now look at the word right before the verb: **رَبِّكُمَا** — *rabbikumā* — "your Lord." And I want to draw your attention to two separate things hidden inside this one word. One is about *whose* favors these are. The other is about *who* is being addressed. Both are doing quiet, enormous work.

### First: The Meaning of Rabb

The word **رَبّ** — *Rabb* — we usually translate as "Lord." And that is correct, but English flattens it. The root **ر-ب-ب** (*r-b-b*) carries a meaning that "Lord" does not capture on its own. It is the root from which *tarbiyah* comes — the slow, patient, sustained raising and nurturing of something from smallness to fullness. A *Rabb* is not a distant master who issues orders. A *Rabb* is **the one who takes a thing in its helplessness and nurtures it, stage by stage, into completeness.**

Think of what raises a child: not a single act, but ten thousand acts across years. Feeding, protecting, teaching, correcting, all unseen and unthanked, all aimed at bringing a helpless creature to its full stature.

That is the word sitting underneath the favors. The verse does not say "favors of *Allah*," using the name of power. It says favors of your **Rabb** — *the One who has been nurturing you, gift by gift, your entire existence.*

[PAUSE]

So now read the verse again with that loaded into it:

*Which of the favors of the One who has raised you from nothing, sustained you through every breath, and is even now bringing you toward your completion — which of those will you call a lie?*

The choice of *Rabb* makes the *takdhīb* almost unbearable. It is not the ingratitude of a stranger refusing a stranger. It is the ingratitude of the raised toward the One who raised them.

### Second: The Dual — كُمَا

Now the second secret in this word. Look at the ending: *rabbi-**kumā**.* And then the very end of the verse: *tukadhdhib-**ān**.*

In English we have "you" — and it covers one person or a thousand. Arabic is more precise. It has a singular *you*, a plural *you*, and — uniquely — a **dual**: a form that means *exactly two.* Not one, not many. Two.

And this entire surah, all thirty-one refrains, is in the **dual.** *Your Lord* — the Lord of you two. *Will you two deny.*

Two *what*?

The classical commentators discuss this, and al-Qurtubī reports that the position of the majority — *al-jumhūr* — is that the two being addressed are **al-thaqalān**: the two "weighty" species who carry the burden of moral accountability. **Mankind and the jinn.** The seen species and the unseen one. The surah itself supports this; earlier it had said, *We shall attend to you, O you two weighty ones* (55:31), and *O company of jinn and mankind* (55:33). Al-Qurtubī even mentions a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhī in which the Prophet ﷺ, having recited this surah to his companions and found them silent, told them that when he recited it to the jinn, **their response was better than yours** — for at each refrain they would answer, *Not one of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that image. A whole unseen species, listening to this same question, and answering it out loud. *Not one. Not a single favor do we deny.*

The dual does something to you when you realize it. It means **you are not addressed as a faceless crowd.** You are addressed as one of exactly two kinds of beings in all of creation who were given the weight of choice — the capacity to either affirm the favor or call it a lie. The angels cannot deny; they have no such freedom. The animals cannot deny; they have no such burden. Only two species in the cosmos were handed the terrible dignity of being *able* to say "this is a lie" to the face of God. And you are one of them.

### What This Word Reveals

*Rabbikumā* tells us two things at once. The favors belong to **the One who nurtured you** — which makes denying them an intimate betrayal, not a distant offense. And the question is aimed at **the two species who alone can deny** — which means the question is not rhetorical decoration. It is addressed to your specific, dangerous freedom.

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ — The Question That Demands a Count

Now the opening of the verse. **فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ** — *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾ.* "So by which of the favors…"

### The Interrogative That Forces an Inventory

Notice the form of the question. It does not ask, *will you deny the favors of your Lord?* — a question you could brush off with a vague "no" or "yes." It asks **بِأَىِّ** — *bi-ayyi* — **"by *which*?"** *Which one.* Name it.

This is a question that will not accept a general answer. It demands that you point. If you are going to deny, says the verse, then *show me which one.* Put your finger on the specific favor you are prepared to call false. The sun? The water? The air now entering your lungs? Your own eyesight reading these words? The fact that you exist at all rather than not?

[PAUSE]

The genius of *bi-ayyi* is that it turns a vague feeling of grievance into a forced inventory. We are very good at a *general* sense of dissatisfaction — *my life is hard, things aren't fair, I haven't been given enough.* The verse refuses to let us stay in that fog. It says: fine. Itemize it. Walk through the favors one by one and tell Me, **specifically, which of these is the lie.**

And the moment you are forced to be specific, the grievance collapses. Because no one — not the most miserable, not the most wronged — can actually point to the sunrise and say *that is false.*

### The Word ʾĀlāʾ

And the favors themselves: **ءَالَآء** — *ālāʾ.* From the root **أ-ل-و** (*ʾ-l-w*). It is a plural — and notice, the surah never uses the singular. It is always *ālāʾ*, the favors, *in the plural*, because the gifts of the Rabb never come one at a time. They come as a flood. Al-Jalalayn and al-Muyassar both gloss it simply: *niʿam* — blessings, bounties. Every refrain in the surah is asking about *these* — not a single favor, but the entire pouring abundance of them.

### Deeper — And This Is the Thirty-First Time

Now here is where our specific verse, ayah 77, becomes different from all the others.

Remember: this is the **last** refrain. By the time you arrive here, the surah has spent seventy-six verses filling the *ālāʾ* — the favors being asked about — with content. The first time the question was asked, back at ayah 13, you had heard about creation and the earth's fruits. But now, at ayah 77, the word *ālāʾ* has been loaded with **everything**: the sun and moon, the seas and ships, the pearl and coral, the promise that His Face endures, the justice of the Day, and — right before this very verse — **Paradise itself.** Two gardens, and then two more, green and gushing, with fruit and rest and pure companions.

So when the question comes the thirty-first time, the word "favors" is no longer abstract. It is full to bursting. *Bi-ayyi ālāʾ* now means: *after all of this — after I have shown you the cosmos and the sea and the Garden — now, point to one. Which is the lie?*

[PAUSE]

The first asking was a question. The thirty-first asking is a verdict waiting for the defense to speak — and there is nothing left to say.

### What This Construction Reveals

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾ* tells us that denial cannot survive specificity. Ingratitude lives in vagueness; the moment Allah demands that you *name the lie*, the case against the favors falls apart in your hands. And by the thirty-first time, the favors named include Paradise — so the question is no longer testing you. It is showing you that you have no answer.

[PAUSE — major]

## Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three words have built together.

**Tukadhdhibān** — to deny is to *call a true thing a lie*, the very act of the disbeliever. **Rabbikumā** — and the true thing belongs to the One who *nurtured you into being*, while the question is aimed at your specific, weighty freedom. **Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾ** — and you are commanded to *name* the lie, to point at one specific favor, until by the thirty-first time you stand among the gifts of creation and Paradise with your finger raised at nothing.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about a verse — but about the human heart itself, and about the strange mercy of being asked the same question thirty-one times.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Why We Call Our Gifts Lies

Let me begin where you actually live, not in the seventh century but in your own week.

There is a word psychologists use: **hedonic adaptation.** It describes something you already know from the inside. When something good enters your life — a new home, a recovery from illness, a relationship, even something as small as a working body — there is a spike of gratitude. And then, predictably, almost mechanically, the spike fades. Within weeks the new thing becomes the baseline. It stops registering as a gift and starts registering as *normal*. And once it is normal, it becomes invisible. And once it is invisible, the smallest thing that goes wrong with it feels like an injustice.

You did not lose the favor. You lost the *ability to see it as a favor.*

This is the quiet machinery of the human heart, and the verse names it with terrifying precision. Because watch what hedonic adaptation actually does, step by step. First, the gift becomes normal. Then normal becomes invisible. Then invisible becomes *owed* — *of course* I can see, *of course* I can breathe, *of course* there is food. And here is the final stage, the one the verse is naming: once a gift feels *owed*, then any interruption of it feels like a *broken promise* — like a **lie**.

[PAUSE]

Do you see how we arrive at *takdhīb* without ever deciding to? No one wakes up and consciously chooses to call God's favors false. We get there by the slow erosion of attention. The favor fades from view, becomes an entitlement, and then the first time it falters — the first illness, the first loss, the first unanswered prayer — the heart cries out: *this isn't fair, this was supposed to be mine, I was promised better.* And in that cry, without naming it, the heart has just performed *takdhīb*. It has taken the ten thousand favors it stopped seeing and, on the strength of the one that faltered, declared the whole arrangement a lie.

### The Illumination

This is why the verse asks *bi-ayyi* — *which one?* It is the cure aimed precisely at the disease. Hedonic adaptation works by keeping the favors **general and invisible**. The verse heals by forcing them to become **specific and visible.** *Name the lie. Point to it.*

The moment you are made to itemize — *the breath, the eyes, the heartbeat that has not asked your permission to keep beating, the morning that came whether or not you deserved it* — the entitlement breaks. You cannot hold "life owes me" and "I can see this sunrise that I did nothing to earn" in the same hand. The specific favor, *named*, exposes the general grievance as the actual lie.

### Bringing It Home

So here is the thing this changes about tomorrow.

The opposite of *takdhīb* is *taṣdīq* — affirming a thing as true. Gratitude, in this verse's own grammar, is not primarily a *feeling*. It is an act of **truth-telling.** It is the heart looking at a real favor and saying, simply, *this is real. This is true. This came from my Rabb.*

Which means gratitude is something you can *do* even when you do not feel it — because it is not a mood, it is an acknowledgment of fact. Tonight, before you sleep, you do not need to manufacture an emotion. You need only to do what the jinn did: walk through the favors and, at each one, refuse the lie. *I will not deny this. Or this. Or this.* You are not performing for God. You are simply telling the truth about your own life — which is the one thing the ingrate cannot do.

## Theme Two: The Mercy of Being Asked Thirty-One Times

Now the second thing, and it is the deepest thing about *this* verse, the last verse.

Let me ask you a human question first. When does someone repeat themselves?

There are two reasons, and they are opposite. The first is impatience — when a person says the same thing over and over because they are frustrated, drilling it into you because you are not listening. But there is a second reason, and you know it from the people who love you most. A mother repeats *be careful* not because she is exasperated but because she **cannot bear** for you to be hurt, and the words keep coming because the love behind them keeps overflowing. The repetition is not nagging. It is the love refusing to stop.

[PAUSE]

Which kind of repetition is this surah?

Look at where it sits. Surah **Ar-Rahman** — named for the very attribute of overflowing, encompassing mercy. The whole surah is the speech of *the Most Merciful*, and a merciful speaker who repeats is not drilling you. He is **reaching for you, again and again, refusing to let the question be the last word until you answer it.**

And here is what struck the scholars and should strike us. This refrain appears thirty-one times — and not all of them follow descriptions of gardens and pearls. Several of them follow descriptions of the **Fire**, of the judgment, of the boiling water. The Most Merciful counts even the *warning* among the *ālāʾ* — among the **favors**. Because what is a warning, from One who loves you, except a mercy? To be told *there is a cliff ahead* is itself a gift. So the refrain follows the Fire as faithfully as it follows the Garden, and at each one it asks the same impossibly gentle question: *and this too — will you call this a lie?*

### The Illumination of the Last One

Now come to ayah 77, the thirty-first. The final reaching.

For thirty asks, the listener has been silent. Allah has shown creation, sustenance, justice, the Fire, the warning, and now — last of all — **Paradise.** And then He asks one more time. Not a thirty-first time because thirty was not enough information. There is no new information after thirty. He asks a thirty-first time because **mercy gives the final chance.** Because a merciful Lord does not close the door on the heels of His own description of the Garden without turning around, one last time, and saying: *you have seen it now. You have seen all of it. So — which?*

And then — silence. Because there is no answer. There never was. And in that silence, the surah does something breathtaking. It does **not** end on the question. The very next verse, ayah 78, the seal of the entire surah, is:

**تَبَارَكَ ٱسْمُ رَبِّكَ ذِى ٱلْجَلَـٰلِ وَٱلْإِكْرَامِ**

*Blessed is the name of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor.*

[PAUSE]

Notice what happened to the grammar. For seventy-seven verses it was *rabbi**kumā*** — *your Lord*, in the **dual**, addressing the two weighty species, holding them at the distance of the questioned. And the instant the questions stop, the dual dissolves into the singular — *rabbi**ka*** — *your* Lord, intimate, close, one-to-one. The interrogation is over. The defendant has no answer to give. And instead of a sentence, instead of a verdict, the surah turns and **blesses the Name of the Lord** who asked.

The structure itself is the mercy. The surah opened with a Name — *Ar-Rahman* — and closes with a Name — *the name of your Lord, full of Majesty and Honor.* The thirty-one questions are held inside two Names like a child held inside two arms. The whole interrogation was never about catching you in the lie. It was about walking you, favor by favor, all the way to the point where you finally see that there is no lie to be found — and then sealing it with blessing.

### Bringing It Home

So what does the thirty-first asking change about how you live?

It tells you that the question is still open *for you.* You are not at ayah 78 yet. You are somewhere in the middle of the refrains, and the Most Merciful is still asking, still reaching, still counting even your warnings as favors. The repetition in your own life — the recurring reminder, the verse that keeps finding you, the second and third and thirtieth chance — that is not God's impatience. That is *Ar-Rahman* refusing to let the question be the last word.

The only thing left for you to do is what the jinn did. To answer before the asking stops. To say, into the silence the verse leaves open: *Not one, my Lord. Not a single favor do I deny.*

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see the whole shape.

Surah Ar-Rahman is built like a single held breath between two Names. It opens: **Ar-Rahman.** It closes: **the name of your Lord, Owner of Majesty and Honor.** Between those two Names, thirty-one times, one question beats like a pulse — *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.* The favors pour in: creation, the cosmos, the sea, justice, the Fire-as-warning, and finally the two Gardens and the two below them.

And our verse, ayah 77, is the **last beat of that pulse** — the final reaching of mercy before the breath is released into blessing. It comes after Paradise, when there is nothing left to show, addressed in the dual to the two species who alone can deny, demanding that they name a single favor to call false — and then, finding no answer possible, the surah lets the dual melt into the singular and seals everything with the blessing of the Name.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same truth approached from two sides.

The verb *tukadhdhibān* showed us that denial is **calling a true thing a lie** — and the theme of hedonic adaptation showed us *how* a heart slides into that lie without ever choosing it: through the slow fading of favors into entitlements. The word *rabbikumā* showed us that the favors belong to **the One who nurtured us** and that the question targets our **specific freedom** — and the theme of repetition showed us that this same nurturing Lord asks thirty-one times not to trap that freedom but to *rescue* it. The construction *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾ* demanded that we **name the lie** — and the final silence proved we never can.

It is all one motion: a Merciful Lord, walking His ungrateful creation favor by favor toward the moment they finally tell the truth.

## Questions to Carry

- When something good in my life faded from a *gift* into a *given*, into something I am *owed* — when exactly did that happen, and did I notice?
- The last time I felt life had treated me unfairly — if I had been forced to name *which specific favor* was the lie, what would I actually have pointed to?
- The reminders that keep returning to me — the same lesson, the same verse, the same correction — have I been hearing them as nagging, when they were mercy refusing to stop reaching?
- The jinn answered the question out loud. If I answered it out loud tonight, favor by favor, how long would my list be before I ran out of breath?
- Am I living as though I am at ayah 78 — the case closed — or as though I am still inside the refrains, still being asked, still given the chance to answer?

## One-Sentence Distillation

To deny a single favor is to commit the disbeliever's very act — calling a true thing a lie — and after the Most Merciful has counted out every gift from creation to Paradise and asked thirty-one times *which one is false*, the only honest answer the heart can give is silence, and silence here is gratitude.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need to feel a great wave of emotion to obey this verse. You need only to stop lying. Gratitude, in this verse's own grammar, is not a mood — it is *taṣdīq*, the act of telling the truth about your own life. So before you sleep tonight, do the one thing the verse asks: name them. The breath. The sight. The morning that came uninvited. And at each one, refuse the lie. Let this be the night you finally answer the question that has been asked of you, gently, more times than you ever noticed.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, Ar-Rahman — You who have nurtured us from nothing and poured out favors we stopped seeing — do not let our hearts call Your gifts a lie. Teach us the answer the jinn gave: that not one of Your favors will we deny. And when the questioning is over, gather us under the blessing of Your Name, the Owner of Majesty and Honor.* Āmīn.
