# Which One Will You Call a Lie?

## The Question You Cannot Answer

> فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ
>
> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*
>
> **So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?**

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Verse

Let me ask you to do something before we even touch these words.

Imagine you are sitting with someone you love, and they begin to recount — slowly, patiently, one by one — everything they have ever done for you. Not to shame you. Not to collect a debt. Just to remind you, because they can see that you have begun to forget.

And every few sentences, they stop, look at you, and ask the same quiet question:

*"After this one too — will you tell me I made it up?"*

They keep going. Another gift. The same question. Another gift. The same question. Again and again, until you lose count of how many times they have asked, and the asking itself starts to feel like a kind of mercy — because a person who had given up on you would not keep stopping to ask.

[PAUSE]

This is the emotional architecture of Surah Ar-Rahman.

The entire surah is built around a single sentence that returns, by the traditional count, **thirty-one times**: *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — "So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?" It comes after the creation of the human being. It comes after the sun and the moon set to a reckoning. It comes after the two seas that meet and do not overflow. It comes after the description of the Hour, after the gardens promised to the God-fearing, after the rivers and the fruits and the pure companions.

And it comes here — at verse 75 — in one of the gentlest, most intimate corners of the whole surah. We are no longer being shown the cosmos. We are being shown the reward. The two lesser gardens, dark green with foliage. Two gushing springs. Fruits and dates and pomegranates. Good and beautiful companions, sheltered in pavilions. We are deep inside the description of what awaits — and right there, in the middle of the gift being unwrapped, the question lands again:

**Which of these favors — *which one* — will the two of you call a lie?**

Now here is what I want you to feel before we go a single step further. We have heard this refrain so many times by verse 75 that there is a real danger we stop hearing it at all. It washes over us. It becomes background. And that — that exact slippage, where a repeated gift becomes invisible — is not a flaw in how we read the surah.

**It is the precise spiritual disease the surah was sent to diagnose.**

So today we are not going to let it wash over us. We are going to slow down on a single occurrence of a sentence you may have read a hundred times, and ask: why *this* verb? Why *which*? Why *two* of you? And what would it actually cost a human being to answer this question honestly?

Stay with me. There is something stunning hiding in plain sight.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Ālāʾ* — The Favor That Cannot Be Counted, and *Ayy* — "Which One?"

Let me teach you something about how questions work before we open the word.

There is a difference between asking someone *"Did I help you?"* and asking *"Which of the things I did for you didn't count?"* The first question can be answered with a single "no." It invites denial. But the second question is a trap of a very particular kind — a merciful trap. To answer it, you cannot just wave your hand. You have to go *item by item*. You have to pick one. You have to point at a specific gift and say, *this one — this one was nothing.*

The Arabic word for "which" here is **أَىّ — *ayy***. It is an interrogative that demands *specification*. It does not ask "do you deny?" It asks "deny *which?*" It forces a finger to be pointed. It forces the one being questioned to single out an individual favor from a crowd of them and reject *that one, by name.*

Now hold that, and look at what *ayy* is reaching into.

The favors are called **ءَالَآء — *ālāʾ***. The root is hamza-lām-wāw (أ ل و). And here is the first remarkable thing: *ālāʾ* is a **plural**. The lexicographers — you will find this in Lisān al-ʿArab and in al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt* — gloss *ālāʾ* simply as *niʿam*, favors, bounties, blessings. But it arrives already in the plural, a word built for multiplicity, a word whose very shape says *more than you can hold in one hand.*

Let's sit with the collision of these two words.

*Ayy* says: **point to one.**
*Ālāʾ* says: **there are too many to count.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the bind the verse has placed us in? Allah does not ask, "Will you deny My favor?" — singular, deniable. He gathers every blessing into one vast, uncountable plural — *ālāʾ* — and then hands you the interrogative *ayy* and says: *fine. Out of all of these, choose the one you'll call false.*

And the human being stands there, mouth open, finger raised — and cannot land it anywhere.

Because *which one* would you choose? The breath you just took to form your objection — that was an *āla*. The eyes reading this — an *āla*. The fact that you have a tongue capable of denial at all is itself one of the favors you would have to use the favor to deny. The mind that constructs the argument against gratitude is running on borrowed bounty. **You cannot raise a hand to point at a single favor and reject it without that very hand being the favor.**

This is what the great recurring question of Ar-Rahman does. It does not argue with you. It simply asks you to *specify*, knowing — as Ar-Rahman knows everything — that specification is impossible. The honest human being is rendered beautifully, permanently silent.

There is a related verse that throws light on this. In Surah Ibrahim (14:34), Allah says: *"And if you should count the favor of Allah, you could not enumerate it."* The word there is *niʿmah*. The point is the same: the favors exceed counting. So when Ar-Rahman asks *which one*, the question is not really expecting an answer. It is exposing that no answer exists.

What does this single pairing reveal? That **ingratitude is not a position you can actually hold — it is only a position you can forget your way into.** No one, looking clearly at even one favor, can point to it and say *false.* The denial only survives in the dark, in distraction, in the not-looking.

## Element Two: *Tukadhdhibān* — To Deny Is to Call It a Lie

Now we come to the heart of the verse, and to do it justice I need to teach you a small piece of how Arabic verbs are built.

In Arabic, a verb can be intensified or transformed by its *form* — its pattern. The simplest form, Form I, of the root k-dh-b (ك ذ ب) is *kadhaba* — "he lied." It describes a person speaking a falsehood.

But this verse does not use Form I. It uses **Form II — *kadhdhaba*** — with a doubled middle letter. *Tukadhdhibān* is the Form II imperfect, in the dual. And Form II does something specific: here it takes the meaning of *declaring something to be a lie*, of *treating a thing as false, calling it false, branding it a lie.*

Let me make the difference concrete. *Kadhaba* (Form I) is what a liar does. *Kadhdhaba* (Form II) is what someone does to the truth *when it is standing right in front of them* — they look at it and pronounce it false. This is the word the Qurʾān uses again and again for the rejecters of the messengers: *kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* — "they called Our signs lies." It is the verb of **takdhīb**: the active branding of a present truth as falsehood.

Now feel the weight of the choice.

Allah did not ask, *"Which favor will you fail to *thank* Me for?"* He did not ask, *"Which favor will you *disbelieve in*?"* — there are words for that, like *takfurūn*. He chose the most confrontational verb available:

**Which of these favors will you call a *lie*?**

[PAUSE]

Why is this so devastating? Because the other favors of Allah you might at least claim not to perceive. You might say of the unseen, "I never saw it, so I didn't believe it." But you cannot say that of a *favor you are standing inside of.* You are not being asked to deny a theory. You are being asked to deny an *experience* — the food in your stomach, the spring water of the garden, the air, the eyes, the promise of reward laid out in the verses just before this one.

And to call an experience you are *living inside of* a lie — that is not skepticism. **It is calling your own life a fabrication.** It is looking at the warmth on your skin and saying "there is no sun." The verb *tukadhdhibān* exposes ingratitude for what it secretly is: not a reasoned doubt, but a denial of the obvious, a *takdhīb* of the self-evident.

And notice — this is the lexically attested core of the word, not an interpretive stretch. *Kadhdhaba* means precisely *to brand as false something presented as true.* The Qurʾān's most consistent target for this verb is the one who rejects clear signs. By using it for *favors*, Ar-Rahman is quietly telling us: **to be ungrateful for a favor is spiritually identical to denying a prophet's miracle. It is the same act — calling the undeniable false.**

What does this verb reveal, then? That **ingratitude is not a small sin of the heart's temperature. It is a lie told about reality** — and the most reckless lie of all, because the evidence against it is the very life of the one telling it.

[PAUSE — major]

## Element Three: *Rabbikumā* and *Tukadhdhibān* — Why "the Two of You"?

Read the verse one more time and listen for a sound you may have been skating past:

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi **Rabbikumā** tukadhdhib**ān***.

Two endings carry the same hidden grammar. *Rabbi-**kumā*** — "your Lord," where *-kumā* is the dual possessive: *the Lord of you **two***. And *tukadhdhib-**ān*** — "**the two of you** deny." This entire question, all thirty-one times it sounds across the surah, is addressed not to *you* singular, and not to *you* plural — but to *you two*. A dual. Two parties, held together inside one question.

Who are the two?

Here the classical commentators are clear and agreed. Al-Qurtubī tells us this is the speech of Allah to **mankind and the jinn** — *al-thaqalān*, "the two weighty ones" — and he notes this is the position of the majority. And he points us to the surah's own internal evidence, which is the most beautiful kind of proof: a few verses earlier Allah says *"We shall attend to you, O you two weighty ones (ayyuhā al-thaqalān)"* (55:31), and *"O company of jinn and humankind..."* (55:33). The surah itself names its two addressees. The dual is not a guess; the text decodes itself.

Al-Qurtubī also relates a narration reported by al-Tirmidhī, from Jābir, in which the Prophet ﷺ recited Sūrat al-Raḥmān to his Companions and, finding them silent, told them that when he recited it to the jinn, *they* responded better — for at every "which of the favors of your Lord will you deny," they answered: *"None of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny."* (I relay this as it is transmitted in the commentary tradition.)

Now let me draw out what this dual does to *us*, the human reader.

Teach yourself first this everyday truth: there is a particular kind of accountability that only exists in the presence of a witness. When you are alone, you can let yourself believe a comfortable lie. But put one other person in the room — someone facing the same evidence you are — and suddenly the lie has nowhere to hide. You cannot quietly deny the sun is shining when someone is standing beside you, squinting in the same light.

This is what the dual address accomplishes. Allah does not let the human being face this question in the privacy of his own self-deception. He yokes us to another entire order of creation — the jinn — and asks *both* of us at once: *which of these, you two, will you call a lie?* And the surah has already let us overhear the jinn's answer. They picked *none.* They could not find one favor to point at.

[PAUSE]

So the dual leaves the human being exposed in a very specific way. You are not the only creature being asked. There is another, made of smokeless fire, who heard the same question — and who, by the report, answered honestly: *not one, my Lord, not one.* The question becomes, gently: *and you? Standing beside them? Will you be the one who pretends he cannot see?*

And there is a second, sweeter layer in *Rabbikumā* — "your Lord," the two of you. Of all the names Allah could have used in the question — and remember, the surah is named after one of them, *Ar-Rahmān* — He signs each repetition not with "God" or "the Mighty," but with ***Rabb***: the Lord, the One who creates and *nurtures*, who *raises a thing stage by stage to its completion.* The root r-b-b carries exactly that: not just ownership, but *cultivation*, *upbringing*, *the patient bringing-up of something from nothing to fullness.* So the question is really: *which favor of the One who has been raising you, the two of you, this whole time — will you call a lie?* The very word for "Lord" is itself an inventory of favors. He hides another *āla* inside the name He uses to ask about the *ālāʾ*.

What does this construction reveal? That **this question is not a private interrogation but a cosmic one** — two creations summoned together, neither able to hide behind the other — and that even the way Allah names Himself in the asking (*Rabb*) is one more gift the listener would have to deny to answer "yes."

## Bridge: From the Words to the Reality

Look at what these three features have built, before we go to the depths.

*Ayy ālāʾ* — "which favor" — has shown us that ingratitude has no place to put its finger; you cannot point at one favor and reject it, because the pointing finger is itself the favor.

*Tukadhdhibān* — "you call a lie" — has shown us that to be ungrateful is not to doubt but to *fabricate*; it is calling your own life a forgery.

*Rabbikumā... tukadhdhibān* — "the two of you, about your Lord" — has shown us this is no solitary musing but a question put to all of creation at once, signed in the name of the One who has been raising us the whole time.

These are facts of the language. Now let us ask what they reveal not just about words — but about the human heart, and about the strange mercy of a God who asks the same question thirty-one times.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Ingratitude Is Not an Argument — It Is an Amnesia

Let me begin not with the verse but with something psychologists have a name for, because you have lived it.

It is called **hedonic adaptation**. Here is how it works. The first day you have something you longed for — the new home, the recovered health, the person beside you — you can hardly believe your good fortune. You are *awake* to it. But the human nervous system is built to stop noticing constants. Within weeks, the miracle becomes the floor. The floor becomes invisible. And soon you are standing on a gift you no longer see, reaching past it, faintly dissatisfied, hunting for the next thing.

This is not a moral failure you chose. It is the default setting of the mind. The favors that never leave you are the favors you stop perceiving. You will spend an hour grieving a thing you lost and not one second thanking God for the ten thousand things you still have, *because the ten thousand are silent.* They do not announce themselves. They just hold you up, unnoticed, like the floor.

Now bring the verse back.

*"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*

[PAUSE]

Do you see now why the verb is *tukadhdhibān* — "call a lie" — and not "fail to thank"? Because Ar-Rahman is naming something deeper than rudeness. The surah understands that we do not deny God's favors by *arguing* against them. Almost no one stands up and makes the case that air is not a blessing. **We deny them by *not seeing* them.** And to live inside a gift while behaving as though it is not there — to be sustained, breath by breath, by a mercy you treat as nonexistent — is, functionally, to call it a lie. Your *attention* says it isn't real. Your inattention is the *takdhīb.*

This is why the whole surah is built on *repetition.* Stay with this — it is one of the most beautiful pieces of design in the Qurʾān. The disease is *forgetting the constant.* So the cure Allah administers is to *break the constant.* He will not let the favor go silent. Thirty-one times, He stops the recitation and asks again. *And this one? And after this one too? Which?* He is doing to the listener, deliberately, the one thing the numbing mind cannot survive: **He keeps making the gift speak.**

Think of what that means about the nature of *dhikr*, of remembrance, in your own life. You were not built to notice the floor on your own. Left to yourself, you adapt, you numb, the favors dissolve into background, and you drift into a low-grade, unspoken *takdhīb* — not denying God with your tongue, but denying His gifts with your inattention every single day. The five prayers, then, are not a tax. They are Allah doing for you what the refrain does in the surah: **stopping you, five times, to make the favor speak again before it goes silent and invisible.** The structure of your worship is engineered against hedonic adaptation. It is the *Rabb* — the One who raises you stage by stage — interrupting your numbness on a schedule, because He knows your nervous system better than you do.

So what changes tomorrow?

Here is the specific practice the verse hands you. Tonight, before you sleep, do the impossible thing the verse dares you to do: try to *point at one favor and call it nothing.* Try it honestly. Pick the most ordinary thing — that your lungs filled while you weren't watching, that your heart beat sixty times this minute without your permission, that you could read these words. Hold it up and try to say *this one is a lie, this one doesn't count.* You will find you cannot. And in the failure to deny even one, you will have done what the jinn did: arrived, honestly, at *"none of Your favors, my Lord, do I deny."* That sentence — reached not by being told but by *trying and failing to deny* — is worth more than a thousand recited thank-yous. Make the failure to deny your nightly habit, and watch the floor become visible again.

## Theme Two: The Mercy of Being Asked — and Asked, and Asked Again

Now I want to turn the whole verse over and look at its underside, because there is a tenderness here that the confrontational verb almost hides.

Begin with a human picture. Imagine a parent whose child has wronged them, has been careless and ungrateful, has taken everything and acknowledged nothing. A parent who had given up would go *silent.* Silence is what abandonment sounds like. The relationship that has truly died is the one where no one bothers to ask the question anymore.

Now look at what Ar-Rahman does. The human being has, by any honest measure, earned silence. We are the creation that took the trust the heavens and earth refused to carry (33:72). We are *ẓalūm, jahūl* — given to wrong, given to heedlessness. And what does the Lord of all the worlds do, facing this creature who keeps forgetting?

**He keeps asking.**

[PAUSE]

He does not turn away. He does not move on. Thirty-one times across this one surah, *Rabbukum* — the One raising you — stops, turns to the two of you, and asks again, with what I can only describe as a kind of patience that borders on pleading: *which one? After all of this — which favor will you call a lie?*

Feel the asymmetry of it. The One who needs nothing from you is the One doing the asking. The favors are entirely yours; the gratitude would benefit only *you*; and yet it is *He* who keeps initiating the conversation, *He* who refuses to let it lapse into silence. **The question itself is one of the favors.** To be asked is to still be in the relationship. To be questioned is to not yet be abandoned. A God who had written you off would not keep stopping the surah to give you another chance to look up and see.

And here the dual address — *the two of you* — turns from exposure into something almost comforting. You were never meant to face the weight of existence and gratitude *alone.* Allah binds you to the jinn, to a whole parallel creation carrying the same trust, asked the same question, walking the same road of accountability beside you. You are part of *al-thaqalān* — the two weighty ones — and there is a strange dignity in that weight. To be *asked* this question at all is to be counted among the creatures heavy enough to bear an answer. The stars are not asked. The mountains were offered the trust and declined it. But *you* — fragile, forgetful you — are addressed directly by the Lord of the worlds, in a surah named for His mercy, again and again.

That is not the treatment of something disposable. **That is the treatment of something beloved.**

So consider where this verse sits. We are at verse 75, deep inside the description of the *reward* — the gardens, the springs, the pure companions in their pavilions. And right here, in the middle of describing what He has prepared for those who feared their Lord, He stops to ask the question once more. As if to say: *even this — even Paradise itself, even the eternal home I am building for you — even this you could let slip into background, could stop seeing, could quietly disbelieve. So I will ask again. Which of these, too, will you call a lie?* The refrain guards even the description of Heaven against our genius for not noticing.

What does this change about tomorrow?

This: the next time you feel the pull of remembrance — the adhān cutting across your afternoon, a verse surfacing in your mind, a sudden inexplicable urge to turn back to Allah after a long drift — **recognize it for what it is.** That is not random. That is the refrain. That is *Rabbukum*, the One raising you, stopping the recitation of your day to ask you, one more time, gently: *and this favor too — will you call it a lie?* Do not treat the prompting to remember as an interruption. Treat it as the single most hopeful fact about your life — that you are still being asked. The asking has not stopped. Answer it the way the jinn did, out loud if you can: *Lā bi-shayʾin min niʿamika Rabbanā nukadhdhib* — "None of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny." And know that the very fact you got to say it is one more favor you just received.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture: A Refrain That Means Something New Each Time

Here is what we could not have seen at the beginning.

The genius of this sentence is that it is *identical* every time and *means something different* every time. The words never change — *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — but what they point back to changes with every appearance. After the seas: which of *those* favors? After the Day of Judgment: which of *those*? And here, at verse 75, after the gardens and the springs and the companions: which of *these* — the favors of the reward itself?

The fixed refrain is the constant; the shifting context is the variable. And that is the whole spiritual lesson rendered as *form*: **the favors are endlessly varied, but the right response to every single one is exactly the same.** No matter what He shows you — creation, provision, judgment, or Paradise — the honest answer never changes. *Not this one either, my Lord. Not one.*

## Bringing It Together

Watch how the two halves of our journey were the same truth approaching from opposite directions.

The *language* showed us that ingratitude is impossible to perform honestly: you cannot point at one favor (*ayy ālāʾ*) and call it a lie (*tukadhdhibān*) without using a favor to do it, and without another whole creation (*-kumā*) standing beside you in the same light.

The *themes* showed us *why we attempt the impossible anyway*: because the mind numbs to the constant, and ingratitude survives not as an argument but as an amnesia — and *why Allah will not let us get away with it*: because He keeps asking, refusing the silence of abandonment, signing every question with *Rabb*, the One still raising us.

One truth, two directions: **You cannot honestly deny even one favor — and the proof that you are still loved is that He keeps giving you the chance to try, and fail, and surrender.**

## Questions to Carry

1. Which favor have I been standing inside of for so long that I have stopped seeing it entirely — and what would it take to make it speak again?
2. When I feel the prompt to remember Allah — the adhān, a verse, a stirring — do I experience it as an interruption, or as the favor of *still being asked*?
3. The jinn answered, *"None of Your favors do we deny."* If I am honest, is my *life* — my attention, my reaching past what I have — currently giving the same answer, or the opposite one?
4. What would change if I treated my five prayers not as obligations but as Allah stopping the recitation of my day, on a schedule, to keep the favors from going silent?
5. If I tried tonight to point at one single favor and call it nothing — where, exactly, would my finger land?

## One-Sentence Distillation

To deny even one favor of your Lord, you would have to call your own life a fabrication — and the mercy of Ar-Rahman is that He keeps stopping to ask you which one, long after you have earned His silence.

## The Closing Invitation

You will read this refrain again — perhaps soon, perhaps in this very surah, in just two more verses. When you do, I invite you not to let it wash over you. Let it stop you, the way it was built to. Hear it as a question addressed to *you*, by name, in the dual — *the two of you* — by the One who has been raising you, stage by patient stage, your entire life. And answer it. You already know there is only one honest answer, and you already know you cannot find a single favor to point at and reject.

So say what the jinn said. And then notice that the breath you used to say it was, itself, one more favor — and begin again.

## A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, Ar-Rahmān, Lord who has raised us breath by breath from nothing — we cannot find one of Your favors to deny, and we are ashamed of every moment our inattention called Your gifts a lie. Keep asking us. Do not let the favors go silent in our hearts. And let our lives, not just our tongues, answer: none of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny.*

*Āmīn.*
