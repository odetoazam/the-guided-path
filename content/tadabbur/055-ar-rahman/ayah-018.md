# Which One? — The Question That Itemizes the Universe

## Surah Ar-Rahman 55:18

[ayah:55:18] فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Room That Went Silent

Before we can hear this ayah, we have to rebuild the room it was recited in.

A narration reported by al-Tirmidhi from Jabir ibn Abdillah — and cited by al-Qurtubi at the opening of his commentary on this surah — describes the Prophet ﷺ reciting Surah Ar-Rahman to his companions. The whole surah. This refrain, returning thirty-one times like waves against a shore. And the companions sat in silence.

When he finished, he told them — in the narration's words — that the jinn had given **a better response than you**. Because every time the jinn heard this question, they answered it. They didn't let it hang in the air. They said: *None of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny.*

[PAUSE]

Sit in that room for a moment. The companions weren't being ungrateful. They were doing what we all do with overwhelming speech — receiving it, absorbing it, letting it wash over them. And the Prophet ﷺ gently corrected something we would never have noticed on our own: **this ayah is not a statement to absorb. It is a question that expects an answer.** Out loud. From you.

Thirty-one times the question comes. And here is what should stop us: by the third occurrence — our ayah, 55:18 — the listener already knows the refrain. The surprise is gone. So why does it keep coming? What kind of question needs to be asked thirty-one times of someone sitting right in front of you?

And there's a smaller puzzle hiding inside the big one. The question doesn't ask *"Do you deny the favors of your Lord?"* That would be a yes-or-no question, and we know the answer we'd give. It asks something far more uncomfortable: ***which one?***

Hold that word. *Which.* Everything in this reflection flows from it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: فَبِأَىِّ — The Question That Refuses to Stay General

Before we open this word, let's learn something about how questions work — in any language.

There are questions that ask about the *whole*, and questions that ask about the *parts*. If someone says, "Do you like my cooking?" — you can answer with a polite, vague yes, and the conversation moves on. Nothing was risked. But if they say, "Which of the dishes I've ever made for you was bad?" — suddenly there is nowhere to hide. To answer at all, you'd have to go through the dishes *one by one*, hold each up, and declare a verdict on it. The question forces an inventory.

The first kind of question can be deflected. The second kind makes you **audit**.

Now look at the ayah. It does not say *a-tukadhdhibāni bi-ālā'i rabbikumā* — "do you two deny the favors of your Lord?" It opens with **بِأَىِّ** — *bi-ayyi* — "by **which**." The interrogative *ayy* in Arabic does exactly what "which" does in English: it presupposes a set and demands that you select from it.

Notice something about the word order. In Arabic, the neutral place for this phrase would be after the verb. Here, *bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā* is pushed to the **front** of the sentence, before the verb *tukadhdhibān* even arrives. The favors come first. The denial comes last. Grammatically, you are made to walk through the favors before you're allowed to reach the verb of denying them.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with what this means. A human being can deny blessings *in general* with ease. "Life is hard." "Nothing ever works out for me." "What do I have to be grateful for?" These sentences cost nothing, because they deny a blur, an abstraction. But Allah's question dissolves the blur. *Which one?* The eyesight reading these words right now? The water you drank this morning without checking whether it was safe? The two horizons of the previous ayah — the summer sunrise and the winter sunrise, holding the year together? Pick one. Name it. Stand before it and say: *this one is a lie.*

**You cannot do it.** Not because you're too pious — but because denial only survives in the general. The moment a favor is itemized, denial collapses.

And now the thirty-one repetitions begin to make sense. The refrain is not redundancy. It is **the audit itself**, conducted in real time. The surah names a favor — the sun and moon in calculated courses, the human taught speech, the two easts and two wests — and then immediately presents the invoice line: *and this one? Will you deny this one?* Then the next favor. *And this one?*

The question is asked thirty-one times because the inventory has thirty-one stations. Each repetition is not the same question. It is the same *form* pointed at a different gift.

[PAUSE]

One question repeated until you can no longer answer it vaguely — that is what *bi-ayyi* accomplishes. The general ingrate is forced to become a specific witness.

### Element Two: ءَالَآء — The Word Chosen Instead of "Blessings"

Arabic has a perfectly common word for blessings: *niʿam*, the plural of *niʿmah*. The Quran uses it often. And in fact, when the scholars behind al-Tafsir al-Muyassar render this ayah into simple Arabic, that is exactly the word they reach for: *fa-bi-ayyi **niʿam** rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — "so which of the blessings of your Lord will you deny?"

So the meaning is established. But here is the question a careful reader must ask: **if niʿam was available, why did Allah say ءَالَآء — ālāʾ?**

Let's first establish what we know. The root, as documented in the classical lexicons, is *ʾ-l-w*, and *ālāʾ* is a plural meaning favors, benefits, bounties — the lexicons treat it as synonymous in meaning with *niʿam*. That much is solid ground.

Now notice something about where this word *lives* in the Quran. Outside of Surah Ar-Rahman's thirty-one refrains, *ālāʾ* appears in only a handful of places — and look at the company it keeps. In Surah Al-A'raf, the people of Hud are told: *"remember the ālāʾ of Allah"* (7:69) — as a warning, before destruction. The people of Salih, the same: *"remember the ālāʾ of Allah and do not spread corruption"* (7:74). In Surah An-Najm — the surah immediately preceding Ar-Rahman in the mushaf — the question comes in the singular address: *"Then which of the ālāʾ of your Lord do you dispute?"* (53:55).

Every single time this word appears, **it appears in confrontation**. Not in the language of celebration — in the language of reckoning. *Ālāʾ* is the word for favors *at the moment they are being presented as evidence*.

Here is what this invites us to consider — and I flag this as a reflective reading built on that observed pattern, not a grammatical ruling: *niʿmah* is the blessing as you enjoy it. *Ālāʾ* is the blessing as it will be read back to you. The same gift, but now entered into the record, now standing in the witness box. The word itself carries the atmosphere of a courtroom.

[PAUSE]

And one more thing — say the word aloud. *Ālāʾ.* It is almost nothing but open vowels and glottal stops: a word that opens like a horizon, with no heavy consonants to grip. It is among the most open-throated words in the language — as if the favors being named are too vast, too encompassing, to be pronounced with a closed mouth.

So this single word does double work: its *sound* is expansive like the gifts it names, and its *usage pattern* is forensic, like the question it serves. Mercy in the vowels. Reckoning in the deployment.

### Element Three: تُكَذِّبَانِ — Denial Is Not Something You Lack. It Is Something You Do.

This is the deepest of our three elements, and to see it we need to learn one feature of Arabic morphology — gently, with an everyday example.

Arabic builds verbs on patterns, and one of these patterns — what grammarians call Form II, made by doubling the middle root letter — frequently carries a *declarative* meaning: not doing the thing, but **declaring something to be the thing**. From *ṣ-d-q* (truth) comes *ṣaddaqa* — not "to be truthful," but "to declare true," to believe. And from *k-dh-b* (lying) comes our verb: **كَذَّبَ** — *kadhdhaba* — not "to lie," but **"to declare a lie."** To look at something and pronounce the verdict: *false.*

This distinction changes everything about how we read this ayah. The verb here is not "which favors do you *disbelieve in*" — as if denial were a passive absence of faith, a gap, a vacancy. The Form II verb *tukadhdhibān* describes an **act**. A verdict actively issued. To deny a favor is to look at something real — something currently sustaining you — and stamp it: *lie*.

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because the verb has two more secrets.

First: it is in the **imperfect tense** — the Arabic tense of ongoing, repeated, continuing action. Not "which favors *did* you deny" — a closed file — but "which favors *do you keep on denying*," a live, renewing act. Denial, this tense whispers, is not an event in your past. It is a subscription you renew daily, every time you consume a gift without registering the Giver.

Second — and here the whole surah opens up — the verb is **dual**. *Tukadhdhibāni*: "you **two**." And the possessive matches it: *rabbikumā*, "the Lord of you **two**."

Who are the two?

Here we have genuine scholarly discussion, and rather than picking one answer, let the opinions function as camera angles on the same reality. Al-Qurtubi records that the position of the overwhelming majority — *al-jumhūr* — is that the address is to **humans and jinn** together. And he doesn't merely assert it; he builds the case from inside the surah itself: the surah has just said *"He created the human"* (55:3, 55:14) and *"He created the jānn"* (55:15) — both species formally introduced. Later it will say *"We will attend to you, O two weighty species"* (55:31) — *ayyuhā ath-thaqalān*, explicitly dual. And later still: *"O company of jinn and humans"* (55:33). The Jabir narration we opened with — where the jinn answer the question aloud — supports the same reading. The surah's internal evidence, the grammar, and the narration all converge.

So picture what this means. This question is not addressed to you alone. It is addressed to **two entire orders of creation simultaneously** — the children of clay and the children of fire, every being in existence capable of moral response — summoned into one courtroom, asked one question, thirty-one times.

[PAUSE]

Put the three pieces of the verb together now. *Declarative* — denial is a verdict you issue, not a void you contain. *Imperfect* — it is ongoing, renewed each day it isn't repented from. *Dual* — and you are not issuing it in private; you issue it in a courtroom shared with an unseen species who, when they heard the same question, had the humility to answer it.

What this single verb reveals: **ingratitude is not an emotion. It is testimony — given continuously, on the record, before witnesses you cannot see.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

Look at what the construction has shown us. A question engineered so it cannot be answered in general — only favor by favor. A word for "favors" that carries the atmosphere of evidence. A verb that turns ingratitude from a passive state into an active, ongoing, witnessed declaration.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About why blessings disappear from our sight while still in our hands, and about what it costs — and what it heals — to finally answer a question that most of creation lets hang in the air.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Disappearing Gift

There is a finding in modern psychology so consistent it has become almost a law: human beings **adapt** to good things. Researchers call it *hedonic adaptation*. The new home that thrilled you becomes, within months, simply "the house." The recovery from illness that had you weeping with relief becomes, within a year, just "feeling normal." The salary raise, the marriage, the child sleeping through the night — each one arrives as a miracle and is quietly reclassified as a baseline.

Psychologists frame this as a feature of attention: the mind is built to notice *change*, not *constancy*. Whatever holds still long enough becomes invisible. Your own heartbeat is the nearest sound to you on earth, and you haven't heard it all day.

Now feel the weight of what this means spiritually. The most faithful blessings — the ones that *never leave* — are precisely the ones guaranteed to vanish from perception. Sight. Breath. The sun rising on schedule from its summer station and its winter station, year after year, as the previous ayah just described. The blessing's very reliability is what erases it. **We do not lose our blessings. We lose the ability to see them — and we lose it *because* we never lose them.**

[PAUSE]

And here is where this ayah stops being a verse you read and becomes a treatment you undergo.

What does hedonic adaptation require to be broken? Attention researchers and clinicians converge on the same answer: **deliberate, specific, repeated noticing.** Not "I'm grateful for everything" — the mind cannot hold "everything"; it slides off. The interventions that actually work in the research are itemized: name *particular* things, *regularly*, in *concrete* detail.

Now look back at the structure we uncovered in Part 1. *Bi-ayyi* — which **one**? Specific. Thirty-one repetitions — regular. Each refrain attached to a named, concrete favor — the taught speech, the fruit and the palm trees, the two horizons. The refrain of Surah Ar-Rahman is, almost point for point, the protocol that re-sensitizes a numbed perception. Fourteen centuries before anyone measured adaptation, this surah was built as its undoing.

The question *"which favor will you deny?"* works on the heart the way a doctor works on a numb limb — pressing one point at a time: *do you feel this? do you feel this? do you feel this?* — thirty-one times, until sensation returns.

[PAUSE]

But the surah is more honest than the psychology, because it names what adaptation really is. The research calls the numbness neutral — just how minds work. The ayah's verb refuses that comfort. To live amid favors while registering none of them is not a neutral processing quirk. It is *takdhīb* — an ongoing verdict of "false" issued against gifts that are demonstrably real. Every unnoticed sunrise is, in the deep grammar of this verse, a small testimony against the truth.

That sounds harsh until you see the mercy in it: **a verdict, unlike a brain feature, can be reversed.** You cannot repent from neurology. You can repent from testimony. The ayah indicts us precisely in order to hand us back the pen.

**The practical application — tonight, not someday:** answer the question once, properly. Not "alhamdulillah for everything." Choose *one* favor — one this surah would have named — and hold it the way *bi-ayyi* demands: singly, specifically. The eyes you read this with. The language you think in, which you were *taught* (55:4) and did not invent. Hold the one favor, and say what the jinn said: *Not this one, my Lord. This one I do not deny.* One favor per day, named, would mean the question never again finds you silent.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Answering Out Loud — The Better Response

There is a particular modern experience almost everyone knows: the message you saw and didn't answer.

Not refused — just... left. You read it. You meant to respond. Days passed, and the unanswered message became a low hum of debt in the background of your life. And here is the strange psychology of it: leaving a message unanswered *feels* like neutrality, like doing nothing. But the sender does not experience your silence as nothing. Silence, to the one who asked, **is** an answer. It says: *this did not matter enough.*

Now return to that room where the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah. The companions — sincere, believing hearts — heard the question thirty-one times and treated it the way we treat most sacred speech: as something to receive. And the correction came, gentle and devastating: the jinn responded better than you. Because per the narration, each time the question came, the jinn *answered the message*: **"None of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny."**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with how strange and beautiful this is. The beings of smokeless fire — the species we associate with whispering and harm, the species of Iblis — are held up, in this narration, as the model of adab with this surah. Why? What did they understand?

They understood the grammar. We saw it in Part 1: this is a real interrogative, *bi-ayyi*, a genuine question — and the dual *tukadhdhibāni* meant it was addressed to *them*, personally, not to humanity in the abstract. A real question from a real questioner, addressed to you by name, demands an answer. The jinn heard a question. The companions, that day, heard recitation. The difference between those two ways of hearing is the difference this entire reflection has been circling.

Because notice what answering does that listening cannot. The verb of denial, we learned, is *declarative* — a verdict spoken against the favors. What is the only thing that can overwrite a spoken verdict? **A spoken verdict.** Counter-testimony. *Takdhīb* — declaring false — is undone by its exact opposite: declaring true, one favor at a time, out loud, on the record. The jinn's response is not politeness. It is the formal retraction of the testimony of ingratitude. They stood in the courtroom the dual grammar built, and they entered their plea.

And we mostly... leave the message on read.

Not from defiance. From the assumption that worship is something that happens *to* us — that our role in revelation is to be moved by it. But this surah, alone among the surahs in the intensity of its insistence, is built as a **dialogue with a missing half**. Thirty-one question marks. Thirty-one spaces left blank for your voice. Recited without response, Surah Ar-Rahman is a conversation in which one party — the Lord of the two easts — keeps asking, and the honored guest keeps saying nothing.

[PAUSE]

Here is the paradox to carry without rushing to resolve it: Allah does not need the answer. He knows what hearts contain. The question is asked thirty-one times *not because the Questioner lacks information, but because the answerer lacks formation* — and is formed by answering. Each spoken "*not this one, my Lord*" is a small act of construction: the building of a self that has gone on record, before two worlds, as a witness *for* its Lord rather than against Him.

**The practical application:** change how you meet this surah forever. The next time you hear Surah Ar-Rahman — in salah behind an imam you may pause and respond inwardly; in your own recitation or listening, respond aloud — answer the refrain the way the jinn did: *Lā bi-shay'in min ālā'ika rabbanā nukadhdhib* — "None of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny." Thirty-one questions. Thirty-one answers. Walk out of the surah having testified thirty-one times. The narration of Jabir suggests this is not extra credit. It is the response the recitation was waiting for.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal: A Surah Built in Twos

Now step back, and let the placement of this particular refrain — the third of thirty-one — show you something.

Look at the verses it seals. The human, created from clay (55:14) — and the jānn, created from smokeless fire (55:15). One pair. Then: Lord of the two easts, and Lord of the two wests (55:17) — in the classical reading al-Qurtubi and others discuss, the sun's two rising points and two setting points, the summer and winter stations of the horizon. Another pair. And the question that follows is itself built of pairs: *rabbikumā* — Lord of you **two**. *Tukadhdhibāni* — will you **two** deny.

Dual creation. Dual horizons. Dual address. Dual verb. By the time this third refrain sounds, the grammar of the surah and the architecture of the cosmos are speaking in the same voice: **everything in the witnessed world comes in pairs — and so does the audience of revelation.** The two species stand between the two horizons, under one Lord whose very title in this surah — Ar-Rahman, the surah's first word and only name for Him here — is singular. Many pairs. One Giver. The duals are the courtroom; the singular is the throne.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery from two directions.

The language showed us a question that cannot be answered in general (*bi-ayyi*), about favors framed as evidence (*ālāʾ*), against a denial that is an ongoing spoken verdict (*tukadhdhibān*, Form II, imperfect, dual). The themes showed us why such a question is needed: because constancy makes gifts invisible, and invisibility hardens into testimony against the Giver — and because the only repair is counter-testimony, specific and spoken, the way the jinn gave it.

One sentence underneath both: **gratitude, in this surah, is not a feeling you wait to have — it is an answer you owe.**

### Questions to Carry

1. If I had to actually answer the question — *which* favor would I deny? — and I cannot name one, then what exactly has my ingratitude been resting on all these years?
2. Which blessing in my life has been so reliable, for so long, that I can no longer see it? What would its one-day absence reveal?
3. When revelation asks me a direct question, do I hear a question — or do I hear "recitation" and let it pass through me unanswered?
4. The jinn answered better than the companions that day. Who in my life — someone I'd never think to learn from — might be responding to Allah better than I am?
5. What testimony am I currently giving with my daily life — not my words — about the favors of my Lord?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Denial of blessings survives only in the general — so Ar-Rahman asks *which one*, thirty-one times, until the inventory of favors leaves the heart nowhere to hide and nothing to say except the jinn's answer: *none of them, our Lord.*

### The Closing Invitation

You are invited — not commanded, invited, in keeping with a surah that opens with the name of overflowing mercy — to stop being the silent half of this dialogue. Once a day, one favor, named and answered. And the next time this surah is recited in your hearing, let it find in you what it found in the jinn: not an audience, but a witness.

### Du'a

O Ar-Rahman — Lord of the two easts and the two wests, Lord of the two worlds You question in one breath — none of Your favors do we deny; not the sight, not the speech You taught us, not the horizons You hold for us morning after morning. Forgive us every gift we consumed in silence, and make our tongues answer You before the Day when the favors themselves are made to speak. Āmīn.
