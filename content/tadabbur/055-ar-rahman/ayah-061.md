# So Which of the Favors of Your Lord Will You Both Deny?

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, exactly as they fall.

**فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ**

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

*"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*

If you have spent any time with Surah Ar-Rahman, you already know this verse. You may know it the way you know the chorus of a song you have heard a hundred times — so familiar that it has gone almost invisible. This single line repeats **thirty-one times** across this one surah. It is the most repeated verse in the entire Quran placed inside a single chapter. It comes back, and back, and back, like a wave that will not stop returning to the shore.

And here is the strange thing about something repeated thirty-one times: we stop hearing it. The mind files it away as "the refrain" and moves past it to get to the next image — the sun and the moon, the two seas, the pearls, the gardens. The refrain becomes the spacing between the gifts. The punctuation.

I want us to do the opposite today. I want us to stop *at* the punctuation. Because I think the most astonishing thing in this surah is not any one of the favors. It is the question Allah keeps asking *about* the favors.

Let me rebuild the room for you.

There is a narration carried by al-Tirmidhi, reported from Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh, that when the Prophet ﷺ recited Surah Ar-Rahman to his companions, they were silent. And he said something like: *the jinn, when I recited it to them, gave a better response than you.* Every time he reached **"fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān,"** they answered: *"Lā bi-shayʾin min niʿamika rabbanā nukadhdhib, fa-laka l-ḥamd"* — "Not by anything of Your favors, our Lord, do we cry lies. So to You belongs all praise." (We hold this as a reported narration, not a verse — but feel what it shows us: the verse is built to be *answered*.)

So picture it. The surah is not a monologue. It is a courtroom and a celebration at once. Allah lays down a gift. Then He turns and asks: *which of these will you call a lie?* And the listening creation is meant to answer back, gift after gift: *none of them, none of them, none of them.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is the hook. Two small puzzles hidden in plain sight.

The first: the verse is addressed to **two** of someone. Not "you" singular. Not "you" plural. *You two.* Arabic has a word-form reserved for exactly two of a thing, and Allah reaches for it here — *rabbi-**kumā**,* your Lord, the Lord of you-two; *tukadhdhib**ān**,* will you-two deny. Who are the two?

The second: of all the words for blessing in the Arabic language, Allah did not say *niʿma.* He said **ālāʾ** — a word that arrives already plural, a word with no singular in common use. As if these favors do not come one at a time at all.

And underneath both: why phrase it as a *question*? Allah is not gathering information. So what is a question *for,* when the One asking already knows, and the one answering cannot say anything but "none"?

Stay with me. Let's open these slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "You Two" — the grammar of who is being asked

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because if you do not have this, the verse cannot land.

English has two numbers: singular and plural. One book, or many books. The moment you have more than one, English lumps them all together — two, ten, a thousand, all just "books."

Arabic has **three** numbers. Singular. Plural. And, sitting between them, a dedicated form for **exactly two** — the *muthannā,* the dual. It is its own grammatical category with its own endings. When Arabic wants to say "two" of something, it does not use a number and a noun. It *bends the word itself* into a shape that means "a pair."

Think about what that tells you about the language's mind. A pair is not "a small plural." A pair is its own kind of thing. Two eyes. Two hands. Two banks of a river. The dual is the grammar of things that come *matched,* that belong *together,* that face *each other.*

Now look at the verse.

**رَبِّكُمَا — *rabbi-kumā*** — "your Lord," where the "your" is dual. The Lord of you-two.

**تُكَذِّبَانِ — *tukadhdhibān*** — "will you-two deny," the verb itself bent into the dual.

Allah is not addressing a crowd. He is not addressing one heart. He is addressing **a pair.** [PAUSE]

So we have to ask the question the grammar forces on us: who are the two?

Here the classical commentators are clear, and al-Qurṭubī lays out the reasoning carefully. This is the address to **al-ins wa-l-jinn** — humankind and the jinn — and he calls it the position of the *jumhūr,* the majority. He builds it from the surah's own architecture: earlier Allah says **"sa-nafrughu lakum ayyuha **th-thaqalān**"** — "We will attend to you, O you two weighty ones" (55:31). And then, plainly, **"yā maʿshara l-jinni wa-l-ins"** — "O company of jinn and humankind" (55:33). The surah names its two audiences out loud. So when the refrain says *you-two,* it is gathering them both into the dock together.

*Al-thaqalān.* The two weighty ones. Sit with that name for a moment. Of all the creation — the angels who do not disobey, the animals, the mountains that Allah says would have crumbled under the Quran — only two species are called "the weighty ones." Why weighty? Because these two carry something the rest do not: the freedom to refuse. The capacity to say *no.* The angels cannot deny a favor. The mountain cannot call a gift a lie. Only the human and the jinn are heavy enough — burdened enough with choice — to be capable of *takdhīb.*

[PAUSE]

Now here is what the dual does to *you,* sitting and listening today.

When you read "will you deny" as a single "you," it feels like an accusation pointed at your chest. *You. Yes, you. What's wrong with you?* It isolates. It singles out. It is the voice of *why am I being picked on.*

But the dual does something else entirely. It places you **shoulder to shoulder with an entire parallel creation.** You are not the lone defendant under a spotlight. You are standing in a vast hall, and beside you — invisible, but addressed in the very same breath — stands the whole world of the jinn, asked the very same question, in the very same words. The question is not *personal persecution.* It is **cosmic.** It is the question put to every weighty, free, choosing creature that Allah ever made.

There is a strange mercy in that. The burden you carry — the terrifying freedom to be ungrateful — you do not carry it alone. It is the shared weight of the *thaqalān.* You were never singled out. You were *included.*

So what does this single grammatical ending — this *-kumā,* this *-ān* — reveal? That the question of gratitude is not a niche human anxiety. It is the defining test of every creature heavy enough to refuse. To be addressed in this dual is to be told: *you are one of the two species in all creation capable of looking at a gift and lying about it. That is your dignity. And that is your danger.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: "Deny" — but a very particular kind of denying

Now the verb. This is the heart of the verse, and it has been mistranslated into something softer than it is in nearly every English rendering.

We read "deny." In English, "deny" sounds passive, almost intellectual. *I deny that this is true.* It sounds like a position you hold, a box you fail to check, an absence of belief.

The Arabic is not that. Let me teach you the form first.

Arabic verbs are built on roots — usually three consonants carrying a core meaning — and then those roots are poured into **patterns,** called *forms,* numbered I through X. Form I is the bare, simple action. But the higher forms reshape the meaning. **Form II** — where you *double the middle consonant* — characteristically carries **intensity, repetition, or the act of *declaring* something to be the case.** It turns an action into something deliberate, forceful, done *thoroughly.*

Take the root for "knowing," *ʿ-l-m.* Form I, *ʿalima,* means "he knew." Double that middle letter into Form II, *ʿallama,* and it becomes "he *taught*" — he didn't just know, he actively, deliberately *transmitted* knowing into another. The doubling is the work. The doubling is the *intensity.*

Now our verb. The root is **k-dh-b** — and the bare Form I, *kadhaba,* simply means "to lie, to speak falsehood." But the verse does not use Form I. It uses **Form II: *kadhdhaba* → *tukadhdhibān*.** The middle letter is doubled. (The Leeds morphological corpus confirms it: root k-dh-b, Form II.)

And what does Form II of *kadhaba* mean? It does not mean "to lie." It means **"to declare something to be a lie. To brand it false. To cry lies against it. To give the lie to it."**

Do you feel the difference? [PAUSE]

This is not the quiet, passive "I don't quite believe it." This is an **active verdict.** This is standing in front of a thing that is *true* — a real gift, sitting right there in front of you — and pronouncing, out loud, *"that is a lie."* It is not the absence of faith. It is the **manufacture of a denial.** It takes effort. It takes a kind of audacity. You have to look at the sun and say *that is nothing.* You have to look at your own breathing and say *that is owed to me.* You have to look at the water in your throat and call it a lie.

And notice — the verse attaches a little preposition, *bi-,* "by which." *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi... tukadhdhibān.* You do not just deny in the abstract; you **cry lies *against* the favors themselves.** The denial has a target. It lands *on the gift.*

So the question is not, as we lazily read it, "which favor do you happen not to believe in?" The question, in its full force, is:

**"Which of these favors — these real, present, undeniable gifts of the One who nurtures you — will you stand in front of and brand a lie?"**

[PAUSE]

And here the rhetorical structure completes the trap. This is a question whose only honest answer is *none.* Every commentator reads it this way — the *istifhām,* the question-form, here carries rebuke and demand for affirmation. Allah is not asking *whether.* He is daring you to *point.* Pick one. Name a single favor you are willing to look at and call false.

And you cannot. Because the moment you try — the moment you reach for any gift to deny — your own breath, taken to speak the denial, was itself a favor. Your own tongue, forming the lie, was the gift you were about to reject. The denial eats itself.

That is what the doubled letter, the Form II, reveals. Ingratitude is not a gap. It is not innocent forgetting. It is an **act** — a deliberate, effortful, almost impossible *branding of the truth as a lie.* And the verse exposes it as not only ugly but *impossible to perform honestly.*

[PAUSE — let this sit]

### Element Three: "Favors" — the word that comes already plural

One more word, and this one is quieter, but it completes the picture.

Of all the words Arabic has for blessing — and it has many — the most common is *niʿma.* Allah uses *niʿma* often, elsewhere. But not here. Here He chose **ءَالَآء — *ālāʾ*.** (Root *Alw,* confirmed in the corpus.)

The lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — gloss *ālāʾ* as *niʿam,* favors, bounties. So at the level of dictionary meaning, yes, it means "favors." But here is the feature worth noticing, and I want to be careful and stay only with what the form actually shows us: **ālāʾ arrives as a plural.** It is not the singular blessing that you then add an *-s* to. In common usage it comes pre-multiplied, a word for favors *in their plurality,* favors *as a flood.*

Think about what that does, paired with the question "which?"

The interrogative **أَيّ — *ayy*** — "which one?" — is a word that asks you to *single out.* To reach into a group and *pick.* "Which of these?" presupposes a set you could, in principle, sort through.

But Allah pairs "which one?" with a noun that **refuses to be singular.** *Which one of the un-countable?* It is like being walked into the ocean and asked: *which drop will you call dry?* The question itself is built to defeat you. You are invited to pick a favor to reject, and handed a word that means *there is no single favor — they come as an unbreaking tide.*

This is the same truth Allah states plainly elsewhere: **"And if you tried to count the favor of Allah, you could not number it"** (16:18, 14:34). But here in Ar-Rahman He does not *tell* you they are uncountable. He *makes you feel it* — by daring you to count to *one,* and watching you fail.

[PAUSE]

**So let us gather what the words have shown us before we go further.** Three small features, three large truths:

The **dual** told us: this question is for every creature weighty enough to refuse — and you stand among them, never singled out, never alone.

The **doubled verb** told us: ingratitude is not a passive absence but an active, effortful, almost impossible *branding of the truth as a lie.*

The **pre-plural noun** told us: the favors do not come one at a time to be sorted and rejected; they come as a flood you cannot count to one.

Now let's ask what all this reveals — not just about words, but about the shape of your own life, and the strange mercy hidden inside a question repeated thirty-one times.
