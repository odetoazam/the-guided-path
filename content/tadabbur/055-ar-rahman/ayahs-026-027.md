# Everything Perishes — The Two Ayahs That Hold All Reality in Two Verbs

## The Ayahs

[ayah:55:26] كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ

*Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.*

[ayah:55:27] وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو ٱلْجَلَـٰلِ وَٱلْإِكْرَامِ

*Wa yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.*

**Everyone upon it is perishing.**

**And there remains the Face of your Lord — Owner of Majesty and Honor.**

---

## Rebuilding the World

Sometimes the Quran gives you a long ayah — a panorama you walk through slowly. Sometimes it gives you a short one, and the entire universe fits inside it.

These two ayahs are the second kind.

Seventeen words total. They sit in the middle of Surah Ar-Raḥmān — the surah named after Allah's most encompassing attribute, the surah whose refrain *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* ("then which of your Lord's favors will you two deny?") repeats thirty-one times. And right here, in the heart of that surah, Allah pauses the listing of favors to say something structurally different. He drops the refrain momentarily. He delivers two short sentences. Then the refrain resumes.

These two ayahs are what the refrain is really resting on. They are the ground under the favors. Without them, the favors float.

[PAUSE]

Let me build the world you need to inhabit before we open the words.

The Arabs of the Prophet's ﷺ time lived with a particular kind of cosmology in their daily life — one that most modern readers do not share. They lived with the permanence of mountains, the reliability of stars, the endurance of ancestral poetry, and the stability of tribal names that stretched back generations. Things lasted. A poem recited three generations ago was still recited today. A lineage remembered for two centuries was still remembered. In their world, permanence was assumed — death was the exception inside a world that was otherwise solid.

Into that world, these two ayahs landed with the force of a shattering.

*Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.* **"All who are upon it — are perishing."**

Not some. Not most. *All.* Not "will perish eventually." Not "will die when their time comes." *Fān* — actively, right now, in process. **Perishing, as you read this.**

This is not a statement about death. This is a statement about the ontology of everything under the sky. The mountains you thought were permanent — perishing. The poetry you thought was immortal — perishing. The tribes, the bloodlines, the reputations, the civilizations you are working to build — perishing. The word *man* — "whoever" — in Arabic grammar covers conscious beings: humans, jinn, angels. *Everyone.* Not a single conscious being upon the earth is excluded from this verb.

[PAUSE]

**The Hook:**

These two ayahs contain the entire structure of reality in two Arabic verbs. One verb describes everything that is not Allah. One verb describes Allah. And the two verbs are placed in a single sentence in a way that, if you hear it in Arabic, will never leave you.

By the end of this reflection, I want you to hold these two verbs side by side — *fān* and *yabqā* — and understand why classical Muslims, including scholars who carried enormous worldly responsibilities, said that meditating on these two ayahs for even a few minutes changed how they made every decision afterward.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Fānin* — The Active Participle of Perishing

Before we open this word, I want to teach you a specific Arabic grammatical category. Because once you understand it, the ayah will unfold.

In Arabic, there is a form called the *ism al-fāʿil* — the **active participle.** In English, we sometimes render it with an "-ing" ending. *Running, eating, perishing.* But the Arabic active participle is doing something more than the English "-ing" form. It is naming the subject by the action it is currently in.

If I say in English *"he is a runner,"* I am describing a profession or a habit. If I say *"he is running,"* I am describing a temporary act. The Arabic *ism al-fāʿil* collapses these. *ʿĀdin* — one who is in the state of running right now, such that running is their current identity. The participle names the person by the action they are currently performing.

[PAUSE]

Now listen to what Allah says. *Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.*

Not *kullu man ʿalayhā sa-yafnā* — "all who are upon it will perish." That would have used the future tense. A warning about what is coming.

Not *kullu man ʿalayhā yafnā* — "all who are upon it perish." That would have used the present-imperfect. A description of a repeated event.

Allah used the active participle. *Fān.* **Every conscious being upon the earth is, right now, in the state of perishing.**

This is not a prediction. This is a description of what is currently happening. The state is not "alive." The state is *perishing.* You are, as you read this, in the middle of perishing. The cells of your body are turning over. The minutes of your life are elapsing. The attention span in which you are holding this thought is itself draining. **Everyone you love is, right now, perishing.** Everyone you have ever met who is still alive is, right now, perishing.

[PAUSE]

Classical tafsirists — al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Rāzī, al-Qurṭubī — each spent careful time on this active participle. Al-Rāzī noted that Allah could have used any of several tenses to make the point, and chose this one deliberately: to prevent the reader from relegating death to a distant event. Ibn Kathīr emphasized that the *ism al-fāʿil* makes the verdict *present* rather than *future.* Al-Qurṭubī gathered the grammatical consensus: this is the Arabic form that *refuses to let the action escape into another time.*

Build the composite. Every classical scholar saw the same thing: Allah used the one grammatical shape that does not allow you to postpone the reality.

[PAUSE]

Now feel what this does. When you hear "you will die someday," your mind does something clever — it files the statement into the future, which means it files it into a time you do not currently inhabit. The anxiety is managed because the event is not now. This is how most people spend their lives — knowing they will die, but not living in the actual presence of it.

But when you hear "you are perishing" — in the present active form — the mind has nowhere to file it. There is no future to displace it into. The perishing is the current description. You cannot move this to a time after the present, because the present is when it is happening.

The active participle is the grammatical shape of *being unable to look away.*

One clear statement: **Allah did not predict death. He described your current state. The distinction changes everything, because you cannot live in the distant shadow of perishing the same way you cannot avoid the fact of perishing now.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Yabqā* — The One Verb That Stays

Now the second ayah. And notice what Allah does with the grammar.

*Wa yabqā wajhu rabbika.*

"And there remains the Face of your Lord."

The verb *yabqā.* Root: **ب-ق-ي.** *Baqā* — to remain, to endure, to persist. And look at the form. Imperfect tense. Form I. 3rd person masculine singular.

The imperfect tense in Arabic is ongoing — it expresses continuous, unfinished action. It is sometimes translated as "remains," sometimes as "is remaining," sometimes as "will remain." It expresses an enduring state without closing off time.

This is the verb that sits opposite *fān.* And the pairing is the heart of the ayah.

[PAUSE]

Listen to what just happened structurally.

Ayah 26: *Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.* Everything — participle — *perishing.*
Ayah 27: *Wa yabqā wajhu rabbika.* And — imperfect — *remains* — the Face of your Lord.

Allah placed two verbs in two different grammatical states. One is a participle (nominal, state-describing, bound to what is currently happening). The other is an imperfect verb (continuous action, ongoing, unbounded). The first describes a temporary state that is itself the process of ending. The second describes an activity that does not end.

It is as if Allah said: *Everything else is a verb that has already begun its own conclusion. I am the one verb that is not ending.*

[PAUSE]

Classical scholars noted this structural choice. In Arabic rhetoric, you can achieve contrast through many devices — two opposite nouns, two opposite verbs, an adjective paired with a noun. The choice Allah made here — participle vs. continuous verb — creates a specific kind of contrast. It is not "everything dies, but Allah lives." It is more precise than that.

*Everything is the state of its own ending. Allah is the ongoing act of remaining.*

This is not a comparison of two beings. This is a revelation that being itself has two modes: the mode of created things (participial, temporary, self-concluding) and the mode of Allah (continuous, enduring, unbounded). And only one of these modes is stable. Only one of them can be built upon. Only one of them will be there when you finish the sentence you are currently speaking.

[PAUSE]

Now look at what Allah attaches to the verb. Not "Allah remains." Not "the existence of Allah remains." *Yabqā wajhu rabbika* — "remains **the Face** of **your** Lord."

Two specifications. **The Face.** **Your Lord.**

*Al-Wajh* — the Face. Classical scholars differed on how to read this word. Ibn Kathīr affirmed it as an attribute of Allah whose reality is known to Him alone — not to be compared to any created face, and not to be stripped of its meaning either. Al-Rāzī and others preferred a reading where *al-wajh* here refers to whatever is directed toward Allah — the *dhāt* (essence) or the aspect of reality that faces Him. The Ahl as-Sunnah position holds that whatever meaning *al-wajh* carries here, it is affirmed without how (*bilā kayf*) — we say what Allah said, we do not ask how.

But notice what *wajh* does *in this specific context.* The contrast is not "the essence of Allah remains." It is "the *face* of Allah remains." Why?

Because a face is what you turn toward. A face is what receives attention. In human experience, you do not speak to a body — you speak to a face. You do not love a shadow — you love what a face reveals. Allah, in this ayah, names the aspect of Himself that *you direct your life toward.* Whatever else you understood about existence — everything you spent your attention on — was perishing. The Face. The direction your life was supposed to have been oriented toward. *That* remains.

[PAUSE]

And the second specification: *rabbika.* **Your Lord.**

Not *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* — Lord of the worlds. Not *Allāh* — the divine name. *Rabbika.* Your Lord. Second person singular. Individual.

The *kāf* suffix makes the address personal. You, the single reader. Your own Lord. The Lord who raised you (from the same root **ر-ب-ب** we walked through in the 17:23 reflection). The one whose lordship was not theoretical — it was specifically over you, over the particular arc of your life, over every breath you have taken and will take.

When you read *wajhu rabbika,* the ayah is not making a general cosmological statement. It is making a personal one. **The one direction you were meant to face — the one orientation that would have survived everything else — is still there. Still remains. Still is what you must turn to.**

One clear statement: **The second ayah is not a metaphysical description of who outlasts the universe. It is an invitation. It tells you precisely *what* you should be orienting your life toward, because it is the only *what* that will be there when you arrive.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Dhū al-Jalāli wa-l-Ikrām* — The Two Attributes of What Remains

Allah does not let the ayah end at "the Face of your Lord." He adds two more words. Two descriptive attributes. And these two attributes contain the whole theology of what you are turning toward.

*Dhū al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.* "Owner of Majesty and Honor."

Let me walk you through each.

*Al-Jalāl.* Root: **ج-ل-ل.** This is greatness, majesty, awesomeness, overwhelmingness. The word carries the sense of something so great it makes other things small by comparison. Classical tafsirists emphasize that *jalāl* is the aspect of Allah that *humbles.* When you encounter the *jalāl* of Allah, you become aware of your own smallness, your own dependence, your own accountability. *Jalāl* is the divine attribute that reduces you to your true size.

[PAUSE]

*Al-Ikrām.* Root: **ك-ر-م.** Form IV verbal noun. *Ikrām* is not just "honor" or "generosity" — in its Form IV shape, it specifically means *the act of ennobling someone, raising them up, dignifying them.* Where *jalāl* makes you small, *ikrām* makes you large. Where *jalāl* humbles, *ikrām* elevates. Where *jalāl* shows you that you are nothing without Him, *ikrām* shows you that *He* is the one who grants you value beyond your own deserving.

Classical scholars built a famous theological reading on this pairing. Al-Qurṭubī, al-Rāzī, and others note that Allah's dealing with His creation unfolds along two axes. The *jalāl* axis — the awe-inspiring, humbling, sovereign dimension. And the *ikrām* axis — the ennobling, dignifying, generous dimension. These two are not in tension. They are complementary. *Ikrām* requires *jalāl* to be meaningful (a gift from someone unimpressive is not ennobling), and *jalāl* without *ikrām* would be pure overwhelming force without relationship.

[PAUSE]

And now look at what this means for the ayah.

The Face that remains is *dhū al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.* The One who endures is the One who both humbles you and raises you. The One you are oriented toward is the One who will reduce you to your smallest truthful self — and then, out of His own generosity, dignify you beyond anything you could have claimed.

This is why the ayah is so stunning in its ultimate effect. It does not only say "everything else will be gone and Allah remains." That alone might produce despair or terror. It says: *everything else will be gone, and what remains is the One whose very nature is to humble you honestly and then ennoble you freely.*

The two attributes are the emotional truth of your destination. You are walking toward a meeting with the One who will not let you pretend to be greater than you are — and will not let your smallness be the final word either.

[PAUSE]

One clear statement: **The two attributes are the mercy hidden inside the severity. Everything is perishing — yes. But what remains is not an indifferent cosmic principle. It is a Face attached to your own Lord, whose two characteristic modes toward you are the humbling that tells the truth and the ennobling that gives more than the truth required.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

We have walked through three grammatical facts that, taken together, rewrite the landscape of existence. The active participle that refuses to let perishing be future. The imperfect verb that refuses to let remaining be finished. And the paired attributes that name the One who remains as the One who humbles and ennobles.

Now let us ask what these seventeen words are asking you to do with your life.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The Audit of Everything You Are Building

Let me begin far outside the ayah.

Every human being, whether they think about it or not, is currently building something. You are building a career, or a reputation, or a family, or a body of work, or a bank account, or a circle of relationships, or a set of experiences. You are spending your hours, your attention, your effort — they are going somewhere. Something is being constructed.

And you assume, at some level, that what you are constructing will *be there* when you arrive at the end of the construction. You are not building castles in the air — you are building things you expect to touch. That is why you are spending the hours. Nobody rationally invests decades in a thing they believe will vanish.

But here is the quiet question this ayah asks.

[PAUSE]

*Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.*

Every conscious builder upon the earth is perishing. Which means every construction is being performed by a builder who is in the process of ending. And the materials being used — also perishing. And the structure being built — also perishing. And the audience for whom you are building — also perishing.

This is not a discouraging ayah. It is a clarifying one. Because the ayah is not saying *everything you do is pointless.* It is saying *everything you do falls into one of two categories.* Either what you are building is perishing along with the world — or it is connected, through some thread, to the One who remains.

And the entire spiritual life is the project of *attaching* what you build to the part of reality that endures.

[PAUSE]

Classical scholars, when writing about this ayah, often paired it with the famous hadith of the three things that follow a person to the grave — *ṣadaqa jāriya* (flowing charity), *ʿilm yuntafaʿ bihi* (beneficial knowledge), *walad ṣāliḥ yadʿū lahu* (a righteous child praying for them). These three are not arbitrary. They are the three known channels through which a perishing being can attach their actions to what remains. Charity given for Allah — the *niyyah* (intention) connects it to the Face. Knowledge shared for Allah — the transmission itself becomes an enduring thread. A child raised for Allah — the prayers after your death keep the connection active.

This is the practical shape of *yabqā wajhu rabbika* as a life principle. **Build what you build, but the part of it that survives is precisely the part you directed toward His Face.**

[PAUSE]

Now, here is the pragmatic application. Consider the major investments of your time and attention right now. Your work. Your relationships. Your body. Your knowledge-acquisition. Your possessions. Your plans for the next decade.

For each of these, ask two questions:

1. How much of this is attached to the Face of Allah — oriented toward Him, intended for Him, connected to something He said is worth pursuing?
2. How much of this is attached only to the perishing world — intended primarily for outcomes that themselves belong to the category of *fān?*

You are not being asked to reject the dunyā. The ayah does not say *stop building.* It says: understand what you are building, and make sure some fraction of it is attached to what will remain. Because the portion that is not so attached is — by the ayah's own grammatical force — *currently perishing.* It is disappearing as you build it.

[PAUSE]

Classical Muslims — including scholars who ran kingdoms, managed large households, traveled extensively for trade, wrote hundreds of books — did not use this ayah to retreat from the world. They used it to *reorganize* their activity. You can build a business *for* Allah (honest dealings, fair employment, halal provision for your family, charity from its profits) or *only for yourself* (accumulation, ego, status). The outer shape of the business can be identical. The inner orientation makes one attached and the other perishing.

This is not a question you answer once. It is a question you return to at intervals. Because the attachment can slip. The business that started attached can drift. The relationships that started for Allah can become about their own comfort. The audit is not one-time — it is a daily, weekly, monthly recalibration.

[PAUSE]

**The core insight:** *Kullu man ʿalayhā fān* is not a threat — it is a filter. Everything you are building is either attached to the Face or it is perishing. The ayah is asking you to sort your life by this filter, before the sorting happens without your consent.

**Practical application:** Pick one major activity in your life — your job, a primary relationship, a habit you are building, a creative project. Tonight, before you sleep, ask honestly: *what portion of this is attached to the Face of my Lord, and what portion is only perishing?* Name them. Do not judge yourself for the answer — just see clearly. Then ask: *what is one small shift I could make tomorrow to increase the attached portion by even 5%?* A better intention. A halal-er version of the same activity. A moment of dhikr embedded inside it. A fraction of its output given in ṣadaqa. The shift does not have to be large. It only has to attach.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Fear of *Jalāl* and the Mercy of *Ikrām* — Why You Need Both

Let me end with the hardest and most consoling theme in the ayah.

Modern religious discourse — in Muslim circles as in others — tends to emphasize one of two divine attributes at the expense of the other. Some voices emphasize Allah's *jalāl* — His might, His judgment, His punishment, the severity of the accounting. Some voices emphasize Allah's *raḥma* — His mercy, His generosity, His love, the ease of forgiveness. Each strand contains truth. Each, alone, produces a warped spiritual life.

The *jalāl*-only Muslim becomes a person afraid of Allah in a way that does not draw them closer. Their relationship becomes transactional, anxious, rigid. They can barely pray without the fear of doing it wrong. Their faith contracts rather than expands.

The *ikrām*-only Muslim — or more precisely, the only-*raḥma* Muslim — becomes a person comfortable with their own shortcomings, assuming forgiveness is automatic, losing the urgency that accompanies the awareness of accountability. Their faith softens into sentiment.

Allah, in this ayah, forces the integration. *Dhū al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.* He is both. He is the Face that humbles you honestly, *and* He is the Face that ennobles you beyond your deserving. And the Face that remains — the only thing that does remain — is the Face that carries both qualities inseparably.

[PAUSE]

Here is what this means practically.

When you stand in prayer, you should feel both. The *jalāl* — the awareness that you are standing before the One who holds all creation in His power, who sees every particle of your inner life, who could, at any moment, withdraw the breath He is currently lending you. And the *ikrām* — the awareness that this same Being chose to give you His Face to face, chose to teach you how to address Him, chose to call you "My servant" in language that carries intimacy, chose to ennoble a clay-creature with a direct line of speech to Himself.

If your prayer carries only the first, it becomes terror. If it carries only the second, it becomes sentimentality. The prayer of the believer is both, at once, in proportion.

[PAUSE]

And the same is true of your sins and your repentance. When you realize you have sinned, the *jalāl* is what keeps the realization honest — what prevents you from softening the seriousness of what you did. You have offended the *dhū al-jalāl* — this is not a small matter. But the *ikrām* is what gives you permission to return. The one you offended is also the one whose nature is ennobling beyond deserving. The same Face holds both facts. And so your repentance is real grief *plus* real hope, in the same act.

When you get stuck in only fear, you stop returning — you despair. When you get stuck in only hope, you stop being serious — you drift. The pairing holds you in the posture the ayah itself models: awed, and returning. Humbled, and held.

[PAUSE]

Classical Muslim spiritual teaching sometimes named this posture *bayna al-khawf wa-l-rajāʾ* — between fear and hope. The believer is suspended between these two, with the ayah itself supplying the architecture. Because the Face that endures has these two qualities fused in it. If you are oriented toward *Him,* you will naturally carry both.

And this is the quiet mercy hidden in the ayah's closing phrase. Allah did not describe the One who remains as *dhū al-jalāl* alone. That description alone would have produced terror with no way out. He did not describe Him as *dhū al-ikrām* alone. That description alone would have produced a comfort with no weight. The conjunction *wa* — "and" — between them is not decorative. It is the theological architecture of how the believer must live.

**You cannot approach only the *jalāl* and be well. You cannot approach only the *ikrām* and be well. You approach the Face, which is both — and both is what saves you.**

[PAUSE]

**The core insight:** The believer is not called to fear and not called to only trust. The believer is called to orient toward a Face whose two attributes are woven together so tightly that the experience of Allah is both the humbling and the ennobling at once. The ayah is a corrective to any one-sided piety.

**Practical application:** The next time you pray, consciously hold both. Before takbīr, remind yourself: *I am standing before dhū al-jalāl — the Owner of Majesty.* Then immediately: *and dhū al-ikrām — the One who ennobles me beyond deserving by inviting me into this standing.* Let both truths enter your chest in the same breath. Notice what happens to the quality of your prayer. If one feels stronger than the other, that is information about which side of your spiritual life needs balance.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architectural Reveal

Now look at what this passage has actually done.

Two ayahs. Seventeen words. And the structure is this:

Ayah 26: *Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.* Universal subject. Participle. Perishing.
Ayah 27: *Wa yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.* Continuous verb. Specific subject (Face, your Lord). Dual attributes.

Notice the asymmetry. The first ayah describes *everything* in the most efficient possible way — one noun, one participle. Three words in Arabic (*kullu, man-ʿalayhā, fān*). Because everything, despite its vast apparent diversity, is actually doing one thing — perishing. The grammar collapses the multiplicity into a single description.

The second ayah describes *the One* in more detailed language — a continuous verb, a specific subject (*wajh*), a possessor (*rabbika*), two characterizing attributes (*jalāl*, *ikrām*). Because the One who remains cannot be collapsed into a single word. His reality requires naming, and each name opens another layer.

**This is itself the revelation.** The universe is multiplicity reducing to a single fate. Allah is unity requiring multiple names to begin to describe. The architecture of the two ayahs *is* the theological claim they are making: everything converges on ending, and the One ending does not is inexhaustible.

[PAUSE]

And notice where this passage sits in Surah Ar-Raḥmān. The surah is a litany of favors — gardens, fruits, pearls, mates, sun, moon, stars, rain. Each favor is mentioned, then the refrain: *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān?* — *"which of your Lord's favors will you two deny?"*

Right in the middle, Allah pauses the litany to say: *and all of this is perishing. What remains is My Face.*

It is as if the surah is saying: *Here are the gifts. Enjoy them. Thank Me for them. But do not confuse the gifts with the Giver. The gifts will be gone. The Giver will remain. Direct your gratitude through the gifts to Me, because I am the one you will still have when none of the gifts are left.*

The architecture of the surah is: praise of favors → declaration of their perishing → resumption of praise. The two ayahs are the vertical axis that holds the horizontal favors in place. Without them, the favors would be idols.

### The Emotional Arc

We started outside — in a world that assumes permanence. We walked through the active participle that refuses to let perishing be future. We walked through the continuous verb that refuses to let remaining be finished. We walked through *wajh* as the Face you are oriented toward, *rabbika* as your specific Lord, and the two attributes that fuse severity and mercy. We walked through the audit of what you are building and the integration of *jalāl* and *ikrām* in a single spiritual posture.

What we discovered is this: **these seventeen words are not merely a description of reality. They are an instruction manual.** They tell you how to spend the rest of your life. Orient toward the Face. Attach what you build to what remains. Hold *jalāl* and *ikrām* in the same breath. And do not be deceived by the apparent permanence of anything other than Him — the participle *fān* applies to all of it, right now, as you read.

4. The ayah says *wajhu rabbika* — the Face of *your* Lord. Can you locate, in your current life, the specific direction your life is oriented toward? What is your true *qibla* — not geographically, but in terms of where your attention, effort, and hope actually point most days?

5. If you were told you had exactly one year left to live, which of your current major commitments would instantly reveal themselves as primarily *fān* — and which would reveal themselves as already attached? What does that thought-experiment reveal about the calibration you are living with right now, when you assume time is abundant?

### One Thing to Remember

> **Everything you can see is in the process of perishing — even as you look at it. The only part of reality that is not perishing is the Face your life was made to turn toward. Spend your hours attaching what you build to that Face. Nothing else will be there when you arrive.**

### The Closing Invitation

If these two ayahs want one thing from you tonight, it is this.

Before you sleep, recite the two ayahs to yourself in Arabic if you can, in translation if that is what you have. Slowly. One phrase at a time.

*Kullu man ʿalayhā fān.*

Let the active participle land. You are perishing. The people in your life are perishing. The things you are worried about are perishing. The things you are proud of are perishing.

Then.

*Wa yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām.*

The Face of your Lord — the One who humbles you honestly and ennobles you freely — remains. Is still there. Will be there tomorrow when you wake. Will be there when the career ends, the children scatter, the body weakens, the last breath leaves. Will be there as the Only One who was still there through all of it.

Orient toward Him. Now. While the orienting still costs you the choice.

### Du'a

Allāhumma — *dhā al-jalāli wa-l-ikrām* — You who remain when everything else is in the process of ending, let our orientation be toward Your Face. Attach the work of our hands to what does not perish. Let the hours we spend carry the thread that ties them to the only reality that endures.

And when we stand before You — as we will — let us arrive having built the portion that survived the *fān,* and leave behind, cleanly, the portion that was only temporary. Hold us in the fusion of Your *jalāl* and Your *ikrām:* humble us with truth, ennoble us with Your generosity, and let neither face of You be absent from our prayer.

*Āmīn.*
