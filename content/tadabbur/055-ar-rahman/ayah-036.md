# The Warning Was Also a Gift
### A Tadabbur on Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 36

---

## Introduction

> [ayah:55:36] فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ
>
> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*
>
> **So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?**

You already know this verse.

Even if you have never studied a word of Arabic, even if you could not find Surah Ar-Rahman in the *mushaf* without the index — if you have sat in a single *tarawih* night, this sentence has washed over you again and again until it became part of the furniture of your memory. It is the most repeated verse in the Qur'an. Thirty-one times in a single *surah*. It returns like a tide.

And that familiarity is a problem. Because when a thing returns that often, the heart learns to let it pass. You hear the *qari* reach it and something in you relaxes — *ah, the refrain, I know this one* — and you coast. The words become a resting place rather than a question. Which is a strange fate for a sentence that is, grammatically, one of the most confrontational things Allah says to His creation.

So let us do something with this verse that its very familiarity has made almost impossible. Let us treat it as if we had never heard it before.

**Rebuild the world it fell into.**

Surah Ar-Rahman opens with a name — not "Allah," not "your Lord" at first, but *Ar-Rahman*, the Entirely Merciful, the mercy that is a permanent attribute and not merely an occasional act. And then the *surah* does something no other *surah* does in quite this way. It becomes a gallery. Allah walks the two great created species — the *jinn* and mankind, together called *ath-thaqalān*, "the two weighty ones" — through room after room of what He has made. He taught the Qur'an. He created man. He taught him speech. The sun and the moon by exact calculation. The stars and the trees prostrate. He raised the sky and set the balance. The two seas that meet and do not overrun. Pearls and coral. Ships like mountains riding the waves.

And after each room in the gallery, the same voice turns to the two species standing there and asks: *So which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?*

Picture it. You are being given a tour of everything. Not by a curator — by the One who made every piece. And at each exhibit He does not say "look what I made." He turns, faces you and the *jinn* beside you, and asks you to your face whether you intend to call this a lie.

Now here is the thing that makes **this** occurrence of the refrain — verse 36 — different from all the others, and the reason we are sitting with this one and not any of the other thirty. Look at what comes right before it. Verse 35:

*"There will be sent upon you a flame of fire and smoke, and you will not be helped."*

*Yursalu ʿalaykumā shuwāzun min nārin wa nuḥās, fa-lā tantaṣirān.*

A flame. Molten brass. Smoke that chokes. And then the flat, final clause: *you will not be helped.* No rescuer. No exit. This is not a description of a garden. This is the moment in the tour where Allah shows the two species what happens to those who tried to flee His dominion — the challenge of the previous verse, *if you can pass beyond the limits of the heavens and the earth, then pass.* You cannot. And this is what waits.

And then — immediately, in the very next breath — the same refrain that followed the pearls and the coral and the sun and the moon:

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?*

**[PAUSE]**

Do you feel the vertigo of that? He has just described fire. And He calls it — He files it under the same heading as the fruit and the rivers — He calls it an *āla'*. A favor.

That is the door we are going to walk through today. Not "count your blessings." You have heard that. Something stranger. Something that, if it lands, will change what you are able to be ungrateful for.

The question is not really *which of the favors will you deny.* The question underneath it is: **what if the thing you are most tempted to deny is a favor — is the very thing that was trying to save you?**

Stay with me.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### The first word we will sit with: *āla'* — the favor that refuses to be counted

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because if I don't, this word will slide past you the way it has slid past you thirty-one times.

In English, when we want to talk about a category of things, we often reach for the singular and let it stand for the whole: "man is mortal." In Arabic there is a particular kind of plural — a plural of *paucity* and a plural of *abundance*, and there are broken plurals whose very shape tells you the singular has, in a sense, shattered and scattered into a spray of instances too many to line up in a row. When a speaker chooses one of these forms, they are not just saying "more than one." They are saying: *do not try to enumerate this. It exceeds the count.*

Now. The word in our verse is *ālā'* — آلاء.

Here is the first remarkable thing. The classical grammarians and lexicographers debated the singular of this plural, and they came back with several: *ilā*, *aluww*, *ilw*, *alan*. The word is grounded in the root — <!-- documented -->the letters *hamza–lām–wāw* — carrying the sense of a bestowal, a granting, a favor freely given. But notice what happened in that debate: **the plural is so familiar and the singular so rare that the scholars could not fully agree on what one favor, by itself, is called.**

Sit with that.

We know exactly what the plural is. We use it. But the singular — a single, isolated *āla'*, one favor standing alone, extracted and set on the table by itself — is almost a theoretical object. In the living language, favors do not come one at a time. They come already plural. They come as *ālā'*, as a spray, as weather.

The grammar is enacting the theology. **You cannot isolate a single favor of Allah** because no favor of Allah arrives alone. The breath you are taking as you read this did not arrive by itself; it came bundled with a functioning brainstem, an atmosphere with oxygen in it, ribs that did not collapse, a heart that fired without your permission, a night of sleep that let you wake to take it. To point at one favor and hold it up — *this one, I acknowledge this one* — is almost linguistically awkward, because the moment you grip it, a hundred others are hanging off it like fruit off a branch you thought you were holding by the single apple.

And now hear the sound of it. Say it slowly. *Ā-lā'.* Two long open vowels, the mouth falling open, a soft glide, ending on a breath. It is a word you can barely close your mouth around. It *spills.* The phonetics of the word are the theology of the word: an open, spilling, uncontainable softness.

**Notice something.** The verse does not say "which of the favors." It says *bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikum* — "by **which** of the favors." *Ayy.* Which one. The interrogative that demands you point. Allah has taken the one thing that resists being pointed at — the uncountable, spilling, always-already-plural favor — and He has aimed the sharpest question of specificity straight at it. *Which one. Name it. Put your finger on the single favor you are prepared to call a lie.*

It is a trap, and it is a mercy, and they are the same thing. Because you cannot answer. You cannot name one. The moment you try to isolate a favor to deny, the plural-ness of the word defeats you — everything you would deny is holding hands with everything you would never dream of denying. The verse is built so that its own question cannot be honestly answered *yes*.

**[PAUSE]**

And here is the absence — the thing that is not there, which we are commanded to read as carefully as the thing that is. Look at the whole *surah* and ask: **which** favors, exactly? The verse never restricts. It does not say "which of the favors of provision" or "which of the favors of the garden." It leaves the category wide open — *ālā'*, favors, full stop, unqualified. And because verse 35 just described fire, that open, unqualified category has been forced to stretch to include *the warning itself.* The absence of any limiting word is what lets the fire in the door. Had Allah said "which of My pleasant gifts," the flame of verse 35 would be excluded. He did not. He left the word bare. And into that bareness walks the most uncomfortable favor of all.

One favor invites us to consider: the warning is not the opposite of the gift. **The warning is inside the category of the gift.**

### The second word: *tukadhdhibān* — the verb that catches in the throat

We have been softly saying that this verse "asks whether you will deny." Now I want to slow down on that verb, because English is doing it a violence.

Teach the concept first. In Arabic, verbs come in patterns — the grammarians call them *forms*, numbered I through X. Form I is the plain, bare action. But when you double the middle root letter, you move into Form II, and Form II characteristically does something specific: it *intensifies*, it makes the action deliberate, causative, thoroughgoing. Form I *ʿalima* is "he knew"; Form II *ʿallama* is "he taught" — he actively, repeatedly, deliberately caused knowing. The doubling is not decoration. The doubling is labor. Form II is the form of *effort applied to an action.*

Now the verb in our verse. The root is <!-- documented -->the letters *kāf–dhāl–bā'*, the root of *kidhb*, of falsehood, of the lie. Form I of this root, *kadhaba*, means simply "he lied, he spoke an untruth." But the verse does not use Form I. It uses **Form II**: *kadhdhaba* — with the middle letter doubled — and the meaning shifts. *Kadhdhaba* does not mean "to lie." It means **to declare something a lie. To brand a truth as false. To take a real thing and actively, effortfully treat it as a fabrication.**

Do you see the difference? *Kadhaba* is a man who says something untrue. *Kadhdhaba* is a man standing in front of the sunrise, insisting it is a painting. The truth is right there — the favor is right there, spilling, uncountable — and *takdhīb* is the work of the will that looks at it and says: *no. This is not a gift. This is not from Him. This is not real.*

**Denial, in this verse, is not a passive state. It is a job.** It takes effort. You have to hold the truth off with both hands. The verb tells you that ingratitude is not a vacuum, not merely the *absence* of noticing — it is an *action*, a doubled, intensive, laboring action, a thing the *nafs* rolls up its sleeves to do.

And now — hear it. This is the part I need you to actually pronounce, out loud if you can. *Tu-kadh-dhi-bān.* Feel where it happens in your mouth. The doubled *dhāl* under the *shadda* — the tongue catches against the teeth, the sound plosive, clotted, effortful. It **stumbles.** Compare it to the word we just left: *ālā'*, which spilled open and soft off the lips like water. Now *tukadhdhibān* — it catches, it clots, it labors in the throat.

**Notice what Allah has done.** He placed the softest, most open, most spilling word in the sentence — *ālā'*, the favors — and He ended the sentence on the most clotted, effortful, stumbling word — *tukadhdhibān*, the denying. The gift flows. The denial catches. The very *sound* of the verse enacts its meaning: receiving is effortless and open; denying is hard, ugly labor against the grain of your own mouth.

You have to *work* to deny. The tongue itself resists it.

One way to sit with this: every time you have caught yourself insisting your life is a poverty when it is drowning in provision — every time you have looked at a spread of mercy and called it a lack — that was not laziness. That was *labor*. The *nafs* worked to produce that. It is easier, phonetically and spiritually, to open your mouth and receive than to clot it shut and refuse. And still we refuse. Still we do the harder thing.

### The third element: *Rabbi-kumā* — the "your" that is a "you two"

We have looked at what is given (*ālā'*) and at the labor of refusing it (*tukadhdhibān*). Now look at who is being asked. Because there is a grammatical feature buried in the middle of this verse that English simply cannot carry, and it changes the entire emotional temperature of the sentence.

The word is *Rabbikumā*. "Your Lord." But *kumā* is not the ordinary "your." Arabic, almost uniquely among the languages people speak, has a **dual** — a grammatical number that is neither singular nor plural but exactly *two*. Not "you" (one). Not "you all" (many). But *you two.* A form reserved for a pair, and only a pair.

Teach the weight of that. In English if I say "your Lord" you hear it as spoken to you, one soul, privately — or vaguely to everyone. But *Rabbikumā* cannot mean either of those. It means, precisely and only: **the Lord of you two.** And the verb at the end, *tukadhdhibān*, is also dual — "will *you two* deny." The whole sentence is aimed, grammatically, at exactly two parties.

Who are the two?

Go back to the world we rebuilt. This *surah* is addressed to *ath-thaqalān* — the two weighty species, **the *jinn* and mankind.** Both were named as created (the human from clay "like pottery," the *jinn* from "a smokeless flame of fire," 55:14–15). Both were challenged in verse 33 — *O company of jinn and men, if you can pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth, then pass.* And so when verse 36 says *Rabbikumā* — the Lord of you two — it is turning to face both species at once and asking them the question together.

**Here is what that does to you, the individual human reading this.** You are not being addressed alone in a quiet room. You are being addressed *in the presence of another entire species.* You are one of a pair standing in a courtroom, and the pair is not you-and-your-neighbor — it is *all of humanity* on one side and *all of jinn-kind* on the other, and the two of you are being asked, together, whether you will call the favors a lie.

Feel the exposure of that. There is a particular kind of accountability that only exists when you are not the only one being asked. When a teacher addresses the whole class, you can hide. When a teacher looks at two students — *you two* — and asks a question, there is nowhere to go. You cannot dissolve into the crowd, and you cannot pretend it was meant for someone else. *You two.* Named. Paired. Pinned.

**[PAUSE]**

And notice the divine name chosen — not "which of the favors of *Allah*," not "of the *Mighty*," not "of the *Overwhelming*," though the fire of verse 35 would have justified any of those. He says *Rabb.* The *Rabb* is the one who **nurtures, sustains, raises, and brings to completion** — the word carries the image of the master who tends what is his, the way you tend a garden or raise a child from helplessness. Of all the names, He reaches for the one that means *the one who has been growing you this whole time.*

So the full weight of the address lands like this: *the One who has been nurturing you two from clay and from flame, tending you like a garden, has just shown you fire — and even the fire He shows you is part of the tending. Which of the favors of the One raising you will you two brand as a lie?*

The name and the number are doing the same work the two earlier words did. *Rabb* insists the whole thing — including the warning — is nurture. And *kumā* makes sure you cannot escape into anonymity while you decide how to answer.

### The bridge out of the linguistic journey

Look at what these three have shown us, together.

*Ālā'* — the favor that cannot be isolated or counted, that spills, that has been forced by the bare grammar to include even the fire.

*Tukadhdhibān* — the denial that is not absence but *labor*, the clotted, effortful work of branding a gift a lie, so hard the tongue resists it.

*Rabbikumā* — the address that pins you as one of a named pair before the Lord who has been nurturing you the whole time, so you cannot hide while you answer.

Three words. And already the "count your blessings" reading is gone, replaced by something with teeth. But we have only looked at the words. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about language — but about the shape of a human life. About you, this week, with the things you have been calling problems.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The favor you're standing in that you've stopped seeing

Let me tell you about a machine in your head.

There is a well-documented feature of human cognition that psychologists call the *negativity bias.* Put simply: bad is stronger than good. A single insult outweighs five compliments. One thing that goes wrong on a Tuesday can define the whole Tuesday, while the ninety-nine things that went right — the car that started, the water that ran hot, the knees that carried you up the stairs, the child who was breathing when you checked — register as nothing at all. Not as small positives. As *nothing.* They do not even make it onto the ledger.

And there is a companion machine: *hedonic adaptation.* Whatever good arrives, you normalize it, fast. The raise that thrilled you in March is the baseline salary you resent by September. The apartment you prayed for becomes the apartment you complain about. The spouse you begged Allah for becomes the person whose chewing annoys you. The mind is a treadmill: every gift, no matter how large, slides backward under your feet until it is simply *the floor* — invisible, unfelt, the neutral ground from which you scan the horizon for the next lack.

Now put those two machines together and you get a very specific kind of creature. A creature standing waist-deep in a river of provision, cognitively engineered to see only the far bank where the water hasn't reached yet. A creature for whom every favor becomes floor, and only the absence of favor registers as figure.

**This is who the verse is talking to. This is who *you two* are.**

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

Remember what we found in the word *ālā'* — that it cannot be isolated, that it comes as weather, always plural, always spilling. Now lay that against the negativity bias. The verse describes reality: favors, uncountable, everywhere, spilling. Your cognition describes the opposite: a floor of nothing, and a horizon of lack. **The gap between what is actually there and what your machinery lets you see — that gap is exactly the space where *takdhīb* happens.** You are not denying the favors by arguing against them. You are denying them by *not seeing them at all.* The floor does not lie about being a floor. It just stopped being noticed as a gift.

And here is where the verb we studied bites down. We said *tukadhdhibān* is Form II — deliberate, laboring, effortful. You might object: *but I'm not deliberately denying anything, I just don't notice.* And the verse's answer is quietly devastating. The reason you don't notice is that your *nafs* is doing continuous, low-level labor to keep the favors filed under "floor." Every act of adaptation — every time you take the working eyes and the running water and the un-cancer'd body and slide it beneath your feet into the invisible — is a small labor of *takdhīb.* Not a lie you speak. A truth you work to keep from feeling.

**[PAUSE]**

Let me bring this home. Tonight, before you sleep, you will do a thing. You will lie down on something soft, in a room with a controlled temperature, having eaten, behind a door that locks, with clean water within arm's reach and a device in your hand that holds the entire Qur'an and can summon a doctor to your door. Kings a thousand years ago could not command a fraction of it. And there is a real chance — be honest — that the last thought in your head before sleep will be a complaint. A resentment. A lack. Something you don't have, someone who wronged you, a horizon where the water hasn't reached.

That is not a moral failure to feel guilty about. It is a *machine*, and now you know its name, and now you know the verse was built to interrupt it. Because the question is not rhetorical. *Which one.* The verse hands you the one tool your machinery cannot survive: it makes you *point.* Adaptation works by keeping everything vague, floor-like, un-itemized. But you cannot adapt to a favor you are forced to name out loud. *Which one will you deny — the eyes? Say it. The eyes? The child's breath? The water? Which one.* The moment you are made to itemize, the treadmill jams. The floor turns back into a gift the instant you are forced to point at it and consider calling it a lie.

**What this changes tomorrow:** Not "be more grateful" — that is a mood, and moods don't come on command. Something mechanical instead. Once a day, out loud, make yourself do what the verse does: *point at one and try to deny it.* Take the most invisible floor-favor you have — the one so normal you've never once thanked Allah for it — and say to yourself, *this one, from my Rabb, shall I call this a lie?* Watch the machine jam. That is the verse operating on you as designed. That is *tadabbur* becoming *ʿamal.*

**[Major PAUSE]**

### Theme Two: When the warning is the mercy — and you're too busy being afraid to see it

Now the harder one. The one this specific verse — verse 36, sitting where it sits — was chosen to open.

Think about the last time someone who loved you told you something you did not want to hear.

A parent who said *the way you're living is going to cost you.* A friend who said *he is not good for you* when you were still in love with him. A doctor who looked at the scan and did not soften it. A teacher who told you the truth about your work instead of the comfortable thing. In the moment — what did you feel? Not gratitude. Almost never gratitude. You felt attacked. Judged. You felt the warning as an *aggression*, as the opposite of love, and part of you wanted to defend, to argue, to write off the messenger. *Who are they to say that to me.*

And then — sometimes years later — you understood. That the hardest thing anyone said to you was the most loving thing anyone did for you. That the people who only ever told you what you wanted to hear were not, in the end, the ones who loved you most. That a warning is what love looks like when love can see a cliff you're walking toward and you cannot.

Hold that feeling. Now look at where verse 36 sits.

Verse 35 was fire. A flame, molten brass, smoke, *and you will not be helped.* It is, unmistakably, a threat. It is the doctor refusing to soften the scan. It is the cliff, named. And every instinct in the human *nafs* receives a threat the way we just described — as aggression, as the opposite of favor, as the thing to defend against, argue with, write off.

And into that exact instinct, Allah drops the refrain: *So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?*

He is doing to the fire what the years did to your parent's hard words. He is telling you, *in the very moment of the threat*, what it will take you a lifetime to understand on your own: **this warning is an *āla'*. This is a favor. The fire I am showing you is not Me withdrawing My love — it is My love, refusing to let you walk off the cliff in silence.**

Remember what the bare, unqualified grammar of *ālā'* did — it left the category open, it let the fire in the door. And remember the name: *Rabb*, the one who nurtures. A *Rabb* who only ever showed you the garden and never the fire would not be nurturing you. He would be flattering you. The gardener who never warns you which berries are poison, out of a desire to only ever be pleasant, is not tending the garden. **The warning is not the exception to His nurture. It is the proof of it.**

**[PAUSE]**

Now feel the full force of the confrontation, personalized. Because verse 36 is not really asking whether you'll deny the sun and the moon — nobody is tempted to be ungrateful for the sun. It is asking, in context, something far more pointed: *Will you deny that the warning was a favor?* Will you be the person who receives the most loving thing — the honest warning, the un-softened truth about where you're headed — and *brands it a lie?* Calls it harsh, calls it fear-mongering, calls it a God too angry to love, and turns away from the one clear voice that was trying to save you?

That is the *takdhīb* this verse is really hunting. Not the failure to thank Allah for rain. The failure to recognize that the fire He described was mercy in the shape of terror. The labor — remember, *tukadhdhibān* is labor — of holding off the most loving thing anyone ever told you because it did not arrive as a compliment.

There is a version of every one of us that does exactly this with the whole of the *dīn.* We receive the *ḥalāl* and the *ḥarām*, the warnings, the accountability, the description of the Fire, and we experience it as a God who is *against* us, restricting us, threatening us — when the entire architecture of it is a *Rabb* who loves you too much to let you walk toward the cliff without a word. To read the warning as aggression is to make the same mistake you made with your parent, your friend, your doctor — except this time the cliff is not a bad relationship or a failed class. This time the cliff is everything.

**What this changes tomorrow:** the next time the *dīn* tells you something you don't want to hear — a limit, a warning, an accountability you'd rather not face — catch the instinct to feel *attacked*, and run it through this verse. Ask: *is this the fire in verse 35, and am I about to do verse 36 to it? Am I about to brand a rescue as a cruelty?* Then do the thing you eventually did with the people who loved you enough to be honest. Say *thank you* to the hard thing. Not because it stopped being hard. Because you finally saw who was speaking, and why.

**[Major PAUSE]**

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture — why thirty-one times

We began by noticing that this verse repeats. Thirty-one times through the *surah*, until familiarity nearly buried it. I want to end by showing you that the repetition is not redundancy. It is the whole design.

Walk back through Surah Ar-Rahman with what we now know. The refrain does not come only after the beautiful things. Yes, it follows the sun and the moon, the pearls and the coral, the two gardens with their spreading branches and flowing springs. But it also follows *this* — verse 35, the flame and the molten brass. And later it follows the description of the criminals seized by their forelocks and feet. **The same sentence closes the rooms of mercy and the rooms of terror.**

That is the architecture, and it is the summit view that proves the climb. By placing the *identical* refrain after both the garden and the fire, Allah builds, across the whole *surah*, a single structural argument that no individual verse could make alone: **the favors and the warnings are the same category.** The garden is an *āla'*. The fire, *described in advance so that you might flee it*, is also an *āla'*. The structure itself — refrain after mercy, refrain after terror, refrain after mercy again — is Allah teaching you, through repetition, the exact lesson our two words taught in miniature: that the open, uncontainable, always-plural favor (*ālā'*) is wide enough to hold the warning, and that to deny any of it — the pleasant or the frightening — is the same clotted, effortful labor of *takdhīb.*

The repetition is not Allah running out of things to say. **The repetition is the mercy of the *Rabb* who knows the negativity bias, who knows adaptation, who knows that we hear a truth once and file it under floor.** So He does not say it once. He says it thirty-one times, because He is nurturing a creature that forgets — a creature that needs the same true thing said again and again before it finally lodges. The refrain repeats *because you adapt.* Its repetition is itself an *āla'*, a favor tailored to the exact defect of the mind that receives it.

### Bringing it together

Look at how the two halves of today were one thing all along.

The linguistic journey found: a favor that cannot be counted or isolated (*ālā'*), a denial that is deliberate labor against a resisting tongue (*tukadhdhibān*), and an address that pins you as one of a named pair before the One nurturing you (*Rabbikumā*).

The thematic depths found: a mind engineered to turn every favor into invisible floor, and a heart tempted to brand the most loving warning as an aggression.

They are the same insight from two directions. The words told us *what* is happening — favors spilling, a will laboring to refuse them. The themes told us *where* it happens in your actual Tuesday — in the treadmill of adaptation, in the flinch away from the hard truth. And both arrive at one place: **you are surrounded, right now, by favors so total you cannot point to their edges, including the favors that arrived disguised as warnings — and the only question left is whether you will do the hard, throat-clotting labor of calling them a lie, or the easy, open, spilling thing of finally receiving them.**

### Questions to carry

- When you look honestly at your own life this week — what have you been filing under "floor" that a stranger, seeing it for the first time, would call a miracle?
- Think of the last warning you received — from the *dīn*, from a person who loves you, from your own conscience — that you experienced as an attack. What if it was verse 35, and what did you do with it?
- If you were forced, right now, to *point* — *bi-ayyi* — to name one single favor of your *Rabb* and call it false: could you? And if you cannot, what does that say about every complaint you'll make tomorrow?
- Where in your life are you flattering yourself — surrounding yourself only with voices that tell you what you want to hear — and calling that comfort, when the *Rabb* would call it the absence of nurture?
- What would actually change about the last thought before you sleep tonight, if you truly believed the warnings were love?

### The one sentence to carry

**Even the fire He showed you was a favor — and to hear "you cannot escape" and brand it anything but love is the one denial your own tongue was built to resist.**

### The invitation

You will hear this verse again. Probably this Ramadan, standing in *tarawih*, tired, somewhere around the middle of the *surah*, the *qari* will reach it and something in you will relax into the familiar. Let it not relax this time. Let it *pin* you. Let the dual *kumā* find you standing in the row and ask *you* — you two, you and everything unseen beside you — the real question. Not *are you grateful.* But: *which one. Point. Which favor of the One who has been raising you from clay, including the ones that frightened you, are you going to spend your labor calling a lie?*

And then do the easier thing. Open your mouth instead of clotting it. Receive.

### A closing du'a

*Yā Rabb — You who nurture us from clay and from flame, who file even Your warnings under Your mercy: unstick the tongue of our hearts from the labor of denial. Let us not brand as a lie the favors we've grown too used to see, nor the warnings You sent because You loved us enough to be honest. Make us of those who, when asked which of Your favors they will deny, can only fall silent — because they can no longer find a single one.* Āmīn.

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?*
