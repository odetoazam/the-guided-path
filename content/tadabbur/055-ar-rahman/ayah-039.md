# The Silence of Judgment: When No One Is Asked

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with how short they are.

> فَيَوْمَئِذٍ لَّا يُسْـَٔلُ عَن ذَنۢبِهِۦٓ إِنسٌ وَلَا جَآنٌّ
>
> *Fa-yawma'idhin lā yus'alu ʿan dhanbihi insun wa lā jānn.*
>
> **"Then on that Day, none will be asked about his sin — neither man nor jinn."**

Eight words. One breath. And inside it, something that should stop us cold.

Before we go anywhere near what it means, I want to rebuild the room we are standing in. Because this verse does not arrive in a vacuum. It arrives inside the most rhythmic, most insistent surah in the entire Qur'an.

You know this surah even if you have never studied it. It is the one with the refrain that returns thirty-one times — **فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?"* Over and over. Like a wave that will not stop coming in. And notice — *Rabbikumā*. **Your Lord** — but dual. Spoken to *two*. The whole surah is a conversation held with two audiences at once: humankind and the jinn. The two species of created, accountable, free-willed beings. Classical scholars call them **al-thaqalān** — the two weighty ones.

So for pages, Allah has been speaking to *both of you*. He created you both. He gave you both the sun on its reckoning, the stars and the trees that prostrate, the sky He raised, the balance He set down, the fruit and the date-palm and the fragrant grain. *Which favor will you two deny?* It is almost tender. It is the speech of a Lord listing, one by one, every gift He has placed in front of two children.

And then the surah turns.

The sky splits open. Verse 37 — it becomes **wardatan ka-d-dihān**, red like a rose, molten like oil dragged through fire. The gentleness does not vanish, but the scene has changed. We are now standing on the Day. The Day toward which all those gifts were quietly pointing the whole time.

And it is *here*, with the sky bleeding red above two trembling species, that Allah says: **on that Day, no one will be asked about his sin.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is the hook. Hold it lightly, because we are going to spend the next hour earning it.

Everywhere else in the Qur'an, the Day of Judgment is a Day of *questioning*. **"By your Lord, We will surely question them all"** (15:92). You will be asked about your hearing, your sight, your heart. About every favor. About the very gifts this surah keeps listing.

So which is it? Are we questioned, or not?

And underneath that — a deeper, quieter dread that most of us have never named: **what does it mean to stand before the only One who matters, and not be asked a single thing?**

Stay with me. Because the answer is not what your relief wants it to be.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb With No Asker — *yus'alu* in the Passive

Let me teach you something about language before we touch the verse. It is small, and it changes everything.

In every language, there is a difference between an **active** sentence and a **passive** one. "The judge asked the defendant" — that is active. We know who is doing the asking. Now compare: "The defendant *was asked*." Notice what just happened. The judge disappeared. The action is still there — someone, somewhere, is asking — but the one *doing* it has been lifted out of the sentence and hidden.

We do this in English all the time, often to soften or to obscure. "Mistakes were made." By whom? The passive lets you not say.

Arabic has this exact tool, and it is sharper than ours. It is called **al-mabnī lil-majhūl** — literally, *"built for the unknown."* The verb is constructed so that the doer is deliberately concealed. And Arabic, being precise, changes the very shape of the word to signal it.

Now look at our verse.

The word is **يُسْـَٔلُ** — *yus'alu*. From the root *sa'ala* — to ask. And it is in exactly this form. The passive. *"Is asked."*

[PAUSE]

Notice what Allah did *not* say. He did not say *"the angels will not ask them."* He did not say *"I will not ask him."* He left the asker out.

Let's sit with this, because it is doing real work.

When you build a verb "for the unknown," you focus the listener's entire attention on one thing: **the not-asking itself**. The event of questioning has been cancelled — and by removing the asker, the verse makes the *cancellation* the whole point. It is not "the angels happen not to ask." It is: across this entire Day, from every possible direction, *the question does not come.*

Here is what is remarkable. Think about what a question *is*, between two people. A question is an **opening**. When someone asks you "why did you do it?" — painful as it is — they are giving you a door. A chance to speak. To explain. To say "let me tell you what really happened." Every interrogation, however hostile, contains a hidden mercy: *you get to give your account.*

And the passive voice here quietly seals that door. No one is asking. Which means — no one is waiting to hear you.

We will return to that. For now, just hold the shape of the word: **a question with the questioner erased, so that all you can feel is the silence where the question should have been.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Dhanbihi* — "His Sin," Singular and Owned

Now look at what they are *not* being asked about. The phrase is **عَن ذَنۢبِهِۦ** — *ʿan dhanbihi* — "about his sin."

Two things in this small word.

First, the root. *Dhanb* — the root letters are *dhā-nūn-bā* (ذ-ن-ب). And the lexicographers note something the Arabs heard in this word that we miss: the same root gives us *dhanab* — the **tail**. The thing that *trails behind* an animal. A *dhanb*, a sin, in the deep grain of the Arabic, is the thing that **follows behind you.** The deed is done — but it does not stay in the past. It trails. It comes after. It is attached to you and it walks where you walk.

Let that image stand for a moment. We tend to think of a sin as a moment that *happened* — back there, finished. The Arabic says no. It is a *tail*. It is still attached. On that Day it has followed you all the way to the splitting sky.

Second — and do not rush past this — the word is **singular**, and it carries a possessive: *dhanb-**ihi***. *His* sin. Not "their sins." Not "the sins of mankind." **His.** One being, one sin, owned.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the friction. The entire surah, for thirty verses, has been speaking in the **dual** and the plural. *Rabbikumā.* You two. Which favors will *you both* deny. It has been a shared address, a collective standing-together of two whole species before their Lord.

And then, at the moment of reckoning, the grammar contracts to a single point. **His sin.** The plural crowd of "you two" dissolves into one individual holding one trailing deed.

This is what accountability actually is, and the grammar performs it before the meaning even arrives. You stood in the crowd your whole life. You denied the favors *together*. You sinned in the comfort of "everyone does it." And on that Day the crowd evaporates and there is only *you*, and the one thing that has been walking behind you the entire time.

### Element Three: *Insun wa lā jānn* — Two Indefinite Strangers

Now the last two words. **إِنسٌ وَلَا جَآنٌّ** — *insun wa lā jānn* — "man, and not jinn." Humankind, and the jinn. The two weighty ones. The same two who have been addressed by that dual *Rabbikumā* the whole surah long.

Here is the grammatical detail that matters. Both words are **indefinite** — *nakirah*. Not *al-insān*, "the human." Not "the jinn-kind." Just — *insun*. *A* human. *Jānn*. *A* jinn. Bare. Unspecified. Any one of them.

In Arabic, an indefinite noun inside a negation — *"not asked... a human, nor a jinn"* — sweeps the widest possible net. It means: **not a single one. No exceptions. Not one human, not one jinn, anywhere in the gathering.**

So the address that began so personal — *your* Lord, you *two* — ends in total anonymity. *A* human. *A* jinn. You have become indefinite. One unnamed figure in an ocean of unnamed figures, each holding his own trailing tail.

[PAUSE]

And listen to the sound of that final word. **جَآنٌّ** — *jānn*. Say it. There is a long, held *ā*, and then the *nūn* doubled and stopped — *jānnn*. The root is *janna* — *jīm-nūn-nūn* — and it means *to cover, to conceal, to hide in darkness*. It is the root of *jinn* (the hidden ones), of *janīn* (the embryo, hidden in the womb), of *jannah* (the garden, hidden by its own foliage). The jinn are, by their very name, *the concealed*.

And the verse drags this hidden, veiled creature out into the open light of a sky split red — and says: even *you*, the one whose entire nature is concealment, will stand uncovered. The hidden ones are not hidden anymore.

---

### Bridge

So the linguistic construction has shown us three things, and they fit together like a closing hand.

A question whose asker has been erased — so that all that remains is silence. A sin that is singular, owned, and trailing behind a single individual. And two whole species reduced to bare, indefinite, *uncovered* strangers.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because the verse hands us a riddle, and the riddle is the door.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Terror of Not Being Asked

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

Imagine you are called into a room. Something has gone wrong, and you know your part in it. You are dreading the conversation. You walk in, rehearsing your explanation — *let me explain, it wasn't what it looked like, if you'll just hear me out.*

And the person behind the desk does not look up. They do not ask you anything. They slide a folder across the table — and it already contains everything. Timestamps. Recordings. Every message you thought was private. They do not need your account. They were never going to ask. The case was closed before you walked in.

Tell me honestly — which is worse? The hostile interrogation where at least you get to *speak*? Or the silence where there is nothing left to ask, because it is all already known?

[PAUSE]

*That* is the terror this verse is unlocking. Not the terror of being grilled. The terror of being **finished with**.

We have an instinct — a deep one — that we will get to *explain ourselves*. It runs under everything. "If I could just tell my side." "If they understood the context." Our whole sense of fairness is built on the assumption that judgment includes a *hearing*. That we will be asked.

And the verse says: on that Day, *lā yus'alu* — you are not asked. Now we understand why Allah hid the asker. Because the point was never *who* asks. The point is that **the asking is over.** There is nothing to establish. Nothing to clarify. The folder is complete.

Now — and here is where the classical scholars open the depth — this does not contradict the verses about questioning. Sit with this carefully, because it is precise.

The mufassirūn gathered around this verse and gave us a composite picture, each looking from a different angle:

**ʿIkrimah**, as Qurtubi reports, said the Day is *long*, and it has many **stations** — *mawāṭin*. At some stations there is questioning; at others there is none. The Day is not one moment but a whole geography of standings, and this verse photographs one of them.

**Al-Hasan al-Baṣrī and Qatādah** — and this is narrated from Ibn ʿAbbās through al-ʿAwfī — said: they are not asked *in order to learn anything from them*, because Allah already knows their deeds and the angels have already written them down. The questioning that gathers information is meaningless here. There is no information left to gather.

And **Mujāhid** points us to the very next verses: the angels do not need to ask the criminals *who they are*, because — verse 41 — **يُعْرَفُ الْمُجْرِمُونَ بِسِيمَاهُمْ** — *"the criminals will be known by their marks."* Their faces give them away. Guilt has become *visible*. It is written on them.

Do you see how every angle converges? They are not asked — **because there is nothing left that a question could accomplish.** The knowledge is complete. The record is written. The guilt is on the face. The tail is plainly attached.

[PAUSE]

This is the inversion the verse performs. In this life, *not being asked* feels like relief — *phew, I got away with it, no one questioned me.* On that Day, *not being asked* is the most absolute thing that can be said about you. It means your case required no testimony. It means you were so fully known that even the courtesy of a question would have been redundant.

And now — feel where we are in the surah. **Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.** *Which of your Lord's favors will you deny?* For thirty verses He asked you that, gently, again and again, *giving you the question* while it still mattered. The asking happened *in the dunya.* The whole surah is the question, asked in mercy, while there was still time to answer. The Day of *lā yus'alu* is simply the Day the questions stop.

**So what does this change about tomorrow?** It means the time to be *asked* — to be confronted, examined, called to account — is *now*. Every pang of conscience, every "why did you do that?" that rises in you, every uncomfortable question your own heart puts to you — that is the mercy of the question, arriving while it can still change the verdict. The person who lets himself be questioned now, gently, by his own conscience and his Lord, is spared the Day when the questions are over. *Do not run from the questions while they are still being offered to you.*

### Theme Two: Standing Alone — The Dissolving of the Crowd

Now the second thread, and it begins with something almost everyone reading this has felt.

We sin in *company*. Not always literally — but psychologically, we almost never sin alone. We sin inside a crowd of justifications. *Everyone does this. It's normal. The whole culture is like this. I'm not worse than anyone else.* The crowd is a blanket. It is warm. It tells you that you are one of many, and that whatever you are carrying, everyone is carrying it too, so it can't be that heavy.

The modern world has industrialized this feeling. We have never been more surrounded — feeds, group chats, the endless sense that "people like me" are all doing the same thing. The diffusion of responsibility is total. *If everyone is responsible, no one is.*

Now bring back the grammar.

For thirty verses, Allah held you inside the *crowd of two*. *Rabbikumā.* You two. The favors *you both* received. The denial *you both* are capable of. It was, in a strange way, comfortable — even the warning came addressed to a *we*.

And then: **ʿan dhanbihi.** *His* sin. Singular. Owned. The crowd is gone. The "everyone" has vanished. There is no "you two" anymore — there is *one* being and *one* tail trailing behind *him*.

[PAUSE]

This is the loneliest grammatical move in the surah, and it is deliberate. Because here is the truth the crowd was hiding from you: **the crowd was never going to stand with you.** It felt like a "we" the whole time, but accountability was always singular. The blanket was an illusion. You were always going to walk in alone, holding the one thing that followed *you*.

And notice the mercy folded even into this severity. *Dhanbihi* — *his* sin. You will not be charged with anyone else's. The man beside you will not be loaded onto your scale. In a world where we are forever absorbing collective guilt and collective blame, the Day of Judgment is, in this one respect, scrupulously *fair*: you carry *yours*. No more. *"No bearer of burdens bears the burden of another"* (35:18). The same grammar that strips away your comforting crowd also guarantees that not one atom of injustice will be done to you.

So the verse does two things to the crowd at once. It dissolves the false "we" that let you feel light. And it promises that the aloneness, terrifying as it is, is *exactly* fair.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It means you must learn to do, *now*, the accounting that the Day will do anyway. The Prophet ﷺ — and this is preserved in the tradition of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb — taught the believers to *"call yourselves to account before you are called to account."* To step out of the crowd voluntarily. To ask yourself, alone, in the dark — *what is the tail that follows me?* — while the question is still yours to ask. The one who singles himself out now is not ambushed by his own singularity later.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see how the verse is built, because the structure is the proof of everything we have said.

Look at its shape:

**Fa-yawma'idhin** — *then, on that Day* — the time. The verdict-moment.
**lā yus'alu** — *no one is asked* — the silence at the center.
**ʿan dhanbihi** — *about his sin* — the singular, trailing, owned thing.
**insun wa lā jānn** — *man, nor jinn* — the two, made anonymous and uncovered.

The verse moves from a *Day*, to a *silence*, to a *single sin*, to *two whole species reduced to nameless strangers*. It contracts. From the cosmic to the personal to the utterly alone. The grammar *is* the journey of the Day itself — from the splitting of the whole sky down to one being and the one tail behind him, with no question coming to break the silence.

And it sits — do not forget — between a sky bleeding red (38) and faces that betray their owners (41). Above you: the universe undone. Beside you: your own face, confessing without a word. And in the middle, this verse: the silence where the question used to be.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **passive verb** that erased the asker — and the **terror of not being asked** — are one thing: a judgment so complete that questioning has become unnecessary, and the silence is the sentence.

The **singular, owned, trailing sin** — and the **dissolving of the crowd** — are one thing: the truth that accountability was always individual, that the "we" was always a blanket, and that you will walk in holding only what followed *you*.

And the whole verse, sitting inside *Ar-Raḥmān*, the surah of Mercy — tells us the deepest thing of all. **The asking *was* the mercy.** Thirty-one times: *which favor will you deny?* That was the question, offered freely, while it still mattered. *Lā yus'alu* is simply the day the Most Merciful stops asking — because the time for the answer has passed.

### Questions to Carry

1. When my conscience asks me "why did you do that?" — do I treat it as an intrusion to silence, or as the mercy of a question arriving while it can still change my verdict?
2. What is the *dhanb* — the tail — that I know is following me right now, that I have been telling myself is finished and back there in the past?
3. Where in my life am I hiding inside a crowd, telling myself "everyone does this," when accountability was always going to be *singular*?
4. If I were handed the complete folder of my own deeds today — would I want to be *asked*, or would I be afraid that there is nothing left to ask?
5. Have I been answering the question this surah keeps putting to me — *which of His favors will you deny?* — or have I been letting it wash past me like a refrain I no longer hear?

### One-Sentence Distillation

On the Day when the evidence is so complete that no question remains to be asked, the silence itself is the verdict — so let yourself be questioned now, while the asking is still mercy.

### The Closing Invitation

I am not asking you to be afraid. I am inviting you to do something braver than fear: to **let yourself be asked.** Tonight, alone, step out of the crowd. Take the one tail you know is following you, and put the question to yourself that the Day will not bother to ask — *why?* And then answer it, and turn, while the One who is asking is still *Ar-Raḥmān*, still listing His favors, still leaving the door of the question open. The silence is coming. Speak before it does.

### Brief Du'a

O Allah, *Ar-Raḥmān*, You who ask us gently while there is still time — do not let us be among those for whom the asking has ended. Question our hearts now, in this life, so that we may answer; and let no sin trail behind us to that Day uncovered and unforgiven. Make us of those who called themselves to account before they were called, and who stood — not in a crowd, but cleansed and singular — needing no question, because of Your mercy and Your forgiveness. Āmīn.
