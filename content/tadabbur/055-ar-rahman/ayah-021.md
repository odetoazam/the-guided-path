# Which One Will You Call a Lie? The Question Addressed to Two Worlds

## Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 21

[ayah:55:21] فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question That Will Not Stop Asking

Before we enter this ayah, we need to stand where its first listeners stood.

It is Makkah, in the early years. The Quran is arriving surah by surah into a city that has decided to resist it. And one of the things this city resists — strange as it sounds — is a *name*. When the Prophet ﷺ spoke of *al-Raḥmān*, the Quraysh recoiled. The Quran itself records their reaction: *"And when it is said to them, 'Prostrate to al-Raḥmān,' they say, 'And what is al-Raḥmān?'"* (25:60). They knew Allah as a name. But this name — the Intensely, Overwhelmingly Merciful — they treated as foreign. Suspicious. Deniable.

So a surah comes down that is named with that exact name. It opens with that name standing alone as a complete verse: *al-Raḥmān*. And then it does something no other surah does at this scale: it builds itself around a single question, repeated **thirty-one times**. After the sun and moon. After the earth laid out for creatures. After fruit and date palms and grain. After the two seas. After every single exhibit, the same eleven Arabic syllables return:

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

And here is the detail that should stop you before we go any further. The question is not addressed to *you*, singular. It is not addressed to *you all*, plural. It is addressed — with perfect grammatical precision — to **exactly two**.

Two who? And why does it matter that, according to a narration preserved in the classical sources, one of those two *already answered the question out loud* — and answered it better than we did?

That is where we are going. But we have to earn it. Let's begin with the grammar of being two.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Dual — A Question Built for Exactly Two

English gives you two ways to address people: one, or more than one. *You* and *you all*. Arabic gives you three. Between the singular and the plural sits the **dual** — a complete grammatical machinery for *exactly two*. Verbs conjugate for it. Pronouns exist for it. When an Arabic speaker uses the dual, nothing is vague: the audience is two, no more, no fewer, and both are being looked at simultaneously.

Now listen to our ayah again. *Rabbi-**kumā*** — "the Lord of *you two*." *Tukadhdhib-**āni*** — "will *you two* deny." Twice in four words, the grammar insists: **two addressees**.

Who are they?

Notice that the surah has been quietly preparing this answer. Al-Qurtubi, gathering the discussion, records that the majority of scholars — the *jumhūr* — held that the two are **humankind and the jinn**. And what's beautiful is *how* the classical discussion arrives there, because it isn't one argument. It's several camera angles converging on the same scene.

One angle: back in verse 10, Allah said He laid out the earth *lil-anām* — for all living creatures — a word wide enough to cover both species. Another angle, and stay with me here, because this one is structural: the surah has already named exactly two creations by name. Verse 3: *khalaqa al-insān* — "He created the human." Verse 15: *wa khalaqa al-jānn* — "and He created the jinn." Two creations, formally introduced, like two parties named at the top of a court document. The dual pronoun simply picks up what the surah already put on record. A third angle: the surah itself removes all doubt a few verses ahead — *"We will attend to you, O you two weighty ones"* (55:31), and then, explicitly, *"O company of jinn and humankind"* (55:33).

These are not competing opinions. They are one composite portrait: **the only two creations who carry moral accountability are standing side by side in the same courtroom, hearing the same question, at the same moment.**

[PAUSE]

Now the narration. Al-Qurtubi cites — recorded by al-Tirmidhi — the report of Jabir ibn Abdillah: the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah to his companions, and they sat silent. And he told them that the jinn had been a better audience: each time the refrain came, the jinn answered aloud — *none of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny.*

Sit with what that means. This question, grammatically aimed at two species, is — by this narration — a question one of the two has already answered. The unseen half of the courtroom has entered its plea. The transcript is waiting on us.

**What the dual reveals: you have never once heard this question alone. There is an audience you cannot see, addressed in the same breath as you — and their answer is already on record.**

### Element Two: Ālā' — The Word the Quran Saves for Deniers

Here is something worth knowing about how the Quran chooses words. Arabic has a common, warm, everyday word for blessing: *niʿmah*, plural *niʿam*. The Quran uses it constantly — *"If you tried to count the niʿmah of Allah, you could not enumerate it"* (16:18). When al-Muyassar glosses our ayah, it uses exactly this word: *fa-bi-ayyi **niʿam** rabbikumā*. So the basic meaning is settled: **ālā' means favors, blessings**. The lexicons agree.

But then ask the question a careful reader always asks: *if niʿam was available, why ālā'?*

Trace where *ālā'* actually appears in the Quran. Outside this surah, it is rare — and every appearance has the same atmosphere. Hud, standing before the people of 'Aad as they mock him, on the edge of their destruction: *"Remember the **ālā'** of Allah"* (7:69). Salih, before Thamud, in the same posture: *"Remember the **ālā'** of Allah"* (7:74). And in Surah al-Najm, in the middle of a confrontation with Quraysh's denial: *"So which of the **ālā'** of your Lord do you dispute?"* (53:55).

Do you see the pattern? *Niʿmah* is the word for favor in the language of gratitude. ***Ālā'* appears where favor meets denial.** It is the word prophets reach for when they are holding up blessings *in front of people who are refusing to see them*. What this usage pattern invites us to consider — and I flag this as a reflection drawn from observing the Quran's own habits, not a dictionary entry — is that *ālā'* is the inventory read out as evidence. Not favors enjoyed. Favors *exhibited*.

And in Ar-Rahman, this word appears thirty-one times. The entire surah is that exhibition, conducted with the specific vocabulary of a case being made.

**What this single word reveals: the surah is not a thank-you list. It is an evidentiary proceeding — and ālā' is the word stamped on every exhibit.**

### Element Three: Tukadhdhibān — Denial Is Not Silence. It Is a Verdict.

One more concept to learn, and it is the deepest of the three.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — forms — and changing the form changes what the action *does*. Form I is usually the simple act. Form II, made by doubling the middle root letter, can intensify the act, or cause it — but it has another function that matters enormously here: the **declarative** function, where the verb means *to declare something to have a quality*. To pronounce it so.

The root here is *k-dh-b* — lying, falsehood. Form I, *kadhaba*: he lied. But our verb is Form II — the Leeds corpus confirms it — ***kadhdhaba***: not *to lie*, but **to declare something a lie**. To look at a thing and pronounce the verdict over it: *false*.

Feel the difference. The ayah is not asking "which favor will you lie about?" It is asking: **"which favor will you stand over and pronounce false?"** Takdhīb is not an absence of gratitude. It is an *act* — a gavel coming down. The denier is not someone who failed to notice the favor. The denier is someone performing a judgment *against* it.

And now bring in the little word that makes this unanswerable: ***bi-ayyi*** — "which *one*?" The question refuses to be answered wholesale. You cannot dismiss the whole inventory with a wave. The interrogative forces itemization: pick one. Name it. The sun? The barrier holding the seas apart? The tongue you would use to pronounce the denial? **The question is constructed so that any specific answer convicts the one who gives it** — because to name a favor is to handle it, and no one can hold a favor in their hands and call it nothing.

Notice the tense, too. *Tukadhdhibān* is imperfect — ongoing, renewable action. Denial here is not a past event filed away. It is a verdict that has to be *re-pronounced* to stay in force. Which means — and let this land gently — it can also stop being pronounced. At any moment.

One more thing. An absence. The surah asks this question thirty-one times, and not once does it record an answer from the human side. The jinn's answer survives in the narration. Ours is the white space after every refrain. The Quran left the silence in the text — what that silence invites us to hear is that **the answer is still being written, and we are the ones writing it.**

**What the verb reveals: between you and every blessing stands a moment of judgment — and you are the judge whose ruling is being asked for, again and again, in real time.**

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Verdict You Didn't Know You Were Issuing

Think about the last thing you wanted badly and then got.

Maybe it was a phone. A job. An apartment. A diagnosis coming back clear. Remember the first day — the disbelief of actually having it. Now be honest about month three. The phone is the thing you're vaguely annoyed with. The job is the thing you complain about at dinner. The apartment is the place with the noisy radiator. Psychologists have a name for this: **hedonic adaptation** — the treadmill by which every gain becomes the new baseline, invisible within weeks, and the heart resets to wanting.

The modern framing treats this as passive. Drift. Nobody's fault — just how brains work.

The ayah's diagnosis is more severe, and more honest. *Tukadhdhibān* — Form II, declarative. The Quran does not describe the faded blessing as something you *forgot*. It describes a standing **verdict**: somewhere in the heart, a ruling was issued — *this doesn't count. This is owed to me. This is nothing.* That is takdhīb of a favor: not denying it exists, but denying it *signifies*. Denying that it points back to a Giver, that it arrived from mercy rather than from entitlement.

And here is where the imperfect tense becomes terrifying and liberating at once. The verdict is renewable. Every morning you wake under a roof and feel nothing, the gavel quietly falls again. But — *every morning the question is also asked again*. Thirty-one times in one surah, because the heart needs reversal proceedings more than once.

So the practical discipline this ayah hands you is not "be more grateful" — too vague to survive a Tuesday. It is the ayah's own mechanism: **the audit by name**. *Bi-ayyi* — which one? Once a day, take one favor — not "everything, alhamdulillah" but *one*: the specific person, the specific working lung, the specific narrow escape — hold it up the way the surah holds up the seas, and answer the question about *that one item*: do I declare this false, or do I declare it from my Lord? Itemization is how the surah defeats denial. Make it how you defeat yours.

### Theme Two: The Mercy of Being Asked Thirty-One Times

There is a kind of repetition we all resent. The notification that won't stop. The reminder email. Repetition, in our world, usually means *pressure*.

So face the difficulty squarely: why does the surah *named for mercy* — al-Raḥmān — repeat what sounds like an accusation thirty-one times? Wouldn't once suffice?

Look more carefully at where the repetitions fall, and the whole thing inverts.

The refrain never repeats *in a vacuum*. Each occurrence follows a **different exhibit**. It is not the same question thirty-one times — it is the same words pointed, each time, at a new favor. After the celestial order. After the earth and its fruit. And here, in our ayah, after something remarkable: verses 19 and 20 — *He released the two seas, meeting; between them a barrier they do not transgress* — and then, immediately, our question. Before the pearls. Before the coral of verse 22.

Stay with that placement. The favor being held up at *this* refrain is not the treasure. It is the **barrier**. The limit. The thing that *withholds* — that keeps salt from sweet, that says *this far and no further*. The surah counts the boundary itself among the *ālā'*, and asks you about it before showing you a single pearl. Every physical image in this surah is a stepping stone to something unseen, and this one points directly at a reality we spend our lives resenting: **the restraints in your life — the door that didn't open, the limit you couldn't cross — are listed in the inventory of favors.** The barrier comes before the pearl in the text because it comes before the pearl in reality: the seas can only yield their treasure *because* they are held in order.

And now the repetition reveals its nature. A question asked once is an interrogation. A question asked thirty-one times, each time gently re-aimed at a new gift, is something else: it is a teacher who will not let the student fail, re-asking until the answer is found. It is — dare we say it — the question functioning as **defense counsel**. Because the question contains its own escape: *which one?* And the only truthful answer — the one the jinn gave, the one the Prophet ﷺ told his companions the jinn gave — is *none. None of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny.* The question is repeated until you learn to say that.

Remember the inter-surah strand here. The surah just before this one, al-Qamar, is also built on a refrain — *"So is there any who will remember?"* — repeated after story upon story of *punishment*. Then Ar-Rahman answers it with a refrain repeated after favor upon favor. Two adjacent surahs, two refrains: one warns through what was destroyed, the other woos through what was given. The Quran asked if anyone would remember — and then immediately gave us thirty-one chances to.

The practical application: change how you hear the refrain when you recite this surah. Don't read it as a formula rushing past. Read each occurrence as *addressed to your day* — and answer it. Out loud if you can, the way the unseen listeners did. *Lā bi-shay'in min niʿamika rabbanā nukadhdhib.* Make the white space after the question stop being empty.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Step back and look at what was built around these four words.

A surah named with the name Makkah denied. Two creations formally introduced — *khalaqa al-insān*, *khalaqa al-jānn* — then addressed together in the dual, as the only two beings whose answer to anything matters morally. A special word, *ālā'*, drawn from the vocabulary prophets use when favors must be defended against deniers. A verb in the declarative form, exposing ingratitude as a verdict rather than a lapse. An interrogative that forbids wholesale dismissal and demands you name names. And the whole question, at this fourth station, positioned exactly between the barrier and the pearl — so that the limit is counted as a favor before the treasure ever surfaces.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two directions: **grammar made denial into an act, and the surah made gratitude into an answer.** The dual told us we are not asked alone; the narration told us the others already answered; the repetition tells us the court has not yet closed.

### Questions to Carry

- If I had to actually answer *bi-ayyi* — name one specific favor I have been treating as nothing — which one would my honesty force me to name first?
- What verdicts am I currently renewing without noticing — what blessings have I ruled "owed to me"?
- Which barrier in my life have I been resenting that may belong in the inventory of favors — the limit that is quietly producing the pearl?
- The jinn answered aloud. What would change in my recitation of this surah if I did too?
- When the question is asked of me for the final time — not in a surah, but in the standing before my Lord — what do I want my record of answers to already contain?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Ingratitude is not forgetting a favor but pronouncing a verdict against it — and the mercy of al-Raḥmān is that He re-asks the question after every single gift, favor by favor, so the verdict can be reversed before it is sealed.**

### The Closing Invitation

The next time you recite or hear Surah Ar-Rahman, let the refrain stop being background rhythm. Thirty-one times, a question will be aimed at you and at an audience you cannot see. You are invited — not commanded, invited, the way a question always invites — to do what the better listeners did: answer it.

### Du'a

O Allah, al-Raḥmān — none of Your favors do we deny. Forgive us every verdict our hearts pronounced against Your gifts while our hands were still holding them, teach us to see the barrier as mercy before we ever see the pearl, and when You ask us — for You will ask us — let our answer be the one You taught the unseen to give: *lā bi-shay'in min niʿamika rabbanā nukadhdhib*, and to You belongs all praise. Amin.
