# The Mountains That Run

### Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 24

> وَلَهُ ٱلْجَوَارِ ٱلْمُنشَـَٔاتُ فِى ٱلْبَحْرِ كَٱلْأَعْلَـٰمِ
>
> *Wa lahu'l-jawāri'l-munsha'ātu fi'l-baḥri ka'l-aʿlām*
>
> "And to Him belong the ships, raised high, running through the sea like mountains."

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing on the Shore

I want you to begin somewhere very specific. Not in a mosque, not in a book. On a shore.

Stand at the edge of the sea — the way a human being stood at the edge of the sea fourteen hundred years ago, and the way a human being stood at the edge of the sea ten thousand years before that. The water goes out further than your eyes can hold. It moves with a power you did not give it and cannot take away. If you walked into it, it would close over your head without a flicker of concern. The sea is the oldest picture humanity has of the thing that is bigger than us — the thing we do not command.

And then, out on that surface that should swallow anything heavy, something is moving. Something enormous. A shape of wood and iron and cargo, weighing more than a hundred men, sitting *on top of* the water as though the water had agreed to hold it. And it is not drifting. It is **going somewhere** — cutting a line across the surface, carrying people and grain and lives from one edge of the unknown to the other.

Now — this is the moment Surah Ar-Rahman freezes for us.

This surah is a song. It has a refrain that returns again and again like a wave returning to the same stretch of sand: *fa-bi'ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* — "So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?" And in between those refrains, Allah lays out His gifts one by one. The sun and the moon by precise reckoning. The stars and the trees in prostration. Man shaped from clay like pottery. The jinn from smokeless fire. Two seas set side by side with a barrier between them. Pearls and coral lifted out of the deep.

And then — the ships.

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want to plant in you before we go a single word further. In this entire catalogue of the things Allah made — the sun, the moon, the stars, the seas, the pearls — the ship is the one thing on the list that *humans build*. We do not manufacture stars. We do not pour the seas. But the ship — we cut the wood, we shape the hull, we raise the mast with our own hands.

So why does Allah say, of the one thing in the whole list that we make ourselves —

**"And to Him belong the ships"?**

Not "and We gave you the ships." Not "and you built ships." *To Him belong.* The grammar of ownership, placed at the very front of the sentence, over the one object on the table that has our fingerprints all over it.

Stay with that question. It is going to open into something about you that you did not expect to find sitting in a sea.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "وَلَهُ" — The Ownership Placed First

Let me teach you a small thing about Arabic before we touch the ayah, because it will change what you hear.

In English, word order is mostly fixed. "The book belongs to Him" — subject, verb, then the owner at the end. But Arabic is a language that can pick up a phrase and *move it to the front*, and when it does that, the movement itself carries meaning. Putting a word first is how Arabic raises its voice. It is emphasis, and very often it is **restriction** — a quiet "only."

Think of how you would say it in English if you were correcting someone. If a man stood on the dock, proud of the vessel he financed and built, and said "this is *my* ship," and you wanted to gently turn his face — you would not say "the ship belongs to Allah." You would say: "**To Him** that ship belongs." You front the words. You put the ownership before the object. The stress lands on the *to Him*.

That is exactly what the ayah does. It does not say *al-jawāri lahu* — "the ships are His." It says **wa lahu'l-jawār** — "and to *Him* belong the ships." The little phrase *lahu*, "to Him," is lifted up and placed before the ships themselves.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the weight of where this falls. Of everything in the surah's list, this is the object that most tempts a human being to say "mine." You did not build the moon, so you will not claim it. But the ship? You sweated over the ship. You know the man who cut the timber. You remember the cost. The ship is the proudest thing on the list that a human hand can point to and say: *I made this.*

And right there — at the exact place where human ownership feels most earned — Allah moves the *lahu* to the front. **To Him.**

Let's sit with what is actually being claimed here, because it is more precise than it first sounds. Allah is not denying that you built the ship. The surah is not erasing human craft. Notice what is being claimed as His: not the carpentry. The **floating**.

Here is what I mean. You can cut every plank. You can hammer every nail. You can raise the mast and load the cargo with your own arms. And then you push it into the water — and at that moment, the one thing that matters most is entirely out of your hands. **Will it float?** The buoyancy. The agreement between the iron and the water that the iron will not sink. That law — that the displaced water pushes back up with exactly enough force to hold a hundred tons on its surface — *you did not write that law and you cannot suspend it.* You built the shape. He gave it the sea that holds the shape up.

So when Allah fronts *lahu* — "to Him" — over the ship, He is reaching past the part you made to the part you only borrowed. You own the wood. **He owns the holding-up.**

And now read the absence. The ayah never mentions the builder. Arabic could so easily have said "the ships *you raised*" or "the ships *you sail*." The human hand that everyone knows is there — is simply not named. It is left out. And that omission is not an oversight; it is the whole argument. At the one place you most expect your own name to appear, your name is gone, and only *His* possession is written.

[PAUSE]

One word reveals this: **lahu, moved to the front.** Over the one thing you were sure was yours.

---

### Element Two: "ٱلْجَوَارِ" — They Do Not Sail. They Run.

Now look at what the ships are *called*.

Allah does not use the flat word for "ship," *safīnah* or *fulk*. He calls them **al-jawār** — and to feel this word, you have to know its root.

Let me teach the root first, on its own, away from ships. The root is **j-r-y**, and its core meaning is *to run, to flow, to course*. It is the verb of moving water. When the Qur'an describes the rivers of Paradise, it says *tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhār* — rivers that **run** beneath. When it describes the sun crossing the sky, it says *wa'sh-shamsu tajrī li-mustaqarrin lahā* — the sun **runs** to its appointed resting place. The same root. Rivers run. The sun runs. Flow, course, the unbroken motion of something alive and going somewhere.

So *al-jawār* — the noun built from that root — does not mean "the floating things." It means **the running ones. The coursers.** Literally: the ships that *run*.

[PAUSE]

Notice what Arabic has done here. It has named the ship not by what it *is* — a hull, a vessel — but by what it *does*: it runs. And of all the verbs it could have borrowed to describe a thing of dead wood crossing the water, it borrowed the verb of **flowing water itself.**

Sit with that for a moment. The sea is water that runs. And the ship — the foreign, heavy, man-made thing set down upon the sea — is given the *same verb* as the water beneath it. The ship does not fight the sea. In the language, the ship *joins* it. It becomes a runner among the runners. The river runs, the sun runs, and now this vessel of iron runs with them, folded into the same coursing motion that holds the cosmos together.

Here is what is remarkable. We tend to imagine the ship as conquering the sea — man's defiance of the deep, his refusal to drown. But the Qur'an's word does not say *defiance*. It says **belonging**. The ship that survives the sea is not the one that fights the water but the one that has been let into the water's own motion — running where the running is allowed.

And remember where we just were. *To Him belong* these runners. The running itself — that coursing, flowing, alive motion that the ship borrows from the rivers and the sun — that, too, is His. He set the rivers running. He set the sun running. And He let your ship run.

One word reveals this: **al-jawār — the runners.** Your vessel was admitted into the motion of the rivers and the stars.

---

### Element Three: "ٱلْمُنشَـَٔاتُ" — The Raised, and the Raising

Now we reach the word that the classical reciters themselves heard two different ways — and both ways are true, and together they open the deepest door in the ayah.

The word is **al-munsha'āt**. Its root is **n-sh-'**, and I need you to hold this root carefully, because it is one of the great roots of the Qur'an. Its meaning is *to rise up, to grow, to be brought into being, to be raised*. It is the root used for a child *growing up*. It is the root used in *anshaʾakum* — "He **brought you into being**" (Surah Al-Anʿām). It is the root of *al-nashʾa al-ūlā*, "the first creation," and *al-nashʾa al-ākhira*, "the **second** raising" — the resurrection itself (Surah An-Najm 53:47).

So this is not a neutral shipbuilding word. *N-sh-'* is the root of **being raised into existence.**

The form here is Form IV — *anshaʾa* — which here carries its originative force: not merely "to grow" but "to **bring** into being, to raise up, to originate." Plank set upon plank until a thing that was not, *is*.

Now — the reciters. As al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī both preserve, the majority read it **munsha'āt**, with a fatḥa — a *passive* participle: "the ones that are **raised up**, the ones **brought into being**." But Ḥamza and Abū Bakr read it **munshi'āt**, with a kasra — an *active* participle: "the ones that **do** the raising, the ones that **initiate** and set the journey in motion."

Do not treat these as two competing options where you must pick a winner. Treat them as two camera angles on a single object — the way the early scholars treated them. Let both meanings stack.

[PAUSE]

So gather the layers the tradition itself gathered:

Qatāda heard *al-makhlūqāt* — "the **created** ones," fashioned for the running, brought into being for this very purpose. Mujāhid heard the **raised sail** — the ships whose sails are *hoisted up*; "and if its sail is not raised," he said, "it is not among the *munsha'āt*." Al-Akhfash heard *al-mujriyāt* — "the ones **made to run.**" And the second reading, *munshi'āt*, adds: the ones that **rise and initiate the voyage** themselves.

Now hold all of it at once, because the word holds all of it at once:

A ship is **raised** — built up, plank lifted upon plank, lumber that lay flat on the ground now standing as a hull. A ship is **raised** again — its sail hoisted toward the sky to catch the breath of the wind. A ship is **brought into being** — originated, created for the running. And the ship **rises and runs** — it sets out, it initiates, it courses across the water.

Raised. Raised. Brought into being. And running.

[PAUSE]

Now — bring back the root. *N-sh-'*. The root of *anshaʾakum* — "**He brought you into being.**" The root of *al-nashʾa al-ākhira* — "the **second raising**," when the dead are lifted from the earth.

Do you see what the word has quietly done?

It has described the ship in the exact vocabulary it uses to describe **you**. You were *munshaʾ* — raised into being from a clay He shaped, lifted from nothing into a standing, breathing form, hoisted up out of non-existence the way a hull is hoisted up out of scattered wood. You were *brought into being for a running* — set down on a sea you did not make, given a span of motion across it. And one day you will be *munshaʾ* a second time — *al-nashʾa al-ākhira* — raised again from the water of the grave.

The ship is not just a favor on the list. **The ship is a portrait of the one looking at it.**

One word reveals this: **al-munsha'āt — the raised ones.** Raised into being, raised toward the sky, running across a sea toward a shore — described in the very root by which He describes the raising of your soul.

---

### Element Four: "كَٱلْأَعْلَـٰمِ" — The Mountains That Move

And then the ayah ends with an image that should stop you, if you let it land plainly.

**Ka'l-aʿlām** — "like mountains."

The classical commentators are unanimous on the surface meaning. Al-Jalālayn: *ka'l-jibāl* — like mountains, in size and height. Al-Baghawī: *al-ʿalam* is the **tall mountain**, and the ships on the sea are likened to mountains. These great ships, sails raised, sit upon the water as high and as heavy as mountains.

But feel the choice. Of all the things Allah could compare a moving ship to — a bird, a cloud, a horse, anything that *moves* — He chose the one thing in all creation that is the very **symbol of not-moving.** A mountain. The Qur'an itself calls the mountains *awtād* — pegs, stakes driven into the earth to hold it steady (Surah An-Naba'). A mountain is the picture of permanence. Immovability. The thing that was there before you and will be there after you, that does not shift, does not travel, does not yield.

And Allah sets that immovable thing **on the surface of the sea — and lets it run.**

[PAUSE]

A mountain that moves. A peak that travels. The heaviest, most fixed, most earthbound image we possess — lifted onto water and sent coursing across it like a river.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bi'l-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing to an unseen reality through a thing your eyes can hold. Because what is the lesson sitting inside a floating mountain? It is this: **the laws of weight that you take as iron, that you build your certainties upon, are themselves held in a hand.** "Mountains do not float" is one of the most obvious truths you own. And Allah suspends it before your eyes, every day, in every harbor, and asks you — through the refrain that beats through this entire surah — *fa-bi'ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*: "So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"

There is one more thread in the word, gently. The root of *ʿalam* — *ʿ-l-m* — is the root of *ʿalāmah*, a **sign, a marker, a landmark.** A mountain is called *ʿalam* in part because travelers *navigate by it* — it is the fixed point on the horizon by which you find your way. The primary sense here, as the tradition says, is mountain-in-size. But the resonance is worth a breath: the ship is "like a landmark." And it is itself a moving sign — a thing you were meant to look at and *read*. An *āyah*. The whole surah is asking you to read its signs and not deny them, and here is a sign so large it looks like a mountain, gliding past your shore.

One word reveals this: **ka'l-aʿlām — like mountains.** The immovable, set moving — so that you would know the laws you lean on are leaned on Him.

---

### Bridge

So look at what these four words have built, one upon the other.

**Lahu** — fronted, restrictive: the ownership of the one thing you were sure was yours, lifted away and given to Him. **Al-jawār** — the runners: your vessel admitted into the coursing motion of the rivers and the sun. **Al-munsha'āt** — the raised ones: described in the exact root by which He raised *you* into being and will raise you again. **Ka'l-aʿlām** — like mountains: the immovable set moving, the law of weight held open in His hand.

The construction has shown us a ship. Now let us ask what it reveals not just about a ship — but about reality itself, and about the one standing on the shore.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Things You Are Sure Are Yours

Begin where you actually live — not on a literal shore, but in your ordinary day.

You have things you built. Maybe it is a career you assembled brick by brick over fifteen years. Maybe it is a body you trained, a mind you sharpened, a reputation you earned, a family you raised, a bank balance that represents a thousand early mornings. These are your *ships*. The heavy, expensive, hard-won things that you cut and shaped and pushed into the water with your own hands. And of all the things in your life, *these* are the ones you most naturally point to and say: **mine.** I made this. The moon you will concede to God easily. Your achievements, your competence, your control — those you hold close.

Now here is the quiet, repeated experience of being a human being. You build the ship perfectly — and then something you did not control decides whether it floats.

You prepare for the interview flawlessly; an economy you did not design decides whether the company is hiring. You eat well, you train, you do everything right; a cell you cannot see decides whether the body holds. You pour twenty years into a child; a heart you cannot reach into decides what they become. **You built the hull. You never owned the buoyancy.**

[PAUSE]

This is the thing the modern soul is most desperate to deny, and most exhausted by denying. We have built a civilization on the promise that if you engineer the ship well enough, you control the floating. That competence is sovereignty. That the right system, the right plan, the right effort, *guarantees* the outcome. And then we lie awake at night, because somewhere underneath we know the secret the sea has always known: the holding-up was never ours.

And here is where this ayah does something I find almost tender. It does not shame you for building. The ship is on the list of *favors*, not sins. Allah is not telling you to stop building ships. He is telling you where to put the word "mine." **You own the wood. He owns the water.** And the moment you get that line in the right place — the carpentry is yours, the buoyancy is His — something unbearable lifts off your chest.

Because if the floating was yours, then every storm is your failure and every wave is your fault, and you must lie awake holding up a hundred tons by force of will. But if the floating is *His* — *wa lahu'l-jawār* — then your job was only ever the carpentry. Do the work. Cut the wood well. Raise the mast straight. And then push it into a sea that belongs to Someone who has been holding mountains on its surface since before you were born.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow? Tomorrow, do your work like a master carpenter — with full effort, full excellence, holding nothing back from the part that is actually yours. And then, at the water's edge, where the buoyancy begins — *let go of the part that was never yours.* The hand-off from your effort to His decree has a name in our tradition: it is *tawakkul*. Build the ship like everything depends on you. Launch it knowing the floating belongs to Him. That is not the death of effort. That is effort, finally, set free from the weight it was never meant to carry.

---

### Theme Two: You Are the Ship

Now let the word *al-munsha'āt* come back, because it has been waiting to tell you who the ship is.

Picture your own life from above, the way you would watch a vessel from the cliff. There was a moment when you were not. And then you were **raised** — *anshaʾakum* — lifted out of non-existence, plank upon plank, a hull assembled in a darkness you cannot remember. You were set down upon a sea you did not choose, on a day you did not pick, between shores you did not draw. And from that moment you have been **running** — *jāriyah* — coursing across the water, carried by winds you do not command, toward a harbor you cannot yet see.

You are the ship. Raised, launched, running, bound for a shore.

[PAUSE]

And the sea you are running across — that is this *dunya*, this life. Vast. Beautiful. Utterly indifferent to whether you sink. It will not pause for your grief. It will not still itself for your fear. It is the oldest picture of the thing that is bigger than us — and you are a small raised thing moving across its face.

So here is the question the word presses into you: **what keeps a ship safe on the sea?**

Not its weight — weight is what drowns. Not its hardness — the sea breaks harder things than wood. We saw it already in *al-jawār*: the ship survives not by fighting the water but by being let into the water's own motion. **Running where the running is permitted.** A ship that tries to go where the sea will not carry it — that turns broadside to the wave, that insists on its own direction against the current of how things actually are — that ship is lost. The vessel that lasts is the one whose running has been folded into a larger running. The one moving *with* the deep order, not against it.

And what is that, for a soul? It is the *ṣirāṭ* — the straight path. It is *islām* — the surrender that is not defeat but *seamanship*. To live in surrender is not to lie flat and let the sea do what it wills. It is to set your sail to the wind He sends, to run in the lane He opened, to let your small motion be taken up into the vast lawful motion of His decree — the same motion that runs the rivers and the sun. The surrendered soul is not the drowned soul. It is the **sailing** soul. The one running *like a mountain* — heavy with cargo, high above the water, and held.

[PAUSE]

And then — the last layer of the root, the one that turns the whole image toward home. *N-sh-'* is also the root of *al-nashʾa al-ākhira* — the **second raising.** You were raised once into this life. The voyage has an end. Every ship on every sea is, whether it knows it or not, *running toward a harbor* — and the running is not the point. **The docking is the point.** No ship was built to stay on the water forever. It was raised to *arrive*.

So the question is not only *am I running?* You are running; you cannot help it; the sea carries every vessel forward whether it steers or not. The question is: **toward which shore have I set my sail?** Because the same root that raised you the first time will raise you the second — *al-nashʾa al-ākhira* — and on that day the only thing that will matter about the whole voyage is which harbor you were running toward all along.

What does this change about tomorrow? Tomorrow, stop asking only "am I moving fast enough?" — the sea guarantees the motion. Ask instead: "is my sail set toward the harbor I actually want to arrive at?" Pick one lane of your life where you have been running broadside to His order — fighting the current of how He has told you things go — and turn the hull. Run *with* the deep order. Let your small motion be taken up into His. That is what it means to be a raised thing that arrives, and not merely a raised thing that drifts.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing the Two Directions Together

We came at this ayah from two sides, and I want you to see now that they were always one insight.

From the **words**, we learned: the ownership is His (*lahu*, fronted over the one thing you were sure was yours); the motion is His (*al-jawār*, your vessel let into the running of the rivers and the sun); the raising is His (*al-munsha'āt*, the root by which He brings souls into being); and the laws you lean on are His (*ka'l-aʿlām*, the mountain He set moving on the water).

From the **themes**, we learned: you built the hull but never owned the buoyancy — so do the carpentry and hand Him the floating. And: *you are the ship* — raised, running, bound for a harbor, kept safe not by fighting the sea but by surrendering into its lawful motion, until the second raising brings you in to dock.

Two directions. One thing. **You are a raised vessel running across a sea that belongs to Him, and your peace is not in owning the water but in trusting the One who holds the mountains on its surface.** The linguistic journey and the human journey were the same journey the whole time. That is the proof in the architecture: the word He chose to name the ship is the word He uses to name the raising of your soul. He did not compare you to the ship. He named the ship with *your* name.

---

### Questions to Carry

1. What is the "ship" in your life — the hard-won thing you most quietly call *mine*? And can you name, precisely, the part of it that is the carpentry (yours) and the part that is the buoyancy (His)?

2. Where are you running *broadside to the wave* — insisting on a direction against the current of how Allah has told you things actually go? What would it mean to turn the hull and run *with* the deep order instead?

3. When you lie awake holding up a hundred tons by force of will — which weight is the carpentry you are responsible for, and which is the floating you were never meant to carry?

4. If every ship is running toward a harbor whether it steers or not — toward which shore is your sail actually set? Not the one you would name out loud. The one your daily running is quietly carrying you toward.

5. You were raised once. When you let yourself remember that you will be raised again — *al-nashʾa al-ākhira* — does it change which harbor suddenly seems worth steering for?

---

### One-Sentence Distillation

You build the ship, but the floating belongs to Him — and you yourself are a raised vessel running across the sea of this life like a mountain that should sink, held up the whole way by the same hand, bound for a shore where you will be raised a second time.

---

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this asks you to stop building. Build your ships well — your work, your body, your family, your life — with the full excellence of a master carpenter who holds nothing back from the part that is genuinely his.

The shift is smaller and deeper than effort. It is *where you put the word "mine."* On the wood — yes, all of it, work it with everything you have. On the water — let go. The buoyancy was always His. The running was always folded into His running. The raising was always His, the first time and the last.

And so the next time you stand at any shore — a literal one, or the shore of some outcome you cannot control — and you see a thing too heavy to float moving steadily across water too deep to stand in, let the surah's old refrain rise in you like the tide it is: *fa-bi'ayyi ālā'i rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*. **So which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?** Not the mountain that runs. And not the small raised vessel — *you* — that He has been holding above the deep since the day He brought you into being.

---

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, to You belong the ships and to You belongs the sea, and to You belongs this small raised vessel that I am. You built me, plank upon plank, out of a nothing I cannot remember, and set me running across waters I did not choose. Let me work the wood with all my strength and hand You the floating without fear. Set my sail toward the harbor that is You, keep me running in the lane Your mercy opened, and when You raise me the second time, let me arrive — held, the whole way, by the hand that holds the mountains on the water. *Āmīn.*
