# Which One Would You Call a Lie?

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah first. Four words. Listen to them before we say anything about them.

> [ayah:55:47] فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

If you have spent any time with Surah Ar-Rahman, you already know this line. You know it the way you know the chorus of a song — it comes back, and back, and back. Thirty-one times across this single surah, Allah returns to this exact sentence. After He mentions the sun and the moon held to a reckoning — the question. After the stars and the trees that bow — the question. After He sets down the scale of justice and forbids us to cheat it — the question. After the two seas that meet and do not overflow into each other, after the pearl and the coral, after the ships raised like mountains on the water — every time, the same words: *So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

### The world this came into

To feel why this surah lands the way it does, you have to picture its first audience.

A narration cited by al-Qurtubī tells us this surah was recited not only to the people of Makkah but, in its address, to a second audience entirely — and we'll come to who they are. But hold the human scene first. These were people for whom the cosmos was not yet "explained." The sun was not a fusion reaction to them; it was a presence they woke to and lived under. The sea was not a body of saltwater on a map; it was a terror and a provider, a thing that carried their ships and could swallow them. The night sky was not light-pollution-washed; it was a ceiling of fire, close enough to touch.

And into that world comes a surah that does something almost unbearable in its tenderness. It does not open with a command. It does not open with a threat. It opens with a Name — **Ar-Raḥmān**, The Most Merciful — and then it begins to *list*. It teaches the Qurʾān. It creates the human being. It teaches him speech. The sun, the moon, the star, the tree, the sky, the balance, the earth, the fruit, the grain, the fragrant herb...

It is a surah that reads like someone opening box after box of gifts in front of you. And every few boxes, the Giver stops, looks at you, and asks one quiet question.

### Where we are standing

Our ayah — 55:47 — sits at a particular hinge. Just before it, in ayah 46, Allah has said: *"And for the one who fears the standing before his Lord are two gardens."* He has just turned a corner. The surah has been warning the criminals, describing the Day they will be seized. And now He pivots toward the people of *taqwā* — those who feared standing before Him — and He promises them gardens. Then, before He even describes those gardens, before He tells us about their branches and springs and fruits in the ayahs that follow, He stops.

And He asks it again.

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to sit with before we go a single step further.

We are trained, all of us, to read past a refrain. A repeated line feels like a chorus, like decoration, like the part you can hum without thinking. Our minds are built to discount repetition — to hear something the sixteenth time and register *nothing*.

But this is the speech of the One who does not waste a syllable. He could have said it once. He chose to say it thirty-one times. [PAUSE]

So the question is not only *what does this sentence mean.* The question is: **why would the Most Merciful ask you the same thing thirty-one times — and why would He phrase it not as "do you deny," but as "which one"?** As if He is handing you the entire catalogue of existence and saying: *point. Point to the one you'll call a lie.*

Let's find out.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We have three things to open in this ayah. A word He chose for "favors" when a simpler word was available. A grammatical number that most languages don't even have. And a verb that is far more violent than "deny" lets on.

### Element One: *Ālāʾ* — the word He reached for

#### First, the concept

In any language, a writer choosing a word is also choosing the words he *didn't* use. When you say someone is "thin," you've decided against "slender," against "frail," against "skeletal." Each had a different temperature. The choice tells you something.

Arabic has a very common, very ordinary word for blessings or favors: *niʿma*, plural *niʿam*. It's the everyday word. It's the word al-Muyassar actually uses to *explain* this ayah — he glosses it as *niʿam rabbikum*, the favors of your Lord. It's the word you'd reach for in conversation.

But that is not the word Allah used here.

#### Open the word

He used **ءَالَآءِ — *ālāʾ*.**

Say it slowly. *Ā-lāʾ.* Notice what your mouth does. It opens. The long *alif*, the gentle hamza, the second long vowel — the word is almost entirely open air and flow. There is nothing clipped about it. Compared to the tight, closed *niʿam*, the word *ālāʾ* spills.

#### Let's sit with this

*Ālāʾ* is the rarer, weightier word. The lexicographers — al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt*, the compilers of *Lisān al-ʿArab* — gloss it simply as *niʿam*, favors. So at the level of dictionary meaning, yes, it means blessings. But notice: it is a **plural with no singular anyone agrees on.** The classical grammarians offer four or five candidate singulars for it — *ilā*, *alan*, *ilan*, *ulā* — and they don't settle. 

Stay with that strangeness for a moment. Here is a word for "favors" whose singular has almost been *lost* — a word that seems to exist most naturally in the plural, as a mass, a flood, a heap. You can barely isolate *one* of them. The word itself resists being counted down to a single unit.

And what is the surah doing? **Pouring out favors faster than you can count them.** Sun, moon, star, tree, sky, scale, earth, fruit, grain, herb. The word He chose for these is a word that, by its very grammar, comes in abundance and resists singularity.

#### Here's what's remarkable

There is a verse elsewhere in the Qurʾān — *"And if you should count the favor of Allah, you could not enumerate it"* (16:18). That is the *meaning* baked into the very shape of *ālāʾ*. He didn't only say the favors are uncountable in another surah. Here He chose a *word for favors that is itself almost uncountable* — a plural whose singular slips through your fingers.

So when He then asks *"which of the ālāʾ will you deny,"* He is using a word that already tells you the answer is impossible to give. You cannot point to *one*, because the word He used doesn't break cleanly into ones. He has stacked the question against you with the vocabulary itself. [PAUSE]

That is the first thing this single word reveals: **the favors are not a list you could finish denying even if you tried. The word for them was chosen to overflow your counting.**

### Element Two: The dual — *Rabbikumā*, *Tukadhdhibān* — the "you two"

#### First, the concept

Here is something English simply cannot do, and it changes how we read this entire surah.

In English we have singular and plural. One, or more-than-one. "You." That's it — the same word whether I'm speaking to one person or a stadium.

Arabic has a third category, sitting precisely between them: **the dual.** A distinct grammatical form for *exactly two*. Not one. Not "many." Two. It has its own verb endings, its own pronouns. When Arabic wants to address precisely a pair, it has a dedicated grammatical shape to do it, and that shape is unmistakable.

#### Open the feature

Now look at our ayah. Look at the endings.

*Rabbi-**kumā*** — your Lord. Not *rabbikum* (your Lord, to a crowd). *Rabbi-kumā* — **the Lord of you two.**

*Tukadhdhib-**ān*** — will you deny. Not *tukadhdhibūn* (will you-all deny). *Tukadhdhib-ān* — **will you two deny.**

The entire surah is built on this dual. Over and over: *kumā... ān... kumā... ān.* Allah is not addressing a crowd. He is addressing, with surgical grammatical precision, **two.**

#### Let's sit with this — who are the two?

This is where al-Qurtubī gives us the answer of the majority. He says this address is to **al-ins wa al-jinn** — humankind *and* the jinn. The two weighty species, *al-thaqalān*, as the surah itself names them later: *"We will attend to you, O two weighty ones"* (55:31). And later still: *"O company of jinn and mankind"* (55:33). The surah tells us, in its own words, who the "two" are.

Al-Qurtubī notes the textual fingerprints: earlier the surah said *"He created the human"* (55:3) and *"He created the jānn"* (55:15) — both creations named. And he cites a narration reported by al-Tirmidhī — an account in which the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah to the jinn, and they responded *better* than the people had: every time he reached *"so which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny,"* they answered, "Not one of Your favors, our Lord, do we deny — all praise to You." (I'll name it as a narration al-Qurtubī relays; treat it as the tradition reports it, not as my own certification of its chain.)

#### Here's what's remarkable

Two entire orders of created beings — beings made of clay and beings made of fire, beings you can see and beings you cannot — and Allah does not address them as an undifferentiated mass. He uses the dual. The grammar of *exactly two.*

Why does that matter to *you*, sitting here, who are only one of those two species?

Because the dual refuses to let you hide in a crowd. [PAUSE]

A plural — "O people" — lets you disappear into the multitude. You can always think: *He means them, broadly, the masses, society.* But the dual is narrow. It points. And when you, a human being, hear *"the favors of the Lord of you two,"* you realize there is no large anonymous "everyone" being addressed. There is your species — and there is one other. You are *half* of the address. The spotlight is split two ways and one of those ways is squarely on you.

The dual is intimacy disguised as scope. It feels cosmic — it's addressing all jinn and all mankind — but its *grammar* is the grammar you'd use for two people standing in front of you. He has made the whole of creation feel like a private conversation between Him and you (and one other). 

That is what the dual reveals: **you are not in the audience for this question. You are one of the only two people in the room.**

### Element Three: *Tukadhdhibān* — the verb is not "deny," it is "call-it-a-lie"

#### First, the concept

Arabic verbs are built on roots — usually three consonants carrying a core meaning — and then poured into *forms*, patterns that bend that core meaning in specific directions. The simplest is Form I: the bare action. But there are nine more forms, and each one *does something* to the root.

Form II — you make it by doubling the middle consonant — typically takes a simple action and makes it intensive, or makes it *declarative*: not just *to do* X, but to *pronounce* something to be X, to actively render the verdict.

Let me give you an everyday example. The root *ṣ-d-q* means "to be true." In Form II, *ṣaddaqa*, it means "to declare something true, to actively believe it, to ratify it." You're not just being truthful — you are issuing a verdict of truth over something.

Hold that. Because our verb is the exact opposite verdict.

#### Open the word

The root here is **k-dh-b** — to lie. In Form I, *kadhaba*, it just means: to lie, to speak falsely.

But the ayah does not use Form I. It uses **Form II: *tukadhdhibān*** — the middle letter doubled, *kadh-dha-ba.* And Form II of this root does not mean "to lie." It means **takdhīb**: *to declare something a lie. To pronounce it false. To look at a thing and issue the verdict: that is a lie.*

#### Let's sit with the difference

This is everything. Hear the gap between two readings:

"So which of the favors of your Lord will you *fail to notice*?" — that would be passive. A lapse. Forgetfulness.

That is **not** what the ayah says.

The ayah says: "So which of the favors of your Lord will you **pronounce to be a lie**?" — and that is an *act*. A verdict. A judge banging a gavel. *Takdhīb* is not the quiet sin of taking things for granted. It is the loud sin of looking at a gift, in the face of the Giver, and saying: *false. I reject it. I call it a lie.*

And now feel the *sound* of the word. *Tu-kadh-dhi-bān.* That doubled letter — the *shadda* on the *dhāl* — makes the tongue strike twice, hard, against the same spot. After the open flowing *ālāʾ*, this word is a clenched fist. The favors flow open; the denial strikes shut. The very phonetics enact the contrast: **the mercy spills, the rejection clamps.** [PAUSE]

#### Here's what's remarkable — the interrogative

And now lay the last piece on top: the word *bi-ayyi* — **"which?"**

He did not ask, *"Will you deny the favors of your Lord?"* That's a yes-or-no question. You could answer "no" and move on, comfortable.

He asked: **"WHICH one will you call a lie?"**

That is a question that *presumes the gift and demands you specify your target.* It hands you the whole inventory of existence — the sun, the air filling your lungs right now, the water, the speech in your mouth, the two gardens just promised — and says: *go ahead. Point. Name the one. Which of these, specifically, is the lie?*

There is no honest finger to point. That is the trap, and it is a merciful trap. The question is designed so that the only truthful answer is silence — and then surrender. The jinn, in that narration, understood exactly this. They didn't argue. They said: *not one, our Lord. Not one.*

So the third element reveals: **the sin in view is not forgetting — it is the active verdict of rejection. And the question is shaped so that no specific gift can survive being named as the lie. You are being asked to find a defendant, and there is none.**

---

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have built together. He chose *ālāʾ* — a word for favors that overflows counting. He cast the whole question in the **dual** — so that you cannot hide in any crowd; you are one of the only two addressed. And He used *takdhīb* with *"which"* — turning the question into a demand to name a single gift you'd dare pronounce false, when no such gift exists.

The grammar has cornered you. Gently, but completely.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way the human heart actually works, and what this question is really doing to it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Why He asks thirty-one times — and why repetition is the mercy, not the monotony

#### Start where you live

Let me start nowhere near the Qurʾān. Let me start with your phone.

The first time you held a phone that fit the whole of human knowledge in your palm, that let you see the face of someone a continent away, that carried a thousand books — do you remember the wonder? Of course you don't, not really. Because here is the most documented fact about the human mind: **we adapt to everything.** Psychologists call it *hedonic adaptation*. Whatever astonishes us on Monday is invisible by Friday. The new car becomes "the car." The miracle becomes the baseline. The gift becomes furniture.

This is not a moral failing you happen to have. It is the default operating system of every human nervous system. We are *built* to stop seeing what is constantly present. Your own heartbeat, right now — you weren't feeling it until I said the word. It never stopped. You stopped *registering* it.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Now hear the refrain again, with that in mind.

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

And again. *So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

And again. Thirty-one times.

We assumed, at the start, that the repetition was a chorus. It is not a chorus. **It is a corrective for hedonic adaptation.** [PAUSE]

Allah, who built that nervous system, knows exactly how it fails. He knows that if He listed His favors once — sun, moon, sea, fruit — you would adapt by the third line. The sun would become "the sun." So He does not let the list run uninterrupted. He breaks it, every few favors, and drags your attention *back to the Giver.* The repetition is not Him being unable to move on. The repetition is Him refusing to let *you* move on — refusing to let the gifts blur into background.

Every single time the refrain returns, it is a hand on your shoulder turning your face back toward the One giving. It is the antidote, administered thirty-one times, to the one disease He knows you have.

#### Build it further

And notice *where* this particular repetition — our ayah 47 — lands. It comes right after *"for the one who fears the standing before his Lord are two gardens"* (46), and right *before* those gardens are described (48 onward).

So He promises Paradise. Then — before He even shows it to you — He stops and asks the question *again.*

Why interrupt your own good news? Because even Paradise can become a thing you adapt to in the *anticipating.* Even the promise of gardens can become a line you skim. So He plants the refrain right at the threshold of the reward: *before you walk into this garden I am describing — name the favor you would call a lie.* He makes you arrive at Paradise through the door of gratitude. You don't get to receive the next gift until you've reckoned with the last one.

#### What this changes tomorrow

Here is the practical edge of it. The refrain is teaching you a *rhythm*, and the rhythm is portable. The lesson is: **insert the question manually, because your nervous system won't insert it for you.**

The favors are flowing whether or not you notice — that's settled. What's not settled is whether you'll *register* them. So you do for yourself what the surah does for its reader: you interrupt the stream. Between the gift of waking and the gift of the first breath of the day — insert the question. Between one bite and the next — insert the question. Not "do I have favors" (you'll skim past that) but the surah's sharper form: *which of these, exactly, would I dare call a lie?* And watch the honest answer — *not one* — pull your face back toward the Giver, just as it was built to.

### Theme Two: Denial is an exertion — and the question that won't condemn you

#### Start where you live

Think about the last time you were ungrateful. Not cruel — just ungrateful. The meal was fine and you complained about the salt. The day was safe and whole and you fixated on the one email. The body carried you through everything you asked of it and you cursed it for one ache.

Now — be honest about what that ingratitude *felt* like from the inside. It did not feel like an act. It felt like nothing. It felt passive, automatic, like water finding the low ground. You didn't *decide* to overlook the thousand working things and lock onto the one broken thing. It just... happened. That's the experience of ingratitude: it feels like an absence, a not-doing.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

And here is where the verb we opened earlier returns to indict us — gently, but precisely.

The Qurʾān does not call it *forgetting*. It uses *takdhīb* — Form II — **the active verdict.** *To pronounce it a lie.*

Stay with how strange that is. From the inside, ingratitude feels like nothing — like a lapse. But Allah names it as a *verdict*. As though every overlooked favor is not a thing you failed to see, but a thing you actively *ruled false.* [PAUSE]

What is He telling us about ourselves? He is telling us that **there is no neutral ground in front of a favor.** We imagine three options: deny it, thank it, or just... not-think-about-it. The third feels safe and empty. But the ayah collapses the third into the first. To receive a gift and turn from the Giver is not a blank — it is, in the ledger of reality, a *kadhdhabtu*: I called it false. Indifference is not the absence of a verdict. Indifference *is* the verdict. The clenched, doubled-letter slam of *tukadhdhibān* is exactly the sound of a thing we experience as effortless drift.

#### Build it — but now feel the mercy in the form of the question

If that were all, this would be terrifying. The ayah would be a charge sheet.

But return to *how He asks.* He does not say: *"You have denied the favors of your Lord."* — a statement, a conviction, gavel down.

He asks: *"So **which** of the favors of your Lord will you deny?"* — and an open question is not a verdict. **It is an invitation to defend yourself that He already knows you cannot lose.** [PAUSE]

Think about what a question does that a statement does not. A statement closes. A question *opens* — it hands the floor to the one being asked. By phrasing your own ingratitude as a question put *to you*, Allah is doing something astonishing: He is letting *you* be the one to discover that there is no favor you can honestly name as a lie. He is not forcing the conclusion down. He is walking you to the edge of it and letting you look over yourself.

This is the mercy hidden in the grammar. The God who could have condemned the two weighty species in a single declarative sentence instead *asks them a question* — thirty-one times — each time leaving the door open for them to answer rightly. And the jinn, in that narration, walked through it: *not one, our Lord, do we deny.*

He did not want their conviction. He wanted their confession. And a confession can only come through a door left open.

#### What this changes tomorrow

So the practice here is not to flog yourself for ingratitude as a *crime*. It is to catch the moment of drift — the salt, the email, the ache — and recognize it for what the ayah says it is: a small verdict of *false* issued against a real gift. And then to do what the open question invites: answer it. Out loud if you can. *Which of these would I call a lie? Not this breath. Not this sight. Not this name I can still speak. Not one, my Lord.*

You will find that you cannot complete the denial. That is not your strength. That is the question doing to you exactly what it was built to do.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed

Now stand back and see the shape of the whole thing.

Surah Ar-Rahman is built like a colonnade — pillars of blessing, and between every cluster of pillars, the same arch: *so which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?* The refrain is the architecture. It is the load-bearing repetition that holds the whole surah up. Take it out and the surah collapses into a flat list. Leave it in and the list becomes a *conversation* — gift, question, gift, question — the Giver pausing after each offering to look you in the eye.

And our ayah, 47, is placed at the most deliberate possible spot: the seam where warning becomes reward, the threshold of the two gardens. He builds the arch *right there*, at the door of Paradise, so that the last thing you do before entering the description of the reward is answer the question of the gift. Gratitude is the doorway. There is no other way in.

### Bringing it together

Watch how the two halves of this reflection turn out to be one insight seen from two sides.

The **linguistic** journey showed us that He cornered us deliberately: a word for favors too vast to count (*ālāʾ*), a grammatical dual that won't let us hide in a crowd, and a question — *"which?"* — demanding we name a gift we'd call false, when none exists.

The **thematic** journey showed us *why* He would do that: because our nervous system erases what's constant (so He repeats the question thirty-one times), and because our ingratitude *feels* like nothing while in reality it is a verdict (so He names it *takdhīb* — and then, mercifully, returns it to us as a question instead of a sentence).

They are the same thing. **The cornering is the mercy.** He builds a question you cannot answer wrongly, and asks it again and again, precisely *because* He does not want you to fall — He wants you, like the jinn, to arrive at the only true answer yourself: *not one, my Lord. Not one.*

### Questions to carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. If I had to *name out loud* the one favor of my Lord I would call a lie — what would I say? And how long can I hold that name before it dissolves?
2. Which gift in my life has quietly become "furniture" — present, working, and completely invisible to me?
3. The favors flow whether I notice or not. What is it costing *me* — not Him — that I have stopped registering them?
4. When I am ungrateful, do I experience it as a *verdict* I am issuing? What changes if I do?
5. He asks rather than condemns. Where in my life have I mistaken His open question for His silence?

### One-sentence distillation

Before He will even show you Paradise, the Most Merciful stops and asks you — you, one of only two species in the room — to name a single favor of the Lord who raised you that you would dare call a lie; and the fact that He *asks* instead of condemns is itself the favor you cannot deny.

### The closing invitation

You don't have to manufacture gratitude. You don't have to *feel* a wave of it on command. You only have to do what the surah trains you to do: **interrupt the stream and answer the question.** Let the refrain become a rhythm in you — a hand on your own shoulder, turning your face back to the Giver, thirty-one times a day if that's what it takes. The answer is already in you. He built the question so that you'd find it.

### A brief duʿāʾ

*O Lord of the two weighty ones — Lord who poured out favors faster than I could ever count them — do not let my heart issue against Your gifts the verdict of a lie. When I drift into forgetting, turn my face back, as You turn it back in every line of this surah. And let the only word I have for Your favors be the word the jinn found before me: not one, my Lord. Not one do I deny. All praise belongs to You.*
