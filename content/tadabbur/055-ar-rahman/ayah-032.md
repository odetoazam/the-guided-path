# The Question You Cannot Answer With "Yes"

## Surah Ar-Rahman, Ayah 32

> فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ
>
> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*
>
> **"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Verse You Already Know

Let me begin by admitting something. Of all the verses in the Qur'an, this might be the one you have heard the most without ever stopping on it.

Thirty-one times in a single surah, this sentence returns. It comes back like a wave that has decided it will not stop coming back. If you have ever heard Surah Ar-Rahman recited — and almost every Muslim has — then this line is already living somewhere inside you, half-memorized, worn smooth by repetition. *Fabi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

And precisely *because* it repeats, we have stopped hearing it.

This is the strange tragedy of repetition. The thing said most often becomes the thing noticed least. A wedding ring stops being felt on the finger. A photograph on the wall becomes invisible by the third week. And a verse recited thirty-one times in one sitting becomes, by the tenth time, a kind of beautiful background music — a refrain we sway to without listening.

So here is what I want us to do tonight. I want us to take *one* of these thirty-one occurrences. Just one. And I want us to slow it down so completely that it stops being music and becomes a question again — a real question, addressed to a real "you," demanding a real answer.

[PAUSE]

### Rebuilding the World

To feel this verse, you have to know *where it lands*. Because here is something most of us never noticed: the refrain does not always come after the same kind of thing.

Walk through Surah Ar-Rahman and watch where this question falls. After the sun and the moon swimming in their reckoned orbits — *which of the favors will you deny?* After the two seas meeting, after the pearls and the coral, after the gardens with their spreading branches and flowing springs and fruit hanging within reach — *which of the favors will you deny?* These make sense to us. A garden is a gift. A pearl is a gift. Of course we should not deny them.

But now come to verse 31. Listen to what Allah says right before *our* verse tonight:

> سَنَفْرُغُ لَكُمْ أَيُّهَ ٱلثَّقَلَانِ
>
> **"We will turn to attend to you, O you two weighty ones."**

This is not a garden. This is not a pearl. This is the announcement of the Reckoning. *Sanafrughu lakum* — "We shall free Ourselves for you," an idiom of a sovereign turning His full, undistracted attention toward you for the purpose of judgment. It is, by its tone, a warning. A shadow falling across the surah. The mood darkens. You brace.

And then — *immediately* — comes the refrain:

> **"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the collision? Allah has just promised to bring you to account — and He calls it an *ālāʾ*. A **favor.** A blessing. He frames the coming of Judgment Day itself as one more item on the list of gifts you should be ashamed to deny.

That is the hook. That is the thing I could not get past when I sat with this verse. Everywhere else the refrain crowns a mercy we can see — a fruit, a fountain, a star. Here it crowns *the day you will stand and answer for your life* — and insists that this, too, is a kindness.

How? How is the Reckoning a favor?

Hold that question. We are going to earn the answer slowly. But first, we have to open the words — because the words have been waiting this whole time to tell us something we walked past thirty-one times.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: آلاء — The Word That Only Comes in Multitudes

Let me start with a small puzzle about language.

In English, some words refuse to be singular. You don't have one "scissor." You don't own a single "trouser." You can't experience one "suds." These words exist only in the plural, because the things they name simply do not occur alone. A single blade isn't scissors; scissors is the *meeting* of blades. The plural is built into the meaning.

Hold that idea. Because Allah did not say *fabi-ayyi niʿmati rabbikumā* — "which **blessing** of your Lord." The ordinary, familiar word for a blessing is *niʿma*, and the Qur'an uses it often. But here, in this refrain, the chosen word is **آلاء — ālāʾ.**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with this word.

*Ālāʾ* is a **plural.** Favors. Bounties. Gifts — in the multitude. And here is the remarkable thing the lexicographers wrestled with: its singular form is so rare, so contested, so barely-used, that scholars disagreed on what it even is. Some said the singular is *ilan*, some said *ilun*, some said *aluwwun* — they reached for it and could barely find it. The classical lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab among them) record the dispute precisely because the singular almost never appears in living speech.

Notice what that means. **There is, in practice, no singular here.** The word for Allah's favor, in this surah, is a word that only comes in crowds.

Stay with me, because this is not a grammatical curiosity. This is theology hiding inside morphology.

What is Allah teaching us by choosing the word whose singular has nearly vanished from the language? He is telling us that **His favors do not arrive one at a time.** You cannot isolate a single blessing of Allah, hold it up alone, and examine it in a vacuum — because no blessing of His ever came to you alone. The eye that reads is held by a face that is held by a neck by a spine by a heartbeat by an atmosphere by a sun at exactly its distance. Pull on any one *ālāʾ* and ten thousand others come up attached, like roots.

The very word refuses the lie we tell ourselves — the lie that we can be grateful for *this* while denying *that*. *Ālāʾ* says: you were never given a *this* without a *that*. They came braided together. They only come in multitudes.

[PAUSE]

And so the question — *which of the favors will you deny?* — already has a trap built into the noun. Because to deny one *ālāʾ* you would have to deny the whole braid it came in. There is no clean denial. There is no surgical ingratitude. The word itself has closed that door before the question even finishes.

That is what this single word reveals: **Allah's generosity is not a series of separate gifts but one seamless, unbroken flood — and the word for it cannot even be made singular.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: تُكَذِّبَان — Not "Disbelieve," But "Call It a Lie"

Now to the verb. And this is where the verse stops being gentle.

When we hear "deny," we tend to imagine something passive — a turning away, an overlooking, a forgetting. A man too busy to notice. That sounds almost innocent. *I didn't deny anything; I just wasn't paying attention.*

But look at the verb Allah actually chose: **تُكَذِّبَان — tukadhdhibān.**

Let me teach you the shape of this word, because the shape is the whole point.

The root is *k-dh-b*, which carries the meaning of *lying, falsehood*. The simplest form of this root, Form I, would be *kadhaba* — "he lied," "he spoke a falsehood." But that is not the form here. Here the verb is in what Arabic grammarians call **Form II** — built by doubling the middle letter, *kadhdhaba*. (The Leeds corpus confirms it: form II, imperfect, dual.)

And Form II does something specific. One of its core functions is what's called the **declarative** or **estimative** sense: not to *do* the action, but to **declare something to be** that thing, to **pronounce it** so, to **treat it as** so. *Kadhdhaba* does not mean "to lie." It means **"to declare something a lie."** **"To give the lie to."** **"To call the truth false to its face."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference. There is an ocean between *I overlooked your gift* and *I looked at your gift and called it a fraud.*

The first is forgetfulness. The second is **an accusation.**

So the verse is not asking, "Which favor did you happen to forget?" It is asking: **"Which of the favors of your Lord will you stand before and call a lie?"** Which of these gifts — the eyes, the seas, the sun in its orbit, the very Day He set aside to give you justice — which of them will you point to and say: *this is false, this is not really a kindness, this did not really come from Him?*

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the easy excuse. We like to think our ingratitude is innocent — a matter of being distracted, busy, tired. The verb forbids that comfort. In the logic of *takdhīb*, there is no neutral ground. To receive a gift you know is a gift, and live as though it weren't, is not absent-mindedness. It is, in the eyes of the verse, a kind of **calling-the-truth-a-liar.** An active verdict, rendered by your living, against a Giver you cannot honestly deny.

That is heavy. But hold it — because the heaviness is exactly what makes the way out so beautiful, and we will come to the way out.

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: رَبِّكُمَا and تُكَذِّبَان — The Relentless "You Two"

There is one more feature, and once you see it, you cannot un-see it in any recitation again.

Look at the two endings: *rabbi-**kumā**.* *Tukadhdhi-**bān**.* Both carry the **dual.**

Arabic, almost uniquely among the world's living languages, has a separate grammatical number for *exactly two* — not singular, not the general plural, but a dedicated form meaning **"you two," "the pair of you."** And this entire refrain, all thirty-one times, is locked in that dual. *Your* — but the *your* of two. *You deny* — but the *you* of two.

Who are the two?

The surah tells us, just one verse before, in verse 31: *ayyuha **al-thaqalān*** — "O you two **weighty ones.**" And two verses after, in verse 33, it names them outright: *yā maʿshara **al-jinni wa-l-ins*** — "O assembly of **jinn and mankind.**" The classical commentators are agreed on this — al-Qurtubi reports it as the position of the *jumhūr*, the overwhelming majority, and al-Muyassar states it plainly without qualification: the two weighty ones are humanity and jinn, the two creations charged with accountability. The two addressed throughout Ar-Rahman are **the two species of accountable creation: mankind and jinn.**

[PAUSE]

Why does this matter? Why not just say "O people"?

Because the dual does something the plural cannot. The general plural — "O all of you" — lets you hide in the crowd. You become a face in a sea of faces; the address dilutes; you can assume it's meant for someone else, the masses, *them*. But the dual is intimate and exposing. It is two. It is small enough to feel personal and large enough to leave you nowhere to stand.

And think about *which* two it gathers. It binds together **mankind and jinn** — the two creations made of utterly different stuff, fire and clay, the seen and the unseen, who share almost nothing. And what is the one thing they are made to share, the one bond strong enough to put them in the same grammatical pair? **They both received His favors. They will both be brought to account. And they are both capable of denial.**

The dual is saying: across every kind of creature that can choose, the situation is *identical*. There is no being with a mind and a will who stands outside this question. Wherever there is a recipient of gifts who can either be grateful or call them a lie — *this verse is talking to you. The pair of you. Personally.*

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these four words have built.

A noun — *ālāʾ* — that cannot even be made singular, because His favors only ever come in floods.

A verb — *tukadhdhibān* — that strips away the excuse of forgetfulness and names our ingratitude for what it is: a verdict, an accusation, calling the truth a lie.

And a number — the dual — that removes every crowd we might hide inside and stands us up, individually, alongside every other creature that can choose.

The grammar has assembled a courtroom. A flood of undeniable evidence, a charge that admits no innocent version, and a defendant who cannot blend into the masses.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the strange mercy of being made to stand in that courtroom at all.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Eye That Stops Seeing — and the Question That Makes It See Again

Let me start where you live.

There is a phenomenon every human being is subject to, and modern psychology has a name for it: **hedonic adaptation.** It means this — whatever you are given, you adjust to, and stop feeling.

The first morning after you recover from a long illness, you walk outside and the ordinary air feels like a miracle in your lungs. You think: *I will never take breathing for granted again.* Three days later, you have. The new house that thrilled you becomes simply "the house." The phone that felt like science fiction becomes the thing you're annoyed at for being slow. The spouse whose face once stopped your heart becomes the person you forget to look at over breakfast.

This is not a moral failing of bad people. It is the *default setting* of the human nervous system. We are built to notice **change**, not **state.** A blessing that arrives is felt; a blessing that stays becomes invisible. The eye stops seeing what is always there.

[PAUSE]

And here is the spiritual catastrophe hiding inside an ordinary psychological fact: **if we only feel blessings as they arrive and not as they remain, then the longer Allah keeps a gift flowing to us, the less we thank Him for it.** Gratitude, left to its nature, decays. The most constant mercies — the heartbeat, the eye, the breath working in your chest as you read these words — become precisely the ones we forget to notice. They have been present too long. They have become part of the furniture of existence.

[PAUSE]

This is why the refrain is not there to remind us what the gifts were. We already know what they are. The refrain is doing something more surgical: it is designed to interrupt the nervous system's automatic adjustment. Thirty-one times in one sitting, the same question rises and refuses to be answered passively. It does not let you settle back into comfortable blindness. It keeps asking. *Which one? Name one that is not a mercy. Try.*

And you cannot. Not honestly. The eyes that found these words were not your engineering. The neural pathways that allow you to understand language were not your achievement. The heartbeat sustaining you through this very sentence arrived before you had a name. You received everything before you could ask for anything.

[PAUSE]

Notice, too, what the question does *not* say. It does not say "remember the favors." That would be an assignment — something you complete and set aside. It asks: **which will you deny?** A challenge, not an instruction. The phrasing assumes you already know the favors are real. It only asks whether you will be honest enough to say so.

That asymmetry is precise. It does not invite you to build a gratitude list. It invites you to try — honestly — to make a case against gratitude, and discover you cannot.

The refrain is therefore not repetition for emphasis. It is repetition as *therapy* — thirty-one doses of the same antidote against the same disease. Every time the wave returns, the eye is forced to look again. Every time the question lands, the furniture disappears and the miracle reappears underneath it.

And here, after verse 31, the question performs its most demanding task: it arrives at the moment of maximum discomfort and asks whether even *that* — even the announcement of the Reckoning — is something you can honestly call a cruelty.

## Theme Two: The Two Sides of the Scale — Why Judgment Is an ālāʾ

Now we can return to the question I left suspended.

You remember it. How is the Reckoning a favor? How does *sanafrughu lakum* — We shall attend to you for judgment — earn the right to be crowned by the *ālāʾ* refrain?

Here is the answer the classical commentators give. And it is precise.

The early exegetes, reading verse 31 and what it announces, did not understand *sanafrughu lakum* as pure threat. Al-Muyassar states the meaning plainly: *We shall attend to your reckoning and your recompense for the deeds you performed in this world, O two weighty ones — mankind and jinn — punishing the disobedient and rewarding the obedient.* The Reckoning is not one-sided. It moves in both directions. And when you see it that way, the question the refrain is asking becomes entirely different.

[PAUSE]

Because the announcement of the Reckoning is not only the announcement that wrongdoers will be caught. It is equally, in the same breath, the announcement that **those who obeyed will be rewarded.**

Think about what that means for the fabric of every life.

Every prayer offered in the hours before anyone else was awake — it was registered. Every act of forgiveness extended to someone who did not deserve it — it is in the account. Every coin given in secret, every night of grief borne with patience, every time a parent absorbed a hardship silently so a child would not feel it — all of it exists somewhere, recorded in full, waiting.

Without the Reckoning, every one of those invisible acts evaporates. They happened; no one witnessed; they dissolve into the air as if they never occurred. The sincere and the cynical end the same way, with nothing left to distinguish them.

With the Reckoning, none of it disappears. The ledger is opened completely, and every act of faithfulness performed in secret is brought forward in full. *That* is the favor. Not only that sinners are caught — though justice for the wronged is itself a mercy — but that goodness, real goodness lived out in the dark when no audience was watching, **cannot go unacknowledged.** The One who turns His undivided attention to you on that Day is the same One who saw every silent act of faithfulness you performed when you thought no one noticed.

[PAUSE]

This reframes the announcement entirely. *Sanafrughu lakum* — We shall attend to you — is not a sovereign issuing a threat. It is a witness who missed nothing telling you: *the record is complete. Everything you did is here. Everything.* And for anyone who ever tried — who stumbled and rose, who gave more than was comfortable, who believed when believing was costly — that completeness is not a terror. It is the best thing that could be said.

The Reckoning is called an *ālāʾ* because it is the fulfillment of a promise that every act of goodness has weight. It is the moment when the world stops pretending that integrity and corruption end the same way. It is the scale finally, and permanently, set right.

[PAUSE]

This is why the refrain appears here, crowning the announcement of judgment, rather than only after the gardens and the pearls. The gardens are a mercy to the body. The Reckoning is a mercy to the soul that tried. They belong on the same list. They are both *ālāʾ* — multitudinous, braided, impossible to separate from everything else He has given.

## Theme Three: You Cannot Outrun This Attention — and Why That Is a Gift

There is one more layer, and it comes from looking at what the surah does immediately *after* our verse.

Verse 33 — which follows directly — throws down what reads at first as a strange challenge: *O assembly of jinn and mankind, if you are able to pass beyond the zones of the heavens and the earth, then pass — but you will never pass except with divine authority.* The invitation is given. The escape route is named. And then, in the same breath, it is sealed. You cannot pass the zones of creation except by His permission. There is nowhere outside.

And then — again, immediately — comes the refrain: *Which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

[PAUSE]

Notice what the structure is doing. The surah announces the Reckoning. It then invites you to try to escape it. It then declares that escape is impossible. And it calls all three of those moves — the announcement, the challenge, the inescapability — *favors.*

Why? How is it a favor to be told you cannot run?

For the person who has done wrong and would rather not be found: it is not a favor. It is exactly the reckoning they hoped to avoid. But this verse is not only talking to them.

It is also talking to the one who was wronged and received no justice in this life. The one whose suffering was real and went unaddressed. The one whose oppressor was powerful enough, or clever enough, or simply lucky enough, to never be held to account in this world. For that person, the inescapability of the Reckoning is not a threat. It is the only comfort sufficient to the weight of what they endured.

**The same accounting that terrifies the wrongdoer protects the wronged.** They are not two different mechanisms. They are the same one — total, universal, escape-proof — and the side you stand on determines whether it feels like a wall closing in or a door finally opening.

[PAUSE]

Al-Tabari, commenting on verse 31, records something worth pausing on: the reciters were not even unanimous on the exact form of *sanafrughu*. The Medinan and Basran schools heard it with the first-person divine plural — *We* shall attend to you. The Kufan schools heard it with a third-person form — *He* shall attend, or *it* shall come. Both readings are accepted within the tradition. The ikhtilaf is real but the theological substance is identical in both: the Reckoning arrives not from an indifferent cosmic process but from the direct attention of Allah Himself. Whether we say *We shall attend* or *He shall attend*, it is the same certainty — undelegated, personal, complete.

And that personal-ness is itself part of the mercy. The Reckoning is not a bureaucratic sorting. It is the One who made you, turning toward you with full knowledge of everything you were — every context, every pressure, every private struggle — and attending to you completely. The One doing the accounting is the One who knew you before you knew yourself.

[PAUSE]

So when verse 32 places the refrain after the announcement of this Reckoning, and verse 34 places it again after the challenge to flee and the confirmation that fleeing is impossible, the surah is building a single sustained argument: the accountability that encompasses you on every side, from which there is no exit and no hiding, is not a cage. It is the structure that makes justice permanent. It is the guarantee that nothing true about you will be lost.

Which of the favors of your Lord will you deny? Not the one that holds you to account. That one least of all.

# PART THREE: THE APPLICATION

## The Question You Were Always Being Asked

Let me bring this down from the abstract to the personal.

The verse is four words. *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.* In the time it takes to say it — barely two seconds of breath — it has done the following:

It has named His gifts in a word that cannot be made singular, because they never come alone.

It has named our denial in a verb that cannot be made innocent, because to receive a gift you know is real and live as though it isn't is not absent-mindedness — it is a verdict.

It has named the addressees in a number that cannot be generalized away, because the dual pulls you out of the crowd and stands you up alone alongside the other species of accountable creation, face to face with the question.

And it has placed all of this immediately after the announcement of the Reckoning — the moment of greatest resistance, the moment when you are most tempted to say *this is not a mercy* — and insisted, with the full weight of the refrain, that it is.

[PAUSE]

So the question is real. It is being asked now, to you, in this present moment. And it is asking for an honest answer.

Which favor will you deny?

The air? The one moving through your lungs right now, the one you have not had to remember to breathe once in your entire life, that has been arriving unbidden, unearned, sixty times a minute for every minute of your existence?

The mind? The one that is able to receive this question, that can follow the thread of an argument across centuries of scholarship, that can feel the weight of a four-word verse in a language it did not grow up speaking?

The Day that is coming — the one on which everything invisible about your life will be made visible, every prayer made real, every act of faithfulness honored, every cruelty answered? The one from which you cannot escape, and from which the wronged among us should not want to escape?

[PAUSE]

The question does not require a spoken answer. It requires a lived one.

To not deny the *ālāʾ* is not to say "alhamdulillah" in a moment of feeling. It is to build a life whose direction acknowledges the Giver. It is to treat the gifts with the attention they deserve — the air, the eye, the mind, the time, the relationships — as things that were handed to you on trust, not constructed by you and owed to no one. It is to remember, when the Reckoning feels like a shadow, that for everyone who lived with care, that shadow resolves into light.

[PAUSE]

That is the way out I promised you.

The verse is heavy because *takdhīb* is heavy — because calling a gift a lie, even by the quiet testimony of a life lived without acknowledgment, is a serious thing. But the door out of that weight is not complicated. It is just the honest answer to a four-word question. It is a "no" — spoken by how you live — to the question of which favor you will deny. *None of them. Not the garden and not the Judgment. Not the pearl and not the accounting. None of them. I received all of it.*

That answer, held consistently, is what the Qur'an calls *shukr* — gratitude not as a feeling but as a **posture**, a fundamental orientation toward existence as received rather than owned, as gift rather than given. And it is the posture the refrain is patiently, wave by wave, thirty-one times in a single sitting, trying to build in us.

[PAUSE]

The wave has not stopped coming back. Even here. Even now. Even after the announcement of the Reckoning.

*Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān.*

So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?
