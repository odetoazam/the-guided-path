# And Below Them, Two More Gardens

## وَمِن دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ
### *Wa min dūnihimā jannatān*
### "And besides those two — two more gardens."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Verse

I want you to imagine that you have been listening to the most beautiful description you have ever heard.

For sixteen verses, the voice of Ar-Rahman — the All-Merciful — has been building a place in front of your eyes. A garden. No — two gardens. Spreading branches. Two springs that flow and never stop. Of every fruit, a pair. Couches lined with brocade, the fruit of both gardens hanging low enough to gather without reaching. Companions whose gaze is lowered, untouched, like rubies, like coral.

And running through all of it, again and again, like a wave that keeps returning to the shore:

**فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ**
*"So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?"*

By the time you reach verse sixty-one, your heart is full. You have been shown the summit. You have been shown what the human being was made for. There is nothing left to add. The picture is complete. The garden has been given. Surely now the surah will turn to something else.

And then comes verse sixty-two.

Four words. Four words only:

**وَمِن دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ**

*"And below those two — two more gardens."*

[PAUSE]

Stay with what just happened.

You thought you had reached the top. You thought the description was the whole of the reward. And the All-Merciful says, almost in passing: *there is more.* Beneath what I have just shown you, there is another pair of gardens entirely.

Here is the question I want us to carry through this entire reflection. That word — **دُونِ**, *dūn* — translated here as "below" or "besides." Why would Allah introduce a gift by telling you it is *lower*? When was the last time someone offered you something and led with the word "lesser"? We hide that word. We bury it. "It's not as good, but—" And yet the All-Merciful opens the second half of His Paradise with it, unashamed, deliberate, placed at the very front of the sentence.

What does it mean that even what Allah calls *lower* is still a garden?

Let's find out together.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word *Dūn* — "Below," "Short Of," "Besides"

Before we touch the verse, let me give you the concept by itself, in a world you already know.

Imagine a mountain with two ledges. You are standing on the high ledge, the summit ledge, looking out. Now someone says to you: "There is another ledge *dūn* this one." In Arabic, that single word can mean two things at once, and both are true of a lower ledge. It can mean **beneath** it — lower in height. And it can mean **short of** it, or **other than** it — a different place, set apart. The lower ledge is both *below* the summit and *besides* the summit. One word holds both.

That is *dūn*. The classical lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab among them — define it first as **the opposite of "above"**: beneath, lower, inferior in rank. And from there it stretches to mean *short of something*, *this side of something*, *other than something, besides*.

Now look at the word in the verse:

**وَمِن دُونِهِمَا**
*"And from* dūn *of those two..."*

The two pronouns at the end — *himā* — are dual. "Those two." Those two gardens you were just shown. And the gift that is about to be named sits *dūn* them.

Notice that the English translators had to choose. Some write "besides those two." Some write "below those two." They had to pick one. But the Arabic refuses to pick. It hands you both at the same time.

Let's sit with this, because here is where the early commentators heard the depth.

Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both preserve the same conversation among the earliest readers of this verse. Ibn ʿAbbās — may Allah be pleased with him — said *dūnahumā fi'l-daraj*: **below them in rank, in degree.** Ibn Zayd said *dūnahumā fi'l-faḍl*: **below them in excellence, in merit.** Not below in *location* — below in *worth*. These two gardens are lower not because they sit at a lower altitude, but because they are a lesser station of honor.

It is worth noting that classical commentators did not all read this descent the same way. Ibn ʿAbbās anchored *dūn* in rank (*daraj*), while others read it primarily as a spatial or relational "besides" — two further gardens added to the accounting, not necessarily ranked beneath. Al-Qurṭubī himself records both positions without fully resolving them, which is itself instructive: the word *dūn* is spacious enough to hold both the hierarchy and the addition simultaneously, and perhaps that ambiguity is precisely the point. You cannot read this verse without confronting both possibilities at once.

[PAUSE]

So here is what the word has just done, in four letters.

Allah did not say "and *there are also* two gardens." He did not say "and *additionally*, two gardens." He chose the one word that announces, openly, *these are the lower ones.* He front-loaded the ranking. He told you their grade before He told you anything else about them.

And what is the very next word?

**جَنَّتَانِ.** *Gardens.*

Here's what is remarkable. The word that means "lesser" is immediately followed by the word "garden." The lower is still a garden. The lesser is still Paradise. The discount, if you can even call it that, is still infinite.

We will come back to what that does to the human heart. For now, hold onto this: **Allah was not embarrassed by the word "lower." He led with it. Because in His Paradise, even the floor is a garden.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Jannatān* — and the Hidden Root Beneath "Garden"

Now the second word. **جَنَّتَانِ** — *jannatān*, "two gardens," in the dual.

Let me again teach the concept before we open it.

In Arabic, words grow from three-letter roots the way trees grow from seeds. And the root of *jannah* — garden — is **ج ن ن**, *j-n-n*. Do you know what that root means, all by itself, before it ever becomes a garden?

**To cover. To conceal. To veil. To hide from sight.**

This is one of the most quietly astonishing roots in the entire Quran, because of what else grows from it. From *j-n-n* comes **janīn** — the unborn child, *concealed* in the womb. From *j-n-n* comes **jinn** — the unseen beings, *hidden* from our eyes. From *j-n-n* comes **majnūn** — the one whose reason has been *veiled over*, "covered" in the mind. From *j-n-n* comes **junnah** — a shield, the thing that *covers* and protects you in battle.

And from that same root comes **jannah** — the garden. Why? Because a garden, a true garden, is a place so thick with foliage that the leaves *cover the ground.* The canopy *conceals* what is beneath. You walk in and the world is hidden from you; you are hidden from the world. A garden is, at its root, *a covered place. A concealed place. A protected, veiled, hidden-away place.*

Classical commentators on this passage — including al-Zajjāj in his grammatical analysis of Quranic language — observed that the *tashd*īd*, the doubled consonant in *jann-*, performs the idea of intensity and enclosure: the *shaddah* on the *nūn* is not mere grammar but sound enacting meaning, the word folding inward on itself the way a garden's canopy folds light inward and holds it.

[PAUSE]

Now feel the verse again with that in your ears:

**وَمِن دُونِهِمَا جَنَّتَانِ**
*"And below those two — two concealed places."*

Two more *hidden* gardens. And where are they? *Dūn* — beneath, behind, set further in. The first pair was already concealed. And behind the concealed, there is *more* concealed. Veil behind veil. Garden behind garden.

This is the same Quran that tells us, in another surah, *"No soul knows what has been hidden for them of comfort for the eyes"* (32:17) — *ukhfiya lahum*, "hidden away for them." The reward is, by its very nature, the *hidden thing*. And here in Ar-Rahman, Allah lifts one corner of the veil, shows you a garden, and then says: *and behind it, another.* He is not showing you the end. He is showing you that there is no end you can see from where you stand.

Notice something about the sound, too. Say it slowly: *jannatān.* That doubled "n" — the shadda — makes the word fold in on itself, nestle, enclose. It *sounds* like being wrapped, covered, tucked away. The word performs its own meaning. The mouth makes a hidden, enclosed place even as it says "hidden, enclosed place."

So the lesson of the second word completes the first. The first word, *dūn*, told us: this is the lower one, and it is still a garden. The second word, *jannatān*, tells us: and even this lower garden is a *concealed treasure* — protected, veiled, kept — and there is more of it than your eyes were ever shown.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The Dual — and the Garden That Comes in Pairs

There is one more feature, and it runs like a heartbeat through this entire surah: **everything comes in twos.**

*Jannatān* — two gardens. Not *jannah*, one garden. The dual. And this is not a one-time event. Verse 46 already gave us *jannatān* — two gardens — for the first group. Now verse 62 gives us *jannatān* again — two more. Pairs, and pairs of pairs. And the refrain itself addresses *two*: *rabbikumā* — "your Lord," of *you two*; *tukadhdhibān* — "will *you two* deny."

The whole surah is built in twos. Why?

In Arabic, the dual is not just "two." The dual carries a sense of *completeness through pairing* — of a thing made whole by its partner. Earlier in this very surah Allah said *wa min kulli shay'in khalaqnā zawjayn* — "and of every thing We created a pair." The created world is paired. The springs come in pairs. The fruits come in pairs. And the gardens come in pairs.

Classical commentators noticed that the surah addresses *two* receivers — jinn and human — throughout, and that this dual address resonates structurally with the dual gardens. Al-Rāzī, commenting on the surah's architecture, observed that the dual form is not ornamental repetition but a theological statement: the mercy of Ar-Rahman is comprehensive, extended to both orders of creation, and mirrored back in the doubled gift. The grammar of the surah IS its argument about divine generosity.

What this invites us to consider — and here I am moving from grammar to reflection, so hold it gently — is that the reward mirrors the design. The God who made *everything* in twos does not give you a single, solitary garden. He gives in pairs, abundance doubled, and then says *and besides those two, two more.* The generosity is not just large. It is *structured* to overflow — built, from the ground up, to come more than once.

[PAUSE]

**The bridge.** So three words have shown us three things. *Dūn* — that even the lower tier is still Paradise, and that the classical scholars themselves debated whether this "lower" means ranked-below or simply set-beside, both readings sanctioned by the lexicon. *Jannatān* — that the garden is a concealed treasure, and there is more of it veiled behind what you were shown. And the dual — that the very architecture of the gift is abundance, doubled and doubled again, in a surah that speaks in pairs to a creation made in pairs.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About what it means to live in a universe where the *least* of what God gives is still infinite, and where you have spent your whole life afraid of being second.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Terror of Being "Lower" — and the Garden That Heals It

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of pain that follows the modern human being everywhere, and it has a precise shape. It is not the pain of *having nothing*. It is the pain of *having less than someone else.* It is the silent math we run every single day: their house, your apartment. Their marriage, your loneliness. Their kids' grades, your kid's struggle. Their feed, your life. We are not, most of us, afraid of being poor. We are afraid of being *behind.*

Psychologists have a name for it now — they call it social comparison, relative deprivation. You can give a person a good salary and they will be content until they learn what the person next to them earns, and then the same salary becomes an insult. The number didn't change. The *ranking* did. We are creatures who can be made miserable by a gift simply because someone else got a slightly bigger one.

And this terror does not stop at the edge of this world. It crawls into our faith. How many of us, quietly, carry this fear: *I am not one of the great ones. I am not the scholar, not the night-prayer warrior, not the one who memorized it all, not the foremost. I'm just... trying. I'm in the back. If I make it at all, I'll be in the cheap seats of Paradise.*

We are afraid of being *dūn.* Below. Lesser. Second tier.

Now bring that fear, that exact fear, to this verse.

[PAUSE]

Because this verse is *about* the second tier.

The commentators are clear about who these lower gardens are for. Al-Baghawī narrates the view of Ibn Jurayj: there are four gardens here — two for *al-muqarrabīn, al-sābiqīn*, the brought-near, the foremost; and two for *aṣḥāb al-yamīn, al-tābiʿīn* — the companions of the right hand, the followers. Al-Māwardī, as al-Qurṭubī reports, raises the same possibility: the two *dūn* gardens are for the followers, *li-quṣūri manzilatihim ʿan manzilatihi* — because their station falls short of the higher station.

It bears noting that this is one position among several that classical scholars held. Some among them — including a strand within al-Qurṭubī's own survey — read both pairs of gardens as belonging to the *same* rank of believer, the *sābiqūn*, with the second pair added simply as further extension of a single reward, not as a descending tier. The ikhtilāf is real. What is not in dispute is that Allah chose *dūn* — and then kept describing, kept naming fruits and springs and beauty, without the language of description diminishing one degree.

So these gardens — the ones introduced with the word "lower" — *these are the gardens of people like us.* The ones who didn't make the front row. The followers, not the foremost. The strugglers, not the saints.

And what does Allah call this consolation prize, this second-tier, runner-up, back-of-the-line reward?

**A garden.** Two of them. Veiled, concealed treasures, with springs and fruit and palms and pomegranates, and the same refrain of mercy washing over them: *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?"*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the mercy of it?

The All-Merciful did not hide the ranking from you to spare your feelings. He told you, plainly, *these are below.* And then He made *the below* a place you cannot even imagine the floor of. He let the word "lesser" stand — and then He filled "lesser" with eternity.

This is the healing the comparing heart has been starving for. In this world, "lower" means *lack.* Lower income, lower grade, lower status — every "lower" is a subtraction, a thing you don't have. But in the economy of Ar-Rahman, "lower" is still *jannah.* The gap between the foremost and the followers is real — Allah does not pretend it away — but it is a gap *between two infinities.* It is the difference between an ocean and an ocean. You may get the "lesser" ocean. You will not, for one moment of eternity, feel its lack.

There is a hadith that makes this almost unbearable in its beauty.
