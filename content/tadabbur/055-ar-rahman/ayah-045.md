# Which Favor? — When the Warning Itself Is the Mercy

> فَبِأَىِّ ءَالَآءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ
>
> *Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*
>
> **So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question That Will Not Let You Pass

There is a verse in the Qur'an that Allah repeats thirty-one times. Thirty-one. In a single surah. No other refrain in the entire Book returns with such relentlessness.

If you have ever heard Surah Ar-Rahman recited, you know the feeling. The reciter moves through a description — the sun and the moon following their reckoning, the stars and the trees bowing down, the heaven raised high and the balance set, the two seas meeting with a barrier between them, the pearl and the coral, the ships sailing like mountains — and then, like a wave returning to the same shore, the question comes again:

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

And again.

And again.

We are going to sit with one single instance of that refrain. Verse forty-five. And I want to tell you at the outset: of all thirty-one places this question appears, the one we have chosen is, in a sense, the strangest. Because most of the time, the question follows something beautiful. A garden. A blessing. A gift you can hold in your hand. *Of course* you wouldn't deny that favor — who denies a garden?

But verse forty-five does not come after a garden.

Let me read you what comes just before it. Listen:

> *"This is Hell, which the criminals deny. They will circulate between it and scalding, boiling water."* (55:43–44)

Fire. Punishment. The guilty dragged in circles between flame and scalding water.

And then — immediately, with no breath in between — Allah asks:

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

[PAUSE]

Sit with that for a moment. Allah has just described *Hell*. And He calls it — He files it under — **favors**. *Ālāʾ*. Bounties. Graces. Gifts.

How is Hell a favor? How is the threat of punishment something we are being asked to *thank* Him for?

That is the question this verse plants in us. And by the time we are done, I hope you will see that the answer is one of the most tender things in the entire Qur'an — that hidden inside what looks like the harshest verse in the surah is one of the gentlest mercies Allah ever showed His creation.

But to see it, we have to build the world first. We have to understand who is being spoken to, what word Allah chose for "favor," what word He chose for "deny," and — most of all — why this question takes the shape of a *question* at all, when Allah, of all beings, already knows the answer.

Let us begin.

---

## Rebuilding the World: Who Is Standing in This Room?

Before we touch a single word, we need to know who Allah is talking to. Because this verse is not addressed to "you" the way you might assume.

In English, when I say "you will deny," the word *you* could be one person or a thousand. English is blunt that way. But Arabic is not blunt. Arabic counts.

And the verb here — *tukadhdhibān* — and the pronoun attached to "your Lord" — *rabbi-**kumā*** — are not singular. They are not plural either.

They are **dual**.

This is addressed to *two*. "Which of the favors of your Lord — you **two** — will you **two** deny?"

Two what?

The overwhelming consensus of the early commentators — what al-Qurtubī calls *qawl al-jumhūr*, the position of the majority — is that the two being addressed throughout this surah are **mankind and the jinn**. The two species of accountable, choosing, morally responsible creatures. Elsewhere in this very surah Allah names them directly: *yā maʿshara l-jinni wa-l-ins* — "O company of jinn and mankind" (55:33). And He calls them together *al-thaqalān* — "the two weighty ones" (55:31).

So picture the room. This is not a private conversation. This is a cosmic address. Every human being who has ever lived or will live, and an entire parallel creation we cannot see, are all standing in the same hall, and the same question is put to all of them at once: *Which of your Lord's favors will the two of you deny?*

And here is a detail the early scholars preserved, reported by al-Tirmidhī from the companion Jābir ibn ʿAbdillāh. When the Prophet ﷺ recited Surah Ar-Rahman to his companions, they fell silent. And he said to them — as the narration relates — that the jinn, when this surah was recited to them, gave a *better* response than the people did. Every time the Prophet ﷺ reached *"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*, the jinn would answer:

*"Lā bi-shayʾin min niʿamika rabbanā nukadhdhib, fa-laka l-ḥamd"* — **"There is not a single one of Your favors, our Lord, that we deny — so to You belongs all praise."**

[PAUSE]

Hold onto that. Because that — *that response* — is the answer the verse is hunting for. The whole question is built to draw that sentence out of you. We will come back to it.

For now, simply notice the climate of this surah. It is not a surah of argument. It is a surah of *display*. Allah is not debating anyone. He is unveiling. Gift after gift after gift, like a host opening every door in a vast house and saying, with each one: *and this — was this not also from Me?* The refrain is the host turning to look at you after each room.

Now we are ready to open the words.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ālāʾ* — The Word for a Favor You Did Not Ask For

Let us start with the word at the heart of the verse: **ءَالَآءِ — *ālāʾ***.

We translate it "favors." That is correct, but it is thin. Let me first teach you something about how Arabic holds a word like this, and then we will see what Allah is doing with it.

In Arabic, there are several words that all get flattened into the English "blessing." There is *niʿma* — a favor, a grace, a gift of comfort. There is *faḍl* — bounty, the overflow of generosity. And there is this word, *ālāʾ*.

*Ālāʾ* is a plural — and notice, it is **only** a plural. It comes pouring out already in the plural form, the way some things in life never arrive singly. You do not receive one *ālāʾ*. The word itself refuses to be counted one at a time. Say it slowly: *ā-lāʾ*. Long open vowels, the mouth wide, the sound spilling outward. The word *sounds* like abundance — like something poured rather than handed.

Now here is the first thing to notice, and it is an absence. Look at the verse again:

**"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"**

*Which of the favors.* Allah does not say "this favor." He does not point. He uses the interrogative *ayy* — "which one?" — over an open, uncountable plural. And the moment He does that, He has done something to you grammatically that you may not have felt consciously.

He has asked you to **pick one**.

Stay with this. If I say to you, "Name one star you'd be willing to erase from the sky," your mind does not go to a star. Your mind goes to the *impossibility of choosing* — to the realization that the sky is full, that you never counted them, that you couldn't begin. The question "which one?" only works when there is a multitude. And by phrasing it this way, Allah has, in the grammar itself, told you that His favors are a multitude you cannot itemize — *before* you have even tried to answer.

This is the same truth He states plainly elsewhere: *"And if you should count the favors of Allah, you could never enumerate them"* (16:18; 14:34). But here in Ar-Rahman He does not *tell* you that. He makes you *feel* it, by handing you an impossible task: point to the one you'd return.

[PAUSE]

And now we arrive at the strangeness we began with. Because in *this* verse — verse forty-five — the "favor" Allah has just placed on the table is **Hell**.

So how, in the name of everything, is the fire a favor?

Here the early commentators say something I want you to hear carefully. Al-Baghawī, commenting on exactly this place in the surah, writes that everything Allah has mentioned — from *"Everyone upon the earth will perish"* all the way to here — is *mawāʿiẓ wa-zawājir wa-takhwīf*: **admonitions, deterrents, and warnings.** And then he says: *wa-kullu dhālika niʿmatun mina-llāhi taʿālā* — **"and all of that is a favor from Allah"** — *li-annahā tazjuru ʿani-l-maʿāṣī* — **"because it restrains from sins."**

[PAUSE]

Let that land. The warning is the favor.

The *takhwīf* — the very act of being frightened away from the fire — is itself one of the *ālāʾ*. Not the fire as torment, but the fire as *told to you in advance*, while you still have feet to turn and a heart to repent. The mercy is not that Hell is pleasant. The mercy is that you were *warned* — clearly, repeatedly, in time.

We will return to this in the second half, because it is the beating heart of the whole verse. But for now, lock in the linguistic discovery: by filing the warning under *ālāʾ*, Allah has redefined what counts as a gift. A gift is not only what soothes you. A gift is also what saves you — even when it arrives as a warning that makes you tremble.

One clear statement of what this single word reveals: **In Allah's accounting, the fence at the cliff's edge is as much a favor as the garden behind you.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Dual — Why "You Two," and Why It Should Frighten Us a Little

Now let us turn to a feature most people read straight past, because English cannot even render it. The dual.

Let me teach the concept first, plainly. In English we have two numbers: one (*book*) and more-than-one (*books*). That's it. Arabic has *three*: the singular (*one*), the plural (*three or more*), and — sitting precisely between them — the **dual**, a grammatical form reserved for exactly *two*. It has its own verb endings, its own pronouns. *Kitāb* is one book. *Kitābān* is two books — not by adding a number word, but by changing the shape of the noun itself.

Now look at our verse. *Rabbi-**kumā*** — "the Lord of you-two." *Tukadhdhibā**n*** — "you-two deny." The dual runs through the entire refrain, all thirty-one times.

We said the two are mankind and jinn. But I want you to feel *why* the dual, specifically, is doing work here — why Allah did not simply say "O people" in the plural.

Here is what the dual does. The plural lets you hide. When Allah says "O mankind," you can shelter inside the crowd. *He means humanity. He means society. He doesn't mean me, specifically.* The plural is a place to disappear.

The dual will not let you disappear.

By narrowing the address to *two* — two species, two of you in this room — Allah has shrunk the crowd down to almost nothing. There is mankind, and there is jinn, and that is *all*. You are not one of billions in this verse. You are one of *two*. And when a question is put to a room of two, you cannot pretend it was meant for someone else. **There is no one else.** The dual is the grammar of being singled out while standing next to exactly one other.

[PAUSE]

And notice the tenderness folded inside the severity. The pronoun is *rabbi-kumā* — "your Lord," *your* Lord, the two of you. He does not say "the Lord" coldly, at a distance. Even as He confronts you with the question, He reminds you of the relationship: *rabb* — root *r-b-b* — is not merely "master." It is the one who *nurtures, sustains, raises, and brings to completion.* The same root as the mother who *rears* a child. So the full force is: *"Which of the favors of the One who has been nurturing the two of you — every breath, every moment — will the two of you cry lies against?"*

The accusation and the affection are in the same word. He confronts you *as your nurturer*. That is harder to bear than a stranger's rebuke, and far more merciful.

What this feature reveals: **You were never addressed as a statistic. The question was always pointed at you, by Someone who has only ever raised you up.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Tukadhdhibān* — The Difference Between Doubting and Crying Lies

Now the final word, and the most pointed: **تُكَذِّبَانِ — *tukadhdhibān***. "Will you (two) deny."

The root is *k-dh-b* — the root of *kadhib*, a lie. And here is the first thing to teach. There are many words in Arabic for not-believing. There is *kufr* — to cover over, to reject. There is *shakk* — to doubt. There is *inkār* — to refuse to acknowledge. Allah did not use any of those.

He used a form of *kadhaba* — and not even the simple form. Look at the shape of the word. There is a small doubling sitting on the middle letter, the *dhāl*, pronounced with a *shadda*: *tukadh-dhibān*. That doubling is not decoration. In Arabic grammar, taking the basic verb and doubling its middle root letter moves you into what scholars call **Form II** — and the Leeds corpus confirms this verb is precisely Form II, imperfect, dual.

Let me teach what Form II does, generally, before we apply it. The simple form, *kadhaba*, means "to lie" — to *tell* a falsehood. But Form II, *kadhdhaba*, is different in kind. It is intensive and declarative. It does not mean "to lie." It means **"to call something a lie. To pronounce it false. To give the lie to it. To actively reject as falsehood."**

Do you feel the difference? *Kadhaba* is an internal act — you simply hold something untrue. *Kadhdhaba* is an act *aimed at a thing* — you take what is true and you stamp "FALSE" across its face. It is not passive disbelief. It is an accusation hurled at reality.
