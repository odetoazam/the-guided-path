# Which One Will You Point To and Call a Lie?

## فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

**Fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān**

*"So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?"*

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Sentence You Have Already Heard

There is a sentence in the Quran that returns and returns and returns.

If you have ever listened to Surah Ar-Rahman recited from beginning to end, you know the feeling. A blessing is named — a fruit, a spring, a garden, a pair — and then, like a tide coming back to the same shore, the same words wash over you again:

**فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ**

*So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny?*

Thirty-one times across this single surah, that question comes back. It is the most repeated refrain in the Quran. And the verse we are sitting with today — 55:53 — is one of those returns. Not the first. Not the last. One wave among the thirty-one.

Now here is the danger, and I want to name it at the very start, because it is the exact danger this verse was built to defeat.

**When something repeats, we stop hearing it.**

You know this. The clock on your wall ticks every second of your life and you have not heard it in years. The hum of the refrigerator. The feel of your own clothes on your skin. The blessing of a working pair of eyes reading these words right now. Repetition, in the human heart, almost always means *fading*. The more often a thing comes, the less we feed on it.

So a verse that repeats thirty-one times runs straight into the deepest weakness of the human heart — the tendency to grow numb to what surrounds us. [PAUSE]

And yet — Allah chose to repeat it.

That is the first wonder. Allah, who knows the human heart better than the human heart knows itself, knows exactly what repetition does to us. He knows we go numb. And He repeats anyway. Which means the repetition is not an accident of style. It is a *treatment*. The refrain is not background music. It is medicine being administered again and again, deliberately, to a patient who keeps forgetting he is sick.

So let me ask you to do something difficult before we even open the words. I want you to refuse the numbness. I want you to hear this verse as if you have never heard it — because today we are going to discover that these four small words are not a slogan. They are a question with a hook in it. A question that, once you understand its grammar, you cannot wriggle out of.

Here is what we will find. This verse does not ask, *"Do you deny?"* It asks, *"**Which one** do you deny?"* — and that single shift changes everything. It does not let you escape into vagueness. It backs you into a corner, hands you the entire universe of gifts, and says: *go ahead. Point to one. Pick it up in your hand. And call it a lie.* [PAUSE]

Stay with me. By the end, I hope you will not be able to hear this refrain the same way again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word "You" That Comes in Twos

Let me begin somewhere that may surprise you — with a feature of this verse that does not even translate into English.

In English we have one word for "you." One word whether I am speaking to one person, or two, or two thousand. "You will deny." It is flat. It carries no number.

Arabic is not like that. Arabic counts. It has a form for *one* of you, a different form for *two* of you, and a third form for *many* of you. And the form Arabic uses for exactly two has a name — the **dual**, *al-muthannā*.

Think about how rare that is in your daily speech. You almost never need a special word that means "exactly the two of you." We reserve that kind of precision for moments of intimacy — a parent to two children, a teacher turning to two students who were whispering, a judge addressing two parties in a dispute. To single out *two* is to say: *I see you both. Neither of you is hidden in a crowd.*

Now look at our verse. Look at how the dual saturates it:

**رَبِّكُمَا** — *rabbi-**kumā*** — "your Lord," where *kumā* means *the Lord of you two.*

**تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *tukadhdhibā**ni*** — "you both deny," where that final *-āni* is the dual ending stamped onto the verb itself.

Twice in four words, Allah reaches for the dual. *Your* Lord — the two of you. You *two* deny. [PAUSE]

So the immediate question is: **who are the two?**

Here the classical scholars are clear, and the surah itself answers. The dominant view — the view of the majority, recorded by al-Qurtubī — is that the two are **al-thaqalān**: *the jinn and mankind.* The two weighty species, the two beings given freedom and accountability, the two who will be questioned. Earlier in this very surah Allah says, *"We will attend to you, O two weighty ones"* (55:31), and *"O company of jinn and mankind"* (55:33). So when the refrain says *your Lord — you two — you two deny*, it is gathering the jinn and the human race into one address and speaking to them as a pair.

Al-Qurtubī even preserves a narration — reported through Jābir and transmitted by al-Tirmidhī — in which the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah to a gathering and noted that when he reached each refrain, *the jinn gave a more beautiful response than you did.* (We pass this on as it was narrated — a report carried in the collections, not a verse — but it tells us how the early community understood the *kumā*: two real audiences, both listening, both answering, or failing to.)

Now — here is where it opens. [PAUSE]

Let us sit with this. Why would Allah address two species *together*, in the dual, instead of speaking to each separately?

Notice something. The jinn and humankind could hardly be more different. One is made of smokeless fire, the other of clay. One is largely unseen, the other walks in daylight. They live on different terms, in different conditions, with different histories. If you wanted to find two creations with *less* in common, you would struggle.

And yet Allah binds them into a single "you two." Why?

Because for all their difference, they share the one thing this verse is about: **they both receive favors, and they are both capable of denying them.** The grammar is making a claim about what unites all accountable beings across the entire creation. Strip away the fire and the clay, the seen and the unseen, and what remains is a creature who is *given to* and who must *respond.* The dual is saying: this question is not parochial. It is not a human peculiarity. It reaches every being in the cosmos that was ever handed a gift and given the freedom to call it a lie. [PAUSE]

And there is one more layer, gentler than the rest. When you are spoken to in a crowd, you can hide. The "you" of a thousand people lets each person think, *he doesn't mean me specifically.* But the dual does not let you hide. The dual is the grammar of being *seen*. To be addressed as one of exactly two is to lose your anonymity. Allah does not ask this question into a faceless mass. He narrows the address until you can feel His gaze. *You — and you. Which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?*

**What this single feature reveals:** the verse refuses to let you disappear into the crowd. It counts you. It names you as one of two. The denial it is about to ask after is not a statistical denial spread thin across humanity — it is *yours*, personally, unhideably.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Question That Will Not Take "No" for an Answer

Now I want to show you the hook I promised at the start. It lives in the very first word.

**فَبِأَيِّ** — *fa-bi-ayyi* — "so by which..."

Let me teach the device before we use it, because it is subtle and it is everything.

There are two completely different kinds of questions in human speech. The first is a **yes-or-no question**: *Did you eat?* *Are you coming?* *Do you deny?* A yes-or-no question hands you an exit. You can simply say *no*, and the matter closes. The denial stays abstract. Nothing is named.

The second kind is a **"which" question** — what grammarians call an *interrogative of specification.* *Which one did you eat?* *Which road did you take?* And a "which" question does something a yes-or-no question can never do: **it assumes the thing already exists, and forces you to single out an instance of it.**

Think of a parent who comes home to find a plate of cookies gone. They do not ask, *"Did you eat a cookie?"* — that invites a denial. They ask, *"Which one of you ate the cookies?"* The "which" presupposes the act. It moves the conversation past *whether* and straight to *point to it.* [PAUSE]

Now hold that, and look at what Allah did **not** say.

He did not say, *"Do you deny the favors of your Lord?"* — *a-tukadhdhibāni bi-ālāʾi rabbikumā?* That would have been a yes-or-no question. It would have left an exit. A person could square his shoulders and answer, *No, I deny nothing,* and walk away with the denial safely abstract.

Instead He said: **فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ** — *so by **WHICH** of the favors...?*

Do you feel what that does? [PAUSE]

The "which" presupposes the favors. It takes them as established, undeniable, already laid on the table — the springs, the fruit, the pairs, the gardens, the eyes that read, the breath that rises and falls — and it says: *I am not asking you whether favors exist. We are past that. I am asking you to come forward and select one. Reach into this ocean of gifts. Pick one up. Hold it in your hand. And now — call it a lie.*

And that is the trap, the most merciful trap ever set. **Because you cannot do it.**

Try. Try in your own heart right now to complete the sentence. *The favor I deny is ______.* Will you name your eyesight? You are using it. Your next breath? It is filling your chest as you read. The water that became your blood? The pairing of the fruit, the alternation of night and day, the heart still beating under your ribs after all these years without your permission or your attention? [PAUSE]

Every single candidate you reach for *disproves the denial in the act of being named.* The question is engineered so that to answer it honestly is to be silenced — and to be silenced here is to be brought, gently, to gratitude. The "which" does not argue with you. It does not lecture. It simply hands you the universe and asks you to find the lie inside it. And you search, and you search, and you come up empty-handed.

That is why this is not a rhetorical flourish. The grammar is doing the entire spiritual work. A yes-or-no question could be brushed off with a word. A "which" question cannot — because it has already conceded nothing and presupposed everything. [PAUSE]

**What this single word reveals:** Allah did not come to debate the existence of His gifts. The verse begins *past* the debate. It assumes the gifts as the floor you are standing on, and asks only that you try to point at one and deny it — knowing that the very act of pointing will defeat you.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: ālāʾ — The Favors That Cannot Be Counted

Now to the word at the heart of it. **آلَاءِ** — *ālāʾ.* "Favors." "Bounties." "Blessings."

Two things about this word repay long attention.

**First: it is a plural with a contested, almost vanished singular.**

The lexicographers — you find this in *Lisān al-ʿArab* and in al-Rāghib's *Mufradāt* — record *ālāʾ* as a plural, but they differ over what its singular even is. Some say *ilā*, some *aluw*, some *ilْy*. Several candidate singulars are offered, and the matter is left genuinely open.

Sit with what that means. The word arrives in the plural so naturally, so completely, that the singular has nearly dissolved out of usage. We do not normally speak of *a* favor in this register. The favors come as a flood, a multitude, never as a tidy unit you could isolate and inspect. The very shape of the word resists being broken down into ones. [PAUSE]

And does that not match the reality it points to exactly? Try to isolate *one* favor of Allah and you find it was never alone. You want to thank Him for your eyesight — but eyesight is nothing without light, and light is nothing without a sun held at its distance, and the sun is nothing without the eye's ten thousand collaborating parts, and all of it is nothing without the brain that reads the signal, the blood that feeds the brain, the heart that moves the blood. Pull on one favor and the whole web comes with it. **You cannot find the singular** — and neither, almost, could the Arabs who built the word. The grammar of *ālāʾ* is a confession that the gifts come woven, uncountable, plural all the way down.

Elsewhere Allah says it plainly: *"If you tried to count the favor of Allah, you could not number it"* (16:18; 14:34). Here, the word *ālāʾ* whispers the same truth quietly through its own broken singular. [PAUSE]

**Second — and do not pass over this — notice whose favors they are.**

Not *ālāʾ Allāh.* Not "the favors of God" in the bare name. The verse says **آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا** — *the favors of your **Lord*** — *Rabb.*

Let us open that word, because the choice is deliberate. *Allah* is the proper name of the Divine Essence. But *Rabb* — from the root *r-b-b* — names a relationship. The *Rabb* is the one who owns, who nurtures, who sustains, who takes a thing from its first formless beginning and carries it, stage by stage, all the way to its completion. A *Rabb* is not a distant proprietor. A *Rabb* is the one who *raises you* — the way that word lives in English when we say someone was *raised*: fed, protected, corrected, brought up from helplessness to maturity by hands that never stopped working on you. [PAUSE]

So the favors in this verse are not handed over by a remote benefactor signing a check. They flow from your *Rabb* — the one who has been sustaining you, cell by cell and breath by breath, since before you had a name. The favors are the *evidence of His rububiyyah*, the visible proof of an invisible, ceaseless act of nurture. To deny a favor, then, is not merely to be ungrateful for an object. It is to deny the *hand* behind it — to look at the milk and deny the mother, to look at the harvest and deny the rain. [PAUSE]

**What this single word reveals:** the gifts are plural beyond counting, woven beyond separating — and they are not gifts from a stranger. They are the favors of the One who has been raising you the entire time. *ālāʾ rabbikumā.* The bounty, and the relationship that the bounty proves.

### The Bridge: From Grammar to Reality

Stand back now and see what these three small features have built together.

The **dual** counted you and refused to let you hide. The **"which"** presupposed the favors and dared you to point at one and call it false. And **ālāʾ rabbikumā** revealed those favors as uncountable, inseparable, and flowing from the very Lord who is raising you.

Put them in one breath and the verse becomes something almost frightening in its precision: *You — seen, named, one of exactly two accountable species — come forward and select a single one of the innumerable, interwoven gifts of the One who has sustained you since before you existed, and declare it a lie.* [PAUSE]

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the human heart itself, and about the strange world of pairs this verse was placed inside.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Heart That Goes Blind to What It Has

Let me start not with the Quran but with something psychologists have measured, because you live inside it every day.

They call it **hedonic adaptation** — sometimes "the hedonic treadmill." It is one of the most reliably documented facts about the human mind, and it is this: *we return to a baseline.* Give a person something they longed for — a raise, a house, a recovery, a marriage — and for a little while their happiness spikes. And then, with almost mechanical reliability, it drifts back down to where it started. The new thing becomes the normal thing. The normal thing becomes invisible. And the heart goes hunting for the next spike. [PAUSE]

You have felt this. The phone you saved for months to buy is now just the slab in your pocket you are mildly annoyed at for being slow. The home you prayed to be able to afford is now the place with the leaky tap. The health you would have traded anything for, in the week you were sick — when did you last thank God for it? The body adapts to the gift and then stops *seeing* it. We do not lose our blessings. **We lose our ability to perceive them.** We go blind to what we hold.

This is not a modern discovery. The modern science only measured what the Quran diagnosed fourteen centuries ago — and prescribed a treatment for. Because now you understand the repetition. [PAUSE]

Why does Allah ask *"which of the favors will you deny?"* thirty-one times in one surah? Because once is not enough to break the treadmill. Once is a thought; you nod and move on and adapt to *that* too. The numbness is relentless, so the cure must be relentless. The refrain comes back, and comes back, and comes back, because the heart's drift toward blindness is itself constant — and the only thing that can hold a constant pressure is a constant counter-pressure.

Watch what each return *does* in the surah. Allah names a gift — the sun and moon on their reckoning, the two seas, the fruit, the springs, the gardens — and then immediately: *which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?* Name. Question. Name. Question. He will not let you receive a gift and slide past it. The instant a blessing is mentioned, the verse spins you back to face it before you can adapt. It is a discipline of **re-seeing**, administered in thirty-one doses, each one snapping the eyes back open before they can close again. [PAUSE]

And here is the quiet brilliance of it. The verse does not command, *"Be grateful!"* — which the numb heart hears and ignores. It asks a *question*, and a question demands that you *look.* You cannot answer *"which favor will you deny?"* without first turning your gaze across the favors to search for a candidate. The grammar forces the very act of looking that hedonic adaptation had stopped you from doing. To even attempt an answer, you must *see your blessings again* — and seeing them again is the whole cure. [PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practice the verse hands you, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. When you catch the refrain in your day — and you will now, you will hear it everywhere — let it do to your life what it does in the surah. Name one gift you have completely stopped seeing. The water in your tap that a billion people would weep for. The two eyes. The fact that you woke up at all this morning, your soul returned to you when so many were not given theirs back. Name it. And then ask yourself the verse's own question about *that specific thing*: *Will I call this a lie? Will I go on treating it as nothing?*

The treadmill runs on autopilot. The verse is the hand reaching over to switch the autopilot off — thirty-one times, because once was never going to be enough.

### Theme Two: A World Made in Twos — and the One Who Has No Pair

Now I want you to notice where, exactly, this particular refrain was placed. Because the position is not random, and it teaches.

Our verse, 55:53, comes immediately after 55:52:

*"In both gardens, of every fruit, **two kinds.**"* — *fīhimā min kulli fākihatin **zawjān.***

A *pair.* Every fruit in two kinds. And then, on the heels of that pairing, the refrain lands: *which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?* The gift it is responding to, in this instance, is the gift of **pairing** itself. [PAUSE]

And notice what kind of gardens these are. The passage we are standing inside opened at 55:46: *"But for him who fears the standing before his Lord, there will be two gardens."* Classical commentators understood these gardens — the springs, the paired fruits, the refrain itself — as described specifically for a particular people: those who held themselves accountable before the moment of reckoning arrived, those who had *taqwā*. Al-Tabari makes the implication explicit in his gloss on this refrain: the ālāʾ being interrogated here are those "which He bestowed upon the people of His obedience" (*allatī anʿama bihā ʿalā ahl ṭāʿatih*).

That changes the reach of the question. [PAUSE]

At this point in the surah, the "which" extends in two directions at once: *backward*, toward the gifts already woven into the world you live in — the breath, the fruit, the pairs — and *forward*, toward the garden-gifts *promised* to those who feared their Lord's station. The same Lord who has already filled your world with uncountable favors has sealed a word: that for the obedient, the gardens await, with their flowing springs and every fruit in two kinds. To deny the promise is no easier than to deny the water already at your lips. Which of these ālāʾ — the given, or the promised — will you call a lie? The question has always been broader than we thought. It is not only a question about what you hold in your hand right now. It is also a question about whether you trust the hand that is still holding you. [PAUSE]

And look again at the refrain — it is *itself* spoken in pairs. *Your Lord — you two. You two deny.* The whole surah comes to us doubled: two gardens, two springs, two kinds of fruit, two weighty species, an address built on the dual. Ar-Rahman is a surah in twos.

So let us follow where the pairing points — because remember our first principle: the physical image is never the destination. The fruit is not the point. The pair is a stepping stone. To what?

Begin where you live. Look at your own world and notice how *thoroughly* it is built in pairs. Day and night. Male and female. Sweet and bitter. Land and sea. The two of every kind of fruit. Heat and cold. Sleep and waking. Question and answer. The Quran itself draws your eye to this: *"And of all things We created **pairs**, that perhaps you may remember"* (51:49). *"Glory to Him who created all the pairs — of what the earth grows, and of themselves, and of what they do not know"* (36:36). [PAUSE]

Pairing is everywhere. It is so woven into reality that you stopped noticing it — the very blindness we just spoke of. Two of nearly everything.

Now ask the question the Quran wants you to arrive at. *That perhaps you may remember* — remember **what**? If everything that is created comes in pairs, then what does the universal law of pairing point toward by its single, total exception?

It points toward the One who is **not** paired. [PAUSE]

This is the destination. A world made entirely of twos is a world signed, in every fruit and every season, with a signature pointing at the One who stands alone with no second, no partner, no opposite, no match. *Qul huwa Allāhu **Ahad*** — *Say: He is Allah, the One.* *Allāhu **as-Samad*** — the Self-Sufficient upon whom all lean while He leans on nothing. *Lam yalid wa lam yūlad* — He did not beget, and was not begotten. *Wa lam yakun lahu kufuwan ahad* — and there is **none comparable to Him**, no pair, no equal, no second of His kind. (Surah 112.) [PAUSE]

Do you see what the pairing was for? Every pair in creation is a finger pointing away from itself. The two kinds of fruit say: *we are paired, we are created — but the One who paired us is Himself unpaired.* Pairing is the mark of the creature; absolute Oneness is the mark of the Creator. So when this refrain lands right after *"of every fruit, two kinds,"* it is not merely thanking you for variety at the table. It is showing you a sign: *here is My pairing — woven through the gardens, through the seas, through your own selves — and through all of it, will you fail to read the One who has no pair? Which of these favors, these signs, will you call a lie?* [PAUSE]

And there is a tenderness in the pairing too, not only a proof. To be made in pairs is itself a mercy. Allah did not leave you single and unmatched in the way that *He* is single and unmatched — because for a creature, to be unpaired is to be alone. *"And among His signs is that He created for you, from yourselves, mates, that you may find rest in them"* (30:21). The fruit comes in two kinds so the garden is rich. You come paired so you are not alone. The pairing that points to His Oneness is, at the same time, His gift to you — a gift precisely *because* you are not built to bear the aloneness that belongs to Him alone. [PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

Let the pairs become legible again. The next time you notice an opposite — light after dark, the cool after the heat, the rest after the labor, the companion beside you — let it do its intended work. Let it be a finger pointing past itself. Train your eye to read the world as *signed*: every two whispering of the One. That is the difference between living in a warehouse of objects and living in a *creation* — a world thick with signatures, every pair a sentence about its Author. The denier walks through a warehouse. The one who remembers walks through a signed letter. The favors are the same. The eyes that read them are not.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now, having done the work, let us see the structure whole.

Surah Ar-Rahman is built like the swing of a pendulum. Gift — refrain. Gift — refrain. Thirty-one times the pendulum swings back to the same point: *which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?* It is the spine of the surah, the bone that everything else hangs on. And our verse, 55:53, is one vertebra of that spine — placed deliberately after the pairing of the fruit, so that the gift it answers for is the gift of a world made in twos.

And notice how the surah opens. Before any of this — before the gardens, before the refrain — it begins: *Ar-Rahmān. ʿallama-l-Qurʾān. Khalaqa-l-insān. ʿallamahu-l-bayān.* "The Most Merciful. Taught the Quran. Created the human. Taught him speech — *al-bayān*, clear expression." The very first favors named in the surah are the gift of the Quran and the gift of *language itself.* [PAUSE]

Feel how that closes the circle. The surah opens by giving you *speech* — and then, thirty-one times, it asks you a *question* that can only be answered in speech. The first favor is the tongue that can say *Alhamdulillah* — and the refrain keeps handing that tongue back to you and asking: *now, with the very faculty I gave you, will you deny the One who gave it?* The gift and the test of the gift are the same gift. You cannot even deny the favors without using a favor — the speech He taught you — to do it. The architecture is a closed loop with no exit, and the only honest way through it is gratitude.

### Bringing It Together

See now that the linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight approaching from two directions.

The **dual** that refused to let you hide, and the **hedonic treadmill** that makes you go blind to your gifts — these are one thing. The numbness wants you anonymous, abstract, asleep; the dual wakes you, names you, makes the question *yours.*

The **"which"** that handed you the universe and dared you to find a lie in it, and the **pairing** that signs every fruit with the mark of the Unpaired One — these are one thing too. Both are saying: *the evidence is total. It is in your hand and on your tongue and in every pair your eyes fall on. There is no candidate for the lie. There never was.*

And *ālāʾ rabbikumā* — favors beyond counting, from the Lord who raises you — is simply the name of everything the rest of the verse was pointing at.

### Questions to Carry

- If I had to complete the sentence — *"The favor of my Lord that I deny is ____"* — what would I honestly write? And what happens in me when I try?
- Which blessing have I gone most blind to precisely *because* it never failed me — the breath, the sight, the heartbeat, the safety I woke up in this morning?
- When the refrain returns and returns, do I hear medicine, or do I hear background noise? What would it take for me to hear it as the question it is?
- Every pair I will see tomorrow points past itself to the One with no pair. Will I read the sign, or walk through the warehouse?
- The same tongue that can deny is the tongue He taught me. What will I choose to do with it today?

### One-Sentence Distillation

*A world made entirely of innumerable, interwoven pairs — handed to two seen and named creatures by the Lord who raises them — leaves no single favor a person can point to and honestly call a lie.*

### The Closing Invitation

You will hear this refrain again — maybe today. When you do, do not let it pass as a familiar melody. Let it stop you. Let it ask you its real question. Reach for one favor, just one, and try to deny it — and feel the attempt collapse in your hands. That collapse is not defeat. It is the verse working. It is the treadmill switching off. It is the numbness lifting for one clear moment in which you actually *see* what surrounds you.

You do not have to manufacture gratitude. You only have to stop denying. The favors were never in question. Only whether you would look.

### Du'ā

*O Allah, our Rabb — the One who has raised us from nothing, breath by breath, who taught us the speech we now turn back toward You — open our eyes to the favors we have gone blind to, and let us never be among those who, handed an entire universe of Your gifts, search it for a lie. You are the One with no pair, and every pair You made points home to You. Make us of those who read the sign, and answer Your question with the only honest answer: not one, our Lord. Not one of Your favors will we deny.*

*Āmīn.*
