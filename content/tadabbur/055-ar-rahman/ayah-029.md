# The Asking and the Answering

> يَسْـَٔلُهُۥ مَن فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِى شَأْنٍ
>
> *Yas'aluhu man fi-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ; kulla yawmin huwa fī sha'n.*
>
> **Whoever is in the heavens and the earth asks Him; every single day He is engaged in a matter.**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Let me ask you to do something before we open this ayah.

Stop, for a moment, and count the things you needed today that you did not provide for yourself.

The air you breathed — you did not manufacture it. The heart that beat roughly a hundred thousand times since you woke — you did not instruct it. The light that reached your eyes, the food that became your body, the strangers whose work grew your wheat and carried your water, the immune cells that fought a battle you never knew was being fought. You woke up this morning already in receipt of ten thousand gifts you never requested in any language you can speak.

And yet — here is the strange thing — you probably moved through your day with a quiet, unexamined feeling of self-sufficiency. The feeling that you are, more or less, holding yourself up.

This is the great forgetting that the human being lives inside. We are the most dependent creatures imaginable, suspended over non-existence at every instant, and we walk around feeling independent.

Now hold that feeling in your mind, because we are about to step into a surah that wants to dismantle it — gently, beautifully, with the most tender refrain in the entire Quran.

### Where We Are Standing

Surah Ar-Rahman is unlike any other chapter. It is built around a single question, repeated thirty-one times like waves striking a shore: **فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ** — *So which of the favors of your Lord will you two deny?*

The whole surah is a gallery. Allah walks us past the sun and moon held to their reckoning, the stars and the trees that prostrate, the sky He raised and the balance He set, the two seas, the pearls, the ships like mountains on the water. Favor after favor after favor. And between each, that question returns: *which of these will you deny?*

But just before our ayah, something shifts. The gallery of beautiful things suddenly opens onto a chasm. Listen to what comes immediately before:

> **كُلُّ مَنْ عَلَيْهَا فَانٍ ۝ وَيَبْقَىٰ وَجْهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو الْجَلَالِ وَالْإِكْرَامِ**
>
> *Everyone upon it is perishing. And there remains the Face of your Lord, Possessor of Majesty and Honor.* (55:26-27)

Everyone. Perishing. Every face you have ever loved. Your own face. All of it, passing.

And then — *which favor will you deny?* — and then our ayah:

> *Whoever is in the heavens and the earth asks Him; every single day He is engaged in a matter.*

[PAUSE]

So here is the question I want you to carry into this reflection, the hunger I want to plant in you now:

The surah has just told us that all of creation is **perishing**. Now it tells us all of creation is **asking**. Why these two truths, back to back? What does it mean that the same creatures who are fading away are, at every moment, reaching out their hands? And what kind of Lord answers an entire universe of askers — every single day — without ever being depleted, distracted, or done?

Let's find out together.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yas'aluhu* — The Verb That Never Stops

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the whole ayah turns on it.

In English, when we say "he asks," the word sits flat. It doesn't tell you whether he asked once and stopped, or whether he is asking right now, or whether he asks habitually, perpetually, without end. We have to add words — *he keeps asking, he is always asking* — to get that.

Arabic doesn't need the extra words. Arabic builds it into the verb itself. There is a form called the *māḍī*, the completed action — *sa'ala*, "he asked," done, finished, in the past. And there is a form called the *muḍāriʿ*, the imperfect — and this form means the action is **ongoing, renewing, happening now and continuing.** It is the tense of the unfinished, the perpetual, the living present.

Now look at the first word of our ayah.

Allah does not say *sa'alahu* — "they asked Him." He says:

> **يَسْـَٔلُهُۥ** — *yas'aluhu* — "**is asking** Him," "**keeps asking** Him," "**goes on asking** Him."

[PAUSE]

The imperfect tense. The verb that does not close.

Sit with this for a moment. The asking described here is not a historical event. It is not something creation did once, at its origin, when it needed to be set in motion. It is happening **as you read this sentence.** Right now, in this instant, every creature in the heavens and the earth is asking Him. And in the next instant, again. And the next. The verb never resolves into the past tense because the asking never ends.

And notice *where* Allah places this verb. In Arabic you can lead with the subject — "whoever is in the heavens asks Him" — or you can lead with the verb. Allah leads with the verb: *Yas'aluhu* — *Asks-Him* — and only *then* tells you who. The act of asking comes first, before the askers are even named. As if to say: before I tell you who they are, know **what** they are doing. Their identity is secondary. Their asking is primary. The asking is the most important fact about them.

Notice something else, quietly. That little *hu* fused onto the end — *yas'alu-**hu*** — "asks **Him**." Not "asks." *Asks Him.* The object is locked into the verb itself, inseparable, one breath. You cannot say the act of asking, in this ayah, without saying the One asked. The petition and its Destination are a single word. There is no asking in this universe that floats free, addressed to no one. Every reaching-out has an address, whether the one reaching knows it or not.

Here's what's remarkable. Pull back and look at the scope: *man fi-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — **whoever is in the heavens and the earth.** That word *man*, "whoever," is the word Arabic uses for beings with intellect — angels, jinn, human beings. The classical commentators — al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi — note that this sweeps in *every* sentient creature: angel and human and jinn alike. The luminous angels who never disobey, drowning in worship at the Throne — they ask. The prophets — they ask. You, in your smallness — you ask. There is no rank so high in all of creation that it graduates out of asking.

[PAUSE]

Let that undo something in you. We tend to imagine that holiness means needing less. That the spiritual goal is to ascend toward some self-sufficiency, to need nothing, to stand alone. But the ayah draws the opposite picture. The closer a creature is to Allah — the angels at the Throne — the *more* fully it lives in the posture of asking. Asking is not what you do until you grow strong enough to stop. **Asking is what created beings most truly are.** The angel and the beggar are, in this one respect, identical: both, at this instant, with open hands.

What does this single verb reveal? That dependence is not your weakness. It is your **nature.** The imperfect tense tells you it will never be outgrown, never completed, never put behind you. To be a creature is to be — permanently, this very second — in the act of asking.

[PAUSE — let it land]

### Element Two: *Sha'n* — The Word He Left Open

Now to the second half, and to a word so vast that Allah deliberately refused to fill it in.

> **كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِى شَأْنٍ** — *kulla yawmin huwa fī sha'n* — Every day, He is in a *sha'n*.

Let me first teach you a feature of Arabic that English readers walk right past.

In Arabic, a noun can be *definite* — "the matter," *al-sha'n*, a specific known thing — or *indefinite*, marked by a soft *-n* sound at the end called *tanwīn*: *sha'n-in*. Now, usually we think "indefinite" means *less* — vague, unimportant, "a matter, whatever." But Arabic has a startling move. Sometimes the indefinite is used not to diminish a thing but to make it **too big to name.** The grammarians call it *tanwīn al-tafkhīm* — the *tanwīn* of magnification. When a thing is so immense, so multiple, so beyond cataloguing that naming it would shrink it, Arabic leaves it indefinite. Open. Unbounded.

That is what Allah does here. Not *al-sha'n*, "the matter." But *sha'n-in* — **"a matter"** — left wide open.

And what does *sha'n* mean? The lexicons — Lisān al-ʿArab, al-Rāghib's Mufradāt — give it a rich field: an affair, a state, a concern, a great undertaking, a matter of weight and consequence. Not a trivial task. A *significant business.* An affair that matters.

So now hear it: **Every day, He is in an affair.** Unnamed. Unbounded. Beyond your power to list.

Why would Allah leave it open? Look at how the classical mufassirūn rush to fill the space — and notice that they cannot agree, because no single answer fits. Al-Jalalayn lists: *He gives life and gives death, He raises and abases, He enriches and impoverishes, He answers a caller, He grants a petitioner* — and then trails off with *wa ghayri dhālik*, "and other than that." Al-Muyassar: *He honors and humbles, He gives and withholds.* The commentators are pointing at an ocean and naming the waves they can see, then admitting the rest is beyond counting.

[PAUSE]

That is the *tanwīn* of magnification working exactly as designed. The indefinite *sha'n* is Allah telling you: *the scope of what I am doing, at every instant, in every corner of the heavens and the earth, is more than your language could hold, so I will not insult it by naming it.*

Stay with me, because there is one more layer, and it is the one that overturns everything.

Put the two halves of the ayah together. The first half: every creature is *asking* — *yas'aluhu*, imperfect, ongoing, never stopping. The second half: every day He is in *a matter* — *kulla yawmin*, "every day," also ongoing, also never stopping.

Do you see the symmetry? **The unending asking of all creation is met by the unending acting of the One.** The petitions never cease — and the response never ceases. Creation's hands are always open; His giving is always in motion. The two halves of the ayah are a perfect pair of perpetual-motion: the eternal question and the eternal answer, locked together.

And here is the thing that should stop your breath. Among the favors al-Baghawi and al-Jalalayn record under *sha'n* is *ijābatu dāʿin, wa iʿṭāʾu sāʾil* — **answering one who calls, granting one who asks.** Look back at the verb: *yas'aluhu* — they *ask*. *Sāʾil* — the asker. **It is the same root.** *S-'-L.* The askers of the first half are answered in the *sha'n* of the second half. The ayah folds back on itself. The whole universe is asking, and His perpetual *affair* — His unnameable, unbounded daily business — *is, in part, the answering of that very asking.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So look at what these ten words have shown us through their grammar alone.

The imperfect verb *yas'aluhu* told us: asking is not an event but a permanent state — creation, at every instant, with open hands. The sweep of *man fi-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* told us: no rank escapes it, not even the angels. And the open, magnified *sha'n* told us: the One they ask is, at every instant, engaged in an answering too vast to name — an affair that never closes, perfectly mirroring the asking that never closes.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about what it means to exist at all.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lie of Self-Sufficiency

Let me start where you actually live, not where the ayah ends.

You live in a civilization that has made *independence* its highest word. The self-made man. The self-help shelf. *Be your own person. Owe nothing to anyone. Stand on your own two feet.* We hand our children the dream of needing nobody, and we call the day they need nobody the day they "made it." Dependence, in this story, is the embarrassing condition you are supposed to grow out of. The infant depends; the adult provides. To still be asking, still be needing, still be reaching out your hands — that is failure, that is weakness, that is the thing we hide.

And so we build lives engineered to feel self-sufficient. We accumulate. We secure. We diversify our income and lock our doors and stock our shelves, and the deepest motive underneath it all is the longing to reach a place where we will never again have to ask anyone for anything.

Now bring the ayah down onto that life like sunlight.

> *Whoever is in the heavens and the earth **asks** Him.*

**Whoever.** Including the angels who have never sinned and never tired. Including every prophet. Including the most self-sufficient billionaire and the most independent mind that ever lived. The ayah is not describing the weak among us. It is describing the *structure of being created.* To exist as a creature — any creature, at any altitude — is to be, at this instant, in a state of asking.

Here's what that means, and I want to say it slowly. **The independence you are chasing does not exist.** It was never available. It is not a height you have failed to reach; it is a place that is not on the map of creation at all. The only being in all of reality who asks of no one is Allah Himself — *al-Ṣamad*, the Self-Sufficient, the One toward whom all need flows and from whom none flows out. Self-sufficiency is a *divine* attribute. To chase it as a creature is to chase the one thing that, by definition, a creature can never be.

[PAUSE]

Now, does this crush you? It should do the opposite. Feel the relief in it.

If asking were a *failure*, then every open hand would be a confession of defeat, and you would be right to hide your needs and white-knuckle your way toward an independence that never comes. But the ayah tells you that the angels at the Throne are asking *right now.* Asking is not the posture of the failed creature. **It is the posture of the truthful one.** The one who asks is simply the one who has stopped lying about what he is.

Think about what the alternative actually costs. The whole exhausting performance of self-sufficiency is a performance of a falsehood. Every time you pretend you are holding yourself up, you are spending enormous energy maintaining a fiction that the next gust of wind could expose — one diagnosis, one phone call, one failed heartbeat, and the illusion of independence shatters and reveals what was always true underneath: you were being held the entire time. By Someone. The ayah is inviting you to stop the performance early, voluntarily, while it is still a choice — to lower your hands' pretense and open them honestly.

The Quran says it directly in another place, and it could be the caption to this entire theme:

> **يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ أَنتُمُ الْفُقَرَاءُ إِلَى اللَّهِ ۖ وَاللَّهُ هُوَ الْغَنِيُّ الْحَمِيدُ**
>
> *O humankind, you are the poor — utterly in need — toward Allah; and Allah is the Rich, the Praiseworthy.* (35:15)

Not *you have some needs.* **You ARE the poor.** *Al-fuqarā'* — poverty is not your circumstance, it is your noun, your identity, your essence. And He is *al-Ghaniyy* — the Rich. The whole of reality is arranged as a single relationship: an infinite need turned toward an infinite Wealth.

**What changes tomorrow?** This: the next time you feel the sting of having to ask — for help, for forgiveness, for provision, for mercy, in du'a — catch the old reflex that calls it weakness, and replace it with the truth. *This open hand is not my failure. It is the most honest thing about me. The angels are doing exactly this, right now, at the Throne.* Make a du'a today for something you have been too proud to ask for — and feel the lie of self-sufficiency loosen its grip, just slightly. That loosening is the beginning of freedom.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Giver Who Is Never Depleted

Here is a fear so deep we rarely name it. We are afraid of being too much.

You have felt it. The hesitation before asking a friend for one more favor — *I've already asked so much.* The guilt of being the needy one in a relationship, always taking, afraid that the well of the other person's patience is running dry. We know, from every human relationship we have ever had, that givers get tired. Generosity has a bottom. Ask a person enough times and you will eventually reach the edge of what they have to give — their energy, their attention, their forgiveness. Every human giver is a vessel with a finite floor, and we organize our asking around the fear of scraping it.

We carry that fear straight into our relationship with Allah. *I've asked for this before. I've made this same du'a a hundred times. I've returned to this same sin and come crawling back for forgiveness again — surely there's a limit. Surely He tires of me. Surely I am, by now, too much.*

Now hear the second half of the ayah, and hear it as the precise medicine for this exact fear:

> **كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِى شَأْنٍ** — *Every single day, He is engaged in a matter.*

[PAUSE]

The entire universe of askers we met in the first half — every angel, every human, every jinn, ceaselessly petitioning — turn now and look at the One they are asking. Is He overwhelmed? Is He depleted? Is He rationing His attention, triaging the petitions, telling the latecomers to wait until tomorrow because today's mercy has run out?

No. *Every day, He is in an affair.* The giving is not slowing. It is not thinning out as the requests pile up. **Every single day** — *kulla yawmin*, no day excepted, no day a day off — He is fully, freshly, inexhaustibly *engaged.* New life is being breathed into being. New provision is being apportioned. The dying are being received and the born are being delivered, and the sinner who returns for the thousandth time is being forgiven for the thousandth time as if it were the first — all of it, today, and all of it again tomorrow, with no sign anywhere of a bottom being reached.

This is the difference between a vessel and a *spring.* A vessel gives from a fixed store; the more it pours, the less it holds, and you are right to fear its floor. But a spring is not diminished by what flows from it — the flowing *is* its nature, and it would be stranger for the spring to stop than to give. Allah is not a vessel of mercy that you are slowly draining. **His giving does not subtract from Him.** Every favor He has ever bestowed, on every creature, since the first instant of creation, has not lowered His wealth by a single atom. This is *al-Ghaniyy* — the Rich whose richness is not the size of a treasury but the nature of His being.

And here is where this theme reaches back and embraces the first one. Remember where this ayah sits. Just two verses earlier: *Everyone upon the earth is perishing, and there remains the Face of your Lord* (55:26-27). The contrast is the whole point. Creation is the realm of *fanā'* — perishing, running out, reaching its floor. You will run out of days. The people you love will run out of days. Every created vessel will, in the end, be emptied. **But the One you are asking is the realm of *baqā'* — remaining, enduring, never running out.** That is *why* you can ask Him every day forever. You are pouring your perishing, finite need into an Imperishable, infinite Wealth. The asymmetry is not cruel. It is the only thing that makes asking *safe.*

[PAUSE]

Think of what this dismantles. The shame of the repeat-asker — gone. You cannot ask Him too many times, because the limit you fear is a feature of *vessels,* and He is not a vessel. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah is *displeased* with the one who does *not* ask Him — the exact inverse of every human relationship you have ever known. With people, asking too much strains the bond. With Allah, *not* asking strains it. He has built a universe of ceaseless askers and declared Himself, every single day, ceaselessly engaged in answering — and then told you the one thing that would offend Him is for you to fall silent and stop coming.

**What changes tomorrow?** This: take the one need you have been ashamed to bring back to Him — the recurring du'a you have started to feel embarrassed about, the same forgiveness you have asked for too many times to count — and bring it again, today, *without the apology.* Drop the silent disclaimer of *I know I keep asking.* He is, this very day, *in a matter.* Your asking is not the strain on the relationship. Your silence would be. Pour the perishing thing into the Imperishable One, and do it again tomorrow, and know that the spring has no floor for you to find.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and see what we could not see at the beginning — the shape of the whole.

Watch how these verses are built, in sequence:

- **55:26-27** — *Everyone upon the earth is perishing; there remains the Face of your Lord, Possessor of Majesty and Honor.* — The creature is **finite.** The Lord **remains.**
- **55:28** — *So which of the favors of your Lord will you deny?* — The hinge. The question.
- **55:29** — *Whoever is in the heavens and the earth asks Him; every day He is in a matter.* — The finite creature **asks.** The remaining Lord **answers, endlessly.**

Do you see it now? Verse 29 is not a new subject. It is the *practical meaning* of verses 26-27. Because you are perishing, you ask. Because He remains, He can answer. The two halves of our single ayah — *creation asking* and *the Lord acting* — are the two halves of verses 26-27 brought down to earth and made into a relationship you can live inside. **Impermanence is the reason you reach out. Permanence is the reason there is Someone there to receive it.** The ayah is the bridge between your finitude and His infinity, and the surah engineered it that way.

### The Two Roads Meet

Look at where our two journeys arrive — the linguistic and the thematic — and see that they are one insight seen from two directions.

The **grammar** showed us two unending verbs locked in symmetry: *yas'aluhu* — creation asking, in the imperfect, never closing — and *kulla yawmin huwa fī sha'n* — the Lord acting, every day, never closing. The **themes** showed us the same two truths as a way of living: that *asking is your honest nature, not your shameful failure*, and that *the One you ask is a spring, not a vessel, and has no floor for you to reach.*

They are the same discovery. The grammar of the imperfect tense *is* the lifting of your shame. The open, magnified *sha'n* *is* the bottomless spring. The ayah does not teach two lessons. It teaches one — **that to exist is to ask, and to be Allah is to answer, forever** — and it teaches it once in the language of verbs and once in the language of the human heart.

### Questions to Carry

Take these with you, genuinely open, and let them work on you:

1. Where in your life are you spending your strength to *perform* a self-sufficiency that the angels themselves do not possess?
2. What is the one need you have stopped bringing to Allah because you have decided you have asked too many times — and what would it mean to bring it again today, without apology?
3. If asking is the most honest thing a creature can do, what does your *silence* in du'a actually confess?
4. You are, right now, one of the *man fi-l-arḍ* who is asking. What are you asking Him for, in the wordless language of your living — and is it what you would choose to ask, if you said it out loud?
5. Knowing that *every day He is in a matter* — that this very day He is engaged, giving, answering — what do you expect from today that you have not bothered to ask for?

### One Sentence to Hold

**To exist is to be perpetually asking; to be Allah is to be perpetually answering — and His answering, unlike everything else in this perishing world, has no floor.**

### The Invitation

You came into this reflection carrying the quiet, exhausting fiction that you hold yourself up. The ayah does not scold that fiction. It simply opens a window and lets you see what the angels have always seen: that *everyone* is asking, that the open hand is the truest hand, and that the One it opens toward is, this very day and every day, inexhaustibly engaged in the work of answering.

So you might let something set down today. Not a resolution to need less — that road is closed, it was always closed. But a permission to need *honestly.* To stop hiding the asking, and to make it your worship. To bring the repeated du'a back without the apology. To live, openly and without shame, as what you have been all along: one of those in the heavens and the earth who, at this very instant, is asking Him.

### A Du'a From These Words

*O Allah, You who remain when all else perishes, who are engaged every single day in a matter too vast for us to name — we are among those in Your heavens and Your earth who ask of You, and we will never stop, because asking is the truth of what we are. Do not let us forget the lie of needing no one. Make our open hands honest, make our asking constant, and meet our perishing, repeated, unworthy petitions with the answering that, in You alone, has no end and no floor.*

*Āmīn.*
