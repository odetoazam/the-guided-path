# The Circle Returns: How an Evil Assumption About Allah Becomes a Home

## Introduction — The Year the Believers Came Home Empty-Handed

Let me give you the Arabic first, then we'll slow everything down.

> وَيُعَذِّبَ ٱلْمُنَٰفِقِينَ وَٱلْمُنَٰفِقَٰتِ وَٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ وَٱلْمُشْرِكَٰتِ ٱلظَّآنِّينَ بِٱللَّهِ ظَنَّ ٱلسَّوْءِ ۚ عَلَيْهِمْ دَآئِرَةُ ٱلسَّوْءِ ۖ وَغَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَعَنَهُمْ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُمْ جَهَنَّمَ ۖ وَسَآءَتْ مَصِيرًا

*"Wa yuʿadhdhiba al-munāfiqīna wa al-munāfiqāti wa al-mushrikīna wa al-mushrikāti, al-ẓānnīna billāhi ẓanna al-sawʾ. ʿAlayhim dāʾiratu al-sawʾ, wa ghaḍiba Allāhu ʿalayhim wa laʿanahum wa aʿadda lahum jahannam — wa sāʾat maṣīrā."*

"And He may punish the hypocrite men and hypocrite women, and the polytheist men and polytheist women — those who assume the worst of Allah. Upon them is the turn of evil; Allah's wrath is upon them, and He has cursed them and prepared for them Hell — and evil it is as a destination."

Now let's rebuild the world this verse fell into.

It is the sixth year after the Hijra. The Prophet ﷺ has set out from Medina with around fourteen hundred companions — not to fight, but to perform ʿumrah, the lesser pilgrimage. They wear the white cloth of the pilgrim. They drive sacrificial animals before them. Every signal they send says: *we come in peace, we come to worship.*

And at a place called Hudaybiyyah, just outside Mecca, they are stopped. The Quraysh will not let them in. After tense negotiation, a treaty is signed. And on its surface, the treaty looks like a humiliation. The believers must turn around and go home without entering Mecca. They must wait a full year. Some of its clauses seem to tilt entirely in the enemy's favor. Companions who had given everything — who had pictured themselves weeping at the Kaʿba — now had to mount their camels and ride back the way they came, the pilgrimage unperformed.

Imagine the silence on that return journey.

And now imagine something else. Imagine the people who *didn't even come.* Back in Medina, there were those who had stayed behind — and Allah will name them directly a few ayahs later in this very surah. While the believers were at Hudaybiyyah, these people sat in their homes and ran a calculation in their hearts. The calculation went like this: *Muhammad and his people have walked into the lion's mouth. They will not come back. Mecca will swallow them. This whole thing is finished.*

[PAUSE]

That calculation — that quiet, private, almost reasonable-sounding bet against the Prophet ﷺ — **is the subject of this ayah.**

Here is the hook. Surah Al-Fath opens by calling Hudaybiyyah a *clear victory.* Then it describes Allah pouring tranquility — *sakīna* — into the hearts of the believers. Then it describes Him opening the gates of Paradise to them. And then comes our ayah: the third movement, the dark one. The same divine plan that sent peace into one set of hearts sends punishment toward another.

But watch *what the punished are actually punished for.* You would expect the indictment to be: they were hypocrites, they were idolaters. And those words are there. But Allah does not stop at the labels. He reaches past the labels to name **one thing they all shared** — one disease underneath the four different masks.

And that one thing was not a weapon they raised, or a battle they fought, or a word they spoke.

It was a *thought they had about God.*

Let us go and find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One — The Verb That Begins It All: *Yuʿadhdhiba*, and the Chain You Cannot See

Before we touch the word, let me teach you a small thing about Arabic verbs — because once you see it, this ayah opens differently.

In English, "to break" and "to shatter" are two unrelated words. In Arabic, very often, the *intensified* version of a verb is built right into the *shape* of the original. You take the simple verb and you double its middle letter — you press down on it, you put your weight on it — and the meaning thickens. It becomes more, harder, repeated, total. The grammarians call this Form II. You can almost *hear* the intensification, because that doubled letter is physically held longer on the tongue.

Now look at the first word.

> وَيُعَذِّبَ — *wa yuʿadhdhiba*

The root is *ʿ-dh-b* (عذب) — punishment. And this is not the plain form. It is Form II. The middle letter is doubled — *ʿadh-dha-ba* — you hold the *dh*, you lean into it. This is not a light, glancing punishment. The very architecture of the word is heavy.

But here is the first thing I want you to notice — and it's about a letter you might have skipped right over.

The word begins with **wa** — "and." *And* He may punish.

"And" connecting to *what?*

[PAUSE]

This is the part most of us read past. This verb does not stand alone. It is the third link in a chain that began two ayahs earlier, and the chain is invisible unless you go back and pick it up. Let me lay the three links side by side:

In **48:4**, Allah describes His purpose: *so that the believers may increase in faith* — He sent down *sakīna*, tranquility, into their hearts.

In **48:5**, the purpose continues: *so that He may admit* the believing men and the believing women *into gardens* beneath which rivers flow.

And in **48:6** — our ayah — the same grammatical thread continues: *and so that He may punish* the hypocrites and the polytheists.

Do you see what just happened? **Sakīna, then gardens, then this.** They are not three separate announcements. They are *one sentence,* one unbroken expression of a single divine purpose. The peace poured into the believers' hearts and the punishment prepared for the others *flow from the same source* — the same victory, the same plan, the same moment at Hudaybiyyah.

This is staggering when you sit with it. We tend to imagine mercy and justice as two different departments of God, run by two different moods. This grammar refuses that. **The very act that was tranquility for one heart was exposure for another.** The "victory" of Hudaybiyyah didn't just reward the faithful as a separate event from punishing the doubters. It was a single light — and a single light both warms and reveals. It warmed the believers into *sakīna*. It revealed the doubters for what their hearts had quietly become.

And now — sit with the intensive form one more time. The classical commentators reach for something beautiful here. Al-Qurtubi explains that this punishment came "by the delivering of *humūm* to them" — *humūm*, anxieties, griefs, the gnawing worries — "because of the rising of the word of the Muslims." Look at the symmetry that opens up: the believers received **sakīna** — settledness, calm. And the punishment of the doubters is described as **humūm** — the opposite — a mind that churns, that cannot rest, that is eaten alive by the very success it bet against.

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The person who assumes the worst is *already* being punished, before any external judgment lands. The anxious, suspicious, always-bracing-for-betrayal heart **is itself a small hell.** The intensified verb has already begun its work in this life. The doubled letter is being held down on them right now.

So this single verb reveals: the mercy and the justice are one act, and the punishment begins as the inner unrest of a heart that chose suspicion over trust.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two — *Al-Ẓānnīn billāhi ẓanna al-sawʾ*: The Crime That Was Only a Thought

Now we arrive at the heart of the ayah. And I want to build it slowly, because everything turns here.

First, the structure. Allah has just named four groups: hypocrite men, hypocrite women, polytheist men, polytheist women. Four labels. And then He adds a phrase that hangs off all four of them at once:

> ٱلظَّآنِّينَ بِٱللَّهِ ظَنَّ ٱلسَّوْءِ — *al-ẓānnīna billāhi ẓanna al-sawʾ*

"Those who assume, about Allah, the assumption of the worst."

Let me teach you the word *ẓann* before we use it. The root is *ẓ-n-n* (ظنن). *Ẓann* is not knowledge. It is not certainty. It is the *lean* of the mind — the guess, the supposition, the assumption you make when you don't actually know. It's the story you tell yourself to fill a gap. When the future is uncertain and you cannot see what God will do, *ẓann* is which way your heart tilts in the dark.

And notice the *form* of the word Allah chose for these people: **al-ẓānnīn.** This is an active participle — and an active participle in Arabic describes not a one-time act but a *characteristic,* an ongoing state, a settled trait. He did not say "those who once thought." He said: **those who are, by their very nature, assumers.** This is the disposition of their hearts. Suspicion of God was not a slip they made on a bad afternoon. It was the weather of their inner life.

Now here is the thing I most want you to see. Look at *where* this phrase sits.

Four labels — hypocrites, hypocrites, polytheists, polytheists — male and female, exhaustively named. Why name all four so completely? Because the gender pairs and the two categories make the net total: **no one slips through the category.** And then, after closing every exit, Allah does not let the labels be the final word. He reaches *underneath* all four and names the single thing they have in common:

They assumed the worst of Allah.

[PAUSE]

Think about what this is telling us. The Munāfiq and the Mushrik look like very different people. One claims to believe and secretly doesn't; the other openly worships others alongside God. Different theology, different posture, different mask. But Allah X-rays all four masks and finds the *same skeleton underneath.* And the skeleton is not the outward sin. It is **ẓann al-sawʾ** — a thought about God.

What was the thought? The commentators are unanimous and specific. Al-Jalalayn: they assumed "that He would not aid Muhammad ﷺ and the believers." Al-Baghawi, almost word for word: "that He would never grant victory to Muhammad and the believers." Al-Qurtubi adds the concrete picture they held in their minds: *that the Prophet ﷺ would not return to Medina at all* — that he was riding to his death.

So the "worst assumption" was not blasphemy shouted in the street. It was a quiet sentence in the heart: *God is not going to come through. His Messenger is going to fail. The promise is not going to be kept.* It sounds almost like realism. It sounds like a sensible read of the odds.

And **that** — Allah declares — is the disease beneath hypocrisy and idolatry both.

[PAUSE]

Let me say this as plainly as I can, because it is the center of the whole reflection. The Qur'an here locates the deepest sin not in an action but in an *interpretation.* Not in what these people did, but in how they read God. They looked at a hard moment — believers turned away from Mecca, a treaty that smelled of defeat — and they wrote a story about God in which God loses, God abandons, God does not keep faith. They assumed the worst of the One who is never anything but the best.

And notice the precision of the Arabic: *al-ẓānnīna **billāhi*** — they assume the worst *about Allah Himself.* Not about the situation. Not about the strategy. About *Him.* The grammar pins the suspicion directly onto God's own character. This is the line being drawn: you may not understand His plan — that is allowed, that is human. But to conclude from your confusion that *He* is failing, that *He* has let go of the rope — that is the assumption that condemns.

This is the great inversion of the surah. The world judged the believers by appearances and called them defeated. These people read the appearances and bet against God. And Allah says: the believers, in their *sakīna,* held the better assumption. They could not see the victory either — but they did not write a story in which God fails. That difference — invisible, internal, a mere lean of the heart in the dark — is the difference between the gardens of ayah 5 and the Hell of ayah 6.

One clear statement, then we move: **The thing being punished here is a thought about God — and the specific thought is that He will not keep His word.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three — *ʿAlayhim dāʾiratu al-sawʾ*: The Wheel That Turns Back

Now watch what Allah does to these people. He does not just announce punishment. He gives us an *image* — and the image is doing precise, devastating work.

> عَلَيْهِمْ دَآئِرَةُ ٱلسَّوْءِ — *ʿalayhim dāʾiratu al-sawʾ*

"Upon them is the *dāʾira* of evil."

The root is *d-w-r* (دور) — to turn, to revolve, to go around in a circle. From it comes *dāʾira* — a circle, a turning, a rotation. And in the Arabic of that era, *dāʾira* had a very particular shade: it meant a *turn of fortune,* a wheel of fate that comes around — specifically, a calamity that *circles onto* you. The pre-Islamic Arabs spoke of *dawāʾir,* the turning misfortunes of time, the way the wheel of luck spins and lands on a person.

And here is where you must hold two ayahs in your mind at once. Elsewhere in the Qur'an, in Surah at-Tawba (9:98), Allah describes hypocrites who "wait for the *dawāʾir* to turn against you" — who sit and hope the wheel of misfortune spins onto the believers. *That* is exactly the posture of the people in our ayah. At Hudaybiyyah, in Medina, they were watching the wheel, betting it would land on the Prophet ﷺ. *Let it circle onto them,* they thought. *Let the misfortune come around to Muhammad and his people.*

And Allah's response is contained in two words and a piece of word order. Look:

> **ʿAlayhim** dāʾiratu al-sawʾ

In normal Arabic order you might expect the subject — "the circle of evil" — and then where it lands. But Allah *fronts* the landing place: **ʿalayhim** — "upon THEM" — comes first. And in Arabic, when you move a phrase to the front like that, you create restriction and emphasis. The meaning becomes: *it is upon them — them specifically, them and no one else — that this circle falls.*

[PAUSE]

Feel the justice of this. They wished the wheel onto the believers. Allah takes the very wheel they were spinning — the *dāʾira,* the circle, the thing that by its nature *comes back around* — and He says: it lands on *you.* You aimed the misfortune outward; the grammar pins it back onto your own chest. The circle, true to its name, completed its turn and came home — to its origin. To the ones who set it spinning.

A circle always returns to where it began.

That is not decoration. That is the entire theology of the verse compressed into one geometric word. The evil you *wished* described a circle in the air and landed on the one who wished it. Al-Jalalayn glosses *dāʾirat al-sawʾ* as "humiliation and punishment." Al-Baghawi: "destruction and ruin." The thing they hoped would crush the believers became the thing that crushed them.

And now — the deepest layer. Hold the three elements together for a moment.

In Element Two we found *ẓanna al-sawʾ* — the **assumption** of evil. An *inner* thing. A thought.

Here we find *dāʾirat al-sawʾ* — the **circle** of evil. An *outer* thing. A fate.

Same word. *Al-sawʾ.* Twice.

[PAUSE]

What you *assume* of God in the dark of your heart does not stay in the dark of your heart. The *sawʾ* you projected onto Allah's plan becomes the *sawʾ* that encircles your life. The inner thought becomes the outer weather. You called God's promise "evil odds" in the privacy of your mind — and the evil you imagined onto Him became the evil that closed in a ring around you. The grammar is showing you the mechanism: *ẓann al-sawʾ* hardens into *dāʾirat al-sawʾ.* The assumption becomes the atmosphere.

We'll see, at the very end, that this is not even the last time the word appears.

One clear statement: **They spun the wheel of misfortune at God's people; the wheel, being a wheel, returned — and the evil they imagined of God became the evil that surrounded them.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So the words have shown us a structure. A single verb told us mercy and justice flow from one act. A single phrase told us the deepest crime is an interpretation — a settled assumption that God will fail. And a single image told us that assumption does not stay inside; it circles outward and becomes a fate.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way a human heart actually moves through the world.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One — The Story You Tell Yourself About God

Let me start where you live.

You are waiting for a text back. Three hours of silence. And in those three hours, your mind does not stay neutral. It writes a story. For some of us the story is: *they're busy, they'll reply when they can.* For others, the story is: *they're done with me. I've been abandoned. I knew this would happen.* Same silence. Same three hours. Same absence of information. Two completely different worlds — built entirely out of *ẓann,* out of which way the heart leans when it cannot see.

Now scale that up. The job you didn't get. The diagnosis you're waiting on. The duʿāʾ you've made a thousand times that has not been answered. The prayer for a child, a spouse, a way out, that has met nothing but silence. In every one of those gaps — and life is *made* of these gaps — your heart writes a story about God. And there are only ever two stories.

Story one: *He is withholding because He is against me. He has forgotten. He does not keep His word. The silence means abandonment.*

Story two: *He is the Most Merciful. The silence is not absence. He is doing something I cannot yet see. The promise stands even when the evidence doesn't.*

[PAUSE]

This ayah just told you something that should stop your breath: **the choice between those two stories is not a small thing.** It is the very thing that divided the people of the gardens from the people of the Fire. The munāfiqūn at Hudaybiyyah were not punished for a battle they lost or a crime they committed. They were punished for *which story they told themselves in the dark.* They looked at God's silence — the believers turned back, the treaty signed — and they wrote story one. *He's not going to come through.*

And here is what makes it so frightening and so hopeful at once: **they had no more information than the believers did.** Nobody at Hudaybiyyah could see the future. The believers couldn't see that in two years Mecca would fall without a battle. The doubters couldn't see it either. The data was identical. The difference was *entirely* in the lean of the heart. One group held *husn al-ẓann* — a good assumption of God — with no evidence. The other held *sūʾ al-ẓann* — the worst assumption of God — with no evidence. And on that invisible difference, eternity turned.

There is a sacred narration, a *ḥadīth qudsī,* where Allah says: *"I am as My servant assumes Me to be."* Anā ʿinda ẓanni ʿabdī bī. Read it through this ayah and it lands like a verdict. The assumption you carry about God is not a passive description of your mood. It is an *active force.* It shapes what you receive. The servant who assumes God is generous meets a generous God. The servant who assumes God abandons — meets, in some real sense, the God he insisted on.

[PAUSE]

So let me make this concrete, because it must change something by tomorrow.

The next time you are in a gap — the next time God is silent and you cannot see the plan — notice that your heart is *about* to write a story. There is a half-second before the story forms. In that half-second, you have the freedom the munāfiqūn refused to use. You can lean the other way. You can say: *I do not know what He is doing. But I refuse to write a story in which He fails. I will hold the good assumption with no evidence, because the good assumption is the truer one — it matches who He actually is.*

That is not naivety. The believers at Hudaybiyyah were *right.* The good assumption turned out to be the accurate read of reality. Husn al-ẓann is not wishful thinking — it is *better forecasting,* because it factors in the one variable the cynic always leaves out: the character of God Himself.

What does this change about how you live tomorrow? It means the next disappointment is not just something that happens *to* you. It is a question being put *to* you. *Which story will you write about Me now?* And you have practiced enough cynicism. Practice the other thing. Catch the story as it forms, and bend it toward the God who has never once broken a promise.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two — The Boomerang of the Heart

Here is something we all know but rarely name.

When you wish someone ill — when you secretly hope a rival stumbles, when a small part of you wants the person who hurt you to suffer, when you *bet against* someone and quietly root for their failure — something happens to *you.* Not to them. To you. The bitterness you brew for them is a poison you drink yourself. The person you resent sleeps fine; you're the one lying awake at 3 a.m. rehearsing their downfall. The ill-wish circles back. It always lands on the sender first.

We have a hundred folk-words for this. *What goes around comes around. The chickens come home to roost. He dug a pit and fell in it himself.* Every culture knows it because every human heart has felt it. You launch something dark at someone else, and somehow it curves through the air and returns to your own hand.

The Qur'an has a word for that curve. *Dāʾira.* The circle.

[PAUSE]

Look again at what these people did. They didn't just doubt God privately. They *wished the wheel of misfortune onto the believers.* They sat in Medina and rooted for the Prophet's destruction. They were *spinning a circle* — aiming calamity outward at God's people. And Allah's response was not to invent a new punishment for them. It was simply to let the circle *be a circle.* "Upon them is the turn of evil." The thing you spun comes back. You don't even need a separate judgment — the geometry of malice does the work. What you launch returns to the one who launched it.

This is one of the quiet mercies hidden inside a verse about wrath. Allah is teaching us the physics of the heart so we can stop hurting ourselves. Every time you wish ruin on another, you are not loading a weapon aimed at them. You are spinning a wheel that — by its nature, by the meaning of the word itself — must come back around to you.

And here is the layer underneath the layer. *Why* did they wish the believers ill? Go back to Theme One. Because they had already assumed the worst of God. The two are connected. **A heart that thinks badly of God will think badly of God's people.** Once you've decided the universe is rigged against you — that God withholds, that the promise is a lie — then everyone who's doing well becomes a threat, and their success becomes something you have to root against. Sūʾ al-ẓann about God *metastasizes* into sūʾ al-ẓann about everyone. The cynicism that starts at the top — with God — seeps down into every relationship you have.

[PAUSE]

So watch the chain the ayah has built. It starts as a *thought* — *ẓann al-sawʾ,* the assumption of evil about God. That thought turns sour into ill-will toward His servants. That ill-will spins outward as a wish for their ruin — a *dāʾira,* a circle. And the circle, being a circle, comes home: *dāʾirat al-sawʾ ʿalayhim,* the turn of evil is *upon them.* A thought became a posture became a fate.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means you guard the *first* link. You don't wait until you're spinning wheels of resentment to catch yourself. You catch it at the source — at the thought about God. Because if you can hold *husn al-ẓann* about Allah — if you can trust that He has not abandoned *you,* that your portion is secure, that His promise to you stands — then you no longer need anyone else to fail. Their success stops threatening you. You can be glad for the person who got what you wanted, because your heart isn't running the calculation that says *there isn't enough, God is against me, I have to root against the others.* The cure for the boomerang is upstream. It is trusting God so completely that you have no ill to wish on anyone.

And practically, tonight: is there a circle you are currently spinning? A person you are quietly betting against, a downfall you are half-hoping for? Name it. And then understand — with the full weight of this ayah — that it is curving back toward you right now. Let it go. Not for their sake. For yours. Stop the wheel before it lands.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal — The Three Faces of *Al-Sawʾ*

Now I can show you the thing that was hidden in plain sight the whole time. I asked you to hold the word *al-sawʾ* — "evil," "the worst" — and to notice it appearing twice. I held one back.

Walk through the ayah and count. The root *s-w-ʾ* (سوء) appears **three times,** and it traces the entire journey of these souls from a thought to an eternity:

**First:** ظَنَّ ٱلسَّوْءِ — *ẓanna al-sawʾ* — "the assumption of the **worst**." This is the *thought.* It lives in the heart. It is invisible. No one can see it. It is just a lean in the dark.

**Second:** دَآئِرَةُ ٱلسَّوْءِ — *dāʾiratu al-sawʾ* — "the circle of **evil**." This is the *circumstance.* The thought has now escaped the heart and become a fate that encircles the life. The inner has become the outer.

**Third:** وَسَآءَتْ مَصِيرًا — *wa sāʾat maṣīrā* — "and **evil** it is as a destination." From the very same root, *s-w-ʾ,* now describing Hell itself. This is the *eternal home.* The thought has become a fate has become a *forever.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The ayah is built as a descent through three layers of one word:

> An **evil thought** → becomes an **evil circumstance** → becomes an **evil eternity.**
> *Sawʾ* of the heart → *sawʾ* of the life → *sawʾ* of the final home.

The *maṣīr* — the destination, from the root *ṣ-y-r,* "to become" — is literally "what you become." And what they *became* is the externalization, the made-permanent form, of the *sawʾ* they first whispered about God. The assumption didn't just predict their fate. In a terrible sense, it *was* their fate, growing. They said "the worst" about God; "the worst" became their circumstance; "the worst" became their address.

A thought, left to grow, becomes a world.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes were the same truth approached from two directions.

The grammar showed us: mercy and justice flow from one act (the chained verb), the deepest crime is an interpretation of God (the active participle *al-ẓānnīn*), and that interpretation circles outward into fate (the fronted *dāʾira*). The human heart showed us: the story you tell yourself about God in the silence is the most consequential thing about you, and the ill you wish on others curves back to land on you first.

And the architecture of *al-sawʾ* married them: because what you *assume* about God (the linguistic center) is precisely what *becomes* your circumstance and your eternity (the human stakes). Your *ẓann* is the seed. Your *maṣīr* is the tree. They are the same word three times because they are the same thing at three ages.

### Questions to Carry

1. In the gaps of my life — the unanswered prayers, the closed doors, the silences — what story am I *actually* writing about God? Not the story I'd recite out loud, but the one my heart believes at 3 a.m.?
2. Is there someone whose failure I am quietly hoping for? And if so — can I feel the wheel already curving back toward me?
3. When God's plan looks like a defeat, the way Hudaybiyyah looked like a defeat — do I have the courage to hold the good assumption *with no evidence,* the way the believers did?
4. If it is true that "I am as My servant assumes Me to be" — what kind of God have I been *summoning* by the assumptions I carry?
5. What small *sawʾ* — what tiny worst-case story about God — am I letting grow, that I would not want to become my *maṣīr?*

### One-Sentence Distillation

What you assume of Allah in the privacy of your heart does not stay private — the evil you suppose of Him circles outward to surround your life and finally becomes the very home you arrive at.

### The Closing Invitation

You will be in a gap soon. Maybe you are in one now. There will be a silence from God, and your heart will reach for a story to fill it. This ayah is not asking you to pretend you understand the plan. The believers at Hudaybiyyah didn't understand it either — they wept on the way home. It is asking you for something smaller and harder: in the half-second before the story forms, *refuse to write the one where God fails.* That refusal is not naïve. It is the truest reading of reality there is, because it is the only story that accounts for *who He actually is.* Let the good assumption be your default. Let the wheel of resentment fall still in your hands. And trust, against the evidence, the One who turned a treaty that looked like defeat into a clear and shining victory.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You are as we assume You to be — so let us assume of You only the best. In every silence and every closed door, guard our hearts from the assumption that You would ever abandon us. Make our *ẓann* of You beautiful, our trust in Your promise unshakeable, and our final *maṣīr* the gardens of the ones whose hearts You filled with *sakīna.* Āmīn.
