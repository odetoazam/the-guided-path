# The Pronoun That Guards the Line

## Honoring the Messenger, Glorifying the Lord — Surah Al-Fath, Ayah 9

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Hear them before we understand them.

> [ayah:48:9] لِّتُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَتُعَزِّرُوهُ وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ وَتُسَبِّحُوهُ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

*Li-tuʾminū billāhi wa rasūlihi — wa tuʿazzirūhu wa tuwaqqirūhu — wa tusabbiḥūhu bukratan wa aṣīlā.*

> *That you may believe in Allah and His Messenger, and that you may support him and revere him, and that you may glorify Him morning and evening.*

Eight words. Four verbs. And inside those eight words, Allah does something so quiet that most of us read straight past it for our whole lives.

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this verse, you have to stand where its first listeners stood.

It is the sixth year after the Hijrah. The Muslims have set out for Makkah — not for war, but for ʿumrah, dressed in iḥrām, driving the sacrificial animals before them. They are stopped at a dusty place called Hudaybiyyah, just outside the sacred precinct. And there, instead of the pilgrimage they came for, they are handed a treaty. The terms look, on the surface, like a humiliation. Turn back this year. A man who flees Makkah to join you must be returned; a man who flees you to Makkah need not be. Some of the strongest Companions are shaken. ʿUmar himself asks aloud: *are we not upon the truth?*

And it is **into that exact moment** — a moment of confusion, of bruised pride, of a community wondering whether their love and their loyalty have led them somewhere foolish — that this surah descends and calls it **a clear opening.** *Innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā.* A victory. The thing that felt like a step backward, Allah names a conquest.

And then He turns to the One who carried all of that on his shoulders. To the man who sat and made that treaty while his own Companions could barely contain their grief. He says, in the verse just before ours: *We have sent you as a witness, a bringer of glad tidings, and a warner.* And then — our verse — He tells the believers what that Messenger was sent to draw out of them.

So this is not an abstract list of duties. This is a verse about **what you owe to a man who just absorbed a community's disappointment so that Allah could give them something greater.** What do you give back to someone like that? And — here is the question the verse will not let us avoid — how do you give it to him **without crossing a line that must never be crossed?**

### The hook

There are three small pronouns in this verse. Three times the word *hu* — "him." And the classical scholars stopped at those three pronouns and asked a careful question: *who is "him"?*

Because the answer is not the same all three times.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. Allah has placed, inside the grammar itself, the single most important boundary in all of human devotion. And He has drawn it not with a lecture, not with a warning — but with the quiet movement of a pronoun. Let's go and find it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The *lām* of purpose — why the Messenger was sent

Before we reach the pronouns, look at the very first letter. The verse opens with a single attached letter doing enormous work: **لِ** — *li-*.

Let me teach this before we use it. In Arabic, there is a small word — really just a letter — called the *lām al-taʿlīl*, the "*lām* of purpose." You know it in your own speech. When you say, "I locked the door **so that** no one would come in," that little phrase "so that" reaches back and explains *why* you did the previous thing. It binds an action to its reason.

That is exactly what this *li-* does. It reaches back to the verse before it — *"We sent you as a witness, a bearer of glad tidings, a warner"* — and it answers the question: **sent for what?** *Li-tuʾminū.* **So that you may believe.**

Notice something. The entire mission of the final Messenger of Allah — the witnessing, the glad tidings, the warning — all of it is bent toward one purpose, and that purpose is named with this *lām*. The Messenger was not sent to be admired. He was not sent to found a dynasty or win a war. He was sent **so that something would happen inside you.** The grammar makes him a means, and you — your belief, your devotion, your turning toward Allah — the intended end.

Here's what's remarkable, and we should be honest about it because the classical reciters preserved it carefully. This opening verb was recited two ways. The majority read it *li-tuʾminū* — "**so that you** may believe," speaking directly to you. But Ibn Kathīr and Abū ʿAmr, as al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record, read it *li-yuʾminū* — "so that **they** may believe," reporting about the believers. And the same split runs through all four verbs of the verse.

Now — do not treat this as two rival verses. Treat it as a composite. The variation itself is a mercy. Read one way, the verse turns and points its finger straight at you: *you, here, now — believe, support, revere.* Read the other way, it widens to embrace every believer who ever lived and ever will: *that they may believe.* The Qurʾān holds both the intimate address and the timeless description in the same breath. You are spoken to **and** you are spoken about. The mission was for you specifically, and for all of them across all of time.

**This single letter tells us: the Messenger's whole life was a purpose-clause aimed at your heart.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: The verb *ʿazzara* — the love that defends

Now the verbs begin to land. The first response after belief is **وَتُعَزِّرُوهُ** — *wa tuʿazzirūhu*. Most translations give you "and honor him." Hold that translation loosely, because the root is deeper and stranger than "honor."

The root is **ʿ-z-r**. And here we need to slow all the way down, because this root carries two things at once that English struggles to hold together.

Let me teach the form first. This is a **Form II** verb — in Arabic, the form where the middle root-letter is doubled. *ʿAzara* would be the simple form; *ʿazzara*, with the doubling, is the intensive. Form II takes a meaning and **presses on it** — it makes it deliberate, repeated, emphatic. Think of the difference between "to break" and "to *shatter*." So whatever this root means, the doubled form means it with force.

Now the root itself. Look at what the classical scholars saw in it. Al-Qurṭubī records two readings side by side. Qatādah said *tuʿazzirūhu* means *tanṣurūhu* — **you aid him, you defend him, you stand between him and those who would harm him.** Al-Ḥasan and al-Kalbī said it means *tuʿaẓẓimūhu* — **you magnify him, you hold him in honor.** Al-Baghawī gives you both in one breath: *tuʿīnūhu wa tanṣurūhu* — you support and aid him.

Two meanings. Aid, and honor. And here is the thing the lexicons quietly reveal: in this root, **they are not two meanings. They are one.**

Because the older sense buried in *ʿ-z-r* is the sense of **restraint, of warding off, of prevention.** *Al-ʿazr*, the lexicographers note, carries the sense of *al-radd* and *al-manʿ* — turning back, holding off. This is why, in Islamic law, *taʿzīr* came to mean a disciplinary measure — a restraint placed on wrongdoing. So when Allah says *tuʿazzirūhu*, He is not asking for a feeling. He is asking for a stance. He is saying: **stand around him. Form a wall. Turn back what comes at him.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened to "honor"?

We tend to think honoring someone is a soft thing — a warmth in the chest, a respectful word. But this root tells us that **to truly honor someone is to defend them.** That love which does not rise to protect is not yet *taʿzīr*. The honor Allah asks for the Messenger is the kind that puts its body between him and the danger. It is love with a spine.

And remember where this verse fell. Hudaybiyyah. A moment when defending the Messenger did not mean drawing a sword — it meant **trusting his judgment when every instinct screamed against it.** Some of the Companions defended him there not with weapons but by swallowing their confusion and obeying. That, too, is *taʿzīr*. To support him when supporting him costs you your pride.

**This verb tells us: the honor owed to the Messenger is active. It defends, it shields, it turns back what threatens him.** [PAUSE]

### Element Three: The three pronouns — and the line drawn in grammar

Now we arrive at the heart of the verse. This is what the scholars stopped at. This is the thing hidden in plain sight.

Watch the three verbs in sequence, and watch the little *hu* — "him/Him" — riding at the end of each:

- **وَتُعَزِّرُوهُ** — and you support **-hu**
- **وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ** — and you revere **-hu**
- **وَتُسَبِّحُوهُ** — and you glorify **-hu**

Three times: *-hu, -hu, -hu.* In English we are forced to choose — "him" or "Him," lowercase or capital — and in choosing we hide the very thing the Arabic exposes. Because in Arabic, the pronoun is **identical** all three times. The same sound. The same letter. *Hu.*

So the question the classical commentators asked is exactly the right one: **each time, who is *hu*?**

Let me give you their reading, as a composite, because each scholar is holding the same reality from a slightly different angle.

The second verb is **tuwaqqirūhu** — from the root **w-q-r**, again a Form II intensive. The root *waqr* means *weight, gravity, heaviness*. To *waqqara* someone is to treat them as weighty, to give them gravitas, to hold them in awe. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī gloss it: *tuʿaẓẓimūhu wa tufakhkhimūhu* — you magnify him, you exalt his station. This is the inward posture: not just to defend the Messenger outwardly, but to carry him in your heart as someone **heavy with honor**, someone before whom you lower your voice. (And indeed, just one surah away, in al-Ḥujurāt, Allah will command exactly that — *do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet.*)

Now here is the careful work the scholars did. Al-Baghawī says it plainly: the pronouns of *tuʿazzirūhu* and *tuwaqqirūhu* **return to the Prophet ﷺ.** *Hādhihi al-kināyāt rājiʿah ilā al-nabiyy.* You support **him** — the Messenger. You revere **him** — the Messenger. And then al-Baghawī adds a single, breathtaking instruction: *wa hāhunā waqf* — **"and here is a stop."**

Here. Is. A pause.

[PAUSE]

A break in the breath. A line in the sand. Because the third verb is not like the first two.

**وَتُسَبِّحُوهُ** — *wa tusabbiḥūhu* — "and you glorify **-hu**." Same sound. Same *hu*. But al-Baghawī, al-Jalālayn, and the body of commentators are unanimous: this pronoun does **not** return to the Messenger. It returns to **Allah.** *Tusabbiḥū-hu* — *ayi Allāh*, says al-Jalālayn. You glorify **Him** — God.

Do you see what Allah has done?

He has run three verbs together, each ending in the identical pronoun *hu*. He has let the first two flow toward His Messenger — *support him, revere him* — bringing the Prophet so close, so beloved, that the pronoun almost blurs into the divine. And then, at the third verb, **without changing a single letter of the pronoun**, He bends it back to Himself. *Tasbīḥ* — glorification, the declaration that Allah is utterly beyond every imperfection — **snaps the line back into place.**

This is not an accident of grammar. This is the grammar **doing theology.**

Because *tasbīḥ* is a word that can only ever land on one Being. You can support a man. You can revere a man. You can honor, defend, magnify, exalt a man. But you cannot *glorify* a man — not in the sense of *subḥān*, declaring him free of every flaw, beyond every limit. That word has exactly one rightful object in all of existence. The moment the verse reaches *tusabbiḥūhu*, the pronoun **cannot** mean the Messenger, no matter how identical it sounds — because the verb itself refuses him.

[PAUSE]

Sit with this. The single greatest danger in religious history — the danger that swallowed nation after nation before us, who took their prophets and their righteous men and lifted them into objects of worship — Allah guards against it **not with a warning, but with a verb.** He lets you love the Messenger to the very edge. *Support him. Revere him.* And then the language itself stops you at the cliff: *glorify — Him.* God alone.

The grammar is the guardrail. The pronoun is the line.

**These three pronouns tell us: you may pour your love and defense and reverence onto the Messenger to the fullest — and the instant you reach worship, the word turns, by its own nature, to God.** [PAUSE — a long one]

### Bridge

Look at what these three elements have shown us. A *lām* that bends the Messenger's whole mission toward your heart. A verb, *ʿazzara*, that turns honor into active defense. And three identical pronouns that draw, in the quietest grammar imaginable, the most important line a human soul ever has to keep.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: How to love a human being without losing your soul

Start where you actually live.

You have, at some point, loved someone you looked up to. A teacher who changed your life. A parent. A scholar whose words cracked you open. A mentor. And you know — if you are honest — how slippery that love is. How it wants to grow legs and become something else. How easily admiration tips into idolizing. How the human who helped you starts, in your private heart, to occupy a space that is too large for any human.

Our age has made an industry of this. We do not have idols of stone anymore. We have **algorithms** built to manufacture them — to take a person and inflate them, frame them, light them, until millions feel a devotion to a stranger that is frankly religious in shape. We call it being a fan. But watch what it does. It hands your inner life to a human being who cannot hold it. And when that human inevitably fails — because every human fails — the devotion curdles into a betrayal that wrecks something inside the one who gave it.

The human heart, it turns out, is built to attach **upward.** It is built to find something greater than itself and to pour devotion into it. That capacity is not a flaw. It is the most precious thing about you. The only question — the question your entire life is secretly answering — is: **where does the pouring stop? At a creature, or at the Creator?**

Now hear the verse again with this in your bones.

*Wa tuʿazzirūhu wa tuwaqqirūhu* — **support him, revere him.** Allah does not say: keep your distance from the Messenger. He does not say: be cool, be measured, don't get carried away. He commands the opposite. **Love him fiercely. Defend him with your body. Hold him heavy in your heart. Lower your voice before him.** The Qurʾān gives the Messenger a station so high that elsewhere it says, *whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah,* and *say: if you love Allah, follow me, and Allah will love you.* This is not a tradition that is shy about the Prophet's rank.

And **then** — *wa tusabbiḥūhu* — the verb turns. **Glorify Him.** God.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the genius of it? Allah does not protect you from the danger of misplaced worship by **dampening your love.** He does not make you a lukewarm believer who keeps everyone at arm's length to stay safe. He lets you love the Messenger all the way to the edge of the human — and then, at the precise millimeter where love would become worship, He moves the pronoun and catches you. *Tasbīḥ* is reserved. The summit is occupied. It is taken, eternally, by the One who alone deserves it.

This is the resolution to the deepest tension in the religious heart. **You do not have to choose between loving the creation and worshipping the Creator.** You were terrified you might — that to love the Messenger fully was to risk shirk, and to be safe from shirk was to love him coldly. The verse dissolves the fear. You give the Messenger *taʿzīr* and *tawqīr* — the fullness of human love, defense, reverence. You give Allah *tasbīḥ* — the worship that belongs to Him alone. **Two different verbs. Two different objects. No collision.**

And here is what it changes about tomorrow. Look at every love in your life — your shaykh, your parent, your spouse, the people you admire. The verse does not ask you to love them less. It asks you to **know which verb you are using.** Honor them, defend them, revere the righteous among them — *tuʿazzirūhu, tuwaqqirūhu.* But keep *tasbīḥ* — keep the part of you that declares something flawless, beyond limit, ultimately worthy — **reserved.** The moment you feel a human starting to occupy that final room in your heart, remember the *waqf*. Remember al-Baghawī's stop. *And here, there is a pause.* The room is taken.

### Theme Two: The rhythm that holds a soul together — *bukratan wa aṣīlā*

Now look at the last two words, because they do something the rest of the verse has not done. *Wa tusabbiḥūhu* — glorify Him — **بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا** — *bukratan wa aṣīlā* — **morning and evening.**

Start, again, with your own life. You know that a soul without rhythm comes apart. The research on this is not even controversial anymore — human beings are circadian creatures; we are stabilized by repetition, by anchors in the day, by the small rituals that tell the nervous system *you are held, you are oriented, the day has a shape.* Strip those away — let a person drift through formless time, no morning marker, no evening close — and watch how quickly the inner world frays. We are not built for the shapeless. We are built for the **rhythm.**

Now notice something precise in the Arabic. *Bukratan* and *aṣīlā* are both **indefinite** — there is a *tanwīn*, the little "-an / -ā" ending, on each. Allah does not say *al-bukrah wa al-aṣīl* — "**the** morning and **the** evening," one specific dawn, one specific dusk. He says *bukratan wa aṣīlā* — **a** morning and **an** evening. Any morning. Every morning. Morning **as such.** The indefinite opens it up into a cycle that never closes. Not one dawn, but dawn after dawn after dawn, for as long as you live.

So the glorification of Allah is not given to you as an event. It is given as a **rhythm.** Bracketed at both ends of the day. *Bukratan* — when the day is born, before the world has its hands on you. *Aṣīlā* — when the light slants gold and the day is spent and you are emptied out.

[PAUSE]

Think about what those two moments are. The morning is the moment of **possibility** — before the emails, before the demands, before the self has armored up for the day's battles. To glorify Allah *bukratan* is to hand the day to its Owner before anyone else can claim it. And the evening — *aṣīlā* — is the moment of **accounting,** when the day has taken what it wanted from you and you are tired and the wins and losses are tallied. To glorify Allah *aṣīlā* is to close the day by returning it, used-up, to the One who lent it.

And here is what is so merciful about pairing *tasbīḥ* with these two times. We just learned that *tasbīḥ* — glorifying Allah, declaring Him beyond every imperfection — is the verb the soul most needs and most forgets. It is the antidote to making idols. And Allah does not leave it to your inspiration. He does not say "glorify Him whenever you feel moved." He **schedules** it. He hangs it on the most reliable rhythm in your existence — the turning of the day itself. As long as the sun rises and sets, you have your two appointments with the truth that **God alone is glorified.**

[PAUSE]

Because look at the danger we found in the first theme. The drift toward idolizing the creation — that does not happen in a dramatic moment. It happens by erosion, slowly, in the unattended hours. And what guards against slow erosion? Not a single grand declaration. A **rhythm.** Morning and evening, you re-draw the line. Morning and evening, you remind the heart: the summit is taken. Twice a day you perform the *waqf* — the stop — that keeps your devotion ordered.

This is why the same pairing, *bukratan wa aṣīlā*, echoes across the Qurʾān as the heartbeat of the worshipping life. It is the rhythm of the angels, the rhythm of the remembrancers, the rhythm Allah ties to His own name being raised in houses He permitted to be built.

What does it change about tomorrow? This: **do not wait to feel devotion.** Build the appointments. Let there be a morning moment — even a breath of *subḥān Allāh* before your feet hit the floor — where you hand the unspent day to its Owner. Let there be an evening moment where you give the spent day back. You are not waiting for the rare hour when your heart soars. You are doing what the verse does — anchoring the soul to a rhythm that holds it together when feeling fails. The morning and the evening will come whether you are ready or not. Meet them with His name.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse at once, because its shape is the proof of everything we found.

Watch it move:

1. **لِّتُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ** — *that you may believe in Allah and His Messenger.* The verse begins in the unseen — **belief, in the heart**, and it names both Allah and the Messenger together, side by side, the two objects of faith.

2. **وَتُعَزِّرُوهُ** — *and support him.* Now belief moves **outward** into action — the body, the defense of the Messenger in the world.

3. **وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ** — *and revere him.* And back **inward** — the posture of the heart toward him, the awe, the weight.

4. **وَتُسَبِّحُوهُ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا** — *and glorify Him, morning and evening.* And finally **upward and out of time** — past the Messenger, to Allah alone, sealed into the eternal rhythm of the rising and setting day.

Belief → defense → reverence → glorification. Heart, then world, then heart, then heaven. And along the way, the pronouns rise toward the Messenger and then turn, at the last verb, to God. **The architecture itself is the journey of a believing life** — and its summit, its final word, is not the Messenger however beloved, but *tasbīḥ* of the Lord, twice a day, forever.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar found a line — three identical pronouns, two bending to the Messenger, the third turning to Allah. The themes found why that line is the most important one a human can keep — because the heart is built to pour devotion upward, and its entire fate hangs on where the pouring stops.

The grammar found a rhythm — *bukratan wa aṣīlā*, indefinite, unending. The themes found why the soul cannot survive without it — because the line, once drawn, must be re-drawn every day against the slow erosion that turns love into idolatry.

One verse. It tells you to love the Messenger with everything — and it tells you, in the same breath, in the same pronoun, that glory belongs to God alone. And then it hands you the schedule for remembering the difference.

### Questions to carry

- Where in your life have you let a human being — a teacher, a leader, a parent, a figure you admire — quietly move into the room that *tasbīḥ* should occupy? What would it mean to honor them fully **and** keep that room reserved?
- The verse asks for *taʿzīr* — honor that **defends.** Whose honor are you willing to actually stand for, not just feel warmly about?
- Is your remembrance of Allah an **event** that depends on your mood, or a **rhythm** that holds whether you feel it or not?
- When was the last morning you handed the unspent day to its Owner before the world claimed it? When was the last evening you gave the spent day back?
- If your love for the creation is meant to point *past* the creation to the Creator — does it? Or does it stop at the gift and forget the Giver?

### One-sentence distillation

Love and defend and revere the Messenger to the fullest height the human can be raised — and the moment your devotion reaches glorification, let the very grammar of the verse turn it, morning and evening, to Allah alone.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to be afraid of loving deeply. That was the lie — that to keep your *tawḥīd* safe you had to keep your heart cold. This verse invites the opposite. Love the Messenger fiercely. Defend him. Hold him heavy and dear. And know, with the certainty of the pronoun that turns, that the highest room in you is already, eternally, taken. Let that knowledge free you to love everything else in its proper place — and to begin, this morning and this evening, the rhythm that keeps the line.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who alone are glorified beyond every imperfection — give us the love that defends Your Messenger and the reverence that lowers our voices before him, and keep for Yourself alone the worship that is Yours alone. Teach our hearts the difference, and bind us to it, morning and evening, until the last of our days. *Subḥānaka* — morning and evening — *subḥānak.*
