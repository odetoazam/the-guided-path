# The Still Photograph and the Time-Lapse: How One Seed Becomes a Field

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by reading you the verse, and then I want to put you somewhere.

> **مُّحَمَّدٌ رَّسُولُ ٱللَّهِ ۚ وَٱلَّذِينَ مَعَهُۥٓ أَشِدَّآءُ عَلَى ٱلْكُفَّارِ رُحَمَآءُ بَيْنَهُمْ**
>
> *Muḥammadun rasūlu llāh, wa-lladhīna maʿahu ashiddāʾu ʿalā l-kuffāri ruḥamāʾu baynahum...*
>
> *Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah; and those who are with him are firm against the deniers, merciful among themselves. You see them bowing and prostrating, seeking bounty from Allah and His pleasure. Their mark is on their faces from the trace of prostration. That is their likeness in the Torah. And their likeness in the Gospel is like a seed that sends out its shoot, then reinforces it, so it grows thick and stands firm upon its stalk, delighting the sowers — that He may enrage the deniers through them. Allah has promised those among them who believe and do righteous deeds forgiveness and a great reward.*

Now the place I want to put you.

### Rebuild the world

It is the sixth year after the Hijrah. The Prophet ﷺ has set out from Madinah with around fourteen hundred companions, unarmed for battle, dressed in the white of pilgrimage. They are going to the Sacred House. They have seen it in a dream — the Prophet ﷺ saw them entering Makkah, performing ʿumrah, heads shaved, in peace. So they came. Hearts pounding. Years of exile, and now — home.

And they were stopped.

At a place called Hudaybiyyah, on the edge of the sanctuary, the Quraysh blocked them. No pilgrimage this year. And then came the negotiation, and the terms of the treaty — terms that felt, to many of those fourteen hundred, like swallowing glass. *Go back this year. Come next year.* And the clause that nearly broke them: if a Makkan flees to you as a Muslim, you return him; if one of you flees to them, they keep him. It read like surrender. ʿUmar himself came to the Prophet ﷺ, his chest tight with it: *Are we not upon the truth? Then why do we give in?*

They turned the camels around and went home — without the Kaʿbah, without the ʿumrah, carrying a treaty that looked like defeat.

And on that road back, Surah Al-Fath descended. *Innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā* — **"Indeed, We have granted you a clear victory."**

[PAUSE]

A victory. The word Allah chose for the worst day many of them could remember was *victory*.

The entire surah is an argument with their eyes. It is Allah saying: *you are looking at a defeat; I am showing you a seed.* And every theme in the surah builds toward the final verse — this verse — which is where the seed is finally named. After all the talk of the treaty, the pledge under the tree, the promise of gardens, the surah does not end with an army. It does not end with a sword or a conquest.

It ends with a farmer, standing at the edge of a field, looking at a crop — and smiling.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to hold as we begin. This single verse describes one community **twice** — once as it appears in the Torah, and once as it appears in the Gospel. Two descriptions, side by side, of the same people.

Why two? Why would Allah, in the most economical speech ever spoken, give us the same portrait in two frames?

I am going to suggest to you that they are not the same portrait at all. One of them is a photograph. The other is a film. And the difference between them is the difference between *who you are* and *what you are becoming* — and by the end, I want you to see which one of those two you have been measuring your own faith by, and which one Allah measures it by.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Two Prepositions — *ʿalā* and *bayna*

Let me teach you something small about language before we touch the verse. In any language, the little words — the prepositions — carry geometry. They tell you *where things stand in relation to each other.*

Think about the English difference between "I stood **by** him" and "I stood **over** him." Same verb. Same two people. But "by him" puts you shoulder to shoulder, level, beside. "Over him" puts you above — dominant, looming. The whole relationship changed, and not one noun moved. Only the preposition.

Arabic is exquisitely sensitive to this. Hold that in mind, because Allah is about to describe the believers' character using two adjectives — and the entire meaning is carried not by the adjectives, but by the two tiny words attached to them.

Here is the phrase:

> **أَشِدَّآءُ عَلَى ٱلْكُفَّارِ رُحَمَآءُ بَيْنَهُمْ**
>
> *ashiddāʾu **ʿalā** l-kuffāri, ruḥamāʾu **baynahum***

"Firm **upon/against** the deniers, merciful **among/between** themselves."

Now look at the two prepositions.

*ʿalā* — "upon, over, against." This is the preposition of elevation, of being *above* something. When you are *ʿalā* something, you have the higher position; the thing is beneath you, and you are not moved by it. The classical commentators reach for an image here. Al-Baghawi says they are *ka-l-asadi ʿalā farīsatihi* — **like the lion over its prey** — no softness reaches them in that posture. Immovable. Above the pressure, not crushed under it.

*bayna* — "between, among." The corpus tags this word not even as a preposition but as a *locative* — a word of place. And its place is *inside.* Not above, not below — within the circle. When you are *bayna* people, you are level with them, woven among them.

Do you feel the geometry now? [PAUSE]

With the forces arrayed against the truth, the believer stands **above** — unbent, the higher ground, the lion that the prey cannot move. And among his own, the very same believer stands **within** — level, woven in, no one above and no one beneath.

The grammar has drawn a diagram of the believing heart. One posture facing outward. The opposite posture facing inward. And the verse never had to explain it — it just chose two prepositions and let the geometry speak.

Let's sit with this, because here is what's remarkable. We are tempted to think these are two different *kinds* of people: the fierce ones and the gentle ones. The verse refuses that. It is the *same* "those who are with him." The same person is *ashiddāʾ* in one direction and *ruḥamāʾ* in the other.

And now look closer at the two adjectives themselves.

### Element Two: One Temperature, Two Directions — the Intensive Forms

*Ashiddāʾ* (أَشِدَّاء) is the plural of *shadīd*, from the root *sh-d-d* — strength, firmness, intensity. *Ruḥamāʾ* (رُحَمَاء) is the plural of *raḥīm*, from *r-ḥ-m* — mercy, the same root as *Raḥmān, Raḥīm*, the womb itself.

Here is the thing I do not want you to miss. *Shadīd* and *raḥīm* are built on the **same intensive pattern** — the *faʿīl* form, the pattern Arabic uses for a quality that runs *deep and strong*. These are not mild words. *Shadīd* is not "somewhat firm." *Raḥīm* is not "a little kind." Both are turned up to maximum.

So the verse is not describing a person who is *intense* in one area and *lukewarm* in another. It is describing a person whose intensity is **the same temperature in both directions.** The exact heat that makes them immovable in the face of falsehood is the heat that makes them tender within the family of faith. It is one fire. It just burns toward two different things.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it dismantles something we believe about ourselves. We tend to think strength and tenderness are *opposite* settings — that you have a fixed amount of one or the other, and to add gentleness you must subtract firmness. As if the heart were a dial sliding between "hard" and "soft."

The verse says no. In the believer, *shidda* and *raḥma* are not on opposite ends of one dial. They are two outputs of a single, deep, intense soul — and a soul that has no *shidda* at all does not become "gentle," it becomes *weak*; and a soul that has no *raḥma* does not become "strong," it becomes *cruel*. The Quran is describing wholeness, not balance. Not a watered-down middle. A person fully strong **and** fully merciful, because both flow from the same depth.

You find the mirror of this in Surah al-Māʾidah, where Allah describes the people He loves: *adhillatin ʿalā l-muʾminīna aʿizzatin ʿalā l-kāfirīn* — "humble toward the believers, mighty against the deniers" (5:54). Al-Baghawi cites that very verse here, because it is the same architecture: one heart, two faces, and which face you meet depends entirely on which way you stand toward the truth.

### Element Three: The Chain of Growth — *akhraja, fa-āzara, fa-staghlaẓa, fa-stawā*

Now we come to the verse's beating heart, and I need to teach you one more small thing first — about a single letter.

In Arabic there is a letter, *fāʾ* (فَ), that you attach to the front of a verb to mean "and then — immediately." Grammarians call it *fāʾ al-taʿqīb*, the *fāʾ* of immediate succession. It is different from the ordinary "and" (*wa*), which just adds things in a loose pile. The *fāʾ* says: *this happened, and right on its heels, this — no gap, no pause.* It is the letter of momentum.

Now watch what Allah does with it. Here is the parable from the Gospel:

> **كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْـَٔهُۥ فَـَٔازَرَهُۥ فَٱسْتَغْلَظَ فَٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِۦ**
>
> *ka-zarʿin **akhraja** shaṭʾahu **fa**-āzarahu **fa**-staghlaẓa **fa**-stawā ʿalā sūqih*

"Like a seed that puts out its shoot, **then** reinforces it, **then** grows thick, **then** stands firm upon its stalk."

Four verbs. Three *fāʾ*s between them. Listen to the momentum.

First: **أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُ** — *akhraja shaṭʾahu*. The verb *akhraja* (root *kh-r-j*, Form IV) means "to bring out, send forth." And *shaṭʾ* — this is a precious, rare word. The *shaṭʾ* is the first tiny shoot, the seedling sprout, the slender green thread that breaks the soil. It is the most fragile thing in the whole agricultural world. One careless step ends it. **That is where the crop begins** — not as a field, but as a single thread of green.

Hold that, because at Hudaybiyyah, that is exactly what those fourteen hundred companions were. A *shaṭʾ.* A fragile shoot. A treaty that looked like it could be crushed by one careless step.

Then — *fāʾ*, immediately — **فَآزَرَهُ** — *fa-āzarahu*. The verb *āzara* means to support, to reinforce, to back something up and make it strong. The shoot is reinforced. What was a lone thread is braced.

Then — *fāʾ*, immediately — **فَٱسْتَغْلَظَ** — *fa-staghlaẓa*. This is Form X of the root *gh-l-ẓ*, thickness, coarseness, density. *Istaghlaẓa* — "it grew thick, it became stout." And here I want you to *hear* the word, not just read it. **Istaghlaẓa.** The mouth has to work. The *gh*, the *l*, the heavy *ẓ* — thick, dense consonants. The word is *thick in the mouth* the way the stalk is becoming thick in the field. The sound is doing the meaning. This is no accident in the speech of the One who chose every letter.

Then — *fāʾ*, immediately — **فَٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِ** — *fa-stawā ʿalā sūqih*. *Istawā* — it stood erect, balanced, upright — *ʿalā sūqih*, upon its stalks (*sūq*, the plural of *sāq*, the stem). The thread that broke the soil is now a crop standing tall on its own legs.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what those three *fāʾ*s just did? They removed every gap. The verse compresses the entire life of a plant — sprout, reinforcement, thickening, standing — into one unbroken rush. There is no pause where doubt could enter. No "and then, years later, perhaps." It is *shoot — braced — thick — standing.* The growth is depicted as **inexorable.** Once the seed is in the ground and Allah's hand is on it, the sequence cannot be stopped any more than you can stop a planted field from rising in spring.

And here is what's remarkable, and I almost don't want to say it out loud because I want you to feel it first: the *people who turned their camels around at Hudaybiyyah, heartbroken, were standing inside the first word of that sequence.* They were the *shaṭʾ.* They could not see the *istawā*. Nobody standing at the sprout can see the standing field. But Allah, who planted them, narrated the whole sequence in advance — and joined it with the letter that means *unstoppable.*

### Bridge

So look at what these three pieces of language have shown us. The two prepositions drew the geometry of a heart — above falsehood, within the family of faith. The two intensive adjectives showed us that this is one deep soul, not a watered-down compromise. And the chain of *fāʾ*s showed us that this community is not a static thing at all — it is a *growth*, narrated with the grammar of momentum, from a fragile thread to a standing crop.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself. About the shape of your own soul, and the shape of how anything good actually grows in this world.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Integrated Self — Why You Were Never Meant to Choose Between Strong and Kind

Let me start where you already live.

Somewhere along the way, most of us absorbed a quiet lie: that we have to *pick.* You can be the strong one or the soft one. The boundary-setter or the nurturer. The person who stands their ground or the person everyone feels safe with. And so we split ourselves. We build a hard outer shell for the world and we save our tenderness for a tiny protected interior — or, just as often, we do the reverse: we are endlessly accommodating to everyone outside and we bring our sharpest edges home, to the very people who deserve our softness most.

Be honest with yourself for a second. Which way do you split?

Because most of us have it *exactly backwards from the verse.* We are *ruḥamāʾ* — soft, agreeable, conflict-avoidant — out there, toward pressures and falsehoods that should find us immovable. We bend where we should stand. And then we are *ashiddāʾ* — sharp, short, hard — *baynahum*, at home, among our own, toward the spouse and the child and the brother who should meet our mercy. We stand where we should bend.

The verse holds up a mirror and the reflection is reversed. *Ashiddāʾu ʿalā l-kuffār* — firm where it counts, toward what is genuinely corrosive, toward falsehood and oppression and the pull to abandon the truth. *Ruḥamāʾu baynahum* — and then, the moment you turn toward your own, the fire becomes warmth.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the movement I want to walk you through, because it goes deeper than "be tough outside, soft inside."

We said the *shidda* and the *raḥma* come from the same intensity. Sit with what that means for your own life. It means **the firmness and the gentleness are not in competition for the same resource.** When you imagine they are — when you think "if I'm too kind, I'll be walked over; if I'm too firm, I'll be cruel" — you are treating your soul like a fixed pie, slicing it between two slices. And so you end up *mediocre at both*: a little bit firm, a little bit kind, never fully either, and exhausted from managing the trade-off.

The verse says: stop slicing. Deepen the whole soul instead. A person who is *deeply* anchored in the truth does not have to ration their kindness — their firmness is held somewhere else entirely, in their relationship to falsehood, not in their relationship to people. They can pour out mercy on their family without fear, *because* their backbone is not made of harshness toward their family; it is made of immovability toward what is wrong. The strength is on a completely different axis from the warmth. That is why both can run at full intensity at once.

And here is where it becomes worship, not just psychology. Look at what sits *between* the two faces in the verse. Immediately after "firm against the deniers, merciful among themselves," before anything else, comes: *tarāhum rukkaʿan sujjadan* — "you see them bowing, prostrating." The thing that lets a soul be both fully strong and fully merciful is *placed right there in the middle of the description*: a life spent on its knees before Allah.

Why does the prostration go in the middle? Because the *sujūd* is where the dial gets recalibrated. The person who empties themselves before Allah every day has nothing to prove to other people — so they have no need to be hard with their own to feel strong, and no fear of being soft with their own that they'll be diminished. The bowing handles the ego. And once the ego is handled, *shidda* and *raḥma* stop fighting over you, and each goes to its rightful place.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the specific, uncomfortable application. Pick the relationship where you are most often *ashiddāʾ* — sharp, impatient, hard — when you should be *ruḥamāʾ.* Almost certainly it is someone close. A parent. A spouse. A child. This week, when the sharpness rises toward them, catch it and ask one question: *am I being firm toward a falsehood here, or am I just discharging my ego onto someone who is safe?* Nine times out of ten it is the second. And the cure is not in the relationship at all — it is in the *sujūd* the night before, where you put the ego down so it is not lying around the house waiting to be thrown at the people you love.

### Theme Two: The Field and the Farmer — Why Growth, Not Arrival, Is the Whole Point

Now the second theme, and I want to start, again, where you live.

We are people of the instant. We have been trained by everything around us to expect the *result* now — the notification, the delivery, the transformation. And we have quietly imported that expectation into our faith. We want to be *done.* We want to wake up one morning already patient, already disciplined, already the person we keep promising Allah we will become. And when we are not — when we are still struggling with the same sin in year three that we struggled with in year one — we conclude that nothing is growing, that we are a failure, that the seed was barren.

Now look at what Allah shows the Gospel-portrait of His people to be. Not a finished monument. Not a completed building. **A seed in mid-growth.**

*Ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾahu* — like a seed that *put out its shoot.* And remember what the *shaṭʾ* is: the most fragile thing in the field, a green thread one step from being crushed. That is the *first frame.* Allah did not begin the parable with the harvest. He began it with the most vulnerable possible stage and called it the start of glory.

Let that land on you if you are early in your turning back to Allah, if your prayer is shaky and your habits half-formed and you feel like a fraud. **You are a *shaṭʾ.*** That is not an insult. That is the name Allah gives to the beginning of the very crop that ends up *delighting the farmers* and *enraging the deniers.* The shoot is not a lesser thing than the field. It is the field, at the only stage it could possibly be in right now.

And then — the three *fāʾ*s. Watch the movements, because each one transforms the one before:

The shoot is **braced** (*fa-āzarahu*) — what was alone is now reinforced. This is the stage where the community holds the new one up. Where the older shoots support the younger. You are not meant to grow alone; the parable itself has the plant strengthening *its own offshoots.* The reinforcement is built into the design.

Then it **thickens** (*fa-staghlaẓa*) — and here is the part we resist. Thickening is slow. It is invisible from day to day. Nobody watches a stem get thicker; you only notice it was happening when one day the thing that used to bend in the wind doesn't bend anymore. **Spiritual growth is mostly *istighlāẓ*** — the imperceptible thickening that you cannot feel happening and can only verify in retrospect. The prayer that used to be agony and is now, somehow, just normal. The anger that used to own you and now, somehow, you can see coming. You did not feel it thicken. It thickened anyway.

Then it **stands** (*fa-stawā ʿalā sūqih*) — upright, on its own stem, no longer needing to be propped. The new Muslim becomes the one who supports new Muslims. The shaky one becomes the steady one. The shoot that had to be braced is now the stalk that braces others.

[PAUSE]

And now the line that reframes everything: *yuʿjibu z-zurrāʿ* — **"it delights the sowers."** The farmer who planted it, looking out over the field, is *amazed and gladdened* by it.

Who is the sower? At the human level, the Prophet ﷺ and those who plant faith. But sit with the image. The deepest joy in the verse belongs to the one who *planted* and then *watched it grow* — not to the one who arrived instantly. The whole parable is a tribute to **patient cultivation.** Allah did not say "like a field that appeared overnight." He chose the one image in human experience that *cannot* be rushed, that *must* take its season, and He made *that* the description of His beloved community. Growth — slow, staged, organic, season-bound growth — is not the obstacle on the way to the prize. **Growth is the prize.** It is what delights the sower.

There is one more movement, and it is the sharpest. *Li-yaghīẓa bihimu l-kuffār* — "**so that** He may enrage the deniers through them." Listen to that little word *yaghīẓa* — from the root *gh-y-ẓ*, a rage that chokes, that boils in the chest. And hear it against *istaghlaẓa* from a moment ago — both carry that heavy, throat-thickening sound. The crop *thickened*; and the same thickening *chokes* the throats of those who hoped it would wither.

Here is what I want you to see in that. The enraging of the deniers is not something the believers *do.* They are not out there trying to provoke anyone — look at the verse, they are *bowing, prostrating, seeking Allah's bounty and pleasure*, heads down, eyes on their Lord. The rage of the opposition is a **byproduct of their growth**, not a goal of their effort. They simply grew — green, thick, standing, beautiful — and the mere existence of the flourishing field was unbearable to those who wanted it dead.

Sit with how freeing that is. You do not have to spend a single ounce of your energy on those who resent your faith. You do not have to argue them into silence or perform your strength for them. You just have to *grow.* Keep your head in the *sujūd*, keep seeking *faḍlan mina llāhi wa-riḍwānan* — His bounty and His pleasure — and let the flourishing do what flourishing does. The field does not strategize against the man who wished it dead. It just stands there, golden and alive, and that is the whole rebuke.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Stop auditing yourself for *arrival* and start auditing yourself for *direction.* Tonight, do not ask "have I become the person I want to be yet?" — that is the instant-result lie, and the answer will always crush you. Ask instead: *am I a little thicker in the stem than I was a year ago?* Is one thing that used to bend me now holding? That is *istighlāẓ.* That is real. And if even one thing has thickened, then the seed is alive, the sower is pleased, and your only job is the next season's growth — not the harvest, which was never yours to schedule.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Architectural Reveal: The Photograph and the Film

Now I can show you the thing I promised at the very beginning — why the verse describes one community *twice.*

Go back and look at the structure. The verse splits cleanly in half.

**The first half** — *firm against the deniers, merciful among themselves, you see them bowing, prostrating, their mark upon their faces from the trace of prostration* — Allah ends by saying: *dhālika mathaluhum fī t-Tawrāh.* **"That is their likeness in the Torah."**

**The second half** — *like a seed that puts out its shoot, then braces it, then thickens, then stands* — Allah introduces by saying: *wa-mathaluhum fī l-Injīl.* **"And their likeness in the Gospel."**

Now look at *what kind* of description each one is.

The Torah-portrait is **static.** It is a list of *qualities*: firm, merciful, bowing, prostrating, marked by worship. It is a *snapshot.* It tells you **who they are** at any given instant — freeze the frame, and this is what you see. It is a *photograph.*

The Gospel-portrait is **dynamic.** It is not a single quality at all — it is a *sequence of verbs*: sprouted, braced, thickened, stood. It is a *time-lapse.* It tells you **what they become** over a season — let the frames *run*, and this is the motion you see. It is a *film.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it now? They are not two descriptions of the same thing. They are two *kinds* of seeing the same people. One captures their *character* — frozen, true at every instant. The other captures their *trajectory* — unfolding, true only across time. The Torah shows you the *cross-section.* The Gospel shows you the *growth.* And you need both to see a believer truly, because a human being is *both* a quality you can name today *and* a motion you can only trace across years.

And here is why this is the perfect ending for the surah that began at Hudaybiyyah. The companions, on that heartbroken road home, were judging themselves by the *photograph* — and the photograph, that day, looked like defeat. Stopped pilgrims. A humiliating treaty. A frozen frame of loss. But Allah closes the surah by handing them the *film* — *and you should see what this seed becomes.* The frame they were trapped in was just *akhraja shaṭʾahu*, the first green thread. The defeat was the sprout. And the sprout is joined, by the unbreakable *fāʾ*, to the standing field.

### Bringing It Together

Now see how the two halves of our reflection were the same insight from two directions.

The *integrated self* — strong and merciful at once, from one deep soul — that is the **photograph.** It is who you are when the frame is frozen: a single heart, immovable toward falsehood, tender toward the family of faith, recalibrated daily in the *sujūd.*

The *field and the farmer* — sprout to standing crop, growth as the prize — that is the **film.** It is what you become when the frames run: not arriving, but thickening, season by season, into the very firmness and mercy the photograph describes.

And they meet in one place. *You become the photograph by living the film.* You do not wake up one day already *ashiddāʾu ʿalā l-kuffār, ruḥamāʾu baynahum.* That integrated heart is the *standing crop* — the *istawā ʿalā sūqih.* You get there by being a faithful *shaṭʾ* today, letting yourself be braced by the community, and trusting the slow, invisible *istighlāẓ* that you cannot feel but Allah can see — all of it held together by the head that keeps returning to the floor in prostration.

### Questions to Carry

1. In the relationship where I am sharpest, am I being *ashiddāʾ* toward an actual falsehood — or just discharging my ego onto someone who is safe to wound?
2. Where in my life have I quietly reversed the verse — soft toward what is corrosive, hard toward what is precious?
3. Am I measuring my faith by the *photograph* (have I arrived?) when Allah measures it by the *film* (am I growing?)?
4. What is one thing in me that has *thickened* — *istaghlaẓa* — in the last year, that I have refused to give myself credit for?
5. Am I spending energy trying to silence those who resent my faith — when the verse says my only job is to grow, and let the flourishing do the rest?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Allah's beloved community is one deep soul wearing two faces — firm toward falsehood, tender toward its own — and Allah does not ask you to *arrive* as that person overnight, but to be a faithful green shoot today and trust the unstoppable, invisible growth He has already narrated to its standing harvest.

### The Closing Invitation

So here is the shift I want to leave with you. Stop standing at the edge of your own field, demanding to see the harvest, despairing because all you can find is a fragile green thread. That thread *is the verse.* That thread is *akhraja shaṭʾahu* — the exact stage Allah chose to begin the description of the people who delight Him. Your job is not to be the standing crop tonight. Your job is to keep your head returning to the floor, to seek nothing but *faḍlan mina llāhi wa-riḍwānan* — His bounty and His pleasure — and to let yourself be braced by people who are further along, and to trust that the thickening is happening even on the days you cannot feel it.

The sower is patient. He planted you knowing the season it would take. And the same verse that calls you a fragile shoot also promises, in its final breath — *waʿada llāhu lladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāti minhum maghfiratan wa-ajran ʿaẓīmā* — to **"those who believe and do righteous deeds among them, forgiveness and a great reward."** Not to the ones who arrived. To the ones who *kept growing.*

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who planted the seed and know its season — make us a faithful shoot today, and do not let us despair at our own fragility. Brace us with people of truth, thicken in us what we cannot feel thickening, and let our only aim be Your bounty and Your pleasure. Make us firm where firmness is worship and merciful where mercy is worship, and gather us, when the field finally stands, among those You promised forgiveness and a great reward. *Āmīn.*
