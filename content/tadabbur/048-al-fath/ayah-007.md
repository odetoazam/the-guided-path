# The Hosts You Cannot See

## وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًۭا

*Wa lillāhi junūdu-s-samāwāti wal-arḍ, wa kāna-llāhu ʿazīzan ḥakīmā.*

**"And to Allah belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth. And Allah is ever All-Mighty, All-Wise."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing at Hudaybiyya

Let me take you to a plain about nine miles outside of Mecca. The year is the sixth after the Hijra.

Fourteen hundred men have walked here from Medina. They are not an army. They left their weapons behind — or carried only the sheathed swords a traveler is permitted. They are wearing the two white cloths of pilgrimage. They have brought sacrificial animals, garlanded and marked. Every external signal they can send says the same thing: *we have come to worship, not to fight.* They have come to make ʿumrah at the House their grandfather Ibrāhīm built.

And they are stopped.

Mecca will not let them in. Messengers go back and forth. There is tension, a near-skirmish, a pledge taken under a tree. And then — a treaty. The Treaty of Hudaybiyya.

Now, sit with what that treaty actually said, because this is the soil everything grows from. The Muslims would turn around and go home **without** performing the pilgrimage they had traveled all this way for. They could come back next year. If anyone fled from Mecca to the Muslims, the Muslims had to return him; but if a Muslim fled to Mecca, Mecca would *not* return him. And when the document was being written, the Meccans objected to the words "Muḥammad, the Messenger of Allah" — and the Prophet ﷺ, peace be upon him, allowed them to be erased.

Put yourself among those fourteen hundred. You have walked for days. You can see the hills of Mecca. And you are being told: go home, you got nothing, and sign terms that read — to every set of eyes in that camp — like the terms of the weaker party. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, may Allah be pleased with him, the man whose whole being was certainty, came to the Prophet ﷺ in distress and asked: *Are we not upon the truth? Then why should we give in regarding our religion?*

That is the emotional climate. Not fear of death. Something subtler and more corrosive: the feeling of being **small**. Outnumbered. Out-maneuvered. The quiet humiliation of having to accept terms when every cell in your body says you deserved better.

And it is into *that* climate — into a surah that opens with the staggering words **"Indeed, We have granted you a clear victory"** (*innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā*) — calling this apparent surrender a *victory* — it is into that climate that our verse falls.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. There are people standing on that plain, and standing in the shadows of Medina, who have done the math. They have counted the believers. They have counted the swords. They have counted Mecca, and beyond Mecca they have counted Persia and Rome, the two superpowers of the known world. And their arithmetic has produced a conclusion they keep quiet but hold firmly: *this small band of men cannot possibly win. Allah's promise to this Prophet will fail.*

Our verse is the answer to their arithmetic.

But here is what I want you to hold onto, because it is the strangest and most beautiful thing about these eight words: **this exact sentence — "And to Allah belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth" — appears twice in this same surah, just three verses apart. And each time, it ends with a different name of Allah.**

Why? What changes between verse four and verse seven that the same sentence must wear a different seal?

That question is going to take us somewhere. Let's begin.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "To Allah belong" — The Word Order That Argues

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds a sentence, because the whole force of this opening lives in an arrangement we don't have in English.

In ordinary speech, you might say: "The hosts of the heavens and earth belong to Allah." Subject first, then where it belongs. That is the calm, neutral order. Information delivered.

But Arabic can pick up the ending and throw it to the front. It can say, in effect: *"And **to Allah** — the hosts of the heavens and the earth."* The phrase that tells you *whose* they are comes first, before you even hear *what* is being owned.

Grammarians have a name for this — *taqdīm*, the fronting of what would normally come later. And there is a settled principle about what fronting does: **putting something first that you'd expect to come later creates emphasis and, very often, exclusivity.** It says: *this, and not anything else.* It is the difference between "I gave the book to him" and "It was to **him** I gave the book." The second one is answering a silent challenge. It is correcting someone.

Now look at our verse. It does not begin "the hosts belong to Allah." It begins **"wa lillāhi"** — *and to Allah* — with that little prefixed *lām*, the letter that in Arabic carries the meaning of ownership and belonging, the *lām* of possession. The owner is named before the owned. The destination is announced before the cargo.

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with why.

Remember who is being answered. The verse just before this one described people — hypocrites and idolaters — who **"assumed an assumption of evil"** about Allah (*ẓanna ẓanna-s-saw'*). And what was their assumption? Read it in the historical light al-Qurṭubī preserves: when the treaty was signed, one of the leading hypocrites is reported to have sneered — *Does Muḥammad imagine that if he makes peace with the people of Mecca, or conquers it, he'll have no enemy left? Where then are Persia? Where is Rome?* As if to say: you are counting one small city, and forgetting two empires.

Their entire error was a **misattribution of ownership**. They looked at the field of power and assigned the armies to Persia, to Rome, to Mecca, to the visible holders of force. They drew up a ledger of who had the soldiers, and Allah's side of the ledger looked thin.

So the verse does not gently inform them. It **corrects** them, and it corrects them with word order. *And to **Allah** — belong the hosts.* The ownership is fronted because the entire dispute is about ownership. You thought the armies belonged to the empires? No. **To Allah.** First word. Before anything else. The grammar itself is seizing back a title deed the doubters had quietly transferred to someone else.

This is the first thing the verse does, and it does it before you even reach the noun. **It moves the soldiers of the universe out of the column marked "Persia and Rome" and into the column marked "Allah" — and it does it by the order of the words alone.**

### Element Two: *Junūd* — What Exactly Are These "Hosts"?

Now the noun itself. **جُنُودُ** — *junūd*. The plural of *jund*. We translate it "hosts," "armies," "soldiers."

Here is where I want you to resist the first picture that comes to mind. When we hear "the armies of the heavens and the earth," we reach immediately for angels — and yes, the angels are in this. We know from elsewhere in the Qur'an that on the Day of Badr the believers were aided by ranks of angels, that Allah sent down *junūdan lam tarawhā* — "hosts you did not see" (9:40, 33:9). The angels are unquestionably among these hosts.

But notice that the verse does **not** say "the angels." It says *junūd as-samāwāti wal-arḍ* — the hosts **of the heavens and the earth.** And the classical commentators sat with that breadth and refused to shrink it.

Al-Qurṭubī records the view plainly: *"yadkhulu fīhi jamīʿ al-makhlūqāt"* — **all created things enter into this.** Every created thing is a soldier of Allah. Not a metaphor for being obedient — a literal member of the standing army of the King.

[PAUSE]

Stay with that, because it changes how you see the world outside your window.

If the hosts of the heavens and the earth all belong to Allah, then the wind that scattered the confederate armies at the Battle of the Trench was a soldier under orders. The sea that opened for Mūsā and closed on Pharaoh was a soldier. The tiny birds that pelted the army of the elephant were soldiers. The cold, the heat, the fever in an enemy's camp, the rain at the right hour, the dust storm, the heart attack, the misplaced message, the sudden change of mind in a king's court — soldiers. The cell in your own body that turns malignant or refuses to; the clot that forms or dissolves — soldiers.

We think of an army as the thing standing on the field holding weapons. The Qur'an is telling the people at Hudaybiyya — who could see exactly how few weapons stood on their field — that **the field they could see was not the battlefield.** The real muster roll of the universe was never visible to them. They counted fourteen hundred men. They should have been counting *the heavens and the earth*, every atom of which already answers to the One whose promise they were trusting.

And notice the pairing: **the heavens AND the earth.** Not just the lofty unseen forces above — the angels, the cosmic powers. Also the earth. Also the ordinary, the near, the underfoot. The dust you are standing on at Hudaybiyya is part of the army you think you don't have.

The disbelievers did the arithmetic of the visible. The verse hands them a calculator that includes the invisible — and tells them their sum was off by *everything*.

### Element Three: *Wa kāna* — The Word That Reaches Back Before Time

Now we come to a small word that English almost cannot translate without lying. The verse continues: **وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ** — *wa kāna-llāh*. Most translations render it "And Allah **is**" — but the verb *kāna* is, on its face, a past-tense verb. *Was.* "And Allah **was** Mighty, Wise."

So which is it — *is*, or *was*? And why would the verse describe Allah's might and wisdom in the **past** tense, here, in the moment His people most need to know He *is* mighty *right now*?

Let me teach you the rule, because it is one of the most quietly profound features of Qur'anic Arabic. When *kāna* is attached to Allah and followed by His attributes — *kāna-llāhu ʿazīzan ḥakīmā*, *kāna-llāhu ghafūran raḥīmā* — the grammarians and commentators understood it not as a past that has ended but as **a description of timeless, unceasing reality.** Al-Jalālayn, commenting on this very phrase, captures it in four words: *"lam yazal muttaṣifan bidhālik"* — **"He has never ceased to be so."**

[PAUSE]

Feel the difference. "Allah is Mighty" is true of this moment. But "Allah *kāna* Mighty" reaches backward through all of time and says: there was never an instant in which He was not. Before Hudaybiyya. Before Mecca was built. Before Persia and Rome were empires. Before the heavens and the earth whose hosts He owns even existed. He *was* Mighty. He *was* Wise. The attribute is not a response to the crisis. It is older than the universe the crisis is happening in.

Why does this matter so much to the man standing at Hudaybiyya feeling small?

Because the temptation in the moment of apparent defeat is to think Allah's might is something that *switches on* when we win and *switches off* when we lose. To read victory as evidence He is strong and setback as evidence He has, for now, withdrawn. The *kāna* forecloses that entire way of thinking. His might did not increase the day of Badr and it did not decrease the day of Hudaybiyya. **He was Mighty while you signed a treaty that felt like surrender. He was Mighty the whole time you were walking home empty-handed.** The unchanging tense is telling you that what *looks* like the ebb and flow of divine support is only the ebb and flow of *your sight*. The Mighty One was never anywhere other than where He has eternally been.

So the little word *kāna* does something the present tense could not. It detaches Allah's might from your scoreboard. It tells you His strength is not a variable in your equation — it is the constant that was true before your equation was ever written.

### The Bridge

Look at what these eight words have done with three pieces of grammar.

The fronting moved the armies of the universe out of the doubters' ledger and back to their rightful Owner. The word *junūd* expanded "your tiny band" into "the heavens and the earth." And the verb *kāna* lifted Allah's might out of time altogether, so that no defeat could be read as its absence.

That is the architecture of consolation, built syllable by syllable.

But now I want to ask what this reveals — not about words, but about reality itself. Because there is a question we have not yet answered. The question of the two endings. And to reach it, we have to leave grammar and walk into the human heart.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Arithmetic of the Visible

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You have, at some point, lain awake at night doing math. Not numbers on paper — the other kind. The kind where you total up the forces arrayed against you and total up what you have to meet them with, and the second number is so much smaller than the first that your chest tightens. The bills against the income. The diagnosis against the body. The size of the wrong done to you against your power to make it right. The drift in a marriage against your strength to pull it back. The doubt in your own faith against the certainty you wish you had.

This is the most natural thing a human being does. We count. We count what we can see, because what we can see is all the data we have. And almost always, the count comes out against us. The visible odds are bad. They are *usually* bad, because the things that frighten us are precisely the things that look bigger than us.

[PAUSE]

Now I want you to see that the people Allah is correcting in this passage were not fools. The hypocrite who said *"where are Persia and Rome?"* was, by the arithmetic of the visible, **completely correct.** Fourteen hundred unarmed pilgrims against two empires and a hostile Mecca — if you score that on what the eye can count, it is not even close. He didn't make an error of multiplication. He made an error of **inventory.** He counted only what he could see and called that the total.

And that is exactly our error, almost every night we lie awake. Our despair is rarely irrational. It is *under-informed*. It is arithmetic done on a partial inventory and mistaken for the full sum.

So watch what the verse does. It does not tell the frightened believer, *your odds are actually good.* It does not lie about the visible. It says something far stranger: **the visible was never the battlefield.** *Wa lillāhi junūdu-s-samāwāti wal-arḍ.* The column you forgot to add — the column with the heavens and the earth in it, the winds and the angels and the cells and the kings' hearts and the very dust — that column belongs entirely to the One who made you the promise. Your inventory wasn't slightly off. It was missing creation.

Move through this in stages, the way the heart actually moves.

First, the recognition: *my fear is real, but my count is incomplete.* That alone loosens something.

Then, deeper: *the part of the inventory I cannot see is not neutral — it belongs to Allah.* The unseen is not an empty darkness where anything might be lurking. It is a barracks, and the soldiers in it answer to my Lord.

And deeper still — here is where it turns, so stay with me — **the very fact that I cannot see them is part of the test.** Allah repeatedly sends "hosts you did not see." Why unseen? Because if you could see the angelic ranks standing behind your fourteen hundred, there would be no trust, only sight. The invisibility of the army is not a deprivation. It is the precise condition under which *tawakkul*, reliance, becomes possible at all. You are not asked to believe in odds you can verify. You are asked to trust an Owner whose forces are, by design, off your screen.

[PAUSE]

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you do with the count. Not stop counting — you are a human being, you will count. But when the visible total comes back against you, you have a second move now. You can say: *this is the arithmetic of the visible, and it was never meant to be the whole sum. The rest of the ledger belongs to Allah.* You hand the unseen column to its Owner. That is what *tawakkul* actually is — not pretending the bad odds aren't bad, but knowing that the odds you can see are a fragment of a battlefield you can't.

The believers at Hudaybiyya went home empty-handed. Within two years, Mecca opened to them almost without a sword raised. The hosts they couldn't see had been moving the entire time they thought they had lost.

### Theme Two: Might That Is Married to Wisdom

Now the second movement, and it begins with a frustration you already know.

Imagine you believe — really believe — the first theme. Allah owns the hosts of the heavens and the earth. His power is total, eternal, unceasing. Good. Then comes the obvious, aching question, the one that has shaken faith in every generation:

*Then why doesn't He use it? If He has every army, why am I still losing? Why did the believers, with the angels at their command, get turned away from the House and made to sign a humiliating treaty? If You have all the soldiers, Lord — where were they at Hudaybiyya?*

This is not a cynic's question. This is ʿUmar's question. *Are we not upon the truth? Then why do we give in?* It comes from the most sincere place in the believing heart.

And the verse anticipates it. Because notice — it does not end with one name. It ends with **two**, bound together: *ʿazīzan ḥakīmā* — **Mighty, Wise.**

Let me show you why those two, side by side, are the entire answer.

[PAUSE]

Think about power and wisdom as two separate things in the world you know.

Power without wisdom is the toddler with the hammer, the dictator with the army, the strong man who breaks everything he touches because strength is all he has. Raw might, unguided, is not a blessing — it is a terror. We have all been hurt by someone who had power over us and no wisdom in how to use it.

And wisdom without power is the wise old advisor with no authority, the brilliant plan no one will fund, the person who knows exactly what should happen and can do nothing to make it happen. Wisdom without the strength to act is just a beautiful, useless ache.

Now — *al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm.* The Mighty AND the Wise. **The only safe possessor of total power is one who possesses total wisdom along with it.** Allah pairs these two names again and again precisely so we never imagine one without the other. His might is never blind force, and His wisdom is never empty knowing. The two are welded.

So return to your question — *if You have the armies, why didn't You use them at Hudaybiyya?*

The answer is in the second name. **He did not fail to use His power. He used His wisdom to govern His power.** The treaty that humiliated the believers was not the limit of what Allah *could* do — He owns the hosts of the heavens and the earth; He could have leveled Mecca that afternoon. It was the expression of what He, in His wisdom, *chose* to do. And what looked like surrender turned out to be the door. The peace of Hudaybiyya gave Islam two years of open roads, and in those two years more people entered the faith than in all the years of fighting before it. The "defeat" was the *fatḥ* — the opening, the clear victory — the surah is named for.

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to make in your own life.

When we are in pain, we pray for *ʿizza* — for the might. *Lord, You have the power, use it, crush this thing that is crushing me, and use it now, on my timeline.* And when the might doesn't arrive on our schedule, we quietly conclude one of two things: either He isn't really Mighty, or He doesn't really care.

But the verse offers a third reading, and it is the true one. **The might is fully present and fully engaged — governed every instant by a wisdom you cannot yet see.** The delay is not the absence of His power. It is the presence of His wisdom. The treaty is not Him failing to act. It is Him acting in a way that is decades wiser than your prayer.

This is the hardest and most freeing thing a believer can learn: that *al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm* means you will sometimes get the wisdom *instead of* the visible might, and that this is not Him withholding the better gift — **the wisdom is the better gift.** You wanted Mecca today. He was opening it for good.

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes your duʿāʾ. You can still ask for the might — you should. But you learn to ask the way the believers at Hudaybiyya eventually learned to follow: *Lord, You are al-ʿAzīz, so I know You can. You are al-Ḥakīm, so I trust that if You haven't, there is an opening in it I cannot see yet.* That is not resignation. It is the highest form of trust — trust extended not just to His strength, but to His timing.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architectural Reveal: Why the Same Verse Has Two Endings

Now I can answer the question I planted at the very beginning. The one about the two endings.

Go back three verses, to 48:4. There, Allah describes how **"He sent down tranquility — *sakīna* — into the hearts of the believers, that they might increase in faith along with their faith."** And then, word for word, comes our sentence:

*"...wa lillāhi junūdu-s-samāwāti wal-arḍ"* — and to Allah belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth.

The exact same eight words open both verses. But watch the seal.

Verse 4 ends: *wa kāna-llāhu **ʿalīman** ḥakīmā* — **All-Knowing,** All-Wise.

Verse 7 ends: *wa kāna-llāhu **ʿazīzan** ḥakīmā* — **All-Mighty,** All-Wise.

One word changed. *ʿAlīm* — Knowing — became *ʿAzīz* — Mighty. Why?

[PAUSE]

Look at who each verse is *for.*

Verse 4 is about the **believers.** It is about *sakīna*, tranquility, being lowered gently into their hearts. It is the most intimate, interior thing in the surah — Allah reaching into the chest of a frightened man at Hudaybiyya and setting down peace. And what does the verse about the inside of the believer's heart seal itself with? *ʿAlīm.* **The Knowing.** Because the One who places calm in a heart must first *know* that heart — know its fear, know its exact shape, know precisely what tranquility it needs. The armies of the heavens and the earth are mentioned, but their relevant face, turned toward the trembling believer, is the face of **intimate knowledge.** *He knows you. That is why He could comfort you.*

Verse 7 comes right after Allah's **wrath upon the hypocrites and idolaters** — the ones who thought evil of Him, who did the arithmetic of the visible and bet against His promise. And what does *this* verse seal itself with? *ʿAzīz.* **The Mighty.** Because the relevant face of the cosmic army, turned toward the one who *doubts and mocks*, is not gentle knowledge — it is overwhelming, undeniable **power.** *You counted Persia and Rome and bet against Me? To Me belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth, and I am the Mighty.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

This is the staggering thing. **The hosts are the same hosts.** The sentence is identical. The angels, the winds, the dust, the seen and the unseen — one single army belongs to Allah. But that one army shows the believer the face of a Lord who *knows* him, and shows the doubter the face of a Lord who *overpowers* him. *Same forces. Different face. Depending entirely on which side of the promise you have chosen to stand on.*

The variation isn't decoration. It is theology written in the gap between two words. Where you stand toward Allah determines which of His names is turned toward you.

### Bringing It Together

See now how the linguistic journey and the two themes were one insight approached from two directions.

The grammar moved the armies into Allah's column (*the fronted lām*), expanded them past anything you could count (*junūd of the heavens and the earth*), and rooted His might before time itself (*kāna*). That is the architecture of trust.

And the themes lived inside that architecture: your fear is real but your inventory is incomplete — the unseen column belongs to your Lord. And His total power is never raw; it is married, every instant, to a wisdom that sometimes hands you an opening dressed as a defeat.

And the two endings tie it shut: the same omnipotence is comfort to the one who trusts and reckoning for the one who scoffs. *ʿAlīm* for the heart being healed. *ʿAzīz* for the heart that mocked. *Ḥakīm* — wise — for both, always, because His every act toward every soul is governed by a wisdom older than the worlds.

### Questions to Carry

1. When you lie awake doing the math of what's against you, which column are you forgetting to add — and to Whom does that column belong?

2. You have prayed for the *might* and felt it didn't come. Is it possible you were given the *wisdom* instead — an opening you couldn't yet recognize as an opening?

3. The same army shows one face to the truster and another to the scoffer. Honestly — which face of Allah is currently turned toward you, and what does that reveal about where you are standing?

4. The hosts are unseen *by design*, so that trust is possible. Where in your life right now is Allah asking you to trust an inventory you cannot verify?

5. Can you name a "Hudaybiyya" in your own past — a moment that felt like loss and turned out to be the door?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Every soldier of existence already belongs to Allah, eternally Mighty and eternally Wise — so what looks like your defeat is only the arithmetic of the visible, and the unseen column was His the whole time.**

### The Closing Invitation

Tonight, when you reach for the count — and you will — try the second move. Add the column you can't see, and hand it to its Owner. Not as a denial of your fear, but as the completion of your arithmetic. You are not asked to feel strong. You are asked to remember Whose the hosts are.

The believers walked home from Hudaybiyya with nothing in their hands and everything in motion above their heads. So might you.

### A Brief Duʿāʾ

*O Allah, to You belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth, and not one of them moves but by Your command. When my own count comes back against me, let me remember the column I cannot see is Yours. You are al-ʿAzīz — so I know You can. You are al-Ḥakīm — so when You do not, I trust there is an opening in it I cannot yet see. Turn toward me the face You turn toward those who trust You, and never the face You turn toward those who doubted Your promise. Āmīn.*
