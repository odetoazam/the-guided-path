# And Another You Could Not Reach

## The Promise Already Encircled

---

> وَأُخْرَىٰ لَمْ تَقْدِرُوا۟ عَلَيْهَا قَدْ أَحَاطَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَا ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرًا

*Wa-ukhrā lam taqdirū ʿalayhā qad aḥāṭa-llāhu bihā, wa-kāna-llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrā.*

**"And [there is] another that you were not [yet] able to reach — Allah has already encompassed it. And Allah is ever, over all things, fully Able."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Returning Home With Empty Hands

Let me put you somewhere first. Before we touch a single word, I want you to stand where the first listeners stood.

It is the sixth year after the Hijra. A little over a thousand believers have walked out of Madinah toward Makkah. They are not armed for war. They are in the white cloth of pilgrimage, driving sacrificial animals ahead of them, hearts pulling toward a city most of them were born in and were driven out of. They want one thing: to walk around the Kaʿbah. To come home to the House.

And they are stopped.

At a place called Hudaybiyyah, on the very edge of the sanctuary, the road closes. Negotiations drag on for days. And in the end, the Prophet ﷺ signs a treaty that, on its face, reads like a humiliation. They will not enter Makkah this year. They will turn around and go back. The terms seem lopsided — if a Makkan flees to the Muslims, he must be returned; if a Muslim flees to the Makkans, he is not.

Some of the most devoted companions are crushed. ʿUmar himself comes to the Prophet ﷺ, his chest tight with confusion: *Are we not upon the truth? Then why do we give in like this?*

They had come for victory and they are walking home with empty hands.

[PAUSE]

And then this surah descends. Surah Al-Fath. *The Victory.* And it does something almost shocking — it takes the moment they experienced as a defeat and renames it. **"Indeed, We have granted you a clear victory."** Not *we will.* Not *we might.* **We have.** Already. The thing you carried home as loss, Allah is calling a conquest.

Then the surah begins to open its hands. It speaks of spoils — *maghānim* — that the believers will take. And in the verse just before ours, verse 20, Allah names a near reward: spoils close at hand, *hastened* for them. The classical commentators are nearly unanimous: this is **Khaybar**, the fortified settlement they would soon take. A reward you can see. A reward your hands can reach.

And then comes our verse. Verse 21. And it pivots.

It turns away from what is near and graspable, and it gestures toward **something else.** Something the believers, standing there in their disappointment, *could not yet reach at all.* A thing beyond their power. A thing that, in that moment, would have seemed impossible — that this small, embattled community would ever lay hands on.

And about that impossible thing, Allah says one staggering word.

He says: *I have already wrapped My arms around it.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what I want you to feel the hunger for tonight. There are things in your life that are near — and you can act on those. And there are things that are utterly beyond your reach — the future you cannot touch, the outcome you cannot force, the door that will not open no matter how hard you push. We spend so much of our lives anxious about *exactly that second category.* The things outside our hands.

This verse is about the second category.

And it does not say *be patient and maybe one day.* It says something far stranger and far more comforting. It says: **the thing you cannot reach is not unheld. It is already encircled. Already in a grip. Just — not yours yet.**

Let's go find out what that means.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Word With No Noun — *Wa-ukhrā* (وَأُخْرَىٰ)

### First, a small thing about language

Let me teach you something about how speech works before we open the word.

Imagine a parent comes home holding two wrapped gifts. They hand the first one over and say, *"Here, this is for finishing your exams."* The child opens it. Then the parent sets the second box on the table — still wrapped — and says simply: *"And another one."*

And then... they don't say what it is.

What just happened in that sentence? Notice that the phrase *"and another one"* has no noun attached. *Another* — another *what?* Grammatically, something is missing. There is a gap where the name of the thing should be. And yet you, the child, are not confused. You are *electrified.* Because the unnamed gift is more thrilling than the named one. The mystery is the gift.

In Arabic, this is a recognized and deliberate feature of eloquent speech — *ḥadhf*, the artful omission of a word the listener's mind will supply. The speaker leaves a space on purpose, and the space *does work* that a filled-in word could not.

### Now look at how the verse opens

**وَأُخْرَىٰ** — *wa-ukhrā.* "And another."

That is how the verse begins. With *another.* From the root أ-خ-ر (*Axr*), which carries the meaning of *that which comes after, the other, the latter one.* It is a feminine singular form — *ukhrā* — and grammatically it is hanging there, an adjective, a descriptor... with no noun beside it.

Another *what?*

The classical grammarians had to ask exactly this. And listen to how al-Jalālayn answers it — he says *ukhrā* is **a description of an implied "spoils" (*maghānim muqaddaran*), standing as the subject of the sentence.** In other words, the verse is saying *"and there are OTHER spoils"* — but the word *spoils* itself has been deliberately dropped. Allah leaves the noun unspoken.

Sit with that for a moment.

### Why leave it out?

Here is the first layer. The verse before this one — verse 20 — was specific. It named a near reward. The commentators tie it firmly to Khaybar: a place, a known target, a thing the believers could picture and march toward. Defined. Graspable. *Named.*

And then verse 21 turns to *the other thing* — and removes the name.

Do you feel the contrast Allah has built into the very grammar? The reward within your reach gets a definite shape. The reward beyond your reach is left **open.** Unnamed. Unbounded.

And this is exactly right — because **a thing beyond your power cannot be contained by your imagination either.** If Allah had named it, He would have shrunk it down to the size of what those believers could conceive. By leaving it unnamed, He keeps it as large as His knowledge, not as small as their expectation.

[PAUSE]

### Deeper still — what the commentators saw in the gap

And here is where it gets remarkable. Because the scholars, looking into that empty space where the noun should be, did not all see the same thing — and that is not a flaw, it is the *point.*

Look at what al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī preserve. Among the narrations:

- Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan, Muqātil, Ibn Abī Laylā: the "other" is **the great conquests still to come — the lands of Persia and Rome,** and indeed every land the Muslims would later open. Al-Baghawī adds a piercing detail: *the Arabs could not have dreamed of fighting Persia and Rome — they were as good as servants to those empires — until Islam made them able.*
- Al-Ḍaḥḥāk, Ibn Zayd, Ibn Isḥāq: it is **Khaybar** itself, promised before it was given.

Now — notice something. Hold the two readings together. One says *the near thing,* one says *the far empires.* But the *grammar* already told us this is the thing **you could not yet reach.** And which of those two best fits a thing utterly beyond the believers' power? The mightiest empires on earth — Persia and Rome — that a band of a thousand pilgrims, turned back from a single town, could not in their wildest hopes have imagined conquering.

This is why I want you to read the *unnamed-ness* as load-bearing. The empty space in the grammar is the same size as the impossibility in their hearts. Allah left the noun out **because the thing was bigger than the word they would have used for it.**

**What this single word reveals:** when Allah leaves something unnamed, He is not being vague — He is being *vast.* The blank is not an absence of meaning. It is room for a promise too large to fit inside a name.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Power That Ran Out and the Power That Did Not — *Taqdirū* and *Qadīr* (تَقْدِرُوا … قَدِيرًا)

### A concept first: the same word, two owners

Let me show you something writers and speakers do.

Suppose I said to you: *"You could not carry it. But the One carrying it carries everything."* Notice I used the verb *carry* twice — once denied to you, once affirmed of Another. The repetition is not lazy. It is a hinge. By using the *same word* for both, I force you to measure one against the other. Your *carrying* and His *carrying* are placed on the same scale — and one of them collapses.

This device — taking a single root and turning it against itself, weakness on one side, power on the other — is one of the most quietly devastating tools in Arabic. And this verse uses it.

### Watch the root appear, and then appear again

The verse says you *"lam taqdirū ʿalayhā"* — **you were not able to reach it.** *Taqdirū* is a verb from the root ق-د-ر (*qdr*), the root of *power, capacity, the ability to do.* So Allah opens by naming the believers' **incapacity.** *You had no power over it.* Your *qudra* — your strength, your reach — ran out before it got there.

And then the verse closes — six words later — with this:

**وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرًا** — *and Allah is ever, over all things, **Qadīr.***

*Qadīr.* Same root. ق-د-ر again. *The One of complete power.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see what Allah has done? In a single short verse He has placed your power and His power **side by side, built from the same letters,** so that you cannot avoid comparing them.

*You — lam taqdirū — your power did not reach.*
*He — qadīr — His power reaches all things.*

The very faculty you ran out of is the faculty He possesses without limit. It is the same word. Your version of it failed at the edge of *one* thing. His version of it does not fail at the edge of *any* thing — *ʿalā kulli shayʾin,* over **all** things.

### Let's sit with the precise shape of each

Notice the believers' power is denied in a *specific, bounded* way: *lam taqdirū ʿalayhā* — you had no power over **it,** this one thing. Particular. Local. Your incapacity is attached to a single object.

But His power is affirmed in the most *unbounded* way the language allows: *ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrā* — over **every single thing,** Able. Universal. No object left out.

So the architecture of the sentence is: *your limit is small and specific; His ability is total and without exception.* And the same root — *qdr* — runs through both like a wire, so that the smallness of yours is measured against the totality of His in the same breath.

This is the mercy hiding inside the grammar. Allah is not mocking your weakness by naming it. He names your weakness **only to set His strength beside it** — so that the place where *you* stop becomes the exact place where you can see that *He* doesn't.

**What this repetition reveals:** the end of your power is not the end of the matter. It is the precise location where His power becomes visible. *Lam taqdirū* is not the verse's bad news. It is the doorway to *qadīr.*

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Arms Already Wrapped Around It — *Qad Aḥāṭa* (قَدْ أَحَاطَ)

### The image before the meaning

Here is the heart of the verse. And I want to approach it slowly, because everything turns on a single physical picture.

The word is **أَحَاطَ** — *aḥāṭa* — from the root ح-و-ط (*HwT*). And before it ever means anything spiritual, it means something you can *see with your eyes.* The root gives us *ḥāʾiṭ* — a **wall.** A *ḥāʾiṭ* is the wall that goes *around* a garden, an enclosure that surrounds a thing on every side. To *aḥāṭa* something is to **encircle it, to surround it completely, to wrap fully around it** until there is no side of it left exposed.

Picture it literally. Picture two arms closing around an object until it is entirely held. Picture a wall built in a full circle around a garden so that nothing can get in and nothing can get out without permission. Picture a fortress wall surrounding a treasure. That is *iḥāṭa.* **Total encirclement.**

The grammarians note this is a Form IV verb here — a transitive, intensive shaping of the root: not merely *to be around* something, but to actively, fully *encompass and take it within one's encirclement.*

### Now place it where the verse places it

Read the line again:

*"And [there is] another that you could not reach — **qad aḥāṭa-llāhu bihā** — Allah **has already encircled it.**"*

[PAUSE]

Feel the timing of that. The thing you *cannot reach* — Allah has **already** wrapped around. Look at the two verbs against each other:

- Your verb: *lam taqdirū* — **you had no power** over it. Your arms don't extend that far.
- His verb: *aḥāṭa* — He has **completely surrounded** it. His arms have already closed.

The thing that is *outside your reach* is *inside His encirclement.*

This is the load-bearing image of the entire verse, so let me say it as plainly as I can: **the thing beyond your grasp is not floating free in the dark, unowned and uncertain. It is already inside a circle. It is already held. It is already surrounded on every side by the One who placed the wall around it — and He placed that wall around it *for you.***

Al-Baghawī catches this beautifully. He says Allah's encompassing of it is *as though He has guarded it and kept it safe for you, withheld it from everyone else, until you come and take it.* The *iḥāṭa* is not just *knowing about it.* It is **protective custody.** A treasure walled off and reserved. Held in trust until the hand that is meant to receive it is ready.

### And then — *qad,* the little word of certainty

Don't miss the tiny particle in front of the verb: **قَدْ** — *qad.*

In Arabic, when *qad* sits before a past-tense verb, it is the particle of **confirmation, of certainty** — *taḥqīq.* It means *surely, indeed, already, it is a done thing.* It removes all doubt from the verb that follows.

So Allah does not say *Allah will encompass it.* He does not say *Allah might secure it.* He says *qad aḥāṭa* — **He has ALREADY, CERTAINLY, encircled it.** A completed act. A closed circle. Finished — even though, from where the believers stood, the thing itself had not even *begun* to come into their hands.

[PAUSE]

This is the staggering part. From the human side, the future conquest was *unreachable, unimaginable, not yet anything.* From Allah's side, it was **already wrapped, already secured, already a done deed.** The verse lets you stand at the seam between those two views of the same reality — your *not yet* and His *already* — and see them in a single line.

**What this word reveals:** what looks to you like an open, uncertain future is, from the vantage of the One who made time, a thing already encircled and held in trust. Your "I cannot reach it" and His "I have already secured it" are describing the very same thing.

## Bridge: From Words to Reality

So what have these three words built for us?

*Ukhrā* — an unnamed promise, left vast on purpose, too large for a word to hold.
*Taqdirū / qadīr* — your power runs out at the edge of one thing; His power has no edge at all.
*Qad aḥāṭa* — and the very thing your power could not reach is **already**, completely, wrapped in His.

Three words. One picture. The future you cannot touch is not unheld.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about a seventh-century conquest — but about *reality itself,* and about the way you are living right now.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Everything You Are Anxious About Lives in the Category of "Another You Could Not Reach"

### Start where you actually live

Let me begin not in Hudaybiyyah but in your own chest.

Think, for a moment, about what genuinely keeps you up at night. I am willing to guess it is almost never the thing in front of you. The dishes in the sink do not give you insomnia. What gives you insomnia is the *thing you cannot reach from your bed.* The diagnosis you are waiting on. The child who has wandered from the path and will not be argued back. The marriage that may or may not come. The job in a city you have no foothold in. The country you can't go home to. The version of your life that sits on the far side of a door you cannot open.

Psychologists have a clean way of naming this. They talk about the difference between your *locus of control* — the small zone of things you can actually act on — and everything outside it. And nearly all of human anxiety, they have found, is generated not by the inner zone but by our desperate, exhausting attempts to *control the outer one.* We lie awake trying to grip things that were never in our hands.

Here is the quiet brilliance of this verse: **it speaks directly to the outer zone.** It is not a verse about the reward you can work for — that was verse 20, the near spoils, the Khaybar you can march to. *This* verse is about *the other one.* The one *"you could not reach."* The category that holds everything you are afraid of.

### Let the verse illuminate it

So watch what Allah does with that category — the unreachable one.

He does not say: *let it go, it doesn't matter.*
He does not say: *try harder, force it.*
He does not even say: *be patient and perhaps someday.*

He says: *I have already encircled it.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how that reframes the whole experience of anxiety? Your anxiety is the feeling that the unreachable thing is *loose* — drifting, ownerless, subject to chaos, liable to go any way at all. That is the lie under the sleeplessness: *no one is holding this.*

And the verse answers the lie at its root. The thing is not loose. *Qad aḥāṭa.* It is encircled. It is in a grip. It has a wall around it. The only thing that is true about your situation is *lam taqdirū* — **you** cannot reach it. But you were never the one holding it up in the first place.

### Build it further — the relief of not being the wall

Here is a movement I want you to make with me, because it changes everything.

We tend to think our job is to be the wall around our own futures. To stay vigilant. To worry hard enough, plan hard enough, grip tight enough, that nothing slips. We appoint ourselves the guardian of outcomes we have no power over — *lam taqdirū* — and then we wonder why we are exhausted.

This verse fires you from that job.

There is *already* a wall around the thing. *Qad aḥāṭa-llāhu bihā.* The encircling is done, and it was never done by you, and it never could have been — your arms don't reach that far and they were never meant to. The relief on offer here is not *"the thing will work out."* The relief is deeper: **the thing is already held, and you are not the one holding it, and you can finally put down a weight you were never strong enough to carry and were never asked to.**

Al-Baghawī's image again: He has *kept it safe for you.* Picture a parent holding a child's most precious thing up on a high shelf — out of the child's reach, yes, but precisely *because* it is being protected, not because it is being denied. The unreachability and the safety are the *same fact.* It is high because it is held.

### Connect it to the struggle, and to a practical turn

This is the hardest and most freeing distinction in the believing life: **the difference between the near reward you act on and the far reward you trust.**

The companions at Hudaybiyyah had to do *both.* They marched on Khaybar — they *acted* on the near thing. And they entrusted Persia, Rome, the unimaginable horizon — they *released* the far thing to the One who had already encircled it. They did not confuse the two. They did not try to conquer Rome that year, and they did not sit idle waiting for Khaybar to fall into their laps.

**So here is what this changes about tomorrow.** Take the thing that is eating you right now and ask one question: *is this in the category of verse 20, or verse 21?* Is this a near thing — something I can actually act on today, a call I can make, a step I can take, a sin I can leave? Then act. Pour yourself into it; that is your Khaybar. **Or** is this a far thing — an outcome, a heart, a future, a thing genuinely beyond my reach? Then this verse is for it. Say the words back to your own fear: *lam taqdirū — I cannot reach it. Qad aḥāṭa — and He already has.* And then sleep. Because you have just handed the wall back to its Builder.

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: His "Already" Is Bigger Than Your "Not Yet" — Living Inside Divine Time

### Begin with how time feels to us

Let me take you into something stranger and more wondrous.

We live inside time like a train inside a tunnel. We can see only the small lit circle of *now,* and a little of where we just came from, and *nothing at all* of what is around the next bend. The future, to us, genuinely does not exist yet. It is dark. Unmade. That darkness is not a metaphor — it is the actual condition of being a creature inside time. We cannot see ahead because, for us, *ahead has not happened.*

And this is the deepest source of human dread. Not pain — we can bear present pain. **The unknown.** The unlit track. The not-yet that could hold anything.

### Now hear what the verse quietly claims

The believers at Hudaybiyyah were deep in the tunnel. To them, the conquest of empires was pure darkness — *not yet,* and frankly *never,* as far as their eyes could see. *Lam taqdirū.* Out of reach. Out of sight. Unmade.

And into that darkness Allah speaks a past-tense verb. *Qad aḥāṭa.* **He has already encircled it.**

Stay with me, because this is the wonder. From inside the tunnel, the thing has *not happened.* From the One who is not in the tunnel — who made the tunnel, who stands outside of time and sees its whole length at once — the thing is **already done.** *Aḥāṭa* is past tense not because the conquest had occurred in their timeline, but because in *His* relationship to time, it was as settled and complete as something finished long ago.

[PAUSE]

You are being shown, in one verb, that **your "not yet" and Allah's "already" are looking at the very same event from two sides.** What is future to you is not future to Him. What is dark to you is fully lit to Him. The thing you experience as a terrifying *maybe* is, from where He sees, a settled *certainly.*

### Deepen it — this is what *iḥāṭa* really means

And now the word *aḥāṭa* opens even wider. Because Allah's *iḥāṭa* — His encompassing — is not only of *places.* The Qur'an uses this same encircling for His **knowledge:** *He encompasses all things in knowledge.* His encompassing of the future conquest includes encompassing every step that will lead to it — every heart that will soften, every battle, every treaty, every year between *now* and *then.* The whole road is already inside His circle.

So when He says *qad aḥāṭa bihā,* He is telling those frightened pilgrims: *the entire path from your empty-handed walk home today to the day this is yours — all of it — is already within My encompassing. I am not waiting to see how it turns out. I see how it turns out. I have surrounded the outcome and every step toward it.*

This is why the verse seals itself the way it does: *wa-kāna-llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrā.* Notice the verb *kāna* — *was, is, ever-has-been.* The grammarians point out that this construction expresses a **timeless, permanent attribute,** not a passing state. Al-Jalālayn says plainly: *He has never ceased to be thus.* Allah is not *becoming* able. He *is, always, eternally* able — over **every single thing.** His power, like His sight, is not stuck inside the tunnel with you.

### Connect to the universal struggle and bring it home

Here is the human paradox this resolves. We are commanded to live forward — to strive, to plant, to act — while being utterly unable to *see* forward. That is a kind of agony unique to conscious beings: to be responsible for a journey whose end we cannot see.

This verse hands you the missing piece. **You cannot see the end. But the End is already seen.** You cannot reach the thing. But the thing is already reached — encircled — by the One who has *never ceased* to be able over all things. You are asked only to walk your lit circle of *now* faithfully — to take your steps, do your Khaybar — while *trusting* that the dark track ahead is not dark to Him, and not loose, and not unmade. It is encircled.

[PAUSE]

**So what does this change about tomorrow?** The next time you feel that specific dread of the unknown — the future you cannot see and cannot control — try this. Don't pretend to see it; you can't. Instead, relocate it. Move it, in your heart, from *the category of things floating loose in the dark* into *the category of things already inside His circle.* Say it: *I cannot see it. He has already encompassed it. And He has never once ceased to be able over all things.* That is not denial of your uncertainty. It is the placing of your uncertainty into Hands that hold it.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Now That We Can See It

Step back and look at the whole verse as a single built structure. Now that we have done the work, the design is visible.

The verse is, quite literally, a movement **from human limit to divine limitlessness, with a hinge of certainty in the middle:**

1. **وَأُخْرَىٰ** — *And another* — an unnamed, unbounded promise, left vast on purpose.
2. **لَمْ تَقْدِرُوا عَلَيْهَا** — *you had no power over it* — the human edge. Where you stop. *Qdr,* denied.
3. **قَدْ أَحَاطَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَا** — *Allah has already encircled it* — the hinge. The thing past your edge is inside His circle. Certain. Complete.
4. **وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرًا** — *and Allah is ever, over all things, fully Able* — the divine edge, which is no edge. *Qdr,* affirmed without limit.

Watch the root *qdr* form the two outer pillars: *taqdirū* (your failed power) and *qadīr* (His total power). And between them, holding up the roof, the verb *aḥāṭa* — the encircling that bridges the gap your power could not cross. **Your incapacity and His omnipotence are the two walls; His encompassing is the bridge between them.** The verse is a building, and you are meant to walk through it from your weakness to His strength.

## Two Roads to One Truth

The **linguistic journey** showed us: a promise left unnamed because it was too large for a name; a single root turned against itself so your power and His are measured on one scale; and a past-tense verb of encircling, sealed with *qad,* declaring a future thing *already* done.

The **thematic journey** showed us: that everything we fear lives in the category of "what you could not reach" — and that this exact category is the one Allah declares *already held;* and that His timeless *already* is bigger than our frightened *not yet.*

They are the same insight arriving from two directions. From the side of *words:* the unreachable is already encircled. From the side of *life:* you can put down the weight of guarding your own future, because it was never loose and never yours to hold up. **What is beyond your power is already within His grip.**

## Questions to Carry

Let these stay open. Don't answer them quickly.

1. What is the *"ukhrā"* in my life right now — the thing I cannot reach, the one that occupies most of my fear? Have I been treating it as *loose,* or as *encircled*?
2. Where have I appointed myself the wall around an outcome — exhausting myself guarding a future my arms were never built to reach?
3. Can I tell the difference, today, between my *Khaybar* (the near thing I must act on) and my *ukhrā* (the far thing I must entrust)? Am I striving where I should strive and releasing where I should release — or have I confused the two?
4. When I feel the dread of the unknown, do I believe, really believe, that my *"not yet"* is His *"already"*?
5. If *qad aḥāṭa* is true — if it is already encircled — what would change in how I sleep tonight?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The future you cannot reach is not unheld — it is already encircled by the One whose power has no edge; your "I cannot" is simply the doorway to His "I already have."**

## The Closing Invitation

You don't have to manufacture certainty about your future. You couldn't anyway — *lam taqdirū,* and that is not your failure, it is your design. What this verse invites is something gentler and far stronger than certainty: the willingness to move one thing, in your heart, from the dark loose unknown into the circle that is *already* drawn around it. Act on your near things. Hand the far things back to the One who has never ceased to be able over all things. And then breathe — because the wall is already built, and you were never the one holding it up.

## A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who have encircled all things, the thing I cannot reach — I know now that You have already wrapped it in Your grip. So let me stop grasping at what was never in my hands, and let me trust the wall You have drawn around my future. You who have never ceased to be able over all things — be enough for me over the one thing I fear most.*

Āmīn.
