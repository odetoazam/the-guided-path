# The Law That Was Never Once Rewritten

## Surah Al-Fath, Ayah 23

> سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلَّتِى قَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلُ‌ۖ وَلَن تَجِدَ لِسُنَّةِ ٱللَّهِ تَبْدِيلًا

*Sunnata-llāhi-llatī qad khalat min qablu — wa lan tajida li-sunnati-llāhi tabdīlā.*

**"[Such is] the way of Allah which has already passed away before. And never will you find in the way of Allah any substitution."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing at Hudaybiyyah

Let me take you to a place outside Mecca, in the sixth year after the Hijrah.

The believers have come for pilgrimage — unarmed, in the white cloth of the consecrated, hearts set on the House. They are stopped. Negotiations drag on. And then comes a treaty whose terms feel, on the surface, like humiliation. A ten-year truce. Turn back this year; come next. If one of *yours* flees to *us*, we keep him; if one of *ours* flees to *you*, you must return him.

The Companions are stunned. ʿUmar — ʿUmar — goes to the Prophet ﷺ and asks, almost in protest: *Are we not upon the truth? Then why do we accept disgrace in our religion?*

And the Quran descends and calls this very moment **al-Fath** — the Opening. The Manifest Victory. The conquest. About a treaty that *looked* like a defeat.

Now hold that tension in your chest, because that is the soil this verse grows in. The surah has just said something staggering in the verse before ours: that if the disbelievers had actually fought you here, *they* would have turned their backs and fled, and found no protector and no helper.

And then — our verse. Allah does not say, "and this would have been a nice outcome." He does not say, "I would have helped you, as a favor." He reaches for a different category of word entirely. He says: this is **sunnatullāh**. A law. A pattern that has *already passed before*. And — here is the hook — He adds something almost frightening in its finality:

**You will never find any substitution in it.**

Not "rarely." Not "almost never." *Never.* Once. Ever. In all of history.

What kind of confidence is that? What is being claimed about reality itself? Stay with me — because the answer is buried in the grammar, and once you see it, you will never read the word *sunnah* the same way again.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word With No Verb — *Sunnata Llāh* in the Accusative

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we touch the verse, because it will change what you see.

In Arabic, there is a construction grammarians call the **cognate accusative** — the *maf‘ūl muṭlaq*. Here is how it works in plain language. Imagine you tell someone, "He hit me." Now imagine instead you say, "He hit me — *a hitting*." That second, strange-sounding addition — "a hitting" — is not redundant in Arabic. It is an *intensifier*. It takes the bare claim and stamps it, seals it, confirms that this happened *definitively and exactly so*. The noun in the accusative case loops back and grips the verb, removing all doubt.

In English we would say it differently. We'd say, "He really did hit me," or "He hit me, make no mistake." That emphatic seal — *that* is the work the accusative noun is doing.

Now look at the very first word of our verse:

> **سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ** — *Sunnata-llāh*

Notice the ending. *Sunnat**a**.* That little *-a* is the accusative case. And here is what should stop you: **there is no verb in front of it.** The sentence opens with a noun already in the accusative, hanging there, governed by a verb that was never written down.

The classical grammarians caught this immediately. Al-Jalālayn says plainly: *sunnata-llāh* is a **maṣdar mu'akkid** — an emphatic verbal noun confirming the meaning of the entire statement that came before it. As if Allah, having described the defeat of the disbelievers and the victory of the believers, then drives a stake into the ground and says: **سَنَّ اللَّهُ ذَٰلِكَ سُنَّةً** — *Allah established that, definitively, as a sunnah.*

Al-Qurṭubī confirms the accusative is *intiṣāb ‘alā al-maṣdar* — set in the accusative precisely as a verbal noun of confirmation.

Sit with what this does emotionally. The verb is missing because **the verb is too small a thing to say out loud.** It is *assumed*. The pattern is so settled that Allah does not even narrate the act of establishing it — He simply names the established thing, in the case-ending that means *this is sealed*. It is the difference between a man saying, "I promise to pay you back," and a man silently placing the deed to his house in your hand. One is a statement. The other is a fact you can *hold*.

**This word carries no verb because it needs none. It is not an action Allah is taking. It is a law He is naming.**

### Element Two: The Generations That *Emptied Away* — *Khalat*

Now look at what Allah says this sunnah *did*:

> ٱلَّتِى **قَدْ خَلَتْ** مِن قَبْلُ — *which has already passed away before.*

The verb is **خَلَتْ** — *khalat*, from the root **خ-ل-و** (*kh-l-w*). And here we have to slow down, because translation flattens this completely.

We render it "passed away." But the root *kh-l-w* does not primarily mean "to pass." Its core meaning is **to be empty, to be vacant, to be cleared out.** From this same root comes *khalwah* — seclusion, the empty place you withdraw to. From it comes *khalā'* — empty space, a void. When the Arabs said a place *khalat*, they meant it had been *emptied of its people* — that those who filled it are gone, and the space stands vacant where they once stood.

So when Allah says the generations *khalat min qablu*, the picture is not a gentle "passing." It is an **emptying.** Picture a great hall, full of people — generation after generation of the powerful and the proud, the believers and the tyrants, the armies of Pharaoh, the people of ʿĀd and Thamūd, the confederates at the Trench. And then watch the hall *empty out*. They drain away. The space they occupied stands vacant.

And here is the contemplation. **What emptied out?** The *people* emptied out. Whole nations. The mighty and the weak alike. *They* are the ones who *khalat* — who vacated, who cleared the field of history and left it empty behind them.

But the *sunnah* did not empty out.

Do you feel that? The grammar holds two things in tension. The relative pronoun الَّتِي — "which" — connects the *sunnah* to *khalat*. The sunnah is described as having "gone before." But what the root actually paints is generations *emptying away while the law that governed them remained.* The vessels shatter and drain; the water that filled them — the pattern, the law — is still there for the next vessel. The people of every age pour themselves into history and pour themselves out of it. The sunnah they lived under does not pour out. **It is what's left standing in the empty hall.**

This is *istidlāl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing at something you can see, an emptying room, a draining vessel, generations vanishing — to make you feel something you cannot see: that beneath the parade of vanishing nations runs a single current that never vanishes.

### Element Three: The Door Allah Bolted Shut — *Lan ... Tabdīlā*

Now the verse turns and faces *you* — and in the original, faces the Prophet ﷺ directly:

> **وَلَن تَجِدَ** لِسُنَّةِ ٱللَّهِ **تَبْدِيلًا** — *and you will never find, in the way of Allah, any substitution.*

There are two pieces of grammar here, and each is a hammer.

First: **لَن** — *lan*. Arabic has several ways to say "no." There is *lā*, which negates broadly. There is *mā*, which negates the past. And there is **لَن**, which negates the *future* — and not mildly. *Lan* is the particle of **emphatic, decisive negation of what is to come.** It does not say "this probably won't happen." It says: **this will never happen, and I am closing the door on the possibility.** When you say *lan* you are not making a prediction. You are bolting a door.

And notice the address. **تَجِدَ** — *tajida*, "you will find" — is second-person masculine singular. Al-Muyassar makes the addressee explicit: *O Prophet, you will never find...* This is spoken first to the most beloved of Allah's creation, the one carrying the weight of Hudaybiyyah, the one whose Companions are shaken. And through him, it is spoken to every single person who has ever stood in a moment that *looked* like defeat and wondered whether the rules still held. The "you" is the Prophet ﷺ. And then the "you" is also you.

Second: the object of the search — **تَبْدِيلًا** — *tabdīlā*. We translate it "change," but again, that is too thin. This word is built on the root **ب-د-ل** (*b-d-l*), whose core is *badal* — a **substitute, a replacement, a swap.** And the Leeds corpus confirms its form: this is a **Form II verbal noun**, from *baddala*. Form II in Arabic intensifies and makes transitive — it is the form of *deliberately doing* something to something. *Baddala* is not "to drift, to change on its own." It is **to actively substitute one thing in the place of another** — to pull out X and put Y where X stood.

So Allah is not merely saying "the law won't change over time." He is saying something sharper: **you will never find that anyone — or anything — has reached in and swapped this law out for a different one.** No substitution has ever been performed. The pattern has never once been pulled from its place and replaced.

And it is *indefinite* — *tabdīlan*, not *al-tabdīl*. In Arabic, an indefinite noun under negation means **not even one instance, not any kind whatsoever.** Not "you won't find *the* substitution." *You will never find a substitution — not one, not of any sort, ever.*

Now hold the two pieces together. *Lan* bolts the future. *Tabdīlā*, indefinite, denies even a single past instance. Between them, they wall off the whole of time — backward and forward — from a single exception. **The law that emptied every generation before you has never once, in the entire record of creation, been swapped for another. And it never will be.**

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have built.

A noun with no verb — because the law is too settled to need one announced.

A verb of *emptying* — because everything pours out of history except the pattern.

A double-walled negation — because no exception has ever been found, and the door to one is bolted.

Three pieces of grammar, and every one of them is saying the same thing from a different angle: **what you are looking at is not a divine mood. It is the architecture of reality.**

Now let us ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about how to *live*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Terror and the Mercy of a World That Runs on Law

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of anxiety that defines the modern person, and it is the anxiety of a world that feels *arbitrary*. You work hard and get passed over; someone cuts every corner and gets promoted. The careful driver is hit; the reckless one walks away. You scroll through a feed of outcomes that seem to obey no rule at all — and somewhere underneath, a quiet dread sets in: *maybe there is no pattern. Maybe it's all just noise. Maybe nothing I do reliably leads anywhere.*

Psychologists have a name for what this does to a person. When an animal — or a human — is placed in an environment where outcomes seem disconnected from actions, where reward and punishment arrive randomly, it eventually stops trying. It collapses into what's been called *learned helplessness*. The cruelest thing you can do to a living being is not to punish it. It is to make its world **unpredictable** — to sever the line between what it does and what happens.

Now hear the verse again: *wa lan tajida li-sunnati-llāhi tabdīlā.* **You will never find a substitution in the way of Allah.**

Do you feel what this is offering? It is the exact opposite of the arbitrary world. It is the announcement that **reality is not noise.** That beneath the surface chaos — beneath the treaty that looks like defeat, beneath the believer who suffers and the tyrant who struts — there runs a law so fixed that not one exception has ever been recorded in the entire ledger of creation.

But sit with the *full* weight of this, because it is not only comfort. A fixed law is a terrifying thing if you are on the wrong side of it. The same sunnah that *guarantees* the believer's outcome *guarantees* the tyrant's. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both gloss this sunnah the same way: *naṣr awliyā'ihi ‘alā a‘dā'ihi* — **the aiding of His allies over His enemies.** It cuts both ways. The law that is your shelter is your enemy's sentence. Pharaoh did not get an exception. ʿĀd did not. The confederates at the Trench did not. The hall emptied of all of them, and the law that emptied them was the same.

So the question the verse forces is not "is the world reliable?" It is: **which side of the unchanging law am I standing on?** Because the law itself will not move to accommodate me. *Lan tajida tabdīlā.* It will not be swapped out because I prefer different terms. The only variable in the entire equation — the one thing that is *not* fixed — is *me.* My alignment. My side.

And there is the mercy hiding inside the terror. In a fixed-law universe, **your effort is never wasted.** The line between what you do and what happens is not severed — it is *welded*. Stand with His allies, walk His path, and you are not gambling. You are not hoping the dice land kindly. You are stepping onto a current that has carried every believer before you and has *never once* reversed its direction.

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes what you do in the moment that *looks* like Hudaybiyyah — the moment that looks like loss. The arbitrary-world mind says: *the rules just broke, give up.* The sunnah-mind says: *the surface is lying to me; the law has not moved; my only job is to make sure I am on its right side and keep walking.* That is the difference between despair and *tawakkul*.

### Theme Two: You Are Not the First — The Past as the Proof of the Promise

Here is the second place you live, and it is a lonelier one.

When you are in the middle of a hard thing — a grief, a test of faith, a long stretch where doing right seems to *cost* you instead of reward you — the deepest lie the heart tells itself is: *this is unprecedented. No one has stood exactly here. There is no map for this.* The struggle feels like the first of its kind, and that loneliness is its own second wound on top of the first.

Now look at what Allah chose to anchor this promise in. He did not anchor the unchanging law in the *future*. He did not say "watch and see, it will hold." He anchored it in **the past**: *al-latī qad khalat min qabl* — **which has already passed before.**

Why the past? Because the past is the one thing that *cannot be argued with.* A promise about the future asks for trust. A pattern in the past offers *evidence.* Allah is not asking the shaken Companions at Hudaybiyyah to take His word on faith alone — He is pointing over their shoulders at the entire emptied hall of history and saying: *look. Look at Badr. Look at the Trench. Look at every prophet's people before you. The law you are doubting right now is the law you can watch operating in every generation that has already drained away.*

And remember *qad* — قَدْ — the little particle of certainty before *khalat*. It does the same work in the past that *lan* does in the future. *Qad khalat* — it **definitively has** already passed. The verse is bracketed by certainty on both ends: *qad* sealing what is behind you, *lan* sealing what is ahead. You are standing in the one safe place — the present — held between a proven past and a guaranteed future, and the same law runs through all three.

So when the lie comes — *you are the first, there is no map* — the verse answers: **you are not the first, and the map is the oldest thing there is.** The believer who is struggling tonight is walking a road already worn smooth by every believer who emptied out of this world before her. The exact law that vindicated them has not been swapped. *Lan tajida tabdīlā.* The terms that held for the people of the Cave, for the family of Yāsīn, for the believer of Pharaoh's court — those *same terms* are still in force. Not similar terms. *The same ones.* No substitution has been performed.

There is a strange and beautiful comfort in being *un*original in your suffering. It means you are not a test case. You are not the experiment where God might, for the first time, let the pattern break. **You are simply the next vessel being filled by a water that has filled ten thousand vessels before you and spilled none.**

What does this change about tomorrow? It changes where you look for strength when you feel alone in a trial. The instinct is to look *forward* and squint anxiously at an outcome you cannot see. The verse redirects your gaze *backward* — to the emptied hall, to the generations who walked exactly this and were carried exactly as promised. Their vindication is not a story. It is *evidence admitted into the record* — *qad khalat* — and that evidence is the proof of your own promise. Read the lives of those who passed. Not as history. As *forecast.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Ring Around an Unmoving Center

Now that we have done the work, look at the *shape* of the verse — because the form is enacting the meaning.

> **سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ** ٱلَّتِى قَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلُ ۖ وَلَن تَجِدَ **لِسُنَّةِ ٱللَّهِ** تَبْدِيلًا

The phrase **sunnatu-llāh** appears at the opening — and **sunnatu-llāh** appears again near the close. The verse is *bracketed* by the name of the law. It opens with it and shuts with it, forming a ring. And what sits cradled in the center of that ring? The two movements of time: *qad khalat min qabl* — the sealed past — and *lan tajida tabdīlā* — the bolted future.

The structure *is* the message. An unchanging law, named at both ends, enclosing all of time in between. The form does not move because the content does not move. **The verse is shaped like the very thing it describes.**

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight seen from two directions.

The grammar told us: a verb-less accusative *(too settled to announce)*, a verb of *emptying (everything drains but the pattern)*, and a double-walled negation *(no exception, ever)*.

The themes told us: the world is not arbitrary noise but welded law — terrifying if you misalign, merciful if you don't; and you are never the first to walk a hard road, because the law that carried those who emptied before you has not been, and will never be, swapped out.

One claim, two faces: **Reality has a grain. It does not change to suit us. Our entire freedom lies in which way we lay ourselves against it.**

### Questions to Carry

1. When my life *looks* like Hudaybiyyah — like a loss, a humiliation, a closed door — do I read the surface, or do I trust the law beneath it?
2. The one variable in the equation is *me*. Honestly: which side of the unchanging sunnah am I currently standing on?
3. When I feel utterly alone in a trial, do I look forward into the dark and despair — or backward into the emptied hall and take evidence?
4. If God's law genuinely never substitutes, what am I still treating as negotiable that He has already sealed?
5. Whose life — among those who have *qad khalat*, already passed before me — should I be reading not as a story, but as a forecast of my own?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The vindication of those who stand with Allah is not a hope He might honor but a law that has run, unbroken and never once swapped, through every generation that has emptied into the past — so the only question reality leaves you is which side of that law you are standing on.

### The Closing Invitation

Let something shift. The next time the surface of your life tells you the rules have broken — that doing right has stopped paying, that you are uniquely abandoned — let the word *lan* rise up in you. *Lan tajida tabdīlā.* The door is bolted. The law has not moved. The hall is full of those who stood where you stand and were carried exactly as you will be. Your only work is alignment, and then the patient walking. Not as a gamble. As stepping onto a current that has never, in all of creation, reversed its direction.

### Du'ā

O Allah, You whose way has never once been substituted, anchor our hearts to the law beneath the surface of things when our eyes are deceived by what looks like loss. Let us read the lives of those who passed before us as the proof of Your promise to us, and place us — and keep us — on the side of Your unchanging sunnah, among Your allies and never Your enemies.
