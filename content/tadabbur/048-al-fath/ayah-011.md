# The Excuse Written Before It Was Spoken

## Introduction: A Caravan, a Calculation, and a Sentence That Arrives Early

Let me put you in the dust of a particular morning.

It is the sixth year after the Hijra. The Prophet ﷺ has had a dream — he sees himself and the believers entering the Sacred Mosque in Mecca, heads shaved, in safety, performing the rites. He shares it. And so a journey is announced. Not a war. A pilgrimage. He brings with him the *hady* — the sacrificial animals, garlanded, marked — so that any watching eye, friend or enemy, would understand at a glance: **this man is not coming to fight. He is coming to worship.**

And before he sets out, he sends word to the Bedouin tribes living in the deserts around Medina. The Ghifār, the Muzaynah, the Juhaynah, the Aslam, the Ashjaʿ — this is who al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī, on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, tell us these people were. He calls them to come out with him. To walk beside the Messenger of Allah toward the House of Allah.

Now here is what you have to feel to understand this ayah. **They did the math.**

They looked at the situation the way a careful person looks at a risk. Mecca is Quraysh's stronghold. Quraysh has wanted this man dead for years. He is walking, lightly armed, into the lion's mouth with a few hundred followers. The cold calculation said: *this caravan is not coming back. These people are walking to their deaths. And if I go, my wealth is untended, my family is unprotected, and most likely my own life is forfeit.*

So they stayed home. They stayed home and they waited to see who would survive.

[PAUSE]

And then — by the decree of Allah — the caravan *did* come back. Not with a slaughter behind it, but with a treaty. With Sūrat al-Fath itself, opening with the words *innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā* — "Indeed, We have granted you a clear victory." The very thing they bet against became the greatest opening in the Prophet's mission.

Now the men who stayed behind have a problem. The Messenger is back. He will look at them. And they will need something to say.

Here is the hook. Here is what should make you lean forward.

**Allah tells the Prophet ﷺ what they will say — before they say it.** Not what they said. What they *will* say. The excuse is printed, set in type, and handed to the Prophet ﷺ before the men who will speak it have even opened their mouths. And once you understand *why* Allah does that — why He scripts an excuse in advance — you will never hear your own excuses the same way again.

Let us open it slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Sa-yaqūl* — The Sentence That Arrives Before the Speaker

Let me teach you something small about Arabic that is about to do enormous work.

In Arabic, when you want to say something *will* happen, in the near and certain future, you attach a tiny prefix to the front of the verb: **سَـ**, *sa-*. It is the difference, in English, between "he says" and "he is going to say." It points forward. It promises. It says: *this is coming.*

Now look at the very first word of our ayah.

> **سَيَقُولُ لَكَ ٱلْمُخَلَّفُونَ**
> *Sa-yaqūlu laka l-mukhallafūn*
> "Those who were left behind **are going to say** to you..."

Sit with the strangeness of this for a moment.

These men have not yet spoken. The Prophet ﷺ has not yet returned to them. The conversation in which they offer their excuse has not yet happened. And Allah, from above the seven heavens, leans down and **writes their excuse out in full before a single one of them clears his throat.**

Notice something. When you can predict exactly what someone is going to say, it means one of two things. Either you know that person better than they know themselves — or the thing they are about to say was never really *theirs* to begin with. It was a script. A reflex. A line that any person in their spiritual condition would reach for.

Here both are true at once.

Allah is telling His Messenger: *Don't be wounded by what they're about to tell you. Don't spend a second wondering whether it's sincere. I have already shown you the inside of this. The words are coming, and here they are, and here is what is underneath them.*

[PAUSE]

Stay with me, because there is a mercy hidden in the cruelty of being predicted.

To have your excuse known in advance is exposure. It is the opposite of privacy. But think about *who* is being protected here. The Prophet ﷺ is a man of overwhelming gentleness. When someone comes to him weeping, "Our wealth and our families kept us busy, *so ask forgiveness for us*" — that last phrase, *fa-staghfir lanā*, "so seek forgiveness for us," is engineered to move him. It wraps the excuse in religious language. It puts the request inside the house of God so that to refuse it feels like refusing mercy.

Allah inoculates His Messenger against the manipulation **before it lands.** That is what *sa-yaqūl* does. It is a father telling his trusting son, before the conman knocks: *here is exactly what he will say to you, and here is what is really going on.*

And now turn the lens around onto yourself. Because this is the uncomfortable part.

**How many of your own excuses could be written in advance?** How many of them are not discoveries you arrive at honestly in the moment, but scripts you reach for — the same script, every time, the moment a duty becomes inconvenient? "I'm too busy." "It's not the right season." "When things settle down." If Allah were to write the next sentence you will say to excuse yourself from Him — would you be surprised by it? Or would you recognize it, word for word, because you have said it a hundred times before?

The *sa-* of *sa-yaqūl* is a mirror. The most damning thing about a bad excuse is not that it is false. It is that it is **predictable.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Two: *Al-Mukhallafūn* — The Name They Did Not Choose

Now we come to the word that, once you see it, reorganizes the entire ayah. I want to teach you the grammar first, with a clean everyday example, because the whole weight rests here.

Arabic verbs grow on three-letter roots. The root here is **خ-ل-ف**, *kh-l-f*, which carries the meaning of *being behind, coming after, succeeding, remaining when others have moved on.* From this single root the Arabs built a whole family of words about what is left behind.

Now, Arabic can take a root and put it into different "forms," and the form changes the meaning in precise ways. Listen to the difference between two of them:

- There is a form that means *to hold oneself back*: **تَخَلَّفَ**, *takhallafa*. This is the reflexive — you do it to yourself. The person who *takhallafa* is a **mutakhallif**: "one who held himself back." It implies agency, decision, a reason. *I chose to stay.*

- And there is a form that means *to be left behind, to be made to remain, to be cast off*: the passive participle **مُخَلَّف**, *mukhallaf*. This is something done *to* you. You did not stay; you *were left.* You were discarded, set aside, passed over.

Think of two travelers at a station. One steps off the platform and says, "I decided not to board." That is the *mutakhallif* — the man with a reason. The other stands frozen as the train pulls away without him, and we say, "He got left behind." That is the *mukhallaf* — the man who was passed by.

Now. Which word does Allah use?

> **ٱلْمُخَلَّفُونَ**
> *al-mukhallafūn*
> "the ones who **were left behind.**"

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

These men want to be *mutakhallifūn* — men with reasons. Their entire excuse, "our wealth and our families kept us occupied," is a bid to be seen as people who made a difficult, understandable choice. *We weighed it. We had responsibilities. We held ourselves back for legitimate cause.* That is the dignity they are reaching for.

And Allah, in a single word, **denies them the dignity of having chosen.** He does not call them the ones who held themselves back. He calls them the ones who *were left behind.* The grammar strips the agency out of their hands. You did not make a shrewd decision, the word says. **You were passed over. The caravan of honor moved on, and you were not in it.**

Now go one layer deeper, because the classical scholars heard something here that we should not miss.

The form *mukhallaf* is passive — *left behind by someone.* By whom? Al-Jalālayn answers this directly. The *mukhallafūn*, he says, are *"alladhīna khallafahum-Allāhu ʿan ṣuḥbatika"* — **"those whom Allah left behind, away from your companionship."**

Stay with that, because it is staggering and it is double-edged.

On the surface, these men think they engineered their own absence. They think they pulled the strings — assessed the risk, protected their assets, kept themselves out of a death march. They feel like the *authors* of their staying.

But the grammar tells a deeper truth: **a man who is unfit for the company of the Messenger is not kept out by his own cleverness. He is left behind by Allah.** The disqualification came first, from above. The "decision" they are so proud of, the calculation they think saved them — that was simply the *form* their being-left-behind took. When the heart is empty of real trust, Allah lets the caravan of the sincere move on without you, and you mistake your exclusion for your strategy.

This is one of the most sobering things in the Qur'an about how guidance and abandonment actually work. The people who stayed home thought they were the subjects of the sentence — the ones doing the action. The word *mukhallafūn* tells them they were the **object.** Something was done to them. They were left.

[PAUSE]

And here is the question the word forces on each of us. When you absent yourself from what Allah is calling you toward — the prayer, the cause, the hard road that the sincere are walking — do you experience it as *I chose not to go?* The word *mukhallaf* whispers a more frightening possibility: that the inability to rise and go *is itself the sign.* That sometimes our cleverest excuse is not the cause of our staying behind. It is the symptom of our having already been left.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Yaqūlūna bi-alsinatihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim* — Speech Amputated at the Tongue

Now Allah does something forensic. Having quoted their excuse, He performs an autopsy on it.

> **يَقُولُونَ بِأَلْسِنَتِهِم مَّا لَيْسَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ**
> *yaqūlūna bi-alsinatihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim*
> "They say **with their tongues** what is **not in their hearts.**"

Let me draw your attention to something that should, grammatically, be completely unnecessary.

When I tell you "she said," you already know she said it with her tongue. With what else would she say it? The phrase "with their tongues," *bi-alsinatihim*, is — on the face of it — redundant. We do not normally specify the organ of speech. So why does Allah add it?

Here is the principle: **in the speech of Allah, when something that should be obvious is stated anyway, the statement is doing work. The "extra" word is the whole point.**

By saying *bi-alsinatihim* — "with their tongues" — Allah is **localizing** their speech. He is drawing a circle around exactly how far the words traveled. The words made it as far as the tongue. **And no further.** They were generated in the mouth, performed by the muscle, pushed out into the air — and they never once descended the short distance into the heart.

Picture it almost physically. There is the tongue, manufacturing sentences. And there is the heart, sealed, untouched, with the curtains drawn. *fa-staghfir lanā* — "seek forgiveness for us" — rolls off the tongue, sounds devout, and the heart that should be the source of those words has no part in them at all. The words are **orphans.** They have a mouth for a parent and nothing behind them.

[PAUSE]

This is the Qur'an's precise anatomy of hypocrisy. Notice it is not that they said something *false about the facts.* Maybe their wealth genuinely did keep them busy. Maybe there were real animals to feed and real children at home. The lie is not in the inventory. **The lie is in the geography.** The words live in the wrong place. They live in the tongue while pretending to come from the heart. *fa-staghfir lanā* asks for the fruit of repentance — forgiveness — without the root of it, which is regret. They want the Prophet's prayer to do for them what their own hearts refused to do.

And now — this is why I had you hold the *first* word of the ayah, *sa-yaqūl*. Watch how it connects.

Allah could predict their excuse in advance precisely *because* it lived only in the tongue. **Tongue-speech is predictable.** When words have no roots in the heart, they are not the living, surprising things a sincere person says — they are mechanical, reflexive, the same script every time. A heart produces speech you cannot fully anticipate, because a living heart is alive. A tongue running without a heart produces speech you could print in advance. The predictability of *sa-yaqūl* is the *proof* of the diagnosis in *bi-alsinatihim*. The two ends of the ayah are the same insight seen from two directions: **what comes only from the tongue can be known before it is spoken.**

[PAUSE]

Hold that against your own worship for one honest moment. *Subḥān-Allāh.* *Astaghfirullāh.* *Allāhu akbar.* When these leave you — how far do they travel? Are they tongue-words or heart-words? You can feel the difference. You have had mornings where "Allāhu akbar" reorganized your whole chest, and mornings where it was lip-movement over a heart already three steps into the day's worries. The ayah is not only describing distant Bedouins. It is handing you an instrument and asking you to measure the distance, in your own *dhikr*, between your tongue and your heart.

### The Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, fitted together.

*Sa-yaqūl* — the excuse can be written in advance, because it was never alive.
*Al-mukhallafūn* — they think they chose to stay; the word says they were left.
*Bi-alsinatihim* — their devout-sounding words never reached the chamber they claimed to come from.

Three windows, one room. A heart that withheld itself, a self that mistook its disqualification for its strategy, a mouth that performed what the heart would not feel.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about *their* words — but about reality itself. Because the second half of the ayah turns from diagnosing the men to dismantling the *calculation* that made them this way.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Blessing Turned Into the Alibi

Let me start where you actually live, far from Ḥudaybiyyah.

You know the feeling of a full inbox at 9pm. You know the weight of a mortgage, the pull of a child who needs to be picked up, the legitimate, real, non-imaginary gravity of a life full of responsibilities. And you know — be honest — how often *that very weight* becomes the reason you give yourself for the thing you don't do. The prayer you postpone. The Qur'an you mean to open. The good you keep deferring. *I have so much on my plate right now.*

We rarely excuse ourselves from God with our sins. We almost always excuse ourselves with our **duties.** With the good and heavy things. This is one of the most respectable forms of spiritual retreat, and the ayah catches it in the act.

> **شَغَلَتْنَآ أَمْوَٰلُنَا وَأَهْلُونَا**
> *shaghalatnā amwālunā wa-ahlūnā*
> "Our **wealth** and our **families** kept us occupied."

Look at the two things they name. Not gambling. Not idleness. Not vice. They name the two greatest blessings a human being is given: **wealth and family.** *Amwāl* — the provision Allah Himself bestowed. *Ahl* — the loved ones Allah Himself gathered around them. These are not their sins. **These are His gifts.** And they have taken His gifts and built a wall out of them — a wall between themselves and Him.

[PAUSE]

Notice the verb, because it is precise. *Shaghalatnā* — "they kept *us* busy." The wealth is the subject of the sentence. The family is the subject. And *we* — the men — are the object, the ones acted upon. "Our wealth busied *us.* Our families occupied *us.*" Do you hear the move? They have made themselves the **passive victims of their own blessings.** As though the wealth reached up and grabbed them by the collar. As though the children physically barred the door.

This is the deep dishonesty inside the respectable excuse. It dresses a *choice* as a *condition.* "I didn't have time" pretends time is something that happens to you rather than something you allocate. "My family needed me" can be true and still be a curtain, because the same family was not, in fact, holding you down — *you* chose them over the call, and then renamed your choice as their demand. The grammar of *shaghalatnā* is the grammar of every adult who has ever blamed his calendar for his priorities.

And here is the heart of it, the thing that should land in the chest. **The very gift becomes the veil over the Giver.** Allah gives you wealth as a means to reach Him — and you let it become the reason you don't. He gives you a family as a trust to raise toward Him — and you let them become the excuse for staying away from Him. Nothing is more tragic than a blessing weaponized against the One who blessed you with it. The *amwāl* and *ahl* were supposed to be the *cargo* of your journey to Allah. The *mukhallafūn* turned them into the **anchor.**

[PAUSE]

So what does this change tomorrow? One concrete thing. The next time you hear yourself reach for the words "I'm too busy" or "my family needs me" as a reason to skip what Allah is asking — **stop and ask which way the blessing is pointing.** Is this wealth, this family, this work, functioning as a road *toward* Him or a wall *against* Him? The same child can be the reason you pray *Fajr* (so he sees you do it) or the reason you miss it (you were up with him). The blessing is neutral. Where you aim it is everything. Refuse to let the gift become the alibi.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: Who Holds Anything For You Against Allah?

Now Allah turns from the excuse to the *engine* underneath it — and this is where the ayah stops being about seventh-century Bedouins and becomes about the deepest anxiety of the modern soul.

Begin again where you live. We are a civilization built on **risk management.** We insure the house, the car, the body, the trip. We diversify the portfolio. We keep the savings, build the cushion, read the reviews, hedge the bets. Underneath all of it is a single, almost unconscious belief: **that with enough foresight and enough control, I can secure my own outcomes.** That safety is something I engineer. That if I am clever enough, I can keep harm away and pull benefit toward me.

That belief is *exactly* what the *mukhallafūn* were running on. Their staying home was a risk-management decision. *If I go, I might die and lose everything. If I stay, I protect my wealth, my family, my life.* It was, on its own terms, a perfectly rational hedge. They were managing their downside.

And now listen to how Allah dismantles the entire premise — not the decision, the *premise.*

> **قُلْ فَمَن يَمْلِكُ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا إِنْ أَرَادَ بِكُمْ ضَرًّا أَوْ أَرَادَ بِكُمْ نَفْعًا**
> *Qul fa-man yamliku lakum mina-llāhi shayʾan in arāda bikum ḍarran aw arāda bikum nafʿā*
> "Say: Then **who holds any power for you against Allah**, if He intends **harm** for you or intends **benefit** for you?"

The verb is *yamliku* — from *milk*, **ownership, control, possession.** Who *owns* anything for you, against Allah? And it is a question shaped like a question but built to be an answer: **no one.** Not one being in creation holds a single atom of leverage over your outcome if Allah wills otherwise.

[PAUSE]

Now watch the precision, because Allah uses *their own two categories* and hands them back, broken.

The whole logic of staying home was: **avoid *ḍarr* (harm — death, loss), secure *nafʿ* (benefit — wealth, safety).** Those are the only two variables in any risk calculation: minimize harm, maximize benefit. And Allah names *both* of them — *ḍarran aw nafʿā* — and places both of them entirely outside human reach. *If He intends harm for you, who can shield you? If He intends benefit for you, who can withhold it?* The two outcomes you stayed home to control are the two outcomes you were never the owner of.

And notice the order: **harm is named first.** That was their primary driver — fear of *ḍarr,* fear of death. Allah leads with the very thing they feared most and says: you cannot buy your way out of it by staying home. The man who went with the Prophet and the man who stayed behind are equally unable to add or subtract one grain from what Allah intends. So the "safety" you secured by staying home was **never real.** You did not move the needle on your own fate. You only forfeited the honor of the caravan and kept the illusion of control.

[PAUSE]

Feel how liberating this is once it stops being a threat and becomes a truth. The exhausting project of the modern self — the endless hedging, the insurance against every contingency, the lying-awake trying to seal every gap through which harm might enter — rests on a false premise: *that I am the owner of my outcomes.* I am not. *Mā yamliku aḥadun lakum mina-llāhi shayʾā* — no one owns anything for you against Allah. Which means the white-knuckled grip you keep on your own safety is **gripping nothing.** You were never holding the wheel. And the moment you truly know that, two things you thought were opposites turn out to be one: you can stop being afraid, *and* you can stop calculating. Because neither the fear nor the calculation was ever in contact with the actual levers of your life.

This is not fatalism. The believers who went to Ḥudaybiyyah were not reckless; they took the *hady,* they walked with wisdom. The point is not "don't act." The point is **act, and then release the outcome to its only Owner.** Tie your camel — and know that the rope was never what kept it.

And then the seal of the ayah:

> **بَلْ كَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا**
> *bal kāna-llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīrā*
> "Rather, Allah is ever, of all you do, **fully Aware.**"

That word *bal* — "rather, nay" — is a hinge. It sweeps away everything that came before. *You thought your calculation was hidden, your real motive private, your devout words convincing? Nay.* And the name He chooses to close with is not *ʿalīm* (the One who knows) but **khabīr** — the One whose knowledge reaches the *hidden inner workings* of a thing, beneath the surface, behind the curtain. It is the perfect word to end an ayah that began by exposing words that lived "in their tongues but not in their hearts." The *khabīr* is precisely the One from whom the gap between tongue and heart **cannot be hidden.** He does not hear the excuse. He reads the chamber the excuse pretended to come from.

[PAUSE]

What does this change tomorrow? When the anxiety rises — the spinning, hedging, what-if dread of loss — meet it with the ayah's question turned inward: *who holds anything for me against Allah?* No one. Then do the wise thing, and let the rest go to its Owner. And when you speak to Him, remember He is *khabīr.* He is not impressed by the polish of your tongue and He is not fooled by it either. Which is terrifying if you are hiding — and the deepest relief in the world if you are tired of performing, and you just want, finally, to be **known.**

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of this ayah are one motion.

The **first half** diagnoses the men: they were *left behind* (mukhallafūn) by a heart that withheld itself; they turned their *blessings* (wealth and family) into a wall; they spoke with *tongues* what was never *in their hearts*.

The **second half** dismantles the *reasoning* that produced them: they stayed home to control *harm* and *benefit* — and Allah, the *khabīr* who reads beneath the tongue, tells them that no one ever owned those outcomes at all.

Do you see that these are the same insight from two directions? **A heart full of real trust would never have needed the excuse, because it would never have believed the calculation.** The whole disease — the tongue-deep words, the blessing-wall, the staying-behind — grows from a single root: the belief that *I am the owner of my outcomes, and I must protect them, even from God's own caravan.* Dissolve that one false belief — *who holds anything for you against Allah?* — and the excuse has nothing left to stand on. The linguistic autopsy and the theological demolition meet in the middle: **what the tongue was protecting, no one ever owned.**

### Questions to Carry

1. If Allah were to write, in advance, the next excuse you will offer Him — would it surprise you, or would you recognize it word for word?
2. Where in your life is a genuine blessing — your work, your wealth, your family — quietly functioning as a *wall* against the One who gave it, rather than a road toward Him?
3. When you make *dhikr*, how far does it travel? Is it tongue-deep, or does it reach the heart it claims to come from?
4. What outcome are you currently gripping with white knuckles — and what would change if you fully believed that no one, including you, holds any power over it against Allah?
5. Are you exhausted from *performing* for a God who already sees beneath the performance — and what would it feel like to simply be *known* by the *khabīr*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you turn your blessings into your excuse and stay behind to protect outcomes you never owned, you reveal a heart that trusted its own grip more than its Lord — and He, who scripts your excuse before you speak it and reads beneath every word your tongue performs, was never deceived for a moment.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not here to make you despair at the distance between your tongue and your heart. It is here to make you *honest* about it — because honesty is the first thing the *khabīr* rewards. The invitation is small and total at once: the next time the respectable excuse rises in you, dressed in the language of duty and need, **stop and ask which way your blessing is pointing, and remember Who owns the outcome you are so afraid of.** Then go where He is calling — not because you can guarantee the result, but because you finally understand that you never could, and that the only safety was ever in being *with* His caravan, not in the cleverness that kept you home.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, *Khabīr* of all that is hidden, You who read what my tongue conceals from itself — let my words reach my heart before they reach the air, and do not leave me behind among those who excuse themselves with the very gifts You gave. You who alone hold harm and benefit, untie my grip from outcomes I never owned, and let me walk with the sincere, even when the road looks like loss. *Āmīn.*
