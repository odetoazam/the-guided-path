# When the Tight Place Opens

## Surah Al-Fath, Ayah 17

---

### The words before us

> [ayah:48:17] لَّيْسَ عَلَى ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ حَرَجٌ وَلَا عَلَى ٱلْأَعْرَجِ حَرَجٌ وَلَا عَلَى ٱلْمَرِيضِ حَرَجٌ ۗ وَمَن يُطِعِ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ يُدْخِلْهُ جَنَّـٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَـٰرُ ۖ وَمَن يَتَوَلَّ يُعَذِّبْهُ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

*Laysa ʿalā l-aʿmā ḥarajun wa-lā ʿalā l-aʿraji ḥarajun wa-lā ʿalā l-marīḍi ḥaraj. Wa-man yuṭiʿi-llāha wa-rasūlahu yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā l-anhār. Wa-man yatawalla yuʿadhdhibhu ʿadhāban alīmā.*

**"There is no blame upon the blind, nor any blame upon the lame, nor any blame upon the sick. And whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, He will admit him into Gardens beneath which rivers flow; but whoever turns away, He will punish him with a painful punishment."**

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Let me take you back to a specific season in Madinah — to the months after a treaty that, on its surface, looked like a defeat.

The believers had set out toward Makkah to perform ʿumrah. They never reached the Kaʿbah. At a place called Hudaybiyyah, they were turned back, made to sign an agreement whose terms felt humiliating — return this year, come back the next; if one of theirs flees to you, send him back; if one of yours flees to them, they keep him. Men who had given everything wept. And then Allah named this very moment, in the opening verse of this surah, a *fatḥ* — an opening, a clear victory.

That word — **fatḥ** — sits over this entire surah like a sky. It means an *opening*. A door that was shut, swinging wide. A thing that was sealed, breaking open.

Now hold that word in one hand.

Because the surah moves on. It turns to the bedouins who had stayed behind when the Prophet ﷺ called — who made excuses, who said *our properties and our families kept us busy* (48:11). And it speaks of a coming call, a future campaign, a real test of who will rise when summoned. And just before our ayah, in 48:16, the warning lands hard:

*"…you will fight them, or they will submit. So if you obey, Allah will give you a good reward; but if you turn away as you turned away before, He will punish you with a painful punishment."*

[PAUSE]

Now imagine you are in that gathering. And imagine you are blind. Or your leg is crippled. Or your body is wasted with chronic illness. You hear *"if you turn away, He will punish you with a painful punishment."* And your heart drops into your stomach. Because you cannot march. You cannot lift a sword. Your body will keep you behind no matter how much your soul wants to go.

The classical commentators preserve exactly this moment. Among the narrations — reported by al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās — when the threat of 48:16 came down, **ahl al-zamāna**, the people of chronic disability, cried out: *"How is it with us, O Messenger of Allah?"* What about us? We cannot go. Are we under the threat too?

And this ayah came down.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Watch what Allah does with the word *ḥaraj*. Watch what He does with the bodies of three named, specific, broken people. And then watch the strangest move of all — watch where He refuses to put a single word, and how that one missing word may be the most merciful silence in the surah.

The surah is called *The Opening*. Let us see what gets opened here.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: ḥaraj — the word that sounds like a closed throat

Before we touch the ayah, let me give you a picture.

Imagine you are walking through open country and you come to a place where the trees grow so densely packed, so tangled and thorned and interlaced, that you cannot pass. There is no gap. The branches lock into each other. You stand at the edge of it and there is simply no way through.

The Arabs had a word for that thicket. They called it **ḥaraj** — or its cousin *ḥarij* — from the root **ḥā–rā–jīm** (ح ر ج). In *Lisān al-ʿArab*, the dense, impenetrable cluster of trees is *al-ḥaraj*. The core sense of this root is **narrowness, constriction, a tightness with no opening** — a space so closed that nothing can move through it.

Now say the word out loud. *Ḥaraj.* Feel where it lives in your body. The **ḥ** is a pharyngeal — it tightens the back of the throat, a constriction of the breath itself. And it closes on a **j**, blocked, abrupt. The word *sounds* like a throat squeezing shut. The mouth performs the meaning. This is a word you cannot say without your own throat narrowing.

[PAUSE]

So when the Quran uses *ḥaraj* for sin, for blame, for guilt — and it does, again and again — it is not naming a legal category. It is naming a *sensation*. Sin is the chest that will not open. Guilt is the inner thicket you cannot walk out of. Blame is the tightening in the throat when you have failed and there is no way through.

Hold onto that, because now the contrast lands.

This surah opened with **fatḥ** — *opening*. And the first thing Allah does for these three broken people is lift the **ḥaraj** — the *constriction*. Do you see it? The whole surah is the drama of opening versus tightness. *Fatḥ* against *ḥaraj*. And the believers who wept at Hudaybiyyah, who felt the world close around them — Allah is teaching them across the whole surah that what looks like a tight place in His hand is an opening. And here, in miniature, for the blind man and the lame man and the sick man who feel the world tightening around their inability — He says the tight place is not yours to carry.

**Laysa ʿalā l-aʿmā ḥaraj.** *There is no ḥaraj upon the blind.*

Notice one more thing the grammar does. *Ḥaraj* arrives **indefinite** — with the tanwīn, the *-un* ending: *ḥarajun*. In Arabic, when you negate an indefinite noun like this — *laysa… ḥarajun* — you are not saying "there is no particular blame." You are saying **there is not a single trace of blame, not any blame at all, none of any kind**. The indefinite under negation sweeps the whole category to zero. Not reduced blame. Not lessened blame. Not *some* blame they'll be forgiven for. **No blame whatsoever** — the thicket is not merely thinned, it is gone. The ground is open country.

What this one word reveals: *Allah did not lighten the burden of the incapable. He erased it.*

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: the threefold repetition — why He names each one

Now I want you to notice something Allah does *not* do, because it tells you who He is.

He could have written it efficiently. Any of us would have. *"There is no blame upon the blind, the lame, and the sick"* — one *ḥaraj*, three nouns, done. That is how a human administrator writes a policy. List the exempt categories, attach the ruling once, move on. It is clean. It is economical.

That is not what Allah does.

> *laysa ʿalā **l-aʿmā** ḥarajun*
> *wa-lā ʿalā **l-aʿraji** ḥarajun*
> *wa-lā ʿalā **l-marīḍi** ḥaraj*

He repeats it. Three times. **No ḥaraj on the blind. No ḥaraj on the lame. No ḥaraj on the sick.** Each person gets his own clause. Each limitation is named separately, and the release is pronounced over each one individually.

Let me give you a human picture of what this is.

Imagine a teacher returning a difficult exam to a class of struggling students. One way: she stands at the front and says, "Everyone who failed, don't worry about it." Comprehensive. Efficient. And somehow it touches no one, because it touched no one *in particular*. Now another way: she walks to each desk, kneels, looks the student in the eye, and says by name — *you, this was not your fault; and you, this was not your fault; and you, this was not your fault.* Same ruling. Entirely different reality. The second one is not issuing a policy. She is *seeing* people.

[PAUSE]

That is the difference between lumping and naming. And Allah, the Most High, who could have lumped, **names**.

Because — stay with me — a disability is not experienced as a category. It is experienced as *mine*. The blind man does not feel himself a member of "the exempt." He feels his *own* darkness, particular and private, the specific way *his* world is shut. The lame man feels *his* leg, the exact betrayal of *his* body when he tries to rise. To each of them, the limitation is the loneliest thing in the world, because no one else lives inside it.

And Allah meets each one *inside* his own limitation. Not "you people." **You. The blind one — no ḥaraj on you. You. The lame one — no ḥaraj on you. You. The sick one — none on you either.**

There is a second thing the order may be doing, and here I am offering you what the sequence invites us to consider rather than a settled ruling. The blind — a permanent, total condition. The lame — permanent but partial; he walks, just not into battle. The sick — often temporary, the most "recoverable" of the three. From the most fixed limitation to the most fluid. As if the release descends through every gradient of human incapacity, so that no one anywhere on the spectrum can say *but surely He didn't mean someone like me.* The net is woven so that no one falls through.

What this repetition reveals: *Allah's mercy is not wholesale. It is retail. It comes to your address, knows your name, and speaks your particular limitation out loud before it lifts it.*

[PAUSE — a long one]

## Element Three: the missing word — the silence where the object should be

Now we come to the strangest and most beautiful thing in the ayah. And it is, fittingly, a thing that *isn't there*.

The ayah turns from the exempt to everyone. Two paths open. Watch the grammar of both with great care.

The first path:

> **wa-man yuṭiʿi-llāha wa-rasūlahu** — *and whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger…*

The verb is **yuṭiʿ**, "obeys," from the root *ṭā–wāw–ʿayn* (ط و ع). And notice: the obedience has an **object**. Two objects, in fact, both named, both explicit. *Obeys whom?* **Allah. And His Messenger.** The target is spelled out. The one who obeys has somewhere clear to face, someone definite to orient toward. The verb of obedience comes with a direction attached.

Now the second path:

> **wa-man yatawalla** — *and whoever turns away…*

The verb is **yatawalla**, from the root *wāw–lām–yāʾ* (و ل ي). And here — stop. Listen to the silence.

*Turns away from… whom? From… what?*

**Allah leaves it blank.**

[PAUSE]

He does not say "whoever turns away *from Allah and His Messenger*," even though that perfect parallel was sitting right there, three words back. He had just named both. To complete the symmetry would have cost Him nothing. Instead He severs the object clean off. **Wa-man yatawalla** — *and whoever turns away* — and the sentence just… leaves him there, facing nothing, the object of his rejection deliberately unspoken.

Read the absence as carefully as the presence. Why would Allah withhold the object here, when He supplied it so fully a breath earlier?

Sit with this. I think the silence is doing at least two things.

**First:** obedience requires an object, because obedience is *orientation*. You cannot obey into the void; you must face something. The grammar insists on it — *yuṭiʿu* reaches out and grabs *Allah and His Messenger* and holds onto them. But turning away does not face anything. That is its whole nature. The one who turns away is not turning *toward* an alternative — he is just turning, into nothing, into the open dark with his back to the only direction that exists. The missing object *is* the meaning. There is nothing there to name, because turning away is precisely a movement toward no-thing. The empty grammatical slot is the empty place his soul now faces.

**Second** — and this is more tender than it first appears — notice what Allah refuses to do. He will not let the act of rejection grab His own name in that intimate, direct grip. Obedience gets to hold *"Allah and His Messenger"* close. Rejection is not granted that closeness even as the thing it pushes against. It is shown the door without the dignity of a named adversary. *You turned away — and We will not even dignify your turning by writing down what you turned from.*

And there is a third thing, hidden in the **form of the verb** itself.

*Yatawalla* is not the simplest form of this root. It is **Form V** — *tafaʿʿala* — the reflexive pattern. The form whose grammatical signature is that the action **curls back onto the doer**. When a verb takes Form V, it carries the sense of doing something *to or for oneself*, the action looping back to its own source. *Taʿallama* — to teach *oneself*, to learn. *Takabbara* — to make *oneself* great, to grow arrogant. And **tawallā** — to turn one's *own self* away.

Do you feel what that does? Compare it to the obedience verb. *Yuṭiʿ* reaches outward — it goes to Allah, it goes to the Messenger, it spends itself on something beyond the self. But *yatawalla* is reflexive — it bends back inward. The one who turns away is not, in the end, doing something to Allah. **He is doing something to himself.** The verb's own shape testifies that the harm of turning away has only one address, and it is the turner's. He wraps the act around his own neck.

[PAUSE]

And then watch the consequence verb: **yuʿadhdhibhu**, "He will punish him," from *ʿayn–dhāl–bāʾ* (ع ذ ب). This is **Form II** — *faʿʿala* — the doubled, *intensive* form. Not merely *ʿadhaba* but *ʿadhdhaba*, the pattern that intensifies the action. And then the Quran *doubles down on the doubling*: **yuʿadhdhibhu ʿadhāban alīmā** — He will punish him with a *punishment* — the verb's own noun laid beside it for weight — and that punishment is **alīm**, "painful," a word that stretches its middle vowel into a long *ī*, a sound that aches as you say it: *a-lī-m*. The reflexive turning that curled back on the self meets an intensified pain that the grammar will not let you soften.

What this missing word reveals: *Obedience faces Someone. Turning away faces no one — and lands on no one but yourself.*

---

### The bridge

Look at what these three discoveries have shown us, sitting together.

The word **ḥaraj** taught us that Allah deals not in legal categories but in *sensations* — He reaches for the tightness in the chest and the closing of the throat, and He opens it. The **threefold repetition** taught us that His mercy comes to each person by name, kneeling at each desk, never lumping the broken into a crowd. And the **missing object** after *yatawalla* taught us that the real catastrophe of turning away is that it faces nothing and harms only the one who does it.

Three pieces of grammar. But now let us ask what they reveal not only about words — but about *reality itself*. About what it actually means to be a limited human being standing before an unlimited God.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: When your body becomes your accuser

I want to begin not in the seventh century, but in something you already know.

There is a particular kind of suffering that has nothing to do with pain and everything to do with *falling short*. You have felt it. It is the feeling of watching everyone else do the thing you cannot do. The friend who runs the marathon while your knees are gone. The colleague who works the eighty-hour weeks while your body, or your mind, or your circumstance, simply will not let you. The parent in the hospital bed who cannot be the parent they were. The believer who watches others pray the night prayer, fast the long fasts, travel for Hajj, sit upright in the rows — and cannot.

There is a voice that comes with that feeling. And the voice does not say *"you are in pain."* It says *"you are not enough."* It turns your very limitation into an accusation. Your body, your situation, the thing that already hurts — it becomes a finger pointing at you, whispering that you are *less*, that you are *behind*, that you are *guilty* for what you cannot give.

[PAUSE]

That voice was in the gathering at Madinah. Read again what the disabled men said when the warning came down: *"How is it with us, O Messenger of Allah?"* That is not a legal question. That is the cry of a heart that has already convicted itself. They heard "whoever turns away will be punished," and their first instinct — the instinct of every sincere soul with a limitation — was *that must include me; I, too, will be left behind; my body has already failed the test.*

This is the deepest reflex of the conscientious heart: to assume that incapacity is the same as guilt. That if I cannot do, I must be blamed for not doing.

And into that, Allah speaks. Not "calm down." Not "it's complicated." He speaks the word *ḥaraj* and He kills it dead. **Laysa ʿalā l-aʿmā ḥaraj.** The thing you were about to carry — the tightness, the self-blame, the conviction — *it is not on you.* Put it down. It was never yours to hold.

But here is where the ayah becomes breathtaking, and where it refuses to be merely consoling.

[PAUSE]

Because a lesser mercy would have stopped there. A lesser mercy says: *you are excused — now step aside.* You are exempt — which also means you are *out*. You don't have to run the race, but you also don't get the medal. The blame is lifted *and so is the reward.* You are released into irrelevance. And for a soul that wants nothing more than to give, *that* exemption is its own quiet grief. *I'm excused. I don't matter to the mission anymore.*

Watch what Allah does instead. The very next breath after lifting the blame is **not** about the disabled at all — and that is the mercy. He does not say "the blind, the lame and the sick are excused, *the end.*" He pivots immediately to the universal door: **wa-man yuṭiʿi-llāha wa-rasūlahu yudkhilhu jannāt** — *and whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, He admits him into Gardens.*

Whoever. **Whoever.**

The door of the Garden is not the door of the battlefield. The battlefield has a height requirement, a strength requirement, a working-legs-and-eyes requirement. **The Garden does not.** Its only requirement is the orientation of the heart — *whoever obeys.* And obedience of the heart is something a blind man can do as completely as the strongest warrior who ever drew a sword. *More* completely, sometimes, because his obedience costs him the grief of being held back, and he obeys anyway.

This is why the Prophet ﷺ said of those left behind in Madinah by genuine excuse — in a sound, marfūʿ narration — that there were men in the city who, *"you did not traverse a valley nor march a march but they were with you; their excuse held them back."* **With you.** The body stayed in Madinah; the reward marched every mile. The verb *yudkhilhu* — "He admits him," Form IV, Allah Himself causing the entry — does not check your legs at the gate. It checks where your heart was facing.

[PAUSE]

So feel the full architecture of this consolation. First Allah lifts the *ḥaraj* — *you are not blamed.* Then, before you can sink into the loneliness of being excused, He throws open the Garden — *and you are not excluded either.* The exemption from the duty is not an exemption from the reward. You are released from what your body cannot do, and welcomed into what your heart can.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** there is something you cannot do. Everyone has it — an illness, a constraint, a closed door, a body or a circumstance that will not bend. The lie is that this lack is a verdict on your worth. The truth of this ayah is that Allah has already separated your *capacity* from your *standing before Him*. So stop letting the thing you cannot do file charges against your soul. Ask instead the only question the Garden asks: *in the matter that is actually in my hands — the obedience of the heart — which way am I facing?* Give Him that. The legs, the eyes, the strength, the circumstance — He never asked you for those. He gave them to you or withheld them as He willed. He asks you only for the direction of your face.

---

## Theme Two: The line that actually divides us

Let me show you how we secretly rank human beings — including ourselves.

We rank by *capacity*. By output. By what a person can do, produce, accomplish, contribute. It is the deep grammar of the modern world and it is older than the modern world. The strong above the weak. The able above the disabled. The productive above the dependent. The one who can march above the one who must stay behind. We don't always say it. But we feel it — and we feel it hardest about ourselves, on the days we cannot perform.

So watch this ayah quietly demolish that entire ranking system.

It opens with three men who, by the world's measure, are at the *bottom* of the capacity ladder: the blind, the lame, the sick. The ones who cannot contribute to the great task of the moment. And the world's logic says: these are the lesser ones, the burden, the left-behind.

And then the ayah draws its real line — and the line does not fall where the world draws it. The line is not *able versus unable.* Watch where it actually falls:

> **wa-man yuṭiʿ** — whoever obeys → the Garden
> **wa-man yatawalla** — whoever turns away → the painful punishment

[PAUSE]

That is the dividing line of the human race. Not capacity. **Orientation.** Not what your body can do. Which way your heart turns. And here is the staggering consequence the ayah forces on you: a blind man who *obeys* is on the Garden side of the line — and a man with two good eyes and two strong legs who *turns away* is on the punishment side. The strong man's strength bought him *nothing*. The blind man's blindness cost him *nothing*. The entire ladder we spend our lives climbing — the world's ladder of capacity — is revealed to be standing in the wrong field altogether. It does not even touch the line that matters.

Now connect this to the missing word we found earlier, because the two themes are about to become one insight.

Remember: *yuṭiʿ* — "obeys" — came with its object named. Allah and His Messenger. A clear direction to face. But *yatawalla* — "turns away" — had its object stripped off, and the verb itself was reflexive, curling the act back onto the one who did it. Put that grammar next to this theme and watch what it says about the two kinds of people.

The obedient person is *oriented*. His life faces a direction. There is a Someone at the center of it, named and definite, around whom everything arranges itself. Even when his body fails — *especially* when his body fails — his face is turned toward that center. He is the blind man whose eyes are dark but whose heart is pointed straight at his Lord.

The one who turns away is *disoriented* — in the most literal sense, he has lost his *orient*, his East, his direction. The grammar gave him no object because he is facing nothing. And the reflexive form told us the truth of his condition: in turning from the only direction that exists, he has not harmed that direction at all — he has only spun himself loose into the void, *to himself,* by himself, his back to the light, the punishment landing on no one but the one who turned.

[PAUSE]

So here is the whole ayah as a single thought:

The world ranks you by what you can *do.* Allah ranks you by which way you *face.* And the most able body in the world, facing the wrong way — or facing nothing at all — is lost; while the most broken body in the world, facing Him, is on its way to Gardens beneath which rivers flow.

This is almost unbearably good news, and it is also a warning with teeth.

It is good news because it means your limitations were never the obstacle. The thing you thought disqualified you — your weakness, your illness, your inability to keep up — Allah just told you it has no bearing on the line that divides the saved from the lost. You were never ranked by your capacity. You can stop grieving the capacity you lack.

And it is a warning with teeth because it strips every excuse from the *able*. If the blind man is held to the obedience of his heart, then the man with every faculty intact is held to it tenfold. Your strength is not a credit in your account. It is not a substitute for facing Him. The bedouins this surah rebukes had *working bodies* and stayed home; they had every capacity and turned away — and capacity did not save them, because capacity was never the currency. The one thing required of every body, strong or broken, is the turn of the heart toward its Lord.

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** notice how often, today, you will measure yourself and others by capacity — by productivity, by what got done, by who showed up and performed and who fell short. And notice that Allah is not, in the end, asking that question of you. He is asking one question, and He will ask it on the day the ledgers open: *which way were you facing?* So tonight, in the one arena that is fully yours regardless of what your body or your circumstance allows — the orientation of your heart in obedience — turn. Even if you can do nothing else. Even if you are the blind man, the lame man, the sick one in the back of the gathering. Turn your face toward Him. That turn is the whole game.

[PAUSE — major pause]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The architecture, now that we can see it

Step back and look at the shape of the whole ayah, because it is built with intention.

It begins by **emptying out** — three negations, *no ḥaraj, no ḥaraj, no ḥaraj* — clearing the ground, lifting the weight off three named and broken people until the field is open and level.

Then, onto that cleared and level ground, it sets the **real division** — two paths, perfectly weighted against each other: *whoever obeys… whoever turns away.* The Garden, and the painful punishment.

And notice the ring that binds this ayah to the one before it. Ayah 48:16 ended its warning to the able-bodied bedouins with **yuʿadhdhibkum ʿadhāban alīmā** — *He will punish you with a painful punishment.* Our ayah, 48:17, ends with **yuʿadhdhibhu ʿadhāban alīmā** — *He will punish him with a painful punishment.* The exact phrase, echoed. But watch what the echo *does.* In 48:16 the painful punishment hangs over the *capable* who might turn away. And 48:17 quietly walks the disabled *out* from under that shadow — *no ḥaraj on you* — and then re-aims the very same threat with precision: it falls on **whoever turns away**, able and unexcused. The threat did not disappear. It was *re-pointed*, away from the broken who cannot, onto the whole who will not. The mercy and the warning are the same sentence, sorting the room.

## The two directions, one insight

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey have been climbing the same mountain from two faces.

The grammar showed us a verb of obedience that *reaches outward* to a named Lord, and a verb of turning-away that has *no object* and *curls back on the self.* The themes showed us that human beings are divided not by capacity but by *orientation* — which way the heart faces. These are the same discovery. **The whole ayah is about direction.** *Ḥaraj* is the soul facing a closed thicket; its lifting is an *opening,* a *fatḥ.* Obedience is the face turned toward Someone. Turning away is the face turned toward nothing, and the harm of it loops home to the one who turned. From the smallest particle of grammar to the largest division of humanity, this ayah says one thing: **what matters is the direction you face.**

## Questions to carry

- What is the thing I cannot do — and have I been letting it file charges against my worth that Allah already dismissed?
- When I rank myself and others, am I using the world's ladder of *capacity*, or Allah's line of *orientation*?
- The obedient heart faces a named Someone. When I am still and honest — what is my heart actually facing?
- Where in my life am I *turning away* in small reflexive movements — and have I noticed that the harm of it lands on no one but me?
- I have capacities the disabled men of that gathering did not. Am I treating my strength as a *credit* — or as a *trust I will be asked about*?

## The whole ayah in one sentence

*Allah lifts every trace of blame from the body that cannot, so that He can hold the whole of humanity to the one thing every heart can do — turn and face Him.*

## The invitation

Let something shift. Stop carrying the *ḥaraj* of what your body or your life will not allow — Allah took it off you by name. And stop trusting in the capacities you do have as though they were the test. The test is the turn of the face. It is available to the blind and the lame and the sick and the strong, equally, tonight, right now, costing nothing but the willingness to face Him. The surah is called *The Opening.* The opening is offered to you in the only place it has ever mattered: the direction of your heart.

## A closing duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who lifted the *ḥaraj* from the blind and the lame and the sick by their very names — lift from our hearts the blame we were never meant to carry, and the tightness we mistook for our own guilt. Turn our faces fully toward You and Your Messenger, and never let us be among those who turn away to nothing and lose only themselves. Open for us, as You open all things, and admit us by Your mercy into Gardens beneath which rivers flow. *Āmīn.*
