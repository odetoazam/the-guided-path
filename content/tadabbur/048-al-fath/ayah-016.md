# The Ones Who Were Left Behind — and the Call That Refuses to Let the Label Be Final

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin by placing the verse in front of you, and then I want us to do something with it that we don't usually do. I don't want us to read it as a verse about a war. I want us to read it as a verse about a label — and about what Allah does with people who have already failed.

> قُل لِّلْمُخَلَّفِينَ مِنَ ٱلْأَعْرَابِ سَتُدْعَوْنَ إِلَىٰ قَوْمٍ أُو۟لِى بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ تُقَـٰتِلُونَهُمْ أَوْ يُسْلِمُونَ ۖ فَإِن تُطِيعُوا۟ يُؤْتِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ أَجْرًا حَسَنًا ۖ وَإِن تَتَوَلَّوْا۟ كَمَا تَوَلَّيْتُم مِّن قَبْلُ يُعَذِّبْكُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

*Qul lil-mukhallafīna mina-l-aʿrābi satudʿawna ilā qawmin ulī baʾsin shadīdin tuqātilūnahum aw yuslimūn, fa-in tuṭīʿū yuʾtikumu-llāhu ajran ḥasanā, wa-in tatawallaw kamā tawallaytum min qablu yuʿadhdhibkum ʿadhāban alīmā.*

"Say to those who were left behind of the Bedouins: 'You will be called to a people of great might; you will fight them, or they will submit. So if you obey, Allah will give you a good reward; but if you turn away as you turned away before, He will punish you with a painful punishment.'"

### Rebuilding the world

To feel this verse, you have to stand where these men were standing.

It is the sixth year after the Hijra. The Prophet ﷺ announces that he is setting out for Mecca — not for war, but for ʿUmrah, to circle the House. He calls the believers of Medina and the Bedouin tribes camped around it to come with him. Around fourteen hundred answer.

But a number of the desert Arabs — the *aʿrāb*, the Bedouins of the surrounding wilderness — do the math in their heads. Mecca is the lion's den. The Quraysh are at the height of their hostility. To walk into their territory unarmed, for a pilgrimage, with no guarantee of return — this looks, to a certain kind of calculating mind, like a march toward death. And these were people, as the surah will say, who suspected that **the Prophet and the believers would never come back to their families at all** (48:12). So they stayed home. And later — Allah tells us in ayah 11 — they came with their excuses ready: *"Our properties and our families kept us busy, so ask forgiveness for us."* Smooth words. Pre-packaged. The kind of excuse you build in advance because you already know, somewhere inside, that you'll need one.

Then something they did not predict happened. The expedition did not end in a massacre. It ended in a treaty — the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah — which the Quran itself calls *al-Fath*, **the Victory**, the opening. The very thing they fled from turned out to be the thing Allah was about to bless beyond anything they imagined.

So now picture the emotional climate. These men gambled on staying safe — and safety turned out to be the losing bet. They are sitting in their tents with the bitter taste of having been *wrong*. And there is a new campaign coming, with spoils attached, and they would love to come along now that they can smell the profit (that's ayah 15). And Allah says: no. You don't get to attach yourself to *that* reward.

This is the moment our verse drops. And here is the question I want to plant in you before we go a single word further:

### The hook

When a person has already failed — already stayed behind, already made the excuse, already been caught — what does Allah *call* them? And once He has named them by their failure, is that name the end of the story, or the beginning of one?

Watch what Allah does with these men in a single verse. Watch the grammar move them. Because by the time this ayah ends, the people who began it as objects — things that *happened*, that *were left* — will have been handed back the steering wheel of their own destiny. [PAUSE]

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مُخَلَّفِينَ — the word that lets you off the hook (and won't)

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because the whole verse turns on it.

In English, we have an active voice and a passive voice. "I left them behind" is active — I did it. "They were left behind" is passive — something was done to them; we don't even have to say by whom. Notice what the passive voice does to responsibility. It quietly removes the hand. *Mistakes were made.* By whom? The sentence doesn't say. The passive voice is the native language of avoiding blame. We reach for it instinctively. *"It didn't get done."* *"Things came up."* *"I got left out."*

Arabic has this too, and it has something extra. It has verb *forms* — patterns stamped onto a root that change its meaning. Take the root **خ-ل-ف** (kh-l-f), which has to do with what comes *behind*, what is left *after*. The simplest form, *khalafa*, just means to come after, to succeed someone. But press it into **Form II** — *khallafa*, with the doubled middle letter — and it becomes deliberate, transitive: **to leave someone behind, to make them stay back, to deliberately keep them in the rear.**

Now Allah does not call these men *mukhallifīn* — the ones who *left* others behind, the active doers. He calls them **مُخَلَّفِينَ — al-mukhallafīn — the ones who were left behind.** It is the passive participle of that deliberate Form II. The *ones who got left*. The acted-upon. The objects.

Sit with this, because it is strange.

These men were not left behind by anyone. **They left themselves behind.** They made the choice. They built the excuse. Nobody barred the door of their tent. So why does Allah describe them with the very grammar of victimhood — *the ones who were left behind*, as though it happened *to* them?

Let's not rush to one answer. Let me give you two, and I want you to feel both at once, because I think the Quran intends both.

**The first reading:** this is the name they would give themselves. This is exactly how a person who skips out describes their own absence. Listen to how we do it. We don't say "I abandoned the project." We say "I got left out of the loop." We don't say "I quit on them." We say "things fell apart." The passive participle *mukhallaf* is the self-flattering label — *I didn't fail to show up; I was somehow left behind, it was circumstances, it was my property and my family, it was beyond me.* Allah is naming them by **the very excuse they are clutching.** He meets them inside their own self-narration.

**The second reading** — and this is heavier — runs underneath the first. There is a teaching in the tafsir tradition, drawn from the logic of the whole surah, that says: when a person repeatedly chooses to fall back, Allah *makes that their reality.* He leaves them behind. *Karihallāhu-nbiʿāthahum fa-thabbaṭahum* — "Allah disliked their going forth, so He held them back" — that is the parallel verse in Sūrat at-Tawba (9:46). The chilling possibility folded into the passive voice is this: **you keep stepping back, and one day you discover that the stepping-back is no longer your choice — it has become your sentence.** You wanted to be left behind, and so you were *left* behind — passively now, by a decree, because you spent your active choices buying exactly that.

Do you feel how the single word holds both? It is at once the alibi they tell themselves *and* the verdict that alibi earns. The grammar that lets you off the hook is the same grammar that hangs you on it. [PAUSE]

And here is what makes it land. Allah did not have to use this word. He is the One choosing the description. He could have called them *al-mutakhallifīn* — the ones who deliberately stayed back, active, owning it. He chose the passive. He chose to name them the way they name themselves — and in doing so He holds up a mirror so precise that they cannot look at it without seeing the lie inside their own excuse.

What this single word reveals: **the labels we use to escape responsibility are heard, exactly, by Allah — and He will repeat them back to us until we hear what they actually confess.** [PAUSE]

### Element Two: سَتُدْعَوْنَ — the future that reopens a door you thought was shut

Now watch what comes immediately after the label. Having named them by their failure, what is the very next word out of Allah's command?

Not condemnation. Not "you are finished." The word is **سَتُدْعَوْنَ — *satudʿawna* — "you *will* be called."**

Let me open this slowly, because three things are packed into it.

First, the *sīn* on the front — that little prefix **سَـ**. In Arabic it is the particle of the near future, like the English "will" or "shall." It throws the whole sentence forward in time. Allah is not talking about what they *did*. He has turned their face away from the wreckage of the past and pointed it at something that **has not happened yet.** There is a tomorrow in this verse. For people drowning in a yesterday they cannot fix, the most merciful word in the language may simply be: *you will*.

Second — and this is exquisite — it is **passive again.** *Tudʿawna* — "you will be *called*." The same passive voice as *mukhallafīn*. But watch what has changed. A moment ago, the passive made them objects of their own shameful inertia — *the ones who got left*. Now the passive makes them objects of **a summons from above** — *the ones who will be called*. The same grammatical posture, but the hand behind the action is no longer their cowardice. It is **Allah's invitation.** They are still being acted upon — but now the One acting upon them is the One opening the door.

Do you see the artistry? The verse takes the exact grammatical shape of their disgrace — *to be left* — and transfigures it into the shape of their second chance — *to be called*. Passive to passive. From *left behind* to *called forward*. Same voice; opposite destiny.

Third: the root itself. **د-ع-و** (d-ʿ-w) is the root of *daʿwah* — a call, an invitation, a summons. It is the same root from which we make *duʿāʾ*, the call we send *up* to Allah. Here the direction reverses: Allah sends the call *down* to them. The men who would not answer the Prophet's call to ʿUmrah are being told — **you will be called again.** The door you slammed is going to be knocked on a second time.

Now feel the weight of that against everything we just said. These are men Allah has just barred from the spoils of the next raid (that was ayah 15 — *"you shall not follow us"*). You would think the file is closed. You would think a God who has caught your suspicion, exposed your excuse, and named you by your retreat would be done with you.

Instead: *you will be called.*

This is the theology hiding in a verb tense. **Allah does not close the file on the one who failed.** The barring in ayah 15 was not exile; it was discipline. The very next breath holds out a fresh door — a *harder* door, a door to a people of "great might," but a real one, and an honorable one. The failure was not made permanent. It was made into a doorway. [PAUSE]

But notice the test embedded in the gift. The call is coming. Whether it becomes your redemption or your ruin will depend on one thing only — not on what you did last time, but on **whether you answer this time.** Mercy here is not the cancellation of the test. Mercy is being *given another test* when you had forfeited the right to one.

What this single word reveals: **with Allah, being named by your failure is not the verdict — it is the setup for a summons. The door reopens; the only question He leaves you is whether you will walk through it.** [PAUSE]

### Element Three: تَتَوَلَّوْا۟ ... تَوَلَّيْتُم — the mirror in which your past predicts your future

Now we come to the hinge of the whole verse, and to a piece of grammar so deliberate it should make us shiver.

At the end of the ayah, Allah lays out the two roads. *Fa-in tuṭīʿū* — "if you obey" — *yuʾtikumu-llāhu ajran ḥasanā* — "Allah will give you a good reward." And then the other road: *wa-in tatawallaw* — "but if you turn away" — and here He adds five words that He did not need to add: **كَمَا تَوَلَّيْتُم مِّن قَبْلُ — *kamā tawallaytum min qablu* — "as you turned away before."**

Let me teach you the verb first. The root is **و-ل-ي** (w-l-y), which is about nearness, about turning toward, about taking a side. From it comes *walī* — a close ally, a protector. But press it into **Form V** — *tawallā* — and it becomes **reflexive**: literally, *to turn oneself away*, to take one's own self and pivot it in the opposite direction. Form V folds the action back onto the doer. This is not being turned away by someone. This is **you, turning you.** The whole movement originates in the self and lands on the self.

And Allah uses this reflexive verb **twice in one clause** — once in the future, *tatawallaw* ("if you turn yourselves away"), and once in the past, *tawallaytum* ("as you turned yourselves away") — joined by the word **كَمَا — *kamā* — "just as," "in the very same way."**

Stay with what He is doing. He is holding a mirror between two moments of your life. He is saying: *the way you turned your own self away before — that exact motion — is the motion that threatens you now.* Your past is not being dragged up to shame you. It is being held up as **evidence.** Allah is showing them that the pattern of retreat is not a one-time event; it is a *groove* a person wears into their own soul, and the soul slides down it again unless something breaks the slope.

And here is the precise terror and mercy of *kamā*. It is the most honest word in the verse. It refuses to flatter them. It says: *I know exactly what you are. I watched you do this before. The likeliest thing in the world is that you will do it again — because turning away is what you do.* There is no naïveté in this God. He is not pretending the past didn't happen.

But — and this is everything — **He says it as a warning, not as a sentence.** [PAUSE] Think about the difference. If your past *were* your sentence, why warn you? You only warn someone whose future is still open. The word *kamā*, "just as you did before," would be pure cruelty if the outcome were fixed. It is instead pure mercy *disguised* as severity, because it means: **I am telling you the pattern precisely so that this time you can break it.** The whole point of naming the groove is that you might, by Allah's leave, climb out of it.

And now look at the architecture of the two consequences, because the asymmetry is deliberate. If you obey: *yuʾtikumu-llāhu ajran ḥasanā* — **Allah** gives you a good reward; His name is right there in the sentence, the Giver named, the gift "good." If you turn away: *yuʿadhdhibkum ʿadhāban alīmā* — He punishes you with a painful punishment. Notice the verb *yuʿadhdhib* is **Form II**, the intensive — and notice that on the road of obedience the reward is tied to *Allah's giving*, an act of generosity beyond what is earned, while on the road of turning away the punishment is tied precisely to *your turning*. The reward is more than you deserve; the punishment is exactly what you chose. **Mercy overflows the deed; justice merely matches it.** That asymmetry is the signature of the One speaking.

What this construction reveals: **your past is read by Allah as a pattern, not a prison — and He tells you the pattern out loud precisely because you still have the power, this time, to refuse to repeat it.** [PAUSE — major pause]

### The bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, standing together.

*Mukhallafīn* — Allah names them by the failure, in the very passive voice they hide inside.
*Satudʿawna* — and then He reopens the door, in that same passive voice, transfigured into a summons.
*Tatawallaw kamā tawallaytum* — and He warns them, with total honesty, that the old groove is still cut into them — *so that they might not slide down it again.*

The grammar itself is a redemption arc. It begins with men as objects of their own cowardice and ends with men as agents holding two open roads in their hands. Allah does the work of moving them from passive to active inside a single verse — from *the ones who were left* to *the ones who will obey, or turn away.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way Allah deals with a human soul that has already let Him down.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The grammar of our excuses — and the God who hears the passive voice

Let me start where you live, not where the Bedouins lived.

You know the feeling of the unmade phone call. The text you've been meaning to answer for nine days. The relative you haven't visited. The prayer you keep "getting to." The dream you've been "putting off." And you know — be honest — the *exact language* you use in your own head about these things. You almost never say: *I am choosing not to do this.* You say: *I haven't gotten around to it. It slipped. I've been so slammed. Things have been crazy.* Notice the grammar. **It is all passive, all weather, all circumstance.** The "I" who is choosing has quietly stepped out of the sentence. The avoidance is described as if it were a season that happened to you, like rain.

This is the most human thing in the world. It is also exactly what the Bedouins did. *"Our properties and our families kept us busy"* (48:11) — listen to it — *our property* is the subject, *we* are the object. The wealth did the keeping; they were merely kept. They built a sentence in which they were not the actor. And Allah's response, across these verses, is devastatingly gentle and devastatingly precise: He picks up their grammar — *the ones who were left behind* — and hands it back to them so they can hear what it actually confesses.

Here is the spiritual principle, and it is one of the most useful things you will ever internalize: **the structure of the language you use about your own avoidance is the structure of the lie you are telling yourself.** Whenever you catch yourself narrating your own inaction in the passive voice — *it didn't happen, it fell through, I got too busy* — there is almost always an active verb you are hiding from. *I declined. I avoided. I chose comfort. I was afraid.*

And why does this matter so much? Because **you cannot repent of a passive sentence.** You cannot turn back from "things came up." There is no agent in it to turn. Tawba — return — requires an *I* who went somewhere to come back. The passive voice doesn't just excuse the failure; it dissolves the very self that would need to repent. This is why Allah's naming them *mukhallafīn* is an act of mercy and not just exposure: He is forcing the hidden "I" back into the sentence, so that there is someone present who *can* turn around.

Let me push one layer deeper. The frightening thing the verse hints at — through that passive participle that becomes, by the parallel in Sūrat at-Tawba, almost a decree — is that **a self-excuse repeated long enough hardens into a self.** The first time, staying behind is a choice you make. The hundredth time, staying behind is simply *who you are*; the choosing has gone underground; you have become, in truth, "one of the left-behind," not as a description of an act but as a name for a person. We build our character out of the verbs we keep choosing, and then one day the character chooses for us. That is the real danger in *kamā tawallaytum min qablu* — "as you turned away before." It is not that the past act is held against you. It is that **the past act, unrepented, becomes the template the future is poured into.**

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It means the first act of any real return is *grammatical*. Before you can fix the thing, you have to **re-write the sentence in the active voice.** Stop saying "I haven't gotten around to praying Fajr" and say "I have been choosing sleep over my Lord." It sounds harsher. It is actually the door out. Because the moment you say *I chose*, you have just established that there is an *I* who can choose otherwise — tomorrow, before the sun. Naming the failure in the active voice is not self-flagellation. It is the resurrection of the agent who can repent.

The practical application is small and exact: **this week, catch one passive sentence you tell yourself about a duty you're dodging, and translate it into the active voice — out loud, with "I."** Watch what it does. The excuse cannot survive contact with the word *I chose*. That translation is the whole of tawba in miniature. [PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The reopened door — that failure is a chapter, not a verdict

Now let me take you to the other side of the verse, to the part that I think can genuinely change how a person carries their worst memory.

There is a particular kind of despair that has nothing to do with not believing in Allah. It is the despair of someone who believes completely — and is *certain they have already disqualified themselves.* The one who broke the fast they swore to keep. The one who relapsed again after the hundredth repentance. The one who wasn't there when their family needed them and can never undo it. The one who, when the real test came — the call to courage, to honesty, to sacrifice — *turned away*, and now lives with the private conviction that the door of being-someone-good closed behind them on that day. This person doesn't doubt Allah's mercy in the abstract. They just quietly believe it is for other people. They have filed themselves, permanently, under *mukhallaf*. **Left behind. Past tense. Closed.**

Now bring this verse to that person.

These Bedouins are not hypothetical failures. They are the genuine article. They suspected the Prophet ﷺ and the believers would be slaughtered (48:12). They sat at home while fourteen hundred better men walked into danger. They came back with rehearsed excuses. And then — caught, exposed, and barred — they tried to glom onto the next campaign for the loot, and were told *no, not that one.* If anyone had earned the closed door, it was these men.

And the very first thing Allah says to them after the closing of the one door is: **سَتُدْعَوْنَ — you will be called.**

Let that detonate slowly. The God who saw the suspicion in their hearts, who heard the excuse before they finished building it, who knows with total certainty (*kamā tawallaytum*) that turning away is their established pattern — **that** God is the One holding open a fresh door. Not a lesser door. A *harder, more honorable* door — a summons to face "a people of great might." He is not offering them a consolation prize. He is offering them the chance to be exactly the kind of men they failed to be at Hudaybiyyah. The redemption He offers is *shaped like the failure*: you fell short on the field of courage, so I will call you back to a field of courage.

Here is the principle, and I want you to take it like medicine: **In the economy of Allah, failure is never the verdict. It is the chapter before the summons.** The barred door of ayah 15 and the opened door of ayah 16 are not contradiction — they are the two halves of how Allah disciplines the ones He has not given up on. He withholds the reward you tried to grab *for free* (the easy raid, the cheap redemption) precisely so that He can offer you the reward you must *earn* (the hard call, the real return). The closing of the cheap door is itself a mercy, because it pushes you toward the door that will actually remake you.

And notice the history, because Allah verified this promise in time. There is a beautiful report preserved by al-Baghawi — and it is an account of the companions, an *athar*, narrated from Rāfiʿ ibn Khadīj — who said: *"We used to recite this verse and not know who these people [of great might] were, until Abū Bakr called us to fight Banū Ḥanīfa"* — the apostates of the Ridda wars. The early scholars then differed about precisely whom the verse intended: Ibn ʿAbbās, Mujāhid and ʿAṭāʾ said it was Persia; Kaʿb said the Romans; al-Ḥasan said both; others said Hawāzin and Thaqīf, or the people of the Ridda. Hold all of these lightly and notice *why* they differ: **the Quran never names the people.** It says only *qawmin ulī baʾsin shadīd* — "a people of great might," unnamed. The scholars dispute precisely because Allah left the door open-ended.

And that absence is the gift. Because if the verse had named "Persia," it would have expired with Persia. By leaving the enemy unnamed, Allah made the summons **portable into every life.** *You will be called to a people of great might* — and the "great might" you will be called to face may not wear armor at all. It may be the addiction you swore you'd left. The fear that owns you. The grudge you cannot set down. The prayer you keep abandoning at the threshold. **Everyone reading this has, waiting somewhere in their future, a "people of great might" — a hard thing they failed once and will be called to again.** The unnamed enemy is the verse reaching across fourteen centuries to put its hand on *your* unfinished battle.

So what does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It means you are permitted — *required*, even — to stop treating your worst day as your final grade. The man who stayed home from Hudaybiyyah was not, it turns out, "the man who stayed home from Hudaybiyyah." He was "the man being prepared for Yamāma." The label was a chapter title, not the book. And the only way to find out which chapter your failure was — the last one or the second-to-last — is to **still be standing, and listening, when the next call comes.** Because the call *is* coming. *Satudʿawna.* The summons is not in doubt. Only your answer is.

The practical application is this: **identify the one "people of great might" in your own life — the hard thing you failed before — and decide, now, in advance, before the call comes, what your answer will be.** The Bedouins failed because they decided their answer in the moment of fear. Decide yours in the moment of clarity, which is now. Pre-commit to obedience, so that when the door reopens — and it will — the groove your foot finds is the new one, not the old. ## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we have walked through it, stand back and see the shape of the whole verse, because it is built with intention.

It opens with a **passive label of disgrace** — *mukhallafīn*, the ones who *were left.*
It pivots on a **passive verb of mercy** — *satudʿawna*, the ones who *will be called.*
And it closes by handing them **two active roads** — *tuṭīʿū* / *tatawallaw*, "if you obey" / "if you turn away" — with the consequences laid out: reward that Allah's own generosity overflows, or punishment that merely matches the deed.

Watch the movement of agency across those three stations. At the start, these men are pure objects — things that were left, with no verb of their own. By the end, they are pure subjects — *they* obey, *they* turn, and the entire future hinges on their choice. **Allah uses the body of the verse to walk them from being acted-upon to being actors.** The grammar is not decoration around the meaning. The grammar *is* the meaning. The verse does to these men, in language, exactly what Allah is offering to do with their lives: take a passive failure and hand it back a steering wheel.

### Bringing it together

And now you can see that our two themes were never two. They were one truth approached from two sides.

The first theme said: *the passive voice of your excuses dissolves the self that could repent — so name your failure in the active voice and resurrect the agent.* The second theme said: *failure is a chapter, not a verdict — the door reopens, and a summons is coming.* But these are the same insight. **The reopened door (satudʿawna) is useless to a self that has dissolved into excuses (mukhallafīn).** The summons can only be answered by an "I" — and the whole work of the verse is to drag that hidden "I" back out of the passive fog and stand it upright in front of two open roads, in time to choose. Allah reopens the door *and* hands you back the self that can walk through it. The mercy is double, and it is one mercy.

### Questions to carry

- What is the one duty in my life that I describe to myself only in the passive voice — *"it didn't happen," "I got too busy"* — and what is the active verb I am hiding from?
- Who, or what, is the "people of great might" already waiting in my future — the hard thing I failed once and will be called to again?
- When that call comes, will my foot find the old groove of turning away, or have I cut a new one in advance?
- Have I quietly filed myself, permanently, under "left behind" — and is that label mine to assign, or Allah's?
- Do I believe, really believe, that the door Allah closed on my failure was the closing of a *cheap* door so that He could open a *costly, honorable* one?

### One-sentence distillation

**"Left behind" is the name your excuse gives you, but Allah's next word is "you will be called" — for with Him your failure is never the verdict, only the chapter before the summons, and the one thing He leaves entirely in your hands is whether you answer.**

### The closing invitation

So here is what I would invite you to let shift. Not a resolution to never fail again — that is the old self-flattery in a new coat. Something smaller and truer. Tonight, take the one failure you are still carrying as a *verdict*, and quietly re-file it as a *chapter*. Say of it, in the active voice, *"I turned away"* — and then say the next line of the verse over it, the line Allah refused to leave out: *"and you will be called."* Then start listening for the call. Because the most dangerous thing is not to have failed at Hudaybiyyah. The most dangerous thing is to stop listening for Yamāma.

### A brief duʿāʾ

O Allah, You who heard our excuse before we finished building it and named us by it so that we might finally hear it ourselves — pull the hidden "I" out of the fog of our turning-away and stand it upright before You, able to return.

You who close the cheap door only to open the costly one, do not let us file ourselves under "left behind" when You have already said *"you will be called."*

And when the people of great might appear in our path — the fear, the habit, the hard obedience we failed once — let our foot find the new groove, let us answer, and let us be among those to whom You give the good reward that Your generosity makes greater than we ever earned. *Āmīn.*
