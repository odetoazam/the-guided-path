# The Battle That Never Happened

## Surah Al-Fath, Ayah 22

> وَلَوْ قَٰتَلَكُمُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لَوَلَّوُا۟ ٱلْأَدْبَٰرَ ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُونَ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

*Wa law qātalakumu-lladhīna kafarū lawallawu-l-adbāra thumma lā yajidūna waliyyan wa lā naṣīrā.*

**"And if those who disbelieve had fought you, they would surely have turned their backs in flight; then they would not have found any protector or any helper."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me take you back to a dusty plain about nine miles outside of Mecca, in the sixth year after the Hijra.

A group of believers — around fourteen hundred of them — have walked for days. They are not carrying weapons of war. They are wearing the white cloth of pilgrimage. They have come, unarmed, to visit the House of Allah, the Ka'bah, the place toward which they turn five times a day and have never once been allowed to enter as free worshippers. Their hearts are full. Some of them have not seen Mecca, their home, in six years. They can almost smell it.

And they are stopped.

The Quraysh — the people of Mecca — block the road. Word comes back: *you will not enter this year.* Negotiators ride back and forth across the plain. And after long, tense, humiliating bargaining, a treaty is drawn up at a place called Hudaybiyyah.

Now, sit with what this treaty actually said. **Turn around and go home this year — no pilgrimage.** Come back next year, and you may stay only three days. And here is the clause that broke hearts: *if anyone flees from Mecca to join you in Medina, you must send him back. But if anyone leaves you to return to Mecca, they get to keep him.* It felt one-sided. It felt like surrender. One of the strongest, most devoted of the companions came to the Prophet ﷺ, his voice shaking, and asked: *Are we not upon the truth? Then why do we give in to humiliation in our religion?*

[PAUSE]

This is the emotional weather into which Surah Al-Fath descends. *Al-Fath.* **The Victory.** A surah named "The Clear Victory" — revealed about the very day that felt like the clearest defeat.

And in the middle of this surah, Allah does something that I want you to find strange. He begins listing favors. *I gave you spoils. I restrained hands that were reaching for you. I held people back.* He is counting blessings the believers can barely see. And then He counts one they cannot see at all — because **it never happened.**

He tells them about a battle that was never fought.

Here is the question I want you to carry into this hour: Why would Allah describe, in detail, the outcome of a war that did not take place? Why spend a verse of the Quran — eternal, preserved, recited until the end of time — on something that never occurred?

Stay with that. Because the answer is going to change how you read your own life.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Word That Opens a Door to What Never Was — *لَوْ*

Before we touch the verse, I want to teach you a small thing about your own language, because you already know this concept — you've just never named it.

Imagine a parent who says to a grown child: *"If you had been driving on that road last night, you would have been in that pileup."* Notice what that sentence does. It describes a crash the child was never in. The child is safe, at home, alive. And yet the parent is making them feel the cold of an accident that never touched them — *so that they understand the size of the thing they were spared.* The sentence isn't about the crash. **It's about the rescue.**

Arabic has a precise tool for exactly this. It is the particle **لَوْ — *law*.**

Teachers of Arabic grammar call *law* **حرف امتناع لامتناع** — *the particle of refraining-because-of-refraining.* That's a mouthful, so let me unpack it simply. *Law* introduces a condition that **did not happen**, leading to a result that therefore **also did not happen.** It is the grammar of the counterfactual. The road not taken. The thing that almost was, and wasn't.

This is different from *إِنْ — in* — which is the ordinary "if" of real possibility: *if it rains, take an umbrella.* That door is open. *Law* is the "if" of the closed door. *If this had happened — but it didn't.*

Now look at how our verse begins:

> **وَلَوْ قَٰتَلَكُمُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟**
> *"And **if** those who disbelieve **had fought** you..."*

The very first meaningful word out of the gate is *law.* And the moment an Arab listener heard it, their entire mind reframed. They knew instantly: **this fight did not occur.** Allah is not warning of a future battle. He is not narrating a past one. He is opening a door onto a battle that exists only in the realm of what-would-have-been.

Let's sit with this, because it's extraordinary. Of all the things Allah could speak about — and His speech is finite in words but infinite in meaning — He chooses to tell the believers about an event that never entered existence. He reaches into the space of the *might-have-been* and pulls out a scene, fully formed.

Why?

Here's what's remarkable. Go back to the parent and the car crash. The counterfactual is never really about the disaster. It's always about the **deliverance.** When you say "you would have been in that crash," the listener doesn't end up thinking about twisted metal. They end up thinking: *I was spared. Something kept me off that road.*

So when Allah says *"and if they had fought you..."* — He is not informing the believers about combat tactics. He is making them feel, in their bones, **the size of what they were saved from.** The treaty that felt like humiliation? It was the road they didn't drive. The "defeat" of Hudaybiyyah was Allah steering them off a highway they couldn't see was about to collapse.

[PAUSE]

The classical commentators differ slightly on *who* the would-be attackers were — and this difference is itself a gift, because it widens the scene. Qatāda, as al-Qurtubī reports, understood it as the Quraysh themselves at Hudaybiyyah. Al-Baghawī and al-Qurtubī also preserve another reading: the tribes of Asad and Ghatafān, and the allies of the people of Khaybar, who were poised to strike the believers from behind while they were away. Don't treat these as competing options to pick between. Treat them as **camera angles on the same mercy.** From every direction — the army in front at Mecca, the tribes lurking at the rear near Khaybar — hands were raised to strike. And from every direction, the blow never landed.

The word *law* holds all of it. One particle, and an entire averted war opens up behind it.

What this single word reveals: **Allah counts among His favors not only what He gave you, but what He never let reach you.** The catastrophe that never arrived is also a gift — and here He names it, so you would not fail to thank Him for a mercy you couldn't see.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The Reciprocal Form That Becomes a One-Sided Rout — *قَاتَلَ*

Now look at the verb itself: **قَٰتَلَكُمُ — *qātalakum* — "had fought you."**

I need to teach you something about Arabic verbs, because the *shape* of this word is carrying meaning that the translation throws away.

Arabic verbs come in patterns — what scholars call "forms," numbered I through X. The base form is simple action. But there is a particular pattern, **Form III**, the *mufāʿala* pattern, with a long stretched vowel after the first letter — *qā-tala* — and this form has a signature meaning: **mutual, reciprocal action.** Two parties doing something *to each other.*

Here's the everyday way to feel it. In Arabic, *kataba* means "he wrote." But *kātaba* — Form III — means "he **corresponded** with someone" — letters going both ways. *Sabaqa* means "he preceded." But *sābaqa* — Form III — means "he **raced** against someone" — two runners, side by side, straining against each other.

So the base verb *qatala* simply means "to kill." But our verb is not *qatala.* It is **قَاتَلَ — *qātala*** — Form III. And it means **to engage in mutual combat. To fight a two-sided battle.** Both armies meeting. Both swinging. Both committed. A real war between two real forces.

Notice what the form sets up. The grammar promises you a **symmetrical** encounter — they fight you, you fight them, blow for blow.

And now watch what happens in the very next word.

> **لَوَلَّوُا۟ ٱلْأَدْبَٰرَ**
> *"...they would surely have **turned their backs**."*

The reciprocal verb sets up a two-sided clash — and the result is instantly, totally **one-sided.** The form *qātala* gathers them into a mutual battle, and before a single blow is described, **they break.** They turn. They run.

Stay with the precision of this. Allah does not say *"you would have killed them."* He does not say *"you would have defeated them."* The believers are barely the subject of the action at all. The entire weight of the sentence falls on **what the enemy does: they turn their backs and flee.** The victory is described not as something the believers *achieve* but as something the disbelievers *suffer.*

Why does that matter? Because it quietly removes the believers' swords from the center of the story. The treaty meant they never had to lift those swords. And the verse honors that by telling them: *you would not have needed to do much. The fleeing would have been theirs, not the fighting yours.* The outcome was never really resting on the believers' strength. It was resting on something else entirely — which the next verse, 48:23, names directly: **سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ** — *the unchanging way of Allah* — that has passed before, in which you will find no alteration.

What this word reveals: **the form promised a fair fight, and Allah collapsed it into a rout — to show that the engagement was never symmetrical to begin with.** When Allah is your backing, the "two-sided battle" is a fiction. There is only one side that stands, and one side that turns.

## Element Three: The Root That Means Both "To Turn Away" and "To Draw Near" — *و-ل-ي*

This is the one I have been waiting to show you. Of everything in this verse, this is the discovery that I think will stay with you.

Let me first lay the two pieces side by side. Watch the verse closely:

> لَ**وَلَّوُ**ا۟ ٱلْأَدْبَٰرَ ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُونَ **وَلِيًّا** وَلَا نَصِيرًا
> *"...they would surely have **turned** their backs; then they would not find any **protector**..."*

Two words. **لَوَلَّوُا۟** — *lawallaw* — "they turned." And **وَلِيًّا** — *waliyyan* — "a protector." In English they look unrelated. One is a verb of fleeing; the other is a noun for a guardian.

But here is the thing the translation cannot show you. **These two words come from the very same root: و-ل-ي — *wāw-lām-yāʾ.***

The morphological corpus confirms it precisely: the verb *wallaw* (Form II) and the noun *waliyy* both trace to the identical three-letter root, w-l-y. Allah has placed two children of the same root in a single breath of revelation. In Arabic rhetoric this deliberate echo is called *jinās* — a resonance that the ear catches even when the mind hasn't named it. And once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

So what does this root *mean*, at its core?

The lexicographers — Ibn Manẓūr in *Lisān al-ʿArab*, al-Rāghib in his *Mufradāt* — anchor the root و-ل-ي in one fundamental idea: **nearness. Closeness. The state of two things being adjacent, with nothing between them.** *Al-walī*, says the lexicon, is fundamentally *al-qarīb* — **the near one.** From this single seed grows the whole family: the *walī* is the one who stands so near you that he can protect you. *Wilāya* is the bond of nearness — guardianship, alliance, loyalty, love. To be someone's *walī* is to close the distance between you, to stand at their side with nothing in the gap.

Now hold that — *nearness* — and look at the verb again.

**وَلَّى — *wallā*** — also from this root — means **to turn.** And specifically, in this verse, *wallā al-dubur* — **to turn the back.** Think about what turning your back physically *is.* It is taking your face — the part of you that connects, that faces, that draws near — and **rotating it away.** It is the unmaking of nearness. It is taking the gesture of closeness and reversing it.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what Allah has done here?

The same root that means **to be near as a protector** is the root used for **turning your nearness away in flight.**

Let me say it as plainly as I can. The disbelievers, in this imagined battle, would **وَلَّوْا** — they would *turn away*, reverse their nearness, present their backs. And then, in the desolate aftermath, they would search for a **وَلِيّ** — someone to *draw near* and protect them — **and find none.**

The very gesture of their flight is the etymological mirror of the protection they then cannot find. They turn away nearness — and are left with no one near. They unmake closeness with their own backs — and then discover the universe offers them no closeness in return. The fleeing and the friendlessness are not two separate facts. **In the root, they are the same motion.** To turn your back on the source of nearness is, by the deepest logic of the language, to be left with no one to be near you.

And now feel the asymmetry. Remember — earlier in this very surah, and across the whole Quran, the believers are told who *their* walī is. *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū* — **Allah is the walī of those who believe** (2:257). The believer is never without nearness. The One who is nearer than the jugular vein stands as his *walī.* But the one who turns his back? The verse withholds the word from him in the only place it could have rescued him. He looks for a *walī* — and the sentence gives him nothing.

What this reveals — and I'll mark this as the interpretive heart, what the shared root *invites* us to consider: **protection is not a resource you find lying around in the world. It is a relationship of nearness. And you cannot turn your back on the One source of nearness and still expect to find nearness waiting for you in the rubble.** The walī you fail to find is the nearness you yourself turned away from.

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us, woven into a single thread.

*Law* opened a door onto a battle that never happened — and made us feel the size of a rescue we couldn't see. *Qātala* promised a fair, two-sided fight — and collapsed it into a rout, to prove the contest was never symmetrical. And the root *w-l-y* took the act of fleeing and the absence of a protector and revealed them to be **the same motion** — the unmaking of nearness, twice.

Three linguistic discoveries. But notice — they are all circling one truth: **everything in this verse turns on a relationship you cannot see.** The averted war, the certain rout, the missing protector — none of them are about armies. They are about who you are *near* to, and what stands behind you that you didn't put there.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Wars God Fights for You That You Will Never See

Let me start where you actually live.

Tonight, before you sleep, you will thank Allah — if you remember to — for the things that *happened.* The meal you ate. The money that came in. The diagnosis that came back clear. We are, all of us, trained to be grateful for the *present* goods, the visible arrivals, the blessings we can point to.

But here is a question almost no one asks at the end of the day: **What did I get saved from today that I never even knew about?**

The near-miss on the highway you didn't notice because you were changing the radio station. The infection your immune system crushed before you felt a single symptom. The argument that would have ended a friendship, defused by a text that, for no reason you can name, you decided not to send. The version of your life that was hurtling toward you — and got quietly steered away.

You cannot thank Allah for these. **You don't know they happened.** And that is exactly the problem this verse is solving.

[PAUSE]

Because watch what Allah is doing at Hudaybiyyah. The believers are grieving a defeat. They wanted Mecca; they got a treaty. They wanted to enter the House; they got turned around in the dust. From inside their experience, Allah's favor is *invisible* — it looks like loss.

So Allah opens the *law* — the counterfactual door — and shows them the battle that didn't happen. *"And if they had fought you, they would have turned their backs..."* He is, in effect, saying: **let Me show you the road you didn't drive on.** Let Me make visible, just this once, one of the wars I fought on your behalf that you were never meant to see.

Think about how staggering that is. Allah is not only the One who gives you what arrives. He is the One who *intercepts what was coming.* And most of that interception happens in total silence. The tribes of Asad and Ghatafān, sharpening their swords to strike the believers' undefended rear near Khaybar — the believers in Medina were sleeping. Their children were playing. They never felt the blade that was being raised, because Allah lowered the arm before they ever knew it existed.

Now bring it home to your own life. The same surah says, two verses later, *He restrained their hands from you and your hands from them* (48:24). **Restrained hands.** How many hands has Allah restrained from reaching you that you will never, in this life, be able to count? How many doors has He locked that you spent the night frustrated, banging on, calling it a closed door — never knowing it was a wall holding back a flood?

Here is the movement I want your heart to make. We say *al-ḥamdu lillāh* for what we receive. But there is a deeper, harder, more trusting gratitude: **al-ḥamdu lillāh for what I will never know He spared me from.** Praise to Him for the catastrophes that never had names. Praise to Him for the wars He won before I woke up.

And this reframes every disappointment you are carrying right now. That "defeat" — the job you didn't get, the marriage that didn't happen, the city you couldn't move to, the entry to Mecca you were denied — what if it was a treaty of Hudaybiyyah? What if it *looked* like loss precisely because it was a rescue, and rescues from invisible disasters always look like losses from the inside?

**Practical application:** Tonight, add one line to your gratitude that you have never said before. Not "thank You for what You gave me" — but *"thank You for what You turned away from me that I will never see."* And the next time a door slams in your face, before you grieve it, pause and ask: *Is this a closed door, or a wall holding back a flood I cannot see?* You may not get the answer in this life. But asking the question is itself an act of trust — and trust is the entire point of a verse about a battle that never happened.

## Theme Two: To Turn Your Back on Nearness Is to Be Left With No One Near

Let me begin this one somewhere very human, very modern, and maybe a little uncomfortable.

There is a pattern psychologists describe in human attachment. When a person has been hurt — or fears they will be — sometimes, instead of moving *toward* the people who could hold them, they preemptively turn *away.* They withdraw first. They go cold before they can be left cold. They build the distance themselves, so that when the loneliness comes, at least it was *their* choice. And then — here is the tragedy — they sit inside that self-made distance and ache, genuinely ache, that *no one is close to them.* They turned their back on nearness, and then grieve that there is no one near.

Hold that human picture. Because the verse just gave us its exact spiritual architecture, hidden inside a single root.

We found it: **وَلَّوْا** — they turned their backs — and **وَلِيّ** — a protector — are the same root, *w-l-y*, whose core meaning is **nearness.** The one who *turns away nearness* (wallā) goes looking for *one who is near* (walī) — and finds none.

This is not Allah being withholding. **This is the structure of reality itself.** Let me build it slowly.

[PAUSE]

Protection — real protection, the kind that holds when the battle comes — is not an object you acquire. It is not a wall you buy, an army you hire, a savings account you build. The verse is teaching us that *waliyy*, protection, is fundamentally **a bond of nearness.** It is relational. It exists in the *closeness* between two parties, in the gap with nothing in it.

Which means protection has a logic, and the logic is this: **you can only be protected by what you are near to.** Nearness is the channel through which protection flows. Cut the nearness, and you have cut the channel — no matter how badly you later want the protection back.

Now watch the disbelievers in the verse. Their flight is not just cowardice. At the deepest level, *turning the back* is the physical enactment of a spiritual choice they made long before the battle: they turned away from the one Source whose nearness is the only nearness that ultimately holds. *Alladhīna kafarū* — those who *covered over*, who turned their faces from Him. And in the moment of terror, they spin around looking for a *walī*, someone near enough to protect — **and the sentence gives them silence.** Not because Allah is cruel. But because **you cannot draw on a nearness you spent your life turning away from.** The walī they cannot find is the nearness they themselves refused.

And now feel the believers' side of this — because the contrast is the whole gift. The believer is the one who *faces.* Five times a day he turns his face — *wajh* — toward the House, toward his Lord. The entire posture of faith is the *opposite* of turning the back. It is the refusal to *wallā.* It is staying near. And so when *his* battle comes, he reaches for a walī and finds — *Allāhu waliyyu-lladhīna āmanū* — **Allah Himself, near.** The nearness he maintained in peacetime is the nearness that holds him in war.

[PAUSE]

Let the paradox sit, because it is beautiful and it does not need resolving: **the protector you find in the storm is the nearness you kept in the calm.** You do not acquire a walī in the moment of terror. You discover, in the moment of terror, whether you stayed near.

This is the most practical theology imaginable. It means your relationship with Allah is not an emergency service you can summon for the first time when the battle comes. It is a *nearness* — and nearness is built in the quiet, undramatic, daily refusal to turn your back. The night prayer no one sees. The Qur'an opened on an ordinary Tuesday. The remembrance whispered while you wash the dishes. These are not "good deeds" piling up in an account. **They are you, staying near.** They are you, keeping your face turned, so that the day the *law* — the counterfactual catastrophe — comes hurtling toward you, you turn around and find Someone already there.

And the warning, gentle but real: every time we turn our backs — through the deferred prayer, the averted gaze from what we know is right, the slow drift of a heart going cold — we are not just "sinning." In the logic of *w-l-y*, **we are unmaking nearness.** We are widening the gap. And no one wants to be the person who, in the hour of the real battle, spins around looking for a Protector and meets only the distance he himself built.

**Practical application:** Pick one small act of *staying near* this week — one that no one will see and no one will praise. Two rak'ahs in the depth of the night. Ten minutes with the Qur'an before you touch your phone in the morning. And as you do it, name it to yourself for what it actually is: *I am not earning anything. I am keeping my face turned. I am staying near, so that I am never the one who turns around and finds no one there.*

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at how this single verse is built, because the structure is the proof of everything we've found.

The verse moves in two halves, and the hinge between them is the root *w-l-y.*

**First half:** *law qātalakum* — *"if they had fought you"* — Form III, the two-sided battle — *lawallawū al-adbār* — *"they would turn the backs."* This is the **action**: the turning-away.

**Second half:** *thumma lā yajidūna waliyyan wa lā naṣīrā* — *"then they find no protector, no helper."* This is the **consequence**: the nearness-not-found.

And notice the word **ثُمَّ — *thumma* — "then."** It is not the *thumma* of "and immediately." It carries a sense of *interval*, of aftermath, of *and then, in the desolation that follows.* First the back turns. *And then* — in the silence afterward — the search begins, and finds nothing. The structure stages it: the turning comes first, the friendlessness comes after. **Cause, then effect. Turn away nearness — then, find no nearness.** The architecture of the verse *is* the theology of the verse: the consequence is built into the order of the words.

And the final two words — *waliyyan wa lā naṣīrā*, "protector nor helper," both **indefinite**, both inside a negation — in Arabic that construction means **absolute, total absence.** Not "few protectors." Not "no good protector." *Not a single one, of any kind, anywhere.* The sentence trails off into the tanwīn, the soft *-an* and *-ā* endings, like a search dissolving into empty air. Even the *sound* of the verse's ending is the sound of finding nothing.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight, approached from two directions.

From the **words**, we learned: *law* shows the rescue, *qātala* shows the contest was never fair, and *w-l-y* shows that flight and friendlessness are one motion.

From the **themes**, we learned: Allah fights wars for us we never see, and protection is a nearness you either keep or unmake.

And they meet here: **The verse describes an enemy who turned his back on the only Nearness that holds — and so faced an invisible defeat with no one near to save him. And it was revealed to a people who had just kept their faces turned, in the dust of Hudaybiyyah, when everything in them wanted to grieve.** It is a single mirror held up in two directions: *this is what turning away looks like — so see what your staying-near has earned you.*

## Questions to Carry

1. What "defeats" am I grieving right now that might be treaties of Hudaybiyyah — losses that are actually rescues from a road I couldn't see?

2. When did I last thank Allah not for what He *gave* me, but for what He *turned away* from me that I will never know about?

3. In the ordinary, unwatched hours of my week — am I keeping my face turned, or am I slowly turning my back?

4. If the real battle came tomorrow and I spun around looking for a Protector — would I be discovering our nearness for the first time, or returning to a nearness I'd been keeping all along?

5. Whose "hands" might Allah be restraining from me, right now, that I am too busy to notice — and how would my heart change if I truly believed He was?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The One who turns the catastrophe away before it reaches you is the same One whose nearness you cannot find on the day you turn your back — so keep your face turned, and trust the losses that are really rescues.**

## The Closing Invitation

You came into this hour, perhaps, carrying a Hudaybiyyah of your own — some door that closed, some "defeat" that still aches. Let this verse offer you a different lens. Not the demand that you feel grateful — but the *invitation* to wonder: what if the thing that looks like loss is a battle Allah is fighting for you, in a silence so deep you mistook it for absence? And let it offer you a second invitation, quieter still: to keep your face turned in the calm, so that you are never the one searching the rubble for a nearness you let go of. Nothing here is owed. It is simply offered — the way the whole of Al-Fath was offered to grieving people who could not yet see their victory.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, *Waliyy* of those who believe — You who turn away the wars we never see and restrain the hands we never feel — let us never be among those who turn their backs and then find no one near. Keep our faces turned toward You in the quiet, so that in every battle, seen and unseen, we find that You were already there. And for every catastrophe You spared us that we will never know to name — *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* **Āmīn.**
