# To Him Belongs Whatever Is

## Surah Ash-Shura, Ayah 4

> لَهُۥ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

*Lahu mā fi-s-samāwāti wa mā fi-l-arḍ, wa huwa-l-ʿAliyyu-l-ʿAẓīm.*

**"To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth, and He is the Most High, the Most Great."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me set the scene before we touch a single word.

This surah does not begin with a story. It begins with sound — two clusters of disconnected letters, **Ḥā-Mīm**, then **ʿAyn-Sīn-Qāf** — letters whose full meaning Allah kept with Himself. And then, almost the moment the letters finish, the surah does something startling. It talks about *itself*. About the very act happening to the listener in that moment.

"Thus has He revealed to you, and to those before you, **Allah, the Mighty, the Wise.**" (42:3)

Stop and feel where you are. You are standing inside a revelation that is *announcing itself as it arrives*. The Prophet ﷺ is receiving speech from beyond the world, and the speech turns around and says: *this — what is happening to you right now — this is wahy. This is Me, reaching you.*

And then comes our ayah.

Now — pause and ask yourself what you would expect next. A man is receiving the speech of God. The most intimate thing imaginable is taking place: the Creator is *talking to a creature*. If you were writing this, what would you say in the very next breath?

Most of us would lean into the intimacy. We'd say something warm, something close.

Instead, the next breath does the opposite. It pulls the camera back — back past the rooftops, past the clouds, past the stars, past everything you have ever seen or could ever imagine — and it says:

**"To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth, and He is the Most High, the Most Great."**

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want us to carry through this entire reflection. *Why here?* Why, at the exact moment Allah is being most near — speaking, revealing, reaching down to a human heart — does He describe Himself as the One who owns all things and stands utterly above them?

We are taught to think nearness and greatness pull in opposite directions. That the closer something is, the smaller it must be; the greater it is, the further away. This ayah is going to take that assumption and quietly dismantle it.

Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word That Comes First — لَهُ

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we look at the ayah, because without it the first two words will slide right past you.

In ordinary speech — in any language — word order carries meaning beyond the dictionary. "I paid you" and "you, I paid" are not the same sentence. The second one is answering a question. It's saying: *you* specifically, not someone else. English does this clumsily, with stress and rearrangement. Arabic does it with surgical precision.

There is a principle in classical Arabic rhetoric: **taqdīm mā ḥaqquhu-t-taʾkhīr yufīdu-l-ḥaṣr** — when you move forward a word that grammatically "belongs" later, you create *restriction*. Exclusivity. You are not just saying a thing is true; you are saying it is true *of this one alone, and of no other.*

Here is an everyday version. If I say "this book is mine," I've stated ownership. But if I say "**mine** is this book" — putting *mine* first — I'm now excluding everyone else. *Mine, not yours. Mine, not the library's.* The fronting does the work.

Now look at how the ayah opens.

> **لَهُۥ** مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ

The natural order would be: *whatever is in the heavens and earth belongs to Him.* Subject first, then "to Him." But that is not what Allah said. He fronted it. He put **لَهُ — "to Him" — first.**

And by the rule we just learned, that single act of rearrangement transforms the meaning. This is no longer "everything belongs to Him." It is now:

**"To Him *alone* belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth."**

[PAUSE]

Let's sit with what that excludes. Every claim of ownership you have ever made. Every deed, every title, every *this is mine*. The fronting of that one tiny word — two letters in Arabic, *la-hu* — silently voids every competing claim in existence. There is no second owner. There is no co-signer on the deed of the cosmos.

This is why al-Jalālayn, glossing this verse, unpacks "to Him belongs" in three layers: **mulkan wa khalqan wa ʿabīdan** — *His by ownership, by creation, and by servitude.* He owns it because He made it. He owns those in it because they are His servants. The ownership is total — not the ownership of a buyer, who acquires what already existed, but the ownership of the One from whom existence itself came.

Notice something. We use the word "own" all the time, but we have never once owned anything the way this ayah means it. Everything we "own" we received, will lose, and did not bring into being. Our ownership is custody wearing a costume. **His** is the only ownership that was never borrowed.

That's the first word. One word, fronted, and the whole map of who-owns-what is redrawn.

### Element Two: The Word He Left Open — مَا

Now look at *what* He says belongs to Him.

> لَهُۥ **مَا** فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَ**مَا** فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ

The word is **مَا** — "whatever," "that which." And I want you to notice what Allah did *not* do here, because the absence is the entire point.

He did not list. He could have. He could have said: to Him belong the angels, the stars, the throne, the seas, the mountains, the creatures of the deep. The Quran elsewhere does name things. But here, at the foundation, He uses the most open word in the language — **mā** — a relative pronoun that reaches out and gathers *everything without naming anything.*

Think about why this matters. The moment you make a list, you create an edge. A list, however long, ends. And the human mind, the moment it sees an edge, starts to wonder what lies beyond it. List the stars, and I think about the dark between them. List the oceans, and I think about what's under the floor of them.

**مَا** has no edge.

It sweeps in everything in the heavens — named and unnamed, seen and unseen, the things we have instruments to detect and the things we will never detect. It sweeps in everything in the earth — down to what is buried, what is microscopic, what no human eye has ever fallen upon. It includes things that exist that you have no concept of. It includes the contents of a galaxy no telescope will ever resolve.

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. By *refusing to specify*, Allah said more than any list could. A specification is a boundary. The unspecified **mā** is the absence of boundary. It is precisely *because* He does not tell you what is in the heavens and earth that you understand it is beyond your telling.

And notice the pairing: **the heavens** and **the earth**. This is a way of saying "everything" by naming the two ends of the spectrum — the highest reach above you and the ground beneath your feet, and by implication, all that lies between. It is the totality of the spatial cosmos, the whole stage on which every created thing has ever stood.

So now hold the first two elements together. The fronted **لَهُ** said: *to Him alone.* The unspecified **مَا** said: *absolutely everything, with no edge.* Put them in one hand:

**To Him alone — and to no other — belongs absolutely everything, named and unnamed, in the entirety of existence.**

That is nine-tenths of the ayah, and we have not yet reached His names.

### Element Three: The Two Names That Close It — ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

> وَهُوَ **ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ**

*And He is al-ʿAliyy, al-ʿAẓīm.* The Most High. The Most Great.

Both names carry the definite article — **al-** — and in Arabic, when a name of magnitude takes the definite article, it does not merely describe; it *confines the quality to its bearer.* Not *a* high one among high things. **The** High — the one in whom highness fully and truly resides, beside whom every other "high" thing is borrowing the word.

Let's open each one, because they are doing different work, and the difference is the heart of this ayah.

**ٱلْعَلِىُّ** comes from the root *ʿayn-lām-wāw* (Elw) — the root of *elevation, height, rising above.* It is the same root from which we get "ʿuluww," loftiness. Now — here is the physical-to-spiritual stepping stone the Quran loves. Height is something you can *see*. A mountain is high. A bird climbs high. The stars are high above us. But Allah is not "high" the way a mountain is high. The physical image of height is a stepping stone pointing at a reality that has no location at all: **transcendence of rank, of power, of being.** Al-Muyassar captures this beautifully — He is al-ʿAliyy *bi-dhātihi wa qadrihi wa qahrihi* — high in His **essence**, high in His **rank**, and high in His **dominance**. Above all things in what He *is*, above all things in His *worth*, above all things in His *power to subdue.*

So when you hear *al-ʿAliyy*, do not picture a place far up. Picture a reality so far beyond every category of created things that the word "above" is the closest your language can crawl.

**ٱلْعَظِيمُ** comes from the root *ʿayn-ẓāʾ-mīm* (EZm) — *greatness, magnitude, immensity.* If al-ʿAliyy is about elevation — being *above* — al-ʿAẓīm is about magnitude — being *immense beyond measure.* Al-Muyassar: He is al-ʿAẓīm *alladhī lahu-l-ʿaẓamatu wa-l-kibriyāʾ* — the One to whom belong all majesty and grandeur.

And listen — say the word aloud. *ʿA-ẓīm.* It is a heavy word. The *ẓāʾ* is a thick, deep, resonant letter; the long *ī* before the closing *m* makes the word *swell* in the mouth before it lands. The sound itself is large. The word for greatness is built to feel great when you speak it. The phonetics carry the meaning.

[PAUSE]

Now put the two names side by side, because together they map something complete.

*Al-ʿAliyy* answers: **how far is He above?** Beyond reach. Beyond category.
*Al-ʿAẓīm* answers: **how vast is He?** Beyond measure. Beyond containing.

One is the dimension of *transcendence*. The other is the dimension of *magnitude*. A thing could be high but small, or vast but low. Allah is named here as both at once — utterly above, and utterly immense. There is no direction in which the creature can go to find an edge of Him. Reach up: al-ʿAliyy, He is above it. Reach out: al-ʿAẓīm, He exceeds it.

---

### The Bridge

So the grammar has shown us its hand. The fronted **لَهُ**: His alone. The boundless **مَا**: everything without edge. The twin names **ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ**: above all, vaster than all.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about the strange ache in the human chest that this ayah was sent to touch.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Exhaustion of Owning, and the Relief of "To Him Belongs"

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with sleep. It's the tiredness of *holding on.* You feel it at 3 a.m. when you run the ledger of your life — the job that could slip, the relationship that could fracture, the health that could turn, the money that could vanish, the reputation that one bad day could undo. You lie there gripping a hundred things at once, terrified to set any of them down, because you have quietly come to believe that *they are yours and your grip is what keeps them.*

This is the human condition before the ayah touches it. We walk through the world as small, anxious owners. We sign our names to things. We say *my* career, *my* family, *my* body, *my* future — and the word "my" feels like security, but it is actually the source of the dread. Because the thing you believe you own is the thing you are now terrified to lose.

Psychologists have a name for one slice of this: the *endowment effect.* The moment we feel we possess something, we value it more, and we fear its loss more sharply than we ever desired its gain. Ownership doesn't bring peace. It manufactures anxiety. The more you have, the more exposed you become.

Now bring the ayah into that 3 a.m. room.

**لَهُ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ.**

*To Him alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth.*

Feel what that fronted **لَهُ** does to your grip. It does not say your things will be taken. It says something far more freeing: **they were never yours to begin with.** The career, the family, the body, the future — they are in the heavens and the earth, and whatever is in the heavens and the earth belongs to *Him alone.* You were never the owner. You were the *steward.* You were holding, for a while, what was always His.

[PAUSE]

Now — does that feel like loss or like relief? Sit with it honestly, because the answer is the whole point.

The world tells you that letting go of ownership is loss. The ayah reveals it as the laying down of a weight you were never built to carry. The steward sleeps. It is the one who believes he is the *owner* who lies awake — because the owner thinks the outcome rests on his grip. The steward knows the Owner never sleeps, *wa lā taʾkhudhuhu sinatun wa lā nawm.*

Here is the deeper turn. We think surrendering ownership to Allah makes us poorer. But look closely at the logic of the ayah. If everything belongs to Him *alone*, and He is your Lord who loves you, then you have not lost your possessions — **you have traded a flimsy, terrified, temporary ownership for a relationship with the actual Owner of all of it.** The child who owns one toy and clutches it in fear is poorer than the child of the man who owns the whole factory. The second child owns nothing — and lacks nothing.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the grip. When the fear rises — over the job, the diagnosis, the wayward child, the bill — the practice is to whisper the ayah back to yourself and *consciously open the hand.* "This was never mine. *Lahu mā fi-s-samāwāti wa mā fi-l-arḍ.* It is Yours. I am only holding it for You." You will find that you can still work for it, still strive, still protect — but without the strangling certainty that its survival depends on your trembling fingers. You return the deed to the One whose name is on it. And you breathe.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The Great One Who Stooped to Speak

Now to the question we have carried since the introduction. *Why these two names — al-ʿAliyy, al-ʿAẓīm — here, at the moment of revelation?*

Let me reach you through something ordinary first.

Imagine the most important, most powerful, most unreachable person you can think of. Now imagine that person — not their assistant, not their office, but *them* — picks up the phone and calls *you* by name. To talk. To explain something to you because they want *you* to understand.

What makes that call overwhelming is not the call itself. It is the *gap.* It is the distance between how high they are and how small you are. If your neighbor calls, it's nothing. If the unreachable one calls, the smallness of you and the greatness of them is exactly what makes the moment shatter you.

Now read the passage again in order.

42:3 — *Thus has He revealed to you...* — the call is placed. God is speaking to a human being.

42:4 — *...and He is the Most High, the Most Great.*

Do you see what Allah has done? He has told you who is on the line. He did not soften the gap to make the revelation feel comfortable. He *widened* it. He said: the One reaching down into your world with speech is not a near, manageable deity at your level. He is **al-ʿAliyy** — transcendent beyond every category — and **al-ʿAẓīm** — vaster than the heavens and earth that are merely His property.

[PAUSE]

And here is the wonder the whole ayah was built to deliver.

We assumed at the start that nearness and greatness pull apart — that the greater He is, the further He must be. The ayah dissolves that. **It is precisely because He is so great that His speech to you is so staggering.** A small god speaking to you would be unremarkable. *This* God speaking to you — the Owner of everything that exists, the One above all rank and beyond all measure — *this* God forming words a human heart can receive: that is the most extraordinary act of nearness conceivable.

His transcendence does not make the revelation distant. **It makes it precious beyond reckoning.**

Stay with this, because it reorders your whole relationship to the Book. When you open the Quran — when you read even one ayah — you are not reading a religious text. You are receiving a phone call from al-ʿAliyy al-ʿAẓīm. The Owner of the heavens and the earth, who needs nothing from you, who is above and beyond you in every dimension, has bent the full weight of His greatness into *words you can hold in your mouth and your chest.* The gap that should have made Him silent is exactly the gap that makes His speech a mercy you could never have earned.

Let a paradox sit here without rushing it: the most transcendent Being is the most intimately communicative. He is furthest above — and He speaks closest. We were wrong to think we had to choose.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes how you sit with the Quran. Before you read, pause for the length of one breath and remember *who is speaking.* Not to perform reverence — but because remembering the greatness of the Speaker is what turns reading into *listening.* The same words, read as a task, inform you. Read as the speech of al-ʿAliyy al-ʿAẓīm reaching down to *you specifically*, they transform you. The difference is not in the ink. It is in your awareness of the One on the other end of the line.

[PAUSE]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now step back and look at the shape of the whole ayah, because its structure is its argument.

It moves in two motions. The first half is about **what He has**: *To Him alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth.* The second half is about **what He is**: *and He is the Most High, the Most Great.*

And notice the order. He establishes *ownership* of all things first — then *transcendence above* all things. The sequence matters. If He only owned everything but were *of* the same order as His creation, He would be the greatest thing *within* the system. But the names come to lift Him out of the system entirely: He owns the heavens and the earth, **and He is not contained by them** — al-ʿAliyy, above them; al-ʿAẓīm, vaster than them. The possession does not shrink Him to the size of His possessions. He holds all of it, and exceeds all of it.

And here is the architectural seal. This exact closing — **وَهُوَ ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ** — is the very phrase that ends the greatest single ayah about Allah's dominion in the entire Quran: **Āyat al-Kursī** (2:255). There, after His knowledge encompasses all, after His Throne extends over the heavens and earth, after His sustaining of all things tires Him not — it closes: *and He is the Most High, the Most Great.* The same words that crown the verse of the Throne are placed here to crown the act of revelation. As if to say: the One whose Kursī contains the heavens and earth is the same One now placing His speech into the heart of a man. The dominion and the speech come from one source, sealed with one phrase.

### Bringing It Together

The grammar and the themes were the same insight approached from two directions.

The fronted **لَهُ** and the boundless **مَا** built the truth of *total ownership* — which became the relief of laying down what was never ours.

The names **ٱلْعَلِىُّ ٱلْعَظِيمُ** built the truth of *total transcendence* — which became the wonder that this transcendent One stooped to speak.

And the two meet in one place: **the only One great enough to own everything is the only One worth surrendering everything to — and the proof that He is worth it is that He, the High and the Great, chose to speak to you.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What am I gripping right now as *mine* that this ayah is gently asking me to recognize as *His* — held by me only in trust?
2. Where in my life does the fear of *losing* something reveal that I have quietly believed I *own* it?
3. When I open the Quran, am I reading a text — or receiving a call from al-ʿAliyy al-ʿAẓīm?
4. If His greatness is exactly what makes His speech precious, how would my recitation change if I truly remembered *who is speaking*?
5. What would it feel like to be the steward who sleeps, instead of the owner who lies awake?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The One to whom everything belongs and who is above and beyond everything is the very One who bent His greatness into words and spoke to you — so loosen your grip on what was never yours, and listen.

### The Closing Invitation

Something can shift after this — not as an obligation, but as an opening. The next time the 3 a.m. fear arrives, you have an answer older than your anxiety: *Lahu mā fi-s-samāwāti wa mā fi-l-arḍ.* And the next time you open the Book, you have a remembrance that turns reading into listening: *wa huwa-l-ʿAliyy-l-ʿAẓīm.* One ayah, holding both the relief for your grip and the reverence for your reading. Let it do both.

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, to You alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth — loosen our grip on what was never ours, and let us rest as stewards in the keeping of the only Owner. You are al-ʿAliyy, above all that we fear; You are al-ʿAẓīm, vaster than all that we carry. You did not stay silent in Your greatness — You spoke. So make us those who *listen.* Āmīn.
