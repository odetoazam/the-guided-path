# The Runners and the Wind: On Borrowed Motion

## Introduction

Let me read these three verses to you slowly, and then let's build the world we need to stand inside before they can land.

> [ayah:42:32] وَمِنْ ءَايَـٰتِهِ ٱلْجَوَارِ فِى ٱلْبَحْرِ كَٱلْأَعْلَـٰمِ
> *Wa min āyātihi-l-jawāri fi-l-baḥri ka-l-aʿlām*
> "And among His signs are the ships that run upon the sea like mountains."

> **إِن يَشَأْ يُسْكِنِ ٱلرِّيحَ فَيَظْلَلْنَ رَوَاكِدَ عَلَىٰ ظَهْرِهِۦ**
> *In yashaʾ yuskini-r-rīḥa fa-yaẓlalna rawākida ʿalā ẓahrih*
> "If He wills, He stills the wind, and they lie motionless on its surface."

> **إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَءَايَـٰتٍ لِّكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ ۝ أَوْ يُوبِقْهُنَّ بِمَا كَسَبُوا۟ وَيَعْفُ عَن كَثِيرٍ**
> *Inna fī dhālika la-āyātin li-kulli ṣabbārin shakūr. Aw yūbiqhunna bimā kasabū wa yaʿfu ʿan kathīr.*
> "Indeed, in that are signs for everyone deeply patient and deeply grateful. Or He could wreck them for what they have earned — yet He pardons much."

### Rebuild the world

You have to understand what a ship meant to the first people who heard this.

A ship was the most ambitious thing human hands had ever made. To build one, you needed forests felled and shaped, iron worked, ropes woven, sails cut, and the accumulated knowledge of generations of sailors who had learned the moods of the sea by burying the ones who got it wrong. A ship was civilization's masterpiece. It was the proof that the human being could take the most hostile, trackless, bottomless place on earth — the open ocean, where you cannot stand, cannot rest, cannot drink, cannot see land — and *cross it*. Move cargo across it. Move armies across it. Move whole families to new lives across it.

When a sailor stood on the deck of a great ship under full sail, leaning into a strong wind, the bow cutting white water, the whole massive structure surging forward — he felt something very specific. He felt **mastery**. He felt that the human being had conquered the sea.

And Allah, in these verses, walks straight up to that feeling. He does not deny the ship its grandeur — He calls it *like a mountain*. Classical commentators are precise about what this simile measures: al-Jalālayn glosses *al-aʿlām* as "like mountains in their *enormity*" — *al-jibāl fī-l-ʿiẓam*. It is not merely a visual image. It is a measure of magnitude — the ship assessed at its maximum, in the full weight of its greatness. And this matters: it is at the ship's maximum that the demonstration lands hardest. He grants the ship everything the sailor feels. And then, in seven Arabic words, He removes the one thing the sailor never thought to question.

He shows that even this mastery runs entirely on a wind He can still in an instant.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I want you to sit with it for the next hour.

The ship in this verse is given a name. Allah does not call it *al-safīnah*, the ordinary word for a ship. He calls it **al-jawār** — *the runners*. The thing that runs. He names it by its motion.

And then the very next verse takes its motion away.

He names it for the one thing He is about to prove it does not own.

Why would Allah call something "the runner" in the exact breath before He shows you it cannot run on its own? That is not an accident. That is the entire lesson, folded into a single word. Let's open it.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *al-Jawār* — The Name That Is a Loan

Before we touch the word, let me teach you a small piece of Arabic, because everything here depends on it.

In Arabic, there is a form called the **active participle** — *ism al-fāʿil*. It is the form that turns a verb into a *doer*. From the verb "to write," *kataba*, you get *kātib* — "the one writing," "a writer." From "to know," you get *ʿālim* — "the one who knows." The active participle does something subtle: it doesn't just say an action happened. It attaches the action to a thing as its ongoing description. A *kātib* isn't someone who wrote once. It's someone characterized by writing. The act becomes the identity.

Now hold that.

The word Allah uses is **ٱلْجَوَارِ**, *al-jawār*. Its root is *j-r-y*, which means **to run, to flow, to course** — the way water runs, the way a current flows. *Al-jawār* is the active participle, plural: **"the running ones," "the coursers," "the runners."**

The classical commentators are unanimous on the lexical point. Al-Qurṭubī explains it plainly: a single ship is a *jāriyah* — "named *jāriyah* because it runs in the water." Al-Baghawī: "*al-jawārī* means the ships, singular *jāriyah*, and it is *al-sāʾirah* — the one that travels, that moves." Al-Ṭabarī goes a step further, and his word choice is significant: he does not open this passage by calling the ships *āyāt* — signs. He calls them *ḥujaj* — divine proofs, arguments, evidence that is logically binding rather than merely evocative. The ships, he says, are "proofs against you, O humanity" that Allah "is capable of all He wills, and nothing He intends presents any difficulty to Him" — *lā yataʿadhdhar ʿalayhi fiʿlu shayʾ arādahu*. The sailor standing on the deck is not being invited to feel wonder. He is standing inside a proof he has not yet read.

Let's sit with this.

Allah is the One who chose this name. He is not reaching for a neutral word. The Arabic language had a perfectly good plain noun for a ship — *safīnah*, *fulk*. He bypasses them. He reaches for the participle of *motion* and makes motion the ship's very identity. In this verse, the ship is not a thing that happens to be moving. The ship *is* "the moving one." Movement is its name.

Notice something.

A name like that is a claim. To call something "the runner" is to say: running is what this is. Running is its nature, its character, the thing that defines it. When you hear "the runner," you assume the running comes from *within* it — the way a horse runs because a horse is a running creature, the way a river runs because that is what a river *is*.

And here is where the verse turns the knife so gently you almost don't feel it.

[PAUSE]

Read the next words: **إِن يَشَأْ يُسْكِنِ ٱلرِّيحَ** — "If He wills, He stills the wind." And the runners stop.

Stay with me. Allah does not say "if He wills, He breaks the ships." He does not touch the ships at all. He does not crack a single plank. He reaches *past* the ship entirely and touches something the ship does not own and never owned: **the wind.**

He stills the wind — and "the runners" cannot run.

Do you see what just happened to the name? The ship was called "the runner" as though running were its nature. And then Allah shows you, with surgical precision, that its running was never its own. It was on **loan from the wind**. The thing the ship was *named for* — its motion, its identity, the deck-feeling of mastery — was borrowed the entire time. The mountain of timber and iron and human genius sits dead in the water the instant Allah withholds something invisible, something the builders never built, something no shipwright ever put into the hull.

**The name "runner" was always a courtesy.** The running was the wind's. The ship just received it.

And that — that gap between the name and the source — is not really about ships at all. That is the first stepping stone, and we'll cross to where it leads in Part Two. For now, hold the discovery: *Allah named the thing for a power it did not possess, to teach you that the power was always His to lend and His to recall.*

### Element Two: *Yuskin* and *Rawākid* — Two Kinds of Stillness

Now I want to slow down on the stillness itself, because Allah uses two different words for "not moving," and the difference between them is the difference between peace and death.

First, a tiny grammar note so you can see the gears turning. The verb is **يُسْكِنِ**, *yuskin*. Its root is *s-k-n*, which is one of the great roots of the Quran. From *s-k-n* you get *sukūn* — stillness, calm, rest. You get *sakīnah* — the tranquility Allah sends down into hearts. You get *maskan* — a dwelling, a home, the place where you come to rest. And you get *sakana* — "to become still, to settle, to be at peace."

The form here, *yuskin*, is the causative: not "to be still" but "**to make** still." Allah doesn't merely let the wind die. He *stills* it — actively, deliberately, with intent. The calm is not an absence. It is an act.

So the wind receives *sukūn* — a word soaked in peace, rest, home, and tranquility.

But look at what the *ships* receive. **فَيَظْلَلْنَ رَوَاكِدَ** — "and they remain *rawākid*." This is a different word entirely. The root is *r-k-d*, and *rawākid* is the plural of *rākid* — **stagnant, dead-still, motionless** in the way that *standing water* is motionless. Arabic uses this very root for a pond that does not flow, water gone still and stale. Al-Muyassar glosses it precisely: *sawākin* — "motionless, frozen in place, not running."

Now hold the two words side by side and feel the difference in your body.

**The wind is given *sukūn* — the stillness of peace.**
**The ships are given *rukūd* — the stillness of a corpse.**

Same physical fact: nothing is moving. But Allah refuses to use one word for both, because they are not the same reality. When the wind goes still, that is *sukūn* — calm, rest, the world settling. But the *exact same stillness*, the moment it touches the ship, becomes *rukūd* — stagnation, helplessness, a great proud mountain of a vessel sitting dead and useless on a flat sea, going nowhere, with all its sails hanging slack.

Let's sit with this, because it's astonishing.

The same stillness is peace for the one and paralysis for the other. **What is rest for the wind is ruin for the runner.** Why? Because the wind is *at home* in stillness — *sukūn* is the wind's natural state to return to. But the ship has no business being still. A still ship is a contradiction, a runner that cannot run, a purpose suspended. For the ship, stillness is not rest. It is the cancellation of everything it is for.

And here is the quiet terror underneath the calm: the verse describes the gentlest possible catastrophe. There is no storm here. No waves, no lightning, no wreck — that comes in the next verse. *This* disaster is a **flat, calm, sunny, windless sea.** Nothing is wrong. Nothing is broken. The weather is beautiful. And the ship is utterly, completely finished. It will sit there until the food runs out and the water runs out and the people on it die under a clear blue sky — not because anything attacked them, but because Allah simply, quietly, *withheld a breeze.*

And it cost Him nothing. Al-Ṭabarī's framing resonates here with precision: nothing Allah intends "presents any difficulty to Him." The stilling of the wind is not a feat. It is not a divine exertion. It is the gentlest withdrawal — the cessation of a gift that was being given continuously, moment by moment, gust by gust, without the sailors ever noticing it was being given at all.

[PAUSE]

He does not need to send a disaster to undo you. He only needs to send *calm*. He only needs to stop giving. The most frightening thing in this verse is how little force it takes — not a blow, just the withdrawal of a gift you never noticed you were being handed, breath by breath, gust by gust.

What this single contrast reveals: **your motion through life is not stillness held back; it is a gift continuously given. The default is *rukūd*. Movement is the mercy.**

### Element Three: *Ṣabbār* and *Shakūr* — The Two Words That Hold a Whole Believer

Now look at who all of this is *for*. The verse does not say these are signs for "people," or for "the wise," or even for "the believers." It says: **لِّكُلِّ صَبَّارٍ شَكُورٍ** — "for *every deeply patient, deeply grateful* one." Two qualities, and only two. Let's understand why Allah chose exactly these, and why He intensified both.

Here is the grammar that matters. Arabic has a set of forms called *ṣiyagh al-mubālagha* — **forms of intensification**. They take a simple quality and crank it up. A person who is merely patient is *ṣābir*. But the verse does not say *ṣābir*. It says **صَبَّار**, *ṣabbār* — on the pattern *faʿʿāl*, the pattern of someone who does a thing *constantly, intensely, as a way of life.* Not "patient." **Deeply, habitually patient.** And it does not say *shākir*, "grateful." It says **شَكُور**, *shakūr* — on the intensive pattern *faʿūl* — not "grateful," but **overflowing with gratitude, grateful through and through.**

So Allah is not describing someone who is occasionally patient and remembers to say *alhamdulillah* after a good meal. He is describing a person whose patience and gratitude have become their *texture* — soaked all the way through.

But the real question is: why *these two*? Of all the qualities a person could have — faith, knowledge, fear, hope, love — why does Allah say the signs of the sea are specifically for the patient-and-grateful?

Because — and this is the genius of placing these words *here*, at the end of a sea-voyage image — **patience and gratitude are the two halves of the voyage.**

Think about what a sea journey actually is. It is the wind blowing — and then the wind stilling. It is the surge forward — and then the dead calm. It is the days of fair weather — and then the days of motionless dread. The voyage *is* the alternation. And the believer crossing the sea of this life faces exactly that alternation, every single day:

When the wind blows — when you are moving, succeeding, when the breath comes easy and the work goes well and the family is whole — what does that moment demand of you? **Gratitude.** *Shukr.* Because the wind is not yours and you must not mistake the surge for your own engine.

And when the wind stills — when you are becalmed, stuck, when the prayers seem unanswered and the illness lingers and nothing moves no matter how hard you pull the ropes — what does *that* moment demand? **Patience.** *Ṣabr.* Because the stillness is also from Him, and the wind will return in His time, not yours.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it now? *Ṣabbār shakūr* is not a random pair of virtues. **It is the complete map of how to be on the sea.** Patience for the calm. Gratitude for the wind. The believer who has both can never be destroyed by the voyage, because both poles of the crossing are covered. There is no position on the sea — sailing or becalmed — for which they are not equipped.

And the classical commentators were precise about the interior structure of that patience. Al-Muyassar identifies three domains in which the *ṣabbār* is exercised: patience in obedience to Allah, patience in refraining from what He has prohibited, and patience upon His painful decrees — *ṣabr ʿalā ṭāʿat Allāh, wa ʿan al-maʿāṣī, wa ʿalā aqdār Allāh al-muʾlimah*. All three live on the ship. To keep praying, to keep working, to keep giving — while nothing appears to be moving — is patience in obedience. To not reach for the forbidden shortcut, the wind that does not belong to you — is patience against transgression. And to accept the flat, windless sea as His decree, without demanding He explain Himself, without mutiny at the ropes — that is patience upon the painful decree. The becalmed ship in this verse is not merely an image of hardship. It is the classroom where all three forms of ṣabr are simultaneously tested.

The *ṣabbār shakūr*, then, is not an idealized figure of spiritual ease. She is someone who has sailed long enough to know what every kind of weather demands — and who has the interior formation to meet it.

## Part 2: The Complete Account

### The Third Option and the Mercy Behind All of It

We have spent a long time with the *ṣabbār shakūr* — and rightly so, because that phrase is the heart of the passage. But there is a third possibility in this sequence, and we have not yet opened it. And when we do, we will find something that quietly reframes everything we have learned.

Verse 34 gives us **two** alternatives to the ship running safely:

**أَوْ يُوبِقْهُنَّ بِمَا كَسَبُوا۟** — "Or He could wreck them for what they have earned."

**وَيَعْفُ عَن كَثِيرٍ** — "Yet He pardons much."

Let's take the first part. The word is **يُوبِقْهُنَّ**, *yūbiqhunna*. Its root is *w-b-q* — and this root does not carry a gentle meaning. It means **to perish, to be brought to utter ruin, to be destroyed.** The form here, *yūbiq*, is causative: not "they perish" but "He *causes* them to perish." He wrecks them. He ends them. And it is linked explicitly to *bimā kasabū* — "for what they earned." The destruction, if it comes, is not arbitrary. It is a harvest. The sailors' sins, their arrogance, their ingratitude, their forgetting of the Giver when the wind filled the sails — these are the *kasb*, the earnings, that would justify their end.

This puts the becalmed ship in a new light. Go back to Element Two. The worst scenario we discussed was the calm, sunny, windless sea — the *rukūd* without a storm, dying slowly under a clear blue sky. But the verse now tells us there was a *worse* option on the table all along: wreckage. Destruction. Loss without waiting. The helpless, becalmed ship was actually the *merciful* version of the disaster. The wrecked ship didn't even get that.

Stay with this geometry. Three possibilities:

1. **The wind blows** — they sail, they arrive, they live. The *āyāt* are visible to those who carry *ṣabr* and *shukr*.
2. **The wind stills** — they sit dead on the surface, helpless, waiting. The mercy here is that at least they are waiting.
3. **He wrecks them** — they go down, for what they earned.

Most ships throughout history have sailed. Some have been becalmed. Some have been wrecked. The verse does not pretend otherwise. But here is what the verse is pointing to: between outcome 1 (safe passage) and outcome 3 (deserved destruction), there is not an empty gap. That gap is filled by one thing, and the verse names it explicitly.

[PAUSE]

**وَيَعْفُ عَن كَثِيرٍ** — "yet He pardons much."

Notice what the verse is doing. The destruction — *yūbiqhunna*, "He could wreck them" — hangs on the *aw* ("or"), framed as a possibility, an alternative road that branches off from the main path. The wrecking is what the earnings of the sailors call for: *bimā kasabū*. And then, without a new conditional bracket: **وَيَعْفُ عَن كَثِيرٍ** — "and He pardons much." Not "or He pardons." Not "if He wills, He pardons." Simply: He pardons. *Much.* The destruction is the alternative the earnings would produce. The pardon is what is actually, continuously operating — it is what is happening on the water right now, in every crossing that ends at the far shore rather than the bottom of the sea.

[PAUSE]

Now bring this back to the ship.

Every ship that has ever left harbor and arrived at the other shore — did it arrive because its sailors were good enough? Because they earned safe passage? The verse says *no*. The verse says the sailors earned wreckage. Wreckage is what *kasb* produces. And yet the ship arrived. Why?

**Because He pardoned much.**

Every crossing that succeeded was not a testament to human seamanship. It was a testament to divine mercy that was greater than human sin. The ship did not earn its arrival. The pardon purchased it.

And now — now — the full meaning of "borrowed motion" opens up completely.

In Part 1, we discovered that the ship's motion was borrowed from the wind. The runners do not run on their own. Their running is a loan from a wind they did not make and cannot control. We called this "borrowed motion" at the level of *physics* — the mechanism of the crossing.

But there is a deeper borrowing underneath the physical one. The ship is not merely borrowing its *motion* from the wind. It is borrowing its *continued existence on the water* from the mercy that keeps the wrecking from coming. Every moment the ship floats and runs is a moment Allah has chosen to pardon rather than destroy. The ship is a *pardoned ship* — and it does not know it. The sailors feel mastery. They feel the surge. They feel the arrival. But what they are actually feeling is mercy given continuously, wrecking indefinitely postponed, *kasb* met not with justice but with *ʿafw*.

**The motion is borrowed from the wind. The voyage itself is borrowed from the pardon.**

This is the full account. This is what "On Borrowed Motion" means.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Measure of *Kathīr*

There is one word left in this passage that deserves its own moment. The verse does not say "He pardons." It says **وَيَعْفُ عَن كَثِيرٍ** — "He pardons *much*."

*Kathīr* — much, many, a great amount. And it is deliberately indefinite. Not "He pardons some things." Not "He pardons the small transgressions." Not "He pardons what falls below a certain threshold." Just: *much*. The quantity is vast and the boundary is unnamed.

Think about what this means for the sailing metaphor. The sailors earned destruction — but the pardon is described as *much*. Not small. Not proportional to the scale of the sin. *Much*. The sins of the crossing are presumably not minor — arrogance, ingratitude, forgetting the Giver when the wind blew fair, failing patience when it stilled. And the verse says: He pardons *much* of that. The *kathīr* of the pardon is greater than the *kathīr* of what would warrant destruction.

Ibn Kathīr, commenting on this passage, connects it immediately to the verse that follows: "those who dispute Our signs may know that there is no place of refuge for them." The movement is intentional. The *ʿafw kathīr* of verse 34 is precisely what makes the warning of verse 35 so urgent. The mercy is vast — but it has a shape. The one who receives it is the one who reads the signs, not the one who disputes them. The *ṣabbār shakūr* is the one who *sees* the ship, sees the wind, sees the calm, sees the wreckage that did not come — and turns all of it into awareness. The disputer looks at the running ship and says "my skill, my vessel, my engineering," and in doing so, steps outside the radius of the *ʿafw*. Not because the mercy is withdrawn by grudging divine accounting — but because the sign has been refused. You cannot receive what the sign offers if you will not read the sign.

[PAUSE]

And so the passage — three verses, thirty-seven Arabic words — builds a complete picture of a human life:

You are a runner on borrowed wind.
The becalming is a mercy.
The wrecking you deserved did not come.
And the difference between you and the wreck is *ʿafw* — pardon — given in quantities the verse describes only as *much*.

What is the right response to all of this? The verse answers before it asks the question.

**صَبَّار. شَكُور.**

Patient. Grateful. All the way through.

## Conclusion

I want to close with the simplest possible formulation of what we have learned, because the simplest one is the truest.

You are the ship.

You are called "the runner" — because running is your nature, your ambition, your identity. You have built yourself. You have equipped yourself. You have launched yourself across the open water with great confidence.

And every moment you are moving — the wind is a gift you did not make.

And every moment you are not wrecked — the pardon is a mercy you did not earn.

The *ṣabbār* knows this when the wind goes still. She does not rage at the flat sea. She does not blame the ship. She does not abandon course. She waits — patiently, habitually, in obedience — because she knows the wind is His to withhold and His to send. And she does not reach for the forbidden wind in the meantime. And she accepts the painful decree of the becalmed surface, because she knows it is His hand and not misfortune.

The *shakūr* knows this when the wind blows. He does not take credit for the surge. He does not check his rigging and feel pride. He looks up at the filled sail and says *alhamdulillah*, because he has read the sign: the wind is a gift, continuous, not owed.

And the one who is *both* — the *ṣabbār shakūr* — crosses the sea of this life without ever mistaking the voyage for their own achievement. Not because they are passive. Not because they stop working the sails and the ropes. But because they know, in their bones, what is borrowed and what is owned.

They own the gratitude. They own the patience. Everything else is a loan.

---

*وَيَعْفُ عَن كَثِيرٍ*

*And He pardons much.*

May we be among those covered by the *kathīr* of His *ʿafw* — not because we deserved it, but because He gave it. And may our response to that, every day, on every sea, be *ṣabr* and *shukr*, all the way through.
