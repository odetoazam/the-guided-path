# Stand Straight by the Measure You Were Given

## Introduction: The Order Given After the Argument Ended

Let me first put the words in front of you. Hear them in the Arabic, even if you don't yet know what they mean — because the sound is part of the meaning here.

**فَلِذَٰلِكَ فَٱدْعُ ۖ وَٱسْتَقِمْ كَمَآ أُمِرْتَ**

*Fa-li-dhālika fa-dʿu, wa-staqim kamā umirta...*

"So to that, invite — and stand straight as you have been commanded, and do not follow their desires. And say: 'I believe in whatever Book Allah has sent down, and I have been commanded to deal justly among you. Allah is our Lord and your Lord. For us our deeds, and for you your deeds. There is no argument between us and you. Allah will gather us together — and to Him is the final destination.'"

Now, before we open a single word, I want you to feel where this verse sits. Because this is not the opening of a conversation. **This is what is said after the conversation is over.**

Rebuild the moment. The Prophet ﷺ is in Mecca. Around him are people who share his city, his language, his ancestry, his marketplace — and who reject the very thing he cannot stop carrying. Beyond them are the People of the Book, who have scriptures of their own and who dispute about whose path is the true one. The whole atmosphere is one of *contestation*. Whose religion. Whose father Abraham. Whose claim. The air is thick with argument.

And just two verses before this one, Allah has made a stunning diagnosis. He says: this is one religion — the same thing entrusted to Noah, to Abraham, to Moses, to Jesus, to you (42:13). And then He says something that should stop us cold: the people who divided over it **did not divide because they were confused.** They divided *after* knowledge came to them — **بَغْيًا بَيْنَهُمْ** — out of transgression, out of rivalry, *between themselves* (42:14).

So the rift is not a misunderstanding. It is a *wanting*. People knew, and chose otherwise, because of what they desired.

[PAUSE]

Now — *now* — comes verse 15. *Fa-li-dhālika.* "So — because of *that* —". Because the division is desire and not ignorance, here is your posture. And what follows is one of the most complete instructions in the entire Quran for how a person of truth is supposed to stand in a world that will argue with them forever.

Here is the question I want you to carry into this verse. We will spend the next hour answering it.

**How do you give everything to a truth, invite people to it with your whole life — and at the same time completely let go of whether they accept it? How do you stand firm and release your grip at the very same moment?**

Because that is what this verse teaches. And it teaches it not through advice — but through the precise shape of its words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Istaqim* — The Verb That Means "Keep Straightening"

Let me teach you something about how Arabic verbs work before we touch the word, because if you don't have this, the word will pass you by.

Arabic verbs come in families. From a single root — a skeleton of usually three letters — the language builds different "forms," and each form bends the basic meaning in a predictable direction. Some forms make a verb causative — they make you *cause* something to happen. Some make it intensive. And one particular form, the one grammarians call **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern, very often carries the sense of **seeking something, asking for it, taking it on, working to bring it about and keep it.**

You already know this form even if you've never named it. The Arabic word for *seeking forgiveness* — *istighfār* — is Form X built on the root for forgiveness. Not "to forgive," but "to *seek* forgiveness." The form adds the reaching, the asking, the effort of going after a thing.

Now hold that, and look at the second command in our verse.

**وَٱسْتَقِمْ** — *wa-staqim.*

The root underneath it is **ق-و-م**, *q-w-m* — the root of *standing*. The simplest verb from it, *qāma*, just means "he stood." Plain. A single act. You stood up.

But Allah does not say *qum* — "stand." He says ***istaqim*** — and that is Form X. The seeking form. The taking-on form. The form of effortful, ongoing pursuit.

Sit with what that does.

[PAUSE]

*Qum* would be a moment. A posture you adopt once. But *istaqim* is not a moment — it is a *campaign*. It is "**keep seeking your own straightness.** Keep standing yourself back up straight. Work, continuously, at remaining upright." It contains within its very shape the assumption that something is always pulling you off the vertical — and that uprightness is therefore not a state you achieve but a state you *maintain*, breath by breath, against a constant pull.

Notice something. The English word "steadfast" hides this. We hear "stand firm" and we picture a statue — set down once, immovable forever. But the Arabic is not a statue. The Arabic is a person on the deck of a moving ship, constantly adjusting, constantly re-finding the vertical, *because the floor will not hold still.* Istiqāma is not the absence of pull. It is the unbroken response to the pull.

This is why, when the Prophet ﷺ was asked to summarize the whole religion in words he would not need to ask anyone about ever again, he gave a famous answer: *"Say: I believe in Allah — then *istaqim*."* Then *keep straightening.* The whole path, after belief, is this single verb.

And here is what's remarkable about its placement. Look at what wraps around *istaqim* in our verse. Before it: *fa-dʿu* — "invite." After it: *wa lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾahum* — "and do not follow their desires." The follow-verb there, *tattabiʿ*, is itself a form (Form VIII) that means to *actively pursue and attach yourself to* something, to fall into step behind it.

So look at the architecture of the commands. **Invite them** — move toward the people. **Keep straightening yourself** — hold your own vertical. **Do not fall into step behind their desires** — do not let the people you are moving toward pull you off your line.

The whole drama of daʿwah is in those three verbs. You must go *toward* people without being pulled *by* them. *Istaqim* is the word that makes that possible — because it already knows you are being pulled.

**What this single word reveals: uprightness is not a position you take once. It is a pull you answer for the rest of your life.**

### Element Two: *Umirta... Umirtu* — The Twice-Spoken Passive That Removes the Self

Here is the second feature, and it lives in a grammatical detail most translations quietly erase.

Let me first give you the concept. In every language there is a difference between an *active* sentence and a *passive* one. "I gave the command" is active — the doer is front and center. "I was given the command" is passive — and notice what happens: the doer disappears, and *I* become the one who *receives*. The passive voice takes the spotlight off the person and points it at something above them.

Now watch how many times Allah uses the passive in this verse — and watch *who* is in it.

The Prophet ﷺ is the most authoritative human being who will ever walk the earth. He is the one being told to *invite*, to *stand straight*, to *speak*. Every command is aimed at him. And yet, twice, when the verse describes the source of his authority, it puts him not as the commander but as the **commanded**:

**كَمَا أُمِرْتَ** — *kamā umirta* — "as **you have been commanded**."

**وَأُمِرْتُ لِأَعْدِلَ** — *wa-umirtu li-aʿdila* — "and **I have been commanded** to deal justly."

Both are passive. *Umirta. Umirtu.* "I was commanded." And the One who commanded is not named in the grammar at all — He doesn't need to be. The passive *removes* the Prophet from the position of source, and leaves the source unspoken because it is unmistakable.

Let this land. The single most influential man in history is instructed to ground every word he says, not in his own greatness, not in his own conviction, not even in his own certainty — but in the bare fact: **I was told to.** I am under orders. I did not author this. I received it.

[PAUSE]

And now look at the little word that sits beside the first one: **كَمَا**, *kamā* — "**as**," or more precisely, "*exactly as*, *in the measure that*."

"Stand straight **kamā umirta** — *as* you were commanded." Not more than you were commanded. Not less. Not according to your own zeal, not according to what the moment seems to demand, not according to how much resistance you're facing. **The measure of your standing is the measure of the command.** *Kamā* is a ruler laid against the verb. It says: the command itself defines exactly how upright, and no further.

Notice how this protects him from two opposite dangers at once. On one side, the danger of *doing less* than commanded — collapsing under pressure, softening the message to make peace. On the other side — and this one we miss — the danger of doing *more* than commanded. Of adding to the religion out of love for it. Of zeal that overshoots the instruction. *Kamā umirta* fences both. Stand exactly as straight as you were told. The straightness is not yours to calibrate.

And here is the quiet beauty of why this is *mercy* and not burden. If your standing were measured against the world's resistance, you could never stand straight enough — the resistance is infinite. But your standing is measured against the *command*, which is finite, defined, and given. You can actually fulfill it. The passive that removes you as the source is the same passive that **frees you from owning the result.** You were commanded to stand a certain way. Whether the world bends is not in the command.

**What this twice-spoken passive reveals: the truest authority is the one most thoroughly under orders. To carry a truth rightly is to remember, always, that you did not make it.**

### Element Three: The Geometry of *Bayna* — and the Argument That Stops

Now I want to show you something about the *shape* of this verse — something hiding in a single small word that Allah repeats, and in one word He pointedly takes away.

The word is **بَيْنَ**, *bayna* — "between." It is not a noun for a thing you can hold. It is a word for a *relationship* — the space *between* two parties. And in this one verse, Allah uses it **four times**. Listen for it:

- *li-aʿdila* **بَيْنَكُمْ** — "that I deal justly **between** you."
- *lā ḥujjata* **بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ** — "no argument **between** us and **between** you."
- *Allāhu yajmaʿu* **بَيْنَنَا** — "Allah will gather **between** us."

Four times, the verse keeps returning to the *space between people.* That is what this whole ending is about — not what's inside you, not what's inside them, but the charged, contested territory *between* you. And watch what Allah does to that space across the four uses. He does three different things to it, in sequence.

**First**, He fills it with justice: *I am commanded to deal justly* **between** *you.* Pause on who "you" is here. The address is to the very people who reject him. And it is unspecified — *bayna-kum*, "between you all," with no qualifier carving out the deniers. He is commanded to be just **between** them — *to* them — even as they oppose him. Justice does not wait for someone to deserve it by agreeing with you. The first thing that fills the space between the believer and his opponent is *fairness*.

The athar reported from Ibn ʿAbbās captures the flavor: that *li-aʿdila* means *I was commanded not to wrong you, not to burden you with more than Allah has obligated.* Justice as restraint. Justice as not-overreaching, even against those who overreach against you.

**Second** — and this is the absence I want you to read as carefully as any presence — He *empties* the space of something. **لَا حُجَّةَ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ** — "**no argument** between us and you."

The word *ḥujjah* (root **ح-ج-ج**) means a proof, a contention, a case you press, the back-and-forth of disputation. And here, in a surah whose very name is *Ash-Shūrā* — *consultation, mutual deliberation* — in a context soaked in argument from the surrounding verses, Allah suddenly says: **between us, no more arguing.**

Read that absence. After you have invited. After you have stood straight. After you have said *I believe* and *I am commanded to be just* — the disputation **stops**. Not because you won. Not because they conceded. The arguing simply ceases to be the point. *Lā ḥujjah* is the laying down of a weapon you no longer need to hold.

(The classical commentators note an interpretive dimension here: al-Jalalayn observes that this was revealed before the later command relating to active struggle — one reading of the verse's place in the sequence. But the meaning for the heart stands across the readings: there comes a point where the case has been made, and the endless re-litigation is released.)

**Third**, having emptied the space of argument, Allah tells you who *fills it instead*: **ٱللَّهُ يَجْمَعُ بَيْنَنَا** — "**Allah** will gather between us." The space between us and those who reject us — the space we were straining to control by winning — *He* takes it. The gathering is His act, at a time of His choosing, for a verdict that is His to give.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the geometry now? The same little word, *bayna*, traces an entire emotional journey across the verse:

> Fill the space between you with **justice** → empty it of **argument** → and hand the space itself to **Allah**, who will gather everyone in it at the end.

And bracketing this, in the middle, is the cleanest statement of separated responsibility in the Quran: **لَنَا أَعْمَالُنَا وَلَكُمْ أَعْمَالُكُمْ** — *for us our deeds, and for you yours.* Look at the perfect symmetry of it — *lanā / wa lakum*, *aʿmālunā / aʿmālukum* — two columns standing side by side, equal in grammar, separate in account. Your ledger is yours. Mine is mine. We will not be sharing a verdict.

And the final word seals the journey with a *direction*: **وَإِلَيْهِ ٱلْمَصِيرُ** — "and to Him is the *maṣīr*." That word, *maṣīr*, comes from the root **س-ي-ر**, *s-y-r*, **to travel, to journey, to move along a road.** *Maṣīr* is not just "destination" as a static place — it is *the becoming, the place all journeying arrives at.* The whole verse, which began with motion — *invite, stand, do not follow* — ends by revealing that all of this motion, yours and theirs, every deed in both columns, is a journey along a single road that bends back to one point. **To Him.**

**What this geometry reveals: you are responsible for filling the space between you and others with justice and with witness — and you are forbidden from owning the verdict that fills it at the end. That belongs to the One the whole road leads back to.**

### Bridge

So the words have shown us a shape. *Istaqim* — keep straightening against a constant pull. *Umirta, umirtu* — and remember you did not author what you carry; you only received it. *Bayna* four times — fill the space between you with justice, empty it of argument, and hand it to Allah.

Three commands and one geometry. Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being is actually supposed to *live* among people who will not agree with them.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Inviting With Everything, While Releasing the Outcome

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, at some point, tried to *convince* someone who mattered to you. A parent about a decision. A sibling drifting from the deen. A friend headed somewhere you could see was a cliff. A spouse. A teenager. And you know the particular exhaustion of it — the way your peace becomes hostage to *their* response. You said the right thing, and they shrugged, and something in you curdled. You make the case again, harder. They resist harder. And now you are not inviting anymore — you are *fighting*, and you are losing yourself in the fight, because somewhere along the way **their acceptance became the measure of your worth.**

We do this with the people we love most. We confuse *carrying* a truth to someone with being *responsible for* whether they take it. And so we either burn out, or we start bending the truth itself to make it more swallowable — softening it, shaving its edges — because if we can just find the version they'll accept, we'll finally be free of this ache.

Now bring the verse to that ache.

Look at what Allah commands and — just as importantly — what He does *not*.

He commands: **invite** (*fa-dʿu*). He commands: **stand straight** (*wa-staqim*). He commands: **don't get pulled off your line by their wanting** (*wa lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾahum*). He commands: **say "I believe"** (*wa qul āmantu*). He commands: **be just to them** (*wa-umirtu li-aʿdila*).

Count what's in that list. Every single command is about *your* action, *your* posture, *your* witness. And then count what is *not* in the list. **Nowhere — nowhere — are you commanded to make them believe.** That verb never comes. The outcome is simply not assigned to you.

[PAUSE]

This is the release the whole verse is built to give you. Remember *kamā umirta* — "stand straight *as you were commanded.*" Your standing is measured against the **command**, not against their **response**. If their response were the measure, you could never stand straight enough — there is no amount of correctness that *guarantees* another person's heart. But the command is finite. The command you can fulfill. And once you've fulfilled it, you are *done* — not because they came around, but because the part that was yours is complete.

This is why *istaqim* and *lā tattabiʿ ahwāʾahum* are spoken in the same breath. The pull that knocks you off your straightness is, so often, *the desire to be accepted by the very people you're inviting.* You start adjusting the message to win the room. And the moment you do, you've stopped standing straight and started following — *tattabiʿ*, falling into step behind their desires. The verse names the exact mechanism by which sincere people lose themselves: **they confuse winning the person with serving the person.**

So what changes tomorrow?

The next time you carry a truth to someone — about the deen, about their life, about anything that matters — do the whole of *your* part with everything you have. Invite beautifully. Stand straight. Be scrupulously *just* to them even as they push back. Say plainly what you believe. And then — at the exact point where your part ends and their heart begins — **stop.** Hand that border to Allah. *Allāhu yajmaʿu baynanā.* He will gather us. The verdict on their heart was never on your desk.

You are responsible for the invitation. You were never responsible for the answer.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: "For Us Our Deeds, For You Yours" — Disagreement Without Hostility

Now the second theme, and again, let me begin where you breathe.

We live in an age that has lost the ability to disagree and still let each other *be*. Open any feed. Every disagreement escalates instantly to enmity. To differ with someone is to be required to *defeat* them, to expose them, to make them recant. We have a culture that cannot leave an argument un-won. And it is *exhausting* — this low, constant hum of having to relitigate everything, to have the last word, to never let a wrong opinion stand un-corrected in your presence. We mistake this for conviction. It is mostly just an inability to release.

Now hear the dignity of how Allah ends this verse.

**لَنَا أَعْمَالُنَا وَلَكُمْ أَعْمَالُكُمْ** — "For us our deeds, and for you your deeds."

Feel the *posture* of that line. It is not "you are wrong and I am right." It is not even, at this moment, an argument at all. It is a *drawing of two columns.* Here is my account. There is yours. Stated with a strange, calm symmetry — *lanā / wa lakum* — as if to say: *we are two separate ledgers walking toward the same Reckoner.* There is no contempt in it. There is no gloating. There is *clarity*, and there is *distance*, and there is — beneath it — a kind of respect: your choices are genuinely yours, and they are weighty enough that you, not I, will answer for them.

And then: **لَا حُجَّةَ بَيْنَنَا وَبَيْنَكُمْ** — "no argument between us and you."

This is the verse refusing the thing our age cannot refuse. After everything has been made plain — the truth declared, the justice promised, the columns drawn — **the disputation is laid down.** Not because the truth doesn't matter. It matters infinitely; that's the whole reason for *fa-dʿu*, the inviting. But there is a difference between *witnessing* the truth and *endlessly fighting about it*, and a mature soul learns where one ends and the other begins.

[PAUSE]

This is one of the hardest pieces of spiritual maturity there is: **to hold a conviction with total firmness and hold the person who rejects it without hostility.** Most of us can only do one or the other. Either we're firm and we become harsh — every disagreement a battle. Or we're gentle and we go soft on the truth itself — peace bought by erasing the line. This verse does the thing that seems impossible: it is *immovable* on the truth (*istaqim, kamā umirta, āmantu*) and *non-hostile* toward the one who denies it (*li-aʿdila baynakum, lanā aʿmālunā wa lakum aʿmālukum, lā ḥujjah*). Firm spine. Open hand. Both at once.

And what makes both possible at once is the very last clause — the secret engine of the whole thing: **ٱللَّهُ يَجْمَعُ بَيْنَنَا وَإِلَيْهِ ٱلْمَصِيرُ.** "Allah will gather us, and to Him is the destination."

*This* is why you can lay the argument down without abandoning the truth. You are not dropping the case — you are **moving it to a higher court.** The verdict is not cancelled; it is *deferred to the only Judge who can actually render it.* When you truly believe that everyone — you and the one who wronged you, you and the one who rejected you — is on a single road that bends back to one Throne, you no longer need to win the argument *here.* You can let it stand un-won, because *un-won* doesn't mean *unjudged.* It means judged *there*, by Him, perfectly.

So what changes tomorrow?

The next time you are in the disagreement that you cannot seem to release — the family member, the comment thread, the old wound — try the move this verse teaches. Make your witness plainly and justly. Draw the two columns: *this is what I hold, and that is what you hold.* And then, instead of reaching for the last word, hand the verdict upward: *Allāhu yajmaʿu baynanā.* "Allah will gather us." Let it go *to Him*, not *away.* You will feel something unclench that has been clenched for a very long time. That unclenching is the verse doing its work.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Now Visible

Look back now and see how the whole verse was built. It moves in a perfect arc, from motion to rest, from you to Him:

> **fa-dʿu** (invite) → **wa-staqim** (stand straight) → **wa lā tattabiʿ** (don't be pulled) → **qul āmantu** (declare your faith) → **umirtu li-aʿdila** (be just between them) → **lanā aʿmālunā wa lakum aʿmālukum** (separate the ledgers) → **lā ḥujjah** (lay down the argument) → **Allāhu yajmaʿu baynanā** (He gathers) → **wa ilayhi al-maṣīr** (and to Him is the road's end).

It begins with your most active outward motion — *go, invite* — and it ends with the most complete surrender — *to Him is the destination.* And the hinge in the middle, turning effort into release, is *bayna*: the space between you and others, which you fill with justice, empty of argument, and finally **hand to Allah.**

### The Two Discoveries Were One

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same insight approached from two directions.

The grammar taught us: *istaqim* is a pull you answer continuously, and *umirta* means you are not the source — you only received. The themes taught us: invite with everything, but release the outcome, because the outcome was never assigned to you. **These are the same truth.** You stand straight *as commanded* (your part, finite, fulfillable) precisely so that you can let go of the response (His part, never yours). The passive voice that removes you as the *source* of the truth is the same grace that removes you as the *owner* of the result. You did not make it; you do not have to make them take it. **You are the one who carries — never the one who decides.**

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I confused *carrying* a truth to someone with being *responsible for* whether they accept it — and what has that confusion cost me?
2. When I "stand firm," is my measure *kamā umirta* — the command — or is it secretly the resistance I'm facing, or the approval I'm craving?
3. Is there an argument I am still fighting that the verse would have me *lay down* — not by conceding the truth, but by moving the verdict to a higher court?
4. Can I name one person I disagree with deeply, toward whom I could be *more just* tomorrow — *li-aʿdila baynakum* — without softening one inch of what I believe?
5. Do I actually live as though *wa ilayhi al-maṣīr* is true — as though every road, mine and theirs, bends back to Him — or do I behave as though the final word has to be spoken *here*, by *me*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Invite with all you have and stand straight by the measure you were given — deal justly even with those who reject you, then lay down the argument and hand the verdict to the One toward whom every road is already traveling.

### The Closing Invitation

This verse is not asking you to care *less.* The Prophet ﷺ cared so much that Allah elsewhere had to comfort him lest he destroy himself with grief over those who would not believe. *Fa-dʿu* — *invite* — is a command to care with your whole life. What the verse invites is something subtler and far more freeing: care with everything, *and* set down the one weight that was never yours to lift — the weight of the result. Stand straight. Be just. Speak your faith. And let the space between you and every soul who disagrees become, at last, not a battlefield you must win, but a road you both walk — *to Him.*

### A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, who commanded Your Messenger to stand straight and to be just even toward those who turned away — let me carry what You have entrusted without ever imagining I authored it. Keep me straightening when the floor will not hold still. And teach my heart to lay down the arguments I cannot win, trusting that You will gather us, and that to You is the destination. Āmīn.
