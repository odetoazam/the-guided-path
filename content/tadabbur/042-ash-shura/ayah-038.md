# The Affair That Is Consultation
### Surah Ash-Shura, Ayah 38

> وَٱلَّذِينَ ٱسْتَجَابُوا۟ لِرَبِّهِمْ وَأَقَامُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ وَمِمَّا رَزَقْنَٰهُمْ يُنفِقُونَ

*Walladhīna-stajābū li-rabbihim wa-aqāmu-ṣ-ṣalāta wa-amruhum shūrā baynahum wa-mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn.*

**"And those who have responded to their Lord, and established the prayer, and whose affair is consultation among themselves, and who spend out of what We have provided them."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me put you somewhere first.

It is Makkah. Years before the migration to Madinah. There is no Muslim state. There is no land the believers can call their own, no treasury, no army, no council chamber where decisions get made. The believers are a small, pressured minority — boycotted, mocked, some of them tortured, many of them poor. They have almost nothing.

And in the middle of this — in a *Makkan* surah, revealed to a community that governs nothing — Allah describes the believers with a word so central that He names the entire surah after it.

He does not name the surah after the prayer. He does not name it after charity. He does not name it after patience under torture, though they had plenty of that.

He names it **Ash-Shura.** The Consultation.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how strange that is. A community with no power to administer is being told that one of the marks of belonging to Allah is that **their affair is consultation among themselves.** Before they had a city to run, Allah was already shaping how they would run it. Before they had anything to decide, He was already teaching them *how* believers decide.

And He places this — this matter of how human beings make decisions together — in a list of the very highest things. Look at where it sits. On one side: *responding to their Lord* and *establishing the prayer* — the most vertical, most sacred acts a soul can perform. On the other side: *spending from what We provided them* — the most tangible, economic, hand-in-pocket act of generosity.

And right in the middle of prayer and charity, Allah sets **how they make decisions together.**

Here is the hook. Why would the way a group of human beings talks through a problem be placed on the same shelf as prayer? Why would Allah dignify *consultation* — something that looks so ordinary, so human, so much like a meeting — by surrounding it with the holiest acts of the religion?

There is something hidden in the grammar of this verse that answers that question. And once you see it, you will never sit in a decision the same way again.

Let's go slowly.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Istajābū* — The Answer That Answers an Answer

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

Arabic builds meaning out of verb *patterns*. The same three root letters can be poured into roughly ten different molds, and each mold bends the meaning in a particular direction. One of those molds — what grammarians call **Form X** — very often carries the sense of *seeking, asking for, reaching toward something.* You take a root and you make it into a request.

Now hold that, and let's look at how the verse opens.

The first thing Allah says about these people is not that they prayed. It is:

> ٱسْتَجَابُوا۟ لِرَبِّهِمْ
> *istajābū li-rabbihim* — "they responded to their Lord."

The root here is **jwb** — to answer, to respond. And the verb is in that Form X pattern. *Istajāba.* This is not the plain, flat word for "answer." It is the intensified, complete, wholehearted form of responding — the kind of answer you give with your whole self when someone you love calls your name.

Notice something. The believer's *very first* described quality is not an action they initiate. It is a **response.** They *answered.* Which means — before they answered — somebody **called.**

[PAUSE]

The whole spiritual life, this verse quietly tells us, begins not with us reaching up, but with Allah reaching down. He called first. *Istajābū li-rabbihim.* Their lordship — *rabb*, the One who nurtures, sustains, raises a thing from infancy to completion — their *Rabb* called them, and they turned and answered.

Now here is what is remarkable, and you have to stay with me for it.

This exact verb — *istajāba* — is the verb Allah uses for **Himself.**

In Surah al-Baqarah, when Allah speaks of the slave who calls upon Him in du'a, He says: *u, ujību da'wata-d-dā'i idhā da'ān* — "I respond to the call of the caller when he calls upon Me." And the believer's instructed reply: *fal-yastajībū lī* — "so let them respond to Me." (2:186) The same root. The same Form. The God who *responds* to His servant asks the servant to *respond* to Him.

Do you feel the reciprocity opening up?

You call on Allah in your weakness — He *istajāba*, He responds. He calls you to Himself through His messengers, through the Qur'an, through the ache in your own chest at 3am — and the believer is the one who *istajāba* back. The relationship is a conversation. A call and an answer. An answer to an Answerer.

So the believer is, at the very root of his identity, **a responder.** Not the initiator of the relationship — the One who initiated is Allah — but the one who, when the call came, did not stay silent.

[PAUSE — let this land]

And here is the quiet, devastating question that hides inside this opening word: *the call has already gone out.* It went out to you. The only question the verse leaves on the table is — **did you answer?**

That is the first word. One clause, and Allah has already established that faith is a reply to a love that moved first.

### Element Two: The Sentence That Refuses to Be a Verb

Now I need to show you something you would walk straight past in translation — and it is, I think, the single most beautiful thing in this verse.

Let me first teach you the structure, in plain English.

In Arabic there are two fundamentally different kinds of sentences. There is the **verbal sentence** — it starts with a verb, and it describes an *action, an event, something that happens in time.* "He prays." "She gives." Verbs are events. They begin and they end.

And there is the **nominal sentence** — it has no verb at its heart; it links two nouns. And a nominal sentence describes not an event but a *state* — something stable, ongoing, a settled fact about how things simply *are.* "The sky is blue." Not "the sky turned blue this morning" — just: that is its nature.

Hold that distinction. Now watch the verse breathe.

Look at the qualities Allah lists. *Istajābū* — they responded. A verb. *Aqāmū* — they established. A verb. *Yunfiqūn* — they spend. A verb.

Action. Action. Action. Things the believers *do.*

But right in the center, when Allah comes to consultation, **the grammar changes.** He does not say *yatashāwarūn* — "they consult." That would have been a verb. That would have made it one more action on the list.

He says instead:

> وَأَمْرُهُمْ شُورَىٰ بَيْنَهُمْ
> *wa-amruhum shūrā baynahum* — "and their affair **is** consultation among them."

This is a **nominal sentence.** No verb. "Their affair" — *is* — "consultation." It is not framed as something they *do.* It is framed as something they **are.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what just happened?

Prayer is an act they perform. Giving is an act they perform. But consultation is not described as an act at all. It is described as **the very condition of their collective life.** Their affair *is* shūrā the way the sky *is* blue. It is not an event that occurs at meetings. It is the atmosphere they breathe. It is woven into what they fundamentally *are* as a community.

Allah could have made shūrā a fourth verb — one more thing on the to-do list of the righteous. Instead He lifted it out of the realm of *actions* and placed it into the realm of **identity.** A believer doesn't *do* consultation occasionally, the way he prays at fixed times. A believing community simply *is* consultative, all the way down, as a permanent state of its being.

And there is one more thing the absence of the verb does. A verb has a tense — it happened, or it is happening, or it will. A nominal sentence has no tense. It is timeless. By refusing the verb, Allah made shūrā a standing, ever-present truth about His people — not something they did once in Madinah, not a historical episode, but a permanent description that reaches across every century, including ours.

This is what reading *absence* teaches you. The missing verb is not a gap. The missing verb is the whole point.

### Element Three: *Shūrā* — Drawing Out the Honey

And now the word itself. *Shūrā.*

The root is **$wr** — shīn, wāw, rā. And the classical lexicographers preserve for us the original, physical, sensory image buried inside this root. Listen.

*Shārtu al-ʿasal.* It means: **I extracted the honey from the hive.** The act of reaching carefully into the honeycomb and drawing out what is sweet and pure, separating it from the wax and the comb.

[PAUSE]

That is the image Allah chose for how human beings should make decisions together.

Sit with this, because it is doing real work. When you consult — when a group genuinely talks a matter through — what are you doing? Every person around that circle is a comb. Inside each of them is a little honey — a piece of insight, an angle no one else can see, a fear worth hearing, a hope worth weighing. And *shūrā* is the patient act of **drawing the honey out of every comb** and gathering it into one vessel.

The decision you reach through consultation is not one person's opinion imposed on the rest. It is *honey* — gathered, extracted, drawn out from many sources, refined into something sweeter and purer than any single mind could have produced alone.

And notice what this image implies about the people you sit with. *Everyone has honey.* The quiet one in the corner has honey. The person you usually overrule has honey. The one whose temperament irritates you has honey. The root word assumes that wisdom is *distributed* — that Allah did not deposit all the insight in one head, but scattered it across the community, precisely so that we would *need* each other to gather it.

Now feel the form of the word. *Shūrā.* It sits in an Arabic noun-pattern (the *fuʿlā* shape) used for great abstract realities — the same shape as *dhikrā*, remembrance; *bushrā*, glad tidings. It is not a small word. It is a noun of weight and dignity, naming a whole reality, not a passing act.

And here the classical commentators add a detail that completes the picture. Al-Baghawi and the Jalalayn both gloss this clause with the same phrase: *yatashāwarūna fīhi **wa lā yaʿjalūn*** — "they consult one another over it **and they do not hasten.**"

There it is. You cannot extract honey in a hurry. Rush the hive and you crush the comb, you get wax and stinging and a mess. Honey comes out *slowly,* with patience, with care. The very image of *shūrā* contains within it the command not to rush. The believer who consults is the believer who has conquered his own haste — who is willing to sit, and listen, and draw the sweetness out slowly, rather than slam down a decision because his ego cannot bear to wait.

[PAUSE]

So what does this one word reveal? That Allah did not see collective decision-making as a dry political mechanism — a vote, a procedure, a show of hands. He saw it as **honey-gathering.** Something sweet, something patient, something that honors every soul in the room as a vessel of God-given insight.

---

### Bridge

So look at what the construction has shown us.

The believer is, at root, a *responder* — someone whose life is an answer to a call that came first. Their consultation is not an action they perform but a *state of being* they inhabit. And the very word for that consultation carries an image of patiently drawing sweetness out of every soul in the room.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the way you carry your own decisions. About the kind of life this verse is quietly calling you toward.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Loneliness of the One Who Decides Alone

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a story our culture tells us, over and over, about strength. The strong person *trusts their gut.* The great leader *makes the hard call alone.* The founder, the visionary, the one who "doesn't need a committee." We have a whole mythology of the lone decider — the person decisive enough, certain enough, *important* enough to not have to ask anyone.

And underneath that mythology is a quiet assumption: that asking is weakness. That needing other people's input means you are not really capable. That the truly strong stand alone.

Now I want you to notice something honest about your own life. Think of the worst decisions you have ever made. Not the unlucky ones — the genuinely *bad* ones, the ones you regret. How many of them were made *alone,* in a hurry, when you were certain you were right and didn't want anyone to slow you down?

[PAUSE]

The ego does not want shūrā. Let's be honest about that. There is something in the *nafs* that experiences asking as humiliation. To consult is to admit, out loud, "I do not have the full picture. I might be wrong. I need you." And the ego would rather be wrong in private than corrected in public. It would rather protect its image of competence than actually become competent.

And so the lone decider is not, in truth, strong. He is *afraid.* Afraid of the exposure that comes with asking. Afraid of hearing something he doesn't want to hear. He dresses his fear up as decisiveness, but it is fear.

Now bring the verse back. *Wa-amruhum shūrā baynahum.* Their affair *is* consultation. Remember the grammar — this is who they *are,* not merely what they do. Which means the believing community is built, at the level of its identity, on the opposite of that ego. It is built on the open admission: *no one of us has all the honey.* Wisdom is distributed. I need you, and you need me, and we draw it out of each other.

And here is the layer that should stop you. *Who* is being described here? Not weak people. The people Allah praises in this very passage are the ones who, when wronged, *defend themselves* (42:39). They are not doormats. They are strong. And *these* are the ones whose affair is consultation. So shūrā is not the refuge of the timid. **It is the discipline of the strong.** It takes a *greater* strength to ask than to command — because asking requires you to risk your own ego in front of others.

Think about what this means at the very top of the religion. The Prophet ﷺ himself — who received *revelation,* who had access to the most direct guidance any human has ever had — was told in Surah Aal-Imran: *wa shāwirhum fi-l-amr* — "and consult them in the affair." (3:159) The one human being who least "needed" anyone's opinion was commanded to consult. Why? Because consultation was never only about getting the right answer. It was about *honoring* the people in the room, binding their hearts, and modeling that even prophetic strength does not decide alone.

[PAUSE]

So here is what changes about tomorrow. The next time you face a real decision — and you feel that pull to just *decide,* to settle it quickly, to not have to ask — recognize that pull for what it is. It is not strength. It is the ego protecting itself from exposure. And the believer is the one who, precisely *there,* slows down, opens his hands, and draws out the honey. Ask. Name the people in your life who have insight you lack, and actually go to them. Not to perform humility — to *gain* it, and to gain what they know.

The lone decider thinks he is on a throne. He is actually just alone.

### Theme Two: The Faith That Will Not Stay Private

Now I want you to step back and see the *shape* of the whole verse, because its architecture is a teaching all by itself.

Watch the order Allah chose. He begins with the most vertical thing imaginable: *they responded to their Lord.* The soul turning to its Maker. Then He stays vertical: *and they established the prayer.* The body bowing, the forehead on the ground, the most intimate standing of a servant before God.

Then — without any seam, without any "and now, separately, in worldly matters" — He moves outward: *and their affair is consultation among themselves.* Suddenly we are among *people.* We are in the circle, the meeting, the shared life. And then He goes further out still, to the most tangible thing of all: *and from what We provided them, they spend.* Now we are at the level of the wallet, the open hand, the wealth that leaves your grip and lands in someone else's need.

Do you see the movement? **From the prayer mat to the meeting circle to the open hand.** From the most private vertical act to the most public horizontal one. And there is no break in the list. Allah strings them together with a single rhythm, as if to say: *these are not four separate things. They are one life, flowing outward.*

[PAUSE]

This is the answer to the question we opened with. Why is consultation placed between prayer and charity? Because Allah is refusing the lie that faith is private.

We live with that lie everywhere. "Keep your religion to yourself." "What's between you and God is your business." And there is a truth in protecting the privacy of worship from showing off. But there is a poison in it too — the idea that faith is a sealed chamber in your heart that never touches how you treat people, how you make decisions, how you handle money.

This verse demolishes that wall. The same *istijāba* — the same wholehearted response to the Lord — that puts you on the prayer mat is the response that makes you consult the people around you and open your hand to the poor. **It is one continuous act of answering God.** Your prayer and your meetings and your generosity are not in different categories. They are the same faith, expressed at three different distances from your own heart.

And look at what consultation and spending have in common, sitting on the far side of prayer. *Shūrā* is the sharing of your **opinion, your insight, your honey** — you give up the monopoly on being right. *Infāq* is the sharing of your **wealth** — you give up the monopoly on what you own. Both are acts of *opening the hand.* Both are the death of "mine." The believer who has truly responded to his Lord becomes a person who holds nothing in a closed fist — not his certainty, and not his money.

Notice too how the spending is phrased: *mimmā razaqnāhum* — "from what **We** provided them." Not "from what they earned." Not "from their wealth." Allah reminds them, in the very clause about giving, that what is in their hands was *given to them in the first place.* You are not generous with what is yours. You are a channel for what was always His. And once you truly grasp that the wealth was never originally yours, the fist has no reason to stay closed.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow? It means you stop quarantining your faith. The honesty you found in prayer is the honesty you bring to how you decide with others. The surrender you tasted with your forehead on the ground is the surrender that opens your wallet without resentment. Pick one closed fist in your life this week — a fixed opinion you refuse to consult on, or a generosity you keep postponing — and open it. That open hand *is* your prayer, continued.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we've done the work, let me show you how the whole verse fits together, because its design is the proof of its message.

Four clauses. Watch them mirror.

> **Responded to their Lord** — *vertical: receiving the call*
> **Established the prayer** — *vertical: answering in worship*
> **Their affair is consultation** — *horizontal: opening the mind to others*
> **They spend from what We gave** — *horizontal: opening the hand to others*

Two clauses reaching *up* to God. Two clauses reaching *out* to creation. And the hinge between them — the place where the vertical turns horizontal — is **shūrā.** Consultation is the precise pivot where a heart that has answered God turns to face His servants. That is why the surah is named after it. Shūrā is the point where worship becomes society.

And look at the deeper architecture. The verse opens with the believer as a *responder* — someone defined by answering a call. And then the rest of the verse is simply *what that answer looks like* when it works its way all the way through a human life: into the prayer, into the meeting, into the wallet. The whole verse is one word — *istajābū* — unfolding outward until it reaches the poorest person in the community.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The grammar told us shūrā is a *state of being,* not an act — and the theme showed us why: because a faith that has truly answered God doesn't *perform* consultation occasionally, it *becomes* the kind of soul that cannot bear to decide alone or hold alone.

The root told us shūrā means *drawing honey from every comb* — and the theme showed us why that matters: because the lone decider's tragedy is that he throws away everyone else's honey and calls his loneliness "strength."

The architecture told us consultation is the hinge between prayer and charity — and the theme showed us why: because faith was never meant to stay sealed in the heart. It flows. Up to God, then out to His people.

### Questions to Carry

1. Think of a decision you are facing right now. Are you about to make it *alone* — and if so, is that strength, or is it the ego protecting itself from being seen as uncertain?

2. Whose honey are you ignoring? Who in your life has insight you have quietly decided not to draw out — and why?

3. The verse says the believer is one who *answered.* The call has already gone out to you. In which corners of your life are you still pretending you never heard it?

4. Where is your closed fist — the opinion you won't open to consultation, or the wealth you won't open to the needy? What would it cost you to open it?

5. Is your faith one continuous life flowing from prayer mat to meeting to open hand — or have you walled it into a private chamber that never touches how you treat people?

### One-Sentence Distillation

A believer is one whose whole life is an answer to a call that came first — and that answer is so complete that it refuses to pray alone, refuses to decide alone, and refuses to hold alone.

### The Closing Invitation

You came in thinking consultation was a meeting. A procedure. Something committees do. Leave knowing it is one of the holiest things Allah names — the precise point where a heart that has bowed to God turns and bows, in humility, to the wisdom He scattered across His servants.

So here is the invitation, gently. The next time the ego whispers *just decide, don't ask, don't look weak* — recognize it. And do the harder, stronger, sweeter thing. Slow down. Open your hands. Draw out the honey. Not because you are weak — but because you have answered a Lord who Himself responds, and you have learned from Him how to listen.

### A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You called, and we ask You to make us among those who answered. Loosen our closed fists — over our opinions and over our wealth. Teach us to draw out the honey You placed in one another, to consult without arrogance and to give without counting, until our prayer and our decisions and our generosity become one single answer to Your call. Āmīn.*
