# Whatever You Differ In: The Direction of a Returning Heart

## The Ayah

> [ayah:42:10] وَمَا ٱخْتَلَفْتُمْ فِيهِ مِن شَىْءٍ فَحُكْمُهُۥٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ رَبِّى عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ

*Wa mā ikhtalaftum fīhi min shay'in fa-ḥukmuhu ilā 'llāh. Dhālikumu 'llāhu rabbī, ʿalayhi tawakkaltu wa ilayhi unīb.*

> "And whatever you differ over — of anything — its ruling rests with Allah. That is Allah, my Lord; upon Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I keep turning back."

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

I want you to imagine a city full of people who can no longer agree.

This is not hard for us. We live in it. But I want you to feel the specific texture of the world this ayah first landed in.

Mecca, in the years before the migration. A small, pressured community of believers, surrounded on every side by people who had a position. The Quraysh had a position: the old gods, the old ways, the dignity of the ancestors. The People of the Book — the Jews and Christians the early Muslims encountered and heard about — had positions, ancient and elaborate, about God and prophecy and law, and they did not agree with each other either. And into this dense, contested air comes a man saying: there is one God, and He has sent one message, the same message, to every prophet who ever walked.

Imagine the noise that meets that claim. *Whose* version? *Says who?* By what authority do you overturn what our fathers held, what the scholars of the older books decided, what everyone around us takes for granted?

[PAUSE]

Notice what the surah is named. **Ash-Shura** — Consultation. A surah named for the way a community talks things through, decides together, manages its disagreements. And right at its heart sits a problem the whole human race has never solved: *what do you do with difference?* What do you do when you and the person across from you both feel the ground beneath your feet, and it is not the same ground?

There are only ever a few exits from that room. You can fight until one of you wins. You can walk away and call it peace. You can pretend the difference isn't there. Or you can do something none of those three allow — you can both look *up*.

This ayah is the Prophet ﷺ being taught the fourth exit. And here is what should make us lean in: it does not end where you'd expect. A verse about *disagreement* ends with the most intimate, personal words a heart can say — *my Lord, upon Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I keep turning back.*

How does a verse about an argument turn into a love letter?

That's the question I want us to chase. Stay with me.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Shape of the Word "to Differ"

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because the word is hiding inside its own shape.

Arabic builds meaning from three-letter roots, and then it bends those roots into *forms* — templates that add a layer of meaning on top of the bare root. Think of a root as a raw idea and a form as the grammar of *how that idea happens*. The same three letters, poured into different molds, come out meaning to *do* a thing, to *make someone else* do it, to *do it to yourself*, or to *do it back and forth with another person*.

One of those molds is what grammarians call **Form VIII** — the pattern *iftaʿala*. You can spot it: a little *t* gets infixed into the body of the word. And one of the most common things Form VIII does is turn an action *reflexive or reciprocal* — it folds the action back onto the doers, makes it something people take up *among themselves*, against and toward one another.

Hold that. Now look at the second word of our ayah.

> ٱخْتَلَفْتُمْ — *ikhtalaftum*

The root is **خ-ل-ف** (*kh-l-f*). And here is the first wonder. That root, in its bare meaning, is intensely physical. *Khalf* means **behind**. *Khalafa* — to come behind, to succeed someone, to be at their back. It is a word about *position in space* — who is in front, who is behind, who has turned which way.

Now pour that root — *behind, turning, position* — into Form VIII, the reciprocal mold. *Ikhtalafa*. What do you get?

You get a picture, not a definition.

[PAUSE]

You get people **turning their backs on one another and walking off in different directions.** That is what "to differ" *is*, at the root: it is not an abstract clash of ideas floating in the air. It is bodies in motion. Two people who were facing the same way, now facing away. Each one putting the other *behind* him. Each one walking toward his own horizon.

Notice what the Arabic has done. It has refused to let "disagreement" stay clean and intellectual. It has made it *spatial*, *bodily*, *relational*. To differ is to fracture a formation. It is to take what was one direction and split it into many backs.

Let's sit with this, because it changes everything about what comes next.

When we disagree, we tell ourselves a story: *this is about the truth. I am simply tracking the facts.* The word *ikhtalaftum* quietly tells a different story. It says: yes, there is content to your dispute — but there is also *motion*. There is the pull to walk off. There is the satisfaction of putting the other behind you. The form of the verb has built the relational damage right into the meaning of the word. You cannot say "we differed" in this Arabic without also saying "we turned from each other."

And here's what's remarkable — and I want you to file it away, because the ayah is going to pay it back at the very end. The cure for a problem named with the root of *turning away* is going to be a word built from the root of *turning back*.

The disease is in the grammar. So is the medicine.

## Element Two: "Of Anything" — The Door Left Wide Open

Read the opening clause again, slowly, and watch for a word that is doing more work than it looks:

> وَمَا ٱخْتَلَفْتُمْ فِيهِ مِن شَىْءٍ
> "And whatever you differ over — *of anything* —"

That phrase, *min shay'in* — "of any thing" — is the part I want to open.

Here's a small piece of Arabic to carry. There's a difference between saying "whatever you differ over" and saying "whatever you differ over, *of anything*." The first is general. The second is *deliberately, almost aggressively* general. *Shay'* is the broadest noun the language has — "thing," the word you reach for when you want to name no category at all. And it arrives here **indefinite** — *shay'in*, "a thing," "any thing" — with no adjective, no limit, no fence around it.

In Arabic, when you place an indefinite noun like this in a context of generality, you are not narrowing. You are throwing the doors open as wide as they will go. *Min shay'in* means: *and I do mean anything. Large or small. Sacred or trivial. The thing you'd go to war over and the thing you'd forget by morning.*

[PAUSE]

Now notice the absence — because this is where it gets striking.

Allah does *not* tell us *what* we differ over. He had every opportunity. He could have said "whatever you differ over **in your religion**." He could have said "**in matters of law**." He could have named the dispute. And in fact — listen to this — the classical commentators, reading the surrounding verses, *do* supply the content. Al-Baghawi and al-Qurtubi explain it as the matters of *religion* over which the disbelievers contended with the believers. Al-Muyassar reads it as "the affairs of your religion." They are right to draw that out from the context.

But the ayah *itself* does not say it. The ayah leaves the object **unspecified**, wide open, *min shay'in* — "of anything."

Why does that absence matter?

Because a fence would have let us out.

If the verse said "whatever you differ over *in religion*," then every one of us could quietly exempt the rest of our lives. *Ah — this is about theology. About the big doctrinal questions. My argument with my brother about the inheritance, my cold war with my coworker, the thing my spouse and I cannot stop circling back to — that's just life, that's not what this ayah is about.*

The open door closes that exit. *Min shay'in.* Of anything. The verse refuses to let you decide in advance which of your disagreements are too small to bring to God.

Stay with me here, because there's a deeper layer.

The unspecified object does something to the *scale* of God. If the ruling of one category of dispute rests with Allah, He is the Judge of a domain. But if the ruling of *anything you could possibly differ over* rests with Allah — every register, from the cosmic to the domestic — then He is not the Judge of a domain. He is the Judge of *the whole field of human difference itself.* There is no disagreement so small it falls beneath His ruling, and none so vast it exceeds it.

The grammar made Him bigger than any subject the verse could have named. By naming nothing, it gave Him everything.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: "I Have Trusted" and "I Keep Turning" — The Two Tenses of a Heart

Now we come to the part I have been waiting to show you. It is small. It is two verbs. And it is, I think, the most beautiful thing in the ayah.

The verse ends with the Prophet ﷺ declaring his posture toward Allah in two movements:

> عَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ وَإِلَيْهِ أُنِيبُ
> "Upon Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I keep turning back."

Two verbs. *Tawakkaltu* — I trusted. *Unību* — I turn. They feel like a matched pair, two ways of saying "I'm devoted to God." And if that's all you heard, you'd have heard something true. But you'd have walked past the wonder.

Let me give you the one piece of grammar that unlocks it.

Arabic verbs live in two basic tenses. The **perfect** — *fiʿl māḍī* — describes an action as **complete, accomplished, done**. A closed event. And the **imperfect** — *fiʿl muḍāriʿ* — describes an action as **ongoing, continuous, renewing**, happening and going on happening.

Now look — *really* look — at the two verbs.

*Tawakkaltu* — "I trusted" — is **perfect**. Completed. Done. A closed act.

*Unību* — "I turn back" — is **imperfect**. Ongoing. Continuous. Still happening.

[PAUSE]

He did not match them. He could have. *ʿAlayhi tawakkaltu wa ilayhi anabtu* — "I trusted, and I turned" — two completed acts, a tidy parallel. Or *ʿalayhi atawakkalu wa ilayhi unīb* — "I trust and I turn" — two ongoing ones. The verse had a perfectly symmetrical option in either direction.

It chose the *asymmetry*. One verb finished. One verb never finishing. And once you see it, you cannot un-see what it's teaching.

**Trust — tawakkul — is a decision you make once and it holds.**

*Tawakkaltu.* Past tense. Done. I have already settled the question of who carries my affairs. It is not relitigated every morning. It is a foundation poured and cured — *I placed my trust upon Him*, and that placement is complete. When the storm of disagreement breaks, the believer does not, in that moment, *decide* to trust God. He is *already* standing on trust he laid down long ago. The perfect tense makes tawakkul a *floor* — solid, finished, beneath your feet before the trouble ever came.

**Turning back — inābah — is a decision you make again, and again, and again, as long as you live.**

*Unību.* Imperfect. Unfinished. I turn, and I will go on turning. Because the heart does not return to God *once*. It drifts, and returns, drifts, and returns. Inābah is not a destination you arrive at; it is a *direction you keep choosing*. The imperfect tense makes it a motion that has no last instance this side of the grave. You turned back yesterday. That does not exempt you from turning back today.

Do you see the architecture of a spiritual life hidden in two tenses?

A **fixed foundation** and a **continuous return.** Trust is the bedrock — laid once, holding always. Turning back is the daily walk home — never completed, never allowed to stop. One is *settled*. One is *ongoing*. And a human being needs both, because we are creatures who require something that does not move *and* a way back from how far we wander.

[PAUSE]

And now — now — look at the root of that final word, and watch the whole ayah close like a hand.

*Unību* is from the root **ن-و-ب** (*n-w-b*) — *nāba, yanūbu* — to return, to come back round, to turn toward repeatedly. *Inābah*: the turning of the heart back to its Lord.

Remember where we started? The disease was *ikhtalaftum* — from **kh-l-f**, *to turn your back, to walk off behind your own position.* The whole ayah opened with human beings **turning away** from each other.

And it ends with the Prophet ﷺ **turning back** to God.

[PAUSE]

The motion that broke the community — turning away — is answered, at the very last word, by the motion that heals the soul — turning back. The verse is built on a single gesture, the turn, and it shows you the two directions it can go. You can turn your back on your brother, *ikhtilāf*. Or you can turn your face to your Lord, *inābah*. The Arabic has placed the wound and the cure at the two ends of the same verse, made of the same human capacity to *turn* — and dared you to notice which way you've been facing.

---

## Bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us.

*Ikhtalaftum* told us that disagreement is not abstract — it is bodies turning away, a formation fracturing. *Min shay'in* told us no dispute is too small or too large to fall under God's ruling — He is Judge of the entire field of human difference. And *tawakkaltu / unību* showed us the two postures a heart holds toward God — a trust laid down once, and a return renewed forever — and tied the cure for difference back to its disease through the simple act of turning.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we are actually supposed to *live* in a world that will not stop disagreeing with us.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: What To Do With the Argument You Cannot Win

Let me start where you live.

You have an argument right now. Maybe more than one. There is a person — a relative, a colleague, a friend who became less of one, a voice in a group chat — and between you and them sits a difference that will not close. You have made your case. They have made theirs. You have, perhaps, made your case *several times*, with increasing clarity and decreasing patience. And it has not moved them an inch.

And here is the thing about that unwon argument: **it lives in you.** You replay it in the shower. You compose the perfect reply on your drive home. You feel the small hot coal of it when their name comes up. The disagreement isn't sitting out *there* between you and them. It has moved *in*. It is renting space in your chest.

We live in a civilization that has built machines to maximize exactly this. Our entire information world is engineered to find the people who differ from us and put their worst sentence in front of our eyes, so that we will turn our backs on them with feeling. *Ikhtalaftum* — turning away — is now a business model. We are more practiced at it, more constantly invited into it, than any humans who have ever lived.

So what does the ayah say to do with the argument you cannot win?

It says something that sounds, at first, like surrender. *Fa-ḥukmuhu ilā 'llāh* — "its ruling rests with Allah."

Now — be careful here, because this is easy to misread, and the scholars guard us against the misreading. This is **not** "agree to disagree, nothing is true, who's to say." Listen to al-Qurtubi: this is the Messenger ﷺ telling the believers to say to those who contend with them, *its ruling is to Allah, not to you* — and Allah *has* ruled; He has made clear that the matters of religion are received from His clarification. And al-Muyassar adds the mechanism: referred to Allah means referred *to His Book and the Sunnah of His Messenger ﷺ.* This is not the dissolving of truth. It is the *relocation of the gavel.*

Here is the move, and it is one of the most freeing things in the religion.

[PAUSE]

The verb is *ḥukm* — ruling, verdict, judgment. The picture is a courtroom. And in every argument you have ever had, you have been doing three jobs at once: you've been the *advocate* making your case, the *jury* certain you're right, and — this is the exhausting one — you've appointed yourself the *judge* who must deliver the final verdict and *make the other person accept it.*

That third job is the one breaking you.

Because the advocate's job, you can do — present the truth as clearly and beautifully as you can. The believer is *not* told to go silent. But the *judge's* job — the work of rendering the final, binding verdict that settles the matter for all time and compels the other heart to bow — **that was never your job.** *Fa-ḥukmuhu ilā 'llāh.* The gavel is His. It was always His. You picked it up, and it has been crushing your hand ever since.

And notice the preposition, because it carries the whole psychology. Not *ḥukmuhu ʿinda 'llāh* — "its ruling is *with* Allah," sitting in His possession. But *ḥukmuhu* **ilā** *'llāh* — its ruling **to** Allah, *toward* Him. *Ilā* is the preposition of *direction and destination*. The matter doesn't just *belong* to Him — it *travels* to Him. You can take the whole hot, unresolved, exhausting weight of the dispute and *send it in His direction.* It has somewhere to go that is not your chest.

What changes about tomorrow?

Tomorrow you will have the argument again — or you'll be tempted to. And you get to ask yourself one question: *Which job am I doing right now?* Am I being the advocate — offering truth clearly, then leaving it? Or have I picked up the gavel again, trying to force a verdict that only Allah can render? The moment you notice the gavel in your hand, you can set it down. You can say the words of this ayah and *mean* them: *its ruling rests with You. I have made my case. The verdict travels to You now. I am taking my hand off it.*

That is not losing the argument. That is being released from the impossible job of ending it.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: A Floor Beneath You and a Road Home

Now I want to go to the most intimate place in the verse — those last two verbs — and ask why on earth they're here.

Think about how strange the structure is. The Prophet ﷺ has just been told how to handle the disbelievers' disputes: refer the ruling to Allah. That's a teaching about *other people*. And then, with no transition, the verse turns completely inward and personal: *That is Allah, my Lord; upon Him I have placed my trust, and to Him I keep turning back.*

Why does a verse about handling *them* end with the most private words about *Him*?

Because you cannot let go of the argument until something is holding you.

Let me put it the way it actually feels. The reason we *can't* hand the verdict to Allah — the reason we clutch the gavel — is fear. *If I stop fighting this, I lose. If I don't force them to admit I'm right, then maybe I'm not. If I let go of controlling the outcome, I'll fall.* The grip on the argument is really a grip on a ledge. We hold on because we are afraid of what happens if we don't.

And the ayah knows this. So before it asks you to let go, it shows you what's beneath you.

*ʿAlayhi tawakkaltu* — "upon Him I have placed my trust." Remember the tense: **perfect, completed, done.** This is the floor. Before this argument ever started, before this person ever wronged you, before the difference ever opened — the believer had *already* laid down his trust on Allah. It's not something you scramble to find in the middle of the conflict. It's the ground you were already standing on. *That* is why you can take your hand off the ledge: there is no fall. The floor was poured long ago. *Tawakkaltu.* Past tense. Settled. Beneath you, holding.

[PAUSE]

But a floor is not enough for a creature like us. Because we move. We drift. We pick the gavel back up an hour after we put it down. We forgive in the morning and seethe again by night. If all the verse gave us was a fixed foundation, we'd fail it constantly, because we are not fixed.

So the verse gives us the second verb. *Wa ilayhi unīb* — "and to Him I keep turning back." **Imperfect. Ongoing. Never finished.**

This is the road home, and it has no last mile. *Unību* admits — builds right into its grammar — that you *will* wander again. That you'll grip the ledge again, fight the argument again, turn your back again. And it does not condemn you for it. It simply keeps the road open. *I keep turning back.* Not "I turned back once and arrived." *I am, as a permanent condition of being alive, a person on his way back to God.*

Do you feel the mercy in that imperfect tense?

[PAUSE]

A religion that only offered tawakkul — the settled, completed foundation — would be a religion for the already-righteous, and would crush everyone else. A religion that only offered inābah — endless turning with no firm ground — would be exhausting, a treadmill with no floor. But Allah gives the Prophet ﷺ *both*, in one breath, in two tenses. **Stand on the trust you've already laid down. And keep walking back to Me every time you drift off it.**

And here is the universal struggle this answers. Every human heart is torn between two fears: the fear that there's nothing solid to stand on, and the fear that if I fall short of the solid thing, I'm finished. This ayah heals both in five Arabic words. There *is* something solid — *tawakkaltu*, the finished foundation. And falling short of it is not the end — *unību*, the road that's always open. The bedrock and the path home. Held together. Offered to you.

What changes about tomorrow?

Tomorrow you will drift. You'll know you have, when you feel that hot coal of the argument flare up again, or when you realize you've been carrying the gavel for a week. And the imperfect tense of *unību* is the gift waiting for you: you don't have to have *never left.* You just have to **turn.** Again. The turning is not a sign you've failed at the religion — *the turning is the religion.* The most beloved of God's servants are not the ones who never wandered. They are the *awwābūn*, the *munībūn* — the ones who keep coming back.

Make the turn small and frequent rather than large and rare. Each time you catch yourself gripping a difference too tightly, that *is* the moment of *inābah*. Don't wait for a grand return. Turn now, turn again tomorrow, and let the imperfect tense be true of you.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Revealed

Now that we've done the work, let me show you how the whole verse is built — because it has a shape, and the shape *is* the argument.

Watch the two great movements of direction, and notice they point the same way:

> ... فَحُكْمُهُۥٓ **إِلَى ٱللَّهِ** ... وَ**إِلَيْهِ** أُنِيبُ
> "...its ruling [travels] **to Allah** ... and **to Him** I keep turning back."

The same preposition, *ilā* — *toward, to, in the direction of* — appears twice, and both times the destination is Allah. First it is the **disputed matter** that travels to Him: *ḥukmuhu ilā 'llāh.* Then it is the **disputing self** that travels to Him: *ilayhi unīb.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The verse takes the *argument* and points it at God. Then it takes the *person who was arguing* and points *him* at God. The disagreement and the disagree-er end up facing the same way, headed for the same destination. The whole verse is a single act of *re-orientation* — taking everything that was turned outward, toward the opponent, toward the fight, toward the ledge — and turning it *upward and homeward*, toward the One.

And between those two *ilā*-movements sits the still center of the verse: *Dhālikumu 'llāhu rabbī* — "That is Allah, my Lord." The fixed point. The matter turns to Him. The self turns to Him. And He does not turn. He is the *Rabb* — the Lord, the Sustainer — around whom everything else revolves.

That is the proof, written in the structure. The cure for a world spinning off in a thousand directions (*ikhtalaftum*) is a single shared direction (*ilā 'llāh*).

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two sides.

The word *ikhtalaftum* showed us, in its very shape, that difference is *turning away.* The theme showed us how that turning-away lives in our chest and exhausts us. The unspecified *min shay'in* showed us that *nothing* is too small or large to fall under God's ruling. The theme showed us why that matters: it strips us of every excuse to keep the gavel. And the two tenses — *tawakkaltu* finished, *unību* unfinished — showed us, in pure grammar, the two things a heart needs: a foundation laid once, and a road home that never closes.

One sentence, three words, and an entire way of being in a contentious world.

## Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical.

1. Which of my arguments am I still holding the gavel on — clutching a verdict that was never mine to render?
2. When I "make my case," am I being the advocate, who speaks and lets go — or have I quietly appointed myself the judge, who must force the other heart to bow?
3. Is my *tawakkul* a foundation I laid down *before* the trouble — or am I scrambling to find trust only in the middle of the storm?
4. The most beloved servants are the ones who *keep turning back.* When I drift, do I treat the turning as a fresh start — or do I treat my drifting as proof that I've failed and stop turning at all?
5. In the directions my life is facing right now — toward whom, toward what — how many of them point *ilā 'llāh*?

## One-Sentence Distillation

When people turn away from each other over anything at all, hand the verdict to the One whose verdict it always was — and turn your own face back toward Him, standing on a trust already settled and walking a road home that never closes.

## The Closing Invitation

You will disagree with someone before this day is over. That is not a failure of your character; it is the condition of being one human among others. The invitation of this ayah is not to stop differing. It is to stop *carrying* it the way you've been carrying it.

Set down the gavel. You were never the judge. Make your case as the advocate, beautifully, and then send the matter in His direction — *ilā 'llāh* — and feel how much lighter your chest becomes when the verdict is no longer yours to force.

And then, having pointed the argument home, point *yourself* home too. Stand on the trust you laid down long ago. And when you notice you've wandered — and you will — don't despair at the distance. Just turn. *Unību.* Again. As many times as it takes. The road home was built without a last mile, on purpose, for someone exactly like you.

## A Du'a From These Words

> O Allah — *dhālika anta Rabbī* — You are my Lord, and the ruling of every matter rests with You. Take from my hands the gavel I was never meant to hold; let me make my case and then leave the verdict to You. Make my trust in You a foundation already poured, solid beneath me before the storms arrive — *ʿalayka tawakkaltu.* And keep open before me the road of return, so that every time I wander, I find my face turning back toward You — *wa ilayka unīb* — until the last of my turnings brings me home to You.

Ameen.
