# The One Who Answers You, Not Just Your Request

> وَيَسْتَجِيبُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّـٰلِحَـٰتِ وَيَزِيدُهُم مِّن فَضْلِهِۦ ۚ وَٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ

*wa-yastajību alladhīna āmanū wa-ʿamilū ṣ-ṣāliḥāti wa-yazīduhum min faḍlih, wal-kāfirūna lahum ʿadhābun shadīd*

**"And He answers those who believe and do righteous deeds, and increases for them from His bounty. But the disbelievers — for them is a severe punishment."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Words

Let me ask you to step into a feeling before we step into the ayah.

Think about what it is to send something out and wait. A message left on "delivered" but never "read." A voicemail into a void. A form submitted to an office, followed by silence — and then, weeks later, an automated reply that you can tell no human being ever touched. Think about being on hold. The music. The recorded voice that says *your call is important to us* in a tone that proves it isn't. Think about applying for something that mattered to you and never hearing back at all — not a yes, not a no, just nothing. The cruelty of nothing.

We are, perhaps more than any people who have ever lived, a civilization of the unanswered. We send and send and send. And the silence accumulates. And slowly, without quite noticing, a person can come to believe a quiet and devastating thing about themselves: *that they are the kind of thing the world does not respond to.*

Now hold that ache, and come to the world of this surah.

Surah Ash-Shura — the surah of *consultation*, of mutual response — is a surah about a relationship. Not a relationship of paperwork and processing, but of speech and answer. It is obsessed with the idea that Allah *communicates* — that revelation itself is Allah turning toward humanity and addressing it directly. The believers in Makkah hearing these words were a small, pressured, often frightened community. They prayed in the dark. They made duʿāʾ that, from the outside, looked entirely unanswered — the persecution continued, the boycott tightened, the powerful stayed powerful. And into that long silence, into that very specific ache of *am I being heard at all*, comes this ayah.

It comes right after Allah has described Himself, one verse earlier, as the One who **accepts repentance from His servants and pardons sins**. So the relationship is already established as warm, already turned toward us. And now He goes further.

Here is the hook. I want you to notice what this ayah does **not** say. It does not say "Allah answers the *prayers* of those who believe." Read it again. *He answers those who believe.* He answers **them**. The people. Not the request — the person.

Stay with that gap. We are going to live inside it. Because in that single missing word lives a difference so vast it could reorganize your entire relationship with your Lord.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: يَسْتَجِيبُ — The Verb That Goes Both Ways

#### First, the everyday concept

In any language, some verbs only point one direction. *I gave you the book* — the giving flows from me to you. But there is a special category of words that describe **two parties turning toward each other** — words where you genuinely cannot always tell, from the word alone, who is reaching toward whom. Think of the English word "embrace." If I say *they embraced*, who initiated it? The word doesn't say. It describes the meeting itself, not the direction.

Arabic has a verb form built for exactly this kind of mutual movement, and Allah chose it here.

#### Opening the word

The verb is **يَسْتَجِيبُ** (*yastajību*), from the root **ج-و-ب** (*j-w-b*), which carries the basic sense of *answering, responding, cutting through to give a reply*. But notice the shape it's been poured into. The bare root would give us *jāba*. What we have here is the tenth form — **اِسْتَفْعَلَ** (*istafʿala*) — *ist-a-jāba*. That added *ist-* at the front is doing something. In this form the verb gains the flavor of **seeking, requesting, answering-a-request**. It is not just "to reply." It is *to respond to one who has asked.* The asking is built into the word.

So before we even get to the meaning, the word itself contains a relationship: a caller and an answerer.

#### Let's sit with the ambiguity

Now here is something remarkable, and the classical scholars saw it clearly. Look at the words that follow: **يَسْتَجِيبُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟** — *yastajību alladhīna āmanū.*

Who is the one answering, and who is being answered?

There are two readings, both preserved in the tradition, and **both are true at once**.

In one reading — the one al-Jalalayn, al-Qurtubī, and al-Baghawī carry — *alladhīna* (those who believe) is the **object**. The sentence means: *And He — Allah — answers those who believe.* Allah is the responder. The believers are the ones whose call is received. Al-Qurtubī notes the grammar that supports this: *Allah answers those who believe* — He accepts the worship of the one who is sincere to Him in his heart and obeys Him with his body.

But in the second reading — the one al-Muyassar carries — *alladhīna* is the **subject**. The sentence means: *And those who believe answer their Lord* — they respond to what He calls them to, and they yield to Him.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? The verse describes a relationship of perfect mutual responsiveness, and the grammar itself **refuses to fix the direction**, because the direction goes both ways. The believer answers Allah's call — *labbayk* — and Allah answers the believer's call. The same word holds both motions. It is the linguistic embrace.

Here's what's remarkable: this is not a flaw in the verse, an ambiguity to be resolved and tidied away. It is the meaning. A relationship in which only one party responds is not a relationship; it is a transaction or a command. What this verse paints is the rarest thing in human experience — **two who turn toward each other**, and the turning is so mutual the language cannot separate it into a giver and a receiver.

#### And notice the tense

One more thing before we leave this word. *Yastajību* is in the imperfect — the form Arabic uses for the **ongoing, the continuous, the not-yet-finished**. He doesn't say *He answered* (past, sealed, done). He says, in effect, *He keeps answering, He goes on answering, He is answering now.* Whatever you sent up last night into what felt like silence — the verb is in the present tense. The answering has not stopped.

What this single word reveals: **the relationship between you and your Lord is not a one-way petition into a void. It is a living, two-directional, ongoing turning-toward.**

### Element Two: The Missing Word — He Answers *Them*, Not *It*

#### The concept first

Here is a principle we'll lean on throughout: in a text as precise as the Quran, **what is left unsaid is chosen as carefully as what is said.** When a sentence has a slot where you expect a word, and the slot is left empty, you must ask: why did Allah leave this open?

#### The gap

When you say in Arabic "He answered," the mind immediately reaches for an object: He answered *what*? Answered *the prayer*. Answered *the request*. The natural completion of *yastajību* is the thing that was asked.

Al-Jalalayn, reading the verse, supplies the implied object for us — *He answers them regarding what they ask.* Notice that the commentator had to **add** "what they ask," because **the verse itself does not contain it.**

Look again: *He answers those who believe.* The object of the answering — the request, the *thing asked for* — is simply **not in the verse.** Allah removed it.

#### Stay with me, because this is the whole heart of it

Imagine two ways a powerful person could relate to you.

In the first, you bring a request. They evaluate the request. If the request meets their criteria, the request is granted. You are, in this scene, essentially irrelevant — you are a delivery mechanism for a request. The transaction is between the request and the criteria. You could be replaced by anyone bringing the same paper. This is the office. This is the algorithm. This is the form that gets auto-replied to. *Your call is important to us.*

In the second, you come to someone who loves you, and you speak — and **they turn to face you.** Maybe they grant exactly what you asked. Maybe they grant something better. Maybe they hold you in the not-yet. But in every case, *the response is to you.* You are not a delivery mechanism. You are the one being answered.

[PAUSE]

When Allah deletes the object — when He says *He answers those who believe* instead of *He answers the prayers of those who believe* — He is telling you which kind of relationship this is. **You are not a request-bringer whose paperwork is being processed. You are a beloved who is being turned toward.** The answer is aimed at *you*, the whole of you, not merely at the item on your list.

This reframes every duʿāʾ you have ever made that you thought went unanswered. You were measuring whether the *item* arrived. But the verse was never about the item. It says He answers *you*. And there are a thousand ways to answer a person that are not the granting of their exact request — the delay that protects them, the "no" that is mercy, the something-else that they would have asked for had they known what He knows. The Prophet ﷺ taught, in a well-known narration, that no Muslim makes a duʿāʾ free of sin or severing of kin except that Allah gives him one of three: He hastens the answer, He stores it for the Hereafter, or He turns away from him an equivalent harm. Three forms of *being answered* — and only one of them is the item arriving on schedule.

What this missing word reveals: **the silence you mistook for being ignored was Him answering the person rather than the request.**

### Element Three: وَيَزِيدُهُم مِّن فَضْلِهِ — And He Increases Them From the Overflow

#### The concept first

Some gifts are owed and some are pure surplus. If you do a job and receive your wage, the wage is *owed* — it is justice, and there's a quiet dignity in it, but no tenderness. If, after paying you exactly what was agreed, your employer adds something with no reason but affection — that addition is a different *category* of thing. It is not justice. It is generosity. And the human heart knows the difference instantly.

This verse contains both categories, in sequence, and the seam between them is everything.

#### Opening the words

After "He answers those who believe and do righteous deeds," the verse adds: **وَيَزِيدُهُم مِّن فَضْلِهِ** — *wa-yazīduhum min faḍlih* — *and He increases them from His bounty.*

Two words to open here.

**يَزِيدُ** (*yazīdu*), from **ز-ي-د** (*z-y-d*): to increase, to add **over and above** what is already there. The word inherently means *more than the baseline.* You cannot "increase" something from zero — *ziyāda* presupposes there is already a full amount present, and then *something further* is laid on top.

So the structure is: first He **answers** them — that's the response their faith and deeds called for. Then, *on top of that*, He **increases**. The answer was the floor. The increase is what's stacked above the floor.

**فَضْل** (*faḍl*), from **ف-ض-ل** (*f-ḍ-l*): and here is the jewel. The root meaning of *faḍl* is **surplus, excess, what is left over and overflows** — the part that is *more than enough*, the remainder beyond the necessary. It is the opposite of a precisely measured wage. *Faḍl* is the overflow that spills past the rim of the cup.

#### Now feel the seam

Watch the logic of the verse with me.

The believers *believed* and *did righteous deeds* — they earned a response, and they get it: *He answers them.* So far, this could almost feel like justice — like the wage. You put in faith and works; the answer comes back. Fair.

But Allah does not stop at fair. He says: **and He increases them from His faḍl** — from His *overflow.* And note the tiny preposition: **مِّن** — *min* — "**from**" His bounty. Not "His bounty," but "**from** His bounty." A portion of it. A scoop from an ocean. Why "from"? Because the whole of His faḍl could never be received by a created thing; the rim of any cup we could hold would shatter. So He pours *from* it — a measure your vessel can survive — and even that measure is more than you asked, more than you earned, more than the answer required.

[PAUSE]

Here is the seam, and I want you to stand right on it. The **answer** is the response to your deeds. The **increase** is not. The increase is *min faḍlih* — from His overflow — and overflow, by definition, **is not owed to anyone.** You cannot earn a surplus; the moment it's earned it ceases to be surplus and becomes wage. The increase is the precise point where Allah stops dealing with you in justice and begins dealing with you in *generosity.*

And al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī preserve a beautiful glimpse of what this increase might be. In a narration from Ibn ʿAbbās, the *increase from His bounty* includes that **He grants the believers intercession for their brothers** — that the answer Allah gives you becomes so abundant it overflows onto people you love. Your own answered life becomes a bounty poured out on others. (This is reported as an *athar* — a saying of Ibn ʿAbbās — not as a statement of the Prophet ﷺ; hold it as the early community's reading, not a marfūʿ hadith.) Al-Baghawī adds simply: *over and above the reward of their deeds, as a pure favor from Him.* Over and above. *Tafaḍḍulan minh.* Out of sheer grace.

What these words reveal: **with your Lord, the answer is the beginning, not the end. He responds to what you earned, and then He keeps pouring — from a surplus that was never a wage and could never be earned.**

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge: From Words to Reality

Look at what these three discoveries have built.

A verb that **goes both ways** — a relationship of mutual turning. A **deleted object** — He answers *you*, not merely your request. And an **increase from overflow** — He starts in justice and ends in generosity.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about Arabic words — but about the shape of reality itself, and about the two ways a human life can end.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Dignity of Being Heard

Start where you live. You know the specific loneliness of the unanswered. Not the pain of being told "no" — that's clean, that's closure. The other thing. The *nothing.* The read receipt that never turns on. The sense, accumulating over years, that you are speaking into a universe that does not speak back.

And here is the quiet danger in it: a person who is never answered slowly stops believing they are *answerable.* They begin to suspect they are not the kind of being toward whom anyone turns. This is, beneath many of our anxieties, the deepest fear — not that we'll be refused, but that we don't *register*. That we're noise.

Now let the ayah illuminate it. *He answers those who believe* — present tense, ongoing — and He answers **them**, not their paperwork.

Let me build this in movements.

**First movement:** The very fact that Allah frames Himself as *the One who answers* is staggering when you consider Who is speaking. This is the Owner of the heavens and the earth, before Whom the galaxies are a footnote — and He defines part of His own relationship to you by His **responsiveness to you.** Elsewhere He says it even more intimately: *And when My servants ask you about Me — indeed I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls Me* (2:186). Notice He didn't delegate that verse to the Prophet to answer; He interrupts and answers in the first person, as if He could not bear to have a middleman stand between Him and the question of His nearness.

**Second movement:** Reflect on the *imperfect tense* again. He keeps answering. Which means the silences you've experienced were never the absence of an answer — they were a *form* of answer you couldn't yet read. The "no" was an answer. The "wait" was an answer. The "I'm giving you something you didn't know to ask for" was an answer. You were never not being answered. You were misreading the genre.

**Third movement — the paradox, and let it sit:** How can unanswered prayer and a God-who-answers both be true? Here is the resolution that doesn't dissolve the mystery: you were tracking the *item*. He was answering the *person.* A parent answers a child reaching for the knife — by not giving it. To the child, that is an unanswered request and even a betrayal. To the parent, it is the most loving possible *answer.* The gap between those two readings is exactly the gap between your knowledge and His. *He answers those who believe* — and faith, *īmān*, is precisely the trust that the answer aimed at *you* is wiser than the answer aimed at your *list.*

**Fourth movement — connect it to the universal struggle:** Every human being is, at bottom, asking one question with their whole life: *am I seen? does my existence land anywhere?* The atheist existentialists answered: no, the universe is indifferent, you are a brief noise in the dark. This verse is the categorical opposite. The structure of reality, it says, is *responsive*. At its foundation is not indifference but a Listener — One who answers the *caller*, turns toward the *person*, and is doing so right now.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Make one duʿāʾ this week not as a request submitted, but as a person speaking to Someone who is turned fully toward you and *will* answer you — in one of His three ways. And when the item doesn't arrive on your timeline, train yourself to say: *I have been answered. I am simply still reading the answer.* That single reframe can heal years of quiet abandonment.

### Theme Two: The Economy of Grace — and the Severed Sentence

Start where you live, again. We are formed by transaction. You get what you pay for. You earn what you work for. Calories in, calories out. The receipt, the wage, the score. We've internalized a universe of exact exchange, and it has a hidden cost: it makes us approach even God like a vending machine. Insert worship. Receive outcome. And when the outcome doesn't dispense, we kick the machine, or walk away convinced it's broken.

Now the ayah. *He answers them — and increases them from His overflow.* There is a category here the vending machine cannot contain: **the surplus that is not owed.** *Faḍl.* The overflow.

**First movement:** Realize how much of your life is already *faḍl* and not wage. The breath you didn't earn. The eyes reading these words. The morning you woke to. None of it was a wage for prior work — you didn't exist to earn the right to exist. It was all *min faḍlih* before you ever made a single duʿāʾ. The increase the verse promises is not a strange new thing; it is the *continuation* of how He has always dealt with you: starting past the line of what you're owed.

**Second movement:** Here the verse does something you might have rushed past. It introduces the believers with full, active, warm verbs: *He answers them, He increases them.* And then it turns to the disbelievers — and **watch the grammar change.** It does not say "and He punishes the disbelievers." It says: **وَٱلْكَـٰفِرُونَ لَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ** — *and the disbelievers — for them is a severe punishment.*

Notice what's missing. With the believers, **Allah is the subject of every verb** — *He* answers, *He* increases. He is grammatically *attached* to them, acting upon them, turned toward them. With the disbelievers, there is **no verb connecting Allah to them at all.** The sentence becomes flat, nominal: *for them, a punishment, severe.* Allah's name does not even appear in their clause. The active, turning-toward relationship — the whole engine of the first half — is simply **gone.**

[PAUSE]

Let that grammatical absence speak, because it is the most chilling thing in the verse. The punishment of *kufr* is announced first by what is *withheld at the level of the sentence itself*: the answering, the increase, the turning-toward — none of it is denied to them in so many words. It just **isn't there.** They fall outside the relationship. The verse doesn't even bother to connect God to them with a verb. The severance *is* the structure.

**Third movement — the two indefinites:** *ʿadhābun shadīd* — "a punishment, severe." Both words carry *tanwīn*, the indefinite ending — *a* punishment, *a* severe. In Arabic rhetoric this indefiniteness, here, is not vagueness; it is **magnification through the unspecified** — a punishment of a kind and a severity left undescribed precisely because describing it would *limit* it. And listen to the sound of the last word: **شَدِيدٌ** — *shadīd* — built on a doubled, hardened *dāl*, the *shadda* clamping down on the consonant. The word is *hard to say*; it makes the mouth press and hold. The sonic texture enacts the meaning. After the flowing, open vowels of *yastajību* and *faḍlih*, the verse closes on this clenched, heavy syllable. The mercy is melodic; the warning is a stone.

**Fourth movement — the asymmetry is the mercy:** Why does Allah place the believers' destiny *first* and at length, and the disbelievers' *last* and in a clipped half-line? Because the verse is **weighted toward hope.** The architecture itself is an invitation. He spends His words describing the answering and the overflow, and almost begrudges the words for the punishment — a brief, severe coda. The whole verse leans toward the open door. The severe ending is real, but it is positioned as the thing you walk *away from* by walking *toward* the answering. The grammar is not just describing two fates; it is **recommending one.**

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Stop approaching Allah like a vending machine that owes you the exact item for the exact coin. Approach Him as the One who *answers the person* and then *overflows past what's owed* — and let that dissolve both your entitlement (you are not owed; it is faḍl) and your despair (the relationship runs on His generosity, not your perfect ledger). And let the severed second half do its quiet work: the worst thing is not the punishment named in five words. The worst thing is to fall **outside the sentence where Allah is the One acting upon you.** Stay inside that sentence. That is what *īmān* and *ʿamal ṣāliḥ* purchase: not merely reward, but *being the object of His verbs.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now look at the whole verse from above, and see how its shape *is* its message.

It is built as two unequal halves, and the inequality is deliberate:

- **First half (long, warm, active):** *He answers* — *those who believe and do righteous deeds* — *and He increases them* — *from His bounty.* Allah is the relentless subject. Verb, verb. Relationship, then overflow.
- **Second half (short, cold, severed):** *And the disbelievers — for them — a punishment — severe.* No divine verb. No relationship. A nominal sentence that has been cut off from the One who was acting in the first half.

The verse *demonstrates*, in its very grammar, the two destinies it describes. To believe and act is to live **inside the clause where God is turned toward you, answering and overflowing.** To reject is to be exiled to the clause where He is not even the subject — left with a heavy, hard, undescribed weight, and no one's hand reaching down.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two sides.

The **two-way verb** and the **deleted object** were grammar's way of saying: *this is a relationship, and you — not your request — are its object.* That is the dignity of being heard.

The **increase from overflow** and the **severed second sentence** were grammar's way of saying: *He deals with His own in surplus, not wage — and the alternative is not a harsher wage but exile from the relationship altogether.* That is the economy of grace.

One God. One verse. Turned fully toward those who turn to Him — answering the person, overflowing past the owed — and the only tragedy is to stand outside that turning.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I made duʿāʾ and "nothing happened," was I tracking whether the *item* arrived — or whether *I* was answered? Which of His three answers might I have already received without recognizing it?
2. Do I approach my Lord like a vending machine that owes me an outcome, or like the One who answers the *person* and overflows past what I'm owed?
3. How much of my life right now is *wage*, and how much is *faḍl* — surplus I never earned? Have I ever thanked Him for the overflow as distinctly as I petition Him for the order?
4. The verse leans its whole weight toward hope and spends only five words on the warning. Does my own inner picture of Allah match that weighting — or have I inverted it?
5. What would change if I lived, today, as someone *inside the sentence where Allah is the One acting upon me*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

When you turn to Allah in faith and action, He does not merely grant your request — He turns toward *you*, answers *you*, then pours out more than you asked from an overflow that was never owed; and the only true loss is to fall outside the sentence where He is the One reaching toward you.

### The Closing Invitation

You have spent your life, perhaps, learning to expect silence — trained by a world of unanswered messages to suspect you are not the kind of being toward whom anyone turns. This verse invites you to lay that suspicion down. Not because every item on your list will arrive, but because the One who matters is, *in the present tense, right now,* turned fully toward you — answering the person you are, and waiting to overflow. You don't have to earn the overflow. You couldn't; it's *faḍl*. You only have to stay inside the clause: believe, and act on it. Step into the sentence where He is the subject and you are the one He answers. The door is the longer, warmer half of the verse, and it is held open on purpose.

### A Du'a From These Words

*O You who answer the one who calls — answer *me*, not merely my requests; turn toward the person I am, and let me learn to read Your answers in every form You send them. Keep me inside the sentence where You are the One acting upon me — believing, and acting on it — and pour over me from the overflow I could never earn, until even my answered life spills as mercy onto everyone I love.* Āmīn.
