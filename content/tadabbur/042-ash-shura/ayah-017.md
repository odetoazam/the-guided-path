# The Balance That Descended

## ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَٱلْمِيزَانَ ۗ وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ قَرِيبٌ

*Allāhu lladhī anzala l-kitāba bi-l-ḥaqqi wa-l-mīzān. Wa mā yudrīka laʿalla s-sāʿata qarīb.*

**"Allah is the One Who sent down the Book in truth, and the Balance. And what will make you realize — perhaps the Hour is near."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me ask you to do something before we open this verse.

Think about the word *justice*. Sit with it for a moment. Where do you imagine it comes from?

If you are a person of the modern world — and you are — you have absorbed an answer so deeply you've never had to say it out loud. **Justice is something we build.** We argue it into existence. We legislate it. We march for it. We draft it in conventions and amend it across centuries. Justice, in the story our age tells, is a human achievement — fragile, hard-won, always under construction, always one election or one war away from collapse. It is *ours*. We made it. And because we made it, we can lose it.

Hold that picture. Because the verse we are about to open says something that quietly contradicts it — and most people read straight past the contradiction without noticing it was there.

Now place yourself in Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ is reciting a surah whose very name — *Ash-Shura* — means mutual consultation, the just ordering of affairs. Around him are men who dispute. They argue about this Book. *Why a Book? Why him? Why now?* And running underneath every one of their objections is a deeper unease they will not name: a fear of being held to account. A discomfort with the idea that there might be a final reckoning — a day when the scales come out and nothing is hidden.

To people standing in exactly that posture — disputing the Book, secretly unsettled by the thought of a Hour — Allah does not give a long argument. He gives them one sentence. And in that sentence He tells them *what He actually sent down* — and then, mid-breath, He turns and asks a question that no human being on earth can answer.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. We are taught that justice is built from below, by us, slowly, upward. This verse says it was **sent down** — *anzala* — from above. The same verb used for the Book is used for the Balance. Revelation descended. And justice descended *with it*, in the same motion, as the same kind of thing.

And then, before you can even absorb that, the verse does something almost vertiginous. It turns to the Prophet ﷺ himself — the most knowing of creation — and says: *and what would even let you know? Perhaps the Hour is near.*

Why would a verse about the **Balance** suddenly swerve into the **timing of the Hour**? What does justice descending have to do with not knowing when the end will come?

Stay with me. The answer is the whole point of the verse — and it is hiding in the seam between its two halves.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Anzala* — The Direction Justice Travels

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we use it, because everything in this first movement turns on it.

Arabic, like every language, has ordinary verbs and it has *shaped* verbs — verbs bent into a form that adds a layer of meaning. One of the most important of these is what grammarians call **Form IV**. You take a simple action and you reshape it so that it now means *to cause that action to happen to something else.*

Here is the everyday version. In Arabic, *nazala* (نَزَلَ) means "it came down" — rain comes down, a guest comes down to stay. Simple. Intransitive. It just descends. But reshape it into Form IV and you get *anzala* (أَنزَلَ) — **to send it down, to cause it to descend, to bring it down deliberately from a height by your own act.**

The difference matters. *Nazala* is something falling. *Anzala* is **someone causing it to fall, on purpose, from above.** There is an agent. There is a direction. There is intention.

[PAUSE]

Now open the verse:

**ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلَ ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ — "Allah is the One Who *sent down* the Book."**

Good. That, we expect. The Book — the Qur'an, and as al-Qurtubi and al-Muyassar note, the revealed Books in general — descended. Revelation comes from above. Familiar territory.

But watch what Allah attaches to that same verb. He does not start a new sentence. He does not give the next noun its own verb. He simply continues, and the next thing rides on *anzala* exactly as the Book did:

**ٱلْكِتَـٰبَ ... وَٱلْمِيزَانَ — "the Book ... *and the Balance.*"**

Grammatically, this is unmistakable. *Al-mīzān* — the Balance — is coordinated directly with *al-kitāb* as a second object of the one verb *anzala*. There is no separate sending. There is no lesser sending. **Whatever it means for the Book to be "sent down," it means in precisely the same way for the Balance.**

So now we have to ask the question the grammar is forcing on us. What *is* this Balance?

Notice something. The mufassirūn here are not reaching for anything exotic. Ibn ʿAbbās, and as al-Qurtubi tells us *"and most of the commentators,"* say plainly: **al-mīzān is al-ʿadl — justice.** Qatāda, Mujāhid, and Muqātil, as al-Baghawi records, say the same: justice is *called* a balance because the balance — the physical weighing-scale — *is the instrument of fairness and equity.* It is the tool that does not lie. You put the goods on one side and the weight on the other and the beam settles where the truth is, indifferent to who is watching, indifferent to who is powerful.

Stay with that image, because it is doing real work. A scale has no opinion. It cannot be flattered. It does not care if the man placing the goods is a king or a beggar — it gives the same answer to both. That is *why* justice borrows its name. Justice, at its purest, is the human refusal to let the beam be tilted by power.

And now — **here is what is remarkable** — the verse tells us this Balance was *sent down.* Form IV. From above. By Allah. With intention.

[PAUSE]

Go back to where we started. We said the modern story is that justice is something we *build* — upward, from below, by argument and struggle. The verb in this verse points in the opposite direction. *Anzala.* **Downward. From above. Given, not constructed.**

This does not mean human beings have no role in justice — we are the ones who must *carry* the scale and *use* it. But the *standard itself* — the true measure, the weight against which the goods are judged — that did not originate with us. We did not vote it into being. We received it. The Book tells you what is true; the Balance tells you how to *weigh* it. And both came down the same staircase.

Think of what this does to a person standing in a corrupt court, or living under a tyrant, or simply watching the powerful bend the rules and get away with it. The despairing thought is always: *justice is just a story we tell, and the strong have stopped believing it.* But if the Balance was **sent down** — if it has the same origin as the Book itself — then injustice is not the absence of a thing that was never real. It is the **tilting of a beam that Allah Himself set level.** The standard does not evaporate when humans abandon it. It stands. It was sent. And a sent thing has a Sender who has not forgotten it.

What this single coordination — *the Book and the Balance, on one verb* — reveals is this: **truth and justice are not two projects. They are one descent.** To receive revelation and ignore justice is to have caught only half of what fell.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Two: *Qarīb*, Not *Qarība* — The Grammar That Refuses to Settle

Now we move to something so small that most translations cannot even show it to you. It lives entirely in the Arabic, in a single missing letter.

Let me teach the concept first.

In Arabic, words have gender, and adjectives are supposed to agree with the nouns they describe. If a noun is feminine, its adjective normally takes a feminine ending — usually a *tā' marbūṭa*, that little rounded "-ah" sound at the end. *Kabīr* (big, masculine) becomes *kabīra* (big, feminine). Agreement. Tidy. Expected.

Now look at the end of our verse:

**لَعَلَّ ٱلسَّاعَةَ قَرِيبٌ — "perhaps the Hour is near."**

*As-sāʿah* — the Hour — is a **feminine** noun. By the ordinary rule, "near" describing it should be *qarība* (قَرِيبَة), with that feminine ending. But that is not what the verse says. It says **qarīb** (قَرِيبٌ) — the *masculine* form. The expected feminine ending is simply… not there.

This is not a slip. The Qur'an does not slip. And the early grammarians noticed it immediately and stopped to explain it.

Al-Baghawi gives us the answer directly: *"He did not say qarība, because its feminine is not a real feminine"* — meaning *as-sāʿah* is grammatically feminine but not feminine in essence, the way a woman or a mother is — *"and its underlying meaning is al-waqt"* — **the time, the moment.** Al-Kisā'ī, he reports, fills it in the same way: the meaning is *"its coming is near"* — *ityānuhā qarīb* — and *coming* (ityān) is masculine.

Let me slow down, because something quietly profound is happening in this grammatical "irregularity."

[PAUSE]

The verse could have said *qarība* and described **the Hour** as a fixed, namable thing — a noun you could point to, schedule, circle on a calendar. Instead, by dropping the feminine ending, the language reaches *past* the noun "the Hour" to the thing underneath it: **the moment of its arrival, its coming, its *when*.** The grammar refuses to let "the Hour" sit there as a settled object. It keeps pointing at the *timing* — and timing is exactly the thing that has been hidden from us.

Do you feel it? The form of the word enacts its meaning. A feminine ending would have *fixed* the Hour into a definite shape. Its absence keeps it **unfixed, unscheduled, hovering** — near, but uncircled. The word will not let you pin it down, *because the reality it names cannot be pinned down.* You are not allowed to know the date. And the very grammar of the sentence will not give you a noun stable enough to hold.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* working at the level of a single dropped letter: a physical, audible feature of the word — the missing "-ah" — points to an unseen reality: the deliberately hidden timing of the End.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: *Wa Mā Yudrīka* — The Question Asked of the One Who Knows Most

We come to the strangest and most tender moment in the verse. To feel it, we have to notice *who is being spoken to* — and that requires understanding a feature of Qur'anic speech the scholars call **iltifāt**: the sudden turn.

The verse opens in the third person, describing Allah from a distance: *"Allah is the One Who sent down..."* We are listening to a statement *about* Him. And then, without warning, the camera spins around and the verse speaks directly to a *you*:

**وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ — "And what will make *you* know?"**

The "you" is the Prophet ﷺ. The speech turns from describing Allah to addressing His Messenger face to face. That turn alone should make us sit up: the most intimate possible audience is being addressed.

Now the verb. *Yudrīka* is Form IV again — that same causative shape. The simple root *darā* (دَرَى) means "to know, to be aware." Reshape it to Form IV, *adrā*, and it means **"to make someone know, to give someone awareness."** So *mā yudrīka* is not asking "what do you know?" It is asking something sharper: **"What could possibly *make* you know? What would even bring this knowledge to you?"**

And the implied answer is devastating in its gentleness: **nothing would.** Nothing in creation can hand you this knowledge — because it has not been given, not even to you, O Messenger of Allah.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the weight of this. The man being addressed is the one to whom the Book is descending. The one Allah chose above all creation. The one who, in other verses, is taken on the Night Journey through the heavens. And about the *timing of the Hour*, Allah turns to *him* and says: *what would even let you know?*

We learn elsewhere in the Qur'an (7:187) that when they asked the Prophet ﷺ directly about the Hour, he was commanded to say its knowledge is with Allah alone. Here, in Ash-Shura, that same fence is built — but built *lovingly*, as a turning-toward rather than a turning-away.

And then comes the word that changes the entire emotional temperature of the verse: **لَعَلَّ — laʿalla. "Perhaps."**

Let me show you something the grammarians caught here too. Al-Jalālayn notes that *laʿalla* in this position **suspends the verb *yudrīka* from doing its normal grammatical work** — the verb would ordinarily demand two objects, but instead the whole clause that follows, *laʿalla s-sāʿata qarīb*, steps in and fills that space. In plain terms: the *content* of what you cannot be made to know is itself **"perhaps the Hour is near."**

So the structure says: *You cannot be made to know — and the very thing you cannot be made to know is this: maybe it's almost here.*

[PAUSE]

*Laʿalla* — "perhaps" — is a word of expectation, of leaning-toward, of hope and anticipation held open. Not "the Hour *will* be near," which would be a threat. Not "the Hour is far," which would be a relief. But **"perhaps it is near"** — a door left deliberately ajar.

Why "perhaps" and not certainty either way? Because certainty in *either* direction would kill the very thing the verse is trying to keep alive in you. If you *knew* it was far, you would relax your grip on the Balance. If you *knew* the exact date, you would game it — repent the night before and sin freely until then. But *"perhaps it is near"* — that you cannot escape. It will not let you postpone and it will not let you despair. It keeps you, every single day, in the posture of someone who might be weighed *tonight*.

What this turn reveals is something we almost never let ourselves feel: **not-knowing is itself a mercy and a discipline.** The hidden Hour is not Allah withholding out of distance. It is Allah keeping you awake.

---

### The Bridge

Look at what these three small features have shown us. *Anzala* told us the Balance **came down** — justice is given, not invented. The dropped feminine in *qarīb* told us the Hour's timing is **deliberately unfixed** — it will not be pinned. And *mā yudrīka* with *laʿalla* told us that even the Prophet ﷺ is kept in **"perhaps,"** so that the not-knowing stays alive in the heart.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how a human being is meant to *live*.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Scale You Carry Now Is the Scale You Will Face Then

Let me start where you actually live.

You have, right now, an internal sense of fairness. You feel it sting when you are wronged. You feel it — if you are honest — twinge when you cut a corner against someone who trusted you. Psychologists call it many things: moral intuition, the sense of equity, the conscience. We treat it as a private instrument, a personal gauge that we calibrate ourselves, that some people have more of and some less, that culture shapes and circumstance erodes.

And here is the modern anxiety about it: *what if my scale is just mine?* What if my sense of justice is only a story my upbringing told me, with no more authority than anyone else's story? In a relativistic age, this is the quiet terror — that the Balance in my chest weighs nothing, points to nothing, answers to no one.

The verse walks straight into that terror and reorders it.

It says: there is a Balance, and it was **sent down.** Your inner sense of fairness is not a private invention — it is the *echo*, in you, of a measure that has an origin outside you. When you feel that something is unjust, you are not merely expressing a preference. You are feeling the tug of a beam that Allah set level. The Balance is real because its Sender is real.

[PAUSE]

But the verse does not stop at *"justice was sent down."* Watch where it goes next. The instant it has named the Balance, it pivots — **"and what will make you know, perhaps the Hour is near."**

Why? Because the *mīzān* — the Balance — is the word the Qur'an uses for **both** scales.

There is the Balance sent down *now*: the standard you are meant to weigh your life by, day after day. And there is the Balance set up *then* — on the Day the Qur'an calls the Day of Reckoning, where, as Surah Al-Anbiyā' tells us (21:47), *the just scales* are laid down and *not a soul is wronged by even the weight of a mustard seed.* Same word. Same root, *w-z-n.* **One Balance, two appointments.**

Do you see what the verse has done by placing them side by side? It has told you that **the scale you carry now and the scale you will stand before then are the same instrument.** The fairness you practice in a private transaction no one sees, the honesty in how you weigh a colleague's faults against their good, the corner you do or do not cut — these are not rehearsals for some *other* test. They are *already on the scale.* The weighing has begun. The Day of Judgment is not when the weighing starts; it is when the reading is announced.

[PAUSE]

And now the pivot to the Hour makes complete sense. *Why* does a verse about the Balance suddenly mention that the Hour might be near? Because **the nearness of the Hour is what keeps the Balance honest.**

Imagine you knew, with certainty, that the reckoning was a thousand years away. The Balance in your chest would slacken. *Plenty of time. Tilt the beam a little today; I'll level it later.* But "perhaps the Hour is near" removes the *later.* It collapses the distance between the scale you carry and the scale you'll face until they are almost touching. It says: *weigh truly now, because "now" may be all the runway you have.*

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the specific application. Take one transaction tomorrow that no one will audit — a moment where you could shade the truth slightly in your favor and never be caught. A price you quote. A fault you could hide. An hour of work you could fudge. In that exact moment, remember: the Balance was *sent down*, it is real, and *perhaps the Hour is near.* Then level the beam — not because someone is watching, but because the One who sent the scale never stopped watching, and the reading may be read sooner than you think.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: Living in the "Perhaps" — The Mercy of the Hidden Date

Let me begin, again, with something you already know in your bones.

Human beings are *terrible* with deadlines they can't see and wonderful at exploiting deadlines they can. Tell a student the exam is in three months, and the studying begins in the final week. Tell a person they have decades to get their life right, and the getting-right is perpetually deferred to a tomorrow that keeps receding. We are procrastinating creatures. We bank on time we have not been promised. We live, almost all of us, as if the end is always *later* — later than today, certainly; later than this year; comfortably, vaguely later.

Now hear the verse speak into exactly this tendency: **wa mā yudrīka laʿalla s-sāʿata qarīb** — *"and what would even let you know — perhaps the Hour is near."*

Notice what Allah did *not* do. He did not tell us the date. He could have. And consider, for a moment, the two alternatives He withheld, and why each would have been a kind of ruin.

If He had told us the Hour was **far** — say, ten thousand years off — He would have handed every procrastinating heart the permission it craves. We would have exhaled. We would have postponed repentance to a generation that was not ours. The Balance would have gone slack across all of humanity at once.

And if He had told us the **exact date**, He would have handed us something worse: the ability to *game* it. To sin freely with one eye on the calendar and schedule our repentance for the final week, like the student cramming before the exam. Our worship would have become a deadline-driven panic rather than a way of life. Love would have curdled into mere timing.

[PAUSE]

So instead He gave us *"perhaps."* **Laʿalla.**

This single word is one of the most merciful pieces of architecture in the human condition, and we rarely recognize it as mercy. The hidden date is not Allah being withholding. It is Allah refusing to let us cheat — and refusing to let us despair.

Think about how *"perhaps it is near"* actually functions in a living heart. It cannot be exploited in either direction. You cannot relax, because it might be tonight. You cannot panic-and-postpone, because there is no date to postpone *to*. The only response that fits "perhaps it is near" is to live, every ordinary day, in a state of quiet readiness — to keep your scale level *now*, to settle your debts *now*, to seek forgiveness *now*, not because the alarm has sounded but because you can no longer pretend you know when it won't.

[PAUSE]

And here is the tenderness I do not want us to miss. This "perhaps" was first spoken to the **Prophet ﷺ himself** — *mā yudrīka*, what would even let *you* know. The most beloved of creation was not exempted from living inside the "perhaps." He, too, was kept in readiness rather than given the date. So when *you* feel the discomfort of not knowing — the slight existential vertigo of a deadline you cannot see — understand that you are standing exactly where the best of creation stood. The not-knowing is not a punishment reserved for the weak in faith. It is the very air the Prophet ﷺ breathed.

There is a strand of narration, reported by al-Baghawi from Muqātil — and we should hold it as *one* reported occasion, not as the single settled cause — that the Prophet ﷺ mentioned the Hour while some of the mushrikīn were present, and they responded with mockery and disbelief. If that is the setting, then notice the genius of the response: to the mockers, the verse does not offer proof of the date — it offers the *"perhaps."* You laugh because you assume it is far. *And what would let you know?* Your laughter is built on a knowledge you do not possess.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the application. Tonight, before you sleep, do one thing as though it might be the last instance of it you are given — one prayer prayed as if it were your final prayer, one apology you have been deferring, one act of *settling* a thing left unlevel. Not in panic. In the quiet posture of *"perhaps."* And then wake tomorrow and live the ordinary day — but live it as a person who has stopped pretending to know how much runway is left.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole verse, because its two halves were never two subjects. They were one.

> **First half:** Allah sent down the Book *in truth*, and the Balance.
> **Second half:** And what would let you know — perhaps the Hour is near.

The first half gives you the **standard**: revelation to know the truth by, and a Balance to weigh your life by — both descended, both given, both real. The second half gives you the **urgency**: the moment of reckoning is hidden, unfixed, and possibly *close*. And between them sits the hinge word, *al-mīzān* — the Balance — which is the **same instrument** in both halves: the scale you carry now, and the scale you will stand before then.

The verse is built like the scale it describes. On one pan: *the Book and the Balance, sent down.* On the other: *the Hour, perhaps near.* And the beam between them is your life, right now, being weighed.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight approached from two directions.

*Anzala* showed us, in grammar, that justice **descends** — and the theme showed us, in living, that the scale in your chest answers to a Sender. The dropped feminine in *qarīb* showed us, in a single missing letter, that the Hour's timing is **deliberately unfixed** — and the theme showed us that this unfixing is precisely the mercy that keeps us honest. *Mā yudrīka* with *laʿalla* showed us, in syntax, that even the Prophet ﷺ is kept in "**perhaps**" — and the theme showed us that "perhaps it is near" is the only thought that can neither be relaxed nor gamed.

One verse. The Balance was sent down, and the Hour is hidden — *so that you would weigh truly, every day, with no idea how many days are left.*

## Questions to Carry

1. When I feel that something is *unjust*, do I treat that feeling as a private preference — or as the tug of a Balance that was sent down and still stands?
2. Where in my life is the beam slightly tilted in my own favor, in a place no human being will ever audit?
3. If I knew the Hour were ten thousand years away, what would I let myself do today that I would never do if I knew it were tonight? And what does that gap reveal about where my scale already sits?
4. Have I been deferring something — an apology, a repentance, a settling — to a "later" whose existence I have no right to assume?
5. The Prophet ﷺ himself was kept in "*perhaps*." Can I make peace with not knowing — not as anxiety, but as the air the best of creation breathed?

## The One-Sentence Distillation

**Allah sent down both the Book and the Balance so that you would live justly now — and hid the Hour's timing behind "perhaps it is near" so that the not-knowing would keep your scale honest every single day.**

## The Closing Invitation

You do not need to live in fear. The verse does not ask for fear. It asks for *level*. It invites you to stop pretending two things: that justice is a story you can rewrite when it's inconvenient, and that the reckoning is a deadline safely far away. Drop both pretenses, and a strange lightness arrives — the lightness of a person who has stopped gaming the scale, who weighs truly because the beam was set level by the One who is near, and whose accounting may be read sooner than the heart dares to admit. Live in the "perhaps." It is the most honest place there is.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You who sent down the Book in truth and the Balance — keep my scale level in the moments no one sees, and let my private weighing match the weighing I will face before You. You hid the Hour in mercy; let "perhaps it is near" stay awake in my heart, so that I neither presume on time I was never promised nor despair of the door You left open. Make me ready, this ordinary day, for the day I cannot see.* Āmīn.
