# The Two Harvests
### Surah Ash-Shura · Ayah 20

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen to it first in the Arabic. Let the sound carry before the meaning settles.

> **مَن كَانَ يُرِيدُ حَرْثَ ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ نَزِدْ لَهُۥ فِى حَرْثِهِۦ ۖ وَمَن كَانَ يُرِيدُ حَرْثَ ٱلدُّنْيَا نُؤْتِهِۦ مِنْهَا وَمَا لَهُۥ فِى ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ مِن نَّصِيبٍ**

*Man kāna yurīdu ḥartha l-ākhirati nazid lahu fī ḥarthih — wa man kāna yurīdu ḥartha d-dunyā nu'tihi minhā, wa mā lahu fī l-ākhirati min naṣīb.*

"Whoever desires the **harvest** of the Hereafter — We increase for him in his harvest. And whoever desires the **harvest** of this world — We give him something of it, but he has no share in the Hereafter."

### Rebuild the world

You need to stand for a moment where the first listeners stood.

Mecca. A trading town in a valley with almost no agriculture of its own. The people who heard these words were not farmers — they were merchants. Their entire mental world ran on a single logic: capital in, profit out. You put a sum into a caravan, the caravan crossed the desert to Syria or Yemen, and months later it came back, and you counted what you had made. Everything was measured. Everything was apportioned. A good year was a known quantity.

And these merchants were anxious people. The Quran kept asking them to give, to spend, to look past the caravan toward something they could not see. And the deepest fear underneath all their resistance was a quiet, reasonable, human fear: *If I pour myself into this unseen thing you keep talking about, I will lose the seen thing I already have. If I invest in the Hereafter, I forfeit the world.*

That is the fear this ayah was sent to dismantle.

But notice what Allah does **not** do. He does not argue with them as merchants. He does not say "whoever wants the *profit* of the Hereafter," "whoever wants the *return* of the Hereafter." He reaches past the marketplace entirely and hands them a word from the world of soil and rain and patient waiting. He calls it **ḥarth** — a *cultivation*. A *sowing*.

And the moment you notice that He could have used a hundred commercial words and chose a farming word instead — the whole ayah cracks open.

### The hook

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows.

When Allah describes the person who lives for this world, He says: *We give him something of it.* A clean transaction. A payout.

But when He describes the person who lives for the Hereafter, He does **not** say "We give him his harvest." He says something stranger. He says, **"We increase for him in his harvest."**

In *his* harvest. As though the harvest were already his — and Allah is doing something *to* it.

Why does one person get *given*, and the other person get *increased*? And why does Allah, who has just divided humanity into exactly two groups, slam one door shut with total finality — *no share at all* — while leaving the other door conspicuously, deliberately, mercifully open?

[PAUSE]

Let's go slowly. There is more here than a verse about wanting Heaven.

# PART 1 — THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: حَرْث — He Did Not Say "Harvest." He Said "Cultivation."

Before we touch the word, let me put an ordinary picture in front of you.

Imagine two people you know. One says, "I want a harvest." The other says, "I am going out to *farm*." Listen to the difference. The first person wants an outcome — full baskets, a loaded table, the *result*. The second person has named not a result but a *life*: rising before dawn, breaking the soil, dropping seed into the dark, covering it over, walking away, and waiting through a season he does not control, for a plant he cannot see growing.

In English our translations almost always render this word "harvest," and that pulls us toward the first person — the one who wants the *result*. But the Arabic does the opposite.

Now the word itself.

The word is **حَرْث** — *ḥarth* — from the root **ḥā-rā-thā (ح ر ث)**. Open any classical lexicon — Lisān al-ʿArab, the Mufradāt of al-Rāghib — and you find that the root does not primarily mean the gathered crop at the end. It means **the tilling of the land, the casting of seed into the earth, the labor of cultivation.** *Al-ḥarth: ilqā'u l-badhri fī l-arḍ* — the throwing of the seed into the ground. It is the *working* of a field, not the *reaping* of one.

And here is what the mufassirūn do with it — and they are in remarkable agreement. **Al-Qurṭubī** says plainly: *al-ḥarth — al-ʿamal wa-l-kasb.* The ḥarth is the *deed* and the *earning*. **Al-Baghawī** says the same: *al-ḥarth fī l-lughah: al-kasb* — in the language, ḥarth means acquisition, what you work for. **Al-Jalālayn** glosses *ḥartha l-ākhirah* as "*kasbahā*" — the earning of the Hereafter. And **al-Muyassar** expands it beautifully: the one who *wants the ḥarth of the Hereafter* is the one who "fulfilled the rights of Allah and spent in calling people to the religion."

So sit with what Allah has actually said. He did not say "whoever wants Paradise." He did not say "whoever wants reward." He said: **whoever wants the *cultivation* of the Hereafter.**

Do you feel the shift? You cannot *want* a harvest the way you want a thing on a shelf. **A harvest is not something you take. It is something you grow.** And growing has a shape: you plant something small, you bury it where no one can see it, you cannot rush it, and the connection between your labor and the yield is real but hidden and delayed.

Let's sit with this, because it reorders everything.

When Allah names the goal a *ḥarth*, He is telling you that the Hereafter is not a destination you arrive at. It is a *field you are sowing right now.* Every act of worship is a seed going into dark soil. You will not see it sprout today. You bury it — a prayer no one witnessed, a charity you told no one about, a tongue you held back when it ached to speak — and you walk away, and it disappears from view, and *that disappearance is not loss. It is planting.*

Notice something. The farmer is never disturbed that his seed vanished into the ground. He would be alarmed if it *didn't* — a seed sitting on top of the soil is a seed that will rot or be eaten. The seed has to go down into the dark to do its work. [PAUSE]

How much of your best worship has gone down into the dark — unseen, unthanked, seemingly swallowed by the earth of your ordinary days — and a quiet voice told you it was wasted?

The word *ḥarth* is Allah correcting that voice. **It is not wasted. It is planted.** And what is planted in this soil does not stay buried.

Here is what is remarkable. The word *ḥarth* contains, built into its very meaning, the two things the anxious heart most needs and most lacks: **patience with delay**, and **trust in the unseen.** A farmer who cannot tolerate delay and cannot trust what he can't see is not a farmer at all. By naming your striving a *cultivation*, Allah has already told you the two muscles this path will build in you.

That is what this single word reveals: **the Hereafter is not earned like a wage; it is grown like a crop — and your today is the soil.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: كَانَ يُرِيدُ — The Settled Aim of the Heart, and the Two Verbs That Answer It

Now look at *how* the wanting is described, because Arabic is doing something precise that English flattens.

First, a small grammatical thing that turns out to be enormous. The phrase is **مَن كَانَ يُرِيدُ** — *man kāna yurīdu.* Two verbs stacked together. **كَانَ** (*kāna*) — "was," from the root *k-w-n* — is in the perfect tense, the tense of settled, completed reality. And **يُرِيدُ** (*yurīdu*) — "wants/desires," from the root *r-w-d* — is in the imperfect, the tense of ongoing, continuous action.

When Arabic puts *kāna* in front of an imperfect verb like this, it does not mean a single past wish. It means **a habitual, continuous, settled state.** Not "whoever wanted, once." Rather: **whoever's standing, ongoing, characteristic orientation is to want this.**

Teach yourself the difference with an everyday example. There is a difference between the sentence "he helped someone" and the sentence "he *was* a helper" — *kāna yusāʿid.* The first is an event. The second is a *character.* The first describes what you did on Tuesday. The second describes who you *are* across all your Tuesdays.

So Allah is not asking what you *wished for* in some bright moment of resolve. We all have those. The new-year version of ourselves wants the Hereafter intensely — for an afternoon. Allah is asking about the **kāna yurīdu** — the wanting that has hardened into a disposition, the default direction your heart drifts when you stop steering it. Where does your wanting *settle* when you are tired, distracted, unobserved? That is your *kāna yurīdu.* That is the seed actually going into the ground.

[PAUSE]

This is mercy, by the way, not severity. It means a single weak moment does not define your field. And it means a single grand gesture does not, either. What is being planted is the *standing orientation* — and a standing orientation is something you can quietly turn, degree by degree, with no audience and no announcement.

Now — the two verbs Allah uses to *respond* to these two orientations. This is the hinge of the whole ayah.

To the one whose heart is set on the Hereafter, Allah says: **نَزِدْ لَهُۥ فِى حَرْثِهِ** — *nazid lahu fī ḥarthih* — "We **increase** for him in his harvest." The verb is **نَزِدْ**, from the root *z-y-d* — to *increase*, to *multiply*, to *add more on top of.*

To the one whose heart is set on this world, Allah says: **نُؤْتِهِ مِنْهَا** — *nu'tihi minhā* — "We **give** him of it." The verb is **نُؤْتِهِ**, from the root *a-t-y*, in Form IV — the form of *giving, granting, handing over.*

Two different verbs for two different hearts. And the gap between them is the entire meaning.

**نُؤْتِهِ** — "We give him" — is the language of a transaction. A thing changes hands. It is complete in the giving. You hand someone a coin; the coin is now theirs; nothing further happens. It is finished.

**نَزِدْ** — "We increase" — is the language of *growth.* You cannot "increase" nothing. To increase something is to act upon a quantity that is *already there* and make it *more.* And here is the detail the mufassirūn will not let us miss: **al-Jalālayn** and **al-Baghawī** both explain *nazid* as *bi-l-taḍʿīf* — by **multiplication** — "the single good deed to ten, and beyond, to as much as Allah wills." Not addition. **Multiplication.** The compounding is open-ended; the ceiling is left undefined and rising.

Do you see the two economies now? The world-seeker receives a **payout** — a fixed sum, handed over, done. The Hereafter-seeker receives **compounding** — a yield that multiplies on itself with no stated ceiling.

And there is one more thing in that verb, the thing the hook pointed at. Allah says "We increase for him in **حَرْثِهِ** — *ḥarthih* — **his** harvest." The harvest is attributed to *him.* The labor remains *his*, honored as his own, and Allah adds *into* it. But for the world-seeker, the world is never called "his." It is just **منها** — "of *it*." He is given a slice of something that was never his to begin with and never becomes his.

That is what these verbs reveal: **the one who lives for the world is paid; the one who lives for the Hereafter is multiplied — and even his labor is given back to him as his own.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: مِنْهَا and مِن نَّصِيبٍ — Read the Silence

We have looked at two presences. Now we have to read an *absence* — because in this ayah the silence is louder than the speech.

Look first at the small word **مِنْهَا** — *minhā* — "of it." Inside it sits the preposition **مِن** (*min*). In Arabic, *min* in a context like this is **tabʿīḍiyyah** — it means **"some of," "a portion of," "a part of."** Not "We give him *it.*" — "We give him **some** of it." A part. A slice.

This is not a careless word. **Al-Baghawī**, citing Qatādah, draws the line exactly: *nu'tīhi bi-qadri mā qasama Llāhu lah* — "We give him **according to the measure Allah has apportioned** for him." Not everything the world-seeker wants. Not even most of it. Only the **portion** decreed. The man who sells his whole self for the world does not even get the *whole world.* He gets a measured slice of it — and **al-Muyassar** adds the quiet devastation: *wa-ḥdahā* — the world **alone**, and nothing else.

Stay with that. The person who gives everything for the dunya is, by the precise grammar of this ayah, *underpaid.* He spends a soul and receives a fragment. The arithmetic never closes.

Now the absence — and this is the deepest point in the verse.

Watch what Allah says about each person, and watch what He *doesn't* say.

To the Hereafter-seeker, Allah promises increase in his harvest — **and then He stops.** He says nothing about the world. He does **not** say "and he will have no share of the dunya." That silence is not an oversight. **It is a gift.** By refusing to close the worldly door on the Hereafter-seeker, Allah leaves it standing open. The one who farms for eternity is *not thereby starved of the world.* The world is simply not mentioned, because it is not in danger.

This is precisely the fear we began with — *if I invest in the Hereafter, I lose the world* — and Allah answers it not with an argument but with a **silence.** He declines to threaten the Hereafter-seeker with worldly loss. The threat the merchants feared most is the one threat the ayah pointedly refuses to make.

But to the world-seeker, Allah does **not** stay silent. He adds a full, closing clause: **وَمَا لَهُۥ فِى ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ مِن نَّصِيبٍ** — *wa mā lahu fī l-ākhirati min naṣīb* — "and he has, in the Hereafter, **no share at all.**"

And look at the grammar of that finality. The word is **نَّصِيبٍ** — *naṣīb*, from the root *n-ṣ-b* — a *share*, a *portion*, an *allotment.* And before it sits **مِن** again — but this *min* is doing the opposite job from the one in *minhā.* Here, in a negative sentence, **مِن** is what the grammarians call **min al-zā'idah li-stighrāq al-nafy** — the *min* that **drowns the negation in totality.** Without it, the sentence would mean "he has no share." *With* it, it means **"he does not have so much as a sliver of a share — not the faintest trace of a portion."** It seals the negation to the floor. Al-Baghawī gives the reason in four words: *li-annahu lam yaʿmal li-l-ākhirah* — "because he did not work for the Hereafter." He planted no seed in that field. So when he walks across it looking for a crop, there is nothing — *because nothing was sown.*

[PAUSE]

Now stand back and see the whole shape of the asymmetry, because it is the proof of everything:

- The Hereafter-seeker: his harvest is **multiplied**, it is called **his**, and the world is **not denied** to him.
- The world-seeker: he is handed a **measured fragment**, it is never called **his**, and the Hereafter is denied to him **utterly — not a trace.**

This is not two symmetrical outcomes. **It is a profoundly unequal generosity, tilted entirely toward the one who aimed high.** The man who aimed at the eternal was not charged the temporal. The man who aimed only at the temporal lost the eternal *and* was shortchanged on the temporal too.

That is what the silence reveals: **Allah closes the door only on the one who shut it himself.**

### Bridge

Look at what the three words have shown us, standing together. *Ḥarth* told us the Hereafter is grown, not grabbed. *Kāna yurīdu* told us it is grown from the settled aim of the heart, not a passing wish. And the asymmetry of *minhā* against *min naṣīb* told us that the divine response is not an even trade but a generosity weighted entirely toward the high aim.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about the actual shape of a human life.

# PART 2 — THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: Your Intention Is the Seed — and a Seed Decides Everything Before Anything Grows

Let me start where you already live.

You have, at some point, watched two people do the *exact same action* and somehow live two entirely different lives. Two students in the same lecture hall, taking the same notes. Two people at the same charity dinner, writing the same check. Two worshippers in the same row, making the same prostration. From the outside, the actions are identical. You could film them and not tell them apart.

And yet you have the strong, undeniable sense that something is profoundly different between them — that one is building something and the other is performing something. You can't point to it on the video. It isn't in the visible motion at all.

Modern life has a word adjacent to this. We talk about "intrinsic versus extrinsic motivation," and the research is unambiguous: *why* you do a thing predicts whether it lasts, whether it deepens, whether it survives the day the reward disappears. The student who studies for love of the subject and the student who studies for the grade look identical on exam day — and live completely different intellectual lives. We *know* this. We feel it in our bones.

This ayah takes that intuition and pushes it all the way down to the floor of reality.

Because here is what Allah has done. He has divided all of humanity — every single person who has ever lived — into exactly **two groups.** And He has divided them **not by what they did, but by what they wanted.** Not by the action. By the *ḥarth* — the seed. *Man kāna yurīdu* — whoever's settled wanting was *for this* versus *for that.*

Think about how radical that is. Two men give a thousand coins to the poor. One *wanted* the Hereafter; the other *wanted* to be seen, or to feel superior, or to buy a clean conscience. **They planted in two different fields.** And the ayah says their harvests will not merely differ in size — they will differ in *kind.* One walks into eternity and finds a multiplied crop waiting. The other walks into eternity and finds *nothing — not a trace* — because what he planted, he already harvested here, in the form of the world's regard, and there is no second crop from a field he sowed for show.

Let the paradox sit for a moment without resolving it: **the most decisive thing you do today is something invisible.** Not the deed — the *aim behind the deed.* The seed is buried before any plant appears. By the time the action is visible, the deciding has already happened, underground, in the dark of your intention, where only you and Allah can see.

This is why the people of knowledge guarded their intentions more fiercely than their actions. They understood that a magnificent deed with a rotten seed yields nothing, and a small deed with a sound seed yields a multiplied crop. **al-Muyassar's** description of the Hereafter-farmer was not "the one who did the most." It was the one who *fulfilled the rights of Allah and spent in His cause* — a description of orientation, of where the heart was facing while the hands moved.

So here is the change for tomorrow — and it is small, which is the point, because seeds are small.

Before the next thing you do — the prayer, the kindness, the work, the restraint — take the half-second to *aim* it. Not to perform it differently. To *want* it differently. Whisper, internally, *this one is for the harvest that lasts.* You will not change what the action looks like. You will change which **field** it is planted in. And the fields, this ayah promises, are not equally fertile. One multiplies with no ceiling. The other pays out once and closes.

You are sowing all day long, every day, whether you aim or not. The only question this ayah asks is: **into which ground?**

[PAUSE]

## Theme Two: The Fear of Loss Is Backwards — You Lose the World by Worshipping It, and Keep It by Aiming Past It

Now the second movement — and it speaks straight to the oldest fear in the human chest.

Here is the fear, named plainly. *If I really commit to the next life — if I give my time, my money, my comfort, my ambition to something I cannot see — I will fall behind in the life I can see. The people chasing the world will pass me. I will be the fool who bet on the invisible and lost the visible.*

That fear is not stupid. It is the most natural calculation in the world. And almost everyone, somewhere in their chest, is quietly running it.

So watch what this ayah does to it — because it does not soothe the fear. It *inverts* it.

Begin with the modern picture. Think of the person who has organized their entire life around acquisition — more money, more status, more security, more. And notice something the wealthy and the strivers themselves report, again and again: the world, pursued as an end, **does not fill up.** The portion always feels short. There is a strange, documented phenomenon — the more singularly someone chases enough, the more *not-enough* it feels. The target moves. The slice never satisfies, because it was never the whole thing to begin with.

The ayah named this *fourteen centuries before the studies.* **نُؤْتِهِ مِنْهَا** — "We give him **some** of it." *Some.* A portion. The world-worshipper does not get the world; he gets a measured fragment, *bi-qadri mā qusima lah* — by the exact decreed amount, no more, no matter how hard he runs. He spends his whole soul and is handed a slice. **The math of pure worldliness never closes — and the ayah told us why: the world was never designed to be a final harvest. It is a field that yields once and goes barren.**

Now the inversion. What about the one who aimed past it — at the Hereafter?

Read the silence again, because it is the whole answer to the fear. Allah promises that person increase in his harvest — **and refuses to threaten him with worldly loss.** He does not say "and he will have no share of the world." That door is left standing open. The one who farms for eternity is *not deprived* of the world along the way.

Hold the two halves together and the fear collapses:

- Chase only the world, and you get a *fragment* of the world and *nothing* of the Hereafter.
- Aim for the Hereafter, and you get a *multiplied* eternal harvest — and *the world is not taken from you* in the bargain.

[PAUSE]

So which one, exactly, is the foolish bet?

The Quran says this elsewhere with the same logic — the one who wanted *the hastened thing,* We hastened to him a measure of it, and then assigned him to loss (17:18); and the one who wanted the Hereafter *and strove for it* as one should strive — *their* striving is the one that is thanked (17:19). And it tells the seeker plainly: *seek, by what Allah has given you, the abode of the Hereafter — and do not forget your portion of the world* (28:77). Do you hear the order? The Hereafter is the aim. The world is not forbidden — it is simply demoted from *god* to *provision.* You don't bow to it; you *use* it, on the way to the field that lasts.

This is the secret hidden in the merchant's own language. The world makes a terrible *god* and a perfectly fine *tool.* Worship it, and it shrinks to a fragment and abandons you at death with nothing to follow. Aim past it, and it quietly serves you, *and* it follows you into eternity transformed — because every worldly thing you spent for the sake of the lasting field was a seed, and seeds don't die in the ground. They multiply.

What does this change tomorrow? It changes the *fear itself.* The next time the calculation runs — *if I give this, if I commit to this, I'll fall behind* — answer it with the ayah. You will not fall behind. The grammar of this verse guarantees that the high aim is the only one not charged a worldly penalty. The fear had it exactly backwards. **You do not protect your share of the world by worshipping it. You forfeit it by worshipping it. You keep it — and gain infinitely more — by aiming past it.**

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Step back and see the shape of the whole ayah, now that we've walked it.

It is built as a perfect, deliberate **mirror — that refuses to stay symmetrical.**

> **Whoever wants** the harvest of the **Hereafter** → We **increase** for him in **his** harvest → *[silence about the world]*
>
> **Whoever wants** the harvest of the **world** → We **give** him **some** of it → and **no share at all** in the Hereafter

The two halves are constructed to look like twins — *man kāna yurīdu ḥartha…* opening each one identically. Your ear expects a balanced trade: aim here, lose there; aim there, lose here. **And then the symmetry breaks**, exactly where it matters. The Hereafter-seeker's worldly loss — the thing the mirror leads you to expect — *never arrives.* The sentence that should have closed his worldly door is simply not there. Meanwhile the world-seeker's eternal door is sealed with a *min* that drowns it in negation. The architecture sets up an even scale and then **tips it, hard, toward the one who aimed high.** The structure itself is the argument: *this is not a fair trade between equals. It is a generosity weighted toward the heart that reached for what lasts.*

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey were the same discovery approached from two sides.

The word *ḥarth* taught us that the Hereafter is **grown, not grabbed** — and that became the truth that your **intention is the seed**, deciding everything in the dark before any plant appears.

The two verbs — *nazid* against *nu'tihi* — taught us the difference between **compounding and a payout** — and that became the inversion of the **oldest fear**: that the world, worshipped, pays out once and goes barren, while the Hereafter, aimed at, multiplies without ceiling and doesn't even charge you the world.

And the silence — the door left open for one, sealed for the other — taught us the deepest thing of all: **Allah closes the door only on the one who first shut it himself.**

### Questions to carry

- When my wanting *settles* — when I stop steering it, tired and unobserved — which field does it drift toward? What is my real *kāna yurīdu*?
- How much of my best, most sincere worship have I quietly mourned as "wasted" — when it was only *buried*, the way every seed must be?
- Where in my life am I running the backwards calculation — protecting my "share of the world" by clutching it — when the ayah says that clutching is exactly how it's lost?
- If someone could see only my intentions and not my actions, into which field would they say I have been planting?
- What is the half-second of *aiming* I could add before my next prayer, my next gift, my next act of restraint — to move it from one field to the other?

### One-sentence distillation

**You are sowing all day long whether you aim or not — and the only question that decides your eternity is into which of two fields your heart is quietly planting: the one that multiplies without ceiling, or the one that pays out a fragment and goes barren.**

### The closing invitation

Nothing in this ayah asks you to do *more.* It asks you to *aim.* The actions of your day can stay exactly as they are — the same prayer, the same work, the same kindness — and yet land in an entirely different field, and yield an entirely different harvest, depending on one invisible thing you do before you act. That is not a burden. That is the lightest possible mercy: that the deciding factor of your whole eternity is something free, silent, and always within your reach. You do not need new soil. You need only to turn, by a few degrees, the direction you are already facing.

### A brief du'ā

*O Allah, You who named our striving a cultivation — turn the settled wanting of our hearts toward the harvest that lasts. Let every seed we bury in the dark of our ordinary days be planted in the field You multiply, and not the field that pays out once and goes barren. And by Your subtle kindness, al-Laṭīf, do not let us forfeit, by worshipping this world, the share You so freely offered us in the next.* Āmīn.
