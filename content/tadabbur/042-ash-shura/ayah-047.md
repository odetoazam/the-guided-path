# Answer While Answering Is Still Possible

## The Ayah

> **ٱسْتَجِيبُوا۟ لِرَبِّكُم مِّن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِىَ يَوْمٌ لَّا مَرَدَّ لَهُۥ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ مَا لَكُم مِّن مَّلْجَإٍ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَمَا لَكُم مِّن نَّكِيرٍ**

*Istajībū li-rabbikum min qabli an ya'tiya yawmun lā maradda lahu mina-llāh. Mā lakum min malja'in yawma'idhin wa-mā lakum min nakīr.*

**"Respond to your Lord before there comes a Day from Allah that cannot be turned back. You will have no refuge that Day, nor will you have any means of denial."**

---

## Introduction: The World Behind the Words

I want you to feel the moment this verse drops into, before we touch a single word of it.

Surah Ash-Shura is Meccan. The community of believers is small, pressed, surrounded. The surah's whole architecture has been building one case, patiently, from many angles: **this revelation is real, it is from Allah, and the entire affair — yours, mine, history's — returns to Him.** Verse after verse has been like a hand opening: *look at the heavens, look at the rain He sends down after they despair, look at how He answers, look at how the whole thing belongs to Him.*

And the people being addressed are doing what people do. They are not, most of them, slamming the door. They are doing something far more human and far more dangerous. They are saying: *later. Not yet. I hear you. I'll get to it.*

Mecca was full of people who found the message beautiful and inconvenient at the same time. People with trade caravans to run, with status to protect, with a settled life that the message threatened to rearrange. The message wasn't being refused outright. It was being **postponed.**

[PAUSE]

And into that exact human posture — the postponement, the "I'll deal with it" — Allah sends a verse that does something startling. It does not argue anymore. It has argued for forty-six verses. Now it simply says: **answer.** One word. An imperative. *Istajibū.*

And then it tells you why you cannot afford to wait.

Here is the hook. I want you to hold a question in your mind through everything that follows. This verse is going to strip away, one by one, three things every human being relies on to feel safe — the ability to turn a bad outcome back, the ability to run somewhere safer, and the ability to say *no, that wasn't me.* Three exits. The verse closes all three.

But the strangeness — the thing I cannot get past — is the *name* Allah uses for Himself when He issues this terrifying warning. He does not say "respond to your Judge." He does not say "respond to the Compeller." Listen again: **istajibū li-rabbikum.** *Respond to your* ***Lord*** *— your Nurturer, your Sustainer, the One raising you.*

Why, at the edge of the most final warning in the surah, does He call Himself by the most tender of His names?

Stay with me. That question is the whole verse.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Istajibū* — The Answer to a Call You Forgot You Heard

Let me teach you something before we open the word, because the word will not land otherwise.

In Arabic, verbs are built on patterns, and the patterns carry meaning. There is a simple way to say "answer" — *ajāba.* It's a clean, transitive verb: someone speaks, you answer. But that is **not** the word Allah chose here.

The word here is **istajībū** — from the tenth pattern, what grammarians call Form X. And Form X very often carries a sense of *seeking, responding-into, answering-back-toward.* It is not the flat "answer." It is the **responsive answer** — the answer that completes a call already sent.

Here is the everyday version. Imagine someone calls your name across a crowded room — someone who loves you, who has been looking for you. You don't just "produce a sound." You *turn.* You answer *toward* them. Your whole body reorients to the source of the call. That turning-toward — that is *istijāba.*

Now open the word: **ٱسْتَجِيبُوا۟ لِرَبِّكُم** — *istajībū li-rabbikum.*

[PAUSE]

Notice something. The verb does not say "respond *to a command.*" It says respond *to your Lord* — *li-rabbikum.* You are not being told to comply with a rule. You are being told to **turn toward a Person who is calling you.**

And here is what is remarkable — here is the thing I need you to see. The root of *istajībū* is **j-w-b.** Hold that root. Now travel a few surahs forward, to Surah Ghāfir, where Allah says: **ٱدْعُونِىٓ أَسْتَجِبْ لَكُمْ** — *"Call upon Me, I will respond to you"* (40:60). And in Surah al-Baqarah: *"I respond to the call of the caller when he calls Me"* (2:186).

Same root. **J-w-b.** *Astajib.*

Do you feel it? The verb Allah uses for *Himself answering your prayer* is the very same verb He now uses to ask for *your answer to Him.* When you raise your hands in the dark and whisper *yā Rabb* — you are asking Allah to make *istijāba.* To respond. To turn toward you.

This verse turns the same word around and hands it back. **You have spent your whole life asking Him to respond to you. Now He asks: will you respond to Me?**

[PAUSE]

It is a covenant of call-and-response. He responds to the one who calls. He is now calling. The question the verb quietly asks is the most intimate question in the surah: *the line has always been open in your direction — will you ever answer when the call comes the other way?*

And He signs the call with **rabbikum** — *your Lord, your Nurturer.* Al-Baghawi reads the call concretely: *answer the caller of Allah* — meaning the Prophet ﷺ. Al-Jalālayn reads it as: *answer Him through tawḥīd and worship.* The classical readings braid together into one image: the One who has been *raising you* your entire life — feeding you before you could ask, sustaining you while you forgot Him — is the One issuing the call. The warning that follows is severe. But the One warning you is not a stranger. He is the One who has never stopped nurturing you. **The severity is not the opposite of the tenderness. It is its proof.** You warn most urgently the one you most want to save.

What this single word reveals: **Faith is not first compliance. It is first a turning-toward — an answer to a Voice that has been calling, in the language of the One who has been raising you all along.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Lā Maradda lahu* — The Day That Cannot Be Turned Back

Now the verse tells us what we are racing against. *Min qabli an ya'tiya yawmun* — *"before there comes a Day."* And then it describes the Day with three words that I want to slow all the way down for: **لَّا مَرَدَّ لَهُۥ مِنَ ٱللَّهِ** — *lā maradda lahu mina-llāh.*

Let me teach the key word. **Maradd** comes from the root **r-d-d.** And r-d-d is one of the most ordinary, livable words in the language. It means *to return something, to turn it back, to send it back, to repel it, to give a refund, to revoke.*

You know this root from daily life without knowing it. When you *return* a purchase — that's radd. When a guard *repels* an attacker — radd. When you *take back* what you said — radd. When a decision is *reversed* on appeal — radd. The whole texture of human life runs on radd: the ability to send things back, undo them, reverse them, get a do-over.

Now hear the verse: **lā maradda lahu** — *there is no turning-it-back.* No radd. No return. No reversal. No appeal.

[PAUSE]

Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī both land on the same point: once Allah has decreed this Day and set it as an appointed time, **no one can repel it.** *Lā maradda lahu mina-llāh* — there is no turning it back, and the phrase *mina-llāh* seals it: this is *from Allah.* It is not a force you might out-negotiate, out-run, or out-wait. Its source is the One against whom there is no higher court.

Let me sit with you in why this is so frightening, and I'll do it through the very root we just learned.

Every single thing in your life right now is *radd-able.* That is the secret architecture of how you feel safe. You sent the angry text — you can delete it, apologize, *take it back.* You made the bad investment — there might be a way to *recover.* You wasted the year — you tell yourself you can *make it up.* You hurt someone — you can *return* to them and mend it. Underneath all your anxiety is a quiet, load-bearing belief: **almost everything can be turned back.**

And it can. In this life. *That is precisely what this life is* — the realm of radd. The realm of the do-over. The realm where repentance itself is the greatest radd of all: you turn back to Allah, and He turns your sins back into good (25:70).

[PAUSE]

The Day in this verse is defined by the *absence* of the one mechanism your whole sense of safety is built on. **Lā maradda.** It is the one event that arrives and *stays.* The one transaction with no refund. The one verdict with no appeal. The first irreversible thing you will ever encounter — because everything before it was reversible, and that is exactly why you kept assuming you had time.

Read the absence carefully. The verse does not threaten you with a punishment here. It threatens you with the *loss of radd itself.* It is telling you: the door you have been counting on — the "I can always fix it later" door — that door, on that Day, is simply gone.

What this word reveals: **You live your whole life inside the mercy of reversibility — and you mistake that mercy for permanence. The verse names the one moment when reversibility ends, so that you will use it while you still have it.**

[PAUSE]

## Element Three: The Three Sealed Exits — *Maradd*, *Malja'*, *Nakīr*

Now step back and look at the shape of the whole verse, because Allah has built something architectural here, and you can only see it once all three pieces are on the table.

Watch the three negations fall, one after another, like three doors closing down a corridor:

1. **لَّا مَرَدَّ لَهُۥ** — no *turning the Day back.* (You cannot stop it from coming.)
2. **مَا لَكُم مِّن مَّلْجَإٍ** — no *refuge.* (Once it comes, you cannot flee it.)
3. **وَمَا لَكُم مِّن نَّكِيرٍ** — no *denial.* (And you cannot disown what you did within it.)

Three exits. The first is the exit *before* — can I prevent it? No. The second is the exit *during* — can I escape it? No. The third is the exit *after the verdict* — can I dispute it? No.

Let me open the two new words.

**Malja'** — root **l-j-A** — is a *place of refuge,* the spot you run to when you're hunted. The Arabs used it for the cleft in the mountain where you shelter from a storm, the fortress you bolt into when the raiders come. Al-Jalālayn and al-Qurtubī gloss it plainly: *no place to flee to that would save you from the punishment.* Listen to the sound of the word itself — **mal-ja'** — it ends on a hamza, a hard glottal stop, the throat snapping shut. The very sound of the word is a door slamming. There is something in your gut that knows what it is to scan a room for the exit. The verse says: scan all you want. *Mā lakum min malja'.*

[PAUSE]

And then the strangest, deepest of the three: **nakīr** — root **n-k-r.**

This root is rich, and the classical commentators give us two readings that, together, complete the picture. Don't pick between them — let them layer.

**Reading one — al-Jalālayn:** *nakīr* is *your* denial. *Inkār li-dhunūbikum* — the denying of your own sins. In this life, denial is your last refuge. When everything else fails, you can still say: *that wasn't me. I didn't do that. You can't prove it.* Denial is the exit you keep behind the other exits, the one you think no one can take from you because it lives inside your own mouth. The verse says: even *that* is gone. *Wa-mā lakum min nakīr* — you will have no power to deny. Why? Because, as the Qur'an tells us elsewhere, your own hands and feet and skin will testify. The instrument of denial is confiscated.

**Reading two — al-Baghawī:** *nakīr* is *someone else's* objection on your behalf — *min munkir yughayyiru mā bikum* — *no objector to change the state you are in.* In this life, when a verdict goes against you, someone can stand up and *protest* — an advocate, a powerful friend, a wāsiṭa who pulls strings, someone who says "wait, change this." The verse says: on that Day there is no munkir. No one rises to object. No one alters your condition.

[PAUSE]

Put the two readings together and feel the completeness of it. **You cannot deny it yourself, and no one will deny it for you.** The exit inside your own mouth is sealed, and the exit through someone else's intervention is sealed. Both directions of *nakīr* — outward and inward — closed.

Now see the architecture entire. The verse has walked you down a corridor and locked every door in sequence:

- You can't stop the Day from coming — **no maradd.**
- When it comes, you can't run from it — **no malja'.**
- When the verdict lands, you can't dispute it, and no one disputes it for you — **no nakīr.**

[PAUSE]

This is not cruelty. This is the most precise mercy in the surah. Because here is what the locked corridor of *that* Day is meant to do to you on *this* day: it is meant to drive you back to the **one door that is still open.** The first word. *Istajibū.* Answer.

The verse is structured like an hourglass. The wide-open top is the call: *respond to your Lord.* Then it narrows — Day, no return, no refuge, no denial — down to a single sealed point. And the entire weight of that narrowing pours back onto the one word at the top. **The only escape from the Day with no exits is the answer you give before it arrives.**

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about the strange way the human heart handles time.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Lie of "Later" — Why We Postpone the One Thing That Matters

Let me start where you actually live.

You know the feeling of a task you keep moving to tomorrow. Not a small task. A *real* one — the conversation you need to have, the doctor's appointment you're avoiding, the apology you owe, the project that could change your life. You don't refuse it. You'd be offended if someone said you were refusing it. You're going to do it. *Soon.* Just not today.

Psychologists have a precise name for the engine underneath this. They call it **temporal discounting.** Your mind systematically treats the future as less real than the present. A reward tomorrow feels vivid; the same reward in a year feels like a rumor. And a *cost* in the future — the consequence of not acting — barely registers at all, because the future-you who pays it doesn't feel like *you.* He feels like a stranger you've never met.

So you outsource the hard thing to that stranger. *He'll* handle it. *He'll* pray properly. *He'll* repent. *He'll* turn toward Allah seriously. You, today, get to keep your comfort. And the genius of the trap is that it never feels like a decision. You never once decide *not* to turn to Allah. You simply decide, a thousand times, *not yet.*

[PAUSE]

This is exactly the disease this verse was revealed against. Al-Muyassar says it directly: *fī al-āyati dalīlun ʿalā dhammi al-taswīf* — **in this verse is a proof of the condemnation of procrastination** — *wa-fīhā al-amru bil-mubādarati ilā kulli ʿamalin ṣāliḥin yaʿriḍu lil-ʿabd* — **and a command to rush toward every good deed that presents itself to the servant.** And then a line worth carving into the wall: *fa-inna lil-ta'khīri āfātin wa-mawāniʿ* — **for delay has its diseases and its obstacles.**

Delay is not neutral waiting. Delay is *active rot.* Every "later" makes the next "later" easier. The heart that postpones today is *training itself* to postpone — the muscle of turning-toward atrophies with every unanswered call, until one day the call comes and you find the muscle no longer moves.

Now here is where the verse does something the language of "temporal discounting" can never do.

Psychology can diagnose the trap. It cannot close it. Why? Because the whole trap runs on one assumption: **that the future is yours.** That the stranger you're outsourcing to will actually arrive. That there *is* a later.

And the verse goes straight for that assumption and severs it. *Min qabli an ya'tiya yawmun lā maradda lahu.* Respond **before** a Day comes that cannot be turned back. The verse doesn't tell you to be more disciplined. It tells you something about reality you have been hiding from: **the "later" you keep banking on is not guaranteed, and the one "later" that *is* guaranteed is the one where the doors are already locked.**

[PAUSE]

The stranger you've been outsourcing your faith to — future-you, who will finally get serious — that stranger may step out of the present directly into the Day with no maradd, no malja', no nakīr. You have been mailing your most important responsibility to an address that may not exist when the package arrives.

What does this change about tomorrow — literally tomorrow?

It relocates the deadline. Right now, somewhere in you, the deadline for turning seriously toward Allah is filed under *eventually.* The verse moves it to *now,* not by guilt, but by removing the fiction that holds *eventually* in place. The practical application is almost embarrassingly concrete: **the next good deed that presents itself to you — the next prayer, the next moment of dhikr, the next chance to give, the next istighfār — do not file it under later. Do it in the gap between hearing it and reasoning your way out of it.** Al-Muyassar's phrase: *kulli ʿamalin ṣāliḥin yaʿriḍu lil-ʿabd* — *every* good deed that *presents itself.* The presenting is the call. The doing is the *istijāba.* Answer in the gap. That gap is the whole battlefield.

## Theme Two: The Refuges We Build — and the One That Doesn't Run Out

Here is something true about you, and about me, and about every human who has ever lived: **we are refuge-building creatures.**

Watch yourself for one day and you'll see it. We construct shelters everywhere — financial cushions, backup plans, exit strategies, the relationship we keep "just in case," the savings we won't touch, the skill we keep current so we always have somewhere to land. This is not a flaw. It's wisdom. The whole posture of a prudent life is: *don't get caught with nowhere to run.* Have a malja'. Always have a malja'.

And it goes deeper than money. Our *psychological* refuges are even more elaborate. When the truth about ourselves gets too close, we run to a refuge: *busyness.* We bolt into distraction. We flee into the next notification, the next series, the next argument online — anything to avoid sitting alone with the question of what we're actually doing with our lives, and Who we'll answer to. Denial — *nakīr* — is the innermost refuge of all: the cleft in the mountain inside our own minds, where we hide from facts about ourselves by simply refusing to look at them.

[PAUSE]

Now hold all of that — every shelter you've ever built — against the verse: *mā lakum min malja'in yawma'idhin.* **You will have no refuge that Day.**

I want to be careful here, because this can be heard as pure terror, and it is not. It is something far more surgical. The verse is not gloating that your refuges will fail. It is *teaching you something about what a refuge is.*

Every refuge you have ever built has one thing in common: it is **finite.** Savings run out. Plans collapse. The relationship you kept "just in case" walks away. Distraction stops working at 3 a.m. when you're alone with yourself. Even denial cracks — the testimony of your own hands and skin, as the Qur'an describes the Day, is simply the final cracking of the last refuge of self-deception. **Every malja' you build is a mountain cleft that the storm eventually finds.**

So the verse is not telling you that you'll be left with *nothing.* It's exposing that everything you *thought* was something was, all along, finite — temporary shelter mistaken for permanent ground.

[PAUSE]

And now go back — all the way back — to the first word. *Istajibū li-**rabbikum.*** Respond to your **Lord.**

Do you see what He did? He told you to run *toward* something in the very same breath that He told you every other refuge would fail. The One who says *you will have no malja' that Day* is the One who said, at the start, *answer your Rabb now.* He is not a storm closing in on you with nowhere to hide. **He is the only ground that does not run out — and He told you so *before* He told you about the Day.**

The Qur'an makes this devastatingly explicit elsewhere, with this very root. When Allah describes the believers caught in the terror of the battle of Tabūk, He says they found *"no malja' from Allah except* ***to Him***" — *lā malja'a mina-llāhi illā ilayh* (9:118). Read that slowly. There is no refuge *from* Allah — except *toward* Allah. He is the only shelter from Himself. The storm and the cleft in the mountain are the same Reality, and you face the storm by running *into* Him, not away.

[PAUSE]

That is the secret the whole verse has been guarding. The Day removes every finite refuge precisely so that you will stop mistaking the temporary for the permanent — and turn, *now,* while turning still works, toward the one Refuge that was never finite to begin with: the Rabb who has been calling you the whole time.

What does this change about how you live tomorrow?

It re-sorts your refuges. Not by abandoning the prudent ones — keep your savings, keep your plans; the Prophet ﷺ tied his camel *and* trusted Allah. It re-sorts them by *rank.* The practical move is to notice, the next time fear or anxiety rises in you, where your mind *runs first.* To the bank balance? To the distraction? To the person? To the plan? Just notice the direction of the first flight. And then, deliberately, *redirect the first flight* — make the first refuge you run to *Him.* *Ilayh.* Build the reflex now, in the realm of radd, where reflexes can still be built — so that it is already built on the Day when no other refuge answers.

[PAUSE — major pause]

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture, Now Visible

Look at what the verse actually was, now that we've walked through it.

It is an **hourglass.** It opens at its widest — a single, tender, wide-open command: *istajibū li-rabbikum,* respond to your Lord. Then it narrows, deliberately, door by door:

- *Lā maradda* — the Day cannot be turned back.
- *Mā lakum min malja'* — you cannot flee it.
- *Wa-mā lakum min nakīr* — you cannot deny it, and no one denies it for you.

Down and down, to a single sealed point. And then every ounce of weight from that sealed bottom pours straight back up to the one open word at the top. **The verse spends nineteen words closing exits for one reason: to make you sprint through the only one still open.** The threat is real, but it is in service of the invitation. The locked corridor exists to point at the unlocked door.

## The Two Discoveries Were One Discovery

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey arrived at the same place from two directions.

The **words** showed us a verb — *istajibū* — that is the mirror of the verb Allah uses for answering *our* prayers. The same root, *j-w-b,* running both ways: He responds to the one who calls; now He calls, and asks if we will respond.

The **themes** showed us why we don't: because we discount the future and bank on a "later" that may not come, and because we hide in refuges that are all, secretly, finite.

And they meet here: **the One asking you to answer is the only Refuge that doesn't run out, calling in the language of the One who has been nurturing you all along — and the only time you have to answer is the time that is reversible, which is now.** The tenderness of *Rabb* and the terror of *lā maradda* are not in tension. They are the same mercy. He shows you the locked doors of *that* Day so you will not waste the open door of *this* one.

## Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. Allah has been making *istijāba* to me my whole life — responding to calls I made and calls I didn't even know I made. When the verb turns around — when *He* calls — **what is my honest response time?** Is it *labbayk,* or is it *later*?

2. What am I currently outsourcing to "future-me"? Name the specific thing. **Why am I so sure that stranger will arrive — and arrive with the door still open?**

3. When fear rises in me, where does my mind run *first*? What does the direction of that first flight reveal about which refuge I actually trust?

4. Of all the refuges I have built, **which ones are finite and I am treating as permanent?**

5. If I truly believed there is a Day with *no maradd* — no return, no undo — what is the one thing I would stop postponing before I finish this reflection?

## The One Sentence

**Answer the One who is nurturing you now — while answering is still reversible — because a Day is coming that cannot be turned back, offers no place to run, and accepts no denial; and the only escape from its sealed doors is the open door of your answer today.**

## The Invitation

Nothing in this verse asks you to become someone else overnight. It asks for one motion: a *turning-toward.* *Istijāba.* The next time a good deed presents itself to you — even a small one, even the very next prayer — let there be no gap between hearing the call and answering it. Don't file it under *later.* Answer in the gap. That single redirected reflex, repeated, is the whole of what this verse is asking. You are not being commanded by a stranger. You are being called home by the One who has never stopped raising you.

## A Du'a From These Words

*O Allah, You who answer the one who calls — make me one who answers when You call. Do not let me squander the days of turning-back on the assumption that they are endless. When my heart looks for somewhere to run, let it run to You, for there is no refuge from You except to You. And let me respond to You, my Lord, before the coming of the Day that cannot be turned back.*

*Āmīn.*
