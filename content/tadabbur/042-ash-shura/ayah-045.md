# The Stealthy Glance: When the Eye Loses Its Power

## Introduction — A Scene You Are Made to Watch

Let me read it to you first, slowly, the way it was meant to be heard.

**وَتَرَىٰهُمْ يُعْرَضُونَ عَلَيْهَا خَاشِعِينَ مِنَ ٱلذُّلِّ يَنظُرُونَ مِن طَرْفٍ خَفِىٍّ**

*Wa tarāhum yuʿraḍūna ʿalayhā khāshiʿīna mina-dh-dhulli yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyy.*

"And you will see them being exposed to it, humbled by disgrace, looking with a stealthy glance."

**وَقَالَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ إِنَّ ٱلْخَٰسِرِينَ ٱلَّذِينَ خَسِرُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَهُمْ وَأَهْلِيهِمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ**

*Wa qāla-lladhīna āmanū inna-l-khāsirīna-lladhīna khasirū anfusahum wa ahlīhim yawma-l-qiyāmah.*

"And those who believed will say, 'Indeed, the true losers are those who lost themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection.'"

**أَلَآ إِنَّ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ فِى عَذَابٍ مُّقِيمٍ**

*Alā inna-ẓ-ẓālimīna fī ʿadhābin muqīm.*

"Unquestionably, the wrongdoers are in a lasting punishment."

[PAUSE]

I want you to notice something before we go anywhere near the meaning. This ayah does not tell you a fact. It does not say *the wrongdoers will be punished*. It makes you **watch**.

The very first word is a verb of seeing, and it is pointed at you: *wa tarāhum* — "and you will see them." You are not informed. You are positioned. You are placed at a vantage point, your eyes are opened, and a scene begins to move in front of you. This is the Qur'an doing something it does only at its most serious moments: it stops describing and starts *showing*.

So let us rebuild the world this scene first landed in.

These words came down in Mecca, to a small, mocked community. Imagine the social texture of that city for a believer. You walk through the marketplace and the powerful men of Quraysh — the ones with lineage, with caravans, with influence — look *through* you, or look *at* you with that particular look the strong give the weak: half a glance, dismissive, the eyes barely moving. To follow Muhammad ﷺ in those years was to be the one who is looked down upon. To be the one whose gaze had no weight in the room. To be, socially, beneath notice.

Now hold that world in your mind, because this surah, Ash-Shura, has been building toward a reckoning. Just before our verse, in ayah 44, the Qur'an shows these same powerful men at the edge of the Fire, and it lets us hear them speak. They look at the punishment and they say: *is there any way to return?* Any road back? Any second chance? It is the most human sentence in the world — *let me go back and do it differently*.

And our ayah, 45, is the answer to that plea. But watch *how* it answers. It does not say "no." It does not give them a road. It simply lets the camera keep rolling. It shows you what happens next.

Here is the question I want you to carry through everything that follows. In a scene about Hellfire, about the ultimate punishment, why does Allah focus our attention — of all the things He could have shown us — on **their eyes**? On the way they are *looking*? What does the geometry of a single averted glance know about the nature of punishment that the language of flame and pain does not?

[PAUSE]

Stay with that. Let's go find out.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: *Yuʿraḍūna* — Paraded, Displayed, Made to Be Seen

Before we touch the Arabic, let me give you an ordinary human experience to hold.

Think of the most exposed you have ever felt. Not physically — socially. The moment when something you desperately wanted to keep hidden was suddenly visible to a room full of people. The presentation where your mind went blank and everyone was watching you fail. The secret that got out. The instinct in that moment is always the same, and it is older than language: you want to *shrink*. You want the floor to open. You want to become small, to become invisible, to not be seen.

Now hold onto the fact that the deepest human reflex under disgrace is the reflex *to hide*. We will need it in a moment.

Here is the word: **يُعْرَضُونَ** — *yuʿraḍūna*.

It comes from the root *ʿayn-rā-ḍād* (ع-ر-ض), and that root, across the language, means to present something, to display it, to lay it out in the open for inspection. From this root the Arabs spoke of *ʿarḍ* — a military parade, where the troops are marched out and shown for review. From this root comes the displaying of merchandise, goods laid out on a stall so that buyers can look them over. The root carries the sense of being *set out before eyes*.

And notice the form Allah chose. It is **passive**. *Yuʿraḍūna* — "they are *being* displayed." Not "they walk toward it." Not "they approach." They do nothing. The verb gives them no agency at all. They are the object. Something is being done *to* them. They are *handled*, *positioned*, *exhibited* — the way you would lay out goods, or march out captives.

Let's sit with this, because something quietly devastating is happening here.

These are the men of Mecca who, in the world we just rebuilt, *did the looking*. Who *displayed* their wealth, *paraded* their lineage, decided with a glance who in the room mattered and who did not. The whole architecture of their power was the power to inspect others and be inspected by no one above them.

And now the verb has been turned inside out. The ones who displayed are *being displayed*. The inspectors are the inspected. **They are merchandise on the stall now. They are the parade now.** Whatever they were is being laid out, in the open, for the gaze of all creation.

[PAUSE]

Now bring back what we held at the start. The deepest reflex under disgrace is to hide. And here is the precise nature of this moment: it is *total exposure*, the one thing the disgraced soul cannot bear, made permanent and inescapable. They are placed where every eye can see. Notice — before a single word about flame, before any mention of physical pain, the first stroke Allah paints is the stroke of *being seen*. The punishment, at its very first layer, is exposure itself.

What this single passive verb reveals: **for the soul that built its life on being above the gaze of others, the first torment is simply to be looked at.**

[PAUSE — let that settle.]

### Element Two: The Unnamed *Hā* — The Thing Too Present to Name

I want to show you something that is almost invisible, and once you see it you will not be able to un-see it.

Look again: *yuʿraḍūna ʿalay-hā* — "they are displayed before **it**."

Before *it*. There is a pronoun there — *hā*, "it," feminine. And here is the strange thing. Pronouns refer back to a noun. When I say "the door — open it," the "it" points back to "the door." That is how language works; the pronoun leans on a named thing that came before.

But read the verse again from its first word. There is no feminine noun before this *hā*. Nothing has been named. The verse opens, "and you will see them displayed before *it*" — and the *it* points back to **nothing on the page**.

The classical commentators noticed this immediately. Al-Qurtubī stops and explains: the feminine pronoun here stands for **the Fire** — *an-nār*, which in Arabic is feminine — even though the Fire was never mentioned. He notes that Allah "referred to the punishment with the feminine marker," and that had the wording followed a named masculine word like *al-ʿadhāb* (the punishment), the pronoun would have been masculine instead. So the *hā* reaches past the words on the page and lands on a thing that everyone knows, but no one wrote down.

Now teach yourself the principle, because this is one of the Qur'an's most powerful tools. In any language there are things we *do not name* — and we don't name them for two opposite reasons. Sometimes we don't name a thing because it is too trivial to bother with. And sometimes we don't name a thing because it is **too present, too looming, too dreadful** to require naming. When a family is sitting in a hospital waiting room and someone says quietly, "Has it spread?" — nobody asks "has *what* spread?" The unnamed thing is the only thing in the room. To name it would almost be to reduce it.

That is what is happening here. **The Fire is not named because the Fire does not need naming.** It is the unspoken *it* that fills the entire scene. It is so overwhelmingly present in that moment that the grammar simply gestures toward it — *that* — and every soul knows exactly what *that* is. The absence of the name is not a gap in the meaning. The absence *is* the meaning. The thing is too vast to fit inside a noun.

[PAUSE]

Here is what I find remarkable. This is the opposite of how we live now. In this world, we *name* our small fears endlessly — we say them, we list them, we turn them over — while the one reality that should fill the whole room, the meeting with our deeds, the standing before Allah, goes politely unmentioned for years at a time. On that Day the proportions are corrected. The thing we refused to name becomes the only thing present. The unnamed *it* becomes everything.

What this near-invisible pronoun reveals: **the gravest realities are not the loud ones we keep naming — they are the silent ones we keep avoiding, and their silence is not their smallness, it is their size.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Min Ṭarfin Khafiyy* — The Geometry of a Broken Gaze

Now we arrive at the heart of the verse — at those eyes I promised you we would return to.

*Yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyy.* "They look with a stealthy glance."

Let me teach you the two words at the center of it, because the whole scene is compressed into them.

The first is **طَرْف** — *ṭarf*, from the root *ṭā-rā-fā* (ط-ر-ف). And *ṭarf* does not mean the eye. It means the *edge* of the eye — the rim, the corner, the eyelid's margin. By extension it means a *glance*, because a glance is what happens at the very edge of the eye, the sideways flick, the look that uses only the corner. It is not the full, frontal, open gaze. It is the eye operating at its periphery.

The second is **خَفِيّ** — *khafiyy*, from *khā-fā-yā* (خ-ف-ي), the root of all hiddenness, faintness, concealment. A *khafiyy* glance is a hidden one, a faint one, a weak one — a look that is trying not to be a look.

Put them together and feel the physical posture the words are sculpting: *yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyy* — **they steal glances from the corners of their eyes, faintly, furtively.** They cannot look straight at the Fire — it is unbearable. But they cannot look away either — the dread will not release them. So the eye does the only thing left: it darts. A sideways flicker. A glance from the edge. The look of someone being led to a thing so terrible they can only take it in slivers, out of the corner of a half-closed eye.

The commentators gathered around this image like cameras around the same moment, each catching a different angle — and I want to give you the composite, because no single angle holds it all. Al-Baghawī reports several readings, and together they build one figure. One: they *steal* their looks at the Fire out of sheer terror and the disgrace weighing on them. Another: the *min* here functions almost like "with" — they look *with* a weakened, broken eye. Another, hauntingly: they cannot even open their eyes fully — they see only through a sliver, a partial opening. And a fourth, the most piercing: that this is the look of a heart, not an eye at all, because they are raised up that Day with their sight gone, and the only "looking" left to them is the faint, hidden looking of the heart toward a doom it can feel but cannot face.

Hold all four together and you do not get four different scenes. You get one — rendered with overwhelming precision. **A gaze that has lost all of its power.**

[PAUSE]

Now let me show you why this is the genius of the verse, why Allah, of everything He could have shown, shows us *this*.

Go back to Mecca one more time. What was the signature of the arrogant man? It was his *gaze*. The full, frontal, commanding look. The eyes that swept a room and assigned everyone their worth. The look that stared down the weak. The whole posture of pride lives in the eyes — we even say a proud man "looks down" on people, "won't give you the time of day," "looks right through you." Power has always been written in how a person *looks at* the world: directly, fully, owning the gaze.

And here, in a single phrase, Allah dismantles exactly that. He does not say their wealth is gone or their lineage is gone. He goes straight to the eyes — the very seat of their arrogance — and He breaks the gaze itself. The full look becomes a furtive flicker. The commanding stare becomes a stolen sliver from the corner of a half-shut eye. **The instrument of their pride is turned into the instrument of their disgrace.** The thing they used to look *down* with is now the thing they cannot even lift.

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing to an unseen reality through a thing you can see and feel. You have *felt* this geometry. You know in your own body the difference between the confident gaze and the shamed flinch. He takes that bodily knowledge you already have and uses it to let you feel, from the inside, what the collapse of a soul looks like.

What this phrase reveals: **pride and disgrace are not abstractions — they are postures, and they are written first and most truly in the eyes. On that Day the proud gaze does not merely lose its objects of contempt; it loses its very ability to look.**

[PAUSE — major pause.]

### Bridge

Look at what these three pieces of grammar have shown us, together. A passive verb that turned the inspectors into the inspected. An unnamed pronoun that proved the dreadful thing is too present to name. And a broken, sidelong glance that located the entire collapse of pride inside the corner of an eye.

Three small grammatical facts. One unified portrait: the precise dismantling of a proud soul, rendered not in fire and smoke but in posture, exposure, and the geometry of a gaze.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. What is this scene actually telling us about what it means to be punished? And what, according to the very next line, does it actually mean to *lose*?

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: Shame Is the Architecture of the Punishment

Let me begin not in the Hereafter but in a feeling you know intimately. I want to draw a line that researchers of the human heart have drawn in our own time, because the Qur'an drew it first.

There is a difference between **guilt** and **shame**, and almost everything depends on it. Guilt says: *I did something bad.* Shame says: *I am bad.* Guilt is about an act, and an act can be repented, repaired, left behind. But shame is about the self — and you cannot leave your self behind. That is why guilt, however painful, can move you toward repair, while shame moves you toward one thing only: the desire to *disappear*. To hide. To not be seen. Researchers describe the body of shame in exactly the terms you'd expect: the head drops, the shoulders curl in, the gaze falls and turns away. Shame is the emotion that makes a person want to vanish from sight.

Now hold that picture — the dropped head, the curled posture, the averted eyes — and read our verse again. *Khāshiʿīna mina-dh-dhull* — humbled, collapsed, brought low, *from disgrace*. *Yanẓurūna min ṭarfin khafiyy* — looking only with a furtive sliver of a glance.

Do you see it? **The Qur'an is not describing the physics of fire here. It is describing the phenomenology of shame** — and describing it with a precision that took human psychology fourteen centuries to catch up to. The lowered posture. The collapsed body. The averted, broken gaze. This is the anatomy of total shame, drawn from the inside.

And here is the movement I want to walk you through, because it changes what you think punishment even *is*.

We tend to imagine Hell primarily as *pain* — heat, fire, the body in agony. And that is real; the Qur'an names it elsewhere without flinching. But notice what Allah chose to foreground *here*, in the opening stroke of this scene. Not the burning. The *being seen*. *Yuʿraḍūna* — paraded, displayed, exposed. He builds the first layer of the punishment out of the exact thing that shame cannot survive: **exposure.** The soul that most needs to hide is placed where it can never not be seen. And not seen by strangers only — seen by *those who believed*, the very ones it once looked down upon, who are now watching and pronouncing the verdict.

Stay with the cruelty of that geometry. In this world, when shame strikes, there is always a mercy built in: the moment passes. The room empties. You go home. Time carries the witnesses away. The unbearable exposure has an exit, even if it is only the exit of *later*. And the closing word of our verse slams that exit shut: *ʿadhābin muqīm* — a *lasting*, *abiding*, *resident* punishment. The exposure does not pass. The room never empties. There is no *later* to walk into.

[PAUSE]

Let me connect this to the universal struggle, because it is not only "their" story. Every one of us is already managing shame, every day. The whole machinery of modern life — the curated profile, the performed self, the careful editing of what we let others see — is, at bottom, a vast system for *controlling who sees what*. We spend enormous energy managing our exposure. We are all, in our own way, terrified of being fully seen.

And the believer's response to that terror is the most counterintuitive turn in the whole religion. The believer does not try to *hide* from the One who sees. The believer chooses, *now*, in this life, to stand fully exposed before Allah — *I have nothing hidden from You; You see all of it, the worst of it; and I turn to You anyway.* This is what *istighfār* is. It is voluntary exposure to the only Witness whose verdict is final — done *now*, while that exposure can still end in mercy, rather than *then*, when it ends in *muqīm*.

So here is what this changes about tomorrow. The discipline this theme asks of you is not to *manage* your exposure better. It is to choose your Witness. To take the thing you are most ashamed of — the act, the pattern, the self you'd most want to hide — and instead of burying it deeper, to *uncover it before Allah today*, in the privacy of a sajdah where no human eye is on you. Because every shame brought willingly before Him now is one less thing waiting to be *displayed* then. **You will be seen. The only question you get to answer is *when*, and *by whom first*.**

[PAUSE — major pause.]

### Theme Two: The Only Loss That Counts Is the Loss of Your Self

Let me start with money, because that is where we all start.

When you hear the word "loser" — *the one who lost* — what comes to mind? The one whose business failed. The one who lost the money, the deal, the house, the status. We have a very clear, very worldly picture of loss: it is when the things you accumulated leave your hands. Loss is *subtraction from your possessions*.

Now watch what the believers say in this verse, because they are about to redefine the word entirely.

The word is **خَسِرُوا** — *khasirū*, from *khā-sīn-rā* (خ-س-ر). And this is, at its root, a **commercial** word. *Khusr* in the language of the Arabs is the merchant's word for *net loss* — the red ink, the deficit left when the books are balanced and you have ended with less than your capital. It is the same root that opens Sūrat al-ʿAṣr — *inna-l-insāna lafī khusr* — humanity is in *loss*, in deficit, in the red. This is the vocabulary of trade, of ledgers, of final accounting.

So the believers are using the language of business — but listen to what they put on the losing side of the ledger. *Inna-l-khāsirīna-lladhīna khasirū anfusahum* — "the true losers are those who lost **themselves**." Not their wealth. Not their houses. **Their own selves.** *Anfusahum* — their souls, their persons, the "I" at the center of everything.

[PAUSE]

Let this land, because it overturns the entire economy we live inside.

Every human being is a trader. You did not choose to be one; you simply are. You have been handed a fixed capital — your years, your breaths, your attention, your one irreplaceable self — and from the first day you have been *spending* it. Every hour is a transaction. You trade your life-capital for *something*: for money, for status, for pleasure, for comfort, for the approval of people who will not be there on the Day. The only question that has ever mattered is what you are getting in exchange — and whether, when the ledger finally closes, you have ended in profit or in *khusr*.

And the believers, watching from the other side, announce the result of the worst possible trade. There is a man who spent his entire capital — every breath, every year, the whole of his one self — *acquiring the world*. And he succeeded. He got the wealth, the name, the gaze that swept the room. But look at what it cost him: it cost him *the self he spent to buy it*. He traded the only non-renewable asset he had for things he had to leave at the door of the grave. When the books close, the world he bought is gone from his hands, and the self he paid is gone too. He did not just fail to profit. **He lost the principal.** He is in the red on the one account that cannot be reopened. *That*, say the believers, is the only loss worth the name.

And there is a second word on that losing side, and I do not want you to miss it: *wa ahlīhim* — "and **their families**." They lost themselves *and their families*.

Think about what that adds. A human being is anchored by two things: by *who they are* — the self — and by *who they belong to* — the bonds, the people, the belonging. These are the two deepest needs in the human soul, the vertical and the horizontal: identity and connection. And the verse names the ultimate catastrophe as the loss of *precisely these two*. Not just the self, but the self *and* the web of love it was meant to be held within. They arrive at the Day having lost the answer to "who am I" *and* the answer to "who is mine." Stripped of identity and stripped of belonging in a single stroke — alone, exposed, in the red, with no one beside them and no self left to stand on.

[PAUSE]

Here is the universal door back into this. We are all, right now, quietly afraid of *loss* — and almost always we are afraid of the wrong loss. We grieve the lost job, the lost money, the lost opportunity. We lie awake over subtractions from our possessions. And meanwhile the one loss the Qur'an calls the *real* one — the slow leaking-away of the self, the trading of our irreplaceable years for things that cannot cross the grave with us — proceeds quietly, unmourned, unnoticed, every single day. We are diligent accountants of the wrong ledger.

So what does this change about tomorrow? It asks one question of every major expenditure of your life — and "expenditure" here means your *time*, your *attention*, your *effort*, the actual currency of your soul. Before you pour your one capital into a thing, ask: *when this purchase is complete, will I still own myself? Or am I spending the principal?* There are pursuits that, when you finish them, leave you *more* yourself, more anchored, closer to Allah and to the people He gave you. And there are pursuits that, when you finish them, have quietly consumed the buyer. The believer's whole financial discipline of the soul is to keep trading *toward* the self and never *away* from it — to end the day with the principal intact, and growing.

## Closing Synthesis

### The Architecture — A Root That Stands Twice

Now that we have done the work, let me show you something built into the bones of this verse that you could not have seen at the start.

There is a single Arabic root, *qāf-wāw-mīm* (ق-و-م), whose basic meaning is *to stand, to rise up, to be established*. And it appears in this verse **twice**, at two decisive moments — and the two appearances speak to each other across the whole ayah.

The first time, it is the name of the Day: *yawma-l-**qiyāmah*** — the Day of *Standing-Up*, the Day of Rising, the Day when all of creation is raised to its feet to stand before its Lord.

The second time, it is the closing word, the divine seal: *ʿadhābin **muqīm***. *Muqīm* — from that very same root — means *that which stays, abides, takes up residence, stands and does not leave*.

Do you feel the architecture? The Day of *Standing* — *qiyāmah* — gives way to a punishment that *stands and stays* — *muqīm*. The rising-up of all souls leads, for the wrongdoers, to a torment that takes up permanent residence. The same root that names the *event* names its *duration*. The verse opens onto a Day of rising and closes onto a punishment that, having risen, never sits back down. **The standing becomes a staying.** And that resonance is not decoration — it is the proof that the lasting-ness of the consequence is woven into the very fabric of the Day that produces it. You stand up that Day; for some, the standing never ends.

### Bringing It Together

Watch how the two halves of this verse are the same insight approached from two directions.

From the *outside*, the camera shows us a body: paraded, collapsed, eyes broken into a furtive sliver — the visible posture of total shame and total loss.

From the *inside*, the believers name what that posture *means*: this is a soul that has lost itself and its people, a trader ruined on the only account that mattered.

The averted eye and the empty ledger are the same catastrophe seen twice. The broken gaze *is* the bankruptcy made visible. The man who cannot lift his eyes is the man who spent his self and has nothing left to stand behind a gaze *with*. The shame in the body and the loss in the books are one reality — and the verse hands us both, the seen and the unseen, the maḥsūs and the maʿqūl, so that the thing we cannot picture (a soul in the red) becomes something we can: a man stealing glances from the corner of a half-shut eye.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Leave them open.

1. The deepest reflex of shame is to hide. Where in my life am I spending energy *hiding* from the only Witness whose verdict is final — when I could be *uncovering* to Him now, while it still ends in mercy?

2. When I close the books on an ordinary day — its hours, its attention, its effort — am I ending in profit, or am I quietly spending the principal? What did I trade my one self *for* today?

3. The verse names the ultimate loss as the self *and* the family. Am I tending the two anchors — who I am, and who is mine — or am I sacrificing both at the altar of things that cannot cross the grave?

4. Whose gaze am I actually living for? The full, frontal gaze of people in the room — or the gaze of the One before whom I will one day be *displayed*?

5. If the unnamed *it* of this verse — the looming reality I keep declining to name — were named honestly in my life, what would it be? And why have I not been saying its name?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The ruin of a soul is not first fire but *exposure* — to be paraded, eyes broken by disgrace, before the One it spent its whole self avoiding — and the only loss that has ever counted is the loss of that self, traded away for a world it had to leave at the door.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this verse asks you to be afraid in the small, paralyzing way. It asks you to *re-price* your life. To see that you are a trader, that your capital is leaking out of you by the hour, and that you still — *today* — hold the power to direct the trade. The wrongdoers in this scene begged, one verse earlier, for a way to return and spend differently. You have not been refused that. You are living inside the very return they were pleading for. The door they were knocking on from the wrong side, you are standing in front of, open, right now. Walk through it while it is still a door and not a verdict.

### A Brief Duʿā'

O Allah, You who see what is displayed and what is hidden — let me uncover myself before You now, in mercy, rather than be exposed before You then, in disgrace. Keep my gaze whole by keeping it lowered to You. And when my ledger is closed, let me not be among those who lost themselves; let me have spent my one self in buying nearness to You, and let me find that I still belong to You, and to the people You gave me, on the Day of Standing. Āmīn.
