# No Place to Turn Aside
### Surah Ash-Shura, Ayah 35

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **وَيَعْلَمَ ٱلَّذِينَ يُجَٰدِلُونَ فِىٓ ءَايَٰتِنَا مَا لَهُم مِّن مَّحِيصٍ**

In transliteration:

> *wa-yaʿlama-lladhīna yujādilūna fī āyātinā mā lahum min maḥīṣ*

And in plain English:

> "And so that those who dispute concerning Our signs may know — that for them there is no place of refuge."

Nine words. That's the whole ayah. Nine words, and one of them is the small word "not."

Now, before we open a single one of these words, I want to put you somewhere. Because this verse does not begin where your eye begins. It begins three verses earlier, out on the water.

### Rebuild the world

Picture the world of the people who first heard this. Mecca. A trading people. A people whose livelihood, whose wealth, whose entire imagination of "fortune" was tied to the caravan and to the sea. They knew what it was to load goods onto a ship and watch the coastline shrink to nothing behind you.

And Allah had just been speaking, in the verses right before this one, about exactly that. Listen to the run-up:

In verse 32, He says: *"And among His signs are the ships that run upon the sea like mountains."* Like mountains — these huge, heavy, impossible things, gliding on water as if weightless.

In verse 33: *"If He willed, He could still the wind, and they would remain motionless on its surface."* Sit with that picture for a second. The wind dies. The great ship — the mountain on the water — just stops. No oars are enough. No shouting helps. You are in the middle of the sea, and you are not moving, and the only thing that moves a sailing ship is a breath of air that you do not own and cannot command.

Then verse 34: *"Or He could wreck them for what they have earned — and He pardons much."*

And then — verse 35. Our verse. *"And so that those who argue about Our signs may know that they have no place of refuge."*

Do you feel the architecture already? Allah has just painted the single most vivid picture of *no-escape* available to that audience: a human being, stranded on the open sea, surrounded on every side by water, with no wind, no shore, no exit. And out of that picture, He lifts a single word and hands it to you for the disputers: **maḥīṣ.** No place to flee.

The physical sea is not the subject. The sea is the stepping stone. What Allah is showing you is a condition of the human soul — a condition that the open sea can only hint at.

### The hook

Here is what I need you to carry into the next hour. There are two kinds of people who end up with no escape. There is the believing sailor on the becalmed sea, who looks around, sees there is no refuge, and turns — with his whole heart — to the only One who can move the wind. That man's "no escape" becomes the doorway to God.

And there is another person in this verse. Someone who is *also* going to arrive at "no escape." But he arrives there differently. He arrives there **arguing.**

And what I want you to see — what this verse has been waiting to show you — is that **his arguing was itself the running.** The very thing he thought was his strength, his defense, his exit — was the flight. And the flight has no place to land.

Stay with me. Let's open the words.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yujādilūna* — the shape of a verb that means "to push back"

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch this word, because the entire weight of this first insight lives in the *shape* of a single verb, and you need to be able to see the shape.

Arabic verbs come in families, called "forms." Same three root letters, but you can pour them into different molds, and each mold changes the meaning in a predictable way. Think of it like this. In English we have "to see," and then "to oversee," and "to sight," and "to envision" — same idea of seeing, bent into different shapes. Arabic does this far more systematically. The root carries the raw meaning; the mold tells you *how* the action behaves.

Now, one of these molds — grammarians call it Form III, the *fāʿala* pattern — has a very particular flavor. Form III is the form of **directed, sustained engagement with another party.** When you take a verb and put it in Form III, you are usually saying: this action is aimed at someone, pushed toward someone, carried on back-and-forth with someone. It is not a private, internal act. It is an act *against* or *toward* a counterpart, and it tends to be effortful and drawn-out.

Hold that. Now here is our word.

> **يُجَٰدِلُونَ** — *yujādilūna*

The root is *j-d-l* (ج د ل). And the bare root, in the lexicons, is striking. *Jadala* in its origin means **to twist tightly, to braid, to make a rope firm by twisting strand against strand.** The Arabs spoke of *al-ajdal*, the firmly-twisted. The image underneath "argument" in Arabic is a **rope being twisted** — strand pulled against strand, tension wound into tension, until the thing is hard and tight and will not give.

And now Allah pours that root — twisting, tension, strand-against-strand — into Form III, the mold of *directed, sustained pushing-against-another.*

Do you see what the word is, before it even means anything? *Yujādilūna* is not "they had a doubt." It is not "a question crossed their mind." It is people **actively, effortfully, repeatedly twisting themselves against the signs of God.** Form III tells you it is aimed. The root tells you it is tense, wound-up, strand against strand. And the tense — the imperfect, *yujādilūna* — tells you it is **ongoing.** Not "they argued once." They are *arguing, and arguing, and arguing.*

Notice something. This is a verb of **work.** Disbelief, in this verse, is not portrayed as laziness or as a gap in information. It is portrayed as **labor.** It takes effort to keep twisting the rope. It takes energy to keep pushing against. The disputer in this ayah is not resting. He is exerting himself. He is *busy.*

And here is what's remarkable, and I want you to feel the irony land slowly. All of that effort — all of that twisting, all of that directed force — is being spent on **fleeing.** We'll see in a moment that the last word of the verse is "escape." So the picture Allah is drawing is of a person spending enormous, sustained, exhausting energy... to get away. The argument is not a search for truth. The argument is a sprint. He is running, and the running takes everything he has.

[PAUSE]

Let me say clearly what this single word reveals, and then we move on: **The dispute against God's signs is not a failure of the mind. It is an exertion of the will.** Form III, against an other; the imperfect, never finished; the root, a rope twisted tight against itself. This is not someone who *couldn't* see. This is someone who is *working* not to.

That distinction is going to matter enormously when we reach the verse's verb of *knowing.*

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Maḥīṣ* — the word for the exit that isn't there

Now to the last word. The word the whole sentence has been falling toward.

> **مَّحِيصٍ** — *maḥīṣ*

Let me teach the concept first. In Arabic, there is a noun-pattern — the *mafʿil* pattern — that very often names **a place.** *Masjid*, from the root "to prostrate," is "the place of prostration." *Maghrib*, from "to set," is "the place of setting," the west. The *ma-* on the front, and that internal shape, frequently tells you: this is the **location** where the action happens.

So when you see *maḥīṣ*, your ear should immediately hear: **the place of *ḥayṣ*.** Now what is *ḥayṣ*?

The root is *ḥ-y-ṣ* (ح ي ص), and the lexicons are precise and beautiful about it. *Ḥāṣa yaḥīṣu* means **to swerve aside, to turn away, to dodge, to shy off from a thing.** Lisān al-ʿArab glosses it as *al-ʿudūl wa-l-hurūb* — turning aside and fleeing. It is the motion of an animal that bolts sideways. It is the flinch of a body that twists away from a blow. It is not a calm, planned walking-away. It is the **dodge.** The desperate sidestep. The last-second swerve to avoid being caught.

So *maḥīṣ* is **the place you swerve to. The bolt-hole. The sideways exit. The dodge that works.**

And listen to the word itself — *maḥīṣ.* Say it. There is a long, drawn *ee* sound and then it closes on that thin, hissing *ṣ.* It has the sound of something narrow, something that slips and slides and then hisses shut. The word *sounds* like a desperate squeezing-through that finds the gap sealed.

Now watch what Allah does with it.

> **مَا لَهُم مِّن مَّحِيصٍ** — *mā lahum min maḥīṣ*

Three things are happening in these four little words, and each one tightens the rope.

First: **mā** — the flat, total negative. "There is not."

Second — and don't miss this, because it is doing real grammatical work — that little word **min.** In English we'd translate "any." *Mā lahum min maḥīṣ* — "there is not, for them, **any** place of escape." In Arabic grammar this *min* is what's called *min al-istighrāqiyya*, the *min* of total comprehensiveness. Its only job is to slam the door on exceptions. Without it, you might read "they have no escape" and a sliver of your mind might whisper *...but maybe one.* With this *min*, that sliver is gone. **Not one. Not a single one. Not the smallest, narrowest, most hidden bolt-hole.** The *min* exists to negate even the exception you were about to imagine.

Third: the word is **indefinite.** Not *al-maḥīṣ*, "the escape." Just *maḥīṣ* — escape of any kind whatsoever, in the abstract, in every form it could possibly take. Every category of exit. Every species of dodge. Negated.

Put it together and feel the totality: *There is not, for them, any — of any kind — place to swerve aside to.*

[PAUSE]

Now bring back the first word. *Yujādilūna* — they are twisting, sustained, directed, effortful, against the signs. And *maḥīṣ* — there is no place to swerve. Do you see how the two words speak to each other across the verse?

**The arguing was an attempt at *ḥayṣ*.** The argument *is* the swerve. The disputation is the body twisting sideways to dodge the blow of the truth. They are *ḥāʾiṣīn* — they are dodging — through the very act of disputing. And the verse ends by telling you that the swerve has nowhere to go. *There is no maḥīṣ.* You can dodge all you like; there is no place that the dodge arrives at.

The man on the sea cannot swerve, because there is only water. The man in this verse cannot swerve, because there is only God. The sea was always just the picture. *Maḥīṣ* is the meaning.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Wa-yaʿlama* — the verb that the sentence cannot let stand still

There is one more thing, and it is hidden in the very first word, and it is the kind of thing the Quran does that nothing else does.

> **وَيَعْلَمَ** — *wa-yaʿlama*

"And [so that] they may know."

I want to tell you something the classical scholars noticed about how this word is *read*, because the grammar itself is enacting the meaning.

The reciters of the Quran differ — by sound transmission, all valid — on the final vowel of this verb. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both record it: Nāfiʿ and Ibn ʿĀmir read it *wa-yaʿlamu*, with a final *u* (rafʿ), making it a fresh, standing statement — "And He knows..." or "And they will know..." as a new, independent clause. The majority read it *wa-yaʿlama*, with a final *a* (naṣb).

And the grammarians explain *why* that *a* appears, and this is the part I find astonishing. Al-Baghawī says it is *ṣarf* — the verb has been **"turned."** Here is the situation. The earlier verbs in the passage were in a clipped, jussive mood. And Arabic has an instinct: it does not like to pile clipped form upon clipped form, jussive after jussive after jussive — *istikhfāfan wa-karāhatan li-tawālī al-jazm,* "for lightness, and out of dislike for successive clippings." So at this verb, the language **turns aside** from the expected form into the *naṣb*. The grammar itself *swerves.*

Sit with that for one moment. In the very verse whose final word is *maḥīṣ* — "a place to swerve aside" — the central verb is one the grammarians describe as having *itself swerved*, turned aside from its expected path. The form of the verse performs the meaning of the verse. The disputers seek a swerve and find none; the verb seeks relief and is turned. I offer that as wonder, not as doctrine — but the Author of this speech does not place such things by accident.

Now the meaning of the word itself. *Yaʿlamu* — from *ʿilm*, **knowledge.** And here is the deep, quiet irony, and it is the hinge of the entire ayah.

Who is the subject of "they may know"? **The disputers.** *Wa-yaʿlama-lladhīna yujādilūn* — "and so that those-who-dispute may know." The same people. The arguers. The ones twisting the rope.

Think about what an arguer is *claiming.* When you dispute, when you contend, when you spend all that Form III energy pushing against something — what are you implicitly asserting? **That you know.** Argument is a posture of knowledge. "I have grounds. I have a case. I see something you don't." The disputer is, by the very act of disputing, **performing knowing.**

And Allah says: a day is coming when they *will* know. *Wa-yaʿlama.* They will finally, actually, truly **know.**

Do you feel the blade in that?

The thing they were *pretending* to have — knowledge — they did not have. What they had was *jidāl*, the counterfeit. The twisting. The performance of certainty over an emptiness. And the verse promises that the counterfeit will one day be exchanged for the real thing, all at once, at the worst possible moment: **they will know — that there is no maḥīṣ.** Their first true act of knowledge will be the knowledge that there is no way out.

Al-Muyassar and al-Baghawī both gloss it plainly: they will know there is no refuge, no shelter, *idhā ʿāqabahum* — "when He brings the reckoning upon them." That is *when* the knowing arrives. Not in the comfortable seminar of dispute. In the moment of consequence.

[PAUSE]

And one last quiet detail. Notice the verse never tells you *what* they were disputing. *Yujādilūna fī āyātinā* — "they dispute concerning Our signs." Which sign? It doesn't say. Because it does not matter. This is not a verse about a particular argument that could be won with a particular answer. The *content* of the dispute is left blank on purpose. What is being described is not a position. It is a **posture toward God.** A man can dispute one sign today and a different sign tomorrow and have "won" neither and nothing has changed — because the disputing was never really about the sign. It was the swerve. It was the *ḥayṣ.* And the swerve has no destination.

[PAUSE]

So here is what the three words have shown us together. The arguing is an exertion — sustained, willed, twisting work *(yujādilūna).* It is, underneath, a flight — a swerve away from the blow *(the whole verse bends toward maḥīṣ).* And it is a counterfeit of knowing that will one day be confiscated and replaced with the real thing at the one moment it can no longer help — *they will know* there is no escape *(wa-yaʿlama).*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about you and me.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The argument is the running

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You have, somewhere in your life, an argument you keep ready. We all do. There is some claim that, if it were fully true, would *cost* you something — would require you to change, to stop, to surrender, to give up a thing you love or a story you tell about yourself. And against that claim, you have built an argument. You have it loaded. You can deploy it in a half-second. You've rehearsed it so many times it feels like conviction.

Here's the tell. Watch what happens in your body when someone presses on that exact spot. The argument comes out *fast.* It comes out *hot.* It comes out before you've really thought. That speed, that heat — that is not the temperature of someone calmly weighing evidence. **That is the temperature of someone fleeing.**

Because here is the thing modern psychology has circled and named and re-named a dozen ways — rationalization, motivated reasoning, defensive avoidance — but which this verse named fourteen centuries ago in a single word. *Jidāl.* The argument is very often not a tool for *finding* the truth. It is a tool for *not having to face it.* It is a wall you build, brick by rhetorical brick, between yourself and a conclusion you cannot afford to reach.

And the Quran's insight cuts deeper than the psychology, because the psychology will tell you *that* you're avoiding. The Quran tells you *what the avoidance is.* It is *ḥayṣ.* It is the swerve. It is the animal-bolt of a soul twisting sideways to keep from being caught by something it has, on some level, already seen.

Let me make this concrete, because this is not only about the loud unbeliever. This is about the believer too. The person who, when reminded of a prayer they've been neglecting, immediately produces five reasons their situation is different. The person who, when the truth of some habit is laid plainly before them, *changes the subject into a debate* — suddenly it's a discussion about the scholars, about the conditions, about whether the evidence is really so clear — and notice: the moment the conversation became an *argument*, the demand on the heart was lifted. That is the swerve doing its work. **The argument is so effective as an escape precisely because it feels like engagement.** You feel like you're dealing with the truth. You're talking *about* it constantly. But every sentence is a step sideways.

[PAUSE]

Now here is the movement, the turn that makes this verse not just diagnosis but mercy.

The man on the becalmed sea in verse 33 — what saves him is the *end* of his swerving. Look at what al-Qurṭubī says about that sailor: when the sea surrounds him and the ship goes still, *"they know there is no refuge but Allah, and no one to repel His decree if He wills their destruction — so they make their worship sincerely His."* The collapse of the swerve is the *birth* of sincerity. The moment he stops trying to dodge is the moment he turns, fully, to God.

So the question this verse lays at your feet is not *whether* you will arrive at "no escape." Everyone arrives there. The believer and the disputer both reach the place where there is no *maḥīṣ.* The only question is **whether you arrive there still arguing.**

Because if you arrive there having let go of the argument — having stopped twisting the rope, having let the swerve come to rest — then "no escape" is not a terror. It is the most freeing sentence in existence. *There is nowhere else to go.* All the exits you were exhausting yourself searching for were never real, and now you can stop searching, and turn, and fall into the only refuge that was ever there. The man with no *maḥīṣ* from God has, in the same instant, no *maḥīṣ* into anything *less* than God. The closing of every false door is the flinging-open of the only true one.

But if you arrive there *still arguing* — still mid-swerve, still twisting — then you will simply discover, at the worst possible time, that the swerve had no destination. That every brick in the wall was stacked over an abyss. That, as the verse says, *you will know* — and the first true thing you know will be that there was never anywhere to run.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Find the one argument you keep loaded. The fast, hot one. And ask yourself the single honest question: *Am I making this case because it's true — or because if I stopped making it, I'd have to change?* You don't have to win the inquiry today. You just have to stop pretending the swerve is a search. That alone turns you, by a few degrees, back toward the only refuge.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: The soul that keeps an exit — and the One reality that has no back door

Let me tell you about a habit so deep in us that we don't even see it as a habit. We see it as just... being a person.

We keep an exit. On everything.

We sign the lease but we note the early-termination clause. We start the job but we keep the resume warm. We enter the relationship but we hold a part of ourselves in reserve — a small door, kept oiled, in case we need to leave. We commit to the diet, the plan, the resolution — *unless.* There's always an *unless.* The human soul is an exit-builder. We are constantly, quietly, surveying every room we enter for where the back door is. It is, in worldly life, often wisdom. Don't put all your money in one place. Don't burn every bridge. Keep your options open.

The root of this is the same root as our verse. The exit we keep is a *maḥīṣ* — a place to swerve to if things go wrong. And the soul that keeps it is, in a sense, a soul perpetually in mid-flinch — never fully *in* the room, because always half-turned toward the door.

Now most of life rewards this. And so we generalize it. We come to assume, deep beneath conscious thought, that **everything has a back door.** That this is simply how reality is built. That for any pressure, there is somewhere to swerve.

And then this verse says one thing, very quietly, about the one relationship where it is not true.

*Mā lahum min maḥīṣ.* From God — there is no exit clause.

[PAUSE]

This is the reality the whole verse exists to deliver, and the open sea was the gentlest way Allah could deliver it. Because on land, the lie of the back door holds. There's always another road, another town, another option. But the sea strips the lie away. Out on the water, when the wind dies, you finally *see* the truth that was always true on land too: that you were never actually in control of the thing that moves you. You just couldn't see the water all the way around you.

The disputer's whole project — and ours, in our smaller way — is to live as though the relationship with God has an exit clause like everything else. As though, if the demands get too heavy, there's a side door. *I'll deal with it later. I'll repent eventually. Surely there's a reading of this that lets me off. Surely a God this merciful wouldn't really...* — and there it is, the swerve, the search for the *maḥīṣ.*

And the mercy of the verse — because this *is* mercy, delivered now, while it can still help — is that it tells you the truth about the architecture **before** you're standing in the becalmed boat. There is no back door from God. Not because He is a trap, but because **He is the ground of everything.** You cannot exit into a place He does not hold up. Every "elsewhere" you might flee to is itself sustained by Him. The exit and the room are made of the same divine sustaining. There is no *out,* because there is no *outside.*

Now — and this is the paradox I want to leave standing, not resolve — the very fact that there is no escape *from* Him is identical to the fact that there is no end *to* Him. The soul that finally understands *mā lahum min maḥīṣ* is freed from the most exhausting labor a human being undertakes: the endless, lifelong surveying of every room for the back door. Imagine setting that down. Imagine, for the first time, being *fully in the room* — fully present before your Lord — because you have finally understood there is nowhere else, and so nothing is held in reserve, and the door you kept oiled your whole life can at last just... close. And in the closing, you are not trapped. You are *home.* A man stops looking for the exit only when he is finally somewhere he never needs to leave.

The disputer experiences "no escape" as a wall slamming shut. The lover of God experiences the identical reality as an embrace from which he never wants to be released. Same four words. *Mā lahum min maḥīṣ.* Two completely different worlds — and the only difference is which way your heart was facing when the door closed.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Pick one place in your life with God where you've kept an exit clause — one *"I'll deal with it later,"* one *"surely there's a way out of this obligation,"* one reserved door. And try, just once, closing it on purpose. Not because you're trapped — but as an act of trust. Say: *here, with You, I am keeping no back door.* Watch what happens to your heart when, in even one small place, you stop scanning for the exit. That is a foretaste, now, of the freedom the disputer will never taste — because he insists on scanning until the very end.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the four verses together, because the structure is the proof.

- **42:32** — The ships running on the sea: a sign, a gift, motion granted.
- **42:33** — *If He willed, the wind would still:* the gift suspended. You are stranded. No escape — by becalming.
- **42:34** — *Or He could wreck them... and He pardons much:* no escape — by destruction; and yet, mercy held open.
- **42:35** — *And those who dispute will know there is no maḥīṣ:* the physical no-escape of the sea lifted up into the spiritual no-escape of accountability.

Allah did not begin with the abstract truth. He began with a ship. He let you feel the wind die. He let you stand on the motionless deck with the water on every side. *And only then* — when you already knew in your body what "nowhere to flee" feels like — did He say the word for your soul: *maḥīṣ.* The sea was the soil. The stranded sailor was the seed. The word "escape" was the harvest. He prepared you to understand the unseen by letting you feel the seen.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The grammar showed us: *jidāl* is sustained, effortful, twisting work *(Form III, the rope drawn tight)* — and it bends, across the whole verse, toward *maḥīṣ,* the swerve, the dodge. So the argument **is** the running. That's the language.

The themes showed us: we argue to avoid surrender, and we keep an exit on everything — until we meet the one reality that has no exit clause. That's the life.

They meet in the verse's quiet verb of knowing. *Wa-yaʿlama* — **they will know.** The disputer's tragedy is that he confused his arguing for knowing. He had the posture of certainty over an emptiness. And the day comes when the counterfeit is confiscated and the real thing is handed to him all at once — and his very first act of true knowledge is the knowledge that there was never any way out.

The believer learns this now, while it is still mercy. The disputer learns it then, when it is only verdict. Same knowledge. Different door.

### Questions to carry

1. What is the one argument I keep loaded — the fast, hot one — and if I'm honest, am I making that case because it's *true*, or because if I stopped, I'd have to *change*?
2. Where, in my relationship with God, have I quietly kept an exit clause — an *"I'll deal with it later,"* a reserved back door?
3. When pressure comes onto a truth I'd rather not face, do I *engage* it — or do I *change it into a debate* so the demand on my heart is lifted?
4. The sailor's "no escape" became his sincerity. Is there a place in my life where the closing of every exit might actually be the opening of the only door that matters?
5. If I will one day *know* there is no *maḥīṣ* — would I rather learn it now as freedom, or then as terror?

### The one-sentence distillation

The very arguing by which a soul tries to dodge the truth is itself the flight that has no place to land — and the one who counterfeits knowledge through dispute will, at last, truly *know*: that from God there was never any place to turn aside.

### The closing invitation

You don't have to win the inquiry tonight. You only have to stop calling the swerve a search. Find one loaded argument and hold it honestly. Find one reserved exit and, as an act of trust, let it close. Not because you are trapped — but because the only One with no back door is also the only One you would never, in your right heart, want to leave. The closing of every false door is the opening of the only true one. Let one door close tonight.

### A brief duʿā

> O Allah, You who still the wind and move the ships — we confess the arguments we have kept loaded to flee from You, and the exits we have oiled against the day we might want to leave. Take from us the labor of the swerve. Let us learn now, as mercy, what the heedless will learn too late as verdict: that there is no refuge from You except in You. And when every door closes, let us find that we are not trapped — but home.

*Āmīn.*
