# The One Who Receives the Turning

## Surah Ash-Shura, Ayah 25

> وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى يَقْبَلُ ٱلتَّوْبَةَ عَنْ عِبَادِهِۦ وَيَعْفُوا۟ عَنِ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تَفْعَلُونَ

**Wa huwa alladhī yaqbalu at-tawbata ʿan ʿibādihi wa yaʿfū ʿani-s-sayyiʾāti wa yaʿlamu mā tafʿalūn.**

*"And it is He who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons misdeeds, and He knows what you do."*

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let's place ourselves in the moment this was spoken.

Two ayahs earlier, the Prophet ﷺ has been told to say something that must have sounded almost impossibly gentle: *"I ask of you no reward for this — except love for those near of kin."* No payment. No tribute. Only that you not hate the people closest to him.

And then, in the very next breath, the surah records what some of them threw back at him. Not gratitude. An accusation. **"Or do they say: he has fabricated a lie against Allah?"** (42:24). Think about the weight of that. The most serious charge a human being could make against a messenger — that he is putting words in God's mouth, that the entire revelation is a forgery, that the man standing before them is a liar in the name of the Divine.

So now hold the emotional climate. There has been slander. There has been the ugliest thing a person can say. The atmosphere is charged with accusation, suspicion, betrayal.

And what comes next?

You would expect thunder. You would expect a verse of warning, of threat, of "watch what you say about Me." That is what the human heart braces for after an insult of that magnitude.

Instead, the very next thing Allah chooses to tell them about Himself is this:

**He is the One who keeps receiving the turning of those who turn back to Him. He keeps wiping away the wrongs. And He knows — fully, exactly — what you do.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the question I want us to carry through everything that follows. Why *here*? Why, at the exact moment of accusation, does Allah introduce Himself not as the Avenger, but as the One who receives repentance? And why does He end this sentence — a sentence soaked in mercy — with the words *"and He knows what you do"*?

Because something is hidden in the architecture of this ayah. The order of the words is not random. The choice of one small preposition is not random. And the sudden shift from talking *about* them to talking *to you* — that is not random either.

Let's go slowly. There is more here than the eye catches on the first reading.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: The Preposition That Carries the Sin Away

I want to start with something almost invisible. A two-letter word. The kind of word your eye slides over without stopping.

But first, let me teach you something about how prepositions work — in any language — before we look at the Arabic.

Think about the difference between these two English sentences:
- "She took the money **from** him."
- "She took the burden **off** him."

Both are "taking." But the second one — *off* — carries a sense of relief, of removal, of a weight being lifted *away* from the person. The preposition is doing emotional work. It is telling you the *direction* of the action and what happens to the person on the other end.

Arabic is exquisitely sensitive to this. And in this ayah, Allah does not say He accepts repentance *min* (مِن) His servants — the ordinary, expected word for "from." He says He accepts it **ʿan** (عَنْ) His servants.

عَنْ.

Now, the core meaning of عَنْ in Arabic — documented across the classical lexicons — carries the sense of *mujāwaza*: moving **away from**, passing **off of**, distance and removal. When an Arab says a thing happened *ʿan* something, there is a sense of departure, of moving off and beyond.

So sit with this. The expected phrase would be "He accepts repentance *min* — from — His servants," meaning simply He receives it. But the chosen phrase, "He accepts repentance *ʿan* His servants," opens a second layer that the ordinary word would not.

What this construction invites us to feel is this: **He does not merely receive the repentance. He receives it in a way that lifts it off them.** The turning is taken, and in the taking, the weight is moved *away from* the servant. عَنْ is the preposition of a burden coming off a back.

[PAUSE]

Let me be precise about what is grounded and what is interpretation. The lexical meaning of عَنْ — that it carries *mujāwaza*, the sense of "off of / away from" — that is solid, documented Arabic grammar. The application — that this colors the acceptance of repentance with the image of a weight being removed — is the contemplative invitation the grammar opens. I want you to hold it as an invitation, not as a grammatical proof. But it is an invitation the language genuinely extends.

And notice how this matches everything else the Quran says about *tawba*. The root of "repentance" here — **t-w-b** (توب) — does not actually mean "to feel sorry." Its root meaning is **to return**. To turn back. To come home to a place you had wandered from.

So look at the whole picture the words are painting. The servant *returns* — that is tawba. And Allah *receives that return in a way that carries the burden off of him* — that is the عَنْ. The vocabulary of this verse is the vocabulary of a journey home where, the moment you arrive, your luggage is taken from your hands.

That is the first thing hidden in a two-letter word.

## Element Two: The Tense That Never Stops

Now I want you to notice something about the *shape* of the three verbs in this ayah. Let me teach the concept first.

In Arabic, as in English, a verb can point to a finished action or a continuing one. "He forgave" is a closed event — it happened, it's over. "He forgives" is open — it is his ongoing nature, something he does and keeps doing.

Arabic has a specific verb form for this open, ongoing, habitual sense. It is called the *muḍāriʿ* — the imperfect. And here is what is remarkable about our ayah:

**Every single verb in it is in this ongoing, never-closing form.**

- *yaqbalu* (يَقْبَلُ) — He **keeps** accepting.
- *yaʿfū* (يَعْفُو) — He **keeps** pardoning.
- *yaʿlamu* (يَعْلَمُ) — He **keeps** knowing.

Not "He accepted." Not "He pardoned once." The grammar refuses to close the door. These are not three things Allah did. They are three things Allah *is doing*, continuously, in the eternal present — including this very moment, as you read.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. Why does it matter that the tense is open rather than closed?

Because the human heart, when it sins, instinctively believes the window has closed. *I had my chance. I crossed a line. The offer expired.* That is the lie the nafs whispers — that mercy was a one-time event you've already missed.

But the grammar of this ayah will not allow that lie to stand. *Yaqbalu* — present, ongoing, unfinished — means the receiving is happening now, and now, and now. There is no past tense here for you to have missed. The acceptance is not a door that opened once in history. It is a door that has never been anything other than open.

Here's what's remarkable: this connects to the famous statement of the Prophet ﷺ — that Allah stretches out His hand by night so the wrongdoer of the day may turn back, and stretches out His hand by day so the wrongdoer of the night may turn back, *until the sun rises from the west*. The hand is not extended once. It is extended continuously. That hadith is the lived experience of this grammatical tense. The *muḍāriʿ* is Allah's hand, still outstretched.

## Element Three: The Erasure — and the Sudden Turn Toward You

Two more things, and they belong together.

First, the word *yaʿfū* (يَعْفُو) — "He pardons." The root is **ʿ-f-w** (عفو). I want you to know what this root physically pictures, because the Quran loves to point at the unseen through the seen.

In the language of the Arabs, *ʿafā* is used for the wind that blows over a campsite until the traces of the tents are **effaced** — gone, smoothed over, as if no one had ever camped there. It is used for vegetation that overgrows a path until the path *disappears*. The core image is not "to excuse." It is **to erase until no trace remains.**

So *yaʿfū ʿani-s-sayyiʾāt* is not "He overlooks your bad deeds." It is far more than that. It is: **He effaces them. He blows over the campsite of your sin until the sand is smooth and there is no evidence a tent ever stood there.**

[PAUSE]

There is a difference between someone who *forgives but remembers* and someone who *erases*. We have all been "forgiven" by people who keep the receipt. Who file the wrong away and produce it later. The human "pardon" is so often a pardon with a footnote.

*ʿAfw* has no footnote. It is the wind over the sand. The classical scholars note this is a higher station than mere forgiveness (*maghfira*) — *maghfira* covers the sin over (the root means to cover, like a helmet), but *ʿafw* removes it entirely. To cover is to hide what is still there. To efface is to make it as though it never was.

And now — the final move of the ayah, and the most psychologically stunning.

After all this mercy — the receiving, the lifting away, the erasing — the sentence ends:

**wa yaʿlamu mā tafʿalūn** — *"and He knows what you do."*

Notice what just happened grammatically. For the whole verse, Allah has been speaking *about* people in the third person — "His **servants**," "they," them, those over there. And then, in the last three words, He turns and speaks *to* you directly: **"what YOU do."**

This shift — from speaking *about* someone to suddenly speaking *to* them — is a known and deliberate feature of Quranic rhetoric, called *iltifāt*. It is the verbal equivalent of a speaker who has been describing a crowd to an audience, and then suddenly turns, looks directly into your eyes, and says: *"and I know what you did."*

[PAUSE]

Feel the jolt of that. The verse could have stayed comfortable and distant — "He accepts repentance from His servants and pardons their misdeeds and knows what *they* do." You would have nodded along, thinking about other people. But the grammar reaches out of the page, grabs you by the collar, and makes it personal: **what *you* do.**

That is the genius of the placement. Let's give it the attention it deserves in Part Two.

### Bridge

So the language has shown us three things. A preposition that lifts the burden off the back. A tense that never closes the door. And a word that erases without a trace — followed by a sudden, direct turn toward *you*: *He knows what you do.*

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the deepest fear in the human heart.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Terror of Being Known

Let me start somewhere very human, far from the page.

There is a particular kind of loneliness that the most "connected" generation in history knows intimately. It is the loneliness of being *partially* known. You have a version of yourself for work. A version for your parents. A version for your friends. A polished version for the screen. And underneath all of them, there is a self that no one has met — the one who knows what you actually did, what you actually thought, the thing you would die before anyone discovered.

And here is the quiet agony of it: **the people who love you, love a version of you that doesn't include your worst moment.** And the worst moment, the hidden thing, sits inside you whispering: *if they knew the whole of it, they would not stay.*

This is why intimacy is terrifying. To be fully known is to risk being fully rejected. So we manage. We curate. We let people close — but not all the way close. We keep one room locked.

[PAUSE]

Now bring that human reality to the last three words of our ayah.

*Wa yaʿlamu mā tafʿalūn* — **"and He knows what you do."**

Watch *where* Allah places this. He does not open with it. He does not say, "I know what you do — so beware, here is My mercy if you behave." That would make the mercy *conditional on Him not knowing too much*. That would make it the human kind of forgiveness — the kind that survives only as long as you keep the worst hidden.

No. He places His complete knowledge **at the end**, *after* the receiving, *after* the lifting of the burden, *after* the total erasure. The structure is saying something the human heart can barely hold:

**The acceptance came first. The knowing was there all along.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see what that dismantles?

You have spent your life believing that to be loved you must remain partly hidden — that the locked room is the price of belonging. And here is the One who *had the key the whole time*. There is no room He has not seen. *Yaʿlamu mā tafʿalūn* — He knows what you do, what you did, the thing you have never said aloud, the version of you that no human being has met.

And He is *still* the One who receives your turning. He is *still* the One who lifts it off your back. He is *still* the One who erases it like wind over sand.

**The mercy is not offered to the curated self. It is offered to the fully-known self.** The very self you were sure was unlovable is the self the offer was written for.

This is the thing no human relationship can give you, and it is why this ayah lands where nothing else can. Every human pardon is granted in partial ignorance — they forgive what they *know*. Allah's pardon is granted in total knowledge. He is the only one who can forgive the *whole* of you, because He is the only one who has seen the whole of you.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time the hidden thing whispers *you are too far gone, if anyone knew*— answer it with the order of this ayah. He already knows. The knowing did not cancel the welcome. The welcome was built *for* the known. So you can stop performing for the One you cannot fool — and that exhaustion of pretending, you are allowed to set it down.

## Theme Two: Why Mercy Showed Up After the Insult

Now let's return to where we began — the world around this ayah.

Remember the sequence. Slander first (*"he has fabricated a lie against Allah"*), and then *this* — the announcement of the God who receives the turning. Let me put a human face on why that ordering is almost unbearable in its mercy.

Imagine you have been deeply, publicly wronged by someone. They have lied about you, attacked your integrity, accused you of the one thing you would never do. And now imagine that in the very moment of that accusation, your response is to announce — not your hurt, not your defense — but the standing fact that *your door is open to them if they ever turn back.*

That is not weakness. That is a kind of strength most of us have never touched. It is the strength of someone whose mercy is not *reactive*. It does not rise and fall with how they are treated. It is structural. It is simply *who they are*, regardless of what was just done to them.

[PAUSE]

Now lift that to the Divine, where it belongs.

The placement of this ayah teaches us that Allah's mercy is not a *response* to our goodness. If it were, it would have no business appearing right after an accusation of forgery. It appears there precisely to show you that **the offer of return does not depend on the worthiness of the one returning.** The people had just done the worst thing. And the open door is announced *to them*, in that moment, unprompted.

One of the narrations preserved by the classical commentators — and there are differing accounts of the occasion here, so I offer this as one of the traditions, reported among others — connects this verse to people who had harbored ugly suspicions in their hearts about the Prophet ﷺ, and then regretted it. And the verse comes as the reassurance: *that turning, too, He receives.* Even the sin of the heart. Even the suspicion you are ashamed you entertained.

[PAUSE]

And here is where the human experience of repentance gets transformed.

We tend to think of tawba as *us* climbing back up to *Him*. A long, exhausting climb, and we are never sure we've climbed high enough. But look again at the grammar we uncovered. The verb is *yaqbalu* — **He receives.** The motion that the verse emphasizes is not your climbing. It is His receiving. The preposition عَنْ told us He lifts the weight *off* you as He takes it. The tense told us the door was never closed.

So the truth is the reverse of what the tired heart believes. **You are not clawing your way up to a reluctant God. You are taking one step of return toward a God who is already in continuous motion toward you** — already receiving, already lifting, already erasing. Your job was never to complete the climb. Your job was to *turn*. The receiving was always His.

There is a related ayah where this becomes almost overwhelming: when the three who were left behind finally repented, the Quran says Allah *"turned toward them so that they would turn"* (9:118) — His turning came *first*, to *enable* theirs. The same root, *t-w-b*, used for both Him and them. Your tawba is the echo. His was the original sound.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Stop waiting until you feel "ready" or "clean enough" to return. That readiness is a myth that keeps the door theoretically open and practically shut. The ayah does not say He receives the *qualified*. It says He receives *the turning* — *at-tawba* — the bare act of facing back toward Him. Turn now, as you are, mid-mess, and let the receiving be His problem. It already is.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture Revealed

Now that we've done the work, look at how the whole ayah is built. It has a hidden order, and the order *is* the message:

1. **يَقْبَلُ ٱلتَّوْبَةَ عَنْ عِبَادِهِ** — He receives the turning, lifting it off His servants.
2. **وَيَعْفُوا۟ عَنِ ٱلسَّيِّـَٔاتِ** — He erases the wrongs without a trace.
3. **وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تَفْعَلُونَ** — and He knows what you do.

The deepest pastoral move in the entire verse is that **number three comes last.** Every other arrangement would have wounded. *"He knows what you do — and accepts repentance"* makes the mercy sound like a concession wrung from a God who'd rather not. But *"He receives, He erases — and He knows"* tells you the knowing was the ground the mercy stood on the whole time. He was never going to be surprised by your file. He wrote the offer already holding it.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes are the same truth from two directions.

The **عَنْ** that lifts the burden off the back — that is the relief of being able to set down the locked-room self.

The **never-closing tense** — that is the answer to "I've missed my chance."

The **ʿafw that erases like wind over sand** — that is the only pardon with no footnote, because it is granted by the only One who saw the whole of you.

And the **sudden turn to "what *you* do"** — that is Allah reaching off the page, past the version of you that other people know, to address the self that no one has met — and welcoming *that* one.

## Questions to Carry

1. Which locked room have you assumed disqualifies you — and what changes when you realize the One whose welcome matters most already has the key?
2. Have you been treating tawba as a climb *you* must complete, or as a turn that *He* receives? Which one does the grammar of this ayah describe?
3. Whose forgiveness, in your life, has come *with a footnote* — and have you assumed Allah's works the same way?
4. If His mercy showed up right after an accusation of forgery, what does that say about the things you've decided are "too much" to bring back to Him?
5. What would you stop performing if you truly believed you could not fool the only One worth being honest with?

## One-Sentence Distillation

**The same One who sees everything you have ever done is the One still reaching to receive your turning and erase the deed without a trace — so the knowledge you fear is the very ground your forgiveness was built on.**

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to earn your way back into being known. You are already fully known — and the verdict of the One who knows you completely is not rejection but an open, never-closing door. The invitation is simply to stop hiding from the One you cannot hide from, and to turn — as you are, today — toward the receiving that was there all along.

## A Brief Duʿā

O Allah, You who keep receiving the turning and lifting it off our backs — we turn to You now, mid-mess, not waiting to be clean. You who erase the wrong until no trace remains — efface what we are ashamed even to name. And You who know exactly what we do: let the knowing that we feared become the mercy that we trust. *Āmīn.*
