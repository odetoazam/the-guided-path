# The Argument That Has No Ground to Stand On

### Surah Ash-Shura, Ayah 16

> [ayah:42:16] وَٱلَّذِينَ يُحَآجُّونَ فِى ٱللَّهِ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا ٱسْتُجِيبَ لَهُۥ حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ وَعَلَيْهِمْ غَضَبٌ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ

*Wa'lladhīna yuḥājjūna fi'llāhi min baʿdi mā stujība lahu ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍatun ʿinda rabbihim wa-ʿalayhim ghaḍabun wa-lahum ʿadhābun shadīd.*

> "And those who argue concerning Allah after He has already been answered — their argument is collapsed in the sight of their Lord; upon them is wrath, and for them is a severe punishment."

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Behind the Words

Let me ask you to do something before we even open this ayah.

I want you to picture a courtroom. The trial is over. The evidence has been heard. The verdict has been delivered, the gavel has come down, the people have filed out of the room and gone home. And now — *now* — one man stands up in the empty courtroom and begins to argue his case. Loudly. Passionately. Bringing exhibit after exhibit. Demanding to be heard.

There is something almost unbearable about that image. Not because his points are necessarily weak — but because of the **timing**. He is arguing into a room where the matter has already been settled. The energy he is pouring out has nowhere to land.

Hold that picture. Because that is, very precisely, the human situation this ayah is describing.

To feel the full weight of it, you have to know where we are standing in the surah.

This is Surah Ash-Shura — "Consultation" — a Meccan surah threaded through with one great theme: that the message Muhammad ﷺ brought is not a new religion, not a private invention, but the **same** revelation given to Nuh, to Ibrahim, to Musa, to ʿIsa — one continuous light passed down across the whole human story. *"He has ordained for you of religion what He enjoined upon Nuh"* (42:13). The same. Always the same.

And so just one ayah before ours, in verse 15, Allah instructs His Prophet on exactly how to carry himself. *Call to this. Stay firm. Don't follow their desires.* And then He gives him a line to say — and listen to the last words of it:

*"Allah is our Lord and your Lord. For us our deeds, and for you your deeds. **There is no argument between us and you** — lā ḥujjata baynanā wa baynakum. Allah will gather us together, and to Him is the destination."*

**No argument.** *Lā ḥujjata.* The matter is so clear, the truth so settled, that there is nothing left to dispute. Close the file.

[PAUSE]

And the very next breath — verse 16, our ayah — opens with: *"And those who argue concerning Allah…"*

*Wa'lladhīna yuḥājjūna.*

Do you see what just happened? Allah says: *there is no argument.* And immediately tells us about the people who argue **anyway**. The same root — Ḥ-J-J — appears in both. In verse 15 there is *no ḥujja*. In verse 16, *their ḥujja* is something. We are going to find out what.

So here is the hook. Here is the question I want lodged in you for the next hour:

What exactly happens to an argument raised against a truth that has already won? Not refuted — *too late.* Not wrong — *out of time.* What does Allah call an argument like that? And what does He tell us about the ground beneath the feet of the one who keeps making it?

The answer is one Arabic word. And that word is a picture. Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yuḥājjūna* — The Verb of Two People Pushing

Let's begin with a feature of Arabic that English simply does not have, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

In English, a verb mostly tells you *what* is happening: he ran, she spoke, they argued. Arabic does that too — but it can also tell you, baked right into the *shape* of the verb, the **manner and direction** of the action. The Arabic verb comes in patterns — scholars call them "forms" — and each pattern bends the bare meaning in a particular way.

Here is an everyday example to anchor it. Take a simple root meaning "to know." In one form it means *he knew.* Shift the pattern, and it becomes *he taught* — he caused knowing in someone else. Shift it again, and it becomes *they exchanged knowledge back and forth.* Same three letters. The **shape** carries the relationship.

Now hold onto that, because one of these patterns is built specifically for **two parties pushing against each other.** Grammarians call it the third form — the *mufāʿala* pattern. It takes an action and makes it mutual, reciprocal, contested. Picture the difference between *carrying* something and *wrestling* someone. Wrestling needs two. The form itself implies an opponent, effort, a back-and-forth straining for the upper hand.

[PAUSE]

Now look at the word Allah chose:

**يُحَآجُّونَ** — *yuḥājjūna.*

The root is Ḥ-J-J. And the verb is in exactly that third form — the form of two parties contending. Notice you can even *hear* it: that long, stretched *ā* in the middle — *yu-ḥĀĀ-jjūna* — the vowel held out like the action itself being dragged out, strained, prolonged. This is not the verb for a quiet private doubt. This is not someone sitting alone wondering. This is the verb for **someone who has stepped into the ring.** Someone arguing *at* you, *against* you, pushing to win.

Now sit with the root itself, because it is doing something extraordinary here.

Ḥ-J-J is one of the great roots of the Arabic language. From it comes **ḥujja** — a proof, an argument, the very thing you bring to *win* a case. From it comes the word for setting out toward a fixed destination with intent — the same root that gives us *Ḥajj*, the pilgrimage. At its heart, this root is about **aiming at a target, advancing toward a goal, pressing a point home.**

So feel the picture forming. These people are not drifting. They are *aiming.* They are marshalling their proofs, advancing on a target, pressing. They have a *ḥujja* and they are wielding it.

And what is the target? *Yuḥājjūna **fi'llāh*** — they argue **concerning Allah.** About God Himself. The most absurd contest imaginable — to step into the ring against the One who made the ring, the ring's floor, and the legs you're standing on.

Here is what is remarkable. Allah does not say their argument is *weak.* He does not say they *lost.* He uses the verb of a vigorous, mutual, full-effort contest — *yuḥājjūna* — and then, in the very next words, He is going to tell us that all of that straining effort lands on… nothing. He grants them the full dignity of the struggle in the grammar, precisely so that the collapse, when it comes, is total.

[PAUSE]

But there's one more thing the verb won't tell us on its own — *when* are they arguing? And that is where the next phrase changes everything.

---

### Element Two: *Min Baʿdi Mā Stujība Lahu* — Arguing Into a Closed Room

Read the phrase again: *min baʿdi mā stujība lahu* — "**after** He has already been answered."

Let's do this slowly, because two small things here carry enormous weight: the word **"after,"** and a verb that has been deliberately set in the passive.

First, "after." *Min baʿd.* Remember our empty courtroom. The whole sting of this ayah is in the timing. They are not arguing *before* the truth arrived, when arguing might at least make sense. They are arguing *after.* After what?

Look at the verb: **ٱسْتُجِيبَ** — *ustujība.* The root is J-W-B, "to answer, to respond." And this is a special form of it — the tenth form, the one that carries the sense of *a call being met, a summons being answered favorably.* This is, in fact, the very same verb the Quran uses for **Allah answering prayers** — *ujību daʿwata'l-dāʿ*, "I answer the call of the caller" (2:186); *fa'stajāba lahum rabbuhum*, "so their Lord answered them" (3:195).

So the image is of a **call that has been answered.** The daʿwa — the invitation to the truth — went out, and it was met. People responded. They came. The classical commentators fill in the picture: *min baʿdi mā stujība lah* means after the people had already responded to the call and entered into the faith — because the proof had become manifest, the truth had become plain, the matter had been settled in the open. As al-Baghawi puts it: *after the people responded to him and entered his religion, because his sign had become clear.*

[PAUSE]

Now feel the full shape of it. The call went out. **It was answered.** The truth stood up and walked among people and they embraced it. The verdict came down. The room emptied.

*And then* these people stand up to argue.

This is why I keep returning to the courtroom. It is not that they are too dim to make a point. It is that they are making it **into a settled reality.** They are pushing against a door that is already open. They are litigating a case that has already been decided — not by a weak judge they might appeal, but by the manifest arrival of the truth itself.

And now notice something easy to miss. The verb *ustujība* is **passive.** "He has *been* answered." Allah does not say *"after the people answered."* He hides the people. He lifts them out of the sentence.

Why? Because in Arabic, when you move a verb to the passive, you turn the spotlight off the *doer* and onto the *event itself.* The grammar is telling you: it does not matter *who* answered, how many, which tribe, which year. **The answering happened.** It is a completed fact, standing on its own, independent of any particular crowd. The truth has been received by the world — full stop. The reality is established and these arguers cannot reach behind it.

And there is a tender ambiguity in *lahu* — "answered **to Him.**" To whom was the call answered? To Allah, whose call it ultimately is? To the Prophet ﷺ who carried it? To the message itself? The Arabic lets all of these stand at once, and they collapse into one truth: **the response has been given. The thing they are arguing against is not a proposal. It is a fact that already lives in the world.**

So now we have the full setup. We have people in the full vigor of contention — *yuḥājjūna.* We know their timing is hopeless — *after the answer was already given.* All the tension is loaded. Allah pulls the trigger with a single word — and it is the most physical, the most visual word in the entire ayah.

---

### Element Three: *Dāḥiḍa* — The Ground That Slides Out From Under You

Here is the verdict on their argument:

**حُجَّتُهُمْ دَاحِضَةٌ** — *ḥujjatuhum dāḥiḍa.*

"Their *ḥujja*…" — there is that root again, Ḥ-J-J. Their **proof,** their **argument,** the very thing they were so confident wielding. The noun and the verb come from the same root, so in Arabic it almost rings like *their arguing-thing is…* — and then comes the word that lands like a dropped weight:

**Dāḥiḍa.**

Now. Most translations give you "false," "invalid," "null," "refuted." Those are all true. But they are flat. They are the *meaning* with the *picture* drained out of it. And this ayah lives in the picture.

The root is D-Ḥ-Ḍ. And what does it actually describe in the Arabic of the desert? **Slipping. The foot losing its grip. Ground that gives way.** From this root the Arabs called a slippery, treacherous patch of ground a *madḥaḍa* — a place where your foot shoots out from under you. To *daḥaḍa* is for the foot to skid, to slide, to find no purchase.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. Don't rush past it.

Allah does not say their argument is *weak,* as though it were a frail structure that might still bear a little weight. He does not even say it is *broken,* as though it once stood and then fell. He reaches for the image of **a foot on wet, sliding ground.**

Think about what that is actually like. You go to put your weight down — you *commit* — and in the instant you trust it, the ground is gone. There is no moment of standing. There is no platform, however small, where you get to plant yourself and be heard. The very act of leaning your weight into it is the act of falling.

**That** is *dāḥiḍa.* Their argument is not a structure that fails inspection. It is **ground that cannot be stood on.** Every time they put their weight on it, it slides out from under them.

And here is where the whole ayah opens, because do you see the physical reality pointing to the unseen one?

This is *istidlāl al-maʿqūl bi'l-maḥsūs* — Allah teaching the unseen through the felt. We all know, in our bodies, the terror of slipping. The half-second of weightlessness when the foot goes. The lurch. Allah takes that bodily memory — something every human carries — and uses it to show you something you could never see directly: **what it actually is to build your life's stance against the truth of God.** It looks, from the inside, like standing firm. It looks like having a *ḥujja,* a position, a platform. But in reality there was never any ground there at all. The whole time, they were standing on a slope of ice, mistaking the moment before the fall for solid earth.

[PAUSE]

And where is this argument *dāḥiḍa* — where does the slipping happen? *ʿinda rabbihim* — "**in the sight of their Lord.**"

Notice carefully: not *ʿinda'llāh,* in the sight of God-the-distant. But *ʿinda **rabbihim*** — in the sight of **their Lord.** *Rabb* — the root R-B-B — is not a word of cold authority. It is the word for the one who **owns, nurtures, sustains, raises up.** The master who feeds, who tends, who brings to maturity. And it is fixed to them — *rabbi-**him,** **their** Lord.*

Feel the ache in that. These are people arguing *against* Him — and the ayah still calls Him **their** Lord. The very breath they use to argue, He is sustaining. The very mind that forms the argument, He shaped. He never stops being their *Rabb* even as they contend with Him. Their argument slides to nothing precisely in the presence of the One who is, this whole time, *holding them up.* The ground gives way exactly where the Sustainer is watching.

[PAUSE]

So let me draw the linguistic thread together before we step back.

We have a verb built for two parties straining against each other — *yuḥājjūna* — full of effort and intent and aim. We have a timing that makes all that effort hopeless — *after the call was already answered, after the truth already stood up in the world.* And we have a verdict that is not a refutation but a **picture of falling** — *dāḥiḍa,* the foot on ground that was never solid.

Three words. A struggle, a closed door, and a slope of ice.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about an argument long ago, but about the way a human heart can stake everything on ground that isn't there.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Strange Comfort of Arguing After You've Lost

Let me start not with the ayah, but with something you already know intimately.

You have watched someone lose an argument and refuse to stop. Maybe you've *been* that someone. The facts have come in. Some quiet part of you already knows the truth has shifted. And yet — you keep talking. You reach for one more point, one more angle, one more "but what about." You are not really trying to win anymore. You are trying not to *land.*

Psychologists have names for the machinery of this. **Motivated reasoning** — when the conclusion you need comes first and the "evidence" gets recruited afterward to defend it. The **sunk cost** instinct — when you keep pouring resources into a position precisely *because* you've already poured so much that walking away feels like admitting it was all wasted. There is a peculiar human truth here: **the more a person has invested in a stance, the harder it becomes to abandon it — even after reality has already moved on without them.**

Now bring the ayah back.

*Wa'lladhīna yuḥājjūna fi'llāhi min baʿdi mā stujība lah.*

This is that exact human machinery — lifted up to its highest and most serious object. These are people arguing about *God,* with full vigor, *after* the truth has already been answered and embraced. The commentators give us faces for them. Some, like Qatada, say they were People of the Book who argued, *"Our scripture came before yours, our prophet before yours, so we are better"* — clinging to a precedence that the arrival of the final message had already moved past. Others, like Mujahid as Qurtubi reports, say they were those who **imagined the old order would simply come back** — *tawahhamū anna'l-jāhiliyyata taʿūd* — who thought that if they just kept arguing, reality would rewind to before the truth arrived.

Do you feel how *modern* that is? *"They imagined the days before could return."* That is the sunk-cost heart exactly. It is the man in the empty courtroom who believes that if he just argues long enough, the verdict will un-happen.

[PAUSE]

And here is what the ayah exposes that the psychology textbooks cannot. It tells you **what that ground actually is.** *Dāḥiḍa.* It is not a position. It is a slope. The whole time the arguer feels he is standing firm — *building* his case, *holding* his ground — there was never any ground there. The feeling of having a platform was the illusion. He was already falling; he just hadn't looked down.

So let this be the first thing we carry home. There is a kind of arguing that is really just a refusal to land. And the tragedy of it is not that the points are bad — it is that the person has staked his whole standing on terrain that cannot bear weight, and mistaken the lurch of the fall for the firmness of conviction.

What does this change about tomorrow? It asks of us a brutally honest question, and not about *them* — about *us.* Where in my own life am I still arguing after the answer has already come? Where am I pouring effort into defending a stance — about a relationship, a grudge, a habit, a thing I already half-know is wrong — not because I still believe it, but because I've invested too much to let it fall? The mercy hidden in this ayah is that **landing is allowed.** You are permitted to stop arguing and let your foot find real ground. The arguers in this ayah are condemned not for *having been wrong* — everyone is wrong sometimes — but for *digging in after the truth had already been answered.* The door was open. They chose to push on the wall.

### Theme Two: What Are You Standing On?

Let me ask you the plainest possible question, and I want you to actually feel it in your feet.

**What are you standing on?**

Not your shoes, not the floor. Your *life.* The whole weight of who you are — your sense of what is true, what matters, where you're heading, who you'll answer to — all of that weight is resting on *something.* Some foundation. Some ground you have decided, consciously or not, will hold you.

Here is the thing about a foundation: **you don't find out if it holds until you put your weight on it.** A bridge looks identical whether it can bear a truck or not — right up until the truck. A patch of ground looks like ground whether it's bedrock or ice — right up until you step.

This is the genius of the word *dāḥiḍa,* and now I want to turn it from a verdict on *them* into a mirror for *us.* Because the ayah is not really giving us a weather report on some long-dead arguers. It is showing us a category of foundation — *the ground that gives way* — and inviting us to check our own footing before we put our full weight down.

Think about everything a person can stand on. Wealth — and then the market turns, or the body fails, and the foot slides. Status — and then someone younger arrives, or the crowd moves on, and the foot slides. Being right, winning, being the cleverest in the room — and then a stronger argument comes, or you meet a question you can't answer, and the foot slides. **Every ground except one is, in the end, *dāḥiḍa.*** It holds beautifully until the moment you actually need it to hold — and that is the one moment it gives way.

[PAUSE]

And now look at where the ayah places the firm ground, because it does, by contrast, tell us.

It says the argument collapses *ʿinda rabbihim* — in the sight of **their Lord, their Sustainer.** The whole ayah is built on a hidden contrast. There is the ground they *chose* — their *ḥujja,* their argument, which turns out to be ice. And there is the One they were *standing in front of the whole time* — their *Rabb,* the One sustaining them, holding them up even as they argued against Him.

Do you see it? The firm ground was never the argument. The firm ground was the *Rabb* Himself. They were standing on a slope of ice while leaning *away* from the only bedrock in the universe — and the bedrock was right there, holding them up even in their defiance, still *theirs.*

This is the unseen reality the slipping foot was pointing to all along. *Maḥsūs* to *maʿqūl* — the felt to the understood. Every one of us knows the panic of the foot losing its grip. Allah takes that panic and says: **this is what it is to build your life on anything other than Me.** And the inverse, unspoken but shining underneath, is the promise the rest of the Quran makes explicit — that the one who takes hold of God has *istamsaka bi'l-ʿurwati'l-wuthqā,* "grasped the most trustworthy handhold, which never breaks" (2:256). The opposite of *dāḥiḍa.* The grip that does not slip.

What does this change about tomorrow? It makes the slipping a **gift.** Every time the worldly ground gives way under you — a plan collapses, money goes, status fades, an argument you were sure of falls apart — you can curse it as loss, or you can read it as the ayah teaches: *this ground was always dāḥiḍa; it was never going to hold; thank God I found out now and not on the Day the verdict comes down.* The slip is a mercy that sends you looking for bedrock while there is still time to find it. So the practice is simple and daily: when the ground moves, instead of grabbing for another patch of ice — another argument, another worldly handhold — let it teach you to reach for the *Rabb* who was holding you the entire time.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture: A Single Word, Spoken Twice

Now let me show you the structure we walked past at the very beginning, because you are ready to see it now.

Go back to verse 15 — the ayah right before ours. Allah tells His Prophet to say: *"lā **ḥujjata** baynanā wa baynakum"* — "**there is no argument** between us and you." The matter is settled; the case is closed.

And verse 16 — our ayah — opens: *"wa'lladhīna **yuḥājjūna**…"* — "and those who **argue**…" — and ends its first clause with *"**ḥujjatuhum** dāḥiḍa"* — "**their argument** is collapsed."

The same root — Ḥ-J-J — three times across two ayat. First, God declares: *there is no argument.* Then He describes those who manufacture one anyway. Then He tells you what it's made of: **slipping ground.**

Read together, the architecture *is* the message. Allah closes the case in verse 15. Verse 16 is simply what becomes of any *ḥujja* raised after the close: it doesn't get refuted — there's no one left to refute it. It just **slides into nothing** in the presence of the Lord. The structure proves the point the words make. The argument against a closed case was never an argument. It was a foot on ice.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the themes are the same truth approached from two directions.

From the *words,* we found: a vigorous verb of contention (*yuḥājjūna*), hopelessly mistimed (*after the answer*), landing on a picture of falling (*dāḥiḍa*).

From the *themes,* we found: the human heart's refusal to stop arguing after it has lost, and the universal danger of building a life on ground that cannot bear weight.

They meet in one place. **The most strenuous effort, aimed at the wrong target, at the wrong time, lands on nothing at all** — and the only ground that ever held was the *Rabb* we were arguing against the whole time, who never once stopped holding us up.

And notice, finally, how the ayah ends. *wa-ʿalayhim ghaḍab* — "and **upon** them is wrath." *wa-**lahum** ʿadhābun shadīd* — "and **for them** is a severe punishment." That little word *lahum* — "for them," the word of *belonging,* the word you use for a gift, a possession, a prize. They strained and aimed and argued to win something *for themselves.* And the only thing the ayah finally records as *theirs* — *lahum* — is the punishment. They reached out to grasp a prize. What was placed in their hands was the consequence of reaching for ice.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I still arguing after the answer has already come — defending a stance not because I believe it, but because I've invested too much to let it fall?
2. When the ground gives way beneath me — a loss, a failure, a collapse — do I reach for another patch of ice, or do I let it send me looking for bedrock?
3. What, honestly, am I standing on? If I put my full weight on it tomorrow, would it hold?
4. He is called *their Rabb* even as they argue against Him. Where is He sustaining *me* in the very moments I act as though He isn't there?
5. Is there a door already open in my life that I am exhausting myself pushing against the wall beside?

### One-Sentence Distillation

To argue against the truth of God after it has already been answered and embraced is to put your whole weight on ground that slides out from under you — and the only firm ground was the Lord you were standing in front of the entire time.

### The Closing Invitation

This ayah is not, in the end, a threat aimed outward at "those people." It is a question turned gently inward. It does not ask you to *win.* It asks you to **land.** To stop straining on the ice and let your foot find the One who has been holding you up all along — *your Rabb,* present, sustaining, near. The door is open. You are invited to stop pushing on the wall and simply walk through.

### A Brief Du'a

O Allah, our *Rabb,* who sustains us even when we argue against You — keep our feet from the slipping ground. Do not let us spend our strength on arguments that collapse in Your sight. Plant us on the bedrock that never gives way, and let the only thing that is ever *ours* before You be Your mercy, not Your wrath. Āmīn.
