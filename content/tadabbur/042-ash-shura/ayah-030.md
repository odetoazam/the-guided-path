# What Strikes You Was Aimed

### Surah Ash-Shura (42), Ayah 30

> **وَمَآ أَصَـٰبَكُم مِّن مُّصِيبَةٍ فَبِمَا كَسَبَتْ أَيْدِيكُمْ وَيَعْفُوا۟ عَن كَثِيرٍ**
>
> *Wa mā aṣābakum min muṣībatin fa-bimā kasabat aydīkum, wa yaʿfū ʿan kathīr.*
>
> **"And whatever misfortune befalls you, it is because of what your own hands have earned. Yet He pardons much."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Question Under the Question

I want you to think about the last hard thing that happened to you.

Not a catastrophe necessarily. It can be small. The diagnosis. The rejection letter. The phone call at the wrong hour. The friendship that went cold. The money that didn't come. The door that closed exactly when you needed it open.

And I want you to notice the question your heart asked first. Because it's almost never *"what should I do now?"* That's the second question. The first question, the one that rises up before you can stop it, is some version of:

**"Why is this happening to me?"**

Sit with how strange that question is. Underneath it is an assumption so deep we never examine it — the assumption that this *shouldn't* be happening, that there has been some kind of mistake, that the universe has either singled you out unfairly or, worse, that it hasn't singled you out at all — that it doesn't know you exist, that what hit you was blind, random, meaningless. A rock falling in an empty universe that happened to land on you.

These are the two terrors a human being lives between. Either the cosmos is *cruel* — it sees me and it is against me. Or the cosmos is *indifferent* — it does not see me at all, and my pain is noise. We swing between feeling persecuted and feeling abandoned.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that, and let me set the scene of where this ayah lives.

We are in Surah Ash-Shura. A Meccan surah. The Prophet ﷺ and a small, pressured community are living under hardship — boycott, mockery, loss, the slow grinding weight of being a minority that the powerful have decided to crush. This is a surah about revelation, about who really runs the affairs of the world, about consultation and the gathering of believers. And right in the middle of all of it, Allah turns from the grand machinery of the heavens — and He turns to *you*. To the hardship in *your* hands. To the misfortune that lands in *your* life.

And in ten words — just ten — He does something to that first, helpless question of the heart that I have never seen any philosophy do. He doesn't tell you the universe is cruel. He doesn't tell you it's indifferent.

He tells you something far more unsettling, and far more merciful.

He tells you it was **aimed**. And then He tells you that what landed is the *small* part — that the larger part He held back.

Let me show you how He says it. Because every word here is load-bearing.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مُّصِيبَة — The Thing That Hits Its Mark

Let me teach you something about how Arabic builds words before we open this one.

In Arabic, a single root — usually three consonants — is like a seed. From that one seed grows a whole family of words, and every member of the family carries the genetic code of the root's core meaning. So if you want to understand a word at its depth, you don't just look at the word. You look at its *siblings*. You ask: what else grows from this seed? Because the other words in the family will tell you what this word *really* means, underneath the translation.

So here is the word the whole ayah turns on:

> **مُّصِيبَةٍ** — *muṣībah* — "misfortune," "calamity," "the thing that befalls you."

We translate it as "disaster" or "affliction," and we move on. But look at its root: **ص-و-ب**, *Ṣ-W-B*.

Now let me show you its siblings. Stay with me, because this is where it opens.

From this exact root comes the word **صَوَاب** — *ṣawāb* — which means *correctness*, *being right*, *hitting the truth*. When you answer a question correctly in Arabic, your answer is *ṣawāb*. From this same root comes **أَصَابَ** — *aṣāba* — the verb that opens our ayah, *aṣābakum*, "befell you" — but its base meaning is **to hit the target**. An archer who *aṣāba* is an archer whose arrow struck the mark. And from this same root comes **صَيِّب** — *ṣayyib* — a word the Qur'an uses elsewhere for a rain cloud that comes *down*, that descends and lands.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The word for your calamity — *muṣībah* — is from the same family as the word for *hitting the bullseye*. From the same family as the word for *being correct*. From the same family as *rain that falls exactly where rain falls*.

A *muṣībah* is not a random rock in an empty universe. Linguistically, in the very bones of the word, **a muṣībah is an arrow that found its target.** It was loosed. It was aimed. It traveled. And it struck *precisely where it was meant to strike* — which is *you*.

This is the first thing the ayah does, and it does it before the sentence even finishes. The choice of *this* word — out of all the words for suffering in Arabic — quietly tells you: what hit you was not a misfire. It was *ṣawāb*. It was *correct*. It hit the mark.

Now — that should be terrifying. An aimed arrow means an Archer. And our hearts immediately flinch toward the first terror: *the cosmos is cruel, it sees me and it loosed an arrow at me.*

But watch what the rest of the ayah does with that Archer. Because Allah is about to tell you who loosed the arrow — and it is not who you think.

---

### Element Two: كَسَبَتْ أَيْدِيكُمْ — Earned by Your Own Hands

> **فَبِمَا كَسَبَتْ أَيْدِيكُمْ** — *fa-bimā kasabat aydīkum* — "it is because of what your hands have earned."

Two words to open here. First the verb, then the noun. Both are doing quiet, enormous work.

The verb is **كَسَبَتْ** — *kasabat* — from the root **ك-س-ب**, *kasaba*: **to earn, to acquire, to gain through effort.**

Notice the word He did *not* use. He did not say *what befell you is because of what was decreed upon you*. He did not say *because of fate*. He did not even say *because of your sins* in the passive, abstract sense. He used the language of **earning** — *kasb*. And earning is the most active, the most *yours*, of all relationships a person has to a thing. A gift is given to you. An inheritance falls to you. But your *earnings* — your *kasb* — those you *made*. Those have your fingerprints on them. The classical commentators read *kasabat* here plainly as what we acquired *of sins, of wrongdoing* — the things we did, the choices that were genuinely ours.

So now go back to the arrow. The arrow was aimed; it hit its mark. And the ayah has just told you whose bow it was loosed from.

**Yours.**

Not in the sense that Allah is not the One who decrees — He is. But in the sense that the *content* of what struck you, the ayah ties to *your own earning*. The arrow that found you was fletched with your own choices. This is the answer to the first terror — *is the cosmos cruel?* No. The cosmos is *just*. What reached you is connected, through a thread you cannot always see, to what your own hands sent out.

And it is the answer to the second terror too — *is the cosmos indifferent?* No. You are not noise. You are not a rock falling in an empty room. You are in a *relationship* so precise that what you do and what befalls you are *linked*. You matter enough to be answered.

[PAUSE]

Now the noun. He says *kasabat* **أَيْدِيكُمْ** — *aydīkum* — "your **hands**." Earned by your *hands*.

And here you should stop, because this is strange. Most of what we earn that is harmful, we do not earn with our hands. We earn it with the tongue — the lie, the backbiting, the cruel word. We earn it with the eyes, with the heart, with the intention, with the silence when we should have spoken. The *hand* is a small part of the machinery of human wrongdoing.

So why "hands"?

Imam al-Jalalayn answers this directly, and it is beautiful in its simplicity: **عبر بالأيدي لأن أكثر الأفعال تزاول بها** — "He expressed it as *hands* because most actions are *carried out* by them." This is a known device in Arabic and in the Qur'an: naming the part to mean the whole. The hand stands in for the entire engine of human *doing* — your agency, your capacity to *act upon the world*.

But notice what that does emotionally. He could have said "because of *what you earned*." Instead He made it physical. He pointed at the part of you that *reaches out*, that *grasps*, that *does*. He put it in your hands — the part of you that you watch move, the part of you that you cannot pretend is someone else's.

There is a mercy hidden even in this severity. Because to say *your hands earned this* is to say: **you are not a victim of a random universe. You are an agent in a responsive one.** And an agent can change. A victim can only wait. The moment Allah locates the cause in your hands, He has also handed you the only thing that could ever set you free — the knowledge that your hands can earn *otherwise*.

[PAUSE]

But we are not done. Because so far this ayah, for all its precision, is heavy. *What hit you was aimed; you fired it; it found you.* If the ayah stopped here, it would be just — perfectly just — and we would be crushed under it. Every one of us would be buried under the avalanche of our own earning.

It does not stop here. Watch the last three words. Watch what happens to the *grammar*.

---

### Element Three: وَيَعْفُوا۟ عَن كَثِيرٍ — And He Keeps On Pardoning Much

> **وَيَعْفُوا۟ عَن كَثِيرٍ** — *wa yaʿfū ʿan kathīr* — "Yet He pardons much."

I need to teach you one feature of Arabic to unlock this, and it is the most important grammatical observation in the whole reflection. It is about **tense** — about *when* a verb is happening.

Arabic, broadly, has two tenses. The **perfect** (*al-māḍī*) — a completed action, done, finished, sealed. And the **imperfect** (*al-muḍāriʿ*) — an ongoing action, happening now, continuing, renewing, not yet closed.

Now look back across the whole ayah at the verbs, and watch the tense:

- **أَصَابَكُم** — *aṣābakum*, "befell you" — **perfect.** Done. Completed. The arrow has already landed.
- **كَسَبَتْ** — *kasabat*, "earned" — **perfect.** Done. Completed. The deed is in the ledger; the ink is dry.

And then the final verb:

- **يَعْفُوا۟** — *yaʿfū*, "He pardons" — **imperfect.** Ongoing. Continuous. *Happening, and still happening, and not yet finished.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? Feel the asymmetry of *time* He has built into ten words.

Your sin is in the *past tense*. It is finished, sealed, fixed — *kasabat*, you earned it, it is done. The thing that struck you is in the *past tense* — *aṣāba*, it landed, it is over.

But His pardon is in the **present continuous.** *Yaʿfū.* He is pardoning. He pardons, and pardons, and goes on pardoning. While you read this, He is pardoning. The earning was a single closed act; the pardoning is an *unceasing stream*. Your wrong was a moment. His forgiveness is a *climate*.

This is not decoration. This is the deepest consolation in the verse, written in nothing but the shape of a verb. **He froze your debt in the past and let His mercy run in the present.**

Now the second word: **كَثِيرٍ** — *kathīr* — "much." He pardons *much*.

And notice — it is *indefinite*. He does not say "He pardons your sins." He does not give it a number, a boundary, a list. *Kathīr* — "much," "a great deal," uncountable, unspecified. The earning was specific — *what your hands earned*, a definable thing with edges. But the pardon is left *boundless on purpose*. The grammar refuses to put a fence around His mercy.

And here is the staggering thing the classical scholars draw out. Read the logic of the ayah carefully: He says the misfortune that *did* reach you is from your earning — *and yet He pardons much.* The implication is unmistakable. **What reached you is not the bill. What reached you is the rounding error.** If He held you to account for everything your hands earned, what would befall you would be beyond bearing. The affliction you are crying under is the *fraction* He let through. The *kathīr* — the "much" — is what He *blocked*.

There is a narration that al-Baghawi preserves on this very ayah — reported through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, a *mursal* report tracing it to the Prophet ﷺ — in which the Prophet ﷺ said, when this verse came down, words to the effect of: *"By the One in whose hand is Muḥammad's soul, no scratch of a stick, no stumble of the foot, no throb of a vein, except by a sin — and what Allah pardons is more."* (We hold it gently for its chain, but its meaning sits exactly inside the words of the ayah.)

"And what Allah pardons is more." That is just the verse said back to us. *Wa yaʿfū ʿan kathīr.*

[PAUSE — sit here]

So look at what these three movements have built. The word *muṣībah* told you the arrow was *aimed* — you live in a universe that answers, not one that is blind or cruel. *Kasabat aydīkum* told you the arrow carried *your own* earning — you are an agent, not a victim. And *yaʿfū ʿan kathīr* told you that what landed is the *smallest mercy-filtered fraction* — and that the One filtering it is, right now, in the unbroken present tense, holding back the rest.

The verse that looks like an *indictment* is, when you read its grammar, an **acquittal**.

Now let's ask what this means not just about words — but about how you are going to walk through the rest of your life.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: From a Random Universe to a Responsive One — and the Mercy of Not Misreading It

Let me start where you actually live, before I bring the ayah back.

Psychologists talk about something called **locus of control** — whether a person experiences the events of their life as coming from *outside* them (external) or as connected to their *own* agency (internal). And decades of research found something at first surprising: people with a more *internal* locus — people who feel that what happens to them is connected to what they do — tend to be more resilient, less anxious, less depressed, more able to recover from hardship. While people who experience everything as happening *to* them, from an outside they cannot touch, tend toward something the researchers grimly named *learned helplessness* — the collapse that comes when you believe nothing you do connects to anything that happens.

Read that again and then read our ayah. Because what does the ayah do, in its very first move, to the helpless cry of *"why is this happening TO me"*?

It dissolves the *"to me."* It turns the misfortune from something that happens *to* you, from an outside you cannot reach, into something connected — *fa-bimā kasabat aydīkum* — to your own hands. It moves your suffering from external to internal. From "the universe is doing this to me" to "this is in a conversation with what I have done."

Now — most people hear that and feel *blamed*. And I need you to feel the opposite, because the opposite is the truth of it. To be *blamed* is to be condemned and left there. But to be told *your hands are in this* is to be told **you are not powerless.** The most frightening universe is not the just one. The most frightening universe is the *random* one — the one where your goodness buys you nothing and your effort means nothing and a rock falls on the righteous and the wicked with equal blindness. *That* is the universe that produces despair. The ayah refuses to let you live there. It says: you are inside a relationship. You are being *answered*. What you do *reaches* the world and the world *reaches back*. You matter that much.

[PAUSE]

But now I have to be very careful, and so does the ayah, because there is a cruel misreading sitting right here, and Islam has always guarded against it.

The cruel misreading is this: *every misfortune is a punishment, therefore every suffering person is guilty, therefore I may look at the sick, the bereaved, the poor, the afflicted, and conclude they earned it.* This is the theology of Job's false friends. It is poison. And the same classical commentators who explained *kasabat aydīkum* slammed that door shut in the same breath.

Look at what Imam al-Jalalayn adds immediately after explaining the verse — he says: **"As for those who are not sinners** — what befalls them in this world is **to raise their ranks in the Hereafter."** *Wa ammā ghayr al-mudhnibīn fa-mā yuṣībuhum fī'd-dunyā li-rafʿi darajātihim fī'l-ākhirah.*

So the affliction of the righteous is not a debt collected — it is a *rank purchased*. The same arrow, when it strikes the sinner, is a mercy of *purification*; when it strikes the beloved of Allah — the prophets, who suffered *most* — it is a mercy of *elevation*. The Prophet ﷺ himself suffered immensely, and not a particle of it was for sin.

So this ayah is not a tool to *judge other people's suffering*. It is the opposite. It is a tool to read *your own*. The verse is in the second person plural addressed *to you* about *your* hardship. It hands *you* a lens for *your* life. It does not hand you a verdict for anyone else's. The moment you turn it outward to explain someone else's cancer or someone else's poverty, you have stolen a private gift and turned it into a weapon — and you have forgotten that the very same affliction might be, for them, a ladder you cannot see.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the *first question*. When the hard thing comes, the helpless reflex *"why is this happening to me"* gets replaced with two better questions, and only ever pointed at yourself: **"Is there something in my own hands I need to look at?"** — and, just as importantly — **"And how much, that I never see, is being pardoned right now?"** One question turns you toward repentance. The other turns you toward gratitude. The ayah is engineered to produce *both* from a single wound.

### Theme Two: Living Under Unearned Reprieve

Now let me take you somewhere most of us never let ourselves go.

Imagine, for one honest minute, that the verse stopped after seven words. *Whatever befalls you is because of what your hands have earned.* Full stop. No mercy clause. Imagine a universe of *perfect, immediate, complete justice* — where every single thing your hands earned came back to you, in full, instantly, with no delay and no discount.

Sit in that universe for a second. Every harsh word — answered. Every hidden arrogance — answered. Every prayer you rushed, every trust you betrayed in small ways, every eye you should have lowered, every parent you were short with, every secret unkindness no one saw — *all of it*, returned to you on the spot, at full price.

[PAUSE]

None of us would survive a single afternoon.

That is the universe of pure justice. And the most important word in this ayah is the word that tells you **you do not live in that universe.** *Wa yaʿfū ʿan kathīr.* And He pardons much.

You live, every single day, inside a *gap*. A gap between what your hands have actually earned and what has actually reached you. And that gap is not empty. That gap is *filled with Him.* It is filled with active, present-tense, ongoing pardon — *yaʿfū*, He is doing it now. Every morning you wake to a *reprieve you did not earn.* The very fact that you are reading this in relative wellness, that the catastrophe your sins genuinely merited has not arrived — that is not because you are good. It is because He pardons much.

Here is the reframe that I want to lodge in your chest. Most of us walk around with a low, background sense of being *owed*. We have a ledger in our heads and we feel the world is behind on its payments to us. *I deserve better than this. Why don't I have what I deserve?*

This ayah turns the ledger around and shows you the *other* column — the one you never look at. And on that side is written, in the present continuous tense, *kathīr.* Much. So much pardoned that what got through to you is the small, survivable, merciful fraction.

The Muslim who internalizes this verse stops asking *"why is this happening to me"* and starts being quietly astonished at *"why is so little of it happening to me."* This is the secret psychology of the people closest to Allah. They are not people who suffered less. They are people who saw the *kathīr* — the mountain of pardon behind the pebble of pain — and were broken open with gratitude by it.

[PAUSE]

And notice the relationship the verse hands you. It does not say "the system pardons" or "justice is tempered." It says **He** pardons — *yaʿfū*, the hidden subject is Allah Himself. This is not a mechanism. This is a *Someone*. The same Someone who aimed the arrow with perfect precision is the One who, with that same precision, *withheld the rest.* The Archer and the Forgiver are the same. The Hand that lets the small affliction through is the Hand that holds back the flood. You are not at the mercy of a machine. You are in the care of a Lord who is, at every instant, doing the math of mercy on your behalf — and rounding *down*.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you do in the *first thirty seconds* of a hardship. The untrained heart, when struck, reaches for *complaint* — *why me, this isn't fair, I don't deserve this.* The heart trained by this ayah reaches for two things instead. First: *istighfār* — "O Allah, forgive me; let this small thing be the cleansing of something larger." And second — and this is the one that will change your life — a strange, almost paradoxical *gratitude*: *"O Allah, if this is what got through, thank You for everything You held back. Thank You for the kathīr."*

Try it. The next time something lands on you, before you do anything else, say: *Alḥamdulillāh ʿalā mā ʿāfā* — praise God for what He pardoned. Watch what it does to the affliction. It doesn't make the pain disappear. It makes the pain *legible*. It turns a meaningless blow into a readable letter — and the letter is signed by Someone who loves you enough to send you the small bill and quietly pay the rest.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the linguistic discoveries and the themes turn out to be the same truth seen from two sides.

The word **muṣībah** — the arrow that hits its mark — defeated the lie that the universe is *random*. You live in a responsive cosmos, not a blind one.

The phrase **kasabat aydīkum** — earned by your own hands — defeated the lie that the universe is *cruel*. What reached you is *just*, and it is connected to you, which means you are an *agent*, not a victim — and which means it is also *changeable*.

And the final clause — **yaʿfū ʿan kathīr**, the present-tense pardon of the uncountable "much" — defeated the final lie, the one we are least aware of: that we are *owed*. It revealed that we live not in a universe of debts collected but in a universe of debts *forgiven*, inside a daily, undeserved reprieve.

Three lies. Ten words. One precise, merciful answer to the oldest question the human heart asks when it is struck.

And see the architecture of it, now that we've walked through it: the verse moves from the **past** (what struck you, what you earned — sealed, finished) into the **present** (what He pardons — flowing, unfinished). It moves from the **definite** (a specific misfortune, a specific earning, with edges) into the **indefinite** (*kathīr* — boundless, fenceless mercy). It moves from **your hands** to **His pardon**. Every axis of the verse runs in the same direction: *from the small, closed, just thing you did — toward the vast, open, merciful thing He is doing.* The shape of the sentence is itself the message. Justice is a point; mercy is an ocean — and the sentence flows, as your life flows, from the point into the ocean.

### Questions to Carry

1. When the last hard thing struck me, which question did my heart ask — *"why is this happening to me?"* or *"what in my own hands is this speaking to?"* What would change if I learned to ask the second?
2. Have I ever used this ayah — or one like it — to silently judge someone *else's* suffering? What would it mean to reserve it only for my own?
3. If everything my hands have earned came back to me today, in full and at once — what does that imagine tell me about the *kathīr*, the "much," that is being pardoned while I complain about the little?
4. The pardon is in the *present tense* — He is forgiving *now*. Am I living as someone perpetually owed, or as someone perpetually, mercifully, *let off*?
5. What would it look like, tomorrow, to meet my next small hardship first with *istighfār* and *gratitude* — before I let myself reach for complaint?

### One-Sentence Distillation

What strikes you was aimed, and it carried your own earning — but it is the small, merciful fraction of a debt you could never pay, while the God who could hold you to all of it is, in this very moment and without end, pardoning much.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to manufacture some heroic patience to live inside this ayah. You only have to let it correct one reflex. The next time something lands — the small one, the medium one, even the large one — let your first move not be *"why me."* Let it be a quiet looking-inward and a quieter astonishment outward: *Look how little got through. Look how much He held back.* That single shift, repeated, will slowly turn your hardships from evidence that you are forgotten into the most intimate proof that you are seen, answered, and — above all — spared.

### A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, You who aim with perfect justice and pardon without measure — let the little that reaches me be the cleansing of the much I have earned, and let me never forget the *kathīr* You hold back from me every hour I do not even notice. Make my first response to every wound *istighfār* and gratitude, not complaint. And You who are pardoning *now*, in the unbroken present — keep on pardoning me. *Āmīn.*
