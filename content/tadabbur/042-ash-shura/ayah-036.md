# The Traveler's Provision: Better and More Lasting

### Surah Ash-Shura, Ayah 36

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me first put the words in front of you, three ways.

**The Arabic:**

> فَمَآ أُوتِيتُم مِّن شَىْءٍ فَمَتَـٰعُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ وَمَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ

**The sound of it, roughly:**

> *Famā ūtītum min shay'in famatāʿu-l-ḥayāti-d-dunyā — wa mā ʿinda-llāhi khayrun wa abqā lilladhīna āmanū wa ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn.*

**And the meaning, in plain English:**

> "So whatever you have been given of anything — it is but the passing enjoyment of the worldly life. But what is with Allah is better and more lasting, for those who believe and put their trust in their Lord."

Seventeen words. A verse you could memorize before your tea cools. And yet it does something to your whole life if you let it — it walks into the room where you keep everything you've ever wanted, everything you've worked for, everything you're afraid of losing, and it quietly re-labels all of it.

### Let me rebuild the world this verse first landed in.

This is a Meccan surah. The believers hearing these words are, by any worldly measure, **losing**. They are a small, pressured, sometimes hunted community. The people opposing them have the houses, the caravans, the standing, the sons, the security. If you had stood in the marketplace of Mecca and asked, "Who is winning?" — no one would have pointed at the Muslims.

And into that — into a community that can see, with its own eyes, that the comfort and the abundance is sitting in the *other* camp — Allah sends a verse about who actually owns what lasts.

Notice what He does *not* do. He doesn't say, "Be patient, soon you'll have houses and caravans too." He doesn't promise them the dunya is coming. He does something far more radical. He reaches into the very category the powerful are bragging about — *the stuff, the having, the enjoyment* — and He tells everyone, winners and losers alike, what that category actually is.

The classical commentators even differ on exactly who is being addressed here. Al-Qurtubi reads the address as aimed at the disbelievers — *don't boast about your wealth and ease.* Al-Jalalayn reads it as spoken to the believers and others together. Al-Baghawi captures the heart of it: believer and disbeliever **stand equal** in this one fact — the dunya is a small enjoyment for both of them; the difference only shows up later, in what waits with Allah. Hold those together. They're not three rival readings. They're three camera angles on a single truth that does not care which camp you're standing in.

### The hook.

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then not let go of:

Allah does not say what you've been given is *bad*. He does not say it is *little*. He says it is **a mata'** — and then, of what is with Him, He uses not one comparative but two. *Better.* *And more lasting.*

[PAUSE]

Why two? If something is *better*, isn't that enough? Why does He need to also tell us it is **more lasting** — and why is that second word the one He chooses to end the thought on, the one He lets the verse trail off into?

Stay with that question. By the end, I think you'll find that the second word is the whole verse.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: "You have been given" — the verb that hides its giver

Let me teach you something about language first, in the most ordinary way.

There is a difference between saying *"I earned this"* and *"this was given to me."* The first sentence has a hero in it: **me.** The second sentence has a gap in it — *given to me by… whom?* The giver is missing. Grammar has a name for this. We call it the **passive voice**: a sentence where the thing receiving the action is named, but the one *doing* the action is left out.

We use the passive all the time without noticing. "Mistakes were made." "The package was delivered." Sometimes we use it to hide who's responsible. But sometimes — and this is the important part — the doer is left out *because everyone already knows who it is*, and naming him would almost be an insult to the obvious.

Now look at the very first verb of our verse:

> **أُوتِيتُم** — *ūtītum* — "you have been given."

This is a passive verb. The grammar is precise here: it is Form IV of the root *a-t-y* (to come / to bring), and Form IV passive carries the exact sense of *being made to have, being handed something.* And it is **passive**. Allah does not say *"whatever you took,"* or *"whatever you earned,"* or *"whatever you built."* He says *whatever you* ***were given.***

Let's sit with this.

Think of everything you would put in the column of "things I have." Your income. Your home. Your intelligence. Your spouse. Your children. Your health. The years behind you. In your own internal grammar, how do you phrase that list? For most of us, the sentence runs: *I made this. I earned this. I built this. I chose this.* Active voice. Me, the hero, the doer.

And this single word reaches into that sentence and **removes you as the doer.** Not by arguing with you. Not by lecturing you about gratitude. Just by changing the grammar. *Whatever you were given.* The hand that placed it there was not yours.

Notice something. The giver is left unnamed in the verb — but the verse does name Him, just a few words later: *what is ʿinda Allah, with Allah.* So the hidden agent of "you were given" and the named owner of "what is with Allah" are the **same** One. The verse withholds His name at the start and then supplies it at the turn — so that the moment you realize who gave you everything is the same moment you realize Who holds the better thing. The grammar makes you arrive at Him twice.

[PAUSE]

And there is a tense buried here too, easy to walk past. *Ūtītum* is in the **perfect** — completed, done. *You have already been given.* Whatever is in your hands right now, the giving of it is finished, sealed, in the past. It is not a stream still flowing toward you that you must protect. It is a deposit already made. You are not the source. You are the address it was delivered to.

What this one word reveals: **you have never, not once, been the origin of anything you hold.** You have only ever been the one who received.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: "Mata'" against "Abqā" — the snack and the home

Here is the second thing I want to open, and it lives in the contrast between two words.

First, teach yourself one Arabic word and you will read the Quran differently forever:

> **مَتَاع** — *mata'.*

A *mata'* is not just "enjoyment," though that's how we translate it. From the root *m-t-ʿ*, a *mata'* is, at its core, **the provision a traveler carries** — the things you pack for a journey to use *along the way*. The dates and the water and the bread you take for the road. By its very nature a *mata'* is something **meant to be consumed and used up.** You don't build your life around your travel snacks. You eat them, and they're gone, because their entire purpose was to get you through the trip — not to *be* the trip.

So when Allah says of everything you've been given —

> **فَمَتَـٰعُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا** — *it is but the mata' of the worldly life* —

He is not insulting your provisions. He is **telling you their category.** They are road-food. They are real, they are useful, they are *given by Him for the journey* — and they are meant to be used up on the way. The error is never in having them. The error is in mistaking the snack for the destination. In building your heart's home inside the lunchbox.

And notice the word for this lower life — *ad-dunyā*, from the root *d-n-w*, which means **the near, the close, the lower.** The dunya is literally "the nearer one." Not the worse one — the *closer* one. The one right in front of your face. And things right in front of your face have a way of looking like the only things there are.

Now hold that word — *mata'*, the consumable — against what comes next.

> **وَمَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ** — *and what is with Allah is better and more lasting.*

There it is — the two comparatives I asked you to wait for. **خَيْرٌ** — *khayr* — better. And **أَبْقَىٰ** — *abqā* — more lasting, from the root *b-q-y*, which means **to remain, to endure, what is left when everything else is gone.**

Here is why He needed both words, and why I told you the second one is the whole verse.

If Allah had only said *what is with Him is better,* a clever heart could still bargain: *"Better, yes — but I can see and touch mine right now. I'll take the lesser thing I can hold over the better thing I can't."* The word *khayr* alone can be out-argued by the nearness of the dunya.

So He closes that exit. He adds **أَبْقَىٰ** — *more lasting.* And this is the comparison that cannot be argued with, because it is not comparing *quality* — it is comparing *duration*, and one side of the comparison has a duration of **zero remaining.** A *mata'* is, by definition, the thing that runs out. You cannot weigh a thing that lasts forever against a thing that is already being consumed and call it close. The moment you set *abqā* — endless — next to *mata'* — being used up — the contest is over. It was never a contest.

[PAUSE]

And listen to the *sound* of that final word. *Abqā.* It ends on an open, trailing vowel — *aaa* — the *alif maqṣūra* that doesn't close, doesn't stop, just opens and continues. The verse itself does not snap shut on a hard consonant. It **lasts** in your mouth. The word for "more lasting" is, fittingly, the one that lingers.

This same scale appears across the Quran, almost word-for-word. *"Whatever you are given is but the enjoyment of the worldly life, and what is with Allah is better and more lasting"* (28:60). *"What is with you runs out, and what is with Allah remains"* (16:96). *"But you prefer the worldly life, while the Hereafter is better and more lasting"* (87:16-17). It is as though Allah keeps holding up the same two objects — the thing that runs out, the thing that remains — and asking us, gently, again and again: *which one are you building on?*

What this contrast reveals: **the question was never "good versus bad." It was always "passing versus lasting" — and on that scale, no honest heart can choose the snack over the home.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: "Believed" then "keep trusting" — the verb that never stops

Now look at the very end of the verse, at the two things said about the people for whom the lasting thing is reserved:

> **لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ وَعَلَىٰ رَبِّهِمْ يَتَوَكَّلُونَ** — *for those who believed, and upon their Lord they keep placing their trust.*

I want you to notice something most translations smooth over completely. **These two verbs are in different tenses.** And the Quran does not change tense by accident.

Let me teach the distinction simply. In Arabic, the **perfect** tense describes an action as *completed, whole, done* — a finished event. The **imperfect** describes an action that is *ongoing, renewed, still happening* — a continuing reality.

Now watch:

- **ءَامَنُواْ** — *āmanū* — "they believed." This is the **perfect.** Form IV of *a-m-n*. Completed. A done thing. A foundation laid.
- **يَتَوَكَّلُونَ** — *yatawakkalūn* — "they keep placing their trust." This is the **imperfect.** And it is Form V of the root *w-k-l* — and Form V, the *tafaʿʿala* pattern, is the form of **doing something to and for yourself**, taking an action *upon your own self.* *Tawakkul* is not trust that happens to you. It is trust you *actively place*, again and again, with your own hands.

Do you see the architecture? **Belief is stated as finished. Trust is stated as never-finishing.**

Let's sit with why that's exactly right.

*Īmān* — belief — has a moment of birth. There is a day, an hour, a sentence — *"I believe"* — when the foundation is poured. It is a thing you can say you *did.* Done. Perfect tense. Right.

But *tawakkul* — handing the weight of your life over to your Lord, relying on Him with your outcomes — that has **no completed form**, because the moment you finish trusting Him for *this* worry, a new worry walks in the door, and the trusting must begin again. You do not trust Allah once and graduate. The diagnosis comes back, and you must place your trust *again.* The deal falls through, and you place it *again.* The child is late coming home, and you place it *again.* It is *yatawakkalūn* — present, ongoing, renewing, for as long as you are alive and have a heart that can fear.

The grammar is telling you the truth about your own spiritual life: **you converted once. You surrender daily.**

[PAUSE]

And notice one more buried jewel — the word order. He says *wa ʿalā rabbihim yatawakkalūn* — literally, *"and upon their Lord they place their trust."* In Arabic, putting *"upon their Lord"* **before** the verb is a way of saying *upon their Lord — and Him alone.* The object is fronted for emphasis and restriction. Not upon their savings. Not upon their plan. Not upon their own competence. **Upon their Lord** they keep placing it. The grammar quietly closes every other door.

And which name does He use? Not *upon Allah* — but *upon their **Rabb***, from *r-b-b*: the One who **nurtures, sustains, raises, and cares for** what is His. They don't entrust their lives to a distant power. They entrust it to the One whose entire relationship to them is *raising them, day by day, toward what is better and lasting.* Of course you can keep handing the weight back to Him. He is the one already carrying you.

What this reveals: **the people of the lasting thing are not people who trusted once. They are people who never stop handing their lives back to the One who gives.**

### The bridge.

So look at what the words have shown us, before we ask what it all means for a life.

A verb that **erased you as the source** — you were *given*, you did not *make*.

A pair of words that **re-categorized everything you own** — not bad, not little, but a traveler's snack against an eternal home.

And a tense — a quiet shift from *did* to *keep doing* — that **redefined the spiritual life** as a thing you renew rather than a thing you complete.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way you are actually living, right now, today.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Everything you hold is already on loan

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a thing economists named the **hedonic treadmill**, and you've felt it even if you've never heard the term. You want the thing — the raise, the house, the phone, the recognition. You get it. There is a spike of joy. And then, with a reliability that is almost funny, the joy *settles*, the new thing becomes the normal thing, and your wanting quietly relocates to the *next* thing. You run, and run, and the finish line moves with you. Nobody on the treadmill is lying about their happiness. The happiness was just real and *temporary*, every single time.

Here's the thing. The treadmill is not a malfunction. It is the dunya **behaving exactly as Allah described it.** It is a *mata'.* It is *meant* to be consumed and leave you hungry again, because its entire design is to keep you moving down the road, not to let you build a permanent house out of road-food. The treadmill feels like a trap only when you think you were supposed to *arrive* on it. The verse is telling you: you were never supposed to arrive here. This was always the journey, never the home.

Now let the verb from Part One come back and do its work.

*"Whatever you have been **given**."* Passive. The giver, hidden then named, is Allah. Which means: every single thing on your treadmill — every prize you reached for and every prize you're still reaching for — **was placed in your hands, not generated by them.** You are not running on the treadmill as an owner accumulating. You are a guest being served, course after course, on a journey that has a destination *past* the table.

And here is where it becomes liberation rather than loss. Think about how differently you hold a thing you *own* versus a thing you've been *lent.* The thing you own, you grip — you fear its loss, you measure yourself by it, you bleed when it's scratched. The thing you've been lent, you *use with gratitude and an open hand,* because you always knew it wasn't ultimately yours. You enjoy the loaned car fully — and you can give it back without your identity collapsing.

The verse is offering you the second posture for your *entire life.* Your wealth: on loan. Your health: on loan. The people you love: entrusted to you, on loan. Not to make you love them less — to free you to love them *without the terror of ownership.* Because the one thing a borrower never has to fear is the ultimate question *"what if I lose it?"* — he already knows the answer. It was never his. It returns to the One who gave it. And that One has something **better and more lasting** waiting.

[PAUSE]

So let me ask you, plainly, through a human door: **what are you gripping right now as if it were yours?** What is the thing whose loss you secretly believe would end you? Look at it honestly. And then try, just for a moment, to re-read its label in the grammar of this verse. Not *mine.* Not *earned.* **Given.** A *mata'.* Beautiful, real, from Him — and meant to be held with an open hand on a road that leads somewhere better.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Take the one possession or outcome you are most anxious about losing. Say over it, deliberately: *"This was given. It is a mata'. What is with Allah is better and more lasting."* Not to make yourself care less — to make yourself grip less. You will find you can hold it more lovingly once you stop holding it in fear.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: Trust is not a destination you reach — it's a verb you renew

Now the second theme, and again, let me start where you live.

Almost all of our anxiety is, underneath, a **crisis of control.** We lie awake not because the worst has happened, but because we cannot *guarantee* it won't. We over-plan, over-check, over-prepare — refreshing the inbox, rehearsing the conversation, running the numbers a fourth time — and what we are really doing in all of it is trying to *be the one in charge of the outcome.* We are trying to be the source. The active-voice hero of our own sentence. The one who makes it happen.

And we have already learned, from a single passive verb at the start of this ayah, that **we never were.** *You were given.* You have never once been the source of any good in your life. You have only ever been the address it arrived at. Which means all that exhausting effort to *control the source* is effort spent trying to be something you have never, for one second, actually been.

This is where *yatawakkalūn* — the never-finishing verb — becomes the most merciful word in the verse.

If *tawakkul* — reliance on Allah — were a thing you had to *complete*, a state you had to *achieve* and then maintain perfectly, it would crush you. Every spike of anxiety would be proof of failure: *"I'm supposed to trust Him fully and here I am worrying again — I've failed at tawakkul."* That is how many sincere people actually feel, and it is a quiet torment.

But the verse does not say *"those who* ***achieved*** *trust."* It says *those who* ***keep placing*** *it* — ongoing, present, renewable. Which means the worry that walked back into your heart this morning is **not a failure of tawakkul. It is the invitation to it.** You don't trust once and stay trusting. You trust, the trust gets spent down by a new fear, and you trust *again.* That re-placing — that is not the system breaking. **That is the system working.** *Yatawakkalūn* is, by its very tense, a verb that assumes you will need to do it again tomorrow. Allah built the renewal into the grammar.

[PAUSE]

And notice He didn't pair belief with belief. He paired *believing* with *trusting.* Why these two together as the description of the people who get the lasting thing? Because **you can believe a truth and still grip your life in terror.** Belief is the foundation — *āmanū*, done, poured. But trust is what you build on it, brick by brick, every day, every fear — *yatawakkalūn*, never done. A person can have flawless *aqīdah* and a heart clenched like a fist. The verse is describing people whose conviction has actually *traveled down into their hands* — who don't just affirm that Allah provides, but who *let go* on the strength of it, again and again.

And remember Whom they let go *to.* Their *Rabb.* The One whose whole relationship to them is *nurturing them toward what is better and lasting.* Tawakkul is not flinging your life into a void and hoping. It is handing the weight back to the One who was carrying you the whole time, who *gave* you everything in your hands in the first place, and who is holding something better still. Of course you can let go. You were never the one holding it up.

[PAUSE]

So, through the human door: **where are you exhausting yourself trying to be the source?** Which outcome are you white-knuckling as though your grip is what holds it in place? And can you hear, in the ongoing tense of *yatawakkalūn*, the permission to set it down — not once and forever, which would be a burden, but *today*, which you can actually do — knowing that tomorrow you'll set it down again, and that this is not weakness but the very practice the verse is describing?

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time anxiety returns over something you cannot control, do not treat it as proof you've failed at trust. Treat it as the *cue to perform the verb again.* Say *ʿalā rabbī atawakkal* — *upon my Lord I place my trust* — and consciously open your grip on the outcome. Then expect to do it again in an hour. That repetition is not your failure. It is the life of *yatawakkalūn.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture, revealed.

Now that we've done the work, stand back and see how the verse is built. It is two balanced halves, hinged on a single word — *and* — and each half answers the other.

> **مَا أُوتِيتُم مِّن شَىْءٍ** … *whatever you were given of anything*
> → **فَمَتَـٰعُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا** … *is the passing provision of the nearer life*
>
> **وَمَا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** … *and what is with Allah*
> → **خَيْرٌ وَأَبْقَىٰ** … *is better and more lasting*

Two parallel "whatever / what" clauses. The first leads to a thing that is *near* (*dunyā*) and *consumed* (*mata'*). The second leads to a thing that is *with Allah* (*ʿinda-llāh* — the nearness that is His, not ours) and *enduring* (*abqā*). The verse holds up two objects side by side and lets the structure itself make the argument: *near-and-running-out* on the left, *His-and-remaining* on the right. And then it tells you who gets to claim the right-hand side — *those who believed and keep trusting.* The architecture is a scale, and the verse hands you the weights and lets you watch which way it tips.

### Bringing it together.

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **passive verb** said: *you are not the source.* The theme of **loaned possessions** lived that out: so hold everything with an open hand.

The **two comparatives** said: *this is passing, that is lasting.* The theme of the **treadmill** lived that out: so stop expecting the road-food to be the home.

The **ongoing tense of *yatawakkalūn*** said: *trust is renewed, never finished.* The theme of **control and anxiety** lived that out: so set the weight down again today, and again tomorrow, without shame.

One verse. One message, said by the grammar and said by the life at the same time.

### Questions to carry.

1. If I genuinely believed everything in my hands was *given* — not earned, not mine, on loan — what is the first thing my grip would loosen on?
2. What am I treating as a destination that Allah has labeled a *mata'* — a thing meant to be used up on the way, not arrived at?
3. When anxiety returns over the same worry for the hundredth time, do I hear it as failure — or as the cue to perform *yatawakkal* again?
4. Is my belief living up in my head as a finished fact, or has it traveled down into my hands as a daily letting-go?
5. What is the "better and more lasting" thing I claim to want — and does my calendar, my spending, my worry, show that I actually believe it is *better?*

### The one-sentence distillation.

Everything you've been handed is a traveler's provision to be spent on the road; what waits with Allah is both better and unending — and it belongs not to those who merely believed once, but to those who keep handing the weight of their lives back to their Lord.

### The closing invitation.

This verse is not asking you to despise the dunya, or to stop enjoying what you've been given, or to feel guilty for wanting good things. The provisions are *from Him,* and they are *good.* It is asking for something gentler and far harder: **a re-labeling.** That you look at the whole inventory of your life and quietly change one word over all of it — from *mine* to *given*, from *destination* to *mata'.* And that you let the trusting be a thing you do *today*, knowing you'll do it again, rather than a summit you keep failing to permanently reach.

Hold the road-food with gratitude. Walk toward the home. And keep handing the weight back to the One who has been carrying you the whole way.

### A brief du'a.

> O Allah, You who *gave* us everything we hold and named it a passing provision — loosen our grip on what is running out, and fix our hearts on what is *with You*, better and more lasting. And as the fear returns to us, again and again, let us return to trusting You, again and again — upon You, our Lord, and upon You alone, we place it down. Āmīn.
