# The Three Regrets: Living Now So the Soul Never Has To Say "If Only"

---

## INTRODUCTION

Listen first, before you understand anything. Just to the sound.

> أَن تَقُولَ نَفْسٌ يَٰحَسْرَتَىٰ عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطتُ فِى جَنۢبِ ٱللَّهِ وَإِن كُنتُ لَمِنَ ٱلسَّٰخِرِينَ

*An taqūla nafsun yā-ḥasratā ʿalā mā farraṭtu fī janbi-llāhi wa in kuntu lamina-s-sākhirīn.*

> **"Lest a soul should say, 'Oh, my deep regret over how I neglected the side of Allah — and indeed I was among the mockers.'"**

And then a second voice:

> أَوْ تَقُولَ لَوْ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ هَدَىٰنِى لَكُنتُ مِنَ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ

> **"Or it should say, 'If only Allah had guided me, I would surely have been among the God-conscious.'"**

And then a third:

> أَوْ تَقُولَ حِينَ تَرَى ٱلْعَذَابَ لَوْ أَنَّ لِى كَرَّةً فَأَكُونَ مِنَ ٱلْمُحْسِنِينَ

> **"Or it should say, when it sees the punishment, 'If only I had one more turn — that I might be among the doers of good.'"**

Three sentences. Three voices. One soul.

### Rebuild the world

I want you to feel where these verses sit, because the placement is the entire meaning.

Three verses before this, Allah said the most generous thing He has ever said to a human being who has ruined his own life:

> *"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves — do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins. He is the Forgiving, the Merciful."* (39:53)

Read that and your chest opens. There is no sin too large. There is no file too thick. The door is not just unlocked — it is torn off its hinges.

Then, immediately:

> *"And turn back to your Lord and submit to Him, before the punishment comes upon you and then you will not be helped. And follow the best of what was revealed to you from your Lord..."* (39:54–55)

Turn back. **Now.** Before.

And then — these three verses. They begin with a single Arabic word, *an* — "**lest**." Do all of this *lest* a soul should say. Hurry through the open door, *lest* you become the voice in verse 56. *Lest* you become the voice in 57. *Lest* you become the voice in 58.

So understand what kind of verses these are. They are not a threat hurled at an enemy. They are the most tender thing a parent does — grabbing the child's wrist at the edge of the road. The warning *is* the mercy. Allah is not describing the regret to frighten you for sport. He is letting you **hear your own future voice** while there is still time to make sure you never have to use it.

### The Hook

Here is what should keep you in your seat tonight.

These are three different regrets, spoken by the same soul — and they are not random. They are arranged in a precise order, and that order is a map of how a human heart deceives itself, right up to the last second.

The first regret blames *me*. The second regret blames *God*. The third regret blames *time*.

And buried in the very first one is a phrase so strange that the grammarians stopped and argued over it for centuries — a soul grieving not over Hell, not over sin in the abstract, but over something it calls **"the side of Allah."** *Janb Allah.* The side. The flank. As though God had a side, and you had spent your life with your back to it.

Let's go and find out what a soul sees, at the end, that it could not see at the beginning.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word "lest" — and the command that isn't there

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse, because it unlocks everything.

In English, when we want someone to act, we usually say the action: "Study, *so that* you don't fail." The command — *study* — comes first. The consequence comes second.

Arabic can do something stranger and more powerful. It can give you **only the consequence**, and leave the command completely unspoken — so loud in its absence that you supply it yourself.

That is what is happening here. The verse opens:

> أَن تَقُولَ نَفْسٌ — *An taqūla nafsun* — "Lest a soul should say..."

Notice: there is no verb of command in this sentence. No "do this." It just begins with *an* — and the classical grammarians had to explain what this *an* is even doing here.

Listen to them work. **Al-Baghawī** writes that *an taqūla* means *li-allā taqūla* — "**so that** no soul **says**." He even gives you a parallel from elsewhere in the Quran: Allah says He placed firm mountains in the earth *an tamīda bikum* (16:15) — meaning *li-allā tamīda*, "**lest** it sway with you." Same construction. Same hidden "lest."

Then Baghawī relays the great grammarian **al-Mubarrad**, who fills in the missing word out loud: *"baadirū wa-ḥdharū an taqūla nafs"* — "**Hurry. Beware.** Lest a soul should say." And he relays **al-Zajjāj**: it means *"fear that you should come to a state in which you say these words."*

**Al-Jalālayn** himself opens his commentary by supplying the same missing imperative, but with one telling addition: *fābādirū qabla an taqūla nafsun* — "**Hasten, then, *before* a soul should say...**" The word *qabla* — before — does not appear anywhere in the verse. Al-Jalālayn inserts it because the logic of the construction demands it. The urgency is not merely "do something." It is: *do it in time.* Every grammarian who tries to complete this sentence ends up writing the word *before* — because the whole verse is suspended on an unspoken *before* that the verse refuses to make comfortable for you by stating outright.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened?

The command was never written — **because the command is you.** The verse hands you a consequence with a hole in front of it, shaped exactly like an action, and your own conscience has to step forward and fill it. *Hurry. Beware. Turn back. Do something — lest...*

**Al-Qurtubi** adds the grammarians' precise readings: the Kufans say *an* here means *li-allā* (so that... not), the Basrans say it means *ḥadhara an* (out of fear that). Either way the meaning lands in the same place. This whole verse is suspended from an unspoken imperative. It is a sentence that only completes itself when you move.

Sit with the mercy of that design. Allah could have said: "Do this or I will punish you." Instead He says, in effect: *Here is what you will sound like if you don't. I'll let you hear it. Now — you decide.* He treats you as someone capable of being moved by your own future grief. That is not the speech of a tyrant. That is the speech of the One who knows that the most powerful warning a person can receive is the sound of their own voice, too late.

### Element Two: "A soul" — the terrifying gift of the missing name

Now look at the next word. Allah does not say "the disbeliever will say." He does not name anyone. He says:

> نَفْسٌ — *nafsun* — "**a** soul."

In Arabic this is *nakirah* — indefinite. No "the." No name. No description. Just... *a* soul. Some soul. A soul.

Why does this matter? Let me show you with something from your own life. There is a difference between a teacher saying "Ahmad failed the exam" and a teacher saying "**A student** failed the exam — anyone could have been this student." The first one lets everyone else relax: *not me.* The second one makes the whole room go quiet. *Could that be me?*

**Al-Qurtubi** stopped right here. He relays the question of **al-Zamakhsharī**: *"Why is it indefinite — 'a soul'?"* And the answer he gives is layered, like a hand of cards laid down one at a time:

- It means **some** soul — specifically the soul of the disbeliever, the one who turned away.
- Or it means a **particular, singled-out** soul — distinguished from all others either by how stubbornly it clung to denial, or by how severe its punishment is.
- Or — and here Qurtubi cites the poetry of al-Aʿshā — the indefinite is used for **multitude**: not one soul, but soul after soul after soul, too many to count, each saying the same words.

**Al-Ṭabarī** carries the indefiniteness across all three verses and finds in it something even larger. He reads the three *nafsun* constructions not as one soul cycling through three stages of grief, but as three *distinct* souls — each a different type of person who failed. The first *nafsun* is the soul that neglected and mocked. Then *nafsun ukhrā* — "**another soul**" — the one that deflects blame onto God. Then *ukhrā* — "yet another" — the one standing at the foot of the punishment, bargaining for time. On this reading, the Quran is not merely tracing one person's inner collapse; it is painting three portraits of three categories of spiritual evasion, each given an unnamed, empty seat. Whether you read these as one soul's escalating grief or three kinds of people — the classical scholars held both — the coverage is the same: no form of turning away is left without a witness.

[PAUSE]

Do you see the genius of leaving the name out?

If Allah had named the person, you would have checked the name against your own and breathed out. *That's not me. I'm safe.* By refusing to name anyone, Allah makes the seat empty — and an empty seat is an invitation. The verse does not point at someone across the room. It leaves a blank where the name goes, and lets the most honest person in the room quietly write their own.

**This is what the missing name does: it removes your alibi.** You cannot say "he means the obvious sinner, the famous hypocrite." He means *a soul*. He means whichever soul fits the description when the time comes. The grammar itself is asking you the only question that matters: *will it be yours?*

### Element Three: "The side of Allah" — *janb Allah*

Now we arrive at the phrase the grammarians could not walk past — and neither can we. The soul cries:

> يَٰحَسْرَتَىٰ عَلَىٰ مَا فَرَّطتُ فِى جَنۢبِ ٱللَّهِ

> "Oh, my regret, over what I **neglected** — *fī janbi-llāh* — **in the side of Allah.**"

Three words here are load-bearing. Let me open them one at a time.

**First: *yā-ḥasratā.*** The root is *Ḥā–Sīn–Rā* — *ḥasr*. And the lexical image underneath this root is physical and brutal. *Ḥasr* is to **strip something bare**, to uncover it, to wear it down to exhaustion. The Arabs called a worn-out, broken-down camel *ḥasīr* — driven so hard it can go no further and collapses, stripped of all its strength. *Ḥasrah* is the grief that does that to a heart: the grief of something irrecoverable, that strips you bare and exhausts you because there is **nothing left to do about it.** It is not the regret of "I'll fix it tomorrow." It is the regret of a thing that can never, ever be fixed.

And notice the form of the cry: *yā ḥasratā.* This is a **vocative** — the soul is *calling out* to its own regret as if regret were a person. **Al-Baghawī** explains that the original is *yā ḥasratī* ("O my regret"), but in a cry of anguish — *istighātha*, calling for help — the Arabs turn that final *yā* into an *alif*: *yā ḥasratā.* It is the sound a language makes when it stretches a word in pain.

Think about what it means to *summon* your regret by name. It is to say: *Come now, my regret — this is your moment. I called you too late to do anything but be here. Come and sit with me.*

[PAUSE]

**Second: *farraṭtu* — "I neglected."** The Leeds corpus confirms this is **Form II** — the form built by doubling the middle root letter, *farraṭa*, a form that in Arabic typically carries **intensity or repetition**. Now here is what is buried in the root *Fā–Rā–Ṭā*. The base meaning *faraṭa* is **to go ahead, to precede, to run out in front.** And *tafrīṭ* — the Form II — is its dark twin: it is to let something **slip ahead of you and be left behind**; to fall short; to let the moment run on without you. The Form II intensity tells us this was not one missed prayer, one slipped resolution. It is a *pattern* of letting things slide — day after day, the soul standing still while everything that mattered ran on ahead and left it behind. The thing that "got away" was the soul's whole life with its Lord.

**Third — and this is the heart of it: *fī janbi-llāh.*** The root *Jīm–Nūn–Bā* means **side, flank.** Literally: *fī janbi-llāh* is "in the side of Allah." Your *janb* is your flank, the part of you that turns toward or away from things. It is the same root from which Arabic gets *junub* (one beside you) and *ijtanaba* (to turn your side to something and avoid it).

Now — the mufassirūn render the *meaning* clearly so we are not lost. **Al-Jalālayn** says *fī janbi-llāh* means *fī ṭāʿatih* — "in His obedience." **Al-Muyassar** expands it: "what I wasted of the right of Allah and fell short in His obedience." So as a matter of *meaning*, the soul is grieving over the rights of Allah that it neglected. That is settled.

But ask the question tadabbur always asks: **why this image?** Allah chose the word *side*. He could have said "what I neglected of His commands," "of His worship," "of His law." He said: *the side of Allah.*

[PAUSE]

Here is what the physical image is pointing toward — and I offer this as what the word *invites* us to feel, beyond the plain juristic meaning.

A *side* is relational. You stand at someone's side. You turn your side toward those you love and your back toward what you reject. When two people walk together, side by side, that *janb* is the geometry of closeness. To neglect "the side of Allah" is not first about breaking a rule on a list. It is about a **relationship you stood next to your whole life and never turned toward.** Allah was, the entire time, *right there* — at your side. The *janb* was available. And the soul spent its years with its flank turned the other way, busy, distracted, *farraṭa* — letting it all slip past — until the day it turns, finally, and sees how close He had been the entire time, and cries: *yā ḥasratā* — over the **side of Allah** I never once stood beside.

That is why the word is *side* and not *law*. A neglected law produces guilt. A neglected *side* — a neglected nearness — produces *ḥasrah*: the grief of something irrecoverably close that you can never get back.

### Element Four: "And indeed I was among the mockers"

And then the confession turns a shade darker. The soul does not stop at *I neglected*. It says:

> وَإِن كُنتُ لَمِنَ ٱلسَّٰخِرِينَ — "and indeed I was **among the mockers.**"

**Al-Jalālayn** notes the grammar: this *in* is *in mukhaffafa* — the "lightened" *inna*, an emphatic — so the force is "and **truly** I was among the mockers." The root of *sākhirīn* is *Sīn–Khā–Rā* — *sukhriyah*, **mockery, ridicule.** Al-Muyassar names what was mocked: "the command of Allah, His Book, His Messenger, and the believers in Him."

Notice the escalation inside a single sentence. First: passive neglect — *I let it slip.* Then: active contempt — *I mocked the very thing I was neglecting.* The soul is confessing not just that it walked past the side of Allah, but that as it walked past, it **sneered** at the people who were standing there.

And here is the quiet devastation: the *sukhriyah*, the mockery, was the soul's *defense mechanism.* You ridicule what you have decided not to take seriously, because ridicule keeps it at arm's length. As long as you can laugh at the religious, the committed, the ones who pray — you never have to face the possibility that they saw something you missed. The mockery was the wall the soul built so it would never have to feel the *ḥasrah*. And in the end the wall comes down anyway, and the regret it was built to keep out is the first thing through the door.

### The bridge

Look at what these four words have shown us. An unspoken command, suspended over the verse, waiting for you to complete it. A soul with no name, so the seat stays open. A grief that strips you bare, over a *nearness* — a side — you never turned toward. And a mockery that was only ever a wall against that grief.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of a human heart trying to avoid the truth until the very last moment.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The three regrets are the three lies, in order

Let me start where you already live.

You have replayed a moment. Everyone has. Some decision, some thing you said or didn't say, and your mind ran the tape again and again with a different ending. Psychologists call this **counterfactual thinking** — the "if only" reflex. *If only I had taken the other job. If only I had called him before he died. If only I had studied that one chapter.*

And here is what researchers noticed about how the human mind does this, and it is uncanny how exactly the Quran maps it. When we face a loss we caused, the mind moves through *postures*. First it accuses the self. Then, when that's too painful, it looks for someone or something else to blame. And finally, when blame runs out, it *bargains* — it begs the universe for a do-over it knows it cannot have.

Now look again at the three verses. Watch the soul move through the exact same three postures — except this time there is no waking up from it.

**Regret one (39:56): the soul blames itself.** *"What I neglected... I was among the mockers."* This is honest. Brutally honest. The finger points inward: *I did this. I let it slip. I sneered.* This is the truest of the three.

**Regret two (39:57): the soul blames God.** *"If only Allah had guided me, I would have been among the God-conscious."* Feel the shift. The accusation has quietly slid from *I* to *He*. *It wasn't really my fault — I was never given the guidance.* But this is the lie, and the whole Quran is its refutation. Guidance came. A Book came. A Messenger came. The mockers in verse 56 *mocked the very guidance* they now claim in verse 57 they never received. This is the self-deception of the heart in its purest form: rewriting history to make the loss someone else's doing. The same soul that admitted "I was among the mockers" one verse later pretends it was never offered anything to mock.

Al-Jalālayn's gloss of verse 57 adds a further precision. The *hidāyah* the soul weeps for is *al-hidāyah bi-l-ṭāʿah* — guidance *toward* obedience, the divine enabling to actually comply. The soul is not complaining that no messenger came, no Book arrived. It is claiming it was never given the inner *tawfīq* — the success — to act on what it received. But the confession of verse 56 had already severed this excuse at the root: the soul was not merely uninspired to obey. It was among the *sākhirīn* — the **mockers.** You do not mock what you merely find difficult. You mock what you have *decided* to reject. The second regret tries to locate the failure outside itself; the first regret had already confessed precisely where it lived.

**Regret three (39:58): the soul bargains with time.** *"If only I had one more turn..."* The word is *karrah* — root *Kāf–Rā–Rā* — and its image is military: to **wheel your horse around and charge back in.** One more pass. One more run at it. *Send me back, just once more, and watch what I'll do.* This is the final posture: when you can no longer blame yourself or God, you bargain with the clock. And it is answered everywhere else in the Quran with a single devastating fact — *they will not be returned* (cf. 23:99–100, 6:28). The door these verses were warning you about has, by verse 58, closed.

[PAUSE]

Now lift your eyes and see the whole shape.

**Past — deflection — future.** The first regret looks *backward* at what the self did. The second deflects *sideways* onto God. The third reaches *forward* for time it will never get. The soul's regret has spanned its entire relationship with time — and in all three directions, there is nothing it can grasp. That is what *ḥasrah* is: grief in every direction at once, with nothing left to hold.

And here is the part that should change how you live tomorrow morning. **You run all three of these tapes already.** Right now, in this life, you blame yourself, you quietly blame your circumstances and even your Lord ("I would pray more if my life were easier"), and you bargain with time ("I'll get serious next Ramadan"). The difference — the *only* difference — between you and the soul in these verses is that **your tape is still running while the door is still open.** The verse is letting you hear the final cut of a film you are still shooting. You can still change the ending.

**So what changes tomorrow?** Catch yourself in the *second* regret while it's still cheap. The next time you hear yourself say "if only my situation were different, I'd be more devout" — recognize that voice. That is verse 57, rehearsing. That is the soul learning to blame God for a guidance that is, in fact, in your hands right now. Answer it the way the Quran answers it: the guidance already came. The side of Allah is right here. Turn toward it before the line you're rehearsing becomes the line you're stuck with.

### Theme Two: You can repent from the future

Let me give you the second thing, and it grows straight out of the first.

We think of repentance as something we do about the *past.* You sinned; you turn back; you seek forgiveness for what is behind you. True. But these verses teach a second kind of *tawbah* that almost no one talks about — repentance from the *future.*

Here is the everyday version. A wise person does not only apologize for the crash. A wise person looks at the icy road ahead and *slows down now* — repenting, in a sense, from an accident that has not happened yet, refusing to let it happen. The intelligence is not in the apology afterward. It is in seeing the future clearly enough to act before it arrives.

That is the entire design of these three verses. Go back to *an taqūla* — "lest a soul should say." Remember what the grammarians filled into the silence: *baadirū, iḥdharū* — "**hurry, beware.**" Allah is not asking you to repent for a regret you already have. He is letting you **stand inside your own funeral, hear your own voice cracking with *yā ḥasratā*, watch yourself reaching for a *karrah* that will never come** — and then He pulls you back out, sets you down in this moment, with the door still open, and says: *now you've seen it. Don't let it be you.*

[PAUSE]

This is one of the most merciful things in the entire Quran, and we read past it because it is dressed as a warning.

Allah gave you the one thing the soul in verse 58 would trade the universe for. *Karrah.* One more turn. The soul screams for it across the closed door — *if only I had one more turn* — and the answer is silence, because that turn is gone. But **you** — you reading this, you breathing right now — **you are living inside the *karrah* that soul is begging for.** This day, this hour, this unremarkable Tuesday, is the "one more turn" that the damned would give everything to have. It is in your hands. You are holding the answer to a prayer that, on the other side, is never granted.

What does that change tomorrow? It reframes the most ordinary moment of your life. The wudu you can barely be bothered to make. The prayer you rush. The Quran sitting closed on the shelf. The apology you keep not making, the sin you keep meaning to leave. Every one of those is a *karrah* — a turn the people of regret are weeping for. You don't need a dramatic life change. You need to recognize that the boring, available, right-now moment in front of you **is itself the granted miracle.** Turn toward the *janb* — the side of Allah that has been there the whole time — while turning still costs you almost nothing.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now that we have done the work, stand back and see how perfectly these three verses are built.

> أَن تَقُولَ نَفْسٌ — *Lest a soul should say...*
> أَوْ تَقُولَ — *Or it should say...*
> أَوْ تَقُولَ — *Or it should say...*

Three times, the same verb of speech. *Taqūla. Taqūla. Taqūla.* Like three doors down the same dark corridor, each opening onto the same room of grief from a different angle. And notice the third one carries a detail the first two don't: *ḥīna tarā al-ʿadhāb* — "**when it sees the punishment.**" The regret was always there; by the third verse the soul can now *see* what is coming, and still all it can produce is a wish for time it won't get. The structure walks you from neglect, to denial, to the foot of the punishment itself — and the only thing that escalates is the desperation. Nothing the soul says ever once becomes an *action*, because the time for action is precisely what it no longer has.

And remember where this whole corridor is hung: from a single word three verses earlier — *do not despair of the mercy of Allah* (39:53) — and from the command in 39:54, *turn back to your Lord and submit, before...* The most hope-filled verse in the Quran and the most piercing regret in the Quran are **neighbors.** That is not an accident. Mercy is the door. The three regrets are the picture, taped to the inside of that door, of what waits if you walk past it. The same Hand drew both.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one insight seen from two sides.

The grammar refused to name the soul — *nafsun*, indefinite — so the seat stays open for you. The grammar left the command unspoken — *an taqūla* — so you would have to supply the verb with your own life. And the meaning of it all is the two themes: that the heart deceives itself in three escalating postures right up to the last second, and that the cure is to repent from a future you have been allowed to see in advance. The empty name and the missing command are not decoration. They are the verse reaching out of the page, leaving a blank where *you* go, and a silence where your *action* goes.

The soul grieves over the *side of Allah* — *janb Allah* — a nearness it never turned toward. You can turn toward it now. That is the whole of it.

### One-sentence distillation

Allah lets you hear your own future voice crying *"if only"* — over a nearness you never turned toward, a guidance you pretend you never got, and a second chance you'll never receive — for one reason only: so that, while the door is still open, you turn toward His side *now.*

### The closing invitation

You are not being asked to overhaul your life tonight. You are being asked something smaller and far more urgent: to recognize that you are living inside the answered prayer of the damned. The *karrah* is in your hands. The *janb* — the side of Allah — is right beside you, as it always was. Turn one degree toward it before you sleep. That single turn, made now while it costs almost nothing, is the whole difference between the soul in these verses and the soul reading them.
