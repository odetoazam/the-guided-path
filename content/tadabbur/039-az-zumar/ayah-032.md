# The Two Directions of a Lie

## Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 32

> فَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّن كَذَبَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ وَكَذَّبَ بِٱلصِّدْقِ إِذْ جَآءَهُۥٓ ۚ أَلَيْسَ فِى جَهَنَّمَ مَثْوًى لِّلْكَـٰفِرِينَ

*Faman aẓlamu mimman kadhaba ʿalā-llāhi wa kadhdhaba bi-ṣ-ṣidqi idh jāʾahu — alaysa fī jahannama mathwan lil-kāfirīn.*

**"So who is more unjust than one who lies against Allah and denies the truth when it comes to him? Is there not, in Hell, an abode for those who reject?"**

---

## Introduction: Rebuilding the World

Let's begin by standing where the first listeners stood.

Surah Az-Zumar — *the Crowds*, *the Throngs* — is a Meccan surah, revealed into a city whose entire economy and identity were braided around idols. The Kaʿbah, the house Ibrahim عليه السلام had raised for the worship of the One God, had become a gallery of three hundred and sixty statues. And the people of Makkah were not crude or stupid. They were sophisticated. They had theology. They had answers ready. When you challenged their idols, they did not say, "We have no god." They said, "*These bring us closer to Allah*" (39:3). They had a story about God. A confident, inherited, socially-reinforced story about God.

That is the world this verse drops into. Not a world of people who deny that God exists. A world of people who say a great deal *about* God — and most of it manufactured.

Now feel the texture of the surah around this ayah. Just before our verse, Allah has spoken of death, of souls taken in sleep and released again, of the Day when the people will *dispute before their Lord* (39:31). The air is thick with the question of who was right. Who told the truth about God, and who told lies? And into that charged atmosphere comes a question — sharp as a blade, quiet as a verdict.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. This single verse contains a man committing **two crimes** — and at first they look like one crime, the same sin said twice. But they are not the same. They point in opposite directions. One is a sin of *speaking*. The other is a sin of *listening*. And the Arabic marks the difference with a single doubled letter that most translations cannot carry across.

By the end, you will see why these two — the lie you tell about God, and the truth you refuse from God — are not two sins at all. They are the front and the back of the same closed door.

Let's open it.

# PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Aẓlamu* — The Question That Already Knows Its Answer

Before we touch the word, let's understand the *kind* of question being asked.

In every language, there is a question that is not really asking. If a mother walks into a wrecked kitchen and says to her child, "Who made this mess?" — she is not gathering information. She knows. The question is a frame around a fact. The Arabic rhetoricians had a precise name for this: *istifhām taqrīrī* — the **interrogative of affirmation**. A question shaped so that the listener cannot help but supply the answer themselves, and in supplying it, *owns* it.

Hold that, because this verse opens and closes with one.

The opening: **فَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ** — *faman aẓlamu* — "So who is more unjust...?"

The word **أَظْلَمُ** comes from the root *ẓ-l-m* (ظلم). And here it sits in the form the grammarians call *afʿal al-tafḍīl* — the form of comparison and superlative. *Aẓlam.* "More unjust." "Most unjust." It is the same construction as *akbar* (greater), *aʿlam* (more knowing).

So the question is: "Who is **more** unjust than this person?"

Now — what does *ẓulm* actually mean? This matters enormously, so let's slow down.

We translate *ẓulm* as "injustice" or "wrongdoing," and that is correct, but it is thin. The deep, lexical meaning of *ẓulm* — attested across the classical dictionaries — is **putting something in the place that is not its place**. *Waḍʿ al-shayʾ fī ghayri mawḍiʿih.* Misplacement. Taking a thing and setting it where it does not belong.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. *Ẓulm* is not first about cruelty. It is about **disorder** — about a thing torn out of its rightful position. When you oppress a person, you have put them below where they belong. When you commit *shirk*, you have taken the worship that belongs to the Creator and set it before a stone — you have put worship in the wrong place. The Quran calls *shirk* "a tremendous *ẓulm*" (31:13) precisely for this reason: it is the ultimate misplacement.

So when the verse asks *faman aẓlamu* — "who is more *misplacing*, more disordering of reality, than this one?" — it is asking about a person who has not just sinned, but who has thrown the deepest structure of existence out of joint.

And because this is *istifhām taqrīrī*, the question of affirmation, the answer is placed in your own mouth: **No one.** *Lā aḥada aẓlamu.* No one is more unjust. The four classical commentators are unanimous on this — al-Jalālayn opens his gloss with exactly these words: *"faman — ay, lā aḥada aẓlamu."* "So who — meaning, no one is more unjust."

Notice what Allah has done. He has not *told* you this person is the worst of the unjust. He has *asked* you. And you answered. The verdict is now yours.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: The One Letter That Changes Everything — *Kadhaba* and *Kadhdhaba*

Here is the heart of the verse. Stay very close with me for this.

The person in this ayah does two things:

> **كَذَبَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ** — *kadhaba ʿalā-llāh* — "lied against Allah"

> **وَكَذَّبَ بِٱلصِّدْقِ** — *wa kadhdhaba bi-ṣ-ṣidq* — "and denied the truth"

Look at the two verbs. **كَذَبَ** and **كَذَّبَ**. To the eye they are almost identical. To the ear, the difference is a single doubled letter — a tiny *shaddah*, a small stress on the middle consonant: *kadhaba* … *kadh-dhaba*. Both come from the very same root: *k-dh-b* (كذب), the root of falsehood and lying.

And yet they are not the same word. They are not even pointing the same direction.

Let me teach the structure first, with something from ordinary life.

In Arabic, the base verb — what grammarians call Form I — names a simple action. But when you *double* the middle letter, you move into **Form II**, and Form II characteristically *intensifies* the action, or makes it *transitive* in a particular way — it turns the action outward, makes it *do something to* an object. Think of the everyday contrast: *ʿalima* means "he knew"; double the middle letter to *ʿallama* and it becomes "he *taught*" — he made knowing happen in someone else. The doubled letter is a kind of engine. It drives the meaning outward, or drives it harder.

Now apply that here.

**كَذَبَ — *kadhaba*** (Form I) means simply: **to lie. To fabricate. To say what is false.** It is the sin of *production*. You make a falsehood and you put it out into the world. And the verse tells us precisely the falsehood's *target*: **عَلَى ٱللَّهِ** — *ʿalā-llāh* — "**against** Allah," "**upon** Allah." This is to invent something and attach it to God's name. The classical commentators name it exactly: al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, and al-Baghawī all gloss it the same way — *to claim that He has a partner, that He has a child* — to manufacture a god of your own design and stamp God's name on the forgery.

[PAUSE]

Now the second verb.

**كَذَّبَ — *kadhdhaba*** (Form II) does **not** mean "to lie." It means something the English word "lie" cannot hold: **to call something a lie. To declare a true thing false. To reject it as falsehood.** The doubled letter has turned the action outward — it now has a target out in the world, and the target is **بِٱلصِّدْقِ** — *bi-ṣ-ṣidq* — "**the truth**."

Do you feel the difference now?

*Kadhaba ʿalā-llāh* — he **manufactured** a falsehood and aimed it **at God**.

*Kadhdhaba bi-ṣ-ṣidq* — he took a **truth** and **branded it a lie**.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

These are opposite motions. The first is a sin of the tongue going *out* — speaking what is false. The second is a sin of the heart slamming *shut* — refusing what is true. One produces a lie. The other destroys a truth. One says *yes* to falsehood. The other says *no* to reality.

And here is what is breathtaking: **it is the same root.** *K-dh-b.* The very same three letters that, in the first verb, describe a man *generating* falsehood, describe in the second verb a man *seeing* falsehood where there is none. As if the Quran is showing you — at the level of the consonants themselves — that these are not two unrelated faults. They grow from a single soil. **The person who is willing to invent lies about God is, by that same disease, unable to recognize the truth from God when it arrives.** The forger of currency cannot tell the real coin from the counterfeit, because his whole eye has been trained on the fake.

The faculty that fabricates and the faculty that rejects are the same faculty, broken in the same place.

## Element Three: *Al-Ṣidq* and *Idh Jāʾahu* — The Truth, and the Moment It Knocked

Now look at *what* he rejected. The verse does not say "he denied the Quran" — though the commentators tell us the Quran is precisely what is meant (al-Jalālayn, al-Qurṭubī, al-Baghawī all gloss *al-ṣidq* here as *the Quran*). The verse says he denied **ٱلصِّدْق** — *al-ṣidq* — **the truth, the truthfulness itself.**

This is a deliberate and devastating choice of word. *Al-ṣidq*, from the root *ṣ-d-q* (صدق), is not merely "a true statement." It is *truthfulness* as a quality — sincerity, reliability, the perfect correspondence between what is said and what is real. It is the opposite of the entire root we just left, *k-dh-b*. The Quran could have named itself. Instead it calls itself *al-Ṣidq* — Truth-Itself-Arriving — so that the man's crime is enlarged before your eyes: he did not reject *a* truth, a *claim*, a *book* he could quibble with. He rejected **Truthfulness** when Truthfulness came to his door.

And then — the timing. **إِذْ جَآءَهُۥ** — *idh jāʾahu* — "**when it came to him.**"

The little word *idh* fixes the crime to a specific instant: the very moment of arrival. From the root *j-y-ʾ* (جيء), *jāʾahu* — "it came to him." Not "after he studied it for years." Not "once he had weighed the evidence." The grammar locks the rejection to the *moment it reached him*. The truth knocked, and in the same breath he called it a lie.

[PAUSE]

This is the aggravating circumstance. Think of the difference between a man who slowly, painfully, after long searching, cannot bring himself to believe — and a man who slams the door the instant he hears the knock. The verse describes the second man. The truth did not fail to reach him. It reached him *fully* — *jāʾahu*, it came right *to him* — and his response was instantaneous rejection. He never let it in long enough to be examined. *That* is the *ẓulm*. Not ignorance. **Refusal.**

## The Closing Question: *Alaysa* — and the Word *Mathwā*

The verse opened with a rhetorical question. It now closes with one — and this is the second *istifhām taqrīrī*, the bookend:

> **أَلَيْسَ فِى جَهَنَّمَ مَثْوًى لِّلْكَـٰفِرِينَ** — *alaysa fī jahannama mathwan lil-kāfirīn* — "Is there not, in Hell, an abode for those who reject?"

*Alaysa* — "Is there not...?" Again, a question that demands you answer it yourself. Al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī both name it explicitly: *istifhām bi-maʿnā at-taqrīr* — a question that means *affirmation*. The unspoken answer, which Arabic supplies with the word *balā*, is: **"Yes. There certainly is."** Al-Jalālayn even writes the answer into his commentary: *balā* — "yes, indeed." Once more, the verdict is placed in your mouth. You do not hear God declare the sentence. You hear yourself confirm it.

But linger on one word in the sentence: **مَثْوًى** — *mathwan*.

We translate it "abode," "dwelling," "lodging." But *mathwā* carries something the English misses. Al-Qurṭubī pauses on this very word and traces its root: it comes from *thawā bi-l-makān* (ثوى بالمكان) — **to settle in a place, to reside there, to stay.** *Thawāʾan wa thuwiyyan* — a lasting staying. He notes a fine grammatical point: the form *mathwā* is built from *thawā* (to reside), not from another root — and *thawā* is the verb of **permanent residence**, of *coming to rest and remaining*.

[PAUSE]

So the closing word is not "prison." It is not "punishment." It is *home address*. *Mathwā* is the place where you **finally settle** — where the traveling stops and you stay.

Feel the terrible quiet of that. The whole verse has been about a man in *motion* — manufacturing lies, slamming doors, rejecting truth at the instant it arrives. Restless, busy, productive in his falsehood. And the verse ends by telling us where all that motion *comes to rest*. Where he *settles*. The frantic activity of denial has a final destination, a place the soul stops moving — and it is named *mathwā*, the dwelling, the residence, the address you do not leave.

The crime was about what he did with the truth *when it came to him*. The sentence is about where he *comes to rest* forever. And the two are bound by the same logic: **where you settle is built from what you did with the truth when it arrived at your door.**

### Bridge

The linguistic construction has shown us three things, layered into one another. A question whose answer is *no one* — this is the worst of the unjust. A single doubled letter dividing one root into two opposite crimes — *manufacturing* a lie about God, and *rejecting* the truth from God. And a final word, *mathwā*, that turns a punishment into an *address* — the place where a restless denier finally comes to rest.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About the human heart, and the strange double-motion of how we deceive ourselves.

# PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The God You Invent and the God You Refuse

Let's start where you live.

There is a phenomenon every honest person recognizes in themselves. We tend to construct a version of God that is *comfortable* — a God who happens to approve of exactly what we already want to do, who is offended by exactly the people we already dislike, who would never ask of us the one thing we are unwilling to give. We build, quietly and over years, a god in our own image. A god who agrees with us.

Psychologists call a cousin of this *projection* — attributing our own preferences to an external authority so we never have to feel the friction of correction. We do it with God constantly. "God just wants me to be happy." "God wouldn't mind." "My God isn't like that." Notice how often that phrase — *my* God — is doing the work of dismissing a claim we'd rather not face.

Now hold that ordinary, almost invisible habit against the first verb of our ayah: **كَذَبَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ** — *to lie against Allah.* To say of God what God did not say. To attach your own preference to His name.

[PAUSE]

The Makkans did it at full volume — partners, daughters, intermediaries of stone. But the verse is not really about statues. It is about the *motion of the soul* that produces statues. The motion that would rather **edit God down to a manageable size** than stand before the God who is actually there. Every age has its idols, and the modern idol is rarely carved. It is *narrated*. It is the comfortable god we talk into being, sentence by sentence, until he fits inside our life without disturbing it.

And here is where the two verbs lock together into a single mechanism. Watch closely.

If a person spends years *manufacturing* a god who agrees with them — *kadhaba ʿalā-llāh* — then what happens on the day the **real** truth arrives? The day *al-ṣidq* knocks — a truth that does *not* agree with them, that demands the very thing they were unwilling to give? They cannot receive it. They are not *able* to. Because they have trained their entire faculty of judgment on the counterfeit. The real coin, when it lands in their palm, feels wrong — *because it does not match the fakes they made.* So they do the only thing the disease permits: **كَذَّبَ بِٱلصِّدْق** — they call the truth a lie.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The inventing and the rejecting are not two separate sins. **The first one disables you for the second.** The man who builds a comfortable god *guarantees* that he will reject the true one when He comes. The forgery is what blinds him to the original. This is why the verse uses the same root, *k-dh-b*, for both motions — the lie he speaks and the truth he refuses are the same illness expressed twice. One root. Two symptoms. A single closed heart.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It means the most dangerous spiritual work you do is not the loud, obvious sin. It is the **quiet editing**. The slow, comfortable construction of a God who never asks you to change. Because every brick in that construction is also a brick in the wall that will keep the real truth out. The practical application is almost frightening in its simplicity: *whenever you find yourself saying "God surely wants…" or "God wouldn't mind…" — stop and ask: am I describing God, or am I forging Him?* Am I receiving *al-ṣidq*, or am I manufacturing a coin that fits my hand? That single question, asked honestly, is the difference between the two verbs of this ayah.

## Theme Two: Where the Running Stops — *Mathwā* and the Architecture of a Life

Now let's turn to that last, quiet word, and what it does to how we understand a whole life.

Think about how a permanent address actually forms. No one *chooses* their final dwelling in a single dramatic act. You drift toward it. A thousand small decisions — which street felt like home, which neighborhood you kept returning to, which door you knocked on again and again — and one day, without a single ceremony, you realize: *this is where I live now.* This is where I settled. The address was not an event. It was the **sum of a direction**.

The Arabic *mathwā* — the place where you *come to rest and stay* — carries exactly this. It is not a verdict dropped on you from the sky at the end. It is the **destination of a direction you were already walking.**

Now look back at the man in our verse. Every motion described is a *refusal of truth at the moment it arrives*. *Idh jāʾahu* — when it came to him, he turned it away. Picture that not as a single event but as a *habit*. Truth came to him — and he turned it away. Truth came again — and he turned it away. A life is made of these knocks. Each one is small. Each one feels survivable, deferrable, negotiable. *I'll think about it later. Not now. My god wouldn't mind.* And every deferral is a step in a direction.

And the verse tells you, with that final word, where the direction *settles*. *Mathwā.* The running stops. Not because a punishment was imposed from outside, but because **a person eventually becomes a permanent resident of the choice they kept making.** You move into the house you kept walking toward.

[PAUSE]

This is why the verse binds the crime and the consequence with such tight logic. The crime was about *the moment of arrival* — what he did when truth knocked. The consequence is about *the final residence* — where he stops moving. And in between is nothing but repetition. The instant of rejection, repeated until it becomes an address.

There is something almost merciful hidden in this, and we should not miss it. If *mathwā* is the sum of a direction, then **the direction is changeable while you are still walking.** The horror of the verse is reserved for the *kāfir* — the one whose rejection has *settled*, who has come to rest in the refusal. But every knock that has not yet been answered is a knock you can still answer. The door the verse describes as slammed shut — *kadhdhaba bi-ṣ-ṣidqi idh jāʾahu* — is, for the one still living, a door that can still be opened the next time truth arrives.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It reframes the small moments. The next time a truth arrives that you would rather not face — a correction from the Quran, a reminder from a believer, a quiet certainty in your own chest that you have been avoiding — recognize it for what it is: **a knock that is building your address.** Not a single isolated choice, but a brick in the dwelling where you will one day settle. The practical application: treat the *small* arrivals of truth as seriously as you imagine you would treat a *great* one. Because there is no great one. There is only the sum of the small ones. *Mathwā* is built quietly.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architectural Reveal

Now that we have done the work, let me show you something Allah placed in the *seam between this verse and the next* — something you cannot see until you have understood the two verbs.

Our verse, 39:32, describes a man defined by two motions: **وَكَذَّبَ بِٱلصِّدْقِ** — he *called the truth a lie* — using Form II of *k-dh-b* against *al-ṣidq*.

Now hear the very next ayah, 39:33:

> **وَٱلَّذِى جَآءَ بِٱلصِّدْقِ وَصَدَّقَ بِهِۦٓ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُتَّقُونَ**
> "And the one who **brought the truth** and **confirmed it** — those are the people of God-consciousness."

Look at what Allah has done. He has placed two human beings side by side, and built them from the *same two roots, mirrored*.

The man of verse 32: *kadhdhaba bi-ṣ-ṣidq* — he **branded the truth false**. (Form II of *kadhaba* — falsehood — aimed at *al-ṣidq*.)

The man of verse 33: *jāʾa bi-ṣ-ṣidqi wa **ṣaddaqa** bih* — he **brought the truth and confirmed it as true**. (Form II of *ṣadaqa* — truthfulness — *ṣaddaqa*.)

[PAUSE]

Feel the perfect symmetry. *Al-ṣidq* — **the truth — stands in the exact center of both verses**, unmoved. It is the hinge. And humanity divides according to a single question: *what did you do when al-ṣidq came to you?* One man took the root of *falsehood* and threw it *at* the truth — *kadhdhaba.* The other took the root of *truthfulness* and *joined himself to* the truth — *ṣaddaqa.* Same grammatical form, Form II, the engine that drives the action outward. Opposite roots. Opposite destinies. *Mathwā* in Hell for the one — *taqwā* and the rank of the muttaqūn for the other.

This is the surah named *Az-Zumar*, **The Groups**, the surah of humanity parting into two throngs. And here, in the seam between two verses, you can watch the parting happen at the level of a *single consonantal root*. The whole division of mankind, compressed into the difference between *kadhdhaba* and *ṣaddaqa* — between throwing falsehood at the truth and binding yourself to it.

That is the proof. The architecture *is* the argument.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same insight approached from two sides.

The grammar showed us two verbs from one root — *manufacturing* a lie about God and *rejecting* His truth — and we discovered they are a single disease, because the forger of gods is exactly the man who cannot recognize the real one. The themes showed us *why*: the comfortable god you build is the wall that keeps the true God out, and the truth you keep turning away, knock by knock, slowly becomes the address where you finally settle — your *mathwā*.

One sin, two directions. One root, two crimes. One question — *what did you do when al-ṣidq came to you?* — and two throngs, parting forever.

## Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life have I been *describing* God when I was really *forging* Him — attaching His name to what I already wanted?
2. When truth has arrived at my door — a correction, a reminder, a certainty I'd been avoiding — what has been my instinct in the *first instant*? To open, or to brand it false?
3. If *mathwā* is the sum of a direction, what direction am I actually walking — not in my intentions, but in my repeated small responses to truth?
4. Is there a knock I am deferring right now, telling myself *later*, *not yet*, *my God wouldn't mind*?
5. The man of 39:33 *brought* the truth and *confirmed* it. Beyond merely not rejecting truth — am I one who *carries* it, *joins* myself to it, *confirms* it?

## One-Sentence Distillation

There is no greater injustice than to manufacture a god who agrees with you and then to brand the real truth a lie the moment it arrives — for the same broken faculty that forges the false coin cannot recognize the true one, and where a soul finally settles is built, knock by knock, from what it did with the truth when it came.

## The Closing Invitation

This verse is not, in the end, a verse about *them* — the idolaters of Makkah with their stone daughters of God. It is a mirror held up to the quietest motion of your own heart: the editing, the deferring, the comfortable narration of a God who never disturbs you. The invitation is not to fear the loud sin. It is to notice the silent one — and to treat the next small arrival of truth as what it truly is: a knock that is building your home. You are still walking. The address is not yet sealed. Open the door.

## Du'ā

O Allah, Truth itself, *al-Ḥaqq* — keep our tongues from forging anything in Your name, and keep our hearts from branding Your truth a lie when it arrives. When *al-ṣidq* knocks at our door, let us be of those who *bring* it and *confirm* it, not of those who turn it away. And let the place where we finally settle be a dwelling of Your mercy, and never the *mathwā* of those who refused. *Āmīn.*
