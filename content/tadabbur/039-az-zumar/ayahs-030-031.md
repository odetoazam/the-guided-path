# The Appointment You Share With Your Enemy

### Surah Az-Zumar, Ayahs 30–31

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. In Arabic:

> **إِنَّكَ مَيِّتٌ وَإِنَّهُم مَّيِّتُونَ**
> *Innaka mayyitun wa innahum mayyitūn*
>
> **ثُمَّ إِنَّكُمْ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ عِندَ رَبِّكُمْ تَخْتَصِمُونَ**
> *Thumma innakum yawma-l-qiyāmati ʿinda rabbikum takhtaṣimūn*

And in plain English:

> *"Indeed, you are going to die, and indeed, they are going to die. Then indeed you, on the Day of Resurrection, before your Lord, will dispute."*

Now I want you to imagine the moment these words landed.

### Rebuild the world

Mecca. The message has been public for years. A man stands in a city that raised him, that called him *al-Amīn* — the trustworthy — and that now treats him as a wound it wants to close. His enemies have tried mockery. They have tried bargaining. They have tried boycott and violence. And underneath all of it, there is a quieter strategy, the patient one, the one they whisper among themselves: *just wait.*

Just wait. He is one man. He is mortal. He has no son who survives him. **Let him die, and this whole thing dies with him.** Among the narrations for these verses, al-Jalalayn records that they were revealed precisely because the opponents were counting down — *istabṭaʾū mawtahu*, they found his death slow in coming. They were watching the man's mortality like a clock, waiting for it to run out.

Picture that kind of waiting. Not the heat of an argument — the cold patience of someone who believes that time is on their side. Someone who believes that if they simply outlast you, they win.

Now hear what Allah says to His Prophet in response.

He does not say, *"Do not worry, you will outlive them."* He does not promise the Prophet a longer life. He does not deny the premise. He says something far stranger, far more disarming:

**"Indeed, you are going to die."**

[PAUSE]

Stop there. Of all the ways to comfort someone whose enemies are praying for his death — Allah's opening move is to confirm it. *Yes. You will die.* The thing they are waiting for will happen.

So where is the consolation? Where is the answer to their patience?

It is hiding in the next three words. And then it is hiding in the entire second verse — in a single shift from "you" to "they," and in a word at the very end of verse 31 that turns a graveyard into a courtroom.

Here is what I want you to feel before we even begin: these two short verses take the cruelest hope of the Prophet's enemies — *he'll die and it'll be over* — and they detonate it from the inside. **Not by denying death. By following death past the grave.**

Let's go slowly.

---

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The word that means "not yet dead" — مَيِّتٌ

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about Arabic, because without it the first word will pass you by completely.

Arabic loves to compress an entire sentence into a single word-shape. One of its favorite tools is what grammarians call the *active participle* — a noun-form built off a verb that names **the one who does the action** or **the one in the state of the action**. From the verb "to write," *kātib* — a writer, one writing. From "to know," *ʿālim* — a knower. The shape itself carries the meaning: *this is the one who is in this state.*

Now here is the subtle thing. In English, "dead" and "dying" and "mortal" are three different words. In Arabic, around the root for death — **م و ت**, *m-w-t* — there is a distinction so fine that most people walking past it never notice it. And it is *load-bearing* for this verse.

Look at the word Allah chose:

> **مَيِّتٌ** — *mayyit*

It has a *shadda*, a doubling, sitting on that middle letter. *May-yit.* The tongue presses and holds. There is a heaviness built into the very pronunciation.

Now — why does that matter? Listen to what the classical grammarians tell us, because this is not my invention; it is recorded by **al-Qurṭubī** and by **al-Baghawī**, both citing the great early linguists **al-Farrāʾ and al-Kisāʾī**:

> *"al-mayyit* — with the doubling, the heavy form — is the one **who has not yet died but will die.** *al-mayt* — lightened, without the doubling — is the one **from whom the soul has already departed.**"

[PAUSE]

Sit with that. Arabic has two words. One means *a corpse — the soul is already gone.* The other means *a living person who carries death inside them as a certainty not yet arrived.*

And al-Baghawī adds the punchline: *"and that is why it is **not** lightened here."* Allah deliberately uses the heavy form, the *mayyit*, the one-who-will-die-but-still-lives.

Do you see what that does to the verse?

The Prophet ﷺ, standing alive in front of his enemies, is not being called a corpse. He is being called *the one carrying an appointment.* The death is real, it is certain, it is coming — **but it is in the word's grammar that he is still alive.** The shadda holds the life and the death together in a single breath. *May-yit.* Alive now. Dying for certain.

Let's go one layer deeper, because al-Qurṭubī gives us a stunning parallel from ordinary Arabic speech. He says it is like the Arabs saying of a man at a meal: *"he is one who will fall sick from this food"* — not because he is sick yet, but because the sickness is written into what he is eating. The illness is latent. Loaded. Inevitable but not yet here.

That is what *mayyit* says about every one of us. **Death is not an event that happens to you at the end. It is a condition you are already living inside.** You are not someone who will, one day, become mortal. You are *mayyit* — mortal right now, carrying it, the way the man at the table is already carrying the sickness in the food he has chosen.

And here is the quiet devastation of it for the Prophet's enemies. They were waiting for him to *become* a corpse — to cross from one category to the other. But the verse has already told them: **he is mayyit, and so are you.** You are not the doctors watching the patient. You are in the next bed. The word that describes him describes you, with the very same shadda.

> *What this single word reveals: death is not the line at the end of your life that someone else crosses. It is the state you are standing in right now — and so is everyone you are waiting to outlive.*

### Element Two: The pronoun that will not let anyone stand outside — from "you" to "they" to "you all"

Here is a second tool of Arabic you need before we move.

Arabic does not bury its pronouns the way English does. In English I can say "you will die" and the "you" is a small grey word, easy to skip. In Arabic, the pronoun is fused right onto the particle of emphasis, *inna*, and it lands like a finger pressed to the chest. *Inna-ka* — *indeed-YOU.* *Inna-hum* — *indeed-THEY.* *Inna-kum* — *indeed-YOU-ALL.* You cannot mumble it. The grammar makes you point.

Now watch the camera move across these two verses. The classical scholars call this movement *iltifāt* — the turn, the shift of address that the Quran uses to keep you from settling into a comfortable seat.

**First:** *Innaka* — *Indeed YOU* (singular). One man. The Prophet ﷺ, addressed alone. "You are going to die."

**Then:** *Innahum* — *Indeed THEY.* The pronoun turns away from the Prophet and points at his enemies, the ones doing the waiting. "And they are going to die."

So far this reads almost like a standoff. You over here, them over there. Two sides of a feud, each mortal.

**But then verse 31 opens, and the pronoun changes shape one more time:**

> **ثُمَّ إِنَّكُمْ** — *Thumma inna-**kum*** — *Then, indeed, YOU — all of you.*

[PAUSE]

Feel that. The "you" has gone *plural.* The two camps — the singular Prophet and the plural enemies — have been swept into a single second-person word. *Innakum.* All of you. Together. The same pronoun now wraps around the Prophet and his persecutors at once.

This is breathtaking craftsmanship. In verse 30 they are kept apart — *you* and *they.* Death is described as something each side does separately. But the moment death is past and the Resurrection is named, the grammar fuses them: *you-all.* The line that separated the believer from his enemy in this world **dissolves at the threshold of the next.** There are no longer two camps standing apart. There is one defendant pool standing before one Judge.

And notice — the listener is included. The enemies were imagining themselves as spectators of the Prophet's death, safely outside the frame. The pronoun reaches out and pulls them *into* the frame. You thought you were watching. *Innakum* — you are in it.

This is why I said in the introduction that these verses detonate the enemies' hope from the inside. Their entire strategy depended on standing *outside* — on being the survivors, the ones who watch and outlast. The grammar denies them the outside. There is no outside. *Innaka, innahum, innakum* — the singular, the plural, and then the fused plural that holds them both. No one gets to be the spectator of someone else's death. **Everyone is in the dock.**

> *What this shift reveals: the world sorts us into "me" and "them," the surviving and the dying. Death and what follows it erase that sorting. Before the Lord there is only "all of you."*

---

### Element Three: The word that turns a grave into a courtroom — تَخْتَصِمُونَ

Now the final word of verse 31, and it is the destination of everything.

Teach yourself one last piece of Arabic. There is a verb-pattern called *Form VIII* — the *iftaʿala* pattern — and one of its core jobs is to make an action **reflexive or mutual**: something the parties do *to and with each other.* From the root for "gathering," you get *ijtamaʿa* — to come together. From the root for "difference," *ikhtalafa* — to differ with one another.

Now take the root **خ ص م**, *kh-ṣ-m* — the root of *opposition, of being adversaries, of the two sides of a lawsuit.* A *khaṣm* is your opponent in a dispute, your adversary across the courtroom. Pour that root into the mutual Form VIII pattern and you get:

> **تَخْتَصِمُونَ** — *takhtaṣimūn* — *you will contend against one another, you will press your case against each other, you will quarrel as adversaries before the bench.*

This is not a soft word. It is a courtroom word. It is the word for two parties each insisting *I am right and he wronged me,* each demanding judgment against the other.

And where does this contending happen? Read the phrase that precedes it, because the verse stacks three things before it lets the verb fall:

> **يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ** — *on the Day of Standing-Up* (al-Qiyāmah, from the root *q-w-m*, the day creation literally stands)
> **عِندَ رَبِّكُمْ** — *in the presence of your Lord*
> **تَخْتَصِمُونَ** — *you will contend.*

[PAUSE — let this build]

Do you see the architecture? The dispute that the enemies were having with the Prophet — the mockery, the plots, the patient waiting for him to die — *that quarrel does not end at the grave.* The word *thumma* at the start of the verse — "**then**" — tells us there is a sequence. First you die. Then *they* die. **Then** — *thumma* — comes the part they never planned for. The quarrel reopens. In a higher court. Before the Judge who cannot be lobbied, outwaited, or outlived.

This is the answer to their cold patience. They thought death was the *end* of the argument — outlast him and the case is closed in your favor. The verse says: death is not the end of the argument. **Death is the adjournment.** The case is moved, not dismissed. *ʿinda rabbikum* — *in the presence of your Lord* — it is reconvened in the one venue where the verdict is real.

And now read the *absence*, because Arabic teaches as much by what it withholds as by what it says.

**Allah does not tell us what they dispute about.** *Takhtaṣimūn* has no stated object. He does not say "you will dispute about the message" or "about who was right." The object is left open — and that openness is the point. It widens the verse until it covers *every* unresolved dispute a human being carries to the grave. Every wrong you were sure you'd gotten away with because the other person died first. Every account you thought was closed because you outlasted the one who could testify against you. The unspecified object means: **whatever your quarrel was, it is on the docket.**

> *What this word reveals: no conflict is ever truly settled by survival. The one who dies first has not lost the case; the one who lives longest has not won it. Every dispute is merely adjourned to the only court whose verdict holds.*

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

Look at what these three words have built between them.

*Mayyit* told us death is not an event we await but a state we are already in. The shifting pronouns told us death erases the line between "me" and "them" until there is only "all of you." And *takhtaṣimūn* told us that what waits on the far side of death is not silence but a reckoning — the reopening of every quarrel we thought the grave had closed.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about how we are actually living, right now, today.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The thing we are all, quietly, waiting to outlive

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a strange machinery in the human heart, and it runs all the time beneath our awareness. It is the machinery of *outlasting.* We do it constantly, in small ways and large. We watch the people we are in conflict with — the difficult parent, the colleague who blocked us, the sibling we fell out with, the public figure we despise — and some buried part of us is *waiting.* Waiting for them to be gone. Waiting for time to settle the score we couldn't settle ourselves. We rarely say it out loud. We might be ashamed to. But the calculation runs: *if I just outlast this, I win.*

Modern psychology has a clean name for the larger version of this. It is called **mortality denial** — or, in the research that followed Ernest Becker's work, *terror management.* The finding is consistent and a little frightening: human beings cannot function while staring directly at their own death, so we build elaborate psychological structures to keep it out of view. We throw ourselves into projects that will outlive us. We treat our own death as a rumor about someone else. We become, in the deepest sense, **spectators of mortality** — death is something that happens to *the dying,* to *them,* to the people in the obituaries, never to the "you" reading this sentence.

The Prophet's enemies were the purest possible case of this. They had turned their own mortality denial into a *strategy.* They were so completely outside the frame of death that they could stand around waiting for another man's death as a solution to their problems — never once turning the lens on themselves.

And into that exact posture, Allah drops one word: *mayyit.*

Not *"they will die."* First — **"you are going to die."** *Innaka mayyit.* The lens turns. The spectator is put in the picture. The one doing the waiting is told: *the clock you are watching is your own.*

Here is what I want you to feel about the mercy of this. We usually hear "you are going to die" as a threat. But look at the context — these words were sent down as **consolation to the Prophet ﷺ.** They are framed as comfort. How is "you will die" a comfort?

Because the thing that poisons the human heart is not death. It is the *fantasy of outlasting* — the bitter, exhausting, secret project of waiting for our enemies to be gone. That fantasy is a cage. It chains your peace to another person's funeral. And the verse breaks the cage with a single fact: **you are not the survivor in this story. Nobody is.** *Innaka mayyit wa innahum mayyitūn.* You will die. They will die. The waiting was always pointless, because the one waiting is in the same line.

There is a freedom in that you may not expect. When you truly absorb that you are *mayyit* — not "will become mortal someday" but *am, right now, a person carrying death* — the entire machinery of outlasting falls silent. You stop watching others' clocks. You start watching your own. And watching your own clock is the beginning of every good thing: of urgency, of repentance, of mending what can still be mended, of refusing to waste a single one of the days you can still see.

The companions understood this. It is why the remembrance of death — *dhikr al-mawt* — was never morbid for them. It was the great clarifier. The Prophet ﷺ taught, in a well-known narration, to *"remember often the destroyer of pleasures"* — death — not to make life grim, but to make it *honest.* To pull you out of the spectator's seat and into your own life.

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Tonight, name the person you are quietly waiting to outlast — the grudge you've outsourced to time, the score you've left for death to settle. And then say over yourself the word the Prophet was given: *I am mayyit.* I am in the same line. The waiting ends here. What do I need to mend, forgive, or finish *while I can still see the clock?*

### Theme Two: The case you think you won

Now the second theme, and it goes somewhere harder.

Begin again with something you know. Think of an argument from your life that was never resolved — not patched up, not forgiven, just *ended* by circumstance. The other person moved away. Or stopped speaking to you. Or died. And the conflict simply... stopped. There was no verdict. No one said "you were right." It just went quiet.

Notice what your mind did with that silence. For most of us, when a conflict goes quiet without a verdict, we slowly award the verdict *to ourselves.* If they're gone and I'm still here, telling the story, then in some unexamined way — *I won.* The survivor becomes the historian, and the historian always writes himself as the one who was right.

The enemies of the Prophet ﷺ were running exactly this logic, scaled up to cosmic size. *Let him die. Then there's no one left to make the case. The argument goes quiet, and silence will be read as our victory.* They were not just waiting for a man to die. They were waiting to inherit the verdict by default.

And the second verse walks straight up to that fantasy and dismantles it word by word.

*Thumma* — **then.** There is an "after" you did not budget for. You thought the timeline ended at the grave. It doesn't. There's a *then.*

*Innakum* — **all of you.** The one you outlived is standing next to you. He didn't disappear. He's been summoned too.

*Yawma-l-qiyāmah* — **on the Day of Standing.** A day when everything that was buried *stands back up.* Including the case you thought you'd buried.

*ʿinda rabbikum* — **in the presence of your Lord.** Not your court. Not the court of public opinion you spent years managing. *His* court — where the survivor has no home-field advantage, where the historian doesn't get to write the verdict, where outliving someone earns you exactly nothing.

*Takhtaṣimūn* — **you will contend, both of you, as adversaries.** The dead opponent gets his voice back. The case is *reopened,* with the one party who can't be silenced presiding.

[PAUSE]

Let this paradox sit, because the verse does not rush to soften it: **the one who dies first has not lost, and the one who lives longest has not won.** Everything we do to "win" a conflict through time and survival is undone. The quarrel you successfully avoided, the apology you never gave because they died before you had to, the wrong you committed against someone who can no longer accuse you — *takhtaṣimūn.* It is all on the docket. *ʿinda rabbikum.*

And remember the absence we found: Allah did not name *what* the dispute is about. He left the object open. Which means this verse is not only about the Prophet and his enemies. It is about *you* and everyone you have an unsettled account with. The open object reaches across fourteen centuries and lands the verse in your own relationships. Every "I'll just let it go because they're gone now." Every "it doesn't matter anymore, that was years ago." The verse whispers: *it is adjourned, not dismissed.*

Now — here is where this becomes mercy and not just terror, because the same fact cuts both ways.

If you are the *wronged* one — the person who was crushed in an earthly dispute, who never got justice, who watched the powerful walk away clean — this verse is the most stabilizing promise in the world. **Your case is not closed.** The fact that you couldn't win it here, that you died or will die with the injustice unanswered, does not mean it vanished. *ʿinda rabbikum takhtaṣimūn.* There is a venue where your testimony cannot be suppressed and the verdict cannot be bought. The Day of Standing is, for the wronged, the Day their case is finally *heard.*

And if you are the one who *did* the wrong — the verse is a summons sent in advance, and a summons sent in advance is an act of mercy. Because you are still *mayyit* — still in the living-but-mortal state, still on this side of the adjournment. The court has not yet convened. Which means the one thing the enemies of the Prophet never did, you still can do: **settle out of court.** Go to the person. Return what you took. Ask forgiveness while forgiveness is still cheap — while the other party can still grant it, before the case moves to a bench where the price is incalculably higher.

The Prophet ﷺ warned about exactly this in a well-known narration: *whoever has wronged his brother in his honor or his wealth, let him seek his pardon today, before a Day comes when there is no dinar and no dirham* — only deeds, traded across the courtroom of the Resurrection. That hadith is *takhtaṣimūn* spelled out. The dispute is real. The currency is your good deeds. **Settle now.**

**What this changes about tomorrow:** Take one unsettled account — one person you wronged, even slightly; one apology you've been letting time bury. And settle it out of court. A message. A return. A sincere "I was wrong." Do it while you are still *mayyit* and not yet *khaṣm* before the bench. And if *you* are the wronged one carrying an injustice no one ever answered — hand the case file to the only Judge who never loses evidence, and let the weight of it leave your chest. *ʿinda rabbikum* — it is in better hands than yours.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture

Now stand back and see how these nine words are built, because the structure is the proof.

Verse 30 is the world *as the enemies saw it* — divided. *You* (the Prophet) over here. *They* (the enemies) over there. Two camps, each mortal, separated by the very grammar: *innaka… innahum.* Death described as something each side does on its own side of the line.

Verse 31 is the world *as it truly is* — fused and summoned. *Thumma* — then — and suddenly the two camps collapse into one pronoun, *innakum,* hauled together to one place, *ʿinda rabbikum,* for one purpose, *takhtaṣimūn.*

The two verses are a before-and-after. Before: the human illusion of separate fates, of survivors and the dead, of winners by outlasting. After: the single reality of one courtroom where the separation was never real. **The structure itself enacts the lesson** — the verses divide, and then they fuse, exactly as death divides us in the world's eyes and the Resurrection fuses us before the Throne.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight all along, approached from two directions.

The word *mayyit* — "you are dying right now" — is the same truth as *stop waiting to outlast anyone.* The shifting pronouns ending in *innakum* — "all of you, together" — are the same truth as *there are no survivors and no spectators.* And *takhtaṣimūn* before your Lord — the reopened case — is the same truth as *no conflict is ever closed by death; settle out of court while you still can.*

Three words. One message: **The death you await for your enemy is the appointment you share with him. And the quarrel you think you've outlived will be the first thing standing back up.**

### Questions to carry

- Who, in the honest basement of my heart, am I waiting to outlast? And what would change if I truly believed I am in the same line as them?
- Where have I let *time* settle a score I was too proud or too afraid to settle myself?
- Is there a dispute I think is "closed" only because the other person can no longer speak? Is it actually closed — or only adjourned?
- If my case were reopened *ʿinda rabbī*, before my Lord, tomorrow — which of my quarrels would I most wish I had settled out of court tonight?
- And the gentler one: is there an injustice done to *me* that I am still carrying, that I could finally set down by handing it to the only Judge who never loses the file?

### One-sentence distillation

You are not the survivor who outlasts his enemy — you are *mayyit* standing in the same line he stands in, and every quarrel you bury will stand back up before the only Judge whose verdict is real.

### The closing invitation

Let this not stay information. Let it move one specific thing. Before you sleep tonight, do two small acts. First: name the person you've been waiting for time to deal with, and release the waiting — say over yourself, *I am mayyit, I am in the same line, the clock I'm watching is my own.* Second: pick one account you can settle out of court — one apology, one return, one "I was wrong" — and begin it. While you are still on this side of *thumma.* While the court has not yet convened.

### Du'ā

*Yā Rabb,* You who named us *mayyit* so we would stop pretending we are spectators — pull us out of the seat of those who wait to outlast others, and seat us in our own short lives with honest urgency. Settle our hearts toward everyone we have wronged before the Day we stand contending *ʿinda Rabbinā,* and for every injustice we could not answer here, be the Judge who never loses the case. Let us arrive at that standing with our quarrels already laid down. *Āmīn.*
