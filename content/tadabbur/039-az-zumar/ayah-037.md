# No One Can Unmake What Allah Has Made

### Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 37

> وَمَن يَهْدِ ٱللَّهُ فَمَا لَهُۥ مِن مُّضِلٍّ ۗ أَلَيْسَ ٱللَّهُ بِعَزِيزٍ ذِى ٱنتِقَامٍ

*Wa man yahdi-llāhu famā lahu min muḍill. Alaysa-llāhu bi-ʿazīzin dhī-ntiqām.*

**"And whoever Allah guides — for him there is no one who can lead astray. Is Allah not Exalted in Might, the Master of Retribution?"**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Threat Behind the Verse

Let me take you to the moment this verse was first heard.

A man is standing more or less alone. He has been telling his own people — his uncles, his cousins, the merchants he grew up among — that the stone figures they have circled their whole lives are nothing. That the gods their fathers carved and named and fed are empty. Imagine the audacity of that, in that world. He is not just disagreeing with their theology. He is dismantling the entire spiritual economy of a city.

And they push back. Not only with arguments. **With threats.**

The verse right before ours tells us exactly what they did:

> *"...and they threaten you with those besides Him."* (39:36)

Sit with the strangeness of that. They are threatening him *with their idols.* "Be careful. Insult al-Lāt, mock al-ʿUzzā, and you'll see what they do to you. You'll be struck down. Cursed. Made mad." This was a real fear in that world — that the offended gods would reach out and break the one who defied them. Among the narrations preserved in classical commentary, the disbelievers warned of exactly this kind of supernatural harm.

So picture the emotional climate. One human being, telling the truth, surrounded by people insisting that unseen powers are about to come for him. The loneliness of conviction. The slow, grinding pressure of *everyone* telling you that you've lost your way and the universe itself is going to punish you for it.

Into that pressure, Allah speaks. And He speaks in **two matched questions.**

Verse 36: *Is Allah not sufficient for His servant?* — **A-laysa-llāhu bi-kāfin ʿabdah.**

Verse 37: *Is Allah not Exalted in Might, the Master of Retribution?* — **A-laysa-llāhu bi-ʿazīzin dhī-ntiqām.**

And wedged between those two questions is a single, quiet, almost unremarkable-looking sentence:

> *Whoever Allah guides — for him there is no one who can lead astray.*

**The Hook:** Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin. The disbelievers threatened him with the power of false gods. You would expect the answer to be about *guidance* being sweet, or true, or comforting. Instead, the verse about guidance ends with **might** and **retribution.** Why does a sentence about being guided close with a warning about Allah's power to avenge? What does protection have to do with vengeance? And why is the promise phrased not as "Allah will help the one He guides" but as something stranger — *there is no one, anywhere, who can lead him astray?*

There is something stunning buried in the grammar of this verse. Let's go find it.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Conditional That Hands Everything to Allah

Before we open the verse, let me teach you one small piece of how Arabic builds an *if-then* sentence — because the entire weight of this ayah hangs on it.

In English, when we say "if," we usually keep the verb in its ordinary form. "If he *studies*, he passes." The verb sits there unchanged. But in Arabic, a conditional sentence physically *reshapes* the verb. When you say "whoever does X" — *man yafʿal* — the verb gets clipped. Its ending is cut off, dropped into a state called **jazm**, apocopation. The word is literally shortened, trimmed at the end, to mark that it lives inside a condition.

It's a small thing. But it means that in Arabic you can *hear* a condition. The verb sounds truncated, suspended, leaning forward toward its result.

Now open the verse:

> وَمَن يَهْدِ ٱللَّهُ — **wa man yahdi-llāh**

Not *yahdī* with a long, settled final vowel. **Yahdi** — clipped. Cut short. The ending shaved off by the condition.

Let's sit with this for a moment, because the clipping is doing something. The verb *to guide* is rendered incomplete in form — and then immediately, in the very next breath, comes the One who completes it: **Allah.**

Notice *who* is the subject of that verb. **Whoever Allah guides.** Not "whoever guides himself." Not "whoever finds the path." Not "whoever is intelligent enough, or sincere enough, or lucky enough." The grammar puts the action entirely in Allah's hands. *Man yahdi-llāh* — the guided one is the **object**; Allah is the **subject.** You do not guide yourself into this sentence. You are guided into it.

Here's what's remarkable. We tend to think of guidance as something we achieve. We "found Islam." We "did our research." We "came back to the deen." And in one sense, the effort is real. But the grammar of this verse quietly relocates the source. The decisive act of guidance is *His*. You are the one being led; He is the One who leads.

[PAUSE]

And this matters enormously for what comes next. Because if *you* were the source of your own guidance, then you could also be the source of your own ruin. What you built, you could un-build. What you reasoned your way into, someone could reason you out of. But if **Allah** is the one who placed you on the path —

— then who, exactly, is going to remove you from it?

That question is the whole verse. Let's keep going.

---

### Element Two: The Word "Min" That Empties the Universe

Now we arrive at the response to the condition — the "then" half of the if-then:

> فَمَا لَهُۥ مِن مُّضِلٍّ — **famā lahu min muḍill**

The surface translation: *"then there is no misleader for him."* Clean enough. But there is a single, tiny word in there that most translations cannot carry into English, and it changes everything.

The word is **مِن — min.**

Let me teach you what it's doing. Normally *min* means "from" or "of" — "I drank *min* the water," from the water. But Arabic has a second use of *min*, one that appears specifically after a negation. Grammarians call it **min zā'idah** — an "extra" *min*, though "extra" is a terrible name for it, because it is not extra at all. It is an intensifier. It takes a negation and makes it **total.**

Here's the everyday feel of it. In English, if I say "there is no money in my pocket," you might think — okay, no significant money. Maybe loose change. But if I say "there is **not a single bit** of money in my pocket," I've closed every loophole. Not a coin. Not a cent. Nothing. That second sentence — *not a single bit* — is what *min* does in Arabic after a negation. Grammarians call this **istighrāq**: comprehensive, all-devouring negation. It sweeps the entire category clean.

So look again:

> فَمَا لَهُۥ مِن مُّضِلٍّ

Without the *min*, it would mean: "he has no misleader." With the *min*, it means:

**"He does not have, anywhere in existence, so much as a single solitary misleader."**

Not one. Not a powerful one or a weak one. Not a human one or a hidden one. Not an idol, not a tyrant, not a doubt, not a desire, not a whispering shaytān, not a clever argument, not a crisis of faith at three in the morning. The *min* reaches out and **empties the entire universe** of anyone capable of leading this person astray.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the scale of that? The disbelievers stood there threatening the Prophet ﷺ with their gods. *Watch out, the idols will reach you.* And the answer is not "the idols are weak." The answer is far more total: **for the one Allah has guided, there does not exist — in the whole of creation — a single agent of misguidance with power over him.** The threat isn't rebutted. The entire *category* of threat is declared empty.

Stay with me, because there's one more layer, and it's the deepest.

Notice the **silence** in this phrase. Notice what is *missing.* The verse says "there is no misleader for him." It does **not** say *who* the misleader might have been. It does not say "no shaytān." It does not say "no disbeliever." It does not specify an object — "no one to mislead him *from such-and-such.*" It leaves the noun bare, indefinite, unqualified: **muḍill** — *a misleader,* any misleader at all.

In Arabic, an indefinite noun inside a comprehensive negation becomes the widest possible statement. By refusing to name a specific enemy, the verse refuses to set a limit. The moment you name the enemy — "no shaytān can mislead you" — you've implied that maybe something *other* than a shaytān could. So the verse names no one. It just declares the role itself vacant. **The position of "the one who leads you astray" has no occupant.** It is an empty chair, in an empty room, in a universe swept clean by a single two-letter word.

---

### Element Three: The Word "Misleader" and the One Who Causes the Loss

Let me slow down on that word **muḍill** itself, because its shape carries a teaching.

It comes from the root **ḍ-l-l** — the root of *ḍalāl*, being lost. And here we step from grammar onto a stepping-stone, a physical image. *Ḍalāl* in the language of the Arabs is the image of **losing your way in open country.** The desert traveler who can no longer find the track. The water that sinks into the sand and vanishes — *ḍalla al-māʾ*, the water "got lost" in the earth. To be in *ḍalāl* is to be the person wandering in trackless waste, every dune identical to the last, the path gone, the sun offering no help, walking and walking and arriving nowhere.

That is the physical picture. But of course the verse is not about geography. It is pointing at the unseen reality the desert can only gesture toward: the soul that has lost the path to its Lord. The heart wandering. The life that moves and moves and arrives nowhere true.

Now, the word in our verse is not the simple *ḍāll*, "one who is lost." It is **muḍill** — and that one extra dimension matters. *Muḍill* is built on the **causative** pattern. The root verb *ḍalla* means "to go astray" — to get lost yourself. But shift it to this form — *aḍalla* — and it means "to **cause** someone else to get lost." To take a person who was on the road and *lead them off it.* To be the false guide who points down the wrong valley.

So *muḍill* is not someone who is merely lost himself. He is someone with the **power to lose you.** An active agent. A hand on your shoulder turning you the wrong way. The treacherous guide.

Now feel the full weight of the sentence. The desert is the deadliest place on earth to have a treacherous guide, because out there you cannot find the way alone — you are utterly dependent on the one who leads. And the verse says: for the one Allah has guided, **there is no treacherous guide.** Not a single false hand will turn you off the road. Because the One leading you is not a fallible Bedouin who might himself be mistaken — He is the One who *made* the desert, who *set* the stars you navigate by, who *is* the destination.

[PAUSE]

And here the architecture of the verse begins to reveal itself. Think about the matching it sets up. *Man **yahdi**-llāh* — whoever **Allah guides.** *Famā lahu min **muḍill*** — has no **misleader.** The verse places **the Guide** and **the misleader** on opposite scales of a balance. On one side: Allah, doing the guiding. On the other side: the entire empty category of those who would mislead. And the balance does not even tip. It simply collapses. Against the One who guides, the misleader is not outmatched — **he does not exist.**

---

### The Bridge

So look at what these ten Arabic words have built, just at the level of language.

The conditional verb hands the act of guidance entirely to Allah — *you* are guided, you do not guide yourself. The tiny word *min* then empties the entire universe of anyone who could undo what He has done. And the word *muḍill* tells us precisely what has been emptied out: not lostness in the abstract, but every false guide, every treacherous hand that might turn you off the road in the dark.

Three pieces. One meaning: **what Allah has established in a soul, no power in creation can dislodge.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because that last clause of the verse — the part about might and retribution — is still waiting for us, and it is going to explain *why* the universe can be emptied of misleaders in the first place.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Quiet Terror of Losing Your Faith

Let me start somewhere you already live.

If you have ever truly believed something — not casually, but with your whole chest — you have probably also tasted the fear of losing it. It is one of the most particular kinds of anxiety there is. You come back to your prayer after years away, and it is beautiful, and then a small cold voice says: *how long will this last?* You read something, or meet someone, or simply lie awake, and a doubt slides in sideways, and your stomach drops — not because the doubt is strong, but because you suddenly realize **your faith is a thing that could be taken from you.**

This is, I think, one of the great unspoken anxieties of religious life in our moment. We live inside an endless argument. Every conviction is contested. Every screen offers a thousand confident voices explaining why you are wrong, why your tradition is a fairy tale, why the path you're walking is a path to nowhere. And underneath the arguments runs the deeper dread: *I found my way back, but I am not sure I can keep it. What if I drift? What if, in ten years, this light in me has gone out and I don't even notice it leaving?*

Hold that fear in your hand for a moment. The fragility of it. The sense that faith is a candle in a windy room and the world is full of doors swinging open.

Now let the verse speak into exactly that room.

> *And whoever Allah guides — for him there is no one who can lead astray.*

Here is the first thing the verse does to that anxiety. It relocates the *source.* Remember the grammar: you are not the one who guided yourself onto this path. **Allah** guided you. And that single relocation changes the entire physics of your fear. Because a candle *you* lit, the wind can blow out. But the verse is not describing a candle you lit. It is describing a light **He** kindled — and the question is no longer "can I keep this from going out?" but "**who has the power to extinguish what Allah has lit?**"

And the answer, swept clean by that little word *min*, is: **no one.** Not a single one. *Famā lahu min muḍill.*

Let me be careful and honest here, because this is sacred and I won't overstate it. The verse is not a magic charm that abolishes your responsibility. The same surah is *drenched* in calls to sincerity, to turning back, to worshipping Allah with a purified heart. Guidance, once given, is still something you walk in, still something you can turn away from by your own choice — the Quran is unambiguous that human beings can and do reject what they were shown. So this is not "you're saved, relax forever." 

What the verse *does* abolish is something more precise and more freeing: **the fear of external defeat.** It tells you that no outside force — no argument, no enemy, no idol, no mocker, no shaytān, no circumstance — has the *power* to wrench guidance out of a heart that Allah has settled. The lock cannot be picked from the outside. The only door is the one you open yourself, from within. And that means the thing you actually need to guard is not the whole hostile universe out there — it is the small, manageable, *yours-to-tend* matter of your own sincerity.

[PAUSE]

Do you see how that re-sizes the fear? The anxiety told you: *the world is enormous and full of forces that could destroy your faith, and you are small.* The verse answers: *the world has been emptied of forces that can destroy your faith. There is only one chair in this room, and it is the one you sit in. Tend that, and the universe cannot touch you.*

**What this changes about tomorrow:** The next time the cold voice says *how long will this last,* you can answer it precisely. *This was not mine to begin with — it was given. And what He gives, no one out there can take. My only job is to keep my own hand on the door from the inside — to keep asking the One who guided me to keep me guided.* That is, in fact, exactly why we are commanded to say *ihdina-ṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* — guide us to the straight path — seventeen times a day. Not because guidance keeps slipping away, but because the One who can secure it against all of creation is the One we keep returning to ask.

### Theme Two: Why the Guide Must Also Be the Avenger

Now we come to the puzzle I left hanging at the very beginning. The strange ending.

A verse about *guidance* closes with two of the heaviest words in the Quran:

> أَلَيْسَ ٱللَّهُ بِعَزِيزٍ ذِى ٱنتِقَامٍ — *Is Allah not Exalted in Might, the Master of Retribution?*

Why? Why does a sentence consoling someone about being guided end on **might** and **vengeance**? It feels almost like a key change in the wrong direction. We were talking about being safely led, and suddenly the language turns to power and punishment. What is going on?

Let me begin, again, where you live.

Think about what it actually feels like to hold an unpopular conviction in a hostile crowd. It is not only that you might be argued out of it. It is that you feel **exposed.** Outnumbered. Unprotected. There is a specific loneliness to being the only one in the room who believes a thing, and a specific fear that comes with it — *what if they don't just disagree with me; what if they come for me?* The disbelievers were not merely debating the Prophet ﷺ. They were **threatening** him. *Our gods will reach you.* And the deepest wound of a threat is the implication: *you have no one. You stand alone. There is no one strong enough behind you to make us pay for what we might do.*

Now feel how the verse answers that exact wound.

First, let me open the word **ʿazīz.** It comes from the root *ʿ-z-z*, and the root's oldest physical meaning is striking: it described **land that is hard, impenetrable, that cannot be reached or trodden** — and, by extension, anything **rare, precious, and unconquerable.** To be *ʿazīz* is to be the fortress that cannot be stormed, the height that cannot be scaled, the thing so strong and so rare that it is simply beyond your reach. So when Allah is *al-ʿAzīz*, it does not only mean "mighty" in some vague way. It means: **unassailable. Unreachable. Impossible to overpower.** The very thing the lonely, threatened believer feels he is *not* — exposed, reachable, conquerable — Allah declares Himself to be, *on the believer's behalf.*

Then comes **dhī-ntiqām** — *Master of Retribution.* And here, too, the shape of the word matters. *Intiqām* is built on a form — *intaqama* — that carries the sense of **taking** retribution, of justice *seized and exacted,* not merely felt. It is not a passive disapproval. It is active, deliberate, certain settling of accounts. The One who, when His servants are wronged and His truth is mocked, **does** something about it.

Now put the two together and watch the puzzle dissolve.

The believer's fear was: *I am led onto this path, but I am alone and exposed, and the powers of this world can come for me.* And the verse answers in perfect sequence: *Whoever Allah guides has no misleader* — your path is secure — **and** *Allah is the Unassailable* — your Guide cannot be overpowered — **and** *the Master of Retribution* — and those who threaten you will answer to Him.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel how the ending is not a key change at all — it is the **foundation** revealed? Now we finally understand *why* the universe could be emptied of misleaders back in the first half of the verse. *How* can it be true that "there is no one who can lead him astray"? Because the One doing the guiding is **al-ʿAzīz** — unconquerable. A weak guide's protection is only as good as the guide's strength; anyone stronger could shove him aside and steal his traveler. But no one is stronger than al-ʿAzīz. There is no power "above" Him to overrule His guidance. *That* is the engine underneath the promise. The misleaders' chair is empty because the only One with real power has already taken the seat of your Guide — and **He cannot be moved from it.**

And this teaches us something tender about guidance itself, something we might otherwise miss. **Guidance is not only direction. It is protection.** A guide who shows you the road but cannot defend you on it is not much of a guide in dangerous country. The full mercy of being guided by *al-ʿAzīz dhī-ntiqām* is that the same hand that points the way also **stands behind you with absolute power.** You are not pointed down a road and abandoned to the predators on it. You are led by the One whom no predator can reach — and who will, in His own time, settle every account with anyone who tried to drag you off the path.

[PAUSE]

**What this changes about tomorrow:** When you feel outnumbered for your faith — at work, in your family, online, anywhere the crowd makes you feel small and exposed for what you believe — return to this verse and feel its real shape. You are not standing alone. You are standing with the One who *cannot be reached,* and the threats arrayed against you are not facing *you* — they are, in the end, facing **Him.** That does not make you arrogant; *ʿizza* is His, not yours. But it does make you *steady.* The steadiness of someone who knows that the strongest thing in existence is on the side of the truth they're holding.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now step back and look at the whole verse at once — and at the pair it belongs to.

Allah set two questions on either side of a promise, like two pillars holding up a single arch:

- **Verse 36:** *Is Allah not **sufficient** for His servant?* — He is enough.
- **The promise (37):** *Whoever Allah guides has no misleader.* — He secures.
- **Verse 37:** *Is Allah not **Exalted in Might, Master of Retribution**?* — He cannot be overpowered, and He settles accounts.

Read in sequence, it is a complete answer to a frightened, threatened, outnumbered heart. *Is He not enough for you?* — so you need no other. *Then know your guidance is unbreakable from the outside.* *And is He not unconquerable, the Avenger?* — so the ones threatening you are the ones who should be afraid.

The two rhetorical questions, by the way, are doing one more quiet thing. *Alaysa* — "is it not?" — is the form of question that is really a statement you cannot disagree with. When Allah asks "*Is Allah not* Exalted in Might?", He is not seeking information. He is drawing the answer out of *you,* making your own heart say the *yes.* The grammar makes you a participant. You don't receive the conclusion — you *speak* it.

### The Two Roads, One Destination

The linguistic journey and the thematic journey turn out to be the same discovery approached from two directions.

From the **words**, we found: a verb that hands guidance to Allah, a *min* that empties the universe of misleaders, and a *muḍill* that names the precise danger that has been removed.

From the **themes**, we found: the fear of losing faith, answered by the relocation of its source to Allah; and the fear of standing alone, answered by the might of the Guide who stands behind you.

Both roads arrive at the same single truth: **What Allah establishes, no creature can undo — because the One who establishes it cannot be overpowered.** The grammar is the engine; the consolation is the journey; *al-ʿAzīz* is the reason both are true.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where in my life am I treating my faith as something *I* built and must defend alone — rather than something *He* gave and guards?
2. When I feel my conviction is fragile, am I afraid of an outside force taking it — when the verse tells me the only door is the one *I* open from within?
3. Who or what do I secretly experience as a "misleader" with power over me — and what would change if I truly believed that chair is empty?
4. When I feel outnumbered for my beliefs, do I feel alone — or do I feel the presence of *al-ʿAzīz* behind me?
5. Do I think of guidance as only *direction* — or have I let it become also *protection*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**When Allah Himself is your Guide, no power in all of creation can lead you astray — because the One leading you is the Unassailable, behind whom every threat must ultimately answer.**

### The Closing Invitation

You came into this verse perhaps carrying a quiet fear — that your faith is a candle in a windy room. I'd invite you to leave it carrying something else: the knowledge that it was never a candle you lit, and never a room *you* have to defend. It is a light He kindled, in a room He has swept clean of every hand that might reach for it. Your one task is small and gentle and *yours* — to keep turning back to Him, to keep your own sincerity tended, to keep saying *guide us.* The rest of the universe, with all its threats, has been answered already.

### A Du'a from These Words

*O Allah, You who guide — guide me, and let no misleader find a path to my heart. O al-ʿAzīz, the Unassailable, the One no power can overcome — let my faith rest in Your strength and not in my own, and keep me, from the inside, turning always back to You. Āmīn.*
