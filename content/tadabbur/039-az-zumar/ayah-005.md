# The God Who Is Still Wrapping the Night

## Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 5

> خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ بِٱلْحَقِّ ۖ يُكَوِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ عَلَى ٱلنَّهَارِ وَيُكَوِّرُ ٱلنَّهَارَ عَلَى ٱلَّيْلِ ۖ وَسَخَّرَ ٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ ۖ كُلٌّ يَجْرِى لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى ۗ أَلَا هُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفَّٰرُ

*Khalaqa-s-samāwāti wal-arḍa bil-ḥaqq. Yukawwiru-l-layla ʿalā-n-nahāri wa yukawwiru-n-nahāra ʿalā-l-layl. Wa sakhkhara-sh-shamsa wal-qamar. Kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā. Alā huwa-l-ʿAzīzu-l-Ghaffār.*

> "He created the heavens and the earth in truth. He wraps the night over the day and wraps the day over the night, and He has subjected the sun and the moon — each running for a term appointed. Unquestionably, He is the All-Mighty, the Oft-Forgiving."

---

## INTRODUCTION

### Rebuilding the World

Before we open a single word, let's place ourselves where this ayah was first heard.

We are in Mecca. The people being addressed are not atheists. This is important, and we tend to forget it. The Quraysh believed in Allah. They believed He created the heavens and the earth. If you asked them, *who made the sky? Who sends the rain? Who holds the sun on its course?* — they would have answered without hesitation: **Allah.** The Quran itself records this. *"And if you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would surely say: Allah."*

So the problem in Mecca was never that they denied a Creator. The problem was that they believed in a Creator who, in their imagination, had *retired*. A God who built the machine, set it running, and then stepped back — far away, exalted, unreachable — and so He needed intermediaries. Idols. Intercessors. Angels imagined as daughters. A son imagined to bridge the gap. The verse just before this one, ayah 4, confronts exactly that: *"Had Allah intended to take a son, He could have chosen from what He creates whatever He willed. Exalted is He."*

[PAUSE]

And then comes ayah 5. Watch what it does. It does not argue about the son directly. It does something far more devastating. It reaches up to the sky they all look at — the night they all sleep through, the day they all work through, the sun and moon they all navigate by — and it tells them something about those ordinary things that quietly demolishes the entire idea of an absent, retired God.

This is the strand running through the opening of Az-Zumar: **sincerity belongs to Allah alone** — *alā lillāhi-d-dīnu-l-khāliṣ* (39:3). And the whole case for that sincerity is built, here, out of the night sky.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin — and then I want us to spend the next hour earning it.

In the middle of this verse, in the heart of a sentence about the heavens and the earth, Allah suddenly changes something. He changes *tense*. He says "He **created**" — past, finished, done. And then, without warning, He switches to the present: He **wraps**. He is *wrapping*, right now. Not wrapped. Not has wrapped. **Wraps** — continuously, as you listen to this.

That single shift, from a finished past to an unfinished present, is the whole argument against the retired God. And the word He chooses for "wraps" — *yukawwiru* — is one of the most physically vivid, geometrically loaded words in the entire Quran.

Stay with me. By the end, the night sky will not look the same.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yukawwiru* — The Wrapping of a Round Thing

#### Teaching the concept first

Let me start far away from the verse, with something in your hands.

Imagine winding a long cloth around a ball. A turban around a head. Bandage around a wrist. Notice what that action requires: the thing you are wrapping *around* must be **round**. You cannot "wind" cloth around a flat board — you fold it, you lay it, you stack it. But to *wind*, to *coil*, to *wrap layer over layer in a continuous motion* — that needs a curved surface. The roundness is built into the verb.

Hold that physical intuition. We are going to need it.

#### Opening the word

Allah says:

> يُكَوِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ عَلَى ٱلنَّهَارِ
> *Yukawwiru-l-layla ʿalā-n-nahār*
> "He wraps the night over the day."

The verb is *yukawwiru*, from the root **k-w-r** (ك-و-ر). And I want to tell you where else this exact root lives in the Arabic language, because the lexicon is doing theology here.

#### Deepening

In classical Arabic — documented in *Lisān al-ʿArab* — the verb *kāra al-ʿimāmah* means **to wind the turban around the head**. The noun from this family, *al-kūr*, is the coil, the winding. And from this same root-field comes the word for something rounded and gathered up into a ball.

So when Allah says *yukawwiru-l-layl ʿalā-n-nahār*, the image is not night and day taking turns like two people clocking in and out. The image is **cloth being wound around a sphere**. The night is wrapped over the day the way a turban is wound around a head — smoothly, continuously, layer chasing layer, around something *curved*.

Let's sit with this.

The classical mufassirūn, each looking at this word, saw a different facet of one motion. Look at how they gather:

- **Al-Ḍaḥḥāk** said it means *He throws one over the other* — this part cast upon that part, which is, he notes, the very meaning of *takwīr* in the language: laying a thing's parts one upon another.
- **Qatāda** said it is like *"He covers the night with the day"* (7:54) — one drawn over the other like a veil pulled across.
- **Al-Ḥasan and al-Kalbī** said it is the *gradual* thing: night shortens and the day lengthens, then the day shortens and the night lengthens — what is taken from one is folded into the other, slowly, across the seasons.

Now — notice something. We are tempted to ask, *"Which one is correct?"* But that is the wrong question. Each of these is a camera angle on a single physical reality: the **smooth, gradual, rolling overlap** of night and day as it sweeps continuously around. The throwing-over, the veiling, the seasonal lengthening — they are all *takwīr*. They are all what happens when something is wound around a curve.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is remarkable, and I want to say this carefully because I do not want to claim more than the language gives us. The Quran did not need to use *yukawwiru*. There are other, simpler verbs for the night following the day — and the Quran uses them elsewhere: *yughshī* (covers), *yūliju* (enters one into the other). It had a whole vocabulary available. But *here*, in the verse arguing for the One who never stepped away, it reaches for the verb of **winding cloth around a round head**. The verb whose physical logic only fully makes sense on a curved surface.

We do not need to turn this into a science lecture to feel the weight of it. The classical scholars, with no satellites, heard *roundness* in this root because the Arabic language *put* it there. The word's own anatomy carries a shape. What this invites us to consider is simply this: the description Allah chose, fourteen centuries ago, is the description of a process that wraps — and wrapping wants a sphere.

#### The tense — the heart of it

But now the deepest thing about *yukawwiru*, and it has nothing to do with geometry.

It is **present tense.** Imperfect. *Yukawwiru* — "He wraps," ongoing, unfolding, incomplete, *happening*.

Look back at how the verse opened. *Khalaqa* — "He created." Past tense. Perfect. Finished. The heavens and the earth: a completed act. Done.

And then — *yukawwiru.* He **wraps.** Present. Living. Now.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the seam? Allah does not say "He created the heavens and the earth, and He *wrapped* the night over the day" — matching past with past, as if all of it were sealed in the distant beginning. He breaks the tense. The creation is finished; **the governing is not.** The wrapping of night over day is something He is doing *as this verse is recited* — last night, tonight, the night after you are buried.

This is the answer to the retired God. The Meccans imagined a Creator in the past tense. The verse answers in the present tense. He did not wind up the cosmos and walk away. **He is wrapping the night around the day right now**, and He will be wrapping it tonight whether or not you are awake to see it.

That single word reveals: *the Creator never left the room.*

### Element Two: *Sakhkhara* — The Sun and Moon Pressed Into Service

#### Teaching the concept first

Here is a small human picture. Think of the difference between a wild horse and a trained one. The wild horse has all its power — but that power serves no one but itself. The trained horse has the *same* power, the same muscle and speed, but now it is *harnessed*: it carries, it pulls, it serves a purpose outside itself. Nothing was added to the horse. Something was *bent* — its will, its motion, made to serve.

Arabic has a precise word for that bending of power into service. Hold the image of the harnessed horse.

#### Opening the word

> وَسَخَّرَ ٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ
> *Wa sakhkhara-sh-shamsa wal-qamar*
> "And He subjected the sun and the moon."

The verb is *sakhkhara*, from **s-kh-r** (س-خ-ر), and it is a **Form II** verb. In Arabic, Form II — *faʿʿala*, with the doubled middle letter — characteristically carries a *factitive* force: to *make* something become a certain way, to *render* it so. *Sakhkhara* means precisely: **to render subservient, to press into service, to harness.** Al-Muyassar glosses it beautifully — *dhallala-sh-shamsa wal-qamar* — "He made the sun and the moon docile, tamed, *for the benefit of His servants.*"

#### Deepening

Now feel the size of what is being said. The sun: a furnace so vast our entire world is a speck beside it. The moon: the mover of every ocean tide on the planet. These are, to any human eye, the two most *powerful*, most *untouchable* objects in existence. The ancient world looked up at exactly these two and made them *gods*. They were the obvious candidates for worship — the brightest, the highest, the most clearly mighty.

And the verse says: Allah **harnessed** them. *Sakhkhara*. The very objects nations bowed to are, in this sentence, the *trained horses* — pressed into service, carrying a load, running a route assigned to them. The thing you might worship is itself a servant under harness.

Do you see what this does to the argument of the surah? The whole opening is fighting *shirk* — the worship of anything alongside Allah. And here the two grandest possible rivals for worship — sun and moon — are shown not as masters but as *the harnessed*. You do not worship the horse that pulls the cart. You look for the One who tamed it.

#### The tense again — notice the pattern

And watch the tenses one more time, because the verse is building a structure:

- *Khalaqa* — He **created** the heavens and earth — **past.**
- *Yukawwiru* — He **wraps** night and day — **present.**
- *Sakhkhara* — He **subjected** the sun and moon — **past.**

The two great *acts of origin* — bringing the cosmos into being, and harnessing the celestial bodies — are in the perfect tense: settled, accomplished, structural. But nestled in the very center, the *daily, repeating, living* act — the wrapping of night over day — is in the present. The architecture itself tells the story: **the framework was set in the past; the life within it is ongoing.** The sun was harnessed long ago, *and* it is still being made to run *as we speak* — *kullun yajrī* — each one *running*, present tense, this instant.

That word *reveals*: even the "finished" acts are not abandoned acts. The harness still holds.

### Element Three: *Kullun Yajrī li-ajalin Musammā* — Each Running to a Named Term

#### Teaching the concept first

When you board a flight, the ticket has a destination and a time. The plane is in motion, powerful, free-looking against the clouds — but every second of that freedom is *aimed.* It is running *toward* a fixed point that was named before it left the ground. Motion plus a deadline. Freedom inside an appointment.

Hold that: motion that is already aimed at an end it did not choose.

#### Opening the words

> كُلٌّ يَجْرِى لِأَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى
> *Kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā*
> "Each one running for a term appointed."

*Yajrī* — from **j-r-y** (ج-ر-ي) — "to run, to flow, to course." The sun and the moon are not hanging; they are *running.* And they run *li-ajalin musammā* — toward **a term that has been named.** *Ajal* is an appointed span, a deadline. *Musammā* — "named, specified" — from the root of *ism*, a name. Not just a deadline: a **named** deadline. One that has already been spoken.

#### Deepening

Here is the thing I most want you to feel. *Ajal musammā* — "an appointed, named term" — is not a phrase the Quran reserves for the stars. It is the *same phrase* the Quran uses for **you.** For your death. *"It is He who created you from clay, then decreed a term — and a term named with Him"* (6:2). The lifespan of a human soul and the lifespan of the sun are described with the **identical words.**

[PAUSE]

So when Allah tells you the sun is *running toward a named term*, He is not only describing astronomy. He is holding up the largest, brightest, most permanent-*seeming* object you will ever see — the sun that rose before your grandparents and will rise after your grandchildren — and He is telling you: **even this is on a deadline. Even the sun has an appointment it is running toward and cannot postpone.**

The sun looks eternal. It is not. It is *running.* And the same word that clocks the sun's death clocks yours.

Mortality is not a flaw the Quran apologizes for. Here it is woven into the structure of the cosmos itself — *kullun*, "each one," *every* celestial body, under the same appointment as you. You are not the exception to a deathless universe. You are a small participant in a universe that is, top to bottom, **running toward a named term.** And the One who named it is the One who never stepped away.

That phrase reveals: *nothing in creation is idling; all of it is in motion toward an end already spoken.*

#### The bridge

So look at what these three words have shown us, before we step out of the language and into the heart of it.

*Yukawwiru* showed us a Creator in the **present tense** — still wrapping the night, never retired. *Sakhkhara* showed us the grandest objects of worship reduced to **harnessed servants.** *Kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā* showed us that all of it — sun, moon, and you — is **running toward a named appointment.**

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about how you will walk out of this room and live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The God Who Is Still Working

#### Begin where you already live

Let me describe a feeling you may know, even if you have never named it.

There is a particular kind of loneliness that is not about other people. It is the suspicion — quiet, rarely spoken — that even if God exists, He is *far.* That He set things in motion a long time ago and that the universe now runs on its own rules, like a clock ticking in an empty house. You can believe in Him completely and still *feel* this: that He is in the past tense. That the miracles happened *back then,* to *other* people. That the heavens were opened for prophets, long ago, and that what you have is the leftover machinery, running cold.

The philosophers gave this a name centuries ago: the **watchmaker God.** The God who built the watch, wound it, and walked away. And here is the uncomfortable truth — *most* of us are functional deists when we are not paying attention. We believe God *created.* We are far less sure He is *creating.* We believe He *acted.* We are not sure He is *acting* — tonight, in this room, in this exact hour of our small life.

[PAUSE]

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

And into precisely this loneliness, the verse breaks its own grammar.

*Khalaqa* — He created. Past. Yes. The watchmaker did wind the watch.

But then — *yukawwiru.* He **wraps.** Present. The same God, in the same breath, in the same sentence, refuses to stay in the past tense. He will not let you file Him under "once, long ago." He says: the night that will fall on you tonight — *I* am wrapping it. The dawn that will find you tomorrow — *I* am turning it. Not a law of physics running on its own in an empty house. **Me. Now. Still.**

The Quran could have kept the whole verse in the past. It would have been grammatically smooth, parallel, tidy. Instead it deliberately fractures the parallelism — *created … wraps … subjected* — past, present, past — so that the *one living, repeating, daily act* sits glowing in the present tense at the center. It is as if Allah reached into the most ordinary, overlooked event in your life — *night falling* — the thing so routine you have never once thanked Him for it — and said: *that is Me, working, right now.*

#### Deepening through the human door

Think about what this means for the texture of an ordinary day.

You did not consider, this morning, that the sunrise required anyone. It just… happened. That is the deepest reach of the watchmaker illusion: not that we deny God, but that we have *outsourced* Him to physics. The sun comes up because of orbital mechanics. The night falls because of rotation. We have explanations, and the explanations feel like they have *replaced* Him.

But the verse does not deny the mechanism. It does something subtler. It tells you *who is running the mechanism.* *Sakhkhara* — He harnessed the sun. The orbit is real — and the orbit is a *harness,* and there is a Hand on it. The night does wrap because the earth turns — and the turning is *yukawwiru,* and it is *His* wrapping. The explanation was never a competitor to God. It was a description of His ongoing work.

So the question the ayah leaves you with is not *"Do you believe God created the universe?"* You probably do. The question is: **Do you believe He is wrapping tonight's darkness around you personally — that the God of the opened heavens is on duty in your specific, unremarkable evening?**

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes tomorrow

Here is the practical shift, and it is small enough to actually do.

Tonight, when the light goes — when the room dims and you reach for a switch — stop for the length of one breath and notice it as *an act,* not an event. *Yukawwiru.* He is wrapping it, now. Tomorrow, when light returns, receive the dawn as a *delivery,* hand-turned, addressed to you. You do not need to feel a wave of emotion. You only need to relocate God from the past tense to the present — from "He created" to "He is, right now, wrapping this night over my day." The deism in us dies a little every time we catch Him *in the act.* And He is always in the act. The verse promises it in its very grammar.

### Theme Two: The Almighty Who Forgives

#### Begin where you already live

Let me ask you to picture the most powerful person you have ever stood in front of. A judge. A boss who held your livelihood. Someone whose single word could lift you or end you.

Now notice what power does to mercy in your experience of human beings. In our world, the two almost never travel together. The powerful are rarely the forgiving. We *expect* might to be hard, cold, unbending — *because* it is mighty. And we expect mercy to come from the gentle, the soft, the ones who do not have the power to crush us anyway. We have quietly accepted a trade-off as a law of nature: **the more power someone has over you, the less mercy you dare to hope for.** Strength forgives only when it is too weak to do otherwise.

Hold that assumption. It runs deep, and the end of this verse is about to break it in half.

#### Let the ayah illuminate it

Look at how the verse ends. After the heavens and the earth, after the wrapping of the night, after the harnessed sun and moon and their running toward a named death — after this overwhelming display of cosmic *power* — the verse closes on two names:

> أَلَا هُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفَّٰرُ
> *Alā huwa-l-ʿAzīzu-l-Ghaffār*
> "Unquestionably, He is **the All-Mighty, the Oft-Forgiving.**"

*Al-ʿAzīz* — the Mighty, the Overpowering, the One whom nothing can resist, the One who, as al-Jalālayn glosses, *prevails over His affair and exacts justice from His enemies.* This is the name you expect after a verse about cosmic power. This is the name that *fits.*

And then — *al-Ghaffār.*

[PAUSE]

The Oft-Forgiving. And not the usual word. Not *al-Ghafūr.* **Al-Ghaffār** — from the same root *gh-f-r,* but in the **intensive** form, the form of *abundance and repetition.* The One who forgives **again and again and again** — who covers sin upon sin, who keeps forgiving the *same* servant returning with the *same* weakness, who does not exhaust His pardon. Al-Jalālayn finishes the verse with one word: *al-Ghaffār — li-awliyāʾih* — "the Oft-Forgiving *to those who turn to Him.*"

#### Deepening — the pairing is the point

Now feel the collision of these two names sitting side by side.

By every rule of our human world, this pairing should not exist. *Al-ʿAzīz* — the One with absolute, unanswerable power — should, by our logic, be the *least* forgiving, because He has the *least* need to forgive. He loses nothing by crushing. Nothing forces His hand. The whole reason *we* forgive is often weakness — we forgive because we cannot win, because we need the relationship, because revenge costs too much. **Allah has none of those reasons.** He is *al-ʿAzīz.* He could exact every debt and lose nothing.

And He forgives anyway. *Al-Ghaffār.* Abundantly. Repeatedly.

[PAUSE]

Do you understand what that means? His forgiveness is not weakness dressed as mercy. It is not the soft, cornered pardon of someone with no other option. It is the forgiveness of the One who has *every* option — who holds the sun on a leash and turns the night with His hand — and who *chooses,* from a position of total power, to cover your sin again. The might makes the mercy *staggering.* A weak being's forgiveness is cheap; it had no teeth to begin with. The forgiveness of *al-ʿAzīz* is the most expensive thing in existence — pardon offered by the One who never had to offer it.

This is why the two names are *welded together* here and not separated. Strip away *al-ʿAzīz* and *al-Ghaffār* becomes sentiment. Strip away *al-Ghaffār* and *al-ʿAzīz* becomes terror. Together they are the truth of the God you actually stand before: **infinitely able to crush you, and choosing, again and again, to cover you instead.**

#### The human door — why you needed to hear this *here*

And now remember *where* this verse sits. The whole surah is dismantling shirk, confronting people who took sons and partners and intercessors for God — people in spiritual rebellion. You might expect the verse, after all that power, to end on a *threat.* The mighty Creator, the harnessed sun, the running deadline — *therefore fear Him, therefore tremble.* That is how the logic of power *should* end.

It does not. It ends on *al-Ghaffār.* The intensive forgiveness. To the very people being warned, the door is left not just open but *flung* open: the One you wronged, the One whose signs you ignored while you bowed to stone — He is *the Oft-Forgiving.* Turn, and find Him forgiving you not once, but again, and again.

[PAUSE]

#### What this changes tomorrow

Here is the practical weight of it.

Many of us carry a specific kind of despair: the despair of the *repeat* sin. The thing we have asked forgiveness for a hundred times and fallen into a hundred and one. We can believe God forgives *once.* We struggle to believe He forgives the *same failure, again.* We imagine His patience as a finite account we have nearly drained.

*Al-Ghaffār* is the answer to exactly that despair. The intensive form *is* the promise that the forgiveness is not a limited fund. The One forgiving you is *al-ʿAzīz* — He does not grow tired, He is not diminished, His pardon is not a resource He is running low on. So tomorrow, when you fall into the old thing again and the voice says *"you've used up your chances,"* answer it with the name: **al-Ghaffār.** The forgiveness that *repeats.* Return again. The door that the Mighty leaves open does not wear out, because He who holds it open does not wear out.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see the whole verse as one shape, because it is built with intention.

It opens at the **largest** scale: *the heavens and the earth* — the entire cosmos, created *bil-ḥaqq,* in truth, for a real purpose, not in vain. Then it narrows to the **rhythm** of that cosmos: *the wrapping of night over day,* present tense, ongoing. Then it narrows again to the two specific **bodies** that rule that rhythm: *the sun and the moon,* harnessed, running. Then it narrows to the **destiny** of all of it: *each running to a named term* — the whole magnificent machine aimed at an appointed end.

And then — having walked you from the edge of the universe inward, from the heavens down to the deadline written on the sun — it lands, finally, not on a *thing* at all, but on **Him**: *alā huwa* — "unquestionably, *He*…" — and gives you two names to hold Him by. *Al-ʿAzīz. Al-Ghaffār.*

The verse is a funnel. It pours the entire cosmos through itself and delivers you, at the bottom, into the presence of a God who is *mighty enough to run all of this* and *merciful enough to forgive you in the middle of it.* The scale of the opening exists to make the mercy of the ending land with full weight. You had to see how big He is *before* you were told He forgives — or you would not have understood what the forgiveness cost.

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two halves of our journey were the same insight from two directions.

The **language** showed us a God in the *present tense* — *yukawwiru,* still wrapping the night, never retired to the past. The **themes** showed us that same present-tense God is *al-Ghaffār* — still forgiving, again and again, never retired from mercy. It is one truth: **the God of Az-Zumar is not finished.** He is not finished *creating* — He wraps tonight's darkness with His own hand. And He is not finished *forgiving* — He covers tomorrow's sin with His own mercy. The verse refuses, in both its grammar and its names, to let you have a God of the past. He is working *now.* He is forgiving *now.* The watch was never abandoned, and neither were you.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I picture God, do I picture Him in the past tense — *who created* — or in the present — *who is wrapping this very night around me?* Which tense do I actually live in?

2. The sun and moon — the grandest things I can see — are *harnessed servants.* What have I been treating as a master in my life that is, in truth, only a harnessed thing running its appointed course?

3. *Kullun yajrī li-ajalin musammā.* The sun is on a deadline. If I truly felt that even the sun is *running* toward a named end — and that my term is named with the same word — what would I stop postponing?

4. Which of God's two final names do I struggle to believe in *for myself* — that He is mighty enough to handle my situation, or that He is forgiving enough to take *me* back, again?

5. Is there a repeat sin I have privately decided is beyond forgiveness? What does *al-Ghaffār* — the One who forgives *and forgives and forgives* — say to that decision?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The God who is *still* wrapping the night around your day — who never stepped back into the past tense — is mighty enough to hold the sun on a leash and merciful enough, from that very height of power, to forgive you again.

### The Closing Invitation

Let something small shift tonight. Not a grand resolution — just a relocation of God from *then* to *now.* When the night falls on you, let it be *yukawwiru* — His act, in the present, addressed to you. And when you next stumble and the voice says the door has closed, let the last two words of this verse be your answer. He is *al-ʿAzīz* — He does not need to forgive you. And He is *al-Ghaffār* — He does, anyway, and again. That is the God who is wrapping your night right now.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah — al-ʿAzīz, al-Ghaffār — You who are wrapping this night over the day with Your own hand, never far, never finished: let us feel You in the present tense, working in our smallest hours. And by the name You chose to end this verse — the One who forgives, and forgives again — cover us, return us, and do not let us despair of the door You have left open. You harnessed the sun; harness our wandering hearts to You. Āmīn.*
