# Even the Choicest of Creation Is Still a Creature

### A Tadabbur of Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 4

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the world they fell into.

> **لَّوْ أَرَادَ ٱللَّهُ أَن يَتَّخِذَ وَلَدًا لَّٱصْطَفَىٰ مِمَّا يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ ۚ سُبْحَـٰنَهُۥ ۖ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْوَٰحِدُ ٱلْقَهَّارُ**

> *Law arāda-llāhu an yattakhidha waladan la-ṣṭafā mimmā yakhluqu mā yashā'. Subḥānahu, huwa-llāhu-l-Wāḥidu-l-Qahhār.*

> **"Had Allah intended to take a son, He could have chosen from what He creates whatever He willed. Glory be to Him! He is Allah — the One, the Irresistible."**

Now let me rebuild the world.

This claim — that God has a son, a child, an offspring — was not a fringe idea on the edges of the Arabian Peninsula. It was *everywhere*. The pagans of Makkah looked at the angels and said: these are the **daughters** of Allah. Up north, there were those who said Uzayr — Ezra — was the son of God. Across the wider world of late antiquity, the dominant civilization built its entire theology on the sonship of the Messiah. So when this ayah descends, it is not answering an obscure heresy. It is reaching into the central nervous system of how human beings, across cultures and centuries, had decided to imagine the divine.

And here is what I want you to notice before we even begin. There are two ways to demolish a building.

You can stand outside it and shout that it is ugly, that it should never have been built, that the people who live in it are fools. That is denunciation. It changes no one's mind.

Or — you can walk *inside* the building, calmly, and point to the single load-bearing wall that was never actually there. And the building falls on its own.

[PAUSE]

This ayah does the second thing. It does not begin with "How dare you." It begins with a word that sounds almost like a concession. **Law.** *Had it been so... if He had wanted...* Allah, the Most High, momentarily *entertains* the premise. He picks it up. He turns it over in His hand. And in the act of examining it honestly, He lets it shatter.

Here is the hook. Hold onto it for the next hour. **The single most devastating word in this ayah is not a word of negation. It is a word of supposed agreement.** And buried inside a sentence that seems to say "even if He wanted to," there is a trap so complete that the premise can never recover. By the time you understand *why even the choicest of creation could never be His son*, you will understand something about the gap between the Creator and everything else that the mind almost cannot hold.

Let's walk inside.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Word "Law" — The "If" That Means "Never"

Let me teach you something about your own language before we touch the Arabic.

In English, we have two completely different kinds of "if," and we use them every day without ever noticing the difference.

The first kind is the open "if." *"If it rains tomorrow, I'll bring an umbrella."* This is a real possibility. It might rain. It might not. The door is open.

The second kind is the closed "if" — what grammarians call the counterfactual. *"If I were a bird, I would fly to you."* Notice — I am **not** a bird. The entire sentence is built on something that is not true and never will be. When I say "if I were a bird," the very grammar — *were*, not *was*; *would*, not *will* — quietly announces: **this did not happen, and it is not going to.** The "if" is not opening a door. The "if" is a way of pointing at a closed door to show you it is closed.

[PAUSE]

Arabic has exactly this distinction, and it is sharper than ours.

There is **idhā** and there is **in** — the open "if," for the real possibility. And then there is **law** — *لَّوْ*. And every student of the Arabic of the Qur'an learns this early: **law is the particle of the impossible condition.** *Ḥarf imtināʿ li-mtināʿ.* The grammarians defined it in a phrase: it signals "the non-occurrence of one thing because of the non-occurrence of another." When you hear *law*, the trained ear already knows — **the thing that follows did not happen.**

So look again at the very first word Allah chooses:

> **لَّوْ أَرَادَ ٱللَّهُ...** — *Law arāda-llāh...* — **"Had Allah intended..."**

Stay with this. Before a single argument is made, before the logic even unfolds, the *grammar itself* has already rendered the verdict. The choice of *law* over *in* is not stylistic decoration. It is the whole case in one syllable. Allah is not saying "maybe He did, maybe He didn't, let's see." He is saying: **this belongs to the category of things that are not, and the sentence I am about to build sits entirely inside that category.**

And notice the verb that follows: **أَرَادَ** — *arāda* — "intended," "willed." This is the root *r-w-d*, in its Form IV pattern — the deliberate, directed willing of something, the setting of an intention toward a goal. The grammar is precise: we are not even at the stage of *doing*. We are one full step back, at the stage of *wanting to*. The ayah does not say "if Allah had a son." It says "if Allah had so much as **intended** to take one." It rewinds all the way to the level of the will, the inner inclination — and then declares, through *law*, that even that inclination does not exist.

Here is what is remarkable. The opponents thought they were defending something glorious — that God is so great, so generous, so full of life that He overflows into offspring. And Allah responds by stepping *with* them, all the way back to the root of the idea, to the very first stirring of intention — *law arāda* — and showing that there is nothing there. **The "if" of agreement is the cruelest refutation, because it grants you everything and you still have nothing.**

[PAUSE]

That is the first word. Now look at what He does with the space it opens.

### Element Two: "Yattakhidha" and "Iṣṭafā" — The Two Verbs That Betray the Whole Claim

Here Allah lays a trap so quiet you could read past it a hundred times.

Watch the two key verbs in the sentence. The first is **يَتَّخِذَ** — *yattakhidha* — "to take," "to adopt." The second is **ٱصْطَفَىٰ** — *iṣṭafā* — "to select, to choose the choicest." I want to open each one, because each of them, on its own, quietly destroys the premise it is supposedly serving.

Start with **yattakhidha**. The root is *a-kh-dh*, and the bare verb means simply "to take." But notice the form — this is Form VIII, *ittakhadha*, the pattern that means "to take **for oneself**, to acquire, to adopt, to make something into something." It is the precise verb used for *adoption* — for taking a child who is not yours and making him yours.

Now think carefully about what that verb *presupposes*. You can only "take" something that **already exists, separate from you.** You cannot "acquire" what is part of your own being. The very verb — *yattakhidha* — quietly concedes that whatever this "son" would be, it would have to be something *external*, something *brought in*, something *taken*. It would be an acquisition.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the trap closing? A true son, in the way the opponents imagined — a being of the same essence, an extension of the divine nature itself — could never be described with the verb "to take." You don't *take* your own essence. The word the Qur'an selects to even *frame* their claim already exposes that what they are describing is not begetting at all. It is adoption. It is the bringing-in of an outsider. And an outsider, by definition, is *other than God.*

Now the second verb — and this one is the masterstroke. **ٱصْطَفَىٰ** — *iṣṭafā*.

The root is *ṣ-f-w*. And you need to feel what this root *means* before you feel what the word does. *Ṣafw*, *ṣafwa* — it is the **clear, pure, choicest part** of a thing. When you let a liquid settle and the clear part rises to the top, that clarity is the *ṣafwa*. When you take the cream that rises on milk, the very best that separates itself from the rest — that is the *ṣafwa*. The *ṣafwa* of anything is its purest, highest, most select portion.

And *iṣṭafā* — again Form VIII, "to take the *ṣafwa* for oneself" — means "to select the absolute choicest, to pick out the very best." This is the same verb Allah uses when He says He *chose* — *iṣṭafā* — Adam, and Nuh, and the family of Ibrahim, and Maryam, above all the worlds. It is the verb of divine election. The highest honor a creature can receive is to be among those Allah *iṣṭafā*.

So follow the sentence now with new eyes:

> **لَّٱصْطَفَىٰ مِمَّا يَخْلُقُ مَا يَشَآءُ** — *la-ṣṭafā mimmā yakhluqu mā yashā'* — **"He could have chosen, from what He creates, whatever He willed."**

[PAUSE — let this land]

Do you see it? Even in the *hypothetical*. Even when Allah momentarily entertains the impossible premise — what does He say the "son" would actually be? He would be **selected from what He creates.** *Mimmā yakhluqu* — "from that which He creates." The "son" of the opponents' imagination, when examined honestly, turns out to be nothing more than **the choicest item picked out of the inventory of creation.**

And here is the devastation. *You can promote a creature infinitely high — and he remains a creature.* You can select the cream of all that exists, the very *ṣafwa*, the angel nearest the Throne, the most luminous spirit, the purest soul — and the moment you say he was *selected from what is created*, you have said the one thing that makes "son" impossible. **A chosen creation is still a creation.** The act of *choosing* is itself the confession that this being stands *on the created side of the line* — that he was there, among the made things, available to be picked.

You cannot select your way across the gap between Creator and created. Selection operates *within* a category. It cannot move a thing into a different category of being. The best slave, promoted forever, is still a slave and never the master. The choicest creature, elevated infinitely, is still a creature and never the Creator.

[PAUSE]

So watch what these two verbs have done, working together. *Yattakhidha* — "to take" — quietly establishes that the "son" would have to be **external**, acquired, brought in. *Iṣṭafā* — "to select the choicest" — quietly establishes that this external being would have to be **drawn from creation**, the cream of the made world. Put them together and the entire claim has been dismantled from the inside, using only the vocabulary required to *state* it. The premise could not even be expressed in Arabic without the words themselves revealing it to be false.

This is what it means that Allah walked into the building. He did not shout. He simply *named* what they were claiming — and the naming was the refutation.

And notice the gentle, sweeping indefiniteness of **مَا يَشَآءُ** — *mā yashā'* — "whatever He willed." He does not even bother to name *which* creature. The object is left wide open, almost dismissive: *if it came to that, He could pick anything at all — it makes no difference which.* Because every option is the same: every option is created. When all your choices are equally beneath you, you do not need to specify. The very vagueness is contempt for the premise.

### Element Three: "Al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār" — The Two Names That Seal the Door

Now the ayah turns. After the hypothetical has collapsed, Allah does two things in quick succession. First: **سُبْحَـٰنَهُ** — *Subḥānahu* — "Glory be to Him." This word, from the root *s-b-ḥ*, carries the meaning of *transcendence through distance* — to declare something utterly far above and removed from a defect. *Subḥānahu* is the sharp exhalation of cleansing — *He is far, far above this.* It is the verbal act of lifting Allah clear of the whole contaminated idea.

And then, the seal. Two of the most weighted names in the Qur'an, placed side by side:

> **هُوَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْوَٰحِدُ ٱلْقَهَّارُ** — *Huwa-llāhu-l-Wāḥidu-l-Qahhār* — **"He is Allah — the One, the Irresistible."**

Let me teach you why *these two names*, and why *in this order*, and why *here*.

Think about what the idea of a child actually *implies*. Sit with the human reality of it. A child means, first, **another like you** — a being of your own kind, your peer in nature. And a child means, second, **succession** — continuity beyond yourself, someone who comes *after*, who carries on, who inherits, who picks up where your strength leaves off. Every human longing for a child is woven from these two threads: the longing for a *companion of your own kind*, and the longing for *continuation beyond your own ending.*

Now watch how these two names answer *exactly* those two threads, in order.

[PAUSE]

**ٱلْوَٰحِدُ** — *al-Wāḥid* — "the One." This name, from the root *w-ḥ-d*, does not merely mean "there is one of Him" as a number. It means He is *singular in His very nature* — that there is nothing of His kind, no peer, no second, no being that shares His category of existence. *Al-Wāḥid* severs the first thread: there can be no "another like Him," and therefore no child who is *of His kind*. A son is a peer-in-nature. *Al-Wāḥid* declares the category of "peer to God" to be empty. Forever empty.

And then — **ٱلْقَهَّارُ** — *al-Qahhār*. Feel this word in your mouth. The root is *q-h-r*: to subdue, to overpower, to dominate so completely that the other has no resistance left, no will of its own remaining. And notice the *form* of this name — it is not *qāhir*, "one who subdues." It is *qahhār* — the intensive pattern, *faʿʿāl*, the form of overwhelming, total, ceaseless magnitude. *The One who subdues absolutely, who overpowers all, over whom nothing has any power at all.*

Now — why this name to seal a refutation of *sonship*? Listen carefully. The second thread of childhood was **succession** — continuity, inheritance, someone who carries on after you. But succession only has meaning where there is *weakness*, where there is *ending*, where one strength gives out and another must take over. **An heir is what a being acquires because it will one day be unable.** Kings have heirs because kings die. Fathers raise sons because fathers grow old. The entire logic of offspring-as-continuity is built on the bedrock assumption of *eventual incapacity.*

[PAUSE — major weight here]

And *al-Qahhār* obliterates that assumption. The One who **eternally and totally overpowers everything** has no "after." There is no decline from which a successor must rescue Him. There is no strength that will give out and need to be carried on. There is no throne to be vacated, no kingdom to be inherited, because the *Qahhār* never relinquishes His grip on a single atom of existence for a single instant. **Succession is for those who will be subdued by time. The Qahhār subdues time itself.**

So feel the architecture of these two names. *Al-Wāḥid* closes the door of *peerhood* — there is none of His kind to be His child. *Al-Qahhār* closes the door of *succession* — there is no ending from which an heir would be needed. Between them, the two motives for which any being might "take a son" are both extinguished. The first name says *He has no equal.* The second says *He has no end.* And a child requires both an equal and an end.

[PAUSE]

Notice one more thing — the sonic texture. *Al-Wāḥid* is soft, open, singular — it almost rounds to a close, *wāḥid*, like a single held note. And then *al-Qahhār* lands hard — that doubled, guttural *qhh*, the *q* struck from the back of the throat, the *shadda* hammering the *h*, the *r* rolling to a stop. The mouth itself enacts the meaning: the gentle declaration of oneness, followed by the *crushing*, *overpowering* sound of the One who subdues all things. You hear the irresistibility before you finish parsing it.

---

### Bridge

Look at what the language has shown us. The word *law* delivered the verdict before the argument began — this belongs to the category of the impossible. The verbs *yattakhidha* and *iṣṭafā* exposed that the claim could not even be *stated* without confessing that any "son" would be external and created — the choicest creature is still a creature. And the names *al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār* sealed the two doors through which the very idea of a child could enter: the door of peerhood, and the door of succession.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about the human heart that wanted a son for God in the first place. Because a claim this universal, made across so many peoples, is never *only* a theological error. It is a window into something we *are*.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Heir We Build Because We Are Afraid to End

Let me start where you actually live.

Why do human beings want children? Not the easy answer — *love*, *family*. Go deeper than that, to the place we don't usually say out loud. Beneath the love, there is something else, and you know it if you have ever stood at a graveside or lain awake at three in the morning with the thought of your own ending. We want children, in part, because **we are going to die, and we cannot bear to simply stop.**

A child is a hand reaching past the edge of your own life. It is the part of you that continues when you cannot. We say it openly in the words we use — "carry on the family name," "continue the line," "leave a legacy." And it is not only children. The founder who cannot let go of the company. The artist desperate to be remembered. The empire-builder erecting monuments. The person who needs to be *referenced*, *cited*, *carried forward* by someone, anyone, after they are gone. All of it springs from the same root: **the unbearable smallness of a single, finite life, and the terror of being, in the end, alone and erased.**

We have children because we are not enough by ourselves. We need. We lack. We will weaken. We will end.

[PAUSE]

Now hold that up against this ayah, and feel what the human heart was *really* doing when it gave God a son.

It was projecting. It was taking the deepest structure of human insufficiency — *I am finite, I am lonely, I will end, I need to continue* — and painting it onto the face of God. To say "God has a son" is, at the level the heart doesn't admit, to say "**God, too, needs to continue. God, too, would be incomplete alone. God, too, requires an extension of Himself reaching past some edge.**" It is to make God in the image of our own fear.

And this is the quiet mercy of how the ayah refutes it. It does not merely say "you are wrong." Through *al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār*, it says: **the very thing you were trying to give God is a description of your own poverty.** You wanted to honor Him with a son. But a son is what the *weak* require. To give Him a child is not to exalt Him — it is to drag Him down into the condition of every dying thing that ever clutched at continuity because it could not bear to end.

[PAUSE]

*Subḥānahu* — Glory be to Him. *He is far above this.* Far above the need to continue, because He never ends. Far above the need for a peer, because His oneness is not loneliness — and that is where we are going next.

But before we go, let this turn back on you. Because the lesson is not only about an ancient theological claim. The lesson is about the *engine* underneath it — the engine of projecting our lack onto the world, of needing to be carried forward, of being unable to rest in our own finitude. Here is the application, and it is sharp: **every time you grasp for legacy — to be remembered, to be continued, to leave something that outlasts you — ask honestly whether you are reaching for something good, or whether you are simply afraid to end.** The believer is freed from that fear, because the believer does not need to author his own continuation. *Al-Qahhār* holds your existence beyond death more securely than any legacy you could build. You do not need to become someone's "son" to escape erasure. You need only belong to the One who never ends.

Tomorrow, when you feel that familiar pull to be seen, to be remembered, to leave your mark — pause and ask: *am I building, or am I fleeing?* And then return your continuity to the One whose grip on you does not loosen at the grave.

---

### Theme Two: The Loneliness We Fear, and the Oneness We Misunderstand

Here is a knot in the human heart that this ayah quietly unties.

We hear the word "alone" and we flinch. To us, *oneness* and *loneliness* are almost the same word. To be one is to be unaccompanied, unpartnered, incomplete — *the one left out, the only one, the last one.* Our deepest social fear is to be alone, and so when we imagine ultimate aloneness — a single being with no other of its kind anywhere in existence — we imagine something almost unbearable. We project: *surely even God would want company. Surely even God would not want to be utterly alone. Surely He would want a son.*

And right here is the error so deep we don't even see it. **We assume that oneness is a lack.** We assume the One must be lonely, because *we* would be.

[PAUSE]

But listen to the name again: *al-Wāḥid.* And place beside it the way the Qur'an describes Allah's oneness elsewhere — *al-Aḥad, al-Ṣamad* — the One, the Self-Sufficient upon whom all depend while He depends on none. The oneness of Allah is not the oneness of *the last person in an empty room.* It is the oneness of *fullness* — of a reality so complete that the addition of anything else would not increase it, and from which nothing is missing that another could supply.

Think of it this way. Our loneliness is the ache of a *cup half-empty* — it longs to be filled by another. But what would you call a vessel so full that it overflows infinitely and forever, that contains within itself everything, that could not hold more? You would not call it *lonely*. Loneliness is a hunger. **Al-Wāḥid is not hungry.** His oneness is not the oneness of *something missing*; it is the oneness of *nothing missing.* He is One not because He has no companion to complete Him, but because **there is nothing left to complete.**

[PAUSE — let it breathe]

This is why the desire to give Him a son, however reverent it felt, was secretly an *insult* dressed as praise. It assumed a hunger in the Divine. It assumed an empty place beside Him that a child could fill. And *al-Wāḥid* answers: **there is no empty place.** Not because He sternly refuses company, but because His completeness has no vacancy. The very space a "son" would occupy does not exist — there is no half-empty seat at the side of the Self-Sufficient.

And now feel how this returns to you, because it must.

You were made to *need*. You are the half-empty cup. You ache for companionship, for completion, for another to fill the place inside you — and that ache is not a flaw, it is the *signature of a creature*, the built-in evidence that you are not *al-Wāḥid*, that you were made to lean on another. The disease is not the need. The disease is *where you take it.* When you take your need for completion to other broken cups — to people, to status, to the legacies of Theme One — you are asking the empty to fill the empty.

[PAUSE]

The believer's freedom is this: to know that your hunger has exactly one adequate object, and it is the One who has no hunger of His own. You are *al-faqīr* — the utterly poor, the needy. He is *al-Ṣamad* — the One needed by all, needing none. Your incompleteness was designed to drive you to His completeness. **You will never be *al-Wāḥid.* You were never meant to be. You were meant to belong to Him.**

The practical application is quiet but it will reorder your week. The next time you feel that hollow ache — the loneliness, the sense of something missing, the restlessness that sends you reaching for one more thing to fill you — recognize it for what it is: not a problem to be solved by acquiring another created cup, but **a homing signal** pointing to the only One who is full. Turn it into a single sentence of need spoken to *al-Wāḥid al-Qahhār.* Let your emptiness become *prayer* instead of *grasping.* That is the difference between a heart that is forever hungry and a heart that has found where its hunger belongs.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now stand back and see the whole shape of this ayah, because it is built with terrible precision.

It moves in three strokes. First, *law arāda* — the hypothetical is *granted*, the building is *entered*. Second, *la-ṣṭafā mimmā yakhluqu mā yashā'* — inside the building, the load-bearing wall is shown to be missing: *whatever you select is selected from creation, and the chosen creature is still a creature.* The premise collapses on its own logic. And third, the camera pulls back to the One Himself — *Subḥānahu, huwa-llāhu-l-Wāḥidu-l-Qahhār* — and seals the two doors through which the idea could ever return: no peer (*al-Wāḥid*), no end (*al-Qahhār*).

It is a refutation that never raises its voice. It grants the premise, walks it to its conclusion, and lets it fall — and then names the One whose reality made it impossible from the start.

### Bringing It Together

Now see how the two halves of our journey are the same insight from two directions.

The **linguistic** discovery was this: *you cannot select your way across the line between Creator and created.* The choicest, the *ṣafwa*, the cream of all existence — promoted infinitely — remains on the created side. The gap is not a distance you can close by elevation. It is a difference of *kind.*

The **thematic** discovery was this: *the desire to give God a son was the human heart projecting its own poverty onto the Divine* — its fear of ending (Theme One), its fear of being alone (Theme Two). We wanted to give Him our continuity and our companionship, because *we* cannot live without them.

And these are one truth. The reason the choicest creature can never be His son is the very same reason God needs no son at all: **He is on a different side of the line entirely** — the side of *al-Wāḥid* who lacks no companion, *al-Qahhār* who lacks no future. The creature needs, ends, and is alone. The Creator needs nothing, never ends, and is complete. To attribute a child to Him is to forget which side of the line He is on. To understand *why even the cream of creation could never be His son* is to finally see the line itself.

### Questions to Carry

1. When I reach for legacy — to be remembered, to leave a mark, to be carried forward — am I building something good, or am I quietly fleeing the fact that I will end?
2. Where in my life have I taken my hunger for completion to broken cups — to people, status, achievement — asking the empty to fill the empty?
3. Have I, in subtle ways, made God in the image of my own needs — imagining that He wants what *I* would want, lacks what *I* lack?
4. When I feel the hollow ache of loneliness, do I read it as a problem to solve by acquiring, or as a homing signal pointing me back to *al-Wāḥid*?
5. Do I truly believe that my existence is held by *al-Qahhār* beyond the grave — or am I still trying to author my own continuation?

### One-Sentence Distillation

Even if you elevate the choicest of all creation to its highest possible station, it remains a creature on the created side of an uncrossable line — because Allah is *al-Wāḥid*, who has no peer to be His child, and *al-Qahhār*, who has no end to require an heir.

### The Closing Invitation

Something can shift after this — not as an obligation laid upon you, but as a door held open. The shift is this: to stop projecting your poverty onto your Lord, and to let His completeness expose, and then heal, your own. You are the one who ends; He is *al-Qahhār.* You are the one who is alone; He is *al-Wāḥid.* You are the half-empty cup; He is the One from whom nothing is missing. Stop trying to be self-sufficient. You were never meant to be *al-Wāḥid.* You were meant to belong to Him. Let your need become the rope that ties you to the only One who has none.

### A Du'a

*Yā Wāḥid, yā Qahhār* — You who have no peer and no end — we confess that the emptiness in us is real, and that we have so often carried it to those who are as empty as we are. Lift us above the fear of ending, for our continuance is in Your grip, not in any legacy of our own. Let our hunger drive us only to You, the One from whom nothing is missing. *Subḥānaka* — glory be to You — far above all that we, in our smallness, would ever imagine of You.
