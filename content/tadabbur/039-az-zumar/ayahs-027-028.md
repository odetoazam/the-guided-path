# The Struck Parable: Why Truth Has to Be *Stamped* Into Us

## Introduction: A Verse About the Book, From Inside the Book

Listen to these two lines. Sit with them in Arabic first, even if you don't know the language. Let the sound move past you before the meaning catches up.

> **وَلَقَدْ ضَرَبْنَا لِلنَّاسِ فِى هَـٰذَا ٱلْقُرْءَانِ مِن كُلِّ مَثَلٍ لَّعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ**
>
> **قُرْءَانًا عَرَبِيًّا غَيْرَ ذِى عِوَجٍ لَّعَلَّهُمْ يَتَّقُونَ**

*Wa-laqad ḍarabnā lin-nāsi fī hādhā-l-qur'āni min kulli mathalin laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn. Qur'ānan ʿarabiyyan ghayra dhī ʿiwajin laʿallahum yattaqūn.*

"And indeed We have struck for the people, in this Qur'an, every kind of parable — that they may take heed. An Arabic Qur'an, with no crookedness in it — that they may guard themselves."

Now, here is something strange before we even begin. This is the Qur'an **talking about the Qur'an.** It is the Book describing its own design, telling you *why it was built the way it was built.* It is as if, halfway through reading a letter, the letter paused to tell you exactly what its author was trying to do to your heart, and why he chose these words and not others.

### Rebuild the world

To feel this, you have to be in Mecca. Not the Mecca of pilgrims and millions — the early Mecca, where a small, mocked community is holding onto something that the powerful of the city consider an insult to their fathers. The Prophet ﷺ is reciting, and the response he keeps meeting is not "we don't understand." It is something colder. It is: *we understand, and we are not moved.* They heard the parables. They heard the stories of ʿAd and Thamud, the peoples swept away. They heard the descriptions of the Garden and the Fire. And they shrugged.

So a question hangs in the Meccan air, a question that is really about every human heart in every century: **if the truth is this clear, why doesn't it land?** Why can a person hear the most important words ever spoken and walk away exactly as they came?

And into that exact wound, Allah speaks these two verses. He does not say "We *told* the people." He does not say "We *mentioned* parables." He uses a much stranger, much more physical word. He says **We *struck* them.**

### The Hook

There is a verb here that any Arab of that time would have felt in their body, because it is the verb you use for a blacksmith's hammer, for a minted coin, for pitching a tent, for setting out on a hard journey across the desert. Allah did not *offer* the parable. He **struck** it. [PAUSE]

Why would the Most Gentle, the Most Wise, describe His teaching with a word of *impact*? What does it mean that truth has to be *hammered* into a thing before it holds its shape? Stay with that. Because by the end, I think you'll find that this single verb knows something about you that you may not have admitted to yourself.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: ضَرَبْنَا — "We Struck"

### Teaching the concept first

Before we open the word, let's talk about an ordinary thing: how does a shape become *permanent* in something hard?

Think of a coin. A blank disc of soft metal means nothing — it has no value, no face, no name. To make it a coin, you place it between two dies, and you bring down enormous force. You **strike** it. And in that one violent instant, an image that was only on the die is now *in the metal* — the king's face, the weight, the year — pressed so deep it will outlast empires. You didn't paint the image on. You didn't ask the metal to remember it. You struck it in.

Or think of flint. Stone and steel, lying next to each other, are dark and cold forever. There is no fire *in* them in any way you can see. But **strike** them together, and the hidden thing leaps out — a spark, then a flame.

Hold both of those: striking as *stamping a permanent form into something resistant,* and striking as *forcing out a hidden fire that was there all along.*

### Opening the word

Now the verse. Allah says **ضَرَبْنَا** — *ḍarabnā* — "We struck." The root is **ض-ر-ب** (*ḍ-r-b*), and in the Arabic of the Qur'an it is one of the widest, most physical verbs in the language. Its first meaning is simply *to hit, to strike a blow.* But from that core, the Arabs built an entire world: *ḍaraba fī-l-arḍ,* to "strike out into the land" — to travel hard across distance. *Ḍaraba-l-khaymah,* to "strike" a tent — to drive its pegs into the ground so it stands. *Ḍaraba-d-dirham,* to "strike" a coin — to mint it.

And the expression sitting right here in our verse: **ḍaraba mathalan** — to "strike" a parable. To coin an example.

Notice that Arabic could have said something softer. It could have said *jaʿalnā* — "We made" — and in fact al-Jalālayn glosses the verb with exactly that gentler word, *jaʿalnā,* "We placed." That gloss is helping us understand the *result.* But Allah did not choose the soft word for the act. He chose **the verb of the hammer.**

### Sitting with it

Let's sit with this. Why *strike* a parable, rather than offer one, share one, present one?

Here is what every one of those physical meanings has in common — travel, tent-pitching, coin-minting, the blow itself: **force that establishes something so that it holds.** You strike out into the desert because gentle wishing won't cross it. You strike the tent pegs because a tent that isn't driven in collapses in the first wind. You strike the coin because an image that isn't pressed *into* the metal washes off.

So when Allah says He **struck** the parable for the people, He is telling you something about the nature of truth and the nature of the human heart at the same time. **Truth, with us, does not stay on the surface on its own.** It has to be driven in. It has to be pressed deep enough to hold its shape against the wind of forgetting.

Notice something. The blacksmith does not strike soft butter. You strike what *resists.* The very choice of this verb is a quiet admission about who is being addressed — about a heart that has gone hard enough that gentle placing slides right off it, and only the struck image stays. [PAUSE]

### Deeper

And now bring back the flint. The other face of *striking* is not stamping something *in* from outside — it is forcing something *out* that was hidden within. Strike the stone, and the fire that was always latent leaps into view.

The parables of the Qur'an do both at once. **They press a form into you** — the coin-image of a truth you'll now carry. **And they strike a spark out of you** — they reach for the recognition that was already lying in the human soul, the *fiṭra,* the buried sense of "yes, I always knew this." A good parable doesn't only teach you something new. It strikes against something old in you and makes it flare. When the Qur'an says *the life of this world is like water We send down, and the plants flourish, and then it all becomes dry stubble the wind scatters* — you don't learn a fact. Something in you that already feared exactly this *catches fire.*

That is what *ḍarabnā* is doing. Allah is the One who, in this Book, struck the flint of every human heart, *min kulli mathal* — with every kind of example — hoping the spark would finally catch.

**What this single verb reveals:** Allah did not gently lay the truth beside us and hope we'd notice. He struck it — to stamp it deep enough to last, and to spark the fire that was already buried in us. [PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Two: مِن كُلِّ مَثَلٍ — "Of Every Kind of Parable"

### Teaching the concept first

Imagine you are trying to reach four different people with the same truth — say, the truth that *what you chase in this world will not last.*

One of them is a poet; reach her with an image — a flower that blooms and dies. One is a merchant; reach him with an account — a profit that turns out to be a loss. One is a frightened person; reach him with a story — a city that thought it was safe and was gone by morning. One is proud; reach him with a comparison — a slave with one master and a slave with many quarreling ones, and which of the two has peace.

Same truth. Four completely different doors. Because **hearts are not unlocked by the same key.**

### Opening the phrase

Allah says **مِن كُلِّ مَثَلٍ** — *min kulli mathal* — "of every kind of parable." The word **مَثَل** (*mathal*), from the root **م-ث-ل**, means a likeness, a similitude, a comparison, an example held up as a model. *Kull* means "every," "all." And the little particle **مِن** (*min*) here is doing quiet but important work — it means "of" or "from," a sampling *from* every kind. **From every category of example, We have struck some for them.**

### Sitting with it

Stay with the scope of that claim. Not "many parables." Not "the most important parables." **Of *every* kind.** Al-Qurṭubī reads it beautifully: *min kulli mathalin yaḥtājūna ilayh* — "of every example **they have need of.**" Not every example that exists in the abstract — every example *a human being actually needs* to be reached.

Think about what that implies about the One speaking. To strike for people an example of *every kind they need,* you would have to know **every kind of person there is, and every kind of resistance in them.** You would have to know the door that opens the proud man and the different door that opens the frightened one and the third door for the grieving one. *Min kulli mathal* is not a statement about the richness of a book. It is, underneath, a statement about the **knowledge of the Author** — that not a single human temperament was left without a door cut for it.

### Deeper — and the composite of the scholars

Now, the early commentators give us what looks like different readings, but I want you to see them as camera angles on one reality. Al-Muyassar says these parables are *min amthāl al-qurūn al-khāliyah* — drawn from the examples of the **bygone generations**, struck *takhwīfan wa-taḥdhīran,* "as a frightening and a warning," so the people would be jolted and pull back from the disbelief they were stubbornly *sitting in.* Al-Baghawī and al-Jalālayn keep it simple: *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* means *yattaʿiẓūn* — that they would take the lesson, be admonished. And al-Qurṭubī adds the wide reading: *every example they need,* like the other verse, *"We have neglected nothing in the Book."*

Put the angles together and you get the full sphere: Allah struck for humanity examples of every needed kind — including the hardest kind, the buried cities and vanished peoples — not to decorate the Book, but to **frighten a comfortable heart awake** and to leave no temperament without its key.

**What this phrase reveals:** The variety of the Qur'an's parables is not literary abundance — it is *mercy shaped as precision,* the Author cutting a different door for every kind of person, because He knows there is no single human He is willing to lose for lack of a door. [PAUSE]

## Element Three: غَيْرَ ذِى عِوَجٍ — "With No Crookedness in It"

### Teaching the concept first — and reading an absence

Here is a small thing that, once you see it, you cannot un-see. Allah does not describe the Qur'an in verse 28 by saying it is **straight.** He describes it by saying it has **no crookedness.** He defines it by what is *absent.*

Why does that matter? Because in language, *naming the negative* often carries a force that the positive cannot. If I tell you a man is "honest," that's a quality. But if I tell you "there is not one lie in him, not one bend, not one hidden angle" — I have done something different. I have walked the whole length of him looking for the crack, and reported back that **there is none.** The negative description implies a *search* that came up empty.

### Opening the phrase

The verse describes the Book as **قُرْءَانًا عَرَبِيًّا غَيْرَ ذِى عِوَجٍ** — *qur'ānan ʿarabiyyan ghayra dhī ʿiwaj* — "an Arabic Qur'an, possessing no crookedness." The key word is **عِوَج** (*ʿiwaj*), from the root **ع-و-ج**, which means a *bend, a curve, a crookedness, a deviation* from straightness. It is the warp in a plank of wood, the kink in a path, the swerve in a road that should have run straight.

And *ghayra dhī ʿiwaj* — "not possessing any crookedness." Al-Muyassar unfolds it gorgeously: *wāḍiḥ al-alfāẓ, sahl al-maʿānī, lā labsa fīhi wa-lā inḥirāf* — "clear in its wording, easy in its meanings, no confusion in it and no swerving away."

### Sitting with it

Let's sit with what *ʿiwaj* — crookedness — actually does to a person. A crooked road still gets you somewhere, but it wastes your life in the getting there. Crooked speech — the speech of the manipulator — also "gets somewhere," but it bends you toward a destination *that was never yours,* by an angle so gentle you didn't feel the turn. The danger of crookedness is precisely that it is *subtle.* A wall that is obviously falling, you brace against. A wall that leans one degree off true, you trust — and it crushes you in your sleep.

So when Allah strikes parable after parable into the human heart — coining them, sparking them, cutting a door for every temperament — a fear could arise: *but is the One striking me bending me somewhere I don't want to go? Every powerful message I've ever encountered had an angle, a catch, fine print, someone's interest hidden in the curve of it.* And before that fear can even form, verse 28 closes it: **ghayra dhī ʿiwaj.** This thing that is being struck into you — walk its whole length, search it for the bend, the hidden angle, the place where it serves the speaker against you. **There is none.**

### Deeper — the sister verse

This is not the only place Allah says it. At the very opening of Surah al-Kahf, He says: *al-ḥamdu lillāhi-lladhī anzala ʿalā ʿabdihi-l-kitāba **wa-lam yajʿal lahu ʿiwajā*** — "Praise to Allah who sent down the Book to His servant **and placed no crookedness in it.**" Same root. Same declaration. And there too it comes right at the threshold, as if the straightness of the Book is the first thing you must be assured of before you'll trust it enough to let it strike you.

Now notice the deep harmony with what we already found. A coin is only trustworthy if it is *struck true* — a warped, off-center strike makes a counterfeit. A tent only stands if its pegs are driven *straight.* The verb of verse 27, *ḍarabnā,* "We struck," already carried the demand for true alignment — and verse 28 delivers it: what was struck was struck **without a single degree of bend.**

**What this phrase reveals:** The Qur'an defines its own integrity by absence — by the bend that is *not there.* It invites you to search it for the hidden angle every other powerful voice in your life has carried, and promises that the search will fail. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So the words have shown us three things. A verb of impact — *ḍarabnā* — that truth must be struck, not merely set down, because the human heart resists soft placement. A scope of mercy — *min kulli mathal* — that the striking was aimed with full knowledge of every kind of person there is. And a guarantee of integrity — *ghayra dhī ʿiwaj* — that the thing being struck into you carries no bend, no hidden angle, no betrayal in its curve.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about you.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: The Difference Between Knowing and Remembering

### Begin where you already live

Let me start somewhere very ordinary. You already know everything you need to know to live well.

You know that scrolling at 1 a.m. is hollowing you out. You know that the relationship you've been avoiding repairing is the one that matters most. You know that the thing you're chasing won't feel the way you imagine it will once you have it. You know you will die, and that you have not prepared the way you would if you truly believed it. **None of this is news to you.** You are not suffering from a lack of information.

So why doesn't knowing change anything?

### Let the verse illuminate it

Here is where the verse reaches in. Look at the *purpose* Allah attaches to the striking of the parables: **لَّعَلَّهُمْ يَتَذَكَّرُونَ** — *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* — "that they may **take heed.**" But the word He uses is not "that they may *learn,*" not "that they may *know.*" The root is **ذ-ك-ر** (*dh-k-r*) — to **remember.** And the form of the verb is doing something specific. *Yatadhakkarūn* is **Form V** in Arabic — *tafaʿʿul* — and Form V carries a reflexive, effortful, *self-directed* force. It does not merely mean "they remember." It means something closer to: **they make themselves remember. They bring it back to themselves. They drag the buried thing up into the light of attention again.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the precision of that? Allah did not say the parables were struck so the people would *find out* something they never knew. He said they were struck so the people would **re-member** — re-call, re-collect — something they *already* had and had let sink. The whole human problem, in this one verb, is diagnosed not as **ignorance** but as **forgetting.** This is *ghafla* — heedlessness — the slow silting-over of a truth you already hold.

### Building through it

And now look back at why the verb in verse 27 had to be *strike.* If our problem were ignorance, Allah could simply *inform* us — lay the fact down once, gently, and the gap would be filled. But our problem is not an empty container. Our problem is a **buried** one. The truth is already in us, sinking under the silt of distraction, sliding off the hardened surface of a heedless heart. And what do you do to bring a buried thing back up, to stamp a sliding thing deep enough to hold?

**You strike it.**

The verb and the purpose are one mechanism. *Ḍarabnā* — We struck — *laʿallahum yatadhakkarūn* — so that they would haul the buried truth back into the light of their own attention. The blow is precisely calibrated to the disease. You don't hammer to *add.* You hammer to *re-fix* what keeps slipping loose.

### Let the paradox sit

Here is the part to sit with and not rush. It means the work of the Qur'an in your life is not mainly to **tell you new things.** Most of what will save you, you have heard. The work is to **strike the old truths back into place** before they slip again — because they *will* slip. *Tadhakkur* is in the imperfect tense, *yatadhakkarūn,* the tense of ongoing, never-finished action. You do not remember once. You are a creature who forgets continuously and must therefore be reminded continuously. This is why you can read a verse you have read a hundred times and weep at it on the hundred-and-first — not because you finally learned it, but because on that day the strike landed and the buried thing came up.

### What this changes tomorrow

So here is the practical turn. Stop measuring your relationship with the Qur'an by how much *new* you extract from it. That is the wrong scale, and it will make you abandon the most familiar verses as "done." Start measuring it by how deeply the *known* gets struck back into you. When you sit with these two verses tomorrow, don't ask, *"what's the new insight?"* Ask: *"what do I already know that has gone quiet in me — and will I let this strike it loud again?"* Choose **one** truth you have let sink — about your prayer, your tongue, your time — and let the Book hammer it back into place today. That is *tadhakkur.* That is the entire point of the strike.

## Theme Two: From Remembering to Guarding — Why the Verse Doesn't Stop at the Heart

### Begin where you already live

Have you ever had a moment of total clarity — a funeral, a near-miss on the road, a verse that pierced you — where you *saw* everything correctly, and you swore *this time it's different* — and then within a week the clarity had drained out and you were exactly who you'd been before?

That is the most painful pattern in the spiritual life. Not that we never wake up. That we wake up **and then drift back to sleep.** The remembrance came. It just didn't *hold.*

### Let the verse illuminate it

Now watch what these two verses do that a lesser text would never think to do. There are **two** purpose-clauses, one at the end of each verse, and they are not the same.

Verse 27 ends: *laʿallahum* **yatadhakkarūn** — "that they may take heed / remember."
Verse 28 ends: *laʿallahum* **yattaqūn** — "that they may **guard themselves** / become God-conscious."

The first is *tadhakkur* — remembrance, the awakening *in the heart.* The second is **taqwā** — from the root **و-ق-ي**, whose core meaning is *to shield, to guard, to protect oneself from harm.* *Taqwā* is not a feeling. It is the **action of putting a guard between yourself and what would destroy you.** It is what a person *does* with the clarity once it comes.

### Building through it — the ascent

Feel the architecture of those two endings. Allah did not stop at *yatadhakkarūn.* He did not say, in effect, "I struck these parables so that your heart would have its moment of waking, and that is enough." He built a **second** clause, a second rung, and it climbs from the inner event to the outer guard. From **remembering** to **protecting.** From the spark in the chest to the wall built around the life.

This is the answer to the painful pattern. Remembrance that does not become *taqwā* drains away — because a heart with no guard around it is a struck coin left out in the rain; the image fades. *Taqwā* is what makes the remembrance *hold.* You felt the truth (tadhakkur); now you build the fence (taqwā) — you change the habit, you drop the company, you put the distance between yourself and the thing — and *that* is what keeps you from drifting back to sleep.

### Notice what the two verses gave to each rung

And here is the quiet genius of it. Look at what Allah attached *taqwā* to. The first purpose, remembrance, is attached to verse 27 — *the parables,* every kind, struck to wake the heart. The second purpose, guarding, is attached to verse 28 — *"an Arabic Qur'an, with no crookedness in it."* Why pair *taqwā* with **clarity and straightness** specifically?

Because you cannot **guard** by a crooked guide. To build a wall between yourself and harm, you need a line that is *true* — *ʿarabiyyan,* clear, and *ghayra dhī ʿiwaj,* with no bend. A crooked ruler builds a crooked wall, and a crooked wall is exactly the kind that crushes you in your sleep. The straightness of verse 28 is not a separate compliment to the Book — it is the *precondition* of the *taqwā* in verse 28. **You can only protect your life by a line you can trust to be straight.** The remembrance wakes you; the *straight, clear* word is what you then build your guard along.

### What this changes tomorrow

So the practical turn of this theme picks up exactly where the last one ended. Yesterday you let one buried truth be struck back into place — that was *tadhakkur.* The question of *taqwā* is: **what guard will you now build so it doesn't drain away by Friday?** Not a feeling — a *fence.* If the struck truth was about your tongue, the guard is one conversation you will physically leave. If it was about your time, the guard is one app deleted, one alarm moved. *Taqwā* is remembrance *with a wall built around it.* The verse refuses to let you stop at the warm feeling. It climbs, and it asks you to climb with it.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture Revealed

Now that we've done the work, stand back and see the shape of these two verses, because they are built as a single rising structure — and the structure *is* the argument.

```
Verse 27:  ḌARABNĀ  →  min kulli mathal      →  laʿallahum YATADHAKKARŪN
           (We STRUCK)   (every kind of door)     (that they REMEMBER)
                                                    — the inner awakening

Verse 28:  Qur'ānan ʿarabiyyan ghayra dhī ʿiwaj →  laʿallahum YATTAQŪN
           (clear, with NO crookedness)            (that they GUARD)
                                                    — the outer protection
```

Two verses. Two halves. Each half ends in a purpose, and the purposes **ascend** — from *yatadhakkarūn* (remember) to *yattaqūn* (guard). The first half gives the **method** (a striking, of every kind) and the **inner aim** (remembrance). The second half gives the **medium** (clear, straight, no bend) and the **outer aim** (protection). And the whole thing rests on one foundation: a Book whose every parable was *struck* true, *ghayra dhī ʿiwaj,* so that what you build along its line will stand.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same insight approached from two sides.

From the side of the **words:** Allah *struck* the parable (because the heart resists soft placement) in *every* needed form (because He knows every kind of person) with *no crookedness* (because only a straight line can be trusted).

From the side of the **themes:** that striking is aimed first at *tadhakkur* (hauling buried truth back up — for we suffer not from ignorance but from forgetting) and then at *taqwā* (building the guard that makes the remembrance hold — for we wake and then drift unless we wall it in).

It is one mechanism. **A blow precise enough to re-fix what slips, true enough to build your life along, struck so you would first remember, then guard.**

## Questions to Carry

1. What truth do I already fully *know* that has gone quiet in me — and have I been mistaking my problem for ignorance when it is really *forgetting*?
2. When the Qur'an strikes — when a verse pierces me — do I let the image set and hold, or do I let it wash off by the next morning's noise?
3. Of all the "examples of every kind" struck in this Book, which door was cut for *my* particular temperament — and have I walked through it, or have I been waiting for a different one?
4. Every powerful voice in my life has carried some bend, some hidden angle. Do I actually *believe* this one carries none — and do I live as though I believe it?
5. Where, exactly, has my remembrance failed to become a guard? What is the one wall I keep meaning to build and never do?

## One-Sentence Distillation

Allah did not merely *offer* us the truth — He **struck** it into every shape the human heart could receive, in speech with no hidden bend, so that we would first **remember** what we had let sink, and then **guard** the life we'd been letting slip.

## The Closing Invitation

So let something shift. Not a grand resolution — those are the ones that drift back to sleep. Just this: the next time a verse lands on you, treat it the way you would treat a struck coin in your palm — turn it, feel its weight, let the image press in. Don't ask only *what does this mean.* Ask *what is this striking back into place in me,* and then *what guard will I build today so it holds till tomorrow.* Remembrance, then protection. The verse climbs those two rungs. You are invited to climb them too.

## A Brief Du'a

O Allah, You who *struck* for us, in this Qur'an, every kind of example — strike them into us where we have gone hard, and spark from us the fire You buried in our fiṭra. You who left in Your Book no crookedness, do not let us live by any crooked line; make this clear, straight word the wall we build our lives along. Let our remembrance become a guard, and our guard become *taqwā,* until the day we meet You. Āmīn.
