# The Maker Who Never Walks Away

## Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 62

> ٱللَّهُ خَٰلِقُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍۖ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ وَكِيلٌ

**Transliteration:**
*Allāhu khāliqu kulli shayʾ, wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin wakīl.*

**Translation:**
*Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is, over all things, the Guardian and Disposer of affairs.*

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me ask you to picture something before we touch a single word of this ayah.

Picture a craftsman. A potter, let's say. He sits at the wheel, his hands wet with clay, and over hours he shapes a vessel. He pours himself into it — his attention, his judgment, the memory in his fingers. And then it is finished. He fires it, glazes it, and sets it on a shelf. And then he turns away to the next piece of clay.

The vessel is his. He made it. But he is no longer *holding* it. It sits on the shelf, and whether it cracks in the cold, whether it's knocked off by a careless elbow, whether it's filled with water or left to gather dust — that is no longer his concern. He made it and he moved on. **This is what almost every maker does.** To make is one act. To keep is another. And in our entire experience of the world, the maker who makes is rarely the keeper who keeps.

Now hold that image, because the people first hearing this surah lived in a world drowning in a particular version of that gap.

The pagans of Makkah were not, for the most part, atheists. This is one of the most important things to understand about them. If you had stopped a man on the road and asked him, "Who created the heavens and the earth?" — the Qur'an itself tells us his answer. *La-yaqūlunna Allāh.* "They will surely say: Allah." They believed in a Creator. They were not confused about who made the world.

What they did was something subtler and, in a way, more tragic. They believed Allah created the world — and then they handed the *running* of it to others. Idols for rain. Idols for the harvest. Idols for protection on the journey, for the health of the newborn, for victory in battle. They split the universe in two: a distant Maker who started everything, and a crowd of nearer powers who actually *managed* the day-to-day. The Creator on the shelf. The little gods doing the keeping.

And so Surah Az-Zumar — a surah whose entire pulse is *ikhlāṣ*, the demand that worship be made pure, undivided, addressed to One — comes to close that gap. Not by arguing harder that Allah created everything. They already conceded that. But by showing them that **the One who made it never set it down.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Nine words. In Arabic, just nine. And in those nine words, Allah says two things about Himself — that He creates *all things*, and that He is something *over all things*. Two phrases, perfectly mirrored. And the entire weight of this ayah, the thing that turns it from a statement into a lifeline you can actually hold onto at 3 a.m. when you cannot sleep — that weight rests on one word at the very end. A word that is not "King," not "Powerful," not "Knowing." A word that, of all the names Allah could have placed here, He chose with a precision that should stop us in our tracks.

Let's earn our way to it.

# PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

## Element One: *Khāliq* — the Maker who is still making

Before we look at the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic talks about action — because it will change what you see.

In English, if I want to tell you someone does something, I mostly have two options. I can use a verb: "He creates," "He created." Or I can turn it into a label, a description of *who someone is*: "He is a creator." Notice the difference. "He created the table" tells you about an event — something that happened and is now over. But "He is a carpenter" tells you about an *identity* — something true of him whether or not he happens to be hammering right now.

Arabic has a beautiful tool for this second kind of statement. It's called the *ism al-fāʿil* — the active participle. It takes a root that describes an action and turns it into a standing description of the one who does it. Not "he did" — but "he is a doer-of." It carries a sense of something that clings to a person, that describes their nature rather than a single moment.

Now look at the word Allah uses.

> ٱللَّهُ خَٰلِقُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ

He does not say *Allāhu khalaqa kulla shayʾ* — "Allah created all things," in the past tense, as a finished event. He says ***Allāhu khāliqu*** — using the active participle. **"Allah is the Creator of all things."**

Let's sit with this, because it's easy to slide past.

The pagans were perfectly comfortable with the past tense. *He created.* That was the god they believed in — the one whose creating was *done*, finished, sealed in the past, a god you could thank for the beginning and then leave on the shelf. But Allah does not describe Himself with a closed event. He describes Himself with a *standing identity*. Creator is not what He did once. Creator is **who He is** — a description that does not expire, that is as true right now, in this breath you are taking, as it was on the first morning of the world.

Notice something. The classical commentary of al-Muyassar unpacks this not as a one-time act but as an ongoing relationship: *He is the Creator of all things, their Lord, their Owner, the One who disposes of them.* The making is not severed from the maintaining. They are folded into one continuous reality.

Here's what's remarkable. The Arabic grammatical form itself refuses the deist's god. The potter who walks away cannot be described with this word, because the word means *the one to whom creating belongs as a permanent attribute* — and you cannot permanently be a creator of something you have abandoned. The form quietly insists: the relationship is live. The hand that made you has not let go.

[PAUSE]

So when we hear *khāliq*, we are not being told about a distant first cause who lit the fuse of the universe and stepped back to watch it burn. We are being told about a Maker whose making is **present tense in its very nature** — a Maker who is, in this moment, the One from whom your existence continues to flow.

That is the first stone. Now the second.

## Element Two: *Kull* and *shayʾ* — the totality that leaves nothing out

Look at how the ayah is built. There is a phrase that appears twice, and its repetition is not an accident:

> خَٰلِقُ **كُلِّ شَىْءٍ** ... عَلَىٰ **كُلِّ شَىْءٍ**

*kulli shayʾ* — *kulli shayʾ.* "Of all things" — "over all things."

Let me teach the two words.

*Kull* is the word for totality, for "all" or "every." It is the great gathering word of Arabic — it sweeps an entire category into its arms and lets nothing fall out the bottom.

And *shayʾ* — this is one of the most extraordinary words in the language to place here. *Shayʾ* is the most generic noun Arabic possesses. It means, simply, "thing." It is the widest net you can cast. Anything that can be pointed to, named, imagined, or merely exists in the faintest sense — it is a *shayʾ*. There is no category narrower than it because there is no category wider. When you say *shayʾ*, you have said *whatever can be said to be at all.*

Now watch what happens when *kull* takes *shayʾ* by the hand. *Kullu shayʾ* — "all things," "every single thing," with nothing whatsoever standing outside the circle. The galaxy and the gnat. The mountain and the molecule. The visible and the invisible. Your triumphs and your failures. The cells dividing in your body as you listen and the thoughts moving behind your eyes. The thing you are most proud of and the thing you are most afraid of. **All of it. Every single thing. No exceptions, no remainder, no item too large to have been made and none too small to have been forgotten.**

Stay with me, because here is the structural genius.

The phrase appears *twice*, and it appears in two different roles. First: Allah is the Creator of *kulli shayʾ* — all things. Second: He is, over *kulli shayʾ*, the Wakīl. The same totality, named twice. And the two totalities are **identical in scope.**

Why does that matter? Because it slams shut the very gap the pagans were living in.

Their entire theology depended on the second circle being *smaller* than the first. Yes, they would say, Allah created *all things* — the full circle. But the *managing* of things, the running, the keeping — that circle was carved up among the idols. The making was total; the keeping was distributed. The Creator made everything but governs only some of it; the rest was farmed out.

And this ayah, with surgical precision, makes the two circles the **exact same size.** *Kulli shayʾ* He created — and over *kulli shayʾ*, the identical and complete totality, He alone is the Keeper. There is no thing He made that He does not also maintain. There is no corner of creation that fell out of His hand after being shaped by it. The circle of His making and the circle of His keeping are laid one perfectly atop the other, and between them there is no crack for an idol to slip into.

[PAUSE]

Feel what this does. Every single thing you might be tempted to entrust to something other than Allah — your provision, your protection, your future, your reputation, the outcome you are losing sleep over — every one of those is a *shayʾ*. And there is no *shayʾ* that is outside the second circle. The thing you are anxious about is, by definition, covered by the word *kull.* There is no exception clause with your name on it.

Now we are ready for the word the whole ayah has been walking toward.

## Element Three: *Wakīl* — the One entrusted with everything, appointed by no one

> وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ **وَكِيلٌ**

*Wakīl.*

Let me first put the word into your hands the way your own life already holds it — because, remarkably, this is a word you live with even if you've never opened an Arabic dictionary.

A *wakīl* is an agent. A proxy. The one you appoint to act on your behalf when you cannot act yourself. In the law of nearly every Muslim land, when you sign a power of attorney, the person you empower is your *wakīl.* When you cannot attend in person — to sign the contract, to manage the estate, to represent you in court — you appoint a *wakīl* to stand in your place and *handle it.* The word carries exactly this: entrustment, representation, the handing-over of your affairs to one who will see them through.

And notice *why* human beings appoint a wakīl. We appoint one out of **limitation.** I cannot be in two cities at once, so I send an agent. I do not know the law, so I retain one who does. I am dying, so I name an executor to carry out my will after I am gone. Every human wakīl exists to patch a hole in our reach. The wakīl is a confession of our weakness — a borrowed pair of hands because our own do not stretch far enough.

Now look at the lexical depth the commentators draw out of this word, because *wakīl* is richer than just "agent." Al-Qurṭubī, with characteristic economy, glosses it: *ḥāfiẓ wa qāʾim bihi* — **"the Preserver, the One who upholds it."** Al-Baghawī expands: *all things are entrusted to Him, and He is the One who stands over their preservation.* Al-Jalālayn: *mutaṣarrifun fīhi kayfa yashāʾ* — **"the One who disposes of everything however He wills."** And al-Muyassar gathers it all: *Lord, Owner, Disposer, and the Guardian over all things, managing the entire affairs of His creation.*

So *Wakīl*, in its full weight, holds three things at once:
- The **Keeper** — the one who preserves and protects what is in His charge.
- The **Manager** — the one who runs the affair, who disposes of it, who steers the outcome.
- The **Trustee** — the one to whom the whole matter is committed, completely.

Keeper, Manager, Trustee — all in one word, and all stretched across *kulli shayʾ.* Everything.

But here is the absence we must read — and it is the deepest thing in the ayah.

[PAUSE]

Every wakīl in human language has *two* parties standing behind him. There is the one he represents — the one who *appointed* him. And there is the matter he handles. I appoint a lawyer (the wakīl) to manage my estate (the matter). The wakīl is always the middle term, empowered *by* someone, *over* something.

So when Allah says He is the Wakīl over all things — ask the question Arabic invites you to ask. **Appointed by whom?**

And the answer rings back through the silence: *by no one.* There is no party above Allah who delegated to Him the running of the universe. There is no higher authority that signed Him a power of attorney. He is the Wakīl who was appointed by none — which means He is not a *representative* of some greater power, He IS the power. He is not standing in for an absent principal; He is the Principal who is never absent.

Do you see the staggering inversion? In our world, the wakīl is the mark of *limitation* — we resort to an agent precisely because we are weak, finite, mortal, unable to reach. But when this word is applied to Allah, every trace of limitation burns away. He is the Wakīl not because He delegated His reach to someone else, but because **all of creation has been entrusted to Him by the sheer fact of His being its Lord.** He is the trustee of everything, accountable to no one, sufficient without a partner, the Manager whose management never fails and never sleeps and is never delegated downward to an idol.

The human wakīl says: *you are limited, so let me help.* The divine Wakīl says: *I am limitless, so let Me carry it.*

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Bridging the Linguistic Journey

Step back and see what these three words have built together.

*Khāliq* — the active participle — told us the Maker never set His work down; His creating is His standing nature, alive in this present breath.

*Kulli shayʾ*, repeated and matched in scope, told us there is no thing outside His making *and* no thing outside His keeping; the two circles are exactly the same size, with no gap for any false god to enter.

And *Wakīl* told us *what kind* of keeping this is — total, sovereign, unappointed, unfailing trusteeship over the very thing you are afraid of.

Put them in a line and the surah's whole argument stands up on its own:

*The One who made everything is the same One who keeps everything, and He keeps it not as a borrowed agent but as the unappointed Lord of it all.*

The potter did not walk away from the wheel.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way you are living tomorrow morning.

# PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

## Theme One: The Exhaustion of Being Your Own Wakīl

Let me begin not with the ayah but with a feeling you know intimately.

There is a particular kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with the body. It's the tiredness of the person who feels that if they stop holding everything together, everything will fall apart. You know this person. You may *be* this person. The one who cannot delegate at work because no one else will do it right. The one who lies awake rehearsing every possible version of tomorrow, trying to pre-solve problems that haven't happened yet. The parent who carries the entire weight of a child's future as though their worry were the load-bearing beam holding up the sky.

Psychologists have a name for one face of this. They call it *hypervigilance* — the nervous system stuck in the "on" position, scanning, managing, controlling, never able to set the burden down. And underneath it, almost always, is a single buried belief: **"If I am not managing this, no one is."**

I want you to see something about that belief. It is, in the most precise sense, a *theological* statement. The anxious person who cannot let go is not merely stressed. They have, without ever deciding to, **appointed themselves the wakīl of their own universe.** They have looked at the *kulli shayʾ* — all the things — and concluded that the keeping of it rests on their own two shoulders. They have taken the job that this ayah says belongs to Allah, and they have hired themselves for it.

And here is the cruelty of that arrangement: it is a job you are not equipped to hold. Remember what a wakīl is for? We appoint an agent because we are *limited.* So when you make yourself the wakīl of your own life, you have put a limited, finite, exhausted, mortal creature in a role that requires limitlessness. You have placed the weight of *kulli shayʾ* — every thing — onto a pair of hands that cannot even guarantee your next heartbeat.

No wonder you're tired.

[PAUSE]

Now let the ayah illuminate it. *Wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin wakīl.* He — and the word *huwa*, "He," carries a quiet exclusivity here — *He* is, over all things, the Wakīl.

This is not asking you to stop caring. It is not asking you to stop working, planning, providing, striving. The Prophet ﷺ taught the man to tie his camel *and then* trust in Allah — the trusting did not cancel the tying. What the ayah is offering is something far more merciful than passivity. It is a *transfer of the burden of outcomes.* You do the tying — the action, the effort, the *sabab*, the means. But the *keeping* of the result, the guaranteeing of the outcome, the management of the thousand variables you cannot see and cannot touch — that was never your job. It belongs to the Wakīl. You were never supposed to carry the part of the load marked *kulli shayʾ.* You picked it up. You are allowed to put it down.

This is the secret hidden inside the word *tawakkul* — the great Qur'anic concept of reliance. Look at it: *tawakkul* comes from the very same root as *Wakīl* — w-k-l. To make *tawakkul* upon Allah is, at the root level, **to take Allah as your Wakīl.** To do the human thing — to appoint the agent — but to appoint the right One. To stop being the exhausted, finite, self-hired manager of an unmanageable universe, and to hand the file to the only One whose reach is the same size as the problem: *kulli shayʾ.*

The companions said it in three words at the worst moment of their fear, when they were told the armies had gathered against them: *Ḥasbunā Allāhu wa niʿma al-Wakīl.* "Allah is sufficient for us, and what an excellent Wakīl He is." And the ayah after records what it did: they returned *with grace and bounty from Allah, no harm having touched them.* That is what it looks like to fire yourself from the job you were never qualified for, and to hand it to the One who has held it all along.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Tonight, name one *shayʾ* — one specific thing — that you have been the self-appointed wakīl of. The outcome of a meeting. A diagnosis. A child's path. A relationship you cannot force. And tomorrow, after you have done your honest part — the tying of the camel — say the words out loud and *mean* the transfer: *Ḥasbiya Allāhu wa niʿma al-Wakīl.* Not as a magic phrase, but as an actual resignation letter from a job that was crushing you.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: The God Who Does Not Abandon What He Makes

Let me start somewhere very human.

There is a specific grief in being *made* and then *left.* The child whose parent provided everything material and then was emotionally absent. The employee who was recruited with great fanfare and then forgotten, left to fend on a project no one checks on. The friend who shaped you in an intense season of life and then drifted away without a word. We know, in our bones, the difference between being *created* by someone and being *kept* by them. And we have learned, painfully, that the first does not guarantee the second. People make things — and people walk away from the things they make.

This is the wound the pagans had projected onto the heavens. Their distant Creator was, in a sense, the cosmic version of the absent parent: He had made them and then left the room, handing the real attention — the daily care — to lesser hands. Their god created and walked away. And so they spent their lives running to idols, because at least the idols, they imagined, were *paying attention.*

And here is the quiet devastation of this ayah for that worldview — and the quiet healing of it for ours. Allah binds the two acts together in a single breath and refuses to let them come apart. *Khāliqu kulli shayʾ* — Creator of all things — *wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin wakīl* — and over all things, the Keeper. The making and the keeping in one sentence, with no pause long enough for abandonment to fit inside.

Let's sit with the order. He says *Creator* first, then *Wakīl.* Why that order, and why both?

Because the order is the argument. Consider: who is more invested in a thing — the one who merely owns it, or the one who *made* it from nothing? The Wakīl here is not a hired stranger taking on someone else's property. He is the Wakīl *who is also the Khāliq.* The Keeper is the Maker. And there is no keeper in all of existence more invested than the one whose own creating brought the thing into being. A stranger guards your house for a wage and watches the clock. The one who *built* it with his own hands watches it because a piece of him is in it.

[PAUSE]

So the ayah is not just saying "Allah looks after things." It is saying something far more intimate. It is saying that the very One who *brought you into being* — who chose, out of nothing, that you would exist — is the same One into whose trusteeship your existence is committed every single second. The hand that shaped you is the hand that holds you. You are not a product on a shelf. You are not a vessel the potter fired and forgot. You are a *shayʾ* in the keeping of the One who is, right now, in the active and present sense of *khāliq*, still your Creator.

Notice the difference this makes to the deepest fear underneath all our fears — the fear of being, ultimately, *alone.* Of facing the dark, the diagnosis, the loss, the death, without anyone truly *with* us. This ayah does not answer that fear by promising the trouble away. It answers it by collapsing the gap between Maker and Keeper. The One who is responsible for the fact that you exist has not, for one instant, gone off duty. There is no moment of your life that fell outside His watch the way it fell outside the watch of every human being who ever loved you and then, in sleep or distance or death, stopped being able to watch.

And feel where this lands in Surah Az-Zumar specifically. This is a surah about *ikhlāṣ* — pure, undivided devotion. The reason the surah can demand that all your worship be poured toward One is laid bare right here: because there *is* only One who both makes and keeps. You do not need to scatter your heart across a hundred lesser powers, hedging your bets, lighting a candle here and an idol there. There is no second keeper to appease. The making and the keeping live at a single address. So the heart can finally stop dividing itself — and rest.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** The next time you feel the specific loneliness of carrying something no human being fully sees — a private fear, an unspoken grief, a battle no one around you understands — let this ayah re-frame the moment. You are not unwitnessed. The One who made you is, in the live present tense, your Wakīl over this very thing. Say to yourself, in the dark: *the One who made me has not set me down.* And let that be enough to loosen the grip in your chest, even by a little.

# CLOSING SYNTHESIS

## The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, look back and see the shape of the whole thing — because the ayah is built like a perfect arch, and we could not see it until now.

> **ٱللَّهُ** خَٰلِقُ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ
> **وَهُوَ** عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ وَكِيلٌ

Two halves, mirror-balanced. Each opens by naming Him — *Allāh*, then *huwa* (He). Each contains the identical totality — *kulli shayʾ*, then *kulli shayʾ.* And each closes on a description of what He is — *Khāliq*, then *Wakīl.*

Maker — All Things — / — All Things — Keeper.

The two great acts placed on either side of a perfectly matched center, the totality bridging them like the keystone of an arch. The structure itself *is* the message: there is no daylight between His creating and His keeping. They are the two pillars of one arch, and *kulli shayʾ* — every single thing, including you — rests safely on top.

## Bringing It Together

The linguistic journey and the two themes are the same truth seen from two directions.

From the side of the *words*, we found a Maker whose making never stopped (*khāliq*), a totality with no exceptions (*kulli shayʾ*), and a Keeper appointed by none and failing in nothing (*Wakīl*).

From the side of the *heart*, we found relief from the exhausting job of being our own wakīl, and healing for the fear of being a thing that was made and then abandoned.

They meet in a single sentence: **The One who made you has never once set you down — so you can finally set down the weight you were never built to carry.**

## Questions to Carry

1. What is the one *shayʾ* — name it specifically — that you have appointed *yourself* the wakīl of, and what has that appointment cost you?
2. When you "trust in Allah," are you actually transferring the burden of the outcome — or are you saying the words while quietly keeping the file on your own desk?
3. Where in your life have you been running to a lesser "keeper" — a person, a plan, a sense of control — to manage what is already in the keeping of the Wakīl?
4. If you truly believed the One who made you has not, for one second, gone off duty, what fear would loosen its grip first?
5. What would *ikhlāṣ* — undivided devotion — actually look like in your week, if you stopped hedging your heart across a hundred lesser powers?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The One who creates all things is the very same One who keeps all things, appointed by none and failing in nothing — so there is no thing in your life, and no fear in your heart, that has fallen outside His hand.

## The Closing Invitation

This is not a call to do less. It is a call to *carry* less — to keep the tying of the camel and hand over the keeping of the outcome. To resign, gently and finally, from the impossible position of being the wakīl of your own universe, and to entrust the file to the only One whose reach is exactly the size of the problem: *kull.* Everything. You are invited, simply, to let the Maker keep what He made.

## A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You are the Creator of all things and the Keeper of all things — and I am tired of pretending to be either. Take from my hands the burden I was never built to carry, and let me say with my whole heart, and mean it: Ḥasbiya Allāhu wa niʿma al-Wakīl. You made me; do not let me live as though You had set me down. Āmīn.*
