# The Middleman That Owns Nothing

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read these two verses to you. First in Arabic, then slowly in English, and then I want us to step back — all the way back — before we step in.

> **أَمِ اتَّخَذُوا مِن دُونِ اللَّهِ شُفَعَاءَ ۚ قُلْ أَوَلَوْ كَانُوا لَا يَمْلِكُونَ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ**
>
> *Am ittakhadhū min dūni-llāhi shufaʿāʾ. Qul a-wa-law kānū lā yamlikūna shayʾan wa lā yaʿqilūn.*
>
> **"Or have they taken, besides Allah, intercessors? Say: 'Even though they have no power over anything, and they do not reason?'"**

> [ayah:39:44] قُل لِّلَّهِ ٱلشَّفَـٰعَةُ جَمِيعًا ۖ لَّهُۥ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَـٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ ثُمَّ إِلَيْهِ تُرْجَعُونَ
>
> *Qul li-llāhi-sh-shafāʿatu jamīʿā. Lahu mulku-s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ. Thumma ilayhi turjaʿūn.*
>
> **"Say: 'To Allah belongs intercession, all of it. To Him belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. Then to Him you will be returned.'"**

### Rebuilding the world

To feel why this lands the way it does, you have to hear the verse that comes just before it.

One verse earlier, in 39:42, Allah has just described something almost unbearably intimate. He says that He is the One who takes the souls at the moment of death — *and* He takes the souls of the living when they sleep. Every night, when you lie down and consciousness dissolves, that is not nothing happening. That is your soul being **taken**. And then, of the souls He has taken in sleep, He holds back the ones whose time has come, and He **releases** the others back into their bodies for a fixed term.

Sit with that picture for a moment. Every single night, billions of souls are lifted up. Some are kept. The rest are handed back. And not one of those souls has any say in whether it returns. You did not negotiate your way out of bed this morning. You were **released**.

That is the world the original listeners were sitting in when the next word came.

And the people being addressed — the mushrikūn of Makkah — were not crude atheists. They were not people who said there is no God. They believed in Allah as the Creator. Ask them who made the heavens, who sends the rain, who holds the souls at night, and they would say: *Allah*. Their problem was not the existence of God. Their problem was **access**. They felt the distance. Allah was too high, too vast, too holy to approach directly — so they appointed go-betweens. Idols of stone, angels in their imagination, dead righteous men — figures they hung between themselves and God, saying the famous line the Qur'an records elsewhere: *"We only worship them so that they may bring us nearer to Allah"* (39:3).

They were not denying God. They were **outsourcing** Him.

### The hook

So here is the moment. The One who holds your very soul in His hand every night while you sleep — utterly close, utterly in control — and these people turned around and looked for *someone else* to carry their case to Him.

And Allah's response is not a thundering threat. It is a single, surgical question. He does not even bother, at first, to argue that the intercessors are false. He asks something much more devastating. He asks you to **look at what you've hired**.

What you are about to see is how two verses take the entire human craving for a middleman — a fixer, a connection, someone on the inside — and dismantle it, not by force, but by simply turning on the light so you can see who you've been talking to.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Am* — the word that turns the whole picture around

Let me teach you something small about Arabic before we touch the verse, because the very first word is doing work most translations cannot carry.

In English, when we want to pivot away from something and correct it, we use words like "rather," or "instead," or "no — actually." Imagine a friend insisting their phone is waterproof, and you watch them drop it in a pool and it dies, and you say flatly: *"Waterproof. Right. Instead it's at the bottom of the pool."* That word "instead" does two jobs at once — it pivots, and it carries a quiet contempt. It says: *given everything we just saw, you went and did this?*

Arabic has a particle that does exactly this. It is **أَم** (*am*). Now, *am* usually means "or," as in a simple either/or question. But grammarians identify a second kind — what they call the **munqaṭiʿ**, the "disconnected" *am* — which doesn't really mean "or" at all. It means something closer to *"bal"* — "rather," "instead," "but no —". It breaks off from what came before and turns to a new thing with a tone of rebuke built into it.

And the classical mufassirūn caught this immediately. Al-Qurtubī, opening this very verse, writes: *"Am ittakhadhū — meaning **bal** ittakhadhū"* — **rather**, they took. And he adds something beautiful: that there is an implied, swallowed phrase here. The verses just before spoke of the signs of Allah for *"a people who reflect."* So the full unspoken sense is: *they did not reflect... instead, they took intercessors besides Allah.*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the weight of that *instead*?

Here is the full emotional shape of it. Allah lays out sign after sign — the souls taken at night, the bodies released at dawn, the entire machinery of life and death running through His fingers — and the expected human response is *to reflect, to think, to bow*. And then comes this one word, *am*, like a camera turning sharply: **"Instead... they went and took intercessors besides Allah?"**

It is the sound of a door closing on an excuse. There was a path of reflection right in front of them, lit up, obvious. And they walked past it to go hire a middleman.

Notice what this single particle does that an argument never could. It does not say *"you are wrong."* It says *"look at the choice you made, side by side with what you ignored."* The rebuke is not in the volume. It is in the **juxtaposition**. And that, already, is the whole verse in miniature — because the rest of these two ayahs are nothing but a series of devastating side-by-sides.

What this one word reveals: the tragedy here is not ignorance. It is **substitution**. They had the real thing and chose the imitation.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Ittakhadhū* — the taking that is really a making

Now look at the verb itself: **اتَّخَذُوا** (*ittakhadhū*) — "they took."

Let me again give you the grammar gently, because it is load-bearing. Arabic verbs come in patterns — what scholars call *forms*, numbered one through ten — and each pattern bends the basic meaning of the root in a particular direction. The simple root here is *a-kh-dh* (أخذ), which just means "to take." But this verb is in **Form VIII** (*iftaʿala*) — *ittakhadha*. And one of the things Form VIII characteristically does is fold the action back onto the doer. It carries a reflexive, self-interested flavor. It is not just *"to take"* — it is *"to take **for oneself**,"* to take and adopt and appropriate something into your own world for your own benefit.

So *ittakhadhū shufaʿāʾ* is not "they encountered some intercessors." It is **"they took intercessors *for themselves*."** They appointed them. They commissioned them. They reached out, selected these objects, and installed them in a role.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this, because it quietly demolishes the whole defense the idolater wants to make.

The idolater wants to say: *"These are real intercessors. They have genuine standing with God. I'm just availing myself of an arrangement that exists."* As if the intercession were a real institution and he merely walked in the door.

But the verb exposes the truth. *Ittakhadhū* — **they took them for themselves.** The intercessor did not appoint himself. God did not appoint him. The worshipper appointed him. The entire "standing" of this middleman traces back to one source: the wish of the person who hired him. It is a self-issued license. You drew up the contract, you signed it on both lines, and then you bowed to your own signature.

Here is the everyday shape of it. Imagine someone who tells you, with total confidence, *"My lawyer can get me in to see the judge personally, any time."* And you ask, *"Who hired this lawyer?"* And they say, *"I did."* — *"And who told you he has access to the judge?"* — *"He did. Well... actually, I just assumed it."* The "access" was never real. It was a story the client told himself and then believed because believing it felt like safety.

That is *ittakhadhū*. A relationship invented by the one who needed it to be true.

What this verb reveals: false intercession is not something you *discover*. It is something you **manufacture** — and then forget that you were the one who made it.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: The two emptinesses — *lā yamlikūna shayʾan wa lā yaʿqilūn*

Now Allah turns on the light. He says: *Qul* — **Say** — and gives the Prophet ﷺ a single question to ask. And the question is not *"are these intercessors real?"* The question is far more humiliating to the whole project. He says:

**أَوَلَوْ كَانُوا لَا يَمْلِكُونَ شَيْئًا وَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ**

*"Even though they possess nothing at all, and do not even reason?"*

Two deprivations, stacked. And I want us to take them one at a time, because the order is merciless.

The first: **لَا يَمْلِكُونَ شَيْئًا** — *lā yamlikūna shayʾan* — "they do not own a thing." The root is *m-l-k*, ownership, possession, control. And notice the object: *shayʾan* — "a thing," indefinite, in a negative sentence. In Arabic, an indefinite noun under negation becomes total. It does not mean "they don't own much." It means **"they do not own *anything whatsoever*."** Not a sliver. Not a fraction of the power to help you. Al-Qurtubī and al-Baghawī both specify: they possess nothing **of the intercession itself** — not even the standing they were hired for.

Think about what an intercessor *is for*. An intercessor is supposed to have leverage. Pull. Standing. The entire reason you go to a middleman instead of going direct is that you believe *he has something you don't* — access, influence, a claim. And the verse says: he owns **nothing**. The one quality that would make him worth approaching is the exact quality he completely lacks.

It is like discovering that the "connected insider" you've been paying off to reach the boss... doesn't actually work at the company. Has never met the boss. Has no badge, no desk, no phone number. The premise of the relationship is void.

[PAUSE]

And then — the second deprivation, and this is where it becomes almost unbearable.

**وَلَا يَعْقِلُونَ** — *wa lā yaʿqilūn* — "and they do not even reason." The root is *ʿ-q-l*, the intellect, the faculty of understanding. Al-Qurtubī gives the blunt reason: *"li-annahā jamādāt"* — "because they are inanimate objects." Stones. Wood. Things.

Now line the two up. The first deprivation said: *they have no power.* You could imagine, maybe, that a powerless friend could still *care* about you — could still, out of love, try, even if he fails. But the second deprivation closes even that door. These objects do not have power, **and they are not even aware that you exist.** They do not know you are bowing to them. They do not register your tears, your fear, your desperate hope. Al-Jalālayn spells it out: *they do not even comprehend that you are worshipping them.*

[PAUSE]

Do you see the full horror of the pairing now?

The worshipper has poured out his deepest need — his fear of death, his longing for forgiveness, his hope of being carried before God — and he has poured it into an object that **(1) can do nothing about it, and (2) doesn't even know he's there.**

It is the loneliest possible transaction. A man on his knees, weeping, before something that has neither the power to help nor the awareness to notice. He is, functionally, **completely alone** — but he has decorated his aloneness to look like a relationship.

And now feel the irony coiled inside that last word, *yaʿqilūn*. This whole rebuke is being delivered to **human beings — the creatures whose entire dignity is that they *do* reason.** *Am ittakhadhū... lā yaʿqilūn* — the reasoning creature has handed his fate to the un-reasoning object. The one with intellect has knelt before the one without it. The verse doesn't say this out loud. It doesn't have to. The word *yaʿqilūn* hangs there, holding up a mirror.

What these two emptinesses reveal: the false intercessor fails at *both* ends — he cannot act, and he cannot even *know*. And the one who hired him has quietly surrendered the very faculty — reason — that could have saved him from the mistake.

[PAUSE — let it sit]

### The bridge

So look at what these three pieces have built. *Am* — *instead* of reflecting, they substituted. *Ittakhadhū* — they manufactured the middleman themselves. *Lā yamlikūna... wa lā yaʿqilūn* — and the middleman they manufactured owns nothing and knows nothing.

The grammar has done something the listener cannot un-see. It has not argued the intercessors away. It has simply **described them accurately** — and the accurate description is its own refutation.

But the verse is not finished. It has emptied the false thing. Now it must fill the space with the true one. Because a human being who is shown that his middleman is hollow will not simply stop reaching — he will panic and reach for *another* middleman. So verse 44 does not leave the hand grasping. It takes the hand and places it directly where it always belonged.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The fantasy of the middleman

Let me start somewhere you already live.

There is a particular kind of relief we feel when we find *a guy who knows a guy*. You need something difficult — a permit, a job, an appointment, a way around a wall — and someone says, *"Don't worry, I know someone on the inside, I'll put in a word."* And something in your chest unclenches. Notice that the problem isn't solved yet. Nothing has actually happened. But you *feel* better, immediately, because you now have a **middleman**.

Why does the middleman feel so good?

Because the middleman lets you have the outcome **without the relationship.** You don't have to face the authority yourself. You don't have to be known by them, judged by them, stand exposed before them. You can stay comfortably in the back while your connection works the front. The middleman is a buffer. He absorbs the intimacy and the risk, and passes you only the result.

And here is the spiritual mechanism the verse is exposing: **this is exactly what the idolater wanted from his intercessors.** Re-read their own confession — *"so that they may bring us nearer to Allah."* They wanted nearness to God **without having to actually face God.** They wanted the outcome — divine favor, protection, paradise — but they wanted it routed through a buffer, so they would never have to stand directly, nakedly, before the Holy One and be known.

The intercessor was not really about God's distance. It was about *their* hiding.

[PAUSE]

And now you understand why the answer to a false middleman is so dangerous if you don't watch yourself. When verse 43 strips the idol bare, the natural human reflex is *not* to walk up to the front desk and face the authority. The natural reflex is to go find a **better middleman.** "Okay, that connection was fake — but surely there's a real one out there. Let me find a saint, a relic, a ritual, a special name, a someone, who actually *does* have the access."

This is the addiction underneath idolatry, and it did not die in Makkah. It lives in every heart that would rather have a transaction than a relationship with God. It lives in the person who collects intermediaries — this shaykh's barakah, that grave's blessing, this amulet, that "wasilah" treated as a magic key — not out of love, but because each one is a way to *not have to stand alone before God and be seen.*

The verse calls the whole project by its real name. Every middleman you put between yourself and Allah to *avoid* Allah is, at best, an object that owns nothing and knows nothing. Because **no created thing can stand in that gap.** The gap was never meant to be filled. The gap is the place where *you* are supposed to stand.

What does this change tomorrow? When you next feel the pull to reach for a buffer — a person, a ritual, a "someone who can pray for me because surely God won't listen to me directly" — pause and ask: *Am I reaching for help, or am I reaching for a place to hide?* And then do the terrifying, liberating thing the idolater could not bring himself to do: **go direct.** Lift your own hands. Speak your own name into the dark. The One who holds your soul every single night already knows you are there. You have never needed an introduction.

### Theme Two: *Lahu mulku — to Him belongs it all*, and the collapse of the chain

Now let me show you the most beautiful structural move in these two verses, because verse 44 doesn't just deny the false — it dissolves the very thinking that made the false possible.

Listen to verse 44 again: *"Say: To Allah belongs intercession, **all of it** (jamīʿan). To Him belongs the **dominion** (mulk) of the heavens and the earth. Then to Him **you will be returned**."*

I want you to watch one root travel across both verses. The root *m-l-k* — ownership.

In verse 43, it appeared as a **negation**: the false gods *lā **yamlikūna** shayʾan* — they **own nothing.**

In verse 44, the very same root returns as an **affirmation**: *lahu **mulku** al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — to Him belongs the **ownership** of the heavens and the earth.

[PAUSE]

Feel the architecture. The idols own *not a single thing*. Allah owns *the heavens and the earth, entirely.* It is the same scale laid against itself: total emptiness on one side, total possession on the other. And the word for "all of it," *jamīʿan*, slams the door: intercession is not *mostly* Allah's with a little franchised out to saints and stones. It is *jamīʿan* — **the whole of it, every last particle, belongs to Him alone.**

Here is why that one word demolishes the entire middleman economy.

Think about how intercession works among human beings. The only reason a middleman has any value is that **power is divided.** The clerk can't approve your file, so you go over his head to the manager, who can. The manager can't change the policy, so you find the director, who can. The whole institution of the "connection" exists *because* authority is scattered across many hands, and the game is to find the hand that holds *your* piece.

Now — what happens to the entire game the moment you learn that **one person owns the whole company, top to bottom, and every other "authority" owns literally nothing?**

The middleman vanishes. Instantly. There is no one to go *through*, because there is no one who holds a piece you could route around the owner to obtain. Every door, every desk, every approval — all of it traces back to a single office. *Lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ.* He owns the building, the staff, the policy, the gate, the gatekeeper, and the gate's hinges.

This is why *jamīʿan* is the kill-shot. As long as you believe intercession is *distributed* — that some of it lives with this saint and some with that angel — the middleman makes sense. The moment you know it is *jamīʿan*, all of it, His — the middleman is not just unhelpful. He is **conceptually impossible.** There is nowhere for him to stand, because he would have to stand on a piece of authority that does not exist outside of God's hand.

[PAUSE]

And then comes the final clause, and it does something quietly merciful.

*Thumma ilayhi **turjaʿūn*** — *"Then to Him you will be **returned**."*

Look at that verb. *Turjaʿūn* — it is **passive**. Not "then you will return to Him," as if you might choose your route, but "then you will **be returned** to Him." You will be brought back. The same way the sleeping soul in verse 42 was *released* without consultation — handed back into its body each morning whether it liked it or not — so too, at the end, every soul will simply be **returned**, with no middleman in the loop, no buffer, no intercession-broker negotiating the terms. The chain you built to keep distance between yourself and God ends with you being delivered straight into His presence anyway.

So why even build the chain?

That is the whole journey of these two verses, compressed. You spent your life appointing go-betweens to avoid facing the Owner. And then, *thumma* — "and then," after all of it — you are returned directly to Him, exactly as if the go-betweens never existed. Because they never did own anything. The only relationship that was ever real was the one you were hiding from.

What does this change tomorrow? It means you can stop *managing* God through proxies and start *facing* Him. It means the most efficient thing you will ever do — and the most honest — is to drop the chain of intermediaries and speak to the Owner directly, today, before you are returned to Him and discover you could have been speaking to Him all along. The destination is His presence either way. Tawhid is simply choosing to arrive early, on your knees, by choice.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and see how these two verses are built, because the symmetry is the proof of everything we've said.

**Verse 43 empties. Verse 44 fills.**

- 43 says the false gods own **nothing** — *lā yamlikūna shayʾan.*
  44 says Allah owns **everything** — *lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ.*

- 43 shows intercessors who are **many, scattered, indefinite** — *shufaʿāʾ*, a vague plural.
  44 shows intercession that is **one, gathered, total** — *al-shafāʿatu jamīʿan.*

- 43 ends on objects that **do not even reason** — *lā yaʿqilūn.*
  44 ends on persons who **will be returned to be reckoned** — *ilayhi turjaʿūn.*

The dead objects have no mind; the living souls have full accountability. The verse strips awareness from the false and assigns it, inescapably, to you.

It is one motion: the false middleman is dissolved, and the space he occupied is handed back to its only Owner — to whom, in the end, you go directly anyway.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the thematic depths are the same insight from two directions.

The grammar showed us a middleman that was **manufactured** (*ittakhadhū*), **powerless** (*lā yamlikūna*), and **unconscious** (*lā yaʿqilūn*). The themes showed us *why we manufacture him*: to have nearness without exposure, outcome without relationship, God without facing God. And verse 44 showed us that the entire project is built on a false premise — that authority is divided — when in truth *mulk* is *jamīʿan*, all His, so there is no one to go through and never was.

One word, *am* — *instead* — opened it: you had the real, and chose the imitation. One word, *jamīʿan* — *all of it* — closed it: the imitation never owned a thing to begin with.

### Questions to carry

- Where, in my own life of worship, am I reaching for a **buffer** — so that I can have God's help without having to stand exposed before Him?
- Have I quietly turned anything — a person, a practice, a name, a hope — into an intercessor I *appointed myself*, the way the verse's *ittakhadhū* describes?
- If *all* intercession belongs to Allah alone, what would it look like to ask Him **directly**, today, for the thing I keep asking others to ask Him for me?
- When I imagine being "returned to Him" — *turjaʿūn* — does it feel like a confrontation I'm avoiding, or a reunion I could begin now?
- What is the one prayer I have never dared to make in my own voice, because I assumed I needed someone with more standing to make it for me?

### One-sentence distillation

You spend your life appointing middlemen to keep a safe distance from God — but every middleman owns nothing and knows nothing, all intercession belongs to Him alone, and in the end you are returned straight to Him anyway, so face Him now.

### The closing invitation

This is not an argument to win. It is a buffer to put down.

You do not need a connection to reach the One who holds your soul every night while you sleep and hands it back to you every morning. You never did. The whole apparatus of go-betweens was a way of standing in the back of the room while life happened at the front. These two verses walk to the back of the room, take you gently by the hand, and lead you to the only desk that was ever real — the desk of the One who owns the building, the staff, the gate, and the dawn.

Let the next du'a you make be in your own voice. No broker. No buffer. Just you, returned — by choice this time — to the One to whom you were always going to return.

### A brief du'a

*O Allah, to You belongs all intercession, and to You belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. Strip from my heart every middleman I have placed between myself and You to avoid You. Let me face You directly, with nothing in my hands and nothing behind which to hide — and receive me, when I am returned to You, as one who finally stopped reaching for everything but You.*

*Āmīn.*
