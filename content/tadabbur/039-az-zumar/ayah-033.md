# The One Who Carried It and the One Who Answered

### Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 33

> وَٱلَّذِى جَآءَ بِٱلصِّدْقِ وَصَدَّقَ بِهِۦٓ أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُتَّقُونَ

> *wa-lladhī jāʾa bi-ṣ-ṣidqi wa ṣaddaqa bihi ulāʾika humu-l-muttaqūn*

> **"And the one who brought the truth and affirmed it — those, they are the people of taqwā."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Let me set you down in Makkah, in the years when this surah came.

The air is thick with a particular kind of argument. Not the loud, simple argument of one man swinging at another. A subtler war. A war over a single question that the whole surah keeps circling back to, like a tongue returning to a sore tooth: **who is telling the truth?**

The people of Makkah are not, by and large, fools. They are merchants. They live and die by the reliability of a word. A man who breaks a contract is finished; a caravan financed on a lie collapses in the desert. They know the *value* of truth the way a banker knows the value of a coin. And yet — here is the strange thing — when *the* Truth comes to them, when the very ground of all truths is offered to them through a man they had themselves named **al-Amīn**, the Trustworthy, the Truthful — they turn from it.

So the surah, just before our verse, draws its sharpest line. Listen to verse 32:

> *"So who is more unjust than the one who lies against Allah and denies the truth when it comes to him? Is there not in Hell a dwelling for the disbelievers?"*

Sit with the weight of that question for a moment. **Who is more unjust** — not "who is unjust," but who could possibly be *more* unjust — than someone who does two things at once: invents a lie *about* Allah, and rejects the *truth* the moment it arrives at his door. That is verse 32. That is the floor of the human condition. The lowest place a soul can stand.

And then — without a breath, without a transition — comes verse 33. Our verse.

It is the photographic negative of verse 32. Where 32 had a liar, 33 has a truth-bringer. Where 32 had a denier, 33 has an affirmer. Where 32 ended in Hell, 33 — and you will see this when we get there — ends, two verses later, in everything the soul could want from its Lord.

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Here is what I want you to be hungry for by the time we open this verse fully.

This verse names a single category of human being and crowns them with the highest title the Quran gives to a servant: **al-muttaqūn**, the people of taqwā. The God-conscious. The ones who get the reward. And the verse defines them not by how much they pray, not by how much they fast, not by how much they give — but by their relationship to **one thing**: *aṣ-ṣidq*. The truth.

But it says something stranger than that. It does not say "the people of taqwā are those who *know* the truth." It does not even say "those who *believe* the truth." It splits the act of truth into two motions — **bringing** it and **affirming** it — and it does something with the grammar, something so quiet you could read past it a hundred times, that fuses these two motions into a single person, and then, in the very same breath, multiplies that single person into a crowd.

What is the difference between carrying the truth and answering it? Why does the verse need both? And how does a soul go from *handling* truth — the way a Makkan merchant handles a coin — to *being* truth, so completely that Allah Himself renames you?

Let's find out. Slowly.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: Two Verbs, One Root — The Truth That Must Be Carried *and* Answered

Before we touch the verse, I want to teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning, because without it the heart of this verse stays locked.

Arabic words grow from roots. A root is usually three letters, and it is like a seed — from one seed, a whole family of words grows, and every member of that family carries the scent of the original seed. Take the English idea of "truth." In English, "truth," "true," "truthful," "verify," "trust" are scattered across different roots; they don't *sound* related. But in Arabic, an entire orchard can grow from one root, and your ear hears the family resemblance every time.

Hold that, because it is about to do real work.

Now look at our verse. I want to show you two verbs.

The first: **جَآءَ بِٱلصِّدْقِ** — *jāʾa bi-ṣ-ṣidq* — "he came **with** the truth," or more naturally, "he **brought** the truth."

The second: **وَصَدَّقَ بِهِ** — *wa ṣaddaqa bihi* — "and he **affirmed** it," "held it as true," "answered it with belief."

Now here is the first thing to notice, the thing your eye slides past. The *object* of the first verb is **aṣ-ṣidq** — the truth. And the second verb, **ṣaddaqa**, is built from the **very same root** as that object. Ṣād–Dāl–Qāf. The root of truth itself.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what just happened? The truth he *carried* in the first half of the verse becomes the truth he *enacts* in the second half. The noun becomes a verb. The thing becomes a doing. He doesn't just bring *al-ṣidq* — the truth as an object, a message, a parcel to be delivered — he then **ṣaddaqa**, he *truths* it. He turns it from a thing he holds into a thing he does.

Let me slow this down even further, because the two verbs are doing genuinely different work, and the difference is the whole point.

**جَآءَ** — *jāʾa* — is a simple verb. Form I, the bare root in motion. "He came." It is the verb of *arrival*, of *delivery*. When you say someone *jāʾa bi-* something — came *with* something — you are describing a courier, a carrier, a bringer. The truth here is external. It is in his hands. He has transported it from where it was — from Allah, from revelation — to where the people are. This is the *prophetic* motion. The motion of someone who receives a trust and hands it on.

**صَدَّقَ** — *ṣaddaqa* — is a different shape entirely. It is what Arabic grammarians call **Form II**, and its function here is what they call *declarative* or *estimative* — to *deem* something true, to *treat* it as true, to *pronounce* it true with your whole stance toward it. When you *ṣaddaqa* something, you are not carrying it from outside. You are taking something that has arrived and you are saying, with everything in you: *yes. This is real. I stake myself on this.*

So watch the architecture of this single human being:

The truth comes *to* him from above — that is *jāʾa bi-ṣ-ṣidq*.
And then the truth is answered *from within* him — that is *ṣaddaqa bihi*.

**Reception and response. Carrying and confirming. The hand that delivers and the heart that says yes.**

[PAUSE]

Now, the classical scholars looked at this verse and asked a beautiful question: is this *one* person or *two*? Is the one who *brought* the truth the same as the one who *affirmed* it?

And here the tradition opens into a chorus — not of contradictions, but of camera angles on the same reality.

**Al-Baghawī** reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the bringer of the truth and the affirmer of the truth are *the same one* — the Messenger ﷺ. He *brought* "lā ilāha illā Allāh," and he himself *affirmed* it, conveying it to creation and living it utterly. One man, both motions. He carried it *and* he was true to it.

**Al-Suddī**, also preserved by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī, gives a different angle: the one who *brought* the truth is Jibrīl — who carried the Qur'ān down — and the one who *affirmed* it is Muḥammad ﷺ, who received it with total acceptance. Here the two motions are split across two figures: the angel who delivers, the Prophet who answers.

**ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib**, in al-Qurṭubī's record, reads it as the Prophet ﷺ who brought the truth and Abū Bakr who affirmed it — the Ṣiddīq, the one whose very title is built from this root, the one who said *yes* before anyone else dared.

And **al-Jalālayn** and **al-Muyassar** open the lens widest of all: the one who brought the truth is the Prophet ﷺ and the prophets before him, and the ones who affirmed it are the believers — the companions, and everyone after them, walking his path "to the Day of Judgment."

Now — do not treat these as four options on a multiple-choice test where you must circle one. Treat them as a single sculpture seen from four sides. Look at what they *all* agree on, what no angle denies:

**The truth is never complete in one motion.** It must be *carried* and it must be *answered*. Whether the carrying and the answering live in one heart — the Prophet who both delivered and embodied — or whether they pass down a chain — Jibrīl to Muḥammad, Muḥammad to Abū Bakr, Abū Bakr to the believers, the believers to *you* — the structure is identical. Truth that is only brought and never affirmed dies on the doorstep. Truth that someone claims to affirm but no one ever brought is a fantasy. **Bringing without affirming is a wasted delivery. Affirming without bringing is a delusion.** The God-conscious are the place where the two meet.

What this single, root-echoing verse reveals is this: *truthfulness is not a substance you possess. It is a relay you complete.* Someone, by Allah's mercy, carried it to your door. Taqwā is what happens when you open the door and say *yes* with your whole life.

### Element Two: The Sentence That Refuses to Stay Singular

Now I want to show you something in the grammar that is so quiet, and so devastating once you see it, that I promise you will never read this verse the same way again.

Let me first teach you the tool.

In Arabic, when you want to say "the one who," you use a relative pronoun, and that pronoun *has a number*. If you mean one person, you say **alladhī** — "the one who," singular, masculine. If you mean a group, you say **alladhīna** — "those who," plural. The choice is deliberate. Arabic does not blur the line between one and many the way English sometimes does. A speaker chooses.

So look at how our verse *opens*:

**وَٱلَّذِى** — *wa-lladhī* — "And **the one** who..." **Singular.** One person. He brought the truth. He affirmed it. Singular verbs all the way through: *jāʾa*, *ṣaddaqa* — "he came," "he affirmed." We are watching, grammatically, a single human being.

And then watch how the verse *ends*:

**أُو۟لَـٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُتَّقُونَ** — *ulāʾika humu-l-muttaqūn* — "**those**, **they** are the people of taqwā." **Plural.** *Ulāʾika* — "those," a plural pointing-word. *Humu* — "they," plural. *Al-muttaqūn* — "the God-conscious," plural.

[PAUSE]

Do you see it? The sentence *begins* with one and *ends* with many. It opens its hand around a single figure — "the one who brought the truth and affirmed it" — and then opens its arms around a crowd — "*those*, *they* are the muttaqūn."

This is not a grammatical accident. The classical grammarians caught it instantly. **Al-Jalālayn** says it plainly: *alladhī bi-maʿnā alladhīna* — "the singular 'the one who' here carries the meaning of the plural 'those who.'"

But *why*? Why not just say "those who brought the truth and affirmed it" from the start? Why begin with one and swell to many?

Here is what I believe the verse is doing to your heart, and it is breathtaking.

It begins with **one** so that the description is *precise*. It draws you a single, exact portrait: here is what a true human being looks like — one who carries the truth, one who answers it. Sharp. Particular. You can almost see his face. It is the face of the Prophet ﷺ. It is the face of Abū Bakr. It is one person you could point to.

And then it shifts to **many** — *ulāʾika*, "those" — so that you understand: **this is not a closed club of one.** This portrait is not a relic in a museum, behind glass, belonging only to a sacred past. It is a *category*. A type. A door. *Anyone* who stands where that one stood — who receives the truth and answers it with their whole self — *joins* the plural. Steps into the crowd. Becomes one of "those."

[PAUSE]

So the grammar does something the heart needs desperately. First it says: *the truthful one is rare, exact, singular — do not flatter yourself that this is everyone.* And then it says: *but the door is not locked. Become like him, and the verse stretches to include you. "Those" has room.*

The singular humbles you. The plural invites you.

And then comes the third word, the one that seals the door behind you so that no one else can claim what you've entered.

---

### Element Three: The Small Word That Excludes the World — *Humu*

Look again at the ending: *ulāʾika **humu**-l-muttaqūn.*

That little word — **هُمُ**, *humu*, "they" — sitting between "those" and "the God-conscious." In plain grammar you don't need it. You could say *ulāʾika-l-muttaqūn* — "those are the God-conscious" — and the sentence would be complete. So why is *humu* there?

Let me teach you what it is.

The Arabs called this the **ḍamīr al-faṣl** — the "pronoun of separation," or the "pronoun of distinction." It is a pronoun dropped into the middle of a sentence not to *add* a person — there's no new "they" here — but to do two things at once: to **emphasize**, and to **restrict**.

Think of the difference in English between:

*"Those are the truthful people."*

and

*"Those — they are the truthful people. They and no one else."*

That second sentence, with the emphatic "*they*," does something the first doesn't. It doesn't just describe the subject — it *fences off* the predicate. It says: this quality of truthfulness, of being muttaqūn — it belongs to *these*, and the claim of everyone else to it is hereby cancelled.

That is exactly what *humu* does here. It is a wall of exclusivity. The verse is saying: **those — they, specifically and exclusively, are the muttaqūn.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel why this lands so hard *right here*, in *this* surah, after *that* verse.

Remember verse 32. Remember the liar who invents falsehood about Allah and denies the truth. Makkah was *full* of people who considered themselves the guardians of religion. They tended the Kaʿbah. They fed the pilgrims. They believed themselves the truly pious, the people of God, the inheritors of Ibrāhīm. *They* thought taqwā was theirs by birthright and custodianship.

And the verse drops *humu* like a verdict. **No.** *Those* — the ones who brought the truth and answered it — *they*, and they alone, are the muttaqūn. Not the keepers of buildings. Not the inheritors of titles. Not the loud and the established. The truthful. Only the truthful.

This is a redefinition that overturns an entire social order. Taqwā is not awarded by lineage, by office, by who controls the sacred space. It is awarded by your relationship to the truth — whether you carried it and whether you said yes to it. *Humu* slams the door on every other claimant.

And here is the quiet mercy folded inside the severity: if taqwā belonged to lineage, you could never earn it. But because *humu* attaches taqwā to *ṣidq* — to truthfulness, which is a choice available to every soul in every moment — the door that *humu* closes on the pretenders is the very same door that stands *open* to you. You cannot inherit your way in. But you can be true your way in.

[PAUSE]

### The Bridge

So look at what these three quiet features have shown us, woven together.

**Two verbs from one root** told us that truth is a relay: it must be *carried* and it must be *answered*, and the God-conscious are where carrying and answering meet.

**The singular swelling to plural** told us that this meeting-place is at once a precise, rare portrait *and* an open category — the door humbles you and invites you in the same breath.

**The pronoun *humu*** told us that this door, once entered, belongs *exclusively* to the truthful — no lineage, no title, no custodianship can counterfeit the entry.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About what truth actually *is*, and why Allah would make it the single hinge on which the highest name for a servant turns.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Gap Between Knowing and Being True

Let me begin where you already live.

You have, at some point, *known* something was right and not done it. Everyone has. You knew you should make the call, apologize, return the money, speak up, stay silent, get up for the prayer. You *knew*. The knowledge was complete, sitting in you, fully formed. And you watched yourself not act on it. There was a gap — a strange, painful gap — between the truth you held in your mind and the truth your hands and feet and tongue actually lived.

Psychologists have a hundred names for this gap. The divided self. Cognitive dissonance. Akrasia — the ancient word for knowing the good and doing otherwise. We are, all of us, expert *carriers* of truths we never *affirm*. We transport the truth around inside us like a parcel we never open. We *jāʾa bi-ṣ-ṣidq* — we bring the truth, we possess it, we can even *recite* it — but we do not *ṣaddaqa bihi*. We do not answer it with our lives.

[PAUSE]

This is why the verse needs *both* verbs. Because the most dangerous spiritual condition in the world is to be a carrier who is not an affirmer. To have the truth fully in your hands and to have made no peace between it and your living.

Think about who, in this very surah, is the carrier-without-affirming. It's not the simple denier of verse 32 — he at least is honest about his rejection. The more haunting figure across the Qur'ān is the one who *has* the truth, who could quote it, who patrols its boundaries, who would defend it in argument — and whose life is a quiet, total contradiction of it. The one who brings the truth in his mouth and denies it in his hands.

And the verse refuses to call that person muttaqī. The title is reserved — *humu*, exclusively — for the one in whom the carrying and the answering have *fused*. Where there is no gap. Where what he holds and what he lives are the same single thing.

Let me give you the word for that fusion. In English we call it **integrity** — and the word literally means *integrated*, *undivided*, *whole*, made one. A structure has integrity when every part holds together under load. A person has integrity when the truth they carry and the truth they live are one continuous thing, with no seam, no gap, no hidden contradiction that the next pressure will split open.

[PAUSE]

That is what *ṣaddaqa bihi* is. It is the closing of the gap. It is the moment the carried truth becomes the lived truth. It is the soul saying *yes* not with its tongue but with its whole structure — so that under load, under temptation, under fear, under loss, the structure holds, because there is no seam to split.

And do you see now why the verse builds this *before* it gives the word *muttaqūn*? Because **taqwā is not a feeling. It is structural integrity before Allah.** It is being so completely true — carrying and affirming made one — that there is nothing left in you for fear or desire to pry apart.

**So what changes tomorrow?** This: stop measuring your faith by how much truth you *carry*. You probably carry plenty. You can quote the āyāt. You know the rulings. You possess the truth like a Makkan merchant possesses a coin. Start measuring it instead by the *gaps* — the specific places where what you hold and what you live have come apart. Find one gap this week. Just one. The prayer you delay, the tongue you don't guard, the wealth you don't purify, the parent you don't call. And close it. Not all of them. One. Because *ṣaddaqa bihi* is not a feeling you summon; it is a seam you sew shut, one stitch at a time, until the carried truth and the lived truth are one cloth.

### Theme Two: Why Truthfulness Is a Shield You Build for Yourself

Now I want to take you to the final word, and to a question hiding inside it.

The verse ends by naming the reward of the truthful with the word **al-muttaqūn**. We translate it loosely as "the God-conscious," "the pious," "the righteous." But sit with what it actually *is*.

The root is **waqaya** — to *shield*, to *protect*, to *guard against harm*. From it comes *taqwā*. And *al-muttaqī* — the form the verse uses — is built on a pattern that Arabic uses for **reflexive** action: a doing that turns back on the doer himself. *Ittaqā* — to *make a shield for oneself*. To take protection. To guard *one's own self*.

So *al-muttaqūn* are, at the root, **those who have built themselves a shield.**

[PAUSE]

Now here is the question the verse plants and does not answer out loud, leaving it for you to find: *a shield against what? And built from what?*

Let me start with what we think a shield is. We imagine taqwā as a wall *between* us and Allah's anger — a barrier we erect against punishment, out of fear. And there is truth in that. But look at *where the verse places this word.* It does not arrive after a description of fear. It arrives after a description of **ṣidq** — of bringing truth and affirming it. The verse is telling you what the shield is *made of*.

**The shield is made of truthfulness itself.**

Let me show you why this is not a wordplay but the deepest realism.

What is it that actually destroys a human being from the inside? It is not, in the end, external attack. It is the *internal* split — the gap we just spoke of. The person who carries one truth and lives another is *unprotected*, because he can be reached at the seam. Fear reaches him there: "if you live the truth you'll lose your wealth, your standing, your comfort" — and because his truth was never *affirmed*, never structural, the fear walks right in through the gap and he abandons what he claimed to hold. Desire reaches him there. Doubt reaches him there. Every assault finds the seam between his carried truth and his lived truth and pries it open.

But the one who has *ṣaddaqa bihi* — who has fused the carried and the lived into one whole — **has no seam.** There is no gap for fear to enter. He has, by becoming true, *built himself a shield.* Not a wall outside him. A wholeness inside him. His truthfulness is his protection.

This is why the verse can move, in a single breath, from *ṣidq* to *taqwā* — from truthfulness to self-protection — as if they were cause and effect. **Because they are.** The reflexive shape of *al-muttaqūn* — the self guarding the self — is the *consequence* of *ṣaddaqa bihi*. You guard yourself by being true. There is no other armor. Every other protection — wealth, alliance, status, the lie you tell to stay safe — is a wall with a door in it, and fear always has the key. Only truthfulness has no door.

[PAUSE]

And now lift your eyes to the whole architecture, and feel it lock into place.

Go back to verse 32: the man who **lies** against Allah and **denies** the truth. What is he, in the language we now have? He is a man who has made himself a *gap* and called it a fortress. His lie is his exposure. He thinks he is protected — he is established, he is custodian, he is safe — but he is the most unprotected soul alive, because he is *nothing but* seam. He denied the truth to stay safe, and in denying it he tore himself open. Verse 32 ends in Hell because the unsealed soul has nowhere to hide when reality finally arrives.

And verse 33: the one who brought the truth and affirmed it — *those, they are the muttaqūn.* The ones who, by being true, have nothing left in them to tear. And two verses on, in verse 34, comes their reward: *"They will have whatever they wish with their Lord — that is the recompense of the doers of good."* Everything they could want. Because the soul with no seam has nothing to fear and nothing to lose. It walks into the presence of its Lord *whole*.

[PAUSE]

**So what changes tomorrow?** This: the next time you are tempted to protect yourself with a small untruth — the convenient excuse, the curated image, the silence that lets a falsehood stand, the version of yourself you perform — recognize what you are actually doing. You are not building a wall. You are *cutting a seam* into your own armor. You are making yourself reachable. Real protection — *taqwā* — runs in the opposite direction from what fear tells you. Fear says: bend the truth and you'll be safe. The verse says: the truth, *carried and answered*, **is** your safety. To be true is to be shielded. To lie, even to protect yourself, is to disarm.

The muttaqī is not the one who fears the most. He is the one who, by being true, has the least left to fear.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now let me show you the whole structure, because we've earned the right to see it.

Step back and look at verses 32 and 33 side by side. They are not two separate statements. They are a single mirror, built word against word.

**Verse 32:** *"Who is more unjust than one who **lies** (*kadhaba*) against Allah and **denies** (*kadhdhaba*) the truth (*bi-ṣ-ṣidq*) when it came to him? Is there not in Hell a dwelling for the disbelievers?"*

**Verse 33:** *"And the one who **brought** the truth (*bi-ṣ-ṣidq*) and **affirmed** (*ṣaddaqa*) it — those, they are the muttaqūn."*

Look at the matching:

- Verse 32 has *kadhdhaba* — "denied as false." Verse 33 has *ṣaddaqa* — "affirmed as true." The **same grammatical form**, built from opposite roots: the root of lying against the root of truth. Perfect, deliberate antithesis.
- Both verses contain the exact phrase **bi-ṣ-ṣidq** — "the truth." In 32, the truth *came to him* and he denied it. In 33, the truth *was brought* and it was affirmed. The same truth, knocking on two different doors.
- Verse 32 ends in **Hell** — the dwelling of the disbelievers. Verse 33 flows into **verse 34** — "whatever they wish with their Lord."

Two human beings stand before the identical truth. One denies it to protect himself and is torn open into Hell. One affirms it and becomes the muttaqī, the shielded one, who receives everything. **The truth did not change. The soul's answer to it changed everything.**

That is the proof, written into the very shape of the passage. You did not need me to assert that truthfulness is the hinge of taqwā. The Qur'ān built it into the mirror.

### Bringing It Together

Our linguistic discoveries and our themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

The **two verbs from one root** were the grammar of *integrity* — truth carried becoming truth lived, the seam sewn shut.

The **singular swelling to plural** was the grammar of *the open door* — a portrait precise enough to humble you, a category wide enough to receive you.

The **pronoun *humu*** was the grammar of *the shield* — taqwā fenced off for the truthful alone, because only truthfulness is armor without a door.

And underneath all of it, one reality: **ṣidq is the root of taqwā.** You are protected to exactly the degree that you are true. The God-conscious are not the most fearful, the most titled, or the most established. They are the ones in whom the truth, once brought, was answered — until there was no gap left in them for the world to reach.

### Questions to Carry

1. Where, specifically, in my life am I a *carrier* of truth I have not *affirmed* — where what I hold and what I live have quietly come apart?
2. What "protection" am I clinging to that is actually a seam — a small untruth I keep, believing it keeps me safe, when it is the very place I am most exposed?
3. The truth was *brought* to me — through the Prophet ﷺ, down a chain of those who carried and answered, all the way to this moment. Have I joined the relay, or have I only received the parcel and left it unopened?
4. If taqwā cannot be inherited from lineage or title, and can only be built from truthfulness — what is *one* true act I have been avoiding because a lie felt safer?
5. Do I fear Allah the way the exposed soul fears — bracing against punishment — or am I learning the taqwā of the whole soul, that has, by being true, the least left to fear?

### The One-Sentence Distillation

**Truth is not complete until it is both carried into the world and answered with the whole self — and the soul in whom carrying and answering become one has, by that very wholeness, built itself the only shield that has no door for fear to enter: this, and this alone, is taqwā.**

### The Closing Invitation

You came to this verse perhaps thinking taqwā was a matter of how much you do. Leave it knowing taqwā is a matter of how *true* you are. Not a wall you build against Allah out of fear, but a wholeness you build before Allah out of *ṣidq* — closing, one seam at a time, the gap between the truth you carry and the life you live, until you have nothing left in you for the world to pry apart, and you can stand before your Lord *whole*. The door the verse closes on the pretenders is the same door it holds open for you. You cannot inherit your way through it. But you can be true your way through it. Begin with one seam.

### A Du'ā From These Words

> O Allah, You brought us the truth through Your Messenger ﷺ — let us not merely carry it, but answer it with our whole selves, until what we hold and what we live become one. Make us of *alladhī ṣaddaqa bihi* — those who said yes to the truth and meant it with everything. And by that truthfulness, make us of *al-muttaqūn* — shielded not by our fear but by our wholeness, until we stand before You with no seam left to tear. Āmīn.
