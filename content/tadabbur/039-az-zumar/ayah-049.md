# The Word That Failed the Test

## INTRODUCTION

Let me begin with the words themselves. Listen to them first in Arabic, and let the rhythm settle before we know what it means:

> **فَإِذَا مَسَّ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ ضُرٌّ دَعَانَا ثُمَّ إِذَا خَوَّلْنَـٰهُ نِعْمَةً مِّنَّا قَالَ إِنَّمَآ أُوتِيتُهُۥ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍۭ ۚ بَلْ هِىَ فِتْنَةٌ وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ**

*Fa-idhā massa-l-insāna ḍurrun daʿānā, thumma idhā khawwalnāhu niʿmatan minnā qāla innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm. Bal hiya fitnatun, wa-lākinna aktharahum lā yaʿlamūn.*

"So when adversity touches man, he calls upon Us; then when We bestow on him a favor from Us, he says, *'I have only been given it because of knowledge.'* Rather, it is a trial — but most of them do not know."

I want you to imagine the world this verse first dropped into.

Mecca. A trading city built on the spine of a desert. A place where fortune was visible, countable, worn on the body. A man returns from Syria with his caravan intact, his goods sold, his profit fat in his saddlebags. He walks through the marketplace and people part for him. And if you asked him — *how did this come to you?* — he would not hesitate. He would tell you about his eye for a deal, his timing, his nerve, the routes he knew that others didn't. **He earned it.** Every coin had his name on it before it was ever minted.

Now imagine that same man three months earlier, lost in a sandstorm, his camels collapsing, water gone, the horizon erased. In that moment — what does he say? Does he recite his trading credentials to the dunes? No. In that moment a different voice comes out of him. A voice he did not know he had. A voice that cries out — *to Someone.*

This surah, Az-Zumar, has one beating heart: **al-dīn al-khāliṣ** — that devotion, pure devotion, belongs to Allah alone. And for pages it has been confronting the obvious idolatry, the carved stones, the invented partners. But here, in this single verse, it turns to catch something far more intimate. Something that lives inside the believer too. Not the worship of an idol on a shelf — **the worship of the self in the mirror.**

Here is the hook, and I'll only tease it, because you have to earn the rest. This verse describes a man who, in pain, instinctively knows the truth — that he is dependent, that there is a Lord, that he must call out. And then, the moment the pain lifts and the gift arrives, he **forgets the very truth his own suffering had just taught him.** [PAUSE]

The Quran is about to show us that there is a sentence we say — many of us, most of us — that we think is a statement of competence. And Allah is going to tell us it is something else entirely. It is a *test.* And the saying of it is how we fail it.

Let's go slowly. Because the precision here is almost frightening.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Verb That Barely Happens — مَسَّ

Before we touch the word, let me give you a small picture from ordinary life.

There is a difference between being *struck* and being *brushed.* If someone strikes you — slams into you — your reaction is understandable; anyone would cry out. But imagine you are walking and the lightest thing brushes against your arm. A leaf. A passing sleeve. And you flinch, you gasp, you cry out as though you'd been stabbed. That reaction tells us something — not about the leaf, but about **how thin your composure was to begin with.** The size of the response, measured against the smallness of the cause, exposes you.

Hold that thought.

Now look at the verb Allah chose for how adversity comes to this man:

> **مَسَّ** — *massa.*

The root is *m-s-s.* And in the Arabic of the Quran, this is not the verb of being crushed, or struck, or overwhelmed. **This is the verb of touch.** Light contact. The faintest meeting of one surface with another. It is the same root used elsewhere when the Quran speaks of a *touch* of fire, a *touch* of Satan's whisper, the *touch* between two people. It is gentle. It is glancing. Notice even the sound of it — *massa* — that soft, drawn-out hiss, like something grazing past and gone.

Let's sit with this. Allah did not say "when *catastrophe crushes* man." He did not say "when *ruin overwhelms* him." He said: **when adversity merely *touches* him.** *Massa-l-insāna ḍurr.*

And what is the man's response to this light touch?

> **دَعَانَا** — *daʿānā* — "he calls upon **Us.**"

He cries out. To his Lord. Immediately, fully, with his whole self.

Notice something. The mismatch is the entire point. The cause is small — a touch — and the response is total: he calls upon the Lord of the heavens and the earth. **That gap between the lightness of the affliction and the totality of the cry is Allah holding up a mirror to human nature.** It says: look how close to the surface your need is. Look how thin the membrane is between your comfort and your desperation. It takes so little — just a *touch* — and the whole truth of your dependence comes pouring out of you.

Here's what's remarkable. In that moment of crying out, **the man is theologically correct.** He has, without a lecture, without a teacher, arrived at *tawḥīd.* He knows there is a Lord. He knows he is not self-sufficient. He knows the direction to turn. The pain did not lie to him. The pain told him the truth about himself.

And the Quran will now show us that the tragedy of this human being is not that he doesn't know the truth.

**It is that he abandons the truth the moment it stops hurting.** [PAUSE]

So this single verb, *massa*, has already done its work. It tells us: your dependence on Allah is not a remote fact you reach by hard reasoning. It is *right there*, a touch away, ready to surface at the slightest pressure. The question the rest of the verse asks is — **what happens to that knowledge when the pressure lifts?**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Gift You Were Never Given to Keep — خَوَّلْنَا

Now the scene turns. The adversity passes. And Allah describes what comes next with a verb so precise that most translations cannot carry it.

First, let me build the concept in plain terms.

There is a difference between being *given* something and being made its *steward.* If I hand you a gift — it's yours. Do what you like; it has left my hands forever. But if I make you the manager of my estate, the keeper of my wealth, the one entrusted to oversee my property — I have placed something *vast* in your hands, far more than a gift, you may live off it and direct it and enjoy it. **But it was never yours.** You are answerable for it. And one day there is a reckoning of accounts.

Now hold that distinction up to the verb Allah uses.

He does *not* say *aʿṭaynāhu* — "We gave him" — the plain, ordinary verb for giving. He chose, instead:

> **خَوَّلْنَـٰهُ** — *khawwalnāhu.*

The root is *kh-w-l.* And the classical lexicons are remarkably consistent about its texture. In *Lisān al-ʿArab*, the *khawal* is connected to that which is placed under a person's care and tending — wealth, servants, livestock, the things one is entrusted to *oversee.* Imam al-Rāghib, in his *Mufradāt*, defines *al-takhwīl* with beautiful exactness: it is *iʿṭāʾ al-khawal* — the giving of that which a person is responsible **to tend and look after.** Not a gift flung from the hand and forgotten. A trust placed into the hand, **to be managed.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the precision of this?

When the man was in pain, Allah used a verb of light touch — *massa* — to show how easily affliction reaches him. And now, when the favor arrives, Allah deliberately avoids the verb of casual giving and selects the verb of **stewardship.** *Khawwalnāhu niʿmatan minnā* — "We placed a favor *into his trust*, from Us."

Stay with me, because here is the quiet devastation of it. **The very grammar of the gift already contains the answer to the man's mistake — before he even makes it.** Allah has told us, in the word He chose to describe the blessing, that it was never the man's possession. It was a *trust.* It came with His name on it: *minnā* — "from Us." Look how the verse keeps stamping the source onto the gift: *We* bestowed, a favor, *from Us.* Twice in one breath Allah signs the gift.

And then watch what the man does with a gift that arrives signed, sealed, and stamped *from Us.*

He tears off the label.

### Element Three: The Sentence That Fails the Test — أُوتِيتُهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ

Here is the center of the verse. The man speaks. And what he says is the most psychologically exact sentence in the human heart:

> **قَالَ إِنَّمَآ أُوتِيتُهُ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ** — *qāla innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm.*

"He says: I have *only* been given it because of knowledge."

I need you to see two pieces of grammar here, because together they expose the lie at the heart of the boast.

**First: the word أُوتِيتُهُ — *ūtītuhu.*** Look closely at its form. The root is *a-t-y*, "to give," and Allah casts it here in the **passive voice.** Not *akhadhtuhu* — "I *took* it." Not *kasabtuhu* — "I *earned* it." But *ūtītuhu* — "**I was given** it." The man's own grammar betrays him. His own tongue confesses that the action came *from outside him* — he is the object, not the subject; the receiver, not the doer. The blessing was *done to him.*

**And then, in the same breath, with the next two words, he claims authorship of it.** *ʿalā ʿilm* — "**on the basis of knowledge.**" *My* knowledge. *My* merit. *My* expertise. He has just admitted, in the structure of his sentence, that he was a passive recipient — and he uses that very admission to launch a claim that he was the active cause. [PAUSE]

Do you see the contradiction sitting inside one short sentence? *I was given it — because of what I know.* The first half points away from himself. The second half drags everything back to himself. **He cannot even construct the boast without smuggling in the truth he is denying.**

**Second: the word عِلْمٍ — *ʿilm* — "knowledge," left deliberately indefinite.** No article. No specification. And here the classical commentators open the word like a fan, and rather than forcing us to choose between their readings, let me show you how every reading lands in the same place.

- Qatāda said it means: *on knowledge I have of the ways of acquisition* — "I know how wealth is made; I have the eye, the method, the expertise."
- It is also reported from Qatāda: *on goodness that is in me* — "this came to me because of the merit I carry."
- Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī read it differently: *by knowledge that Allah taught me* — even framing God as the teacher, but only so that *I* end up the qualified graduate.
- And another reading, recorded by al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī: *on knowledge from Allah that I am worthy of it* — "God knows I deserve this; that is why He gave it."

Now look what happens when you lay all four readings side by side. Whether the knowledge is *my expertise*, or *my inner merit*, or *what God taught me*, or *God's recognition of my worth* — **every single path bends back to the same destination: *I deserved this.*** The man is not confused about the source in a hundred different ways. He is certain about his own worthiness in a hundred different ways. The indefiniteness of *ʿilm* is not vagueness — it is Allah showing us that **self-attribution will reach for any vocabulary it can find.** It will even use the language of religion. It will even say "God taught me" — and still make the sentence end in a mirror.

And now hear the thunderclap of Allah's response. One word:

> **بَلْ** — *bal* — "**Nay.**" "Rather."

In Arabic, *bal* is the particle of correction — the word that stops a sentence dead and overturns it. The man has just delivered his confident verdict on his own life. And Allah does not argue with the details. He simply lifts the whole frame and replaces it:

> **بَلْ هِىَ فِتْنَةٌ** — *bal hiya fitnah* — "Rather, **it is a trial.**"

The thing you called a *reward* — it is a *test.*

The thing you read as a *certificate of your merit* — it is an *examination of your gratitude.*

The blessing you held up as *proof that you have arrived* — is the very *question you are being asked.* [PAUSE]

And notice the pronoun *hiya* — "**it.**" To what does "it" refer? The commentators differ beautifully, and again it is a composite, not a contradiction. Al-Jalālayn says *hiya* refers to **the statement** — *the very saying of it* is the trial. Al-Baghawī says *hiya* refers to **the favor itself** — *the blessing* is the trial, a slow drawing-out, an *istidrāj.* And both are true at once. **The gift is the test. And his response to the gift is how the test is graded.** The exam paper and the answer are folded into a single word, *hiya.*

#### The Bridge

Let me gather what these three words have shown us before we ask what they mean for our lives.

*Massa* — adversity only **touched** him, and the truth of his dependence came pouring out.

*Khawwalnāhu* — the favor was placed in his hands as a **trust**, stamped *from Us*, never his to own.

*Ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm* — yet his own tongue confessed he was **given** it, even as he claimed he **earned** it — and that confused, self-crediting sentence is the very thing being tested.

Three words. A complete diagnosis of the human heart. Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the way we actually live.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Reflex That Credits Itself

I want to start where you already live.

There is a pattern psychologists have documented so consistently they gave it a name. They call it **self-serving attribution bias.** And it works like this: when something *good* happens to us, we explain it by pointing inward — *I was smart, I worked hard, I deserved it.* When something *bad* happens to us, we explain it by pointing outward — *the market crashed, the timing was wrong, they were unfair to me, I was unlucky.*

Watch it in yourself this week and you will see it everywhere. You pass the exam: *I studied well.* You fail it: *the questions were unfair.* The deal closes: *I negotiated brilliantly.* The deal collapses: *the economy is brutal right now.* **Success has one author — me. Failure has a thousand authors — everyone and everything else.**

Now hold that modern, clinical finding up against a verse revealed fourteen centuries ago in a desert city. Because look at what Allah described, with perfect symmetry, long before anyone ran a study:

When *ḍurr* — harm — touches him: *daʿānā* — he calls upon **Us.** He points *outward and upward.*

When *niʿmah* — favor — reaches him: *ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm* — he credits **his own knowledge.** He points *inward.*

[PAUSE]

This is the self-serving bias, named and exposed in the Book of Allah. But the Quran does something the psychology paper cannot do. The psychology paper says *this is a cognitive distortion.* The Quran says something deeper and more uncomfortable. It says: **this is a spiritual betrayal.** Because notice *which* truth the man abandons.

In his pain, he had it right. *Daʿānā* — he knew the Lord, he knew his need, he knew the direction. His suffering was a teacher, and he was, for a moment, a perfect student. And then the moment the lesson ended — the moment the pain lifted — he didn't just *forget* the truth. He *inverted* it. He took the very blessing that should have deepened his gratitude and turned it into evidence *against* the One who gave it. **The gift became the argument for his own self-sufficiency.**

There is a name for this exact man elsewhere in the Quran, and it should make us shudder. Turn to Surah Al-Qaṣaṣ, the story of Qārūn — the richest man among the people of Mūsā. When they urged him toward gratitude and generosity, what did he say?

> **إِنَّمَآ أُوتِيتُهُۥ عَلَىٰ عِلْمٍ عِندِىٓ** — "I was only given it because of knowledge **I possess.**" (28:78)

The *same words.* Almost letter for letter. The boast of Qārūn and the boast of the man in our verse are **the identical sentence.** And we know how Qārūn's story ends: the earth swallowed him and his house, and not a faction could help him, nor could he defend himself.

Here is what should stop us cold. We read the story of Qārūn and we think it is about a uniquely arrogant, uniquely wealthy tyrant from ancient history — someone we could never be. And then Az-Zumar takes Qārūn's signature sentence and puts it not in the mouth of a tyrant but in the mouth of **al-insān** — "the human being." Generic. Ordinary. Every one of us. The verse is telling us: *Qārūn's sentence is your reflex.* The boast you read about as a monstrous exception is, in fact, the default setting of the unexamined heart.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Here is the practical edge, and it is sharp. The next time something goes *right* for you — a success, a recovery, an unexpected open door — catch the first sentence that forms in your mind. Just observe it. Is the subject of that sentence *I* — "I earned, I knew, I deserved"? Or is the subject of that sentence *Allah* — "He gave, He opened, He carried me here"? You will be astonished how automatically the *I* arrives first. That automatic *I* **is** the *ʿalā ʿilm* reflex. And the cure is not to deny that you worked — the man in the verse *did* have knowledge, Qārūn *did* have skill. The cure is to relocate the source: *He gave me the knowledge. He gave me the strength to use it. He opened the door my knowledge walked through.* Take *ūtītuhu* — "I was given" — the truth your own grammar already confesses — and let it lead the sentence, instead of burying it under your boast.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: When the Gift Is the Test

Now I want to take you somewhere more unsettling. Because there is a second theme here, and it overturns something most of us believe in our bones.

Most of us carry, without ever examining it, a quiet equation: **blessing equals approval.** If life is good to me — if I am healthy, wealthy, comfortable, rising — then surely this means God is pleased with me. The good things are a *signal.* They are heaven's thumbs-up. And conversely, if life is hard, perhaps God is displeased.

Listen again to what Allah says about the favor the man received:

> **بَلْ هِىَ فِتْنَةٌ** — "Rather, it is a **trial.**"

Not a reward. Not a verdict. Not a certificate of divine approval. **A test.**

The root of *fitnah*, *f-t-n*, originally describes the smelting of gold — placing the ore in the fire to see what is genuine and what is dross. *Fitnah* is the fire that reveals what you are actually made of. And here Allah is telling us something that should rearrange our entire reading of our own comfortable lives: **the blessing in your hands right now is the fire. You are, at this very moment, in the assay. The good thing is not the result of the test — it *is* the test.**

[PAUSE]

Let this sink in, because it cuts against everything our success-worshipping age tells us. We assume the test is the hardship — the illness, the loss, the struggle. And of course hardship *is* a test; the Quran says so plainly elsewhere. But we rarely suspect the blessing. We rarely look at our health, our income, our influence, our talents and think: *this is an examination I am sitting right now, and I do not even feel the exam hall around me.*

That is why the commentators reached for a particular word here: **istidrāj.** Al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar, al-Jalālayn all name it. *Istidrāj* — to be drawn, step by step, gradually, gently, by way of blessings, *toward* something. The terrifying mercy of it is that it doesn't feel like a trial at all. It feels like winning. Each gift confirms the story you tell yourself: *I deserve this, I am favored, I have it figured out.* And every confirmation pulls you one step further from the One who is, all the while, giving you the very rope.

And now the final words of the verse land with their full weight:

> **وَلَـٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ** — "but **most of them do not know.**"

Notice — and read this absence carefully — that the verse opened with a *single* man. *Al-insān.* He. Him. One person. And it closes with a *crowd.* **Aktharahum** — "most of *them.*" The individual has multiplied into the many. Why the shift? Because Allah is telling us this is not one strange man's flaw. **It is the condition of the majority.** Most people walk through their blessings completely unaware that they are walking through a fire. They mistake the assay for an award ceremony.

And look at the exquisite cruelty of the man's specific failure. He boasted *ʿalā ʿilm* — "on the basis of **knowledge.**" Knowledge was his entire claim. And Allah's verdict ends: *aktharahum lā **yaʿlamūn*** — most of them **do not know.** The very faculty he bragged about — knowing — is precisely the faculty he lacks where it matters most. He claimed to *know* why he was given his wealth, and he did not *know* the one thing that would have saved him: **that the wealth was a test he was failing by the very act of claiming to understand it.** [PAUSE] The man who said "I know" is the headline example of "they do not know."

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It asks you to do something with your blessings you have probably never done. Pick one — your health, your income, your home, your children, a talent that comes easily to you. And instead of asking "*do I deserve this?*" — which is the man's question, the losing question — ask: "***What is this testing in me? What is the fire trying to reveal?***" Your wealth is testing your generosity. Your health is testing what you do with your strength. Your knowledge is testing your humility. Your free time is testing your remembrance. Every *niʿmah* is a question with your name on it. The grateful person is simply the one who has noticed they are being asked.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture

Now that we have done the work, step back and see how perfectly this verse is built. It is a mirror, hinged in the middle, and the two halves reflect and reverse each other:

**The first half — adversity:**
Harm *touches* him (passive — it happens *to* him) → he *calls upon Us* (he points *away from himself, toward Allah*).

**The second half — favor:**
We *place a trust* in his hands (active — Allah is the giver) → he *credits his own knowledge* (he points *toward himself, away from Allah*).

Look at the reversal. In *adversity*, the man is genuinely passive — harm merely touches him — yet he *correctly* turns outward to Allah. In *blessing*, Allah is genuinely the active giver — *We* bestowed — yet the man *falsely* turns inward to himself. **He gets the grammar of his life exactly backwards.** When he is the helpless object, he speaks the truth and cries to his Lord. When his Lord is the generous Author, he claims to be the author himself. The verse is shaped like the very inversion it describes. **The structure *is* the lesson.**

### Bringing It Together

Our linguistic journey and our two themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The words showed us: *massa* (how lightly affliction reveals our dependence), *khawwalnā* (how the gift was always a trust, never a possession), and *ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm* (how our own tongue confesses we were given even as we claim we earned).

The themes showed us: the *reflex* that credits the self and blames the world, and the *blindness* that mistakes the test for the trophy.

And they meet in one place. The man's tragedy is not a lack of intelligence — he had *ʿilm*, knowledge, by his own account. His tragedy is a lack of *attribution.* He knew *how* to get the gift. He did not know *Who* gave it. And in that single missing word — *Who* — the entire test was lost.

### Questions to Carry

Sit with these. They are not rhetorical. Let them stay open.

1. When something went *right* for me this week — what was the very first sentence in my mind? Who was its subject: *I*, or *Allah*?

2. Is there a blessing in my life so constant, so familiar, that I have stopped seeing it as a gift at all — and started seeing it as simply *mine*?

3. I am quick to call upon Allah when something *touches* me with pain. Am I as quick to call upon Him — to thank Him — when something touches me with mercy?

4. Which of my blessings have I been reading as a *reward*, that Allah may be offering me as a *test*? And what, specifically, is that blessing testing in me?

5. If the words "*innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilm*" — "I was only given it because of my knowledge" — are the words that condemned Qārūn, how often do I say a quieter, more respectable version of the very same sentence?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The same self that cries "I need You!" when pain only brushes it will claim "I earned this!" when grace floods in — and that very claim is the test it is failing.**

### The Closing Invitation

So here is what this verse invites — not demands, invites.

It invites you to do something almost no one does: to be as honest in your blessing as you are in your pain. You already know how to be sincere when you are suffering — the verse says so; the cry comes out of you on its own, *daʿānā.* The invitation is to carry that same sincerity *across the threshold* — to let the gratitude that should follow be as instinctive, as whole-hearted, as undivided as the desperation that came before.

Because the man in this verse was not destroyed by his wealth. He was destroyed by a *sentence.* And you and I are speaking sentences about our lives all day long. The invitation is simply this: before you say "I earned it," pause — and remember the word your own grammar already knows. *Ūtītuhu.* **I was given it.**

### A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, You who placed every blessing in our hands as a trust and signed each one *from You* — do not let the gift make us forget the Giver. When ease touches us, let us turn to You as quickly as we turn to You in pain. And of all that You have entrusted to us, make us among those who *know* — that it is from You, that it is a test, and that the only safe answer to it is gratitude. Āmīn.
