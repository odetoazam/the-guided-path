# What Surfaces and What Surrounds

## Introduction

Let me give you the words first, and then let's slow everything down.

> وَبَدَا لَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا كَسَبُوا وَحَاقَ بِهِم مَّا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

*wa badā lahum sayyiʾātu mā kasabū, wa ḥāqa bihim mā kānū bihi yastahziʾūn*

**"And the evils of what they earned will become apparent to them, and they will be enveloped by the very thing they used to mock."**

Ten words. That's the whole ayah. And in those ten words there are two verbs that I want us to spend most of our time inside — because Allah did not choose them casually, and once you see what they're doing, you will not be able to un-see it.

But before we open them, we have to build the room this ayah lives in.

### The world this verse responds to

Surah Az-Zumar is named after the *zumar* — the **groups, the crowds**. The whole surah is moving toward one of the most cinematic scenes in the entire Quran: at the very end, humanity is sorted. The deniers are driven toward the Fire *in groups* (39:71). The conscious, the God-aware, are led toward the Garden *in groups* (39:73). Everything in this surah is flowing toward that moment of separation, when the great undifferentiated mass of humanity finally splits along the line that was always invisibly running through it.

And just before our ayah, in 39:47, Allah paints the wrongdoer on that Day. He says that if they owned everything on the earth — and the same again on top of it — they would spend all of it, *all of it*, just to ransom themselves out of the punishment. And then comes a phrase that should make us sit up: **"and there will appear to them, from Allah, what they had never reckoned with."** *Wa badā lahum mina-llāhi mā lam yakūnū yaḥtasibūn.* Something they never counted on. Something that was never on their ledger. Something they did not see coming.

So our ayah, 39:48, is the answer to a question the previous verse plants in your chest. What is this thing that appears? What is it that they never reckoned with?

Here is the answer.

### The hook

Hold onto this and don't let it go: Allah uses *two different verbs* for two different things in this ayah. One thing **appears** to them. Another thing **surrounds** them. One verb is the language of something quietly rising into view. The other is the language of something closing in from every direction.

Why two verbs? Why not one? What is the difference between a thing that *surfaces* and a thing that *encircles*?

And here is the part that should unsettle us before we even begin: **both of these things already belong to them right now.** Neither is new. Neither is imported from outside. The ayah is not describing something that happens *to* them. It is describing the unveiling of something that was always already *theirs*.

## Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

### Element One: The verb *badā* — what was always there, finally surfacing

I want to teach you something about how language works before we touch the Arabic at all.

In English, we have two completely different families of words for things becoming visible. One family is about **creation** — *to make, to produce, to generate.* The thing didn't exist, and now it does. The other family is about **revelation** — *to surface, to emerge, to come to light, to dawn on someone.* The thing already existed; it was simply hidden, and now it's seen.

These are not the same. If I tell you "the truth was *created*," that's a frightening sentence — it suggests truth is manufactured. But if I tell you "the truth *came to light*," you understand instantly: the truth was always the truth. It was just covered. Something was sitting on top of it. And then the covering came off.

Hold that distinction.

The verb Allah uses here is **بَدَا — *badā*.** Its root is *b-d-w*. And this is the root that gives Arabic the word *bādiya* — the open desert, the exposed countryside, the place with nowhere to hide. It's the root behind *badawī* — the bedouin, the one who lives out in the open. To *badā* is **for something to emerge into the open, to become apparent, to surface into view.**

Notice what it is *not*. It is not *khalaqa* — to create. It is not *jaʿala* — to make. Allah does not say the evils were *produced* on that Day. He says they **بَدَا — surfaced.**

Let's sit with this.

The full phrase is **بَدَا لَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا كَسَبُوا** — *"there surfaced, for them, the evils of what they earned."* And look at that second verb buried inside it: **كَسَبُوا — *kasabū* — they earned.** The root *k-s-b* is the root of *acquisition, of earning a wage, of going out and bringing something home.* It is the most *owned* verb imaginable. You don't passively receive what you *kasab* — you work for it, you accumulate it, you bring it back and it becomes yours.

So put the two verbs side by side. The evils were **earned** — they were acquired, accumulated, brought home over a lifetime. And now they **surface** — they rise into view.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what this is saying? **The evil was never absent.** It was earned. It was already in their account. It was already theirs, the whole time, sitting in the ledger. What changes on that Day is not the *existence* of the evil. What changes is the *visibility* of it.

And here is the most precise word in the phrase, the one easy to read past: **لَهُمْ — *lahum* — to them.** "There surfaced **to them**." Not "to Allah." Allah saw it all along. Not "to the angels." Not "to the world." It surfaced *to them* — to the very people who earned it. Which means the one party in the entire universe who *could not see* these deeds for what they were... was the people who committed them.

Stay with that, because it is devastating. They earned the evils. They carried them every day. They lived inside them. And they were the last ones to see them clearly. The deeds were never hidden from Allah. They were hidden **from the doer.** By the doer. The covering that *badā* removes was not a covering Allah placed over the truth — it was the covering *we* place over our own deeds, the rationalization, the renaming, the "it wasn't that bad," the "everyone does it," the "I had no choice."

On that Day, *badā*. The covering slides off. And for the first time, a person sees their own life with their own eyes — *as it actually was.*

What this single word reveals: **the Day of Judgment is not primarily the day you are shown something new. It is the day you are finally shown yourself.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The verb *ḥāqa* — the thing that closes in from every side

Now the second verb. And the contrast is the whole point, so keep *badā* warm in your mind.

Again, let me teach the concept before the word. Think about the difference between something *appearing in front of you* and something *surrounding you*. If a wall appears in front of me, I can turn. I can go around it. There is still an outside. But if a wall *encircles* me — if it closes in from every direction at once — there is no turning, no around, no outside. The geometry itself has trapped me. Appearing leaves you options. **Surrounding takes them all away.**

The verb is **حَاقَ — *ḥāqa*.** The root is *ḥ-y-q*, and listen to the sound of it — that opening *ḥ* (ح) is a deep, constricted, guttural breath from the bottom of the throat, the sound of a passage narrowing. *Ḥāqa* means **to encompass, to encircle, to close in around something until it is completely enveloped.** The lexicographers tie it to the idea of a calamity that *descends and surrounds* so that there is no escaping it from any side.

And here is a fact worth knowing: this exact construction — **وَحَاقَ بِهِم مَّا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ** — *"and there surrounded them what they used to mock"* — is a **recurring refrain across the Quran.** You find it almost word for word in Surah Al-Anʿām (6:10), in Surah Hūd (11:8), in Surah An-Naḥl (16:34), in Surah Ghāfir (40:83), in Surah Al-Jāthiyah (45:33). Allah returns to this exact sentence again and again. When something repeats in the Quran with this kind of fidelity, it is a law being stated — not an incident being reported.

So notice the architecture Allah has built into this one ayah. The evils *badā* — they **surface, they rise into view in front of them.** But the mockery *ḥāqa* — it **surrounds, it closes in around them.** One thing comes into view. The other thing comes into *contact* — total, three-hundred-and-sixty-degree contact.

Why the difference? Why do the deeds merely appear, but the mocked-thing encircle?

Here's what's remarkable. **You can look at something that appears in front of you. You cannot look at something that surrounds you — because you are inside it.** The deeds surface so they can be *seen* — that's the reckoning, the confrontation with the record. But the thing they mocked doesn't surface to be seen. It becomes the *environment.* It becomes the air. It becomes the walls. It is no longer an object of their vision — it is the medium they now exist within.

What is the thing they mocked? We'll come to it fully in a moment. But hold the geometry: the deeds rise up *in front* of them, and the consequence closes in *around* them. Confrontation, and then immersion.

The verb *ḥāqa* reveals: **some realities you will not face as an object across the room. You will face them as the room.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *yastahziʾūn* — the mockery that becomes the world

Now let's name the thing that surrounds them. **مَّا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ** — *"what they used to mock."*

The verb is **يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ — *yastahziʾūn*** — from the root *h-z-ʾ*, and it is in a specific, telling form. It is **Form X**, the *istafʿala* pattern — *istahzaʾa*. Let me teach you what Form X does, because it carries the meaning.

Form X in Arabic very often adds the sense of **seeking, demanding, or actively pursuing** something — turning a state into a deliberate hunt for it. *Ghafara* is to forgive; *istaghfara* (Form X) is to actively *seek* forgiveness. *ʿAlima* is to know; *istaʿlama* is to actively *seek* information. So *hazaʾa* is to mock — and **istahzaʾa, Form X, is to make a project of mockery.** To pursue it, to lean into it, to treat derision as a stance you actively take up and maintain.

And then Allah wraps it in **كَانُوا...يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ** — *kānū... yastahziʾūn* — the past continuous. *Kānū* ("they used to") plus the present-tense verb gives you **ongoing, habitual, repeated action stretched across time.** Not "they mocked once." **"They used to keep on mocking."** It was a habit. A reflex. A lifelong posture. Every time the warning came near, the reflex fired: *make it small, make it a joke, laugh it off.*

Now here is the thing I need you to feel.

**Mockery is, by its very nature, an act of distance.** To mock something is to place yourself *above* it and *outside* it. You look down at the thing. You hold it at arm's length and you say: *this is beneath me, this is absurd, this is not serious, this cannot touch me.* The entire psychological function of mockery is to create separation — to keep the mocked thing small, far away, and harmless.

And what does Allah say happens to that very thing?

**وَحَاقَ بِهِم.** It *surrounds* them.

[PAUSE]

Feel the precision of the reversal. They spent a lifetime pushing the warning **away** — keeping it at a distance, beneath them, outside. And the punishment is not that the thing comes *back.* The punishment is that the thing they spent their whole life keeping *outside* becomes the thing that is now *all around them, on every side, with no outside left.* The exact distance they worked so hard to maintain — collapsed to zero, and then inverted. You cannot mock what surrounds you. Mockery requires an outside to stand in. *Ḥāqa* abolishes the outside.

The very faculty they used — *we are above this, this is a joke, this is far from us* — is answered by a verb that means *there is no above, no outside, no far. You are inside it now.*

This is what the linguistic construction has shown us: two verbs, *badā* and *ḥāqa*, surfacing and surrounding, drawn from a lifetime of *kasb* (earning) and *istihzāʾ* (mocking) — the deed and the derision, both already owned, both simply waiting for the day the covering comes off and the distance collapses.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself, and about us.

## Part 2: The Thematic Depths

### Theme One: The deeds we cannot see — *badā* and the architecture of the blind spot

Let me start somewhere completely human, far from the Day of Judgment.

There is a well-known feature of how the mind works. Every one of us carries a *self-image* — an internal picture of the kind of person we are. *I'm a good person. I'm fair. I'm generous. I mean well.* And the mind works very hard, constantly, quietly, to protect that picture. So when we do something that doesn't fit it, we don't usually update the picture. We do something much easier: we **re-narrate the deed.** We rename it.

The harsh word becomes "I was just being honest." The thing we took becomes "I was owed it." The person we cut off becomes "they were toxic." The prayer we abandoned becomes "I'm working on my relationship with God in my own way." The hours we wasted become "I needed the rest." We don't lie to other people, mostly. We lie to **the one person we can never get away from** — and we do it so smoothly, so automatically, that we don't even feel it happening.

This is the covering. And here is the strange, frightening thing: it works. It genuinely works. The deed becomes invisible *to us* while remaining perfectly visible to everyone else — and to God. We are, of all people, the ones living closest to our deeds and seeing them least clearly.

Now bring back the ayah. **وَبَدَا لَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا كَسَبُوا.** The evils **surfaced** — to *them*. Allah did not say a new evil was created. He said the covering came off the one that was always there. The *sayyiʾāt* were always *sayyiʾāt.* The harsh word was always harsh. The thing taken was always taken. What the Day removes is not the deed — it's the *renaming.* It strips off every comfortable label we ever pasted over our own actions, and underneath, for the first time, we read the deed in its actual handwriting.

[PAUSE]

Sit with how merciful it would be — how unbearably merciful — to have some of that covering removed *now,* while there is still time to act on what we see. Because that is precisely what this ayah is for. It is not a news report about other people's Day. It is a mirror handed to *us,* today, that says: *the labels you've pasted over your own life — are any of them lies? The thing you keep calling "just how I am" — what is its real name? The deed you've decided not to look at directly — what is sitting underneath the label?*

There is a reason the Quran tells us about *their* unveiling in the third person — *lahum, bihim,* "to them, around them." It holds the scene at a slight distance so we can watch it. But the entire force of watching it is the question it forces back onto us: *am I doing now what they did then — earning in the open, and refusing to see?*

The practical shift this asks for is small and concrete and you can do it tonight. Take one thing you do that you've always explained away. Just one. And for sixty seconds, refuse the explanation. Don't argue with it, don't defend it — just look at the deed with the label peeled off, the way it will look on the Day it surfaces on its own. That sixty seconds of voluntary *badā* now is worth more than you can imagine, because it is the one form of unveiling that still leaves room to change the deed before it's sealed.

**The Day of Judgment removes the gap between who we think we are and who we actually are. The believer's whole work is to start closing that gap voluntarily, today, before it is closed for us.**

### Theme Two: Mockery as armor — *istihzāʾ* and the things we keep small so they can't reach us

Now the second theme, and again let's begin where you actually live.

Think about how we, today, handle things that frighten us or make demands on us that we don't want to meet. One of the most common defenses — maybe the defining reflex of our entire cultural moment — is **irony.** Is **mockery.** Is the reflexive smirk.

When something is too serious, too demanding, too convicting, the easiest thing in the world is to make it a joke. To meet it with a smirk instead of a thought. Death, judgment, the soul, God, the warning that this life is going somewhere and we are accountable for it — these are heavy. They make demands. And so a thousand small reflexes rise up to keep them light: *that's a bit much. People who take that seriously are intense. It's all a bit medieval. Let's not be weird about it.*

Notice what mockery is actually *doing* there. It is **defensive armor.** It is not an intellectual position — it is an *emotional maneuver.* You cannot be moved by something you are laughing at. The laugh creates exactly enough distance that the thing can't land. The smirk is a force-field. And the deepest function of *istahzaʾa* — that Form X, the *active, sought, made-into-a-project* mockery — is to keep the serious thing permanently at arm's length so that it never has to be dealt with.

This is why the Quran treats mockery of the truth as so grave. It is not that God's feelings are hurt by a joke. It is that **mockery is the one posture from which a person literally cannot receive guidance.** Every other state can turn — the angry can be softened, the ignorant can be taught, the sinful can repent. But the mocker has built a posture whose entire purpose is to make sure nothing gets in. They have armored themselves *against the cure.*

And now feel the justice in the verb. **وَحَاقَ بِهِم.** The armor was distance. So the answer is the abolition of distance. The thing they held far away — *closes in.* The thing they kept small — becomes the largest fact in their existence, the very air. They spent a lifetime making sure the warning could never *touch* them — and the warning becomes the thing that *envelops* them on every side. The boomerang doesn't just come back; it becomes the sky.

[PAUSE]

There is something almost unbearably exact about this. The mockery was a way of saying *this is not real, this is not serious, this cannot reach me.* And the punishment is simply **reality calling the bluff** — turning out to be more real, more serious, and closer than anything they ever called real in their lives. *Ḥāqa.* It surrounds.

So the question this forces on us is sharp and personal: **What am I keeping at a distance with a smirk?** What demand on my life have I defused by deciding, in advance, that it isn't serious? Where have I used irony — that most sophisticated, most flattering, most modern of defenses — as a way to never have to change?

Because here is the thing about armor: it doesn't only keep the threat out. **It keeps you in.** The same smirk that keeps the warning from reaching you also keeps the mercy from reaching you. You cannot mock the warning of the Fire and receive the promise of the Garden — they come through the same door, and the smirk bars them both.

The practical shift: the next time you feel the reflex to make something sacred small — the reflex to defuse a moment of seriousness about God, death, or your own accountability with a joke or an eye-roll or a "let's not be intense" — *catch it.* Just notice it. You don't have to do anything dramatic. Just notice that the smirk is armor, and ask yourself honestly what it is protecting you from. Often you'll find it is protecting you from the very thing you most need to let in.

**The things you mock to keep them small do not get smaller. They wait. And the day they stop being small, you will not be looking at them from above — you will be standing inside them.**

## Closing Synthesis

### The architecture revealed

Now that we've done the work, look back at the ayah and see how perfectly it is built.

> وَبَدَا لَهُمْ سَيِّئَاتُ مَا كَسَبُوا — وَحَاقَ بِهِم مَّا كَانُوا بِهِ يَسْتَهْزِئُونَ

Two halves. Each half has a verb of unveiling, an owner, and a thing owned.

In the first half: **what they did** — *kasabū*, earned — **surfaces** — *badā* — and confronts them face to face.

In the second half: **how they responded** to the warning about what they did — *istihzāʾ*, mockery — **surrounds** them — *ḥāqa* — and immerses them.

The deed and the denial. The action and the armor. Both *earned.* Both *owned.* Both simply *waiting.* The first rises up in front of them to be seen. The second closes in around them to be lived. And the two verbs — *badā* and *ḥāqa*, surface and surround — are calibrated so precisely that one leaves you an outside to look from, and the other takes the outside away.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the human themes are the same insight arriving from two directions.

The grammar told us: nothing on that Day is *created* — it is *unveiled.* The deeds were earned (*kasabū*); the mockery was a sustained habit (*kānū... yastahziʾūn*). Everything was already there. The Day adds nothing. It only removes the covering and collapses the distance.

The themes told us the same thing in the language of the heart: we spend this life doing two things — *re-naming our deeds so we don't have to see them,* and *mocking the warnings so we don't have to feel them.* And both of those projects have an expiry date. The renaming ends the day the deed surfaces on its own. The mockery ends the day the mocked-thing surrounds you.

Which means the entire ayah is, underneath, a single mercy disguised as a warning. It is telling us *exactly* what is going to be unveiled — so that we can unveil it ourselves, now, while unveiling still leaves room to act. The labels can come off today. The smirk can come down today. And whatever surfaces when they do is a thing you can still change.

### Questions to carry

- What is one deed in my life that I have given a comfortable name — and what is its real name, the one that will *badā*, surface, on its own?
- Where am I using a smirk, an eye-roll, or "let's not be intense" as armor against something I actually need to let in?
- If the covering came off my week — just this past week — what would surface that I have been carefully not looking at?
- Of all the things I keep at a distance, which one would be the most unbearable to have *surround* me? And what is that telling me about where my attention belongs now?
- What would it cost me to voluntarily unveil, today, one thing I have been avoiding — and what might it save me?

### One-sentence distillation

**Nothing you bury in yourself disappears — your deeds wait beneath their labels to surface, and the warnings you laugh off to keep at a distance are the very things that will one day close in around you with no outside left to stand in.**

### The closing invitation

You don't have to wait for the unveiling. That is the whole point of being told about it in advance. The same two verbs that describe the wrongdoer's Day can describe your tonight — *badā,* you let one deed surface honestly before God; and instead of the warning *surrounding* you against your will, you let it *in* on your own terms, while there is still time for it to soften you instead of trap you. The door the smirk was barring — you can simply open it.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You see what we have earned, even when we have hidden it from ourselves — uncover it for us now, gently, while there is still time to turn. Let the truth we have kept at a distance reach our hearts as mercy today, and not surround us as regret tomorrow. And on the Day when everything surfaces, let what *badā* for us be a record we are not ashamed to read, and let nothing we mocked ever become the thing that encircles us. Āmīn.
