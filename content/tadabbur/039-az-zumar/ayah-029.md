# The Man With Many Masters: Why the Divided Heart Can Never Rest

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first. Sit with them before we go anywhere.

> **ضَرَبَ ٱللَّهُ مَثَلًا رَّجُلًا فِيهِ شُرَكَآءُ مُتَشَـٰكِسُونَ وَرَجُلًا سَلَمًا لِّرَجُلٍ هَلْ يَسْتَوِيَانِ مَثَلًاۚ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِۚ بَلْ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ**

*Ḍaraba Allāhu mathalan rajulan fīhi shurakāʾu mutashākisūna wa rajulan salaman li-rajulin, hal yastawiyāni mathalā? Al-ḥamdu lillāh, bal aktharuhum lā yaʿlamūn.*

> "Allah sets forth a parable: a man owned by several partners quarreling with one another, and a man belonging wholly to one master — are the two equal in likeness? All praise belongs to Allah! But most of them do not know."

### Rebuild the world

To feel the weight of this parable, you have to stand inside the world it was first spoken into.

In seventh-century Makkah, slavery was not an abstraction. It was the texture of daily economic life, visible on every street. And there was a specific, recognizable arrangement that everyone in that audience understood instantly — the way you and I instantly understand what it means to "have two bosses who hate each other." It was the **shared slave** — one human being owned jointly by several partners.

Now think about what that actually meant for the man living it. He is not owned by a kind master and a cruel one, where at least the lines are clear. He is owned by *partners* — and these partners do not get along. One wants him in the field at dawn. Another wants him at the market the same hour. One has promised him out to a neighbor; another has other plans. Each of them has a genuine, legal claim on his body, his hours, his energy. And they are fighting *each other* over him.

Picture his morning. He cannot move without betraying someone. Every choice he makes serves one master and wrongs another. There is no path through the day that is not, somewhere, a failure. He is pulled in directions that contradict each other — not occasionally, but as the permanent condition of his existence. He is exhausted before he has done anything, because the exhaustion is not in the work. **The exhaustion is in the being-claimed.**

The Makkan listener knew this man. Maybe owned a share in one. And right beside him in the parable stands another man — owned, also a slave, also not free in the worldly sense — but belonging to **one** master. One voice. One direction. One set of expectations he can actually meet. When his day is done, it is done.

Allah places these two men side by side and asks one question.

### The Hook

Here is what should stop you. This entire parable — the most vivid image of slavery in the Qur'an — is not, in the end, about slavery at all. The slave is a stepping stone. Allah is describing something you cannot see but can feel in your chest right now: **what happens inside a human being who serves more than one ultimate master.**

And the strangest part is this. The word Allah chose for the man at peace — the man owned by one — is built from the very same root as the word *peace* itself. As if Arabic already knew, centuries before psychology had a name for it, that wholeness and singular belonging are not two things.

We are going to take that apart slowly. Stay with me.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مُتَشَـٰكِسُونَ — the word that sounds like what it does

Before we open the word, let me teach you one feature of Arabic, because the entire weight of this image rests on it.

Arabic verbs come in patterns, and each pattern bends the meaning in a predictable way. There is one pattern — scholars call it **Form VI** — that always carries the sense of **mutual, reciprocal action between parties.** Think of it like the difference in English between "to write" and "to *correspond*." Corresponding isn't just writing — it's writing *back and forth*, two people acting on each other. *Taʿāwana* — to cooperate *with one another*. *Takhāṣama* — to dispute *with each other*. Whenever you see this shape, picture not one person acting, but several people locked together, acting *against or upon one another.*

Hold that.

Now the word. Allah describes the partners who own the first man as **مُتَشَـٰكِسُونَ** — *mutashākisūn*.

The root is *sh-k-s*. The lexicons give it a hard, specific meaning. As al-Qurṭubī reports, al-Farrāʾ glossed it as *mukhtalifūn* — people at odds. Al-Mubarrad gave *mutaʿāsirūn* — people who make everything difficult. Al-Baghawī fills in the human portrait: *rajul shakis sharis* — a man who is foul-tempered, abrasive, contrary, who will not accept what is fair, who is out of step with everyone around him. A grinding, friction-making personality.

Now lay the Form VI pattern over that meaning. These are not just difficult men. They are difficult men **locked in mutual difficulty with each other** — *over him.* The reciprocity is the horror of it. Their quarreling is not somewhere else, off to the side. He is the object they are quarreling *over.* Every clash between them lands on his body.

Let's sit with this.

Notice something about the very sound of the word. Say it out loud: *mu-ta-shā-ki-sūn.* It catches in the mouth. It is full of friction — the *sh*, the *k*, the *s*, consonants grinding against each other. Compare it to the smoothness of *salaman*, which we will reach in a moment, a word that flows like water. Allah did not only *tell* you these masters create friction. **He made the word itself grind in your mouth.** The phonetics enact the meaning. You feel the dissonance before you have finished pronouncing it.

[PAUSE]

And here is what is remarkable, and easy to miss. Allah did not say the man has a *cruel* master. Cruelty would at least be coherent — one will, even if wicked. What ruins this man is not cruelty. It is **multiplicity in conflict.** He could survive one harsh owner. What he cannot survive is being legitimately, simultaneously claimed by owners who cannot agree.

This single word tells us what shirk does. It does not give you many gods who lovingly share you. It gives you many claims that *contradict each other* — and you are the ground they fight on.

### Element Two: سَلَمًا — when the word for "belonging" is the word for "peace"

Now turn to the second man. Allah describes him in one word: **سَلَمًا** — *salaman* — "belonging wholly to one man."

Here we touch one of the great deliberate choices in the verse, and the classical readers caught it. Al-Baghawī records the variation in recitation: the reciters of Makkah and Baṣrah read it **سَالِمًا** — *sāliman* — meaning *khāliṣan*, pure, belonging purely and exclusively to him, with no partner and no one to dispute over him. The other reciters read **سَلَمًا** — *salaman* — from the expression *huwa laka salam*, meaning "he is yours, free of any dispute, undivided."

This is not a contradiction to choose between. Build the picture from both at once, the way you would walk around a sculpture to see it whole. One reading shows you his **purity of belonging** — he is *only* this one master's. The other reading shows you the **absence of conflict** — there is no one to fight over him. Same man, two camera angles: he belongs entirely to one, *and therefore* no quarrel touches him. The exclusivity and the peace are the same fact named twice.

And now look at the root. *S-L-M.*

Stay with this, because it is the hinge of the entire verse.

*S-L-M* is the root of **salām** — peace. It is the root of **Islām** — surrender. It is the root of **salāmah** — soundness, wholeness, being intact, free of defect. When you say *as-salāmu ʿalaykum*, you are speaking from this root. When you call this religion *Islām*, you are speaking from this root.

So when Allah wants to describe the man who belongs to one master, He reaches — out of all the words in the language — for the root that *means peace, wholeness, and surrender.*

Do you see what has happened? Allah is not saying "this man is calm" as a separate fact about him. **The very word for his single-belonging IS the word for peace.** In the grammar of the language itself, to belong wholly to One and to be whole, sound, and at peace are not two different states. They are spoken with one breath, from one root.

[PAUSE]

This is *istidlāl* — pointing to the unseen through the seen. The visible image is a slave with one owner. The invisible reality it points to is the human soul that has surrendered to one Lord — and the word *salam* is the bridge, because it carries both meanings at once. The man with one master is at peace *because* the word for his condition is the word for peace. Arabic preached tawḥīd before the parable even reached its question.

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: هَلْ يَسْتَوِيَانِ — the question that already knows its answer

Allah now asks: **هَلْ يَسْتَوِيَانِ مَثَلًا** — *hal yastawiyāni mathalā* — "Are the two equal in likeness?"

Two small grammatical facts carry enormous weight here.

First, the verb *yastawiyān* sits in a pattern — **Form VIII** — that means *to become level, even, equal, balanced.* It is the language of a scale coming to rest, of two surfaces lying flush. The question is literally: *do these two lives balance out to the same thing?*

Second — and this is the quiet brilliance — the verb ends in *-āni*. That ending is the **dual** in Arabic, a special form for *exactly two.* Arabic has singular, then a unique form for *two*, then plural. So the verb itself is locked onto these two men and no one else. *Do these two — these two specific men — come out level?*

And the question opens with **هَلْ** — *hal.* This is the interrogative particle, but here it is what rhetoricians call a question whose answer is already burned into it. It is not asking for information. It is the question a teacher asks when the answer is so obvious that to state it would insult you — *so they make you say it yourself.* "After everything I just showed you — do these two balance?"

You cannot answer yes. The whole architecture of the parable has made yes impossible. And the instant you answer "no" in your own heart, **you have testified to tawḥīd with your own tongue.** Allah did not hand you the doctrine. He built a question you could only answer one way, and let you arrive at the conclusion as if you discovered it.

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

Look at what these three words have done together. *Mutashākisūn* showed us the grinding, mutual conflict of being claimed by many. *Salam* revealed that singular belonging *is* peace, in the very bones of the language. And *yastawiyān* forced us to weigh the two on a scale and feel, ourselves, that they do not balance.

Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the reality you are living inside right now.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The exhaustion of serving many masters

Forget religion for a moment. Let me describe a Tuesday.

You wake, and before your feet touch the floor your phone has already handed you three masters. Your employer wants your focus. Your family wants your presence. The algorithm wants your attention, and it has engineered itself to win. By 9 a.m. you have a notification from a group chat that expects a reply, a deadline that expects priority, a parent who expects a call, and a quiet inner voice that expected, today, to finally rest. Each of these has a *real* claim on you. None of them is wrong to ask. And they cannot all be satisfied, because they contradict each other in time, in energy, in direction.

By evening you are tired in a way that sleep does not fix. Because the tiredness was never in the tasks. **It was in being claimed from too many directions by masters who do not coordinate.**

Now you understand the slave in the parable from the inside. You *are* him, more days than you would like to admit.

This is the genius of Allah's image. He is not describing an exotic ancient horror. He is describing the **default condition of a heart that has not chosen one Lord.** Because here is the thing — you will serve. The human being is built to serve; the only question is *what.* Money. Status. The approval of others. Your own appetites. Fear. An image of yourself you are desperate to protect. These are the *shurakāʾ* — the partners. And they are *mutashākisūn* — they quarrel. The master called Status demands one thing; the master called Comfort demands its opposite. The master called Approval-of-others tears against the master called Desire. You stand in the middle, like the shared slave, unable to take a single step that does not betray one of them.

Let me say it plainly. **Shirk is not first a theological error. It is, before anything else, a way of being torn apart.** The Qur'an's word for it here is not "sin." It is *mutashākisūn* — the lived experience of being pulled into pieces by competing owners.

And then, beside this, the other man. *Salaman li-rajulin.* One master. One direction. One voice to satisfy. Not because his life has fewer demands — he is still a slave, still working — but because all the demands now resolve into one will. When that one is pleased, the day is whole. There is, at last, a *single* place to stand.

The Qur'an says this elsewhere, in a sister verse you should hear beside this one. In Sūrah ar-Rūm (30:28) Allah asks whether you would take your own servants as equal partners in what He has given you — would you split your authority with them and *fear them as you fear your equals?* You would not. No one willingly fractures their own ownership. Yet, Allah says, *that* is exactly what people do with their souls before God.

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It gives you a diagnostic. The next time you feel that specific exhaustion — not tired-from-work but tired-from-being-torn — stop and ask: *how many masters am I trying to satisfy in this moment?* Name them. Status, fear, approval, appetite. And then ask the parable's question on yourself: *can these ever balance?* They cannot. The peace you are looking for is not better time-management among your masters. It is **the dismissal of all but One.** That is what *lā ilāha illā Allāh* actually does — it is not only a creed, it is a *firing* of every false owner, one by one, until a single will remains and the soul can finally stand still.

### Theme Two: Peace is not the absence of a master — it is the rightness of the One

Here is where the modern ear resists, so let us walk into the resistance directly.

We have been taught that freedom means *no master.* That the path to peace is throwing off every claim, owing nothing to anyone, being radically, totally autonomous. The unowned self. The self that answers to nothing.

But notice what Allah does *not* offer in the parable. He does not offer a third man — *a free man, owned by no one.* That option is simply absent. Read the absence carefully, because the Qur'an is rigorous about its silences. The choice is never "many masters versus no master." **The choice is always many masters versus One.** Every human being, the parable quietly insists, is *owned.* The only question is whether by a quarreling committee or by a single rightful Lord.

Why would Allah remove the "free man" option? Because it is a fantasy. The autonomous self, owing nothing, is not what actually appears when people throw off God. What appears is the *first* man — claimed by appetite, by markets, by the opinions of strangers, by fear of death. The one who declares himself owned by no one is, in practice, the one owned by the *most* masters, because he has no single will to organize the rest. He thinks he fired everyone. He only fired the One who could have governed the others.

And now return to that root. *S-L-M.* The man at peace is *salam* — but that same root gives us *Islām*, which means **surrender.** Sit with the scandal of that for a moment. In the logic of this verse, **peace and surrender are the same word.** The path to *salām* (peace) runs directly through *islām* (surrender). Not surrender as defeat — surrender as *finally belonging to the one master worth belonging to,* so that all the lesser claims lose their grip.

This is why the verse turns, at its climax, to an explosion of praise: **ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ** — *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* "All praise belongs to Allah."

Watch what just happened structurally, because it is breathtaking. Allah asks the question — *are the two equal?* — and then, before any human can answer, He answers it Himself, not with "no," but with **praise.** As if to say: the conclusion is so settled, so beautiful, that the only fitting response is gratitude. And look at the *form* of the praise: *al-ḥamdu **li**-llāh.* That little *lām* — "**to** Allah" — is the very same exclusive *lām* from earlier in the verse: *salaman **li**-rajulin*, the man belonging "**to** one master." The parable described a slave whose praise, whose service, whose entire self is *li* — *to* — one owner. And then the verse **performs its own lesson**: *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — all praise is *li-llāh*, to Allah alone. The grammar of the man at peace and the grammar of the praise are *identical.* The verse doesn't just teach belonging-to-One. **It enacts it in its own final words.**

[PAUSE — major]

**What does this change about tomorrow?** It reframes every act of submission you have been resenting. The prayer that interrupts your morning. The desire you restrain. The anger you swallow for the sake of Allah. You have been experiencing these as a master taking from you. The parable says: no — these are the *single voice* replacing the committee. Every "yes" to the One Master is a "no" to a dozen quarreling ones. And on the other side of that surrender is not loss. It is *salam.* The stillness of the man who, at the end of the day, has only one face to have pleased — and has pleased it.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and see the verse whole, because it is built with a symmetry you could not see until the work was done.

It opens with **ضَرَبَ ٱللَّهُ** — *Allah* strikes the parable. It closes with **ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ** — all praise to *Allah.* The verse begins and ends in the One. Between those two pillars stand the two men: the one shredded among *shurakāʾ* (partners, plural, quarreling), and the one whole, *li-rajulin* (to one man, singular). And the hinge in the middle — *hal yastawiyān* — is the question that makes you choose between them.

The form is the meaning. A verse about the supremacy of the One is itself framed, from first word to last, by the One. And its final clause — *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — is not a tacked-on benediction. It is the **answer to the parable, written in the parable's own grammar.** The exclusive *lām* of the peaceful slave becomes the exclusive *lām* of praise. The verse is a closed circle, and at its center is *tawḥīd.*

### Bringing it together

The linguistic journey and the themes were always one insight approaching from two sides.

The *words* showed us: *mutashākisūn* grinds in the mouth like the friction it names; *salam* carries peace and belonging in a single root; *yastawiyān* weighs two lives and finds them unequal.

The *themes* showed us: serving many masters is not a sin to feel guilty about so much as a *fracture* to be healed; and peace is not the absence of a master but the rightness of the One.

They meet here: **To be claimed by many is to be torn apart. To belong wholly to One is to be made whole. And the One worth belonging to is the only One who deserves all praise.** The doctrine of tawḥīd and the human longing for peace are not two subjects. They are the same truth, spoken once in theology and once in the chest.

### Questions to carry

- When I feel that bone-deep exhaustion that sleep does not fix — can I name the masters I was trying to satisfy that day? How many were there?
- Which of my *shurakāʾ* — status, approval, fear, appetite — quarrel with each other most violently inside me? Where do I feel the friction?
- I have been calling autonomy "freedom." Is it possible I have only fired the one Master who could have governed the rest?
- What would it feel like, tonight, to have only **one** face I needed to have pleased?
- The verse answers its own question with *praise,* not with "no." What would change if my response to surrender were gratitude rather than resentment?

### One-sentence distillation

To belong wholly to One is to be at peace, because the word for that belonging *is* the word for peace — while to be claimed by many is to be torn apart on the ground where they quarrel.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to add anything to your life to begin living this. You only have to start *subtracting* masters. The next time you feel pulled in contradictory directions, do not negotiate between the claims. Ask which of them you have wrongly made a lord — and dismiss it, gently, in favor of the One. This is not a heavier burden. It is the lifting of the heaviest one you carry: the burden of trying to please a committee that will never agree. *Salam* is waiting on the other side of *islām.* It always was.

### A brief duʿā'

O Allah, You who set forth this parable and to whom alone all praise belongs — gather the scattered pieces of me back to one Master. Free me from every quarreling owner I have set up beside You, and let my belonging to You be whole, so that I may finally know the peace whose very name is built from the surrender You ask of me. **Al-ḥamdu lillāh.** Āmīn.
