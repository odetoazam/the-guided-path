# Push It to Your Side, Turn Your Face Home

### Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 17

> وَٱلَّذِينَ ٱجْتَنَبُوا۟ ٱلطَّـٰغُوتَ أَن يَعْبُدُوهَا وَأَنَابُوٓا۟ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ لَهُمُ ٱلْبُشْرَىٰ ۚ فَبَشِّرْ عِبَادِ

*Walladhīna ijtanabū aṭ-ṭāghūta an yaʿbudūhā wa-anābū ilā Allāhi lahumu al-bushrā — fabashshir ʿibādi.*

**"And those who keep away from false gods — lest they worship them — and turn back to Allah, for them is glad tidings. So give glad tidings to My servants."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The World Before the Verse

Let me rebuild the room you need to be standing in before this verse can land.

Surah Az-Zumar is a surah about sincerity — *ikhlāṣ*. Three times in its opening it presses the same demand: worship Allah, *making the religion sincerely His*. **Pure.** Undiluted. And the entire surah moves like a courtroom, summoning two kinds of people to stand before you so you can see the difference between them with your own eyes.

Just before our verse, the surah has shown you something terrifying. It has shown you a man on the Day of Judgment who has lost — but not lost the way we usually mean it. The Quran calls it *al-khusrān al-mubīn*, **"the clear loss"** — and then defines it in words that should stop your breath: *"those who have lost themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection."* [PAUSE]

Not lost their wealth. Lost *themselves*. The one possession you thought could never be taken — your own self — gone. A man standing at the end of everything, patting his own chest, and finding no one home.

That is the picture hanging in the air. The smoke of that loss is still in the room.

And then — into that smoke — comes verse 17. Allah turns the camera. He says: *but there is another kind of person.* And He begins to describe them. **The saved.** The ones who did not lose themselves, because long ago they made a choice about what they would and would not bow to.

Here is what I want you to feel before we begin. This verse is not a list of rules. It is a portrait of the rescued — and it is painted with a strange, beautiful economy. In just twelve words, Allah will show you a complete human being's journey: away from something, toward something, and then — watch for this — He will reach into the verse Himself and *claim them*.

The hook is this: the verse begins by talking *about* these people, at arm's length — "those who..." And it ends with Allah no longer talking about them at all. By the final word, He is talking to them, and calling them by a name that does not even finish itself.

Stay with me. Let's find out why.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Ijtanabū* — Keeping It to Your Side

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word.

In Arabic, most verbs are built from a three-letter root, like a seed. And that seed can be planted into different *patterns* — gardeners call them "forms" — and each pattern grows the meaning in a different direction. The simplest form just states the action. But there is a pattern called **Form VIII** — *ifta'ala* — and one of its signature jobs is to turn the action *back onto the doer*. It makes the action **reflexive**. It is the difference between *gathering* something and *gathering yourself together*. The action curves back and involves your own self in it.

Hold onto that. The action curves back onto you.

Now here is the word Allah chose: **ٱجْتَنَبُوا** — *ijtanabū*. Our translations say "those who avoid" or "those who keep away from" the false gods. A clean, flat English word: *avoid.*

But look at the seed it grew from. [PAUSE]

The root is **ج-ن-ب**, *j-n-b*. And do you know what that root means, before it ever becomes a verb? It means **the side.** *Al-janb* — your side, your flank, the part of your torso between your hip and your armpit. It is the most physical, bodily word imaginable. The side of your body.

So now let's rebuild the verb from the ground up. Take the root *janb* — "side." Pour it into Form VIII — the pattern that curves the action back onto yourself. And what do you get?

*Ijtanaba* does not flatly mean "avoid." It means, in its bones: **to put something off to your side. To make yourself stand to one side of it. To keep a flank between you and it.** [PAUSE]

Notice something. This is not the language of a wall. A wall is something you build and walk away from. This is the language of *the body*. It is spatial. It is *ongoing*. To keep something "to your side" is a thing you are doing right now, with your own position, with where you are standing in relation to it.

Let's sit with this image, because the Quran is not describing the things at the side — it is describing a *posture of the soul* using the geometry of the body.

Think of how you walk past something you have decided you will not touch. A man walking past a thing he has sworn off — he does not walk straight at it. He drifts. He puts his shoulder between himself and it. He keeps it in his periphery, never in his hands, never in front of his face. He is *ijtanaba*-ing it, every step. The avoidance is not a single decision made once. It is a *stance he holds in his body* as he moves through the world.

Here is what's remarkable. Allah did not say "those who *destroyed* the false gods" or "those who *hated* the false gods." He said: those who kept them **to one side.** Off the centerline of their lives. Out of the place where worship happens. [PAUSE]

Because the question is never whether the false thing exists in the world. It does. The idols stood in the Kaʿbah. The tyrant sat on his throne. The thing you are tempted by is still in the marketplace, still on the screen, still one tap away. You do not get to make it vanish. **What you get to control is where you stand in relation to it.** Do you keep it to your side — in your periphery, refused the center — or do you let it drift to the front, into your hands, into the place where you bow?

That is what this word reveals. Salvation, in its first movement, is a matter of *positioning.* Of refusing to put the false thing in the center. Of holding it, all your life, off to one flank.

Now — what was it they kept to the side?

### Element One, continued: *Aṭ-Ṭāghūt* — The Thing That Floods Its Banks

The object of their avoidance is **ٱلطَّـٰغُوت** — *aṭ-ṭāghūt*. We translate it "false gods" or "idols," and that is right. But its root tells you *why* it is false.

The root is **ط-غ-ي**, *ṭ-gh-y* — and it means **to overflow, to exceed all bounds, to flood past the limit.** It is the word used for water that bursts its banks. The Quran uses this exact root for Noah's flood — *the water that ṭaghā*, that surged beyond every boundary. It is used for the tyrant Pharaoh — *innahu ṭaghā*, "he transgressed all bounds."

So *ṭāghūt* is not just "an idol." It is **the thing that overflows its rightful place.** [PAUSE]

The classical scholars, as al-Qurtubi gathers them, did not all point to the same object. Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd said the *ṭāghūt* is **Shaytān**. Aḍ-Ḍaḥḥāk and as-Suddī said it is **the idols**, the carved stones. Others said it is **the soothsayer**, the false oracle people ran to. Do not hear these as competing answers, as if we must pick one. Hear them as four photographs of one creature taken from four angles. Whether it wears the face of a whisperer, a stone, or a man claiming to know the unseen — the *ṭāghūt* is **anything that floods past its proper bounds and demands the place that belongs to Allah alone.**

And now feel the two words together, the way Allah set them: *ijtanabū aṭ-ṭāghūt.* They kept *to one side* — the thing that **floods past every side.** [PAUSE]

Do you see the quiet genius of it? The *ṭāghūt* is, by its nature, the thing that will not stay in its lane — it surges, it spreads, it wants to be everywhere, wants to be the center. And the only safe response to a flood is not to argue with it. It is to *get to higher ground, off to the side, and stay there.* You do not negotiate with rising water. You keep your flank to it and you keep moving.

That is the first movement of the saved.

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: *Anābū* — The Face That Turns Back Home

But avoidance alone is not the portrait. A man can keep every false thing to his side and still be standing in the middle of nowhere, facing nothing. Emptiness is not yet faith.

So Allah gives the second movement: **وَأَنَابُوٓا إِلَى ٱللَّهِ** — *wa-anābū ilā Allāh* — **"and they turned back to Allah."**

The word is *anābū*, from the root **ن-و-ب**, *n-w-b*. And this root carries the sense of **returning — and returning again, and again.** It is the root behind *nawba* — a turn that comes around, a recurring shift, the way your turn comes back to you in a rotation. It is *return* with a rhythm built into it, return as something you keep doing.

This is not the cold word for repentance. Arabic has *tawba* for the turning-away-from-sin. This word — *ināba* — is warmer. The scholars describe it as **turning back to Allah with love, with devotion, with the whole face.** It is the word for the heart that doesn't just stop sinning but comes *home* — and keeps coming home. The one who does this is called *munīb*: the one forever turning back toward the door he loves.

Let me give you the everyday picture. There is a difference between a man who stops driving toward a cliff — and a man who *turns the car around and points it at his own house.* The first one has merely avoided death. The second one is *going home.* [PAUSE]

*Ijtanabū* was stopping the car. *Anābū* is turning it toward home — and then driving that road again every single day of your life.

Now notice what Allah has built here, because this is the deep architecture of the whole religion, hidden in two verbs.

First they *kept away* — *ijtanabū*. Then they *turned toward* — *anābū*. **Empty, then fill. Away, then home. Negation, then affirmation.**

This is not an accident of word order. This is *the shape of the testimony itself.* When you say *Lā ilāha illā Allāh*, what do you say first? You do not begin with "there is Allah." You begin with **"Lā ilāha"** — *there is no god.* You **empty the whole sky** of every false claimant. You sweep it clean. You keep every *ṭāghūt* to the side. And only *then*, into that cleared and open sky, you say **"illā Allāh"** — *except Allah.* [PAUSE]

You cannot pour clean water into a cup that is already full of something else. You have to empty it first. The hand that is gripping an idol cannot also be raised to Allah — it has to *let go* before it can *lift.*

This is why the verse refuses to let you start with the turning-toward. You do not get to *anābū* until you have done *ijtanabū*. There is no homecoming without first leaving the place you were lost in. The two verbs are not two suggestions. They are one motion in two beats, and the order can never be reversed.

Stay with this for your own life. We so often want the second beat without the first. We want to *add* Allah to a life still crowded with our little gods — the approval we worship, the fear we obey, the habit we bow to. We want to install Him alongside everything else. But the verse says: no. **First you keep the false thing to your side. Then — only then — does the road home open.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: *Al-Bushrā* and *Fabashshir* — The News That Shows on Your Skin

Now we arrive at the reward, and then at the most intimate turn in the entire verse. And it is built on a single root said twice.

*Lahumu* **al-bushrā** — "for them is **the glad tidings.**" And then: *fa***bashshir** *ʿibādi* — "so **give glad tidings** to My servants."

Both words — *al-bushrā* and *bashshir* — come from the root **ب-ش-ر**, *b-sh-r.* And I need you to know what this root literally means, because English's "good news" loses everything.

*Al-bashara*, from this root, is **the skin** — specifically the outer surface of the skin, the complexion, the face. The very same root gives us *bashar* — the word for a human being, the **"skin-creature,"** the one defined by his flesh and surface.

So what is *bushrā*? It is not merely "good news." It is **news so good that it changes your skin.** News that you cannot keep off your face. The kind of report that floods your complexion before a single word leaves your mouth — the color rising, the eyes widening, the whole surface of you transformed by what just reached your ears. [PAUSE]

The Arabs named good news after the *face* because they understood: the truest joy is the kind your body cannot hide. You can fake a thank-you. You cannot fake the flush that runs up your neck when the news is real.

So when Allah says *lahumu al-bushrā* — "for them is *the bushrā*" — He is not promising them an announcement. He is promising them **a joy that will reach the surface of who they are.** A gladness that gets all the way to the skin.

And notice the second word is a different *form* of the same root. *Bashshir* — *fabashshir* — is **Form II**, the intensified, transitive pattern, the one that means *to actively convey this joy to someone, to deliver it into them.* It is a command: *Prophet — make this gladness reach them. Put the bushrā onto their skin.*

Here is the quiet beauty. The people *kept the ṭāghūt to their side* — and what is a *ṭāghūt*? Often a thing of stone or whisper, a thing with no face, no living surface. They turned away from the dead surfaces of false gods. And in return, Allah promises them a joy *that lives on their own surface*, on the *bashara* of the *bashar*, on the living skin of the human being who turned home.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three, continued: *ʿIbādi* — The Name That Doesn't Finish Itself

And now the final word. The most intimate word in the verse. Watch how the whole grammar of the sentence changes underneath it.

For the first three-quarters of this verse, Allah is speaking *about* these people in the **third person**, at a distance: *"those who"... "they kept away"... "they turned back"... "for **them** is glad tidings."* They are over there. He is describing them to us. The pronouns hold them at arm's length.

And then — in a single word — He turns. [PAUSE]

*Fabashshir* **ʿibādi.** *"So give glad tidings to* ***My servants.***"

This is what the scholars of rhetoric call **iltifāt** — the sudden, deliberate *turn* of address, when the speaker pivots without warning. The Quran was talking *about* them — and now Allah is no longer talking about them at all. He has reached into His own verse and taken hold of them. *"Those who"* has become **"My servants."** The distant relative pronoun — *alladhīna*, "the ones who" — has become a **possessive.** *ʿibādī* — *Mine.* [PAUSE]

Feel the distance collapse. They began the verse as a category. They end it as a *belonging.*

But there is one more thing, and it is almost too tender to say out loud. Look at how the word is written in the muṣḥaf. The full form of "my servants" would be *ʿibādī* — ending in a long *ī*, the possessive "my." But Allah does not spell it out in full. The final *yāʾ* — the very letter that means **"my"** — is **dropped.** Elided. The word is written *ʿibād*, with just a trace, a kasra, where the "my" should be.

The grammarians have their technical reasons for the elision. But sit with what it *does* to the ear and the heart. The word for *belonging to Allah* is so intimate, so close, that **it does not even finish itself.** It trails off. Like a name spoken so often between two people who love each other that the ending gets swallowed — you don't need the whole word; the closeness fills in what the letters leave out. [PAUSE]

These people kept the *ṭāghūt* — the thing that *floods past every boundary* — at a careful distance, to their side. And in return, Allah pulls *them* across every boundary, all the way in, until the word for their belonging to Him is too close to fully pronounce.

That is what this verse reveals. That is the destination the whole sentence was traveling toward.

### Bridge

Look at what these words have shown us. A posture of the body — keeping something to your *side*. A turning of the face — coming *home*, and coming home again. A joy that reaches the *skin*. And a belonging so near the word for it dissolves on the tongue.

The linguistic construction is not decoration. It is a complete choreography of rescue: *away, toward, reward, embrace.* Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. About how a human being is actually saved, and what they are saved *into.*

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: You Must Empty Before You Can Fill

Let me begin where you already live.

You know what it feels like to try to add a good thing to a crowded life. You decide you'll start praying with focus — but your mind is still full of the argument from this afternoon, the notification you haven't checked, the worry running on a loop. You stand to pray and you are *not empty.* You are a full cup, and you're trying to pour something sacred into a vessel already brimming with everything else. And it spills. It doesn't take.

Anyone who has tried to break a habit knows this in their bones. The recovery programs figured out something the self-help books often miss: you cannot simply *will* a new behavior into a life still organized around the old one. You have to *remove* first. Clear the house. Empty the cabinet. Change who you stand next to. The addition fails until the subtraction happens. [PAUSE]

We treat this as a modern, psychological discovery. It is not. It is the structure Allah built into this verse fourteen centuries ago, and into the very testimony of faith.

*Ijtanabū aṭ-ṭāghūt* — **first** they kept the false to their side. *Wa-anābū ilā Allāh* — **then** they turned home. Empty, then fill. And the order is not negotiable, because *that is the order of Lā ilāha illā Allāh.* You sweep the sky empty of false gods before you name the True One into it.

Now let me make this uncomfortable, because the *ṭāghūt* is not only a stone idol in a faraway temple. Remember its meaning: **the thing that floods past its bounds and takes the center.** Ask yourself honestly — what in your life has flooded past its bounds?

Money is meant to be a tool. When it floods its banks, it becomes the thing you'd compromise your prayer for, lie for, lose sleep over — it has become *ṭāghūt.* The approval of people is a natural human need. When it floods its banks, it becomes the master you obey instead of Allah — you'd sin to keep it and abandon the truth to avoid losing it. That is *ṭāghūt.* Even your own fear, your own anger, your own desire — each is meant to sit in its proper place, in its proper proportion. When one of them overflows and starts dictating your choices, it has become a little god, and you are bowing. [PAUSE]

Here is the hard mercy of this verse. It does not ask you to *kill* these things. You cannot kill your need for money or your desire for love. *Ijtanaba* is gentler and harder than killing — it asks you to **keep them to your side.** In their lane. Off the center. Refused the place of worship. To say to the money, the approval, the fear: *you may walk beside me, but you may never walk in front, and you will never have the place where I bow.*

And the moment you do that — the moment you push the flood off to your flank — something opens. The road home, *ilā Allāh*, which was blocked by the crowd of false gods, suddenly clears. You can turn. You can *anāba.* [PAUSE]

**What does this change about tomorrow?** Tonight, before you sleep, name one *ṭāghūt* in your life — one thing that has flooded past its banks and taken a place in the center that belongs to Allah. Don't try to destroy it. That's the trap that makes us give up. Just decide where it will *stand.* Move it to your side. Refuse it the center. And watch how, in the cleared space, turning toward Allah suddenly becomes possible in a way it wasn't before.

You empty so that you can fill. There is no other order.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: From "Those Who" to "Mine" — The Glad Tiding of Belonging

Now let me take you somewhere even the saved sometimes cannot believe they are allowed to go.

There is a particular ache that lives in the heart of the one who is *trying* to come back to Allah. It is the ache of feeling like a *category* and not a *person.* You read the verses about "the believers" and "the righteous" and some quiet voice says: *those are descriptions of other people. People who have it together. I'm just... someone trying. I'm a 'those who' on a good day. I am not anybody's beloved.* [PAUSE]

You feel addressed in the third person. Described from a distance. Included in the group, maybe, but never *named.* Never reached for.

The convert who left everything to come to Islam and now sits alone, wondering if Allah even registers the cost of it. The one who fell, and clawed their way back, and still feels like they're standing at the back of the mosque hoping no one notices they don't quite belong. The believer who does everything right and still feels like an employee of a distant master, never a beloved of a near One. *Those who.* Always *those who.* Never *Mine.*

Now read what Allah does at the end of this verse, and read it *to yourself.*

He describes them at a distance — *those who kept away, those who turned back, for them is glad tidings.* He could have ended it there. *Lahumu al-bushrā.* A complete sentence. A fair reward, cleanly stated, third person, at arm's length. The verse did not *need* another word. [PAUSE]

But Allah does not stop. He turns. *Fabashshir* — *"So give them the good news, Prophet"* — and then He chooses the word He will use for these struggling, returning, formerly-lost people. He could have said *bashshir al-mu'minīn*, "the believers." He could have said *bashshir al-muttaqīn*, "the God-conscious." Instead He says:

***ʿIbādī.*** **"My servants."** **Mine.** [PAUSE]

The distance collapses. The category becomes a belonging. The "those who" that held them at arm's length is gone, and in its place is a possessive pronoun spoken by the Lord of the worlds. *Mine.*

And then — remember? — the word doesn't even finish. The *yāʾ* of "my" is swallowed. *ʿibād*, with only a trace of the "my" left, like a name spoken so close it doesn't need its last letter.

I want you to understand what Allah is teaching the trembling heart here. The glad tidings — *al-bushrā* — is not *only* the Garden. According to the scholars, as al-Muyassar gathers it, the *bushrā* is **in this life and the next**: in this world, a good name and Allah's guidance steadying your steps; in the next, His pleasure and a Garden that does not end. But before any of that — *here, in this verse, right now* — the glad tidings is **the name He calls you by.** [PAUSE]

The first and greatest *bushrā* is hearing the One you were terrified you'd disappointed turn, in the middle of describing you, and call you ***Mine.***

That is the news that reaches the skin. That is the *bushrā* the *bashara* was named for. Not "you passed." Not "you're in the group." But: *you are not a stranger here. You are not a 'those who.' You belong to Me, and the word for it is so close I won't even finish saying it.* [PAUSE]

**What does this change about tomorrow?** The next time you stand to pray feeling like an outsider, like someone tolerated at the edge of mercy — remember the grammar of this verse. Remember that Allah, describing people who had been *lost*, people who had to *keep away* from false gods because they had once been near them, turned at the end and called them *ʿibādī.* Mine. You are not auditioning to become His. The moment you kept the false thing to your side and turned your face home, you were already named. Pray like someone who has been called by name — because you have.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Revealed

Now that we have done the work, step back and see the whole shape of these twelve words, because Allah built them as a single, deliberate journey through space and relationship:

1. **ٱجْتَنَبُوا ٱلطَّـٰغُوتَ** — They put the flood to their **side.** *(Movement away. Third person. Distance.)*
2. **وَأَنَابُوٓا إِلَى ٱللَّهِ** — They turned their face **home.** *(Movement toward. Still third person.)*
3. **لَهُمُ ٱلْبُشْرَىٰ** — For **them** is the joy that reaches the skin. *(Reward. Third person — they are still "them.")*
4. **فَبَشِّرْ عِبَادِ** — So deliver it to... **My servants.** *(The turn. Allah reaches in. They become* ***Mine.****)*

Watch the geometry. The verse moves a person *away* from the false, *toward* the True, and then — in its final beat — it moves *Allah* toward *them.* They take two steps in His direction; He closes the whole remaining distance in one word. They keep the *ṭāghūt* at a careful arm's length; He pulls *them* so close the word for their nearness loses its last letter.

The whole sentence is a homecoming filmed from two cameras: the human walking home, and the One waiting at the door who, seeing them on the road, *runs.*

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the two themes are the same truth seen from two directions.

The grammar taught us: **empty before you fill** — *ijtanabū* must come before *anābū*, the way *lā ilāha* must come before *illā Allāh.* And the theme taught us: that is exactly how a real human being is actually rescued — you clear the center before you can turn home.

The grammar taught us: the distant *"those who"* becomes the intimate, unfinished *"Mine."* And the theme taught us: that is exactly the *bushrā* — the deepest glad tiding is not a reward you receive but a **name you are called by.**

One insight, two directions: **the one who pushes the false to their side and turns their face home is not merely saved — they are claimed, by name, by a nearness too close to fully speak.**

### Questions to Carry

1. What in my life has *flooded past its banks* — taken a place in the center that belongs to Allah alone? Have I named it honestly, or am I pretending it's still in its lane?
2. Am I trying to *add* Allah to a crowded life — or have I done the harder first work of keeping the false things to my side, so there is room to turn?
3. When I stand before Allah, do I feel like a *"those who"* — a tolerated category — or have I let myself believe the word He used: *ʿibādī*, **Mine?**
4. Is my return to Allah a one-time stop, or am I a *munīb* — one who turns home *again and again*, with the rhythm built into the very word *anābū*?
5. What would change in my prayer tomorrow if I truly believed I had already been called by name?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Those who keep the false to their side and turn their face home do not merely earn good news — they are claimed by the Lord of the worlds as His own, with a joy that reaches the skin and a name so near it does not finish itself.**

### The Closing Invitation

This verse does not ask you to be perfect. It asks you to *position* yourself. To keep the flood to your flank and point your face toward the one Door worth facing. And it makes you a promise about what waits on the other side of that small, daily, bodily act of turning: not a verdict delivered from a distance, but a Lord who interrupts His own description of you to call you *Mine.*

You do not have to become someone else to be named. You only have to turn. The naming is already waiting.

### A Brief Du'ā

*O Allah, You who named the returning servant "Mine" before the word could even finish — let me be among those who keep every false god to their side and turn their whole face toward You. Empty my heart of all that has flooded past its bounds, and into that cleared and quiet space, let me turn home to You again and again. And when You speak the glad tiding, O Most Merciful, let me be close enough to hear my name.* **Āmīn.**
