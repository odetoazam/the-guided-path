# Follow the Best of It: The Open Door and the Closing Window

> وَٱتَّبِعُوٓاْ أَحْسَنَ مَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكُم مِّن رَّبِّكُم مِّن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمُ ٱلْعَذَابُ بَغْتَةً وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ
>
> *Wa-ttabiʿū aḥsana mā unzila ilaykum min rabbikum min qabli an yaʾtiyakumu-l-ʿadhābu baghtatan wa-antum lā tashʿurūn.*
>
> **"And follow the best of what has been sent down to you from your Lord — before the punishment comes upon you suddenly, while you do not perceive it."**

---

## INTRODUCTION: The Door That Was Just Thrown Open

I want you to feel where we are standing before we read a single word of analysis.

Two verses before this one, Allah said something so vast that the Prophet ﷺ and the scholars after him called it the most hope-filled verse in the entire Quran. He said: *"Qul yā ʿibādiya-lladhīna asrafū ʿalā anfusihim lā taqnaṭū min raḥmatillāh"* — **"Say: O My servants who have transgressed against their own selves — do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins."**

Sit in that for a moment. He does not say "O sinners." He does not say "O criminals." He says *yā ʿibādī* — **O My servants.** He claims them. The people who have wrecked themselves, who have piled wrong upon wrong until they have lost count, who have written themselves off — He turns to them and addresses them as *His.* And then He tells them not to despair.

So picture the door. It has just been flung open. The widest door in the Book. A person who thought there was no way back is suddenly standing in front of an opening with light pouring through it.

And then — verse 54 — He says: *"And turn back to your Lord and submit to Him, before the punishment comes to you."*

And then — our verse, 55 — He says: *"And follow the best of what has been sent down to you from your Lord, before the punishment comes upon you suddenly, while you do not perceive it."*

Notice the rhythm. Door thrown open. *Come back.* *Follow the best.* And underneath each invitation, the same low drumbeat: **before… before… before.**

Here is the question I want us to carry through everything that follows. If His mercy is so vast that it swallows *all* sins — and He just said it does — then why the urgency? Why does the most merciful verse in the Quran sit two lines away from a warning about punishment arriving without warning? What is the relationship between a door that is infinitely wide and a window that is quietly closing?

[PAUSE]

And there is a smaller phrase, easy to slide past, that I think holds a whole spiritual life inside it. He does not say "follow what was revealed to you." He says **"follow the *best* of what was revealed to you."** The best of it. As though the revelation contains levels — and you are being called, not merely to the floor, but to the summit.

Let us go slowly. Let us earn it.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Aḥsana* — "The Best of What Was Revealed"

#### First, the concept — before we touch the word

In English, when we want to say something is the greatest of its kind, we have one main move: we add "-est." Big, bigger, biggest. Good, better, best. That final form — "best" — is what grammarians call the **superlative** or, in Arabic, the **elative** (*ism al-tafḍīl*, the noun of preferring).

Arabic builds this on a fixed mould: **af ʿal**. From the root for goodness and beauty — *ḥ-s-n*, the root of *ḥasan*, "good," and *iḥsān*, "excellence" — you build **aḥsan**: "better than," "the best." Same mould gives you *akbar* (greatest), *aʿlā* (highest), *aʿẓam* (most magnificent).

So when Allah says *aḥsana mā unzila* — **"the best of what was sent down"** — He is reaching for that elative mould deliberately. Not *al-ḥasan*, "the good." **Aḥsan.** The best.

Now hold that, because it creates an immediate problem — and the problem is the whole point.

#### Opening the word

> أَحْسَنَ مَآ أُنزِلَ إِلَيْكُم
>
> *aḥsana mā unzila ilaykum* — "the **best** of what was sent down to you."

Here is the difficulty that every classical commentator on this verse stopped at. The thing being described — *mā unzila ilaykum min rabbikum*, "what was sent down to you from your Lord" — **is the Quran.** Al-Jalālayn says it plainly: *"huwa-l-Qurʾān"* — it is the Quran. Al-Baghawī, al-Qurṭubī, al-Muyassar — they all agree: the "what was revealed" is the Quran.

But then they all immediately raise the obvious objection. *Wa-l-Qurʾānu kulluhu ḥasan.* **The Quran — all of it — is good.** Every word of it is good. There is no inferior part of it to be left behind. So what could it possibly mean to "follow the *best* of it"? Best as opposed to what? Is some of the Quran less than best?

[PAUSE]

Stay with this tension. This is exactly the kind of place where, if you rush past, you lose the treasure.

#### Going deeper

The commentators give us not competing theories but layers of one answer — let me build the composite for you.

Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, as both al-Qurṭubī and al-Baghawī report, reads it as: *iltazimū ṭāʿatahu wa-jtanibū maʿṣiyatah* — **"Cling to its obedience and avoid its disobedience."** The "best" you follow is the path of doing what it commands.

But it is al-Baghawī who opens the door wider, and this is the part I want you to really hear. He explains *why* the Quran can speak of "the best" of itself. He says:

> The Quran mentioned the **ugly** (*al-qabīḥ*) so that you would avoid it. And it mentioned the **lesser, the merely-permitted** (*al-adwan*) so that you would not become attached to it. And it mentioned the **best** (*al-aḥsan*) so that you would prefer it and choose it.

Do you see what just happened? The Quran is not a flat surface. It is a landscape with elevation. Within its pages, Allah lays out a *spectrum* of human possibility. There are things it names as forbidden — *avoid these.* There are things it names as merely allowed, the lawful-but-low, the permitted-but-uninspiring — *don't sink your heart into these.* And there are things it names as the highest, the most beautiful response a human being can give — **and these are the *aḥsan*, the best — these are what you are being commanded to *follow.***

So "follow the best of what was revealed" does not mean some of the Quran is inferior. It means: **of all the rungs of human action that the Quran maps out for you, climb to the top one.**

#### Deeper still — let this land

Let me make this concrete, because the Quran itself gives us the pattern.

When someone wrongs you, the Quran tells you that you *may* respond in kind — *"the recompense of an injury is an injury the like thereof"* (42:40). That is the floor. It is permitted. It is just. But in the very same breath the verse continues: *"but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is upon Allah."* **That** is the *aḥsan.* The forgiveness is the best.

The Quran says you may divorce — and then says *imsākun bi-maʿrūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsān*, release with excellence. It permits you to answer harshness with proportion — and then says *idfaʿ bi-llatī hiya aḥsan*, **"repel [evil] with that which is best,"** and watch your enemy become a warm friend (41:34).

In almost every domain of life, the Quran gives you a permitted floor and a beautiful summit. And the floor is real — it is not sin to stand on it. But this verse is Allah leaning in and saying: **I did not send you the Book so you would live at the floor. Follow the *best* of it.** Don't just survive the revelation. *Ascend* it.

[PAUSE]

And notice — this is being said *to the very people who just transgressed against themselves* in verse 53. To the ones who wrecked themselves, who are being told not to despair, the next instruction is not "scrape by." It is "follow the best." He calls the broken to the summit. The mercy is not a lowering of the bar. The mercy is that the summit is still open to you.

#### What this one word reveals

*Aḥsan* — one elative noun — tells you that **Allah's mercy and Allah's standard are not in tension. He forgives everything, and He calls you to the highest. The door is wide *and* the path goes up.**

### Element Two: *Min Qabli* — The Word "Before," and the Window You Cannot See

#### The concept first

There is a small word in this verse doing enormous work, and it is the kind of word our eyes are trained to skip: a preposition. *Min qabli* — **"before."** From the root *q-b-l*, which carries the sense of what comes *in front*, what is *prior*, what *faces* you. The same root gives us *qiblah* — the direction you face.

"Before" is a word about *time you still have.* When I say "do this *before* that happens," I am telling you two things at once: there is a deadline, and **right now, you are on the near side of it.** The word "before" only has meaning while the window is still open. The moment the thing arrives, "before" is gone forever.

#### Opening the phrase

> مِّن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِيَكُمُ ٱلْعَذَابُ
>
> *min qabli an yaʾtiyakumu-l-ʿadhāb* — "**before** the punishment comes to you."

Look at the verb attached to it: *yaʾtiya*, from the root *ʾ-t-y*, "to come." It is in the imperfect — *an yaʾtiya* — a verb of motion still in progress, still on its way. The punishment is *coming*. It is described as something already in motion toward you, not yet arrived. And the word *qabl* freezes you in the gap — in the narrow strip of time between "it has not come" and "it has."

This is the third time in three verses that Allah has used this word *qabl.* Verse 54: turn back *before* the punishment comes. Verse 55: follow the best *before* it comes suddenly. Verse 57 will say it again. **Before, before, before.** It is a hammer striking the same nail. He is not warning you about the punishment so much as He is warning you about the *gap* — about the closing window you are standing inside *right now.*

#### Going deeper

Here is what makes this so piercing. Every human being agrees, in the abstract, that they will not live forever. Nobody disputes the deadline. The problem is never *whether* there is a "before." The problem is that we cannot *see* where the line is.

And so the soul does something very specific with an invisible deadline: **it assumes there is always more "before" left.** It assumes a runway. We say *later.* We say *after this phase of my life.* We say *when things settle down, when I'm older, when I've sorted myself out — then I'll follow the best.* We treat the window as if we can see its far edge, and we always place that edge comfortably beyond where we currently stand.

The word *qabl* in this verse is Allah putting His hand on that assumption. He is saying: you are *inside* the "before" right now. This — this breath, this day, this moment of reading — **this is the before.** It will not announce when it ends.

[PAUSE]

#### Deeper still

And now we reach the word that the entire verse has been building toward.

> بَغْتَةً
>
> *baghtatan* — "**suddenly.**"

I want you to say it out loud. *Baghtah.* Feel what your mouth has to do. The *b* clamps shut, then *gh* — that thick, guttural catch from deep in the throat — then the hard stop of *t.* **B-gh-t.** The word *lurches.* It has no smooth approach, no easing in. The sound of the word is an ambush.

This is not an accident of Arabic. The root *b-gh-t* means precisely *to come upon someone suddenly, to take them unawares, to spring upon them.* And Allah chose a word whose very texture — its abrupt consonants, its lack of any long, gliding vowel to soften the entry — *enacts* the meaning. You hear the suddenness before you understand it. The word arrives in your ear the way the thing arrives in your life: **without a runway.**

Place *baghtatan* next to *qabl* and the trap snaps shut. The whole danger of "before" is that you think you can see the deadline. *Baghtatan* says: **you cannot.** It does not come on a schedule you can read. It comes as an ambush. So every grain of "before" you are holding right now is not a guaranteed runway — it is a mercy that could end mid-sentence.

#### The final layer

> وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ
>
> *wa-antum lā tashʿurūn* — "**while you do not perceive it.**"

And here is the cruelest detail of the ambush, and it deserves its own breath. The verb is *tashʿurūn*, from the root *sh-ʿ-r.* Now this root is extraordinary. It is the root of *shiʿr* — poetry — and of *shuʿūr* — feeling, sensation, the most delicate, fine-grained awareness a human has. To *shaʿara* is not just to "know"; it is to **sense, to feel something at the level of intuition, to have an inkling.** The Arabs used this root for the kind of subtle perception by which a poet feels the weight of a word.

And Allah *negates* it. *Lā tashʿurūn* — you will not even *sense* it coming. Not "you will not know the date" — something far more total. You will have **no inkling. No premonition. No faint feeling at the edge of awareness** that something is approaching. The finest instrument of human perception, the one that catches what the mind misses — even *that* will register nothing.

[PAUSE]

So stack the three together, because this is the architecture of the warning: *Qabl* — there is a deadline. *Baghtatan* — it strikes without approach. *Lā tashʿurūn* — and you will not feel it nearing. The window is real, it has no visible far edge, and your most sensitive faculty will give you no warning when you reach it.

#### What these words reveal

Together, *qabl* and *baghtatan* and *lā tashʿurūn* tell you something the abstract knowledge of death never tells you: **the urgency is not in the punishment. The urgency is in the invisibility of the line.** You are being asked to act *now* — not because the end is near in a way you can measure, but precisely because you *cannot* measure it.

[PAUSE — major]

### Bridge

Look at what these two words have done. *Aḥsan* showed us that the call is *upward* — follow the best, ascend the revelation, do not settle at the permitted floor. And *qabl / baghtatan* showed us that the call is *now* — because the runway you imagine is invisible at its far end and ends without sensation.

The summit, and the closing window. The height of the demand, and the urgency of the moment. Now let us ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way a human soul actually lives, and the way it actually fools itself.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lie the Soul Tells About Time

Let me start where you live, not where the verse lives.

You know the experience of "I'll start Monday." The diet starts Monday. The new habit starts after the holidays. The serious prayer life starts after this exam season, after this work crunch, after the wedding, after the move. There is always a clean line on the calendar, just ahead, behind which the better version of you is waiting to begin.

Psychologists have a precise name for this. They call it **temporal discounting** — the brain's built-in tendency to treat a future self as a kind of stranger, and to shove effort and sacrifice onto that stranger's shoulders while keeping ease for the self of right now. There are brain-imaging studies where, when people are asked to think about themselves *years* from now, the regions that light up are closer to the ones used for thinking about *other people* than the ones used for thinking about *me.* The "future you" is, neurologically, almost someone else. And it is very easy to assign hard things to someone else.

This is the machinery the soul uses to procrastinate on return. *Later me* will pray properly. *Later me* will forgive that person, will quit that habit, will follow the best of what was revealed. And it feels safe — because *later me* always seems to have so much runway.

[PAUSE]

Now bring the verse back. *Min qabli an yaʾtiyakumu-l-ʿadhābu baghtatan wa-antum lā tashʿurūn.*

What this verse does to temporal discounting is surgical. It does not argue that you have less time than you think. It says something far more destabilizing: **you do not know how much time you have at all, and you will not be told when it runs out.** *Baghtatan.* *Lā tashʿurūn.* The entire procrastination engine runs on the assumption of a visible runway — "I can see the deadline, and it's comfortably far." The verse reaches in and pulls out that one assumption. Remove the visible far-edge of the runway, and "later me" has no ground to stand on anymore. There may be no later me at all.

Here is the movement I want you to make. The verse is not trying to frighten you into a panic. Panic is just another way of avoiding the present — it's all noise, no action. What the verse is doing is **collapsing the distance between you and your future self.** It is saying: the person who is going to follow the best of what was revealed cannot be a stranger you'll meet on some Monday. That person has to be *you*, now, because *now* is the only "before" you are guaranteed to be holding.

And there is something tender underneath the warning, easy to miss. Why does Allah tell us the punishment comes *baghtatan*, without warning? **Because if you knew the exact hour, you would procrastinate right up to it.** You would game the deadline. The mercy of the *hidden* deadline is that it dignifies *every* moment — it makes *this* breath the one that matters, not some calculable last one. The invisibility of the line is not cruelty. It is what gives the present its weight.

What does this change tomorrow? Something small and concrete. The next time you catch your mind saying *later* about something you already know is the better path — the apology you owe, the prayer you keep shortening, the habit you keep meaning to drop — recognize it as the lie. Name it. And then do the smallest possible version of the right thing *today*, inside the "before" you actually have. Not the whole mountain. Just the first step, taken now, because now is the only time the word *qabl* is still true for you.

### Theme Two: Why the Broken Are Called to the Summit

Let me begin with a feeling you may know well.

When a person has fallen — really fallen, not a small slip but the kind of failure that makes them feel disqualified — something strange happens to their sense of what is allowed to them. They start to believe that excellence is for other people. That the *aḥsan*, the best, the beautiful summit of the religion, belongs to the clean, the consistent, the ones who never wandered. And for themselves, they negotiate downward. *Maybe I can just hold on to the basics. Maybe I can just not get worse. The high path — the night prayer, the deep forgiveness, the radiant character — that's not for someone like me anymore.*

It is one of the most defeating lies the heart tells, and it wears the mask of humility. *Who am I to aim high?*

Now look at where this verse sits. It is spoken to the *musrifūn ʿalā anfusihim* — the ones who **transgressed against their own selves**, the people of verse 53, the ones told not to despair. These are not the spiritual elite. These are the wreckers, the ones who ruined themselves. And what is the instruction given to *them*, just two verses later?

Not "follow the minimum." Not "just don't sin again." **"Follow the *best* of what was revealed to you."** *Ittabiʿū aḥsan.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with that, because it overturns everything the falling soul assumes. The summit is not being reserved for the people who never fell. The summit is being held open *for the ones who did.* Allah does not respond to a wrecked life by lowering the bar to something a broken person can limp over. He responds by saying: the highest thing is still yours. *Aḥsan* is still on the table for you. Come up.

This is the deepest harmony in the passage — and now we can see why the most merciful verse in the Quran sits *next to* the call to the best and the warning of the sudden end. They are not in tension. They are the same gesture. Despair says: *I've fallen, so excellence is closed to me.* The verse says: *you've fallen, so do not despair — and follow the best.* The mercy (verse 53) and the high standard (verse 55) are the two hands of one embrace. He forgives *all* of it — *that's* the door. And He still wants the *best* from you — *that's* the dignity. He refuses to relate to you as damaged goods who should be grateful for the floor. He relates to you as someone capable of the summit, *because* He has forgiven you.

[PAUSE]

There is a paradox here worth letting sit, without rushing to tidy it away. You would think mercy means a lighter demand. *Since He forgives, surely He asks for less.* But this passage does the opposite. The mercy *raises* the call. Why? Because to ask little of someone is, quietly, to think little of them. The parent who has given up on a child asks for nothing. The parent who believes in the child *insists* — keeps the high expectation alive precisely because they still see who that child could become. Allah's command to "follow the best," spoken to the broken, is Him *still seeing who you could be.* The high bar *is* the mercy. It is Him refusing to write you off.

What does this change tomorrow? When you next catch yourself negotiating downward — *I'll just do the bare minimum, that's all I'm capable of after everything* — hear it for what it is. It is not humility. It is despair wearing humility's clothes, and verse 53 forbade you that despair. Pick *one* thing that is the *aḥsan*, not the floor — one act of excellence the lower self says is "not for someone like me." The forgiveness you've been withholding. The prayer prayed with presence instead of speed. The character you show when no one's watching. Reach for the best version of *one* thing. Not because you've earned the summit — because He held it open for you anyway, and to aim low now would be to call His door a lie.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Stand back and see the whole verse now as one motion.

The linguistic journey gave us two discoveries. *Aḥsan* — the elative, the best — told us the call is **upward**: not to the permitted floor but to the beautiful summit, and the commentators showed us a Quran with *elevation*, naming the ugly to avoid, the low to release, and the best to choose. And *qabl / baghtatan / lā tashʿurūn* told us the call is **now**: a real deadline, with an invisible far edge, arriving as an ambush your finest perception will not feel coming.

The thematic journey gave us the same two truths from the inside of a human chest. The lie about time — *later me will do it* — dies the moment the runway loses its visible end. And the lie about worth — *the best is not for someone like me* — dies the moment you realize Allah spoke "follow the best" *to the very people who had wrecked themselves.*

Two directions, one insight. The height of the call and the urgency of the call are the same mercy. He holds the summit open for the broken — *that* is His generosity. And He warns that the window is invisible at its edge — *that* is His honesty. A lesser mercy would lower the bar and hide the deadline to keep you comfortable. This mercy raises the bar and names the deadline, because it actually wants you to *arrive.*

[PAUSE]

### Questions to Carry

- Where in my life am I standing on the *permitted floor* when the verse is calling me to the *aḥsan* — the summit — of that same situation?
- What have I been assigning to "later me," and what would change if I admitted I cannot see the far edge of my own runway?
- Is the voice telling me "the best is not for someone like me" actually humility — or is it the despair that verse 53 forbade me?
- If I truly believed the next ambush could come *baghtatan*, *while I do not perceive it* — what is the one thing I would stop postponing tonight?
- Have I been treating the high standard as a burden, when it might be the clearest sign that Allah has *not* written me off?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**Climb to the best of what your Lord revealed — now, inside the only "before" you are guaranteed to hold — because the door He opened for the broken still leads all the way up, and the window is closing without a sound.**

### The Closing Invitation

You are not being asked to panic, and you are not being asked to despise the floor you have managed to stand on. You are being invited — gently, urgently, by the One who just called you *His* servant even after you wrecked yourself — to lift your eyes from the floor to the summit, and to take the first step toward it *today.* Not because you've earned the height. Because He held it open for you, and the most beautiful response a forgiven soul can give is to reach for the best of what it was given, before.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You called us Your servants even after we transgressed against ourselves — so do not let us answer that nearness by aiming low. Let us follow the best of what You sent down, not the least of it, and gather us into the "before" while it is still ours to hold. And when the hour comes that we will not perceive, let it find us climbing toward the best, and not asleep on the floor. Āmīn.*
