# When the Touch of Harm Undoes Us

## A Reflection on Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 8

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the ayah three times, so it can settle into you slowly.

First, in the Arabic, the way it was revealed:

> [ayah:39:8] ۞ وَإِذَا مَسَّ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ ضُرٌّ دَعَا رَبَّهُۥ مُنِيبًا إِلَيْهِ ثُمَّ إِذَا خَوَّلَهُۥ نِعْمَةً مِّنْهُ نَسِىَ مَا كَانَ يَدْعُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهِ مِن قَبْلُ وَجَعَلَ لِلَّهِ أَندَادًا لِّيُضِلَّ عَن سَبِيلِهِۦ ۚ قُلْ تَمَتَّعْ بِكُفْرِكَ قَلِيلًا ۖ إِنَّكَ مِنْ أَصْحَـٰبِ ٱلنَّارِ

Now in transliteration, so you can hear its shape:

> *Wa idhā massa l-insāna ḍurrun daʿā rabbahu munīban ilayhi, thumma idhā khawwalahu niʿmatan minhu nasiya mā kāna yadʿū ilayhi min qabl, wa jaʿala lillāhi andādan li-yuḍilla ʿan sabīlih. Qul tamattaʿ bi-kufrika qalīlan, innaka min aṣḥābi n-nār.*

And now in plain English:

> *"And when adversity touches a human being, he calls upon his Lord, turning back to Him completely. Then when He grants him a favor from Himself, he forgets the One he used to call upon before, and he sets up rivals to Allah to lead others astray from His path. Say: Enjoy your disbelief a little while — you are among the companions of the Fire."*

### Let me rebuild the world this ayah is speaking into

Picture the moment in Makkah when these words first landed. The surah around them is obsessed with one question — not *whether* you worship, but *how purely*. Az-Zumar opens by demanding the religion be *khāliṣ*, undiluted, for Allah alone. It is a surah about sincerity the way a jeweler is obsessed with the purity of gold. Everything in it is testing for alloy. Testing for the cheaper metal mixed into the precious one.

And right before this ayah, Allah has painted Himself as the One who wraps the night over the day like a cloth folded over a cloth — total command over the cosmos. Right *after* this ayah, in the very next breath, He will show you a different kind of human being entirely: *amman huwa qānitun ānāʾa l-layl* — the one who stands devoutly through the watches of the night, prostrating, standing, fearing the next world and hoping for his Lord's mercy.

So this ayah — verse 8 — is the dark panel between two bright ones. It is the portrait Allah holds up *in between* the folding of the night and the standing of the worshipper. And the portrait is of someone painfully, embarrassingly familiar.

It is the portrait of us on our worst day.

### The Hook

Here is what I want you to notice before we even begin, and then I want you to let it bother you for the next hour.

Allah does not say *when a catastrophe crushes a human being.* He does not say *when disaster strikes* or *when ruin descends.* He uses a word so light, so gentle, that it almost embarrasses us when we understand it. He says harm *touches* him. Just touches. The faintest brush of a fingertip.

And from that mere touch — that brush — the human being turns his *entire being* back to God.

Then the favor comes. And something happens that the Arabic describes with a verb most translations quietly flatten into "gave." But the word is not "gave." The word is far stranger, and far more devastating, and it changes everything about how you will see every blessing you have ever held.

Stay with me. Let's go in.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: مَسَّ — When Harm Merely *Touches*

Before I show you the word, let me teach you the idea underneath it.

In any language, we have a whole spectrum of words for contact. *Crush. Strike. Slam. Hit. Press. Brush. Graze. Touch.* They are not interchangeable. A boxer's fist *slams.* A falling boulder *crushes.* But a feather *brushes.* A fingertip *touches.* When you choose a word from the gentle end of that spectrum, you are making a deliberate statement about how *little* force was involved.

Arabic has this same spectrum, and at the very gentlest end of it sits the verb in our ayah.

Here it is:

> وَإِذَا **مَسَّ** ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ ضُرٌّ
> *"And when harm* ***touches*** *the human being…"*

The root is *mīm-sīn-sīn* (م-س-س). And *massa* is the verb of the lightest possible contact. It is the word used when something merely makes contact with the skin. It is not *aṣāba* (to strike, to hit the mark). It is not *dahama* (to overwhelm). It is *massa* — to touch.

Let's sit with this.

Allah is describing the threshold of suffering at which a human being breaks and runs to God. And He sets that threshold *astonishingly low.* It is not that ruin has to flatten us. It is not that we have to lose everything. Harm merely has to *touch* us — *massa* — and we are already on our knees.

Notice something else. The word for the harm itself — *ḍurrun* — comes to us *indefinite.* In Arabic, leaving a noun indefinite like this, with that final *-un*, often carries the sense of *some* harm, *a* harm, *any* trace of harm. So the picture is not "when great affliction touches him." It is closer to: *"when so much as a touch of some harm brushes against him."*

A bad medical result that turns out to be nothing. A turbulent flight. A scare. A single sleepless night of worry. A delay. The phone call you were dreading. Harm hasn't even *landed* yet — it has only *touched* — and already the human heart, which an hour ago had no time for God, is fully turned toward Him.

[PAUSE]

Here's what is remarkable. This light word is not an insult. It is a diagnosis, and a merciful one. Allah is telling you something true about your own design: **you are built so that the faintest touch of pain points you straight home.** The instinct is good. The instinct is right. The needle of your heart swings true to God the moment the ground shakes even slightly beneath you.

The tragedy of this ayah is not the turning *toward.* The tragedy is everything that comes *after.* And the gentleness of *massa* is what sets the trap — because if it takes so *little* harm to turn us to God, what does it say about us that it takes so little comfort to turn us *away*?

Hold that question. We're coming back to it.

### Element Two: مُنِيبًا — Not Just Calling, But *Turning the Whole Self Around*

Now look at *how* he calls upon his Lord in that moment of harm. Allah does not leave it at *daʿā rabbahu* — "he called upon his Lord." He adds one word:

> دَعَا رَبَّهُۥ **مُنِيبًا** إِلَيْهِ
> *"he calls upon his Lord,* ***munīban*** *to Him."*

Let me teach you the grammar first, because the grammar *is* the meaning here.

In Arabic, you can describe an action, and then add a word that describes *the state the doer is in while doing it.* Grammarians call this the *ḥāl* — the circumstantial state. In English we'd say: "She left the room *crying.*" The leaving is the action; the *crying* tells you her condition as she left. The two happen together, fused.

So here, the action is *daʿā* — he called out. And the *ḥāl*, the condition fused to that calling, is *munīban.* He didn't just call. He called *in a state of munīb.*

Now — what is *munīb*?

The root is *nūn-wāw-bā* (ن-و-ب), and the word *munīb* is built on the fourth verbal pattern of Arabic, from *anāba.* The classical commentators are tightly agreed on its meaning. Al-Jalālayn glosses it simply: *rājiʿan* — "returning." Al-Qurṭubī expands it beautifully: *rājiʿan ilayhi, mukhbitan, muṭīʿan lahu, mustaghīthan bihi* — "returning to Him, humbled before Him, obedient to Him, crying to Him for rescue." Al-Baghawī echoes it: *rājiʿan ilayhi mustaghīthan bihi* — "returning to Him, seeking aid from Him."

But *anāba* is more than "to return." It is to return *the way a person returns who has been gone a long time and finally comes home.* It is the turning-back of the *whole self* — body, will, and face — pivoting around to face the One you had your back to.

Let's sit with the full force of this.

A moment ago — before the harm touched him — where was this person's face turned? Away. Distracted. Toward a thousand other things. And now harm has merely *brushed* him, and in an instant the entire human being *spins on his heel* and is facing God with his whole heart, *crying out, humbled, pleading, obedient.* The word *munīban* contains all of that. It is not a polite prayer. It is the total reorientation of a lost person who suddenly remembers where home is.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this tells us. In the moment of *munīb*, this person is doing tawhīd *perfectly.* For that one trembling moment, he has no rivals to God in his heart — none. There is no idol, no false hope, no backup plan, no rich uncle, no insurance policy, no false god he half-trusts. There is *only Allah,* and his whole face is turned to Him alone.

That is the cruel irony the ayah is building. **This person already knows how to be a perfect monotheist.** He has done it. He achieves flawless sincerity — *the very ikhlāṣ this entire surah is demanding* — in the foxhole. He proves, in the moment of fear, that the pure gold is *in* him.

So when he abandons it the moment the danger passes, he cannot plead ignorance. He has tasted the real thing. He knows the address of home. He simply chooses, when the weather clears, to walk back out the door.

And what makes him walk back out? That brings us to the most important word in the ayah.

### Element Three: خَوَّلَهُ — The Gift That Was Never Yours to Keep

This is the word the whole ayah turns on. And it is the word almost every translation softens into something it does not mean.

Here it is:

> ثُمَّ إِذَا **خَوَّلَهُۥ** نِعْمَةً مِّنْهُ
> *"Then when He* ***khawwala*** *him a favor from Himself…"*

Most translations render it: *"when He gives him a favor"* or *"bestows on him a blessing."* And that is not wrong, exactly — but it is like translating a specific, technical word with a vague, general one. It loses the whole point.

The root is *khāʾ-wāw-lām* (خ-و-ل), and it is built on the second, intensive verbal pattern: *khawwala.* Let me show you what this root actually carries, because the classical Arabic is precise and stunning.

Al-Qurṭubī, in his commentary, does something for us. He pauses on this exact word and explains: *yuqālu khawwalaka llāhu sh-shayʾa ay mallakaka iyyāh* — "it is said *khawwalaka llāhu* the thing, meaning *He put it under your charge.*" And then he reaches for a line of ancient Arabic poetry to nail the meaning down — a verse he attributes to the recitation of Abū ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAlāʾ:

*"There, if they are given charge of wealth (*yastakhwilū l-māl*), they manage it well…"*

And here is the key. Al-Qurṭubī then tells us the related meaning of the root: *wa khawalu r-rajul: ḥashamuhu* — "a man's *khawal* are his servants, his dependents, those placed under his care and management." A single one of them is a *khāʾil* — a steward, a caretaker, a manager of something that belongs to another.

Do you feel the word turning under your hand now?

*Khawwala* does not mean simply "to hand over and walk away." It carries the sense of **putting something into your charge** — entrusting you with the *management* of something *while the real ownership remains elsewhere.* It is the language of stewardship. It is what an owner does when he places his estate, his wealth, his servants, into the hands of a trusted manager. The manager holds it, uses it, benefits from it — but he does not *own* it. He is a *khāʾil.* A steward.

[PAUSE — let this land]

So read the ayah again, now, with the real weight of the word:

*"Then when He puts a favor into his charge, as a trust from Himself…"*

The health you walk around in — *khawwala.* You were given charge of it. The wealth in your account — *khawwala.* You manage it; you do not own it. The children asleep in the next room — *khawwala.* The years of your life, your intelligence, your beauty, your strength, the very breath moving in and out of your lungs right now as you listen — every single one of them: *khawwala.* Placed in your charge. On loan. From Him. *minhu* — "from Himself," the ayah insists, so you cannot mistake the source.

And now watch what the human being does with the trust.

> **نَسِىَ** مَا كَانَ يَدْعُوٓاْ إِلَيْهِ مِن قَبْلُ
> *"he* ***forgets*** *the One he used to call upon before."*

He forgets. *Nasiya.* The steward forgets the Owner. The man entrusted with the estate begins to walk its grounds as though he built them. He starts to say *my* health, *my* wealth, *my* success — as though the loan were a possession, as though the trust were a title deed.

And here the commentators open a precise and beautiful subtlety. *Nasiya mā* — he forgot *what?* The Arabic uses *mā* — "what" — and the scholars read it two complementary ways, and I want you to hold both, because together they are a complete picture.

Al-Jalālayn reads the *mā* as standing in for *man* — "the One." He forgot *the One he used to call upon* — that is, he forgot **Allah Himself.** The very Lord he was clinging to an hour ago, he now cannot locate in his heart.

Al-Baghawī reads it slightly differently: *nasiya ḍ-ḍurra alladhī kāna yadʿū llāha ilā kashfihi* — "he forgot **the very harm** that he had been begging Allah to lift." He forgot the *crisis itself.* He forgot how desperate he was. He forgot the foxhole the moment he climbed out of it.

Put the two readings together and you have the whole tragedy of the human heart: **he forgot God, and he forgot how badly he needed God.** The memory of the rescue and the memory of the Rescuer vanish *together,* the instant the rescue is complete. The steward, the moment the estate is comfortable, forgets both the Owner *and* the storm he was just saved from.

[PAUSE]

And then comes the final descent. Having forgotten the Owner, what does the steward do? *Wa jaʿala lillāhi andādan* — "he sets up rivals to Allah." *Andād* — equals, peers, rivals. He who, in the foxhole, had achieved *perfect* tawhīd — only Allah, no rivals at all — now, in his comfort, *manufactures* rivals. He installs other lords in the throne room of his heart that he had, for one shining moment, swept completely clean.

He had it. He had pure gold in his hands. And he traded it for alloy the moment he stopped being afraid.

### The Bridge

So look at what these three words, laid end to end, have shown us.

*Massa* — it takes only a *touch* of harm to turn him fully to God.
*Munīban* — and when he turns, he turns *completely,* achieving perfect sincerity.
*Khawwala* — but the moment God places a blessing *in his charge as a trust,* he mistakes the trust for a possession, forgets the Owner, and forgets the storm.

This is not a story about a uniquely evil person. The grammar will not let us off that easily. The ayah says *al-insān* — *the human being.* Humanity as such. This is a description of a tendency wired into the clay we are made from.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: Comfort Amnesia — Why We Pray in the Storm and Forget in the Sun

Let me start where you already live.

There is a phenomenon soldiers have a phrase for: *"There are no atheists in foxholes."* When the shells are landing and death is a coin-flip away, even the man who scoffed at prayer his whole life finds his lips moving. Psychologists have studied it. Pilots report it. Cancer wards are full of it. The human being, under the touch of mortal fear, reaches reflexively upward.

But here is the part nobody puts on the poster. The same studies, the same observations, find the *other* half — the half this ayah is really about. The soldier who prayed in the foxhole, six months later, back home, safe — *has forgotten he ever prayed at all.* The patient who wept to God at 3 a.m. before the surgery, by the time of the follow-up appointment where everything is fine, has quietly returned to a life with no room in it for God.

There is a name in modern psychology for the engine underneath this. They call it *hedonic adaptation* — the relentless tendency of the human mind to absorb any new blessing into the background, to take the extraordinary and file it under "normal" within weeks. The raise that thrilled you becomes your baseline salary you now resent. The home you dreamed of becomes the house you complain about. The recovery you begged God for becomes the health you no longer thank Him for, because you have forgotten you were ever sick.

The mind is a machine for forgetting its gifts. That is what hedonic adaptation *is.* And this ayah named it fourteen hundred years before psychology gave it a label.

[PAUSE]

But the Qur'an goes deeper than psychology, because psychology describes the *mechanism* and stops. The ayah describes the *betrayal.*

Watch the structure Allah built into the verse. In the harm, the prayer is *immediate* — *idhā massa… daʿā.* The instant harm touches, the call goes up. No delay. But look at the gift: *thumma idhā khawwalahu… nasiya.* The word *thumma* — "then" — introduces a gap, a stretch of time. The forgetting is not instant. It *seeps in.* It happens slowly, across the comfortable days, one ungrateful sunrise at a time, until one morning the man wakes and the God he clung to is simply… gone from his heart. He didn't decide to forget. He *drifted* into forgetting, on the gentle current of his own comfort.

This is the terror hidden in the gentleness of *massa.* Remember the question I asked you to hold? If it takes so *little* harm — a mere *touch* — to turn us to God, then the symmetry is monstrous: it takes only a little *comfort* to turn us away. We are exquisitely sensitive to pain and catastrophically numb to blessing. A splinter gets our full attention; a working body of thirty trillion cooperating cells gets none.

And here is where the ayah indicts us — gently, but unmistakably. Because most of us hearing this are *not* the disbeliever who sets up literal idols. We will never carve a statue. But ask yourself honestly: How did you pray during the worst week of your life? With what intensity, what humility, what *munīb*-turning of your whole self? And how do you pray now, on an ordinary comfortable Tuesday when nothing is wrong?

If there is a gap between those two — and for nearly all of us there is a canyon — then the ayah is not describing a stranger. It is describing the *direction* our own hearts drift the moment the weather clears. The disbeliever in the ayah is simply the man who let the drift go all the way to its destination. We are on the same road. We have just not walked as far.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practical seed. The cure for comfort amnesia is not waiting for the next disaster to remind you. The cure is to **manufacture remembrance inside the comfort** — to do deliberately, in the sunshine, what crisis does to you automatically in the storm. This is precisely why the next ayah (39:9) shows you the worshipper *standing in the night* — choosing fear and hope *voluntarily,* in the dark, when nothing is forcing him. He has learned to pray like a man in a foxhole *while sitting in a garden.*

Tonight, before you sleep, name three blessings you have completely stopped noticing — three things you begged God for once, or would beg Him for if they were taken. Your sight. A person. A roof. Say them out loud, as specific gratitude. That is the whole discipline: to remember the gift *before* its loss has to remind you. To turn to Him in the garden, so that you are not one of those who only knows the way home from the foxhole.

### Theme Two: The Steward Who Forgot He Was a Steward

Now let me take you back to that astonishing word — *khawwala* — and live inside it for a while, because it holds a second, separate cure for the disease the first theme diagnosed.

Imagine you are handed the keys to a magnificent estate. A letter from the owner explains: *This is yours to manage. Live in it. Enjoy its gardens, eat from its orchards, rest in its halls. Care for it well. I will return.* You are a *khāʾil* — a steward. The word from our ayah's own root.

Now there are two ways to live in that estate.

The first steward never forgets the letter. Every morning he walks the grounds with a quiet gratitude, because every beautiful thing he sees reminds him of the generosity of the One who entrusted it to him. The luxury *increases* his connection to the owner, because each gift is a message *from* the owner. He enjoys the estate *more,* not less, because every pleasure in it is wrapped in the warmth of being trusted and loved.

The second steward, after a few comfortable months, loses the letter behind a drawer. And slowly, imperceptibly, he begins to believe he *built* the estate. He starts saying *my* gardens, *my* halls. He resents the idea of any owner. The thought that someone might *return* and ask for an account begins to feel like a threat, an insult. The very gifts that should have bound him to the owner in gratitude have instead become the *evidence* he uses to deny the owner exists.

[PAUSE]

That second steward is the human being in our ayah. And here is the devastating insight the word *khawwala* unlocks: **the blessing itself becomes the instrument of the forgetting.** It is not that he forgot God *despite* the favor. He forgot God *by means of* the favor. The gift was supposed to be a string tying his heart back to the Giver. Instead he cut the string and kept the gift, and now holds it up as proof he never needed a Giver at all.

This is why the ayah is so insistent on that one small word — *minhu.* "A favor *from Himself.*" Allah will not let the steward pretend the source is murky. The label is stamped on the gift. *From Me.* The provenance is undeniable. Which means the forgetting is never innocent. It is a deliberate looking-away from a label written in plain sight.

And do you see how this reframes everything you own? You have been calling things *mine* your whole life. Your time. Your talent. Your money. Your body. The ayah, through this single word, quietly corrects the deed on every one of them. None of it is *milk* — none of it is owned. All of it is *khawwala* — entrusted. You are a steward walking through an estate, holding a letter you must not lose.

[PAUSE]

Notice what this does to the two great temptations of the human heart — *pride* in good times and *despair* in bad ones. The steward's mindset dissolves both. You cannot be arrogant about wealth that was only ever *placed in your charge* — arrogance about a loan is absurd. And you cannot fully despair when wealth is taken, because the Owner only reclaimed what was always His; He is *re-arranging His own estate,* not robbing you of yours. The man who truly internalizes *khawwala* becomes un-spoilable by comfort and un-breakable by loss. Both, because he never confused the trust with a possession.

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the second seed, and it pairs with the first. Tonight, take the three blessings you named — and instead of just thanking God for them, do one harder thing: **mentally hand them back.** Say, of each one: *This was never mine. It is Yours, placed in my charge. Use me as a good steward of it while it is with me, and take it back whenever You will.* 

This is what transforms a possession into a trust in your own heart. The wealth you steward generously, because it is not yours to hoard. The body you guard from sin, because it is not yours to defile. The children you raise toward Him, because they are not yours to keep. The whole anxious grip of *ownership* loosens, and underneath it you find something the second steward never knew: the deep, unshakeable peace of a person who is holding something precious *on behalf of Someone he trusts completely.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Step back now and see the whole shape of it.

The ayah moves in a single, unbroken arc of decline. *Massa* — harm only *touches* him, and that is enough to turn him fully to God. *Munīban* — he turns *completely,* achieving, in his fear, the perfect sincerity this entire surah is demanding. He holds pure gold. Then *khawwala* — God places a blessing in his charge as a trust. And *nasiya* — across the comfortable days, the steward forgets the Owner *and* forgets the storm, until finally he sets up *rivals* in the very heart he had, an hour before, swept perfectly clean.

The two themes are the same insight from two directions. *Comfort amnesia* is the **disease** — the heart's tragic tendency to forget the Giver the moment the gift arrives. *Stewardship* is the **cure** — the discipline of holding every gift as a labeled trust *from Him,* so that the gift becomes a string pulling you toward God instead of a wall blocking Him from view.

And both pivot on the same hinge: **whether the blessing connects you to the Giver or replaces Him.** That is the entire test of the surah. That is the alloy the jeweler is testing for. Pure gold: the gift makes you remember. Alloy: the gift makes you forget.

### The Last Words — *Qul tamattaʿ bi-kufrika qalīlan*

We have not yet sat with the ending, and we should, briefly, because it is shattering.

After describing all of this, Allah turns to His Prophet ﷺ and commands him to deliver a single line to such a person: *Qul tamattaʿ bi-kufrika qalīlan* — *"Say: Enjoy your disbelief a little while."*

The verb *tamattaʿ* — "enjoy yourself" — is an imperative. A command. But it is the most bitter command in the language, because it is a command no mercy is left in. To tell someone *"go ahead, enjoy"* is what you say when the time for warning is over. The classical commentators — al-Jalālayn, al-Baghawī, al-Muyassar — all read *qalīlan,* "a little while," as *the brief remainder of your life span.* Enjoy the loan a little longer. The estate you forgot was entrusted to you — keep walking its gardens a few more days. *Innaka min aṣḥābi n-nār* — *"you are among the companions of the Fire."*

The steward who spent his whole life pretending he owned the estate will, in the end, be shown the truth he spent that life looking away from. The note will be called in. The accounting will come.

(One note for honesty: the commentators preserve more than one tradition about *who* this ayah first described. Al-Baghawī reports it was said to be revealed concerning ʿUtba ibn Rabīʿa, while Muqātil named another. The narrations differ, and the Qur'an's address — *al-insān,* "the human being" — lifts it above any single man and lays it at the door of every heart, including yours and mine.)

### Questions to Carry

Carry these out of here. Don't answer them now. Let them work on you.

1. How did I pray during the hardest week of my life — and how am I praying today? What does the gap between those two tell me about which direction my heart drifts?
2. Which of my blessings have I quietly relabeled from *trust* to *possession* — saying "mine" about things only placed in my charge?
3. Is there a gift in my life right now that is pulling me *toward* God in gratitude — or one that has become a wall I hide behind from Him?
4. If the Owner returned today and asked for an account of the estate, which steward would He find me to be?
5. What would it look like to pray in the garden the way I once prayed in the foxhole — to remember Him *before* the next loss forces me to?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The faintest touch of harm turns the whole human being to God in perfect sincerity, yet the slow comfort of a gift he mistook for a possession makes him forget both the Giver and the storm — and the only cure is to hold every blessing as a trust *from Him,* so that the gift becomes a road back to God rather than a wall against Him.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't need a catastrophe to find God. You proved that already — your heart knows the way home; it swings true under the lightest touch of fear. The invitation of this ayah is simply this: don't wait for the storm to remember the address. Learn to turn to Him in the calm. Hold what you've been given with an open hand and a remembering heart. Be the first steward — the one who never lost the letter.

### A Brief Du'ā

O Allah, You who placed every blessing in our charge as a trust from Yourself — do not let the gift make us forget the Giver. Keep us turning to You, *munīb,* not only in the storm but in the stillness. And let every favor You entrust to us be a road that leads us back to You, never a wall that hides You from our eyes. Āmīn.
