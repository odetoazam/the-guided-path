# The Desert Named for the Arrival

### Surah Az-Zumar, Ayah 61

> وَيُنَجِّى ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱتَّقَوْا بِمَفَازَتِهِمْ لَا يَمَسُّهُمُ ٱلسُّوٓءُ وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ

*Wa yunajjī llāhu lladhīna ttaqaw bi-mafāzatihim — lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾu wa lā hum yaḥzanūn.*

*"And Allah will deliver those who were mindful of Him to their place of triumph — no evil will touch them, nor will they grieve."*

---

## INTRODUCTION: Rebuilding the World

Before we can hear this verse, we have to stand where its first listeners stood.

The Arabs who first heard these words were a people of the road. Not the road as we know it — paved, lit, signed. Their road was the open land between the settled places: the great stretches of nothing that lay between one well and the next, between one tribe's territory and another's. They had a word for that emptiness. And the word is the entire secret of this verse.

But hold that for a moment. First, feel the geography of the surah itself.

Surah Az-Zumar is named for *throngs* — crowds, processions, groups driven forward in great moving masses. The whole surah is built on a sorting. From its opening it keeps separating humanity into two streams: those who turn to Allah with sincerity, and those who turn away. The two streams never quite touch. They run in parallel through the whole surah, and at the very end of the surah they arrive — one throng driven toward the Fire, one throng led toward the Garden, each in its own procession (39:71–73).

Our verse sits at a hinge. Just before it, in verse 60, Allah shows us those who lied against Him — and He shows us their *faces*, darkened, blackened by what they carried. It is a verse about the surface of a person, the face that grief and shame rise to.

And then, verse 61. The counter-image. But notice — Allah does not now describe the faces of the righteous. He could have. The symmetry would have been neat: their faces blackened, their faces brightened. He does that elsewhere. But here, the opposite of a blackened face is not a bright face.

The opposite of being exposed is being *delivered*.

[PAUSE]

So here is the hook. In a single short sentence — nine words in Arabic — Allah is going to tell the God-conscious four things. He is going to tell them *who acts* on their behalf. He is going to tell them *where they arrive* — using a word that should stop us cold, because it is the same word those desert travelers used for the deadly emptiness they feared most. And then He is going to seal off, with two separate negations, the two entire categories of human suffering. Not one. Both. The suffering that comes *at* you from outside, and the suffering that rises *within* you from inside.

By the end, the question I want you holding is this: **Why would Allah name the destination of the saved with the very same word the Arabs used for the wilderness that kills?**

Let us earn the answer.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yunajjī* — When God Does the Rescuing, Thoroughly

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we touch the word.

In Arabic, a verb is not a fixed thing. It is a skeleton — three consonants — and you can pour that skeleton into different *moulds*, and each mould changes the meaning in a predictable way. Scholars number these moulds. Form I is the bare verb. Form II doubles the middle consonant — and that doubling almost always does one of two things: it either makes the verb *transitive* (it now reaches out and acts on someone), or it *intensifies* the action, makes it thorough, repeated, complete. Often it does both at once.

Here is an everyday picture. In English we have "to learn" and "to teach." They share a core — knowledge moving — but "teach" is "learn" turned outward and made to land on someone else. Arabic does that *with the shape of the word itself*. The doubled middle letter is the sound of an action being pushed all the way through to its target.

Now the word.

The root here is **n-j-w (نجو)**. In its bare form, *najā*, it means: *to be saved, to escape, to come out safe.* It is intransitive — it describes someone slipping free of danger. A man who escapes a flood — *najā*. It happens to him; he comes through.

But Allah does not say *najā*. He says **يُنَجِّى — *yunajjī***. Form II. The doubled middle letter. The mould of *make-it-reach, make-it-thorough.*

Sit with what that does.

It takes "to come out safe" and it turns it into **"to bring all the way out to safety"** — and it makes the one doing the bringing the grammatical subject. So the verse does not say the God-conscious *escape*. It does not say they *come through*. It says: **Allah delivers them.** The verb has been rebuilt so that the action originates in *Him* and lands on *them*.

And the doubling carries the second freight too: not a near-miss escape, not a scramble out of danger by the skin of the teeth — a *complete* deliverance. All the way. Nothing left behind in the danger.

[PAUSE]

Notice something the Arabic does that translation hides. There is another mould Allah could have used for "to save" — Form IV, *anjā* — and He uses it often elsewhere in the Quran. Both forms can mean "to rescue." But Form II, *najjā*, with its doubled, pressed-down middle letter, carries a weight of *thoroughness* that the lexicons associate with the shape itself. The very texture of the word — that held, doubled *j* — is the sound of a rescue that takes its time, that does not let go halfway.

Now here is the detail I most want you to hold. Look at the tense of the two verbs in the first half of the verse.

*Allah delivers* — **yunajjī** — is in the **imperfect**. The form Arabic uses for the ongoing, the continuous, the not-yet-finished, the certain-future. It stretches forward.

*Those who were mindful* — **ittaqaw** — is in the **perfect**. Completed. Done. Finished and sealed.

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the architecture in that?

**Their part is in the past tense. His part is in the future tense.**

The taqwa — the guarding, the God-consciousness — *that* is something they finished in the world. It is over. It happened in the short, closed span of a lifetime, in the perfect tense, sealed shut at death. But the deliverance — that opens forward and never closes. They did a finite, completed thing. He answers it with an unfinished, unfolding, eternal thing.

The grammar is the whole theology of grace in miniature. A closed past act of the servant. An open future act of God, immeasurably larger.

**That is what this one verb reveals: salvation here is not something the righteous achieve. It is something done *to* them, thoroughly, by a Subject named in the very next word — Allah Himself.**

### Element Two: *Bi-Mafāzatihim* — The Word for the Wilderness Is the Word for the Win

Now we come to the heart of the verse. And to do it justice, I have to take you back out into that desert.

The root is **f-w-z (فوز)**. Its meaning is *triumph, success, winning, attaining what you longed for.* It is one of the great words of the Quran's vision of the end — *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm*, the supreme triumph. To *fāza* is not merely to pass a test. It is to *win* — to reach the thing you set out for and to hold it.

From this root comes the noun in our verse: **مَفَازَة — *mafāza***. The form *maf-ʿala* in Arabic typically names a *place* or a *cause* of the action in the root. So *mafāza* should mean, plainly, **the place of triumph** — or **the means of triumph**. "Allah delivers them *bi-mafāzatihim*" — *to*, or *by means of*, their place-of-winning.

So far, clean. Beautiful, even. But now I have to tell you the other thing this word means. The thing that makes the hair stand up.

**In the language of the Arabs, *al-mafāza* is the name for the desert.**

The waterless waste. The killing emptiness between the wells. The stretch of land that travelers feared above almost anything, because to be caught in it was to die of thirst. *That* terrible place — they called it *al-mafāza*. The place of triumph.

[PAUSE]

Why on earth would a people name the most dangerous ground they knew after *winning*?

The lexicographers preserve the answer, and it is one of the most revealing things in the whole Arabic language. They named it *mafāza* **optimistically** — out of *tafāʾul*, the deliberate practice of naming a feared thing by the good outcome you hope for. They did not call the desert "the place of death," though that is what it so often was. They called it "the place of triumph" — because the one who *crosses* it, who comes out the far side, has *won*. He has *fāza*. To name the wilderness for its danger is to surrender to it. To name it for the arrival is to declare, before you even set out, what you intend.

So the word held a tension inside it the whole time. *Mafāza*: the deadly emptiness — and the triumph of crossing it. The same word for the road and for the reward. The same sound for the thing that might kill you and the thing you'll have if it doesn't.

Now read the verse again with that in your ears.

*Wa yunajjī llāhu lladhīna ttaqaw **bi-mafāzatihim***.

"And Allah delivers those who were mindful of Him **to their mafāza**."

[PAUSE]

Do you see what has happened? Allah has taken the word that the Arabs used *hopefully*, *optimistically*, *as a brave name spoken over a dangerous place* — and He has made it **real**. For everyone else, *mafāza* was a hope whispered over the sand: *may this killing waste turn out to be a place of triumph.* For the God-conscious, Allah declares it a *fact*. Their *mafāza* is not a hopeful name for a dangerous road. It is the genuine, achieved, arrived-at **triumph**. The brave name has become the true name.

This is the *istidlāl* — the pointing of the seen at the unseen. The physical thing is the desert, the crossing, the wilderness every traveler knew in his body. And what it points to is the whole of this life: a crossing of dangerous, waterless ground, named hopefully for an arrival nobody can yet see. The believer walks the dunya the way the traveler walked the *mafāza* — provisioning for it, fearing its emptiness, fixed on the far edge.

And remember what the Quran tells us to pack for that crossing: *"Take provision — and the best provision is **taqwa**"* (2:197). The very same word that opens our verse. **Those who were mindful** — *alladhīna ttaqaw* — are precisely the ones who provisioned for the wilderness. And so it is *they*, and not the empty-handed, whom Allah brings out the far side to the triumph the word always promised.

[PAUSE]

One more layer, and this one is for the careful among you. The classical reciters differ on this very word — and the difference is itself a teaching. The majority read it singular: *bi-mafāzati**him*** — "to their *place* of triumph," one triumph, the destination. But a group of the Kufan reciters — Ḥamza, al-Kisāʾī, and Abū Bakr, as al-Baghawī and al-Qurṭubī record — read it plural: *bi-mafāzā**ti**him*** — "to their *paths* of triumph," the many ways and means by which they are brought through to safety. Al-Mubarrad, the grammarian, notes that the plural is perfectly sound, *mafāza* being a *maf-ʿala* noun from *fawz* — just as *saʿāda* (felicity) pluralizes to *saʿādāt*.

Do not choose between them. Hold both as two camera angles on one reality. One reading shows you **the destination** — the single, final triumph they arrive at. The other shows you **the roads** — the countless means by which Allah threads them safely through. Together: many paths, one arrival. The whole crossing, and the far edge.

**That is what this single word reveals: this life is the wilderness the ancients bravely named for a triumph they could only hope for — and for the God-conscious, Allah turns the hopeful name into the true one.**

[PAUSE — major]

### Element Three: The Two Negations — *Lā Yamassuhum* and *Wa Lā Hum Yaḥzanūn*

We have *who* delivers them, and *to what* they are delivered. Now Allah does something with the second half of the verse that I need you to see in its grammar, because the grammar is doing the consoling.

He gives us **two separate negations.** Not one negation covering everything. Two. And the two are not redundant — they fence off two completely different territories of pain.

First: **لَا يَمَسُّهُمُ ٱلسُّوٓءُ — *lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾu*** — "no evil **touches** them."

Teach yourself the verb. The root is **m-s-s (مسس)**, and it means *to touch* — the lightest possible contact. Not to strike, not to wound, not to crush. To *touch*. It is the most delicate verb of contact in the language. The Quran uses it precisely for the faintest brush of something: the *touch* of hardship, the *touch* of Shayṭān, the *touch* of fire.

And *that* is the verb Allah negates. He does not say "evil will not *destroy* them" or "evil will not *overwhelm* them." Those would be lesser promises, because they would leave the door open to being *grazed*. He negates the *touch* itself. **Not even the lightest brush of evil reaches them.** *Al-sūʾ* — the evil, the harm, every bad thing — is held so far off that it cannot so much as make contact.

This is the suffering that comes **at you**. From outside. The harm in the world: pain, loss, fire, fear of what might befall. Allah seals it off at the skin. Nothing touches.

[PAUSE]

But here is what a lesser speaker would have stopped at. A lesser promise would say: *you will be safe.* No harm will reach you. And we would have thought: *enough — what more is there?*

There is more. Because there is a kind of suffering that no external safety can cure. You can be perfectly safe and still be in agony. You can be untouched by any harm and still be drowning. The suffering that does not come *at* you from the world — the suffering that rises *within* you, from your own memory, your own losses, your own grief.

So Allah adds the second negation: **وَلَا هُمْ يَحْزَنُونَ — *wa lā hum yaḥzanūn*** — "nor will they grieve."

The root is **ḥ-z-n (حزن)** — *ḥuzn*, grief, sorrow, the inward ache. And notice — *notice* — the grammar of this clause is different from the first. In the first negation, the verb came first: *lā yamassuhum* (no-touches-them). But in the second, Allah does not say *wa lā yaḥzanūn*. He inserts a pronoun and **fronts** it: *wa lā **hum** yaḥzanūn* — "and not **they** — they shall not grieve."

In Arabic, when you pull a pronoun forward like that and place it before its verb, you are pressing down on it. You are saying: *these ones, specifically, particularly, as for them* — grief does not come near. It is emphasis by word order. The fronted *hum* puts a ring around them. **Whoever else grieves — not these.**

[PAUSE]

Now feel why the two negations had to be two.

*Lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾ* closes the door on **the future and the outside** — no harm will reach them, nothing bad is coming at them.

*Wa lā hum yaḥzanūn* closes the door on **the past and the inside** — no sorrow rises within them, nothing lost weighs on them. Because grief, *ḥuzn*, is almost always grief over something *gone* — a lost person, a lost chance, a lost time. The mufassirūn note exactly this: al-Muyassar glosses it as grief "over the shares of this world that passed them by." The looking-back ache.

So between the two negations, Allah has accounted for the entire span of human suffering. **Harm from ahead. Grief from behind. The world coming at you. The past rising within you.** He does not leave a single direction open. The fear of what might come — sealed. The sorrow of what has gone — sealed.

This is the completeness our modern words grope at. We split suffering into *anxiety* — the dread of the harm not yet arrived — and *depression* — the weight of the loss already sustained. Allah named both, and negated both, fourteen centuries before we built clinics for them. The touch that never comes. The grief that never rises.

**That is what these two clauses reveal: deliverance is not merely safety from harm. It is the sealing of both doors through which suffering enters a human being — and Allah, in one breath, shuts them both.**

### Bridge

Look at what the language has shown us. A verb rebuilt so that *God* is the rescuer and the rescue is *thorough*. A noun that holds the killing wilderness and the won triumph in a single word. And two negations, fitted together like a sealed vault, one for the harm ahead and one for the grief behind.

Now let us ask what all of this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of a human life, and the two sufferings that govern it.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Two Sufferings — What Comes At You, and What Rises In You

Let me begin where you already live.

Think of a night you could not sleep. Not because of noise, not because of pain — your body was fine, your room was safe, the door was locked. And yet you lay there, and two different tides came in.

One tide was *forward*: the meeting tomorrow, the test result, the bill, the conversation you're dreading, the thousand ways the future could go wrong. A bracing, a clenching against something that hasn't happened yet. That is one suffering.

And then, often in the same night, the tide turns and runs *backward*: the thing you said years ago, the person you lost, the version of your life that didn't happen, the door that closed. A heaviness, a sinking, an ache over what is already gone and cannot be changed. That is a *different* suffering.

[PAUSE]

We treat these as one thing — "feeling bad," "stress," "a hard night." But they are not one thing. They face opposite directions. One faces the future and braces. One faces the past and sinks. Modern psychology eventually had to split them, give them different names, treat them in different rooms. The dread of what's coming. The grief of what's gone.

And here is the thing I most want you to see: **almost everything we do to escape suffering only ever addresses one of the two.**

We build security against the future — savings, insurance, plans, control — and we lie awake grieving the past anyway, untouched by all our safety. Or we make peace with the past, we heal an old wound, we forgive — and we still lie awake bracing against tomorrow. We patch one door and the suffering comes in through the other. No human arrangement has ever sealed both. The safest person you know still grieves. The most at-peace person you know still fears.

Now hear the verse again.

*"No evil will **touch** them — nor will **they** grieve."*

[PAUSE]

Allah did not address suffering in general. He performed surgery on it. He took the whole of human misery and He cut it cleanly into its two real halves — the harm that comes *at* you, and the grief that rises *in* you — and He sealed each one with its own clause. *Lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾ:* the future door, bolted. *Wa lā hum yaḥzanūn:* the past door, bolted. The very two doors that no human effort has ever managed to close at the same time.

And look *who* the verse promises this to. Not the powerful. Not the wealthy. Not the ones who built the strongest defenses in this world. **Alladhīna ttaqaw** — those who were *mindful*, who *guarded* themselves, who carried *taqwa*. The deliverance from both sufferings is not engineered. It is *granted* — by the same God who, the grammar told us, does the delivering Himself, thoroughly, all the way.

Here is what changes about tomorrow. You will still have nights with two tides. You are not promised that the doors are sealed *now*; the full sealing belongs to the *mafāza*, the far edge of the crossing. But you are told something about the direction of your whole life: that there is a destination where *both* doors close at once, and that the road to it is not control over the future or mastery of the past — it is *taqwa*, mindfulness of the One who holds both. So tonight, when the forward tide comes, you can say: *the One who will seal that door holds tomorrow already.* And when the backward tide comes: *the One who will seal that door has not lost what I lost.* You are not asked to stop feeling the tides. You are asked to remember Who stands at both doors.

[PAUSE — major]

### Theme Two: Living in the Wilderness That Is Named for the Arrival

Now the second theme. Come back to the desert.

Here is a question worth sitting with: *what does it do to you — to your daily walking — to know what a place is named for?*

Imagine two travelers setting out across the same waterless waste. The first one knows it as "the killing ground." Every step is a step deeper into the thing that wants him dead. The second one knows it as *al-mafāza* — "the place of triumph" — because that is what the Arabs named it: not for its danger, but for the *arrival* of the one who crosses it. Same sand. Same thirst. Same distance. But one man is walking through *death*, and the other is walking toward *winning*. The land has not changed. The *name* has changed. And the name changes the man.

[PAUSE]

That is a picture of your life, and the choice inside it.

This world is, by any honest accounting, a wilderness. It is hard ground. It is waterless in the ways that matter most — it cannot finally quench you, no matter how much of it you drink; the Prophet ﷺ likened the dunya's hold to a traveler who shelters under a tree and then moves on. People die of thirst out here all the time — chasing the next well, the next acquisition, the next mirage of *enough*, and never arriving. By every external measure, the dunya is the *mafāza* in its frightening meaning: the empty, dangerous waste.

But the Arabs, in their wisdom, refused to name it for the danger. They named it for the *arrival*. *Mafāza* — the place of triumph — because the whole meaning of a crossing is not the sand, it is the *far edge*. And what was for them a brave hope, Allah makes for the believer a *certainty*: *yunajjī llāhu lladhīna ttaqaw bi-mafāzatihim.* He brings the God-conscious through, *to their triumph.* The wilderness was named for the arrival all along — and for the believer, the name comes true.

So how do you cross a wilderness?

You do not unpack and live in it. The traveler who forgets he is crossing — who builds a house in the middle of the *mafāza* and calls the sand home — is the one who dies in it. The whole danger of the dunya is forgetting that it is a *crossing* and not a *destination*.

And you do not cross it empty-handed. *"Take provision — and the best provision is taqwa"* (2:197). The verse names your provision in its third word: *alladhīna ttaqaw.* Taqwa is not a feeling. The word itself, from the root *w-q-y*, means to make for yourself a *shield*, a *guard* — the Arabs used it for what a man puts between himself and what would harm him. To have taqwa is to be *provisioned* for the crossing: to be carrying the water, mindful of the One who placed the wells, refusing to mistake the wilderness for home.

[PAUSE]

And notice — the verb *ittaqaw* was in the *perfect* tense. *They guarded themselves.* Past. Finished. The provisioning happens *here*, in the crossing, in the short closed span of the journey. You cannot provision at the far edge; there are no wells to draw from once you arrive. The carrying is done *on the road*. That is the whole urgency of the dunya: it is the *only* place where taqwa can be packed.

What changes about tomorrow? You stop expecting the wilderness to be a garden. So much of our daily disappointment is the quiet, unexamined belief that *this* — this world, this life, this stretch — is supposed to finally satisfy, finally settle, finally be *enough*. And it never is, and we feel cheated, because we forgot we were crossing. To know you are in the *mafāza* is to stop being shocked that it is hard ground. The hardness is not a betrayal. It is the nature of a crossing. And the difficulty of the sand says nothing against the certainty of the far edge — it only means you are still walking. Tomorrow, when the dunya is dry, you can say: *of course — I am in the mafāza. The water is not here. The water is at the arrival the place is named for. Let me check my provision, and walk.*

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Stand back and see the whole now.

The verse is built on a single, sustained movement, and every piece we uncovered is the same insight seen from a different side.

The **verb** told us *who acts*: not the traveler escaping by his own strength, but **Allah delivering** — *yunajjī*, Form II, thorough, all the way, the doubled letter holding the rescue tight. And the **tenses** told us *the shape of the exchange*: their *taqwa* a finished thing in the perfect tense, His *deliverance* an unfolding thing in the imperfect — a small closed past act of the servant answered by an open eternal act of God.

The **noun** told us *where*: *bi-mafāzatihim* — to the wilderness that the Arabs bravely, hopefully named for the triumph of crossing it, which Allah now makes *literally true* for the ones who provisioned with taqwa. The hopeful name becomes the real name.

And the **two negations** told us *what they find there*: both doors of human suffering sealed at once — *lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾu*, no harm reaching them from ahead; *wa lā hum yaḥzanūn*, no grief rising in them from behind. The two tides that no human crossing ever stilled, both gone still at the far edge.

Read as one breath: **The God-conscious provisioned for the wilderness with taqwa; and at the far edge of the crossing, Allah Himself delivers them — thoroughly, by name — to the triumph the place was always called by, where the harm that comes at a person never touches and the grief that rises in a person never comes.**

The linguistic journey and the two themes are the same truth from two directions. The grammar showed you a *rescue* and a *destination*; the themes showed you that you are *being rescued* across a *wilderness*, right now, and that the two sufferings you know are the very two the far edge undoes.

### Questions to Carry

1. Of my two sufferings — the dread of what's coming and the grief of what's gone — which door do I spend my life trying to bolt? And have I noticed that no matter how hard I work at the one, the other stays open?

2. Do I treat the dunya as the *mafāza* — a wilderness I am crossing, provisioned and bound for somewhere else — or have I quietly built my house in the sand and started calling it home?

3. The provisioning happens only on the road. What taqwa am I packing *today*, in the perfect tense, that I am counting on at the far edge?

4. When I imagine being safe, do I imagine only *lā yamassuhumu s-sūʾ* — no harm reaching me — and forget that I can be perfectly safe and still grieving? What would it mean to long for the *second* negation too?

5. The verse says *Allah* delivers — not that they escape. Where in my life am I exhausting myself trying to *escape*, when I am being asked instead to become someone Allah *delivers*?

### One-Sentence Distillation

> Life is a wilderness the ancients bravely named for the triumph of crossing it — and Allah makes that name true for the God-conscious, bringing them through Himself to the one place where neither the harm ahead nor the grief behind can ever reach.

### The Closing Invitation

Nothing in this verse asks you to stop feeling the two tides. You will still have the nights. What it offers is a *direction* for the whole crossing, and a *name* for the ground you are on. So let one thing shift: the next time the dunya is dry and hard, do not read it as a broken promise. Read it as the wilderness being what a wilderness is — and let the dryness send you back to your provision, and your provision back to the One whose name comes right after the rescue. You are not lost in the sand. You are in the *mafāza*. And the *mafāza* is named for the arrival.

### A Brief Duʿā

*O Allah, You who deliver — *yunajjī* — deliver us thoroughly, all the way, leaving nothing of us behind in the danger. Make us of *alladhīna ttaqaw*, those who provisioned the crossing with mindfulness of You. And bring us through this wilderness to the triumph it was always named for — where the harm that comes at us never touches, and the grief that rises in us never comes.* **Āmīn.**
