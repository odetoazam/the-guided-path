# The Cosmos That Counts Your Days

## Seven Heavens at the End of a Divorce

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read you the verse first. Sit with the sound of it before we touch a single word.

> **ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَـٰوَٰتٍ وَمِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ مِثْلَهُنَّ ۖ يَتَنَزَّلُ ٱلْأَمْرُ بَيْنَهُنَّ لِتَعْلَمُوٓاْ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًۢا**

*Allāhu lladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin wa mina l-arḍi mithlahunn. Yatanazzalu l-amru baynahunn, li-taʿlamū anna llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr, wa anna llāha qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā.*

"Allah is the One who created seven heavens, and of the earth the like of them. The command descends, continually, between them — so that you may know that Allah is, over every single thing, fully Able; and that Allah has encompassed every single thing in knowledge."

[PAUSE]

Now — I want you to notice something strange before we go anywhere. Something almost jarring. And I want it to bother you a little, because the discomfort is the doorway.

This is the **last verse of Surah At-Talaq.** And At-Talaq means *divorce.*

So let's rebuild the world this verse arrives into.

### The world of this surah

For eleven verses, this surah has been close to the ground. Painfully close. It has been about the end of a marriage — the most intimate wound a human household can suffer. It has talked about the *ʿiddah*, the waiting period a woman observes after divorce. It has said: **count it.** *Aḥṣū l-ʿiddah* — "count the waiting period precisely" (65:1). It has spoken about where a divorced woman is to live, and commanded that she not be pushed out of her home. It has spoken about the pregnant woman — that her term lasts until she delivers. It has spoken about the man's duty to spend on her, even now, even at the bitter end, *according to his means.* It has said, of the man whose hands are empty: *Allah will give him ease after hardship* (65:7).

This is a surah of small rooms. Of countings on fingers. Of a woman in a house alone, watching the days, wondering what comes next. Of a man with empty pockets, ashamed. Of the most private grief a person carries — the grief that feels too small, too domestic, too *embarrassing* to bring before the Lord of the worlds.

And then — the very last thing this surah says to that woman, to that man — is:

**Seven heavens. And seven earths. And a command descending, ceaselessly, between them.**

[PAUSE]

### The hook

Why?

Why does a surah about a divorce end with the architecture of the entire cosmos? Why, when you are at your smallest — counting days alone in a house — does Allah show you His largest thing?

It would be like a doctor, sitting with a frightened patient over a single test result, suddenly pulling back the curtain to reveal the entire hospital, the entire city, the entire planet. You'd think: *why are you showing me this? I came here about me.*

Hold that question. Because the answer is going to turn the whole thing inside out. By the end, you're going to see that Allah did not change the subject. **He never left the subject.** The seven heavens *are* about your divorce. The descending command *is* about your waiting period. And the One who *encompassed every single thing in knowledge* — that phrase is not cosmic decoration. It is the most personal sentence in the entire surah.

Let's earn it.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

We're going to open three things in this verse. A verb that won't stop moving. A verb that surrounds you like a wall. And a silence — a word Allah *almost* says, and deliberately doesn't.

---

### Element One: The Command That Keeps Descending — *yatanazzalu*

Let me teach you something about Arabic verbs before we look at the word, because without it the word is just "descends" and you'll walk right past the miracle.

In Arabic, a verb is not just *what* happened. The very *shape* of the verb — the pattern of its letters — tells you *how* it happened. There's a whole architecture of forms built on top of a root. And one of those forms — the grammarians call it **Form V** — takes a simple action and stretches it out. It makes it gradual. Progressive. Done in stages, bit by bit, over time, rather than all at once.

Here's an everyday way to feel it. Imagine the difference between someone saying "he *learned* it" and "he *picked it up, piece by piece, over years.*" Same event — learning. But the second one has *duration* baked into it. It happened in installments. That stretching-out, that sense of *gradually, in stages* — in Arabic, that's exactly what Form V does to a verb.

Now. The root here is **n-z-l** (نزل), the root of *descending, coming down.* The simplest form of it is *nazala* — "it came down." One motion. One drop. Done.

But Allah does **not** say *nazala l-amr* — "the command came down." [PAUSE]

He says:

> **يَتَنَزَّلُ ٱلْأَمْرُ**
> *yatanazzalu l-amr*

This is Form V. *Yatanazzal.* And it is in the **present tense** — the tense of the unfinished, the ongoing, the renewing.

So feel what just happened to the verb. It is not "the command came down" — once, in the past, finished. It is **"the command keeps descending — continually, in stages, never stopping, even now, even as I speak this word to you."**

Let's sit with this.

Picture rain. Not a single drop that fell yesterday. Picture rain *falling* — the kind you watch through a window, the kind that does not announce a beginning or an end, that is simply *coming down* and *coming down.* That is the texture of *yatanazzalu.* The command of Allah is not an event that happened. It is a *weather* that never lifts. Right now, between the seven heavens and the seven earths, decree is descending. Provision is descending. Outcomes are being apportioned. It did not pause to read this surah. It has not paused once since creation began.

Now — what *is* this "command," this *amr*, that keeps pouring down?

Here the classical scholars give us not one answer but several, and I want you to receive them not as competing options but as a single reality seen from different angles — like walking around a statue and seeing a new face each time.

**Al-Jalalayn** says the *amr* is the *waḥy* — the revelation — descending, carried by the angel from the highest heaven down to the lowest earth. So one face of the command is **guidance:** the words of God, descending to reach you.

**Al-Baghawī** reports from the scholars of meaning (*ahl al-maʿānī*) that the *amr* is the whole of Allah's wondrous governance: *He sends down the rain, and brings out the plant, and brings the night and the day, the summer and the winter, and creates the living things in all their forms, and moves them from state to state.* So another face of the command is **providence** — the entire running of the world, the machinery of sustenance, descending without pause.

And Qatāda — as Al-Baghawī narrates — adds: *in every earth of His earths, and every heaven of His heavens, there is a creation of His creating, and a command of His commanding, and a decree of His decreeing.* So a third face is **decree** — *qaḍāʾ* — the apportioning of what will be.

Put the faces together and here is the composite: **guidance, provision, and destiny — the word that teaches you, the sustenance that keeps you, and the decree that shapes your tomorrow — all of it is descending, right now, continually, between the heavens and the earths.**

[PAUSE]

Now carry it back to the divorced woman in her small room.

She has been told to *count.* She has been told *Allah will make a way out* (65:2), *will provide from where she does not expect* (65:3), *will give ease after hardship* (65:7). And she might think: *those are beautiful words, but how? The world is so vast and I am so small. Has the machinery of the universe even noticed me?*

And the surah's final answer is: **the command never stopped descending.** The same *amr* that runs the seven heavens is the *amr* that is, this very moment, descending into your waiting period. Your "way out" is not a special favor God has to remember to arrange. It is simply one more drop in a rain that has never once stopped falling. **You did not fall through the cracks of a busy universe. There are no cracks. The command descends between *all* of them — *baynahunn* — between every heaven and every earth, leaving nothing in between unattended.**

That is what the verb *yatanazzalu* is doing. It is telling a frightened person: *the help is not coming. The help is already, continually, on its way down.*

### Element Two: The Knowledge That Surrounds You Like a Wall — *aḥāṭa*

Now to the verse's final word, and one of the most quietly overwhelming words in the Qur'an.

> **وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا**
> *wa anna llāha qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā*
> "...and that Allah has encompassed every single thing in knowledge."

The verb is *aḥāṭa* (أحاط), from the root **ḥ-w-ṭ** (حوط). Before we touch its meaning in the verse, let me give you the picture buried inside the root — because Arabic roots carry physical pictures, and this one is a building.

From this very same root comes the word *ḥāʾiṭ* — **a wall.** The wall that goes *around* a garden, a courtyard, a house. The root *ḥ-w-ṭ* is fundamentally about **encircling, surrounding, enclosing on every side** — the way a wall encloses what is inside it so that nothing within is outside the wall, and nothing escapes its perimeter.

So when Allah says *aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā*, the image is not "Allah knows a lot of things." The image is architectural. **His knowledge is the wall around all of existence.** Every single thing — *kulli shayʾin* — sits *inside* the enclosure of His knowing. There is no thing that is outside the wall. There is no corner of any of the seven earths, no thought in any heart, no day uncounted, that lies beyond the perimeter of His *ʿilm.*

Now notice the *grammatical* form, because here is where we must be precise. This verb is **Form IV** (*afʿala*). Form IV in Arabic has several possible functions — it is not automatically "causative," as is sometimes claimed. Here its function is **intensive and transitive**: it takes the act of knowing and makes it *total, complete, all-surrounding.* Not knowledge *of* a thing — knowledge that *wraps around* the thing entirely, from every side. It is the difference between *seeing* a person and *enclosing* them in your sight so completely that no angle of them is hidden from you.

And notice the tense: *qad aḥāṭa.* The little particle *qad* before a past-tense verb does something important — it **confirms and completes.** It means "indeed, already, definitively." Not "Allah will come to know" or "Allah is getting to know." **Already. Completely. Done.** The wall is already built. The enclosing already total. Before you brought your situation to Him, it was already inside the wall of His knowing.

Let's sit with what that does to a suffering person.

We usually hear "Allah knows everything" as a statement about *Allah* — a fact about His greatness, filed under theology. But look where this verse sits. It is the last word spoken to a woman counting days alone, and to a man too poor to provide. And to *them*, "Allah has encompassed every single thing in knowledge" is not a theology lecture. **It is the end of being unseen.**

Because here is the particular agony of private grief: the *feeling that no one sees the whole of it.* Your closest friend sees a slice. Your family sees the version you let them see. Even you cannot see all of it — you can't see the cause buried in the past, you can't see the outcome waiting in the future. Every human who loves you is standing *outside* some wall of your situation, peering in through one window.

And Allah says: *qad aḥāṭa.* **I am not at the window. I am the wall.** Your whole situation — the part you've told no one, the part you don't understand yourself, the part that hasn't happened yet — all of it is *inside* My knowing, surrounded, enclosed, with nothing left out. *ʿilmā* — "in knowledge" — sits at the very end of the verse, the final breath of the entire surah, as if to say: *this is the note I want you to leave on.*

[PAUSE]

Here's what's remarkable. Go back to verse one of this same surah: *aḥṣū l-ʿiddah* — **"count the waiting period."** The surah opened by commanding *you* to count. It ends by telling you Who has already counted everything. Your fragile, finger-counting *iḥṣāʾ* of a few uncertain days is held inside His total, completed *iḥāṭah* of all things. You count because you can only hold a little. He has already encompassed it all.

---

### Element Three: The Silence — *mithlahunn*, the Earths He Will Not Name

Now the absence. And in tadabbur, we read what Allah *withholds* as carefully as what He says.

Look again at the opening:

> **خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَـٰوَٰتٍ وَمِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ مِثْلَهُنَّ**
> *khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin wa mina l-arḍi mithlahunn*
> "...created seven heavens, and of the earth *the like of them.*"

Notice the asymmetry. The heavens, Allah names directly and numbers openly: *sabʿa samāwāt* — **"seven heavens."** Said plainly. But the earths He does *not* number openly. He says *wa mina l-arḍi mithlahunn* — "and of the earth, **the like of them.**"

He does not say "seven earths." He says "the like of them." He makes you do the arithmetic. He points to the heavens — *seven* — and then, for the earth, He simply says: *the like.* The number is there, but veiled, reached only by reflection.

Why hand you the heavens plainly and the earths by inference?

Here is what the construction *invites us to consider* — and I'll mark this as interpretive, because it goes beyond pure grammar into reflection. The heavens are what you *look up* to — visible, glorious, the obvious stage of God's grandeur. The earth is what you *stand on* — the ordinary, the underfoot, the place of your small troubles, your domestic griefs, your divorce. And Allah's design is symmetrical: *mithlahunn* — **"the like of them."** Whatever majesty, whatever layered order, whatever fullness of creation crowns the heavens, *the like of it* is woven into the earth beneath your feet. The ground of your ordinary life is not a lesser thing. It is built to the *same measure* as the heavens.

The classical commentators — Al-Qurṭubī among them — draw a different but harmonious lesson from this opening: that the creation of seven heavens and their like of earths is a proof of *kamāl al-qudrah*, the perfection of His power — and therefore a proof that **He is able to resurrect, and able to bring to account.** The One who built this much can certainly raise you, judge you, and set right what was wronged in a divorce no court fully saw. So the silence about the number of earths is not emptiness. It is an invitation: *look down at the ground of your small life, and know it was made to the measure of the heavens, by a Power that misses nothing and will settle everything.*

[PAUSE]

### The bridge

So look at what these three constructions have shown us, standing together.

A command — *yatanazzalu* — that never stops descending, continually, into every space between the worlds, including the space your grief is sitting in.

A knowledge — *aḥāṭa* — that has already, completely, built its wall around every single thing, so that nothing of you is unseen.

And an earth — *mithlahunn* — secretly built to the same measure as the heavens, so that the ground of your ordinary pain is no small or forgotten place.

Now let's ask what this reveals — not just about words, but about the shape of reality itself, and about the strange ache of feeling too small for God to notice.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

---

### Theme One: The Terror — and the Cure — of Feeling Cosmically Small

Let me start where you already live.

There's a particular feeling that modern life is very good at producing. Psychologists sometimes call it *cosmic insignificance* — that vertigo you feel looking up at a clear night sky, the sudden sense that you are one speck on one rock among billions, and that nothing you do, nothing you suffer, could possibly *register* at that scale. We are taught to find this feeling humbling, even liberating — "the universe doesn't care, so relax."

But for a person in real pain, that feeling is not liberating. It is **abandonment dressed up as perspective.** When you are grieving a marriage, the last thing that comforts you is being told you're a speck. You don't want to be a speck. You want to be *seen.*

And here is what this surah does that is so unlike the modern move. It shows you the *exact same* overwhelming scale — seven heavens, seven earths, a universe of layered immensity — but it draws the **opposite** conclusion. The modern voice says: *the universe is vast, therefore you are nothing.* The Qur'an says: *the universe is vast — and its Maker has encompassed every single thing in it, including you, in His knowledge.* **Same vastness. Opposite verdict.**

Let me build this slowly, because the difference is everything.

The reason vastness feels like abandonment is that we imagine attention as a *limited* resource. A human being can only attend to so much. If your friend is attending to a thousand things, she has less attention for you. So we assume: a God running seven heavens must, surely, have very little attention left over for one woman's divorce. The scale *steals* the intimacy. That's the unspoken math of cosmic loneliness.

But that math assumes a *finite* knower — a being whose attention is divided, spent, used up. And *aḥāṭa* detonates exactly that assumption. His knowledge is not divided among things; it *surrounds* all things at once, entirely, the way a wall surrounds everything in the courtyard without "spending" itself on any one corner. The seven heavens do not *compete* with you for His attention. **He attends to all of it, totally, simultaneously, with nothing diminished.** The vastness is not a rival to your intimacy with Him. The vastness is the *proof* of it: anyone whose knowing is that total has not overlooked you — *could* not overlook you — because overlooking requires a limit, and He has none.

[PAUSE]

Stay with this through one more turn. Notice the two divine names the verse ends on, and notice that there are *exactly two,* and that they need each other.

*Anna llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin **qadīr*** — Allah is, over every single thing, **fully Able.**
*Wa anna llāha qad **aḥāṭa** bi-kulli shayʾin **ʿilmā*** — and Allah has **encompassed** every single thing in **knowledge.**

**Power and knowledge.** Side by side. And ask yourself why you need *both.*

Imagine a being with infinite power but limited knowledge. Terrifying — strength that might crush you by accident, because it doesn't fully see you. Now imagine a being with infinite knowledge but limited power. Heartbreaking — it sees your every tear and can do nothing. *That* is the worst loneliness: to be perfectly understood by someone who cannot help.

The verse closes the trap from both sides. He **knows** your situation completely — *aḥāṭa* — so His help will never be clumsy, never miss the real ache, never solve the wrong problem. And He is **able** over every single thing — *qadīr* — so His knowing is never helpless. **Perfectly understood, and perfectly able to act.** There is no gap between His seeing and His doing for you to fall through.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes what you do with the thought *"this is too small to pray about."* You know that thought. The minor worry, the private embarrassment, the domestic grief you'd never "bother God with." This verse retires that thought permanently. There is no scale at which you become too small, because His attention is not rationed. The same breath that mentions seven heavens mentions *kulli shayʾin* — *every single thing.* Tomorrow, take the smallest thing — the thing you've been managing alone because it felt unworthy of prayer — and bring it. You are not interrupting the running of the universe. You are *part of* the command that never stops descending.

### Theme Two: When You Cannot See the Outcome, Someone Has Already Encompassed It

Here's the second place you live.

The hardest part of any crisis is not the pain of *now.* It's the *not knowing.* The divorced woman in this surah is not only grieving — she is *uncertain.* What will the next year hold? Will there be provision? Will there be ease? Will she be alright? She has been commanded to *count the days* — but counting days is precisely what you do when you cannot see the end of them. You count *because* the future is dark.

We all know this state. It's the 2 a.m. ceiling-staring. It's running the scenarios — if this, then that; but if that, then this — trying to think your way to a future you cannot see. Modern life calls it anxiety, and at its root anxiety is almost always this: **the unbearable gap between how much my outcome matters and how little of it I can see.** You care infinitely about what happens next, and you can see almost none of it.

Now place *aḥāṭa* into that gap.

You cannot encompass your own future. You are standing *inside* time, able to see only the sliver you're passing through. The past is behind a wall you can't climb back over; the future is behind a wall you can't see past. You are *enclosed* by your own not-knowing.

And the verse says: there is One for whom your future is not behind a wall — because **He is the wall.** *Qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā* — He has *already, completely* encompassed every single thing in knowledge. Your outcome is not a mystery He is waiting to discover alongside you. It is already inside His knowing — surrounded, held, complete — including the part that hasn't happened yet.

[PAUSE]

Let me show you how tightly this is woven into the surah, because it is not a coincidence that *iḥāṭah* is the closing word.

Three verses earlier, this same surah made one of the great promises of the Qur'an: *wa man yattaqi llāha yajʿal lahu makhrajā, wa yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* — "whoever is mindful of Allah, He will make for him a way out, and provide for him **from where he does not expect**" (65:2–3). From where he does not *expect.* From outside his calculations. From a direction his anxious scenario-running never accounted for.

Now hear that promise in light of the final verse. *Why* can provision come "from where you do not expect"? Because **your expectation is the small enclosure of your own knowledge — and His knowledge is the wall around all of it.** What lies "outside your expectation" is not outside *His* encompassing. The very thing you cannot see coming, He has already encompassed. The phrase "from where you do not expect" and the phrase "He has encompassed every single thing in knowledge" are two ends of one rope. The surah promises you unseen help at the beginning, and at the end it tells you the *mechanism*: there is a Knower whose sight has no blind spots, descending His command, continually, into the very gap you cannot see across.

[PAUSE]

So let the paradox sit, because it is a real one and I don't want to resolve it too quickly. You are *still* told to count. *Aḥṣū l-ʿiddah.* You are *still* told to act, to be careful, to do your part. Knowing that He has encompassed everything does **not** excuse you from counting your own days. Faith here is not "He knows the outcome, so I do nothing." Faith is: *I count my small handful of days with care and diligence — and I lay that counting down inside His complete count. I do my finite part, fully, and entrust the infinite part to the One who encompasses it.* The peace is not in *seeing* the outcome. The peace is in knowing the outcome is *seen* — by One who is also able to act on what He sees.

What does this change about tomorrow?

It changes the 2 a.m. ceiling-staring. The work of anxiety is the work of trying to *encompass your own future* — to wall it in with scenarios so that nothing can surprise you. It is exhausting precisely because it is a job you cannot do; you are a finite knower trying to perform an infinite *iḥāṭah.* This verse offers you a different posture: *do your counting in daylight — plan, prepare, take your means — and then, at night, lay it down.* The encompassing is already done, and not by you. You were never meant to be the wall around your own life. Tomorrow, when you catch yourself running the scenarios for the hundredth time, try saying the verse's last two words back to yourself: *shayʾin ʿilmā* — He has encompassed it, in knowledge, already. And then sleep.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the shape of the whole surah, because only now will it make sense.

Surah At-Talaq is built as a single, breathtaking **zoom-out.**

It *opens* at the smallest possible scale: a single household, a single woman, a single set of days to be counted on the fingers. *Aḥṣū l-ʿiddah.* Count.

It moves outward: provision, housing, the man's means, the promise of a way out *from where you do not expect.*

And it *closes* by pulling the camera all the way back — past the rooms, past the city, past the sky — to seven heavens and seven earths and a command descending eternally between them, ending on the words *He has encompassed every single thing in knowledge.*

[PAUSE]

And here is the architecture's proof. The zoom-out is not a *change* of subject. It is the *grounding* of the subject. The surah spends eleven verses telling a hurting person *what to do* and *what is promised* — and then, in the final verse, it tells them **why they can trust any of it.** Every command to count, every promise of a way out, every assurance of provision, rests on the last verse like a building rests on bedrock: *because the One making these promises created the seven heavens, runs them by a command that never pauses, and has encompassed every single thing — including your small, dark, uncertain situation — in a knowledge that is already complete.*

The divorce verses are the *what.* The cosmos verse is the *who.* And the *who* is so vast, so able, and so all-knowing that the *what* becomes unshakable.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes were the same insight, approached from two directions.

*Yatanazzalu* — the command that never stops descending — answered the fear of being *abandoned*: the help is not a special errand God must remember to run; it is a rain that has never once stopped falling, and it falls *baynahunn*, between all of them, leaving no gap unattended.

*Aḥāṭa* — the knowledge that walls in all things — answered the fear of being *unseen* and the fear of the *unknown future*: you are not at risk of falling through the cracks, because there are no cracks; you are inside the wall, and so is your tomorrow.

And *mithlahunn* — the earth built to the measure of the heavens — answered the fear of being *too small*: the ground of your ordinary grief was made to the same scale as the glory above it.

One God. Continually descending command. Completely encompassing knowledge. Fully able power. **That is the One who is counting your days alongside you.**

### Questions to carry

- What is the smallest, most "embarrassing" worry I've been managing alone because it felt too minor to bring before the Lord of seven heavens — and what would change if I truly believed it was already *inside the wall* of His knowing?
- Where in my life am I exhausting myself trying to *encompass my own future* — to wall in every outcome — a job that was never mine to do?
- When I feel like a speck in a vast universe, which verdict do I reach for: *therefore I am nothing,* or *therefore the One who encompasses all things has not overlooked me*?
- The surah commands me to *count* and also tells me He has *encompassed* all counting. Am I doing my finite part fully — *and then* laying it down? Or am I either neglecting my part, or refusing to let go of His?
- If provision can come "from where I do not expect," am I leaving room in my hopes for an answer from a direction I haven't calculated?

### One-sentence distillation

The God who created seven heavens and earths, and whose command never stops descending into every space between them, has already and completely encompassed your smallest, most private, most uncertain trouble in a knowledge that misses nothing — and a power that can act on everything it sees.

### The closing invitation

So here is the shift, offered not as obligation but as relief.

You were never too small. The vastness you feared was the very evidence you were searching for. A God this great is not a God with less attention for you — He is a God with no limit to it. The next time you feel like a speck under an indifferent sky, let this surah turn the feeling inside out: the sky is not indifferent, and you are not a speck lost beneath it. You are *encompassed* — surrounded on every side by a knowing that is already complete, attended by a command that is still, this very moment, descending.

Take your smallest grief tomorrow and bring it. And do your counting in the daylight, fully — then, at night, set it down inside the wall of His knowing, and rest.

### A brief du'a

> O Allah, You who created the seven heavens and their like of earths, and whose command never ceases descending between them — I bring You the small and private trouble I have been carrying alone. You have already encompassed it in knowledge, completely, before I ever spoke it. So I lay down the part I cannot see, trusting the One who sees it, and I keep faithfully the part You have given me to count. You are, over every single thing, fully Able; and You have encompassed every single thing in knowledge. Let that be the last word I fall asleep on.

*Āmīn.*
