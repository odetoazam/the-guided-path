# They Plot, I Plot — and Then: Wait a Little

## INTRODUCTION

Let me give you the words first, and then let me give you the world they fell into.

> إِنَّهُمْ يَكِيدُونَ كَيْدًا
> **innahum yakīdūna kaydā**
> *Indeed, they are plotting a plot.*

> وَأَكِيدُ كَيْدًا
> **wa-akīdu kaydā**
> *And I am planning a plan.*

> فَمَهِّلِ الْكَافِرِينَ أَمْهِلْهُمْ رُوَيْدًا
> **fa-mahhili l-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydā**
> *So give respite to the disbelievers — grant them respite, a little while.*

Eleven words. Three short lines. They close a surah that almost no one slows down for, because it is so brief you can recite the whole thing waiting for an elevator.

But stay with me. Because something happens in these three lines that, once you see it, you will hear every time you feel the world closing in on you.

### Rebuild the world

Picture Mecca in the years before the migration. A small, fragile community has formed around a man saying things that threaten everything — the gods, the trade, the social order, the inherited certainties of an entire city. And the response is not a single open battle. It is something quieter and, in a way, worse.

It is **scheming**.

Meetings in the dark. Whispered strategies. Boycotts drawn up on parchment and hung in the Kaʿba. Bribes offered, then threats, then mockery, then plans to seize him, exile him, or — eventually — to kill him in his sleep so that the blood would be spread across so many clans that no one could be held to account. (The Quran itself records this, later, in Sūrat al-Anfāl: *"when those who disbelieved plotted against you to imprison you, or kill you, or drive you out…"* 8:30.)

This is the texture of the moment. Not a clean confrontation you can face down. A web. A hum of hostility coming from every direction at once, made of secrecy and patience and numbers.

And now notice **where** in the surah these verses sit.

At-Tariq opens with an oath by a star — *aṭ-ṭāriq*, the night-comer, *an-najmu th-thāqib*, the piercing star, the one whose light **drills through darkness**. Then immediately: *in kullu nafsin lammā ʿalayhā ḥāfiẓ* — "there is no soul but that over it is a watcher" (86:4). Then: look at what you were made from — a fluid, ejected, from between the backbone and the ribs. Then: He who made you the first time can return you. Then: the Quran is *qawlun faṣl* — **a decisive word**, a word that separates — *wa mā huwa bi-l-hazl* — and it is not amusement.

A piercing light in the dark. A watcher over every soul. A decisive, separating word.

And *then* — into a city full of people scheming in the shadows against that word — comes:

*Indeed, they are plotting a plot. And I am planning a plan.*

### The hook

Here is what I want you to feel the strangeness of before we even begin.

When the schemers of Mecca brought their whole architecture of hostility against the Prophet ﷺ — the boycotts, the bribes, the assassination plot — Allah's answer is **two words long**: *wa-akīdu kaydā.* And it is built out of the **exact same word** they used.

They schemed a scheme. I scheme a scheme.

Why would Allah describe His response using *their* word? And why, after that, does the surah not end with thunder — but with a command to **give them more time**?

Two words of answer. And then: *wait a little.*

Let's find out why.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The cognate accusative — when a word leans on itself

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you a small feature of Arabic, because the whole power of these lines runs through it.

In English, if I want to intensify a verb, I reach for an adverb. *He ran.* Not enough? *He ran quickly. He ran desperately.* I add a second, different word to push on the first.

Arabic has another move, and it's one English doesn't really have. You can intensify a verb by **repeating it as a noun right after itself.** You take the action and you say it twice — once as the doing, once as the thing done.

It's called the *mafʿūl muṭlaq*, the "absolute object," or the cognate accusative. Imagine saying in English: *"He ran a running."* It sounds odd to us. But in Arabic it is one of the most powerful tools of emphasis there is. It means: **this happened, really happened, fully and without doubt.** The verb is no longer floating; it is nailed down by its own echo.

So when Arabic says *kallama llāhu mūsā taklīmā* — "and Allah spoke to Moses **a speaking**" (4:164) — that doubled word slams the door on any metaphorical reading. It really happened. Speech, literally, fully.

Now hold that. And look at our verse.

> إِنَّهُمْ يَكِيدُونَ **كَيْدًا**
> *innahum yakīdūna kaydā*

The verb is *yakīdūna* — "they plot." And then the noun: *kaydā* — "a plotting." The action, echoed by itself.

This is not a casual description. The doubled word is saying: **their scheming is real, intense, deliberate, total.** This is not a passing thought of opposition. This is a committed, full-throated, all-in plot. Allah is *not* minimizing what they are doing. [PAUSE]

Notice that. Before we get to the divine response, the Quran does something important: it **takes their hostility seriously.** It does not say, "Oh, it's nothing, ignore them." It uses the most emphatic grammatical form available to say: *yes — they are really, fully plotting.* The threat is not imagined. The web is real.

And then comes line two.

### Element Two: The mirror — and the One break inside it

Here is where you need to stay very close.

> إِنَّهُمْ يَكِيدُونَ كَيْدًا
> وَأَكِيدُ كَيْدًا

Read them as shapes, even if you don't read Arabic. *Yakīdūna kaydā. Wa-akīdu kaydā.* Allah answers their cognate accusative **with the very same cognate accusative.** Same root — k-y-d. Same emphatic doubling. The structure of the second line is a near-perfect mirror of the first.

Why would He do that? Why not say "I will destroy them," "I will seize them," "I will overpower them" — language of crushing superiority? Why reach for *their* word and hand it back?

This is a known rhetorical move in the Quran called *mushākala* or *muqābala* — answering speech with its own shape, deliberately mirroring the other party's word back at them. You see it elsewhere: *"they plotted, and Allah plotted, and Allah is the best of plotters"* (3:54). *"and they planned a plan, and We planned a plan, while they perceived not"* (27:50).

The mirroring is doing something an ordinary word of threat could never do. It is saying: **whatever you think you are doing, it is already inside something larger.** Your scheme is not happening *to* My plan from the outside. It is happening *within* it. You make your move; the very move you make is a move on My board.

And the classical commentators hear a second asymmetry hidden inside the sameness — one the shared word almost conceals. The two plots wear the same verb, but they aim at opposite ends. Al-Muyassar reads their *kayd* as bent toward a single purpose: *to push the truth away and prop up what is false.* And His *kayd*? *Li-iẓhāri l-ḥaqq* — to make the truth stand up and be seen. Same action, reversed direction. They scheme to bury the truth; He "schemes" to *reveal* it. Which means the very energy they pour into concealment becomes, on His board, the occasion for the truth to surface — the *qawlun faṣl*, the separating word (86:13), doing its work *through* the very plot meant to silence it.

But here is the thing I most want you to see. The two lines look like a mirror — and a mirror is symmetrical. **Except they are not symmetrical.** There is one break in the glass, and the entire surah lives in that break.

Look at the verbs.

*Yakīdūna* — "**they** plot." Third person **plural**. Many of them. A crowd. The plural carries the whole image of the Meccan elite — the assembly, the committees, the many hands.

*Akīdu* — "**I** plot." First person **singular**. One.

[PAUSE]

Sit with that asymmetry. On one side: a multitude, straining together, pooling their cunning, coordinating across clans. On the other side: **One.** Not "We the heavenly hosts." Not "the armies of the unseen." *Akīdu* — *I.* Singular. Alone. And it is enough.

The grammar is staging the entire theology of the surah in a single contrast of verb-endings. They need **numbers**. He needs none. They need to **coordinate**. He coordinates nothing — He simply *is* the One in whom all coordination already rests. Their plurality is, quietly, an admission of weakness: you only need many hands when no single hand is enough.

And notice the **tense**. *Yakīdūna* is the imperfect — the ongoing, the continuous, the unfinished. They are *plotting and plotting and plotting*, present-tense, restless, never done. *Akīdu* is also imperfect — but from the One for whom the act and its outcome are never in question. Their ongoing plotting is anxiety in motion. His "ongoing" is sovereignty at rest.

Same word. Same shape. **Opposite worlds.** One word does all of that — the choice to mirror their language and then let a single pronoun-ending shatter the symmetry.

What does this single feature reveal? That the distance between the schemer and the One who holds the schemers is not a distance of *more power on the same scale.* It is a difference of **kind**. They are *inside* the thing they think they are fighting. [PAUSE — major pause]

### Element Three: *Ruwaydā* — the word that shrinks a lifetime

Now the surah could end at the mirror. It would be a complete and devastating statement. But it doesn't. It adds a third line — and the third line is the most surprising of all.

> فَمَهِّلِ الْكَافِرِينَ أَمْهِلْهُمْ رُوَيْدًا
> *fa-mahhili l-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydā*
> *So give the disbelievers respite — grant them respite, a little.*

After "they plot, I plot," you brace for the strike. Instead: **give them time.** Twice. *Mahhil… amhilhum* — two commands to grant delay, from the same root m-h-l, laid right next to each other, the way you repeat something to someone whose heart is racing: *easy… easy now.* The repetition is not redundancy. It is the cadence of **reassurance.** To a Prophet ﷺ surrounded by plotters, exhausted by hostility, perhaps aching for the matter to just be *over* — the answer is twice-spoken calm: *give them room. Give them room.*

The classical commentators hear something specific in this command. To a Prophet ﷺ who might, understandably, long for the punishment to come down *now,* the words *mahhil… amhilhum* are a restraint on that very longing — *fa-lā tastaʿjil lahum,* as al-Muyassar puts it: **do not be in haste** to see them struck. Do not rush the reckoning. And yet — this is what turns the respite from mere patience into promise — the same commentators note that the delay has an ending written into it: *wa sa-tarā mā yaḥullu bihim* — *and you will see what befalls them.* The respite is not the story trailing off into silence. It is the pause before an outcome already certain. *Ruwaydā* measures the length of that pause — a little — not the weight of what follows it.

And then the last word. The one the whole surah has been walking toward.

*Ruwaydā.*

This word is a **diminutive**. In Arabic, you can take a word and shrink it — the way English turns "book" into "booklet," or "duck" into "duckling." The shrinking can mean small in size, but it can also mean small in significance, fleeting, brief, *not much.* *Ruwayd* is the diminutive built on the root r-w-d — the root of gentle movement, of going softly. So *ruwaydā* means: *a little. A small while. Just a touch of time. Easy, gently, a little.*

Now feel what that does.

These are people who have given their **entire lives** to opposition. Years of scheming. Boycotts that lasted seasons. Plots refined over time. From the inside, their campaign feels enormous — a life's project, a generational struggle, the great cause of their existence.

And Allah takes all of it — the whole arc of their plotting, every meeting in the dark, every year of effort — and He names its total duration with a **diminutive.** *Ruwaydā.* A little. A trifle. A small while. [PAUSE]

Do you feel the vantage point shifting under your feet? From *inside* the scheme, it is everything. From *above* it — from the One who is *akīdu*, singular and sufficient — the whole thing is a brief flicker. Not even worth a full-sized word. A diminutive will do.

And listen to the **sound** of these words, because the Quran is making you *hear* the meaning. *Kaydā* — say it — *kayd.* It is sharp. Clipped. The *k* and the *d* snap shut like a trap springing, like a snare. That is the sound of plotting: tight, hard, closed.

Now: *ruwaydā.* It rolls. It opens. The *r* and the *w* and the long *ā* — it lingers and softens and falls away, like a hand slowly lowered to say *settle down*, like the last of something dissolving. The sharp, snapping sound of their scheme is answered by the soft, dissolving sound of *a little while.* **The phonetics are doing the theology.** The trap snaps; the calm spreads; the trap, it turns out, was the small thing all along.

What does this one word reveal? That divine patience is not divine **absence**. The respite is not Allah looking away. It is the calm of the One who has already seen the end — so unhurried that He can afford to call their whole struggle *a little.* [PAUSE — major pause]

### Bridge

So look at what these eleven words have shown us through their structure alone. The cognate accusative took their hostility *seriously* — it is real, full, intense. The mirror placed that real hostility *inside* a larger plan, and the single break in the mirror — *they* (many) against *I* (One) — revealed that the difference is not of degree but of kind. And the diminutive *ruwaydā* shrank the whole towering edifice of their plotting into a brief, gentle, dissolving moment.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the way you live inside a world that feels, so often, like it is scheming against you.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The exhaustion of feeling plotted-against

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with the body. It's the tiredness of feeling that forces are arranging themselves against you. Maybe it's an office where you sense decisions being made about you in rooms you're not in. Maybe it's a family situation where alliances seem to have formed and you weren't consulted. Maybe it's the broader feeling — and many of us carry this now — that the world itself is structured against people like you, that there are systems and currents and powers, mostly invisible, mostly out of reach, that do not have your good in mind.

That feeling has a specific quality. It is the feeling of being the **object** of other people's planning. Of being acted *upon.* And it produces a very specific exhaustion, because you find yourself trying to do the impossible: to track and counter plans you cannot fully see, made by people you cannot fully reach, on a scale you cannot match.

This is precisely the Prophet's ﷺ situation in these verses. *Innahum yakīdūna kaydā.* They are plotting — really plotting, the emphatic form, fully. A whole city's worth of hidden coordination aimed at one man and a handful of believers. If anyone had the right to feel the crushing weight of being plotted-against, it was him, in Mecca, in those years.

And now watch what the verse does *not* say to him.

It does not say: *here is your counter-strategy.* It does not say: *here are the seven moves to outmaneuver them.* It does not even say: *here is how to expose their plot.* The believer is not handed a better scheme. [PAUSE]

He is handed **a relocation of the whole problem.** *Wa-akīdu kaydā* — and I, the One, am already planning. The verse lifts the matter out of the arena where the Prophet ﷺ would have to match them plot-for-plot, and places it into the hands of the One whose single word outweighs their multitude.

Here is the movement I want you to feel. The anxiety of being plotted-against comes from believing that the outcome rests on whether **you** can see, track, and counter every move. The verse cuts that belief at the root — not by promising you'll out-scheme them, but by telling you the game was never on your shoulders to win. You are not the lone defender on the board. The board belongs to Someone.

And notice — this is *not* an invitation to passivity or naivety. The verse does not say their plot is unreal or that you should pretend it isn't happening. It used the most emphatic form precisely to *honor* the reality of the threat. The relief it offers is not denial. It is **accompaniment.** You are not facing the web alone. There is a watcher over every soul — the surah told you that at the start (86:4) — and that same watcher is the One saying *akīdu.*

What does this change about tomorrow? When you feel the familiar tightening — the sense that things are being arranged against you — the first move is not to scramble for the counter-plot. It is to remember the asymmetry in the grammar. *They* are many and straining. There is **One** who is not straining at all. Your job is to do what is righteous and within your reach. The outcome — the part that was making you sick with anxiety — was never yours to engineer. *Wa-akīdu kaydā.* Let that be His.

### Theme Two: When "nothing is happening" is the most that is happening

Now the harder theme. Because there is a second kind of pain in these verses, and it's subtler.

It's the pain of the **delay.**

Here is the situation honestly. The believers are being plotted against. Allah declares that He is planning in response. And then the instruction is… *wait.* Give them time. Grant them respite. A little — but still: wait.

If you have ever cried out for something to be *resolved* — for the wrongdoer to be stopped, for the injustice to be answered, for the situation to just *break* one way or the other — you know how heavy a "wait" can be. We are creatures who read **delay as denial.** When nothing visibly happens, the heart concludes that nothing *is* happening. That maybe we weren't heard. That maybe the plotters are simply winning.

The Quran knows this about us. Elsewhere it says of the wrongdoers: *"Let not their going about freely through the land deceive you"* (3:196) — because their freedom of movement *looks* like victory. And it warns the opposite of complacency too: *"And I give respite to them; indeed, My plan is firm"* (7:183) — the respite itself is part of the plan, not a pause in it.

This is the reframe that *amhilhum ruwaydā* is offering, and it is profound. The delay is not the absence of the response. **The delay is the response — in its early form.**

Let me give you the load-bearing way to see this. Think of what respite actually *does.* When someone is given more time, more rope, more freedom to keep acting as they are — what happens? The pattern completes itself. The character is fully revealed. The plot runs its full course and shows what it truly was. Respite is not mercy *withheld from* justice; respite is the **medium through which** the full truth of a person's choices becomes undeniable — to themselves, and to all who watch.

So when Allah says *give them respite, a little* — He is not saying "I'm not acting." He is saying "**the acting includes the waiting.**" The time He grants is not empty time. It is time in which the scheme reaches its full shape, the watcher records every move (*ḥāfiẓ*, 86:4), and the decisive word (*qawlun faṣl*, 86:13) does its separating work — separating the true from the false, slowly, completely, with nothing rushed and nothing missed.

And the diminutive comes back to comfort you here. *Ruwaydā.* When you are *inside* the wait, it feels endless. The verse hands you the other vantage point: from where Allah sits, even the longest respite is *a little.* The wait that is breaking your heart is, on the true scale, brief. Not dismissed — your pain is real — but **brief.** A small while, and then the separating word finishes its work. [PAUSE]

There is also a mercy in the respite that we should not rush past — a mercy aimed at the plotters themselves. *Mahhil… amhilhum.* Every day of delay is also a day in which a schemer could turn back. The history of that very Meccan elite proves it: some of the fiercest plotters of those early years became, later, among the most devoted believers. The respite that *looks* like the wrongdoer escaping is, sometimes, the open door through which the wrongdoer walks home. The delay you resent on behalf of justice may be the very mercy that saves a soul you would have written off. That, too, is held in *akīdu kaydā* — a plan more generous than our impatience can imagine.

What does this change about tomorrow? When you are in the long middle of something unresolved — the injustice unanswered, the prayer seemingly unmet, the situation refusing to break — stop reading the silence as abandonment. *Amhilhum ruwaydā.* The delay is not God absent from your story. It is God **patient** in your story, letting the full truth ripen, recording every moment, holding open the door of return — and counting the whole of it as *a little.* Your task in the wait is not to force the ending. It is to keep doing the decisive, true thing, and to trust that the One who said *akīdu* has not stepped away from the board. [PAUSE — major pause]

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now stand back and look at the whole surah, because the placement of these three verses is the proof of everything we've found.

At-Tariq opens with **a star that pierces the dark** — *an-najmu th-thāqib.* It then declares **a watcher over every soul** — *ḥāfiẓ.* It calls the Quran **a decisive, separating word** — *qawlun faṣl* — and insists *it is not amusement.* And *then* it turns to those who **plot in the dark** — *yakīdūna kaydā.*

Do you see the architecture? *Kayd* — plotting — is a creature of **darkness and concealment.** It works by being hidden, by happening in rooms no one sees, by the many coordinating in secret. And the surah has already, line by line, **dismantled the darkness it depends on.** A star drills through the night. A watcher stands over every single soul. A word comes that *separates* truth from falsehood and is *not* idle play.

Against a light that pierces all dark, against a watcher who misses no soul, against a word that separates with finality — **what is a plot made of secrecy?** *Ruwaydā.* A little thing. A small while. The surah spent its whole length removing the cover that scheming needs to survive, so that by the time we reach *they plot, I plot,* we already know: there is no dark left for their darkness to hide in. [PAUSE]

### Bring it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are one truth approached from two directions.

The **grammar** showed it: a mirror with one break in it — *they* (many, straining, ongoing) against *I* (One, sufficient, at rest) — and a diminutive that shrinks a lifetime of scheming to *a little.*

The **themes** lived it: the exhaustion of feeling plotted-against is cured not by a better counter-plot but by remembering whose board it is; and the pain of the delay is healed by seeing that the delay *is* the plan unfolding, with the whole of it counted as *ruwaydā.*

Same insight, two doors. **What feels enormous from the inside is held, entirely, by the One it can never reach — and on His scale, it is small, and it is brief.**

### Questions to carry

1. Where in my life am I exhausting myself trying to out-track and out-plan forces I was never responsible for controlling?
2. When I feel plotted-against, do I reach first for the counter-scheme — or first for the One who said *akīdu*?
3. Is there a "delay" in my life I have been reading as abandonment, that might in fact be the response, still ripening?
4. Whom have I already written off as beyond saving — and could the respite I resent be the door of their return?
5. If I truly believed that this — *all* of this — is *ruwaydā*, a little while, what anxiety would I be able to set down today?

### One-sentence distillation

While they exhaust themselves weaving plots in the dark, the One who needs no allies answers with a single word and asks only that you give them — and yourself — a little time.

### The closing invitation

You do not have to win the game of schemes. You were never the lone player against the crowd. There is a watcher over every soul, a light that pierces every dark, a word that separates every truth from every falsehood — and behind all of it, One who is unhurried enough to call the whole storm *a little.* The invitation is not to fight harder against the web. It is to do the true thing within your reach, and then to let your grip loosen on the outcome that was always His. *Mahhil… amhilhum ruwaydā.* Easy. Give it a little time.

### A brief du'a

O Allah, You who plan when we cannot see and watch when we cannot reach — when the world feels arranged against us, remind us that we are held inside Your plan, never outside it. Teach our hearts to read Your delays as Your patience and not Your absence, and let what feels endless to us rest small and safe in Your hands. Make us of those who do the true thing and trust the rest to You. Āmīn.
