# The Sky That Returns and the Word That Cuts

## Introduction: Standing Under a Sky That Comes Back

Let me put the words in front of you first, slowly, the way they were meant to be heard.

> **وَالسَّمَاءِ ذَاتِ الرَّجْعِ**
> *wa-s-samāʾi dhāti r-rajʿ*
> "By the sky, possessor of return —"

> **وَالْأَرْضِ ذَاتِ الصَّدْعِ**
> *wa-l-arḍi dhāti ṣ-ṣadʿ*
> "and by the earth, possessor of splitting —"

> **إِنَّهُ لَقَوْلٌ فَصْلٌ**
> *innahu la-qawlun faṣl*
> "indeed it is a decisive word,"

> **وَمَا هُوَ بِالْهَزْلِ**
> *wa-mā huwa bi-l-hazl*
> "and it is not for amusement."

Four short lines. You could read them in fifteen seconds. And yet I want to spend the better part of an hour here, because under these fifteen seconds there is an architecture so precise that once you see it, you will not be able to un-see it.

Let me rebuild the world this verse first fell into.

Imagine a man in Makkah. He has heard this new recitation — that the dead will be raised, that there is a Day when *every secret is put on trial* (86:9), when the things you buried inside yourself will be cross-examined. And his response is the oldest response in the human catalogue: a shrug. A half-smile. *Come on. You expect me to believe that these bones, once they're dust, get reassembled? That the sky and the earth are part of some great moral accounting?* To him it sounded like a story. Like something you say around a fire. Like — and here is the exact word the surah will reach for — **a joke.** Something not to be taken seriously.

Now feel the climate of that moment. The whole surah before this has been building a case. It opened by swearing by *aṭ-Ṭāriq*, the night-visitor, the piercing star that arrives in the dark. It said over every single soul there is a *ḥāfiẓ*, a guardian, watching, recording (86:4). It walked the man back to his own origin — *you were a drop of gushing fluid* (86:6) — and then it made the argument that this entire surah turns on: **the One who made you the first time is able to return you** (86:8). *Innahu ʿalā rajʿihi la-qādir.*

Hold onto that word. *Rajʿ.* Return. Because three verses later, Allah is going to swear an oath, and He is going to reach for the *exact same root* — and most listeners walk right past it.

Here is the hook. Allah does not respond to the man's shrug with a threat. He responds with a sky. He says: *look up.* You want to know whether return is possible? You are standing underneath the proof, and it falls on your head every spring. And then He does something even more startling — He tells you, in plain terms, what category of speech you are dealing with. He tells you this is not entertainment.

Why would the Creator of the heavens need to clarify that He is not joking?

Stay with me. That question is the whole door.

# Part 1: The Linguistic Journey

## Element One: *Ar-Rajʿ* — The Sky That Comes Back

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the word, because the structure here is doing quiet work.

In Arabic, when you want to describe a thing by a quality it *carries* — not a passing quality, but something it *possesses*, something essential to it — you can use the word **ذات** (*dhāt*), which means "possessor of," "owner of," "the one characterized by." It is the feminine of *dhū*. When you say a person is *dhū māl*, you don't mean he happens to have a coin in his pocket; you mean wealth is a feature of who he is. So when Allah says **as-samāʾ dhāt ar-rajʿ** — "the sky, *possessor* of return" — He is not saying the sky did a returning once. He is saying: **return is what this sky is made of. Return is its character. It is the returning-sky.**

Now the word itself.

> **الرَّجْعِ** — *ar-rajʿ*

The root is **ر-ج-ع** (*r-j-ʿ*), and across the whole Arabic language it carries one core meaning: *to return, to come back, to go back to a starting point.* It is the root of *rujūʿ* (returning), of *rajʿa* (a return), of the phrase every Muslim says at the moment of loss — *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*, "we belong to Allah and to Him we are **returning**." Same root. The going-back.

So what is "the return" of the sky?

Here the commentators are nearly unanimous, and it is beautiful in its concreteness. Al-Qurtubī notes that *the general body of commentators* — and the people of the language — say **ar-rajʿ is the rain.** Al-Jalālayn says it plainly: *the rain, because it returns again and again, every season.* Al-Baghawī says the same: the sky of *rajʿ* is the sky of rain, *because it returns every year and repeats.* And he preserves the report of Ibn ʿAbbās: it is the cloud, returning with rain.

But al-Ṭabarī widens the lens one crucial notch further. He glosses *dhāt ar-rajʿ* as the sky that *returns, year after year, with the clouds and with the very provision of the servants* — *tarjiʿu bi-l-ghuyūmi wa arzāqi l-ʿibādi kulla ʿām.* Sit with that second phrase: *arzāq al-ʿibād*, the sustenance of the people. The return is not only water and not only greenery — it is *rizq.* The bread on your table has a cloud behind it; the harvest you eat is water that came back. And the word ran so deep in the Arab tongue that they used *ar-rajʿ* to name standing water itself — al-Ṭabarī cites a line of old poetry praising a blade "white as the *rajʿ*," bright as a pool of returned rain. Return, in other words, was not a metaphor the Quran draped over the sky. It was already the sky's *name.*

But notice — and this is where the word opens — **why is rain called "the return"?** Stop and actually picture the mechanism the Arabs were naming, fourteen centuries before anyone drew a diagram of it.

Water rises. You cannot see it rise — it lifts off the seas and the soil and the leaves as invisible vapor, it climbs, it gathers into the heavy grey bodies of clouds. And then it **comes back down.** What went up returns. The sky is not a thing that *makes* water; the sky is a thing that *gives back* what the earth surrendered to it. *Possessor of return.* The Arabs saw a sky that takes and gives back, takes and gives back, in a rhythm so reliable that they named the sky itself after the act of returning.

[PAUSE]

Now go back to verse 8. *Innahu ʿalā **rajʿihi** la-qādir* — "He is able to **return** him." The dead human being.

Do you see what Allah has done?

He made the argument in verse 8 with a word — *rajʿ*, return — and then three verses later He swore an oath using **the same root**, pointing at a sky that does *exactly that*, in front of your eyes, every single year. The argument and the evidence are bound together by a single syllable. He is saying: *the return of the human being from the dust is not a different kind of event than the return of the water from the sky. You have been watching the resurrection rehearse itself over your head your whole life and calling it weather.*

Al-Khalīl, the great early linguist, added a second layer that makes this almost unbearable in its precision. He said *ar-rajʿ* is not only the rain — it is also **the vegetation of spring**, the green that *returns* to a land that had gone dead and brown. So the "return" is the whole cycle: the water returns, and then the *life* returns. Dead ground, then rain, then green. Which is — line for line — the description the Quran gives elsewhere of the resurrection itself: *and you see the earth lifeless, but when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and grows* (cf. 22:5).

The sky that returns is not a poetic flourish. It is **Exhibit A** in the case for your own return.

One word. *Rajʿ.* The going-back of water is the rehearsal of the going-back of you.

[PAUSE]

## Element Two: *Aṣ-Ṣadʿ* — The Earth That Splits

Now the oath pivots from the sky to the ground, and watch how perfectly the second image rhymes with the first — not just in sound, but in meaning.

> **وَالْأَرْضِ ذَاتِ الصَّدْعِ**
> *wa-l-arḍi dhāti ṣ-ṣadʿ*
> "and by the earth, possessor of splitting."

Same structure exactly. *Wa* (by) + the cosmic body + *dhāt* (possessor of) + the single defining act. The sky is *dhāt ar-rajʿ*; the earth is *dhāt aṣ-ṣadʿ*. Twin oaths, mirror-built.

The root is **ص-د-ع** (*ṣ-d-ʿ*): *to split, to crack open, to cleave, to fissure.* It is a word with an edge to it. *Ṣadʿ* is what happens to a thing under pressure when it finally gives and opens along a seam. We still use a cousin of this root when the Quran commands the Prophet ﷺ *faṣdaʿ bimā tuʾmar* (15:94) — "so **declare openly** what you are commanded" — split the air with it, break the silence open.

So what is the splitting of the earth?

Al-Muyassar gives the agreed reading with lovely specificity: the earth *that cracks open with what passes through it of vegetation* — * at-tashaqquq bimā yatakhallaluhā min nabāt.* The earth splits **because something living is pushing up through it.**

Now I need you to actually kneel down and look at this, because we have all walked past it ten thousand times.

A seed is buried. It is, for all purposes, dead — a hard, dry, sealed little stone. Above it sits the entire weight of the soil, packed, heavy, dark. And then, from inside that buried deadness, something so soft it would tear in your fingers — a pale shoot, a thread of green — pushes upward and **splits the earth open.** The ground, which could crush a man, *cracks* to let the weak living thing through. The earth is *possessor of splitting* — fracturing is its character — because it is forever being broken open from below by life it cannot hold down.

[PAUSE]

And now feel the two oaths together, because this is where the architecture reveals itself.

The sky **returns** — water comes back down.
The earth **splits** — life comes back up.

Both verbs are resurrection verbs. Both are motions of *something dead coming back.* And there is a third place in the Quran where this exact word *ṣadʿ* lives — the Day itself. *Yawma tashaqqaqu l-arḍu* — "the Day the earth splits open from them, [as they come] hurrying" (50:44). The same earth that splits each spring to release a seedling is the earth that will split on the Last Day to release *you.*

So look at what Allah swore by. He did not swear by the mountains for their grandeur or the seas for their vastness. He swore by **the two halves of the resurrection that you can watch happening right now.** Water descending: the return. Earth cracking: the emergence. He took the Last Day, broke it into two everyday miracles, and scattered them across every farm and every rainfall on earth, so that the man who shrugged at resurrection is standing — literally standing — between two witnesses against his own doubt: rain on his head, green at his feet.

The oath is not decoration before the point. **The oath *is* the point.** Allah is swearing by the evidence.

And there is a boundary hidden inside that oath worth naming, because al-Muyassar pauses on exactly this spot. Allah swears by the sky and by the earth — *but this is a right that belongs to Allah alone.* A created being may not swear by any created thing; the one who swears by other than Allah, al-Muyassar warns, has stepped toward *shirk.* So when Allah swears by the returning sky, He is not lending the sky some borrowed majesty. He is doing what only the Maker of a thing may do: **summoning His own handiwork to the witness stand.** The oath is not the creation reaching up to grandeur — it is the Creator calling His witnesses to testify. Which means even the *form* of these verses is a claim: the sky and the earth do not belong to themselves. They belong to the One putting them under oath.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Element Three: *Faṣl* Against *Hazl* — The Cut and the Slack

Now comes the answer to the oath — what the grammarians call the *jawāb al-qasam*, the thing the swearing was leading up to. And here Allah makes a claim about the nature of His own speech that I want to take apart word by word, because the two key words are built to *sound* like what they mean.

> **إِنَّهُ لَقَوْلٌ فَصْلٌ**
> *innahu la-qawlun faṣl*
> "indeed it is a decisive word."

First, feel the weight of the opening. *Inna* — "indeed, truly." Then *la-* — the emphatic *lām*, an extra layer of *yes, certainly.* Arabic does not stack emphasis like this casually. *Innahu la-qawlun* is the grammar of a courtroom verdict, a thing being asserted under maximum pressure: **truly — certainly — it is a word.** Allah is bracing the sentence as if anticipating exactly the objection the doubter is making.

Now the key word: **فَصْل** — *faṣl.*

The root **ف-ص-ل** (*f-ṣ-l*) means *to separate, to divide, to cut apart, to distinguish one thing from another.* The *faṣl* in a book is the *chapter* — the cut that separates one section from the next. The *fayṣal* is the judge or the sword — both of them things that *cut a dispute in two* and end it. *Yawm al-faṣl*, "the Day of Separation," is one of the names of Judgment Day, the day truth and falsehood are finally *cut apart* and can never be confused again.

So *qawlun faṣl* — "a word that is *faṣl*" — means: **a word that cuts.** A word that comes down between truth and falsehood like a blade and separates them so cleanly that the seam can never be re-sewn. Al-Muyassar reads it exactly so: the *it* is the Qur'an itself, *a decisive word, [separating] between truth and falsehood.* This is not speech that suggests, hints, hedges. This is speech that *decides.* It walks into the murk where right and wrong have been smeared together and it draws the line.

Now listen to the word's own sound. *Faṣl.* It begins soft — *fa* — and then it *closes*, hard and final, on *-ṣl*, the tongue snapping shut against the ridge of the mouth. The word itself *cuts off.* You cannot draw it out. It ends like a verdict ends — abruptly, with nothing trailing after.

[PAUSE]

And now the contrast, the final verse, which exists *only* to set off the third:

> **وَمَا هُوَ بِالْهَزْلِ**
> *wa-mā huwa bi-l-hazl*
> "and it is not for amusement."

Look at the negation. Not just *mā huwa hazl*, "it is not a joke." Allah inserts the preposition *bi-*: *mā huwa **bi**-l-hazl.* In Arabic, slipping that *bi-* into a negation *tightens* it, hardens it — it is the difference between "it isn't a joke" and "it is *in no way whatsoever* a joke." The denial is reinforced exactly the way the affirmation before it was reinforced with *inna* and *lām.* Verse 13 is *doubly* affirmed; verse 14 is *doubly* denied. The two verses are perfect inverses, braced against each other.

And the word **هَزْل** — *hazl* — root **ه-ز-ل** (*h-z-l*): *jest, play, frivolity, the speech you don't mean, words said lightly that carry no weight.* The Arabs paired *hazl* and *jidd* as exact opposites — *jidd* is seriousness, *hazl* is play. *Hazl* is what you say when nothing is at stake. It is the wink, the throwaway line, the speech that is loose precisely because it doesn't have to hold anything up.

Now feel *its* sound against *faṣl.* Where *faṣl* snapped shut, *hazl* goes **slack.** The middle of the word is that voiced *z*, a buzzing, loose, lazy sound, and it trails off on the soft *-l* without ever clamping down. The mouth never closes hard on *hazl* the way it does on *faṣl.* One word is a blade. The other is a shrug. **And Allah has placed them one verse apart so that you hear the difference before you even understand it.** The decisive cut, then the loose drift. *This* — He says, of His own Book — is the first. It is *never, in any way,* the second.

This is the answer to the man who shrugged. He treated revelation as *hazl* — light talk, a story, not-to-be-taken-seriously. And Allah, having sworn by the returning sky and the splitting earth, delivers the verdict on the verdict: **this is faṣl. This cuts. Bring your whole life to it, because it has come to decide your whole life.**

[PAUSE — major pause]

### The Bridge

So look at what the language has shown us. Two oaths built on twin verbs of return — *rajʿ*, the water coming back; *ṣadʿ*, the life breaking through — and both of them rehearsals of the very resurrection the doubter laughed at. And then a verdict braced on both sides: *faṣl*, the word that cuts, set against *hazl*, the word that drifts.

Now let's ask what all of this reveals — not just about words, but about reality itself, and about the two diseases of the human heart these verses came to cure.

# Part 2: The Thematic Depths

## Theme One: You Have Been Watching the Resurrection Your Whole Life

Let me start where you actually live.

There is a particular kind of grief that comes not from believing something bad will happen, but from being unable to *feel* that something good is true. Ask a person who is exhausted by life whether they believe in the resurrection, and many will say *yes* — and mean it — and still live as though the grave is the end. The doctrine is in the head. It never made the journey to the chest. They are not deniers. They are simply *numb* to it. The Last Day is an abstraction, a distant rumor, a thing too large and too far to press on a Tuesday afternoon.

This is the deeper disease the doubter in Makkah had, and it is the one most of us carry in a quieter form. It is not loud denial. It is the inability to take return *seriously* — to feel it as real as the chair you're sitting on.

And notice how Allah treats it. He does not pile on more abstraction. He does not say *believe harder.* He does something far gentler and far more devastating: **He points at the weather.**

He says: you cannot picture the dead coming back? Then you have never actually *watched* the rain. You have seen water vanish off a wet street into nothing — gone, evaporated, dispersed beyond any hope of gathering — and then *watched it return*, pulled back out of the sky, every drop, onto land that had cracked dry. You have buried a hard dead seed and *watched the earth split* to let its life through. You have seen brown dead ground go green. **You have been attending the resurrection's rehearsal your whole life and writing it off as the seasons.**

[PAUSE]

Here is the movement I want you to make with me.

The reason resurrection feels unbelievable is that we have decided, somewhere deep down, that *gone means gone.* The water that evaporates is *gone.* The person who dies is *gone.* This is the unspoken physics of the grieving heart. And these two verses walk straight into that assumption and break it open like a seedling breaks soil. Because the water was never gone. It was *held.* It rose, it was carried, it was kept in a form you couldn't see, and it was returned on schedule. *Dhāt ar-rajʿ* — return is the *character* of the sky, not its exception.

What if that is also the character of *you?*

What the verse is quietly retraining is your definition of *gone.* The Quran is teaching you that in the economy of the One who made you, **nothing that returns to Him is lost — it is held.** *Innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn.* The same root, *rajʿ,* you say over your dead — *to Him we are returning* — is the root of the sky that gives its water back, and (as al-Ṭabarī reminded us) the sky that gives back your very provision, year after year, on schedule, without fail. He has been returning your *rizq* your whole life. You are not telling Allah goodbye over the body of someone you love. You are watching evaporation. And the One who never once failed to bring the water back — or the harvest, or the rain, or the spring — is *dhāt ar-rajʿ* over them too.

[PAUSE]

Sit with the mercy of *how* He chose to argue this. He could have overwhelmed the doubter with power — described the trumpet, the splitting sky, the terror. Instead He reached for *rain.* For *spring.* For the most ordinary, most repeated, most overlooked thing in a human life. Because He knows that what convinces the numb heart is not a bigger spectacle but a *closer* one — the realization that the impossible thing has been happening, faithfully, in your own backyard, your entire life. The evidence was never far away and dramatic. It was near and quiet and falling on your head.

**What does this change about tomorrow?**

It means the practice here is not to believe harder — it is to *watch more carefully.* The next time it rains, do not let it be weather. Let it be a sermon. Let it be Allah saying *return is possible* in the only language the numb heart still hears: the language of things you can touch. The next time you see something push green out of dead ground, register it as a witness Allah Himself swore by. Train your eye to read the world the way the verse reads it — not as scenery, but as evidence. The man who learns to see the resurrection in the rain has already half-defeated the grave.

[PAUSE — major pause]

## Theme Two: In an Age That Treats Everything as a Joke, a Word That Refuses to Be One

Now the second disease, and this one is *our* native air more than it was theirs.

We live in the age of *hazl.* Of irony. Of the reflexive shrug. Notice the dominant posture of our culture: nothing is to be taken fully seriously. To be earnest is to be naive. The cool stance is the detached one — the eyebrow slightly raised, the half-smile that holds everything at arm's length so that nothing can ever land on you with full weight. We have built an entire grammar of *hazl*: the meme, the wink, the "it's not that deep," the instinct to dissolve anything heavy into a joke before it can ask something of us.

And here is what that posture is *for.* Irony is armor. If I treat everything as not-quite-serious, then nothing can ever obligate me. If it's all a bit of a joke, I never have to change. *Hazl* is the doubter's shrug raised to a way of life — a sophisticated, exhausting defense against ever being asked to take a single thing, including my own soul, seriously.

Into that exact posture, fourteen centuries early, comes the verse: **wa mā huwa bi-l-hazl.** *And it is not — in any way — a joke.*

[PAUSE]

Feel why this lands so hard on us specifically.

The man in Makkah dismissed the Quran as *hazl* — light talk, not serious. And our entire culture has industrialized his shrug. We don't reject the sacred; we *defang* it by refusing to let anything be weighty. And the verse names that exact move and forbids it — not by scolding the joker, but by *declaring its own category.* It says: *whatever posture you bring, understand what you are standing in front of. This is faṣl. This is a blade. You may treat everything else in your life as hazl if you like — but this one thing was sent to cut, and your shrug will not blunt it.*

There is a strange relief in this. Because the exhausting thing about an ironic life is that nothing holds still long enough to stand on. If everything is a joke, then *you* are a joke, and that is a colder place to live than people admit. The verse offers an exit: **here is one thing that is not loose.** One word that means exactly what it says, that came to decide and not to entertain, that you can put your full weight on and it will not give. In a world of *hazl*, the heart is starving for one thing that is *faṣl* — one fixed point that refuses to dissolve into a wink.

[PAUSE]

Now let the third movement come.

Why does *faṣl* — cutting, separating — even matter? Because the real tragedy of the ironic, *hazl*-soaked life is not that it laughs too much. It is that it can no longer *tell things apart.* When everything is held loosely, truth and falsehood smear together into one grey blur of "who's to say." The man who takes nothing seriously slowly loses the ability to *distinguish* — and a person who cannot tell truth from falsehood is not free; he is lost. He has no line to navigate by.

And *faṣl* is precisely the cure for that. A *qawlun faṣl* is a word that **draws the line back.** It walks into the grey and separates it again into black and white, this and that, truth here and falsehood there. The Quran's gift to a confused heart is not just information — it is *resolution.* It is the blade that ends the dispute you've been having with yourself for years. *This is right. That is wrong. This leads home. That leads off the cliff.* In an age that has lost its edges, the Quran is the thing with an edge.

[PAUSE]

**What does this change about tomorrow?**

It is a posture check at the threshold. We open the Quran — or open a translation, or hear an ayah in passing — with the same loose, scrolling, half-ironic attention we bring to everything else. We bring *hazl* to *faṣl.* And the verse is asking us to change the posture *before* we change anything else. To recognize: *what I am about to read was declared, by its Author, to be the one thing in my life that is not a joke.* 

So the practice is small and total: when you come to these words, *put the armor down.* Drop the irony at the door. Read as though it might actually cut — because that is the only way it can. The man who keeps his ironic distance from the Quran will get nothing from it, not because the Quran failed, but because *faṣl* cannot do its work on a heart that insists everything is *hazl.* You have to let it be serious. You have to let it be a blade. And the astonishing promise of these verses is that if you do — if you bring earnest weight to a *qawlun faṣl* — it will return to you everything the ironic life stole: the ability to tell truth from falsehood, and a single fixed thing to stand on while the rest of the world drifts.

# Closing Synthesis

## The Architecture

Now stand back and see the whole structure at once, because it is a single machine and every part drives the same conclusion.

- **Verse 11** — the sky, *possessor of return.* Water comes back down. *Rajʿ.*
- **Verse 12** — the earth, *possessor of splitting.* Life breaks back up. *Ṣadʿ.*
- **Verse 13** — *indeed it is a decisive word.* The blade. *Faṣl.*
- **Verse 14** — *and it is not, in any way, a joke.* Not the shrug. Not *hazl.*

Two oaths, then a verdict braced on both sides — *inna* and *lām* affirming verse 13, *bi-* hardening the denial in verse 14. And the two oaths are not random scenery: they are the resurrection itself, broken into two everyday miracles — the return of water, the emergence of life — and scattered across the world so that the proof falls on the doubter's head and grows under his feet. The sky and the earth are summoned as *witnesses* — and, as al-Muyassar reminds us, summoned by the only One with the right to put them under oath — and then the verdict is read.

## The Two Roads Meet

See how the linguistic journey and the thematic depths were the same discovery from two directions.

The word *rajʿ* told us the sky *returns* — and the theme told us that *gone* never means *gone* to the One who holds the water, holds your provision, and will hold you. The contrast of *faṣl* against *hazl* told us one word *cuts* and the other goes *slack* — and the theme told us that in an age drowning in irony, the heart is starving for one serious thing to stand on. 

And here is the seam that joins them: **the resurrection is real (the oaths), therefore the Word announcing it is not a joke (the verdict).** The reason the Quran is *faṣl* and not *hazl* is *because* the return is true. You cannot shrug at a Book whose central claim is rehearsed by every rainfall. The man laughed at resurrection and dismissed the Quran as light talk — and Allah answered both with one stroke: *look at the returning sky, the splitting earth — now tell Me this is a joke.*

## Questions to Carry

1. What in my life have I quietly filed under *gone* — a person, a chance, a version of myself — that Allah may simply be *holding,* the way the sky holds the water before the rain?
2. When it rains, what do I see? Weather — or a witness Allah swore by, returning my very provision to me?
3. Where have I let *hazl* — the protective shrug, the ironic distance — keep the Quran from ever landing on me with full weight?
4. Is there a place in my life right now that has gone grey, where truth and falsehood have smeared together — and have I brought it to the one Word that came to *cut* them apart?
5. If I truly believed the Book in front of me was declared by its Author to be *the one thing that is not a joke,* what would I do differently the next time I opened it?

## One-Sentence Distillation

The same Lord who returns the water to the dry earth, splits the soil for the buried seed, and sends back your provision every year has sent you a Word that does not drift and does not joke — it has come to cut truth from falsehood and decide your life, exactly as surely as He will return you.

## The Closing Invitation

You do not have to overhaul your faith tonight. The invitation is smaller and more reachable than that. It is two shifts of the eye. **First:** the next time it rains, let it preach to you — let the returning water say *return is real, nothing given back to Allah is lost.* **Second:** the next time you come to His words, set the armor down at the door — bring earnest weight to a *qawlun faṣl*, and let it be the one serious thing in a world that treats everything as a joke. That is all. Watch the rain differently, and read His Word seriously. The verse asks for nothing more, and nothing less.

## A Brief Du'a

*O Allah, You are *dhāt ar-rajʿ* over all that returns to You — return our hearts to You before our bodies return to the dust, and let nothing we love be ever truly lost in Your keeping. Make Your Book to us a *qawlun faṣl* and never a thing we hold loosely; cut the falsehood out of us with it, and let us be among those who, when they hear Your words, take them seriously enough to be changed. Ameen.*
