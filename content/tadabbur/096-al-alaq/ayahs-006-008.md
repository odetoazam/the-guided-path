# The Overflow: When the Soul Forgets It Was Ever in Need

### Surah Al-Alaq, Ayat 6–8

> **كَلَّآ إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَـٰنَ لَيَطْغَىٰٓ ۝ أَن رَّءَاهُ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ ۝ إِنَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ ٱلرُّجْعَىٰٓ**
>
> *Kallā inna-l-insāna la-yaṭghā. An raʾāhu-staghnā. Inna ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā.*
>
> *"Nay! Indeed, man transgresses — because he sees himself as self-sufficient. Indeed, to your Lord is the return."*

---

## INTRODUCTION: Standing at the Threshold

Let me take you back to a cave.

A man is forty years old. He has climbed, as he often does, to a hollow in the rock above Makkah — a place to be alone, away from a city that worships stones it carved with its own hands. He has never read a book. He has never written a line. And in the dark of that cave, something seizes him. A presence. A command he cannot fulfill: *Read.*

He cannot read. He says so. The command comes again, and a third time, and then the words arrive that will split history in two: **"Read, in the name of your Lord who created. Created man from a clinging clot. Read — and your Lord is the Most Generous. The One who taught by the pen. Taught man what he knew not."**

Sit with what just happened. In the span of five short lines, the human being has been *lifted*. He was a clot of blood — and he was taught. He was nothing — and the pen was placed, by implication, in his hand. The very first thing Allah chooses to reveal to humanity is a verse about humanity's *honor*: that the Most Generous taught you what you did not know. You were ignorant, and He dressed you in knowledge.

And then — without pause, in the very same surah, the very same breath — the tone turns.

[PAUSE]

**"Kallā."** *No. Nay.*

Something is about to be corrected. The same surah that just crowned the human being with the gift of knowledge now turns to look at what the human being *does* with gifts. And what it sees is not pretty. It sees a creature who, the moment he feels full, forgets he was ever empty. It sees the student turn on the Teacher.

Here is the question I want you to carry into these three short ayat — eleven words, no more. The opening of the surah told us the human was *taught* — *ʿallama-l-insāna mā lam yaʿlam*. Now ayah six says the human *transgresses* — *inna-l-insāna la-yaṭghā*. **It is the same word — al-insān — in both places.** The one who was honored is the one who rebels. How does a being go from receiving the pen to raising his hand against the One who gave it?

The Quran is about to answer in a single, surgical word. And the answer is not what you think. It is not pride, exactly. It is not even wealth, exactly. It is something quieter, something that lives inside almost every one of us right now, today — a feeling so ordinary we never name it as the root of ruin.

Let's find it together.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: *Yaṭghā* — The Water That Forgot Its Banks

Before we open the word, let me teach you something about how Arabic builds meaning — because without it, this verse stays flat.

In Arabic, words are not invented in isolation. They grow from roots — usually three letters — and that root carries a single core *image*, a physical picture, from which every related word descends. When you learn the root, you don't just learn a definition; you inherit a photograph. And the Quran, again and again, chooses words whose physical photograph is doing spiritual work beneath the surface.

So here is the word. Ayah six: **لَيَطْغَى** — *la-yaṭghā*. "He surely transgresses." The translators give you "transgresses," and you nod, and you move on, and you have lost almost everything.

The root is **ط-غ-ي** *(ṭā–ghayn–yā)*. Now — what is its photograph?

Let's go to where the Quran itself uses this root, because the Quran is its own best dictionary. In Surah Al-Haqqah, describing the great Flood of Nuh, Allah says: **"lammā ṭaghā-l-māʾu ḥamalnākum fī-l-jāriyah"** — *"When the water* ***overflowed,*** *We carried you in the sailing ship."* (69:11)

[PAUSE]

There it is. The photograph beneath *ṭaghā* is **water breaking past its limit.** A river is a blessing as long as it stays within its banks. It irrigates, it quenches, it carries life. But let that same water rise past the edge — let it *ṭaghā* — and the very thing that gave life now drowns the village. Nothing new was added. No new substance entered the river. It simply *exceeded its bound.*

The classical lexicons confirm exactly this — Lisān al-ʿArab gives *ṭaghā al-māʾ*: the water rose high and burst its limit. And al-Baghawi, commenting on our very ayah, defines *yaṭghā* with precision: **"li-yatajāwaza ḥaddahu wa yastakbira ʿalā Rabbihi"** — *"that he exceeds his limit and grows arrogant against his Lord."* Notice — *yatajāwaza ḥaddahu* — *he crosses his boundary.* Al-Muyassar says the same: *yatajāwazu ḥudūda-llāh* — *he transgresses the limits Allah set.*

Now stay with me, because here is what the photograph teaches that the word "transgresses" cannot.

When you hear "man transgresses," you imagine a man *adding* evil — going out and committing a crime, importing wickedness from somewhere outside himself. But *ṭaghā* says something far more disturbing. The human being doesn't need to add anything. He is already a river full of God-given gifts — intellect, strength, wealth, health, knowledge. **Transgression is not the arrival of something foreign. It is the overflowing of what was already a blessing.** Your intelligence, uncontained, becomes arrogance. Your strength, uncontained, becomes cruelty. Your wealth, uncontained, drowns your own heart. The flood is made of the same water that was your mercy.

This is *istidlal al-maʿqūl bil-maḥsūs* — Allah pointing at an unseen reality (the soul exceeding its proper station before its Lord) by way of a thing you have seen with your own eyes (a river bursting its banks). You have watched a flood on a screen. You have seen water that nourishes turn into water that destroys. Now look at your own soul. **The line between blessing and ruin is not the gift. It is the bank.** It is the limit. It is the *ḥadd* that keeps the water doing what water was made to do.

Let me give you one more layer, and then we'll rest. Notice the **ل** at the front — *la*-yaṭghā. This is the *lām of emphasis*, paired with *inna* at the start: *inna-l-insāna la-yaṭghā*. In Arabic this is the strongest form of assertion available — like swearing an oath and underlining it twice. It is not "man sometimes transgresses" or "man might transgress." It is: *"Indeed, the human being* ***most certainly, without doubt,*** *overflows."* This is a statement about a tendency woven into the species. Not all humans in all moments — but the human, left to himself, ungoverned, *will* flood.

And one quiet absence, easy to miss. Transgresses — *against what?* The ayah does not say. There is no object. He transgresses… limits? Laws? His Lord? Other people? Himself? **The silence is the point.** When the water overflows, it does not choose what to drown. It takes the fields and the homes and the roads and the wells all at once. The unspecified object means: *all of it.* The one who exceeds his bound before God does not exceed it in one neat direction. He floods in every direction at once.

[PAUSE — let this settle]

So we have our diagnosis: the human being floods. Now the Quran tells us *what opens the floodgate.*

### Element Two: *An Raʾāhu-staghnā* — The Illusion That He No Longer Needs

[ayah:96:7] أَن رَّءَاهُ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ

This is one of the most psychologically exact half-lines in the entire Quran, and every word of it is a scalpel. Let's take it apart slowly.

First, **أَن** — *an.* Here it functions as *because* — it gives the *reason* for the flood. So the structure is a tight cause-and-effect: *he floods… because…* Whatever comes next is the single trigger Allah identifies for all human transgression. The whole disease, traced to one source.

Now what is the source? Two words: **رَّءَاهُ** *(raʾāhu)* and **ٱسْتَغْنَى** *(istaghnā).*

Start with *raʾāhu* — root **ر-أ-ي**, "to see." But look at the grammar carefully. *Raʾā* — *he saw* — and then *hu* — *himself.* The seeing folds back on the seer. He **saw himself.** The Quran does not say "because he *became* self-sufficient." It does not say "because he *was* free of need." It says **because he** ***saw himself*** **as free of need.**

[PAUSE]

Do you feel the knife turn?

The transgression is not built on a fact. It is built on a **perception** — and a false one. Because here is the truth the verse is leaning against with its whole weight: *the human being is never, for one instant, actually self-sufficient.* Your next breath is not yours to guarantee. Your beating heart asks no permission and reports to you only when it fails. The food in your hand grew from a seed you did not design, watered by rain you did not send, in soil you did not make, warmed by a sun you cannot approach. At every level, in every second, you are a creature held in being by Another. *Ghinā* — true freedom from need — is impossible for you. It belongs to exactly one Being, and we will come to His Name in a moment.

So when the verse says he *saw himself* self-sufficient, it is naming an **illusion.** Transgression does not begin with real independence. It begins with the *feeling* of independence — a feeling that has no ground under it at all.

Now the second word: **ٱسْتَغْنَى** — *istaghnā.* The root is **غ-ن-ي** *(ghayn–nūn–yā)* — the root of *ghinā*, wealth, and *ghaniyy*, the one free of need. And the form here is what Arabic grammarians call **Form X** — the *istafʿala* pattern, *ist-aghnā*. Teach yourself this pattern once and you will see it everywhere in the Quran. Form X very often means **to seek, to claim, or to regard oneself as having** the quality of the root. *Istaghfara* — to *seek* forgiveness. *Istakbara* — to *deem oneself* great. And so *istaghnā* — **to regard oneself as free of all need.** To look in the mirror and conclude: *I am sufficient. I have enough. I require no one.*

Now connect this to the cause we just uncovered — and watch the whole verse ignite.

*Yatajāwaza ḥaddahu* — he crosses his boundary — *because he regards himself as the Ghaniyy.* But **al-Ghaniyy** — *The Self-Sufficient, The Free of All Need* — **is a Name of Allah.** "Wa-llāhu-l-Ghaniyyu wa antumu-l-fuqarāʾ" — *"Allah is the Free-of-need, and you are the utterly poor"* (47:38). There is only One in all of existence who needs nothing and no one. To claim *ghinā* for yourself is not merely to be mistaken. **It is to reach for a crown that fits only one Head.** The transgression of ayah six and the self-sufficiency of ayah seven are the same act seen twice: the soul overflowing its station is the soul trying to occupy God's.

And here al-Muyassar adds the everyday face of it — the trigger most of us will actually meet: **"idhā abṭarahu-l-ghinā"** — *"when wealth makes him drunk."* It is not always wealth in money. It is wealth in anything — health, youth, intelligence, status, followers, security. Whatever fills you up to the point where you stop reaching. Because notice: the *faqīr*, the one who knows he is poor and needy, keeps his hands open and lifted. The one who feels full lowers his hands. He stops asking. And the soul that stops asking its Lord is a river that has just been told there are no more banks.

Let me bring this all the way home to where you live. Think of the last time you felt *truly* secure — money in the account, health in the body, a plan that was working. Now be honest about what happened to your prayer in that season. Did it deepen — or did it thin out? For most of us, hardship drives us to the prayer mat and ease quietly empties it. *That* is *istaghnā* in miniature. That is the floodwater rising an inch. The verse is not describing Abu Jahl out there. It is describing a Tuesday afternoon when things were going well and you forgot to whisper *al-ḥamdu lillāh.*

[PAUSE]

So now we understand the mechanism completely. Knowledge, wealth, strength — gifts — fill the human up. Filled, he *perceives* himself as needing no one. Perceiving himself free of need, he stops reaching, stops submitting, exceeds his bound — *yaṭghā.* The river floods. And the most chilling part: every step felt *good.* Fullness feels good. Self-sufficiency feels like freedom. The flood feels like power, right up until it drowns you.

The verse has shown us the disease in two ayat. We need the cure. And it arrives in the third — through a single, intimate shift of address that most readers walk right past.

### Element Three: *Ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā* — The Word "Your," and the Direction of Everything

Read the three ayat again and listen for *who is being spoken about.*

Ayah six: **al-insān** — *the human being.* Third person. Distant. A specimen on a table. *He* transgresses. *He* sees himself. There is a coldness to it — the way a doctor names a disease without naming the patient.

And then ayah eight: **"Inna ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā."** *"Indeed, to* ***your*** *Lord is the return."*

[PAUSE]

Did you catch it? The grammar just turned its head. For two ayat we spoke *about* "the human" — *huwa*, he, it, that distant transgressing creature. Now, suddenly, Allah turns and speaks *to* someone: **Rabbika** — *your* Lord. This is what the scholars of *balāghah* call **iltifāt** — the deliberate, electric shift of address that the Quran uses to wake the listener and re-aim the verse like a turning spotlight.

And to *whom* does He turn? To His Messenger ﷺ — and through him, to you, the listener, the one who is *still listening,* the one who has *not* flooded, the one who is reading *in the name of his Lord.* Feel the tenderness of the contrast. The transgressor is held at arm's length: *al-insān*, "the human," anonymous, third person. But the one being addressed is drawn close and given a possessive: *Rabbika*, **"YOUR Lord."** Not "the Lord." Not "Allah" in the abstract. *Yours.* Attached to you. Belonging to you and you to Him.

And look *which* Name is chosen for this intimate turn. Not *Allah*, not *al-Jabbār* (the Compeller), not *al-Muntaqim* (the Avenger) — though the context of transgression could have summoned any of those. The Name is **Rabb** — root **ر-ب-ب** — the *Nurturer, Sustainer, the One who raises a thing stage by stage from nothing to completion, providing for it at every step.*

[PAUSE — this is the heart of it]

Do you see the devastating gentleness of this choice? The transgressor's whole crime was *istaghnā* — "I need no one." And the answer is not "you will be crushed by the Almighty." The answer is: *to your* ***Rabb*** *— the very One whose nurturing you denied, the One who raised you from a clot, who taught you what you did not know, who fed and sustained and held you in being every second you imagined yourself free — to* ***Him*** *is your return.* The cure for "I need no one" is the quiet reminder of the One you needed for everything, all along, named by the exact attribute of His care that your transgression tried to erase.

Now the last word: **ٱلرُّجْعَى** — *al-rujʿā.* Root **ر-ج-ع**, "to return." And notice the definite article, *al-* — **THE return.** Not *a* return among many. The single, defined, inevitable homecoming. The form *rujʿā* (the *fuʿlā* pattern) gives it a ring of finality and totality — the comprehensive, ultimate return to which all smaller returns are merely rehearsals.

And one more grammatical jewel — the word order. In normal speech you might say *al-rujʿā ilā Rabbika* — "the return is to your Lord." But Allah *fronts* the destination: **"ilā Rabbika** *(first)* **r-rujʿā"** — *"to your Lord* **(and to Him alone)** *is the return."* In Arabic, moving a phrase forward like this creates **restriction** — *only* to Him. The river that overflowed its banks, that forgot it had a source, that imagined it generated its own water — that river is going home. Every drop. There is exactly one ocean, and *ilā Rabbika*, to your Lord, is where all the water is already, quietly, flowing back.

This is the bank the floodwater forgot. Not a wall to imprison the river — *the sea it came from and returns to.* The limit (*ḥadd*) that *ṭughyān* despises turns out to be nothing other than the loving gravity of home.

### Bridge

Look at what eleven words have given us. A river that floods when it forgets its banks (*yaṭghā*). A floodgate that opens the instant the soul *imagines* itself full and free (*an raʾāhu-staghnā*) — reaching, in that imagining, for a crown that fits only God (*al-Ghaniyy*). And a single, tender turn of the head — *Rabbika*, **your** Lord — reminding the listener that all this overflowing water has a home it is already returning to (*ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā*).

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the shape of a human life. Because this is not a verse about other people. It is a mirror.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Lie of the Self-Made Soul

Let me start where you actually live, far from any cave.

There is a phrase our entire age is built on. *Self-made.* The self-made man. The self-made woman. We put it on magazine covers. We mean it as the highest praise a person can receive: *nobody helped them. They needed no one. They built it alone.* We have taken the precise spiritual condition this verse diagnoses as the root of ruin — *istaghnā*, "he saw himself as needing no one" — and we have made it our civilization's definition of success.

Sit with how strange that is. The Quran names *istighnā* as the floodgate of all transgression. And we have hung it on the wall as an aspiration.

Now — I am not telling you that ambition is evil, or that building something is sin, or that wealth is forbidden. The Prophet ﷺ honored merchants; some of his closest companions were wealthy. The verse is not attacking the *gift.* Re-read it: the gift was never the problem. The water in the river is good. The problem is the **forgetting of the source.** The problem is the inner sentence — quiet, often unspoken — that begins to play once things are going well: *I did this. I don't really need anyone. I'm fine on my own.*

Here is the thing the verse understands about you that the magazine cover does not. **That sentence is a lie at the level of physics.** Right now, as you read, you are inhaling oxygen produced by trees you never planted, on a planet held at exactly the right distance from a star, your blood carrying iron forged in dying stars before this earth existed, your every thought firing across neurons you did not design and cannot repair. You are the *least* self-made thing in existence. You are sustained, this very second, by ten thousand things you neither built nor control. *Istighnā* is not arrogance about your achievements. It is **a factual error about your nature** — and the verse names it with that surgical *raʾāhu*: he *saw* himself this way. He did not *become* this way. He cannot. No one can.

So why does the lie feel so true?

Because of a cruel feature of blessing. **The better a gift works, the more invisible it becomes.** When you are healthy, you never think about your kidneys; the moment they fail, you think of nothing else. When the money is flowing, you stop noticing it is provision; when it stops, you remember Who provides. Health, wealth, security, capability — they don't announce themselves as gifts. They announce themselves as *normal.* As *yours.* As *you.* And the smoother they run, the more completely you forget they were ever given. This is the trap *istaghnā* springs: it is *strongest precisely when you are most blessed.* The fuller the river, the easier to forget the rain.

Think of Qarun, whose story the Quran tells in Surah Al-Qasas. Given treasures whose very keys burdened strong men, he said the most *istaghnā* sentence in scripture: *"innamā ūtītuhu ʿalā ʿilmin ʿindī"* — *"I was only given this because of knowledge I possess"* (28:78). *I earned it. It's mine. Me.* And the earth — the patient, God-owned earth he walked on as if he had made it — opened and swallowed him and his house. The river that forgot its banks; the flood that came for the one who caused it.

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

Here is the practical turn, and it is small enough to actually do. The antidote to *istaghnā* is not poverty — it is **attention.** It is the deliberate, daily practice of *noticing the source.* The reason Islam fills the believer's day with *al-ḥamdu lillāh* — over food, over a drink of water, over waking up, over *every* small return of breath — is not that God is insecure and needs the praise. It is that **you** are a river, and you are always one comfortable season away from forgetting your banks. Every "*al-ḥamdu lillāh*" is you reaching down and touching the riverbed, reminding the water where it comes from. The believer is not someone who needs less. The believer is someone who has stopped pretending he needs nothing. He keeps his hands open. He stays, gratefully, *faqīr* before his *Ghaniyy.*

Tomorrow, when something goes *well* — not badly, *well* — let that be your trigger. The good grade, the closed deal, the clean scan, the easy morning. In the exact moment you feel full, when the quiet "I've got this" rises in you — that is the floodwater touching the top of the bank. Meet it with three words. *Al-ḥamdu lillāh.* Touch the source. Stay a river that remembers the sea.

### Theme Two: The Mercy of the Return

There is a way to read ayah eight as a threat. *"To your Lord is the return"* — meaning: *you will be hauled back and judged, so beware.* And that reading is true. The tyrant *will* answer. Al-Muyassar says it plainly: *fal-yaʿlam kullu ṭāghiyatin annal-maṣīra ilā-llāh* — *let every transgressor know the destination is to Allah, and He will repay each soul for its deeds.* Justice is real. The flood does not get the last word.

But I want to show you a second face of this ayah — gentler, and I think deeper — because of *where* it sits.

Watch the architecture of these three lines. Ayah six and seven describe a soul **moving in the wrong direction** — overflowing, exceeding, fleeing its limits, running from God under the illusion of self-sufficiency. It is all outward motion, all *away.* And then ayah eight does not chase him. It does not build a higher wall. It simply states a fact about the *shape of reality itself:* **"Indeed, to your Lord is the return."**

[PAUSE]

Think about what that means for the runaway. He imagines he is escaping. He thinks *istighnā* is a door out — out of need, out of dependence, out of submission, out of God's reach. And ayah eight, with infinite calm, informs him: **there is no out. The geometry of existence is a circle, and its center is your Lord.** Every path, including the one you think leads away, is curved. The river that bursts its banks and floods the plain and races, as it imagines, *toward freedom* — where is it going? *Downhill. To the sea.* To the very source all water came from. *It cannot do otherwise.* Even its rebellion is, secretly, a homecoming.

This is why I find ayah eight more merciful than menacing. For the transgressor it is a warning — yes. But for the one who feels lost, who fears he has overflowed too far, who looks at his own floodwater and thinks *I have gone past where I can be called back* — for that soul, "*ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā*" is the most hopeful sentence imaginable. **You cannot get lost from a Lord to whom all returning leads.** You were never outside the circle. The return is not a punishment imposed on you; it is the deepest truth of what you are. You are *from* Him. The verse simply reminds you that you are also, always, headed back *to* Him.

And remember which Name closes the door: *Rabbika.* Not your judge — your *Sustainer.* The One who raised you stage by stage will receive you stage by stage returned. The hands that made you from a clot are the hands you are returning to. For the arrogant, that is terror. For the broken, that is the only home there is.

Here is the human door back into this. We are all, every one of us, somewhere on the river. Some seasons we run within our banks, grateful, contained. Other seasons we feel the water rising — the success that makes us cocky, the security that thins our prayer, the capability that whispers *you don't really need to ask Him for this one.* In both seasons, ayah eight is true and steady underneath us: *ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā.* It is the gravity that never switches off. The pull toward home that operates whether you cooperate or resist.

[PAUSE]

So what changes tomorrow?

Make the return *daily* instead of *final.* The genius of the five prayers is that they are small rehearsals of *al-rujʿā* — five times a day you stop the outward rush, you turn, you put your forehead on the ground, and you *return* to your Lord while you are still alive to choose it. The transgressor's whole problem was that he postponed the return until it was forced on him at death. The believer brings it forward, again and again, so the final return is not a shock but a reunion with a direction he already knows by heart. Don't wait to be carried home by the flood. Turn, today, while turning is still a gift and not yet a verdict.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### Bringing It Together

Look at how the two roads we walked are really one road seen from two ends.

The **linguistic journey** showed us a river: *yaṭghā*, water bursting its banks, the gift become the flood; opened by *an raʾāhu-staghnā*, the soul's illusion that it is full and free, reaching for *al-Ghaniyy*, a Name that belongs to God alone; and answered by the turn of the head — *Rabbika*, **your** Lord — pointing all the floodwater home, *ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā*.

The **thematic journey** showed us the same river inside our own chest: the self-made lie that feels truest exactly when we are most blessed, and the merciful geometry of a return we can either rehearse daily or have forced on us finally.

They are one insight from two directions. **Transgression is forgetting your source. Worship is remembering it. And the return is the source, patiently, drawing you back — whether you remember or not.** The whole of these three ayat is the bank of a river explaining to the water that the limit it resents is nothing but the shape of the sea it came from and is headed toward.

And step back to feel the surah's own architecture. It opened — *Iqraʾ bismi Rabbika* — "Read, in the name of *your Lord*." It pivots here — *inna ilā Rabbika-r-rujʿā* — "to *your Lord* is the return." **From "your Lord" to "your Lord."** The first revelation in human history is bracketed, beginning and turning-point, by that single intimate word *Rabbika.* Begin in His name; return to His presence. Everything the human does in between — including all his overflowing — happens inside that embrace. The honored student of ayah five and the transgressing flood of ayah six are the same *insān*; the only question the surah asks is whether he will *remember the One whose name he was told to read.*

### Questions to Carry

1. In which season does my prayer go quiet — when I am desperate, or when I am comfortable? What does that reveal about where my floodwater rises?
2. Where in my life have I quietly started to feel "self-made" — to feel that I no longer really need to ask? That is the exact place the bank is about to be crossed.
3. The verse says he *saw* himself self-sufficient. What am I currently *seeing* about myself that is, at the level of my next breath, simply not true?
4. If *al-rujʿā* — the return — is the unchangeable shape of my existence, am I rehearsing it five times a day, or postponing it until it is taken out of my hands?
5. Which of my blessings has become so smooth that I have stopped seeing it as a blessing at all — and started seeing it as just "me"?

### One-Sentence Distillation

The human being overflows into ruin the moment he *imagines* he needs no one — and the cure is to remember that the One he denied needing is the very Lord to whom, in the end, all of him is flowing home.

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to be Abu Jahl for this verse to be about you. The flood doesn't begin with tyranny; it begins with a comfortable Tuesday and a forgotten *al-ḥamdu lillāh.* So here is the invitation — gentle, not heavy. The next time things go *well,* let your success become your reminder instead of your forgetting. Touch the source. Keep your hands open before *al-Ghaniyy.* Rehearse the return before it is asked of you. You are a river. You will not stop being one. But a river that remembers the sea does not need to fear the bank — because the bank was only ever the road home.

### A Closing Du'a

*O Allah, our Rabb — the One who raised us from a clot and taught us what we did not know — keep us within the banks You drew out of mercy, not within the flood we mistake for freedom. When ease makes us forget, remind us that we are poor and You alone are the Ghaniyy; and let our return to You be a daily turning of love, not a final verdict of regret. Ameen.*
