# The Forelock and the Forehead: Dragged Down or Brought Down

---

## INTRODUCTION

Let me read you the verses first, and then I want to take you back to a courtyard in Mecca, to a specific morning, to a specific act of cruelty — because these verses are not abstract. They were spoken about something that happened.

> كَلَّا لَئِن لَّمْ يَنتَهِ لَنَسْفَعًۢا بِٱلنَّاصِيَةِ ۝ نَاصِيَةٍ كَـٰذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ ۝ فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُۥ ۝ سَنَدْعُ ٱلزَّبَانِيَةَ ۝ كَلَّا لَا تُطِعْهُ وَٱسْجُدْ وَٱقْتَرِب

*Kallā la'in lam yantahi lanasfaʿam bin-nāṣiyah. Nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭi'ah. Fal-yadʿu nādiyah. Sanadʿuz-zabāniyah. Kallā lā tuṭiʿhu wasjud waqtarib.*

> "No! If he does not desist, We will surely seize him by the forelock — a lying, sinful forelock. Then let him call his council — We will call the guards of Hell. No! Do not obey him. But prostrate, and draw near."

### Rebuilding the world

To feel the weight of these words, you have to stand in the courtyard.

The Prophet ﷺ has been praying at the Ka'bah. Openly. Bringing his face to the ground in the very center of a city whose entire economy, whose entire social prestige, ran on the idols housed in that sanctuary. And one of the most powerful men in Mecca — the classical commentators name him as Abu Jahl, "the father of ignorance" — decides he will not allow it.

The earlier verses of this same surah set the scene. Allah asks, in ayah 9 and 10:

> *A-ra'ayta alladhī **yanhā** ʿabdan idhā ṣallā* — "Have you seen the one who **forbids** a servant when he prays?"

Picture it. A man, standing over another man who has placed his forehead on the ground in worship, telling him: *Stop. Not here. Not this.* The most natural posture of humility in the human body — the forehead lowered to the earth — and someone is standing over it, forbidding it.

This is the climate. Raw confrontation. Power leaning over devotion, trying to crush it.

And then comes our passage. Notice — and hold onto this — the passage does not begin by reassuring the believer. It begins with a single, sharp syllable aimed straight at the aggressor.

### The hook

Here is what I want you to sit with as we begin.

This man forbade a *forehead* from touching the ground. And the punishment Allah promises him concerns the *front of his own head* — his forelock. And the surah ends — its very last word — with a command about the forehead again.

The same region of the body runs through this entire passage like a thread. The head that refuses to bow. The forelock that gets seized. The forehead commanded to come down.

There are two ways for a human head to go down. One of them is the most beautiful act a person can perform. The other is the most humiliating thing that can be done to them.

By the end of these five verses, Allah will have shown us both — and asked us, without ever asking directly, which one we are choosing.

## PART 1: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: كَلَّا — the word that slams the door

Let me teach you something about Arabic before we touch the verse.

In English, when we want to stop someone, we have soft tools and hard tools. "Please don't" is soft. "No." — full stop, one syllable — is hard. The hardness isn't in the meaning alone; it's in the *sound*. The abruptness. The way it ends before you expect it to.

Arabic has a word that is built entirely for this function: **كَلَّا** — *kallā*. The grammarians classify it as a particle of *radʿ* — deterrence, rebuke, the verbal equivalent of a raised hand. It doesn't merely negate a statement. It shoves back against a whole attitude. It is the sound of a door being slammed on someone's arrogance.

Now notice where it sits in our passage.

Our five verses **open** with *kallā* (ayah 15). And our five verses **close** with *kallā* (ayah 19).

> **كَلَّا** لَئِن لَّمْ يَنتَهِ...
> [ayah:96:19] كَلَّا لَا تُطِعْهُ وَٱسْجُدْ وَٱقْتَرِب ۩

Two slammed doors, and everything else happening between them.

Let's sit with this. The first *kallā* is aimed at the aggressor: *No — you think your threats will stop this? No.* The second *kallā* is aimed at the believer, at the Prophet ﷺ himself, and through him at us: *No — do not obey him. Do not yield.* And listen to what the commentators hear folded inside that second refusal. Al-Muyassar reads it as reassurance before it is a command: *the matter is not as this tyrant supposes — he will never reach you, O Messenger, with any harm.* So the "do not obey him" rests on a foundation of "he cannot touch you." The believer is told to stand firm not because the danger is real and he must be brave, but because the danger is hollow. Defiance built on the knowledge that the threat is empty.

Here's what's remarkable. The same word does two different jobs depending on who is standing in front of it. To the one drunk on his own power, *kallā* means: *your power is nothing.* To the one tempted to back down under pressure, *kallā* means: *do not give him an inch.*

It is the same divine "No," turned in two directions. Against the tyrant's confidence. And against the believer's fear.

And structurally, those two "No"s are the walls of the room. Everything in between — the forelock, the council, the guards of Hell — is contained inside this frame of divine refusal. Allah builds a room out of the word *kallā* and places the entire drama inside it.

### Element Two: يَنتَهِ — the prohibition that turns back on the one who made it

This one is quiet, and it is exquisite, and you could read this surah a hundred times and walk past it.

Go back to ayah 9: the man *yanhā* — he forbids. The root is **ن-ه-ي** (*n-h-y*), which carries the meaning of forbidding, prohibiting, telling someone to stop.

Now look at our ayah 15. The verb is **يَنتَهِ** — *yantahi*. "If he does not *yantahi*..." And here is the thing the corpus confirms: *yantahi* comes from **the exact same root**, ن-ه-ي. But it is not the same form.

Let me teach the structure, because it is everything here.

Arabic verbs are built on patterns, and one of those patterns — Form VIII, the *iftaʿala* pattern — very often makes a verb **reflexive**. It turns the action back onto the doer. Take a simple verb that means "to do X to someone," run it through Form VIII, and frequently you get "to do X to *yourself*."

- *nahā* (Form I) = to forbid **someone else**.
- *intahā* (Form VIII) = to forbid **yourself** — that is, to restrain yourself, to cease, to desist.

Do you see what Allah has done?

In ayah 9, this man is *nahā* — he is busy **forbidding the servant** of God from praying. He has appointed himself the one who tells others to stop. And in ayah 15, Allah takes the very same root and runs it through the reflexive form and throws it back at him: *la'in lam **yantahi*** — "if he does not **forbid himself**, if he does not stop *himself*..."

[PAUSE]

Stay with this. The man who made a career of telling others *stop* is now told, in his own vocabulary, in his own root: *the only person you have any business stopping is yourself.*

The grammar performs the justice before the punishment is even announced. He pointed the verb outward at a worshipper. Allah curves the verb back and points it at him. *You want to forbid? Forbid your own hand. You want to make someone stop? Stop yourself.*

And there's a small word in front of it that locks it in. **لَئِن** — *la'in*. The grammarians (and you can see this in al-Jalalayn's reading of the verse) identify that opening *lām* as the *lām* of an oath. This is not a casual "if." It is "if" preceded by a divine swearing. *By Allah, if he does not restrain himself...* The threat is under oath.

Documented fact: *yantahi* is Form VIII of *n-h-y*, the reflexive that means "to desist, to restrain oneself." What this invites us to consider is the perfect symmetry — that the surah uses the same root for his crime and for the demand placed on him, simply by changing the direction the verb faces.

And notice that the classical commentators, in naming *what* he must desist from, quietly widen the frame. Al-Jalalayn glosses *lam yantahi* as failing to desist "from the disbelief he persists in" — *ʿammā huwa ʿalayhi min al-kufr*. Al-Muyassar names it as his refusal to "turn back from his defiance and his harm" — *shiqāq* and *adhā*. Put the two together and the demand is not narrow. It is not merely *stop bothering this one worshipper on this one morning.* It is *stop the whole posture* — the disbelief underneath, the defiance it produces, the harm it reaches out to inflict. The single act at the Ka'bah was only the visible tip of the thing he was called to restrain.

[PAUSE]

### Element Three: لَنَسْفَعًۢا بِٱلنَّاصِيَةِ — the violence in the sound, and the lie in the location

Now we arrive at the threat itself, and I want you to listen to it before you understand it.

> **لَنَسْفَعًۢا بِٱلنَّاصِيَةِ**
> *lanasfaʿam bin-nāṣiyah*

Say it slowly. Feel the mouth working. *Na-sfa-ʿan.* There is a thickness in the middle of that word, a guttural catch on the ʿayn, that does not feel smooth. It feels like grabbing. It feels like force.

The root is **س-ف-ع** (*s-f-ʿ*). And here the classical lexicographers are precise. Al-Qurtubi reports the linguists' definition directly: *safaʿtu bish-shay'* — "I *safaʿtu* a thing" — means **to seize it and yank it with a violent pull**. Al-Baghawi gives the same: *to grab it and drag it hard*. This is not "We will take him." This is "We will grab him by the front of his head and **drag**." And the commentators do not leave the destination unnamed. Al-Jalalayn spells out the whole clause plainly: *la-ajurranna bi-nāṣiyatihi ilā an-nār* — "I will surely drag him by his forelock **into the Fire**." Al-Muyassar sharpens both ends of the gesture: the seizing is *akhdhan ʿanīfan* — a *violent* taking — and it ends with the man *flung into the Fire*. So the drag has a direction and a terminus. It is not a warning shake. It is a haul, by the front of the head, all the way to the edge and over it.

And listen to the very end of the word: *lanasfaʿa**n***. That final energetic *nūn* — the *nūn al-tawkīd*, the nun of emphasis, written in the muṣḥaf as that distinctive curved tail (نًۢا) — is Arabic's way of making a verb shout. It is certainty hammered onto the end of the verb. *We will absolutely, certainly, beyond all doubt — drag.* The sound of the word and the grammar of the word and the meaning of the word all do the same thing at once: they enact a violent, certain seizing.

Now — *by what* will he be dragged? **بِٱلنَّاصِيَةِ** — by the **nāṣiyah**. The forelock. Al-Baghawi defines it plainly: *the hair of the front of the head* — *shaʿr muqaddam ar-ra's*.

Why the forelock? Why not the arm, the neck, the whole body?

Think about what the forelock *is* in human experience. It is the front of the head — the part that faces forward, that leads, that we lift when we are proud. To take someone "by the forelock" is, across cultures, the gesture of total domination — you control the head, you control the person, and you do it from the front, in full view, in maximum humiliation. The Arabs knew this; al-Qurtubi notes the idiom *safaʿa bi-nāṣiyat farasih* — seizing a horse by its forelock to master it.

But here is where it opens into something vast.

This same image appears in Surah Hud, where Allah says of every living creature: *mā min dābbatin illā huwa ākhidhun bi-nāṣiyatihā* — "there is no creature except that He holds it by its forelock" (11:56). Every creature. Already. Right now. Allah is *already* holding the forelock of this defiant man — gently, invisibly, sustaining his every breath. The threat of ayah 15 is not that Allah will *gain* control of his forelock. Allah already has it. The threat is that the gentle hold will, on a coming Day, become a violent drag.

[PAUSE]

And then — ayah 16 — comes the detail that I find almost frightening in its precision.

> [ayah:96:16] نَاصِيَةٍ كَـٰذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ
> *nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭi'ah* — "a forelock — lying, sinful."

Let me teach you the device, because the device *is* the meaning. This is **metonymy** — *majāz mursal* — where you name a part to mean the whole. The forelock cannot lie. The forelock cannot sin. A clump of hair has no morality. So when Allah calls the forelock *lying* and *sinful*, He is doing something deliberate: He is transferring the man's entire moral record onto this single point at the front of his head. The lying forelock means *the lying man*. But Allah chooses to localize it — to pin the lie and the sin to the forelock specifically.

Why localize the lie there, of all places?

Here I want to offer you a modern resonance — and I'll flag it clearly as a reflection, not as classical tafsir. The forelock sits over the very front of the skull, over the region the classical scholars never had a name for but we now do: the prefrontal area, the part of the brain most associated with planning, with decision, with the construction of deliberate deception. The place where a lie is *authored* before it is spoken. The place where a sin is *decided* before the limbs carry it out.

I do not want to overstate this — the verse stands on its own without a single fact of neuroscience. But notice that Allah did not say a "speaking forelock" or a "stealing forelock." He said a **lying** forelock and a **sinning** forelock. The lie and the deliberate wrong — the two things that begin not in the hand or the tongue but in the *decision*, at the front of the head, exactly where the forelock sits.

And the classical commentators split the two adjectives along a precise seam. Al-Muyassar reads *kādhibah* as lying *in its speech* — *fī maqālihā* — and *khāṭi'ah* as sinful *in its deeds* — *fī afʿālihā*. The lie belongs to the word; the wrong belongs to the act. And then he adds an image I cannot shake: *fa-ka'anna al-kadhib wal-khaṭa' bādiyāni minhā* — "it is as though the lying and the sinning were **visibly emanating** from that forelock." As if you could look at the front of his head and *see* the deception and the wrong coming off it like heat off a road. The man's inner falsehood made legible on the very part of him he held highest.

Stay with the order, too: **kādhibah** (lying) comes *before* **khāṭi'ah** (sinning) — word before deed, exactly the seam al-Muyassar drew. What this invites us to consider — and I offer it as reflection — is that the lie comes first. Before a person commits the wrong against others, he first commits a lie inside himself. He tells himself the false story that makes the wrong feel permissible. The deception is the seed; the sin is the fruit. Lying forelock, *then* sinning forelock.

So the man who stood over a worshipper, proud, his forelock lifted high — that very forelock is named as the seat of his lie, and is marked for the drag.

[PAUSE]

### Bridge

Look at what these three words have shown us.

*Kallā* built the room — two slammed doors of divine refusal. *Yantahi* turned the man's own prohibition back onto himself through the curve of the reflexive form. And *lanasfaʿan bin-nāṣiyah* localized his entire moral collapse onto the proud front of his head — the part he lifted, the part that lied, the part that will be seized.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about reality itself. Because the man in this story did not feel afraid. He felt *protected*. He had something he was sure would shield him. And in the next two verses, Allah turns directly to that.

## PART 2: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: "Go ahead — call your people"

Let me start where you already live.

Every one of us has, somewhere in the back of our mind, a list. We don't write it down, but it's there. It's the list of who we'd call if things went wrong. The friend with the lawyer. The relative with the connections. The group chat that would have our back. The followers who'd defend us online. The institution we belong to, the family name, the network. In a crisis, we reach for it. *I know people. I'm not alone. I have backing.*

This is one of the deepest sources of human confidence — not our own strength, but our *coverage*. The web of people around us. Social capital. And the more powerful you are, the larger and more impressive that web becomes.

Abu Jahl had the largest web in Mecca.

Now watch the verse, because Allah uses a single root to expose the whole illusion. Listen:

> **فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُۥ ۝ سَنَدْعُ ٱلزَّبَانِيَةَ**
> *Fal-yadʿu nādiyah. Sanadʿuz-zabāniyah.*
> "Then let him **call** his council — We will **call** the guards of Hell."

The root **د-ع-و** (*d-ʿ-w*) — to call, to summon — appears in *both* verses. *Let him call... We will call.* Allah deliberately uses the same verb for both sides, so that the two acts of "calling" stand face to face, and you cannot help but compare them.

He has a *nādī*. The word means his council, his assembly, his clan — the prestigious circle that met at the Ka'bah, the men of influence, the social muscle of Mecca. *Let him call them.* It's almost mockery — and the commentators feel it. *Go on. Summon your backers. Call your whole impressive network. Let's see them come.*

And the divine response: *Sanadʿu* — "**We** will call." But what does Allah call? **Az-zabāniyah** — the angels who are the guards and enforcers of the Fire, beings of a power that bears no comparison to a council of frightened Meccan men.

Sit inside the mismatch. On one side: a man's social network — real, impressive, but made of flesh, fear, and self-interest. On the other side: the cosmic enforcers of divine justice. He calls *his* people; Allah calls *His*. And it is no contest. It was never a contest. The whole thing was an illusion of coverage.

Here's the movement I want you to feel. The man's confidence was never really *in himself*. It was in his web. Take away the web and he is just a forelock waiting to be seized. And the verse strips the web away in five words.

Now bring it home, because this is not about Abu Jahl. Al-Qurtubi says it explicitly: the verse, *though it came down about Abu Jahl, is an admonition for all people, and a warning to anyone who refuses obedience or prevents others from it.*

So ask yourself — gently, honestly: **what is my *nādī*?** What is the web I am secretly counting on? When I imagine myself safe, what am I leaning on? My income? My reputation? The people who'd vouch for me? The sense that I'm too connected, too liked, too established to really fall?

Because here is the quiet teaching: there is nothing wrong with having people. The error is in *what you think they can do for you* on the day it matters most. Every human network is real coverage against small troubles and *no coverage at all* against the One who holds your forelock. The believer is not someone with no network. The believer is someone who knows which call actually gets answered.

What does this change about tomorrow? It's this: the next time you feel that flush of security that comes from your standing — your connections, your backing — let it remind you, rather than reassure you. Let it prompt the one call that is never on hold: *Yā Allah.* Build *that* relationship the way the world tells you to build your network — deliberately, daily, before the crisis. Because when the *zabāniyah* are summoned, the group chat goes silent.

[PAUSE]

### Theme Two: the two ways a head goes down

Let me ask you a strange question. When was the last time you put your face on the floor?

For most of human life, lowering your face to the ground is unthinkable. It is the posture of total surrender, of defeat, of the prisoner and the captive. We spend enormous energy keeping our heads *up*. The whole project of the ego is to never, ever bring the face down. Pride lives in the lifted head.

And this entire surah began with a man trying to stop another man from doing exactly that — bringing his forehead to the ground in prayer. *A-ra'ayta alladhī yanhā ʿabdan idhā ṣallā* — the man who forbids a servant when he prostrates.

Now look at how the surah *ends*. After all the threat, after the forelock, after the council and the guards — the very last words are not aimed at the aggressor at all. They turn to the believer:

> **كَلَّا لَا تُطِعْهُ وَٱسْجُدْ وَٱقْتَرِب**
> *Kallā lā tuṭiʿhu wasjud waqtarib.*
> "No. Do not obey him. But **prostrate** — and **draw near**."

Feel the structure of the command. First, the negative: *lā tuṭiʿhu* — do not obey him. Don't stop. Don't let the man standing over you win. Then, immediately, the positive — and it is breathtaking in its defiance: **wasjud** — *prostrate*.

The man forbade the prostration. The divine response is not "keep doing what you were doing." It is *more*. *Prostrate.* Do the exact thing he is trying to forbid — and do it as the closing word of the entire revelation.

[PAUSE]

But it doesn't end there. The final word is **waqtarib** — "and draw near." The root is **ق-ر-ب** (*q-r-b*), nearness, and it comes here in Form VIII — *iqtarib* — which carries the sense of actively seeking, reaching for, the nearness. Not "be near" passively. *Move toward. Close the distance. Come.* And al-Muyassar tells us *how* that distance is closed: draw near to Him *bit-taḥabbub ilayhi* — by making yourself beloved to Him, by endearing yourself through worship and obedience. The nearness is not merely spatial, a forehead lowered to a floor. It is relational — the movement of one who wants to be loved, closing in on the One whose love he seeks. *Sujūd* is the body's posture; *taḥabbub* is the heart's reason for taking it.

Now hold the whole passage in your hand and watch the two heads.

There is the forelock — the front of the head — being **seized and dragged**. Downward, by force, away from Allah, toward the Fire. That is one way a head goes down. Against your will. In humiliation. The proud forehead that would not bow is *made* to go down, and dragged where it never wanted to go.

And there is the *sujūd* — the forehead brought **willingly** to the ground. Downward, by choice, *toward* Allah. And the result of that voluntary lowering is the very last word of the surah: *waqtarib* — **you draw near.**

[PAUSE]

Do you see it now? The same motion — the head going down — produces two opposite destinies depending on *who lowers it*.

If you refuse to bring your forehead down to Allah, the forelock will be seized and dragged down for you, and the direction of that drag is *away* — into the Fire, into distance, into the grip of the *zabāniyah*.

If you bring your forehead down yourself, willingly, in *sujūd*, the direction is the exact reverse — *near*. *Iqtarib.* You close the distance. The lowest your body can go becomes the closest your soul can get.

This is the secret the proud man could never understand. He thought keeping his head up was power and bringing it down was weakness. He had it exactly inverted. The lifted head gets dragged. The lowered head gets *near*. There is a famous teaching of the Prophet ﷺ that captures it: the servant is closest to his Lord when he is in *sujūd*. The floor, under your forehead, is the nearest you will ever be.

And there is one more thing — a sign placed in the text itself. This final verse, *wasjud waqtarib*, is one of the verses of prostration in the Qur'an. When a believer recites it, they are recommended to *do* what it says — to go down into *sujūd* right there. So the verse that *commands* prostration *causes* prostration. The instruction and the act collapse into one. You cannot even read about the answer to pride without your own forehead being invited to the floor.

What does this change about tomorrow? Tonight, or in your next prayer, when your forehead touches the ground — do not let it be automatic. Let it be a *choice you are aware of making*. Whisper *iqtarib* to yourself as you go down: *I am not just lowering my head. I am drawing near. I am doing, willingly, the thing pride forbids — and turning the lowest posture of my body into the nearest moment of my life.* The aggressor wanted that forehead to stay up. Bring it down on purpose, and feel the nearness that the forelock-dragged will never taste.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The architecture revealed

Now that we've done the work, let me show you the shape of the whole thing — because it was built with intention.

Stand back and look at the passage as a single structure:

- It is **framed** by *kallā* — "No!" — at the start (ayah 15) and the end (ayah 19). Two walls of divine refusal.
- Inside the first wall: the **threat** to the defiant head — *the forelock seized and dragged* (15–16).
- In the center: the **two callings** — *he calls his council, Allah calls the guards* (17–18). The illusion of coverage, exposed.
- Inside the second wall: the **command** to the faithful head — *prostrate and draw near* (19).

And running underneath all of it, binding it together, is the thread we started with: **the head**. The surah opened (ayah 9–10) with a man forbidding a *forehead* from touching the ground. It closes (ayah 19) with a divine command for that *forehead* to touch the ground. And in between sits the *forelock* — the proud front of the aggressor's head — marked to be seized.

The man tried to stop a head from going down in worship. So Allah promises to bring *his* head down in humiliation, and commands the believer's head down in nearness. The aggressor's project — keeping heads up — fails on every front. Heads go down in this passage. The only question the passage leaves you with is *how*.

### Bringing it together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight seen from two directions.

The grammar of *yantahi* curved the man's prohibition back onto himself — *you who forbid others, the only one you can forbid is yourself.* And the theme of *sujūd* completes the curve: the freedom he denied the worshipper turns out to be the worshipper's nearness and his own loss. The metonymy of the *lying, sinful forelock* localized his collapse to the proud front of his head — and the command *wasjud* localizes salvation to the very same region, the forehead, brought low. The *d-ʿ-w* mirror — he calls, Allah calls — is just the social-capital theme spoken in a single repeated verb.

It all converges on one image: **a head, and the two ways it can go down.**

### Questions to carry

1. What is my *nādī* — the web of people, status, or security I am secretly counting on? And have I ever honestly asked what it can and cannot do for me on the Day my forelock is held?
2. Whose prohibition am I obeying? Is there a "man standing over my prayer" in my life — a voice, a fear, a peer pressure — telling me to keep my forehead up?
3. When I make *sujūd*, am I aware that I am *drawing near* — or has the nearest moment available to me become the most automatic?
4. The lie comes before the sin. What is the false story I tell myself first, that makes the wrong feel permissible later?
5. Pride keeps the head up; nearness requires it down. Where in my life is my head still up out of fear of looking weak?

### One-sentence distillation

The same head that refuses to bow will be seized by its forelock and dragged away, while the head brought willingly to the ground is drawn near — so choose to bring your forehead down before it is brought down for you.

### The closing invitation

This passage does not ask you to be impressive. It quietly dismantles impressiveness — the council, the network, the lifted head — and leaves you with one small, defiant, world-reversing act: putting your face on the floor. Not because you are defeated, but because that is where nearness lives. The invitation is simply this: let your next *sujūd* be a decision, not a reflex. Let it be the place you go *toward* Allah, knowing it is the very thing arrogance can never do.

### A brief du'ā

O Allah, You hold every forelock, and mine is in Your hand right now — let it never be a forelock that lies, never a forelock that is dragged. Make my forehead quick to the ground and my heart quick to draw near, and when the day comes that webs and councils fall silent, let the one call I have built all my life be the call You answer. *Āmīn.*
