# The Gift With No Strings

### Surah Al-Layl, Verses 19–21

> وَمَا لِأَحَدٍ عِندَهُۥ مِن نِّعْمَةٍ تُجْزَىٰٓ ﴿١٩﴾ إِلَّا ٱبْتِغَآءَ وَجْهِ رَبِّهِ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ ﴿٢٠﴾ وَلَسَوْفَ يَرْضَىٰ ﴿٢١﴾

*Wa mā li-aḥadin ʿindahu min niʿmatin tujzā. Illā-btighāʾa wajhi rabbihi-l-aʿlā. Wa la-sawfa yarḍā.*

**"And no one has with him any favor [owed] that must be repaid — [he gives] only seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High. And he is going to be well-pleased."**

---

## Introduction: The World Before the Words

Let me ask you to do something before we open these three verses. I want you to remember the last time someone gave you a gift — and you felt the weight of it.

Not the joy of it. The *weight*.

You know the feeling. Someone does something generous for you, and somewhere in the back of your mind, almost instantly, a small ledger opens. *Now I owe them.* The dinner you have to return. The favor you'll be expected to remember. The birthday you can't now forget. The gift was real, but tangled into the gift was a thread — and the thread pulled you toward an obligation.

This is not cynicism. This is simply how human giving works. Anthropologists have a name for it. They call it **reciprocity** — the invisible web of give-and-take that holds every human society together. When someone gives you something, a debt is created. And the debt is the point. It binds us. It keeps the relationship alive. It is the glue of the social world.

Now hold that in your mind, because the Quran is about to walk us straight into that web — and then cut every single thread.

To feel the force of these verses, you have to know where they sit. Surah Al-Layl has been building toward this moment from its very first breath. It opened with oppositions: *the night when it covers, the day when it shines, the male and the female.* Then it told us the whole point of these oppositions: **"Indeed, your strivings are diverse"** — *inna saʿyakum la-shattā* (92:4). Humanity splits. It splits into two roads. And the rest of the surah draws the two travelers in unforgettable strokes.

There is the one who is stingy, who thinks himself self-sufficient, who calls the truth a lie. And there is the one the surah calls **al-atqā** — the deeply, profoundly God-conscious one — *"who gives his wealth to purify himself"* (92:17-18).

And just as we settle into admiring this generous soul, the surah does something startling. It does not move on to praise him. It *stops* — and it leans in close to inspect his hands as he gives. It asks the question we are usually too polite to ask of a generous person: *But why are you really giving?*

[PAUSE]

Here is the hook. Most of us assume that the danger of giving is **not giving enough**. The Quran is about to tell us that there is a danger hidden *inside* the giving itself — a danger so subtle that the giver may never even see it. And it will show us the one and only way to give that escapes it entirely.

Three verses. Twelve words. And in them, a complete diagnosis of the human heart, and its only cure.

Let's open them.

## PART ONE: THE LINGUISTIC JOURNEY

### Element One: The Absent Creditor — *Wa mā li-aḥadin ʿindahu min niʿmatin tujzā*

Before we touch the verse, let me teach you something about how Arabic — and really, how careful speech in any language — handles **debt**.

Imagine a sentence in English: *"He doesn't owe anyone a favor."* Notice how that sentence is built. There is a debtor (he), a creditor (anyone), and a thing owed (a favor). Three pieces. Take away any one of them and the debt dissolves. No creditor, no debt. No favor, nothing owed.

Now watch what Allah does, because He does not say it the simple way. He says:

> **وَمَا لِأَحَدٍ عِندَهُۥ مِن نِّعْمَةٍ تُجْزَىٰ**

Let's take it apart slowly, piece by piece, because every single piece is load-bearing.

**Wa mā** — "And there is not." A flat, total negation. Not "rarely," not "hardly." There is *not*.

**Li-aḥadin** — "for anyone." The word *aḥad* (root: أ ح د) is one of the great words of the Arabic language. It is the word of absolute singularity — the same root that anchors *al-Aḥad*, the One, in Surah al-Ikhlas. But here it appears in its other function: in a negative sentence, *aḥad* means **"a single soul, anyone at all."** It throws a net over the entire human race and finds no exception. Not one person.

**ʿindahu** — "with him," "owed to him." The word *ʿinda* (root: ع ن د) is a word of nearness, of *holding*. It is the language of an account. When you have something *ʿinda* someone, it sits in their keeping, on their books, waiting.

**Min niʿmatin** — "of any favor." And here the grammar tightens like a knot. That little word *min* before *niʿmah* is what Arabic grammarians call *min* of generality — *min al-istighrāq*. It does not just negate "a favor." It negates **any favor whatsoever, of any kind, however small.** It's the difference between "I don't have money" and "I don't have a single penny of any kind." The *min* hunts down even the smallest possible exception and erases it.

**Tujzā** — and now the final word, and the most extraordinary one. "To be repaid." From the root ج ز ي — *jazā* — the root of reckoning, recompense, requital. The same root that gives us *al-Jazāʾ*, the Recompense, the very name of the Day we will all stand for.

Now here is what I need you to notice, and it is everything.

**Tujzā is passive.** "To be repaid." Not "that *he* would repay." Not "that *someone* would repay." The verb is built in the passive voice, which in Arabic does something specific: **it deletes the doer.** Arabic grammarians call the agent of a passive verb *al-fāʿil al-maḥdhūf* — the *deleted* doer. The one who would have done the repaying is grammatically *erased from the sentence.*

[PAUSE]

Sit with what has just happened across these six words. Allah set up the entire architecture of a debt — the creditor (*aḥad*), the account (*ʿindahu*), the favor (*niʿmah*) — and then, one by one, He demolished every pillar of it. *No* creditor. *No* favor, not even a penny's worth. And the act of repayment itself? **Stripped of anyone to perform it.**

Why does this matter so much?

Imam al-Baghawī, in his commentary, gives us the plain meaning: *tujzā* means "that he would be recompensed and rewarded for it" — *yujāzīhi wa yukāfiʾuhu ʿalayhā*. Al-Qurṭubī sharpens it: the giver "does not give charity in order to be repaid for a favor" — *laysa yataṣaddaqu li-yujāzā ʿalā niʿmah*. And al-Jalālayn gives us the tightest gloss of all: *"and no one has with him any favor that is being repaid."* Al-Ṭabarī, relaying the early Arabic grammarians, spells out the whole scene: this giver "does not spend what he spends as a requital to a person who has a prior hand over him, nor as a return for a favor that person had once done him" — *lā mukāfaʾatan ʿalā niʿmatin salafat minhu ilayhi* — "but rather gives it among the dues of Allah (*fī ḥuqūq Allāh*), seeking the Face of Allah." Note the quiet inversion buried in that phrase: he owes no *human* creditor a thing, yet he still gives as though something *is* owed — not to people, but to Allah, whose *ḥaqq* over him he honors freely.

Put the lexicon and the commentary together and the picture becomes breathtaking. The ordinary human giver — even the generous one — is very often *settling an account.* He gives because someone once gave to him. He returns the dinner. He pays back the kindness. He is, beneath the generosity, **closing a loop.** And there is nothing evil in that; it is the glue of society.

But Allah is describing a giver who has **no open loops.** He is not paying anyone back. No one did him a favor that put him in their debt. He gives into a void of obligation — into pure, unowed space.

And once you see that, you realize the verse has quietly raised the bar of what generosity even *means.* Most of us give where we already owe. This giver gives where **nothing is owed at all.**

That is the first thing the words reveal: **true giving begins exactly where the debt ends.**

[PAUSE]

### Element Two: The Only Exception — *Illā-btighāʾa wajhi rabbihi-l-aʿlā*

Now we arrive at one of the most beautiful grammatical structures in the Quran, and I have to teach you the device before I can show you the wonder.

In Arabic, there is a tool called *istithnāʾ* — exception. It's the work the word *illā*, "except," does. *"Everyone came except Zayd."* You name a category, then you carve one thing out of it.

But Arabic grammarians noticed something. Sometimes the thing you carve out is the *same kind of thing* as the category — *"the students came except one student."* That's a *connected* exception, *istithnāʾ muttaṣil.* But sometimes the thing after *illā* is **a completely different kind of thing** — it doesn't belong to the category at all. *"The students came except — a longing for home."* That second kind has a name: *istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ* — the **disconnected**, the **severed** exception. The *illā* there doesn't mean "except"; it means something closer to **"but rather,"** **"on the contrary."**

Now look:

> **إِلَّا ٱبْتِغَآءَ وَجْهِ رَبِّهِ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ**
>
> *"...except — seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High."*

The verse just told us he is repaying *no favor.* So what is this *"except"* carving out? You'd expect it to name some other favor he's repaying. But it doesn't. After *illā* comes something from an **entirely different realm**: *ibtighāʾa wajhi rabbihi* — *the seeking of the Face of his Lord.*

This is the severed exception. The *illā* here does not connect; it **pivots.** The commentators heard this in the very word: al-Jalālayn glosses this *illā* not as "except" but as *lākin* — "but rather" — reading the line as *"but rather, he did that seeking the Face of his Lord, the Most High."* It is as if the verse says: *"He is paying back no earthly debt — on the contrary, the only thing he is reaching for is the Face of his Lord, the Most High."*

[PAUSE]

Do you feel what the grammar just did? It took the giver's motive and **lifted it clean out of the entire economy of this world.** The verse refuses to place "seeking Allah's Face" on the same shelf as "repaying favors." It will not even let them be the same *kind* of thing. One belongs to the marketplace of mutual debt. The other belongs to a different universe entirely.

Let's go deeper into the two words at the heart of this exception, because they are jewels.

**Ibtighāʾ** — "seeking, longing, striving after." From the root ب غ ي. But notice the *form* of the word. This isn't the bare verb "to want." It's a Form VIII verbal noun — a form that in Arabic carries the sense of **effort, of reaching for something with exertion, of pursuit.** This is not passive wishing. This is the heart in active motion, *straining* toward something. The giver is not merely hoping for Allah's pleasure. He is *hunting* for it.

And what is he hunting for? **Wajh** — "the Face." *Wajhi rabbihi* — "the Face of his Lord."

Now stay with me here, because the word *wajh* is doing precise and profound work. In Arabic, the *wajh* of a thing is its most essential, its most facing, its truest aspect — the part of you that meets the world, that *is* you when you turn toward someone. When the Arabs said you sought the *wajh* of a person, they meant you sought **the person themselves**, their very reality, not their gifts, not their possessions, not what they could do for you.

And that is the whole point. There is a colossal difference between seeking what someone can *give* you and seeking *them.*

The ordinary giver in verse 19 was caught in a world of seeking *things* — repayment, return, the closing of accounts. This giver in verse 20 has left the world of things behind entirely. He wants neither return nor reward as a *transaction.* **He wants the Face.** He wants the One Himself.

Here honesty requires a pause, because the classical commentators do not all read *wajh* at the same altitude. Al-Jalālayn takes it plainly as *ṭalab thawāb Allāh* — "seeking the reward of Allah" — and al-Muyassar frames the giver as one who spends *ibtighāʾa-l-mazīd min al-khayr*, "seeking an increase of good," desiring "his Lord's Face *and His pleasure.*" Others read *wajh* at its lexical root: the One Himself, sought for His own sake. But notice that even the "reward" reading does not drag us back into the marketplace of verse 19 — the reward it names is *thawāb from Allah*, not repayment from people. Whichever way you read it, the ledger of this world stays severed; the only account still open is the one kept by *al-Aʿlā.* The disagreement is only about how high the giver reaches — to the gift of the Most High, or past the gift to the Giver Himself.

And then — *al-aʿlā.* "The Most High." From the root ع ل و, the root of elevation, of loftiness, of rising above. It's a superlative form: not merely "high," but **the Highest, the Most Exalted.** And notice *where* Allah places it. He attaches "the Most High" not to "the Face" but to "his Lord" — *rabbihi-l-aʿlā*, "his Lord, the Most High."

Why does that single adjective land here, at the very summit of the giver's longing?

Because it tells you the *direction* of his reaching. Everyone reaches for something. The miser of this surah reached *downward* — for wealth he clutched, for a self he thought needed no one (92:8). This giver reaches **as high as it is possible to reach.** Past the gift, past the giver of gifts, past every created thing — all the way up to *al-Aʿlā*, the One above whom there is nothing.

[PAUSE]

So here is what these two verses, read together, reveal. Verse 19 emptied the giver's hands of every worldly motive: no debt, no creditor, no repayment. It created a **vacuum.** And verse 20 tells us what rushed in to fill it: *the Face of the Most High, and nothing else.*

A heart cannot give for *nothing.* It always gives *for* something. The miracle of this giver is not that he gives without a reason — it's that his only reason has been purified all the way up to the Throne.

### Element Three: The Word Left Open — *Wa la-sawfa yarḍā*

And now the seal. Three small words that hold an ocean.

> **وَلَسَوْفَ يَرْضَىٰ**
>
> *"And he is going to be well-pleased."*

Let me teach you the grammar first, because it is built like a vault — every part designed to keep a promise.

**Wa** — "And." A simple connector that ties the reward to everything before it.

**La-** — and here is the first treasure. That little *lām* fused to the front is the *lām al-tawkīd* — the **lām of emphasis**, the lām of the *oath-answer.* It is the sound Arabic makes when it swears that something *will* happen. We might render it: *"And most certainly..."* It is a guarantee, spoken with the full weight of divine assurance.

**Sawfa** — "shall, is going to." This is the particle of the future. But Arabic has *two* ways to say the future: *sa-* (the short one, the near future) and *sawfa* (the long one). Grammarians note that *sawfa* often carries the **expansive, the spacious** future — a "will" that stretches out, that takes its time, that is vast. It is not "in a moment." It is "across all that is coming."

Put *la-* and *sawfa* together — *la-sawfa* — and you have something remarkable: **an absolutely certain promise about a spacious, unfolding future.** The certainty of an oath, married to the patience of *sawfa.* It says: *this is coming, it is guaranteed, and it is worth the wait.*

**Yarḍā** — "he will be well-pleased." From the root ر ض و — *riḍā* — the root of deep satisfaction, of contentment, of a heart that has finally come to rest. *Riḍā* is not mere happiness. It is the state in which **the heart stops searching.** It is arrival. It is the soul saying *yes,* and meaning it forever.

Now — here is the wonder, and it is an *absence.* Look at the verb *yarḍā* and ask the question Arabic always invites: *pleased with what?*

**The verse does not say.**

[PAUSE]

The object is gone. There is no "pleased with the reward," no "pleased with the gardens," no "pleased with what he is given." Just — *yarḍā.* He will be pleased. *Full stop.*

This is the same gap we saw in *tujzā* at the very beginning, and it is no accident. The surah opened this passage by *deleting the doer* of repayment, and it closes by *deleting the object* of contentment. And in the Quran, when Arabic expects an object and Allah withholds it, the silence is never empty. **It is a vault left open so that everything can pour in.**

What will he be pleased *with*? The commentators, beautifully, do not narrow it — they let it expand. Al-Qurṭubī says: *bi-l-jazāʾ* — pleased *with the recompense.* And al-Muyassar opens the door wider still, reading the line as a divine promise: *"Allah will give him, in Paradise, that which pleases him"* — *wa-la-sawfa yuʿṭīhi-llāhu fi-l-jannati mā yarḍā bihi.* On this reading the giver does not merely *become* pleased; **Allah keeps giving him until he says, 'Now I am content.'**

Hold both readings at once — because they are the same truth from two directions. *He will be pleased* — and *He will be given until he is pleased.* The man who gave seeking nothing of this world is the one whom Allah will not stop giving until his very soul is satisfied.

And the unspecified object is the masterstroke. Because the deepest fear of every human heart is not "will I be rewarded?" It is **"will it ever be enough? Will I ever stop wanting? Will I ever come to rest?"** And to *that* fear — the fear that nothing will ever satisfy us — the verse gives an answer with no limit and no condition attached. Not "he will be pleased *with X.*" Just: **he will be pleased.** The restlessness ends. The searching stops. The heart, at last, comes home.

### The Bridge

Look at what these twelve words have built. Verse 19 emptied the giver's hands of every worldly debt. Verse 20 filled the emptiness with one thing only — the Face of the Most High. And verse 21 promised, on an oath, with no limit named, that this giver will finally come to rest.

A severed debt. A single, exalted longing. An unbounded contentment.

Now let's ask what this reveals not just about words — but about the architecture of the human heart itself.

## PART TWO: THE THEMATIC DEPTHS

### Theme One: The Tyranny of the Ledger

Let me take you back to where we began — to that small ledger that opens in your mind when someone gives you a gift.

I want you to consider how *much* of your inner life is run by that ledger. Not just gifts. *Everything.*

You do a kindness for a friend, and a part of you files it away — *remember this, they owe you now.* You're patient with a difficult relative, and you find yourself quietly keeping score — *look how much I'm putting up with.* You post something good you did, and you refresh to see who noticed. You help, and you wait — sometimes for years — for the thank-you that never quite comes, and the not-coming of it curdles into resentment.

Psychologists who study relationships have a clinical name for the breakdown this causes. They call it **"score-keeping."** And they have found, across thousands of couples and friendships, that score-keeping is one of the most reliable predictors that a relationship will rot from the inside. The moment love becomes a ledger — *I did this, so you owe me that* — it stops being love and becomes accounting. And no one has ever been nourished by an accountant.

Here is the deeper trap. The ledger does not just poison our relationships with each other. **It quietly turns even our worship into a transaction.** We pray, and we wait for the answer like a customer waiting for a delivery. We give charity, and feel owed a smooth month. We obey, and grow bitter when the hardship comes anyway — *after everything I did?* We have, without noticing, made God a debtor. We have filed Him in the ledger too.

[PAUSE]

Now hear verse 19 again, with all of this on your shoulders:

> **"And no one has with him any favor [owed] that must be repaid."**

Do you feel the *freedom* in it now?

This is a description of a soul that has **closed the ledger.** Not because no one ever did him a favor — but because he has *stopped collecting.* He gives, and the loop does not stay open in his mind waiting to be closed. He releases the gift the way you release a breath. There is no thread attached, no debt created, no quiet expectation lying in wait.

And consider what that means for the giver *himself.* The score-keeper is never free, because his peace depends on other people's behavior — on whether they remember, whether they reciprocate, whether they're grateful. He has outsourced his contentment to people who will inevitably disappoint him. **He has made himself a hostage to everyone he has ever helped.**

But the one who gives only for the Face of Allah has cut every one of those hostage-ties in a single stroke. He is not waiting for anyone's thank-you, because he never gave it to *them* in the first place — he gave it to *Allah*, and slid it across the counter to the only One who never forgets, never miscounts, never fails to repay.

This is why the structure of these verses matters so much. Verse 19 *had* to come before verse 20. You cannot reach for the Face of Allah with hands still full of other people's IOUs. **First the ledger closes. Then the heart is free to reach upward.**

So here is the question for tomorrow — and it's a practical one. The next time you give something, do something, sacrifice something — watch for the thread. Watch for the small loop your mind tries to keep open. *Now they owe me. Now I should be thanked. Now this should pay off.* And when you feel that thread forming, practice cutting it — consciously, deliberately — by handing the deed to Allah instead. *This was for You. The account is Yours. I am collecting from no one here.*

You will be astonished at how much lighter your hands become. The ledger was never protecting you. It was only weighing you down.

[PAUSE — major pause]

### Theme Two: The Hunger That Finally Ends

Let me ask you something honest. When was the last time you felt *enough*?

Not happy — we get happy all the time, in little bursts. I mean *enough.* That deep, rare, settling sensation where the wanting stops. Where you are not reaching for the next thing. Where the soul, for a moment, simply rests.

If you're like most people, those moments are terrifyingly few. Because the human heart, by its very design, is a hunger. You get the job and within weeks you want the promotion. You get the house and you notice its flaws. You get the recognition and it tastes good for an afternoon and then you need more. The philosophers called this the *hedonic treadmill* — you run and run and the scenery never changes, because the wanting regenerates faster than the having can satisfy it.

And here is the bleak truth underneath it: **nothing in this world was ever built to be your final resting place.** Every created thing is finite, and a finite thing cannot satisfy an infinite hunger. You were made with a longing too large for anything that ends. So you keep reaching, and you keep arriving, and you keep finding that arrival was not it.

The Quran knows this hunger intimately. It is the same surah, Al-Layl, that diagnosed the miser as the one who *"thought himself self-sufficient"* (92:8) — *istaghnā* — the one who imagined he had *arrived*, that he needed nothing and no one. And the surah's verdict on that illusion of arrival is devastating: *"his wealth will not avail him when he falls"* (92:11).

So when, in this same surah, Allah turns to the other man — the giver, the God-fearing one — and makes him a promise about the *end* of his striving, listen to how precisely it answers the hunger:

> **"And he is going to be well-pleased."**

[PAUSE]

Remember the grammar. *Yarḍā* — the verb of the heart coming to rest, the searching stopping, the soul saying its final *yes.* And remember the silence after it — *pleased with what?* The verse will not name it, because to name it would be to *limit* it, and this contentment has no limit.

Now feel the contrast the whole surah has been building. Two men. Both want to *arrive.* Both want the hunger to end. The miser tries to end it by *grasping* — by accumulating, by clutching, by declaring himself self-sufficient *now.* And the surah tells us his grasp closes on nothing; the hunger devours him to the end.

The giver tries to end it by *releasing* — by giving his wealth away, by emptying his hands, by reaching past every created thing to the Face of the Most High. And to *him* alone is given the one promise the human heart has been aching for since it was created: **you will, at last, be satisfied.** Not "for a while." Not "with this particular thing." Just — *satisfied.* The treadmill stops. You step off. You are home.

Do you see the staggering paradox? The man who refused to chase satisfaction in this world is the only one promised satisfaction at all. The man who let go of the gift is the one who is given until he is full. **The hunger ends not for the one who feeds it, but for the one who, for the sake of Allah, learned to stop feeding it.**

And here is where al-Muyassar's reading becomes almost too tender to bear. *"Allah will keep giving him until he is pleased."* Think about what that promises. You and I cannot even imagine what would make us *finally* content — we've never experienced it, the wanting has never once fully stopped. But Allah knows. Allah knows the exact shape of the satisfaction your soul was built for. And He is promising, on an oath — *la-sawfa* — that He will pour and pour and pour into you until even *you*, the bottomless one, the one who could never get enough of anything, finally turns and says: *enough. I am content. I want nothing more.*

[PAUSE]

So what does this change about tomorrow?

It reframes the entire question of satisfaction. Stop asking *"what do I need to acquire to feel enough?"* — that question has never once been answered in the history of the human race, and it will not be answered for you. Start asking instead: *"Am I reaching for the Face of the One who has promised me 'enough' — or am I still trying to manufacture it myself, here, with my own clutching hands?"*

And in the small moments — the next time you feel that familiar restlessness, that itch for the next thing, that quiet whisper that says *this still isn't it* — let it remind you, gently, that it isn't supposed to be *it.* Not here. The restlessness is not a malfunction. It is a compass. It is pointing you past everything that ends, all the way up to *al-Aʿlā* — and toward the day a single word will finally be true of you: *yarḍā.* He came to rest.

## CLOSING SYNTHESIS

### The Architecture Beneath the Words

Now that we've done the work, step back and see how these three verses are built — because the structure *is* the argument.

Verse 19 is **subtraction.** It empties: no creditor, no favor, no repayment, no doer. It takes the giver's hands and turns them out, until nothing of this world remains in them.

Verse 20 is **direction.** Into the emptiness it places one thing, and points it straight up: *the Face of the Lord, the Most High.* Not a thing — a *Face.* Not downward — *al-Aʿlā.*

Verse 21 is **arrival.** It seals the journey with an oath about a spacious, certain future, and a contentment so total its object cannot even be named.

Empty the hands. Lift the heart. Come to rest. That is the entire spiritual life, drawn in twelve words.

And here is one historical thread worth holding lightly. Among the narrations preserved for these verses — al-Qurṭubī reports it from Ibn ʿAbbās, through ʿAṭāʾ and al-Ḍaḥḥāk — this passage was connected to the great Companion who spent his wealth freeing the tortured and the enslaved, with no debt to repay and no return to collect; the tradition links *al-atqā* here to Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, who ransomed Bilāl as Bilāl cried out *"Aḥad! Aḥad!"* under torture. It is one tradition among the occasions classical scholars record, not the only one — so we hold it as illumination, not as the verse's sole meaning. But notice the resonance: the giver who freed others, expecting nothing back, embodying *wa mā li-aḥadin ʿindahu min niʿmatin tujzā* in flesh and blood.

### Bringing It Together

The linguistic discoveries and the themes are the same insight from two directions.

The **passive** *tujzā* deletes the one who repays — and that *is* the freedom from the ledger, the cutting of every hostage-thread.

The **severed exception** *illā* lifts the giver's motive out of this world entirely — and that *is* the reaching past every finite thing toward the only One who can satisfy.

And the **objectless** *yarḍā* — the contentment with nothing named — *is* the end of the hunger that no created thing could ever fill.

Subtract the world. Seek the Face. Come, finally, to rest.

### Questions to Carry

- When I give — money, time, patience, love — what loop am I quietly keeping open in my mind, waiting to be closed?
- Whose thank-you am I still waiting for? And what would change if I handed that account to Allah and stopped collecting from people?
- When I obey or worship, am I reaching for *the Face* of my Lord — or for a delivery I expect Him to make on my schedule?
- What am I currently chasing in the belief that *it* will finally make me feel enough? And has anything I've ever acquired actually done that?
- If Allah has promised to give me until I am content, what is my clutching trying to manufacture that He has not already promised to provide?

### One-Sentence Distillation

**The one who empties his hands of every worldly debt and reaches only for the Face of the Most High is the only one ever promised that his hunger will, at last, completely end.**

### The Closing Invitation

You don't have to become a different person tonight. The invitation is smaller, and quieter, than that. It is only this: the next time you give, watch for the thread — and, just once, cut it. Hand the deed to Allah, expect your return from no one here, and notice how the weight lifts. You will be practicing, in one small act, the entire posture of these three verses. And the One who counts what no one else sees has sworn — *la-sawfa* — that nothing given for His Face is ever, ever lost.

### A Du'a

*O Allah, the Most High, empty our hands of every debt we are quietly collecting, and fill them with longing for Your Face alone. Cut the threads that bind our giving to the praise of people, and let us give as the truthful gave — expecting nothing here, reaching only for You. And by Your oath that the sincere will be well-pleased, do not let us come to rest in anything less than You. Give us, until we are content. Āmīn.*
